Introduction and preliminaries

In this paper we give a general and rather natural condition to ensure that the Cesàro space

contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
,
as long as a rearrangement invariant space

A Banach space
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
is said to be a
Banach lattice
if there is a lattice order on
X
, say
\leqslant
, such that
for
x,y\in X
with
\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert y\right\rvert
we have
\lVert x\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert y\rVert_{X}
. By
X_{+}
we denote the
positive cone

of a Banach lattice
X
, that is,
X_{+}=\{x\in X\colon x\geqslant 0\}
.

A mapping
T
between two Banach lattices
X
and
Y
is said to be a
lattice
(or an
order
)
isomorphism
if it is a linear topological
isomorphism which preserves the order
\leqslant
. If, additionally, the operator norm of a lattice isomorphism

T\colon X\rightarrow Y
is equal one, then we will emphasize this fact by calling
T
a
lattice
(or an
order
)
isometry
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a complete
\sigma
-finite measure space. Denote by
L_{0}(\Omega)=L_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
the set of all
(equivalence classes of) real-valued
\mu
-measurable functions defined on a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
.
A
Banach ideal space

X=(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is understood to be a Banach space
X
such that
X
is a linear
subspace of
L_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
satisfying the so-called
ideal property
, which means that if
f,g\in L_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
,

\left\lvert f(t)\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert g(t)\right\rvert

\mu
-almost everywhere on
\Omega
and
g\in X
, then
f\in X
and
\lVert f\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert g\rVert_{X}
.
If it is not stated
otherwise we assume that a Banach ideal space
X
on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
contains a function which is positive
\mu
-almost everywhere on

\Omega
(such a function is called the
weak unit
in
X
). For a function
f\in L_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
we define a
support of

f
as a set
\supp(f)\coloneqq\{x\in\Omega\colon f(x)\neq 0\}
. Moreover, by a
support

\supp(X)
of the Banach ideal space

X
on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
we mean the smallest (in the sense of inclusion)
\mu
-measurable subset, say
A
, of
\Omega
such that

f\chi_{\Omega\setminus A}=0
for all
f\in X
. The existence of the weak unit is equivalent to the following condition that
\supp(X)=\Omega
.
We say that a Banach ideal space
X
is
non-trivial
if
X\neq\left\{0\right\}
.

For two Banach ideal spaces
X
and
Y
on
\Omega
the symbol
X\overset{E}{\hookrightarrow}Y
means that the embedding
X\subset Y
is
continuous with the norm not bigger than
E>0
, that is to say,
\lVert f\rVert_{Y}\leqslant E\lVert f\rVert_{X}
for all
f\in X
. If the embedding

X\overset{E}{\hookrightarrow}Y
holds with some (maybe unknown) constant
E>0
we simply write
X\hookrightarrow Y
. Moreover, the symbol

X=Y
(
X\equiv Y
) means that the spaces are the same as a sets and the norms are equivalent (equal, respectively).

We say that an element
x
in a Banach lattice
X
is
order continuous
(or has
order continuous norm
)
if for any sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
X
satisfying
0\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant\left\lvert x\right\rvert
and
x_{n}\downarrow 0
(that is,

x_{n+1}\leqslant x_{n}
and
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{x_{n}\}=0
)
we have
\lVert x_{n}\rVert\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By
X_{a}
we denote the subspace of all order continuous elements
of
X
. If
X=X_{a}
, that is, every element of
X
is order continuous, then the space
X
is said to be
order continuous
.

By
X^{*}
we denote the
topological dual space
of a Banach lattice
X
. If
X^{dd}=X
then we have the
Yosida–Hewitt decomposition

of the space
X^{*}
, namely

X^{*}=X_{a}^{*}\oplus X^{s},

where
X_{a}^{*}
is the space of all
order continuous functionals
, that is to say,
x^{*}\in X_{a}^{*}
if and only if
x^{*}(x_{n})\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
for any sequnce
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset X_{+}
and
x_{n}\downarrow 0
,
and
X^{s}
is the space of all
singular functionals
, that is to say,
x^{*}\in X^{s}
if and only if
x^{*}(x)=0
for any
x\in X_{a}
.
For an order continuous Banach lattice
X
the spaces
X_{a}^{*}
and
X^{*}
coincide. If
X
is a Banach ideal space on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)

such that
\supp(X_{a})=\supp(X)
then the space
X_{a}^{*}
coincide with the
Köthe dual
space (or
associated space
)
X^{\prime}

X^{\prime}\coloneqq\left\{f\in L_{0}(\Omega)\colon\sup\limits_{\lVert g\rVert_{X}\leqslant 1}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert f(t)g(t)\right\rvert d\mu<\infty\right\}.

Recall that
X\overset{1}{\hookrightarrow}X^{\prime\prime}
and we have the equality
X\equiv X^{\prime\prime}
if and only if the norm in
X
has the
Fatou property
,
that is to say, if the conditions
0\leqslant f_{n}\uparrow f\in L_{0}
with
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset X
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}\|_{X}<\infty

imply that

and
\|f_{n}\|_{X}\uparrow\|f\|_{X}
.

A closed linear subspace
J
of a Banach lattice
X
is called an

(or simply

) if it has
the so-called

which means that if

,

and
\left\lvert g\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert f\right\rvert
then also

(subspaces with this property are also called

). It is clear that

and the

itself are order ideals of

. Non-trivial example of an order ideal is the closure (in norm topology of the Banach ideal space
X
)
of the set of simple functions
X_{b}
(see
[
BS88
, Theorem 3.11, p. 18]
) and
X_{a}
, that is, the subspace of all functions
with order continuous norm in
X
(see
[
BS88
, Theorem 3.8, p. 16]
). An intersection and a sum of any two ideals is again an ideal
(cf.
[
MN91
, Proposition 1.2.2, p. 12]
). If
J
is a closed ideal in a Banach lattice
X
, then
X/J
is a Banach lattice
with respect to the quotient norm, that is to say,
\lVert x\rVert_{X/J}=\inf\{\lVert y\rVert_{X}\colon Q(y)=Q(x)\}
, where

Q\colon J\rightarrow X/J
is the cannonical quotient map (cf.
[
MN91
, Corollary 1.3.14, p. 32]
).

In this paper we will deal with Banach lattices but the main focus will be on
rearrangement invariant spaces
(or
symmetric spaces
),
that is to say, the Banach ideal spaces with the additional property that for any two equimeasurable functions
f,g\in L_{0}

(which means that they have the same distribution functions
d_{f}\equiv d_{g}
, where

d_{f}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mu(\{t\in\Omega\colon|f(t)|>\lambda\})
for
\lambda\geqslant 0
)
and if
f\in X
then
g\in X
and
\|f\|_{X}=\|g\|_{X}
. Due to the Luxemburg representation theorem, it is enough to
consider rearrangement invariant spaces only on three separable measure spaces, namely, the unit interval
\left(0,1\right)
or the semi-axis

\left(0,\infty\right)
with the Lebesgue measure
m
(in this case we will talk about
function spaces
) and the set of positive
integers
\mathbb{N}
with the counting measure
\#
(and then we will refer to
sequence spaces
).
Moreover, by a
non-increasing rearrangement
of a function
f\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we mean the function

f^{*}\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
defined as

f^{\ast}(t)=\mathrm{inf}\{\lambda>0\colon d_{f}(\lambda)\leqslant t\}\text{ for }t\geqslant 0,

under the convention
\mathrm{inf}\{\emptyset\}=\infty
. In the sequence case, however, we need a little modification of the above definition,
namely
x_{n}^{*}=\mathrm{inf}\{\lambda>0\colon d_{x}(\lambda)<n\}
, where
x=\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

The
fundamental function

\varphi_{X}
of a rearrangement invariant space

on
\Omega
is defined by the following formula

\varphi_{X}(t)=\|\chi_{(0,t)}\|_{X}\text{ for }t>0,

(with a fairly obvious modification when
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
\varphi_{X}(n)=\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}\rVert_{X}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
where
\{e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is the canonical basic sequence of
X
) where
\chi_{A}
, throughout, will denote the characteristic function
of a set

. It is well-known that fundamental function is quasi-concave on
\Omega
, that is:
\varphi_{X}(0)=0
;
\varphi_{X}

is positive and non-decreasing;

is non-increasing for

or, equivalently,

\varphi_{X}(t)\leqslant\max\{1,t/s\}\varphi_{X}(s)
for all
s,t\in\Omega
.

Let us recall some examples of rearrangement invariant spaces. Each increasing concave function
\varphi
on
\Omega

generates the
Lorentz function space
\Lambda_{\varphi}
on
\Omega
endowed with the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{\Lambda_{\varphi}}=\int_{\Omega}f^{*}(t)d\varphi(t)=\varphi(0^{+})\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{m(\Omega)}f^{*}(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)dt<\infty.

Recall also that for a
quasi-concave function
\varphi
the

M_{\varphi}
on
\Omega
is defined in
the following way

M_{\varphi}=\left\{f\in L_{0}(\Omega)\colon\lVert f\rVert_{M_{\varphi}}=\sup_{t\in\Omega}\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f^{*}(s)ds<\infty\right\}.

Lorentz and Marcinkiewicz spaces play quite a special role among rearrangement invariant spaces, namely they are the smallest and, respectively,
the largest (in a sense of inclusion) rearrangement invariant spaces with a given fundamental function. More precisely, for a given rearrangement
invariant space
X
with the fundamental function
\varphi
(note that every such a function is equivalent to a concave function) we have the
following embeddings

\Lambda_{\varphi}\overset{2}{\hookrightarrow}X\overset{1}{\hookrightarrow}M_{\varphi}.

Let
F
be an non-decreasing convex function on
[0,\infty)
such that
F(0)=0
and let

be a Banach ideal space on
\Omega
.
The

on
\Omega
is defined as the space of all measurable functions

for which the followng norm, the so-called

, is finite

\lVert f\rVert_{X_{F}}=\inf\left\{\lambda>0\colon\lVert F(f/\lambda)\rVert_{X}\leqslant 1\right\}<\infty.

The spaces

were introduced by Calderón
[

, p. 122]
and Lozanovskiĭ
[

]

This is a Banach ideal space. Moreover, if

is a rearrangement invariant space with the Fatou property, then the space

is like that as well.
Note also that in the case when

the space

is just the

equipped with the Luxemburg–Nakano norm.
On the other hand, if

is a Lorentz space

, then

is the corresponding

.
It is also clear that in the special situation, when

, the Orlicz–Lorentz space

coincide, up to equality of norms,
with the Lorentz space

.
Finally, if

, where

, then the space

is the

of the space

equipped with the norm

and if

for

and

.

2.
About isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}
one more time

Lozanowskiĭ’s well-known result from
[
Loz69
]
gives some geometric characterization of the order continuity property.
More precisely, it states that a Banach lattice
X
contains a lattice isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}
if and only
if the space
X
is not order continuous. Of course, every isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}
is automatically complemented in
the ambient space due to the fact that the space
\ell_{\infty}
is isometrically injective. Note also that a Banach lattice
X
contains a
(lattice) almost isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
whenever it contains a (lattice) isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}
(see
[
HM93
, Theorem 1]

and
[
Par81
, Theorem 3]
). It seems to be worth comparing this statement with the classical James result
[
Jam64
]
(often called simply
the
James’s distortion theorem
), which describes a similar phenomenon but for spaces
c_{0}
and
\ell_{1}
. Moreover, if a Banach space

X
contains an asymptotically isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
, then it contains even an isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
(see
[
Dow00
, Theorem 6]
and

[
CHL17
, Theorem 2.5]
for a
“lattice version”
).
However, in general, the lack of order continuity does not guarantee the existence of
any
isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.
Indeed, let
X
and
Y
be two Banach function spaces on
\Omega
with non-trivial intersection
X\cap Y
such that the first
one is not order continuous and the second one is strictly convex (for example, we can take
X=L_{\infty}
and
Y=L_{2}
).
Then the space

\mathcal{X}=X\cap Y\text{ with the norm }\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}=\lVert f\rVert_{X}+\lVert f\rVert_{Y},

is strictly convex and is not order continuous. Consequently, in view of the Lozanowskiĭ result
[
Loz69
]
, it contains
a lattice isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}
but clearly cannot contain an isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.
Note also that the same
trick
will work if instead of strict convexity we will require that the second space is strictly
monotone. In this case, however, the space
Z
will be strictly monotone and not order continuous, so it will contains a
lattice isomorphic but not lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
. Let us now recall the following classical result.

Theorem A

(Riesz’s lemma)
.

If
Y
is a proper closed linear subspace of a normed space
X=(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert)
, then for any
0<\varepsilon<1
there
exists
x\in S(X)
such that

\emph{d}_{X}(x,Y)\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
.

It can be demonstrated that the above result is in general not true for
\varepsilon=0
. Actually, for a Banach space
X
to have the property
that given a proper closed linear subspace
Y
of
X
there exists
x\in S(X)
with
\emph{d}_{X}(x,Y)=1
it is necessary and sufficient
that the space
X
is reflexive (cf.
[
Dis84
, Notes and Remarks on p. 6]
). In fact, James
[
Jam57
]
proved that a Banach space
X

is reflexive if and only if every continuous linear functional on
X
is norm attaining. Therefore, if
X
is nonreflexive, then there is a linear
functional, say
\varphi
, in
S(X^{*})
which does not achieve its norm. Taking
Y=\text{ker}(\varphi)
we see immediately that
Y
is a proper
closed linear subspace of
X
and there is no such element
x\in S(X)
with
\emph{d}_{X}(x,Y)=1
. On the other hand, if
X
is reflexive
and
Y
is a closed linear subspace of
X
, then applying the Hahn–Banach theorem and bearing in mind the result of James we see that there
exists
\psi\in S(X^{*})
such that
Y\subset\text{ker}(\psi)
and
x\in S(X)
with
\psi(x)=1
. Consequently, we have the following inequalities:

\lVert x-y\rVert\geqslant\left\lvert\psi(x)-\psi(y)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\psi(x)\right\rvert=1\text{ for all }y\in Y,

that is,
\emph{d}_{X}(x,Y)=1
. On the other hand, the existence of an element realizing the distance from the ideal
X_{a}
, where now
X

is a Banach ideal space, is closely related to the fact that the space
X
contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.
Anyhow, for
X=\ell_{\infty}
and
f=\chi_{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\lVert f\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(\chi_{\mathbb{N}},X_{a}=c_{0})=1
and, in a sense, this is the generic case.

Theorem B

(H. Hudzik, 1998
[
Hud98
]
)
.

Let
X
be a super
\sigma

Needless to say, the question whether the condition from Theorem

To fill this gap and avoid the impression (which the authors themselves had) that this condition may only be necessary, we will give

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
X
is a Banach ideal space over a
\sigma
-finite measure space with the semi-Fatou property
such that
\supp(X_{a})=\supp(X)
. The space
X
contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}

if and only if there exists an element
f\in X
with

\lVert f\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=1
.

Proof.

Using Theorem 1 from
[
Hud98
]
we can find a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset S(X)
with

\supp(f_{n})\cap\supp(f_{m})=\emptyset
for
n\neq m
such that
\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}\rVert_{X}=1
.
Take
g\in X_{a}
and observe that

\displaystyle\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}-g\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert f_{n}-g\chi_{\supp(f_{n})}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\lvert\lVert f_{n}\rVert_{X}-\lVert g\chi_{\supp(f_{n})}\rVert_{X}\right\rvert\rightarrow 1,

because
\lVert g\chi_{\supp(f_{n})}\rVert_{X}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Reffering to Theorem
2.1
, if a Banach ideal space
X
contains a lattice isometric copy
of
\ell_{\infty}
, then we can find

\lVert f\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=1

\lVert f\rVert_{X/X_{a}}=1
and there exist a singular functional

S\in S(X^{*})

(note only that
(X/X_{a})^{*}\approx X_{a}^{\perp}=X^{s}
, where

all singular functionals, that is to say, those

for which

for all

).
The converse of this statement is also true but before we give a short proof let us note the following result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. If
S
is a singular functional on
X
then we have the following formula

\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}=\sup\left\{\frac{S(f)}{\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})}\colon f\in X\setminus X_{a}\text{ with }\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1\right\}.

Proof.

First, observe that
S(f)=0
for all
f\in X_{a}
, so

\displaystyle\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{S(f)\colon f\in X\text{ with }\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{S(f)\colon f\in X\setminus X_{a}\text{ with }\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup\left\{\frac{S(f)}{\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})}\colon f\in X\setminus X_{a}\text{ with }\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1\right\},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that if
\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1
, then
\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})\leqslant 1
.
Therefore, we only need to prove the reverse inequality. To do this, take
f\in X\setminus X_{a}
and let

\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset X_{a}
be a sequence that realizes the distance of the function
f
from the ideal
X_{a}
,
that is to say,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})
. Now, since
S(f)=S(f-f_{n})
, so

S(f)\leqslant\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}\rightarrow\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Thus, we obtain that

\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}\geqslant\frac{S(f)}{\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})},

which ends the proof.
∎

Note that the above lemma for Orlicz–Lorentz spaces was proved in
[
KLT19
]
basing on the modular space structure.

Consequently, if there exists a norm attaining singular functional on
X
, say
S
, then
S(f)=\lVert S\rVert_{X^{*}}
for some

i.e.,

(cf. Lemma

) and the space

contains a lattice
isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
in view of Theorem

. What we have said so far may be summarized as follows.

Proposition 2.3

Let

\sigma

Then

contains a lattice isometric copy of

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a Banach sequence space such that
X\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}
, the ideal
X_{a}
is non-trivial and
X_{a}\neq X

Then

Proof.

It is well-known that the space
\ell_{\infty}/c_{0}
contains an isomorphic copy of
c_{0}(\Gamma)
, where
\text{card}(\Gamma)=\mathfrak{c}

and
\mathfrak{c}
denotes the cardinal of the continuum, that is,
\mathfrak{c}=2^{\aleph_{0}}
.
Let us briefly remind that we can proceed as follows: (1) for each irrational number
r

\{q_{n}^{r}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

of distinct rational numbers converging to
r
and put
F_{r}=\{q_{n}^{r}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
; (2) the sets
\{F_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{R}}

form an uncountable family of infinite sets such that

is finite whenever

; (3) observe that
\ell_{\infty}\equiv\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{Q})

(4) the closed linear span of

[

\text{card}(\ell_{\infty}(\Gamma))=2^{\mathfrak{c}}>\mathfrak{c}=\text{card}(\ell_{\infty})\geqslant\text{card}(X)\geqslant\text{card}(X/X_{a}),

Proposition 2.5

(G. Ja. Lozanovskiĭ, 1973)
.

Let
X

\sigma

(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)

Suppose that

c_{0}\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}\overset{T}{\longrightarrow}X\overset{Q}{\longrightarrow}X/X_{a},

where

(see Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 in
[

[

q\colon\ell_{\infty}\rightarrow\ell_{\infty}/c_{0}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ell_{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ell_{\infty}/c_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X/X_{a}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
Q
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}

3.
Some properties of the norm in the quotient space

It is clear that the quotient space
X/M
, where
X
is a normed space and
M
is a closed subspace of
X
, is also a normed space
with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X/M}
defined as

\lVert x+M\rVert_{X/M}\coloneqq\inf\{\lVert y\rVert_{X}\colon y\in x+M\}.

In simple words, this norm measures the distance from a coset to the origin of the space
X/M
, that is

\lVert x+M\rVert_{X/M}=\emph{d}_{X}(x+M,0+M)=\emph{d}_{X}(x,M)\coloneqq\inf\{\lVert x-m\rVert_{X}\colon m\in M\}.

Therefore, the study of the function
x\mapsto\emph{d}_{X}(x,M)
is parallel to the study of certain properties of the quotient space
X/M

and vice versa. For example, de Jonge
[
deJ77
]
proved that a Banach lattice
X
is a
semi-
M
space
(without going into detail,
this class includes Orlicz spaces
L_{F}
with both the Orlicz and the Luxemburg–Nakano norm and Lorentz spaces
\Lambda_{\varphi}
;
see
[
deJ77
, Examples 2.4 and 9.6]
) if and only if
X/X_{a}
is an
AM
-space (or, which is one thing,
X^{s}
is an
AL
-space),
that is to say,

\emph{d}_{X}(\sup\{x,y\},X_{a})=\max\{\emph{d}_{X}(x,X_{a}),\emph{d}_{X}(y,X_{a})\}\text{ for }x,y\in X_{+}.

From this perspective, we will show that if
X
is a rearrangement invariant Banach space, then the quotient space
X/X_{a}
is,
in a sense, also
“rearrangement invariant”
(Theorem
3.5
). This result will turn out to be crucial in the next section,
where we will deal with abstract Cesàro spaces looking for isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}
. But let’s start with some
auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach ideal space on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and let
J
be an order ideal of
X
. If
f\in X
then we have
the following formula

\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)=\inf\{\lVert f-g\rVert_{X}\colon g\in J\text{ {\it with} }\left\lvert g\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert f\right\rvert\}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
f>0
. Of course, to show the above equality we need only to prove that

\inf\{\lVert f-g\rVert_{X}\colon g\in J\text{ such that }0\leqslant g\leqslant f\}\leqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,J).

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset J^{+}
be a sequence realizing the distance, that is,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)
.
Set

\Omega_{n}=\{x\in\Omega\colon f_{n}(x)\leqslant f(x)\}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},

and put

\widetilde{f}_{n}=f_{n}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}+f\chi_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{n}}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Observe that
\widetilde{f}_{n}\leqslant f
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently,

\lVert f-\widetilde{f}_{n}\rVert_{X}=\lVert(f-f_{n})\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert\left\lvert f-f_{n}\right\rvert\rVert_{X}=\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}.

Taking limits on both sides of the above inequality, we see immediately that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-\widetilde{f}_{n}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,J).

Moreover, since
J
is an order ideal of
X
, it follows that
\{\widetilde{f}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset J
and the proof is finished.
∎

The immediate conclusion from the above lemma is the following

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach ideal space on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and let
J
be an order ideal of
X
. If
0\leqslant g\leqslant f\in X
then

\emph{d}_{X}(g,J)\leqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,J).

Proof.

Take
0<g\leqslant f\in X
. Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset J
be a sequence that realizes the distance, that is to say,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert=\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)
. It follows from Lemma
3.1
that we can additionally
assume that
f_{n}\leqslant f
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Put

g_{n}=\min\{g,f_{n}\}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
J
is an order ideal of
X
, it is clear that
\{g_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset J
. Observe also that
0<g-g_{n}\leqslant f-f_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so due to the ideal property of
X
we get that

\lVert g-g_{n}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}.

But this simply means that
\emph{d}_{X}(g,J)\leqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a Banach ideal space on
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and let
J
be an order ideal of
X
. If
f\in X
then

\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)=\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J).

Proof.

To show one of the inequalities let’s take
f\in X
and notice that

\displaystyle\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)\geqslant\inf\{\lVert\left\lvert f\right\rvert-\left\lvert g\right\rvert\rVert_{X}\colon g\in J\}\geqslant\inf\{\lVert\left\lvert f\right\rvert-h\rVert_{X}\colon h\in J\}=\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J).

On the other hand, for each
g\in J_{+}
there is
h\in J
such that

\lVert f-h\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert\left\lvert f\right\rvert-g\rVert_{X}
. Indeed, for
g\in J_{+}
set

h\left(x\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}g\left(x\right)&\text{if}&f\left(x\right)\geqslant 0,\\
-g\left(x\right)&\text{if}&f\left(x\right)<0.\end{array}\right.

Then
h\in J
and
\lVert f-h\rVert_{X}=\lVert\left\lvert f\right\rvert-g\rVert_{X}
, whence
\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J_{+})\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)
.
Since
\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J)=\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J_{+})
we conclude that
\emph{d}_{X}(f,J)\leqslant\emph{d}_{X}(\left\lvert f\right\rvert,J)

and this finishes the proof.
∎

It will turn out right away that the formula expressing the distance of the non-increasing rearrangement of
f
from the ideal
X_{a}

can be given in a fairly computable form.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
\Omega
, where
\Omega=(0,1)
,
\Omega=(0,\infty)
or
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
,
such that the ideal
X_{a}
is non-trivial. Then for
f\in X
we have the following equality

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\rVert_{X},

where
\Omega_{n}=\Omega\cap\left((0,\frac{1}{n})\cup(n,\infty)\right)
.

Proof.

We will only give a proof when
\Omega=(0,\infty)
, because the other cases are completely analogous.

To begin with, due to the fact that the sequence
\{\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is non-increasing
and bounded from below by 0, the limit of the above sequence must exists. Moreover, it is clear that we can assume that
X\neq X_{a}
,
because otherwise there is nothing to prove.

Denote

L=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}.

First of all, it is not hard to see that

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})\geqslant L,

or, equivalently,
\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}\geqslant L
for each
g\in X_{a}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert(f^{*}-g)\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\lvert\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}-\lVert g\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}\right\rvert=L,

where the equality follows from the fact that
g\in X_{a}
.

On the other hand, our assumption that
X_{a}\neq\{0\}
implies that
\{f^{*}\chi_{(1/n,n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset X_{a}

(cf.
[
deJ77
, Lemma 7.3 (ii)]
or
[
KT17
, Theorem B]
), so

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\inf\{\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert\colon g\in X_{a}\}\leqslant\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}-f^{*}\chi_{(1/n,n)}\rVert_{X}=L,

and the proof is done.
∎

Now we prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
\Omega
, where
\Omega=(0,1)
or
\Omega=(0,\infty)
, with the Fatou property.
Then for
f\in X
we have the following equality

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a}).

Proof.

We can assume that
X_{a}\neq\left\{0\right\}
,
X_{a}\neq X
and
f\notin X_{a}
, because otherwise there is nothing to prove.
Moreover, in view of Lemma
3.3
, we can also assume that
f>0
.

Let’s start by noticing that the inequality

(
\heartsuit
)

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})

is true even without additional assumptions
on the function
f\in X
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert f-g\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\}\geqslant\inf\{\lVert f^{*}-g^{*}\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\text{ and }g=g^{*}\}\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a}),

where the first inequality follows essentially from the Calderón–Ryff theorem (see
[
BS88
, Theorem 2.10, p. 114]
; cf. also Lemma 4.6 in
[
KPS82
, p. 95]
)
and the second equality follows from Lemma 2.6 in
[
CKP14
]
.
Consequently, it remains to prove the reverse inequality. To do this, we divide the proof into several parts.

1^{\circ}
. Suppose that
f^{\ast}\left(\infty\right)=0
. Then, as a consequence of Ryff’s theorem (see
[
BS88
, Corollary 7.6, p. 83]
),
there is a measure preserving transformation
\sigma
from the support of
f
onto the support of
f^{*}
such that

f^{\ast}\circ\sigma=f

m
-almost everywhere on the support of
f
. If we were able to show that for each
g\in X_{a}

there exists
h_{g}\in X_{a}
such that
\lVert f-h_{g}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}
, the proof of this part would be complete.
For this, let’s take
g\in X_{a}
and put
h_{g}=g\circ\sigma
. Due to
[
CKP14
, Lemma 2.6]
and the fact that

h_{g}^{*}=(g\circ\sigma)^{*}
, we conclude that
h_{g}\in X_{a}
. Now, since we have the equalities

\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}=\lVert(f^{*}-g)\circ\sigma\rVert_{X}=\lVert f-h_{g}\rVert_{X},

it follows that
\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})
. Combining this inequality with the inequality (
\heartsuit
), we see that we have just
finished the proof in the first case.

2^{\circ}
. Moving on to the second case, let’s assume that
f^{*}(\infty)>0
. Denote

\Omega_{\Box}=\{x\in\Omega\colon f(x)\Box f^{*}(\infty)\},

where
\Box
is one of the following relations:
\geqslant
,
>
,
=
,
<
or
\leqslant
(for example,
\Omega_{>}=\{x\in\Omega\colon f(x)>f^{*}(\infty)\}
).
Let us consider three subcases.

Case 1: Assume that
m(\Omega_{>})=\infty
. Then there exists a measure preserving transformation
\omega\colon\Omega_{>}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

such that
f^{\ast}\circ\omega=f

m
-almost everywhere on
\Omega_{>}
. Take
g\in X_{a}
and put

h_{g}=(g\circ\omega)\chi_{\Omega_{>}}.

We claim that
d_{f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g}}=d_{f-h_{g}}
. Indeed, for all
0\leqslant x<f^{*}(\infty)
we have

d_{f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g}}(x)=\infty=d_{f-h_{g}}(x),

because
h_{g}\in X_{a}
, whence
h^{*}(\infty)=0
. Furthermore, for
x\in\Omega_{\leqslant}

h_{g}(x)=0\text{ and }f(x)\leqslant f^{*}(\infty),

so for each
x\geqslant f^{*}(\infty)
we obtain that

\displaystyle d_{f-h_{g}}(x)

\displaystyle=m\{t\in\Omega\colon\left\lvert f(t)-h_{g}(t)\right\rvert>x\}=m\{t\in\Omega_{>}\colon\left\lvert f(t)-h_{g}(t)\right\rvert>x\}

\displaystyle=m\{t\in\Omega_{>}\colon\left\lvert f(t)\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g}(t)\right\rvert>x\}=d_{f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g}},

and the claim follows. In consequence, we have

\displaystyle\lVert f^{*}-g\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\lVert(f^{*}-g)\circ\omega\rVert_{X}=\lVert f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\lVert(f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}-h_{g})^{*}\rVert_{X}=\lVert(f-h_{g})^{*}\rVert_{X}=\lVert f-h_{g}\rVert_{X}.

Case 2: Suppose now that
m(\Omega_{>})<\infty
and
f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}\in X_{a}
. Put

\Gamma_{f}=\{g\in X_{a}\colon g(x)=f(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega_{>}\text{ and }0\leqslant g(x)\leqslant f^{*}(\infty)\text{ otherwise }\}.

It follows directly from the above definition that
f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}\in\Gamma_{f}
and
\Gamma_{f}\subset X_{a}
. We claim that

(
\spadesuit
)

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f,\Gamma_{f}).

To see this, for each
g\in X_{a}
let us define a function
g_{f}
in the following way

g_{f}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}f\left(x\right)&\text{if}&x\in\Omega_{>}\\
f\left(x\right)&\text{if}&x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{>}\text{ and }g(x)>f^{*}(\infty)\\
g\left(x\right)&&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Now, it is clear that
\lVert f-g_{f}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert f-g\rVert_{X}
and, due to the fact that

m\{x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{>}\colon g(x)>f^{*}(\infty)\}<\infty
, also
g_{f}\in\Gamma_{f}
. But this means that
\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,\Gamma_{f})
.
Since
\Gamma_{f}\subset X_{a}
, the opposite inequality is evident and the equality
(\spadesuit)
is proved. Consequently, since
g\in X_{a}
, so

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\inf\{\lVert f-g\rVert\colon g\in\Gamma_{f}\}=\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(m(\Omega_{>}),\infty)}\rVert_{X}=f^{*}(\infty)\lVert\chi_{(0,\infty)}\rVert_{X}.

On the other hand, since
m(\Omega_{>})<\infty
and
f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}\in X_{a}
, applying Theorem
3.4
,
we conclude that

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert=f^{*}(\infty)\lVert\chi_{(0,\infty)}\rVert_{X},

that is to say,
\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})
.

Case 3: Let
m(\Omega_{>})<\infty
and
f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}\in X\setminus X_{a}
.
Observe that
f^{*}(0^{+})=\infty
, because otherwise, keeping in mind that
m(\Omega_{>})<\infty
,
we would get
f\chi_{\Omega_{>}}\in X_{a}
which is undoubtedly a contradiction. We will show that

(
\diamondsuit
)

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=L\coloneqq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}+f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(\frac{1}{n},\infty)}\rVert_{X}.

Take
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
f^{*}(1/n_{0})>f^{*}(\infty)
and note that this limit exists because the sequence

\left\{\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}+f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(\frac{1}{n},\infty)}\rVert_{X}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is non-increasing for
n\geqslant n_{0}

and bounded from below. Now, there is a measure preserving transformation
\tau\colon\Omega_{>}\rightarrow(0,m(\Omega_{>}))
such that

f^{*}\circ\tau=f
.
Put
T_{n}=\tau^{-1}((0,1/n))
for
n=n_{0},n_{0}+1,...
and denote

f_{n}=f^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}\right)\chi_{T_{n}}

It is clear that
f_{n}\in X_{a}
for
n\geqslant n_{0}
. Moreover, since
f^{*}(0^{+})=\infty
, so

\left(f^{*}-f^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}\right)\right)\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}\geqslant f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ big enough},

and, consequently, we have

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})\leqslant\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-f_{n}\rVert_{X}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\left(f^{*}-f^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}\right)\right)\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}+f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(\frac{1}{n},\infty)}\rVert_{X}\leqslant L.

On the other hand, we get

\displaystyle L\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert f-g\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\text{ and }\left\lvert g\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert f\right\rvert\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf\{\lVert f^{*}-g^{*}\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\text{ and }\left\lvert g\right\rvert\leqslant f\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf\{\lVert f^{*}-g^{*}\rVert_{X}\colon g\in X_{a}\text{ and }0\leqslant g^{*}\leqslant f^{*}\},

where the first equality follows from Lemma
3.1
and second inequality follows from the Calderón–Ryff theorem.
Thus, to finish the proof of
(\diamondsuit)
, it is enough to show that

(
\clubsuit
)

\text{for every }\varepsilon>0\text{ and }h\in X_{a}\text{ with }0\leqslant h^{*}\leqslant f^{*}\text{ we have }\lVert f^{*}-h^{*}\rVert_{X}\geqslant L-\varepsilon.

To see this, take
\varepsilon>0
and
h\in X_{a}
as above. We can find a number, say
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\lVert h^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{m})\cup(m,\infty)}\rVert<\epsilon,

because
h^{*}\in X_{a}
(see
[
CKP14
, Lemma 2.6]
). Hence,

\displaystyle\lVert f^{*}-h^{*}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\lVert f^{*}-h^{*}\chi_{(\frac{1}{m},m)}-h^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{m})\cup(m,\infty)}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\geqslant\lVert f^{*}-h^{*}\chi_{(\frac{1}{m},m)}\rVert_{X}-\lVert h^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{m})\cup(m,\infty)}\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle>\lVert f^{*}-h^{*}\chi_{(\frac{1}{m},m)}\rVert_{X}-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geqslant\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{m})\cup(m,\infty)}+(f^{*}-h^{*})\chi_{(\frac{1}{m},m)}\rVert_{X}-\varepsilon\geqslant\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{m})\cup(m,\infty)}\rVert_{X}-\varepsilon,

and
(\clubsuit)
follows. But this means that the proof of
(\diamondsuit)
is finished as well. However, using Theorem

3.4
again, we see that

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,1/n)\cup(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X},

that is,
\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})
. But this, finally, completes the proof of the last case and, at the same time, also of the whole theorem.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
\mathbb{N}
with the Fatou property. Then for
f\in X
we have the following equality

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a}).

Proof.

The argument in the sequence case (which is probably not a suprise) is analogous to the proof of Theorem
3.5
and,
in fact, it is even easier. But we want to give a few clues that (we hope) will allow to easily modify the mentioned proof.

1^{\circ}
. For a rearrangement invariant sequence space
X
the ideal
X_{a}
is always non-trivial.

2^{\circ}
. Since
X\hookrightarrow\ell_{\infty}
(cf.
[
BS88
, Corollary 6.8, p. 78]
), so
X_{a}\hookrightarrow c_{0}

and this means that
f^{*}(\infty)=0
, whenever
f\in X_{a}
.

3^{\circ}
. Every function supported on the set of finite measure belongs to
X_{a}
.

4^{\circ}
. The inequality (coming from the Calderón–Ryff theorem):

\lVert f^{*}-g^{*}\rVert_{X}\leqslant\lVert f-g\rVert_{X},

works also for every rearrangement invariant sequence space
X
(cf.
[
BS88
, Corollary 7.6, p. 83]
).
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
\Omega
, where
\Omega=(0,1)
,
\Omega=(0,\infty)
or
\Omega=\mathbb{N}
,
such that the ideal
X_{a}
is non-trivial. Then for
f\in X
we have the following equality

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\rVert_{X},

where
\Omega_{n}=\Omega\cap\left((0,\frac{1}{n})\cup(n,\infty)\right)
.

The above corollary can be interpreted in the following way: the function
f\in X
can be approximated by a bounded function supported
in set of finite measure to within
\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon>0
can be arbitrarily small, and this function
can be selected in an explicit way. A similar result, but for Orlicz spaces equipped with an Orlicz norm, can be found in
[
KR61
, Lemma 10.1, p. 84]
.

Corollary 3.8

(E. de Jonge, 1977
[
deJ77
]
)
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
(0,\infty)
with the Fatou property such that
L_{\infty}\hookrightarrow X
.
Suppose that

(
{\mathscr{D}}_{\infty}
)

\forall f\in X\text{ }\forall A\subset(0,\infty)\textit{ with }m(A)<\infty\colon f\chi_{A}\in X_{a}.

Then for all
f\in X
we have the following formula

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=f^{*}(\infty)\varphi_{X}(\infty).

Before we provide the proof, let us note that the above condition (
{\mathscr{D}}_{\infty}
) itself plays in rearrangement invariant
spaces an analogous role to the
\Delta_{2}(\infty)
-condition in the case of Orlicz spaces. In fact, if
X
is an Orlicz space
L_{F}

on
(0,\infty)
generated by the Orlicz function
F
which vanishes outside zero and
F\in\Delta_{2}(\infty)
,
then
L_{\infty}\hookrightarrow X
and
X\in({\mathscr{D}}_{\infty})
. However, the assumptions of the above
corollary are true also in the case of Lorentz spaces
\Lambda_{\varphi}
provided
\varphi(0^{+})=0
and
\varphi(\infty)<\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose that
X
is as above and observe that the sequence
\left\{(f^{*}\chi_{(n,\infty)})^{*}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is bounded and converges uniformly to a bounded function
f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(0,\infty)}
, so in view of the embedding

L_{\infty}\hookrightarrow X
we get

\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(n,\infty)}\rVert_{X}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert(f^{*}\chi_{(n,\infty)})^{*}\rVert_{X}=\lVert f^{*}(\infty)\chi_{(0,\infty)}\rVert_{X}=f^{*}(\infty)\varphi_{X}(\infty),

where the first equality follows from Corollary
3.7
.
∎

Although we believe that the below results (Corollaries
3.9
and
3.11
) are expected, we will present proofs
of these facts as illustrations of the techniques we developed in this paragraph.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\varphi
be a quasi-concave function such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}t/\varphi(t)=0
. Then the Marcinkiewicz space
M_{\varphi}

contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the function
\psi
defined in the following way

\psi(0)=0\text{ and }\psi(t)=\frac{t}{\varphi(t)}\text{ for }t>0.

It follows from
[
BS88
, Theorem 5.2, p. 66]
that
\psi
is also a quasi-concave function as a fundamental function of the space
X^{\prime}
.
In addition, due to the condition
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}t/\varphi(t)=0
, the function
\psi
is continuous (cf.
[
BS88
, Corollary 5.3, p. 67]
).
Therefore, we can find
0<a<1
such that the function
\psi
is strictly increasing on
(0,a)
. Consequently, the function
\psi^{\prime}

is non-increasing and positive
m
-almost everywhere on
(0,a)
. Put
g=\psi^{\prime}\chi_{(0,a)}
. Then

\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\int_{0}^{t}g^{*}(s)ds=\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\int_{0}^{t}g(s)ds=\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\psi(t)=1\text{ for }0<t<a,

and, because the function
t\mapsto\varphi(t)/t
is non-increasing, we also have that

\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\int_{0}^{t}g^{*}(s)ds=\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\frac{a}{\varphi(a)}\leqslant 1\text{ for }t\geqslant a.

This means that
\lVert g\rVert_{M_{\varphi}}=1
. Moreover, we can repeat the above argument with the same result for the function

\psi_{\varepsilon}=\psi\chi_{(0,\varepsilon)}
, where
0<\varepsilon<a
, in place of the function
\psi
. Therefore, using
Corollary
3.7
, we get

d_{M_{\varphi}}(g,(M_{\varphi})_{a})\geqslant\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert g^{*}\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}\rVert_{M_{\varphi}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert g\chi_{(0,\frac{1}{n})}\rVert_{M_{\varphi}}\geqslant\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\psi_{\varepsilon_{n}}\rVert_{M_{\varphi}}=1,

for a properly choosen subsequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant space on
(0,\infty)
. If
L_{\infty}\hookrightarrow X
, then
X
contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.

Let us note in addition that the converse is not true, for example, the Zygmund space
e^{L}
(recall that
e^{L}=L_{F}
, where
F(t)=e^{t}-1
)
contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
, but it is clear that
L_{\infty}\not\hookrightarrow e^{L}
.

Proof.

Take
f=\chi_{(0,\infty)}/\lVert\chi_{(0,\infty)}\rVert_{X}
and let
\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a pairwise disjoint family of an infinite
subsets of
\mathbb{N}
such that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{i}=\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers
such that
0=\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}<...
and
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\omega_{j}=\infty
. Put
\Omega_{j}^{i}=(\omega_{j},\omega_{j+1})

for
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in N_{i}
. Consider the following sequence
\left\{f\chi_{\bigcup_{j\in N_{i}}\Omega_{j}^{i}}\right\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. It is clear that
this sequence is pairwise disjoint,
\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f\chi_{\bigcup_{j\in N_{i}}\Omega_{j}^{i}}\rVert_{X}=1
and

\lVert f\chi_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{j}^{i}}\rVert_{X}=1
, because
\left(f\chi_{\bigcup_{j\in N_{i}}\Omega_{j}^{i}}\right)^{*}=f
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}

and
\lVert f\rVert_{X}=1
. Consequently, in order to complete the proof we can refer to
[
Hud98
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\varphi
be a concave function on
(0,\infty)
such that
\varphi(0^{+})=0
. Then the Lorentz space
\Lambda_{\varphi}
contains
a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
if and only if
\varphi(\infty)<\infty
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
KPS82
, Lemma 5.1]
that the space
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is order continuous if
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
.
Therefore, due to the well-known Lozanovskiĭ result, it does not contain even an order isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}

and proves the necessity.

Now, suppose that
\varphi(\infty)<\infty
. But this means that
L_{\infty}\hookrightarrow X
and it is enough to use Lemma
3.10
.
∎

Let us add that it may happen that the Lorentz space
\Lambda_{\varphi}
contains an order isomorphic copy of
\ell_{\infty}
but not an order
isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
. Indeed, if
\varphi(0^{+})>0
and
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
, then the space
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is not
order continuous but is strictly monotone.

4.
On isometric copies of
\ell_{\infty}
in abstract Cesàro spaces

For a Banach function space on
(0,1)
or
(0,\infty)
we define the
abstract Cesàro function space
CX
as a set

CX=\{f\in L_{0}\colon C(\left\lvert f\right\rvert)\in X\}\text{ with the natural norm }\lVert f\rVert_{CX}=\lVert C(\left\lvert f\right\rvert)\rVert_{X},

where
C
denotes the
continuous Cesàro operator
defined as

C\colon f\mapsto C(f)(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\left\lvert f(t)\right\rvert dt\text{ for }x>0.

However, in the sequence case, by the
discrete Cesàro operator
we will understand the operator
C_{d}
defined as

C_{d}\colon\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\mapsto\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty},

and then the corresponding
abstract Cesàro sequence space
CX
is defined in an analogous way as above, that is to say,
as a set of all sequences
x=\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
C_{d}(x)\in X
for which the following norm

\lVert x\rVert_{CX}=\lVert C_{d}(\left\lvert x\right\rvert)\rVert_{X}

is finite

Because it always should be clear from the context which Cesàro construction we use, we will abuse the notation
a little by writing simply
C
for the continuous as well as the discrete Cesáro operator. Moreover, to avoid
some trivialities and unnecessary complications, let’s assume that we are only interested in non-trivial Cesàro spaces.
Incidentally, if the Cesàro operator
C
is bounded on
X
, then
X\hookrightarrow CX
and, consequently, the space
CX

is non-trivial.

The classical Cesàro spaces
ces_{p}
and
Ces_{p}
are only a special case of this general construction
CX
when
X=\ell_{p}

or
X=L_{p}
, respectively. We will not say more about the history and the current development of the Cesaro space theory,
referring to
[
AM14
]
and the references given there (see also
[
Ben96
]
,
[
AM09
]
,
[
ALM17
]
,
[
CR16
]
,
[
DS07
]
,

[
Jag74
]
,
[
KamK09
]
,
[
KT17
]
,
[
KKM21
]
,
[
LM15
]
and
[
NP10
]
; cf.
[
KMP07
]
).

Let us now formulate and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a rearrangement invariant Banach space on
(0,1)
,
(0,\infty)
or
\mathbb{N}
with the Fatou property such that
the Cesàro operator
C
is bounded on
X
and the ideal
X_{a}
is non-trivial. Suppose there exists an element
f\in X

and
0\leqslant a<b\leqslant\infty
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{X}=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=1

and

\lVert f^{*}\chi_{(0,a)\cup(b,\infty)}\rVert_{CX}=1
.

Then
CX
contains a lattice isometric copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.

The proof of the above theorem makes strong use of Theorem
3.5
from §3 and Theorem 16 from
[
KT17
]
. Note that Theorem 16 from

[
KT17
]
has been proved for rearrangement invariant function, but not sequence, spaces. For the missing proof, we refer to Appendix.

Proof.

Let
f\in X
be as above. Put
A=(0,a)\cup(b,\infty)
and
A^{c}=(a,b)
. In view of Theorem
3.5
, we have the equality

\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=\emph{d}_{X}(f,X_{a})=1.

Note also that
f^{*}\chi_{A^{c}}\in(CX)_{a}
(see
[
KT17
, Lemma 8]
in the case of function spaces; however, simple modification of this
argument works in the sequence case as well). Consequently, taking
h=f^{*}\chi_{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\emph{d}_{CX}(h,(CX)_{a})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert h-g\rVert_{CX}\colon g\in(CX)_{a}\text{ and }0<g\leqslant h\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert Ch-Cg\rVert_{X}\colon g\in C(X_{a})\text{ and }0<g\leqslant h\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(\text{because }h-g\geqslant 0\text{ and }CX_{a}=C(X_{a})\text{, see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{KT17}{}{}, Theorem 16]}})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lVert C(h+f^{*}\chi_{A^{c}})-C(g+f^{*}\chi_{A^{c}})\rVert_{X}\colon C(g)\in X_{a}\text{ and }0<g\leqslant h\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf\{\lVert C(f^{*})-C(g^{\prime})\rVert_{X}\colon g^{\prime}\in CX\text{ and }C(g^{\prime})\in X_{a}\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(\text{since }g+f^{*}\chi_{A^{c}}\in C(X_{a}))

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf\{\lVert C(f^{*})-{g^{\prime\prime}}\rVert_{X}\colon{g^{\prime\prime}}\in X_{a}\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad(\text{we take the infimum on a larger set})

\displaystyle=\emph{d}_{X}(C(f^{*}),X_{a})\geqslant\emph{d}_{X}(f^{*},X_{a})=1.

where the last inequality follows from Corollary
