Complete S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) SE(3) invariants for a comeagre set of C 3 C^{3} compact orientable surfaces in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3}
Yair Hayut, David Lehavi

Abstract
. We introduce invariants for compact C 1 C^{1} -orientable surfaces (with boundary) in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} up to rigid transformations. Our invariants are certain degree four polynomials in the moments of the delta function of the surface. We give an effective and numerically stable inversion algorithm for retrieving the surface from the invariants, which works on a comeagre subset of C 3 C^{3} -surfaces.



1.
Introduction

One of the classical problems in three dimensional computational geometry is
finding invariants of surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
under the group of rigid orientation
preserving affine transformations
SE(3)
, which
distinguish between non-equivalent surfaces, and are continuous with respect to
some reasonable topology.

This paper tackles this problem by borrowing ideas from geometric
invariant theory (G.I.T.). Loosely speaking, we do the following:

(1)

Find an ambient space where
C^{1}
-orientable surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
naturally reside.
Our ambient space is the space of compactly supported tempered distributions
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, where each surface is associated
with its delta function (taking the surface and its normal). We identify
these delta functions with their respective generalized Fourier coefficients.

(2)

Take the convolution of the delta function
with its “mirror images”; first along
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and then along
SO(3)
.

(3)

Bound the set of moduli points over which a surface may not be
recovered from these doubly convolved distributions (the bad points).

The first main result of this paper is an explicit description (in
definitions
3.9
and
5.3
) of
a bound on the set of bad points, for
C^{2}
-surfaces. We show that
this bounding set is relatively meagre when restricted to
C^{3}
-surfaces.
We refer the
reader to Section
2
for the precise definitions as well as the
statement of this result.

Moreover, we show that
the generalized Fourier coefficients of these distributions
are well behaved, and use this nice behavior to give the second main result:

An effective inversion algorithm over a generic point
, i.e. we recover the
surface (away from a comeagre set, and up to the action of
SE(3)
) from
the coefficients of the doubly convolved distribution.

Related work

The problem of finding invariants to surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is studied in three
different disciplines: Mathematics,
Chemistry (as a method to reproduce molecular structure given
Cryogenic Electron Microscopy)
and Computer Science (specifically in Computer Vision).
The approaches employed were also rather diverse.

As we indicated in the introduction, this work is motivated by, but not exactly analogous to G.I.T.
Indeed, in G.I.T. the general plan of attack is:

(1)

Find an ambient space.

(2)

Find elements for which the group action is “problematic”, and discard
them.

(3)

Compute the quotient.

On the one hand our group is nicer than the general linear groups,
which typically appear in G.I.T.: by Schwartz (see
[
14
]
)
real orthogonal groups act stably, so instability (in the G.I.T. sense) may
only come from
translations.
On the other hand,
we define elements as “problematic” in a much harsher way
for two reasons: The
first is that looking at the Fourier
coefficients of the double convolution
is the analogue of only looking at certain degree 4 polynomials in the
coefficients inside the ring
of invariants, so our invariants are limited.
The second is that we consider general surfaces, and not
merely algebraic ones. Moreover, in
order to get effective results, we take successive approximations.

From a classical standpoint, one may approach the problem
by endowing the space of surfaces with some topology, and construct a continuous
(generically) one to one map to some normed space.
The algebraic side of the ideas we work with here —
of using the Fourier coefficients —
originated in
[
7
]
. In
[
15
]
, the authors
presented an effective algorithm for reconstructing functions in

L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
up to
SO(3)
-action from the third degree invariants in their
Fourier decomposition. Similar ideas were rediscovered, although
not as thoroughly developed, in the Computer Vision community;
Specifically see
[
8
]
(building on earlier work in
[
5
]
). In all
these papers, the authors solved a “sub-problem” of the original problem, by
mapping the space of surface to
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, either by
using the Gauss Map, or by projection from some distinguished point;
both maps which are very far from being injective. The two main differences
between our work and the strongest previous
result along this line —
[
15
]
– are that
our arguments are geometric and not algebraic, and that
we work with
SE(3)\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\rtimes SO(3)
instead of
SO(3)
; this
comes at the cost of having a generic solution.

Even in this setting of constructing a moduli problem as a set of moduli points,
there are alternatives to the path we took:
E.g. in
[
12
]
, the author develops an algebra of differential forms
for each surface, and in
[
4
]
, the authors use the classical

slice method

of invariant theory (for
SO(3)
only though): solving the invariant problem
assuming that the
mass distribution of the surface has three distinct eigenvalues, and solving
the moduli problem for a finite group. In a recent work,
[
9
]
, Kogan, Ruddy, and Vinzant solved the corresponding problem for algebraic curves (over
\mathbb{C}^{2}
)
for the action of the projective group and its subgroups, using tools from algebraic geometry.

From a more modern perspective, a “correct” moduli problem is “What is a

good family
of algebraic varieties? Can we describe all good families in
an
optimal
manner?” In the setting of differentiable surfaces, this
approach seems to still be at its infancy —
see
[
3
,
6
,
10
]
.

Finally, in the applied computer science community there is a huge number of
attacks on the general problem of “finding good

SE(3)
-invariants of a surface”. The most prevalent are applying
Deep Learning to images of projections on of the surfaces, some discretizations
of the surface, or even spherical harmonic coefficients of the surface in
some fixed coordinate system — see
[
13
,
16
]
.
It is important to note that in the learning
community one usually looks for
stability
of features under the group
action rather than invariance. Note that here we use stability in the CS sense, i.e. “not changed
by much under some operation”, not to be confused with the G.I.T. term
of stability which appeared above. Very recently there are
attempts to use
these ideas in conjunction with invariant theoretic methods; see e.g.
[
2
]
for an overview.

2.
The main results

Before delving into the precise set up and statement of the main theorem,
we start by looking at a toy problem, which will motivate both the statement
and the outline of the proof: Suppose we want to construct translation
invariants for smooth curves
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. A functional analytic approach
would be to consider for each curve
C
the delta function of the curve
\delta_{C}
,
convolve it with its mirror image,
\delta_{C}\circ(x\mapsto-x)
, and devise some
way to reconstruct
\delta_{C}
– up to translation – from this convolution (e.g. by analyzing the Fourier coefficients of the result).
A more geometric way would be to utilize the curves normal in order
to build a surface which is “close enough” to the second symmetric product of

C
with itself.

To this end denote by
N_{x}C\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
the normal of
C
, and consider the set

S_{C}:=\{(x-y,N_{x}C,N_{y}C)\mid x,y\in C\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
. For sufficiently ”nice”
curves
C
, and away from a subset of positive co-dimension,
S_{C}
is a surface, in a four dimensional space.
Moreover, if

n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
satisfies that
\#\{y\in C|N_{y}C=n\}
is finite, then the intersection of
S_{C}

with
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times\{n\}
is simply a finite number of translated copies of
C
,
embedded into a three dimensional space. For a generic value of
n
, those copies are likely not to intersect.

A combined geometric and functional analytic way is then to redefine
\delta_{C}
as
the delta function of the image of
C
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
, consider the
convolution
(\delta_{C}\circ(x\mapsto-x))*_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\delta_{C}
,
and observe that the support of this distribution is exactly our set
S_{C}
.
Hence, given the Fourier coefficients of of the convolution, we may recover

S_{C}
with accuracy depending on the number of coefficients.

Let us explain how we will attempt to extend
these ideas to the group
SE(3)
and to surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

First, we have to find the ambient spaces in which we work (the obvious
candidate being, similarly to the toy problem above,
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
).
In order to keep the computations tangible, we don’t convolve along
SE(3)
,
but rather in two steps:
once along
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and then along
SO(3)
;
essentially using the normality of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in
SE(3)
. We want the result of
the first convolution to be supported on a 4-fold which is isomorphic to
the second symmetric product of the surface in question with itself,
and the second convolution to be supported on an 8-fold which is isomorphic
to the second symmetric product of that 4-fold. For reasons that will become
clear once we dive into the details of the construction, both isomorphisms are
away from some positive co-dimension subset.

In order to find the relevant ambient spaces we can use in our constriction we
have to perform some rough dimensions estimates: suppose
we have a
k
-dimensional manifold in a
d
-dimensional ambient
spaces on on which an
n
-dimensional group acts (in the example above

d=3,k=1,n=2
), then the convolution would give us an
(2\cdot d-n)
-dimensional
ambient space in which we have a
2\cdot k
-dimensional manifold. If we
repeat this operation twice, for groups of dimensions
n_{1},n_{2}

then we get a
2\cdot(2\cdot d-n_{1})-n_{2}
-dimensional ambient space.
Specifically, in the case
n_{1}=n_{2}=3
, then we
must
have

4\cdot d-9\geq 4k=8\Rightarrow d\geq 4\frac{1}{4}
. Luckily, our
natural candidate
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is 5-dimensional.
We stress that we do not (yet) claim
that we have showed how to perform the convolutions, and in fact, performing
the second one requires some work; we merely claim that this is what we

have
to do.

Since we convolve along
SO(3)
, and use the Gauss map to map our
surfaces to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, we will have two constraints: the first is that the Gauss map is generically
finite; the second, is that its
derivative – the shape operator – is non-constant.

The first is important since eventually we
want to consider fibers along
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and since the first convolution is along

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The second requirement is related to non-degeneracy of the convolutions.

Sadly, this means that
simple putative examples like spheres, planes and tori, fail to be good examples
in our
construction

1

this is a typical problem in invariant theory; e.g. the

we can use the unit sphere

Before proceeding we make a couple of remarks on the intuitive view above:
First, it is extremely light from a functional
analytic point of view. Our only usage of functional analysis is to recover
the support of a distribution from its Fourier coefficients. It might be
possible to find some more clever inversion algorithm which will enable denser
representation of the data than an 8-fold to represent a surface.
The second is that similar dimension arguments can be made for manifolds
in

and the group
SE(n)
.
However, harmonic analysis on these spaces is far more

and

2.1 Definition

(Delta function of a surface)
.

Let
M
be a compact orientable
C^{1}
-surface, possibly with a boundary.
The tempered distribution
\delta_{M}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{cs}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
is the unique
distribution such that for each ball
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, the value of

\int_{B}\delta_{M}
is the surface area of
\{(x,N_{x}M/{\mathbb{R}}^{3})\mid x\in M\}\cap B
.

We take our test functions from the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, so
f_{n}\in W^{2,2}
converges to
f
in the topology of
W^{2,2}
if the functions as well as their first and second derivatives converge to
f
and its first and second derivatives, respectively.

Note that this identification of surfaces with tempered distributions gives a
natural topology on the space of surfaces via the weak-* topology.
We are using Sobolev space since at some points in the argument it would be important that the topology
respects the differential structure (nearby points have similar first and second
derivatives).

2.2 Proposition-Definition

(the topology on the space of surfaces up to
SE(3)
action)
.

Recall that the weak-* topology is induced by semi-norms.
Denote the group of rigid orientable transformations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by
SE(3)
.
The group
SE(3)
acts on
{\mathcal{D}}_{cs}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
via its action
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and the quotient space

{\mathcal{D}}_{cs}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})/SE(3)
is endowed with the
topology base

\{d\in{\mathcal{D}}:\inf_{g\in SE(3)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}|\langle g_{*}d,u_{i}\rangle-a_{i}|<\epsilon\}

for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
,
a_{i}
and test functions
u_{i}
.

2.3 Lemma
.

The topology on
{\mathcal{D}}_{cs}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})/SE(3)
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

Fix distributions
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
with compact support such that
g_{*}d_{1}\neq d_{2}
for all
g\in SE(3)
. Then, for every
g\in SE(3)
there is
u
such that
\langle g_{*}(d_{1})-d_{2},u\rangle\neq 0
. For
g
that contains sufficiently large translation, the supports of
g_{*}d_{1},d_{2}
are going to be disjoint, so we can pick any bump function
u
such that
\langle g_{*}(d_{1})-d_{2},u\rangle
is far from zero at those elements. Now, using continuity and compactness of the subset of
SE(3)
with bounded translations, we can find finitely many test functions
u_{i}
such that for every
g
there is
i
such that
|\langle g_{*}(d_{1})-d_{2},u_{i}\rangle|>\epsilon
. Putting them together and letting
a_{i}=\langle d_{2},u_{i}\rangle
we obtain an open neighborhood of
d_{2}
that does not contain
d_{1}
. By symmetry, one can also find an open neighborhood of
d_{1}
that does not contain
d_{2}
.
∎

We remark that this argument is essentially the statement that finitely many sufficiently thin kernels (say, bump functions or restricted Gaussians) are enough in order to distinguish between elements in
{\mathcal{D}}_{cs}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})/SE(3)
. In other words, declaring the continuity of the functions

\min_{g\in SE(3)}\sum\|(g_{*}(d_{1})-d_{2})*u_{i}\|_{2}

is enough in order to get a Hausdorff topology on the
SE(3)
-orbits.

(Spherical harmonics)

Denote by

In order to work with irreducible representations over the real numbers,

3.
Geometric aspects I: preliminaries and translation invariance

We start this section by considering again our toy model from the beginning
of the former section: A curve in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, which we “lift” to

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
. There we claimed (without proof) that

S_{C}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
is “close” in some intuitive sense to
the second symmetric product, and that its fibers along general points in

{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
are composed of copies of
C
. We maintain that this is easy for the
curve case. However, it is harder to do when the dimensions at hand increase,
which happens in this section and even more so in section
5
.
Our solution borrows ideas from intersection theory. Before presenting it
in the generality we need we will present it in the toy model context.
Define the following sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
:

Y:=\{(g,x,n_{x})|g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},x\in c\},\quad Z:=\{(g,g+x,n_{x})|g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},x\in c\}.

Then
X:=Y\cap Z
parameterizes the points in
S_{C}\cap({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\{(n,n)|n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\})
.
If
X
has a component of dimension
>1
then the Gauss map behaves badly. For example,
it has a non discrete fiber.
In higher dimensions the analog of
X
(and it projections
on the components of
Y,Z
, and subspaces of
X
we may define)
exposes much more information on the degeneracy. That being said, we use
X

as a tool to
bound
the degeneracy, not describe it exactly.

We note that unlike in the toy problem, in our actual result we will have
to deal both with the fact that not all but merely generic fibers (the analogue
of the fibers we took on
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
) work, and even on them we will
have to deal with copies of our surfaces intersecting one another (but luckily,
only isolated points).

the main argument in this section repeats, with some crucial
differences, in Section
5
. We want the notation to
reflect this similarity, and yet keep it so that we can refer the the objects
appearing in this section in the following sections.
Finally, the only normal bundle considered throughout this work is the normal
to
M\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Hence we denote
N_{x}:=N_{x}M/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

3.2 Definition
.

Let
W
be a manifold. Denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}(W)
the collection of
subsets of
W
which are covered by a countable union of

smooth images
of the
closed
n
-dimensional Euclidean ball.
When
W
is clear from the context, we would say that
X
has
\mathcal{I}
-dimension
\leq n
if
X\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(W)
.

By the invariance of domain, for
W
of dimension above
n
,
\mathcal{I}_{n}(W)
is a proper,
\sigma
-complete ideal, and every member of it is meagre and null (of measure zero).
Clearly, every image of a set from
\mathcal{I}_{n}(W)
under a smooth map from
W
to
W^{\prime}
is
in
\mathcal{I}_{n}(W^{\prime})
.

3.3 Notation
.

We consider the action of
G={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by translation. We use
different notations for these two copies of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
since from our perspective
the first is a group whereas the latter is a homogeneous
space of this group.
Let
M\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a compact twice differentiable surface.
Denote the inclusion map
M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by
i_{M/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
.
We typically denote the action of
g\in G
on
m\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by
g\circ m
.
In cases in which this notation might cause confusion, we would denote
the action of
g\in G
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by
\eta_{g,{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
.

We define two 5-folds and one surface in
G\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle Y_{5}=Y_{5}(M):=

\displaystyle\{(g,m,N_{m})\mid g\in G,m\in M\},

\displaystyle Z_{5}=Z_{5}(M):=

\displaystyle\{(g,g\circ m,N_{m})\mid g\in G,m\in M\},

\displaystyle X_{2}=X_{2}(M):=

\displaystyle Y_{5}\cap Z_{5}.

Note that
Y_{5}
is naturally isomorphic to
G\times M
, and so there is a
natural bundles isomorphism:
TY_{5}=TG\oplus TM
.
Moreover, there are pointwise isomorphisms

T_{(g,g\circ m,N_{m})}Z_{5}\cong T_{g}G\oplus T_{m}M
.

3.4 Example

(The unit sphere running example)
.

If
M={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, then

\displaystyle Y_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,n,n)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\},

\displaystyle Z_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,g+n,n)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\},

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(0,n,n)\mid n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\}.

Similarly, it is easy to verify that whenever the Gauss map is injective
on
M
, then
X_{2}=\{(0,x,N_{x})\mid x\in M\}
).

3.5 Example

(Unit disc through the origin non-example)
.

Let
M
is the unit disc
D_{1,0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, where

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
are the points whose dot product with the north pole

N\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is trivial. Then we have:

\displaystyle Y_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,x,N)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},x\in D_{1,0}\},Z_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,x+g,N)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},x\in D_{1,0}\},

and so

X_{2}=\{(g,x,N)\mid x,x+g\in D_{1,0}\}

is 4-dimensional.

3.6 Example

(The cylinder non-example)
.

If
M={\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times[-1,1]
then

\displaystyle Y_{5}

\displaystyle=\{((a,b),(x,y),x)\mid a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},b\in{\mathbb{R}},x\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1},y\in[-1,1]\},

\displaystyle Z_{5}

\displaystyle=\{((a,b),(a+x,y+b),x)\mid a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},b\in{\mathbb{R}},x\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1},y\in[-1,1]\},

and so

X_{2}=\{((0,b),(x,y),x)\mid y,y+b\in[-1,1],x\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\}

which is 3-dimensional.

3.7 Example

(A union of two spheres example)
.

If
M
is a union of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and the translate of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}

by
x
(where
\|x\|>2
), then
(although
M
is disconnected):

\displaystyle Y_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,n,n),(g,n+x,n)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\},

\displaystyle Z_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(g,n+g,n),(g,n+g+x,n)\mid g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\},

and so

X_{2}=\{(0,n,n),(x,n+x,n),(-x,n-x,n),(0,n+x,n)\mid n\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\},

which is isomorphic to a union of four spheres.

3.8 Notation
.

Let us return to the general case.
Denote the inclusion
X_{2}\to G\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
by

i_{X_{2}/G\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}
.
Denote the composition of the inclusion map and the projection maps from

X_{2}
to the first, second
and third components in
G\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
by

\pi_{X_{2}/G},\quad\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}},\quad\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}

respectively. Since
\range(\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}})\subset M
,
we may also define
\pi_{X_{2}/M}:=i^{-1}_{M/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\circ\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
.

Finally we denote

\rho_{X_{2}/M}:=\eta_{\pi_{X_{2}/G}(-)^{-1},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\circ\pi_{X_{2}/M}=i^{-1}_{M/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\circ\eta_{\pi_{X_{2}/G}(-)^{-1},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\circ\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{R}}^{3}},

where the composition in the right term of the last equality is well defined by
the definition of
X_{2}
. Namely,
\rho_{X_{2}/M}(g,x,n)=g^{-1}\circ x\in M
.

Each
x\in X_{2}
corresponds to a point in
Y_{5}
and a point in
Z_{5}
; each
of these points has three components: a
G
-component, an
M
-component,
and an
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
-component.
The function
\pi_{X_{2}/M}
returns the
M
-component of the
point corresponding to
Y_{5}
and
\rho_{X_{2}/M}
returns
M
-component of
the point corresponding to
Z_{5}
. Rewriting the definition of
X_{2}
, we get for all
p\in X_{2}
,

(2)

\pi_{X_{2}/M}(p)=\eta_{\pi_{X_{2}/G}(p),{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}(\rho_{X_{2}/M}(p)).

As the notation suggests, we expect
X_{2}
to be a two dimensional manifold.
Since
\{0\}\times M\subseteq X_{2}
,
X_{2}
always contains a subset of dimension 2. The following definition isolates a rather large class of surfaces which behave as desired, at most places.

3.9 Definition

(Property
\star
)
.

We say that
M
has property
\star
if there is a set
X_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq X_{2}

of
\mathcal{I}
-dimension
\leq 1
such that for any

p=(g,m,N_{m})=(g,g\circ m^{\prime},N_{m^{\prime}})\in X_{2}\smallsetminus X^{\prime}_{1}

the following properties hold:

(1)

\rank S_{m}=\rank S_{m^{\prime}}=2
.

(2)

Denote
n:=N_{m}=N_{m^{\prime}}
.
Then the linear map obtained from the composition of the maps

T_{m}M\xrightarrow{(id,g_{*})}T_{m}M\oplus T_{m^{\prime}}M\xrightarrow{dN_{m},dN_{m^{\prime}}}T_{n}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\oplus T_{n}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\xrightarrow{(x,y)\mapsto x-y}T_{n}{\mathbb{S}}^{2},

is of rank
2
.

Before proceeding to tackle translation invariance, recall the
definition of the
shape operator
and prove two
properties, related to

\star
. The first evaluates the components of the tangent space of
X_{2}
at a
point
p
, and the other is a local consequence of requirement

1
of
\star
, that
enables us to compute the dimension of
X_{2}
. There are a couple of
equivalent definitions for the shape operator. We are using the following one:

3.10 Definition

(The shape operator)
.

Let
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a
C^{2}
surface,
a\in A
a point
then the
shape operator
is the linear map
S_{a}\colon T_{a}A\to T_{a}A
which takes

v
to
-dN_{a}A
.

3.11 Lemma
.

Let
M\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a twice differentiable compact surface,
let
x\in X_{2}
and let
m=\pi_{X_{2}/M}(x),m^{\prime}=\rho_{X_{2}/M}(x)
be the
corresponding manifold points.
Then if requirement
1
of condition
\star
holds for
x
then

(\pi_{X_{2}/M})_{*}
and
(\rho_{X_{2}/M})_{*}
are injective.
In particular, around
x
,
\dim X_{2}\leq 2
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\ker{\pi_{X_{2}/M}}_{*}|_{T_{x}X_{2}}
.
So, the image of
v
under the map

(\pi_{X_{2}/G},\pi_{X_{2}/M},\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{S}}^{2}})_{*}\colon T_{x}X_{2}\to T_{g}G\oplus T_{m}M\oplus T_{s}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}

is of the form
(h,0,t)
.

Let
w:={\rho_{X_{2}/M}}_{*}(v)
. Then, by the chain rule applied to
Equation
2
,
w
is the
derivative of
\eta_{g^{-1},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
, at
g=h
, operating on
0
,
which is simply
0-h
. Hence:

0=S_{m}(0)=t=-S_{m^{\prime}}(w)=S_{m^{\prime}}(h)

i.e.,
h\in\ker S_{m^{\prime}}
.

The proof of the second part is similar.
∎

3.12 Lemma
.

A point
p\in X_{2}
satisfies requirement
2
if and only if

\rank(\pi_{X_{2}/G})_{*}=2.

Proof.

Let
p=(g,m,N_{m})=(g,g\circ m^{\prime},N_{m^{\prime}})
(where clearly
g=m-m^{\prime}
).
So the image of
p
under the map
(\pi_{X_{2}/M},\rho_{X_{2}/M})
is
(m,m^{\prime})
.
Our claim is that the map induced from the composition of maps

X\xrightarrow{p\mapsto(m,m^{\prime})}M\times M\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\xrightarrow{(x,y)\mapsto x-y}G

on the corresponding tangent spaces is of rank 2.
To this end we have to show that in the compositions of linear maps below

T_{p}X_{2}\to T_{m}M\oplus T_{m^{\prime}}M\hookrightarrow T_{m}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\oplus T_{m^{\prime}}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to T_{m^{\prime}-m}G,

the image of the left map intersects trivially the kernel of
the right map (where all the maps are the bundle
maps induced from the maps above).

A vector
(x,y)\in T_{m}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times T_{m^{\prime}}{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, belongs to the kernel
of the left map, if and only if
(\eta_{-m,{\mathbb{R}}^{3}})_{*}(x)-{\eta_{m^{\prime},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}}_{*}(y)=0
if and only if

y=g_{*}x
.
On the other hand, let
t\in T_{p}X_{2}
. Let us denote

x=(\pi_{X_{2}/M})_{*}(t)
,
y=(\rho_{X_{2}/M})_{*}(t)
.
Since

n=N_{\pi_{X_{2}/M}(\tilde{p})}=N_{\rho_{X_{2}/M}(\tilde{p})}
,
by the definition of
X_{2}
,

dN_{m}(x)=dN_{m^{\prime}}(y).

Therefore, by requirement
2
in
(\star)
,

x
and
y
have to be trivial.
∎

3.13 Lemma
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, and assume that
p
is not in the image of the projection of

X_{1}^{\prime}
onto
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. Then, there are only finitely many
m\in M
such that
N_{m}=p
.

Proof.

Let
F=\{m\in M\mid N_{m}=p\}
. By the continuity of the Gauss map,

F
is a closed set. Moreover, if

F
is infinite, then by the compactness of
M
,
F
has an accumulation point.

But such a point would necessarily be in
X_{1}^{\prime}
(as otherwise, by requirement
1
, the Gauss map would be locally injective around this point), and thus
p
belongs to the projection of
X_{1}^{\prime}
.
∎

3.14 Notation
.

Let
M\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a twice differentiable compact surface.
Denote by
M_{2\times 2}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2})^{2}

the set

\{(t,(m_{1},N_{m_{1}}),(m_{2},N_{m_{2}}))|m_{1},m_{2}\in M,m_{1}-m_{2}=t\}.

Denote by
M_{2\times 2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times({\mathbb{S}}^{2})^{2}
the projection of

M_{2\times 2}^{\prime}
on the
product of the first
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and the two copies of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
; denote the
projection
M_{2\times 2}^{\prime}\to M_{2\times 2}
by
\pi_{M_{\times 2}^{\prime}/M_{2\times 2}}
.
Finally denote by

\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}
the diagonal of
({\mathbb{S}}^{2})^{2}
.

3.15 Example

(The unit sphere running example)
.

If
M={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
then
M_{2\times 2}=\{(n-n^{\prime},n,n^{\prime})\mid n,n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\}
.

3.16 Example

(The torus non-example)
.

Let us take
M
to be the torus, with the parametrization

M=\{((R+r\cos(\theta))\cdot\cos(\varphi),(R+r\cos(\theta))\cdot\sin(\varphi),r\sin(\theta))\mid\theta,\varphi\in[0,2\pi)\}.

Computing the normals and the shape operator one can see that for
\theta
such that
\cos\theta\neq 0
(i.e. not the top or bottom circle), the Gauss map is 2-to-1 and the shape operator is of full rank. In particular,
X_{2}
is two dimensional. For every
a,b\in M
such that
N_{a}=N_{b}
and they are not in the top or bottom circle,

a-b=(2R\cos\varphi,2R\sin\varphi,0)

for some
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
. This implies that
M
fails to satisfy
(\star)
(as the projection to
G
is of rank
1
).

Indeed, proposition
3.18
ahead fails for the torus. For any fiber, expect a small set, we will obtain a pair of tori intersecting on a circle.

Let
g\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Let
\tilde{M}=g+M
, the action of
g
on
M
. It is clear that applying the definition of
M_{2\times 2}
for
g+M
would result in the same object, since the normals remain unmodified by the translation and so are the differences between pairs of points. Thus we get:

3.17 Proposition
.

the construction of
M_{2\times 2}
is invariant under the actions of
translations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

3.18 Proposition
.

Suppose
M
satisfies property
\star
, then there is an open dense
subset
W\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, of full measure (relative to the image of the Gauss map),
so that for any point
p\in W
, the intersection
of
M_{2\times 2}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\{p\}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is non-empty and
composed of a finite number
of translations of
M
,
\{g_{1}\circ M,\dots,g_{n}\circ M\}
, intersecting at most
on isolated points along the pullback of the diagonal and its antipodal image,
\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\subset({\mathbb{S}}^{2})^{2}
,
under the projection from
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times({\mathbb{S}}^{2})^{2}
on the second component.

Moreover, each of these copies is continuous in
p
, in the
sense that the set of
g_{i}\in G
is continuous in
p
.

Proof.

First, the set
W
of all
p
satisfying the conclusion of the
proposition is clearly open. We will show that its complement
has
\mathcal{I}
-dimension
\leq 1
. This is enough, as the image of the Gauss
map on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
has positive measure.

Let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{4}\in M
be four points so that

\begin{matrix}(m_{1}-m_{2},(m_{1},N_{m_{1}}),(m_{2},N_{m_{2}})),&\\
(m_{3}-m_{4},(m_{3},N_{m_{3}}),(m_{4},N_{m_{4}}))&\in M_{2\times 2}^{\prime}\end{matrix}

are both mapped to the same point in
M_{2\times 2}
; i.e. we have:

(3)

m_{1}-m_{2}=m_{3}-m_{4},\quad N_{m_{1}}=N_{m_{3}},\quad N_{m_{2}}=N_{m_{4}}.

Reordering the equations, we have that

x_{i}=(m_{i}-m_{i+2},m_{i},N_{m_{i}})\in X_{2},\text{ for }i=1,2.

Note that the fiber
p
is the projection to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
of the point
x_{2}
.

Let us bound from above the solution set to this equation, partitioning to
three cases, where at each case

we obtain a closed set
F_{c}
,
of
\mathcal{I}
-dimension
\leq 1
such that
for every
p\notin F_{c}
, the corresponding case behaves nicely:

Case 1:
The diagonal case; i.e.
N_{m_{1}}=\pm N_{m_{2}}
(and then

N_{m_{3}}=\pm N_{m_{4}}
). Let

F_{1}=\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}(X_{1}^{\prime})\cup a\circ\pi_{X_{2}/{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}(X_{1}^{\prime}),

where

is the antipodal map.
This is a set of
\mathcal{I}
-dimension
\leq 1
.

For every

, the fiber
of the Gauss map is finite.
Note that we know the location of the intersection points beforehand,
since they are on the pullbacks of the diagonals.

Case 2:

Assume
p\notin F_{1}
. So, we have (by the definition) that
x_{2}\notin X_{1}^{\prime}
.
Further assume that
x_{1}\in X^{\prime}_{1}
.
We rearrange the left equality of Equation
3
, to the

m_{1}-m_{3}=m_{2}-m_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since on

this equation on
X_{2}\times X^{\prime}_{1}
is at most of one

-dimensional,
and more precisely for

such that

corresponds to a

Thus, this case contributes at most one

be the projection of this

.

and the corresponding normals are different (since we have already dealt

There is a

Let

.
The points in

for which

is not one to one – and
where all four relevant points of

are in

– are

with the

images of the solutions set of

(4)

f(q_{1})-f(q_{2})=f(p_{3})-f(q_{4}),\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\nabla f|_{q_{1}}}{\|\nabla f|_{q_{1}}\|}=\frac{\nabla f|_{q_{3}}}{\|\nabla f|_{q_{3}}\|},\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\nabla f|_{q_{2}}}{\|\nabla f|_{q_{2}}\|}=\frac{\nabla f|_{q_{4}}}{\|\nabla f|_{q_{4}}\|},

for

.
Denote by

the solution set of Equation

,

,

Define the map:

\displaystyle(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})

(respectively

4.10 Remark

5.
Geometric aspects II: rotation invariance

There are two major differences between our treatment of the rotations
and our previous treatment in the translations, from Section
3

of

for reconstructing the surface, in the case of the rotations

8.
Open Problems

We conclude this paper with a list of open problems.

8.1 Question
.

Are there two smooth manifolds with the same 4-degree invariants which are not in the same
SE(3)
-orbit?
Similarly, are there two separated sequences of smooth manifolds such that their invariants converge pointwise to the same value?

By our main result, this situation cannot occur in manifolds that satisfy
(\star\star)
.

In the definition of properties
(\star)
and
(\star\star)
, the first requirement
is designed to make the reconstruction using fiber selection work, in the sense that most
fibers correspond to finitely many copies of the manifold.

8.2 Question
.

Can we weaken the requirements of
(\star),(\star\star)
, potentially making the corresponding collection of bad points
X_{1}^{\prime},X_{3}^{\prime}
smaller?
In particular, can we replace the first requirements with the conclusions of lemmas
3.11
and
5.5
respectively (i.e. the local injectivity of the projection from
X_{2},X_{4}
to the manifold)?

8.3 Question
.

Is there a better way to pick the fiber in the reconstruction algorithm (instead of
picking it randomly)?
In particular, is there an effective way to select a fiber for which the copies of
M
are
most separated? Can one achieve a better algorithm by considering several fibers together ?

In section
6
, we translated our geometrical algorithm into a
computation based on the coefficients of the delta function of the manifold
(in the generation of the invariants and in the reconstruction).
Our choice of basis makes both computations heavy.

8.4 Question
.

Is there a better choice of
representation, in the functional analytic side, that can reduce the
computational cost of the algorithm, and produce a denser representation of
the invariants?

We are using the functional analytic tools in a very weak sense.
In particular, while concentrating on a single fiber we effectively loss almost all
information from the other parts of the distribution.

8.5 Question
.

Is there a more efficient way to use the information from the invariants
in order to reconstruct the manifold?

A positive answer to the following question will likely require a different, less geometrical, approach.

8.6 Question
.

Is there a way to effectively reconstruct the surface using a collection of 3-degree invariants, or even 2-degree invariants?

8.7 Question
.

In the proof of the main theorem, we stated that for every error
\epsilon
in the coefficients of the reconstructed manifold, there is a sufficiently small
\epsilon^{\prime}
and sufficiently large
d,d^{\prime}
that guarantee this level of accuracy. What is the connection between
\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\prime},d,d^{\prime}
?
