Bayesian Modeling with Spatial Curvature Processes
Aritra Halder, Sudipto Banerjee, Dipak K. Dey

Abstract
Spatial process models are widely used for modeling point-referenced variables arising from diverse scientific domains. Analyzing the resulting random surface provides deeper insights into the nature of latent dependence within the studied response. We develop Bayesian modeling and inference for rapid changes on the response surface to assess directional curvature along a given trajectory. Such trajectories or curves of rapid change, often referred to as wombling boundaries, occur in geographic space in the form of rivers in a flood plain, roads, mountains or plateaus or other topographic features leading to high gradients on the response surface. We demonstrate fully model based Bayesian inference on directional curvature processes to analyze differential behavior in responses along wombling boundaries. We illustrate our methodology with a number of simulated experiments followed by multiple applications featuring the Boston Housing data; Meuse river data; and temperature data from the Northeastern United States.



Keywords—
Bayesian modeling, Directional Curvature, Gaussian Processes, Wombling.

(

Specific inferential interest resides with local features of the surface including rates of change of the process at points and along “spatial boundaries” to understand the behavior of the underlying process and identify lurking explanatory variables or risk factors. This exercise is often referred to as “wombling”, named after a seminal paper by

(

;
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
;
Qu et al. 2021
)
.

Our current contribution develops Bayesian inference for spatial curvature along curves on Euclidean domains. Modeling curvature will require smoothness considerations of the process
(
Adler 1981
;
Kent 1989
;
Stein 1999
;
Banerjee & Gelfand 2003
)
. Observations over a finite set of locations from these processes cannot visually inform about smoothness. Therefore, smoothness of the process is specified from mechanistic considerations which can be introduced through prior specifications as needed.
While Bayesian inference for first order derivatives and directional gradients have received considerable attention
(see, e.g.,
Morris et al. 1993
;
Banerjee et al. 2003
;
Majumdar et al. 2006
;
Liang et al. 2009
;
Heaton 2014
;

;

;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Qu et al. 2021
, for inferential developments involving spatial gradients from diverse modeling and application perspectives)
such processes inform about directional change, but do not enable inference on curvature (departure from flatness) of the spatial surface.

Analyzing surface roughness from sampling considerations can be traced at least as far back as
Greenwood 1984
. We offer full inference with uncertainty quantification about spatial curvature at a point and average curvature along a curve from observed data after accounting for explanatory variables. Considering second-order finite differences we establish a valid spatial curvature process as a limit of such finite difference processes. When formulating directional curvature, we favor the normal direction corresponding to a chosen curve and devise a “wombling” measure to track curvature of the surface along the curve. We derive and exploit analytical expressions of higher order processes to avoid numerical finite differences. The Bayesian inferential framework delivers exact posterior inference for the above constructs on the response as well as latent (or residual) processes.

Spatial Curvature Processes

Let
\{Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}):\mbox{$\bf s$}\in{\cal S}\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\}
be a univariate weakly stationary random field with zero mean, finite second moment and a positive definite covariance function
K(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})=\mathrm{Cov}\left(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})\right)
for locations
\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{d}$}
.
In particular, under
isotropy
we assume
K(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})=\widetilde{K}\left(||\mbox{$\bf s$}-\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}||\right)
, where
||\mbox{$\bf s$}-\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}||
is the Euclidean distance between the locations
\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}

(
Matérn 2013
)
.
Building upon notions of mean square smoothness
(
Stein 1999
, see, e.g.,)
at an arbitrary location
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we focus upon second order differentiability,
\displaystyle Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}+h\mbox{$\bf u$})=Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})+h\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})+h^{2}\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})\mbox{$\bf u$}/2+r_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0},h^{2}||\mbox{$\bf u$}||)
, where
r_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0},h^{2}||\mbox{$\bf u$}||)/h^{2}\to 0
as
h\to 0
in the
L_{2}
sense and
\nabla
and
\nabla^{2}
are the gradient and Hessian operators, respectively.

For the scalar
h
and unit vectors
\bf u
,
\bf v
, we define
\displaystyle Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})=(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}+h(\mbox{$\bf u$}+\mbox{$\bf v$}))-Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}+h\mbox{$\bf u$})-Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}+h\mbox{$\bf v$})+Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))/h^{2}

to be the second order finite difference processes in the directions
\bf u
,
\bf v
at scale
h
. Being a linear function of stationary processes it is well-defined. Passing to limits,
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})=\lim_{h\to 0}Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})
. Provided the limit exists,
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})
is defined as the directional curvature process. If
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is a mean square second order differentiable process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for every
\mbox{$\bf s$}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{d}$}
then
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf v$}
is well-defined with

D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\lim_{h\to 0}\left(h^{2}\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf v$}+\widetilde{r_{2}}\right)/h^{2}=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf v$}
, where
\widetilde{r_{2}}=r_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$},h^{2}||\mbox{$\bf u$}+\mbox{$\bf v$}||)-r_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$},h^{2}||\mbox{$\bf u$}||)-r_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$},h^{2}||\mbox{$\bf v$}||)
.
In practice, we need only work with computing these derivatives for an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, say the Euclidean canonical unit vectors along each axis
\{\mbox{$\bf e$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{$\bf e$}_{d}\}
. If
\mbox{$\bf u$}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i}
, and
\mbox{$\bf v$}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i}
are arbitrary unit vectors, we can compute
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{i}D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i},\mbox{$\bf e$}_{j}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})v_{j}
.
The directional curvature process is linear in the sense that

D^{(2)}_{-\mbox{$\bf u$},-\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
,
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},-\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=D^{(2)}_{-\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=-D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. Since
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf w$},\mbox{$\bf w$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=||\mbox{$\bf w$}||^{2}D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
, where
\mbox{$\bf w$}=||\mbox{$\bf w$}||\mbox{$\bf u$}
and
\bf u
is a unit direction, we henceforth only consider unit directions.
First order directional gradient processes,
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
, are reviewed in
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
and in Section S1.1 of the Supplement. Choosing a direction is emphasized with respect to interpreting the directional curvature processes. Directional curvature is the change in the normal to the surface
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
at
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}
when moving along a slice of the surface in the direction
\bf w
. The associated algebraic sign locally classifies the nature of curvature at
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}
—for instance, convex or concave ellipsoids
(
Stevens 1981
, see)
. A detailed discussion, with illustration, is available in Section S2 of the Supplement.

Since
\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is a symmetric matrix, to avoid singularities arising from duplication we modify
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
as follows. If
vech
is the usual half-vectorization operator for symmetric matrices and
\mathcal{D}_{d}
is the duplication matrix
(
Magnus & Neudecker 1980
)
of order
d^{2}\times d(d+1)/2
then,
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf c$}^{\top}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}vech\left(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\right)

where
\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}=(\mbox{$\bf u$}\otimes\mbox{$\bf v$})^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{d}
and
\otimes
is the Kronecker product for matrices. If
\mbox{$\bf u$}=(u_{1},u_{2})^{\top},\mbox{$\bf v$}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{2}$}
, then
\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}=(\mbox{$\bf u$}\otimes\mbox{$\bf v$})^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{2}=\left(v_{1}u_{1},v_{1}u_{2}+v_{2}u_{1},v_{2}u_{2}\right)^{\top}
.
The process
vech\left(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\right)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)/2}
consists of the pure and mixed second order derivatives in
\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. The distributions needed for inference on directional curvature processes depend on
vech\left(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\right)
rather than
\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. We refer to
(\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top},vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top})^{\top}
as the differential process and
\{\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
,
\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))\}
as the directional differential processes induced by
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
along
\bf u
.

Inference for differential processes requires
(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{{}^{\top}},vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{{}^{\top}})
to be a valid multivariate process. Its existence is derived from the limit of corresponding finite difference approximations,
which yields the cross-covariance matrix depending on fourth (and lower) order derivatives of
K
. We investigate the parent and differential processes
using
a
differential operator
{\cal L}:\mathbb{R}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m=1+d+d(d+1)/2
,
where

{\cal L}Y=\left(Y,\nabla Y^{\top},vech(\nabla^{2}Y)^{\top}\right)^{\top}
. The resulting process
{\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is also stationary with a zero mean and a cross-covariance matrix

V_{{\cal L}Y}(\Delta)=\begin{pmatrix}K(\Delta)&-(\nabla K(\Delta))^{\top}&vech(\nabla^{2}K(\Delta))^{\top}\\
\nabla K(\Delta)&-\nabla^{2}K(\Delta)&\nabla^{3}K(\Delta)^{\top}\\
vech(\nabla^{2}K(\Delta))&-\nabla^{3}K(\Delta)&\nabla^{4}K(\Delta)\end{pmatrix}\;,

(1)

where
\Delta=\mbox{$\bf s$}-\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}
,
\nabla K(\Delta)
is the
d\times 1
gradient,
\nabla^{2}K(\Delta)
is the
d\times d

\nabla^{3}K(\Delta)
is the
d(d+1)/2\times d

\nabla^{4}K(\Delta)
is the

. Under isotropy,
\nabla K(\Delta)=\frac{\nabla\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)}{||\Delta||}\Delta

A_{0}=\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)-\frac{\nabla\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)}{||\Delta||}\right)

\nabla^{2}K(\Delta)=\frac{\nabla\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)}{||\Delta||}I_{d}+A_{0}\frac{\Delta\Delta^{\top}}{||\Delta||^{2}}

\nabla^{3}K(\Delta)=A_{0}\bigg\{\frac{vech(I_{d})^{\top}\otimes\Delta}{||\Delta||^{2}}-3\frac{vech(\Delta\Delta^{\top})^{\top}\otimes\Delta}{||\Delta||^{4}}+\frac{1}{||\Delta||^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial vech(\Delta\Delta^{\top})}{\partial\Delta}\right)\bigg\}+\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)\cdot\frac{vech(\Delta\Delta^{\top})^{\top}\otimes\Delta}{||\Delta||^{3}}

\nabla^{4}K(\Delta)

\begin{split}A_{0}\left\{\frac{A_{1}}{||\Delta||^{2}}-3\frac{A_{2}}{||\Delta||^{4}}+\frac{A_{3}}{||\Delta||^{2}}-(1+A_{4})\left(\frac{2B_{1}}{||\Delta||^{4}}+\frac{B_{1}}{||\Delta||^{3}}\right)+3\left(\frac{4B_{2}}{||\Delta||^{6}}+\frac{B_{2}}{||\Delta||^{5}}\right)\right\}\\

(2)

The resulting multivariate differential process,
{\cal L}Y
, is stationary but not isotropic. Evidently, for the differential operator to be well-defined under isotropy,
\nabla^{4}K({\bf 0})
must exist since

{\cal L}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})

a_{0}=\left(1-\frac{(\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\Delta)^{2}}{||\Delta||^{2}}\right)

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})),\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}))\right)=\frac{3}{||\Delta||^{2}}(5a_{0}-4)a_{0}A_{0}+\frac{6}{||\Delta||}(1-a_{0})a_{0}\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)+(1-a_{0})^{2}\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)

To characterize covariance functions that admit such processes, we turn to spectral theory. Recall that for a positive definite function
K
defined in
\mathbb{R}
, Bochner’s theorem
(
Williams & Rasmussen 2006

establishes the existence of a finite positive spectral measure

on
\mathbb{R}

can be expressed as the inverse Fourier transform of
{\cal F}

K(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda t}{\cal F}(d\lambda)

admits a spectral density,
K(t)=\int e^{-i\lambda t}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda

\nabla^{4}K
to exist, a trivial extension of the result in

possess a finite fourth moment. Examples of covariance kernels that satisfy this condition are (a) the squared exponential covariance kernel with
K(t)=\exp(-t^{2})
(
\sigma^{2}=\phi=1
), and
f(\lambda)=1/2\sqrt{\pi}\exp(-\lambda^{2}/4)
then,
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda^{4}\exp(-\lambda^{2}/4)\,d\lambda=3(\sqrt{2})^{4}=12
; and (b) the Matérn class with fractal parameter,
\nu
;
f(\lambda)
is known to belong to the

(
Stein 1999

with
f(\lambda)=C(\phi,\nu)/(c(\phi,\nu)+\lambda^{2})^{\nu+1/2}
then,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda^{4}C(\phi,\nu)/(c(\phi,\nu)+\lambda^{2})^{\nu+1/2}\,d\lambda<\infty

\nu>2
(since the fourth central moment for the

\nu>2

\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)=\sigma^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{\nu})

{\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})

{\rm div}{(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))}=\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\frac{\partial}{\partial\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})}}=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{1}^{\top}{\cal L}{Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})}

Posterior inference for curvature proceeds by sampling from
P(vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))\mid\mbox{$\bf Y$})=\int P(vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))\mid\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}),\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})\mid\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\mid\mbox{$\bf Y$})\,d\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\,d\nabla Y
. We sample from (

(\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}),vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))
for each sample of

.

\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}),vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\sim{\cal N}_{m_{1}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1},\Sigma_{1}\right)
where

\displaystyle\small\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mu(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0})\\
vech(\nabla^{2}\mu(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}))\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{1}\\
\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mbox{$\bf Y$}}^{-1}(\mbox{$\bf Y$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$})\;,

(5)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla^{2}K({\bf 0})&\nabla^{3}K({\bf 0})^{\top}\\
-\nabla^{3}K({\bf 0})&\nabla^{4}K({\bf 0})\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{1}\\
\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mbox{$\bf Y$}}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{1}\\
\nabla\mbox{$\bf K$}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\;.

3
Wombling with Curvature Processes

Bayesian wombling deals with inference for line integrals

{\Gamma}(C)=\int_{C}g\left({\cal L}Y\right)\,{\rm d}\ell~~\text{ or},~~\overline{\Gamma}(C)=\frac{1}{\ell(C)}\int_{C}g\left({\cal L}Y\right)\,{\rm d}\ell\;,

(7)

where
C
is a geometric structure of interest, such as lines or planar curves, residing within the spatial domain of reference,
\ell
is an appropriate measure, often taken to be the arc-length measure,
g
is a linear function (or functional) of the differential operator
{\cal L}Y
.
\Gamma
and
\overline{\Gamma}
are referred to as the total and average
wombling measures
respectively. The structure
C
is defined to be a
wombling boundary
if it yields a large total (or average) wombling measure. Depending on the spatial domain, geometric structures of interest constructed within them may vary. For example, if we are dealing with surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, choices of
C
are curves and lines within the surface, with the local co-ordinate being
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In higher dimensions they would be planes (curves) or hyperplanes (hypercurves). Specifically, Bayesian curvilinear wombling involves estimating integrals in (
7
) over curves, which tracks rapid change over the spatial domain by determining boundaries (curves) with large gradients normal to the curve
(
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
, see for e.g.,)
. The integrand in (
7
) inherently involves a direction, in particular change measured is always in a direction normal to
C
. Hence,
g({\cal L}Y)
can equivalently be expressed as a linear function (functional) of
\displaystyle{{\cal L}_{\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})}
, where
\mbox{$\bf n$}=\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
denotes the unit normal vector to
C
at
\bf s
. The next few paragraphs provide more detail.

With wombling measures for directional gradients discussed the Supplement, Section S1.2, we construct wombling measures for curvature. Given
C
, depending on the smoothness of the surface, the rate at which gradients change along the curve may present sufficient heterogeneity while traversing the curve. If
C
forms a wombling boundary with respect to the gradient, then wombling boundaries for curvature are subsets of
C
that feature segments with large positive (negative) directional curvature along a normal direction to the curve. Leveraging only gradients, we develop wombling measures for curvature that further characterize such boundaries located for gradients. The wombling measure for curvature in
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
along
C
ascertains whether
C
also forms a wombling boundary with respect to curvature. We associate a directional curvature to each
\mbox{$\bf s$}\in C
,
\displaystyle{g({\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))=D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf n$},\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf n$},\mbox{$\bf n$}}^{\top}vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))}
(a linear function of
\displaystyle{{\cal L}_{\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})}
) along the direction of a unit normal
\mbox{$\bf n$}=\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
to
C
at
\bf s
. Using (
7
) we define
wombling measures
for total and average curvature as,

\Gamma^{(2)}(C)=\int_{C}D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf n$},\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\ell=\int_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\ell\;,\qquad\overline{\Gamma}^{(2)}(C)=\Gamma^{(2)}(C)/\ell(C)\;,

(8)

respectively, where
\ell(C)
denotes the arc-length of
C
. Parameterized curves,
C=\{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)=(s_{1}(t),s_{2}(t)):t\in{\cal T}\subset\mathbb{R}\}
, offer further insights.
As
t
varies over its domain,
\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)
outlines the curve
C
. Implicitly assuming that
C
is regular, i.e.,
||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\neq 0
, allows the tangent and normal to exist at all points on the curve. The unit tangent and normal at each point of the curve are
\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)/||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||
and
\mbox{$\bf n$}=\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))=(s^{\prime}_{2}(t),-s^{\prime}_{1}(t))^{\top}/||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||
, respectively, while
\displaystyle\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf n$},\mbox{$\bf n$}}=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)),\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))}=\left(\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\otimes\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\right)^{\top}{\cal D}_{d}
from Section
2

The arc-length of
C
is
\ell(C)=\int_{\cal T}||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
or
{\rm d}\ell=||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
. If
{\cal T}=[t_{0},t_{1}]
, then
\ell(C)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
and
\Gamma^{(2)}(C)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
.
If
C
is an open curve, then
\ell(C)^{-1}\int_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\ell(C)^{-1}\int_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
is the average directional curvature.
For example,
C=\{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)=(r\cos t,r\sin t),t\in[0,\pi/4]\}
is the arc of a parameterized circle of radius
r
. It follows that
||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||=r
,
\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))=(\cos t,\sin t)^{\top}
and
{\ell(C)}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))r\,dt=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\,dt
. The average curvature in the tangential direction of
C
is
\frac{1}{\ell(C)}\int_{C}\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt=\displaystyle{{\ell(C)}^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\frac{\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)}{||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\frac{\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)}{||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||}||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt}=\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1}))^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1}))-\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0}))^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0}))
.
Hence, the average directional curvature remains path independent and is the difference of directional gradient at the end points of
C
.

For a closed curve
C
,
\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
.
If the surface admits up to three derivatives, i.e.
\nabla^{3}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
exists, the average curvature of the region,
{\cal D}
, enclosed by
C
, is free of
t
. If
\mbox{$\bf F$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(F_{ij}(\mbox{$\bf s$}))_{i,j=1,2}
, with
F_{12}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=F_{21}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
and
F_{ij}=F_{ij}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial^2}{\partial s_i \partial s_j}$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
then,
\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\mbox{$\bf F$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\mbox{$\bf F$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt=\oint_{C}||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||^{-1}\left(F_{11}s_{2}^{\prime}(t)^{2}-2F_{12}s_{1}^{\prime}(t)s_{2}^{\prime}(t)+F_{22}s_{1}^{\prime}(t)^{2}\right)\,dt=\iint\limits_{\cal D}\left\{\frac{\partial F_{11}n_{2}}{\partial s_{1}}+\left(\frac{\partial F_{12}n_{2}}{\partial s_{2}}+\frac{\partial F_{12}n_{1}}{\partial s_{1}}\right)+\frac{\partial F_{22}n_{1}}{\partial s_{2}}\right\}ds_{1}ds_{2}
.
The last equality is obtained using Green’s theorem
(
Rudin 1976
, see for e.g.,)
. This can be interpreted as “flux” in the gradient within
{\cal D}
. Since,
F_{ij}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\nabla^{2}_{ij}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
, the integrand in the last equality require the existence of
\nabla^{3}_{ijk}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
,
i,j,k=1,2
. Denoting,
\widetilde{\nabla}^{3}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(\nabla^{3}_{ijk}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))_{i,j,k=1,2}^{\top}
, vector of unique third derivatives, and
\mbox{$\bf n$}_{0}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(n_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),n_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),n_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),n_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top}
then,

\frac{1}{\ell(C)}\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf n$},\mbox{$\bf n$}}^{\top}vech(\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))\,d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\frac{1}{\ell(C)}\iint\limits_{\cal D}\mbox{$\bf n$}_{0}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\widetilde{\nabla}^{3}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\,d\mbox{$\bf s$}.

(9)

This extends the development in Section 3.2 of

to study the behavior of spatial curvature over closed curves on surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

C
is generally harder than sampling inside
{\cal D}

C
with
\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0})=\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1})=\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}

\displaystyle\oint_{C}\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})d\mbox{$\bf s$}=\oint_{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0})}^{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1})}F_{11}(\mbox{$\bf s$})n_{1}ds_{1}+F_{12}(\mbox{$\bf s$})n_{1}ds_{2}+F_{21}(\mbox{$\bf s$})n_{2}ds_{1}+F_{22}(\mbox{$\bf s$})n_{2}ds_{2}=\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1}))^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{1}))-\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0}))^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t_{0}))=0
, again a consequence of path independence.
This validates the choice of a normal direction to

In practical applications curvilinear wombling is performed by approximating the curve
C
using linear segments. These measures at the segment level are then aggregated to produce a wombling measure for the curve. The curve is segmented using a partition. Consequently, the accuracy of estimated wombling measures for the curve depend on the choice of partition. Figures S2 and S3 in the online Supplement illustrate this concept. Explicitly, let
C
be a regular rectifiable curve and
[a,b]\subset{\cal T}
be a compact interval. Let

be a uniformly continuous function. For any partition,
P
of

,

, with its norm defined as
|P|=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,n_{P}}(t^{\prime}_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i-1})
. A polygonal (piecewise-linear) approximation to the curve is,
\widetilde{C}_{P}=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n_{P}}C_{t_{i}}
, where
C_{t_{i}}=\{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i-1})+t\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i},t\in[0,t_{i}]\}
,
t_{i}=||\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i})-\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i-1})||
and
\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}=||\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i})-\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i-1})||^{-1}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i})-\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i-1}))^{\top}

\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)=\mbox{$\bf s$}(t^{\prime}_{i-1})+t\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}
for

and, hence,
||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||=||\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}||=1

\Gamma(\widetilde{C}_{P})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{P}}\int_{C_{t_{i}}}g\left({\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\right)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
. As

we have,
\Gamma(\widetilde{C}_{P})\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\Gamma(C)=\int_{a}^{b}g\left({\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\right)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||\,dt
. This provides us with an estimate,
\Gamma(\widetilde{C}_{P})
for curvilinear wombling measures associated with any general curve
C

For a point
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{j}
define

in the integrand. The analytic tractability of the line integrals in

is not a concern. Given choices of
\mu(\cdot)
and
K(\cdot)
, they are all one or two dimensional integrals which are efficiently computed using simple quadrature. For example, let
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
be the isotropic Gaussian process with mean
\mu(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mu
and
K(||\Delta||;\sigma^{2},\phi)=\sigma^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})
, where
\Delta=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})^{\top}
.
\nabla^{k}K(\Delta)
,
k=2,3,4
is obtained from (
2
) and related results.

\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{j}(t_{i})=\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{j}(t_{i};\sigma^{2},\phi)=\left\{\Phi\left(\sqrt{2\phi}\left(t_{i}+\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\top}\Delta_{i-1,j}\right)\right)-\Phi\left(\sqrt{2\phi}\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\top}\Delta_{i-1,j}\right)\right\}(c_{1},c_{2})^{\top}
where,
c_{1}=c_{1}(\sigma^{2},\phi,\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp},\Delta_{i-1,j})=-2\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\pi\phi}{\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp}}^{\top}\Delta_{i-1,j}e^{-\phi\left({\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp}}^{\top}\Delta_{i-1,j}\right)^{2}}
,
c_{2}=c_{2}(\sigma^{2},\phi,\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp},\Delta_{i-1,j})=c_{1}(1-2\phi{\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp}}^{\top}\Delta_{i-1,j}\Delta_{i-1,j}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}^{\perp})
, and
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function. These are simple computations with quadrature required only for computing
K_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}(t_{i},t_{i})
.

4
Bayesian Hierarchical Model

We operate under a Bayesian hierarchical model, which is specified as

Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mu(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$})+Z(\mbox{$\bf s$})+\epsilon(\mbox{$\bf s$})\;,

(13)

where
Z(\mbox{$\bf s$})\sim GP(0,K(\cdot;\sigma^{2},\phi))
is a Gaussian process, and
\epsilon(\mbox{$\bf s$})\sim N(0,\tau^{2})
is a white noise process, termed as the nugget
(see
Banerjee et al. 2014
, and references therein)
. The process parameters are
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\{\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$},\sigma^{2},\phi,\tau^{2}\}
. More generally, we can consider a latent specification for response arising from exponential families,
\alpha(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\eta$}}$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}))=\mbox{$\bf x$}^{\top}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}+Z(\mbox{$\bf s$})+\epsilon(\mbox{$\bf s$})
,
Z(\mbox{$\bf s$})\sim GP(0,K(\cdot;\sigma^{2},\phi))
and
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\sim\pi\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\eta$}}$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),\cdot\right)
, where
\alpha
is a monotonic link function,
\pi
is a member of the exponential family and
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\eta$}}
is the natural parameter. Predictive inference on differential processes and curvature wombling proceeds on the latent surface through
P({\cal L}Z\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})
. The joint posterior for differential processes is obtained through,
P(\nabla Z^{\top},vech(\nabla^{2}Z)^{\top}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})=\int P(\nabla Z^{\top},vech(\nabla^{2}Z)^{\top}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\mbox{$\bf Z$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})\,d\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\,d\mbox{$\bf Z$}
, while wombling measures
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}(t^{*})
for a curve
C_{t^{*}}
within the estimated posterior surface for
\bf Z
, are sampled from the posterior,
P(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}(t^{*})\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})=\int P(\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}(t^{*})\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\mbox{$\bf Z$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})P(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})\,d\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\,d\mbox{$\bf Z$}
. Customary prior specifications for
\theta
yield

\begin{split}P(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{$\bf Z$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$})&\propto U(\phi\,|\;a_{\phi},b_{\phi})\times IG(\sigma^{2}\,|\;a_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})\times IG(\tau^{2}\,|\;a_{\tau},b_{\tau})\times\mathcal{N}_{L}(\mbox{$\bf Z$}\,|\;\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mbox{$\bf R$}_{Z})\\
&\qquad\times\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}\,|\;\mu_{\beta},\Sigma_{\beta})\times\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{N}_{1}\big(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{l})\,|\;\mbox{$\bf x$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{l})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}+Z(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{l}),\tau^{2}\big)\;,\end{split}

(14)

where
IG
denotes the inverse-gamma distribution with a shape-rate parameterization,
U
is a uniform distribution and
\mbox{$\bf R$}_{Z}
is the correlation matrix corresponding to
K(\cdot;\sigma^{2},\phi)
. The resulting full conditionals are
\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}\,|\;\tau^{2},\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{$\bf Y$}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(M_{\beta}m_{\beta},M_{\beta})
,
\sigma^{2}\,|\;\phi,\mbox{$\bf Z$}\sim IG(a_{\sigma}+\frac{L}{2},b_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\mbox{$\bf Z$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf R$}^{-1}_{Z}(\cdot;\phi)\mbox{$\bf Z$})
,
\tau^{2}\,|\;\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$},\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{$\bf Y$}\sim IG\left(a_{\tau}+\frac{L}{2},b_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}||\mbox{$\bf Y$}-\mbox{$\bf X$}\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}-\mbox{$\bf Z$}||_{2}^{2}\right)
,
\mbox{$\bf Z$}\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Y$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\sim\mathcal{N}_{L}(M_{Z}m_{Z},\tau^{2}M_{Z})
, where
\bf X
is the
L\times p
matrix with
\mbox{$\bf x$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}_{i})^{\top}
as rows,
M_{\beta}^{-1}=\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}+\tau^{-2}\mbox{$\bf X$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf X$}
,
m_{\beta}=\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\mu_{\beta}+\tau^{-2}\mbox{$\bf X$}^{\top}(\mbox{$\bf Y$}-\mbox{$\bf Z$})
,
M_{Z}^{-1}=\tau^{-2}\big(\tau^{-2}I_{L}+\sigma^{-2}\mbox{$\bf R$}_{Z}^{-1}(\cdot;\phi)\big)
, and
m_{Z}=\mbox{$\bf Y$}-\mbox{$\bf X$}\mbox{\boldmath${\beta}$}
.
\phi
is updated using Metropolis steps with a normal proposal and an adaptive variance.

Under this setup posterior samples for the differential processes and wombling measures result from (
5
) and (
6
). For each posterior sample of
\{\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\}
, we draw
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}(t^{*})\,|\;\mbox{$\bf Z$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\sim{\cal N}_{2}\big(\mu_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{\top}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})\Sigma_{\mbox{$\bf Z$}}^{-1}\mbox{$\bf Z$},K_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*},t^{*})-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{\top}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})\Sigma_{\mbox{$\bf Z$}}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})\big)
,
where
\mu_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*})
, and
K_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{Z}}(t^{*},t^{*})
are computed from (
10
) and (
11
). Algorithms 1 and 2 in the Supplement, Section S4, present further details for posterior sampling.

5.3
Bayesian wombling with curvature processes

For wombling with curvature processes, or
curvature wombling
, we focus on locating curves that track rapid change within the simulated random surfaces. For example, consider the surface produced by the first pattern. If a curve is provided to us, we can evaluate the posterior distribution of the average or total curvature wombling measures to assess their statistical significance. On the other hand, without a given curve, we consider three different approaches for constructing them from a boundary analysis or wombling perspective: (a) level curves:
C_{y_{0}}=\{\mbox{$\bf s$}:Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=y_{0}\}
: Bayesian wombling literature finds that curves parallel to contours often form wombling boundaries
(
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
, see, e.g.,)
and level curves on a surface are parallel to local contours by definition; (b) smooth curves: produces a smooth curve using Bézier splines
(
Gallier & Gallier 2000
, see, e.g.,)
from a set of
annotated
points that are of interest within the surface; and (c) rectilinear curves: produces a rectilinear curve joining adjacent
annotated
points of interest within the surface using straight lines, performs curvature wombling using a Riemann sum approximation (see (S1) in the Supplement).
Curves of types (b) and (c) allow the investigator to specify a region of interest that house possible wombling boundaries. For the surface realization produced by Pattern 1, we consider four different types of curves on the response surface, (A) a closed curve enclosing a trough corresponding to a level curve,
C_{y_{0}=-18}
, (B) a closed curve enclosing a peak corresponding to a level curve,
C_{y_{0}=+18}
, (C) a closed curve that outlines a contour corresponding to a level curve,
C_{y_{0}=+15}
and (D) an open curve along a contour constructed using a Bézier spline. These curves are marked in Figure
2
c.

Discussion and Future Work

We developed a fully model-based Bayesian inferential framework for differential process assessment and curvature-based boundary analysis for spatial processes. Introducing the directional curvature process and its associated inferential framework supplements the directional gradients with inference for their rates of change, while its induction
into the folds of Bayesian curvilinear wombling allows for further characterization of difference boundaries. Adopting a Bayesian hierarchical model allows for Gaussian calibration when characterizing points, regions and boundaries within a surface. This framework is widely applicable; our applications arise from selected disciplines indicating the utilities of mapping curvature process boundaries to understand spatial data generating patterns. Substantive case studies will be reported separately.

(

leveraging dependent circular uniform distributions
(

. We offer some brief remarks. To obtain the direction of maximum curvature for a spatial surface, we solve
\displaystyle{\max_{\mbox{$\bf u$}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf u$}\right|}
, such that
||\mbox{$\bf u$}||=1
, at an arbitrary point
\bf s
. Using Lagrange multipliers and denoting
\kappa(\mbox{$\bf u$})=|\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf u$}|
, define
{\cal O}(\mbox{$\bf u$})=\kappa(\mbox{$\bf u$})-\lambda(||\mbox{$\bf u$}||^{2}-1)
hence,
\partial{\cal O}(\mbox{$\bf u$})/\partial u_{i}=\kappa(\mbox{$\bf u$})^{-1}(\nabla^{2}_{ii}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})u_{i}+\nabla^{2}_{ij}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})u_{j})-\lambda u_{i}=0
,
i,j=1,2
. With
u_{2}/u_{1}=\tan\theta_{pc}
, eliminating
\lambda
we get
\displaystyle{\tan\theta_{pc}=\frac{\nabla^{2}_{22}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\tan\theta_{pc}+\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})}{\nabla^{2}_{11}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})+\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\tan\theta_{pc}}}
.
Defining
h_{1}=h_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(\nabla^{2}_{11}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})-\nabla^{2}_{22}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))/\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
given
\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\neq 0
and solving
\theta_{pc}=\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\left[-h_{1}\pm\sqrt{h_{1}^{2}+4}\right]
.
If
\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=0
then,
\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is diagonal and
\theta_{pc}
corresponds to the direction of
\max\{\nabla^{2}_{11}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),\nabla^{2}_{22}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\}
. We propose that
\Theta=(\theta_{asp},\theta_{pc})^{\top}
follows a dependent circular uniform distribution over
[0,2\pi]\times[0,2\pi]
. Further developments with circular regression methods can proceed to examine the effect of covariates on
\Theta
. Multivariate extensions would involve formulating these differential processes on arbitrary manifolds. This requires simulating a Gaussian process on manifolds and inspecting the covariant derivative. Bayesian curvilinear wombling could then be implemented on curves of interest to the investigator. This would not only involve an inferential framework for normal curvature, but also geodesic curvature for such curves. Spatiotemporal curvature processes can build upon
Quick et al. 2015
to study evolutionary behavior of the curvature processes with respect to variations in the response across time. Finally, we remark that while there have been substantial recent developments in scalable spatial processes for massive data sets—a comprehensive review is beyond the scope of the current article
(
Heaton et al. 2019
, see, e.g.,)
—not all scalable processes admit the correct degree of smoothness for curvature processes to exist. Constructing scalable processes for curvilinear wombling, and subsequent inference, remains a problem of interest in the wombling community.

Review of Directional Gradients and Wombling

8.1
Directional Gradients

For the scalar
h
and unit vector
\bf u
we define
Y^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},h}=\left(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}+h\mbox{$\bf u$})-Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\right)/h
to be the first order finite difference processes at location
\bf s
in the directions of
\bf u
. Being a linear function of stationary processes this is well-defined. Passing to limits, we define
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\lim_{h\to 0}Y^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. Provided the limit exist,
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is defined as the directional gradient process. If
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is a mean square differentiable process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for every
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{d}$}
then
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. Then, if
\mbox{$\bf u$}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i}
, we can compute
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf e$}_{i}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. The directional gradient process is linear in
\bf u
, hence
D^{(1)}_{-\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=-D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
and for any vector
\mbox{$\bf w$}=||\mbox{$\bf w$}||\mbox{$\bf u$}
,
D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf w$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=||\mbox{$\bf w$}||D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. The directional gradient at
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}
in the direction
\bf w
is the slope at
\mbox{$\bf s$}_{0}
of the curve traced out by slicing
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
in the direction
\bf w

(see e.g.,
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
, Section 2, for more details)
.

8.2
Wombling Measures

Wombling measures constructed from (7) for total and average gradient are associated with curves to characterize the magnitude of change. To each point
\mbox{$\bf s$}\in C
, a directional gradient is associated,
g\left({\cal L}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\right)=D^{(1)}_{\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
(also a linear function of
\displaystyle{{\cal L}_{\mbox{$\bf n$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})}
), along the direction of a unit normal
\mbox{$\bf u$}=\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
to the curve. For a curve tracking rapid change in the surface, choice of the normal direction to a curve is motivated by sharp directional gradients orthogonal to the curve;
\ell
is chosen to be the arc-length measure. The rationale behind this choice is to measure change in response with respect to distance traversed on the curve. With reference to (7) the total and average gradients are,
{{\Gamma}^{(1)}(C)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))^{\top}\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt}
and
\overline{\Gamma}^{(1)}({C})={\Gamma}^{(1)}(C)/\ell({C})
respectively
(see e.g.,
Banerjee & Gelfand 2006
, Section 3, for more details)
.

9
Interpretation of Spatial Curvature

Pursuits in geospatial analysis generally encounter surfaces which have canonical coordinate systems (e.g. latitude-longitude, easting-northing etc.). This facilitates a parameterization for the surface that leverages the coordinate system, commonly known as the Monge parameterization
(
O’Neill 2006
;
Pressley 2010
, also called a Monge patch, named after Gaspard Monge, see e.g.,)
—a surface,
S
, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is parameterized by giving its height
Y
over some plane as a function of the orthonormal co-ordinates
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
in the plane,
S=\{{\cal S}\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{2}$}\mapsto\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{3}$}:\mbox{$\bf s$}=(s_{1},s_{2})\mapsto Y(s_{1},s_{2})=Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\}
. A point is then,
(\mbox{$\bf s$},Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))=(s_{1},s_{2},Y(s_{1},s_{2}))
. The two tangent vectors at
\bf s
are,
\mbox{$\bf E$}_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(1,0,\nabla_{1}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top}
and
\mbox{$\bf E$}_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(0,1,\nabla_{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top}
, where
\nabla_{i}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial\mbox{$\bf s$}_{i}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})},\;i=1,2
. Let
\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(\nabla_{1}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),\nabla_{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top}
denote the gradient vector, consider a unit direction vector,
\mbox{$\bf u$}=(u_{1},u_{2})^{\top}\in S\subset\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{2}$}
, then
u_{1}\mbox{$\bf E$}_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$})+u_{2}\mbox{$\bf E$}_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top},\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))^{\top}\in T_{S}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
corresponds to the
directional derivative
of
Y
along the direction
\bf u
, where
T_{S}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is the local tangent plane at
\bf s
, that is generated by
\{\mbox{$\bf E$}_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),\mbox{$\bf E$}_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\}
. The outward pointing normal to the surface
S
, denoted by
\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf E$}_{1}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\times\mbox{$\bf E$}_{2}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(-\nabla_{1}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),-\nabla_{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}),1)^{\top}
, where
\times
denotes the usual cross-product of vectors. Evidently,
\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is orthogonal to the local tangent plane at that point,
T_{S}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. Quantifying the local geometry of a surface, we are interested in how
\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
changes (“tips”) as we move in the direction
\bf u
from the point
\bf s
on the surface—derivatives for
\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
at the point
\bf s
, which lie in
T_{S}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. This is quantified by the
normal curvature
of
S
along a direction
\bf u
. Before defining normal curvature for a surface, we digress briefly to investigate effects of surface curvature on curves—for a curve parameterized by
t
,
C=\{\mbox{$\bf s$}(t)=(s_{1}(t),s_{2}(t)):t\in[a,b]\}
, passing through
\bf s
, if the curvature of
C
is,
\kappa
, the tangent to
C
at
\bf s
,
\mbox{$\bf t$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
, and the principal unit normal, i.e. the normal to
C
on
S
,
\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf t$}^{\prime}(\mbox{$\bf s$})/\kappa
, then we have,
\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\cos(\theta)
,
\mbox{$\bf t$}^{\prime}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\kappa\mbox{$\bf n$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
, which implies
\kappa\cos(\theta)=\mbox{$\bf t$}^{\prime}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. We observe that,

\mbox{$\bf t$}^{\prime}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}$}}\left(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t),Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))\right)^{\top}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mbox{$\bf E$}_{i}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))s_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\mbox{$\bf E$}_{ij}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))s_{i}^{\prime}(t)s_{j}^{\prime}(t)+\mbox{$\bf E$}_{i}(\mbox{$\bf s$}(t))s_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t)\;,

since
\mbox{$\bf E$}_{i}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=0
,
\kappa\cos(\theta)=\mbox{$\bf t$}^{\prime}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})=(\mbox{$\bf E$}_{ij}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$}))\partial s_{i}\partial s_{j}
,
i,j=1,2
, where the expression in the parenthesis is a property of the surface, independent of curve
C
, and is defined as the
second fundamental form
,

\Pi(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{$\bf E$}_{11}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})&\mbox{$\bf E$}_{12}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\\
\mbox{$\bf E$}_{21}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})&\mbox{$\bf E$}_{22}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\cdot\mbox{$\bf N$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}_{11}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})&\nabla^{2}_{12}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\\
\nabla^{2}_{21}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})&\nabla^{2}_{22}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\end{pmatrix}=\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})\;,

where
\mbox{$\bf E$}_{ij}
and
\nabla^{2}_{ij}Y
are the partial differentiation of
\bf E
and
Y
with respect to
s_{i},s_{j}
respectively,
\cdot
is the usual dot product for vectors and
\nabla^{2}_{12}Y=\nabla^{2}_{21}Y
. The second to last equality is obtained under the Monge parameterization. The second fundamental form is invariant with respect to transformations of the local co-ordinate which preserves the sense of
\bf N
, i.e. the transformation does not change an outward (inward) pointing normal to an inward (outward) pointing normal for
S
. Such surfaces are termed as
orientable surfaces
. The Möbius transformation is an example of non-orientable surfaces. The individual terms of
\Pi
, quantify the local geometry of a surface (or curvature) along orthonormal coordinates.

Curvature of the
C
can be attributed to (a) the curvature of the curve itself, and (b) the curvature of the surface on which
C
lies.
\kappa
is the curvature of
C
, termed as
geodesic curvature
. The curvature of the surface is termed as
normal curvature
, computed along a direction
\mbox{$\bf u$}=\mbox{$\bf u$}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is denoted by
\kappa_{n}(\mbox{$\bf u$})
. The normal curvature, which is an intrinsic property of the surface independent of
C
, is of primary interest to us,
\kappa_{n}(\mbox{$\bf u$})=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\Pi(\mbox{$\bf s$})\mbox{$\bf u$}/\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf u$}=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\Pi\mbox{$\bf u$}
, under
\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf u$}=1
.
\kappa_{n}(\mbox{$\bf u$})
is also the
directional curvature
of the
Y
along
\bf u
. For our exploits,
\mbox{$\bf u$}=\mbox{$\bf n$}
, the normal direction to
C
. The sign of
\kappa_{n}(\mbox{$\bf u$})
, or equivalently eigen-values of
\Pi
inform about the nature of curvature at
\bf s
—for example, if
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
denote eigenvalues of
\Pi
, with
K=\det\Pi=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
, if
K>0
, it implies that the surface is bending away from
T_{S}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
; depending on whether
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}<0
(or
>0
),
\bf s
can be locally classified as a concave (convex) ellipsoid
(
Stevens 1981
, for more details see)
.

Gauss 1902
;
Spivak 1999
;
Do Carmo 2016
;
Kreyszig 2019
. Figure

illustrates this discussion.

The detailed calculations for closed form expression of selected covariance functions are presented. We start with the power exponential family of isotropic covariance functions,
\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)=\alpha\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{\nu})
,
0<\nu\leq 2
. It is clear that
\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)
and exists only for
\nu=2
, we have the following form,
\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)=\sigma^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})
. For this choice we have,

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{i}=-2\sigma^{2}\phi\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})\delta_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ii}=-2\sigma^{2}\phi\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(1-2\phi\delta_{i}^{2}),

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ij}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})\delta_{i}\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iii}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(3-2\phi\delta_{i}^{2})\delta_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iij}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(1-2\phi\delta_{i}^{2})\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ijk}=-8\sigma^{2}\phi^{3}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})\delta_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{k},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iiii}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(3-12\phi\delta_{i}^{2}+4\phi^{2}\delta_{i}^{4}),

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iiij}=-8\sigma^{2}\phi^{3}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(3-2\phi\delta_{i}^{2})\delta_{i}\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iijj}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})(1-2\phi\delta_{i}^{2})(1-2\phi\delta_{j}^{2}),

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ijkl}=16\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\exp(-\phi||\Delta||^{2})\delta_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{k}\delta_{l},

where
i,j,k,l=1,2,\ldots,d
. The squared exponential or the Gaussian covariance kernel is the only member of its class that admits such derivatives, although they have been critiqued to produce realizations that are too smooth to be of practical use in modeling (see
Stein 1999
).

Turning to the Matérn class we see that with,
\widetilde{K}||\Delta||=\alpha(\phi||\Delta||)^{\nu}K_{\nu}(\phi||\Delta||)
, where
\nu
is a parameter controlling the smoothness of realizations, that is mean square differentiability and
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of order
\nu
. At
\nu=3/2
and
\nu=5/2
,
\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)
takes the forms,

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)=\begin{cases}\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{3}\phi||\Delta||)e^{-\sqrt{3}\phi||\Delta||},&\nu=3/2\\
\sigma^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{5}{3}$}}\phi^{2}||\Delta||^{2}\right)e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||},&\nu=5/2\end{cases},

where
\sigma^{2}
is the overall process variance. Matérn with
\nu=3/2
is once mean square differentiable, where as Matérn with
\nu=5/2
is twice mean square differentiable at 0. As
\nu\to\infty
, Matérn covariances tend to the Gaussian covariance. Unlike the Gaussian covariance, they do not yield overly smoothed process realizations. For
\nu=3/2
we have,

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{i}=-3\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}e^{-\sqrt{3}\phi||\Delta||}\delta_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ii}=-3\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}e^{-\sqrt{3}\phi||\Delta||}\left(1-\sqrt{3}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ij}=3\sqrt{3}\sigma^{2}\phi^{3}e^{-\sqrt{3}\phi||\Delta||}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i\delta_j}{||\Delta||}$}}.

where
i,j=1,2,\ldots,d
. Since the process is just once mean square differentiable higher order derivatives do not exist. However, for
\nu=5/2
we have,

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{i}=-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{5}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left(1+\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||\right)\delta_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ii}=-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{5}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left(1+\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||-5\phi^{2}\delta_{i}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ij}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\delta_{i}\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iii}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left(3-\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\delta_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iij}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left(1-\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{3}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ijk}=-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25\sqrt{5}}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{5}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i\delta_j\delta_k}{||\Delta||}$}},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iiii}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left[3-6\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}+\sqrt{5}\phi\left(\sqrt{5}\phi+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^4}{||\Delta||^2}$}}\right],

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iiij}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25\sqrt{5}}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{5}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left[\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i}{||\Delta||^3}$}}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{||\Delta||}$}}\left(3-\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\right]\delta_{i}^{2}\delta_{j},

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{iijj}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left[\left(1-\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_j^2}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)+\sqrt{5}\phi\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i^2\delta_j^2}{||\Delta||^3}$}}\right],

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{4}\widetilde{K}(\Delta)\right)_{ijkl}=-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{25\sqrt{5}}{3}$}}\sigma^{2}\phi^{5}e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi||\Delta||}\left(\sqrt{5}\phi+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{||\Delta||}$}}\right)\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\delta_i\delta_j\delta_k\delta_l}{||\Delta||^2}$}},

where
i,j,k,l=1,2,\ldots,d
.

The above expressions for entries of the cross-covariance matrices correspond to the joint process,

with respect to our kernel choices. The following cross-covariance matrices are evaluated at

Proofs and Discussion

For the curvature process formulated in Section 2, we aim to show that the covariance matrix associated with the process
D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}vech\nabla^{2}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is valid (pg. 5 last paragraph). We obtain the expression for the covariance matrix by leveraging the directional finite difference process
Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$})
. For points
\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}
, we denote
\Delta=\mbox{$\bf s$}-\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}
and
\bf u
,
\bf v
are unit vectors specifying direction and
\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x,y)=\Delta+x\mbox{$\bf u$}+y\mbox{$\bf v$}
as a map from
\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{2}$}\to\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{d}$}
, after suppressing dependence on
\Delta
and
\bf u
,
\bf v
, let
g(x,y)=K(\Delta(x,y))=K(\Delta+x\mbox{$\bf u$}+y\mbox{$\bf v$})
denote a map from
\mbox{$\mathbb{R}^{2}$}\to\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}
we compute the covariance ,

\displaystyle C^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\lim_{k\to 0}E\left[Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$})Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\lim_{k\to 0}\frac{1}{h^{2}k^{2}}[g(h-k,h-k)-g(h-k,h)-g(h,h-k)+g(h,h)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-g(h-k,-k)+g(h-k,0)+g(h,-k)-g(h,0)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-g(-k,h-k)+g(-k,h)+g(0,h-k)-g(0,h)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+g(-k,-k)-g(-k,0)-g(0,-k)+g(0,0)],

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(h,h)-g^{\prime\prime}(h,0)-g^{\prime\prime}(0,h)+g^{\prime\prime}(0,0)}{h^{2}}=g^{(iv)}(0,0).

On repeated application of the chain rule and noting that
\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{x}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}
,
\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{y}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf v$}^{\top}
with all other higher order derivatives being 0 we have,

\displaystyle g_{x}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{x}(x,y)\nabla K(\Delta(x,y))=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla K(\Delta(x,y)),

\displaystyle g_{y}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{y}(x,y)\nabla K(\Delta(x,y))=\mbox{$\bf v$}^{\top}\nabla K(\Delta(x,y)),

\displaystyle g_{xx}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{x}(x,y)\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{x}(x,y)^{\top}=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf u$},

\displaystyle g_{xy}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{x}(x,y)\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{y}(x,y)^{\top}=\mbox{$\bf u$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf v$},

\displaystyle g_{yy}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{y}(x,y)\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}_{y}(x,y)^{\top}=\mbox{$\bf v$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf v$},

\displaystyle g_{xxx}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xxx}(x,y)\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial\delta_{i}}\right)+3\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xx}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}}\delta_{j,x}(x,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\delta_{i,x}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{3}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}},

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\delta_{i,x}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{3}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}}=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}\nabla^{3}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf u$}.

Similarly
g_{xxy}(x)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}\nabla^{3}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf v$}
,
g_{yyx}(x)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{3}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf u$}
and

g_{yyy}(x)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{3}K(\Delta(x,y))\mbox{$\bf v$}
. Next,

\displaystyle g_{xxxx}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xxxx}(x,y)\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial\delta_{i}}\right)+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xxx}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}}\delta_{j,x}(x,y)

\displaystyle+3\bigg[\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xxx}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}}\delta_{j,x}(x,y)+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xx}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}}\delta_{j,xx}(x,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xx}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{3}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+3\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\delta_{i,xx}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{3}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}\delta_{i,x}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\delta_{l}^{\prime}(x)\frac{\partial^{4}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}\partial\delta_{l}},

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{d}\delta_{i,x}(x,y)\delta_{j,x}(x,y)\delta_{k,x}(x,y)\delta_{l}^{\prime}(x)\frac{\partial^{4}K}{\partial\delta_{i}\partial\delta_{j}\partial\delta_{k}\partial\delta_{l}}=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x))\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}.

Similarly,
g_{xxxy}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x))\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}
,
g_{yyyx}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x))\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf u$}}
,
g_{xxyy}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x))\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}
and
g_{yyyy}(x,y)=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(x))\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf v$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}
.
Evaluated at
x,y=0
, i.e.
\mbox{\boldmath${\delta}$}(0,0)=\Delta
,
g_{xxyy}
provides us with the required expression,
C^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K(\Delta)\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}
and
var(D^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}))=\lim_{h\to 0}E(Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$}),Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}))=\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}^{\top}\nabla^{4}K({\bf 0})\mbox{$\bf c$}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$}}
which exists if
K^{(iv)}(\Delta)
exists for all
\Delta
, including
\Delta={\bf 0}
.

Note:

In the above proof we make some abuse of notation for brevity of mathematical expressions involved. To clarify,
\delta_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\delta(x,y)
,
\delta_{xx}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$}}\delta(x,y)
and so on,
g_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}g(x,y)
,
g_{xx}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$}}g(x,y)
etc.,
\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
etc.

To derive (2) and the covariance for the directional curvature process, we assume that
Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})
is isotropic, i.e.
K(\Delta)=\widetilde{K}(||\Delta||)
therefore,

\displaystyle C^{(2)}_{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\bf u$}$}},\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\mbox{$\bf u$}$}}}(\mbox{$\bf s$},\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\lim_{k\to 0}E\left[Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},h}(\mbox{$\bf s$})Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})\right],

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\lim_{k\to 0}\frac{1}{h^{2}k^{2}}\bigg[E\left(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}+h(\mbox{$\bf u$}+\mbox{$\bf v$}))Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime})\right)-E(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}+h\mbox{$\bf u$})Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-E(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$}+k\mbox{$\bf v$})Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}))+E(Y(\mbox{$\bf s$})Y^{(2)}_{\mbox{$\bf u$},\mbox{$\bf v$},k}(\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}))\bigg]

where,

x,y\in[a,b]
with
|x-y|<\delta_{1}
,

\displaystyle\big|g(x)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(x)||-g(y)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(y)||\big|<\frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}

Set
\delta=\min\{\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\}
, then
|P|<\delta
, using the mean value theorem we obtain,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}\int_{C_{t_{i}}}g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}(t^{\prime}_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i-1})g(t^{\prime}_{i})||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t^{\prime}_{i})||\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\leq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}(t^{\prime}_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i-1})\sup g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}(t^{\prime}_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i-1})g(t^{\prime}_{i})||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t^{\prime}_{i})||\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\leq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}(t^{\prime}_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i-1})\sup\big|g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||-g(t^{\prime})||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})||\big|\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\;,

where the first inequality follows from the assumption of
C
being regular. Together with the inequality in (
15
) we have,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}\int_{C_{t_{i}}}g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt\right|<\epsilon.

Finally, almost sure convergence yields for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle P\left[\left|\int_{a}^{b}g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{P}}\int_{C_{t_{i}}}g(t)||\mbox{$\bf s$}^{\prime}(t)||dt\right|<\epsilon\right]=1.

Considering a sequence of
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, and using the preceding arguments for each
\epsilon
we can find
\delta\downarrow 0
such that
|P|<\delta
which concludes the proof.

15.1
Temperatures in Northeastern US

Temperatures are historically known to exhibit spatial variation. We focus on a data set that records monthly temperatures across weather monitoring stations in the Northeastern United States during January, 2000 from the
R
-package
spBayes

(
Finley et al. 2007
)
. Temperature gradients and curvature are of interest from an environmental science perspective to track and perform boundary analysis on zones that exhibit significant changes in the surface during a month. Curvature wombling performed on temperature reveals climate zones featuring rapid atmospheric changes. Quantifying such variations in atmospheric conditions is central to statistical modeling in environmental applications. The data consists of temperatures (in degree Celsius) from 356 weather monitoring stations. The probability distribution for temperatures and interpolated spatial plot is shown in Figure
22
. We model the data using the hierarchical model outlined in (13) in the manuscript. We used the following hyper parameters for the model,
\phi\sim{\rm Unif}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{3}{max_{\s\in{\cal S}}{||\Delta||}}$}},300\right)
,
\sigma^{2}\sim IG(2,1)
(mean 1 variance infinite),
\tau^{2}\sim IG(2,1)
(mean 1 variance infinite),
\beta_{0}\sim N(0,10^{6})
, and
\nu=5/2
for the Matérn kernel. We consider
10^{4}
iterations for MCMC chains, with burn-in diagnosed at
5\times 10^{3}
. The posterior estimates from the model fit are shown in Table
8
.
