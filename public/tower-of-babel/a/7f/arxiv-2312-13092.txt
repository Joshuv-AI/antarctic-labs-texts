Chiral perturbation theory and Bose-Einstein condensation in QCD
Jens O. Andersen, Martin Kjøllesdal Johnsrud, Qing Yu, Hua Zhou

Abstract
We present recent results in three-flavor chiral perturbation theory at finite isospin μ I \mu_{I} and strangeness μ S \mu_{S} chemical potentials at zero temperature. The tree-level spectrum for the mesons and gauge bosons in the pion-condensed phase is derived. The phase diagram to 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 2 ) {\cal O}(p^{2}) in the μ I \mu_{I} – μ S \mu_{S} plane is mapped out with and without electromagnetic effects. The phase diagram consists of a vacuum phase and three Bose-condensed phases with condensates of π ± \pi^{\pm} , K ± K^{\pm} , and K 0 / K ¯ 0 K^{0}/\bar{K}^{0} , respectively. Including electromagnetic interactions, the charged Bose-condensed phases become Higgs phases via the Higgs mechanism. We calculate the pressure, energy density, isospin density, and speed of sound in the pion-condensed phase to 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 4 ) {\cal O}(p^{4}) . The results are compared with recent lattice simulations and the agreement is very good for isospin chemical potentials up to approximately 180 MeV. Moreover, by integrating out the s s -quark, we show that the thermodynamic quantities can be mapped onto their two-flavor counterparts with renormalized parameters. The breaking of the U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) symmetry in the Bose-condensed phases gives rise to a Goldstone boson, whose dispersion is linear for small momenta. We use Son’s prescription to construct an effective theory for the Goldstone mode in the pion-condensed phase, which is valid for momenta p ≪ μ I p\ll\mu_{I} . It is shown that its damping rate is of order p 5 p^{5} in the nonrelativistic limit, which is Beliaev’s result for a dilute Bose gas. It is also shown that in the nonrelativistic limit the energy density can be matched onto the classic result by Lee, Huang and Yang (LHY) for a dilute Bose, with an s s -wave scattering length that includes radiative corrections.

III
Thermodynamic potential and thermodynamic quantities

The thermodynamic potential
\Omega
is the object of interest since
we can calculate quantities such as the pressure, charge densities, and energy density from it. The thermodynamic potential typically depends on particle masses
m_{i}
, one or more chemical potentials
\mu_{i}

as well as other parameters that we denote by
\alpha_{i}
.
We have seen that for zero baryon chemical potential, we have two independent chemical potentials e.g.
\mu_{I}
and
\mu_{S}
. However, the phases we discuss in this paper are always described in terms of a single chemical potential, either
\mu_{I}
or
\pm{1\over 2}\mu_{I}+\mu_{S}
. Similarly,
the thermodynamic potential depends on a single additional parameter
\alpha
, which
in QCD can be identified with the rotation angle of the vacuum (see Sec.
IV
)
and is nonzero in the Bose-condensed phases. When we discuss the weakly interacting Bose gas, the parameter is denoted by
v
, which is the condensate density.
In the following we therefore write
\Omega(\mu,\alpha)
.

The thermodynamic potential can be written as a low-energy expansion in

\chi
PT or as an expansion in powers of
the dimensionless gas parameter
\sqrt{na^{3}}

in the case of a dilute Bose gas (See Sec.
IX.1
). Thus we write

\displaystyle\Omega(\mu,\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(\mu,\alpha)+\Omega_{1}(\mu,\alpha)+\cdots+\Omega_{m}(\mu,\alpha)+\cdots\;,

(69)

where the subscript
m
denotes the
m
th-order contribution in the series.
The value of
\alpha
that extremizes the thermodynamic potential for a given
\mu
is found by solving the equation

\displaystyle{\partial\Omega(\mu,\alpha)\over\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;,

(70)

and is denoted by
\alpha^{*}
. The pressure
{\cal P}
is equal to minus the thermodynamic
potential evaluated
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
, i.e.

\displaystyle{\cal P}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Omega(\mu,\alpha^{*})\;.

(71)

The charge density
n
associated with the chemical potential
\mu
is

\displaystyle n({\mu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\partial\Omega(\mu,\alpha)\over\partial\mu}\bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha^{*}}\;.

(72)

On the other hand, the pressure
{\cal P}
is a function of
\mu
alone,

{\cal P}(\mu)=-\Omega(\mu,\alpha^{*}(\mu))
. This yields

\displaystyle{d{\cal P}\over d\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[{\partial\Omega\over\partial\mu}+{\partial\Omega\over\partial\alpha^{*}}{\partial\alpha^{*}\over\partial\mu}\right]\;.

(73)

Since
{\partial\Omega\over\partial\alpha^{*}}={\partial\Omega\over\partial\alpha}\big|_{\alpha=\alpha^{*}}
,
the second term in Eq. (
73
) vanishes, implying that

\displaystyle n(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{d{\cal P}\over d\mu}\;.

(74)

Finally, the energy density
{\cal E}{(n)}
is given by a Legendre transform of
the pressure

\displaystyle{\cal E}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal P}(\mu)+n(\mu)\mu\;.

(75)

The solution
\alpha^{*}
to Eq. (
70
) can also written as a series

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\cdots,

(76)

where
\alpha_{0}
is the LO solution.
Expanding
{\Omega(\mu,\alpha^{*})}
around
\alpha_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal P}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Omega(\mu,\alpha^{*})=-\Omega_{0}(\mu,\alpha_{0})-{\partial\Omega_{0}(\mu,\alpha)\over\partial\alpha}\bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}\alpha_{1}

(77)

\displaystyle-\Omega_{1}(\mu,\alpha_{0})+\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Omega_{0}(\mu,\alpha_{0})-\Omega_{1}(\mu,\alpha_{0})+\cdots\;,

where we have used that the last term in the first line vanishes,
cf. Eq. (
70

To NLO in the expansion, the pressure is given by the NLO expression for the thermodynamic potential evaluated at the LO minimum
\alpha_{0}

For completeness, we show how to obtain the first correction

of the four candidates and the state with the highest pressure is our ground state.
Strictly speaking, we have not shown that our ground state corresponds to a minimum of the tree-level potential. Expanding in fluctuations about this point, it turns out that the linear terms
vanish (see Subsec.
IV.2
) and that the masses are real (see Subsec.
V.2
). Thus
it is locally stable, i.e. at least a metastable point. We will simply assume that it is also the global minimum.

Let us finally comment on the order of the transitions from the vacuum to a Bose-condensed phase. The Bose condensate is an order parameter and in order to calculate it, we need
to couple the system to an external pionic source

In the case of pion condensation, the external field becomes

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2B_{0}M+2iB_{0}\lambda_{2}j\;.

The leading-order thermodynamic potential then reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(\mu_{I},\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f^{2}B_{0}(m_{u}+m_{d}){\cos\alpha}-f^{2}B_{0}m_{s}

(94)

\displaystyle-2f^{2}B_{0}j\sin\alpha-{1\over 2}f^{2}\mu_{I}^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha\;.

The quark and pion condensates are then given by the derivatives of

with respect to

and

\displaystyle\langle\bar{q}q\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{{\partial\Omega_{0}(\mu_{I},\alpha)\over\partial m_{u}}+{\partial\Omega_{0}(\mu_{I},\alpha)\over\partial m_{d}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2f^{2}B_{0}\cos\alpha=\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm vac}\cos\alpha\;,

\displaystyle\langle\bar{q}\gamma^{5}i\lambda_{2}q\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\Omega_{0}(\mu_{I},\alpha)\over\partial j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2f^{2}B_{0}\sin\alpha=\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm vac}\sin\alpha\;,

where
\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm vac}

We note that the sum of the square of the condensates is constant and equals the square of the quark condensate in the vacuum. Thus the quark condensate is rotated into a pion condensate.
Using
\cos\alpha=m_{\pi,0}^{2}/\mu_{I}^{2}

\displaystyle{\langle\bar{q}\gamma^{5}i\lambda_{2}q\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm vac}\sqrt{1-{m_{\pi,0}^{4}\over\mu_{I}^{4}}}\;.

Close to the phase transition where

and
\mu_{I}+m_{\pi,0}\approx 2m_{\pi,0}

\displaystyle{\langle\bar{q}\gamma^{5}i\lambda_{2}q\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\langle\bar{q}q\rangle_{\rm vac}\sqrt{1-{m_{\pi,0}\over\mu_{I}}}\;.

Thus the order parameter is a continuous function of
\mu_{I}
close to the transition, which therefore is of second order. The mean-field critical exponent is
{1\over 2}
, which follows directly from Eq. (

There is another way to see this, based directly on a Ginzburg-Landau analysis of the thermodynamic potential. Expanding the thermodynamic potential Eq. (

\displaystyle\Omega_{{0}}(\mu_{I},\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{-f^{2}m_{\pi,0}^{2}+}{1\over 2}f^{2}\left[m_{\pi,0}^{2}-\mu_{I}^{2}\right]\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle+{1\over 24}f^{2}\left[4\mu_{I}^{2}-m_{\pi,0}^{2}\right]\alpha^{4}\;.

Since the quartic term is positive for

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{{6(\mu_{I}^{2}-m_{\pi,0}^{2})\over 4\mu_{I}^{2}-m_{\pi,0}^{2}}}\;.

IV.2
Fluctuations

The ground state
\Sigma_{\alpha}
minimizes the energy for given values of
\mu_{I}
and
\mu_{S}
. We would like to include quantum corrections and we therefore need to discuss the parametrization of the fluctuations around the ground state. In the vacuum,
\alpha=0
, we have
\Sigma=U\Sigma_{0}U
, where

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{{1\over 2}i{\phi_{a}\lambda_{a}}/f}\;.

(101)

A naive way of parametrizing
\Sigma
for arbitrary
\alpha
is

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U\Sigma_{\alpha}U\;.

(102)

Using this parametrization, one can expand the Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{2}
to
second order in the fluctuations. It turns out that the kinetic terms are not
canonically normalized so one needs a field redefinition, which happens to depend
on the chemical potential. After redefining the field one can calculate the NLO contribution to the thermodynamic potential arising from the functional determinant in the standard way.
The counterterms, given by the static part of the NLO Lagrangian
{\cal L}_{4}
, are
already fixed and do not cancel the divergences
for arbitrary values of
\alpha
, only for the value that corresponds to the
minimum of the tree-level thermodynamic potential, Eq. (
IV.1
).
In other words, we cannot renormalize the NLO thermodynamic potential away from the
classical minimum and therefore not determine the value of
\alpha
that
minimizes it. The problem is that the parametrization Eq. (
102
)
is not valid for nonzero
\alpha
, which was first pointed out in Ref.

Introducing

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}UA_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\;,\hskip 28.45274ptR_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}^{\dagger}U^{\dagger}A_{\alpha}\;,

(103)

where
A_{\alpha}=e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{a}\alpha}
(with
a=2,5,7
depending on the ground state
we consider), the correct parametrization is

\displaystyle\Sigma=L_{\alpha}\Sigma_{\alpha}R^{\dagger}_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}(U\Sigma_{0}U)A_{\alpha}\;.

(104)

Using this parametrization, the kinetic terms are automatically canonically normalized.
Moreover, the
{\cal O}(p^{4})
counterterms cancel the ultraviolet divergences arising
from the functional determinant for all values of
\alpha
, as we shall see in section
VI
.

Using the parametrization Eq. (

) in the Lagrangian (
24

and expanding in powers of the fields, we obtain

\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(0)}+\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(1)}+\cdots
,
where the superscript indicates the order in the fields.
To be specific, we consider pion condensation.
The zeroth-order term is
{{\cal L}_{2}^{(0)}=}-\Omega_{0}{(\mu_{I},\alpha)}
,
from the previous section. The linear term is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left[(\mu_{I}^{2}\cos\alpha-m_{\pi,0}^{2})\phi_{2}-\mu_{I}\partial_{0}\phi_{1}\right]\sin\alpha\;.

The

In the next section, we calculate the masses showing that the point we expand about is a minimum.
We can draw similar conclusions for the charged and neutral kaon condensates.
For a given pair of
\mu_{I}
and
\mu_{S}
, we calculate the
energy of the ground state of different phases and determine which is the global minimum

We close this section with a few remarks on finite density and symmetry
breaking. The conventional view is that the introduction of a chemical potential
\mu
breaks Lorentz invariance explicitly. However, in recent years an alternative view has been put forward by Nicolis and
collaborators
probing
;
nicbreak
, namely that Lorentz invariance is broken spontaneously. At finite chemical potential
\mu
, one uses the grand-canonical Hamiltonian defined as

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal H}-\mu Q\;,

(106)

where
{\cal H}
is the original Hamiltonian of the system and
Q
is the conserved charge that is a consequence of a continuous symmetry via Noether’s theorem and
associated with
\mu
. We are interested in the ground state
|\mu\rangle

of the modified Hamiltonian
{\cal H}^{\prime}
, satisfying

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{\prime}|\mu\rangle=0\;.

(107)

The right-hand side should read
\lambda|\mu\rangle
, but
\lambda
can always
be set to zero via a redefinition of the cosmological constant
probing
.
If the charge
Q
is spontaneously broken, it follows from Eq. (
107
) that time translations (which are generated by
{\cal H}
) are also spontaneously broken since the state
|\mu\rangle
is not an eigenstate of the original Hamiltonian
{\cal H}
.
However, spatial translations are not broken, which singles out the time direction as being special.
The fact that spatial translational symmetries are intact implies that the state
|\mu\rangle

breaks all Lorentz boosts. On the other hand, a new time translation symmetry is unbroken, namely the translations generated by
{\cal H}^{\prime}={\cal H}-\mu Q
. Summarizing, all the Lorentz boosts and the internal symmetry generated by
Q
are all spontaneously broken, while translations (space and time), rotations and the internal symmetries not generated by
Q
remain unbroken. In the case of pion condensation,
Q=Q_{I_{3}}
is the third component of the isospin and the internal symmetry that is broken spontaneously by the Bose condensate is

\mathrm{U}(1)_{I_{3}}
.

The idea is that instead of doing perturbation theory around a time-independent ground state, one expands around a time-dependent ground state.
We have denoted the time-independent ground state by
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pi^{\pm}}
.
Similarly, we denote the time-dependent ground state by
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pi^{\pm}}(t)
, this state can be written as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pi^{\pm}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pi^{\pm}}e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}A_{\alpha}\Sigma_{0}A_{\alpha}e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\;.

The ground state Eq. (
108
) breaks time invariance, hence it is
broken spontaneously.
Perturbation theory is now carried out around this state with the original chiral Lagrangian with
\mu_{I}=0
, which is Lorentz invariant.
This can be seen as follows. The field, now denoted by
\tilde{\Sigma}
,
is parametrized as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\Sigma e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}}

(109)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}A_{\alpha}U\Sigma_{0}UA_{\alpha}e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\;.

It can then be shown that

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\tilde{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}(\nabla_{\mu}\Sigma)e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\;,

(110)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}\chi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\Sigma\chi^{\dagger}e^{{1\over 2}i\lambda_{3}\mu_{I}t}\;,

(111)

where we have used that
\lambda_{3}
and the quark mass matrix commute.
Using
\partial_{\mu}\tilde{\Sigma}
,
\tilde{\Sigma}\chi^{\dagger}

as well as their Hermitian conjugates as building blocks, together with the cyclicity of the trace, we recover the original Lagrangian.

c_{1}\rightarrow{1\over 4fm_{\pi,0}}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{3p^{5}\over 640\pi m_{\pi,0}n_{I}}\;,

(224)

which is the classic result by Beliaev

IX
Dilute Bose gas and the nonrelativistic limit of
\chi
PT

In this section, we first briefly discuss the classic textbook example of Bose condensation, namely that of a nonrelativistic dilute Bose gas
fetter
. The leading correction to the energy density is derived using effective field theory methods. We then show that a pion condensate behaves nonrelativistically close to the phase transition
heman
. Finally, we recover the two-flavor results from Ref.
zhou
by taking the limit
m_{s}\rightarrow\infty
.

IX.1
Dilute Bose gas

The dilute Bose gas has been studied extensively for several decades beginning with the paper by Bogoliubov
bogo
in 1947. The starting point is a nonrelativistic low-energy effective field theory that describes the particles at momenta much lower than the inverse
of the range of their interactions
braaten
. The Lagrangian that describes the system at finite number density is

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}(i\partial_{0}+\mu_{\rm NR})\psi-{1\over 2m}\nabla\psi^{\dagger}\cdot\nabla\psi-{1\over 4}g(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)^{2}

(225)

\displaystyle-{1\over 4}h[\nabla(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)]^{2}-{1\over 36}g_{3}(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)^{3}+\cdots\;,

where the quantum field
\psi^{\dagger}
creates a particle,
\psi
destroys a particle,
\mu_{\rm NR}
is the nonrelativistic chemical potential,
g
, and
g_{3}
are coupling constants.
The ellipses indicate terms that are of higher order in the number fields
\psi
,
\psi^{\dagger}
and/or their derivatives.
The term
(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)^{2}
represents
2\rightarrow 2
scattering and the
coupling
g
is related to the
s
-wave scattering length
a
as
g={8\pi a\over m}
.
The term
{1\over 4}h[\nabla(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)]^{2}

includes the effective range
r_{s}
of the two-body potential, where

h=2\pi a^{2}r_{s}
. The term
(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)^{3}
represents
3\rightarrow 3
scattering.

At zero temperature, the expansion parameter of the dilute Bose gas is the so-called
(dimensionless) gas parameter
\sqrt{na^{3}}
, where
n
is the number density.
Bogoliubov
bogo
obtained the mean-field results for the pressure, number density, and energy density. For example, the energy density is
{\cal E}(n)={2\pi an^{2}\over m}
.
The leading corrections to Bogoliubov’s result for the energy density
were calculated by Lee, Huang, and Yang
LN
;
LN0
for a hard-sphere potential.
Later, part of the next-to-leading order correction was calculated
by Wu
wu
, by Hugenholz and Pines
hug
, and by Sawada
saw
.
A complete next-to-next-to-leading order result was obtained by Braaten and Nieto
braaten

using effective-field theory methods. The result depends not only on the scattering
length
a
, but also on an energy-independent term in the
scattering amplitude for
3\rightarrow 3
scattering. The result is

\displaystyle{\cal E}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\pi an^{2}\over m}\left[1+{128\over 15\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{na^{3}}\right.

(226)

\displaystyle\left.+\left({32\pi-24\sqrt{3}\over 3}\log{na^{3}}+\mathcal{G}\right)na^{3}\right]\;,

where
\mathcal{G}
is a constant involving the coupling
g_{3}
. It was already realized by Hugenholz and Pines that physical quantities depend on
other quantities than the
s
-wave scattering length
a
.
These effects are referred to as nonuniversal effects and are mimicked by e.g.
the terms
{1\over 4}h[\nabla(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)]^{2}
and

g_{3}(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)^{3}
in Eq. (
225
).
A detailed discussion of nonuniversal effects involving these terms and a
comparison with diffusion Monte Carlo calculations
latbec
can be found in Ref.
hermans
.

We now rederive the first two terms in the expansion Eq. (
226
) using the
effective nonrelativistic Lagrangian Eq. (
225
).
The first term is the mean-field result, while the second arises from a one-loop calculation.
The complex field is written as
\psi=v+\tilde{\psi}
, where

v=\langle\psi\rangle
is its expectation value and
\tilde{\psi}
is a fluctuating
quantum field with
\langle\tilde{\psi}\rangle=0
. The fluctuating field is written as

\tilde{\psi}={1\over\sqrt{2}}(\psi_{1}+i\psi_{2})
, where
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are real fields.
To second order in the fluctuations, one finds

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm NR}v^{2}-{1\over 4}gv^{4}\;,

(227)

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{vX\over\sqrt{2}m}\psi_{1}\;,

(228)

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\rm(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}(\dot{\psi}_{1}\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\dot{\psi}_{2})+{1\over 4m}\psi_{1}\left(\nabla^{2}+Y\right)\psi_{1}

(229)

\displaystyle+{1\over 4m}\psi_{2}\left(\nabla^{2}+X\right)\psi_{2}\;,

where the superscript indicates the power of the field, a dot means a time derivative,
X=2m(\mu_{\rm NR}-{1\over 2}gv^{2})
, and
Y=2m(\mu_{\rm NR}-{3\over 2}gv^{2})
. The propagator matrix is

\displaystyle D(\omega,p)={i\over\omega^{2}-E^{2}(p)+i\epsilon}\begin{pmatrix}{1\over 2m}\left(p^{2}-X\right)&-i\omega\\
i\omega&{1\over 2m}\left(p^{2}-Y\right)\\
\end{pmatrix}\;,

where the spectrum is

\displaystyle E(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2m}\sqrt{(p^{2}-X)(p^{2}-Y)}\;.

(231)

The thermodynamic potential in the mean-field approximation is as usual given by minus the static part of the Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(\mu_{\rm NR},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu_{\rm NR}v^{2}+{1\over 4}gv^{4}\;.

(232)

The minimum of

v_{0}=\sqrt{{2\mu_{\rm NR}\over g}}

v_{0}

and Eq. (
231

E_{p}={p\over 2m}\sqrt{p^{2}+4m\mu_{\rm NR}}

p^{2}\ll 4m\mu_{\rm NR}
, and that of a free nonrelativistic particle for large momenta,

p^{2}\gg 4m\mu_{\rm NR}
.

In order to calculate the NLO corrections to the thermodynamic quantities, we need a few divergent loop integrals. We use dimensional regularization to regulate these integrals. The integrals needed were introduced in Ref.
effective
and are of the form

\displaystyle I_{m,n}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2\epsilon}\int{d^{d}p\over(2\pi)^{d}}{p^{2m}\over p^{n}\left(p^{2}+M^{2}\right)^{{n\over 2}}}\;,

(233)

They satisfy the recursion relation

\displaystyle{dI_{m,n}{(M^{2})}\over dM^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}nI_{m+1,n+2}(M^{2})\;,

(234)

which follows directly from the definition Eq. (
233

Evaluating the integrals in dimensional regularization, we find

\displaystyle I_{m,n}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{M^{3+2m-2n}\over(4\pi)^{{{d}\over 2}}}\left({\Lambda\over M}\right)^{2\epsilon}

(235)

\displaystyle\times{\Gamma({d-n\over 2}+m)\Gamma(n-m-{d\over 2})\over\Gamma({n\over 2})\Gamma({d\over 2})}\;.

We specifically need

\displaystyle I_{0,-1}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{16\over 15}{M^{5}\over(4\pi)^{2}}\left[1+{\cal O}(\epsilon)\right]\;,

(236)

\displaystyle I_{1,1}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{16M^{3}\over 3(4\pi)^{2}}\left[1+{\cal O}(\epsilon)\right]\;.

(237)

The integrals are finite in the limit

reflecting that the
ultraviolet divergences are powerlike.

The NLO pressure is given by the NLO thermodynamic potential evaluated at the classical minimum

cf. Eq. (

\displaystyle{\cal P}(\mu_{\rm NR})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Omega_{0}(\mu_{\rm NR},v_{0})-\Omega_{1}(\mu_{\rm NR},v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mu_{\rm NR}^{2}\over g}-{1\over 2}\int_{p}E(p)

\displaystyle{\mu_{\rm NR}^{2}\over g}-{1\over 4m}I_{0,-1}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mu_{\rm NR}^{2}\over g}\left[1-{16(4m)^{{3\over 2}}\sqrt{\mu_{\rm NR}g^{2}}\over 15(4\pi)^{2}}\right]\;,

where
M^{2}=4m\mu_{\rm NR}
, and we have used Eq. (
236

The number density is then given by

\displaystyle n(\mu_{\rm NR})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\mu_{\rm NR}\over g}-{1\over 2}I_{1,1}(M^{2})\;,

(239)

where we have used the recursion relation Eq. (

We can invert Eq. (

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm NR}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}gn+{1\over 4}gI_{1,1}(2mgn)

(240)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{4\pi an\over m}\left[1+{32\over{3}\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{na^{3}}\right]\;,

\displaystyle{\cal E}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal P}+\mu_{\rm NR}n

(241)

\displaystyle{1\over 4}gn^{2}+{1\over 4m}I_{0,-1}(2mgn)\;.

Note that, to the order we are calculating, the terms involving

\displaystyle{\cal E}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\pi an^{2}\over m}\left[1+{128\over 15\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{na^{3}}\right]\;.

X
Summary and Outlook

In this paper, we have discussed various aspects of Bose condensation in QCD
at finite
\mu_{I}
and
\mu_{S}

using chiral perturbation theory, which is the low-energy effective theory describing the pseudo-Goldstone bosons.
We have been focusing on the pion-condensed phase mainly due to the fact
that in this case it is possible to compare our predictions with those of lattice QCD.
However, with relatively little effort similar results for the kaon-condensed phases
can be obtained. Lattice QCD and
\chi
PT agree very well in the region where the latter is
expected to be valid. Depending on taste, one can view this as a check of
\chi
PT as an effective theory of QCD or a check of the simulations.
Bose-condensation in QCD is a very rich system: in the region
\mu_{I}\simeq m_{\pi}
, we have made
contact with the dilute Bose gas and the classic results by Bogoliubov, Beliaev, Lee, Yang and Huang, and others. In the ultrarelativistic limit, the Goldstone mode is exactly linear and propagates with the speed of light.
The speed of sound,
c_{s}=\sqrt{{\mu_{I}^{4}-m_{\pi,0}^{4}}\over\mu_{I}^{4}+3m_{\pi,0}^{4}}
,
is a measure of how relativistic the system is.

The present work can be extended in several directions. Firstly, it would be interesting to calculate the thermodynamic quantities and the phase diagram to order
{\cal O}(p^{4})
including electromagnetic interactions. This would require using Urech’s next-to-leading order Lagrangian
urech1
and the evaluation of a very complicated functional determinant.
In the same vein, one could calculate the meson and gauge boson masses to
{\cal O}(p^{4})
.
A more straightforward extension would be to finite temperature. In a two-flavor
calculation
mojahed
, the critical line between the normal phase and the BEC phase was mapped out in the
\mu_{I}
-
T
plane. Good agreement between
\chi
PT and lattice simulations was only found for temperatures up to approximately 30 MeV. Whether the inclusion of heavier mesons would improve the situation is an
open question.

We have also noticed the disagreement between
\chi
PT and the lattice regarding the
speed of sound for large values of
\mu_{I}
, which is caused by the fact that

\chi
PT has mesonic bound states as degrees of freedom and not quarks.
This problem was addressed in Ref.
kojo
, where the two-flavor quark-meson model was investigated at finite isospin and vanishing temperature.
The model is in qualitative agreement with lattice data from the BEC to the BCS regime, suggesting that it captures the correct degrees of freedom.
