The difference vectors for convex sets and a resolution of the geometry conjecture
Salihah Alwadani, Heinz H. Bauschke, Julian P. Revalski, and Xianfu Wang

Abstract
The geometry conjecture, which was posed nearly a quarter of a century ago, states that the fixed point set of the composition of projectors onto nonempty closed convex sets in Hilbert space is actually equal to the intersection of certain translations of the underlying sets. In this paper, we provide a complete resolution of the geometry conjecture. Our proof relies on monotone operator theory. We revisit previously known results and provide various illustrative examples. Comments on the numerical computation of the quantities involved are also presented.

Introduction

1.1
Fixed points of compositions of projectors

Throughout,

X
is a real Hilbert space

(1)

and

C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
are nonempty closed convex subsets of
X
,

(2)

with projectors

P_{C_{1}},\ldots,P_{C_{m}}
which we also write more simply as

P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
,
and with
m\in\{2,3,\ldots,\}
.
We define the fixed point sets of the cyclic compositions by

F_{m}:=\operatorname{Fix}(P_{m}\cdots P_{1}),F_{m-1}:=\operatorname{Fix}(P_{m-1}\cdots P_{1}P_{m}),\ldots,F_{1}:=\operatorname{Fix}(P_{1}P_{m}\cdots P_{2}).

(3)

Compositions of projectors are often employed in projection methods.
This is a vast area which we will not summarize here; however, we
refer the reader to
[
17
]
as a starting point as well as
the very recent paper
[
19
]
.

1.2
The geometry conjecture, difference vectors, and cycles

The
geometry conjecture
, formulated first in 1997
(see
[
9
, Conjecture 5.1.6]
), states that
there exists a list of vectors

v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
in
X
such that

v_{1}+v_{2}+\cdots+v_{m}=0

(4)

and

F_{m}=C_{m}\cap(C_{m-1}+v_{m-1})\cap\cdots\cap(C_{1}+v_{1}+\cdots+v_{m-1}),

(5)

and analogously for
F_{m-1},\ldots,F_{1}
.
These vectors form the tuple
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
of
difference vectors

and they are sometimes also referred to as displacement vectors or gap vectors.

This conjecture is known to be true when

m=2
or
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\cap\cdots\cap C_{m}\neq\varnothing
;
see
[
9
, Subsection 5.1]
.
The following is known when all sets
F_{i}
are nonempty:
let
f_{1}\in F_{1}
, and set
f_{2}:=P_{2}f_{1},f_{3}:=P_{3}f_{2},\ldots,f_{m}:=P_{m}f_{m-1}
;
we shall refer to the tuple
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
as a
cycle
.
(Cycles are of interest even when
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
are all hyperplanes
— see
[
15
, Chapter 8]
and
[
16
, Chapter 50]
.)
Setting

v_{1}=f_{2}-f_{1},\;v_{2}=f_{3}-f_{2},\;\ldots,\;v_{m-1}=f_{m}-f_{m-1},\;v_{m}=f_{1}-f_{m},

(6)

which turns out to be independent of the cycle chosen,
makes
Eq.

4
true and yields
“one half” of
Eq.

5
, namely:

F_{m}\subseteq C_{m}\cap(C_{m-1}+v_{m-1})\cap\cdots\cap(C_{1}+v_{1}+\cdots+v_{m-1})

and analogously for
F_{m-1},\ldots,F_{1}

(see
[
9
, Theorem 5.1.2]
).
However, this description is not fully satisfying —
it is only
implicit
in the sense
it was not known
what the difference vectors are when the fixed point sets
F_{i}
are empty.
The sole exception to this mystery was the case when
m=2
which allowed
for the
explicit
description of the
two difference vectors by

P_{\overline{C_{2}-C_{1}}}(0),P_{\overline{C_{1}-C_{2}}}(0);

(7)

see
[
7
, Lemmas 2.1 and 2.3]
. Note
that this description is
not
based on the fixed point sets
F_{1},F_{2}
.
In this particular case, these fixed point set have the beautiful
description (see
[
18
, Theorem 2]
)

F_{1}=\big\{{x\in C_{1}}~\big|~{d_{C_{2}}(x)=\inf\|C_{1}-C_{2}\|}\big\},F_{2}=\big\{{x\in C_{2}}~\big|~{d_{C_{1}}(x)=\inf\|C_{1}-C_{2}\|}\big\};

(8)

moreover, the cycles
(f_{1},f_{2})
are precisely the minimizers
of the bivariate function

X\times X\to\mathbb{R}\colon(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\iota_{C_{1}}(x_{1})+\iota_{C_{2}}(x_{2}),

(9)

where
d_{S}
and
\iota_{S}
denote the distance and indicator function of a subset
S
of
X
,
respectively.
(See
[
18
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
, and
[
9
]
for much more on the case
when
m=2
.)
A referee also pointed out that when
m=2
and
F_{1}=F_{2}=\varnothing
one cannot expect
uniqueness of the difference vectors as one may simply separate the sets even further.

The case when
m\geq 3
is very interesting:
The
negative
result of
Baillon, Combettes, and Cominetti (see
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
)
states that when
X
is at least two-dimensional, then
there is
no
function
\varphi
such that
the cycles are precisely the minimizers of the function

\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})+\iota_{C_{1}}(x_{1})+\cdots+\iota_{C_{m}}(x_{m})
.
(When
m=2
, we can pick
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|
or even the
differentiable function
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}
.
For results on underrelaxed projectors, see also
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
.)
Even when cycles exist, the “meaning” of the distance vector was
not understood.

2
The displacement of the circular right shift operator

2.1
Product space and Attouch-Théra duality

From now on, we will also work in the product space

{\mathbf{X}}:=X^{m}

(10)

in which we set

{\mathbf{C}}:=C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{m}\;\;\text{and}\;\;{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}:=\big\{{(x,\ldots,x)\in{\mathbf{X}}}~\big|~{x\in X}\big\}.

(11)

It is well known that the projectors onto these sets are given by

P_{{\mathbf{C}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\big(P_{1}x_{1},\ldots,P_{m}x_{m}\big)

(12)

and

P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\frac{1}{m}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i},\ldots,\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\Big)

(13)

respectively (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Proposition 29.4 and Proposition 26.4(iii)]
).
Next, we define the circular right-shift operator

{\mathbf{R}}\colon{\mathbf{X}}\to{\mathbf{X}}\colon(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto(x_{m},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m-1}).

(14)

Recall (see
Section

1.2
) that

\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\in{\mathbf{X}}

is a cycle
if
z_{1}=P_{1}z_{m}
,
z_{2}=P_{2}z_{1}
,
…,
z_{m}=P_{m}z_{m-1}
, which
can be elegantly reformulated in
{\mathbf{X}}
as the fixed point equation

\mathbf{z}=P_{{\mathbf{C}}}({\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{z}).

(15)

Denote the (possibly empty)
set of all cycles
by

{\mathbf{Z}}:=\operatorname{Fix}(P_{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{R}}).

(16)

In passing, we note that if
Q_{i}\colon(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\to x_{i}
,
then
F_{i}=Q_{i}({\mathbf{Z}})
.
Because
P_{\mathbf{C}}=(\operatorname{Id}+N_{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}
, where
N_{\mathbf{C}}
denotes the normal cone operator of
{\mathbf{C}}
,
it follows that

Eq.

15
is equivalent to

{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{z}\in(\operatorname{Id}+N_{\mathbf{C}})(\mathbf{z})
and to

0\in N_{{\mathbf{C}}}(\mathbf{z})+(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})(\mathbf{z}).

(17)

We view this last inclusion sum problem
as primal (Attouch-Théra) problem
for the pair
(N_{\mathbf{C}},\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})
.
(See
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
for more on Attouch-Théra duality.)
In view of the linearity of
{\mathbf{R}}
,
the Attouch-Théra dual problem simplifies to

0\in N_{{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})+(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}).

(18)

If
\mathbf{z}
is any cycle; equivalently, a solution to the primal problem

Eq.

17
,
then a direct computation (or
[
10
, Proposition 2.4(iii)]
) shows that

N_{{\mathbf{C}}}(\mathbf{z})\cap-(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})(\mathbf{z})
is a nonempty subset of dual solutions.
Even better, both
N_{{\mathbf{C}}}
and
\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}}
are
paramonotone

in the sense of Iusem
[
22
]

by, e.g.,
[
11
, Example 22.4(i) and Example 22.9]
.
It thus follows from
[
10
, Theorem 5.3]
that

(\forall\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbf{Z}})\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}\text{~is the \emph{unique} solution of \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:ATdual}}

(19)

and that

\text{if $\mathbf{y}$ solves \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:ATdual}, then~~}{\mathbf{Z}}=N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\cap-(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\neq\varnothing

(20)

2.2
(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}
and the skew operator
{\mathbf{T}}

Recall the definition of the circular right shift operator
{\mathbf{R}}

(see
Eq.

14
).
By
[
2
, Proposition 2.4]
, we have

P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}.

(21)

Now define

{\mathbf{T}}=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-2k){\mathbf{R}}^{k},

(22)

which is a
skew
(hence maximally monotone) linear operator on
{\mathbf{X}}
, i.e.,

{\mathbf{T}}^{*}=-{\mathbf{T}}

(23)

with

{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{T}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}

(24)

(see
[
2
, Proposition 3.2(ii)&(iii)]
).
Then

[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
states that

(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+N_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}+{\mathbf{T}}.

(25)

This form of
(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}
makes it clear that this operator
is
strongly monotone
with constant
\tfrac{1}{2}
which implies that

the dual problem
Eq.

18
has
at most
one solution

(26)

which is consistent with
Eq.

19
.
(A feature of Attouch-Théra duality is that either both primal
and dual have solutions or they both don’t.
It is possible that there is no cycle and hence no dual solution;
see
Section

5.2.2
.)

We now collect some useful identities.

Proposition 2.1
.

We have
P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}{\mathbf{R}}={\mathbf{R}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
and
hence
P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}{\mathbf{R}}={\mathbf{R}}-P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
.

Proof
. Recalling
Eq.

21
, we observe that

P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}{\mathbf{R}}={\mathbf{R}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k+1}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}=P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

(27)

because
{\mathbf{R}}^{m}={\mathbf{R}}^{0}=\operatorname{Id}
.
It follows that

P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}{\mathbf{R}}=(\operatorname{Id}-P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}){\mathbf{R}}={\mathbf{R}}-P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
.

\hfill\hskip 10.00002pt\blacksquare

For the remainder of this section, let us abbreviate

{\mathbf{Q}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}.

(28)

Clearly,
{\mathbf{Q}}
commutes with
{\mathbf{R}}
, and hence also with
P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

by
Eq.

21
.

Proposition 2.2
.

2{\mathbf{Q}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=(m-1)P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
.

Proof
. Using
Proposition

2.1
, we see that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}kP_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\frac{1}{m}\frac{(m-1)m}{2}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\frac{m-1}{2}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

(29)

as claimed.

\hfill\hskip 10.00002pt\blacksquare

Proposition 2.3
.

-{\mathbf{Q}}(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})=P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}
.

Proof
. Using

Eq.

28
and
Eq.

21
, we obtain

\displaystyle-m{\mathbf{Q}}(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{R}}-\operatorname{Id})(m{\mathbf{Q}})=({\mathbf{R}}-\operatorname{Id})\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}

(30a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k+1}-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}=\sum_{k=2}^{m}(k-1){\mathbf{R}}^{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}

(30b)

\displaystyle=(m-1){\mathbf{R}}^{m}+\Big(\sum_{k=2}^{m-1}\big((k-1)-k\big){\mathbf{R}}^{k}\Big)-{\mathbf{R}}

(30c)

\displaystyle=(m-1)\operatorname{Id}-\Big(\sum_{k=2}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}\Big)-{\mathbf{R}}

(30d)

\displaystyle=m\operatorname{Id}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}=m\operatorname{Id}-mP_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=mP_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}},

(30e)

which completes the proof.

\hfill\hskip 10.00002pt\blacksquare

We are now ready for the main result of this section
which will play a key role in subsequent sections.

Theorem 2.4
.

We have

\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+{\mathbf{T}}=\tfrac{m}{2}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-{\mathbf{Q}}

(31)

and

\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+{\mathbf{T}}\big)^{-1}=\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}}+2P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}.

(32)

Proof
. Using

Eq.

22
,

Eq.

21
, and

Eq.

28
,
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-2k){\mathbf{R}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}{\mathbf{R}}^{k}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k{\mathbf{R}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(-\operatorname{Id}+mP_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\Big)-{\mathbf{Q}}

(33)

which gives

Eq.

31
.

Next, using
Eq.

31
,

Proposition

2.1
,

Proposition

2.2
,
and
Proposition

2.3
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+{\mathbf{T}}\big)\big(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}}+2P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{m}{2}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-{\mathbf{Q}}\big)\big(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}}+2P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})

(34a)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{m}{2}\big(P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}{\mathbf{R}}\big)+mP_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}-{\mathbf{Q}}(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})-2{\mathbf{Q}}P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}

(34b)

\displaystyle=mP_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}+P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}-(m-1)P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}+P_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}

(34c)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Id}.

(34d)

This verifies
Eq.

32
and thus completes the proof.

\hfill\hskip 10.00002pt\blacksquare

Corollary 2.5
.

We have

\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+T\big)^{-1}|_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}=(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}},

(35)

Proof
. From
Eq.

32
, we have

\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+T\big)^{-1}|_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}=(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}}+2P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}=(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}
.

3
The proof of the geometry conjecture

Armed with
Eq.

25
,
write the operator from the dual problem
Eq.

18

as

N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}+(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}=N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+N_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}+{\mathbf{T}}.

(36)

This operator is in general not maximally monotone.
On the other hand,

N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}+N_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}=N_{{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1}+N_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}^{-1}=\partial\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}+\partial\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\subseteq\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}
,
where
\sigma_{\mathbf{S}}
denotes the support function of a subset

\mathbf{S}
of
{\mathbf{X}}
.
Altogether, instead of working with
Eq.

36
, which has
no solution if there are no cycles,
we propose to work with the
the
enlarged
dual problem featuring the
maximally
and strongly monotone
operator

\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+{\mathbf{T}}+\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}.

(37)

Using, e.g.,
[
11
, Proposition 22.11(ii)]
,
the corresponding inclusion problem
always has a unique zero, which we denote by

\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbf{X}}
:

0\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}+{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}+\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}(\mathbf{y}).

(38)

(In fact,
\mathbf{y}
is the resolvent of the maximally monotone
operator
2{\mathbf{T}}+2\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}
,
evaluated at
0
.)
Note that, using
[
11
, Proposition 6.49 and Example 11.2]

\mathbf{y}\in\operatorname{dom}\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}=\operatorname{dom}(\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}}+\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}})=\operatorname{dom}\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}\cap\operatorname{dom}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\subseteq(\operatorname{rec}{\mathbf{C}})^{\ominus}\cap(\operatorname{rec}{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})^{\ominus}
;
thus,

\mathbf{y}\in(\operatorname{rec}{\mathbf{C}})^{\ominus}\cap{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}.

(39)

Now define

\mathbf{e}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}-{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp},

(40)

where
\mathbf{e}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}

because

\mathbf{y}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}
(see
Eq.

39
)
and

{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{T}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}
(see
Eq.

24
).
Note that
-\mathbf{e}=(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Id}+{\mathbf{T}})\mathbf{y}
.
Hence
Eq.

35
yields

\mathbf{y}=(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})(-\mathbf{e})={\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{e}.

(41)

Note that
Eq.

38
is equivalent to

\mathbf{e}\in\partial\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}(\mathbf{y})=\partial\iota^{*}_{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}(\mathbf{y})
,
and hence also to

\mathbf{y}\in N_{\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}}(\mathbf{e}),

(42)

where the superscript “
\mbox{}^{*}
” denotes Fenchel conjugation.
We pause here to record the following result which
provides a certificate for
\mathbf{y}
:

Proposition 3.1
.

(a characterization of
\mathbf{y}
)

The unique solution to
Eq.

38
is the unique vector
\mathbf{y}
satisfying
the following:

\mathbf{y}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp},\;\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}-{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}\in\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}},\;\;\text{and}\;\;(\forall\mathbf{c}\in{\mathbf{C}})\;\left\langle{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}.

(43)

Proof
. As seen,
\mathbf{y}
solves
Eq.

38

if and only if
Eq.

42
holds with
\mathbf{e}
defined in
Eq.

40
.
The latter condition is equivalent to

\mathbf{e}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}-{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}\in\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}
and

(\forall(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})\in{\mathbf{C}}\times{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})

\langle{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}+{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}}\rangle\leq 0
.
Because
\mathbf{y}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}
(see
Eq.

39
)
and
{\mathbf{T}}
is skew (see
Eq.

23
),
the last condition is indeed equivalent to
Eq.

43
.

\hfill\hskip 10.00002pt\blacksquare

Combining
Eq.

40
and
Eq.

42
,
we deduce that

\mathbf{e}\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}\cap\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}.

(44)

(This last intersection
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}\cap\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}

need not be a singleton as we can
see by studying the case when
C_{1}=C_{2}=\cdots=C_{m}=X
and hence
{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{X}}
,
in which case the intersection is
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}
.)

Theorem 3.2
.

With
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{e}
as defined in

Eq.

38
and
Eq.

40
respectively,
the set of cycles is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle=N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\cap(\mathbf{e}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}})

(45a)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}+\big({\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\cap({\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{e})\big).

(45b)

Proof
. First,

{\mathbf{C}}\subseteq\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}

because
0\in{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}
.
Hence

(\forall\mathbf{c}\in{\mathbf{C}})

\left\langle{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{e}}\right\rangle\leq 0
by
Eq.

42
.
It follows that

\sigma_{{\mathbf{C}}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\left\langle{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{e}}\right\rangle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2},

(46)

where the equality follows from
Eq.

23

and
Eq.

40
.

Next,
\mathbf{y}
might even solve the original dual
Eq.

18
in which case

{\mathbf{Z}}
is given by
Eq.

20
. Whether or not this is the case, we

always
have, using

Eq.

25
,
Eq.

40
, and

Eq.

39
,

\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\cap-(\operatorname{Id}-{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=N_{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\cap\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}-{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{y}-N_{{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{y})\big)

(47a)

\displaystyle=N_{{\mathbf{C}}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\cap(\mathbf{e}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}).

(47b)

Altogether, this yields
Eq.

.

which is Lipschitz continuous with constant

because
{\mathbf{R}}
is an isometry.

We point out the cases when
m=2
and
m=3
,
for which
\gamma=1/2
and
\gamma=3/5
respectively,
and
Eq.

133
turns into

\text{\rm\big[$m=2$ and $\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}$\big]}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;(1-\gamma)\operatorname{Id}+\gamma{\mathbf{R}}-2\gamma P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\equiv 0\text{\rm~is $0$-Lipschitz }

(134)

and

\text{\rm\big[$m=3$ and $\gamma=\tfrac{3}{5}$\big]}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;(1-\gamma)\operatorname{Id}+\gamma{\mathbf{R}}-2\gamma P_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=\tfrac{1}{5}{\mathbf{R}}-\tfrac{2}{5}{\mathbf{R}}^{2}\text{\rm~is $\tfrac{3}{5}$-Lipschitz.}

(135)

Note that
Eq.

134
looks at first puzzling because
then
Eq.

125b
turns into

\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=P_{\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}}(0)

and so
Eq.

126
yields
\mathbf{e}=P_{\overline{{\mathbf{C}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}}(0)
;
however, we already observed this directly in
Eq.

61
.

8
Conclusion and future work

Using the framework of monotone operator theory,
we resolved the geometry conjecture completely.
We obtained alternative descriptions of the set of cycles
{\mathbf{Z}}
.
We also sketched numerical approaches for the computation
of the difference vector
{\mathbf{v}}
by using Seeger’s algorithm.

Turning to future research, it is desirable to
devise algorithms for computing
{\mathbf{v}}
without having to employ
Seeger’s algorithm. Moreover, it is interesting to extend
the results in this paper from projectors to (underrelaxed) projectors
or even proximal mappings. We have taken steps in this direction,
and initial progress appears to be quite promising
[
3
]
.
