Edge modes as reference frames and boundary actions from post-selection


Abstract
We introduce a general framework realizing edge modes in (classical) gauge field theory as dynamical reference frames, an often suggested interpretation that we make entirely explicit. We focus on a bounded region M M with a co-dimension one time-like boundary Γ \Gamma , which we embed in a global spacetime. Taking as input a variational principle at the global level, we develop a systematic formalism inducing consistent variational principles (and in particular, boundary actions) for the subregion M M . This relies on a post-selection procedure on Γ \Gamma , which isolates the subsector of the global theory compatible with a general choice of gauge-invariant boundary conditions for the dynamics in M M . Crucially, the latter relate the configuration fields on Γ \Gamma to a dynamical frame field carrying information about the spacetime complement of M M ; as such, they may be equivalently interpreted as frame-dressed or relational observables. Generically, the external frame field keeps an imprint on the ensuing dynamics for subregion M M , where it materializes itself as a local field on the time-like boundary Γ \Gamma ; in other words, an edge mode. We identify boundary symmetries as frame reorientations and show that they divide into three types, depending on the boundary conditions, that affect the physical status of the edge modes. Our construction relies on the covariant phase space formalism, and is in principle applicable to any gauge (field) theory. We illustrate it on three standard examples: Maxwell, Abelian Chern-Simons and non-Abelian Yang-Mills theories. In complement, we also analyze a mechanical toy-model to connect our work with recent efforts on (quantum) reference frames.

A basic idea we will invoke in our construction is that of

We view post-selection as a unifying concept allowing one to incorporate operational constraints into a given overarching theory to select the subsector of that theory consistent with the constraints. These are associated to physical events of a contingent nature. Such constraints may encode the boundary conditions describing the context of a controlled experiment (e.g. a scattering experiment), or more broadly speaking, the state of the world we happen to observe as agents. This broad understanding seems particularly relevant in the gravitational context, where controlled experiments are more the exception than the norm. In (quantum) general relativity, our control/engineering abilities are essentially non-existent, but what we observe around us is nonetheless post-selected from within the set of all possibilities admitted by the global theory.

For Yang-Mills (and in particular Maxwell) theories, the imprint of edge modes on the reduced phase space remains arguably mild: once boundary conditions have been imposed, at most a finite number of global charges survive at the physical level. This is dramatically less than in Chern-Simons theory, which generates an algebra of symmetries parametrized by a space of functions on
\Gamma

This ultimately leads to a refinement of the notion of boundary symmetries, as proposed in

Symmetries are physical transformations in the regional phase space, while meta-symmetries are symplectomorphisms between different regional phase spaces (i.e. different subregion theories), and boundary gauge symmetries arise when the charges turn into additional first-class constraints on the boundary (which will always be the case for Neumann boundary conditions).

Even though we are mainly motivated by (quantum) gravity, our focus in the present article will be on non-gravitational gauge theories, in which the metric is non-dynamical (though can be curved). We however anticipate our interpretation of edge modes as dynamical reference frames to extend (with suitable adaptations) to gravitational theories, a problem we plan to return to in the future. Organization of the paper.
We start in Sec.
2

This serves as a simple illustration of the general formalism to be developed later, in a context where conceptual connections with the existing literature on dynamical reference frames are not obscured by field theory technicalities. Edge modes and post-selection in a mechanical system*

*
This section may be skipped by a quick reader only interested in our field theory constructions. It may serve as an introduction to the covariant phase space formalism for the uninitiated.

As a warm-up exercise, and for the sake of conceptual clarity, we shall illustrate a few illuminating observations in a mechanical toy model before later exploiting them in the field theory context. This model mimics finite region gauge theories through groups of particles subject to translation invariance. Specifically, this model serves to show

Symmetries leave a subregion theory defined by given boundary conditions invariant, while a meta-symmetry maps one such theory into another one defined by different boundary conditions. Boundary gauge symmetries arise in addition to the already present gauge symmetry and only for Neumann boundary conditions.

While the mechanical toy model captures many of the qualitative features we shall later encounter in gauge field theory, it also leads to some differences which we try to highlight. For example, owing to the discrete nature of the degrees of freedom, it does not give rise to corner terms. Furthermore, gauge constraints arise as identities already off-shell and, due to the simplicity of the model, are non-local in contrast to our field theory examples.

2.1
Covariant phase space in mechanics

Suppose we are given a group of
N
particles in 1D Newtonian space, collectively called
M
, subject to an action
S_{M}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L_{M}
with Lagrangian form

L_{M}={\mathcal{L}}(q_{i},\dot{q}_{i})\mathrm{d}t\,.{}

Our configuration space is
{\mathcal{Q}}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
which is the mechanical analog of the space of instantaneous field configurations, i.e. space of field configurations on some Cauchy slice. By contrast, the mechanical analog of the space of field configurations here is the space of spatiotemporal configurations of
M
, in other words, the space of
histories
for the time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, which we take to be
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}:=\{c:[t_{1},t_{2}]\rightarrow{\mathcal{Q}}\,|\,\text{$c$ is a $C^{2}$ curve}\}
and for simplicity often refer to as “field space”.
\mathring{M}
denotes here internal or “bulk” degrees of freedom in distinction of the situation when we include external frame particles below. The action is a function
S_{M}:{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, while the Lagrangian can be thought of as a functional
{\mathcal{L}}[c,t]={\mathcal{L}}(q_{i}(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t))
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
because a history
c=\{q_{i}(t),t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\}
and an instant
t
determine the positions and velocities
(q_{i}(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t))
. For the moment it is irrelevant whether the Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}
features a gauge symmetry.

{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
is itself a smooth infinite-dimensional manifold, allowing us to define standard differential geometric notions on it. In what follows, we will be using the covariant phase space formalism, constructing a phase space from the subspace
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
of solutions to the equations of motion. In line with standard convention for the covariant phase space in field theory, which we adopt throughout this work, we denote the exterior derivative on field space
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
by
\delta
, while reserving the notation
\mathrm{d}
for the exterior derivative of spacetime forms. In the mechanical case, these are forms on the time manifold
[t_{1},t_{2}]
underlying the action
S_{M}
. As such, we will be dealing with
(r,s)
-forms
\mu
, which means
\mu
is an
r
-form on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
and an
s
-form on spacetime. For example, the Lagrangian form
L_{M}
is a
(0,1)
-form. The two exterior derivatives commute with each other (
\left[\mathrm{d},\delta\right]=0
), and both square to zero (
\mathrm{d}^{2}=0
and
\delta^{2}=0
).

The variation of the action on field space
\delta S_{M}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta L_{M}
is determined through the exterior derivative of the Lagrangian
(0,1)
-form

\delta L_{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial q_{i}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right)\delta q_{i}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\mathring{M}}\,,

(1)

where the Lagrangian symplectic potential (a
(1,0)
form) takes the form

\Theta_{\mathring{M}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\delta q_{i}\,

(2)

and gives rise to the Lagrangian presymplectic structure (a
(2,0)

\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\delta\Theta_{\mathring{M}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}\partial q_{j}}\delta q_{j}\delta q_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}\partial\dot{q}_{j}}\delta\dot{q}_{j}\delta q_{i}\,.

(3)

Again in line with standard covariant phase space convention, we keep the field space wedge product between field space one-forms
\delta q_{j}
and
\delta\dot{q}_{i}
implicit, keeping in mind that
\delta q_{j}\delta\dot{q}_{i}=-\delta\dot{q}_{i}\delta q_{j}
; the notation
\wedge
is reserved for the spacetime wedge product.

The presymplectic structure is conserved on-shell, which follows from

\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\mathring{M}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}t\approx 0\,,

(4)

where
\approx
denotes evaluation on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
, i.e. on solutions to the Euler-Lagrange equations of motion
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial q_{i}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}=0
. Indeed, this follows immediately from applying
\delta
to
\delta L_{M}\approx\mathrm{d}\Theta_{\mathring{M}}
, which is implied by equation (
1
).

Defining
4
4

4

We are not performing a Legendre transformation and so
p_{i}
is here simply a function on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
.

p_{i}:=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}
, we find the potential and presymplectic structure in the simple forms

\Theta_{\mathring{M}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta q_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta p_{i}\delta q_{i}\,,

which will become useful shortly.

{\mathcal{L}}(q_{i},\dot{q}_{j})={\mathcal{L}}\left(\{q_{i}-q_{j},\dot{q}_{i}-\dot{q}_{j}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}\right)\,,

\dot{q}_{i}(t)\to\dot{q}_{i}(t)+\dot{\alpha}(t)

\alpha(t)

2.2.1
Internal description of a group of particles

We can select any of the particles of
M
, say particle
N
, as a dynamical
internal
reference frame for the translation group
(\mathbb{R},+)
, relative to which we describe the remaining ones. Indeed, we can build gauge-invariant quantities, such as relative distances
q_{i}-q_{N}
, using the frame degrees of freedom. More formally, note that the frame position
q_{N}
constitutes a (translation) group-valued dynamical reference frame
U=q_{N}
on field space, which transforms by group multiplication (here, the addition) under translations,
g(\alpha)\triangleright U=g(\alpha)+U=q_{N}+\alpha
. The group being Abelian, left and right actions of the group are of course the same. Using this frame, we can turn any functional
f(q_{i}(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t))
into a gauge-invariant one by subjecting it to a frame-dressed gauge transformation defined by
\alpha=-q_{N}+x

x(t)

O_{f,U}(x):=g(-q_{N}+x)\triangleright f=\left(U-x\right)^{-1}\triangleright f:={\left.\kern-1.2ptg(\alpha)\triangleright f\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\alpha=-U+x}}\,,

(8)

where
(U-x)^{-1}
denotes the inverse group element.
7
7

7

For the translation group, we of course have
(U-x)^{-1}=-(U-x)
. The reason we also use the notation
g^{-1}
for the inverse group element here is for facilitating the comparison with the corresponding construction of gauge-invariant observables for general groups in gauge field theory, which will be the focus of Sec.
4

In coordinates, we thus have

O_{f,U}(x,q_{i},\dot{q}_{i})=f(q_{i}-q_{N}+x,\dot{q}_{i}-\dot{q}_{N}+\dot{x})\,,

(9)

which clearly is translation-invariant. This frame-dressed observable is the (Lagrangian analog) of a

and here encode the question “what is the value of

Q_{i|N}(x):=O_{q_{i},U}(x)=q_{i}-q_{N}+x\,.

Since the value of
O_{q_{i},U_{N}}(x)
is constant on any gauge orbit, this value is the same as on the gauge-fixing surface
q_{N}=x

N
is in position
x

Next, we observe that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
constitutes a null direction of the presymplectic structure. To this end, we first note that, owing to our assumption on the Lagrangian in equation (
6
),
p_{i}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}
is translation-invariant, so that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}(p_{i})={\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\delta p_{i}=0

all
of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
8
8

8

This is because
P_{M}
constitutes a primary constraint (upon Legendre transformation) in the language of constrained systems. This is yet another difference to the field theory examples later, where the gauge constraints are
not
identities on field space, but only vanish on the subspace of solutions. The reason is that they are secondary constraints.

P_{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=0\,.

(11)

This yields

{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=-\alpha\delta P_{M}=0\,.

(12)

In particular,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
is also off-shell a degenerate direction of the presymplectic structure. By contrast, in the field theory examples later, the constraints will be related to the equations of motion in such a way that gauge directions in field space will only be on-shell null directions of the presymplectic structure.

We can use the relational observables to split the symplectic potential and presymplectic structure into gauge-invariant and gauge parts, that is
\Theta_{\mathring{M}}=\mathring{\Theta}_{\rm inv}+\mathring{\Theta}_{\rm gauge}
and
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm inv}+\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm gauge}

where

\displaystyle\mathring{\Theta}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}(x)\qquad\mathring{\Theta}_{\rm gauge}:=P_{M}\delta q_{N}=0\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}(x)\qquad\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm gauge}:=\delta P_{M}\delta q_{N}=0\,.

(14)

For distinction from the case with external frame particles below, we have equipped the forms with a
\mathring{}
. A characteristic feature of the gauge contributions
\mathring{\Theta}_{\rm gauge}
and
\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm gauge}
(here, but also in field theory) is that they involve the constraints.
However, in contrast to the field theory case later, the gauge part vanishes identically here.

Let us now consider the construction of a phase space for the particle group
M
. Owing to the degeneracy of the presymplectic form
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}
, the field space
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
is not a phase space. In order to construct a phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
from it, we have to factor out the null directions of
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}
:
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}:={\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\sim
, where
c\sim c^{\prime}
if the histories
c,c^{\prime}
differ by such a null direction. Clearly,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
is a null direction, but there are more: recalling that
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}
is a
(2,0)
-form, we have to fix a time
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
in order to turn it into a genuine two-form
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}(t)
on
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that outside of the space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
,
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}(t)
will depend on the choice of
t
. It is clear that any non-zero vector field
X
which vanishes at
t
will be a null direction of
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}(t)
, simply because it only changes
q_{i}(t^{\prime}),\dot{q}_{i}(t^{\prime})
at times
t^{\prime}\neq t
. Taking both types of degeneracies together, the equivalence class
[c]
consists of all histories that have the same gauge-invariant
(Q_{j|N}(x,t),\dot{Q}_{j|N}(x,t))_{j=1}^{N-1}
at
t
as
c
. The phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
can thus be parametrized by these gauge-invariant variables and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2N-2}
, hence finite-dimensional in contrast to
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
. We can, however, equivalently use
(Q_{j|N},p_{j})_{j=1}^{N-1}
as a coordinate system for
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
. To see this, note that we can write
p_{j}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{Q}_{j|N}}
since
\frac{\partial\dot{Q}_{j|N}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}}=1
and
{\mathcal{L}}\left(\{q_{i}-q_{j},\dot{q}_{i}-\dot{q}_{j}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}\right)={\mathcal{L}}\left(\{Q_{j|N},\dot{Q}_{j|N}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}\right)
, so
p_{j}
is determined by
(Q_{j|N},\dot{Q}_{j|N})_{j=1}^{N-1}
. Conversely,
(Q_{j|N},p_{j})_{j=1}^{N-1}
determine
(Q_{j|N},\dot{Q}_{j|N})_{j=1}^{N-1}
by the implicit function theorem because
\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial\dot{Q}_{j^{\prime}|N}}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{Q}_{j|N}\partial\dot{Q}_{j^{\prime}|N}}
is non-degenerate owing to the gauge-invariance of the involved variables. Hence,
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
is also equipped with the non-degenerate symplectic form
\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm inv}(t)
.

In the present class of models, it turns out that the so constructed phase space, in fact, does not depend on
t
. The reason is that it coincides with the phase space constructed directly from the space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
on which
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}(t)
is independent of
t
thanks to equation (
4
). To see this, note that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
is also a degenerate direction of the pullback of
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}(t)
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}
, and that the set of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
contains any non-zero vector field
X
tangential to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}
which vanishes at
t
. The ensuing equivalence relation
s\sim s^{\prime}
of solutions
s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}
is thus simply the restriction of the field space equivalence relation
c\sim c^{\prime}
to the subspace of solutions. First consider the null directions defined by non-zero vector fields which vanish at
t
(which are comprised of gauge directions
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
for which
\alpha,\dot{\alpha}
vanish at
t
). Factoring out these null directions from
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}
yields a finite-dimensional space isomorphic to
T{\mathcal{Q}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, parametrized by initial data
(q_{i}(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t))
at some time
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Further factoring out all remaining gauge directions
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
, which amounts to identifying the initial data sets
(q_{i}(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t))
and
(q_{i}(t)+\alpha(t),\dot{q}_{i}(t)+\dot{\alpha}(t))
for arbitrary
\alpha(t)
, produces a space which is conveniently parametrized by the relational observables
(Q_{j|N},\dot{Q}_{j|N})_{j=1}^{N-1}
. This is, of course, the same phase space as above, i.e. altogether

{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\sim\,\,={\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\sim\,.

(15)

This is also equivalent to the reduced phase space one would obtain if, instead of the covariant phase space method, one were to first perform a Legendre transformation, followed by a constraint reduction. Indeed, here the Legendre transformation, in coordinates defined by
p_{i}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}
(viewing
{\mathcal{L}}
at fixed
t
as a function
{\mathcal{L}}:T{\mathcal{Q}}\to\mathbb{R}
), would map the space
T{\mathcal{Q}}
of positions and velocities
(q_{i},\dot{q}_{i})
to the kinematical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}:=T^{*}{\mathcal{Q}}
. On
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
,

no longer is an identity, but a non-trivial primary constraint defining a constraint submanifold
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}

from
{\mathcal{C}}
then yields the reduced phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
, coinciding with equation (
15
). However, within the covariant phase space formulation, the Legendre transformation is not a natural map to consider as it relies on fixed
t

The ultimate reason for the coincidence of the two phase spaces, constructed from the field space and space of solutions, respectively, is that
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
constitutes a degenerate direction of
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}

both
on- and off-shell. This will be different in Maxwell, Chern-Simons and Yang-Mills theory later where constraints will only hold on-shell (they are secondary), such that gauge directions
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
are only degenerate directions of the pullback of
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}
. In
those
cases, the equivalence relation
\sim
does not encompass gauge directions and so
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}:={\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\!\sim,{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\!\sim
and
{\mathcal{P}}:=\left({\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\sim\right)/{\mathcal{G}}
, where
{\mathcal{G}}
denotes the group of spacetime gauge transformations, are distinct sets, constituting what are usually known as the kinematical phase space, constraint surface and reduced or physical phase space, respectively; see e.g.
[
56
]
(as well as Sec.
3.2
).
A field theory example where the kinematical and physical phase spaces coincide as here is parametrized field theory
[
56
]
.

Altogether, this constitutes a purely
internal
description of the group of particles
M
. At times it will be interesting though to explore how a subsystem relates to its environment.

2.2.2
External description of the particle group: “extending the Heisenberg cut”

While the internal description of the particle group
M
, e.g. relative to particle
N
, thus lives in the physical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\mathring{M}}/\!\sim
, i.e. on the set of equivalence classes of solutions, the kinematical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
, as we shall now elucidate, can be associated with the description of
M
relative to an
external
reference frame. As just seen, the kinematical phase space is not a natural object to consider in the covariant phase space formulation of the present mechanical model because it is here not obtained through the natural reduction methods of the covariant formulation as in equation (
15
), but through a Legendre transformation.
We nevertheless discuss this physical interpretation of
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
here since it is useful for understanding edge modes conceptually and as we are ultimately interested in field theory where no Legendre transformation is needed in order to construct
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
(see Sec.
3.2
). From the point of view of the covariant method, the following interpretation of
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
will thus be more natural to entertain in field theory.

{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
comprises the physical states of
M
that are distinguishable relative to an internal frame of
M
at any time
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, while
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
can, in a sense to be made more precise below, be viewed as comprising those states that are distinguishable relative to a frame external to
M
. This frame could be fictitious or a physical one that we have thus far ignored. In the sequel, we consider the latter situation and “internalize” such an external frame through a field space and corresponding phase space extension. The set of physical states of the ensuing internal description of
M
relative to the new reference frame will then be equivalent to
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
.
Although we are not in the quantum theory and neither considering measurement interactions, this is somewhat reminiscent of the extension of the Heisenberg cut. In the quantum theory, the below extension is related to what is known as the ‘paradox of the third particle’
[
48
,
99
]
(see also
[
49
]
for a summary).

Suppose the group of particles
M
is not alone in the world and there is a further particle that we call
R_{1}
(see Fig.
1
). It will assume the role of the “internalized” external reference frame and will be the mechanical analog of an edge mode for
M
. To accommodate it, we will have to extend the translation-invariant Lagrangian in equation (
6
) as well as the underlying field space accordingly. In line with the subsequent field theory discussion, let us denote this field space by
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
, which is defined in complete analogy to
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathring{M}}
above, incorporating all histories of the now
N+1
particles (group
M
and frame
R_{1}
). For this
(N+1)
-body problem, we can now repeat the same exercise as before. Specifically, the symplectic potential and presymplectic structure on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
(which depend on
t
) read

\Theta_{\Sigma_{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\delta q_{i}+p_{R_{1}}\delta q_{R_{1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega_{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta p_{i}\delta q_{i}+\delta p_{R_{1}}\delta q_{R_{1}}\,,

(16)

where, in preparation for the field theory case,
\Sigma_{t}
here highlights that the potential depends on the “time slice”
t
both on- and off-shell, in contrast to
\Omega_{M}
which on
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}
will be
t
-independent due to equation (
4
). In contrast to equation (
11
), we now have the identity

P_{MR_{1}}:=P_{M}+p_{R_{1}}=0

(17)

on all of
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
, so
P_{M}
need no longer vanish.

All the relational observables of the previous subsection remain invariant observables on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
. However, thanks to the additional frame particle
R_{1}
we can now turn
all
the kinematical degrees of freedom associated with
M
into gauge-invariant ones. Specifically, we can now also relate the old internal frame, particle
N
, to the “edge mode”
R_{1}
, yielding new relational observables, such as

Q_{N|R_{1}}(y):=O_{q_{N},U_{R_{1}}}(y)=q_{N}-q_{R_{1}}+y\,.

(18)

This permits us to decompose the symplectic potential and symplectic structure once more into gauge-invariant and gauge parts,
\Theta_{\Sigma_{t}}=\Theta_{\rm inv}+\Theta_{\rm gauge}
and
\Omega_{M}=\Omega_{\rm inv}+\Omega_{\rm gauge}
, taking equation (
13
) into account, where:

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}(x)+P_{M}\delta Q_{N|R_{1}}(y)\,,\qquad\Theta_{\rm gauge}:=P_{MR_{1}}\delta q_{R_{1}}{}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}(x)+\delta P_{M}\delta Q_{N|R_{1}}(y)\,,\qquad\Omega_{\rm gauge}:=\delta P_{MR_{1}}\delta q_{R_{1}}\,.

(19)

In particular, observe that only the last term in the invariant parts are dressed by the edge mode
R_{1}
. These are the mechanical analogs of the radiative contributions to the presymplectic potential and presymplectic structure on the boundary that we shall later see in field theory, while the first terms are the mechanical analog of the bulk contribution in field theory. In contrast to the field theory case, no corner term can arise in this discrete model and the gauge parts vanish identically.

It is clear that the physical phase space

{\mathcal{P}}_{M}:={\mathcal{S}}_{M}/\!\sim\,={\mathcal{F}}_{M}/\!\sim

(20)

of
M
and
R_{1}
together is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, equipped with the non-degenerate symplectic form
\Omega_{\rm inv}
, and parametrized by the pairs
(Q_{j|N},p_{j};Q_{N|R_{1}},P_{M})_{j=1}^{N-1}
. Specifically, we can describe
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}
equivalently through a gauge-fixing since, e.g.,
q_{R_{1}}=y
for some

is a globally valid gauge fixing. On the corresponding gauge-fixing surface we have
Q_{N|R_{1}}(y)=q_{N}
such that the coordinates on
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}
become
(Q_{j|N},p_{j};q_{N},P_{M})_{j=1}^{N-1}
. This is equivalent to the Legendre transformation induced natural coordinates
(q_{i},p_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
on the kinematical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}:=T^{*}{\mathcal{Q}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2N}
; in particular, the invariant part of the symplectic structure can (in this gauge-fixing) be written equivalently as
\Omega_{\rm inv}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta p_{i}\delta q_{i}
. In this sense, the set of gauge-invariant states of
M
relative to
R_{1}
is equivalent (even symplectomorphic) to the kinematical phase space associated with
M
only, i.e.
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}\simeq{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm kin}
. This equivalence is rooted in the above observation that we can turn
all
kinematical degrees of freedom associated with
M
into gauge-invariant ones by relating them to the edge mode
R_{1}
.

2.3
Symmetries as frame reorientations vs. gauge transformations

Given the internalized external frame
R_{1}
, we now have to distinguish two types of transformations which are indistinguishable in a purely internal description of
M
on
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
, as they leave all the relations within
M
invariant (see Fig.
1
):

Gauge transformations:

A global translation

q_{i}\to q_{i}+\alpha\,,\quad i=1,\ldots,N\,,\quad\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{R_{1}}\to q_{R_{1}}+\alpha

(21)

of
M
and
R_{1}
leaves the relation between the new frame
R_{1}
and
M
invariant and thus describes the same physical situation. Denote the vector field on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
generating this transformation once more by
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
may be “field-dependent”, i.e. depend on where it is evaluated on field space
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
. It constitutes a null-direction of the presymplectic structure

{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}=\delta C[\alpha]=0\,,

(22)

with ‘‘constraint’’
9
9

9

We write it in quotation marks as it vanishes identically everywhere on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
.

C[\alpha]=-\alpha P_{MR_{1}}=0\,.

(23)

Symmetries (frame reorientations):

A relative translation of
M
and
R_{1}
by a distance
\rho
. While we could equivalently formulate such a transformation as a transformation of only
M
, we shall write it as a transformation purely of the new frame
R_{1}
:

q_{i}\to q_{i}\,,\quad i=1,\ldots,N\,,\quad\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{R_{1}}\to q_{R_{1}}+\rho\,.

(24)

This transformation does
not
leave the relation between
R_{1}
and
M
invariant and thereby changes the physical situation. Since the position
q_{R_{1}}
constitutes the “orientation” of the frame, this transformation amounts to a
frame reorientation
. Denote the vector field on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
generating this transformation by
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
, where
\rho
must now be field-space-
independent
.

For concreteness, let us assume that the only dependence of the Lagrangian for
M,R_{1}
on the velocities is of the form
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm kin}=\frac{1}{4(N+1)}\sum_{I,J=1}^{N+1}(\dot{q}_{a}-\dot{q}_{b})^{2}
, where
I,J
runs over all particles in
M
and the edge mode
R_{1}
, which is tantamount to assuming unit mass for the particles and subtracting the center-of-mass kinetic energy from the total kinetic energy, e.g. see
[
42
,
43
]
. This yields
p_{I}=\dot{q}_{I}-\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{J}\,\dot{q}_{J}
and therefore the variations

\Delta_{\rho}Q_{j|N}={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta Q_{j|N}=0,\quad\Delta_{\rho}Q_{N|R_{1}}={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta Q_{N|R_{1}}=-\rho,\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{\rho}p_{i}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta p_{i}=-\frac{\dot{\rho}}{N+1}\,,{}

where
i=1,\ldots,N
runs over the particles of
M
. Contraction with the presymplectic structure gives

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{\rm inv}=\delta Q[\rho]\,,

(25)

with gauge-invariant
charge

Q[\rho]=\rho P_{M}-\frac{1}{N+1}\dot{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}Q_{j|N}-\frac{N}{N+1}\dot{\rho}\,Q_{N|R_{1}}\,.

(26)

which does not in general vanish even on-shell.
Specifically, for an edge-mode-independent functional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
of the form
f(q_{i},\dot{q}_{i})
, we have, thanks to the analog of equation (
9
) for when
R_{1}
is used as frame,

O_{f,U_{R_{1}}+\rho}(y)=O_{f,U_{R_{1}}}(y-\rho)

(27)

and

\rho\,\frac{\partial O_{f,U_{R_{1}}}(y)}{\partial y}+\dot{\rho}\,\frac{\partial O_{f,U_{R_{1}}}(y)}{\partial\dot{y}}=\big\{Q[\rho],O_{f,U_{R_{1}}}(y)\big\}={\mathcal{Z}}_{O}\cdot{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{\rm inv}\,,

(28)

where
{\mathcal{Z}}_{O}
is the Hamiltonian vector field on
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}
associated with the relational observable
O_{f,U_{R_{1}}}(y)
. In line with the interpretation of frame reorientations, we thus see that the charge generates changes in the frame orientation label
y
of the edge mode for such observables.

Just like the group action
\triangleright
in equation (
7
) corresponds to gauge transformations acting on all dynamical variables, we can now define the group action
\odot

2.5
Subsystem phase space from post-selection on the global space of solutions

Thus far, we have considered the covariant phase space structures associated with the group of particles
M
with and without the two “edge modes”
R_{1},R_{2}
. Accommodating the edge modes required us to extend the field and phase spaces, embedding a setup with fewer into a setup with more particles.
Let us now consider the opposite process: how can we recover the covariant phase space structure of fewer particles from that with more particles? This serves as a toy scenario for recovering local subregion phase space structures from those associated with a global spacetime region in field theory, which we shall elaborate on later. This can be achieved through a post-selection on the global space of solutions.

Suppose that
R_{1},R_{2}
are part of a second group
\bar{M}
of
\bar{N}
particles, the complement of
M
. We shall now illustrate how to recover the phase space structure of the subsystem
M
from the global one for
M\cup\bar{M}
.
To this end, let us assume for concreteness and simplicity that we are dealing with free particle dynamics subject to a translation-invariant Lagrangian of the form
{{\mathcal{L}}_{M\cup\bar{M}}=\frac{1}{4(N+\bar{N})}\sum_{I,J=1}^{N+\bar{N}}(\dot{q}_{I}-\dot{q}_{J})^{2}}
, where
I,J
now runs over all particles in
M\cup\bar{M}
. Then
p_{I}=\dot{q}_{I}-\frac{1}{N+\bar{N}}\sum_{J}\,\dot{q}_{J}
, so that
p_{i}=\dot{q}_{i}-\frac{1}{N+\bar{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dot{q}_{j}-\frac{\bar{N}}{N+\bar{N}}\dot{q}_{\bar{C}}
, where
i=1,\ldots,N
runs over the particles of
M
. Here,
q_{\bar{C}}:=\frac{1}{\bar{N}}\sum_{\bar{j}=N+1}^{N+\bar{N}}q_{\bar{j}}
, where
\bar{j}
labels the
\bar{N}
particles in
\bar{M}
, is the center-of-mass of
\bar{M}
. In other words, the momenta in
M
now only depend on
\bar{M}
through its center-of-mass. We denote the global field space on which the Lagrangian is defined by
{\mathcal{F}}
and the subspace of solutions by
{\mathcal{S}}
. Let us choose particle
N
as the internal frame for group
M
, as before, while we now select the center-of-mass
\bar{C}
of
\bar{M}
as an external frame for
M
and an internal frame for
\bar{M}
. We could in principle choose any of the configuration degrees of freedom in
\bar{M}
as the edge mode, however, to ensure a suitable dynamical decoupling, it will be convenient to choose
q_{\bar{C}}
as the edge mode. Repeating the previous exercises on
{\mathcal{F}}
, we find the presymplectic structure in the form

\Omega=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}+\delta P_{M}\delta Q_{N|\bar{C}}+\left(\delta P_{M}+\delta P_{\bar{M}}\right)\delta q_{\bar{C}}+\sum_{\bar{j}=N+1}^{N+\bar{N}}\delta p_{\bar{j}}\delta Q_{\bar{j}|\bar{C}}\,,

(39)

where
j
labels the
N-1
particles in
M
, except particle number
N
, and
P_{\bar{M}}=P_{\bar{C}}=\sum_{\bar{j}}p_{\bar{j}}
is the total momentum of
\bar{M}
. The relational observables
Q_{N|\bar{C}},Q_{\bar{j}|\bar{C}}
are constructed as before according to equation (
10
).
12
12

12

There is a redundancy in the last term of equation (
39
) as
\sum_{\bar{j}=1}^{\bar{N}}Q_{\bar{j}|\bar{C}}=0
, but this will be of no relevance below.
We now have the identity

P_{M}+P_{\bar{M}}=0\,.

(40)

The third contribution to
\Omega
is the pure gauge part and thus vanishes identically. The first and last contributions pertain solely to
M
and
\bar{M}
, respectively, and are mechanical analogs of the bulk contribution of a subregion. By contrast, the second term
\Omega_{\rm rad}:=\delta P_{M}\delta Q_{N|\bar{C}}
is the mechanical analog of the edge-mode-dressed radiative contribution on the boundary between adjacent subregions
M
and
\bar{M}
in field theory. We will consider the particle
N
as the boundary of
M
and
(Q_{N|\bar{C}},P_{M})
as the “boundary variables”.

In
splitting post-selection
, we shall now post-select on the global space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
those solutions that are compatible with a choice of
gauge-invariant
boundary conditions on particle
N
. This will split the global solution space into subgroup solution spaces for
M
and
\bar{M}
. The “boundary contribution“
\Omega_{\rm rad}
will play an important role in this process. In order to encompass different types of boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin and mixed), let us consider the linear combinations of the translation-invariant data

X=a\,Q_{N|\bar{C}}+b\,P_{M}\,,\qquad\qquad Y=c\,Q_{N|\bar{C}}+d\,P_{M}\,,

(41)

where
a,b,c,d
are real functions of
t
subject only to
ad-bc=1
, in which case they define a symplectic transformation

\Omega_{\rm rad}=\delta P_{M}\delta Q_{N|\bar{C}}=\delta Y\delta X\,.

(42)

The space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
can be parametrized by solutions
{\left(Q_{j|N}(t),Q_{N|\bar{C}}(t),q_{\bar{C}}(t),Q_{\bar{j}|\bar{C}}(t);t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\right)}
and so also by
{\left(Q_{j|N}(t),X(t),q_{\bar{C}}(t),Q_{\bar{j}|\bar{C}}(t);t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\right)}
. In particular, we can thus use the configuration variable
X
to foliate
{\mathcal{S}}

{\mathcal{S}}=\underset{X_{0}}{\bigsqcup}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}:=\left\{\text{all histories $c(t)\in{\mathcal{S}}$}|X(t)=X_{0}(t)\right\}\,,

(43)

where
X_{0}(t)
is some non-dynamical background function specifying the gauge-invariant boundary condition for particle
N
(which, of course, have to be consistent with the equations of motion of the relevant Lagrangian). For example, we could have
a(t_{1})=d(t_{1})=1
and
b(t_{1})=c(t_{1})=0
initially and
a(t_{2})=d(t_{2})=0
and
b(t_{2})=1=-c(t_{2})
at the end, so that the boundary conditions are initially on
X(t_{1})=Q_{N|\bar{C}}(t_{1})
, hence Dirichlet, and finally on
X(t_{2})=P_{M}(t_{2})
, thus Neumann, and so altogether of mixed type. Clearly, different choices of the functions
a,b,c,d
will lead to distinct foliations of
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Post-selection now proceeds by selecting a leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
from the global solution space
{\mathcal{S}}
, i.e. restricting to all solutions consistent with the history
X_{0}(t)
of the variable
X
. Pulling back to any of these leaves, we thus have
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\delta X\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}}}=\delta X_{0}=0
and so

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Omega_{\rm rad}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}}}=0\,.

(44)

Henceforth, we also write the weak equality
\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}
for identities that only hold on
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
(i.e. after the boundary conditions have been imposed), so that
\Omega_{\rm rad}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
.
In other words, post-selection guarantees that there is no (physical) symplectic flux between
M
and
\bar{M}
since
\Omega_{\rm rad}
is the only gauge-invariant contribution to the global presymplectic structure
\Omega
in (
39
) that involves degrees of freedom from
M
and
\bar{M}
that are generically non-constant (the momenta, which depend on
all
particles, satisfy
\dot{p}_{I}\approx 0
).

This also splits the phase space structures associated with
M
and
\bar{M}
. In particular, we can consider the restriction of any leaf to
M
. As can be easily checked, the equations of motion for the
q_{i}
and edge mode
q_{\bar{C}}
decouple from the remaining ones for
\bar{M}
and can thereby be solved independently; we can therefore perform post-selection on this subset. This suggests to define the restriction of the leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
to
M
by including the edge mode in the form

{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}:=\Big\{{\left.\kern-1.2ptc(t)\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M}}=\left(Q_{j|N}(t),Q_{N|\bar{C}}(t),q_{\bar{C}}(t)\right)\,,\,t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\,\Big|\,c\in{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}\Big\}\,.

(45)

Accordingly, we equip this “regional” space of solutions for
M
with the presymplectic structure (cf. equation (
39
))

\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}+\delta P_{M}\delta Q_{N|\bar{C}}+\left(\delta P_{M}+\delta P_{\bar{M}}\right)\delta q_{\bar{C}}=\Omega_{\mathring{M}}+\Omega_{\rm rad}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}\Omega_{\mathring{M}}\,,

(46)

recalling that the second term on the left hand side vanishes on-shell of the boundary conditions, while the last term vanishes identically. On-shell,
the surviving piece of the presymplectic structure for
M
is thus
\Omega_{\mathring{M}}=\mathring{\Omega}_{\rm inv}=\sum_{j}\delta p_{j}\delta Q_{j|N}
given in (
13
), which is the “bulk” non-degenerate symplectic form for
M
on the physical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
discussed at the end of Sec.
2.2.1
. Indeed, using the equations of motion in the form
\dot{p}_{I}\approx 0
and noting that
\dot{Q}_{j|N}=p_{j}-p_{N}
and
\dot{Q}_{N|\bar{C}}=p_{N}+\frac{1}{\bar{N}}P_{M}
, it is straightforward to check that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Omega_{\mathring{M}}+\Omega_{\rm rad}\right)\approx 0
, i.e. the restricted presymplectic structure for
M
is conserved on-shell. It is also gauge-invariant and any
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
constitutes a degenerate direction of it.
13
13

13

Neither conservation nor invariance would be true had we only included the generally non-vanishing
\delta P_{M}\delta q_{\bar{C}}
for the edge mode contribution.

Hence, upon factoring out the degenerate directions of the presymplectic structure from
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
, we recover the phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
of internally distinguishable states for the subsystem
M
from splitting post-selection on the global solution space for
M\cup\bar{M}
. As discussed shortly, this does
not
mean, however, that the dynamics on
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
induced by
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
will coincide with the
isolated
dynamics for
M
defined by the pure subsystem Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}
implicitly considered in Sec.
2.2.1
; depending on the boundary condition
X_{0}
, the Hamiltonian may be distinct.

Splitting post-selection can also be performed at the level of the action, however, we shall only discuss this in the field theory case later. This will lead to a systematic algorithm for generating boundary actions.

2.6
Three types of symmetry transformations (frame reorientations)

Each leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
in the global space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
can be regarded as (the space of solutions of) a specific subsystem theory for
M
(resp.
\bar{M}
), defined by the boundary conditions
X_{0}
. Indeed, since the “edge-mode-dressed” variables
X,Y
contain degrees of freedom from both
M
and
\bar{M}
, these boundary conditions specify how
M
couples to its complement
\bar{M}
. Different choices of
X_{0}
for
X
will lead to distinct subsystem Hamiltonians (e.g., different parameters or explicit time dependence) for the remaining
N-1
particles in
M
. This will be discussed in more detail in
[
101
]
. That is to say, different “subleaves”
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
yield the same physical phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathring{M}}
, but will correspond to distinct subsystem dynamics and thereby contain different sets of solutions for
Q_{j|N}(t)
. We are therefore justified to think of the different
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
as different theories for the subsystem
M
and, consequently, of the global space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
as a “meta-theory” containing all the different subsystem theories.

In Sec.
2.2.2
, we discussed the distinction between gauge and symmetry transformations, originally put forth in
[
3
]
, and interpreted the latter as frame reorientations. Since our subsystem theory is now subjected to boundary conditions when considered as a post-selection of the global theory, we have to explore the consequences for symmetries in the presence of these boundary conditions. In particular, we have to investigate whether the frame reorientations preserve the boundary conditions or whether they give rise to changes of leaves
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
in the foliations of
{\mathcal{S}}
.

For concreteness, let us once more assum a free particle dynamics as in the previous subsection. The vector field
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
generating the frame reorientation (
24
) (for the center-of-mass) then leads to the variations
\Delta_{\rho}p_{i}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta p_{i}=-\frac{\bar{N}}{N+\bar{N}}\dot{\rho}
, where
i=1,\ldots,N
runs over the particles of
M
. This has two consequences: first, it leads to the following variation of the variable fixed through the boundary conditions

\Delta_{\rho}X={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta X=-\rho a-\frac{N\bar{N}}{N+\bar{N}}\dot{\rho}b\,.

(47)

Hence, only symmetries with
\rho
such that
\Delta_{\rho}X=0
will leave the boundary conditions invariant.
Second, restricting
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
to the subleaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
, we can contract it with its presymplectic structure
{\Omega}_{\mathring{M}}+\Omega_{\rm rad}
. Note that, while the pullback of
\Omega_{\rm rad}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
vanishes, the contraction with
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
, which may not be tangential to
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
in
{\mathcal{S}}
, will not in general vanish. This leads to

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\left({\Omega}_{\mathring{M}}+\Omega_{\rm rad}\right)=\delta Q[\rho]

(48)

with charges in the form (cf. (
26
))

Q[\rho]=\rho P_{M}-\frac{\bar{N}}{N+\bar{N}}\dot{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}Q_{j|N}-\frac{N\bar{N}}{N+\bar{N}}\dot{\rho}\,Q_{N|\bar{C}}\,.

(49)

Given that they constitute generators of an Abelian transformation group (translations of
R_{1}
), they generate an Abelian Poisson algebra,
\{Q[\rho],Q[\sigma]\}={\mathcal{Y}}_{\sigma}\cdot\left({\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\left(\Omega_{\mathring{M}}+\Omega_{\rm rad}\right)\right)=0
.

This leads to a further distinction of three types of symmetry transformations (frame reorientations).

Symmetries.

These are frame reorientations
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
which preserve the boundary condition
X=X_{0}
, hence leave
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
in
{\mathcal{S}}
, and feature an
unconstrained charge
. These are symmetries with
time-dependent

\rho(t)
such that

Covariant phase space method in field theory

3.1

In case

{\mathcal{S}}

,

We will make use of a particular class of such reference frames to correctly account for the dynamics of gauge fields in a finite subregion

4.1
Dynamical reference frames in gauge field theory

Let us first specify what we mean by a dynamical reference frame for some local (gauge) symmetry Lie group
G

R

that is as asymmetric under

constitutes a dynamical reference system relative to which we can describe the remaining degrees of freedom of

More precisely, in order for

4.5
Symmetries as edge frame reorientations

Now that we have set up an edge frame field, we are in the position to consider two types of frame transformations as in the mechanical toy setting in Secs.
2.2.2
and

These observations are particularly clear in the example of the group-valued reference frame

34

34

is a non-dynamical background field. We require the boundary conditions — and thus

— to be gauge-invariant, so as to cleanly distinguish between physical boundary conditions and gauge fixing conditions.

First implementing splitting post-selection on-shell, rather than at the level of the action, has several advantages. From the point of view of
M
, this allows us to cleanly disentangle bulk dynamical equations from boundary conditions (which, as we will explain in Sec.
6
, will eventually be enforced dynamically). More generally, it is useful to investigate the relation between the covariant phase spaces of
M
and
\bar{M}
independently from a choice of variational principle. By focusing on the (pre-)symplectic geometry underlying post-selection, we will be readily able to connect our work to previous literature on edge modes.

5.1
Radiative presymplectic structure from a dressing flow in field space

Let us first proceed to show that the group-valued dressing of edge fields introduced in Sec.

Given our working hypotheses for the system of paths

5.2
Foliation with respect to field configurations on the time-like boundary

Splitting post-selection proceeds in three steps (see Sec.
2.5
for this procedure in mechanics): one

1.

foliates the global space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
with respect to suitable field configurations on
\Gamma
; then

2.

restricts to a particular leave in this foliation; and finally

3.

discards the complementary region
\bar{M}
to obtain the looked for presymplectic form
\Omega_{M}
.

We call this process
splitting post-selection
as the second step “post-selects” those solutions in
{\mathcal{S}}
, which satisfy a specific choice of boundary conditions on the interface
\Gamma
, thereby effectively splitting the global space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
for
M\cup\bar{M}
into two, one for each spacetime subregion. Upon the third step, one then focuses on the dynamical problem for the subregion
M
of interest, defined by the selected boundary conditions. Since the latter define the dynamical theory for
M
(and, likewise, for
\bar{M}
), we can think of each leaf in the foliation of
{\mathcal{S}}
as a particular subregion theory. As such, the global solution space
{\mathcal{S}}
assumes the role of a space of subregion theories, in this sense constituting a meta-theory for the local subregions. This will lead us to distinguish symmetries (frame reorientations) on
\Gamma
into (i) genuine symmetries that leave the subregion theory invariant, (ii) meta-symmetries, which change leaf, i.e. subregion theory within
{\mathcal{S}}
, and (iii) symmetries that become gauge transformations on particular boundary conditions. We stress that case (ii) only constitutes meta-symmetries for the subregion theories, not, however, for the global dynamics in
M\cup\bar{M}
, since it maps one solution into another one of the same global theory.
46
46

46

This global theory
{\mathcal{S}}
too is to be defined by some asymptotic boundary conditions that are unaffected by the meta-symmetries (ii).

In more detail, we look for a decomposition of
{\mathcal{S}}
of the form

{\mathcal{S}}=\underset{X_{0}}{\bigsqcup}\,{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}:=\left\{\Phi\in{\mathcal{S}}|X\left[\phi_{\rm rad}\right]=X_{0}\right\}

(120)

for some suitable choice of
X
and
X_{0}
, where
X_{0}
is a (set of) background field(s) that spans a function space on
\Gamma
appropriate for a well-posed boundary value problem, and
X
is a map from field configurations on
\Gamma
to field configurations on
\Gamma
(including the reference frame
U
, which enters the definition of
\phi_{\rm rad}
). The post-selected leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
can be thought of as (the space of solutions to) a specific subregion theory. The boundary condition
X\left[\phi_{\rm rad}\right]=X_{0}
must fix sufficiently many degrees of freedom to give rise to a well-posed dynamical problem in
M
, and not too many so that partial Cauchy data in the interior of
\Sigma
remains freely specifiable. In particular, we will require that there is no physical symplectic flux through
\Gamma
after pulling-back
\Omega
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
. A natural and sufficient requirement seems at first to be that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma,{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}}}=0
. However, in the presence of gauge symmetries such a condition may be overly restrictive, in the sense that it is not always gauge-invariant. In such a situation,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma,{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}}}=0
would amount to imposing a vanishing flux, as well as a non-trivial gauge-fixing condition. To make sure that we only constrain physical degrees of freedom, we instead impose the weaker condition:

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega_{\rm rad}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}}}=0\,.

(121)

In the following, we will denote equalities that only hold on the leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
, that is on-shell of both the bulk equations of motion and the boundary conditions, by the symbol
\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}
. In particular,
\omega_{\rm rad}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
. This ensures that the presymplectic current
\omega
is gauge-equivalent to zero when restricted to
\Gamma
and a specific leaf
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
, but not necessarily identically zero.

To make sure that we do not constrain
\omega_{\rm rad}
more than necessary, we will impose boundary conditions on a Lagrangian submanifold of
{\mathcal{S}}
. In practice, we will choose local and gauge-invariant Darboux coordinates
X
and
Y
(which are fields on
\Gamma
) such that
\omega_{\rm rad}=\delta X\delta Y
. To implement (
121
), it is then sufficient to impose e.g.
\delta X\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
, while leaving
Y
dynamical.

Let us look at a simple but illuminating example in Maxwell theory (which we will discuss again in more depth in Sec.
7.2
). The presymplectic current can be expressed in the canonical Darboux coordinates as
\omega=\delta A\wedge\delta\star F
. Given a
\mathrm{U}(1)
reference frame with phase
{\varphi}(x)
one can define the radiative data (see equations (
65
) and (
66
)):

A_{\rm rad}=A-\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\,,\quad(\star F)_{\rm rad}=\star F\,,\quad\omega_{\rm rad}=\delta A_{\rm rad}\wedge\delta(\star F)_{\rm rad}\approx\delta A\wedge\delta\star F-\mathrm{d}(\delta{\varphi}\delta\star F)\,.

(122)

These coordinates induce two natural foliations. We can foliate the solution space
{\mathcal{S}}
with respect to the pull-back of
\star F
, which amounts to defining
X[A_{\rm rad},(\star F)_{\rm rad}]:=(\star F)_{\rm rad}
. Since
\star F
is gauge-invariant to start with, it follows that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
. Relative to this particular choice of foliation, the dressing by
U
can be safely ignored. By contrast, it becomes relevant again if one decides to impose a boundary condition on
A_{\rm rad}
, that is, if we foliate the solution space with respect to
X[A_{\rm rad},(\star F)_{\rm rad}]:=A_{\rm rad}
. In that case,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega_{\rm rad}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\mathcal{P}}_{f}}}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
while
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\omega\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}\mathrm{d}(\delta\varphi\delta\star F)
does not vanish in general. Note that the boundary condition on
A_{\rm rad}
fixes the pull-back of
F
to
\Gamma
completely, but is stronger in that it also contains relational information about
{\left.\kern-1.2ptA\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}
and
U
. Finally, one can impose boundary conditions on linear combinations of
A_{\rm rad}
and
(\star F)_{\rm rad}
, which we will investigate thoroughly in Sec.
7
.

Let us now briefly discuss the two types of transformations one can perform on the canonical splitting of
A
relative to the reference frame
U=e^{i{\varphi}}
:
47
47

47

We focus exclusively on the connection because its conjugate
\star F
is gauge-invariant in the present Abelian context.

{\left.\kern-1.2ptA\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}=A_{\rm rad}+\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\,.

(123)

The gauge contribution

is unconstrained by purely local gauge-invariant observables, such as
{\left.\kern-1.2ptF\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}
, but is fixed unambiguously by the reference frame data. Gauge transformations act on both

and

in a way that leaves

invariant:

\delta_{\alpha}A=\mathrm{d}\alpha\,,\qquad\delta_{\alpha}{\varphi}=\alpha\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\delta_{\alpha}A_{\rm rad}=0\,.

(124)

By contrast, frame reorientations take the form of a symmetry transformation

which acts solely on

, and thereby changes the radiative part of the connection:

\Delta_{\rho}A=0\,,\qquad\Delta_{\rho}{\varphi}=-\rho\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\Delta_{\rho}A_{\rm rad}=\mathrm{d}\rho\,.

(125)

Depending on the choice of boundary condition, the solutions might not be stable under this transformation, in which case
\Delta_{\rho}
generates a
meta-symmetry
, i.e. a change of post-selected theory
{\mathcal{S}}_{X_{0}}
within the global solution space
{\mathcal{S}}
. For instance, when foliating the solution space with respect to

,

acts transversally to the leaves and thereby changes the boundary condition (that is, the value of

in (

)), unless

is constant on
\Gamma
(in which case
\Delta_{\rho}
does generate a symmetry). As we will see below, this meta-symmetry is a symplectomorphism within
{\mathcal{S}}
. The definition (

) is an Abelian incarnation of the symmetry transformation originally introduced by

]
in Yang-Mills theory. Our construction clarifies its relation to reference frames, and its interplay with boundary conditions.

Our construction has so far been implicit about the choice of edge frame

that goes into the construction of the radiative data

(cf. equation (

. For a fixed frame choice, we can choose different maps

{\mathcal{S}}
. We can consider them as part of one family of foliations, associated with the frame

. They will be related by the transformations relating the different choices of

(e.g., in the mechanical setting of Sec.

, there is a plethora of distinct choices of edge frame fields. It is clear that each of them will lead to a distinct family of foliations (

{\mathcal{S}}

48
48

48

The frame changes, as specific coordinate changes on

6
Boundary actions from splitting post-selection

After exploring splitting post-selection directly at the level of solution spaces, let us extend this discussion by constructing a suitable variational principle for it. Post-selecting the
total
space of solutions
{\mathcal{S}}
of
M\cup\bar{M}
on certain boundary conditions
X=X_{0}
on
\Gamma
will have the effect of turning the global variational problem for
M\cup\bar{M}
into two separate variational problems for
M
and
\bar{M}
. We will begin by considering a
fictitious
boundary
\Gamma
between the two subregions
M
and
\bar{M}
, i.e. a boundary which is not physically distinguished through sources or currents residing on it. In other words, the regular bulk equations of motion of the theory have to hold on the interface
\Gamma
. We shall address the question of
physical
boundaries in a follow-up work
[
101
]
.

In order to have well-defined equations of motion everywhere on the space of field configurations
{\mathcal{F}}
, we shall assume the latter to only contain field configurations
\Phi^{a}
which are
C^{q}
across
M\cup\bar{M}
(incl.
\Gamma
) if the equations of motion are of order
q
in derivatives.

A variational problem for the subregion
M
of interest can be formulated in two equivalent ways. (1) If the field configuration space
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
associated with
M
only contains configurations which satisfy the desired boundary conditions, the role of the boundary action will be limited to canceling the presymplectic potential on the interface
\Gamma
on-shell. This is necessary in order to make the boundary value problem well-defined and consistent with the global variational problem. (2) If instead
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
contains all
C^{q}
field configurations in
M
, it accordingly requires a second type of contribution to the boundary action, with the purpose of imposing the desired boundary conditions as boundary equations of motion.
Post-selecting the global solution space on
X=X_{0}
will first yield the former formulation, which we however then use to construct the equivalent one in terms of boundary actions.

Once translated into the covariant phase space language, post-selection will result in a simple but general algorithm which, given a set of admissible (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin or mixed) boundary conditions, produces a family of appropriate boundary actions implementing these boundary conditions.

6.2.1
General algorithm

In order to render this construction more explicit, let us assume we are given a theory admitting the use of Darboux coordinates on

6.2.2
Non-unicity of the boundary action

The boundary action (
174
) was derived from a particular choice of polarization
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
for the symplectic current
\omega_{\rm rad}=\delta\pi^{\rm rad}.\delta\phi_{\rm rad}=\delta\mathbf{Y}.\delta\mathbf{X}
. Since we are imposing boundary conditions on
\mathbf{X}
, any other polarization
(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})
such that
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
is related to
\mathbf{X}
by an invertible transformation is also admissible. More precisely, any two such polarizations are related by an
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
transformation of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\\
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\end{array}\right)=\mathbf{N}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad\qquad\mathbf{N}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{a}&0\\
\mathbf{c}&\mathbf{d}\end{array}\right)\,,

(176)

where
\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{c}
. It is convenient to parametrize such transformations by the invertible matrix
\mathbf{a}
and the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{c}
.
They generate the subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})

{\mathcal{N}}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{a}&0\\
(\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{s}&(\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{-1}\end{array}\right)|\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})\,,\mathbf{s}\in\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})\right\}\,.

(177)

We can view this set as the matrix product
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{T}}
, where

{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{a}&0\\
0&(\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{-1}\end{array}\right)|\mathbf{a}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})\right\}\simeq\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})

(178)

and

{\mathcal{T}}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{1}&0\\
\mathbf{s}&\mathbf{1}\end{array}\right)|\mathbf{s}\in\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})\right\}\simeq\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})\,.

(179)

As a group,
{\mathcal{N}}
is in fact a semidirect product of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
.
Indeed, introducing the notation
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{a}&0\\
(\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{s}&(\mathbf{a}^{\top})^{-1}\end{array}\right)
, we are led to the semidirect group multiplication law:

\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1})\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{1}\mathbf{a}_{2}+\mathbf{s}_{2})\,.

(180)

We therefore conclude that

{\mathcal{N}}\simeq\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})\,.

(181)

Among the admissible changes of polarization encoded in
{\mathcal{N}}
, which transformations affect the functional form of the boundary action
\ell
? From the construction of the previous subsection, and in particular equation (
169
), it is clear that any canonical transformation that preserves the flux
{\mathcal{E}}:=\mathbf{Y}\delta\mathbf{X}
will also preserve
\delta\ell
(and thereby
\ell
, up to an irrelevant additive ambiguity). Such transformations generate the (normal) stabilizer subgroup
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
.
Ambiguities in the definition of
\ell
are therefore parametrized by the Abelian subgroup
{\mathcal{T}}
(which is not normal). Acting with such a transformation on the polarization
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
yields new coordinates

\mathbf{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{X}\,,\qquad\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{s}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{y}\,,

(182)

where
\mathbf{s}
is a real symmetric matrix. By application of (
174
), the boundary Lagrangian produced by our algorithm in the new coordinates is

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}.\mathbf{X}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{X}.\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}+\pi^{\rm rad}.\phi_{\rm rad}\right)+\ell_{\partial}=\ell+(\mathbf{s}\mathbf{X}).\mathbf{X}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{X}.(\mathbf{s}\mathbf{X})

(183)

\displaystyle=\ell-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0}).\left(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{X}_{0}.\left(\mathbf{s}\mathbf{X}_{0}\right)\,,

(184)

where we have used the fact that
\mathbf{s}
is symmetric in the last line.

We see that
\ell^{\prime}
only differs from
\ell
by:

•

a non-trivial quadratic term
-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0}).\left(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0})\right)
;

•

and a field-independent additive contribution
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{X}_{0}.\left(\mathbf{s}\mathbf{X}_{0}\right)
.

The second term can be ignored since, by construction,
\ell
is only defined up to such additive contributions. By contrast, the first term affects the functional form of
\delta\ell
and is
a priori
non-trivial. However, because it is quadratic in
(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0})
, we have:

\delta\ell^{\prime}=\delta\ell-(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0}).\left(\mathbf{s}\delta\mathbf{X}\right)\,,

(185)

which implies that
\delta\ell
and
\delta\ell^{\prime}
coincide on-shell of the boundary conditions. As a result, this ambiguity only affects the functional form of the boundary action, not the presymplectic structure that results from it.
62
62

62

In fact, this ambiguity in
\delta\ell
is of the general form
(X^{i}-X^{i}_{0})\delta Z_{i}
, and such contributions have been implicitly discarded in the definition (
157
).
It is then clear that we are not changing anything of substance when we change polarization. Likewise, we will show in an upcoming paper that work functionals can be consistently associated to the boundary Lagrangian
\ell
, and are not affected by such quadratic (or higher order) ambiguities.

6.3
Summary of the algorithm

In summary, given a set of boundary conditions
{\bf X}={\bf X}_{0}
to be imposed dynamically, a suitable boundary Lagrangian
(0,d-1)
-form
\ell
can be obtained by implementation of the following steps:

1.

Decompose the presymplectic potential on
\Gamma
into radiative and gauge contributions, relative to a frame
U
:
\theta=\theta_{\rm rad}+\theta_{\rm gauge}
.

2.

Find local Darboux coordinates for the radiative degrees of freedom, so that
\theta_{\rm rad}=\pi_{a}^{\rm rad}\delta\phi^{a}_{\rm rad}
, where
\pi_{a}^{\rm rad}=p_{a}^{\rm rad}\epsilon_{\Gamma}
is the momentum density dual to the frame-dressed field
\phi^{a}_{\rm rad}=(U^{-1}\triangleright\phi)^{a}
.

3.

Find a
(1,d-2)
-form
\theta_{\partial}
and a
(0,d-1)
-form
\ell_{\partial}
such that, on-shell:
\theta_{\rm gauge}+\mathrm{d}\theta_{\partial}+\delta\ell_{\partial}\approx 0
.

4.

Find a linear transformation to new Darboux coordinates
({\bf Y},{\bf X})
such that:
63
63

63

In particular,
X^{a}
must be linear in
\phi^{a}_{\rm rad}
and
p^{\rm rad}_{a}
, as we have assumed in our construction.

\delta\theta_{\rm rad}=\delta{\bf Y}.\delta{\bf X}
.

5.

Define the boundary Lagrangian as:

\ell=\mathbf{Y}.\mathbf{X}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{X}.\mathbf{Y}+\pi^{\rm rad}.\phi_{\rm rad}\right)+\ell_{\partial}\,.

(186)

Then, as we have explained in the main text, the stationary points of the action
S_{M\cup\Gamma}=\int_{M}L+\int_{\Gamma}\ell
in the configuration space
{\mathcal{F}}_{M}
, span the solution space
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
.

Lastly, let us comment on the status of edge modes and the edge-mode-dressed observables in the presence of these various types of symmetries and boundary conditions, especially as far as the reduced phase space is concerned. Let us firstly focus on the case of Dirichlet and Robin boundary conditions. Note that in this case, for any choice of post-selected subsystem theory
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
defined by

, there will exist both symmetries and meta-symmetries (but not boundary gauge symmetries), the former acting tangentially to the leaf, while the latter act transversally. In order to construct the regional reduced phase space

\Omega_{M}
to
{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
. The symmetry transformation — unique up to multiplication by a constant (see above) — does not constitute a degenerate direction and meta-symmetries are not even tangential. The symmetry transformation, which ultimately originates in the edge mode, thus corresponds to a physical direction and thereby a non-trivial transformation on
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}^{X_{0}}

\varphi

{\mathcal{P}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
. By contrast, the standard gauge generators

will constitute degenerate directions of
\Omega_{M}

is specified by the gauge-invariant boundary condition

, as well as suitable gauge-invariant initial data on some Cauchy slice
\Sigma
(cf. the related discussion in Sec.

is thus labeled by the boundary conditions and parametrized by the invariant Cauchy data.

The invariant Cauchy data will thus not only contain regional gauge-invariant data (i.e. only depending on fields in
M

\bar{M}
). For example, it may contain observables relating the connection

{\mathcal{P}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
contains frame-dressed observables and therefore more than the purely regional gauge-invariant data one would find when ignoring edge modes in the first place. In this case, edge modes are therefore of direct physical significance, besides also being a prerequisite for deriving the boundary value problem from the global variational problem through post-selection.

The situation is, however, quite different for Neumann boundary conditions, in which case

\theta_{\rm gauge}=-2F\delta{\varphi}+\mathrm{d}(\delta{\varphi}A_{\rm rad}-{\varphi}\delta A_{\rm rad}+\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}{\varphi})+\delta({\varphi}F)\,.

(245)

The first term includes the constraint
F\approx 0
, the second is a corner contribution, and the third — the difference to the Maxwell case — an exact field space form resulting from the change of polarization to radiative and edge mode degrees of freedom. Given that this last term vanishes on-shell, defining
\theta_{\partial}=-\delta{\varphi}A_{\rm rad}+{\varphi}\delta A_{\rm rad}-\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}{\varphi}
and
\ell_{\partial}=0
would be in compliance with the general algorithm summarized in Sec.
6.3
. However, recalling the discussion at the end of Secs.
6.1
and
6.2.1
, we will find it advantageous to cancel the exact form
\delta({\varphi}F)
even off-shell, by means of a non-vanishing
\ell_{\partial}:=-{\varphi}F
, which in turn will contribute to
\ell_{\rm corr}
. All in all, we are thus led to defining:

\ell_{\rm corr}:=-{\varphi}F-\frac{1}{4}A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}\wedge A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}\,.

(246)

Recalling item (ii) from Sec.
6.1
, the term
-{\varphi}F
can be equivalently motivated by the desire to restore gauge-invariance of the subregion variational problem. Indeed, an important feature of Chern-Simons theory as compared to Maxwell theory is that
S_{M}
fails to be gauge-invariant on its own. More precisely, for fictitious boundaries
S_{M}
is kinematically not gauge-invariant, but on-shell it is because

\delta_{\alpha}S_{M}=\int_{\Gamma\cup\Sigma_{2}\cup\Sigma_{1}}\alpha F\,.

(247)

In principle, we thus do not need to add a piece to the action in order to restore gauge-invariance for a subregion delimited by fictitious boundaries.
69
69

69

In subsequent work
[
101
]
, we shall also deal with physical boundaries on which the bulk equation of motion need not hold in the form
{\left.\kern-1.2ptF\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}=0
on account of possible sources, in which case
S_{M}
will not be gauge-invariant by itself on-shell.
However, it is convenient to have gauge-invariance built-in at the kinematical level, and therefore require the action to be gauge-invariant even off-shell. Including a term
-{\varphi}F
into
\ell_{\rm corr}
achieves just that.

Note that the decomposition (
245
) is not unique as one can shift
{\varphi}F
in the third term by an exact form
\mathrm{d}k
and compensate this change by a shift
-\mathrm{d}\delta k
of the second term. However, this translates into nothing else but the usual ambiguity in the definition of the boundary Lagrangian
\ell
(which is unique up to integration by parts).

Altogether, we are led to introducing the post-selection action (see equation (
159

\displaystyle S_{\rm ps}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}-{\varphi}F-\frac{1}{4}A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}\wedge A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}+\lambda\wedge(A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}-A^{\pm}_{0})

(248)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}-{\varphi}F\pm\frac{1}{2}\ast A_{\rm rad}\wedge A_{\rm rad}+\lambda\wedge(A_{\rm rad}\pm\ast A_{\rm rad}-A^{\pm}_{0})\,,

(249)

where
A^{\pm}_{0}
is a background self-dual (resp. antiself-dual) form entering the boundary condition
A^{\pm}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}A^{\pm}_{0}
, and
\lambda
is a gauge-invariant
1
-form that we can furthermore assume to be antiself-dual (resp. self-dual).
After evaluating the Lagrange multiplier on-shell (
\lambda\approx\frac{1}{2}A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}
), we obtain the following boundary Lagrangian (see equation (
174

\displaystyle\ell^{\pm}

\displaystyle=-{\varphi}F+\frac{1}{2}A^{\pm}_{0}\wedge A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}-\frac{1}{4}A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}\wedge A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}

(250)

\displaystyle=-{\varphi}F+\frac{1}{2}A^{\pm}_{0}\wedge(A_{\rm rad}\mp\ast A_{\rm rad})\pm\frac{1}{2}A_{\rm rad}\wedge\ast A_{\rm rad}\,.

(251)

In terms of the original variables
A

{\varphi}
, this is simply:

\ell^{\pm}=-{\varphi}\mathrm{d}A+\frac{1}{2}A^{\pm}_{0}\wedge(A\mp\ast A-\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\pm\ast\mathrm{d}{\varphi})\pm\frac{1}{2}A\wedge\ast A\pm A\wedge\ast\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\pm\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\wedge\ast\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\,,

(252)

from which we deduce that the frame field
{\varphi}
inherits a non-trivial kinetic term
\pm\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\wedge\ast\mathrm{d}{\varphi}
. By construction, this boundary Lagrangian allows us to impose the constraint
F=0
on
\Gamma
together with the boundary condition
A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}=A^{\pm}_{0}
as dynamical equations of motions.
Indeed, varying the action
S_{M\cup\Gamma}=\int_{M}L[A]+\int_{\Gamma}\ell^{\pm}_{\Gamma}
gives

\displaystyle\delta S_{M\cup\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}2\delta A\wedge F+\int_{\Sigma_{2}-\Sigma_{1}}\delta A\wedge A

(253)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\delta A^{\mp}_{\rm rad}\wedge(A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}-A_{0}^{\pm})-2F\delta\varphi+\mathrm{d}(\delta{\varphi}A_{\rm rad}-{\varphi}\delta A_{\rm rad}+\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}{\varphi})\Big)\,,

(254)

which is stationary up to variation on
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
provided that:
F=0
in the bulk as well as on
\Gamma
, and
A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}=A_{0}^{\pm}
.

We note that our final expression (

. In this, our derivation justifies and generalizes this previous proposal. It is also closely related to the boundary action previously introduced in

. Translated in our own notations, the authors of this paper proposed the boundary Lagrangian

\tilde{\ell}^{\pm}=-{\varphi}F+j\wedge A_{\rm rad}\pm\frac{1}{2}j\wedge\ast j\,,

(255)

in which an extra gauge-invariant and dynamical boundary
1

j
has been introduced. The equation of motion resulting from varying

, and once coupled to the bulk action, the equation of motion resulting from varying

.
A_{\rm rad}
is therefore (anti)self-dual on-shell, and it turns out that we recover the same equations of motion for
(A,{\varphi})
as with
\ell^{\pm}
, again provided that
A^{\pm}_{0}=0
. In this sense, our proposal similarly extends the results of
[
11
]
to non-vanishing
A^{\pm}_{0}
(at least at the classical level, which is the main focus of the present work). In order to accommodate non-vanishing boundary conditions, the action (
255
) can be augmented to:

\tilde{\ell}_{\Gamma}^{\pm}=\int_{\Gamma}-{\varphi}F+j\wedge A_{\rm rad}+\frac{1}{2}A_{0}^{\pm}\wedge(A_{\rm rad}\mp\ast A_{\rm rad})\pm\frac{1}{2}j\wedge\ast j\,,

(256)

which remains linear in
A_{\rm rad}
. By varying
A

, while the variation of

. It follows that
A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}=A^{\pm}_{0}
, as claimed.

Let us conclude this subsection by disentangling gauge transformations from boundary symmetries and their charges. Computing
\omega_{\rm gauge}=\delta\theta_{\rm gauge}
, we find:

\omega_{\rm gauge}=-\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\wedge\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}-2\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\wedge\delta A_{\rm rad}\approx-\mathrm{d}\left(\delta{\varphi}\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}+2\delta{\varphi}\delta A_{\rm rad}\right)\,.

(257)

The (on-shell) consistency relation (
134
),
\mathrm{d}\omega_{\partial}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}-\omega_{\rm gauge}
, can be solved by postulating a boundary presymplectic current

\omega_{\partial}:=\delta{\varphi}\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}+2\delta{\varphi}\delta A_{\rm rad}=2\delta{\varphi}\delta A-\delta{\varphi}\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}\,.

(258)

Vector fields
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
act on
\Omega_{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\omega+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega_{\partial}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\alpha\wedge\delta A+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left(2\alpha\delta A-2\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}\alpha-\alpha\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}+\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}\alpha\right)

(259)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left(-2\mathrm{d}(\alpha\delta A)+2\alpha\delta F\right)+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left(2\alpha\delta A-\mathrm{d}(\alpha\delta{\varphi})\right)

(260)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Sigma}\alpha\delta F\approx 0\,,

(261)

and are therefore gauge symmetries. Defining the constraints

C[\alpha]=2\int_{\Sigma}\alpha F

(262)

for field-independent
\alpha
, we find that they form an Abelian Poisson algebra

\{C[\alpha],C[\beta]\}:={\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\cdot{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}=0\,.

(263)

Next, we can introduce vector fields
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
that only act on the edge reference frame as frame reorientations (see Sec.
4.5
for the corresponding general discussion):

\Delta_{\rho}A:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(A)=0\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\Delta_{\rho}\varphi:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(\varphi)=-\rho\,,

(264)

so that, in particular, the frame-dressed observables vary according to
\Delta_{\rho}A_{\rm rad}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(A_{\rm rad})=\mathrm{d}\rho
and
\Delta_{\rho}A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(A^{\pm}_{\rm rad})=\mathrm{d}\rho\pm\ast\mathrm{d}\rho
. We then have
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\omega=0
, while

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\omega_{\partial}=-2\rho\delta A+\rho\delta\mathrm{d}{\varphi}-\delta{\varphi}\mathrm{d}\rho=-2\rho\delta A_{\rm rad}-\mathrm{d}(\rho\delta{\varphi})\,.

(265)

For a field-independent
\rho
it follows that:

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}=\delta Q[\rho]\,,\qquad\mathrm{where}\qquad Q[\rho]:=-2\int_{\partial\Sigma}\rho A_{\rm rad}\,,

(266)

and the charges
Q
obey a Kač-Moody algebra:

\{Q[\rho],Q[\sigma]\}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\sigma}\cdot{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}=-2\int_{\partial\Sigma}\rho\mathrm{d}\sigma\,.

(267)

We would find trivial charges (
\delta Q[\rho]\approx 0
) if one were to naively impose a boundary condition on the full radiative connection
A_{\rm rad}
. By contrast, with the weaker (anti)-selfdual boundary condition
A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}=A_{0}^{\pm}
,
A_{\rm rad}^{\mp}
is free to fluctuate, so that we can have non-trivial charges. More precisely, the regional covariant phase space is stable under
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
provided that
A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}
is left invariant. Such vector fields are labelled by functions
\rho
on
\Gamma
, such that

\mathrm{d}\rho\pm\ast\mathrm{d}\rho=0\,.

(268)

In the local coordinates
(t,x)
, this is nothing but
\partial_{t}\rho\pm\partial_{x}\rho=0
, with general solution of the form
\rho=\rho(t\mp x)
. Crucially, and in sharp contrast with Maxwell theory, we therefore obtain an infinite-dimensional space of symmetries.
70
70

70

This difference holds more generally in comparison to Yang-Mills theories. As we will see below, an additional specificity of non-Abelian Yang-Mills theories is that the analogue of (
268
) explicitly depends on the background fields defining the boundary condition. As a result, the dimension of the solution space itself depends on this background data.
This entails a non-trivial regional reduced phase space
{\mathcal{P}}_{M}^{X_{0}}
with propagating edge-mode-dressed degrees of freedom. For example, as in the case of Dirichlet and Robin boundary conditions in Maxwell theory, these may include observables relating
A
in the bulk of
M
to the edge frame via Wilson lines. Owing to the topological nature of the theory, such quasi-local observables would have been absent from the regional reduced phase space had one ignored edge modes in the first place.

Given that
\delta A_{\rm rad}^{\pm}=0
, we can define the charges by

Q^{\pm}[\rho]=-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\rho A_{\rm rad}^{\mp}\,,

(269)

so that
Q[\rho]=Q^{+}[\rho]+Q^{-}[\rho]
, where we have added an explicit superscript
\pm
to distinguish our two sets of boundary conditions. On solutions to the boundary conditions
A^{\pm}_{\rm rad}=A^{\pm}_{0}
, the charges
Q^{\mp}[\rho]
are no longer dynamical and thereby drop out of the Poisson-bracket relations in Eq. (
267
). The surviving charges
Q^{\pm}[\rho]
satisfy the Kač-Moody commutation relations. Indeed, owing to the (anti)-selfduality condition (
268
):

\{Q^{\pm}[\rho],Q^{\pm}[\sigma]\}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\sigma}\cdot{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}=-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\rho(\mathrm{d}\sigma\mp\ast\mathrm{d}\sigma)=-2\int_{\partial\Sigma}\rho\mathrm{d}\sigma\,.

(270)

Finally, the vector fields
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}
parametrized by functions
\rho
which fail to satisfy the stability condition (
268
) are to be interpreted as meta-symmetries: owing to (
266
), they do generate symplectomorphisms, but between distinct regional covariant phase spaces. Note that, as a result, boundary gauge symmetries are excluded in this example.

7.4
Yang-Mills theory

As our last example, and in complement to Sec.
7.2
, let us consider Yang-Mills theory for a general (compact and connected) matrix Lie group
G
. This will serve as a non-Abelian illustration of the post-selection and edge frame formalism.

Yang-Mills theory is also one of the two main models originally studied in the pioneering work of Donnelly and Freidel on edge modes
[
3
]
. Where comparable, our technical results will be consistent with theirs. At the same time, our work extends their construction and analysis. For example, 1) by establishing the interpretation of edge modes as internalized external reference frames (see Sec.
4
), we are clarifying their overall physical meaning and that they are already included in the global theory to start with; 2) we are focusing on a spacetime region with a non-trivial time-like boundary, rather than a spatial Cauchy slice, which provides an alternative starting point for the construction of the regional presymplectic structure
\Omega_{M}
(see Sec.
5.3
); 3) we demonstrate how to obtain the regional phase space and variational principle from the global ones via post-selection on edge-mode-dressed boundary conditions and clarify the status of edge modes in the context of different types of boundary conditions. In this regard, our work also complements the discussion in
[
9
,
8
,
6
,
7
]
, where the fate of edge modes in Yang-Mills theory in causal diamonds subject to Neumann boundary conditions was studied in a different formalism and we comment on that below.

The Lagrangian is defined as:

L=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[F\wedge\star F\right]

(271)

where
F:=\mathrm{d}A+A\wedge A
is the curvature two-form of a
G
-connection
A
. We also recall that the covariant exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}_{A}
acts on a Lie-algebra valued form
\eta
as
\mathrm{d}_{A}\eta:=\mathrm{d}\eta+\left[A,\eta\right]
.
71
71

71

Here,
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Lie bracket on Lie-algebra valued forms, which can be defined in terms of the wedge product as
[\eta_{1},\eta_{2}]=\eta_{1}\wedge\eta_{2}-(-1)^{pq}\eta_{2}\wedge\eta_{1}
, where
\eta_{1}
is a
p
-form and
\eta_{2}
is a
q
-form.
The variation of the curvature two-form can be nicely expressed as
\delta F=\mathrm{d}_{A}\delta A
, from which we deduce:

\delta L=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{d}_{A}\delta A\wedge\star F\right]=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{d}_{A}\left(\delta A\wedge\star F\right)\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]=-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\star F\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\,.

(272)

The bulk equations of motion and the presymplectic potential are therefore

\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\approx 0\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\Theta=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\star F\right]\,,

(273)

from which we also read out the presymplectic current
\omega=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\delta\star F\right]
.

The radiative data relative to the reference frame
U
on
\Gamma
is (cf. equations (
65
) and (
66
)):

A_{\rm rad}=U^{-1}AU+U^{-1}\mathrm{d}U\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad(\star F)_{\rm rad}=U^{-1}\star FU\,.

(274)

To determine the radiative presymplectic potential, we first remark that

\displaystyle U\delta(U^{-1}\mathrm{d}U)U^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}(\delta UU^{-1})\,,

(275)

\displaystyle U\delta(U^{-1}AU)U^{-1}

\displaystyle=\delta A+\left[A,\delta UU^{-1}\right]\,,

(276)

and therefore

U\delta A_{\rm rad}U^{-1}=\delta A+\mathrm{d}_{A}(\delta UU^{-1})\,.

(277)

It follows that

\theta_{\rm rad}:=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A_{\rm rad}\wedge(\star F)_{\rm rad}\right]=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge\star F\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{d}_{A}(\delta UU^{-1})\wedge\star F\right]\,,

(278)

or, in other words,

\theta_{\rm gauge}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{d}_{A}(\delta UU^{-1})\wedge\star F\right]=\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\star F\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\,.

(279)

On-shell, the second term vanishes and
\theta_{\rm gauge}
is therefore exact.
We see in particular, as anticipated in Sec.
4.7
, that the (field-space) right-invariant Maurer-Cartan form
\delta UU^{-1}
is the non-commutative analogue of the variation
\delta{\varphi}
that was entering the gauge presymplectic structure of Maxwell and Abelian Chern-Simons theories. The main difference is that
\delta UU^{-1}
is not field-space exact when
G
is not Abelian, which leads to extra contributions to the gauge part of the presymplectic current (as compared to formula (
204
) in Maxwell theory):

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm gauge}=\delta\theta_{\rm gauge}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\delta UU^{-1})(\delta UU^{-1})\star F\right]-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\delta\star F\right]{}

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\delta UU^{-1})(\delta UU^{-1})\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\delta\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\,.

(280)

When the equations of motion are satisfied on the boundary,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Gamma}}\approx 0
,
\omega_{\rm gauge}
becomes exact:

\omega_{\rm gauge}\approx\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\delta UU^{-1})(\delta UU^{-1})\star F\right]-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\delta\star F\right]\,.

(281)

Our consistency condition (
134
) can therefore be solved by the following choice of boundary presymplectic current:

\omega_{\partial}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}\delta\star F\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\delta UU^{-1})(\delta UU^{-1})\star F\right]\,.

(282)

Let us explicitly check that the regional presymplectic structure
\Omega_{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\omega+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega_{\partial}
is invariant under field-dependent gauge transformations, as argued around equation (
140
). Remember that the vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
acts as
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}(A)=[\alpha,A]-\mathrm{d}\alpha=-\mathrm{d}_{A}\alpha
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}(F)=[\alpha,F]
, where

is Lie-algebra-valued and possibly field-dependent. We can first compute

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\omega

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{d}_{A}\alpha\wedge\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge[\star F,\alpha]\right]

(283)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\mathrm{d}_{A}\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge[\star F,\alpha]\right]{}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha[\delta A,\star F]\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta A\wedge[\star F,\alpha]\right]{}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\approx-\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]\,,{}

where we have used Leibniz’s rule for
\mathrm{d}_{A}
in going from the first to the second line, and the commutation relation
[\delta,\mathrm{d}_{A}]\eta=[\delta A,\eta]
(for any Lie-algebra-valued form
\eta

\delta\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\approx 0

{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\omega

{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\delta UU^{-1}=\alpha
, we also have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\omega_{\partial}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta UU^{-1}[\star F,\alpha]\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\left[[\alpha,\delta UU^{-1}]\star F\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\star F\right]\,.

(284)

As anticipated, we conclude that:

{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\delta\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\approx 0\,,

(285)

meaning that the vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}
is a gauge direction, even for a field-dependent
\alpha
. Specifically, invoking Cartan’s magic formula, this entails invariance of the regional presymplectic structure,
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}}\Omega_{M}\approx 0

[
3

U

\omega_{\partial}

5.3
); in particular, invariance under field-dependent gauge transformation is an output, not an input of our procedure, but we recover the same results. For a field-independent
\alpha
, we can furthermore define the constraint generators

C[\alpha]:=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]\,,\qquad\delta C[\alpha]={\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}\,.

(286)

They obey the following Poisson algebra:
72
72

72

Here, it is useful to remember that
g\triangleright(\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F)=g\mathrm{d}_{A}\star Fg^{-1}

\{C[\alpha],C[\beta]\}:={\mathcal{X}}_{\beta}\cdot{\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot\Omega_{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\alpha[\beta,\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F]\right]=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[[\alpha,\beta]\mathrm{d}_{A}\star F\right]=C[[\alpha,\beta]]\,.

(287)

Hence, the constraints are first-class and define a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{g}

On the other hand, the edge reference frame reorientations

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}

\Delta_{\rho}A:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(A)=0\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\Delta_{\rho}UU^{-1}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\delta UU^{-1}=-U\rho U^{-1}\,.

(288)

They act on the frame-dressed observables
A_{\rm rad}
and
\star F_{\rm rad}
like gauge transformations intrinsic to
\Gamma

73
73

73

\Gamma

\mathrm{d}_{A_{\rm rad}}\eta:=\mathrm{d}\eta+[A_{\rm rad},\eta]

\Gamma

\Delta_{\rho}A_{\rm rad}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(A_{\rm rad})=[\rho,A_{\rm rad}]-\mathrm{d}\rho=-\mathrm{d}_{A_{\rm rad}}\rho\,,\qquad\Delta_{\rho}\left(\star F\right)_{\rm rad}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}(\star F_{\rm rad})=[\rho,\star F_{\rm rad}]\,.

(289)

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\omega=0

(290)

Q[\rho]:=-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\star F_{\rm rad}\right]\,,\qquad\delta Q[\rho]={\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}\,,

\{Q[\rho],Q[\sigma]\}:={\mathcal{Y}}_{\sigma}\cdot{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}=-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left[[\rho,\sigma]\star F_{\rm rad}\right]=Q[[\rho,\sigma]]\,.

(292)

\{Q[\rho],C[\alpha]\}={\mathcal{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\mathcal{Y}}_{\rho}\cdot\Omega_{M}=0\,.

(293)

Q

8
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we have provided a self-contained and detailed analysis of edge modes in gauge field theory, which we believe clarifies their physical nature. By embedding a spacetime region
M
with time-like boundary
\Gamma
into a global spacetime
M\cup\bar{M}
, we were able to view the regional dynamics in
M
as the result of a post-selection procedure on the global solution space. This also constitutes a connection between covariant phase space constructions for global spacetimes
[
56
]
and bounded subregions as e.g. in
[
66
,
67
,
11
,
68
]
. This global perspective allowed us to make precise sense of edge modes as dynamical reference frames, and thereby elucidate their interpretation. Our main conclusions are the following.

•

Edge modes can be systematically and consistently interpreted as dynamical reference frames, in the same sense in which they have appeared in the recent quantum reference frame literature
[
41
,
43

42
,
44

45
,
50

49
,
51

52
,
53

54
]
. Here, they are in general necessary to specify gauge-invariant boundary conditions on the time-like boundary
\Gamma
of a finite region
M
. They are non-local functionals of the global configuration fields (defined e.g. via systems of Wilson lines with support in the complementary region
\bar{M}
), which however materialize themselves as local fields on
\Gamma
. See Sec.
4
.

•

Hence, edge modes are a direct manifestation of the relational character of gauge field degrees of freedom
[
102
,
103
]
. Indeed, frame-dressed observables on
\Gamma
are gauge-invariant functionals which relate the region
M
to its complement
\bar{M}
, and that can be precisely interpreted as relational observables

in a covariant phase space setting (see Sec.

).

•

Given a suitable set of gauge-invariant boundary conditions on
\Gamma
(they can be of Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin or mixed type), we have developed a systematic algorithm allowing one to induce a consistent variational principle for subregion
M
from the global one on
M\cup\bar{M}
. This includes the definition of a presymplectic form
\Omega_{M}=\int_{\Sigma}\omega+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega_{\partial}
, and more generally, of an action
S_{M\cup\Gamma}=\int_{M}L+\int_{\Gamma}\ell
. The presymplectic structure is made independent from the choice of partial Cauchy slice
\Sigma
thanks to the inclusion of a corner term verifying the on-shell consistency condition
\omega_{\partial}+\mathrm{d}\omega_{\rm gauge}\mathrel{\hat{\approx}}0
(see Sec.
5
). The boundary Lagrangian form
\ell
can be motivated by similar arguments at the off-shell level (see Sec.
6
).

•

While edge modes are always useful concepts in the construction of the variational problem for subregion
M
, they may sometimes drop out from the resulting on-shell presymplectic structure. However, this only happens for very specific choices of boundary conditions (such as a Neumann boundary condition on
\star F
in Maxwell theory). For generic boundary conditions, the edge reference frame does contribute to the regional presymplectic structure.

•

As was originally emphasized in
[
3
]
, symmetries need to be carefully distinguished from gauge transformations. As we have shown, symmetry transformations of edge degrees of freedom can be interpreted as reference frame reorientations, which makes their physical character all the more clear. At the level of the subregion
M
, the inclusion of boundary conditions necessitates a refinement of this dichotomy. Frame reorientations then split into three further subcategories: proper boundary symmetries, which leave the regional field space (in particular, the boundary conditions) invariant and carry non-trivial charges; boundary gauge symmetries, which also leave the regional field space invariant, but whose charges become trivial on-shell of the boundary conditions; and finally meta-symmetries, which, by changing the boundary conditions themselves, give rise to symplectomorphisms between distinct regional field spaces.

•

Our formalism is in principle applicable to any gauge field theory, as examplified in Sec.
7
(see also Sec.
2
for a mechanical model featuring both gauge symmetries and edge modes). In particular, we do not see any major difference in the physical interpretation of Yang-Mills, Maxwell and Chern-Simons edge modes: they can all be derived in a systematic way by application of our algorithm. However, in contrast to Yang-Mills theories (including Maxwell), Chern-Simons theories do have the peculiarity of supporting an infinite-dimensional algebra of boundary symmetries, even after suitable boundary conditions have been imposed on
\Gamma
(see Sec.
7.3
for an illustration of this claim in the Abelian context). In that, the dynamical role of edge modes is greatly enhanced in such theories.

An obvious example to consider, that was left out of the present work for simplicity, is non-Abelian Chern-Simons theory.
Finally, beyond the definition of consistent variational principles, our main objective is to explore the statistical (and, ultimately, quantum) properties of bounded subregions in field theory, from the point of view of universal fluctuation relations in non-equilibrium thermodynamics.
