The Recurrent Neural Tangent Kernel
Sina Alemohammad, Zichao Wang, Randall Balestriero, Richard G. Baraniuk

Abstract
The study of deep neural networks (DNNs) in the infinite-width limit, via the so-called neural tangent kernel (NTK) approach, has provided new insights into the dynamics of learning, generalization, and the impact of initialization. One key DNN architecture remains to be kernelized, namely, the recurrent neural network (RNN). In this paper we introduce and study the Recurrent Neural Tangent Kernel (RNTK), which provides new insights into the behavior of overparametrized RNNs. A key property of the RNTK should greatly benefit practitioners is its ability to compare inputs of different length. To this end, we characterize how the RNTK weights different time steps to form its output under different initialization parameters and nonlinearity choices. A synthetic and 56 real-world data experiments demonstrate that the RNTK offers significant performance gains over other kernels, including standard NTKs, across a wide array of data sets.

Introduction

The overparameterization of modern deep neural networks (DNNs) has resulted in not only remarkably good generalization performance on unseen data
[
32
,
31
,
7
]
but also guarantees
that gradient descent learning can find the global minimum of their highly nonconvex loss functions
[
15
,
2
,
1
,
39
,
5
]
.
From these successes, a natural question arises: What happens when we take overparameterization to the limit by allowing the width of a DNN’s hidden layers to go to infinity?
Surprisingly, the analysis of such an (impractical) DNN becomes analytically tractable.
Indeed, recent work has shown that the training dynamics of (infinite-width) DNNs under gradient flow is captured by a constant kernel called the
Neural Tangent Kernel
(NTK) that evolves according to a linear ordinary differential equation (ODE)
[
24
,
29
,
4
]
.

Every DNN architecture and parameter initialization produces a distinct NTK. The original NTK was derived from the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)
[
24
]
and was soon followed by kernels derived from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNTK)
[
4
,
35
]
, Residual DNNs
[
23
]
, and Graph Convolutional Neural Networks (GNTK)
[
14
]
. In
[
37
]
, a general strategy to obtain the NTK of any architecture is provided.

In this paper, we extend the NTK concept to the important class of overparametrized Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
, a fundamental DNN architecture for processing sequential data.
We show that RNN in its infinite-width limit converges to a kernel that we dub the
Recurrent Neural Tangent Kernel
(RNTK). The RNTK provides high performance for various machine learning tasks, and an analysis of the properties of the kernel provides useful insights into the behavior of RNNs in the following overparametrized regime. In particular, we derive and study the RNTK to answer the following theoretical questions:

Q: Can the RNTK extract long-term dependencies between two data sequences?

RNNs are known to underperform at learning long-term dependencies due to the gradient vanishing or exploding
[
8
]
.
Attempted ameliorations have included orthogonal weights
[
3
,
25
,
20
]
and gating such as in Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
[
21
]
and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)
[
11
]
RNNs.
We demonstrate that the RNTK can detect long-term dependencies with proper initialization of the hyperparameters, and moreover, we show how the dependencies are extracted through time via different hyperparameter choices.

Q: Do the recurrent weights of the RNTK reduce its representation power compared to other NTKs?

An attractive property of an RNN that is shared by the RNTK is that it can deal with sequences of different lengths via weight sharing through time.
This enables the reduction of the number of learnable parameters and thus more stable training at the cost of reduced representation power.
We prove the surprising fact that employing tied vs. untied weights in an RNN
does not
impact the analytical form of the RNTK.

Q: Does the RNTK generalize well?

A recent study has revealed that the use of an SVM classifier with the NTK, CNTK, and GNTK kernels outperforms other classical kernel-based classifiers and trained finite DNNs on small data sets (typically fewer than 5000 training samples)
[
27
,
4
,
6
,
14
]
.
We extend these results to RNTKs to demonstrate that the RNTK outperforms a variety of classic kernels, NTKs
and
finite RNNs for time series data sets in both classification and regression tasks.
Carefully designed experiments with data of varying lengths demonstrate that the RNTK’s performance accelerates beyond other techniques as the difference in lengths increases.
Those results extend the empirical observations from
[
4
,
6
,
14
,
27
]
into finite DNNs, NTK, CNTK, and GNTK comparisons by observing that their performance-wise ranking depends on the employed DNN architecture.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

[C1]

We derive the analytical form for the RNTK of an overparametrized RNN at initialization using rectified linear unit (ReLU) and error function (erf) nonlinearities for arbitrary data lengths and number of layers (Section
3.1
).

[C2]

We prove that the RNTK remains constant during (overparametrized) RNN training and that the dynamics of training are simplified to a set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) (Section
3.2
).

[C3]

When the input data sequences are of equal length, we show that the RNTKs of weight-tied and weight-untied RNNs converge to the same RNTK (Section
3.3
).

[C4]

Leveraging our analytical formulation of the RNTK, we empirically demonstrate how correlations between data at different times are weighted by the function learned by an RNN for different sets of hyperparameters. We also offer practical suggestions for choosing the RNN hyperparameters for deep information propagation through time (Section
3.4
).

[C5]

We demonstrate that the RNTK is eminently practical by showing its superiority over classical kernels, NTKs, and finite RNNs
in exhaustive experiments on time-series classification and regression with both synthetic and 56 real-world data sets (Section 4).

3
The Recurrent Neural Tangent Kernel

We are now ready to derive the RNTK. We first prove the convergence of an RNN at initialization to the RNTK in the infinite-width limit and discuss various insights it provides. We then derive the convergence of an RNN after training to the RNTK.
Finally, we analyze the effects of various hyperparameter choices on the RNTK.
Proofs of all of our results are provided in the Appendices.

3.1
RNTK for an Infinite-Width RNN at Initialization

First we specify the following parameter initialization scheme that follows previous work on NTKs
[
24
]
, which is crucial to our convergence results:

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt{\bm{W}}^{(\ell)}\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\sigma_{w}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)},\,\,\,\,{\bm{U}}^{(1)}\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\sigma_{u}^{1}}{\sqrt{m}}{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)},\,\,\,\,{\bm{U}}^{(\ell)}\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\sigma_{u}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell)}(\ell\hskip-2.0pt\geq\hskip-2.0pt2),\,\,\,\,{\bm{V}}\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\sigma_{v}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{V}},\,\,\,\,{\bm{b}}^{(\ell)}\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\sigma_{b}{\mathbf{b}}^{(\ell)}\,,

(3)

where

\displaystyle[{\mathbf{W}}^{\ell}]_{i,j},\,\,[{\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell)}]_{i,j},\,\,[{\mathbf{V}}]_{i,j},\,\,[{\mathbf{b}}^{(\ell)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,.

(4)

We will refer to (

For the initial (at time

The derivation of the RNTK at initialization is based on the correspondence between Gaussian initialized, infinite-width DNNs and Gaussian Processes (GPs), known as the DNN-GP. In this setting every coordinate of the DNN output tends to a GP as the number of units/neurons in the hidden layer (its width) goes to infinity. The corresponding DNN-GP kernel is computed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}}\big[[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})]_{i}\bm{\cdot}[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})]_{i}\big],\hskip 2.84526pt\forall i\in[d].

(5)

First introduced for a single-layer, fully-connected neural network by
[
30
]

[
28

16

33

19

36

In the case of RNNs, each coordinate of the RNN pre-activation

converges to a centered GP depending on the inputs with kernel

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(\ell,t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}}\big[[{\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})]_{i}\bm{\cdot}[{\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t^{\prime})}{({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}]_{i}\big]\,\,\,\forall i\in[n].

(6)

As per
[
35
]
, the gradients of random infinite-width DNNs computed during backpropagation are also Gaussian distributed. In the case of RNNs, every coordinate of the vector
{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}}):=\sqrt{n}\big(\nabla_{{\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})\big)

\displaystyle\Pi^{(\ell,t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\mathop{{\mathbb{E}}}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{N}}\big[[{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})]_{i}\bm{\cdot}[{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})]_{i}\big]\,\,\,\forall i\in[n].

3.2
RNTK for an Infinite-Width RNN during Training

We prove that an infinitely-wide RNN, not only at initialization but also
during
gradient descent training, converges to the limiting RNTK at initialization.

Theorem 2

Let
n
be the number of units of each RNN’s layer.
Assume that
\Theta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
is positive definite on
\mathcal{X}
such that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\Theta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}))>0
. Let
\eta^{*}:=2\big(\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\Theta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}))+\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\Theta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})\big)\big)^{-1}
. For an
L
-layer RNN with NTK initialization as in (
3
), (
4
) trained under gradient flow (recall (
1
) and (
2
)) with
\eta<\eta^{*}
, we have with high probability

\displaystyle\underset{s}{\mathrm{sup}}\frac{\|\theta_{s}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}},\underset{s}{\mathrm{sup}}\|\widehat{\Theta}_{s}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})-\widehat{\Theta}_{0}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Remarks.

Theorem
2
states that the training dynamics of an RNN in the infinite-width limit as in (
1
), (
2
) are governed by the RNTK derived from the RNN at its initialization.
Intuitively, this is due to the NTK initialization (
3

3.3
RNTK for an Infinite-Width RNN Without Weight Sharing

We prove that, in the infinite-width limit, an RNN without weight sharing (untied weights), i.e., using independent new weights
{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell,t)}
,
{\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell,t)}
and
{\mathbf{b}}^{(\ell,t)}
at each time step
t

First, recall that it is a common practice to use weight-tied RNNs, i.e., in layer
\ell

{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)}
,
{\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell)}
and
{\mathbf{b}}^{(\ell)}
are the same across all time steps
t

We demonstrate that, when using untied-weights, the RNTK formula remains unchanged.

Theorem 3

For inputs of the same length,
an RNN with untied weights converges to the same RNTK as an RNN with tied weights in the infinite-width (
n\rightarrow\infty
) regime.

Remarks.

Theorem
3
implies that weight-tied and weight-untied RNNs have similar behaviors in the infinite-width limit. It also suggests that existing results on the simpler, weight-untied RNN setting may be applicable for the more general, weight-tied RNN.
The plot on the right side of Figure

Insights into the Roles of the RNTK’s Hyperparameters

Our analytical form for the RNTK is fully determined by a small number of hyperparameters, which contains the various weight variances collected into
\mathcal{S}=\{\sigma_{w},\sigma_{u},\sigma_{b},\sigma_{h}\}
and the activation function.

2

2

From (

merely scales the RNTK and does not change its overall behavior.

In standard supervised-learning settings, one often performs cross-validation to select the hyperparameters. However, since kernel methods become computationally intractable for large datasets, we seek a more computationally friendly alternative to cross-validation. Here we conduct a novel exploratory analysis that provides new insights into the impact of the RNTK hyperparameters on the RNTK output and suggests a simple method to select them a priori in a deliberate manner.

To visualize the role of the RNTK hyperparameters, we introduce the
sensitivity

s(t)
of the RNTK of two input sequences

{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}

{\bm{x}}_{t}
at time
t

\displaystyle s(t)=\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\Theta({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\|_{2}\,.

(21)

Here,

indicates how sensitive the RNTK is to the data at time
t

{\bm{x}}_{t}
, in presence of another data sequence
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
.
Intuitively, large/small

indicates that the RNTK is relatively sensitive/insensitive to the input
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at time
t

The sensitivity is crucial to understanding to which extent the RNTK prediction is impacted by the
input at each time step. In the case where some time indices have a small sensitivity, then any input variation in those corresponding times will not alter the RNTK output and thus will produce a metric that is invariant to those changes.
This situation can be beneficial or detrimental based on the task at hand. Ideally, and in the absence of prior knowledge on the data, one should aim to have a roughly constant sensitivity across time in order to treat all time steps equally in the RNTK input comparison.

This sensitivity analysis provides a practical tool for RNTK hyperparameter tuning. If given a priori knowledge, hyperparameters can be selected that direct the RNTK to the desired time-steps.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have derived the RNTK based on the architecture of a simple RNN.
We have proved that, at initialization, after training, and without weight sharing, any simple RNN converges to the same RNTK. This convergence provides new insights into the behavior of infinite-width RNNs, including how they process different-length inputs, their training dynamics, and the sensitivity of their output at every time step to different nonlinearities and initializations.
We have highlighted the RNTK’s practical utility by demonstrating its superior performance on time series classification and regression compared to a range of classical kernels, the NTK, and trained RNNs. There are many avenues for future research, including developing RNTKs for gated RNNs such as the LSTM
[
21
]
and investigating which of our theoretical insights extend to finite RNNs.

Appendix B
Proofs for Theorems 1 and 3: RNTK Convergence at Initialization

B.1
Preliminary: Netsor programs

Calculation of NTK in any architecture relies on finding the GP kernels that correspond to each pre-activation and gradient layers at initialization. For feedforward neural networks with
n_{1},\dots,n_{L}
number of neurons (channels in CNNs) at each layer the form of this GP kernels can be calculated via taking the limit of
n_{1},\dots,n_{L}
sequentially one by one. The proof is given by induction, where by conditioning on the previous layers, each entry of the current layer is sum of infinite i.i.d Gaussian random variables, and based on Central Limit Theorem (CLT), it becomes a Gaussian process with kernel calculated based on the previous layers. Since the first layer is an affine transformation of input with Gaussian weights, it is a Gaussian process and the proof is completed. See
[
28
,
16
,
33
,
19
]
for a formal treatment.
However, due to weight-sharing, sequential limit is not possible and condoning on previous layers does not result in i.i.d. weights. Hence the aforementioned arguments break. To deal with it, in
[
35
]
a proof using Gaussian conditioning trick
[
9
]
is presented which allows use of recurrent weights in a network. More precisely, it has been demonstrated than neural networks (without batch normalization) can be expressed and a series of matrix multiplication and (piece wise) nonlinearity application, generally referred as
Netsor programs
. It has been shown that any architecture that can be expressed as
Netsor programs
that converge to GPs as width goes to infinity in the same rate, which a general rule to obtain the GP kernels. For completeness of this paper, we briefly restate the results from
[
35
]
which we will use later for calculation derivation of RNTK.

There are 3 types of variables in
Netsor programs
;
A
-vars,
G
-vars and
H
-vars.
A
-vars are matrices and vectors with i.i.d Gaussian entries,
G
-vars are vectors introduced by multiplication of a vector by an
A
-var and
H
-vars are vectors after coordinate wise nonlinearities is applied to
G
-vars. Generally,
G
-vars can be thought of as pre-activation layers which are asymptotically treated as a Gaussian distributed vectors,
H
-vars as after-activation layers and
A
-vars are the weights. Since in neural networks inputs are immediately multiplied by a weight matrix, it can be thought of as an
G
-var, namely
{\bm{g}}_{in}
. Generally
Netsor programs
supports
G
-vars with different dimension, however the asymptotic behavior of a neural networks described by
Netsor programs
does not change under this degree of freedom, as long as they go to infinity at the same rate. For simplicity, let the
G
-vars and
H
-vars have the same dimension
n
since the network of interest is RNN and all pre-activation layers have the same dimension. We introduce the
Netsor programs
under this simplification. To produce the output of a neural network,
Netsor programs
receive a set of
G
-vars and
A
-vars as input, and new variables are produced sequentially using the three following operators:

•

Matmul
: multiplication of an
A
-var:
{\bm{A}}
with an
H
-var:
{\bm{h}}
, which produce a new
G
-var,
{\bm{g}}
.

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}

(27)

•

Lincomp
: Linear combination of
G
-vars,
{\bm{g}}^{i},\,\,1\leq i\leq k
, with coefficients
a^{i}\in\mathbb{R}\,\,1\leq i\leq k
which produce of new
G
-var:

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}{\bm{g}}^{i}

(28)

•

Nonlin
: creating a new
H
-var,
{\mathbf{h}}
, by using a nonlinear function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that act coordinate wise on a set of
G
-vars,
{\bm{g}}^{i},\,\,1\leq i\leq k
:

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}=\varphi({\bm{g}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{g}}^{k})

(29)

Any output of the neural network
y\in\mathbb{R}
should be expressed as inner product of a new
A
-var which has not been used anywhere else in previous computations and an
H
-var:

\displaystyle y={\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}

Any other output can be produced by another
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{h}}^{\prime}
(possibility the same
{\bm{h}}
or
{\bm{v}}
).

It is assumed that each entry of any
A
-var :
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
in the
netsor programs
computations is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma_{a}^{2}}{n})
and the input
G
-vars are Gaussian distributed. The collection of a specific entry of all
G
-vars of in the
netsor program
converges in probability to a Gaussian vector
\{[{\bm{g}}^{1}]_{i},\dots,[{\bm{g}}^{k}]_{i}\}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
for all
i\in[n]
as
n
goes to infinity.

Let
\mu({\bm{g}}):={\mathbb{E}}\big[[{\bm{g}}]_{i}\big]
be the mean of a
G
-var and
\Sigma({\bm{g}},{\bm{g}}^{\prime}):={\mathbb{E}}\big[[{\bm{g}}]_{i}\bm{\cdot}[{\bm{g}}^{\prime}]_{i}\big]
be the covariance between any two
G
-vars. The general rule for
\mu({\bm{g}})
is given by the following equations:

\displaystyle\mu({\bm{g}})=\begin{cases}\mu^{in}({\bm{g}})&\text{if }{\bm{g}}\text{ is input}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}\mu({\bm{g}}^{i})&\text{if }{\bm{g}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}{\bm{g}}^{i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(30)

For
{\bm{g}}
and
{\bm{g}}^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{G}=\{{\bm{g}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{g}}^{r}\}
be the set of
G
-vars that has been introduced
before

{\bm{g}}
and
{\bm{g}}^{\prime}
with distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}}_{\mathcal{G}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{G}})
, where
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{G}|\times|\mathcal{G}|}
containing the pairwise covariances between the
G
-vars.
\Sigma({\bm{g}},{\bm{g}}^{\prime})
is calculated via the following rules:

\displaystyle\Sigma({\bm{g}},{\bm{g}}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\Sigma^{\rm in}({\bm{g}},{\bm{g}}^{\prime})&\text{if }{\bm{g}}\text{ and }{\bm{g}}^{\prime}\text{ are inputs}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}\Sigma({\bm{g}}^{i},{\bm{g}}^{\prime})&\text{if }{\bm{g}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}{\bm{g}}^{i}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}\Sigma({\bm{g}},{\bm{g}}^{i})&\text{if }{\bm{g}}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{i}{\bm{g}}^{i}\\
\sigma^{2}_{A}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{G}})}[\varphi({\mathbf{z}})\bar{\varphi}({\mathbf{z}})]&\text{if }{\bm{g}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}\text{ and }{\bm{g}}^{\prime}={\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}^{\prime}\\

(31)

Where

and

are functions of
G
-vars in
\mathcal{G}
from possibly different nonlinearities. This set of rules presents a recursive method for calculating the GP kernels in a network where the recursive formula starts from data dependent quantities
\Sigma^{\rm in}
and
\mu^{in}
which are given.

All the above results holds when the nonlinearities are bounded uniformly by
e^{(cx^{2-\alpha})}
for some
\alpha>0
and when their derivatives exist.

Standard vs. NTK initialization.
The common practice (which
netsor programs
uses) is to initialize DNNs weights
[{\bm{A}}]_{i,j}
with
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma_{a}}{\sqrt{n}})
(known as

initialization) where generally

is the number of units in the previous layer. In this paper we have used a different parameterization scheme as used in
[
24
]
and we factor the standard deviation as shown in

and initialize weights with standard standard Gaussian. This approach does not change the the forward computation of DNN, but normalizes the backward computation (when computing the gradients) by factor

, otherwise RNTK will be scales by
n
. However this problem can be solved by scaling the step size by

and there is no difference between

and
standard
initialization
[
29
]
.

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{\delta}}^{(t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right)\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{v}}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{n}\left\langle{\bm{h}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{h}}^{(T^{\prime})}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right).

Remember that for any two
G
-var
{\mathbb{E}}\left[[{\bm{g}}]_{i}[{\bm{g}}^{\prime}]_{i}\right]
is independent of index
i
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{h}}^{(t-1)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{h}}^{(t^{\prime}-1)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathrm{V}_{\phi}\big[{\bm{K}}^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big]\hskip 20.0ptt>1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{h}}^{(0)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{h}}^{(0)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\rightarrow\sigma_{h}^{2}.

Hence, by summing the above terms in the infinite-width limit we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}),\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\rightarrow\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T^{\prime}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pi^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\bm{\cdot}\Sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right)+\mathcal{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}).

(35)

Since
\Pi^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=0
for
t^{\prime}-t\neq\tau
it is simplified to

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}),\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pi^{(t,t+\tau)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\bm{\cdot}\Sigma^{(t,t+\tau)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right)+\mathcal{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}).

Multi-dimensional output.
For
f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

i

\displaystyle\left[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})\right]_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{v}}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}_{i}{\bm{h}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}}),

where
{\bm{v}}_{i}
is independent of
{\bm{v}}_{j}
for
i\neq j
. As a result, for The RNTK
\Theta({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle[\Theta({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}\left[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})\right]_{i},\nabla_{\theta}\left[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right]_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}\left[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})\right]_{i},\nabla_{\theta}\left[f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right]_{i}\right\rangle=\Theta^{(T,T^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}).

\displaystyle\Theta({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\Theta^{(T,T^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d}.

\displaystyle\Theta({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\Theta^{(L,T,T^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d}.

B.4
Proof for Theorem 3: Weight-Untied RNTK

The architecture of a weight-untied single layer RNN is

\displaystyle{\bm{g}}^{\left(t\right)}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\sqrt{m}}{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}{\bm{h}}^{\left(t-1\right)}({\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{u}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)}{\bm{x}}_{t}+\sigma_{b}{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}^{\left(t\right)}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\phi\left({\bm{g}}^{\left(t\right)}({\bm{x}})\right)

\displaystyle f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{v}}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}})

Where we use new weights at each time step and we index it by time. Like previous sections, we first derive the forward pass kernels for two same length data
{\bm{x}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
,
{\bm{x}}=\{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathrm{V}_{\phi}\big[{\bm{K}}^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big]+\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{m}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{t}\rangle+\sigma_{b}^{2}.

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0\hskip 20.0ptt\neq t^{\prime}

Since we are using same weight at the same time step,
\Sigma^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
can be written as a function of the previous kernel, which is exactly as the weight-tied RNN. However for different length, it becomes zero as a consequence of using different weights, unlike weight-tied which has non-zero value. The kernel of the first time step and output is also the same as weight-tied RNN. For the gradients we have:

\displaystyle{\bm{\delta}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{v}{\bm{v}}\odot\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}}))

\displaystyle{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\sqrt{n}}({\mathbf{W}}^{(t+1)})^{\top}\Big(\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}))\odot{\bm{\delta}}^{(t+1)}({\bm{x}})\Big)\hskip 20.0ptt\in[T-1]

For
t^{\prime}=t
we have:

\displaystyle\Pi^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathrm{V}_{\phi^{\prime}}\big[{\bm{K}}^{(t+1,t+1)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big]\Pi^{(t+1,t+1)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Pi^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{v}^{2}\mathrm{V}_{\phi^{\prime}}\big[{\bm{K}}^{(T+1,T+\tau+1)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big].

Due to using different weights for
t\neq t^{\prime}
, we can immediately conclude that
\Pi^{(t,t^{\prime})}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=0
. This set of calculation is exactly the same as the weight-tied case when
\tau=T-T=0
.

Finally, with
\theta=\mathrm{Vect}\big[\{\{{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)},{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)},{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T},{\bm{v}}\}\big]
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}),\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{v}}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\bm{v}}},\right\rangle

with

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{W}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{n}\left\langle{\bm{h}}^{(t-1)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{h}}^{(t-1)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{U}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{m}\left\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{t}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\mathbf{b}}^{(t)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\left\langle{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{\delta}}^{(t)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right)\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{v}}},\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}{\partial{\bm{v}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{n}\left\langle{\bm{h}}^{(T)}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{h}}^{(T^{\prime})}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right).

As a result we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}),\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rangle=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pi^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\bm{\cdot}\Sigma^{(t,t)}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right)+\mathcal{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}),

same as the weight-tied RNN when
\tau=0
. This concludes the proof for Theorem 3.

Definition 1

The Jacobian of a neural network is local lipschitz at NTK initialization
\left(\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)
if there is constant
K>0
for every
C
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|{\bm{J}}(\theta,\mathcal{X})\|_{F}<K\\
\|{\bm{J}}(\theta,\mathcal{X})-{\bm{J}}(\tilde{\theta},\mathcal{X})\|_{F}<K\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|\end{cases},\hskip 30.0pt\forall\,\,\theta,\tilde{\theta}\in B(\theta_{0},R)

where

\displaystyle B(\theta,R):=\{\theta:\|\theta_{0}-\theta\|<R\}.

Theorem 4

Lemma 1

Lemma 2

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector whose entries are
independent standard normal random variables. Then for every
t\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-e^{(-ct^{2})}
for some constant
c

\displaystyle\|a\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{t}.

Setting
t=\sqrt{n}
for any
\theta\in R(\theta_{0},R)
. With high probability, we get:

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)}\|_{2},\|{\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\leq 3\sqrt{n},\hskip 10.0pt\|{\mathbf{b}}^{\ell}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{n},\hskip 10.0pt\|{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell,0)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{h}\sqrt{n}.

We also assume that there exists some finite constant
C
such that

\displaystyle|\phi(x)|<C|x|,\hskip 10.0pt|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})|<C|x-x^{\prime}|,\hskip 10.0pt|\phi^{\prime}(x)|<C,\hskip 10.0pt,|\phi^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})|<C|x-x^{\prime}|.

The proof is obtained by induction. From now on assume that all inequalities in (
38
) and (
39
) holds with some
k
for the previous layers. We have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{\sigma_{w}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\left(\ell\right)}{\bm{h}}^{\left(\ell,t-1\right)}({\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{u}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\ {\bm{h}}^{\left(\ell-1,t\right)}({\bm{x}})+\sigma_{b}^{\ell}{\mathbf{b}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{w}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\mathbf{W}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\|_{2}\|\phi\left({\bm{g}}^{\left(\ell,t-1\right)}({\bm{x}})\right)\|_{2}+\frac{\sigma_{u}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\|_{2}\|\phi\left({\bm{g}}^{\left(\ell-1,t\right)}({\bm{x}})\right)\|_{2}+\sigma_{b}^{\ell}\|{\mathbf{b}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(3\sigma_{w}^{\ell}Ck+3\sigma_{u}^{\ell}Ck+2\sigma_{b}\right)\sqrt{n}.

And the proof for (
38
) and (
39
) is completed by showing that the first layer is bounded

\displaystyle\|{\bm{g}}^{(1,1)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{\sigma_{w}^{1}}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\left(\ell\right)}{\bm{h}}^{\left(1,0\right)}({\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{u}^{1}}{\sqrt{m}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\left(\ell\right)}\ {\bm{x}}_{1}+\sigma_{b}^{1}{\mathbf{b}}^{\left(1\right)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(3\sigma_{w}^{1}\sigma_{h}+\frac{3\sigma_{u}}{\sqrt{m}}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|_{2}+2\sigma_{b})\sqrt{n}.

For the gradient of first layer we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\sigma_{v}{\bm{v}}\odot\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{v}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{v}C\sqrt{n}.

And similarly we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(3\sigma_{w}Ck^{\prime}+3\sigma_{u}Ck^{\prime}\right)\sqrt{n}.

For
\theta,\tilde{\theta}\in B(\theta_{0},R)
we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{g}}^{(1,1)}({\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{g}}}^{(1,1)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{\sigma_{w}^{1}}{\sqrt{n}}({\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}^{(1)}){\bm{h}}^{(1,0)}({\bm{x}})+\frac{\sigma_{u}^{1}}{\sqrt{m}}({\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\mathbf{U}}}^{(1)}){\bm{h}}^{(1,0)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(3\sigma_{w}^{1}\sigma_{h}+\frac{3\sigma_{u}^{1}}{m}\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|_{2}\right)\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{n}.

\displaystyle\|{\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{g}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi({\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\|\frac{\sigma_{w}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}({\mathbf{W}}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}^{(\ell)})\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\|\frac{\sigma_{w}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{{\mathbf{W}}}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\|\phi({\bm{g}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}}))-\phi(\tilde{{\bm{g}}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\|\phi({\bm{g}}^{(\ell-1,t)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\|\frac{\sigma_{u}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}({\mathbf{U}}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{{\mathbf{U}}}^{(\ell)})\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\|\frac{\sigma_{u}^{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{{\mathbf{U}}}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\|\phi({\bm{g}}^{(\ell-1,t)}({\bm{x}}))-\phi(\tilde{{\bm{g}}}^{(\ell-1,t)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}+\sigma_{b}\|{\mathbf{b}}^{(\ell)}-\tilde{{\mathbf{b}}}^{(\ell)}\|

\displaystyle\leq(k\sigma_{w}^{\ell}+3\sigma_{w}^{\ell}Ck+k\sigma_{u}^{\ell}+3\sigma_{u}^{\ell}Ck+\sigma_{b})\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{n}.

For gradients we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{v}\|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(L,T)})\|_{\infty}\|({\bm{v}}-\tilde{{\bm{v}}})\|_{2}+\sigma_{v}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\|\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}}))-\phi^{\prime}({\bm{g}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\sigma_{v}C+2\sigma_{v}Ck)\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{n}.

And similarly using same techniques we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\leq(\sigma_{w}C+3\sigma_{w}Ck+\sigma_{u}C+3\sigma_{u}Ck)\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{n}.

As a result, there exists
K_{1}
that is a function of
\sigma_{w},\sigma_{u},\sigma_{b},L,T
and the norm of the inputs.

Now we prove the local Lipchitzness of the Jacobian

\displaystyle\|{\bm{J}}(\theta,{\bm{x}})\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{\ell}{\bm{h}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{\ell}{\bm{h}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{\ell}\right\|_{F}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(1,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{1}{\bm{h}}^{(1,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{nm}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(1,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(1,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{1}\right\|_{F}\bigg)+\frac{\sigma_{v}}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{h}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{w}^{\ell}+K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{u}^{\ell}+\sigma_{b}^{\ell}K_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{w}^{1}+\frac{K_{1}\sigma_{u}^{1}}{\sqrt{m}}\|{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}+\sigma_{b}^{1}K_{1})+\sigma_{v}CK_{1}\bigg).

And for
\theta,\tilde{\theta}\in B(\theta_{0},R)
we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{J}}(\theta,{\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{J}}}(\theta,{\bm{x}})\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{\ell}{\bm{h}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{\ell}\tilde{{\bm{h}}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{\ell}{\bm{h}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{\ell}\tilde{{\bm{h}}}^{(\ell,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{\ell}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{\ell}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(1,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{1}{\bm{h}}^{(1,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(1,t)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{w}^{1}\tilde{{\bm{h}}^{(1,t-1)}}({\bm{x}})\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{nm}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(1,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}\right)^{\top}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(1,t-1)}({\bm{x}})\left(\sigma_{u}^{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\bm{\delta}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{\ell}-\tilde{{\bm{\delta}}}^{(\ell,t)}({\bm{x}})\bm{\cdot}\sigma_{b}^{\ell}\right\|\bigg)+\frac{\sigma_{v}}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{h}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})-\tilde{{\bm{h}}^{(L,T)}({\bm{x}})}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(4K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{w}^{\ell}+4K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{u}^{\ell}+\sigma_{b}^{\ell}K_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(4K_{1}^{2}C\sigma_{w}^{1}+\frac{K_{1}\sigma_{u}^{1}}{\sqrt{m}}\|{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}+\sigma_{b}^{1}K_{1})+\sigma_{v}CK_{1}\bigg)\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|_{2}.

The above proof can be generalized to the entire dataset by a straightforward application of the union bound.
This concludes the proof for Theorem 2.
