The node cop-win reliability of unicyclic and bicyclic graphs
Maimoonah Ahmed, Ben Cameron

Abstract
Various models to quantify the reliability of a network have been studied where certain components of the graph may fail at random and the probability that the remaining graph is connected is the proxy for reliability. In this work we introduce a strengthening of one of these models by considering the probability that the remaining graph is not just connected but also cop-win. A graph is cop-win if one cop can guarantee capture of a fleeing robber in the well-studied pursuit-evasion game of Cops and Robber. More precisely, for a graph G G with nodes that are operational independently with probability p p and edges that are operational if and only if both of their endpoints are operational, the node cop-win reliability of G G , denoted NCRel ⁡ ( G , p ) \operatorname{NCRel}(G,p) , is the probability that the operational nodes induce a cop-win subgraph of G G . It is then of interest to find graphs G G with n n nodes and m m edges such that NCRel ⁡ ( G , p ) ≥ NCRel ⁡ ( H , p ) \operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)\geq\operatorname{NCRel}(H,p) for all p ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] p\in[0,1] and all graphs H H with n n nodes and m m edges. Such a graph is called uniformly most reliable . We show that uniformly most reliable graphs exist for unicyclic and bicyclic graphs, respectively. This is in contrast to the fact that there are no known sparse graphs maximizing the corresponding notion of node reliability.



1
Introduction

Graphs often model real-world networks whose reliability is of paramount importance. To this end there have been many models proposed to quantify the reliability of a network. One of particular interest pioneered by Moore and Shannon
[
16
]
is to assume that various components fail independently with probability
p
and the reliability of the graph is the probability that the graph is connected. While Moore and Shannon originally considered edge failures in their model, in many situations it is more natural to consider a model where the nodes can fail
[
21
,
8
,
5
]
.
To model this situation mathematically, Stivaros
[
21
]
introduced the following. Let
G
be a graph whose nodes are operational independently with probability
p
, for some
p\in[0,1]
and whose edges are operational if and only if both their endpoints are operational. A
connected set
of
G
is a subset
S
of
V(G)
such that the subgraph induced by
S
is connected. The
node reliability
of
G
, denoted
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
, is defined to be the probability that the operational nodes of
G
form a connected set. Let
S_{i}(G)
denote the number of connected sets of
G
of order
i
. Then we can express
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
as the univariate polynomial,

\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}(G)(1-p)^{n-i}p^{i}.

When designing a network, it is desirable to do so in a way that it is most likely that the remaining nodes can communicate with each other even after nodes fail, i.e. to design a graph that maximizes
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
. However, there is some difficulty in determining which values of
p
we wish the network to maximize
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
. This all hinges on how likely it is that nodes will fail and this may change considerably from the time the network is designed as the components age. Therefore, it is of interest to find graphs
G
such that
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)\geq\operatorname{NRel}(H,p)
for all values of
p\in[0,1]
and all graphs
H
with the same order and number of edges as
G
(i.e. can be constructed with the same resources). Such a graph is called
uniformly most reliable
(UMR) with respect to node reliability. For graphs on
n
nodes and
m
edges, UMR graphs with respect to node reliability do not always exist, as was shown for
m=n

[
21
]
, but there are some cases where UMR graphs do exist. A complete list of which is
K_{1,n-1}
and
K_{n}-M
for a matching
M

[
21
]
,
K_{b,b+2}

[
13
]
, and
K_{b,b+1,b+1,\ldots,b+1,b+2}

[
22
]
. The last family is a generalization of
K_{b,b+1,b+2}
, first shown in
[
15
]
. As is readily noted, all known UMR graphs are complete multipartite. As such, there was an appetite to consider variations on node reliability to get a more diverse sample of UMR graphs when nodes can fail.

Let
G
be a graph where each node is operational independently with probability
p
and
t,s\in V(G)
be target nodes . Then the
two-terminal node reliability
of
G
is the probability that the graph is connected and contains
t
and
s
. In a very recent paper
[
5
]
, it was shown that for every
n
and
m
there exists a UMR graph with respect to two-terminal node reliability, however when the target vertices are required to have distance at least
3
from each other, there exists a UMR graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges if and only if
m\leq 8
,
m\geq\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor
, or
n=9
and
m=12
. This complete classification is an important step for node reliability, but two target vertices are not always clearly defined in the network the graph is modelling. Thus, we introduce a new variant on node reliability and demonstrate that it also gives UMR graphs that are not complete multipartite, and in particular, sparse ones. Our model in a nutshell is to find the probability that the graph is not only connected, but also that the well-studied game of Cops and Robber can be won by a single cop.

The game of
Cops and Robber
on graphs introduced in
[
17
]
and independently in
[
18
]
is a two player pursuit-evasion game where one player, a set of
k
-cops, attempts to capture the other, a robber. The game begins by placing each cop on a vertex of the graph. The robber is then placed on a vertex. The players then take turns moving to an adjacent vertex or remaining on their current vertex. The cops win if one can eventually occupy the same vertex as the robber and the robber wins if they can evade capture indefinitely. A graph
G
is
cop-win
if a single cop can always guarantee a win playing on
G
. Cops and Robber has been studied extensively since its first introduction, see
[
4
]
for an overview and
[
20
,
3
,
12
,
2
,
14
]
for some recent work on the game.

For us, the game serves as a stronger notion of reliability. Since node failures may leave the network more vulnerable to attack, the graph being cop-win serves as a proxy for a network where a malicious agent can be efficiently neutralized after node failures. As with node reliability, let
G
be a graph whose nodes are operational independently with probability
p
, for some
p\in[0,1]
and whose edges are operational if and only if both of their endpoints are operational. We introduce the
node cop-win reliability
of a graph to be defined as the probability that the operational vertices induce a cop-win graph. We let
\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
denote the node cop-win reliability of
G
. Let
W_{i}(G)
denote the number of induced cop-win subgraphs of
G
of order
i
. Then we can express
\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
as the following:

\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}(G)(1-p)^{n-i}p^{i}.

Constructing graphs that are UMR with respect to node cop-win reliability is the focus of this paper which will be laid out as follows. In Section
2
we present some preliminary results and definitions. These will be used in Section
3
to show that, in contrast to node reliability, there is a UMR graph with respect to node cop-win reliability among all unicyclic graphs of order
n\geq 5
. This is despite the fact that the UMR graph does not maximize
W_{i}(G)
for all
i
which is of note as all known UMR graphs with respect to node reliability were shown to also maximize
S_{i}(G)
for all
i
. Therefore, we introduce new methods for showing graphs are UMR involving the analytic properties of related polynomials. In Section
4
, we show that there is no UMR bicyclic graph with respect to node reliability, but that there is one for order
n\geq 7
with respect to node cop-win reliability. Our proofs in this case make extensive use of the notion of “pivoting” introduced by Stivaros
[
21
]
for computing the node reliability of a graph recursively. Pivoting has, for whatever reason, not been used to show any graphs are UMR since Stivaros did in 1990. Given the power of this method for inductive proofs of UMR graphs, we expect our methods will inspire further work in this area using pivoting techniques and our new analytic techniques. Finally, we conclude with some open problems for future research.

Note on Terminology:
We note that when node reliability was originally introduced by Stivaros
[
21
]
, it was referred to as the residual connectedness reliability of a network and uniformly most reliable graphs were called uniformly best.

2
Preliminaries

In this work all graphs considered are simple, finite, and undirected. The number of nodes (vertices) of the graph is the
order
of the graph. The
disjoint union
of two graphs
G
and
H
, denoted
G\cup H
is defined as the graph
(V(G)\cup V(H),E(G)\cup E(H))
. The disjoint union of
k
copies of a graph
G
is denoted
kG
. The
join
of
G
and
H
, denoted
G\vee H
, is obtained from
G\cup H
by adding the edges
uv
for all
u\in V(G)
and all
v\in V(H)
. For a graph
G
with
v\in V(G)
,
G-v
denotes the graph obtained by deleting
v
and all of its incident edges. The
closed neighbourhood
of
v
, denoted
N[v]
is the set
\{u\in V(G):uv\in E(G)\}\cup\{v\}
. The graph
G/v
is obtained from
G-v
by adding in all missing edges between any pair of vertices in
N[v]
. A
unicyclic
graph is a connected graph with
|V(G)|=|E(G)|
. A
bicyclic
graph is a connected graph with
|V(G)|+1=|E(G)|
.
For two polynomials
A(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}
and
B(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}b_{k}x^{k}
, we write
B(x)\preceq A(x)
if
m\leq n
and
b_{k}\leq a_{k}
for all
0\leq k\leq n
, i.e.
A(x)
is coefficient-wise greater than
B(x)
.

While there is an elegant structural characterization for cop-win graphs given in
[
17
]
, for our purposes it will be sufficient to use the result from
[
1
]
that connected chordal graphs are cop-win. A graph is
chordal
if it contains no induced cycles of order greater than
3
. There are graphs that are cop-win and not chordal (wheels for example) but it is straightforward to see that
C_{n}
is not cop-win for all
n\geq 4
. Chordal graphs are important for our study of node cop-win reliability because of the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)=\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
if and only if
G
is a chordal graph.

Proof.

Since all cop-win graphs are connected, it is clear that
S_{i}(G)\geq W_{i}(G)
for all
i
. If a graph
G
contains an induced cycle of order
j
, where
j\geq 4
, then we know that
S_{j}(G)>W_{j}(G)
and thus the coefficients
S_{j}(G)
and
W_{j}(G)
will not be equal. Therefore, if
G
contains any induced cycles of order
\geq 4
, then
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)\neq\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
. Furthermore, every induced connected subgraph of a chordal graph is also a connected chordal graph, and every connected chordal graph is cop-win
[
1
]
, therefore,
S_{i}(G)=W_{i}(G)
for all
i
and therefore,
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)=\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
.
∎

For our work on
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
and
\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)
it will be convenient to define two more closely related polynomials. The first was introduced in
[
8
]
and is called the
connected set polynomial
of a graph, denoted
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)
, defined by

\operatorname{CS}(G,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{k}(G)x^{k}.

The second is a new polynomial which we call the
cop-win polynomial
of a graph, denoted
\operatorname{CW}(G,x)
, defined by

\operatorname{CW}(G,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}W_{k}(G)x^{k}.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
n\geq 5
,

1)

\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(U_{n},x)=nx+nx^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=3}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}x^{k}
.

2)

\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(C_{n},x)-x^{n}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}nx^{k}
.

3)

\operatorname{CW}(A_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(C_{n},x)-x^{n}-x^{n-1}=nx+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n-2}(n+k-2)x^{k}+(n-1)x^{n-1}
.

4)

\operatorname{CW}(B_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)=nx+(n+1)x^{2}+\left(\binom{n-1}{2}+1\right)x^{3}+\sum\limits_{k=4}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}x^{k}
.

5)

\operatorname{CW}(F(n-5,0),x)=\operatorname{CS}(F(n-5,0),x)=nx+(n+1)x^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=3}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}x^{k}
.

Proof.

1)
U_{n}
is a chordal graph, so by Lemma
2.1
,
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(U_{n},x)
. Now every subset of
3
vertices that does not contain the universal vertex is isomorphic to
K_{2}\cup 2K_{1}
or
3K_{1}
and therefore disconnected. Further, every subset of
3
vertices that contains the universal vertex is clearly connected. Hence, for
k>2
,
S_{k}(U_{n})=\binom{n-1}{k-1}
.

2) Since any proper connected induced subgraph of
C_{n}
will be chordal, the only connected induced subgraph of
C_{n}
that is not cop-win is the graph itself. Therefore,
\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(C_{n},x)-x^{n}
and
\operatorname{CS}(C_{n},x)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}nx^{k}+x^{n}
from the proof of Theorem 3.3 in
[
8
]
.

3) For
3\leq i\leq n-2
,
S_{i}(A_{n})
is equal to
S_{i}(C_{n-1})=n-1
plus the number of connected sets of order
i
in
A_{n}
that contain the leaf. These sets are in one-to-one-correspondence with the connected sets of order
i-1
in
C_{n-1}
that contain the leaf’s neighbour. In turn, these connected sets are in one-to-one correspondence with the number of subpaths of order
n-2-(i-1)
of
P_{n-2}
. Now, from an identical argument to Lemma 2.3 in
[
10
]
, we have
\operatorname{CS}(A_{n},x)=nx+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n-2}(n+k-2)x^{k}+nx^{n-1}+x^{n}
. Finally, since
n\geq 5
, the only connected induced subgraphs of
A_{n}
that are not chordal are induced by the entire vertex set or by the entire vertex set of
C_{n-1}
. Hence,
\operatorname{CW}(A_{n},x)=\operatorname{CS}(A_{n},x)-x^{n-1}-x^{n}
.

4) and 5) are proved in a very similar manner to 1), so we omit the proofs in these cases.
∎

We can now introduce the key notion of “pivoting” that we will apply extensively throughout our work. The power of this result is that it allows for
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
to be computed recursively and therefore opens up the possibility of inductive proofs that graphs are UMR.

Theorem 2.3

(Pivoting Theorem
[
21
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n
and
v\in V(G)
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)=

\displaystyle(1-p)\operatorname{NRel}(G-v,p)+p\operatorname{NRel}(G/v,p)

\displaystyle-p\left((1-p)^{\deg(v)}\operatorname{NRel}(G-N[v],p)+(1-p)^{n-1}\right).

A straightforward corollary of this (either using the same counting argument or the transformation from
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)
to
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)
) is the following.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n
and
v\in V(G)
. Then

\operatorname{CS}(G,x)=\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)+x\left(\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)+1\right).

These results allow for the following lemma that will be applied extensively throughout our work on unicyclic and bicyclic graphs.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
and
H
be two graphs such that there exists
v\in V(G)
and
u\in V(H)
such that

1)

\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(H-u,x)
,

2)

\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(H/u,x)
, and

3)

\operatorname{CS}(H-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)
.

Then
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(H,x)
.

Proof.

Let
G
,
H
,
u
, and
v
be as in the hypothesis. Then by Corollary
2.4
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{CS}(H,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G,x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{CS}(H-u,x)+x\left(\operatorname{CS}(H/u,x)-\operatorname{CS}(H-N[u],x)+1\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)+x\left(\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)+1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{CS}(H-u,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)

\displaystyle+x\left(\operatorname{CS}(H/u,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\right)

\displaystyle+x\left(\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)-\operatorname{CS}(H-N[u],x)\right).

By assumption, each of
\operatorname{CS}(H-u,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)
,
x\left(\operatorname{CS}(H/u,x)-\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\right)
, and
x\left(\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)-\operatorname{CS}(H-N[u],x)\right)
has all nonnegative coefficients, so their sum does as well. Therefore,
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(H,x)
.
∎

3
Unicyclic Graphs

We are now ready to explore UMR unicyclic graphs with respect to node cop-win reliability. We begin with a lemma that will be helpful for some special cases.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
n\geq 5
and
3\leq k\leq n-2
,

\binom{n-1}{k-1}\geq n+k-2.

Proof.

It is well-known that the sequence
\binom{n-1}{1},\binom{n-2}{2},\ldots,\binom{n-1}{n-1}
is unimodal with mode at
\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
and
\binom{n-1}{k}=\binom{n-1}{n-1-k}
. Therefore, for

3\leq k\leq n-2
,

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{k-1}

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n-1}{n-3}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2n+4\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(since $n^{2}-7n+10\geq 0$ for all $n\geq 5$)}

\displaystyle\geq n+k-2.

∎

It is noted in
[
21
]
that there is no UMR unicyclic graph with respect to node reliability because
\operatorname{NRel}(U_{n},p)
will be largest for values of
p
close to
0
and
\operatorname{NRel}(C_{n},p)
will be largest for values of
p
close to
1
. For node cop-win reliability this issue is avoided, however we cannot prove that
\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)\preceq\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)
to show that
\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)\leq\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
as
W_{n-1}(C_{n})>W_{n-1}(U_{n})
. Therefore, we require a new analytic technique for showing one graph is more reliable than another.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
n\geq 5
,
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)>\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)
for all
p\in(0,1]
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},1)=1>0=\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},1)
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)-\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)
has no roots in the interval
(0,1]
to show that
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)>\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)
for all
p\in(0,1)
. We will look at the cop-win polynomial for our analysis. Note that

\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},p)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},p)=\left(1+p\right)^{n}\left(\operatorname{NCRel}\left(U_{n},\frac{p}{1+p}\right)-\operatorname{NCRel}\left(C_{n},\frac{p}{1+p}\right)\right).

Since
f(p)=\frac{p}{1+p}
is a Möbius transformation, it is a continuous bijection from
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
to
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
(see
[
8
,
6
]
for use of Möbius transformations for roots of graph polynomials and
[
11
]
for further details on Möbius transformations in general). It is also clear that
p\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
if and only if
\frac{p}{1+p}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
. Since
f(p)
is a continuous bijection, and
f(0)=0
and
f(\infty)=1
, it follows that
\frac{p}{1+p}\in(0,1)
if and only if
p>0
. Finally, for
p\neq-1
,
\frac{p}{1+p}
is a root of
\operatorname{NCRel}\left(U_{n},\frac{p}{1+p}\right)-\operatorname{NCRel}\left(C_{n},\frac{p}{1+p}\right)
if and only if
p
is a root of
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},p)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},p)
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},p)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},p)
has no positive real roots.

From Lemma
2.2
, we know that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)

\displaystyle=x^{n}+\sum_{k=3}^{n-1}\left(\binom{n-1}{k-1}-n\right)x^{k}.

(1)

It follows from Lemma
3.1
that the coefficient of
x^{k}
in
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)
is positive for
k=3,4,\ldots,n-2
and is equal to
-1
for
k=n-1
. Therefore, from (
1
), if
x\in(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)

\displaystyle>x^{n}+\left(\binom{n-1}{n-3}-n-1\right)x^{n-2}+\sum_{k=3}^{n-3}\left(\binom{n-1}{k-1}-n\right)x^{k}.

(2)

Now, every coefficient in (
2
) is positive, so it has no positive roots, and therefore,
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)>0
for all
x\in(0,1)
. Similarly, for all
x\in[1,\infty)
,

\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)\geq\sum_{k=3}^{n-2}\left(\binom{n-1}{k-1}-n\right)x^{k}>0.

Therefore,
\operatorname{CW}(U_{n},x)-\operatorname{CW}(C_{n},x)
has no roots in
(0,\infty)
, so
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)-\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)
has no roots in
(0,1)
. Hence,
\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)>\operatorname{NCRel}(C_{n},p)
for all
p\in(0,1]
.

∎

We will require one more lemma before we can show that
U_{n}
is UMR with respect to node cop-win reliability.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a unicyclic graph of order
n
such that
G\not\cong U_{n}
and
G\not\cong C_{n}
and
v\in V(G)
be a leaf. Then
G-N[v]
has at least two edges.

Proof.

Suppose by way of contradiction that
G
is a unicyclic graph of order
n
other than
U_{n}
and
C_{n}
such that
G-N[v]
has at most one edge for some leaf
v\in V(G)
. Let
u\in V(G)
be the neighbour of
v
. Since
v
is a leaf,
v
is incident to exactly one edge. Since
G
has
n
edges and
G-N[v]
has at most one edge, it follows that
u
has at least
n-2
neighbours other than
v
, so that
\deg(u)\geq n-1
. However, the only unicyclic graph with a universal vertex is
U_{n}
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G
be a unicyclic graph of order
n\geq 5
not isomorphic to
C_{n}
. Then
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(U_{n},x)
.

Proof.

If
G=A_{n}
, then the result follows immediately from Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
3.1
. So from now on, suppose
G
is not
A_{n}
. The proof proceeds by induction on
n
. For
n=5
, the result can readily be verified by comparing the two unicyclic graphs of order
5
other than
C_{n}
and
A_{n}
with
U_{n}
. Now suppose the result holds for unicyclic graphs of order
n-1
for some
5\leq n-1
. Let
G
be a unicyclic graph of order
n
that is not
C_{n}
nor
A_{n}
. Let
v
be a leaf of
G
(noting that one exists since
G
is not
C_{n}
). Now,
G-v=G/v
is a unicyclic graph of order
n-1
and is not equal to
C_{n-1}
since
G
is not
A_{n}
. Let
u
be a leaf of
U_{n}
. Note that
U_{n}-u=U_{n}/u=U_{n-1}
.
Thus,
\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(U_{n}-u,x)
and
\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(U_{n}/u,x)
by the inductive hypothesis. Further,
\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)=(n-2)x+2x^{2}+f(x)
where
f(x)
is a polynomial with positive coefficients since
G-N[v]
has at least two edges by Lemma
3.3
. Since
U_{n}-N[u]=K_{2}\cup\overline{K_{n-4}}
, it follows that
\operatorname{CS}(U_{n+1}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.5
,
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(U_{n+1},x)
.
∎

From the previous result, it follows that although
U_{n}
is not UMR with respect to node reliability, it is more reliable than every unicyclic graph other than
C_{n}
. We now have all necessary results to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
n\geq 5
,
U_{n}
is UMR with respect to node cop-win reliability among all unicyclic graphs of order
n
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 5
and
G
be a unicyclic graph of order
n
.

If
G\cong C_{n}
, then
\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)\leq\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
by Lemma
3.2
.

If
G\not\cong C_{n}
, then, by Theorem
3.4
,

\operatorname{NCRel}(G,p)\leq\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)\leq\operatorname{NRel}(U_{n},p)=\operatorname{NCRel}(U_{n},p)

for all
p\in[0,1]
.
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
n\geq 7
, if
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are nonnegative integers such that
n_{1}+2n_{2}=n-5
, then
\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2}),x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. For
n=7
, the result follows as can be checked via the Appendix. Suppose the result holds for all
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
where
n_{1}+2n_{2}=k-5
for all
7\leq k<n
. Now let
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
be two nonnegative integers such that
n_{1}+2n_{2}=n-5
.

If
n_{2}=0
, then
\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2}),x)=\operatorname{CS}(F(n-5,0),x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
by Lemma
2.2
.

If
n_{2}\neq 0
, then let
v
be a leaf of
F(n_{1},n_{2})
with a neighbour of degree
2
. Let
u
be a leaf of
B_{n}
. Now
F(n_{1},n_{2})-v=F(n_{1},n_{2})/v=F(n_{1}+1,n_{2}-1)
and
B_{n}-u=B_{n}/u=B_{n-1}
, so
\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2})-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-u,x)
and
\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2})/v,x)\preceq(B_{n}/u,x)
by the inductive hypothesis. Since
n\geq 7
,
F(0,0)
is an induced subgraph of
F(n_{1},n_{2})-N[v]
, so

\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)=(n-3)x+2x^{2}+x^{3}\preceq(n-3)x+6x^{2}+6x^{3}\preceq\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2})-N[v],x).

Finally, by Lemma
2.5
,
\operatorname{CS}(F(n_{1},n_{2}),x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a bicyclic graph of order
n
with at least one leaf such that
G
is not isomorphic to
F(n_{1},n_{2})
for any nonnegative integers
n_{1},n_{2}
such that
n_{1}+2n_{2}=n-5
. If
v
is a leaf of
G
and
u
is a leaf of
B_{n}
, then
\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)
.

Proof.

Let
G
,
v
, and
u
be as in the hypothesis. Let
w\in V(G)
be the unique neighbour of
v
and consider the graph
G-N[v]
. It is clear that
G-N[v]
and
B_{n}-N[u]
have the same order and since every connected set of order
3
induces a graph with at least
2
edges, it suffices to show that
G-N[v]
has at least one connected set of order
3
. Suppose
G-N[v]
has no connected sets of order
3
, i.e. that
G-N[v]=n_{2}K_{2}\cup n_{1}K_{1}
for some nonnegative integers
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
such that
n_{1}+2n_{2}=n-2
. Since
G
is a bicyclic graph, we must have that exactly two of the
K_{2}
components are joined to
w
. Now, the
K_{1}
components must have been leaves in
G
adjacent to
w
since
G
is a connected graph. Again since
G
is a connected graph, it follows that the
n_{2}-2
remaining
K_{2}
components of
G-N[v]
must have exactly one vertex adjacent to
w
. Therefore,
G=F(n_{1}+1,n_{2}-2)
, which contradicts our assumption. Therefore,
G-N[v]
has at least one connected set of order
3
and therefore,
\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)
.
∎

Note that the previous lemma also applies to Type 3 bicyclic graphs and will be used in the next subsection.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
n\geq 7
,
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
for all Type 1 and Type 2 bicyclic graphs
G
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. For
n=7
, the result follows by direct comparisons as can be checked in the Appendix. Now suppose the result holds for all bicyclic graphs of order
7\leq k<n
and let
G
be a bicyclic graph of order
n
.

Case 1:

G
is a Type 1 bicyclic graph and
G=G_{1}(a,b)
.

Since
n>7
, we may assume without loss of generality that
b\geq 4
. Let
v
be a vertex of degree
2
on
C_{b}
such that both of its neighbours also have degree
2
. Let
u
be a leaf of
B_{n}
. Now,
G-v
is a unicyclic graph of order
n-1
, not equal to
C_{n-1}
,
G/v=G_{1}(a,b-1)
, and
G-N[v]
is a unicyclic graph of order
n-3
. Also,
B_{n}-u=B_{n-1}
,
B_{n}/u=B_{n-1}
, and
B_{n}-N[u]=P_{3}\cup\overline{K_{n-5}}
. Since
\operatorname{CS}(U_{n},x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
from Lemma
2.2
and from Theorem
3.4
, we have
\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-u,x)
. From the inductive hypothesis,
\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}/u,x)
. Since
G-N[v]
is a unicyclic graph of order

,
G
has at least
5
edges and a cycle of length at least

, therefore
\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)+x
. Now, from a similar argument to the proof of Lemma

, it follows that
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)+x^{2}
. However, by definition,
G
and

have the same number of edges, so we can conclude that
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

Case 2:

G
is a Type 1 bicyclic graph and

.

If

, then
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)

.

If

be a leaf of

. Let
u
be a leaf of

, so
\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n-1},x)=\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-u,x)

\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)

. Hence Lemma
2.5
gives
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

Case 3:

G
is a Type 2 bicyclic graph and

.

If

, then let

be a vertex of degree

, it follows that

Further,

is a unicyclic graph of order

. Hence,
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
by Lemma
2.5
.

If

. Let

of

\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(U_{n-1}\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-u,x)

\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

Case 4:

is a Type 2 bicyclic graph and

Let

be a leaf of

. Let

be a leaf of

\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)

\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

∎

Theorem 4.5
.

If
G
is a Type 3 bicyclic graph of order
n\geq 7
with at least one leaf, then
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. For
n=7
, the result follows from a computational check (again, see Appendix). Now suppose
G
is a Type 3 bicyclic graph of order
n\geq 8
with at least one leaf. Let
v
be a leaf of
G
and
u
be a leaf of
B_{n}
.

We note that
\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-N[u],x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G-N[v],x)
by Lemma
4.2
.

If
G-v
again has a leaf, then
\operatorname{CS}(G-v,x)=\operatorname{CS}(G/v,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}/u,x)=\operatorname{CS}(B_{n}-u,x)
by the inductive hypothesis. Therefore,
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
by Lemma
2.5
.

If
G-v
has no leaves, then by Lemma
4.4
and a similar argument to Lemma
2.5
, it follows that
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)+x^{n-2}+x^{n-1}
. It is clear that
S_{n-1}(G)=n-1
as
G
has exactly one cut-vertex (the vertex adjacent to
v
). It is also clear that the cut-sets of order
2
correspond exactly to the independent sets of order
2
of the three paths,
P_{a+2},P_{b+2},
and
P_{c+2}
after subtracting
2
to account for triple counting the independent set common to each. Since a path of order
k
has
\binom{k}{2}-(k-1)=\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}
independent sets of order
2
, it follows that
G
has exactly

\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+2(a+b+c)-1}{2}

cut-sets of order
2
. Since
B_{n}
has exactly
n-1
cut-sets of order
2
, to show that
S_{n-2}(G)\leq S_{n-2}(B_{n})
, it suffices to show that

\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+2(a+b+c)-1}{2}\geq n-1.

Since
n\geq 8
, it follows that
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}\geq 3
. By adding
2n-5
to both sides of this inequality, then dividing both sides by
2
and substituting
a+b+c=n-2
, we obtain

\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+2(a+b+c)-1}{2}\geq n-1

as desired. Therefore,
S_{n-2}(G)\leq S_{n-2}(B_{n})
, which was the last remaining inequality to conclude that
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

There is no UMR bicyclic graph with respect to node reliability.

Proof.

The graph
G_{3}(a,b,c)
of order
n
has no cut-vertices, so
S_{n-1}(G_{3}(a,b,c))=n>S_{n-1}(B_{n})
. Therefore, for values of
p
close to
1
,
\operatorname{NRel}(G_{3}(a,b,c),p)>\operatorname{NRel}(B_{n},p)
, but the inequality is reversed for values of
p
close to
0
from Lemma
4.4
. Also, from Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.5
, it follows that
\operatorname{NRel}(G,p)\leq\operatorname{NRel}(B_{n},p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
and all bicyclic graphs
G
with
G\neq G_{2}(a,b,c)
. Therefore, there is no UMR bicyclic graph with respect to node reliability.
∎

We are now ready to move from node reliability to node cop-win reliability.

Lemma 4.7
.

If
G
is a unicyclic graph with an induced cycle
C
with
|V(C)|\geq 4
, then
G
is not cop-win

Proof.

Suppose that
G
has an induced cycle
C
with vertices labelled around the cycle
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots u_{k}
for some
k\geq 4
but that
G
is cop-win. Let the robber move only on the vertices of
C
. Since the cop can catch the robber, there must be a vertex
v\in V(G)-V(C)
such that
v
is adjacent to at least three consecutive vertices on the cycle, without loss of generality say
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
. But now,
\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},v\}
induces a connected subgraph of
G
with
4
vertices and
5
edges, contradicting
G
being a unicyclic graph. Therefore,
G
is not cop-win.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

If
G
is a bicyclic graph of order
n\geq 7
, then
\operatorname{CW}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CW}(B_{n},x)
.

Proof.

If
G
is a Type 1 or Type 2 bicyclic graph, then the result follows from Lemma
4.3
. If
G
is of Type 3 and has a leaf, then the result follows from Theorem
4.5
.

Otherwise,
G=G_{3}(a,b,c)
. If
n=7
, then the result follows by checking the Appendix. For
n\geq 8
, it follows from Lemma
4.4
that
\operatorname{CS}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)+x^{n-1}
. Now, since
n\geq 8
, it follows that at least one of
a
,
b
, or
c
is at least
2
. Without loss of generality, suppose
a\geq 2
. Further, since
G
is bicyclic it follows that at most one of
b
and
c
are equal to
0
. Without loss of generality, suppose
b>0
. Let
v
be a vertex of
G
on the induced
P_{b}
. It is clear that
G-v
is a unicyclic graph and therefore connected. Also, in
G-v
,
P_{a}
together with the two end vertices induce a cycle
C
with
|V(C)|\geq 4
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.7
, it follows that
G-v
is not cop-win. Therefore,
W_{n-1}(G)\leq S_{n-1}(G)-1
and so, by Lemma
4.4
,
\operatorname{CW}(G,x)\preceq\operatorname{CS}(G,x)-x^{n-1}\preceq\operatorname{CS}(B_{n},x)=\operatorname{CW}(B_{n},x)
.

∎

Corollary 4.9
.

For
n\geq 7
,
B_{n}
is UMR with respect to node cop-win reliability among all bicyclic graphs of order
n
.

Problem 1
.

Are there any families of graphs that have a UMR graph with respect to node

Our work was motivated by the lack of UMR graphs with respect to node reliability, but this could of course be reframed in a model where edges fail. Let

p\in[0,1]
. The
cop-win reliability
of a graph then is defined as the probability that the graph is cop-win.

Problem 2
.

Are there any families of graphs that have a UMR graph with respect to cop-win reliability?

As our work hinged on polynomials, it is natural to wonder about their analytic properties. For the node cop-win reliability, many questions have already been answered via work done on the node reliability of chordal graphs. For example, it follows from
