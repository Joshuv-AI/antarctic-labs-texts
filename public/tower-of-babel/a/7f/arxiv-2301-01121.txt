The Euler characteristic of the moduli space of graphs
Michael Borinsky and Karen Vogtmann

Abstract
. The moduli space of rank n n graphs, the outer automorphism group of the free group of rank n n and Kontsevich’s Lie graph complex have the same rational cohomology. We show that the associated Euler characteristic grows like − e − 1 / 4 ( n / e ) n / ( n log n ) 2 -e^{-1/4}\,(n/e)^{n}/(n\log n)^{2} as n n goes to infinity, and thereby prove that the total dimension of this cohomology grows rapidly with n n .



1.
Introduction

The moduli space
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
of finite metric graphs with fundamental group
F_{n}
was introduced in
[
17
]
as a tool for studying the group
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
of outer automorphisms of a free group. By the main result in that paper
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
is the quotient of a contractible space, called
Outer space
, on which
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
acts with finite stabilizers. Thus the homology of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
with trivial rational coefficients is equal to the homology of
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
.

Kontsevich showed in
[
22
,
23
]
that the homology of
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
can also be identified with the cohomology of his Lie graph complex, which can in turn be identified with the primitive part of the cohomology of the Lie algebra of symplectic derivations of a free Lie algebra (see
[
15
]
for a detailed exposition of Kontsevich’s results). In
[
5
]
Berglund and Madsen found this Lie algebra in a very different context, and proved that its cohomology is a sub-algebra of the cohomology of the block diffeomorphism group of even-dimensional products of spheres.
In more recent years, algebraic geometers have studied
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
as a
tropical analog
of the classical moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{n}
of smooth complex curves of genus
n
. The simplicial completion of Outer space descends to a natural compactification of
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
, which the tropical geometers have dubbed the
moduli space of tropical curves,
by analogy with the Deligne-Mumford compactification of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
13
]
).
In yet another context,
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
may be considered a natural parameter space for the
n
-loop contribution to certain Feynman amplitudes. This direction has been explored, for example, by Bloch, Berghoff and Kreimer
[
6
,
4
]
.

In this paper we prove a formula for the Euler characteristic of
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
, and then determine its asymptotic growth rate. The asymptotic result depends on our results in
[
10
]
, where we determined the asymptotic growth rate of the
rational
or
virtual
Euler characteristic
\chi({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
. This is a rational number closely related to the alternating sum of the Betti numbers, but which has better group-theoretic properties, making it easier to compute.
The rational Euler characteristic of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
coincides with the number Kontsevich referred to as the
orbifold Euler characteristic
of his Lie graph complex. The actual alternating sum of the Betti numbers is denoted
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
in this paper to distinguish it from
\chi({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
, and is called the
integral Euler characteristic
to conform with other terminology in the literature.

If we are primarily interested in the cohomology of the space
\mathcal{MG}_{n}
or (equivalently) the group
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
, then the number
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
is clearly more relevant. Brown
[
12
]
showed that the rational and integral Euler characteristics of a group
\Gamma
are closely related, namely
e(\Gamma)
can be calculated from the rational Euler characteristics of centralizers of finite-order elements by the formula

e(\Gamma)=\sum_{[\alpha]}\chi(C(\alpha)).

Here the sum is over representatives
\alpha
for the conjugacy classes of finite-order elements (including the identity), and
C(\alpha)
is the centralizer of
\alpha
.

The number
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
was calculated for
n\leq 11
by Morita, Sakasai and Suzuki
[
25
]
, using methods from symplectic representation theory. In the present paper we use Brown’s formula, results on centralizers from
[
24
]
, an adaptation of Joyal’s theory of species
[
20
]
and further development of the asymptotic methods of
[
10
]
to first give an effective formula for
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
and then to determine its asymptotic growth rate.

The effective formula is developed in Sections
2
-
3
and summarized in Theorem
3.13
. Based on it, we wrote a computer program to compute
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
for
n\leq 15
. The results are listed in Appendix
A
. Further optimizations of this program enabled the computation of the numbers
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
for all
n\leq 100

[
9
]
.

Section
4
is devoted to proving the following asymptotic result.

Theorem 1.1
.

The integral Euler characteristic
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
has the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))\sim-{e^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{(n\log n)^{2}}\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Here the notation

means that

.
In particular this verifies the fact, suggested by our results on the rational Euler characteristic in

and mapping class groups of punctured surfaces, both of which are often considered analogs of

, have no rational cohomology in their virtual cohomological dimension.

Comparing Theorem

with our results in

The ratio

[

[

).
∎

This solves Problem 6.5 of the paper
[

]
by Morita, Sakasai and Suzuki. More precise asymptotic statements and comments about the rate of convergence are given in Section

.

In his original paper
[
22
]
Kontsevich introduced
commutative
and
associative
graph complexes in addition to the Lie graph complex. The methods of the present paper can be modified to compute the Euler characteristics for both of these other graph complexes as well as to determine their asymptotic behavior (see
[
8
]
); they can also be used to do the same for other moduli spaces of graphs, such as colored graphs or graphs with leaves. As Kontsevich noted, the associative graph complex computes the homology of mapping class groups of punctured surfaces. Both the rational and integral Euler characteristics of these groups for a once-punctured surface were originally computed by Harer and Zagier
[
19
]
. They also computed the asymptotics and deduced the existence of lots of cohomology. Getzler and Kapranov partially extended this to surfaces with more than one puncture (without determining the asymptotics) as an application of their general theory of modular operads

, and we remark that our method of finding the generating function is similar to theirs.

In
[
22
]
Kontsevich also defined odd versions of his graph complexes, and noted that in the Lie case the primitive part of the homology computes the cohomology of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
with twisted coefficients
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
, where the twisting is given by composing the natural map from
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
to
{\operatorname{GL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
with the determinant map. This odd version of Lie graph homology occurs, for example, in the study of groups of homotopy equivalences of odd-dimensional products of spheres
[
27
]
. In a final section, Section

, we explain how to modify our results to compute the Euler characteristic of this odd Lie graph complex, which we denote

. The results also extend to the analysis of the asymptotics, and we find

Theorem 1.3
.

The ratio
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e^{\mathrm{odd}}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))/\chi({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))=e^{\frac{1}{4}}\approx 1.28.

2.
A first formula for
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))

2.1.
The rational Euler characteristic

As noted in the introduction, Brown’s theorem says we can compute
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
by adding up the rational Euler characteristics of centralizers of finite-order elements. One way to compute the rational Euler characteristic of a group
\Gamma
is to find a contractible cell complex
Y
on which
\Gamma
acts properly and cocompactly; the rational Euler characteristic
\chi(\Gamma)
is then given by the formula

\chi(\Gamma)=\sum_{[\sigma]}\frac{(-1)^{\dim(\sigma)}}{|{\operatorname{Stab}}(\sigma)|},

where we sum over all orbits
[\sigma]
of cells,
\sigma
is a representative from the orbit and
{\operatorname{Stab}}(\sigma)
is its stabilizer under the group action. Fortunately, we have such complexes
Y
for centralizers of finite-order elements of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
.

The entire group
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
centralizes the identity. Recall from
[
17
]
that
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
acts properly and cocompactly on the spine
K_{n}
of Outer space.
K_{n}
is a contractible cube complex with one
k
-dimensional cube for each equivalence class
[G,\Phi,g]
of triples
(G,\Phi,g)
, where

•

G
is a connected admissible graph,

•

\Phi
is a subforest of
G
with
k
edges and

•

g\colon F_{n}\to\pi_{1}(G)
is an isomorphism, called a
marking
.

Here by a
graph
we mean a CW-complex of dimension
0
or
1
, a
forest
is a graph without cycles, and a
subforest
of
G
is a subcomplex that is a forest and contains all of the vertices of
G
. We say a graph is of
rank

n
if its fundamental group has rank
n
. A graph is
admissible
if it has no isolated, univalent or bivalent vertices. Two triples
(G,\Phi,g)
and
(G^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime},g^{\prime})
are
equivalent
if there is a graph isomorphism
h\colon G\to G^{\prime}
sending
\Phi
to
\Phi^{\prime}
and inducing an isomorphism
h_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(G)\rightarrow\pi_{1}(G^{\prime})
, such that
g^{-1}h_{*}g=\id.
A pair
(G,\Phi)
consisting of an admissible graph
G
and a subforest
\Phi
will be called a
forested graph
.

The spine
K=K_{n}
is contractible, and the action of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
on
K
simply changes the marking
g
. Thus there is one orbit for each isomorphism class
[G,\Phi]
of connected forested graphs. The stabilizer of a cube
[G,\Phi,g]
is isomorphic to
{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
, the automorphisms of
G
that preserve
\Phi.
Thus,

\chi({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))=\chi(C(\id))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\mathrm{rank}(G)=n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{e(\Phi)}}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|},

where
e(\Phi)
is the number of edges in
\Phi
.

2.2.
The equivariant spine

In
[
24
]
an equivariant version of
K
was introduced, which can be used to study the centralizer of any finite order element of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
(in fact the centralizer of any finite-order subgroup). We briefly summarize the construction. A graph
G
is said to
realize
a finite-order automorphism
\alpha
if there is some marking
g\colon F_{n}\to\pi_{1}(G)
and automorphism
f_{\alpha}
of
G
such that
g^{-1}(f_{\alpha})_{*}g=\alpha.
Every finite-order element of
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})
can be realized on some admissible graph
G,

[
16
,
21
]
.
This translates to the statement that
the action of
\alpha
on
K
has at least one fixed point. The centralizer
C(\alpha)
acts on the entire fixed-point set
K_{\alpha}
of
\alpha
. It follows from
[
24
]
that this fixed point set is contractible, cocompact and has the structure of a cube complex. Specifically,
K_{\alpha}
has one cube for each equivalence class of triples
(G,\Phi,g)_{\alpha}
where
(G,g)
realizes
\alpha
and
\Phi
is a (possibly empty) forest in
G
that is invariant under the action of
\alpha
. Here
(G,\Phi,g)_{\alpha}
is equivalent to
(G^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime},g^{\prime})_{\alpha}
if there is an
\alpha
-invariant automorphism
h\colon G\to G^{\prime}
sending
\Phi
to
\Phi^{\prime}
such that
g^{-1}h_{*}g=\id
, and again we write
[G,\Phi,g]_{\alpha}
for the equivalence class. The dimension of the cube
[G,\Phi,g]_{\alpha}
is the number
e_{\alpha}(\Phi)
of edge-orbits in
\Phi
.

The stabilizer
{\operatorname{Stab}}[G,\Phi,g]_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to
{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\alpha}(G,\Phi),
the automorphisms of
G
that commute with the action of
\alpha
and send
\Phi
to itself, so

\chi(C(\alpha))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]_{\alpha}\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\mathrm{rank}(G)=n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\alpha}(G,\Phi)|},

where
[G,\Phi]_{\alpha}
runs over isomorphism classes of pairs that realize
\alpha
on a connected graph
G
of rank
n
.
Brown’s theorem
[
12
]
then gives

e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))=\sum_{[\alpha]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]_{\alpha}\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\mathrm{rank}(G)=n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\alpha}(G,\Phi)|},

where
[\alpha]
runs over conjugacy classes of finite-order elements
\alpha
.

The group
{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
acts on itself by conjugation, so the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives

|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|=|(\hbox{orbit of }\alpha)|\cdot|{\operatorname{Stab}}_{{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}(\alpha)|
. Since
{\operatorname{Stab}}_{{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}(\alpha)={\operatorname{Aut}}_{\alpha}(G,\Phi)
this gives:

Theorem 2.1
.

e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\mathrm{rank}(G)=n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|}\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)},

where we sum over the set of isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
of connected forested graphs of rank
n
.

2.3.
Disconnected graphs

Recall that the Euler characteristic of a connected graph of rank
n
is
\chi(G)=1-n
.
The graph’s Euler characteristic is often better behaved than its rank.
For instance, the formula in Theorem
2.1
is easier to work with if we drop the requirement that
G
be connected and shift the index by one, i.e. define

(1)

\displaystyle\ee_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|}\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)},

where we sum over all isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
of (not necessarily connected) forested graphs with
\chi(G)=-n
.
In this subsection we show how to recover
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
once the numbers
\ee_{n}
are known.

We begin by deriving new formulas for
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
and
\ee_{n}
.
Define a forested graph
(G,\Phi)
to be
even
if
G
has no automorphisms that induce an odd permutation of the edges of
\Phi
.

Proposition 2.2
.

If we sum over all isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
—

(1)

— of even forested graphs with
\chi(G)=-n
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\,\mathrm{even}\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e(\Phi)}=\ee_{n}.

(2)

— of
connected
even forested graphs with
\chi(G)=-n
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\,\mathrm{even}\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e(\Phi)}=e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1})).

Remark 2.3
.

Conant and Vogtmann showed in
[
15
]
that Kontsevich’s Lie graph complex, as defined in
[
22
]
is quasi-isomorphic to the complex spanned by even forested graphs. Thus this proposition shows that
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
is equal to the Euler characteristic of the Lie graph complex.

Proposition
2.2
is an immediate consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

The sum

\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}

vanishes if
(G,\Phi)
has an automorphism that induces an odd permutation on
\Phi
and is equal to

(-1)^{e(\Phi)}|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|
otherwise.

Proof.

An element
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
induces a permutation
\alpha_{\Phi}\in\SG_{e(\Phi)}
on the
set of edges of
\Phi
. By definition, the number
e_{\alpha}(\Phi)
is equal to the number of cycles in the cycle decomposition of
\alpha_{\Phi}
.
The sign of a permutation is the parity of the number of its even cycles. Since the parity of the number of
odd cycles of a permutation on an
n
-element set is equal to the parity of
n
, we have
(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}={\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})(-1)^{e(\Phi)}
.
The sign function gives a homomorphism from
{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
to the cyclic group of order 2, which is surjective if and only if
{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
contains an odd permutation. If it is surjective, then half of the elements of
{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
have each sign, so

\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})=0;
otherwise
\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})=(-1)^{e(\Phi)}|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Use Lemma
2.4
, Eq. (
1
) and
Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Each pair consisting of a forested graph and an automorphism contributes to the sum in the definition of
\ee_{n}
,
so evaluating this sum means doing a weighted count of forested graphs and their automorphisms. We will do this counting by means of generating functions, i.e. formal power series whose coefficients encode the counts we are interested in.

We can also use formal power series to describe the relation between
\ee_{n}
and
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
.
By standard topological quantum field theory convention, we use the symbol
\hbar
as the formal variable that marks the negative Euler characteristic of the graphs.

Theorem 2.5
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee_{n}\hbar^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-\hbar^{n}}\right)^{e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))}.

Proof.

For any (possibly disconnected) admissible graph
G
and subforest
\Phi\subset G
, set
\theta(G,\Phi)=(-1)^{e(\Phi)}\hbar^{-\chi(G)}
. Since an admissible graph is either trivial or has strictly negative Euler characteristic,
\theta(G,\Phi)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[[\hbar]]
.
The function
\theta
factors over connected components, i.e.

\theta((G_{1},\Phi_{1})\sqcup(G_{2},\Phi_{2}))=\theta(G_{1},\Phi_{1})\theta(G_{2},\Phi_{2}).

By Proposition
2.2
(1),

\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee_{n}\hbar^{n}=\sum_{[G,\Phi]\,\text{even}}\theta(G,\Phi),

where we sum over all isomorphism classes of (possibly disconnected) even forested graphs
[G,\Phi]
.

Each isomorphism class
[G,\Phi]
can be described by giving a set of isomorphism classes of connected graphs together with the multiplicity with which each connected class appears in the disconnected class. In the sum above, a component
[g,\varphi]
of
[G,\Phi]
such that
\varphi
has an odd number of edges can appear at most once, as otherwise
[G,\Phi]
would have an odd automorphism. Components with an even number of edges in the forest can appear with any multiplicity. The sum of the function
\theta
over all even admissible forested graphs hence satisfies the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee_{n}\hbar^{n}=\sum_{[G,\Phi]\,\text{even}}\theta(G,\Phi)=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
e(\varphi)\,\text{odd}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\theta(g,\varphi)\right)\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
e(\varphi)\,\text{even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{m\geq 0}\theta(g,\varphi)^{m}\right),

where the
[g,\varphi]
are isomorphism classes of connected forested graphs and
e(\varphi)
denotes the number of edges of
\varphi
.

Using the fact that
\theta(g,\varphi)
is
-\hbar^{-\chi(g)}
if
[g,\varphi]
is odd and
\hbar^{-\chi(g)}
if
[g,\varphi]
is even and evaluating the sum over
m
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee_{n}\hbar^{n}=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
e(\varphi)\,\text{odd}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\hbar^{-\chi(g)}\right)\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
e(\varphi)\,\text{even}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1-\hbar^{-\chi(g)}}\right)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-\hbar^{n})^{\beta_{\text{odd}}^{n}}}{(1-\hbar^{n})^{\beta_{\text{even}}^{n}}},

where
\beta_{\text{odd}}^{n}
and
\beta_{\text{even}}^{n}
are
the numbers of connected forested admissible graphs without odd edge-automorphisms
with Euler characteristic
-n
with an odd or even number of edges in the forest.
A connected graph with Euler characteristic
-n
has rank
n+1
. Hence, by Proposition
2.2
(2),
\beta_{\text{even}}^{n}-\beta_{\text{odd}}^{n}=e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
.
∎

We can now explain how to calculate the numbers
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
recursively from the numbers
\ee_{n}
. Recall that the classical
Möbius function

\mu
is defined recursively for positive integers by

\mu(1)=1
and
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=0
for all
n\geq 2
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\sum_{n\geq 1}\eee_{n}\hbar^{n}=\log f(\hbar)
be the logarithm of the series
f(\hbar)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee_{n}\hbar^{n}
.
The numbers
\eee_{n}
for
n\geq 1
are given recursively by

\displaystyle\eee_{n}=\ee_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k\eee_{k}\ee_{n-k}

and
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
for
n\geq 1
by

\displaystyle e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))=\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\eee_{n/d}.

Proof.

The recursive expression for
\eee_{n}
in terms of
\ee_{n}
follows by taking the derivative of
\log f(x)
with respect to
\hbar
and using
(\log f(x))^{\prime}={f^{\prime}(x)/f(x)}
. Taking the logarithm of the statement of Theorem
2.5
, using
\log(1/(1-x))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x^{n}/n
and the definition of the Möbius function gives the second formula.
∎

3.
An effective formula for
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))

3.1.
Admissible trees

In the last section we reduced the problem of computing
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
to the problem of doing a weighted count of forested graphs. In order to count forested graphs we will first count trees, then forests, then ways of matching the leaves of the forests to form forested graphs. Throughout the rest of the paper we fix the following conventions and terminology. By a

tree
we mean a connected graph with no cycles. A
forest
is a disjoint union of trees. We give univalent vertices of trees and forests a special role and call them
leaves
; other vertices are called
internal vertices
. A tree or forest is
admissible
if it has no isolated or bivalent vertices.
A
rooted tree
is a tree where one univalent vertex is distinguished as the
root
while the other univalent vertices are still called leaves. We require admissible trees to have either a root or at least one internal vertex, so a single 1-cell which connects two non-root leaves is not allowed. The
internal edges
of a tree or forest are the
1
-cells that are not attached to leaves or the root.
Our trees, rooted trees, and forests will be
labeled
. That means each leaf shall be decorated with a unique element from some given finite set
U
.

The set of all admissible labeled (or labeled rooted) trees forms a combinatorial species in the sense of Joyal
[
20
]
(see also
[
3
]
).
Since our counting problems fit neatly into the theory of species, we review the relevant parts of this theory in the next subsection.

3.2.
Species and generating functions

A
combinatorial species
is a functor from the groupoid of finite sets to itself. More explicitly, a species
\mathcal{S}
associates to every finite set
U
of
labels
, a finite set
\mathcal{S}[U]
of
combinatorial objects
such that every bijection
U\rightarrow V
gives rise to a bijection of sets
\mathcal{S}[U]\rightarrow\mathcal{S}[V]
in a way compatible with composition. In the case
U=\{1,\ldots,n\}
we write
\mathcal{S}[n]
for
\mathcal{S}[U]
. An element of
\mathcal{S}[U]
for some
U
is an
\mathcal{S}
-object
, or more informally, an object of
\mathcal{S}
.

For every species
\mathcal{S}
there is a natural action of
\SG_{n}
on
\mathcal{S}[n]
that permutes the labels.
The orbit of an element
\phi\in\mathcal{S}[n]
under this action is an isomorphism class of combinatorial objects.
We will also call such a class an
unlabeled object
.
The
stabilizer
of an element
\phi\in\mathcal{S}[n]
is the group of relabelings that leaves the object invariant. We will denote this stabilizer by
{\operatorname{Aut}}(\phi)
.

A
set partition
of
U
with
k

blocks
is a collection
\pi=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
of
k
mutually disjoint subsets of
U
such that
\bigcup_{B\in\pi}B=U
.
From a given species
\mathcal{S}
we can construct a new species
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})
by associating to a set
U
all collections of objects
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\}
such that
\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{S}[B_{1}],\ldots,\phi_{k}\in\mathcal{S}[B_{k}]
for some partition of
U
into blocks
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
.
The functor
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})
is the species of sets of size
k
which contain objects of
\mathcal{S}
.
To make the formulas more compact we agree that
{\operatorname{Set}}^{0}(\mathcal{S})[0]
contains one element, the empty set, and that

{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[0]
is empty for all
k\geq 1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be an algebra and
\omega:\mathcal{S}[U]\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
a
weight function
that associates some element of
\mathbb{A}
to each object of
\mathcal{S}[U]
in a way that is independent of the labeling. We can extend the weight function
\omega
to
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})
by setting the weight of the empty set to
1
and
\omega(\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\omega(\phi_{\ell})
.

When working with a formal power series
F(x)=\sum a_{i}x^{i}
, we will frequently use the
coefficient extraction operator

[x^{n}]
to extract the coefficient of
x^{n}
, so
[x^{n}]F(x)=a_{n}
.
We now define two
formal power series, in
\mathbb{A}[[x]]
and
\mathbb{A}[[x,y]]
respectively, by

(2)

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}[n]}\omega(\phi)

and

\displaystyle S_{{\operatorname{Set}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,k\geq 0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}y^{k}\sum_{\Phi\in{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]}\omega(\Phi).

These are exponential generating functions for weighted counts:
[x^{n}]S(x)
is
{1/n!}
times the number of objects
\phi\in\mathcal{S}[n]
, counted with weight
\omega(\phi)
, and
[x^{n}y^{k}]S(x,y)
is
{1/n!}
times the number of elements
\Phi\in{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]
, counted with weight
\omega(\Phi)
.
We will make frequent use of the following
exponential formula
, a standard lemma that relates the power series
S
and
S_{\operatorname{Set}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

\displaystyle S_{{\operatorname{Set}}}(x,y)=\exp\left(y\,S(x)\right).

Proof.

Use the expansion
\exp(X)=\sum_{n\geq 0}X^{n}/n!
and the fact that the number of set partitions of a set of cardinality
n
into
k
blocks with sizes
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
is given by

{n!/(k!m_{1}!\ldots m_{k}!)}
.
∎

We next want to count labeled combinatorial objects while keeping track of automorphisms. For this, we will need more sophisticated generating functions, called
cycle index series
. The terminology comes from the notion of cycle type for a permutation, which we now review.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer. An
integer partition
of
n
is a sequence of positive integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{\ell}>0
and
n=\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{\ell}
. The
\lambda_{i}
are called
parts
of
\lambda
. We write
\lambda\vdash n
or
|\lambda|=n
. The integer
n
is the
size
of the partition and
\ell
, the number of parts, is the
length
of the partition. We will often use the more compact notation
\lambda=[1^{m_{1}}\cdots n^{m_{n}}],
indicating that
\lambda
has
m_{k}
parts of size
k
(the terms with
m_{i}=0
are usually omitted; for example,
\lambda=(4,4,1,1,1)
is written
[1^{3}4^{2}]
). In this notation the length of
\lambda
is
\ell(\lambda)=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{\ell}
, the size is
|\lambda|=m_{1}+2m_{2}+\cdots+nm_{n}
. Each permutation
\pi\in\SG_{n}
factors uniquely as a product of disjoint cycles.
If the orders of these cycles are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}
, where
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{\ell}
, then
\lambda(\pi)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
is a partition of
n
called the
cycle type
of
\pi
.

Given an integer partition
\lambda=[1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\ldots n^{m_{n}}]
let
x^{\lambda}
denote the monomial
x_{1}^{m_{1}}x_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{n}}
. A formal power series in the variables
{\overline{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
is an infinite sum of terms
a_{\lambda}x^{\lambda}
, with
a_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{A}
, such that, for all
n
, we only have a finite number of terms if restrict to partitions with
|\lambda|\leq n
. If
\alpha
is a permutation of cycle type
\lambda
we also define
x^{\alpha}=x^{\lambda}
.

For a given species
\mathcal{S}
, let
\mathcal{AS}
be the species of pairs
(\phi,\alpha)
, where
\phi
is an object of
\mathcal{S}
and
\alpha
an automorphism of
\phi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{AS}[n]=

\displaystyle\{(\phi,\alpha)|\phi\in\mathcal{S}[n]\text{ and }\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\phi)\leq\mathfrak{S}_{n}\}.

Let
\omega
be a weight function that attaches to an object
(\phi,\alpha)
of
\mathcal{AS}
an element
\omega(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathbb{A}
that does not depend on the labeling. The
cycle index series for
\mathcal{S}
weighted by
\omega
is the formal power series
\mathbf{S}
in
{\overline{x}}
whose terms mark the cycle type of the automorphism, i.e.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}})=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AS}[n]}\omega(\phi,\alpha)\,x^{\alpha}.

In other words,
[x^{\lambda}]{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}})
is
{1/|\lambda|!}
times the weighted count of pairs
(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AS}
such that
\alpha
has cycle type
\lambda
.
The cycle index series
{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}})
generalizes the generating function of labeled objects in Eq. (
2
), as we recover

S(x)
by setting
\omega(\phi,\id)=\omega(\phi)
,
x_{1}=x
and all other
x_{k}
-variables to
0
.

We now want to extend our cycle index series on
\mathcal{S}
to one for
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})
, and show how to compute it from
{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}})
. An element
\Phi\in{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]
is a set of
k
elements of
\mathcal{S}
with a total of
n
labels. An element
\gamma\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)
permutes the labels but preserves the set. If some elements of
\mathcal{S}
are isomorphic then
\gamma
may permute them, so
\gamma
induces a permutation
\gamma_{\Phi}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. We introduce a new infinite set of variables
{\overline{y}}=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}
to mark the cycle type of
\gamma_{\Phi}
, and define

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{\operatorname{Set}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\sum_{n,k\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in{\mathcal{A}}{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]}\omega(\Phi,\gamma)\,x^{\gamma}y^{\gamma_{\Phi}}.

Here
\omega(\Phi,\gamma)=\omega(\phi_{1},\gamma_{1})\ldots\omega(\phi_{\ell},\gamma_{\ell})
, where

•

\gamma_{\Phi}
has cycle type
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
of length
\ell

•

\phi_{i}\in\Phi
is a representative of the
i
th cycle, which has size
\lambda_{i}

•

\gamma_{i}
is the restriction of
\gamma^{\lambda_{i}}
to
\phi_{i}.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})
be the series obtained from
{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}})
by replacing each occurrence of
x_{i}
by
x_{ki}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{\operatorname{Set}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}y_{k}\frac{{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}\right),

Ultimately, this proposition goes back to Pólya
[
26
]
.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
combined with the generating function for
wreaths
of
\mathcal{S}
structures.
We give a proof below, but refer to
[
3
]
Chapter 4.3 for a more detailed argument, using a slightly different type of weight function
\omega
.

To prove Proposition
3.2
we first introduce a new species

{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}\mathcal{S}
. An element of
{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}\mathcal{S}[n]
is a pair
(\Phi,\gamma)
where

\Phi=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\}
is a collection of objects in
\mathcal{S}
with a total of
n
labels, and
\gamma\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)
is a permutation of the labels of
\Phi
that cyclically permutes the
\phi_{i}
, i.e.
\gamma_{\Phi}
is a
k
-cycle in
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. In particular, all of the
\phi_{i}
must be isomorphic, i.e. equivalent as unlabeled objects.

Lemma 3.3
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]}\omega(\Phi,\gamma)x^{\gamma}=\frac{{\mathbf{S}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}.

Proof.

Let
(\Phi,\gamma)
be an element of
{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}\mathcal{S}[n]
. We claim that
(\Phi,\gamma)
is equivalent to
a tuple
(\kappa,\pi,\phi,\alpha,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k-1}),
where

•

\kappa
is a
k
-cycle in
\mathfrak{S}_{k},

•

\pi
is a partition of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
k
blocks, each of size
d
,

•

\phi
is an object of
\mathcal{S}[d],

•

\alpha
is an element of
{\operatorname{Aut}}(\phi),
and

•

\gamma_{i}
is an element of
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k-1.

By definition
\Phi=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\},
where
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{S}[B_{i}]
for some
B_{i}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\gamma_{\Phi}
is a
k
-cycle, so we take
\kappa=\gamma_{\Phi}
and
\pi=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
. Since
\gamma
acts cyclically on
\Phi
, all of the
\phi_{i}
are isomorphic, so in particular they all have the same number
d
of labels. The unique order-preserving bijection
B_{i}\to\{1,\ldots,d\}
identifies each
\phi_{i}
with an element
\widehat{\phi}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}[d]
; set
\phi=\widehat{\phi}_{k}
.
The permutation
\gamma^{k}
sends each
\phi_{i}
to itself, so determines an automorphism
\alpha_{i}
of
\phi_{i}
and therefore of
\widehat{\phi}_{i}
; set
\alpha=\alpha_{k}
. Note that all of the
\alpha_{i}
have the same cycle type
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
, so the cycle type of
\gamma
is
k\lambda=(k\lambda_{1},\ldots,k\lambda_{\ell})
. For each
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, the
i
th power of the
k
-cycle
\gamma_{\Phi}
maps
\phi_{k}
to
\phi_{\gamma^{i}_{\Phi}(k)}
; let
\gamma_{i}
be the induced isomorphism
\gamma_{i}\colon\widehat{\phi}_{k}\to\widehat{\phi}_{\gamma^{i}_{\Phi}(k)}
in
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.

Finally, note that
\omega(\Phi,\gamma)=\omega(\phi_{k},\gamma^{k})=\omega(\phi,\alpha)
. Since
{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})[n]
is empty unless
k|n
, we can now write the left hand side of the equation in the statement of the lemma as,

(3)

\sum_{n=dk\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}C_{n,k}\sum_{(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AS}[d]}\omega(\phi,\alpha)x^{k\circ\alpha},

where
k\circ\alpha
has cycle type
k\lambda
and

C_{n,k}
is the number of partitions
\pi
of
n
into
k
equal parts times the number of cyclic permutations
\kappa
in
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
times the number of permutations
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k-1}
in
(\mathfrak{S}_{d})^{k-1}
, i.e.

C_{n,k}=\frac{1}{k!}\frac{n!}{(d!)^{k}}\cdot(k-1)!\cdot(d!)^{k-1}=\frac{n!}{d!k}.

Plugging this into Eq. (
3
) gives the statement of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}(\mathcal{S})[n]=

\displaystyle\{(\Phi,\gamma)|\Phi\text{ is a finite set of objects of $\mathcal{S}$ with a total of $n$ distinct labels, }

\displaystyle\text{ and }\gamma\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)\leq\mathfrak{S}_{n}\}.

Since every permutation
\gamma
can be uniquely decomposed into a product of cycles, we have an isomorphism of species

\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}(\mathcal{S})\cong\bigcup_{\ell\geq 1}{\operatorname{Set}}^{\ell}\left(\bigcup_{k\geq 1}{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})\right).

Therefore the statement follows from an application of Lemma
3.1
to the right hand side, using the
generating function of the species
{\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Cyc}}}^{k}(\mathcal{S})
given by
Lemma
3.3
and summation over all
k
, using the variables
{\overline{y}}
to keep track of the cycle
type of the permutation.
∎

3.3.
Matchings

In order to form a forested graph with an automorphism preserving the forest, we will start with a forest equipped with an automorphism
\alpha
, then pair its leaves by a fixed-point free involution that commutes with
\alpha
. In this subsection we
apply the counting methods from the previous subsection to count the number of such involutions.

A fixed-point free involution is also called a
matching
; it divides the set into orbits of size
2
, so we will use the species
\mathcal{E}
of sets of cardinality
2
, i.e.
\mathcal{E}[2]=\{1,2\}
and
\mathcal{E}[n]=\emptyset
for
n\neq 2
. The generating function (with trivial weight) is
E(x)={x^{2}/2}
.
Matchings on
a set of
2k
elements correspond to elements of
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{E})[2k]
; for example the elements of
{\operatorname{Set}}^{2}\mathcal{E}[4]
are

\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\},\{\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\}
and
\{\{1,4\},\{2,3\}\}
.
By Lemma
3.1
we have

\displaystyle E_{{\operatorname{Set}}}(x,y)=\exp\left(y\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(2k-1)!!\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}y^{k},

where the second equality is obtained using the expansion
\exp(X)=\sum_{n\geq 0}{X^{n}/n!}
and the formula
(2k-1)!!={(2k)!/(k!2^{k})}.
Thus we recover the (easy) fact that the number of
matchings of a set of cardinality
2k
is
(2k-1)!!
, and there are none if the cardinality is odd.

Now consider the species
\mathcal{AE}=\mathcal{AE}[2]
of sets of cardinality
2
with automorphisms on them. A set of cardinality
2
has only two automorphisms, the trivial one (marked by
x_{1}x_{1}=x_{1}^{2}
) and the transposition (marked by
x_{2}
). The cycle index series of
\mathcal{E}
with trivial weight is therefore

{\mathbf{E}}({\overline{x}})=\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AE}[2]}x^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}\right).

A matching of
2k
elements corresponds to an element
\Phi\in{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{E})[2k]
. The automorphisms of such an element
\Phi
are permutations that commute with the corresponding fixed-point free involution
\iota=\iota_{\Phi}
. We may count such permutations using Proposition
3.2
, which gives

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{{\operatorname{Set}}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{y_{k}}{2k}\left(x_{k}^{2}+x_{2k}\right)\right).

Proposition 3.4
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{(\iota,\alpha)}x^{\alpha}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{2k}\left(x_{k}^{2}+x_{2k}\right)\right),

where the sum is over all pairs
(\iota,\alpha)
consisting of a matching
\iota
of
\{1,\ldots,2n\}
and a permutation
\alpha\in\SG_{2n}
that commutes with
\iota
.

Proof.

By definition we have

{\mathbf{E}}_{{\operatorname{Set}}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\sum_{n,k\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{E})[n]}x^{\alpha}y^{\alpha_{\phi}}.

Since
{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}\mathcal{E}[n]
is empty unless
n
is even and
k=n/2
we can rewrite this as

{\mathbf{E}}_{{\operatorname{Set}}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{(\Phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}^{n}\mathcal{E}[2n]}x^{\alpha}y^{\alpha_{\phi}}.

As noted previously, we may identify the pairs
(\iota,\alpha)
in the statement of the proposition with elements
(\Phi,\alpha)
of
\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}^{n}(\mathcal{E})[2n]
. Using Eq. (
4
) and setting
y_{k}=1
for all
k
gives the result.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

The number
\eta_{\lambda}
of matchings that commute with a given permutation
\alpha\in\SG_{n}
of cycle type
\lambda=[1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\ldots n^{m_{n}}]
is given by the formula

\eta_{\lambda}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k,m_{k}},

where

\eta_{k,2s}=\begin{cases}k^{s}(2s-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
\sum_{r=0}^{s}\binom{2s}{2r}k^{r}(2r-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is even}\end{cases}

and

\eta_{k,2s+1}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
\sum_{r=0}^{s}\binom{2s+1}{2r}k^{r}(2r-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is even}\end{cases}.

Remark 3.6
.

This statement can also be proved without the theory of species by elementary combinatorial means. We have used species here as an easy example of the method, which we use heavily in the next sections.
Moreover, the extension to the analogous Corollary
5.8
is done effortlessly using species.

Proof.

Since
\iota
commutes with
\alpha
if and only if
\alpha
commutes with
\iota
, the left hand side of Proposition
3.4
can be written

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{\alpha\in\SG_{2n}}\#\{\iota\in\SG_{2n}|\iota\circ\alpha=\alpha\circ\iota\}x^{\alpha}.

Now note that the number of matchings that commute with
\alpha
depends only on the cycle type of
\alpha
. If we denote the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with cycle type
\lambda
by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{\lambda}
, the formula becomes

=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{\lambda\vdash 2n}|\SG_{2n}^{\lambda}|\eta_{\lambda}x^{\lambda}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}|\SG_{n}^{\lambda}|\eta_{\lambda}x^{\lambda},

where we used the fact that
\eta_{\lambda}=0
if
|\lambda|
is odd. For
\lambda=[1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\ldots n^{m_{n}}]
it is easy to check that the number of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{\lambda}
is

|\SG_{n}^{\lambda}|=\frac{n!}{1^{m_{1}}m_{1}!2^{m_{2}}m_{2}!\cdots n^{m_{n}}m_{n}!},

so the left hand side of Proposition
3.4
is equal to

(5)

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{\eta_{\lambda}}{1^{m_{1}}m_{1}!2^{m_{2}}m_{2}!\cdots n^{m_{n}}m_{n}!}x^{\lambda}.

We now turn to the right hand side of the equation stated in Proposition
3.4
. Using
e^{X}=\sum_{s\geq 0}X^{s}/s!
,
(2s-1)!!={(2s)!/(s!2^{s})}
and
(x+2)^{s}=\sum_{r=0}^{s}\binom{s}{r}2^{s-r}x^{r}
and the definition of the numbers
\eta_{k,m}
above, we find that for even
k
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{x^{2s}}{2^{s}k^{s}s!}=\sum_{s\geq 0}k^{s}(2s-1)!!\frac{x^{2s}}{k^{2s}(2s)!}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\eta_{k,m}\frac{x^{m}}{k^{m}m!}

and for
k
odd, we have

\displaystyle{\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}+2x}{2k}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{x^{s}(x+2)^{s}}{2^{s}k^{s}s!}=\sum_{s\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}\binom{s}{r}2^{-r}\frac{x^{s+r}}{k^{s}s!}=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{s\geq r}\frac{1}{r!(s-r)!}2^{-r}\frac{x^{s+r}}{k^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{s\geq 2r}\frac{1}{r!(s-2r)!}2^{-r}\frac{x^{s}}{k^{s-r}}=\sum_{s\geq 0}\sum_{s/2\geq r\geq 0}\frac{s!}{(2r)!(s-2r)!}k^{r}\frac{(2r)!}{2^{r}r!}\frac{x^{s}}{k^{s}s!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\eta_{k,m}\frac{x^{m}}{k^{m}m!}.

Using these two equations together with the fact that

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{2k}\left(x_{k}^{2}+x_{2k}\right)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{x_{2k-1}^{2}}{2(2k-1)}+\frac{x_{2k}^{2}+2x_{2k}}{4k}\right)

the right hand side of Proposition
3.4
becomes

(6)

\prod_{k\geq 1}\left(\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\eta_{k,m}}{k^{m}m!}x_{k}^{m}\right).

Equating coefficients of (
5
) and (
6
) now gives the proposition.
∎

3.4.

Figure 1.
All admissible rooted trees with
3
leaves, i.e. all elements of
\mathcal{R}[3]
. The first three rooted trees have two internal vertices each and the fourth has one internal vertex. Internal edges are colored in blue. The automorphism groups of the first, second and third rooted tree are generated by the transpositions
(2,3)
,
(1,3)
and
(1,2)
respectively. The automorphism group of the fourth rooted tree is the full symmetric group
\SG_{3}
, i.e. it includes all permutations of the labels.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the species of leaf-labeled admissible rooted trees
\rho
, i.e. all internal vertices of
\rho
must have valence at least
3
and

\mathcal{R}[n]
is the set of all such rooted trees with
n
leaves. The elements of
\mathcal{R}[3]
are depicted in Figure
1
.
The rooted tree
\rho_{0}
with one root, one leaf and one 1-cell has no internal vertices so satisfies the definition, but it plays a special role so we call it the
special rooted tree
.
\mathcal{AR}
is then the species of pairs
(\rho,\alpha)
, where
\rho\in\mathcal{R}
and
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\rho).

Recall that an automorphism of a tree is determined by what it does to the leaves. So, for a rooted tree
\rho\in\mathcal{R}[n]
we can (and will) identify the group
{\operatorname{Aut}}(\rho)\leq\SG_{n}
with the usual simplicial automorphisms of the tree
\rho
.
To each pair
(\rho,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AR}
we assign the weight
\omega(\rho,\alpha)=(-1)^{v_{\alpha}(\rho)},
where
v_{\alpha}(\rho)
is the number of
\alpha
-orbits of internal vertices. The special rooted tree
\rho_{0}
has only the identity automorphism, and the pair
(\rho_{0},\id)
has weight
(-1)^{0}=1
.
The associated generating function

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\rho,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AR}[n]}(-1)^{v_{\alpha}(\rho)}x^{\alpha}

is the
Frobenius characteristic of rooted trees
.
The first few terms are

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}-\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2})+\frac{1}{6}3(x_{1}^{3}+x_{1}x_{2})-\frac{1}{6}(x_{1}^{3}+3x_{1}x_{2}+2x_{3})+\ldots

The first term comes from the special rooted tree
\rho_{0}
. The second term comes from the rooted tree with one internal vertex and two leaves branching from it. We have two automorphisms that either switch the leaf-labels or do not. The third and fourth terms in this sum come from the rooted trees in Figure
1
and their automorphisms.

Remark 3.7
.

The name
Frobenius characteristic

comes from an interpretation of these generating functions in the context of the representation theory of the symmetric group:
We can think of
{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})
as an element of the
ring of
symmetric functions
and each homogeneous part of
{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})
as the image of a certain representation of the symmetric group under the Frobenius characteristic map.
These representations are the vector spaces generated by
the elements of
\mathcal{R}[n]
with the action of
\SG_{n}

which alternates with
(-1)^{v_{\alpha}(\rho)}
as above.
However, in this paper we will not make use of this
representation theoretical interpretation of these objects.

The next proposition shows that the characteristic
{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})
has a remarkably simple form.

Proposition 3.8
.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log(1+x_{n}),

where
\mu
is the Möbius function.

Proof.

The formula will be established by relating pairs
(\rho,\alpha)
recursively to pairs
(\Phi,\gamma)
consisting of a set of rooted trees
\Phi
and an automorphism
\gamma\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)
.

Let
\Phi
be a collection of two or more admissible rooted trees and
\gamma\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)
. Set
\omega(\Phi,\gamma)=(-1)^{v_{\gamma}(\Phi)}
. We can form a new admissible rooted tree
\rho_{\Phi}
by gluing the roots of the trees in
\Phi
together and growing a new root from the resulting vertex. The resulting rooted tree has one new internal vertex, which is fixed by any automorphism, and at least
2
leaves. The natural map
{\operatorname{Aut}}(\Phi)\to{\operatorname{Aut}}(\rho_{\Phi})
is a bijection, so we will use the same name
\gamma
, and we have
\omega(\rho_{\Phi},\gamma)=-\omega(\Phi,\gamma)
.
The only pair
(\rho,\alpha)
which cannot be obtained in this way is
(\rho_{0},\id)
, so

(7)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}-\sum_{k\geq 2}\sum_{n\geq k}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{R})[n]}(-1)^{v_{\gamma}(\Phi)}x^{\gamma},

Proposition
3.2
tells us

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}_{\operatorname{Set}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{n\geq k}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in\mathcal{A}{\operatorname{Set}}^{k}(\mathcal{R})[n]}(-1)^{v_{\gamma}(\Phi)}x^{\gamma}y^{\gamma_{\Phi}}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}y_{k}\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}\right),

Setting
y_{k}=1
for all
k
and subtracting the terms with
k=0
or
k=1
gives the summation term in Eq. (
7
) above, i.e.

(8)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}-\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}\right)-1-{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[1]})\right).

Since
{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[1]})={\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})
this gives

\sum_{k\geq 1}{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})/k}=\log(1+x_{1})

and because
{\overline{x}}_{[k]}=(x_{k},x_{2k},\ldots)
, we also have

\sum_{k\geq 1}{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[kn]})/k}=\log(1+x_{n})

for all
n\geq 1
. Multiplying both sides of this equation with
\mu(n)/n
, summing over
n\geq 1
and using the defining recursion of the Möbius function results in the statement.
∎

3.5.
Unrooted trees

We next use this generating function for rooted trees (weighted by the parity of internal vertex orbits) to find the Frobenius characteristic
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
for the species
\mathcal{T}
of unrooted admissible trees, weighted by the parity of internal
edge
-orbits. Here
\mathcal{T}[n]
consists of labeled trees with
n
leaves, and to a pair
(t,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AT},
where
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(t),
we assign the weight
\omega(t,\alpha)=(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(t)}
, where
e_{\alpha}(t)
is the number of internal edge-orbits of
\alpha
in
t
, and define

(9)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})=\sum_{n\geq 3}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(t,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AT}[n]}(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(t)}x^{\alpha},

Proposition 3.9
.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}-\frac{x_{2}}{2}-(1+x_{1}){\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}).

Proof.

Suppose
t
is an unrooted tree and
\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(t)
. The set of points fixed by
\alpha
is connected, so is either a subtree
t^{\alpha}
or an isolated point in the middle of an internal edge.

Suppose first that there is a fixed subtree
t^{\alpha}
. For each internal vertex
v\in t^{\alpha}
, we can think of
(t,\alpha)
as a collection
\Phi
of at least
3
rooted trees, with
v
as their root, and of
\alpha
as an automorphism of
\Phi
. Note that
e_{\alpha}(t)=v_{\alpha}(t)-1=v_{\alpha}(\Phi)
.
Using Proposition
3.2
and setting
y_{k}=1
for all
k
, the generating functions of such collections
\Phi
is

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}\right)-1-{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[1]})-\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[2]})}{2}-\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[1]})^{2}}{2},

where we have subtracted the terms that correspond to collections with fewer than 3 trees. By Eq. (
8
) in the proof of Proposition
3.8
this is the same as

(10)

\displaystyle x_{1}-{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})-\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[2]})}{2}-\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})^{2}}{2}.

If we place a root in the middle of an internal edge of
t^{\alpha}
, we can view
t
as a pair
\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}
of rooted trees and
\alpha
as a pair
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
of automorphisms of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
respectively.
Since
e
is not adjacent to a leaf neither rooted tree is the special rooted tree. The generating function of such rooted trees is therefore

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{({\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}})-x_{1})^{2}}{2}.

Here we have
e_{\alpha}(t)=v_{\alpha}(t)-1=v_{\alpha}(\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\})
. The sum of expressions (
10
) and (
11
) counts the pair
(t,\alpha)
multiple times, once for each internal vertex of
t^{\alpha}
with sign
(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(t)},
and once for each internal edge of
t^{\alpha}
with the opposite sign. Since
t^{\alpha}
has one more internal vertex than internal edge, this sum leaves us with exactly one contribution from each
(t,\alpha)
, with sign
(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(t)}
, i.e. the sum gives the contribution to
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
from all such pairs.

We still have to account for pairs
(t,\alpha)
with no fixed vertices, i.e. the only fixed point is at the midpoint of an internal edge. The tree
t
can then be viewed as the union of two identical rooted trees
\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}
rooted at this midpoint, which are exchanged by
\alpha
. By Lemma
3.3
these are counted by
{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[2]})/2
. Since in this case
e_{\alpha}(t)=v_{\alpha}(\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\})
, these contribute

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{R}}({\overline{x}}_{[2]})}{2}-\frac{x_{2}}{2}

to
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
, where we have subtracted the term corresponding to the interval since that is not an admissible unrooted tree.
The sum of formulas (
10
), (
11
) and (
12
) now has one term for each pair
(t,\alpha),
weighted by
(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(t)}
, i.e. the sum is equal to
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
.
∎

As an immediate corollary of Proposition
3.9
and Proposition
3.8
we have

Corollary 3.10
.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}-\frac{x_{2}}{2}-(1+x_{1})\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\log(1+x_{k}).

Using
\log(1+x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n+1}x^{n}/n
,
and the definition of the Möbius function, we can expand this power series. As
\mu(1)=1
,
\mu(2)=\mu(3)=-1
and
\mu(4)=0
, the first coefficients are

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}-\frac{x_{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-(1+x_{1})\left(\left(x_{1}-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{x_{1}^{3}}{3}-\frac{x_{1}^{4}}{4}+\ldots\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2}+\ldots\right)-\frac{1}{3}\left(x_{3}+\ldots\right)+\ldots\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}^{3}}{6}+\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{2}+\frac{x_{3}}{3}-\frac{x_{1}^{4}}{12}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{4}+\frac{x_{1}x_{3}}{3}+\ldots

where we omitted terms of total degree higher than
4
.
As expected there are no terms of degree smaller than
3
, as such terms would correspond to trees with fewer than
3
leaves.

We recall from Section
3.2
that setting
{\overline{x}}=(x,0,0,\ldots)
in the Frobenius characteristic
\mathbf{V}({\overline{x}})
for unrooted trees recovers the generating function

V(x)
for unrooted trees
t
weighted by
(-1)^{e(t)}
, where
e(t)
is the number of (internal) edges of
t
, i.e.

(13)

V(x)=x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}-(1+x)\log(1+x).

For a detailed exposition of this formula see
[

As forests are just collections of trees, Proposition
3.2
gives us the following expression for the alternating cycle index series for the species

of forests:

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}_{\operatorname{Set}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in\mathcal{AF}[n]}(-1)^{e_{\gamma}(\Phi)}{x^{\gamma}}y^{\gamma_{\Phi}}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}y_{k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}}_{[k]})}{k}\right).

3.6.
Forested graphs

What we want to do with these forests is to glue their leaves together in pairs to form admissible graphs (which we can only do if there is an even number of leaves). We also want to keep track of the Euler characteristic of the resulting graph
G
; this is the number of trees in the forest minus half the total the number of leaves. It is equivalent to keep track of
-2\chi(G)=\#\{\text{leaves}\}-2\#\{\text{trees}\}
(which is always a positive integer) so we mark this number with a new variable
u
, and define the Frobenius characteristic of admissible forests

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})&=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in\mathcal{AF}[s]}(-1)^{e_{\gamma}(\Phi)}x^{\gamma}u^{s-2k(\Phi)},\end{aligned}

where
\mathcal{F}
is the species of forests and

k(\Phi)
is the number of trees in the forest
\Phi
.

Proposition 3.11
.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}\right),

where
{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})
means that we replace each variable
x_{i}
in
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
with
u^{ki}x_{ki}
.

By Eq. (
9
) the lowest term of
{\mathbf{V}}(u\cdot x)
is of order
u^{3}
, and hence the lowest term of
{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})
is of order
u^{3k}
. Hence,
{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})
is the exponential of a power series in only positive powers of
u
.

Proof.

Starting with the definition of
{\mathbf{V}}_{\operatorname{Set}}({\overline{x}},{\overline{y}})
, the substitution
x_{i}\mapsto u^{i}x_{i}
sends
x^{\lambda}
to
u^{|\lambda|}x^{\lambda}
, and the substitution
y_{i}\mapsto u^{-2i}
sends
y^{\gamma_{\Phi}}
to
u^{2k(\Phi)}
where
k(\Phi)
is the number of trees in
\Phi
. Doing both substitutions gives
{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\eta_{\lambda}[u^{2n}x^{\lambda}]{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})=\ee_{n},

\lambda

\eta_{\lambda}
is the number of matchings that commute with a permutation of cycle type
\lambda
.
The sum is finite since
[u^{2n}x^{\lambda}]{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})=0
unless

.

4.2.
Precise asymptotic statements

To discuss the asymptotic behavior of sequences we use the following standard conventions.

Notation
.

Let
\{c_{n}\}
be a sequence defined for all but finitely many positive integers
n
.
The set
\mathcal{O}(c_{n})
consists of all such sequences
\{a_{n}\}
for which

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|a_{n}/c_{n}\right|<\infty.

The notation
a_{n}=b_{n}+\mathcal{O}(c_{n})
means
a_{n}-b_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(c_{n})
. Recall that the notation
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
means

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=1.

To describe the asymptotic behavior of the numbers
\ee_{n}
we will use the
\Gamma
function, which is defined by
\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{x-1}e^{-z}\mathrm{d}z
for all
x>0
. At integer arguments it agrees with the factorial
n!=\Gamma(n+1)
.
By Stirling’s formula, we have

\Gamma(x)\sim\sqrt{2\pi}x^{x-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-x}

[
1
, Eq. (3.9)]
, i.e.
\Gamma(x)
grows more than exponentially.
Explicitly, we will quantify the asymptotic behavior of
\ee_{n}
using
the sequences
B_{n}
and
L_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\log^{2}n}

and

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}.

It follows from Stirling’s formula that

Lemma 4.1
.

B_{n}\sim-n^{n}e^{-n}/(n\log^{2}n).

Proof.

B_{n}/(-n^{n}e^{-n}/(n\log^{2}n))\sim e^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-\frac{1}{2n})^{n-1}\sim 1
, where we used
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(1+\frac{x}{n})^{n}=e^{x}
.
∎

The remainder of the paper is devoted to proving the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

\ee_{n}
has asymptotic behavior

\ee_{n}=e^{-\frac{1}{4}}B_{n}+\mathcal{O}(B_{n}/L_{n}).

Note that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}L_{n}=\infty
, so it follows that

\ee_{n}\sim e^{-\frac{1}{4}}B_{n}
. By applying Stirling’s formula, i.e. Lemma
4.1
, we moreover find that
\ee_{n}\sim-e^{-\frac{1}{4}}n^{n}e^{-n}/(n\log^{2}n)
.

The following statement from previous work by the authors is needed to prove this theorem.

Proposition 4.3

(Proposition 8.1 and Lemma 8.7 in

\Ch_{n}=B_{n}+\mathcal{O}(B_{n}/L_{n}).

Proof.

We just have to substitute the asymptotic formula for the numbers

in

(where we uncover an obvious typo: there should be no minus sign in

into the formula for

∎

As an immediate corollary we get

Corollary 4.4

There exists a constant

\displaystyle|\Ch_{n}|\leq C~\Gamma\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\text{ for all $n\geq 1$.}

This follows from

\mathcal{O}(\Ch_{n})=\mathcal{O}(B_{n})=\mathcal{O}\left(\Gamma(n-\frac{1}{2})/\log^{2}n\right)\subset\mathcal{O}\left(\Gamma(n-\frac{1}{2})\right).

Hence,

4.3.
Forested graphs with legs

In Section
3
,
we have only needed to consider forested graphs
(G,\Phi)
such that
G
has no univalent vertices. However, to determine the asymptotic behavior of the integral Euler characteristic we will need to do a finer analysis, which involves studying pairs
(G,\Phi)
where
G
is allowed to have univalent (but not bivalent) vertices, while
\Phi
is not allowed to contain any edges adjacent to univalent vertices. We call the edges of
G
adjacent to univalent vertices
legs
and the pairs
(G,\Phi)

forested graphs with legs
. In this subsection we point out that a minor modification of the counting methods from Section
3
counts these more general graphs.

Recall that admissible graphs are constructed by pairing the leaves of an admissible forest
\Phi
. The internal edges of the forest then become a subforest
\Phi_{0}
of the admissible graph
G
. To get the original forest back you cut all the 1-cells of
G
that are not in
\Phi_{0}
; the half-edges that result are attached to the leaves of the original forest
\Phi
. If the graph
G
has legs we are not allowing the subforest
\Phi
to contain them so they must be cut, which results in components with one 0-cell and one half-edge. These are not admissible trees, so we will call these
special components
, and mark them with a new variable
w
. A forest which is allowed to have both admissible and special components will be called an
extended forest
. Matching the leaves of an extended forest results in a graph with a univalent vertex for each special component.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\star}
denote the species of extended forests, and define
F^{\star}(x,u,w)
to be

\displaystyle F^{\star}(x,u,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Phi\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\star}}(-1)^{e(\Phi)}u^{s(\Phi)-2k(\Phi)}w^{j(\Phi)}\frac{x^{s(\Phi)}}{s(\Phi)!},

where
e(\Phi)
is the number of edges of
\Phi
,
s(\Phi)
is the number of leaves,
k(\Phi)
is the total number of components and
j(\Phi)
is the number of special components, i.e.
[u^{r}x^{s}w^{j}]F^{\star}(u,x,w)
is the edge-weighted count of forests with
s
leaves and
(s-r)/2
components, of which
j
are special.

Recall that
V(x)
is the generating function for unrooted trees, weighted by the parity of their internal edges (see Eq. (
13
)).

Proposition 4.7
.

F^{\star}(u,x,w)=\exp(u^{-1}wx+u^{-2}V(ux)).

Proof.

A special component has
1
leaf,
0
edges and
1
component (which is special!), so the term
u^{-1}wx
accounts for special components. The term
u^{-2}V(ux)
accounts for admissible trees as before. Exponentiating gives the generating function for collections, as per Lemma
3.1
.
∎

In
[
10
]
we showed that the rational Euler characteristic
\chi_{n}=\chi({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
is given by

\chi_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{e(\Phi)}}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|},

where the sum is over isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
of connected forested graphs of Euler characteristic
-n
.
We defined the corresponding generating function,

T(\hbar)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\chi_{n}\hbar^{n}
,
in Eq. (
16
).

In
[
10
]
, we also defined an analogous generating function
T(\hbar,w)
for isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
of connected forested graphs with legs, which are weighted in the same way as in the formula above. Here the variable
w
marks the legs of
G
, i.e.
[\hbar^{n}w^{s}]T(\hbar,w)
is the weighted sum over isomorphism classes
[G,\Phi]
such that
G
is connected, has Euler characteristic
-n
, and has
s
legs. We can relate
T(\hbar,w)
to the generating function
F^{\star}(u,x,w)
:

Lemma 4.8
.

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 0}(2m-1)!![x^{2m}]F^{\star}(u,x,w)=\exp\left(\frac{u^{-2}w^{2}}{2}+T(u^{2},w)\right).

Proof.

The coefficient of
u^{2n}w^{s}
in
(2m-1)!![x^{2m}]F^{\star}(u,x,w)
gives the weighted count of forested graphs with
s
legs and
\chi=-n
that can be made by gluing the leaves of extended forests
\Phi
with
2m
leaves. Adding up over all
m
gives the count of all forested graphs with
s
legs and
\chi=-n
. The term
\exp(T(u^{2},w))
counts forests, but misses components with a single internal vertex and two legs. Those are taken care of by adding the term
(u^{-2}w^{2})/2
to
T(u^{2},w)
. ∎

Finally, we recall from
[
10
]
that
T(\hbar,w)
and
T(\hbar)
are related in the following way.

Proposition 4.9

(
[
10
]
, Proposition 3.1)
.

T(\hbar,w)=T(\hbar e^{-w})+\frac{w}{2}+\hbar^{-1}\left(e^{w}-1-w-\frac{w^{2}}{2}\right).

Here
T(\hbar e^{-w})
accounts for connected forested graphs with negative Euler characteristic, the term
\frac{w}{2}
accounts for those with Euler characteristic zero, and the last term accounts for trees.

4.4.
Relating the integral and rational Euler characteristics

Recall from Eq. (
1
) that each triple
[G,\Phi,\alpha]
consisting of a forested graph with
\chi(G)=-n
and an automorphism
\alpha
preserving
\Phi
contributes to
\ee_{n}
. Restricting the sum to only triples with
\alpha=\hbox{id}
gives the exponentiated rational Euler characteristic
\widehat{\chi}_{n}
from Eq. (
16
). To prove Theorem
4.2
we will exploit the fact that we already know the asymptotics of
\widehat{\chi}_{n}
. We will eventually find that the contributions to
\ee_{n}
from triples
[G,\Phi,\alpha]
such that
\alpha
fixes
\Phi
and has order at most
2
dominate the asymptotics of
\ee_{n}
. In this subsection we manipulate our formula for
\ee_{n}
to isolate these contributions, and in the following subsections we will prove that they dominate by bounding the relative size of the remaining terms.

We start with the formula

\displaystyle\ee_{n}=\sum_{\lambda}\eta_{\lambda}[u^{2n}x^{\lambda}]{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})

from Proposition
3.12
. From Proposition
3.11
we have an expression for
{\mathbf{F}}(u,{\overline{x}})
,
so

\displaystyle\ee_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\eta_{\lambda}[u^{2n}x^{\lambda}]\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}\right).

Recall that a permutation is called a
derangement
if it has no fixed points; this is equivalent to saying that the corresponding cycle type has no parts of size 1, so we call it a
deranged partition
.
An integer partition
\lambda
is equivalent to a pair
(m,\delta)
where
m
is the number of parts of size
1
in
\lambda
and

\delta
is the deranged partition obtained from
\lambda
by removing all parts of size
1
.

To make an admissible graph with an automorphism from a pair
(\Phi,\alpha)
consisting of a forest and an automorphism, leaves of
\Phi
that are permuted by
\alpha
in cycles of equal lengths must be paired with each other; in particular, leaves that are fixed by
\alpha
must be paired with each other. By first pairing the fixed leaves we can reduce the expression for
\ee_{n}
above to a sum over
deranged partitions. Specifically, if there are
2m
fixed leaves there are
(2m-1)!!
ways to pair them, so if
\lambda=(2m,\delta)
then
\eta_{\lambda}=(2m-1)!!\eta_{\delta}
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \ee_{n}&=\sum_{\delta}\eta_{\delta}[u^{2n}x^{\delta}]\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m-1)!![x_{1}^{2m}]\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\delta}\eta_{\delta}[u^{2n}x^{\delta}]\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 2}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m-1)!![x_{1}^{2m}]\exp\left(u^{-2}{\mathbf{V}}(u\cdot{\overline{x}})\right),\end{aligned}

where we sum only over deranged integer partitions
\delta
. (Note that the variable
x_{1}
does not appear in the power series
{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})
for
k\geq 2
.) The expression in the first exponential will not change in what follows, so we give it a name:

\mathbf{h}_{1}(u,{\overline{x}})=\sum_{k\geq 2}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}=\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{u^{k}x^{3}_{k}}{6k}+\frac{u^{k}x_{k}{x_{2k}}}{2k}+\frac{u^{k}x_{3k}}{3k}-\frac{u^{2k}x_{k}^{4}}{12k}+\ldots

and our expression reads

(17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \ee_{n}=\sum_{\delta}\eta_{\delta}[u^{2n}x^{\delta}]\exp(\mathbf{h}_{1}(u,{\overline{x}}))\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m-1)!![x_{1}^{2m}]\exp\left(u^{-2}{\mathbf{V}}(u\cdot{\overline{x}})\right).\end{aligned}

Next we look more closely at the term
\exp(u^{-2}\mathbf{V}(u\cdot{\overline{x}})).
We first separate out the contribution to
{\mathbf{V}}({\overline{x}})
of pairs
(t,\alpha)
with

4.5.

In this subsection we show that the numbers

\Gamma

\Gamma
function satisfies

•

\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
for all
x>0
,

•

\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
, and

•

\Gamma
is is
log-convex
, i.e.

(24)

\displaystyle\log\Gamma\left(ta+\left(1-t\right)b+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq t\log\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(1-t\right)\log\Gamma\left(b+\frac{1}{2}\right),

for all
t\in[0,1]
and
a,b\geq 0
.

In fact, these three properties determine the function
\Gamma
completely

[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
. Lemma
4.12
, Corollary
4.13
and Lemma
4.14
below follow easily using these properties.

Lemma 4.12
.

For all
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
0\leq z\leq y\leq x
we have

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Gamma\left(x+z+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(y-z+\frac{1}{2}\right).

Proof.

Adding Eq. (
24
) to itself with
a
and
b
interchanged gives,

\displaystyle\log\Gamma\left(ta+\left(1-t\right)b+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\log\Gamma\left(\left(1-t\right)a+tb+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq\log\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\log\Gamma\left(b+\frac{1}{2}\right).

We can choose
a=x+z
,
b=y-z
and
t=1-z/(x-y+2z)
, as
t\in[0,1]
and
a,b\geq 0
are fulfilled because
z\leq y\leq x
. Exponentiating gives the stated inequality.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

For all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
x,y\geq 0
we have

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq\frac{\Gamma\left(x+y+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

Proof.

Specializing Lemma
4.12
with
y=z
and using
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
gives the bound.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

There is a constant
C
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
x,y\geq 0
we have

(26)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(x+y+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq C^{1+x+y}\Gamma\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right).

Proof.

Specializing Lemma
4.12
to
x=y=(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})/2
and

gives,

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}+1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Gamma\left(x^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(y^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\quad\text{ for all }x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ with }x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\geq 0.

The duplication formula for the
\Gamma
function
[
1
, Eq. (3.11)]
can be written as

(27)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)=2^{-z}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(z+1)/\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right).

Applying this identity once to the left hand side of the inequality above results in

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(x+y+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{x+y}}{\sqrt{\pi}}F(x+y)\Gamma\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right)\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ with }x,y\geq 0.

where

F(z)=\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right)\Gamma\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)/(\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)\Gamma(z+1))
.
To prove the upper bound in the statement it is therefore sufficient to show that there is a constant
C
such that

for all
z\geq 0
.

F(z)
is regular for all

and can apply Lemma

to establish

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right)\Gamma\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)\Gamma(z+1)\quad\text{ for all }z\geq 1.\qed

We now apply these lemmas to bound the numbers

Lemma 4.15
.

There exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\eta_{k,m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(kC)^{m/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)\text{ for all }k\geq 1\text{ and }m\geq 0.

Proof.

Because
\eta_{k,0}=1
and
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
, the statement is obvious if

. In all other cases it is sufficient to prove the bound for
m
large.
By the standard identity

(2n-1)!!=2^{n}\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2})/\sqrt{\pi}

and by the definition of

the statement is true

For even

\displaystyle\eta_{k,m}=\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2r}k^{r}(2r-1)!!=\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2r}k^{r}2^{r}\frac{\Gamma(r+\frac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq 2^{m}\max_{0\leq r\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(2k)^{r}\frac{\Gamma(r+\frac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}},

where we used the floor function

and
\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\binom{m}{2r}\leq 2^{m}
.
The statement follows since
\Gamma(x)
is increasing for sufficiently large

.
∎

Recall that

denotes the length of
\lambda

Corollary 4.16
.

There is a constant
C
such that for all integer partitions
\lambda

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}\leq\frac{C^{|\lambda|}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell(\lambda)+1}{2}\right).

Let

\log k\leq k

Therefore,

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}=\prod_{k=1}^{|\lambda|}\eta_{k,m_{k}}\leq\prod_{k=1}^{|\lambda|}\frac{(kC)^{m_{k}/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{m_{k}+1}{2}\right).

Lemma 4.20

There exists a constant

\displaystyle 1\leq\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{n-m+2r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}\leq\exp\left(C\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious as

We start by proving the bound for all

We have

\displaystyle\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{n-m+2r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}=2^{m}\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{\frac{n-m}{2}+r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}\leq 2^{m}=2^{m\frac{n}{n}}\leq 2^{\frac{m(2m+1)}{n}}\leq 4^{\frac{m(m+1)}{n}},

as

and

We still have to prove the bound for

Recall that

for all

\frac{1}{1-x}\leq e^{\frac{x}{1-x}}
for
x<1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{n-m+2r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}=\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{1+\frac{2r-m}{n}}{1-\frac{m+\frac{1}{2}-r}{n}}\leq\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\exp\left(\frac{2r-m}{n}+\frac{\frac{m+\frac{1}{2}-r}{n}}{1-\frac{m+\frac{1}{2}-r}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\exp\left(\frac{2r-m}{n}+2\frac{m+\frac{1}{2}-r}{n}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right),

where we used

m\leq\frac{n-1}{2}\Rightarrow{1/\left(1-\frac{m+\frac{1}{2}-r}{n}\right)}\leq{1/\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{r}{n}\right)}\leq 2
.
∎

Lemma 4.21
.

There exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle 1\leq\frac{\log(n+1)}{\log(n-m+1)}\leq\exp\left(C\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)

for all integers
n,m
with
0\leq m\leq n-1
and
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious.
We start by proving the estimate for
m>\frac{n+1}{2}
.
Because
\log
grows slower than the exponential, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\log(n+1)}{\log(n-m+1)}\leq\frac{\log(n+1)}{\log 2}\leq{C^{\prime}}^{n}\leq({C^{\prime}}^{2})^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\leq({C^{\prime}}^{2})^{m}\leq({C^{\prime}}^{4})^{\frac{m(m+1)}{n}}.

The bounds remains to be proven for all
m\leq\frac{n+1}{2}
.
Again we use the inequality
\frac{1}{1-x}\leq\exp{\frac{x}{1-x}}\Rightarrow\log\frac{1}{1-x}\leq\frac{x}{1-x}
which holds for
all
x\in[0,1)
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\log(n+1)}{\log(n-m+1)}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\log(1-\frac{m}{n+1})}{\log(n+1)}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{\log(1-\frac{m}{n+1})}{\log(n+1)}}{1+\frac{\log(1-\frac{m}{n+1})}{\log(n+1)}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{-\log(1-\frac{m}{n+1})}{\log(n-m+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\log\frac{1}{1-\frac{m}{n+1}}}{\log 2}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{\log 2}\frac{\frac{m}{n+1}}{1-\frac{m}{n+1}}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{2}{\log 2}\frac{m}{n+1}\right),

where we used
{1/\left(1-\frac{m}{n+1}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{2}
in the last step.
∎

Recall the sequences

B_{n}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(n-\frac{1}{2})}{\log^{2}n}
and

L_{n}=\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
from Section
4.2
. Because these sequences are not defined for
n=1
, it is convenient to use following ones instead

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(n-\frac{1}{2})}{\log^{2}(n+1)}

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(n+1)}{\log\log(n+e)}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{O}(B^{\prime}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(B_{n})
and
\mathcal{O}(L^{\prime}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(L_{n}),
but we also have control over the error:

Lemma 4.22
.

B^{\prime}_{n}=B_{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{B_{n}}{n}\right)

and

1/L^{\prime}_{n}=1/L_{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{nL_{n}}\right)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that the following elementary limits exist:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1/\log^{2}(n+1)-1/\log^{2}n}{1/(n\log^{2}n)}

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log(n+e)/\log(n+1)-\log\log n/\log n}{\log\log n/(n\log n)}.

This can be shown, for instance, by using
\log(n+x)-\log n\leq x/n
.
∎

Lemma 4.23
.

Let

\displaystyle Q_{n,m}={\Ch_{n-m}/B^{\prime}_{n-m}}-1.

There exists a constant
C
such that

\left|Q_{n,m}\right|\leq C^{m+1}/L^{\prime}_{n}

for all
n,m
with
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
and
Lemma
4.22
we have

\Ch_{n}-B^{\prime}_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(B^{\prime}_{n}/L^{\prime}_{n})
.
Because
B^{\prime}_{n}
,
\Ch_{n}
and
L^{\prime}_{n}
are finite for all
n\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\left|{\widehat{\chi}_{n}/B^{\prime}_{n}}-1\right|\leq C^{\prime}/L^{\prime}_{n}

for all
n\geq 1
.
It follows that
for all integers
n,m
with
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
.

\displaystyle\left|Q_{n,m}\right|=\left|\Ch_{n-m}/B^{\prime}_{n-m}-1\right|\leq C^{\prime}/L^{\prime}_{n-m}\leq C^{\prime}\exp\left(C^{\prime\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)\frac{\log\log(n-m+e)}{\log(n+1)},

where we used a constant
C^{\prime\prime}
from Lemma
4.21
.
Using

and the monotonicity of
\log
gives the bound.
∎

Lemma 4.24
.

Let

\displaystyle R_{n,m}=(-1)^{m}2^{m}m!\binom{-\frac{1}{2}(n-m)}{m}B^{\prime}_{n-m}/B^{\prime}_{n}-1.

There exists a constant
C
such that

\left|R_{n,m}\right|\leq\frac{C^{m+1}}{n}

for all
n,m
with
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
.

Proof.

Use

\Gamma(n-\frac{1}{2})=\Gamma(n-m-\frac{1}{2})\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}(n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r)

and

\binom{q}{m}=\frac{1}{m!}\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}(q-r)

to get

\displaystyle R_{n,m}+1

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}2^{m}\frac{\log^{2}(n+1)}{\log^{2}(n-m+1)}\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{-\frac{1}{2}(n-m)-r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log^{2}(n+1)}{\log^{2}(n-m+1)}\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{n-m+2r}{n-m-\frac{1}{2}+r}.

Therefore, by Lemma
4.20
and
4.21
there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
such that

1\leq 1+R_{n,m}\leq\exp\left(C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)

for all integers
n,m
with
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
. Because

for all

, we have

\left|R_{n,m}\right|\leq C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\exp\left(C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)\leq C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\exp\left(mC^{\prime}\right)
for all
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
.
The statement follows from the fact that we can find a constant
C^{\prime\prime}
such that

C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\exp\left(C^{\prime}\frac{m(m+1)}{n}\right)\leq\frac{{C^{\prime\prime}}^{m+1}}{n}

for all
m\geq 0
and
n\geq m+1
.
∎

We are now ready to estimate the numbers

.

Proposition 4.25
.

\displaystyle P_{n}={e^{-\frac{1}{4}}}B^{\prime}_{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(B^{\prime}_{n}/L^{\prime}_{n}\right).

Proof.

With
Q_{n,m}
and

from Lemmas

and

and Eq. (

\displaystyle P_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\widehat{\chi}_{n-m}\binom{-\frac{1}{2}(n-m)}{m}\eta_{2,m}=B^{\prime}_{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}m!}(1+Q_{n,m})(1+R_{n,m})\eta_{2,m}

By Lemma
4.15
there exists a constant
C

\eta_{2,m}\leq C^{m}\Gamma(\frac{m+1}{2})
for all

.
From Lemma
4.23
and

we get a constant

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left|\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}m!}(Q_{n,m}+R_{n,m}+Q_{n,m}R_{n,m})\eta_{2,m}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{L^{\prime}_{n}}+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{nL^{\prime}_{n}}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}(CC^{\prime})^{m}\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)\in\mathcal{O}\left(1/L^{\prime}_{n}\right),

where the sum over

is convergent due to the factorial

. Hence,

\displaystyle P_{n}=B^{\prime}_{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}m!}\eta_{2,m}+\mathcal{O}\left(B^{\prime}_{n}/L^{\prime}_{n}\right).

An application of Lemma
4.17
and using

concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.26
.

The appearance of the constant

e^{-\frac{1}{4}}
in
Proposition
4.25
deserves a discussion.
Asymptotically
\Ch_{n}
and
\ee_{n}
are
equal up to a multiplicative constant and by
Lemma
4.17
, this constant is

.
This constant is reminiscent of constants that
appear in certain counting problems, such as counting the number of regular graphs with a fixed
girth, where the girth of a graph is the minimal length of a cycle.
In this count, numbers
e^{-\lambda}
appear because the
numbers of
k
-cycles of large random regular graphs follow independent Poisson
distributions with rational mean
\lambda

(
[
7
, Thm. 2]
).
Since a Poisson distribution is given by

e^{-\lambda}{\lambda^{k}/k!}
,
constants such as
e^{-\frac{1}{4}}
appear naturally. The computation in Lemma
4.17
suggests
that a large admissible random forested graph
has
r
double edges with probability
e^{-1/4}/(4^{r}r!)
and

m
self-loops with probability
e^{-1/2}/(2^{m}m!)
.
However, such an intuitive analogy to random graph theory is derailed by the
various signs that have to be taken into account, so this seems to make a sound probabilistic interpretation impossible.

4.7.

We still need to prove Proposition

We first show that the coefficients of

Lemma 4.27
.

Let
r\geq 0
and
\delta
be a deranged partition. Then

\displaystyle[u^{r}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-\mathbf{W}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)=\Ch_{\frac{r-|\delta|}{2}}\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{(r-|\delta|)\frac{\mu(k)}{2k}}{m_{k}(\delta)},

where we agree that

\Ch_{\frac{r-|\delta|}{2}}=0

if
r-|\delta|
is odd or negative. In particular,
[u^{0}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-\mathbf{W}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)=1
if
\delta=\emptyset
and
0
otherwise. Also,
[u^{1}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-\mathbf{W}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)=0
for all
\delta
.

Proof.

Use
\widehat{T}(\hbar)=\sum\Ch_{n}\hbar^{n}
and
{\mathbf{W}}({\overline{x}})=-\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\log(1+x_{k})
to get

\displaystyle\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Ch_{n}u^{2n}\prod_{k\geq 2}\left(1+u^{k}x_{k}\right)^{n\frac{\mu(k)}{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Ch_{n}u^{2n}\prod_{k\geq 2}\sum_{m_{k}\geq 0}\binom{n\frac{\mu(k)}{k}}{m_{k}}u^{km_{k}}x_{k}^{m_{k}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\delta}\Ch_{n}u^{2n+|\delta|}x^{\delta}\prod_{k\geq 2}\binom{n\frac{\mu(k)}{k}}{m_{k}(\delta)}.\qed

Using this explicit formula we can now obtain a bound on the coefficients of
\widehat{T}(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})})
:

Corollary 4.28
.

There exists a constant
C
, such that
for all
r\geq 2
and deranged partitions
\delta=[2^{m_{2}}3^{m_{3}}\cdots]
with
|\delta|\leq r
,

\displaystyle\left|[u^{r}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)\right|\leq C\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{r-|\delta|+2\ell(\delta)-1}{2}\right)}{m_{2}!m_{3}!}.

Proof.

We start with the special cases where
\delta
is a deranged partition of
r
or
r-1
. In both cases
r\geq 2
implies
\delta\neq\emptyset
, i.e.
\ell(\delta)\geq 1
and the argument of the
\Gamma
function on the right hand side of the statement is positive.
By Lemma
4.27
and the agreement that
\widehat{\chi}_{\frac{1}{2}}=0
and
\binom{0}{m}=0
for all
m\geq 1
, we find that the left hand side vanishes in these cases and the statement follows.

For
|\delta|\leq r-2
we can apply
Corollary
4.4
to the statement of Lemma
4.27
to get a constant
C
such that
for all deranged partitions
\delta=[2^{m_{2}}3^{m_{3}}\cdots]
,

(29)

\displaystyle\left|[u^{r}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)\right|\leq C\Gamma\left(\frac{r-|\delta|-1}{2}\right)\left|\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{(r-|\delta|)\frac{\mu(k)}{2k}}{m_{k}}\right|.

From the standard expression for the binomial coefficients
\binom{q}{m}=\frac{q(q-1)\cdots(q-m+1)}{m!}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{k=2}^{r}\binom{(r-|\delta|)\frac{\mu(k)}{2k}}{m_{k}}\right|=\prod_{k=2}^{r}\frac{\left|\prod_{s=0}^{m_{k}-1}\left((r-|\delta|)\frac{\mu(k)}{2k}-s\right)\right|}{m_{k}!}\leq\frac{\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{s=0}^{m_{k}-1}\left((r-|\delta|)\frac{1}{2k}+s\right)}{m_{2}!m_{3}!},

where we used
|\mu(n)|\leq 1
. By using

repeatedly we get

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{r-|\delta|-1}{2}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{r-|\delta|+2\ell(\delta)-1}{2}\right)}{\prod_{s=0}^{\ell(\delta)-1}\left(\frac{r-|\delta|-1}{2}+s\right)}.

Combining these observations with Eq. (
29

\displaystyle\left|[u^{r}x^{\delta}]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)\right|\leq C\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{r-|\delta|+2\ell(\delta)-1}{2}\right)}{{m_{2}!m_{3}!}}\frac{\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{s=0}^{m_{k}-1}\left((r-|\delta|)\frac{1}{2k}+s\right)}{\prod_{s=0}^{\ell(\delta)-1}\left(\frac{r-|\delta|-1}{2}+s\right)}.

It is easy to verify that

\prod_{k=2}^{r}\prod_{s=0}^{m_{k}-1}\left((r-|\delta|)\frac{1}{2k}+s\right)\leq\prod_{s=0}^{\ell(\delta)-1}\left(\frac{r-|\delta|-1}{2}+s\right)
by using
\ell(\delta)=\sum_{k=2}^{|\delta|}m_{k}
and
r-|\delta|\geq 2
.
∎

There is a constant

[u^{r}x^{\delta}]\log\mathbf{H}(u,{\overline{x}})=0

Remark 4.32
.

This corollary also follows immediately from the combinatorics: An admissible graph

All coefficients of

Proof.

Our next estimate will make use of the following rough bound on integer partitions.

Lemma 4.35
.

The number of integer partitions of size at most
n
is smaller than

.

Proof.

Writing a string of length
n
of
1
’s with either a

or a comma in between gives a

of
n
, i.e. an integer partition of
n
with an ordering of the parts.
There are

different such compositions of

. Thus the number of integer partitions of
n
is bounded by
2^{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
.
The number of integer partitions of size at most
n
is therefore smaller than

, where the initial

2^{0}+\ldots+2^{n-1}=2^{n}-1
, the statement follows.
∎

Lemma 4.36
.

There is a constant

.

Proof.

The second statement follows immediately from the definition of

By Corollary

, the set

is contained in the set of all pairs

Since

\sum_{r\geq 1}2^{3r}u^{r}=\sum_{r\geq 1}u^{r}8^{r}e^{\log r}/r,

Summing over

on the left hand side of the inequality in the statement of the lemma is equivalent to setting

\displaystyle[u^{r^{\prime}}x^{\ell^{\prime}}]\exp\left(\sum_{(r,\delta)\in J\setminus K}u^{r}x^{\ell(\delta)}\right)\leq[u^{r^{\prime}}]\exp\left(\sum_{(r,\delta)\in J\setminus K}u^{r}\right)\leq[u^{r^{\prime}}]\exp\left(\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{(8e)^{r}}{r}u^{r}\right)=(8e)^{r^{\prime}},

where we used

Lemma 4.41

\displaystyle[u^{2n}x_{2}^{m_{2}}x_{3}^{0}x_{4}^{0}\cdots]\widehat{T}\left(u^{2}e^{-{\mathbf{W}}(u\cdot\bar{x})}\right)=\Ch_{\frac{2n-2m_{2}}{2}}\binom{(2n-2m_{2})\frac{\mu(2)}{4}}{m_{2}}.

Using
\mu(2)=-1
and
\eta_{[2^{m_{2}}]}=\eta_{2,m_{2}}
gives the statement.
∎

5.
The odd forested graph complex

As remarked in Section
2.3
, the Euler characteristic
e({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))
is equal to the Euler characteristic of Kontsevich’s Lie graph complex, which is equal to the Euler characteristic of the forested graph complex. In
[
22
]
Kontsevich defined an
odd version
1
1

1

In
[
29
]
, Willwacher used the opposite notions of “even” and “odd” orientation of a graph, so that Kontsevich’s “odd” graph complexes are Willwacher’s “even” graph complexes.

of the Lie graph complex. The two graph complexes differ only by the definition of the
orientation
of a graph.

Recall that the (even) forested graph complex is generated by all even forested graphs (see Section
2.3
), which are forested graphs with no automorphisms
\alpha
that induce an odd permutation
\alpha_{\Phi}:E_{\Phi}\to E_{\Phi}
on the forest edges
E_{\Phi}
. In other words, all automorphisms
\alpha
of an even forested graph satisfy
{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})=1
.

Every automorphism
\alpha
of a graph
G
also induces automorphisms on its zeroth and first homologies, i.e. we have
\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})}\colon H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})}\colon H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
An
odd
forested graph is a forested graph all of whose automorphisms
\alpha
satisfy

{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=1.

For connected graphs
H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
is one-dimensional and has a canonical orientation.
Hence,
\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=1
for all connected
G
.
The complex spanned by such connected odd forested graphs computes
H^{*}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1});\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}})
, where
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
is the representation obtained by composing the canonical group homomorphism
{\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n})\to{\operatorname{GL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
with the determinant map. The techniques developed in this paper can be used almost verbatim to compute the associated Euler characteristic
e^{\mathrm{odd}}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}))=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\dim(H^{k}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n}),\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}))
, which is equal to the Euler characteristic of Kontsevich’s odd Lie graph complex.

As in Proposition
2.2
we define

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\,\mathrm{odd}\\
G\,\mathrm{connected}\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e(\Phi)}=e^{\mathrm{odd}}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1})),

where we sum over all isomorphism classes of connected odd forested graphs
[G,\Phi]
of Euler characteristic
\chi(G)=-n
. As we did in Section
2.3
we also define

(31)

\displaystyle\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\,\mathrm{odd}\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e(\Phi)},

where we sum over all isomorphism classes of
possibly disconnected
odd forested graphs of Euler characteristic
\chi(G)=-n
.

Consider a disconnected forested graph
[G,\Phi]
which contains two copies of the (possibly disconnected) forested graph
[g,\varphi]
and
let
\alpha
be the automorphism of
[G,\Phi]
that switches the two copies.
As
\alpha
permutes the canonical basis of
H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
and the canonical
decomposition of
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
over connected components, we have

{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\Phi})\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=(-1)^{\xi(g,\varphi)},

where
\xi(g,\varphi)=e(\varphi)+\dim H_{0}(g,{\mathbb{Z}})+\dim H_{1}(g,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Using this, we find a version of Theorem
2.5
for the odd case:

Theorem 5.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}\hbar^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-(-\hbar)^{n}}\right)^{(-1)^{n}e^{\mathrm{odd}}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))}

Proof.

We follow the argument of the proof for Theorem
2.5
. Just as in the even case,
each odd forested graph
[G,\Phi]
can be described by
giving a set of distinct connected odd forested graphs
[g,\varphi]

together with a multiplicity for each connected graph.
Here
[g,\varphi]
can appear
at most with multiplicity one in
[G,\Phi]
if
\xi(g,\varphi)

is odd, and with arbitrary multiplicity if
\xi(g,\varphi)
is even.
Analogously to Theorem
2.5
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}\hbar^{n}=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
\xi(g,\varphi)\,\text{odd}\end{subarray}}\left(1+(-1)^{e(\varphi)}\hbar^{-\chi(g)}\right)\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}[g,\varphi]\\
\xi(g,\varphi)\,\text{even}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1-(-1)^{e(\varphi)}\hbar^{-\chi(g)}}\right),

where we multiply over all connected odd forested graphs
[g,\varphi]
with
\xi(g,\varphi)
odd or even. The statement follows
by observing that
\xi(g,\varphi)
has the same parity as
e(\varphi)+\chi(g)=e(\varphi)+\dim(H_{0}(g,{\mathbb{Z}}))-\dim(H_{1}(g,{\mathbb{Z}}))
and by following the rest of the argument for Theorem
2.5
.
∎

By adjusting signs in Corollary
2.6
, we can then use Theorem
5.1
to compute
e^{\mathrm{odd}}({\operatorname{Out}}(F_{n+1}))
from

\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}.

Recall that for a forested graph
(G,\Phi)
and an automorphism

\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)
, the number
e_{\alpha}(\Phi)
denotes the number of cycles of the permutation on the forest edges,
\alpha_{\Phi}:E_{\Phi}\rightarrow E_{\Phi}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For a forested graph
(G,\Phi)
,
\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}
is equal to

(-1)^{e(\Phi)}|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|
if
(G,\Phi)
is odd or
0
otherwise.

Proof.

The argument for Lemma
2.4
applies.
∎

Each automorphism
\alpha
of a forested graph
(G,\Phi)
also provides us with a permutation
\alpha_{E_{G}\setminus\Phi}:{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}\rightarrow{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}
that permutes the non-forest edges,
a set of permutations
(\alpha_{e})_{e\in{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}}
of order 1 or 2 that might change the orientation of each non-forest edge,
and a permutation
\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi)}
that permutes the connected components of the forest.

Lemma 5.3
.

For a forested graph
(G,\Phi)
and an automorphism

\alpha:(G,\Phi)\to(G,\Phi)
,

\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})={\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi)}){\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}})\prod_{e\in E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{e}).

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}E
and
{\mathbb{Z}}V
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-vector spaces generated by the edge and vertex set of
(G,\Phi)
. We have the exact sequence

0\to H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}E\to{\mathbb{Z}}V\to H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})\to 0.

The spaces
{\mathbb{Z}}E
and
{\mathbb{Z}}V
come with a natural bilinear form, hence we can dualize the usual boundary operator
\partial_{1}:{\mathbb{Z}}E\to{\mathbb{Z}}V
to

\partial_{1}^{*}:{\mathbb{Z}}V\to{\mathbb{Z}}E
. We get the isomorphism
{\mathbb{Z}}V\oplus H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}E\oplus H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
given by
(v,c)\mapsto(c+\partial_{1}^{*}v,\partial_{0}v)
.
An automorphism
\alpha
of
G
also gives the automorphisms

\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V},\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E},\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V\oplus H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})}
and
\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E\oplus H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})}

of the respective vector spaces.
Because
\alpha
acts block-wise on the summands in the direct sums, their determinants factor as follows,

\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V\oplus H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E\oplus H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E})\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})}),

and as

\det X=\pm 1
for all
X\in{\operatorname{GL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, we get

\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E})\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V})
.

The vector space
{\mathbb{Z}}E
can be decomposed
{\mathbb{Z}}E={\mathbb{Z}}E_{\Phi}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}(E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi})
and
\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E}
acts block-wise on both summands as it does not mix forest and non-forested edges. It follows that

\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E_{\Phi}})\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}(E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi})})
.
From
\Phi
, we get the short exact sequence,

0\to{\mathbb{Z}}E_{\Phi}\to{\mathbb{Z}}V\to H_{0}(\Phi,{\mathbb{Z}})\to 0
, where we used the fact that a forest has no first homology. Consequently,
\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}V})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E_{\Phi}})\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi,{\mathbb{Z}})})
and

\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})\det(\alpha_{H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})})=\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}E_{\Phi}})^{2}\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}(E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi})})\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi,{\mathbb{Z}})}).

Ordering and directing the edges
E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}
gives a basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}(E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi})
. The orientation can be changed by switching two edges or reversing the direction of one edge. Hence,
\det(\alpha_{{\mathbb{Z}}(E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi})})={\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}})\prod_{e\in E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{e}).

Fixing an ordering of the connected components of
\Phi
gives
a basis of
H_{0}(\Phi,{\mathbb{Z}})
. Therefore,
\det(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi,{\mathbb{Z}})})
is equal to the sign of the permutation
\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi)}
that
\alpha
induces on the components of
\Phi
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
with Eq. (
31
) results in

Theorem 5.4
.

\displaystyle\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[G,\Phi]\\
\chi(G)=-n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)|}\sum_{\alpha\in{\operatorname{Aut}}(G,\Phi)}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi)}){\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}})\left(\prod_{e\in E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{e})\right)(-1)^{e_{\alpha}(\Phi)}.

Here, we sum over all forested graphs
[G,\Phi]
of Euler characteristic
\chi(G)=-n
.

This statement is the odd version of Theorem
2.1
.
As in Section
3
we can produce a formula for
\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}
by counting forests and matchings separately before combining both expressions to give a counting formula for forested graphs. In the odd case, we have to change a couple of signs in the derivation to accommodate the additional
{\operatorname{sign}}
factors in the statement above.

We define an odd version of our forest generating function
\mathbf{F}
in Eq. (
15
) using the same notation and by weighting each odd permutation of the connected components of the forest with a sign:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{odd}}}(u,{\overline{x}})&=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{(\Phi,\gamma)\in\mathcal{AF}[s]}{\operatorname{sign}}(\gamma_{H_{0}(\Phi)})(-1)^{e_{\gamma}(\Phi)}x^{\gamma}u^{s-2k(\Phi)},\end{aligned}

where
{\operatorname{sign}}(\gamma_{H_{0}(\Phi)})
is the sign of the permutation induced by
\gamma
on the connected components of
\Phi
. This factor will account for the
{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{H_{0}(\Phi)})
term in Theorem
5.4
.
Using the same argument as for Proposition
3.11
, but accounting for a sign flip for each even cycle on the set of components or equivalently, by setting
y_{k}=(-1)^{k+1}
in Eq. (
14
), results in

Proposition 5.5
.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{odd}}}(u,{\overline{x}})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k+1}u^{-2k}\frac{{\mathbf{V}}((u\cdot{\overline{x}})_{[k]})}{k}\right).

To account for the factor

{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}})\left(\prod_{e\in E_{G}\setminus E_{\Phi}}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{e})\right)
in Theorem
5.4
, we have to use a signed version of the matchings that we introduced in Section
3.3
.
A permutation
\alpha\in\SG_{2n}
and a fixed-point free
involution on
\{1,\ldots,2n\}
such that
\alpha\circ\iota=\iota\circ\alpha
give rise to a permutation
\alpha_{\iota}
of the orbits of
\iota
and a set of
n
permutations
\alpha_{e_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{e_{n}}
that permute the elements of each individual orbit of
\iota
. We define

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\iota\circ\alpha=\alpha\circ\iota}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{\iota})\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha_{e_{k}}),

where we sum over all such pairs
\iota
and
\alpha
.

Using these definitions with the argument for Proposition
3.12
and Theorem
5.4
, we get

Proposition 5.6
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\lambda}[u^{2n}x^{\lambda}]{\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{odd}}}(u,{\overline{x}})=\ee^{\mathrm{odd}}_{n}.

Following the argument in Section
3.3
for the derivation of a formula for
\eta_{\lambda}
, the cycle index series for the signed version of a matching of two points is given by

{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{odd}}}({\overline{x}})=\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{(\phi,\alpha)\in\mathcal{AE}[2]}{\operatorname{sign}}(\alpha)x^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}\right).

As in Section
3.3
, we can use Proposition
3.2
to get a generating function for the numbers
\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\alpha}
:

Lemma 5.7
.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{\alpha\in\SG_{2n}}\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k}\left(x_{k}^{2}-x_{2k}\right)\right).

Proof.

Set
y_{k}=(-1)^{k+1}
after applying Proposition
3.2
as each even cycle of the permutation induced on the components is counted with a minus sign this way and the sign of a permutation is equal to
(-1)^{\text{\# of even cycles}}
.
∎

Repeating the computation for Corollary
3.5
while accounting for the changed signs we find,

Corollary 5.8
.

If
\lambda=[1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\ldots n^{m_{n}}]
, then

\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\lambda}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k,m_{k}},

where

\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k,2s}=\begin{cases}k^{s}(2s-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
\sum_{r=0}^{s}(-1)^{r}\binom{2s}{2r}k^{r}(2r-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is even}\end{cases}

and

\eta^{\mathrm{odd}}_{k,2s+1}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $k$ is odd}\\
(-1)^{k/2}\sum_{r=0}^{s}(-1)^{r}\binom{2s+1}{2r}k^{r}(2r-1)!!&\text{if $k$ is even}\end{cases}.
