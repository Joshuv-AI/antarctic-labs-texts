1.
Introduction and main results

In the present paper we construct multiple solutions to the nonlocal Liouville equation

(1.1)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u=(1+\varepsilon\kappa(x))e^{u}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}.

Here,
\kappa\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\alpha>0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is a small parameter and
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the half-Laplace operator, defined for a bounded and sufficiently smooth function
u
as

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u(x):=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mbox{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}\,dy=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(x-\delta,x+\delta)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}\,dy.

The motivation for considering a problem of the form (
1.1
) is twofold. Firstly, this problem naturally arises in Riemannian Geometry. Indeed, if we consider the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y>0\}
equipped with the standard Euclidean metric
g_{0}
and assume that
u
is a solution to (
1.1
), the harmonic extension
U
of
u
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
provides a flat conformal metric
g_{1}=e^{2U}g_{0}
with prescribed geodesic curvature
1+\varepsilon\kappa(x)
on
\mathbb{R}\equiv\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. We refer to the introductions of
[
9
,
8
,
20
,
11
]
and the references therein for more details on the geometric context. More recently, problem (
1.1
) was linked to the analysis of soliton solutions to the Calogero-Moser derivative NLS

(CM)

{\rm{i}}\partial_{t}\psi=-\partial_{xx}\psi+V\psi-(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}(|\psi|^{2})\psi+\frac{1}{4}|\psi|^{4}\psi,\quad\textup{ in }[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}.

Here,
V
is an external given potential and
\psi
is complex valued. Equation (
CM
) with
V(x)=x^{2}
and
V(x)\equiv 0
was introduced in the physics literature as a formal continuum limit of classical Calogero-Moser systems, see
[
1
,
2
]
. From a rigorous point of view, (
CM
) has been very recently analyzed in
[
16
]
. We refer as well to
[
3
]
for recent results concerning both (
L
) and (
CM
). In particular, assuming that
K
is positive, symmetric-decreasing and satisfies suitable regularity and decay assumptions, the authors prove existence and uniqueness of solution to (
L
), as well as some qualitative properties of the solution. In this paper, we intend to establish a sort of counterpart of this, namely that if
K
changes monotonicity one may have multiple solutions.

For
\mu>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, let us introduce

\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}(x):=\log\left(\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\right),

and stress that the functions
\{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}\}_{\mu,\xi}
are the unique solutions to

(1.2)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}U=e^{U}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},\qquad\textup{ satisfying}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{U}dx<+\infty.

We refer to
[
13
, Theorem 1.8]
for a proof (see also
[
12
]
). Note also that, in
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
, one can find an alternative proof that uses the link between (
1.2
) and (
CM
) with
V\equiv 0
. This family of solutions are the so-called
bubbles
.

Accordingly, we will look for solutions to (
1.1
) of the form

(1.3)

u=\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}+\phi,

with
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
to be determined and small in a suitable sense.

As we shall see, the existence of such solutions is bound to the properties of the harmonic extension
\Gamma
of the function
\kappa
to the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\big\{{(\xi,\mu):\xi\in\mathbb{R},\,\mu>0}\big\}
. Define

(1.4)

\Gamma(\xi,\mu):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\kappa(\xi+\mu y)}{1+y^{2}}\,dy\quad\mbox{for }\xi\in\mathbb{R},\ \mu>0.

It is well-known that
\Gamma
is the unique bounded harmonic function in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
which agrees with
\kappa
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\big\{{(\xi,0):\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\big\}
—see, e.g.,
[
10
]
. Let us also stress that the function
\Gamma
is smooth inside
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Moreover, we will say that
(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is a non-degenerate critical point for
\Gamma
if

\nabla\Gamma(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\det D^{2}\Gamma(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})\neq 0.

Having at hand
\Gamma
, our first main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be a non-degenerate critical point for
\Gamma
. Then, there exist two constants
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that the following holds true. For every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
, there exists a solution
u=u_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.1
) in the form (
1.3
), for some
\xi=\xi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu=\mu_{\varepsilon}\in(0,+\infty)
such that
(\xi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})\to(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and with
\phi=\phi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C\varepsilon
.

Theorem
1.1
establishes the existence of solutions to (
1.1
) provided that non-degenerate critical points for the harmonic extension of
\kappa
to the upper half-plane do exist. It is natural to wonder whether this assumption is necessary. The next result shows that the condition on
\Gamma
is almost necessary for the existence of solutions to (
1.1
) of the form (
1.3
). Note that we allow here the remainder function
\phi
to have logarithmic growth at infinity. In other words, we measure its smallness in the weighted
L^{\infty}
norm

\|g\|_{L_{\log,\xi}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\frac{|g(x)|}{\log(2+|x-\xi|)},\quad\textup{ for }g\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\xi,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and let
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
be a sequence of solutions to (
1.1
) of the form
u_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{U}_{\mu_{\varepsilon},\,\xi_{\varepsilon}}+\phi_{\varepsilon}
with
\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\log,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}
, for some constants
C_{0}>0
and
\alpha>1/2
independent of
\varepsilon
, and with
(\xi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})\rightarrow(\xi,\mu)
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Then,
\nabla\Gamma(\xi,\mu)=0
.

Taking into account the connection established in
[
3
, Proposition 4.1]
, as an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we get the existence of ground-state soliton solutions to

(1.5)

{\rm{i}}\partial_{t}\psi=-\partial_{xx}\psi+V_{\varepsilon}\psi-(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}(|\psi|^{2})\psi+\frac{1}{4}|\psi|^{4}\psi,\quad\textup{ in }[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

with

V_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\frac{(\varepsilon\kappa^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon\kappa(x))^{2}}.

More precisely, we have the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be a non-degenerate critical point for
\Gamma
. Then, there exist two constants
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that the following holds true. For every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
, there exists a ground-state soliton solution
\psi=\psi_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.5
) in the form

(1.6)

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon\kappa(x))e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon}}(x)+\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)}}\,,

for some
\xi=\xi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu=\mu_{\varepsilon}\in(0,+\infty)
such that
(\xi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})\to(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and with
\phi=\phi_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C\varepsilon
.

In virtue of Theorem
1.1
, our analysis is reduced to find assumptions on
\kappa
implying the existence of (multiple) non-degenerate critical points for
\Gamma
. We analyze
\Gamma
in detail in Sections
4
and
5
. However, we would like to illustrate here what kind of multiplicity results we are able to prove. To that end, we need to introduce the weighted
C^{2}
space

(1.7)

C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{\kappa\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ :\ \|\kappa\|_{C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})}<+\infty\right\},

equipped with the norm

(1.8)

\|\kappa\|_{C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})}:=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\kappa(x)|+\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(1+|x|^{2+\beta}\right)|\kappa^{\prime}(x)|+\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\kappa^{\prime\prime}(x)|,

for
\beta\in(0,1)
. We then assume that
\kappa\in C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
and that furthermore satisfies:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\circ\

\displaystyle\,\textup{There exists }R_{0}>0\textup{ such that }x\kappa^{\prime}(x)<0\textup{ for all }|x|>R_{0};

(1.10)

\displaystyle\circ\

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\kappa^{\prime}(x)x\,dx<0.

Under these stronger assumptions on
\kappa
we (in particular) prove the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
\kappa_{0}\in C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\beta>0
satisfies (
1.9
) and (
1.10
). Given
N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, suppose that
\kappa_{0}
has exactly
N+1
global maxima and
N
global minima, that these extrema are all non-degenerate, and that
\kappa_{0}
has no other stationary point. Then, there exists a sequence
\{\kappa_{j}\}\subset C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
converging to
\kappa_{0}
in
C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
for which the following holds true. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\varepsilon_{j}\in(0,1)
such that equation (
1.1
) (with
\kappa=\kappa_{j}
) has at least
N
solutions of the form (
1.3
) for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{j}]
.

Arguing as for Corollary
1.3
one can easily obtain a multiplicity result concerning (
1.5
). To state such as result, we need some notation. We define

V_{j,\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\frac{(\varepsilon\kappa_{j}^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon\kappa_{j}(x))^{2}},\quad\textup{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N},\ \varepsilon\in(0,1)\textup{ and }\kappa_{j}\in C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R}).

Corollary 1.5
.

Assume that
\kappa_{0}\in C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\beta>0
satisfies (
1.9
) and (
1.10
). Given
N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, suppose that
\kappa_{0}
has exactly
N+1
global maxima and
N
global minima, that these extrema are all non-degenerate, and that
\kappa_{0}
has no other stationary point. Then, there exists a sequence
\{\kappa_{j}\}\subset C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
converging to
\kappa_{0}
in
C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
for which the following holds true. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\varepsilon_{j}\in(0,1)
such that (
1.5
) (with
V_{\varepsilon}=V_{j,\varepsilon}
) has at least
N
solutions of the form (
1.6
) for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{j}]
.

Remark 1.6
.

(a)

If
\kappa\in C_{*,\beta}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\beta>0
and

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(1+|x|^{1+\beta}\right)|\kappa(x)|<+\infty,

then (
1.10
) is equivalent to the somewhat more natural assumption

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\kappa(x)\,dx>0.

(b)

Naturally, in Theorem
1.4
and Corollary
1.5
, it might happen that
\kappa_{0}
itself gives rise to
N
solutions—i.e., that
\kappa_{j}=\kappa_{0}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. The realization of this possibility is connected with the harmonic extension
\Gamma_{0}
of
\kappa_{0}
(as defined in (
1.4
)) having non-degenerate critical points. See Section
4
and in particular the proof of Theorem
1.4
included there for more details.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is perturbative and uses a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction method. This kind of approach was already used by Ambrosetti, García Azorero & Peral
[
5
]
and Grossi & Prashanth
[
17
]
to deal with the higher dimensional counterparts of (
1.1
). However, our approach is closer to the one recently used in
[
11
]
by the second and third authors. Actually, despite that both the problems under consideration and the kind of results obtained are different, our analysis here and the one performed in
[
11
]
share some technical elements.

As already indicated, having at hand Theorem
1.1
, we are led to analyze the set of critical points for the harmonic extension of
\kappa
to the upper half-plane, namely the set of critical points for the function
\Gamma
given in (
1.4
). Roughly speaking, we want to count the number of critical points of
\Gamma
using only assumptions on
\kappa
. This is a rather classical problem. Indeed, using a conformal map, we could reformulate such problem as follows: given
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, let
u
be the unique solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u=0,\quad&&\textup{ in }\mathbb{D},\\
&u=g,&&\textup{ on }\mathbb{S}^{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

We would like to count the number of critical points of
u
in
\mathbb{D}
using only assumptions on
g
. Despite its apparent simplicity, the answer to this problem is far from being complete. In Theorem
4.5
and Proposition
4.7
we obtain some partial answers using a topological degree approach. However, as highlighted by some of the examples in Section
5
, the general picture is far from being clear.

Organization of the paper

The remaining of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. More precisely, in Subsection
2.1
, we develop the required linear theory for the linearized operator at
\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}
; in Subsection
2.2
, we use these results to address a projected version of the original nonlinear problem; and, in Subsection
2.3
, we deal with the finite-dimensional reduced problem concluding thus the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Section
3
is devoted to show that the condition on
\Gamma
in Theorem
1.1
is almost necessary, namely to prove Theorem
1.2
. In Section
4
we analyze the function
\Gamma
introduced in (
1.4
) and prove a few results of independent interest concerning
\Gamma
—including a genericity result, see Lemma
4.10
. Those results lead to the proof of Theorem
1.4
. In Section
5
we provide some explicit examples of functions
\kappa
such that (
1.1
) has or does not have solutions of the form (
1.3
). We also make some comments and remarks comparing our results with previous ones in the literature. The paper is closed by an appendix where we provide an alternative proof of a statement contained in Lemma
4.10
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3

It is immediate to see that
u
of the form (
1.3
) solves (
1.1
) if and only if
\phi
is a solution of

(2.1)

L_{\mu,\xi}\,\phi=-\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{N}(\phi)\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle L_{\mu,\xi}\,\phi

\displaystyle:=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi-e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}}\phi,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\mu,\xi}

\displaystyle:=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}-(1+\varepsilon\kappa(x))e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\phi)=\mathcal{N}_{\mu,\xi}[\phi]

\displaystyle:=e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}}\left\{\left(e^{\phi}-1-\phi\right)+\varepsilon\kappa(x)\left(e^{\phi}-1\right)\right\}.

As it turns out, the smallness of
\phi
will be evaluated in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. On the other hand, to measure the error term
\mathcal{E}
and the nonlinear term
\mathcal{N}
we will make use of the following weighted
L^{\infty}
norm, defined on a function
g\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
by

(2.2)

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,(1+|x-\xi|)^{1+\sigma}|g(x)|,

with
\sigma\in(0,1)
fixed. We have the following estimate of the size of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{N}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
\mu_{0}\geqslant 1
, there exist two constants
C_{1}>0
, depending only on
\mu_{0}
and
\sigma
, and
C_{2}>0
, also depending on
\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, such that, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu\in\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
,

(2.3)

\left\|\mathcal{E}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon

and

(2.4)

\left\|\mathcal{N}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\varepsilon\right),

for all
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
. Moreover,

(2.5)

\left\|\mathcal{N}(\phi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{2}\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\varepsilon\right),

for all
\phi,\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})},\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}
solves (
1.2
), we see that

(2.6)

\mathcal{E}(x)=\frac{2\mu\varepsilon\kappa(x)}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}},

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. From this identity, estimate (
2.3
) follows at once. To obtain (
2.4
), we observe that
|e^{\phi}-1|\leqslant e^{|\phi|}|\phi|
and
|e^{\phi}-1-\phi|\leqslant\frac{e^{|\phi|}}{2}|\phi|^{2}
for every
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence,

(2.7)

|\mathcal{N}(\phi)(x)|\leqslant\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{e^{|\phi(x)|}}{2}\,|\phi(x)|^{2}+\varepsilon\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}e^{|\phi(x)|}|\phi(x)|\bigg).

In particular, if
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
, we get

|\mathcal{N}(\phi)(x)|\leqslant\frac{8\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\Big(|\phi(x)|^{2}+\varepsilon\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}|\phi(x)|\Big).

which gives (
2.4
). Estimate (
2.5
) is analogous.

2.1.
Linear theory

We study in this subsection the linearized operator
L_{\mu,\xi}
. We begin by analyzing its kernel. Since
\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}
is a solution of (
1.2
), it is clear that both derivatives

(2.8)

Z_{0,\mu,\xi}(x):=\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}(x)=\frac{1}{\mu}-\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}},\quad Z_{1,\mu,\xi}(x):=\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}(x)=\frac{2(x-\xi)}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}

annihilate
L_{\mu,\xi}
. We recall the following known non-degeneracy result, showing that these two functions actually span the bounded kernel of
L_{\mu,\xi}
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
14
,
21
,
11
]
)
.

If
Z\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a solution of
L_{\mu,\xi}\,Z=0
in
\mathbb{R}
, then
Z
is a linear combination of
Z_{0,\mu,\xi}
and
Z_{1,\mu,\xi}
.

Let
\bar{R}>1
be fixed and
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be an even cutoff function satisfying
0\leqslant\chi\leqslant 1
in
\mathbb{R}
,
\chi=1
in
[-\bar{R},\bar{R}]
, and
\chi=0
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\bar{R}-1,\bar{R}+1)
. We set
\chi_{\xi}:=\chi(\cdot-\xi)
. Also, we fix two numbers
\mu_{0}\geqslant 1
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Finally, we consider the space of functions defined by

L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R}):=\Big\{{g\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}):\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}<+\infty}\Big\},

with
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}
as in (
2.2
). The rest of this subsection is devoted to prove the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Given any
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a unique bounded weak solution
\phi
of

(2.9)

L_{\mu,\xi}\,\phi=g+\sum_{i=0}^{1}d_{i}\chi_{\xi}Z_{i,\mu,\xi}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},

for some uniquely determined
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfying the orthogonality conditions

(2.10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi\chi_{\xi}Z_{i,\mu,\xi}\,dx=0\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1.

Moreover,

(2.11)

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})},

for some constant
C_{0}>0
depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
, and
\bar{R}
.

Clearly, after a translation we may reduce to the case
\xi=0
. In the remaining of the subsection we will always assume this to hold.

To prove Proposition
2.3
, we begin with the following a priori estimate.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exist two constants
R_{0}>0
and
C\geqslant 1
, depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
, and
\bar{R}
such that the following holds true. If
R\geqslant R_{0}
and
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a weak solution of

\begin{cases}L_{\mu,0}\,\phi=g&\quad\mbox{in }(-R,R),\\
\phi=0&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R,R),\end{cases}

satisfying

(2.12)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi\chi Z_{i,\mu,0}\,dx=0\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1,

with
\mu\in\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
and
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})
, then it holds

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume that there exists a sequence
\{R_{k}\}
of positive numbers such that
R_{k}\rightarrow+\infty
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
and for which the following holds. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\mu_{k}\in\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
, a function
g_{k}\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})
, and a bounded solution
\phi_{k}
of

(2.13)

\begin{cases}L_{\mu_{k},0}\,\phi_{k}=g_{k}&\quad\mbox{in }(-R_{k},R_{k}),\\
\phi_{k}=0&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R_{k},R_{k}),\end{cases}

satisfying

(2.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{k}\chi Z_{i,\mu_{k},0}\,dx=0\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1,

but for which

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}>k\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}.

By the linearity of the problem, we may further assume that

(2.15)

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\frac{1}{k}.

Let
\rho\geqslant 1
be fixed. We claim that

(2.16)

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C\left(\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}\left((-\rho,\rho)\right)}+\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\right),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
k
and provided
\rho
is sufficiently large but also independent of
k
. This follows from a barrier argument. Consider the function
w_{\sigma}(x):=(1+x^{2})^{-\frac{\sigma}{2}}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. According to
[
11
, Lemma 3.4]
, there exist two constants
c_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\rho_{0}\geqslant 1
, depending only on
\sigma
, such that

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}w_{\sigma}(x)\leqslant-\frac{c_{0}}{|x|^{1+\sigma}}\quad\mbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\rho_{0},\rho_{0}].

From this, it is easy to see that the function

\overline{\phi}_{k}:=\left(\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}\left((-\rho,\rho)\right)}+\frac{2}{c_{0}}\,\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\left(2-w_{\sigma}\right)

satisfies

\begin{cases}L_{\mu_{k},0}\,\overline{\phi}_{k}\geqslant g_{k}&\quad\mbox{in }(-R_{k},R_{k})\setminus[-\rho,\rho],\\
\overline{\phi}_{k}\geqslant\phi_{k}&\quad\mbox{in }[-\rho,\rho]\cup\big({\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R_{k},R_{k})}\big),\end{cases}

provided
\rho\geqslant\rho_{0}
is sufficiently large, in dependence of
\mu_{0}
and
\sigma
only. Since the operator
L_{\mu_{k},0}
enjoys the maximum principle outside of a sufficiently large neighborhood of the origin (see
[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
), up to taking
\rho
even larger we conclude that
\phi_{k}\leqslant\overline{\phi}_{k}
in the whole
\mathbb{R}
. Since an analogous argument could be made to obtain a lower bound for
\phi_{k}
(using
-\overline{\phi}_{k}
as subsolution), we conclude that

|\phi_{k}(x)|\leqslant\overline{\phi}_{k}(x)\leqslant\frac{4}{c_{0}}\left(\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}\left((-\rho,\rho)\right)}+\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\quad\mbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}

which is (
2.16
).

From (
2.16
) and (
2.15
), we deduce that

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}\left((-\rho,\rho)\right)}\geqslant 2\delta,

for all
k
’s sufficiently large and for some
\delta>0
independent of
k
. Hence, for any large
k
there exists a point
p_{k}\in(-\rho,\rho)
at which
|\phi_{k}(p_{k})|\geqslant\delta
. Notice that, by elliptic regularity, the
\phi_{k}
’s are continuous functions satisfying, for any fixed
r>0
,

\|\phi_{k}\|_{C^{\alpha}([-r,r])}\leqslant C

for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and some constant
C>0
independent of
k
and for
k
sufficiently large (in particular, large enough to have that
R_{k}\geqslant r+1
). By compactness and a diagonal argument, we conclude that, up to a subsequence,
\{\phi_{k}\}
converges to a function
\phi_{\infty}
in
C^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
\alpha^{\prime}\in(0,1)
. Up to taking a further subsequence, we clearly also have that
p_{k}\rightarrow p_{\infty}\in[-\rho,\rho]
and
\mu_{k}\rightarrow\mu_{\infty}\in\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
. Recalling (
2.13
), (
2.14
), and (
2.15
), we see that
\phi_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is such that

\begin{cases}L_{\mu_{\infty},0}\,\phi_{\infty}=0&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\infty}\chi Z_{i,\mu_{\infty},0}\,dx=0&\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1,\\
|\phi_{\infty}(p)|\geqslant\delta.\end{cases}

By Lemma
2.2
, we conclude that

\phi_{\infty}=A_{0}Z_{0,\mu_{\infty},0}+A_{1}Z_{1,\mu_{\infty},0}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},

for some constants
A_{0},A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. But then, the orthogonality conditions yield
A_{0}=A_{1}=0
and thus that
\phi_{\infty}=0
in
\mathbb{R}
, contradicting the fact that
|\phi_{\infty}(p)|\geqslant\delta
. The proof is complete.
∎

Thanks to the previous a priori estimate, we may now solve the Dirichlet problem in bounded intervals. We begin with the following lemma, which provides to useful auxiliary functions modeled upon
Z_{0,\mu,0}
and
Z_{1,\mu,0}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Given
\mu_{0}\geqslant 1
, there exist two constants
R_{1}>2\mu_{0}
and
C\geqslant 1
, depending only on
\mu_{0}

\tilde{z}_{0},\tilde{z}_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

\tilde{z}_{0}
even and
\tilde{z}_{1}
odd, satisfying

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=Z_{0,\mu,0}\,\,\mbox{ in }[-2\mu_{0},2\mu_{0}],

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1,\mu,0}\,\,\mbox{ in }[-r,r],

\displaystyle|L_{\mu,0}\,\tilde{z}_{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\log r}\left(\frac{\log(2+|x|)}{(1+|x|)^{2}}+\frac{r}{(r+|x|)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle|L_{\mu,0}\,\tilde{z}_{1}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{(1+|x|)(r+|x|)}

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover,
\tilde{z}_{0}\rightarrow Z_{0,\mu,0}
and
\tilde{z}_{1}\rightarrow Z_{1,\mu,0}
uniformly on compact sets as

.

Proof.

The two functions were constructed in
[

, Section 3]
.

Specifically, we can take

, Lemma 3.8]
corresponding to

The conclusion on the locally uniform convergence to the

Lemma 2.6
.

There exist two constants
R_{0}>0
and
C\geqslant 1
, depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
, and
\bar{R}
, such that the following holds true. For every
R\geqslant R_{0}
and
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a unique weak solution
\phi
to the problem

(2.17)

\begin{cases}L_{\mu,0}\,\phi=g+\sum_{i=0}^{1}d_{i}\chi Z_{i,\mu,0}&\quad\mbox{in }(-R,R),\\
\phi=0&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R,R),\end{cases}

for some uniquely determined
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, which satisfies the orthogonality conditions (
2.12
). Furthermore, it holds

(2.18)

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+|d_{0}|+|d_{1}|\leqslant C\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

We begin by establishing the
L^{\infty}
estimate (
2.18
). Let
\phi
be a weak solution of (
2.17
) satisfying (
2.10
), for some
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. By elliptic estimates,
\phi
is continuous up to the boundary and, in particular, bounded. Hence, Lemma
2.4
yields the estimate

(2.19)

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C\left(\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}+|d_{0}|+|d_{1}|\right),

for some constant
C\geqslant 1
depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
,
\bar{R}
and provided
R
is sufficiently large, also in dependence of these quantities alone. To control the constants
|d_{0}|
and
|d_{1}|
, we take advantage of the two functions obtained in Lemma
2.5
. By testing the equation against the function
\tilde{z}_{1}
, corresponding to
r=R-1
and taking
R
sufficiently large, we get that

d_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi Z_{1,\mu,0}^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big({\phi L_{\mu,0}\,\tilde{z}_{1}-g\tilde{z}_{1}}\Big)\,dx,

where we used that
\tilde{z}_{1}=Z_{1,\mu,0}
on the support of
\chi
. From this and the properties of
\tilde{z}_{1}
, we easily find that

\displaystyle|d_{1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{\mu,0}\,\tilde{z}_{1}(x)|\,dx+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\tilde{z}_{1}(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{1+\sigma}}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dx}{(1+|x|)(R+|x|)}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dx}{(1+|x|)^{1+\sigma}}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(R^{-\tau}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

for any fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
and with
C>0
depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\bar{R}
,
\sigma
, and
\tau
. Testing the equation with
\tilde{z}_{0}
instead, we obtain that

\displaystyle|d_{0}|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{\mu,0}\,\tilde{z}_{0}(x)|\,dx+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\tilde{z}_{0}(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{1+\sigma}}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\frac{\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{\log R}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\log(2+|x|)}{(1+|x|)^{2}}+\frac{R}{(R+|x|)^{2}}\right)dx+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dx}{(1+|x|)^{1+\sigma}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\frac{\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{\log R}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

By combining these two estimates with (
2.19
) and taking
R
sufficiently large to reabsorb the
L^{\infty}
norm of
\phi
to the left-hand side, we are led to (
2.18
).

Note that (
2.18
) yields in particular the uniqueness of a triple
(\phi,d_{0},d_{1})
for a given
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})
. We now address its existence. Let

H:=\left\{\phi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}):\phi=0\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R,R)\mbox{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi\chi Z_{i,\mu,0}\,dx=0\mbox{ for }i=0,1\right\}.

Thanks to the fractional Poincaré inequality,
H
is a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{H}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\big({\phi(x)-\phi(y)}\big)\big({\psi(x)-\psi(y)}\big)}{(x-y)^{2}}\,dxdy.

It is then not hard to see that
\phi
is a weak solution of problem (
2.17
) for some
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and fulfilling the orthogonality conditions (
2.10
) if and only if
\phi\in H
and it satisfies

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{H}-\int_{-R}^{R}e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,0}}\phi\psi\,dx=\int_{-R}^{R}g\psi\,dx\quad\mbox{for all }\psi\in H.

Thanks to estimate (
2.18
) (applied with
g=0
), one can easily show via Fredholm’s alternative that this problem has a unique solution
\phi\in H
for all
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,0}(\mathbb{R})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

By letting
R\rightarrow+\infty
, we obtain the unique solvability of the problem in the whole real line, namely we establish Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

According to Lemma
2.6
, let
(\phi^{k},d_{0}^{k},d_{1}^{k})
be the unique solution of problem (
2.17
) for
R=k\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, with
\phi^{k}
satisfying the orthogonality conditions (
2.10
). Thanks to the bound (
2.18
) and standard regularity estimates for the half-Laplacian, we see that, up to a subsequence,
\{d_{0}^{k}\}_{k}
and
\{d_{1}^{k}\}_{k}
respectively converge to two constants
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\phi^{k}\rightarrow\phi
in
C^{\alpha}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Clearly,
\phi
is a weak solution to problem (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) satisfying estimate (
2.11
). Let now
(\hat{\phi},\hat{d}_{0},\hat{d}_{1})
be another bounded solution. Then, the difference
\psi:=\phi-\hat{\phi}
solves

\begin{cases}L_{\mu,0}\,\psi=\sum_{i=0}^{1}e_{i}\chi Z_{i,\mu,0}&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi\chi Z_{i,\mu,0}\,dx=0&\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1,\end{cases}

with

. By testing the equation for

against the functions
\tilde{z}_{0}
,
\tilde{z}_{1}
of Lemma
2.5
and arguing as in the proof of Lemma

, one easily obtains that

. Hence,
\psi
is an entire solution to

, orthogonal to
\chi Z_{0,\mu,0}
and
\chi Z_{1,\mu,0}
. The nondegeneracy of

\psi=0

. The proof of the proposition is thus complete.
∎

2.2.
Nonlinear theory

Proposition 2.7
.

There exist two constants
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and
C_{\sharp}>0
, depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
,
\bar{R}
, and
\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, such that, if
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
,
\mu\in\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
, and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, then there exists a unique bounded solution
\phi
of

(2.20)

L_{\mu,\xi}\,\phi=-\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{N}(\phi)+\sum_{i=0}^{1}d_{i}\chi_{\xi}Z_{i,\mu,\xi}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},

for some uniquely determined
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, which satisfies the orthogonality conditions (
2.10
) and the bound

(2.21)

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{\sharp}\,\varepsilon.

Proof.

Given a function
g\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
, let
L_{\mu,\xi}^{-1}(g)
be the unique bounded weak solution of problem (
2.9
)–(
2.10
), for some
d_{0},d_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, as given by Proposition
2.3
. Thanks to estimate (
2.11
), we have that
L_{\mu,\xi}^{-1}:L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is a bounded linear operator and it holds

(2.22)

\|L_{\mu,\xi}^{-1}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})},

for some constant
C_{0}>0
depending only on
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
, and
\bar{R}
.

Notice that
\mathcal{E}\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
, thanks to (
2.3
) of Lemma
2.1
. Let then
\tau>0
and consider the closed ball

B_{\tau}:=\Big\{{\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\tau\varepsilon}\Big\}.

If
\phi\in B_{\tau}
with
\tau\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, then also
\mathcal{N}(\phi)\in L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
, by (
2.4
). By virtue of this, we may recast the problem of finding a bounded weak solution to (
2.20
) which satisfies the orthogonality conditions (
2.10
) as a fixed point problem for the map
T:B_{\tau}\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
defined by
T(\phi):=L_{\mu,\xi}^{-1}\left(-\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{N}(\phi)\right)
for
\phi\in B_{\tau}
.

In order to prove the existence of a fixed point in
B_{\tau}
, we employ the contraction lemma. To this aim, we shall show that

(2.23)

\|T(\phi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\tau\varepsilon\quad\mbox{for all }\phi\in B_{\tau}

and

(2.24)

\|T(\phi)-T(\psi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\quad\mbox{for all }\phi,\psi\in B_{\tau},

provided
\tau
is carefully chosen and
\varepsilon
is small enough. To verify (
2.23
), we recall (
2.3
), (
2.4
), (
2.22
), and deduce that, for
\phi\in B_{\tau}
,

\|T(\phi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\left\|-\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{N}(\phi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\tau(\tau+1)\varepsilon\right)\varepsilon,

with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
given by Lemma
2.1
. Consequently, (
2.23
) holds true, provided we take
\tau:=2C_{0}C_{1}
and
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, in dependence of
\mu_{0}
,
\sigma
,
\bar{R}
, and
\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
only. We now address (
2.24
). Given
\phi,\psi\in B_{\tau}
, using estimate (
2.5
) we find that

\|T(\phi)-T(\psi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}\left\|\mathcal{N}(\phi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\sigma,\xi}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{0}C_{2}\left(2\tau+1\right)\varepsilon\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})},

which gives (
2.24
) by taking
\varepsilon
small enough. In light of (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), the map
T
is a contraction of
B_{\tau}
into itself and thus it has a unique fixed point within

. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
2.7
gives the existence of a solution
\phi=\phi_{(\xi,\mu)}
to the projected equation (

) in correspondence to every

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\big\{{(\xi_{n},\mu_{n})}\big\}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\left[\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\mu_{0}\right]
be a sequence converging to some limit point

\phi_{(\xi_{n},\mu_{n})}\rightarrow\phi_{(\xi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Write simply
\phi_{n}:=\phi_{(\xi_{n},\mu_{n})}
and
\phi_{\infty}:=\phi_{(\xi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})}
. By estimate (
2.21
) of Proposition
2.7
, we know that
\|\phi_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{\sharp}\,\varepsilon
and
\|\phi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{\sharp}\,\varepsilon
for every

. Note that each
\phi_{n}
solves the equation

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi_{n}=e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu_{n},\xi_{n}}}\phi_{n}-\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{n},\xi_{n}}+\mathcal{N}_{\mu_{n},\xi_{n}}[\phi_{n}]+\sum_{i=0}^{1}d_{i,n}\chi_{\xi_{n}}Z_{i,\mu_{n},\xi_{n}}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},

for some
d_{0,n},d_{1,n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
(\xi_{n},\mu_{n})
converges to
(\xi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})
, it is not hard to show that the right-hand side of the above equation has
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
norm bounded uniformly in
n
. By this and standard elliptic estimates for
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we infer that
\|\phi_{n}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})}
is also uniformly bounded in
n
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. By Ascoli-Arzelà theorem,
\phi_{n}
thus converges to some
\hat{\phi}_{\infty}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}
. Clearly,
\hat{\phi}_{\infty}
satisfies

\begin{cases}L_{\mu_{\infty},\xi_{\infty}}\,\hat{\phi}_{\infty}=-\mathcal{E}_{\mu_{\infty},\xi_{\infty}}+\mathcal{N}_{\mu_{\infty},\xi_{\infty}}[\hat{\phi}_{\infty}]+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\hat{d}_{i,\infty}\chi_{\xi_{\infty}}Z_{i,\mu_{\infty},\xi_{\infty}}&\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R},\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{\phi}_{\infty}\chi_{\xi_{\infty}}Z_{i,\mu_{\infty},\xi_{\infty}}\,dx=0&\quad\mbox{for }i=0,1,\end{cases}

for some
\hat{d}_{0,\infty},\hat{d}_{1,\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
. However, since we also have that
\|\hat{\phi}_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{\sharp}\,\varepsilon
, the uniqueness granted by Proposition
2.7
enables us to conclude that
\hat{\phi}_{\infty}=\phi_{\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Finite dimensional reduction

Thanks to Proposition
2.7
, the problem of finding a solution
\phi
to (
2.1
) with
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=O(\varepsilon)
is thus reduced to finding
\mu>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
for which the constants

and

appearing in (

) are both equal to zero.

We have the following result, which connects the vanishing of the coefficients

’s to an equation for the gradient of the function

defined in (

).

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
(\xi,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,+\infty)
. Then,
d_{0}(\xi,\mu)=d_{1}(\xi,\mu)=0
if and only if

\nabla\Gamma(\xi,\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{N}(\phi)Z_{1,\mu,\xi}\,dx,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{N}(\phi)Z_{0,\mu,\xi}\,dx\right).

Proof.

In order to find an expression for
d_{0}

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

for some

Remark 4.2
.

As is well-known, the assumption
\kappa\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
ensures that the half-Laplacian of

is well-defined at any point. Indeed, note that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\kappa(x)-\kappa(x+y)-\kappa(x-y)}{y^{2}}\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)}\frac{dy}{y^{2}}+[\kappa^{\prime}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{(-1,1)}\frac{|y|^{1+\alpha}}{y^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant 8\|\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\,[\kappa^{\prime}]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

The proof is by direct calculation. We estimate the difference
\Gamma(\xi,\mu)-\kappa(\xi)
. Since, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R},\mu>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\mathcal{U}_{\mu,\xi}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\,dx=2\pi,

using a change of variable, we get that

\displaystyle\Gamma(\xi,\mu)-\kappa(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\kappa(\xi+\mu y)}{1+y^{2}}\,dy-\kappa(\xi)\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa(x)-\kappa(\xi))\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mbox{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa(x)-\kappa(\xi))\left(\frac{\mu}{(x-\xi)^{2}}-\frac{\mu^{3}}{(x-\xi)^{2}\left(\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}\right)}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\mu(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\kappa(\xi)+\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mbox{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\kappa(x)-\kappa(\xi)}{(x-\xi)^{2}}\frac{-\mu^{3}}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\,dx,\quad\textup{ for all }(\xi,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}.

On the other hand, arguing as in Remark
4.2
, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mbox{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\kappa(x)-\kappa(\xi)}{(x-\xi)^{2}}\frac{-\mu^{3}}{\mu^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\kappa(\xi)-\kappa(\xi+y)-\kappa(\xi-y)}{y^{2}}\frac{\mu^{3}}{\mu^{2}+y^{2}}\,dy=O(\mu^{1+\alpha}),

as
\mu\to 0^{+}
. Hence, the claim readily follows.

Remark 4.6

We refer to

Proof.

Let

Examples and remarks

In this section we provide some examples of functions
\kappa

Example 5.1
.

In order to produce bounded harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
having non-degenerate critical points it is useful to consider the standard conformal mapping

\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{D}:=\big\{{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}<1}\big\}

defined by setting

(5.1)

\Psi(\xi,\mu):=\left(\frac{2\xi}{\xi^{2}+(\mu+1)^{2}},\frac{\xi^{2}+\mu^{2}-1}{\xi^{2}+(\mu+1)^{2}}\right)\quad\mbox{for }(\xi,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

Then, a function
\Gamma
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
if and only if
\Gamma\circ\Psi^{-1}
is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
and
(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is a non-degenerate critical point for
\Gamma
if and only if
\Psi^{-1}(\xi_{\star},\mu_{\star})
is a non-degenerate critical point for
\Gamma\circ\Psi^{-1}
. Hence, to obtain bounded harmonic functions in the unbounded set
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
it suffices to find harmonic functions in bounded sets containing
\mathbb{D}
.
A class of functions that can be easily handled is that of harmonic polynomials. Following are two examples of harmonic polynomials generating admissible
\kappa
’s.

(a)

First, we consider the function
\gamma^{(1)}(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}+1
. Notice that
\gamma^{(1)}
is harmonic and has a unique critical point at the origin, which is non-degenerate. Consequently, the function

\Gamma^{(1)}(\xi,\mu):=\gamma^{(1)}(\Psi(\xi,\mu))=4\,\frac{2\xi^{2}+\mu(\xi^{2}+(\mu+1)^{2})}{(\xi^{2}+(\mu+1)^{2})^{2}}

is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and has a unique non-degenerate critical point at
(0,1)
. Its trace on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is the function

\kappa^{(1)}(\xi)=\Gamma^{(1)}(\xi,0)=\frac{8\xi^{2}}{(1+\xi^{2})^{2}}.

(b)

Next, we construct a harmonic polynomial with (at least) two symmetric non-degenerate critical points. A straightforward computation shows that

\gamma^{(2)}(x,y)=-2x^{4}-2y^{4}+12x^{2}y^{2}-4y^{3}+12x^{2}y+x^{2}-y^{2}-3y+10

is a harmonic polynomial, even with respect to
x
, and having non-degenerate critical points at
\left(-\frac{1}{2},0\right)
and
\left(\frac{1}{2},0\right)
. Pulling
\gamma^{(2)}
back via the mapping
\Psi
leads us to the admissible function

\kappa^{(2)}(\xi)=2\,\frac{75\xi^{6}-35\xi^{4}+25\xi^{2}+7}{(1+\xi^{2})^{4}},

whose harmonic extension
\Gamma^{(2)}
has non-degenerate critical points at
\left(-\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{5}\right)
and
\left(\frac{4}{5},\frac{3}{5}\right)
.

Example 5.2

Example 5.3

Let us consider, for

In order to count the critical points of

F(\xi_{0},\mu_{0},\kappa_{0})=0
. Note that it is enough to prove that the linear map

\kappa\mapsto\partial_{\kappa}F(\xi_{0},\mu_{0},\kappa_{0})[\kappa]\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

is onto. In particular, as
F
is linear in
\kappa
, the proof is reduced to finding two functions
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(A.1)

\partial_{\kappa}F(\xi_{0},\mu_{0},\kappa_{0})[\kappa_{1}]=(1,0)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\partial_{\kappa}F(\xi_{0},\mu_{0},\kappa_{0})[\kappa_{2}]=(0,1).

For
a_{1},a_{2}>0
, we set

h_{1}(y):=\frac{a_{1}}{1+y^{6}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad h_{2}(y):=\frac{a_{2}\,y}{1+y^{6}}.

Clearly, we can adjust
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
in a way that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h_{1}(y)}{1+y^{2}}\,dy=\pi,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h_{1}(y)y}{1+y^{2}}\,dy=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h_{2}(y)}{1+y^{2}}\,dy=0,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h_{2}(y)y}{1+y^{2}}\,dy=\pi.

Then, if
k_{i}
denotes any primitive of
h_{i}
, the functions
\kappa_{i}(x):=\mu_{0}k_{i}\!\left(\frac{x-\xi_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\right)
belong to the space
C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
and satisfy (
A.1
).

Thanks to Theorem
A.1
, we infer that the complement of the set
\mathscr{A}
is meager in
C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
. This being a Banach space, we conclude that
\mathscr{A}
is dense in
C^{2}_{*,\beta}(\mathbb{R})
—the complement of a meager set in a complete metric space is dense, as a consequence of Baire’s category theorem.
∎
