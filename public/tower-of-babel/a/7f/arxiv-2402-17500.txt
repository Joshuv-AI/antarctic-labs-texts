Predicting Instability in Complex Oscillator Networks: Limitations and Potentials of Network Measures and Machine Learning
Christian Nauck (equal contribution), Michael Lindner (equal contribution), Nora Molkenthin, Jürgen Kurths, Eckehard Schöll, Jörg Raisch, Frank Hellmann

Abstract
A central question of network science is how functional properties of systems arise from their structure. For networked dynamical systems, structure is typically quantified with network measures. A functional property that is of theoretical and practical interest for oscillatory systems is the stability of synchrony to localized perturbations. Recently, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been shown to predict this stability successfully; at the same time, network measures have struggled to paint a clear picture. Here we collect 46 relevant network measures and find that no small subset can reliably predict stability. The performance of GNNs can only be matched by combining all network measures and nodewise machine learning. However, unlike GNNs, this approach fails to extrapolate from network ensembles to several real power grid topologies. This suggests that correlations of network measures and function may be misleading, and that GNNs capture the causal relationship between structure and stability substantially better.



Introduction

Networks of coupled oscillators are indispensable for modeling natural and human-made systems. In fields as diverse as ecology, biology, neuroscience, physics, and engineering, important systems including the heart, the brain, firefly populations, chemical reactions, and power grids, are described as oscillators on complex networks
[
61
,
1
,
50
,
2
,
49
,
52
]
. The function of these systems is shaped in considerable parts by their connectivity, which is described by the network’s topology. The paradigmatic model used to understand how system function is shaped by network structure is the Kuramoto model
[
30
,
31
]
and its variants. These feature extremely rich collective dynamical behavior, such as chimera states, frequency clusters, isolated desynchronization, and spatial chaos
[
61
,
1
,
2
,
49
,
52
,
62
,
8
,
7
]
.

These collective phenomena are rooted in synchronization
[
50
]
. In some contexts, such as the brain, complete synchronization indicates dysfunction such as epilepsy. In others, such as power grids, it is a fundamental prerequisite for the system to work at all. Thus, a central question in the fields of Network Science and Complex Systems is how robust the synchronous state is, either with the aim to design controls to disrupt it, e.g., in the context of deep brain stimulation
[
63
]
, or to create a system that has favorable synchronization properties
[
70
,
40
,
41
,
65
,
6
]
(for power grids or neuronal networks).

In practice, the structure vs. function relationship is typically studied by considering correlations between network measures, which quantify network structure, and dynamical properties. Two prominent examples of this are explosive synchronization
[
22
]
, in which the degree determines the synchronization properties, and the probability that large localized perturbations desynchronize the network or lead to further failures
[
41
,
21
,
20
]
. The latter is of particular importance in the context of oscillator models inspired by power grids, because large localized perturbations typically cause single component failures. These, and the resulting desynchronization of the whole network via cascading failures, are the most common causes of large-scale blackouts
[
68
]
. Understanding the probability that such a desynchronization event occurs turns out to be a particularly challenging task for Network and Complex Systems Science
[
68
]
. Despite a decade of effort since
[
41
]
found that dead ends have unfavorable properties, no clear picture has emerged
[
25
,
47
,
27
]
.

Recently, an alternative approach to model function from structure has been developed: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which are machine learning (ML) architectures specifically adapted to working with data on graphs. GNNs and their generalizations work well in different domains, such as epidemic spreading via social networks
[
13
]
, material science
[
54
,
4
,
51
]
and molecular properties
[
3
]
.
Lately, they also succeeded in predicting the desynchronization probability in oscillator networks
[
44
,
46
,
45
]
. The performance reached by these GNNs approaches the level that would make them actually useful in real-world scenarios. However, it is challenging if not impossible to interpret GNNs and translate the results back to an understanding of the mechanisms in the system. The field of explainability methods for GNNs is still nascent,
[
72
,
32
,
55
]
, and existing methods are highly situational
[
36
]
.

ML methods are known to surpass hand-crafted-based methodologies in different fields, such as speech
[
42
]
and vision
[
60
,
73
]
, when sufficient data is available
[
34
]
. For human activity recognition, deep learning outperforms hand-crafted-based methods for in-distribution tasks, but the hand-crafted features achieve better out-of-distribution generalization
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we show that the Network Science approach, when augmented with ML methods (NetSciML), can reach comparable accuracy as GNNs on the challenging task of predicting the dynamic stability when training and evaluating on the same ensemble. Our NetSciML approach is based on the idea of using network measures as input for conventional ML methods. We refer to them by nodewise ML, because the ML model predicts the dynamic stability purely based on nodal features, whereas GNNs directly take the full graph as input, as illustrated in
Figure

1
.

NetSciML combines the benefits of ML while maintaining many of the intrinsic benefits of Network Science. NetSciML predictions can be explained in terms of the underlying network measures, and NetSciML is far more data efficient, in particular, its performance is relatively robust when not much data is available. By a critical comparison, we infer here a clear picture of which network measures contribute to particular instability predictions.

Figure 1:
The goal is the prediction of the dynamic stability (targets) based on power grid models (input). Whereas

(at the bottom) deal with the graph input directly, NetSciML models rely on network measures as inputs.

However, we also see that the approach based on Network Science struggles with providing insights that generalize across different network ensembles. For grids of different sizes, different measures are important. Apparently, the network measures do not capture the governing dynamic behavior, but can only be applied situationally. This is in marked contrast to GNNs, which achieve good predictions when evaluated on ensembles different from the one they were trained on. We conclude that GNNs arrive at their predictions in a genuinely different way than Network Science. The fact that GNNs generalize to new situations, whereas network measures do not, is evidence that GNNs capture the structure vs. function relationship in a more causal way than network measures. Hence, the question of how to extract causal insights from GNN predictions, should be considered a central question of Complex Systems and Network Science research.

Results

We begin by briefly reviewing the underlying oscillator model, the network ensembles and datasets of dynamic stability, as well as the performance of GNNs for predicting the latter. Furthermore, we introduce our collection of network measures, and our own combination of Network Science and Machine Learning (NetSciML).

We then provide the main results of studying network measures and NetSciML in the context of dynamic stability tasks.

Stability of Synchrony in the Kuramoto model

We first explain the challenge of understanding how network properties shape the stability of synchrony in power-grid inspired oscillator networks. Here, such networks are modeled by the paradigmatic second-order Kuramoto model
[
31
]
:

\ddot{\phi_{i}}=P_{i}-\alpha\dot{\phi_{i}}-\sum_{j}^{n}KA_{ij}\sin(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}),

(1)

where
\phi,\dot{\phi},\ddot{\phi}
denotes the phase angle and its time derivatives. The network’s topology is encoded in the adjacency matrix
A_{ij}
. To isolate the impact of the topology, a homogeneous bimodal parametrization is used:
P_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
is the driving power,
\alpha=0.1
is the damping coefficient, and
\Omega_{i}=P_{i}/\alpha
is the natural frequency of the oscillators. By using a relatively large overall coupling strength
K=9
, a synchronous state typically exists for this system; however, it is often not globally stable.

In
[
41
]
it was first suggested to use the probability that a large random perturbation localized at a node desynchronizes the system as a measure of the local stability of synchrony. This measure is called the single node basin stability (SNBS), since the probability
\text{SNBS}\in[0,1]
corresponds to the volume (measured with respect to the probability measure the perturbations are drawn from) of the basin of attraction of the synchronous state associated with the slice of the phase space at a single node.
Figure

2
shows such a slice for a node with relatively low stability.

Figure 2:
Basin landscape of a node of a 20-node grid with relatively low stability (
\mathrm{SNBS}\approx 0.67
). The color indicates the maximum absolute frequency deviation of all nodes at the end of the trajectory. Black initial conditions converge back to the synchronous state, others reach desynchronized states. SNBS is equal to the fraction of black points among all 10,000 perturbations.

Ensembles of power grid models and their dynamic stability

To understand the relationship between network measures and SNBS systematically, we use two random growth network ensembles, following the algorithm of
[
58
]
, with 20 and 100 nodes respectively. These ensembles are defined to mimic power grid properties. Details can be found in
Methods: Details on datasets of dynamic stability
. For these ensembles, a large dataset of networks, labeled with the SNBS, has been generated for the task of training ML methods
[
46
,
45
]
. The datasets consist of 10,000 networks each from the 20 node ensemble and the 100 nodes ensemble, as well as a synthetic network corresponding to the Texan power grid with 1,910 nodes.

We follow
[
45
]
and distinguish between two setups. First, we evaluate the performance of ML models that are trained and evaluated on the same ensemble of grids. We call this setup in-distribution tasks: tr20ev20, tr100ev100. The ML models are trained and evaluated on grids of the same size. Second, we analyze the generalization capabilities by training models on the small grids, evaluating the performance on the ensemble of the large grids. Hence, we predict dynamic stability of a medium-sized grid with 100 nodes when trained on small grids with 20 nodes, which is referred to by tr20ev100.

The ability to predict the dynamic stability of an ensemble of larger networks, or even generated by a different random process, can be interpreted as measuring how well the prediction captures underlying causal relationships, rather than simply fitting statistical relationships specific to the ensemble. In power grid applications, this ability is crucial for practical importance. The computational cost of the dynamic simulations grows at least quadratically with the size
n
of the studied grid. Using ML methods for prediction of dynamic stability becomes useful for real-world application, if they can be trained on datasets of small networks, which are easy to simulate, while still performing well on large, complex grids. However, the synthetic grid models will never fully reflect the situation in a real grid. If we want to use synthetic networks to study real grids, performing well on topologies that only resemble the studied ensemble is essential.

To test this latter point, we also test the trained models on real topologies of different regions. The synthetic Texan grid (1910 nodes) was already introduced by
[
45
]
and is generated by a process that aims to ensure good electrical properties of the generated grid
[
11
]
. We further add the topologies of the transmission grids in France (146 nodes), Great Britain (GB, 120 nodes) & Spain (98 nodes) from
[
53
]
, and Germany (438 nodes) from
[
16
]
to also analyze the out-of-distribution generalization to real-world topologies. In the following we will discuss the tasks to extrapolate from 20 nodes to 100 nodes, tr20ev100, and the task to go from 100 nodes to the synthetic topology for Texas, tr100evTexas, and to the real topologies: tr100evFrance, tr100evGermany, tr100evGB and tr100evSpain.

Thus, we have three types of extrapolation: i) tr20ev100 extrapolates from small to medium grids, with the grids produced by the same algorithm. ii) The task tr100evTexas extrapolates from medium grids using one random growth algorithm, to a large grid produced by a different, more detailed one. iii) Finally, tr100ev[Country] extrapolates from synthetic topology to real topologies. Further information on extrapolating from grids of size 20 to Texas and real topologies is given in
Appendix

SM4
.

Dynamic stability from structure - NetSciML

To understand the full potential of network measures to capture the structure vs. function relationship, we study a wide range of measures on the three datasets, and we use them in combination with nodewise ML methods to predict SNBS. To collect a broad set of network measures, we performed an extensive literature review on network measures proposed to be relevant in this context. We augmented this list with our own measures, as well as further measures implemented in the software package NetworkX
[
19
]
for a total of 46 network measures (see
Figure

3
) and provide more details in the section Methods: NetSciML - Identification of relevant network measures.

We then use these network measures as input features for non-graph, but nodewise ML methods. These NetSciML models take a graph (with nodes labeled by
P_{i}
) as input, compute the set of network measures, and then use ML algorithms to predict nodewise SNBS
[
40
]
based on these network measures. The used ML models are Multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), gradient boosted trees (GBT) in a regression (GBR) or classification setup (GBC), as well as linear/logistic regression (linreg/logreg). For linear regression models, we also include interactions of the input features to model non-linear relations.

In the following, we provide results on the properties of the network measures in relationship to SNBS for the datasets considered. A central finding is that there is no single or small set of network measures that are sufficient to capture SNBS. Then we show that NetSciML can achieve excellent performance on in-distribution tasks while maintaining explainability and when working on small amounts of data. Finally, we observe that NetSciML struggles at out-of-distribution tasks.

Stability can not be explained using single network measures

There is no single known network measure that explains non-linear stability. To investigate the importance of individual measures, the coefficient of determination of SNBS with all studied network measures (see
Figure

3
) is analyzed. Even though there are significant correlations, no parameter can explain all the occurring variance.

The most strongly correlated network measures are second-order centrality, resistance-distance centrality and current-flow closeness centrality. Resistance distance centrality was introduced in the seminal work
[
66
]
, which shows that the linear response to fluctuations can be expressed in terms of this network measure. Here we detect that it is highly relevant for non-linear stability as well. The current-flow closeness is an unweighted version of the resistance-distance, as we have homogeneous edge weights, and it is identical to the resistance distance in this specific dataset.

Remarkably, the second-order centrality
[
23
]
was included serendipitously due to its presence in NetworkX. It is defined as the standard deviation of the return times of a random walk on the grid topology, and has not previously been connected to dynamic stability, to the best knowledge of the authors. We consider it remarkable that such a strong network measure has escaped the community for the last decade. This demonstrates the challenge, that there is no systematic way to construct or identify relevant network measures. It also poses the question, whether a heuristic or even analytic understanding of the relevance of second-order centrality is possible.

Competitive performance when using full set of network measures

When using all network measures, NetSciML can achieve a performance that is competitive with GNNs, and thus in reach of practical relevance for power grid applications, on the in-distribution tasks. The performance achieved by the various NetSciML models is shown in
Table

1
. Especially the gradient boosted regression achieves a high accuracy, almost matching that of the best performing GNN. This is a considerable improvement compared to much simpler versions of this approach, using few network measures, that were used as baseline in
[
46
,
45
]
. Apparently, the non-linear combination of the full set of network measures can capture SNBS. However, we also find that the generalization from grids with 20 nodes to grids with 100 nodes is notably weaker than for GNNs.
Next we will now see that this increased performance comes along with reduced data demand, faster training and enhanced explainability.

NetSciML works well in case of sparse training data

We compare the performance of NetSciML with GNNs, when only little data is available.
Figure

4
shows a direct comparison of the best NetSciML model (GBR) and the best GNN (TAGNet) at predicting SNBS, depending on the amount of available training data. The models were trained with 7, 70, 700 or 7000 grids of either 20 or 100 node ensembles, and evaluated on the corresponding test sets.
Figure

4
suggests that the achievable performance depends more on the total number of nodes in the training set, than on the total number of grids or the grid size. Crucially, GBR achieves much better results in the sparse data setting than TAGNet. We suppose that the GNN is at a disadvantage, because it needs to learn nodal properties that correlate with the target from the data, while GBR has direct access to network measures specifically crafted for this purpose. Furthermore, GNNs are more complex and larger models require more data for training in general. This demonstrates clearly the utility of combining theory and data driven analysis in situations where data is comparatively sparse.

NetSciML offers good interpretability tools

As already noted in the introduction, explaining the decision process of GNNs is no easy task, and the available methods are still in their infancy
[
72
]
. Especially in situations where no ground truth is known, the results of these methods can be highly misleading
[
36
]
.

In contrast, for NetSciML models the tools to interpret and explain the used ML layers are well established, and they therefore maintain the interpretability of the network measures. In this setting, the limiting factor for good interpretability may be the network measures themselves. Even though a number of network measures can be applied intuitively, e.g., the degree of a node, several network measures are complex and can not be interpreted easily.

One widely used tool to quantify the impact of individual input features, network measures in our case, on the final prediction of the model are SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values
[
38
,
37
,
29
,
64
]
.
SHAP values decompose the prediction into (positive or negative) individual contributions from each input. It is then possible to analyze the strength and direction of the contribution for different values the input may take. Even though the exact computation of SHAP values may be computationally expensive for large models, an efficient algorithm is available for tree-based models like GBR
[
37
]
.

Figure

5
shows the distribution of SHAP values for the 12 network measures with the highest absolute SHAP value for the GBR model for the 20 and 100 node ensembles. The features with the highest impact on the model output are largely the same as the features with high
R^{2}
in
Figure

3
. A decisive advantage of the SHAP approach is that a full distribution of feature impacts is obtained. This allows for a more fine-grained analysis – e.g., a negative impact of heavily loaded lines on stability, which may be very pronounced for some cases, becomes apparent. The visible differences between different grids sizes for the Pearson correlation (
Figure

3
) and mutual information (
Figure

12
and
Figure

13
) indicate why it might be difficult for the NetSciML models to show reliable out-of-distribution generalization across grid sizes.

NetSciML does not generalize to real topologies

All NetSciML approaches struggle at the presented out-of-distribution generalization tasks, shown in
Table

2
. GNNs trained on 100 nodes show good generalization to real topologies, reaching
R^{2}>0.8
for all cases except the challenging Great Britain grid (see
Methods - Simulation of additional real power grid topologies
), where we still obtain
R^{2}>0.6
.

We already saw in
Table

1
, that NetSciML shows only weak generalization from the small networks to the medium-sized ones. Here we detect that it largely fails to extrapolate to Texas and the real topologies, too. For France and Germany we see some generalization, albeit not nearly as well as GNNs, however for Spain and Texas NetSciML only weakly generalizes, and for Great Britain we get a complete failure to predict SNBS. In
Appendix

SM4
we exhibit that going from 20 nodes networks to real topologies has the same patterns.

Much of the SNBS research of the past decade implicitly assumed that network measures could capture the underlying causes for high or low basin stability, e.g., that dead ends undermine stability
[
41
]
. However, if network measures were causally determinative for SNBS values, one would expect predictions based on them to generalize well. This is especially true given that GNNs do manage to generalize, and given that NetSciML reaches similar in-distribution performance as GNNs.

At the same time, the failure to generalize is understandable given the results presented above. The correlation of network measures with SNBS varies wildly between the ensembles and the individual topologies, and OMP models select different network measures for their predictions, even when just varying between the 20 node and the 100 node ensembles.

While network measures are, in concert, informative enough to correlate well with SNBS, they do not appear to reliably capture the underlying causes of high or low SNBS. Conversely, it appears that GNNs are making predictions in a way that does capture the underlying mechanisms.

Discussion

The overarching question we have studied is, can network measures capture the relationship between network structure and functional properties of dynamical complex networks? We analyzed a concrete task of practical and theoretical importance: the stability of synchrony in networks of oscillator systems, such as power grids. For this task, no clear picture of the structure-function relationship was reached, despite considerable effort. The potential for the structure-function relationship was further highlighted by the recent success of GNNs in predicting stability from network data.

For the task of predicting the dynamic stability of oscillator networks, the detailed analysis shows that no network measure has exceptionally high correlation with single-node basin stability (SNBS), and no small number of network features suffices to achieve high prediction performance. Remarkably, we serendipitously discover a network measure, the second-order centrality, that has outstanding correlation with SNBS but had not previously been considered in the context of stability. This highlights the issue that, in the context of non-linear dynamical networked systems, there is no systematic way of finding or constructing network measures for a given task. Nevertheless, exploring the previously unknown association between second-order centrality and SNBS, analytically, could enhance our comprehension of the intricate interplay between topology and synchronization.

The results presented here show that NetSciML, the combination of network science in the form of 46 network measures we curated, with machine learning, can successfully match the performance of GNNs for predicting the stability of individual nodes in two ensembles of power-grid inspired networks. NetSciML also has considerably better interpretability, data efficiency and computational efficiency than GNNs. Further, there are tasks such as
[
64
]
and troublemaker identification in
Appendix

SM7
for which NetSciML works well and reliably generalizes.

For SNBS, NetSciML models achieve excellent in-distribution performance on one ensemble, but fail at predicting the properties of nodes in another ensemble or for the various real-world topologies they have not been trained on. This is in marked contrast to GNNs, which achieve good generalization, even in challenging cases like the GB grid. Possible explanations for the poor performance come into view when observing the large differences between the correlations of network measures and SNBS in the two ensembles, as well as the real topologies (
Figure

3
,
Appendix

SM1
). Some network measures show a dramatically different correlation to SNBS for the various ensembles and grids, the variability of the correlation measures from grid to grid is high overall. In addition, there is almost no overlap between the small sets of network measures that perform best for the ensembles of 20 and 100 node networks, respectively.

We interpret this as evidence that network measures capture enough structural information within an ensemble to correlate well with SNBS, but do not capture the mechanism by which the network’s structure shapes SNBS. If the (known) network measures were causally determinative of SNBS, we would not expect NetSciML to fail to generalize to such an extent. Indeed, in
Appendix

SM7
we see that in the case of a stability measure that is well explained by a few network measures, the generalization is largely successful. In general, we believe our study highlights the need to systematically vary ensembles and topologies when studying structure-function relationships in complex dynamical networks.

For future work, it would be very interesting to study systematically how different ensembles need to be for NetSciML to fail, and whether there is a threshold beyond which GNN performance also degrades significantly. Unfortunately, generating SNBS labelled network ensembles is computationally extremely expensive. It would be helpful to find a dynamical property that shows similar performance characteristics but is easier to generate datasets for.

This would also address the limitation that the current study only considers tasks related to the stability of synchronization in a power-grid inspired context. It is of course not possible to draw strong conclusions about the general relationship between network measures and GNNs from a single such study. Similar investigations in other fields of Network Science are challenging future tasks, but would provide a clearer picture of the relative merits of the approaches.

The failure of NetSciML to generalize makes the good generalization of GNNs all the more remarkable. Based on their superior and robust performance, it appears that GNNs capture (maybe indirectly) the mechanism by which network structure shapes function, even where network measures do not. This finding serves as a strong motivation for the advancement of explainability algorithms, as it suggests the potential discovery of novel relations previously unknown to the scientific community. However, as yet, the interpretability of GNNs models is still lacking – existing methods are in their infancy. Not the least due to their great potential for improving our causal understanding of the non-linear dynamics of oscillator networks, we believe more research into inspection and explainability tools for GNNs is needed. Such tools should not just answer the question of how the GNN achieves its prediction from an ML architecture perspective, but should focus on what the GNN can reveal about the structure-function relationship of the underlying system. Our study puts the question of how to extract causal insights from GNNs at the heart of network science.

Methods

Quantification of dynamic stability using single-node basin stability (SNBS)

To quantify the dynamic stability of coupled non-linear oscillators, probabilistic methods are widely used. They are especially useful for analyzing the influence of topological properties on stability. The used datasets contain prediction targets which quantify the asymptotic and transient stability. For asymptotic stability, single-node basin stability (SNBS) is a well established measure
[
40
]
. It quantifies the likelihood of a system to return to a stable state after applying perturbations.

Predicting dynamic stability using ML

There are multiple options to predict the dynamic stability of non-linear oscillator networks. We only consider supervised learning setups, meaning that we train models based on known outcomes. In our case, the targets are the results of dynamical simulations. We distinguish two approaches.

•

Feature-based approach: Network measures are pre-computed and provided as input features for various ML models. We refer to this approach as NetSciML.

•

Topology-based approach: The full topology of the power grid is used as direct input for specialized ML models, so-called Graph Neural Networks (GNN).

The three different pipelines for predicting SNBS are illustrated in
Figure

8
.

Figure 8:
Three different pipelines are used for the training.
GNNs
(at the bottom) deal with the graph input directly, whereas the other models rely on network measures as inputs. On the other hand,
NetSciML
using Multilayer perceptron (MLP) and gradient boosted trees (GBT) are capable of non-linear mapping, so they are directly applied to the primary predictors. Since the (generalized) linear models only learn a linear mapping, nonlinear interactions of the network measures are provided as additional inputs. The outputs are nodal properties describing the dynamical stability.

To evaluate and compare the performance, the
coefficient of determination
(
R^{2}
) is used:

R^{2}:=1-\frac{\mathrm{mse}(\widehat{y},y)}{\mathrm{mse}(y_{mean},y)},

(2)

where
y_{mean}
is the mean of the target values of the test dataset. The
R^{2}
-score captures the mean squared error relative to a null model that predicts the mean of the test dataset for all points. Thus, it measures the percentage of variance of the data explained by the prediction. By design, a model that predicts the mean of the target values has
R^{2}=0
.

For models of which the internal parameters depend on random seeds, we minimize the effect of initialization, by using 5 different initialization per model and compute average performances using the three best, as conducted by
[
46
,
45
]
.
Next, we introduce the key aspects of GNNs and NetSciML.

Graph Neural Networks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are artificial neural networks designed for learning relationships within data structured as graphs. Their adjustable internal weights can be optimized to suit the specific task at hand. They take the graph structure itself as input, along with optional features associated to the nodes and edges. GNNs can generate outputs in the form of global attributes that describe the entire graph, attributes specific to sub-graphs, or properties associated with individual nodes or edges. We shortly introduce the Graph Convolution Network (GCN) developed by
[
28
]
as a paradigmatic graph-convolutional layer. Given an input feature matrix
X
with rows corresponding to nodes and columns corresponding to features, GCN layers compute an outputs feature matrix
H
by:

H=\sigma(\overline{A}X\Theta),

(3)

here
\sigma
denotes the activation function which acts element wise on the matrix entries, the matrix
\Theta
, which acts on the feature space, contains the learnable weights and
\overline{A}
is the normalized adjacency matrix of the graph. The effect is, that each GCN layer averages the features of the direct neighbors. It is possible to increase the considered region by applying multiple GCN layers consecutively.

There are many recent advances based that generalize this type of graph based layer. The more complex GNN layers that we employ in this studied are introduced in detail in
Appendix

SM5
.

NetSciML - Identification of relevant network measures

As predictors for the NetSciML algorithms we use network measures that have been related to power grid stability in previous work, as well as others that have been developed in different contexts, but might be correlated with dynamic stability nevertheless. The feature-based ML algorithms receive all features and targets on a per-node basis and have no inherent way of capturing which nodes belong together in a grid. By incorporating global network measures, we hope to (partially) bypass that limitation.

In one of the earliest applications of basin stability to power grids,
[
41
]
found that leaf nodes correlate with low stability. Subsequently,
[
59
]
predicted single-node basin stability (SNBS) of power grids with logistic regression. They classified nodes as belonging to certain network motifs, such as tree-like subgraphs, and used these as inputs for the regression model, alongside the injected power
P^{d}
and the common network measures, degree, average neighbor degree, clustering coefficient, current-flow betweenness and closeness centrality.

[
47
]
expand upon this work by studying survivability as well as SNBS, and by refining the topological classification scheme. To compute the proposed node categories
[
47
]
, the open source software by
[
57
]
was used. Furthermore, we consider connections between nodes of walks of lengths higher than 1, by computing the row-sum of
A^{k}
, where
k=3
would represent walks of length 3.

To identify local vulnerabilities in power grids with intermittent fluctuations,
[
66
]
use centrality measures based on resistance distance and Kirchhoff indices. Feld et al.
[
17
]
construct very stable and very unstable power grids and study the correlation of basin stability with various power grid specific network measures, such as flow backup capacity, power sign ratio
[
14
]
and universal Kuramoto order parameter
[
56
]
.
[
71
]
predict re-synchronization after individual perturbations in synthetic grid topologies generated with the same model that we use
[
58
]
. As input features for their ML models, they use a few common network measures, such as eigenvector centrality and
k
-core index. Furthermore, they use the specific perturbation vector, the so-called community inconsistency. The
community inconsistency
depends on a free parameter for which the authors do not provide a heuristic choice
[
26
,
24
]
. Since, furthermore,
[

find no large correlation of this measure with dynamic stability, we do not include it in our study.

The related problem of predicting critical links in power grids has been studied by Witthaut et al.
[
69
]
.

For this problem, Titz et al.
[
64
]
used Gradient Boosted Trees to predict de-synchronization event after line failures at high accuracy. They include many networks measures as features, however most of them are only applicable to lines.

Where appropriate, we include the predictors of these previous studies as inputs for our feature-based ML models, as well as further network measures from the broader network science literature, which might be correlated with dynamic stability of power grids, see
Figure

3
. Many measures are used in different references, we only provide up to two references per measure.

Dynamics stability of power grids is a large field of research, and perhaps not surprisingly we can not include every graph theoretic measure that has been linked to it in our study. We took great effort to include the most relevant previous studies, restricting us to those that use similar models and methods. Should new insights suggest significant correspondence of dynamic stability with further network measures, it will be easy to include them in our ML pipeline, which is available online (see Section
Data Availability Statement
). An overview of the input features and their correlation with SNBS is given in
Figure

and in total, there are 46 primary predictors.

Aside from the features shown in
Figure

, we use the measures flow back-up capacity and line outage distribution factor as global network measures with respect to the maximally loaded line in the synchronous states. Their computation involves removing that line from the grid. If thereby the graph gets disconnected, these measures are not defined. For the ML models that do not straightforwardly support missing data points - i.e., linear models and MLPs - we excluded these features. For the models that do regard them - i.e., Gradient Boosted Trees - the SHAP analysis indicates no large influence on the prediction outcomes.

Linear Models: Modeling non-linearities with interaction terms

In linear models, the outcome depends linearly on the explanatory variables. While gradient boosted trees and MLPs are inherently able to capture non-linear dependencies of the targets variables on the input variables, that is not the case for linear and logistic regression. Even though logistic regression models a non-linear relation via the logit function, the log-odds of the targets depend linearly on the predictors. For these models the set of features is augmented by considering polynomial combinations of the inputs features of order up to 2, so-called
interactions
.
Interaction terms
are products of two (or more) predictors, i.e.,
X_{i}\odot X_{j}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Sometimes a specific interaction between two variables is known to be relevant and should be added to the explanatory variables. We make sure to consider all potentially relevant interactions by adding all polynomial terms of order
\leq 2
to the set of independent variables
X^{\prime}
of our model

X^{\prime}=(1,X_{1},\ldots X_{n},X_{1}\odot X_{1},X_{1}\odot X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\odot X_{n}).

(4)

Feature scaling

Based on the 46 primary predictors, and including their interactions, there is a total of 1099 predictors. For in-distribution prediction, all feature-based models standardize the inputs by subtracting the mean and dividing through the standard deviation of the training data. For the out-of-distribution task, the test data is standardized separately, to account for differences in the feature distributions because of the larger grid size. For example, the diameter of the Texan power grid is significantly larger in comparison to the ensembles, hence it is normalized separately to be more aligned with the distribution of the training sets.

Linear Models: Best subset selection

To analyze the performance of small models with just a few independent variables, we perform best subset selections for the linear models.
Given input features
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and target variables
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the best subset selection problem is to find parameters
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, s.t.,

\min_{\beta}\|y-(X\beta)\|^{2}_{2}-\|\beta\|_{0},

(5)

with
\|.\|_{0}
denoting the number of non-zero coefficients of the argument (sometimes called
\ell_{0}
-‘norm’). This is just the least squares formulation of a linear model, with an added regularization term.
As an algorithm for the best subset selection problem we use orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)
[
39
]
, which approximates the subset of network measures, that best fits the variance in the data. If the reduced model captures the main information and achieves good performance, it allows for a more intuitive understanding of the model predictions. We approximate the subsets with 1, 2 and 5 predictors and denote the corresponding models as OMP1/2/5.

Complexity and training details of the used ML models

In general, more complex models are able to learn more complex relations as long as the training data is sufficient. More complex models usually have more parameters which can be optimized to better map the data. However, different architectures may deal with the provided information more efficiently, requiring fewer parameters to achieve comparable or even higher performances.

The following paragraph contains detailed information for the NetSciML and GNN models. For LogReg, the model was fitted with the LBFGS solver and did not fully converge in some experiments. In these cases, the solver was terminated after 1,000,000 steps. For GBT and the regression setup, we use Poisson loss, which leads to slightly better results than mean squared loss, even though the performance is evaluated using
R^{2}
. The MLP is also introduced by
[
46
,
45
]
and consists of six hidden layers and 500 units per layer, leading to 1,507,001 model parameters. For this model, we optimize the batch sizes and learning rates in a hyperparameter study. The linear and logistic regression models have 1 100 parameters, OMP1/2/5 have 2,3 and 6 parameters. GBT has up to 54 250 leaves. Both regression and OMP take primary predictors and interactions as inputs, whereas GBT and MLP only take the primary predictors as input. The used GNN models have the following properties: ArmaNet has 189 048 parameters, GCNNet 523 020 parameters and TAGNet 415 320 parameters.

NetSciML has significantly lower training times

GNN models require considerable training time, especially when conducting hyperparameter studies. Training of NetSciML is significantly faster. The NetSciML approaches based on gradient-boosted trees and linear regression have negligible training times of less than 2 CPU minutes. Fitting the logistic regression takes up to 3.5 hours when trained on 7,000 grids of size 100, due to the more complicated loss function. Even then, it fails to fully achieve the global optimum, when its LBFGS solver is terminated after 1,000,000 steps.

By far the most significant computational effort is required for the hyperparameter studies of the GNNs and the MLP. For this analysis, it is common to analyze hundreds or thousands of possible configurations. It is possible to reduce the computational effort in comparison to the reported final run times, by training fewer epochs and accepting the case of not fully converged setups, but the computational effort is still high. This shows the great advantage of using the NetSciML-approach with much smaller computational costs, because hyperparameter studies are not necessary in case of linear regression, or are much cheaper in case of GBT.

Once the models are trained, ML approaches have the huge advantage of negligible evaluation times. This is especially helpful when evaluating unknown grids of the size of the Texan grid. For such a large grid, the required Monte-Carlo simulations take roughly 15,000 CPU hours
[
45
]
when aiming for an accuracy comparable to current GNNs, whereas the evaluation is basically free for ML methods.

9
]
aggregates the information in a more complex manner. A complete Auto-regressive moving average layer (ARMA) consists itself of multiple Graph Convolutional Skip (GCS) layers:

\overline{X}^{(j+1)}=\sigma(\tilde{L}X^{(j)}W^{(j)}+XV^{(j)}),

(8)

where
j
is an index and
W
and
V
are matrices of trainable parameters. There are two important distinctions from the GCN layers: the aggregation in the first term uses a normalized version of the Laplacian matrix
L
. Second, skip connections are added by recursively re-inserting the initial node features
X
from the first layer to easily stack multiple layers. For better generalization and reduced computational effort, weight sharing is used among different GCS layers, such as:
W^{(j)}=W
and
V^{(j)}=V
, except for the first layer where
W^{(1)}\neq W
.

Multiple ARMA layers are combined in a parallel stacks that helps to increase predictive performance by using:

\overline{X}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{X}_{k}^{(J)},

(9)

where
\overline{X}_{k}^{(J)}
is the output of the last GCS layer in the
k-
th ARMA layer. Larger regions are taken into account by increasing
J
. In the literature, Graph Neural Networks achieve the best performance at predicting the dynamic stability so far
[
46
,
45
]
.

Appendix SM6
Framing a regression problem as a classification task

To apply the classifciation algorithms logistic regression (LogReg) and gradient-boosting classification (GBC) for SNBS prediction we follow the approach outlined in
[
35
]
.
These algorithms do not predict the target class directly, but rather the probability that a given case belongs to a given class.
If the target variable
p
of a regression problem can be interpreted as a probability, as is this case for SNBS, then binary classification algorithms like LogReg and GBC may be modified to directly predict it, with the additional benefit of constraining the target values to the interval
[0,1]
. Note that each case in a dataset with probability targets may be interpreted as belonging to a dummy class ‘
1
’ with probability
p
and to a dummy class ‘
0
’ with probability
1-p
. By duplicating every case in the training data, assigning one copy to class ‘
1
’ and one to class ‘
0
’, and giving dummy sampling weights of
p
and
1-p
respectively to the copies, the regression problem may be transformed into a binary classification problem. The class probabilites predicted by the classifier are then the predicted target values.

Appendix SM7
Classification of troublemaker nodes

SM7.1
Transient stability measures

Asymptotic stability is not sufficient for applications where system bounds might be exceeded during operation and cause failures. Hence, survivability was introduced to quantify the likelihood that the transient behavior of the system is well-behaved, i.e., that its trajectory stays within the desirable region of phase space
[
21
]
. Instead of considering regular survivability, the so-called
troublemakers
– nodes are introduced in
[
45
]
. These nodes amplify perturbations and cause the system to exceed critical thresholds.
As a further prediction task, we used our models to classify all nodes regarding the troublemaker property (TM). We predict a binary outcome variable with two setups: First, a common classification setup is used with two categories: Nodes are either TM or not. Second, we do not directly predict the TM, but instead the maximum frequency deviation of a power grid trajectory. Afterwards, thresholding is applied and nodes are classified as TM, if the maximum frequency deviation exceeds a threshold.
[
45
]
show that both strategies work and depending on the concrete task one or the other have the best performance. When we use gradient boosted trees or logistic models for classification, we apply threshold moving to the classifcation probability cutoff after training to further optimize the
F_{2}
-score. We also compare the ML approaches with a semi-analytic boundary for estimating survivability
[
21
]
, which we adapt to predict troublemakers.

SM7.2
Semi-analytic approach to predict survivability

As a further baseline model for the troublemaker task, we use the semi-analytic bound for survivability, see
[
21
]
. Strictly speaking this is not an ML method, since it relies on an implicit linearization of the physical dynamics of the system in combination with Monte-Carlo sampling. As input it receives the dynamical system described by
Equation

1
with the parameters
A
and
P
. Once the semi-analytic bound for the failure probability has been computed, the target classes are obtained by thresholding at 1 (sanasurv). As a novel insight, we found that the performance of the original sanasurv can be improved for our classification task by relaxing the threshold, and finding a value with optimal
F_{2}
-score via threshold moving (sanasurvTH).

SM7.3
Classification metrics

For the classification tasks, the outcomes are either correct or wrong and can be categorized as: true positives (
TP
), true negatives (
TN
), false positives (
FP
), and false negatives (
FN
). In situations where the positive class is associated with higher levels of risk or cost, the
recall
quantifies how many relevant cases were retrieved:

\mathrm{recall}:=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},

(10)

and the
precision
measures how many retrieved cases are relevant,

\mathrm{precision}:=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.

(11)

A perfect recall of
1
may be trivially achieved by simply classifying all observations as positive. To avoid this, the
F_{\beta}
-score, a weighted average of recall and precision, may be used to measure classification performance:

F_{\beta}:=(1+\beta^{2})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{precision}\cdot\mathrm{recall}}{\beta^{2}\cdot\mathrm{precision}+\mathrm{recall}}.

(12)

For
\beta>1
, the
F_{\beta}
-score gives more weight to recall and less to precision, and vice versa for
\beta<1
. For identifying vulnerabilities of power grids we use the
F_{2}
-score as performance metric, since it is more important to find critical states, than to avoid falsely classifying safe states as critical.

Classification results

For the classification of TM, the results are shown in

F_{2}

Models that are directly trained at the classification task are denoted as
type
