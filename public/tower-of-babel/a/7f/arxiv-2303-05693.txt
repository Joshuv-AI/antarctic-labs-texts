A. Bharali, Bikash Bhattacharjya, Sumanta Borah, Idweep Jyoti Gogoi, Department of Mathematics, Dibrugarh University, Assam, India, Department of Mathematics, Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati, India

Abstract

Introduction

There has been an upsurge of studies related to spectral properties of graph theoretical matrices. Investigation of these properties play a vital role in analysing some properties of networks. In recent times, the extensions of spectral theory of un-oriented networks to mixed networks is a popular topic. In comparison to the un-oriented networks, the mixed networks are much better to model the real world problems. However, we see that many graph matrices for mixed networks appear to be non-symmetric, losing the property that eigenvalues are real.

Recently, many researcher studied the spectral properties of adjacency matrix, Laplacian matrix, normalized Laplacian matrix etc. of mixed networks by incorporating modified versions of these matrices. For details, see

2
Basic Definitions

Throughout the paper, we consider connected simple graph with at least two vertices. Let
X
be an un-oriented graph. We denote an edge of
X
between the vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
by
e_{ij}
. Note that the edge
e_{ij}
can be assigned two orientations. An oriented edge originating at
v_{i}
an terminating at
v_{j}
is denoted by
\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}
. For each edge
e_{ij}\in E(X)
, there is a pair of oriented edges
\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}
and
\overrightarrow{e_{ji}}
. The collection
\overrightarrow{E}(X)\coloneqq\{\overrightarrow{e_{ij}},\overrightarrow{e_{ji}}:e_{ij}\in E(X)\}
is the oriented edge set associated with
X
. Note that each edge of an un-oriented graph is of the form
e_{ij}
. The set
\overrightarrow{E}(X)
is the collection of all possible oriented edges of an un-oriented graph
X
.

A graph
X
is said to be mixed if it has both possibilities of edges that are oriented and un-oriented. If
X
is a mixed graph, then at most one of
e_{ij},\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}
and
\overrightarrow{e_{ji}}
can be in
E(X)
. We write
i\rightarrow j
, if there is an oriented edge from vertex
v_{i}
to vertex
v_{j}
, and
i\sim j
for an un-oriented edge between the vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
. The graph
X_{U}
obtained from a mixed graph
X
by replacing each of the oriented edge of
X
by the corresponding un-oriented edge is called the
underlying graph
of
X
. A
cycle
in a mixed graph is a cycle in its underlying graph. A cycle is even or odd according as its order is even or odd.

A
gain graph
or
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph is a triplet
\Phi\coloneqq(X,{\mathbb{T}},\varphi)
, where
X
is an un-oriented graph,
{\mathbb{T}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, and
\varphi:\overrightarrow{E}(X)\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}}
is a function satisfying
\varphi(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})=\varphi(\overrightarrow{e_{ji}})^{-1}
for each
e_{ij}\in E(X)
. The function
\varphi
is called the
gain function
of
(X,{\mathbb{T}},\varphi)
. For simplicity, we use
\Phi\coloneqq(X,\varphi)
to denote a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph instead of
\Phi\coloneqq(X,{\mathbb{T}},\varphi)
. For a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
\Phi\coloneqq(X,{\mathbb{T}},\varphi)
, the
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
-\Phi
is defined by
-\Phi\coloneqq(X,-\varphi)
. In
[
23
]
, Reff proposed the notion of the adjacency matrix
A(\Phi)\coloneqq(a_{ij})
of a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph, where

a_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})&\hbox{if $i\rightarrow j$}\\
0&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

It is clear that
A(\Phi)
is Hermitian. Thus the eigenvalues of this matrix are real. If
\varphi(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})=1
for all
\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}
, then we have
A(\Phi)=A(X)
, where
A(X)
is the usual
(0,1)
-adjacency matrix of the graph
X
. Thus one can assume a graph
X
as a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
(X,\mathbf{1})
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the function that assign 1 to each edge of
\overrightarrow{E}(X)
.
A
switching function

\zeta
of
X
is a function from
V(X)
to
{\mathbb{T}}
, that is,
\zeta:V(X)\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}}
. Two gain graphs
\Phi_{1}\coloneqq(X,\varphi_{1})
and
\Phi_{2}\coloneqq(X,\varphi_{2})
are said to be
switching equivalent
, written
\Phi_{1}\sim\Phi_{2}
, if there exists a switching function
\zeta:V(X)\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}}
such that

\varphi_{2}(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})=\zeta(i)^{-1}\varphi_{1}(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})\zeta(j).

It is clear from the definition that the gain graphs
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are switching equivalent if and only if there is a diagonal matrix
D_{\zeta}
, where the diagonal entries come from
{\mathbb{T}}
, such that

A(\Phi_{2})=D_{\zeta}^{-1}A(\Phi_{1})D_{\zeta}.

In 2015, Guo and Mohar
[
10
]
introduced a Hermitian adjacency matrix
H(X)
of a mixed graph
X
, where the
ij
-th entry is
{\mathbf{i}},-{\mathbf{i}}
or 1 according as
\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}\in E(X)
,
\overrightarrow{e_{ji}}\in E(X)
or
e_{ij}\in E(X)
respectively, and 0 otherwise. Here
{\mathbf{i}}=\sqrt{-1}
. This matrix has numerous appealing characteristics, including real eigenvalues and the interlacing theorem for mixed graphs etc.

Later in 2020, Mohar
[
21
]
put forward a new Hermitian adjacency matrix
H^{\omega}(X)\coloneqq(h_{ij})
of a mixed graph
X
, which is referred as Hermitian matrix of second kind, where

h_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{if $i\sim j$}\\
\omega&\hbox{if $i\rightarrow j$}\\
\overline{\omega}&\hbox{if $j\rightarrow i$}\\
0&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Here
\omega\coloneqq\frac{1+\mathbf{i}\sqrt{3}}{2}
, a primitive sixth root of unity and
\overline{\omega}\coloneqq\frac{1-\mathbf{i}\sqrt{3}}{2}
. For a mixed graph
X
, let
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})
represent the
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph with the gain function
{\bm{\omega}}:\overrightarrow{E}(X_{U})\rightarrow\{1,\omega,\overline{\omega}\}
, where

{\bm{\omega}}(\overrightarrow{e_{ij}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{if $e_{ij}\in E(X)$}\\
\omega&\hbox{if $\overrightarrow{e_{ij}}\in E(X)$}\\
\overline{\omega}&\hbox{if $\overrightarrow{e_{ji}}\in E(X)$}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
H^{\omega}(X)=A(\Phi)
for
\Phi=(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})
.

With the growing popularity of these Hermitian matrices, the idea of investigating spectral properties of mixed networks based on other graph matrices is also evolved. The matrix
D^{-1/2}A(X)D^{-1/2}
is called the normalized adjacency matrix of an un-oriented graph
X
, where
D=\text{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
and
d_{i}
denote the degree of the vertex
v_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This matrix is popularly known as the Randić matrix
R(X)
.

The matrix
D^{-1/2}H(X)D^{-1/2}
is called the Hermitian Randić matrix, denoted
R_{H}(X)
, of a mixed graph
X
. Similarly, the matrix
D^{-1/2}H^{\omega}(X)D^{-1/2}
is called the Hermitian Randić matrix of second kind, denoted
R^{\omega}(X)
, of a mixed graph
X
.

If
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)\coloneqq(R_{ij}^{\bm{\omega}})
, we find that

R_{ij}^{\bm{\omega}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}&\hbox{if $i\sim j$}\\
\frac{{\bm{\omega}}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}&\hbox{if $i\rightarrow j$}\\
\frac{\overline{{\bm{\omega}}}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}&\hbox{if $j\rightarrow i$}\\
\;\;\;0&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
is Hermitian. Let
L^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=D-H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
and
\mathfrak{L}^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=D^{-1/2}L^{\bm{\omega}}(X)D^{-1/2}
. The matrices
L^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
and
\mathfrak{L}^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
are known as the Hermitian Laplacian matrix of second kind and the normalized Hermitian Laplacian matrix of second kind of
X
, respectively. It is clear that
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=I-\mathfrak{L}^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
, where
I
is the identity matrix of appropriate order.

The Randić matrix
R(\Phi)
of a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
\Phi
is defined by
R(\Phi)\coloneqq D^{-1/2}A(\Phi)D^{-1/2}
. Similarly, the matrix
I-R(\Phi)
is called the normalized Laplacian matrix of a
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
\Phi
. If
X
is a mixed graph, then we see that
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=R(\Phi)
, where
\Phi=(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})
.

A walk (or path) in a mixed graph is a walk (or path) in its underlying graph. The value of a walk
W\coloneqq v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}\cdots v_{i_{\ell}}
in a mixed graph
X
is defined as
R^{\bm{\omega}}_{i_{1}i_{2}}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots R^{\bm{\omega}}_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}}
. A walk is called positive or negative according as its value is positive or negative, respectively. An acyclic mixed graph is defined to be positive. A non-acyclic mixed graph is positive or negative according as each of its mixed cycle is positive or negative, respectively. A mixed graph
X
is called an
elementary graph
if each component of
X
is an edge or a cycle.

3
Spectral properties of Hermitian Randić matrix of second kind

In this section, we characterize some spectral properties of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
. We continue with some known results which are associated to our findings.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denote the set of all
n\times n
matrices with complex entries. For
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the matrix whose entries are absolute values of the corresponding entries of
A
is denoted by
|A|
. The maximum of the absolute values of the eigenvalues of a matrix
A
is called the
spectral radius
of
A
. It is denoted by
\rho(A)
. Further, the spectrum of
A
is denoted by
\operatorname{Spec}(A)
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

A
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph
(X,\varphi)
is positive if and only if
(X,\varphi)\sim(X,\mathbf{1})
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
(X,\varphi)
be a connected and positive
{\mathbb{T}}
-gain graph. Then
X
is bipartite if and only if
(X,-\varphi)
is positive.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Suppose
A
is non-negative and irreducible, and
A\geq|B|
. Let
\lambda\coloneqq e^{i\theta}\rho(B)
be a maximum-modulus eigenvalue of
B
. If
\rho(A)=\rho(B)
, then there is a diagonal unitary matrix
D\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
B=e^{i\theta}DAD^{-1}
.

In
[
13
]
, Kannan et al. studied the normalized Laplacian matrix for gain graphs. They also characterized some spectral properties for the Randić matrix of an un-oriented graph.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
X
be a connected graph. Then
\operatorname{Spec}(R(X))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(-R(X))
if and only if
X
is bipartite.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
be two connected gain graphs. If
\Phi_{1}\sim\Phi_{2}
, then

\operatorname{Spec}(R(\Phi_{1}))=\operatorname{Spec}(R(\Phi_{2}))

Lemma 3.3

(
[
13
]
)
.

If
\Phi\coloneqq(X,\varphi)
is a connected gain graph, then

\rho(R(\Phi))\leq\rho(|R(\Phi)|)=\rho(R(X)).

The following result is an immediate consequence of the preceding lemmas.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph. Then
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=\operatorname{Spec}(R(X_{U}))
if and only if
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},\mathbf{1})
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(R(X_{U}))
, then by Theorem 3.3
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=e^{i\theta}D_{\zeta}R(X_{U})D_{\zeta}^{-1}
, where
D_{\zeta}
is a diagonal unitary matrix. Hence

\displaystyle D_{\zeta}^{-1}R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)D_{\zeta}=e^{i\theta}R(X_{U})

\displaystyle\text{or},\;\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle D_{\zeta}^{-1}D^{-1/2}H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)D^{-1/2}D_{\zeta}=e^{i\theta}D^{-1/2}A(X_{U})D^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\text{or},\;\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=e^{i\theta}D_{\zeta}A(X_{U})D_{\zeta}^{-1}.

Since both the matrices
H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
and
A(X_{U})
are Hermitian,
\theta
is either
0
or
\pi
. This gives that either
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},\mathbf{1})
or
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},-\mathbf{1})
. If
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},\mathbf{1})
, we are done. If
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},-\mathbf{1})
, then by Lemma 3.2, we have
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(-R(X_{U}))
. Again as
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(R(X_{U}))
, we have
\operatorname{Spec}(R(X_{U}))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(-R(X_{U}))
. Thus by Lemma 3.1,
X_{U}
is bipartite. Now applying Theorem 3.2 for the positive gain graph
(X_{U},-{\bm{\omega}})
, we find that
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})
is positive, and hence
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},\mathbf{1})
.

Conversely, if
(X_{U},{\bm{\omega}})\sim(X_{U},\mathbf{1})
, then clearly
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
=
\operatorname{Spec}(R(X_{U}))
.
∎

In order to determine some spectral properties of the matrix
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
, we now provide the following lemma. In what follows,
h_{ij}
always represents the
ij
-th entry of
H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
X
be a mixed graph on
n
vertices, and
{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then

{\mathbf{y}}^{*}H^{\bm{\omega}}(X){\mathbf{y}}=\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(|y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-(|y_{i}|^{2}+|y_{j}|^{2})).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}^{*}H^{\bm{\omega}}(X){\mathbf{y}}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(\overline{y}_{i}h_{ij}y_{j}+y_{i}{\overline{h}_{ij}}\overline{y}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}((\overline{y}_{i}h_{ij}y_{j}+y_{i}{\overline{h}_{ij}}\overline{y}_{j})+\overline{y}_{i}y_{i}+\overline{y}_{j}y_{j}-\overline{y}_{i}y_{i}-\overline{y}_{j}y_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}((\overline{y}_{i}h_{ij}y_{j}+\overline{y}_{i}y_{i})+(y_{i}{\overline{h}_{ij}}\overline{y}_{j}+\overline{y}_{j}\overline{h}_{ij}h_{ij}y_{j})-(|y_{i}|^{2}+|y_{j}|^{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}((y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j})(\overline{y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}})-(|y_{i}|^{2}+|y_{j}|^{2})),\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{as}\;|h_{ij}|^{2}=1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(|y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-(|y_{i}|^{2}+|y_{j}|^{2})).\qed

Using the preceding lemma, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph of order
n
, where
n\geq 2
. If
\operatorname{Spec}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
, then
-1\leq\lambda_{k}\leq 1
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

For a complex vector
{\mathbf{x}}
, let
{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq D^{-1/2}{\mathbf{x}}
, where
D
is the diagonal degree matrix of the underlying graph
X_{U}
. Let
y_{i}
be the
i
-th coordinate of
{\mathbf{y}}
. By Lemma
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{*}R^{\bm{\omega}}(X){\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{y}}^{*}D^{1/2}D^{-1/2}H^{\bm{\omega}}(X)D^{-1/2}D^{1/2}{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{y}}^{*}H^{\bm{\omega}}(X){\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(|y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-|y_{i}|^{2}-|y_{j}|^{2}).

Also,
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}{\mathbf{x}}=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}d_{i}|y_{i}|^{2}
. By Courant-Fischer Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-1)}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}{\mathrm{max}}\;\;\;\underset{\underset{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\;{\mathbf{x}}\neq 0}{{\mathbf{x}}\perp\{{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-1)}\}}}{\mathrm{min}}\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{*}R^{\bm{\omega}}(X){\mathbf{x}}}{{\mathbf{x}}^{*}{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-1)}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}{\mathrm{max}}\;\;\;\underset{\underset{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\;{\mathbf{x}}\neq 0}{{\mathbf{x}}\perp\{{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-1)}\}}}{\mathrm{min}}\frac{\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(|y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-|y_{i}|^{2}-|y_{j}|^{2})}{\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}d_{i}|y_{i}|^{2}}.

(3.1)

Note that
|a+b|^{2}\leq 2|a|^{2}+2|b|^{2}
for two complex numbers
a
and
b
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(|y_{i}+h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-|y_{i}|^{2}-|y_{j}|^{2})\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i<j,\;e_{ij}\in E(X_{U})}(2|y_{i}|^{2}+2|h_{ij}y_{j}|^{2}-|y_{i}|^{2}-|y_{j}|^{2})

\displaystyle=

Theorem 4.1

. Consequently, each non-vanishing term in the expansion of
\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
gives rise to a spanning elementary sub-graph
X^{\prime}
of
X
. Note that a spanning elementary sub-graph may correspond to several non-vanishing terms in the expansion of
\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
.

Let
X^{\prime}
be a spanning elementary sub-graph of
X
that corresponds to a non-vanishing term in the expansion of
\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
. Let
\pi(X^{\prime})
be the set of all permutations that correspond to
X^{\prime}
. Clearly,
|\pi(X^{\prime})|=2^{s(X^{\prime})}
, and
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})}
for
\pi\in\pi(X^{\prime})
. Thus

\operatorname{det}R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=\sum_{X^{\prime}}(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})}\sum_{\pi\in\pi(X^{\prime})}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{1\pi(1)}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{2\pi(2)}\cdots R^{\bm{\omega}}_{n\pi(n)}.

Note that, for each edge component with vertices
v_{k}
and
v_{\ell}
, the corresponding factor
R^{\bm{\omega}}_{k\ell}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{\ell k}
has the value
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{k}d_{\ell}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\ell}d_{k}}}=\frac{1}{{d_{k}d_{\ell}}}
or
\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{d_{k}d_{\ell}}}\cdot\frac{\overline{\omega}}{\sqrt{d_{\ell}d_{k}}}=\frac{1}{{d_{k}d_{\ell}}}
. Furthermore, if for one direction the value of a mixed cycle is
\alpha
, then for the reversed direction its value is
\overline{\alpha}
, the conjugate of
\alpha
. Thus, in the summation of
\operatorname{det}(R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X))
, we have two cases for the cycles having complex values. For a semi-positive cycle, say
C_{1}
, we have
\omega\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{1})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\overline{\omega}\prod_{j\in V(C_{1})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}=(\omega+\overline{\omega})\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{1})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}=\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{1})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}
, and for a semi-negative cycle, say
C_{2}
, we have
-\omega\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{2})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}-\overline{\omega}\prod_{j\in V(C_{2})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}=-(\omega+\overline{\omega})\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{2})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}=-\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C_{2})}\frac{1}{d_{j}}
. In addition, if a cycle
C
has the real values
\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C)}\frac{1}{d_{j}}
or
-\prod_{v_{j}\in V(C)}\frac{1}{d_{j}}
for some direction, then it has the same value for the other direction of
C
as well.

Let
X^{\prime}_{e}
be the sub-graph of
X^{\prime}
consisting of the edge components of
X^{\prime}
. Recall that
Q(X^{\prime}_{e})=\prod_{v_{i}\in V(X^{\prime}_{e})}\frac{1}{d_{i}}
. As a convention, assume that
Q(X^{\prime}_{e})=1
if
X^{\prime}_{e}=\phi
. Let
C_{1},\;C_{2},\ldots,C_{s(X^{\prime})}
be the cycles of
X^{\prime}
of a length at least 3, and for
i\in\{1,\ldots,s(X^{\prime})\}
and
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
, define

W^{\alpha}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W(C_{i})&\text{ if }\alpha=1\\
\overline{W(C_{i})}&\text{ if }\alpha=2.\end{array}\right.

Thus

\operatorname{det}R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)=\sum_{X^{\prime}}(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})}\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,2\}}Q(X^{\prime}_{e})W^{\alpha}_{1}W^{\alpha}_{2}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}.

Observe that for
1\leq k\leq s(X^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}_{1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{k-1}W^{1}_{k}W^{\alpha}_{k+1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}+W^{\alpha}_{1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{k-1}W^{2}_{k}W^{\alpha}_{k+1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-1)^{\beta_{k}}W^{\alpha}_{1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{k-1}Q(C_{k})W^{\alpha}_{k+1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}&\text{ if $W(C_{k})$ is not real }\\
(-1)^{\beta_{k}}2W^{\alpha}_{1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{k-1}Q(C_{k})W^{\alpha}_{k+1}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}&\text{ if $W(C_{k})$ is real },\end{array}\right.

where

\beta_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if $C_{k}$ is negative or semi-negative cycle}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,2\}}Q(X^{\prime}_{e})W^{\alpha}_{1}W^{\alpha}_{2}\ldots W^{\alpha}_{s(X^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{sn}(X^{\prime})}2^{s(X^{\prime})-l_{sn}(X^{\prime})-l_{sp}(X^{\prime})}Q(X^{\prime}_{e})Q(C_{1})\ldots Q(C_{s(X^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{sn}(X^{\prime})}2^{l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{p}(X^{\prime})}Q(X^{\prime}).\;\;\qed

Let
P_{R^{\bm{\omega}}}(X,x)\colon=\operatorname{det}(xI-R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
be the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
of a mixed graph
X
. Now we compute an expression for the coefficients of
P_{R^{\bm{\omega}}}(X,x)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
P_{R^{\bm{\omega}}}(X,x)\coloneqq x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}
, then

(-1)^{k}a_{k}=\sum\limits_{X^{\prime}}(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})+l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{sn}(X^{\prime})}2^{l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{p}(X^{\prime})}Q(X^{\prime}),

where the summation is over all elementary sub-graphs
X^{\prime}
with order
k
of
X
.

Proof.

The proof is based on Theorem
4.1
, and makes use of the fact that the summation of the determinants of all principal
k\times k
sub-matrices of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
is
(-1)^{k}a_{k}
.
∎

In the next corollary, we look at how the coefficients of
P_{R^{\bm{\omega}}}(X,x)
change their shape for different graph structures.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
P_{R^{\bm{\omega}}}(X,x)\coloneqq x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}
.

(i)

If
X
is a tree, then
(-1)^{k}a_{k}=\sum\limits_{X^{\prime}}(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})}Q(X^{\prime})
, where the summation is over all elementary sub-graphs
X^{\prime}
with order
k
of
X
.

(ii)

If the underlying graph
X_{U}
of
X
is
\delta
-regular (
\delta\neq 0
), then

(-1)^{k}a_{k}=\sum\limits_{X^{\prime}}(-1)^{r(X^{\prime})+l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{sn}(X^{\prime})}2^{l_{n}(X^{\prime})+l_{p}(X^{\prime})}\frac{1}{\delta^{k}},

where the summation is over all elementary sub-graphs
X^{\prime}
with order
k
of
X
.

The proof of Corollary
4.1
is straightforward due to the absence of cycles in a tree and the fact that every vertex in a
\delta
-regular graph has degree
\delta
.

In 1999, Bollobás et al.
[
5
]
defined the general Randić index
R^{(\alpha)}(X)
of an un-oriented graph
X
as

R^{(\alpha)}(X)=\sum_{u\sim v}(d_{u}d_{v})^{\alpha}.

Now we find a bound for eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
in terms of
R^{(-1)}(X_{U})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
, then

\lambda^{2}_{1}\geq\frac{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}{n(n-1)}.

Proof.

Let the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
satisfy
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}}^{2}=\text{trace}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{ij}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{ji}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R^{\bm{\omega}}_{ij}{\overline{R}^{\bm{\omega}}_{ij}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|R^{\bm{\omega}}_{ij}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\thicksim j}\frac{1}{d_{i}d_{j}}=2R^{(-1)}(X_{U}).

Also,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}-n\lambda_{1}}=-n\lambda_{1}

or,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{1})^{2}+\sum_{p,q=1,\;p\neq q}^{n}(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{1})(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{1})=(n\lambda_{1})^{2}.

Since
\sum_{p\neq q}(\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{1})(\lambda_{q}-\lambda_{1})
is non-negative, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{1})^{2}\leq n^{2}\lambda^{2}_{1}

or,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda^{2}_{i}}+n\lambda^{2}_{1}\leq n^{2}\lambda^{2}_{1}

or,

\displaystyle 2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+n\lambda^{2}_{1}\leq n^{2}\lambda^{2}_{1}

or,

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{1}\geq\frac{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}{n(n-1)}.\qed

For an
n\times n
matrix
A\coloneqq(a_{ij})
, define

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(A)=\operatorname{min}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\;,\;\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}a_{ij}\bigg\}\;\;\;\;\;\text{and}

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(A)=\operatorname{max}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\;,\;\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}a_{ij}\bigg\}.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
A\coloneqq(a_{ij})
be an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix. Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{n}
be the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
A
, respectively. Then
\lambda_{1}\leq\gamma_{1}(A)\leq\gamma_{2}(A)\leq\lambda_{n}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph, and let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{n}
be the smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
, respectively. Then

\lambda_{1}\leq-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\right)\leq\lambda_{n}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\neq j}R_{ij}^{\bm{\omega}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}R_{ij}^{\bm{\omega}}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\bigg(\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\frac{\overline{\omega}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}.

Also,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}R_{ii}^{\bm{\omega}}=\text{trace}(R^{\bm{\omega}})=0.

Hence by Lemma
4.1
, we have

\lambda_{1}\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\lambda_{n},\;\;\text{where}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{min}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\;,\;0-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg),\;\text{ and}

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{max}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\;,\;0-\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg).

Hence

\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{-1}{n(n-1)}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg)\leq\lambda_{n}.\qed

The following corollary follows easily from Theorem
4.4
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph. If
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{n}
are the smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
, then

\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{1}\geq\frac{1}{n-1}\bigg(\sum\limits_{i\sim j}\frac{2}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}+\sum\limits_{i\rightarrow j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\bigg).

5
Energy of
R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X)

Lu et al. in
[
18
]
investigated the energy for Hermitian Randić matrix
R_{H}(X)
and computed various bounds. We analogously define the energy
\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
. That is,
\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
is the sum of the absolute values of the eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
. We find that most of the results on energy of
R_{H}(X)
also hold good for the matrix
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
due to the fact that
\operatorname{trace}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=\operatorname{trace}(R_{H}(X))=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}}^{2}=2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph of order
n
, and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X)
. Then

\sqrt{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+n(n-1)(\operatorname{det}R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X))^{2/n}}\leq\varepsilon(R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X))\leq\sqrt{2nR^{(-1)}(X_{U})},

where equality holds if
|\lambda_{1}|=\cdots=|\lambda_{n}|
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem 3.5 in
[
18
]
, that can be obtained using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and geometric-arithmetic inequality.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph, and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X)
, where
\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
, and
k
is the number of negative eigenvalues. Then

\varepsilon(R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X))\geq 2(n-k)\bigg(\frac{\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{n-k}}.

Proof.

Given that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
of
R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X)
satisfy
\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{k+1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
. Also,
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
are negative and
\lambda_{k+1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are positive. As
\text{trace}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=0
, we have
\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|=2\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|=2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|
. Now

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}|+|\lambda_{2}|+\ldots+|\lambda_{k}|\geq

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{k}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\lambda_{k+1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(n-k)\left(\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-k)\left(\frac{\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-k}}.

Hence

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|\geq 2(n-k)\left(\frac{\operatorname{det}(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-k}}.\qed

Lemma 5.1
.

If
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
are non-negative and
k\geq 2
, then
\sum^{n}_{i=1}x^{k}_{i}\leq\big(\sum^{n}_{i=1}x^{2}_{i}\big)^{k/2}
.

Lemma
5.1
can be easily proved using the principle of mathematical induction and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
X
be a mixed graph. Then
\varepsilon(R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X))<e^{\sqrt{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}}.

Proof.

Let the eigenvalues of
R^{{\bm{\omega}}}(X)
be
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
. We have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}}^{2}=2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})
. Now

\displaystyle\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{|\lambda_{i}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{|\lambda_{i}|^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{|\lambda_{i}|^{2}}\right)^{k/2},\;\;\text{using Lemma~\ref{lem 5.1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{k!}(2R^{(-1)}(X_{U}))^{k/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sqrt{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}\right)^{k}=e^{\sqrt{2R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}}.\qed

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
and
\rho=\underset{i}{\operatorname{max}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
. Then

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho(n-2)+\sqrt{\rho^{2}(n-2)^{2}+16R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}\right).

Proof.

Suppose
\lambda_{k}
is the largest negative eigenvalue of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
. Then
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}
and
\lambda_{k+1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
. Now

\displaystyle\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))^{2}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|\right)^{2}\right),\;\;\text{as}\;\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|=\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<p\leq k}|\lambda_{i}||\lambda_{p}|+2\sum_{(k+1)\leq j<q\leq n}|\lambda_{j}||\lambda_{q}|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}+4\left[\sum_{1\leq i<p\leq k}|\lambda_{i}||\lambda_{p}|+\sum_{(k+1)\leq j<q\leq n}|\lambda_{j}||\lambda_{q}|\right].

(5.1)

We have
\left(|\lambda_{i}|-\rho/2\right)\left(|\lambda_{p}|-\rho/2\right)\leq\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}
, which implies that
|\lambda_{i}||\lambda_{p}|\leq\frac{\rho}{2}(|\lambda_{i}|+|\lambda_{p}|)
. Similarly,
|\lambda_{j}||\lambda_{q}|\leq\frac{\rho}{2}(|\lambda_{j}|+|\lambda_{q}|)
.

Hence from Equation (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+4\cdot\frac{\rho}{2}\left(\sum_{1\leq i<p\leq k}(|\lambda_{i}|+|\lambda_{p}|)+\sum_{(k+1)\leq j<q\leq n}(|\lambda_{j}|+|\lambda_{q}|)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+2\rho\left((k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}|+(n-k-1)\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+2\rho\left((k-1)\frac{\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))}{2}+(n-k-1)\frac{\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+\rho(n-2)\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)).

After solving the preceding inequality, we get

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho(n-2)+\sqrt{\rho^{2}(n-2)^{2}+16R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}\right).\qed

Theorem 5.5
.

Let the eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
be
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
. Then

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma(n-2)+\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(n-2)^{2}+16R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}\right).

Proof.

Considering
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
, we have
\left(|\lambda_{i}|-\sigma/2\right)\left(|\lambda_{p}|-\sigma/2\right)\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}
. It implies that
|\lambda_{i}||\lambda_{p}|\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}(|\lambda_{i}|+|\lambda_{p}|)
. Similarly,
|\lambda_{j}||\lambda_{q}|\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}(|\lambda_{j}|+|\lambda_{q}|)
. Now from Equation (
5.1
), we get the quadratic inequality
\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))^{2}\geq 4R^{(-1)}(X_{U})+\sigma(n-2)\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))
. Solving this quadratic inequality, we get the required result.
∎

We now provide some basic inequalities that help us in determining two other lower bounds for the energy of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
.

Lemma 5.2

(Pólya-Szegö Inequality
[
8
]
)
.

If
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are positive real numbers for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{M_{1}M_{2}}}\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{2},

where
M_{1}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{max}}a_{i}
,
M_{2}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{max}}b_{i}
,
m_{1}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{min}}a_{i}
and
m_{2}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{min}}b_{i}
.

Lemma 5.3

(Ozeki’s Inequality
[
8
]
)
.

If
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are non-negative real numbers for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}\left(M_{1}M_{2}-m_{1}m_{2}\right)^{2},

where
M_{1}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{max}}a_{i}
,
M_{2}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{max}}b_{i}
,
m_{1}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{min}}a_{i}
and
m_{2}=\underset{1\leq i\leq n}{\operatorname{min}}b_{i}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
,
\rho=\underset{i}{\operatorname{max}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
and
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
. Then

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\geq\frac{\sqrt{8n\rho\sigma R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}}{\rho+\sigma}.

Proof.

Considering
\rho=\underset{i}{\operatorname{max}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
and
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
, by Pólya-Szegö Inequality we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\sigma}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho}}\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|\right)^{2}

or,

\displaystyle 2nR^{(-1)}(X_{U})\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\rho+\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma\rho}}\right)^{2}\left(\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\right)^{2}

or,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\geq\frac{\sqrt{8n\rho\sigma R^{(-1)}(X_{U})}}{\rho+\sigma}.\qed

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)
,
\rho=\underset{i}{\operatorname{max}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
and
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
. Then

\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X))\geq\frac{\sqrt{8nR^{(-1)}(X_{U})-n^{2}(\rho-\sigma)^{2}}}{2}.

Proof.

Considering
\rho=\underset{i}{\operatorname{max}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
and
\sigma=\underset{i}{\operatorname{min}}{|\lambda_{i}|}
, by Ozeki’s Inequality we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}\left(\rho-\sigma\right)^{2}

or,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{8nR^{(-1)}(X_{U})-n^{2}(\rho-\sigma)^{2}}}{2}\leq\varepsilon(R^{\bm{\omega}}(X)).\qed
