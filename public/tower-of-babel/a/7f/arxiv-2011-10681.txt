Analysis and Evaluation of Baseline Manipulation in Demand Response Programs
Xiaochu Wang, and Wenyuan Tang

Abstract
The customer baseline is required to assign rebates to participants in baseline-based demand response (DR) programs. The average baseline method has been widely accepted in practice due to its simplicity and reliability. However, the customer’s baseline manipulation is little-known in the literature. We start from a customer’s perspective and establish a Markov decision process to model the customer’s payoff-maximizing problem. The behavior of a rational customer’s underconsumption on DR days and overconsumption on non-DR days are revealed. Furthermore, we propose an approximated baseline method and show how the consumption distribution and program parameters affect the results. Due to the curse of dimensionality, a linear policy-based rollout algorithm is introduced to obtain a practical approximate solution. Finally, a case study is carried out to illustrate the baseline manipulation, where the simulation results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed methods and shed light on how to properly design baseline methods.



I
Introduction

Dmand response (DR) is considered as an effective and reliable solution to balance the power supply and demand when the power system is under stress
[
1
]
.
In incentive-based DR programs, the program provider reaches an agreement with customers, who are offered rebates for their participation or active load adjustment according to the real-time instructions
[
2
]
.
The customer baseline calculation is critical to those DR programs under which rebates are paid to the customers for consumption below the baseline.
If the baseline is overestimated, the program provider overpays the customers which may result in program deficit. Underestimation of baseline may keep customers away from participation.
Coming up with a perfect baseline method is demanding.
First, the baseline is the counterfactual consumption that cannot be measured directly.
In addition, the implementation of DR program may motivate the customers to change their consumption patterns, which makes the baseline calculation more difficult
[
3
]
.

Accuracy, integrity, and simplicity are three critical characteristics of proper baseline methods
[
4
]
.
Integrity shows that a good baseline is robust to the customer’s attempts to manipulate the system, while the simplicity requires the baseline mechanism to be simple enough for end-use customers.
The average baseline methods have been widely accepted by system operators and third-party entities, e.g., PJM, NYISO, CAISO, and EnerNOC due to their simplicity and reliability
[
5
]
.
The baseline accuracy analysis has attracted wide attention in the literature.
The performance of several popular baseline methods is analyzed in
[
6
]
based on the bias metric.
An elaborated error analysis of the current existing baseline methods, including HighXofY, LowXofY, exponential moving average, and regression method, is given in
[
7
]
. An economic analysis of a peak time rebate program is also carried out. Their case study reveals that the utility pays at least half of its revenue as a rebate solely due to the inaccuracy of baseline methods.

Although the inaccurate estimation of customer baseline is claimed as the main reason for the program inefficiency, baseline manipulation is also a key factor for this issue
[

]
. Customers are encouraged to inflate their baselines to gain higher rebates if the baseline methods were chosen improperly. Such manipulation in a day-ahead load response program is illustrated in

For example, customers put all lights on in a stadium during daytime to increase the probability of getting a large amount of rebates

In the literature, there are few mathematical models in quantifying such manipulations.

II
Markov Decision Process Formulation

A Markov decision process (MDP) is a mathematical framework for solving sequential decision-making problems under uncertainties. For rational customers who want to maximize the expected payoff, they need to consider DR event uncertainty and then identify the optimal policies. Therefore we model the payoff-maximizing problem as a MDP.

II-A

DR Event

A DR event indicates the situation with high wholesale market prices or when the system reliability is under stress. The corresponding day is called a DR-event day, or simply, a DR day. A day without a DR event is called a non-DR day. We use
y_{t}
to indicate whether day
t
is a DR day or not, defined as

{y_{t}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if day $t$ is a DR day},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

A DR event typically lasts from one to several hours, which is referred as the DR duration. In this paper, we only consider one-hour duration for convenience.

II-C

Customer Utility Function

A customer obtains benefits from electricity consumption, captured by the utility function
u_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})
, where
a_{t}
is the customer’s electricity consumption and
z_{t}
is an external parameter indicating the benefit variation on different days.
The utility function is defined as non-decreasing and concave in consumption
[
18
]
.
There are several forms of utility functions in the literature, e.g., the quadratic and exponential utility functions. Our model is valid under any non-decreasing and concave utility functions.

Let
c_{t}(a_{t})
be the cost of purchasing electricity from load serving entities. Specifically, we consider the flat retail price
\omega
and therefore we have
c_{t}(a_{t})=\omega a_{t}
. A rational customer will choose the intrinsic baseline
a_{t}^{B}
as the optimal consumption. The intrinsic baseline can be obtained via

a_{t}^{B}\in\argmax_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t}),

(5)

where
\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})\coloneqq u_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})-c_{t}(a_{t})
is the customer net utility function. The consumption is subjected to a physical constraint
0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}
.

II-D

Formulation of the MDP Model

Consider a finite time horizon of
T
days, indexed by
t=0,1,\ldots,T-1
, with a terminal stage
T
. The state at day
t
is given by
{\bm{s}}_{t}=({\bm{x}}_{t},y_{t},z_{t})
, where
{\bm{x}}_{t}=(x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,Y})
is the recent consumption from the last
Y
non-DR days (with
x_{t,1}
the most recent),
y_{t}
is the indicator of DR events, and
z_{t}
is an external parameter indicating the variation of the utility function.
The decision variable on day
t
is the consumption
a_{t}
. The transition from
{\bm{x}}_{t}
to
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
is deterministic, given by

\begin{split}{\bm{x}}_{t+1}&=(x_{t+1,1},\ldots,x_{t+1,Y})\\
&=\begin{cases}(a_{t},x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,Y-1}),&\text{if }y_{t}=0,\\
{\bm{x}}_{t}=(x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,Y}),&\text{if }y_{t}=1,\end{cases}\end{split}

(6)

whereas the transition from
y_{t}
to
y_{t+1}
is captured by the conditional probability
p(y_{t+1}|y_{t})
. The immediate payoff of the MDP model is

g_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t},a_{t})=\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})+y_{t}r[\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})-a_{t}]_{+},\;\forall\;t<T

(7)

where
[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
, with the terminal payoff as
g_{T}(\bm{s}_{T},a_{T})=0
. The net utility function
\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})
is assumed to be concave in
a_{t}
, and the
r
is the rebate price for load reduction
[\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})-a_{t}]_{+}
.

A rational customer’s problem is to find an optimal policy
\{a_{t}^{*}({\bm{s}}_{t}),\forall t\}
to maximize the expected payoff
\mathbb{E}_{\{y_{t},z_{t},\forall t\}}\left[\mathop{\sum}_{t=0}^{T}{g_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})}\right]
.
Following a dynamic programming approach, we obtain the Bellman equation as

V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})=\mathop{\max}_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{g_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t},a_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}_{t+1}|{\bm{s}}_{t}}[V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})]\},

(8)

for
t<T
, with
V_{T}(\bm{s}_{T})=0
. The maximizers
\{a_{t}^{*}({\bm{s}}_{t}),\forall t\}
for this optimization problem give the optimal policy.

III
Optimal Policy and Structural Results

In this section, we solve the payoff-maximizing problem using dynamic programming. In addition, we establish several structural results, which provide insights into the optimal policies.

III-A

Properties of the Average Baseline Method

To derive structural results of the optimal policies, we show the monotonicity, convexity, and supermodularity of the average methods.

Definition 1

(Monotonicity)
.

For any
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\bm{x}}\leq{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
if
x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{\prime},\;\forall{i}=1,\ldots,n
. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is increasing if for any
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
{\bm{x}}\leq{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
,
f({\bm{x}})\leq f({\bm{x}}^{\prime})
.
f
is decreasing if
(-f)
is increasing.

Proposition 2
.

The average methods, i.e.,
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
,
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
, are increasing in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. Furthermore,
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
is decreasing in
X
, while
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
is increasing in
X
.

Definition 3

(Convexity)
.

A set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex if for any
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
\lambda{\bm{x}}+(1-\lambda){\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
. Given a convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex over set
\mathcal{C}
if for any
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

f(\lambda{\bm{x}}+(1-\lambda))\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f({\bm{x}}^{\prime}).

(9)

f
is concave if
(-f)
is convex.

Proposition 4
.

The HighXofY method
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)
is convex in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
since the pointwise maximum operation preserves convexity. The LowXofY method

Definition 5

(Supermodularity)
.

For any
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define their join as
{\bm{x}}\vee{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=(\max\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\},\ldots,\max\{x_{n},x_{n}^{\prime}\})

{\bm{x}}\wedge{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=(\min\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\},\ldots,\min\{x_{n},x_{n}^{\prime}\})

A function

{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

f({\bm{x}})+f({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\leq f({\bm{x}}\vee{\bm{x}}^{\prime})+f({\bm{x}}\wedge{\bm{x}}^{\prime}).

(10)

f
is submodular if
(-f)
is supermodular.

Proposition 6
.

The HighXofY method
{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X)

Structural Results

Lemma 7
.

The optimal value function
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})
is increasing in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
under the average method
\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})
.

Proof.

The rebate received on day

is given by

\mathcal{R}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},y_{t},a_{t})=y_{t}r[\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})-a_{t}]_{+}.

(11)

Since the baseline
\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})
is increasing in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, one can easily show the rebate
\mathcal{R}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},y_{t},a_{t})
is also increasing in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. According to the Bellman equation (
8
) and the terminal condition, one can easily show that
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})
is increasing in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at stage

∎

Theorem 8
.

Let us define the intrinsic baseline without strategizing as
a_{t}^{B}(z_{t})\in\argmax_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})
. Then we have

a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},y_{t}=1,z_{t})\leq a_{t}^{B}(z_{t})\leq a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},y_{t}=0,z_{t}),

(12)

where
a_{t}^{*}({\bm{s}}_{t})
is the optimal policy with strategizing, given by

a_{t}^{*}({\bm{s}}_{t})\in\argmax_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{g_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t},a_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}_{t+1}|{\bm{s}}_{t}}[V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})]\}.

(13)

We investigate a rational customer’s strategic behavior by comparing the customer’s optimal policies to the intrinsic baseline. The
a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},1,z_{t})
and
a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},0,z_{t})
represent the optimal policies on DR days and non-DR days, respectively.
The above theorem shows that, to maximize the expected payoff, a customer should underconsume on the DR days and overconsume on the non-DR days.
Although the DR program helps to alleviate the supply shortage on DR days, it also encourages the customers to consume more on the non-DR days. The analysis of this manipulation level is the main focus in this paper.
The following theorem shows the optimal policy on DR days.

Theorem 9
.

The optimal policy on a DR day,
a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},1,z_{t})
, is a threshold policy in its baseline
\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})
, given by

a_{t}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{t},1,z_{t})=\begin{cases}a_{t}^{B},&\text{if }\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})<\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}},\\
a_{t}^{U},&\text{if }\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})\geq\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}},\end{cases}

(14)

where
a_{t}^{B}
is the customer’s intrinsic baseline defined in (
5
), and
a_{t}^{U}\in\argmax_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{{\pi_{t}(a_{t};z_{t})+r(\mathcal{B}({\bm{x}}_{t})-a_{t})}\}
is the optimal action with penalty, with
a_{t}^{U}\leq a_{t}^{B}
.
The
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}}
is a threshold value that is given by

\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}}=\frac{\pi_{t}(a_{t}^{B};z_{t})-\pi_{t}(a_{t}^{U};z_{t})}{r}+a_{t}^{U}.

(15)

The above theorem clearly shows that a rational customer will reduce their consumption only if the calculated baseline reaches a threshold. Otherwise, the customer remains the intrinsic consumption.

IV
The Approximated Baseline Method

The impact from program parameters, i.e.,
X
and
Y
, on the optimal policies is little-known under the average method. In addition, how the distribution of the customer’s consumption affects the calculated baseline is still unclear.
To address these issues, we propose an approximated baseline method that takes the program parameters explicitly under which more structural results are revealed.

IV-A

Approximation of Baseline Method

We use the sample mean and extreme order values to formulate an approximation of the baseline. For convenience, we remove the index
t
and let
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=({\mathcal{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}_{Y})
be the recent consumption vector consisting of
Y
independent random variables. To define the approximation of HighXofY, we introduce the largest order statistic (or sample maximum)
{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}=\max\{{\mathcal{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}\}
and
the sample mean
\bar{\mathcal{X}}=(1/X)({\mathcal{X}}_{1}+\cdots+{\mathcal{X}}_{Y})
.
The approximated HighXofY is essentially the convex combination of the sample mean
\bar{\mathcal{X}}
and sample maximum
{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}
, given by

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}(\bm{\mathcal{X}},X,Y)=\lambda\bar{\mathcal{X}}+(1-\lambda){\mathcal{X}}_{\max},

(16)

where
\lambda=\frac{X-1}{Y-1}\in[0,1]
is the weight of the sample mean.
Clearly, when
\lambda=0\;(X=1)
we have
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\mathcal{X}},X,Y)={\mathcal{X}}_{\max}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\mathcal{X}},X,Y)=\bar{\mathcal{X}}
while
\lambda=1\;(X=Y)
. This also shows
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\mathcal{X}},X,Y)={\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\mathcal{X}},X,Y)
under these two cases.
Similarly, after introducing the sample minimum
{\mathcal{X}}_{\min}=\min\{{\mathcal{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{X}}_{Y}\}
and sample median
{\mathcal{X}}_{m}
, one can define the approximated LowXofY and MidXofY as
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}^{X}(\bm{\mathcal{X}},X,Y)=\lambda\bar{\mathcal{X}}+(1-\lambda){\mathcal{X}}_{\min}
and
\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{Y}^{X}(\bm{\mathcal{X}},X,Y)=\lambda\bar{\mathcal{X}}+(1-\lambda){\mathcal{X}}_{m}
, respectively.

The
{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}
in (
16
), however, prevents us deriving structural results since
{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}
is essentially the High1ofY method. Therefore, we come up with an approximation for the sample maximum
{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}
.
Reference
[
21
]
gives an effective formula to estimate the sample maximum from the normal distribution.
Suppose the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is truncated in the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(0,1,\alpha,\beta)
. Then we have the estimation of sample maximum for
\mathcal{N}(0,1,\alpha,\beta)
as

{f}(Y)=\Phi_{\text{t}}^{-1}[(0.5+\varepsilon_{\text{t}})^{1/Y}],

(17)

where
\Phi_{\text{t}}{(\cdot)}
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the truncated normal distribution,
\varepsilon_{\text{t}}
is a parameter calculated by a bisectional searching algorithm that optimizes the match of (
17
) to the expected values
[
21
]
.
The customer consumption is assumed to obey a truncated normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma,\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b})
, which is converted into the corresponding standard normal distribution with truncation
\mathcal{N}(0,1,\alpha,\beta)
, with
\alpha=\frac{\mathsf{a}-\mu}{\sigma}
and
\beta=\frac{\mathsf{b}-\mu}{\sigma}
.
Then the estimation of the sample maximum is
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\max}\approx\mu+{f}(Y)\sigma
.
For a specific sample
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, one can estimate the sample maximum
x_{t,\max}=\max_{1\leq i\leq Y}\{x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,Y}\}
using

\hat{x}_{t,\max}=\bar{x}_{t}+{f}(Y)s_{t},

(18)

where
\bar{x}_{t}=\frac{1}{Y}(x_{t,1}+\cdots+x_{t,Y})
is the sample mean and
s_{t}=\sqrt{s_{t}^{2}}
is the standard deviation with
s_{t}^{2}=\frac{1}{Y-1}\mathop{\sum}_{i=1}^{Y}{(x_{t,i}-\bar{x}_{t})^{2}}
.
Then, based on (
16
), one can obtain the final approximated HighXofY in terms of the recent consumption
{\bm{x}}_{t}
,
X
, and
Y
as

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Y}^{X}({\bm{x}}_{t},X,Y)=\bar{x}_{t}+\frac{Y-X}{Y-1}{f}(Y)s_{t},\;\forall\;Y\geq 2.

(19)

To quantify the goodness-of-fit of this approximation, we define the percent error of approximation as
(\mathcal{B}-\hat{\mathcal{B}})/{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}\times 100\%
,
where
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is the approximated baseline. The
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is used as the denominator because it provides a more stable reference for illustration than the actual baseline. To show the feasibility of this approximation, we use both real data and synthetic data (with additive white Gaussian noise, which can be found in the case study), where the results are shown in Fig.
1
.
Although there are some outliers, the absolute approximation errors are less than 5%.
These results show that the proposed approximated baseline method is effective.

V
Approximate Dynamic Programming via Rollout Algorithm

Solving the MDP problem using dynamic programming is computationally challenging due to the curse of dimensionality
[
22
]
. Therefore, approximate methods are required to solve the MDP problem with a large state space
[
23
]
.
In this section, we apply the rollout algorithm to obtain a simulation-based forward dynamic programming solution.

V-
1
The rollout algorithm

The key idea of the rollout algorithm is starting from some given heuristic and constructing another policy with better performance
[
24
]
.
Essentially, the rollout algorithm is an online forward dynamic programming procedure that selects actions to obtain the maximum expected payoff calculated based on the given heuristic policy.
The improved performance is guaranteed by the performance improvement properties
[
25
]
, which we show later.
Rollout algorithms step forward in time, which requires estimating the payoff-to-go when evaluating decision rules.
Specifically, given a heuristic policy, we calculate the expected payoff-to-go under the heuristic based on a Monte Carol simulation
[
26
]
. The formula in finding the optimal actions under the rollout algorithm is given as

a_{t}^{\text{R}}\in\argmax_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\left\{g_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{s_{i}\}}\left[\mathop{\sum}_{i=t+1}^{T}g_{i}(s_{i},a_{i}^{\text{L}}(s_{i}))|s_{t}\right]\right\},

(22)

where
a_{t}^{\text{L}}(s_{t})
is the given heuristic policy. The sample paths in calculating the expected payoff-to-go can be generated by applying additive white Gaussian noise to customer historical consumption. The DR events along the path are generated according to the conditional probability
p(y_{t+1}|y_{t})
. The details of generating sample paths are given in the case study.

V-
2
The heuristic policy

A heuristic policy is any method to select decision rules in each stage within the space of decision rules. In this paper, a linear policy is selected as the heuristic for the rollout algorithm. A linear policy with
k
parameters can be
a(s;\bm{\theta})=\theta_{1}\cdot\phi_{1}(s)+\cdots+\theta_{k}\cdot\phi_{k}(s)
, where
\phi_{1}(s),\ldots,\phi_{k}(s)
are the extracted features and
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})
is the corresponding parameter vector.
Given the optimal policy on DR days in (
14
), we approximate the policy on the non-DR days based on one feature, given by

a_{t}^{\text{L}}({\bm{s}}_{t}|_{y_{t}=0})=a_{t}^{B}+\theta_{t}\phi({\bm{s}}_{t}),

(23)

where the feature
\phi({\bm{s}}_{t})
can be the maximum of the recent consumption. The idea of this linear policy is to facilitate customers with an easy way to inflate their baseline based on the recent consumption. The best parameter
\theta_{t}^{*}
can be obtained by maximizing the expected payoff from the current stage
t
to the end stage
T
, i.e.,

\theta_{t}^{*}\in\argmax_{0\leq\theta_{t}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\{s_{i}\}}\left[\mathop{\sum}_{i=t}^{T}g_{i}(s_{i},a_{i}^{\text{L}}(s_{i}))|s_{t}\right],

(24)

where the linear policy
a_{t}^{\text{L}}(s_{t})
on non-DR days and DR days are given in (
23
) and (
14
). The sample paths for calculating the expected payoff-to-go are also generated by apply additive white Gaussian noise. For simplicity, a stationary linear policy
{\theta}^{*}=\theta_{t=1}^{*}
is applied for simulation.

V-
3
Performance improvement property
[
25
]

The performance improvement property of the rollout algorithm is illustrated in the following definition.

Definition 16

(Rollout Improvement Property)
.

For a heuristic
a_{t}^{\text{L}}
and a rollout policy
a_{t}^{\text{R}}
, we say
a_{t}^{\text{R}}
rollout improving if for
t=1,2,\ldots,T
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\sum}_{i=t}^{T}g_{i}(s_{i},a_{i}^{\text{L}}(s_{i}))|s_{t}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\sum}_{i=t}^{T}g_{i}(s_{i},a_{i}^{\text{R}}(s_{i}))|s_{t}\right].

(25)

This rollout improvement property is assured based on the construction of
a_{t}^{\text{R}}
in (
22
) and
a_{t}^{\text{L}}
in (
24
).

\gamma

In practice, we choose

3

\check{u}
.

(27)

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we give the baseline manipulation analysis of the used accepted average baseline methods.
We establish a Markov decision process to formulate the customer’s profit-maximization problem. By comparing the optimal policies to the intrinsic baseline, we verify a rational customer’s behavior: underconsumption on DR days and overconsumption on non-DR days.
An approximated HighXofY method is proposed to understand how the distribution of the customer consumption and the selection of program parameters affect the calculated baseline.
The case study shows that the customer baseline manipulation is a single-peaked function in
X
and remains almost the same under different HighYofY methods.
The linear policy based rollout algorithm provides adequate accuracy compared to the results from dynamic programming. These results give practical information for DR program providers to design accurate and robust baseline methods.

V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
, which can be proven by induction.

At stage
t=T-1
, we have
V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})=V_{T}(\bm{s}_{T})=0
. As a result,
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t}|_{y_{t}=0})=\max_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{\pi_{t}(a_{t})\}
is independent of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and thus supermodular. On the other hand,

\begin{split}V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t}|y_{t}=1)&=\max_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{{\pi_{t}(a_{t})+r[\mathcal{B}-a_{t}]_{+}}\}\\
&=\begin{cases}\pi_{t}(a_{t}^{B},z_{t}),&\text{if }\mathcal{B}\leq\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}},\\
\pi_{t}(a_{t}^{U},z_{t})+r(\mathcal{B}-a_{t}^{U}),&\text{if }\mathcal{B}>\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\text{th}}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(43)

According to the result from theorem
9
,
V_{t}(\mathcal{B}|y_{t}=1)
is convex and increasing in
\mathcal{B}
. Based on the assumption,
\mathcal{B}
is increasing and supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. Apply the composition rule from lemma
7
, one can show that
V_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},1,z_{t})
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. Now suppose the
V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})
is supermodular in
{\bm{s}}_{t+1}
, then we need to show
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. When
y_{t}=1
, the optimal value function is

\begin{split}V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t}|y_{t}=1)&=\max_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{{\pi_{t}(a_{t})+r[\mathcal{B}-z_{t}]_{+}}\}\\
&\quad\;+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}_{t+1}|{\bm{s}}_{t}}[V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})].\end{split}

(44)

The first term has been proved to be supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
above. The second expectation term is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}_{t}
when
y_{t}=1
, by assumption. Then the statement is true for
y_{t}=1
. When
y_{t}=0
, we have the optimal value function as

V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t}|y_{t}=1)=\max_{0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}}\{{\pi_{t}(a_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{s}}_{t+1}|{\bm{s}}_{t}}[V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})]}\}.

(45)

By assumption,
V_{t+1}({\bm{s}}_{t+1})
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}=(a_{t},x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,Y-1})
. Therefore, the whole term inside the
\max\{\cdot\}
is supermodular in
({\bm{x}}_{t},a_{t})
. As a result,
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t}|y_{t}=1)
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
since the maximization over a lattice
0\leq a_{t}\leq{\hat{a}}
preserves the supermodularity
[
19
]
. Thus the statement
V_{t}({\bm{s}}_{t})
is supermodular in
{\bm{x}}_{t}
has been proved, as well as the main conclusion in this theorem.
∎
