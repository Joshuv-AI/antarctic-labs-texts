Removing Malicious Nodes from Networks Thanks: A preliminary version of this work appeared in the Proceedings of the 18th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS-19) [ 47 ] .
Sixie Yu, Yevgeniy Vorobeychik

Abstract
A fundamental challenge in networked systems is detection and removal of suspected malicious nodes. In reality, detection is always imperfect, and the decision about which potentially malicious nodes to remove must trade off false positives (erroneously removing benign nodes) and false negatives (mistakenly failing to remove malicious nodes). However, in network settings this conventional tradeoff must also account for node connectivity. In particular, malicious nodes may exert malicious influence, so that mistakenly leaving some of these in the network may cause damage to spread. On the other hand, removing benign nodes causes direct harm to these, and indirect harm to their benign neighbors who would wish to communicate with them. We formalize the problem of removing potentially malicious nodes from a network under uncertainty through an objective that takes connectivity into account. We show that optimally solving the resulting problem is NP-hard. We propose a theoretically guaranteed approximation algorithm for solving it based on Semidefinite Programming relaxation and randomized rounding. We then further scale our technique to deal with large-scale networks by reformulating the problem as a linear programming. A combination of both theoretical and empirical analysis, the latter using both synthetic and real data, provide strong evidence that our algorithmic approaches are highly effective.

Introduction

One of the major problems in networked settings is to identify and remove potentially malicious nodes.
For example, malicious nodes in social networks may correspond to accounts created by malicious parties which spread social spam, hate speech, fake news, and the like, with considerable deliterious effects
[
2
,
9
]
.
Major social network platforms consequently devote considerable efforts to identifying and removing fake or malicious accounts
[
34
,
35
]
.
Nevertheless, evidence suggests that the problem remains pervasive
[
4
,
29
]
.
Similarly, in cyber-physical systems (e.g., smart grid infrastructure), computing nodes compromised by malware can cause catastrophic losses, and mitigation through detection and removal of such malicious nodes is a major problem
[
28
,
46
]
.

A common thread in these scenarios is the tradeoff faced in deciding which nodes to remove: removing a benign node (false positive) causes damage to this node, which may be inconvenience or loss of productivity, and potentially also results in indirect losses to its neighbors; on the other hand, failing to remove a malicious node (false negative) can have deleterious effects as malicious influence spreads to its neighbors. The key observation is that the loss associated with a decision whether to remove a node depends both on the node’s likelihood of being malicious and its
local network structure
. Therefore the central challenge faced in deciding which potentially malicious nodes to remove is to account for the combination of uncertainty about whether particular nodes are malicious, and the indirect (network) effects of the decision. This combination makes the decision about which nodes to remove fundamentally a challenging combinatorial optimization problem.

We consider the problem of choosing which subset of nodes to remove from a network given an associated probability distribution over joint realizations of all nodes as either malicious or benign (that is, we allow the probability that node
i
is malicious to depend on whether its neighbors are malicious, as in collective classification and relational learning
[
26
,
38
]
).
We then model the problem as minimizing the expected loss with respect to this distribution, where the loss function is composed of three parts: the
direct
loss (
L_{1}
) stemming from removed benign nodes, the
indirect
loss associated with cutting links between removed and remaining benign nodes (
L_{2}
), and the loss associated with malicious nodes that remain, quantified in terms of
links
these have to benign nodes (
L_{3}
).

Our first contribution is an efficient algorithmic approach for choosing a subset of nodes to remove by approximately minimizing the expected loss function described above, where the expectation is taken with respect to an exogenously specified node maliciousness distribution for a given network; the distribution can be estimated by any suitable machine learning model, e.g., logistic regression, Markov network, or graph neural network.
However, we show that minimizing the loss is NP-hard, even with highly restrictive settings.
Our solution strategy is resorting to convex relaxation, i.e., we relax the original combinatorial optimization problem to a convex Semidefinite programming (SDP) in continuous domain, which can be solved in polynomial time.
We solve the convex relaxation for a globally optimal solution; then we convert it to an approximate solution in the discrete domain by utilizing a principled randomized rounding procedure.
Finally, we show a theoretical guarantee on the quality of the approximate solution.
For simplicity, we call the approximation algorithm the SDP-based algorithm.

The scalability of the SDP-based algorithm is limited by its computational costs.
To improve the scalability, we reformulate the original optimization problem as a linear programming (LP) with standard linearization and relaxation techniques; the resulting LP can easily handle instances with thousands of nodes.
Due to the improvement on scalability, we are able to experiment on an extensive array of instances parameterized by combinations of the trade-off parameters, i.e.,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}
.
One surprising observation is that the LP formulations of most instances admit integral solutions (hence optimal) despite the NP-hardness; in other words, most instances are “easy”, in the sense that they are solvable in polynomial time; in contrast, we call those instances without integral solutions “hard.
Based on the observation, we design an LP-based heuristic algorithm to solve the problem; the idea is to slightly perturb the trade-off parameters of a “hard” instance such that the perturbed instance can be optimally solved by the LP.

Related Work

There are several lines of research that are related to the problem of choosing which subset of nodes to remove from a network.
The first relevant literature involves the problem of
network node classification
or
collective classification

[
38
,
26
,
36
,
18
]
.
The fundamental task in this line of research is to assign labels to nodes in a network.
In this approach, one makes use of the attributes associated with the nodes as well as the connections among them to learn a joint distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
over the assignment of labels to nodes.
Viewing this problem from the perspective of identifying malicious nodes, a typical next step would be to make the most likely assignment of labels to nodes, which involves the use of inference algorithms (e.g., the sum-product algorithm for computing a maximum a posterior estimate), itself a non-trivial problem
[
43
]
.
Collective classification methods would be an input into our approach, in the form of the learned probability distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
; however, as our experiments demonstrate, our approach is significantly better than the naive baseline which removes nodes that are most likely to be malicious, as it is crucial to account for the network structure not merely at inference time, but also at decision time.

Another related line of research considers a problem of
graph scan statistics
and hypothesis testing
[
5
,
33
,
37
]
. These study the following problem: given a graph
G
where each node is associated with a random variable with an exogenously specified probability distribution, find a subset of nodes that maximizes a scan statistic defined over subsets of nodes (for example, this statistic may generalize log-likelihood ratio).
Arias-Castro et al. 2011
proposed a scan statistic for special graph models.
Priebe et al. 2005
proposed a scan statistic defined over clusters with special geometric structures. These methods do not easily generalize to arbitrary graph models or arbitrary scan statistics.
Sharpnack et al. 2013
employed the generalized log-likelihood ratio as the scan statistic. By assuming that the set of malicious nodes has sparse connections with others, the hypothesis test can be converted to solving a graph cut problem, which is further relaxed into a convex optimization by leveraging the Lovász extension of a graph cut. This method is not scalable to large graphs and it does not account for potentially inaccurate estimates of the node maliciousness distribution.

Our problem is loosely related to the burgeoning field of adversarial machine learning
[
41
]
, although we do not explicitly consider issues of adversarial response in our setting, as well as the broader problem of security games on networks
[
3
]
.
There are several approaches that explicitly consider adversarial response in a networked environment.
Yu et al. 2018
consider how to mitigate the spread of malicious information on social networks by modeling the problem as a Stackelberg game between the defender, who aims to mitigate the spread of malicious information, and an attacker who tries to initiate malicious spread.
Hajaj et al. 2019
conducted behavioral experiments to investigate the impact of adversarial behavior in a networked coordination task.
Vorobeychik and Letchford 2015
formulated the problem of securing a set of interdependent assets as a Stackelberg security game, where interdependence can be abstracted as a graph
G

[

49

and node classification

Model

Consider a network
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}
(
|\mathcal{V}|=n
) is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}
the set of edges.
Each node
i\in\mathcal{V}
represents a user and each edge
(i,j)
represents some connection (e.g., friendship) between user
i
and user
j
.
We assume that
G
is undirected and has no self-loop.
The adjacency matrix
\bm{A}
of the graph is symmetric, with zeros on the diagonal.
The entries of
\bm{A}
are binary if the graph is unweighted (i.e.,
A_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
), or some non-negative real numbers if the graph is weighted (i.e.,
A_{i,j}\in\R_{+}
).
Subsequent presentation focuses on unweighted graphs; generalization to weighted graphs is straightforward.

We explain the problem by first considering complete information about the identity of malicious and benign nodes, and subsequently describe our actual model in which this information is unknown.
Specifically, let
\bm{\pi}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a
configuration
of the network, with
\pi_{i}=1
indicating that a node
i
is malicious, with
\pi_{i}=0
when
i
is benign.
For notational convenience, we also define the complement
\bar{\pi}_{i}=1-\pi_{i}
, i.e.,
\bar{\pi}_{i}=1
if and only if
i
is benign.
Consequently,
\bm{\pi}
(and
\bar{\bm{\pi}}
) assigns malicious or benign labels to each node.
Let the set of malicious and benign nodes be
\mathcal{V}^{+}
and
\mathcal{V}^{-}
, respectively.
Our goal is to remove a subset
\mathcal{S}
of nodes such that the impact of the remaining malicious nodes on the network is minimized, while limiting disruptions caused to the benign subnetwork.

To formalize this intuition, we define a loss function associated with the set
\mathcal{S}
.
This loss function has three components, each corresponding to a key consideration in the problem.
The first part of the loss function is defined below:

L_{1}=\left|\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap\mathcal{S}\right|,

(2.1)

which is the cardinality of wrongly removed nodes, encoding the
direct
loss associated with removing benign nodes; this simply penalizes every false positive, as one would naturally expect.
The loss
L_{1}
ignores the broken
relationships
among benign nodes that result from the decision, which is captured by the second component
L_{2}
as follows:

L_{2}=\Big|\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\mid i\in\left(\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})\right),j\in\left(\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap\mathcal{S}\right)\}\Big|.

(2.2)

The loss
L_{2}
imposes a penalty for cutting connections between benign nodes that are removed (i.e.,
\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap\mathcal{S}
) and benign nodes that remain (i.e.,
\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})
).
In other words,
L_{2}
captures the
indirect
consequence of removing benign nodes
on the structure of the benign subnetwork
.
This aspect is critical to capture in network settings, as relationships and connectivity are what networks are about.
The third component
L_{3}
measures the consequence of
failing to remove malicious nodes
in terms of connections from these to benign nodes:

L_{3}=\Big|\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\mid i\in\left(\mathcal{V}^{+}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})\right),j\in\left(\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})\right)\}\Big|.

(2.3)

The sets
\mathcal{V}^{+}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})
and
\mathcal{V}^{-}\cap(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S})
represent the malicious and the benign nodes that remain in the network, respectively.
Intuitively, we can think of
L_{3}
as encoding remaining deleterious effect that the malicious nodes still exert on the benign nodes, e.g., spammers keep sending annoying emails to normal users.

The total loss combines these three components as a weighted sum, i.e.,
L=\alpha_{1}L_{1}+\alpha_{2}L_{2}+\alpha_{3}L_{3}
, with
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=1
and each
\alpha_{i}
being nonnegative.
Other than this constraint, we allow
\alpha_{i}
s to be arbitrary relative weights of the different components, specified depending on the domain.
For example, if we are concerned about false positives (i.e., wrongly removing benign nodes), but not very much about network structure, we would set
\alpha_{1}\gg\alpha_{2}
.
Alternatively, we would set
\alpha_{3}
to be very large (compared with
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
) to indicate that the loss of false negatives dominates the others.

We now rewrite the loss function in a way that will prove more mathematically convenient.
Define a vector
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
x_{i}=1
if and only if node
i
is removed (
i\in\mathcal{S}
), and
x_{i}=0
if node
i
remains in the network (
i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
).
Recall that the goal of the model is to identify a subset of nodes
\mathcal{S}
to remove so as to minimize the impact of the remaining malicious nodes on the network, while limiting disruptions caused to the benign subnetwork.
This goal is naturally captured by the loss function below:

\displaystyle L(\bm{x})=\alpha_{1}\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x_{i}\bar{\pi}_{i}}}_{L_{1}}+\alpha_{2}\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}^{n}{A_{i,j}x_{i}\bar{x}_{j}\bar{\pi}_{i}\bar{\pi}_{j}}}_{L_{2}}+\alpha_{3}\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}^{n}{A_{i,j}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{x}_{j}\pi_{i}\bar{\pi}_{j}}}_{L_{3}}.

(2.4)

With complete information, it is immediate that the loss is minimized if
\mathcal{S}
contains all, and only, the malicious nodes.
In reality, the identity of malicious and benign nodes is unknown to us, and instead we have a probability distribution over these.
This probability distribution may be obtained by learning probability that a node is malicious given its features from past data, e.g., with a Markov network
[
38
]
or a graph neural network
[
18
]
.
To formalize, let
\bm{\pi}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}
, where
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
captures the joint probability distribution over node configurations (malicious or benign).
For our purposes, we make no assumptions on the nature of this distribution; a special case would be when maliciousness probabilities for nodes are independent (conditional on a node’s observed features), but our model also captures natural settings in which configurations of network neighbors are correlated (e.g., when malicious nodes tend to have many benign neighbors).
The loss function described in (
2.4
) becomes the following expected loss:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[L(\bm{x})]=\alpha_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x_{i}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\pi}_{i}]}+\alpha_{2}\sum_{i,j}^{n}{A_{i,j}x_{i}\bar{x}_{j}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\pi}_{i}\bar{\pi}_{j}]}+\alpha_{3}\sum_{i,j}^{n}{A_{i,j}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{x}_{j}\mathbb{E}[\pi_{i}\bar{\pi}_{j}]}.

(2.5)

To have a compact formulation we re-write the expected loss in matrix notations.
We first define auxiliary matrices
\bm{B},\bm{P}
and
\bm{M}
as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}}

2.1
Computational Hardness

We now show that optimally solving
MINT
is NP-hard, even for highly restrictive settings.
The reduction is from the
Maximum Independent Set
(MIS) problem.

Theorem 1
.

Minimizing
MINT
is NP-hard even when

•

the probability that a node is malicious restricts to
1/2
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[\pi_{i}]=1/2
for all
i
, and

•

the random variables
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{j}
are independent for any
i\neq j
.

Proof.

Given a graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, the MIS problem is to find an independent set in
G
of maximum cardinality.
We consider the special case of
MINT
with the restrictions listed in Theorem
1
; in addition, we ignore the loss of cutting edges among benign nodes (i.e.,
\alpha_{2}=0
), and let the loss of wrongly keeping malicious nodes in the benign subnetwork dominate the others (i.e.,
\alpha_{3}\gg\alpha_{1}
).
The loss function of the special case is as follows:

L^{\prime}=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x_{i}}}_{L_{1}^{\prime}}+\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{3}}{4}\sum_{i,j}^{n}{A_{i,j}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{x}_{j}}}_{L_{3}^{\prime}}.

(2.9)

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the maximum independent set of
G
, and
\mathcal{S}
the set of removed nodes.
We first show that keeping only
\mathcal{K}
is the optimal solution, i.e, removing the set
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{K}
is optimal.
Notice that removing any node from
\mathcal{K}
is sub-optimal, as an extra
\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}
loss is incurred to
L_{1}^{\prime}
.
Next, we show that it is sub-optimal to put any node in
\mathcal{S}
back to
\mathcal{K}
.
Suppose we put a set of nodes
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
back to
\mathcal{K}
; this must introduce additional edges to
\mathcal{K}
, otherwise it is not the maximum independent set.
As
\alpha_{3}\gg\alpha_{1}
, introducing any edge to
\mathcal{K}
is sub-optimal.
Since we cannot add or remove any node from
\mathcal{K}
, we should keep the nodes in
\mathcal{K}
and remove the set
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{K}
.

For the other direction, suppose
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{K}
minimizes
L^{\prime}
.
We show that
\mathcal{K}
is the maximum independent set of
G
.
First, suppose
\mathcal{K}
is not an independent set, which means that there is at least a pair of nodes connected by an edge.
As
\alpha_{3}\gg\alpha_{1}
, removing the nodes decreases
L^{\prime}
, which contradicts that
\mathcal{S}
minimizes
L^{\prime}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{K}
must be an independent set.
Next, we show that
\mathcal{K}
is maximum.
Suppose there exists another set independent set
\mathcal{K}
and
|\mathcal{K}|>|\mathcal{K}|
.
Then keeping the nodes in
\mathcal{K}
would further decrease
L^{\prime}
by decreasing
L_{1}^{\prime}
, which contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{S}
minimizes the
L^{\prime}
.
We conclude that
\mathcal{K}
is the maximum independent set.
∎

It is known that no constant-factor approximation algorithm exists for solving the MIS problem unless P=NP (Section 4.4 at
[
39
]
).
As a result, we have that no constant-factor approximation algorithm exists for solving
MINT

Corollary 2
.

No constant-factor approximation algorithm exists for solving
MINT
unless P=NP.

Algorithm

3.1
SDP-based Approximation Algorithm

In this section we present an approximation algorithm to solve
MINT
.
The general idea of the algorithm is to relax the binary quadratic programming (BQP) to a convex SDP.
Then we solve the SDP for a global optimum, which can be done in polynomial time by using, e.g., the interior point method
[
8
]
.
The global optimum is in continuous space; we convert it to a binary solution of
MINT
by utilizing a classic randomized rounding procedure
[
15
]
.
In what follows, we first describe the convex relaxation and subsequently present the randomized rounding procedure.

3.1.1
Convex Relaxation

We first convert the feasible region to
\{-1,1\}^{n}
by substituting the original decision variable
\bm{x}
with
(\bm{x}+\bm{1})/2
.
The resulting objective function is

\left(\frac{\bm{x}+\bm{1}}{2}\right)^{\top}\bm{Q}\left(\frac{\bm{x}+\bm{1}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{\bm{x}+\bm{1}}{2}\right)^{\top}\bm{b}.

(3.1)

Omitting constant terms, the optimization problem of
MINT
becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{Q}\bm{1}+\bm{b})

(3.2)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}.

In subsequent discussion, we assume that
\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{Q}\bm{1}+\bm{b})\neq 0
; this is to
exclude the pathological case where both
\bm{x}
and the complement
-\bm{x}
achieve the same loss.
The problem in (
3.2
) is a
non-homogeneous
quadratic programming due to the linear term involving
\bm{x}
(i.e.,
\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}(\bm{Q}\bm{1}+\bm{b})
).
For convenience of later analysis, we reformulate (
3.2
) to a
homogeneous
quadratic programming without the linear term.
The idea is to augment the original formulation with an extra dimension.
First, define a real symmetric matrix
\hat{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}

\hat{\bm{Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}(1/4)\bm{Q}&(1/4)\left(\bm{Q}\bm{1}+\bm{b}\right)\\
(1/4)\left(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{Q}+\bm{b}^{\top}\right)&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3.3)

Then, let

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}\in\{-1,1\}^{n+1}.

Next, we convert the homogeneous formulation to an SDP, a convex programming solvable in polynomial time.
The key step is to introduce an auxiliary matrix
\hat{\bm{X}}=\hat{\bm{x}}\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}
and utilize the following relations (see Section 2.2 at

\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{x}}=\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{x}}\right)=\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{x}}\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\right)=\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{X}}\right),

With (

) is reformulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{X}}\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}=\hat{\bm{x}}\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{x}}\in\{-1,1\}^{n+1}.

Notice that the diagonal entries of
\hat{\bm{X}}
are all ones.
In addition, by the definition of
\hat{\bm{X}}
, it follows that
\bm{z}^{\top}\hat{\bm{x}}\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{z}=\left(\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{z}\right)^{\top}\left(\hat{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{z}\right)\geq 0
for any
\bm{z}\in\R^{n+1}
.
Thus, the matrix

is positive semidefinite.
A classic approach to relax the above optimization to an SDP is by replacing the equality constraint with the following

]

\hat{\bm{X}}\succeq\bm{0}\text{ and }\hat{X}_{ii}=1,i=1,\ldots,n+1,

(3.7)

where

means that
\hat{\bm{X}}
is positive semidefinite.
It is direct to verify that any feasible solution of (

Finally, the convex relaxation of (
3.4

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}}\hat{\bm{X}}\right)

(3.8)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{ii}=1,i=1,\ldots,n+1

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}\succeq 0.

We solve (

[
8
]
.
The solution

is a real symmetric matrix in continuous space; however, what we ultimately need is a discrete solution in
\{-1,1\}^{n}

Next, we describe a randomized rounding procedure to generate such a discrete solution from

.

3.1.2
Randomized Rounding

The convex relaxation described in the previous section leads to a lower bound on the optimal value of

In this section, we describe a randomized procedure to fill this gap; the procedure is based on the famous Goemans-Williamson rounding
[

Let

We now utilize the vectors

Let

The feasible solution is obtained with the following rules:

-\text{sign}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{v}_{i}]\right),&\text{if $t=-1$},\end{cases}

The randomness of the expectations comes from the sampling of

We summarize the randomized rounding procedure in Algorithm

After obtaining

In this section we show a performance guarantee for the approximation algorithm.

3.2
LP-based Approximation Algorithm

Although the SDP-based approximation algorithm enjoys nice theoretical guarantees, it is challenging to handle large-scale networks due to computational costs.
In particular, Algorithm
2
involves solving an SDP, which is computationally expensive; the general method to solve an SDP is the
primal-dual interior point method
, which under mild conditions converges to a pre-defined accuracy in
O(n^{1/2})
Newton iterations; each iteration involves roughly
O(n^{3})
operations (see Section 5.7 of
[
40
]
).

In this section, we describe another algorithm that is scalable to large-scale networks.
Specifically, we relax
MINT
to a linear programming (LP) by standard linearization and relaxation techniques.
One surprising observation is that for most instances of
MINT
the resulting LP admits integral solutions; this indicates that most instances of
MINT
are easy in the sense that they are solvable in polynomiam time.
We leverage this observation and propose an LP-based algorithm to solve
MINT
.

3.2.1
Linearization and Relaxation of
MINT

The high-level idea is to transfer the expected loss
\mathbb{E}[L(\bm{x})]
(i.e., Eq. (
2.5
)) from a binary quadratic function to a linear function involving binary variables.
Then we relax the binary variables to continuous domain (i.e.,
[0,1]^{n}
).
A detailed introduction of the linearization and relaxation is in
[
14
]
.

First, we linearize each quadratic term in the expected loss by introducing a new variable
z_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
.
Specifically, we add three constraints to guarantee that
z_{i,j}=1

if and only if
both
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are equal to
1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\leq x_{i}

(3.17)

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\leq x_{j}

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\geq x_{i}+x_{j}-1.

The relaxation step is simply replacing the original feasible region with the continuous region
[0,1]^{n}
.
The resulting LP is as follows.

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{n},\bm{z}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{E}|}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{n}{\Gamma_{ij}\left(x_{i}-z_{i,j}\right)}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\phi_{j}x_{j}}

(
MINT-LP
)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle 0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,\,\,\text{for all $i$}

\displaystyle 0\leq z_{i,j}\leq 1,\,\,\text{for all $(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}$}

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\leq x_{i},\,\,\text{for all $(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}$}

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\leq x_{j},\,\,\text{for all $(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}$}

\displaystyle z_{i,j}\geq x_{i}+x_{j}-1,\,\,\text{for all $(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}$}.

Recall that
\mathcal{E}
is the edge set of
G
.
The coefficient
\Gamma_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}=\alpha_{2}\bm{P}-\alpha_{3}\bm{M}
, and
\phi_{j}
is the
j
-th element of the vector
\bm{\phi}=\alpha_{1}\bm{B}\bm{1}-\alpha_{3}\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{1}
, where
\bm{P},\bm{M}
and
\bm{B}
are defined in (
2.6
).
A quadratic term
x_{i}x_{j}
is in the expected loss if and only if there is an edge between
i
and
j
; thus, the dimension of
\bm{z}
is
m
—the number of edges in
G
.
The total number of variables is
n+m
, and the total number of constraints is
2n+2m+3m
.
Therefore, the size of
MINT-LP
is linear with respect to the size of
G
.

3.2.2
Integral Solutions of
MINT-LP

An interesting observation is that many instances of
MINT-LP
admit integral solutions, i.e., the optimal solutions consist of precisely
1
or
0
.
Intuitively, this means that the hardness result of
MINT
may be too pessimistic and the worst-case instances can be rare in practice.
To verify the intuition, we identify an instance of
MINT-LP
with
\bm{\alpha}
and

, i.e.,

\bm{\alpha}
includes the trade-off parameters, i.e.,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
; the distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
is the estimated joint distribution over the maliciousness of nodes.
We assume a fixed
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
and characterize the “hard” and “easy” instances in the space of
\bm{\alpha}
, which gives us an informal picture of where the “hard” instances reside.

Let
\Delta_{2}=\{\bm{\alpha}\geq\bm{0}\mid\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}=1\}
be the standard probability simplex.
We generate different instances of
MINT-LP
by sampling the trade-off parameters
\bm{\alpha}
from

.

3.2.3
Perturbation-based Algorithm

With the observation from Figure
2
, we describe a scalable algorithm based on
MINT-LP
.
Given an input graph
G
and the joint distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
of maliciousness over nodes, we use
\texttt{MINT-LP}(\bm{\alpha})
to represent an input instance parameterized by the trade-off parameters.
The first step is to solve the corresponding LP, which results in an optimal solution
(\bm{x}^{\ast},\bm{z}^{\ast})
.
If
\bm{x}^{\ast}
is integral, it is also an optimal solution of
MINT
.
If
\bm{x}^{\ast}
contains fractional entries, we slightly perturb
\bm{\alpha}
with a vector
\bm{\delta}\in\R^{3}
, i.e.,
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}=\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\delta}
;
we control the magnitude of
\bm{\delta}
with some
\ell_{p}
norm and a parameter
\epsilon
(i.e.,
\lVert\bm{\delta}\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon
) such that the perturbed trade-off vector is close to the original one.
Intuitively, the small perturbation may “nudge” the fractional instance (
\texttt{MINT-LP}(\bm{\alpha})
) to a nearby one (
\texttt{MINT-LP}(\bm{\alpha}^{\prime})
) that admits integral solutions.
For the instances where the perturbation does not find an easy-to-solve alternative, we obtain an integral solution by randomly rounding the fractional
\bm{x}^{\ast}
, e.g., run a Bernoulli trial for the
i
-th node with success probability
x_{i}^{\ast}
.

Experimental Results

4.1
Results for the SDP-based Algorithm

In this section we present experiments to show the effectiveness of
MINT
.
In particular, we compared the performance of the SDP-based approximation algorithm (i.e., Algorithm
2
) with two baselines.
We focused on synthetic network structures, but derived distribution over maliciousness of nodes (i.e.,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
) using real data.
Specifically, we considered two types of network structures: Barabasi-Albert (BA)
[
6
]
and Watts-Strogatz networks (Small-World)
[
45
]
.
BA is characterized by its power-law degree distribution, where the probability that a randomly selected node has
k
neighbors is proportional to
k^{-r}
.
For the BA network we experimented with three variants, BA-1, BA-2, and BA-3, which differ in the value of the exponent
r
of their power-law degree distributions.
For Small-World networks we also experimented with three variants, SW-1, SW-2, and SW-3, that have different local clustering coefficients. For both networks we generated
20
instances each with
n=128
nodes.

In our experiments, we consider a simplified case where the maliciousness probabilities for nodes are independent.
In addition, we assume that a single estimator (e.g., logistic regression ) was trained to estimate the probability that a node is malicious based on features from past data.
Note that these assumptions are reasonable for the purpose of validating the effectiveness of our model, since the focus of our model is not how to estimate maliciousness probabilities.
For more complex cases, for example, when maliciousness probabilities for nodes are correlated, more advanced techniques (e.g., graph neural networks or Markov networks), can be applied to estimate the maliciousness probabilities, but our general approach would not change.

In all of our experiments, we derived
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
from data as follows.
We start with a dataset
\mathcal{D}
which includes malicious and benign instances (the meaning of these designations is domain specific), and split it into three subsets:
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
, with the ratio of
0.3:0.6:0.1
.
Our first step is to learn a probabilistic predictor
\hat{f}(\bm{x})
of maliciousness from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
.
Next, we randomly assign malicious and benign feature vectors from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
to the nodes on the network, assigning
10\%
of nodes with malicious and
90\%
with benign feature vectors.
For node
i
, we use its assigned feature vector
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
to obtain the
estimated
probability of this node being malicious, i.e.,
\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{i})
.
The distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
is thus
\{\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{i})\mid i\in\mathcal{V},\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\}
, which is used to construct the input to the algorithms.
However, to ensure that our evaluation is fair and reasonably represents realistic limitations of the knowledge of the true maliciousness distribution, we train another probabilistic predictor,
f(\bm{x})
, now using the data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
Applying this new predictor to the nodes and their assigned feature vectors, we now obtain a distribution
\mathbb{P}=\{f(\bm{x}_{i})\mid i\in\mathcal{V},\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\}
which we use to evaluate performance.

We conducted two sets of experiments.
In the first set of experiments we used synthetic networks and used data from the Spam
[
23
]
dataset to simulate the
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathbb{P}
, as described above.
The Spam dataset
\mathcal{D}
consists of spam and non-spam instances along with their corresponding labels.
In the second set of experiments we simulated
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathbb{P}
from the Hate Speech data
[
11
]
collected from Twitter.
The Hate Speech dataset is a crowdsourced dataset that contains three types of tweets: 1) hate speech tweets that express hatred against a targeted group of people; 2) offensive language tweets that appear to be rude, but do not explicitly promote hatred; and 3) normal tweets that neither promote hatret nor are offensive.
We categorized this dataset into two classes in terms of whether a tweet represents Hate Speech, with the offensive language tweets categorized as non-Hate Speech.
After categorization, the total number of tweets is
24783
, of which
1430
are Hate Speech.
We applied the same feature extraction techniques as
Davidson et al. 2017
to process the data.

Baselines

We compared Algorithm
2
with LESS, a state-of-the-art approach for graph hypothesis testing, as well as a simple baseline which removes a node
i
if its maliciousness probability
\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{i})>\theta^{\ast}
, where

\theta^{\ast}
is a specified threshold.
The algorithm LESS was proposed by
Sharpnack et al. 2013
, and considers a related hypothesis testing problem.
The null hypothesis is that each node in the graph is associated with a random variable sampled from the standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, while the alternative hypothesis is that there is a fraction of nodes where the random variables associated with them are sampled from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
with
\mu
other that
0
(in our interpretation, these are the malicious nodes).
The algorithm LESS employs the generalized log-likelihood over a subset of nodes as a test statistic, and the hypothesis test is to find the subset that has the strongest evidence against the null hypothesis.
We remove the subset of nodes found by LESS.
The simple baseline has a trade-off parameter
\alpha
between false-positive rate (FPR) and false-negative rate (FNR) (in our experiments
\alpha=0.5
).
We select an optimal threshold
\theta^{\ast}
that minimizes
\alpha\text{FPR}+(1-\alpha)\text{FNR}
on training data.

5
Discussion

We considered the problem of removing a subset of nodes from a network such that a customized loss function is minimized.
In particular, we designed a model named
MINT
that considers both the likelihood that a node is malicious, as well as the network structure.
Our key insight is for the loss function to capture both the
direct
loss associated with false positives and the
indirect
loss associated with cutting connections between benign nodes, and failing to cut connections from malicious nodes to their benign network neighbors.
We first showed that optimally solving
MINT
is NP-hard, even under very restrictive settings, e.g., the nodes’ probabilities of being malicious are independent.
Due to the hardness results, we proposed an approximation algorithm based on Semidefinite programming (SDP) and randomized rounding.
We also provided theoretical bound to guarantee the quality of approximation.
Experimental results showed that the approximation algorithm outperforms alternative approaches in terms of loss.

Although the SDP-based approximation algorithm enjoys both nice approximation guarantees and good empirical performance, it may be limited by the scalability to large-scale networks.
The approximation algorithm consists of two steps: 1) solving an SDP for an optimal solution in continuous space and 2) converting the optimal solution back to discrete space with a randomized rounding algorithm.
The bottle neck is solving the SDP, which costs
O(n^{3.5})
.
To improve the scalability, we reformulated
MINT
as a linear programming (LP), i.e,
MINT-LP
; empirically, the LP is much cheaper to solve than solving the SDP.
Intuitively, instead of solving the SDP in step 1, we solved the
MINT-LP
.
One interesting observation is that the LP formulations of many instances admitted integral solutions despite the NP-hardness; indeed, worst-case instances may be rare in practice; as a result,
MINT-LP
directly outputs optimal solutions of
MINT
.
For those really hard instances (i.e., the LP formulations do not admit integral solutions), we proposed a simple heuristic algorithm to convert the fractional solutions back to discrete domain.

Currently, we assume that the maliciousness probability distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
is given as an input to
MINT
; the distribution can be estimated by a suitable ML model, e.g., graph neural networks.
However, the training of the ML model usually focuses on minimizing prediction accuracy, which is at most a proxy of the actual loss that we care about.
One future direction is to unify the training of the ML model and the optimization of
MINT
in an end-to-end fashion, such that the training is directly supervised by the decision quality.
