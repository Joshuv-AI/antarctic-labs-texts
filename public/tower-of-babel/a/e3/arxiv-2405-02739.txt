Linear degenerate symplectic flag varieties: symmetric degenerations and PBW locus
Magdalena Boos, Giovanni Cerulli Irelli, Xin Fang, Ghislain Fourier

Abstract
. We conceptualize in the paper the linear degenerate symplectic flag varieties as symmetric degenerations within the framework of type A A equioriented quivers. First, in the larger context of symmetric degenerations, we give a self-contained proof of the equivalence of different degeneration orders. Furthermore, we investigate the PBW locus: geometric properties of the degenerate varieties in this locus are proved by realizing them from different perspectives.



1.
Introduction

For a quiver
\mathcal{Q}
, a representation
M
of
\mathcal{Q}
and a fixed dimension vector
\mathbf{e}
, the quiver Grassmannian is the moduli space of the
\mathbf{e}
-dimensional subrepresentations of
M
.
This represents a vast generalization of the notion of Grassmann varieties, which parametrize the
k
-dimensional subspaces in an
n
-dimensional space.
Quiver Grassmannians, with their reduced structures, are projective varieties

Projective varieties are not necessarily irreducible.
and furthermore, any projective variety is isomorphic to some quiver Grassmannian
[
21
]
. Therefore, few geometric properties are shared by all quiver Grassmannians, and one should specify a particular quiver or a certain family of quivers at the outset.
A specific example of a quiver Grassmannian is the flag variety, which can be described using subrepresentations for the type
A
equioriented quiver.

Quiver Grassmannians for quivers of Dynkin type have gained a lot of attention
[
5
,

,
7
,
9
,
10
,

,
12
,
15
,
16
,
19
,
20
]
.
For example in
[
8
]
, quiver Grassmannians of the equioriented type
A
quiver with arbitrary representation of fixed dimension vector are studied under the name
linear degenerate flag varieties
.
They can be viewed as degenerations (as projective varieties) of the flag varieties: they are fibers of a proper family
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
, called the universal degenerate flag variety, whose generic fiber is the flag variety and the parameterizing space
Y
is the representation variety of an equioriented type
A_{n}
quiver. In
loc.cit.
, the authors analyzed the locus where the fiber has minimal dimension (called flat locus); within it, the locus where the fiber is irreducible (called the flat irreducible locus); and within it, the locus where the fiber is naturally a Schubert variety (called the
PBW locus
). The family
\pi
is acted upon by a group
G
, and every
G
-orbit
G\cdot M
on
Y
is uniquely determined by a rank sequence
r^{M}:=(r_{i,j}^{M}|1\leq i\leq j\leq n)
. These loci are described in terms of rank sequences.

This current project is the first step in analyzing linear degenerate symplectic flag varieties.
The symplectic flag varieties appear naturally as highest weight orbits of the action of the symplectic group, similarly to how the classical flag varieties appear as orbits of the general linear group.

On the other hand, the symplectic flag variety is also naturally the fixed point set of an involution acting on the complete flag variety.
In order to define a universal
symplectic
degenerate flag variety, we consider an equioriented type
A
quiver
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{odd}}
, having an odd number of vertices.
In this case, the universal degenerate flag variety
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
is acted upon by an involution
⋆
whose fixed point family, denoted by
\pi^{\star}:X^{\star}\to Y^{\star}
, is the required universal symplectic degenerate flag variety.
This variety is equivariant with respect to a closed subgroup
H
of
G
.
The points of
Y^{\star}
are called symplectic representations of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}
.

Our first result describes the
H
-orbits and their closures inside
Y^{\star}
in terms of rank sequences.
In fact, this construction is part of a general theory of quivers with self-duality, wherein this situation is termed the
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{odd}},-1)
type.
We describe the orbit closures also in the other type
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{even}},+1)
.
In general, for a positive integer
n
and a sign
\varepsilon=\pm 1
there is the notion of
\varepsilon
-representation of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{n}
, if
\varepsilon=-1
these are called symplectic and if
\varepsilon=1
they are called orthogonal (see Section
2.2
).
In order to formulate the result, recall that the closures of the
G
-orbits in
Y
are also described by “cuts” and “shifts”
[
1
]
.
In order to give a combinatorial way to describe the closures of the
H
-orbits we introduce the notion of “symmetric cuts” and “symmetric shifts” (see Definition
2.10
). The following is our first main result (see Theorem
2.9
and Theorem
2.11
for precise statements):

Theorem
.

Let
n\geq 1
be a positive integer,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{n}
an equioriented quiver whose underlying graph is the Dynkin diagram
A_{n}
, and let
\sigma
denote its non-trivial involution.
Let
M
be a representation of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{n}
.

(i)

The intersection
G\cdot M\cap Y^{\star}
is non-empty if and only if the rank sequence
r^{M}=(r_{i,j}^{M})
of
M
satisfies the following relations:

(1.1)

\displaystyle r_{i,j}^{M}=r_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}^{M};

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{in type }(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{odd}},-1)\text{ and in type }(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{even}},+1),\text{ the ranks }r_{i,\sigma(i)}^{M}\text{ are even}.

(ii)

In the
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{odd}},-1)
and
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\mathrm{even}},+1)
types,
an
\varepsilon
-representation
N
lies in the closure of the
H
-orbit of an
\varepsilon
-representation
M
if and only if
r^{M}\geq r^{N}
if and only if
N
is obtained from
M
by a finite sequence of symmetric cuts and symmetric shifts.

We provide moreover an algorithm to compute for a given symmetric degeneration
M\leq_{\deg}^{\varepsilon}N
a sequence of one-parameter subgroups from
M
to
N
(see Section
5
for some examples).

Let us now turn our attention back to the universal symplectic degenerate flag variety. We consider some special fibers that we call
PBW
-degenerations of the symplectic flag variety, in analogy to
[
8
]
. They are parametrized by a subset
\mathbf{i}
of
[n]
, where the quiver has
2n-1
vertices. In
[
8
]
, such a subset
\mathbf{i}
is naturally associated with an element
w_{\mathbf{i}}
of the symmetric group. Similarly, we associate it here with an element in the Weyl group of the symplectic group.
The second main result of the paper is the following

Theorem
.

A PBW-degeneration of the symplectic flag variety is irreducible, reduced, normal, Cohen-Macaulay, Frobenius splitting and has rational singularities.

We deduce this from the following theorem (Theorem
3.5
) whose proof is given in Section
4
.

Theorem
.

The following varieties are isomorphic:

(i)

the symmetric degeneration
\mathrm{Sp}\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}}
;

(ii)

the Lagrangian quiver Grassmannian
\mathrm{Lag}_{\mathbf{e}}(M^{\mathbf{i}}\oplus\nabla(M^{\mathbf{i}}))
;

(iii)

the Schubert variety
X_{w_{\mathbf{i}}}
;

(iv)

for any
\mathbf{d}\in\mathrm{relint}(F^{\mathbf{i}})
, the
\mathbf{d}
-degenerate flag variety
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{d}}(\rho)
, where
\rho=\varpi_{1}+\ldots+\varpi_{n}
is the sum of all fundamental weights.

The study of other fibers of the universal symplectic degenerate flag variety is part of a future project. Here the similarities to the non-symplectic case are less obvious and the techniques to study Lagrangian quiver Grassmannians have to be developed further.

The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
we recall results on symmetric representation theory, properties of rank sequences and provide the proof of the first Theorem above. In Section
3
we introduce the Langragian quiver Grassmannians, the combinatorics of the Schubert varieties, recall the abelianizations of symplectic flag varieties and state our second main result, which will be then proved in Section
4
. We conclude the paper with the algorithm that computes the degeneration sequences and some examples in Section
5
.

In the paper we fix

2.1.
Recollection: equioriented type
A
quiver

Let
n\geq 2
be a positive integer. We denote by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
the equioriented Dynkin quiver of type
A_{n}
with the following labeling of vertices and arrows:

(2.1)

\textstyle{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}:1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{1}}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{n-1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha_{n-1}}
\textstyle{n.}

We denote by
\sigma
the anti-involution defined on vertices by
\sigma(i)=n+1-i
and on arrows by
\sigma(\alpha_{i})=\alpha_{n-1}
. The pair
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}},\sigma)
is the typical example of a
quiver with self-duality
or
symmetric quiver
(in the terminology of Derksen and Weyman
[
13
]
). In this section, we recall some well known facts about (symmetric) representation theory of
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}},\sigma)
(see
[
4
]
).

The category of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representations is endowed with a self-duality that we denote by
\nabla
, defined naturally as follows: for a given
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representation

(2.2)

\textstyle{M:}
\textstyle{M_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{1}}
\textstyle{M_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{2}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{n-2}}
\textstyle{M_{n-1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{n-1}}
\textstyle{M_{n},}

its (graded) dual is

(2.3)

\textstyle{\nabla M:}
\textstyle{M_{n}^{\ast}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{-f_{n-1}^{\ast}}
\textstyle{M_{n-1}^{\ast}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{-f_{n-2}^{\ast}}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{-f_{2}^{\ast}}
\textstyle{M_{2}^{\ast}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{-f_{1}^{\ast}}
\textstyle{M_{1}^{\ast}}

where we denote by
V^{\ast}=\Hom(V,\mathbb{C})
the linear dual of a vector space
V
and by
f^{\ast}
the linear dual of a linear map
f
. This explains why
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}},\sigma)
is called a quiver with self-duality. We denote by
\mathbf{dim}\,M=(\mathrm{dim}\,M_{1},\cdots,\mathrm{dim}\,M_{n})
the dimension vector of
M
. It is well-known and easy to prove that the indecomposable representations of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
are in bijection with the set
\{\varepsilon_{i,j}|\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}
of positive roots of a simple Lie algebra of type
A_{n}
. For every ordered pair of vertices
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, we denote by
U_{i,j}
the indecomposable representation whose dimension vector is
\varepsilon_{i,j}=\varepsilon_{i}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{j}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}
denotes the
i
-th element of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We sometimes think of
U_{i,j}
as the segment
[i,j]
. For every vertex
k
, the simple at
k
is
S_{k}:=U_{k,k}
, its projective cover is
P_{k}:=U_{k,n}
and its injective envelope is
I_{k}:=U_{1,k}
. We notice that
\nabla U_{i,j}\simeq U_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}
, in particular
\nabla P_{k}\simeq I_{\sigma(k)}
.

Every
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representation
M
can be written in a unique way (up to reordering of summands) as a direct sum
M=\bigoplus_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}U_{i,j}^{m_{i,j}}
where
m_{i,j}
denotes the multiplicity of
U_{i,j}
as a direct summand of
M
. Thus
M
can be thought of a collections of segments which form its
coefficient quiver
. We denote by
\mu(M)=(m_{i,j}|\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n)
the
multiplicity sequence
of
M
.

For every
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we denote by
f_{i,j}^{M}=f_{j-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i}:M_{i}\rightarrow M_{j}
the composite map and by
f_{i,i}^{M}=\mathrm{Id}_{M_{i}}
the identity on
M_{i}
. The
rank sequence
of
M
is the collection
r^{M}=(r_{i,j}^{M})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
of the ranks
r_{i,j}^{M}=\mathrm{rank}(f_{i,j})
. For example, the rank sequence of
U_{i,j}
is

r^{U_{i,j}}_{k,\ell}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\textrm{if }i\leq k\leq\ell\leq j,\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

We notice that

(2.4)

r_{k,\ell}^{\nabla M}=r_{\sigma(\ell),\sigma(k)}^{M},\textrm{ for every }1\leq k\leq\ell\leq n.

Throughout the paper we use the convention that
r_{i,j}^{M}=0
for
i=0
or for
j=n+1
and
r_{i,j}^{M}=+\infty
if
i>j
.

Lemma 2.1
.

A sequence of non-negative integer
r=(r_{i,j}|\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n)
is the rank sequence of a representation of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
if and only if it satisfies the inequalities

(2.5)

\displaystyle r_{i,j}\geq r_{i,j+1},

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq i\leq j<n;

(2.6)

\displaystyle r_{i-1,j}\leq r_{i,j},

\displaystyle\forall 1<i\leq j\leq n;

(2.7)

\displaystyle r_{i-1,j}-r_{i-1,j+1}\leq r_{i,j}-r_{i,j+1},

\displaystyle\forall 1<i<j<n.

Moreover, the relation between the multiplicity sequence
\mu(M)=(m_{i,j})
and the rank sequence
r^{M}
of an
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representation
M
is

(2.8)

m_{i,j}=r_{i,j}^{M}-r_{i,j+1}^{M}-r_{i-1,j}^{M}+r_{i-1,j+1}^{M}

with the convention that
r_{-,n+1}^{M}=0=r_{0,-}^{M}
. In particular, two
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representations are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank sequence.

Proof.

If
M=((M_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(f_{i}:M_{i}\rightarrow M_{i+1})_{i=1}^{n-1})
is a representation of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
then

\begin{array}[]{lr}f_{j}(\mathrm{Im}(f_{i,j}))\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(f_{i,j+1})&(1\leq i\leq j<n),\\
\mathrm{Im}(f_{i-1,j})\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(f_{i,j})&(1<i\leq j\leq n),\\
\mathrm{Im}(f_{i-1,j})\cap\ker(f_{j})\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(f_{i,j})\cap\ker(f_{j})&(1<i\leq j<n).\end{array}

The inequalities (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) follow directly; the inequality (
2.7
) follows from the fact that
r_{i,j}^{M}-r_{i,j+1}^{M}=\dim\mathrm{Im}(f_{i,j})\cap\ker(f_{j})
.
Equation (
2.8
) is obtained by inverting the following obvious formula

(2.9)

r^{M}_{i,j}=\sum_{k\leq i,j\leq\ell}m_{k,\ell}.

To prove the converse, we let
r=(r_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
to be a sequence of non-negative integers satisfying the inequalities (
2.5
), (
2.6
) and (
2.7
). In particular, for every
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, the integer
m_{i,j}:=r_{i,j}-r_{i,j+1}-r_{i-1,j}+r_{i-1,j+1}
is non-negative. Thus, by (
2.9
), the representation
M=\bigoplus U_{i,j}^{m_{i,j}}
is such that
r^{M}=r
.
∎

The Ringel form of

\langle-,-\rangle_{n}:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}

\langle\mathbf{d},\mathbf{e}\rangle_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{d}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{d}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i+1}

We have

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
M
be a
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representation and let
L=U_{q,s}
. Suppose that
L
embeds into
M
and let
\iota:L\hookrightarrow M
be a generic embedding. Let
Q=M/\iota(L)
be the generic quotient. Then the rank sequence
r^{Q}=(r_{k,\ell}^{Q})
of
Q
is obtained from the rank sequence
r^{M}=(r_{k,\ell}^{M})
of
M
as follows:

(2.15)

r_{k,\ell}^{Q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{k,\ell}^{M}-1&\text{if }r_{q,\ell}^{M}-r_{q,s+1}^{M}\leq r_{k,\ell}^{M}-r_{k,s+1}^{M}\text{ and }q\leq\ell\leq s,\\
r_{k,\ell}^{M}&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

An embedding of
L
into
M
is the span of
\{v_{q},v_{q+1},\cdots,v_{s}\}
where
v_{\ell}\in M_{\ell}
is non-zero,
v_{\ell}=f_{q,\ell}(v_{q})
,
v_{\ell}\in\mathrm{Im}(f_{q,\ell})\cap\ker(f_{\ell,s+1})
and
v_{\ell}\not\in\ker(f_{\ell,s})
, for every
q\leq\ell\leq s
.
We notice that in
M_{\ell}
there is a chain of vector subspaces:

(2.16)

(\mathrm{Im}f_{1,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})\subseteq(\mathrm{Im}f_{2,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq(\mathrm{Im}f_{q,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1}).

In order
\iota(L)
to be generic, the quotient
Q=M/\iota(L)
must have the greatest rank sequence among all possible quotients of
M
by
L
.
Thus a generic
v_{\ell}
as above does not belong to a subspace
(\mathrm{Im}f_{k,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})
which is strictly contained in
(\mathrm{Im}f_{q,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})
. Since

\dim(\mathrm{Im}f_{k,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})=r_{k,\ell}^{M}-r_{k,s+1}^{M}

we see that
r_{k,\ell}^{M}-r_{k,s+1}^{M}\leq r_{q,\ell}^{M}-r_{q,s+1}^{M}
for
k\leq q\leq\ell\leq s
. From this we get the first condition of (
2.15
) (for
k\leq q
). Suppose now that
q<k\leq\ell\leq s
. Then
(\mathrm{Im}f_{q,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1})\subseteq\mathrm{Im}f_{k,\ell}\cap\ker f_{\ell,s+1}
and hence
v_{\ell}
must belong to this subspace. We notice that
r_{q,\ell}^{M}-r_{q,s+1}^{M}\leq r_{k,\ell}^{M}-r_{k,s+1}^{M}
(for
q<k\leq s
) and hence we get the first condition of (
2.15
). If
\ell<q
or
\ell>s
there are no conditions to impose. This proves (
2.15
).
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
M
be a
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
-representation and let
L=U_{q,s}
. Suppose that
L
embeds into
M
and let
\iota:L\hookrightarrow M
be a generic embedding. Let
Q=M/\iota(L)
be the generic quotient. Suppose that
L
is not a direct summand of
M
. Then
q>1
and the rank sequence of
L\oplus Q
is

(2.17)

r_{k,\ell}^{L\oplus Q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{k,\ell}^{M}-1&\text{if }r_{q,\ell}^{M}-r_{q,s+1}^{M}=r_{k,\ell}^{M}-r_{k,s+1}^{M}\text{ and }k<q\leq\ell\leq s,\\

In particular,
M\leq_{c}L\oplus Q
.

Proof.

Since
r^{L\oplus Q}=r^{L}+r^{Q}
, formula (
2.17
) follows directly from (
2.15
). Let us prove that
M\leq_{c}L\oplus Q
. If
L
is a direct summand of
M
then

and we are done. Let us assume that
L
is not a direct summand of
M
. Then,
q>1
, because otherwise
L
would be injective and hence
\iota
would be a split mono-morphism. Let us consider the function

f(k,\ell)=r_{q,\ell}^{M}-r_{q,s+1}^{M}-r_{k,\ell}^{M}+r_{k,s+1}^{M}.

By Lemma
2.2
,
f(q-1,s)=0
because
L
is not a direct summand of
M
. Moreover, again by Lemma
2.2
, the following formulas holds:

(2.18)

\displaystyle f(k,\ell)=f(k,\ell+1)+(r^{M}_{q,\ell}-r^{M}_{q,\ell+1}-r^{M}_{k,\ell}+r^{M}_{k,\ell+1})\geq f(k,\ell+1)\geq 0;

(2.19)

\displaystyle f(k,\ell)=f(k+1,\ell)+(r^{M}_{k+1,\ell}-r^{M}_{k+1,s+1}-r^{M}_{k,\ell}+r^{M}_{k,s+1})\geq f(k+1,\ell)\geq 0.

It follows that if
f(k,\ell)=0
then
f(k,\ell+1)=f(k+1,\ell)
. Let
q_{1}\in[q,s]
be the minimal index such that there exists
k\in[1,q-1]
such that
f(k,q_{1})=0
and let
t_{1}
be the minimal index such that
f(t_{1},q_{1})=0
. Similarly, let
t_{2}\in[1,q-1]
be the minimal index such that there exists
\ell\in[q,s]
such that
f(k,\ell)=0
and let
q_{2}
be the minimal index such that
f(t_{2},q_{2})=0
. We notice that by (
2.18
) and (
2.19
),
q\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq s
and
t_{2}\leq t_{1}<q
. We notice that in view of Lemma
2.2
,
U_{t_{1},q_{2}-1}
and

.

) as

(2.20)

r_{k,\ell}^{L\oplus Q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{k,\ell}^{M}-1&\text{if }t_{1}\leq k<q\leq q_{1}\leq\ell<q_{2},\\

Now it is easy to conclude from Lemma
2.4
: if

then

; in this case

is obtained from

2.2.
\varepsilon
-representations

Let
\varepsilon
be either
1
or
-1
. For an
\varepsilon
-form on a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
W
we mean a bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle:W\times W\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\langle v,w\rangle=\varepsilon\langle w,v\rangle
for every
v,w\in W
. Recall from
[
13
,
4
]
that a
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form on
M^{0}
is a non-degenerate
\varepsilon
-form such that the restriction of the form on
M_{i}\times M_{j}
is zero unless
j=\sigma(i)
. We notice that if
M^{0}
admits a
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form, then
M_{i}
and
M_{\sigma(i)}
are dual to each other, in particular they have the same dimension. Moreover, if
n=2h-1
is odd then
M_{h}
inherits a non-degenerate
\varepsilon
-form, thus it is even dimensional if
\varepsilon=-1
.
Let us suppose that
M^{0}
is endowed with a
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form
\langle-,-\rangle
. We consider the involution
{}^{\star}:R(M^{0})\rightarrow R(M^{0})
which associates to
f=(f_{i}:M_{i}\rightarrow M_{i+1})
its anti-adjoint
f^{\star}=(f^{\star}_{i})\in R(M^{0})
given by the formula

\langle f_{i}(v),w\rangle+\langle v,f^{\star}_{\sigma(i)}(w)\rangle=0

for every
i=1,\cdots,n-1
,
v\in M_{i}
and
w\in M_{\sigma(i+1)}
. We notice that
(M^{0},f^{\star})\simeq\nabla(M^{0},f)
.
The variety of
⋆
-fixed points is denoted
by

R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)=\{f\in R(M^{0})|\,f=f^{\star}\};

and point in
R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
are called
\varepsilon
-representations
of
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}},\sigma)
with underlying space
(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)
. Sometimes,
1
-representations are called orthogonal and
(-1)
-representations are called symplectic. We use the notation
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\bullet},\varepsilon)
to distinguish the four cases
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{odd},\pm 1)
and
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{even},\pm 1)
.
The group of isometries

\GL^{\bullet}(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)=\{g\in\GL^{\bullet}(M^{0})|\,\langle gv,gw\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle,\,\forall v,w\in M^{0}\}

of
(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)
acts on
R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
by change of basis and by
[
13
, Theorem 2.6]
(see also
[
4
, Theorem 2.5]
), we have that, for every
M\in R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
,

(2.21)

\GL^{\bullet}(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)\cdot M=\GL^{\bullet}(M^{0})\cdot M\cap R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle).

Given
M,N\in R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
we write
M\leq_{\deg}^{\varepsilon}N
if
N\in\overline{\GL^{\bullet}(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)\cdot M}
.

Every
\varepsilon
-representation can be written in an essentially unique way as a direct sum of indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations. By
[
13
, Proposition 2.7]
, the indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations of
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}},\sigma)
are

\{U_{i,j}\oplus U_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}|\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n,i\leq\sigma(j)\}

in types
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{odd}},-1)
and
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{even}},+1)
, and

\{U_{i,j}\oplus U_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}|\,1\leq i\leq j\leq n,i<\sigma(j)\}\cup\{U_{i,\sigma(i)}|\,1\leq i\leq\sigma(i)\leq n\}

in types
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{odd}},+1)
and
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{even}},-1)
. For this reason, the types
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{odd}},-1)
and
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{even}},+1)
are called the
split types
in
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
M=(M^{0},f)\in R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
be an
\varepsilon
-representation of
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A_{n}}}
. Then, for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
,

(2.22)

f_{i,j}^{\star}=(-1)^{j-i}f_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}.

In particular, in the split types
\langle f_{i,\sigma(i)}(v),v\rangle=0
for all
v\in M_{i}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
j-i
. If
j-i=1
then
f_{i,j}=f_{i}
and

f_{i,j}^{\star}=f_{i}^{\star}=-f_{\sigma(i+1)}=-f_{\sigma(j)}=-f_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}.

Suppose
j-i>1
then for every
v\in M_{i}
and
w\in M_{\sigma(j)}
we have

\langle f_{i,j}(v),w\rangle=\langle f_{j-1}\circ f_{i,j-1}(v),w\rangle=-\langle f_{i,j-1}(v),f_{\sigma(j)}(w)\rangle=\\
=(-1)^{j-1-i}\langle v,f_{\sigma(j-1),\sigma(i)}\circ f_{\sigma(j)}(w)\rangle=-\langle v,(-1)^{j-i}f_{\sigma(j),\sigma(i)}(w)\rangle.

From (
2.22
) we get that

\langle f_{i,\sigma(i)}(v),v\rangle=(-1)^{\sigma(i)-i}\varepsilon\langle f_{i,\sigma(i)}(v),v\rangle.

The number
(\sigma(i)-i)=n+1-2i
is even if
n
is odd, and it is odd if
n
is even. Therefore, in types
(A_{\mathrm{odd}},-1)
or
(A_{\mathrm{even}},1)
we have
\langle f_{i,\sigma(i)}(v),v\rangle=-\langle f_{i,\sigma(i)}(v),v\rangle
concluding the proof.
∎

The following consequence of Lemma
2.7
will be used later. To state it, we recall that for a given representation
M=(M^{0},f)
, a sub-representation
N
of
M
is a graded vector sub-space

N^{0}=\bigoplus N_{i}\subseteq\bigoplus M_{i}=M^{0}

such that
f_{i}(N_{i})\subset N_{i+1}
, for every
1\leq i<n
. For
M\in R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
, a sub-representation
N\subseteq M
is called
isotropic
if
N^{0}
is an isotropic subspace of

3.
Linear degenerate symplectic flag varieties: PBW locus

For a positive integer
n
, set
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. In this section we fix a subset
\mathbf{i}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t}\}\subseteq[n-1]
with
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{t}\leq n-1

i_{0}=0
.

3.1.
Lagrangian quiver Grassmannian

We will define Lagrangian quiver Grassmannian in the special case
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{odd}},-1)
. Thus, in this section we work with the quiver
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
with the following labelling of vertices and arrows:

1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\longrightarrow n-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\omega\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{n-1}^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(n-1)^{*}\longrightarrow\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{1}^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}1^{*}

General theory of Lagrangian quiver Grassmannians will be developed in a separate publication.

Let
M\in R(M^{0};\langle-,-\rangle)
be a symplectic
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
-representation. A subrepresentation
N\subseteq M
is called
Lagrangian
, if the vector subspace

N^{0}=\bigoplus N_{i}\subseteq\bigoplus M_{i}=M^{0}

is Lagrangian, i.e. if
N^{0}=(N^{0})^{\perp}
where
(N^{0})^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal of
N^{0}
with respect to the symplectic form
\langle-,-\rangle
. By identifying
M^{0}
with its dual via the non-degenerate bilinear form, we see that
\nabla(M^{0}/N^{0})
is the annihilator of
N^{0}
and thus,
N\subset M
is Lagrangian if and only if

M/N\cong\nabla N
. We notice that if
N\subset M
is Lagrangian then, since
\omega=\sigma(\omega)
,
N_{\omega}\subset M_{\omega}
is a Lagrangian subspace of the symplectic vector space
(M_{\omega},\langle-,-\rangle|_{M_{\omega}\times M_{\omega}})
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{1^{*}})\in\mathbb{N}^{2n-1}
be a dimension vector. The Lagrangian quiver Grassmannian
\mathrm{Lag}_{\mathbf{e}}(M)
for a symplectic
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
-representation
M
and a dimension vector
\mathbf{e}
is defined to be the set of Lagrangian submodules of
M
of dimension vector
\mathbf{e}
.

The Lagrangian quiver Grassmannian is a closed subset of the quiver Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbf{e}}(M)
and is embedded into the product of Grassmannians
\prod_{i\in Q_{0}}\mathrm{Gr}_{e_{i}}(M_{i})
. One can show that if
M
is projective, then
\mathrm{Lag}_{\underline{\mathrm{dim}}M}(M\oplus\nabla(M))
is irreducible and reduced. Since this result is not necessary in the paper, we omit its proof.

3.2.

3.4.

For the fixed

4.1.

By the results in

4.2.
(1)\Leftrightarrow(3)

The proof is executed in several steps. The strategy of the proof is the same as

Recall that

4.2.1.
Linear degenerate symplectic flag variety as invariant variety

In this part we realize the linear degenerate symplectic flag variety as an invariant variety of a linear degenerate flag variety
[
8
,
9
]
under an involution.

For this, define

\mathbf{i}^{\prime}:=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t},2n-1-i_{t},\ldots,2n-1-i_{1}\}\subseteq[2n-1]

where we write
i_{t+\ell}:=2n-1-i_{\ell}
for

,
9

\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}:=\left\{(V_{1},\ldots,V_{2n-1})\in\prod_{k=1}^{2n-1}\mathrm{Gr}_{k}(V)\mid f_{k}^{\prime}(V_{k})\subseteq V_{k+1}\text{ for $1\leq k\leq 2n-2$}\right\},

where the linear maps

f_{i}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}\mathrm{pr}_{i_{k}+1},&\text{if $i=i_{k}$ for some $1\leq k\leq 2t$};\\
\mathrm{id},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We consider the following well-defined involution

\tau:\prod_{k=1}^{2n-1}\mathrm{Gr}_{k}(V)\to\prod_{k=1}^{2n-1}\mathrm{Gr}_{k}(V),\ \ (V_{1},\ldots,V_{2n-1})\mapsto(V_{2n-1}^{\bot},\ldots,V_{1}^{\bot}),

where the orthogonal spaces are with respect to the symplectic form

. Since
\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}
is a subset of the product of Grassmann varieties, let
(\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}})^{\tau}
denote the invariant space with respect to this action of
\tau

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists an isomorphism
\mathrm{Sp}\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}}\cong(\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}})^{\tau}
.

Proof.

Define two morphisms

\phi:\mathrm{Sp}\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}}\to\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}},\ \ (V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\mapsto(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n},V_{n-1}^{\bot},\ldots,V_{1}^{\bot});

\psi:(\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}})^{\tau}\to\mathrm{Sp}\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}},\ \ (V_{1},\ldots,V_{2n-1})\mapsto(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}).

It remains to show that both maps are well-defined, and the image of
\phi
is
\tau
-invariant.

(i)

The image of
\phi
is in
\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}
. Indeed, since
V_{n}=V_{n}^{\bot}
, it suffices to show that
f_{k}^{\prime}(V_{2n-k}^{\bot})\subseteq V_{2n-k-1}^{\bot}
for

; otherwise since

, we can write

for

\mathrm{pr}_{i_{t+\ell}+1}(V_{i_{\ell}+1}^{\bot})\subseteq V_{i_{\ell}}^{\bot}

\mathrm{pr}_{i_{\ell}+1}^{*}=\mathrm{pr}_{2n-i_{\ell}}
where

is the adjoint map with respect to the symplectic form. This last statement holds according to the choice of the basis

.

(ii)

The image of
\phi
is
\tau
-invariant: it follows from the assumption that

is Lagrangian and hence
V_{n}^{\bot}=V_{n}
.

(iii)

The map

, and the fact that if
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{2n-1})
is
\tau
-invariant, then

is Lagrangian.

∎

C_{j}:=[2n]\setminus\{i_{1}+1,\ldots,i_{j}+1\}
for
1\leq j\leq 2t
. After applying
p_{h_{k}}
and
p_{h_{2n-k}}
we obtain

p_{h_{k}}(v)=\underbrace{\sum_{i\in C_{h_{k}}}\lambda_{i}e_{i}}_{\mathrm{(A)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{h_{k}}\lambda_{i_{j}+1}e_{2n+j}}_{\mathrm{(B)}};\ \ p_{h_{2n-k}}(u)=\underbrace{\sum_{i\in C_{h_{2n-k}}}\mu_{i}e_{i}}_{\mathrm{(C)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{h_{2n-k}}\mu_{i_{j}+1}e_{2n+j}}_{\mathrm{(D)}}.

With respect to
\omega_{W}
, (B) and (D) are orthogonal. Taking the symplectic form of (B) with (C) gives
\sum_{j=1}^{h_{k}}\lambda_{i_{j}+1}\mu_{2n-i_{j}}
. Indeed, it suffices to notice that the dual vector of
e_{2n+j}
is
e_{i_{2t+1-j}+1}
; if
1\leq j\leq h_{k}
,
e_{i_{2t+1-j}+1}\notin\{i_{1}+1,\ldots,i_{h_{2n-k}}+1\}
. Simiarly (A) with (D) gives
-\sum_{j=1}^{h_{2n-k}}\lambda_{2n-i_{j}}\mu_{i_{j}+1}
. The part (A) and (C) gives the remaining summands in (
4.2
) since for any
1\leq\ell\leq 2t
,
(i_{\ell}+1)+(i_{2t-\ell}+1)=2n+1
.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
is then complete.

4.3.
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)

Recall that
\mathbf{e}=(1,2,\ldots,2n-1)
. We will show that the following map

\pi:\mathrm{Lag}_{\mathbf{e}}(M^{\mathbf{i}}\oplus\nabla M^{\mathbf{i}})\to\mathrm{Sp}\mathcal{F}_{2n}^{\mathbf{i}},

(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n-1},V_{\omega},V_{(n-1)^{*}},\ldots,V_{1^{*}})\mapsto(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n-1},V_{\omega})

is an isomorphism.

Let
(V,\omega_{V})
be a symplectic vector space over
\mathbb{C}
of dimension
2n
and fix a basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{2n}\}
of
V
such that
\omega_{V}(v_{i},v_{2n+1-j})=\delta_{i,j}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Using the symplectic form we can naturally identify
V
and
V^{*}
where the dual vector of
v_{i}
is
v_{i}^{*}=v_{2n+1-i}
.

We define a
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
-representation
M=(M_{i},M_{\alpha})_{i\in Q_{0},\alpha\in Q_{1}}
as follows:

-

For
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
M_{i}=V
,
M_{i^{*}}=V^{*}
;
M_{\omega}=V
.

-

For
1\leq i\leq n-1
or
i=\omega
, fix the symplectic basis of
M_{i}
and denote them by
\{v_{1,i},\ldots,v_{2n,i}\}
; fix the dual basis
\{v_{1,i}^{*},\ldots,v_{2n,i}^{*}\}
of
M_{i^{*}}
.

-

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the map
M_{\alpha_{k}}:M_{k}\to M_{k+1}
where
M_{n}:=M_{\omega}
and
v_{i,n}:=v_{i,\omega}
is defined by:

M_{\alpha_{k}}(v_{i,k})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $k\in\mathbf{i}$, $i=k+1$;}\\
v_{i,k+1},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
M_{k}
and
M_{k+1}
are the same space,
v_{i,k}
and
v_{i,k+1}
are the same basis element. Then
M_{\alpha_{k}}
is the projection
\mathrm{pr}_{i_{s}+1}
if
k=i_{s}
, and it is the identity map otherwise. The map
M_{\alpha_{k}^{*}}:M_{(k+1)^{*}}\to M_{k^{*}}
where
M_{n^{*}}:=M_{\omega}
is just the dual map of
M_{\alpha_{k}}
with respect to the symplectic form. That is to say, if
k=i_{s}
then
M_{\alpha_{k}^{*}}
sends
v_{k+1,k}^{*}
to zero and other
v_{\ell,k+1}^{*}
for
\ell\neq k+1
to
v_{\ell,k}^{*}
. It follows from
v_{i}^{*}=v_{2n+1-i}
that
M_{\alpha_{k}^{*}}=\mathrm{pr}_{2n-i_{s}}
. If
k\notin\mathbf{i}
, then
M_{\alpha_{k}^{*}}
is the identity map.

From the construction it follows that
M
is isomorphic to
M^{\mathbf{i}}\oplus\nabla M^{\mathbf{i}}
as
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
-representations. Moreover, the linear maps
M_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,M_{\alpha_{n-1}}
are exactly
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1}
. Taking a Lagrangian subrepresentation
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n-1},V_{\omega},V_{(n-1)^{*}},\ldots,V_{1^{*}})
of dimension vector
\mathbf{e}
of
M
, it follows that for
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
\dim V_{i}=i
and
f_{i}(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i+1}
. Moreover
\dim V_{\omega}=n
, and
V_{\omega}
is Lagrangian in
V
. This shows that the map
\pi
is well-defined. Since the Lagrangian subrepresentation is uniquely determined by the part involving
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n-1},V_{\omega}
(because the remaining part consists of the quotient and the dual map), the map
\pi
is an isomorphism.

We illustrate the proof in the example
n=3
and
\mathbf{i}=\{1\}
. The projective
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\longrightarrow}}{{A_{2n-1}}}
-representation is

M^{\mathbf{i}}=P_{1}^{\oplus 2}\oplus P_{1^{*}}\oplus P_{2}

and

M^{\mathbf{i}}\oplus\nabla(M^{\mathbf{i}})=P_{1}^{\oplus 2}\oplus P_{1^{*}}\oplus P_{2}\oplus I_{2^{*}}\oplus I_{1}\oplus I_{1^{*}}^{\oplus 2}.

M

5.
An algorithm for
\varepsilon
-degenerations

We conclude the section by implementing the algorithm given in the proof of Lemma
2.15
in the case when
(Q,\sigma)
is equioriented of type either
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{odd}},-1)
or
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{A}}_{\textrm{even}},+1)
.

Let
M
and
N
be two
\varepsilon
-representations of the same dimension vector. Let
(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)
be the underlying quadratic space of both
M
and
N
. Assume that
r^{M}\geq r^{N}
. We want to give a sequence
M=Z(0),Z(1),\cdots,Z(k+1)=N
of
\varepsilon
-representations and a sequence
(\lambda_{i}(t))_{i=0,\cdots,k}
of one-parameter subgroups of the group of isometries of
(M^{0},\langle-,-\rangle)
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\lambda_{i}(t)\cdot Z(i)=Z(i+1)
(this sequence exists by Theorem
2.9
).
We follow the algorithm of the proof of Lemma
2.15
: At the
i
-th step, the algorithm has as input two
\varepsilon
-representations
M(i)
and
N(i)
of the same dimension vector such that
r^{M(i)}\geq r^{N(i)}
together with an indecomposable representation
L(i)
. They are constructed so that there exists a one-parameter subgroup
\mu_{i}(t)
which preserves the
\varepsilon
-form such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\mu_{i}(t)\cdot M(i)=M(i+1)\oplus L(i)\oplus\nabla L(i)
and
N(i)=N(i+1)\oplus L(i)\oplus\nabla L(i)
.
We then obtain the required sequence
\{Z_{i}\}
by putting
Z(0)=M
and
Z(i):=M(i)\oplus\bigoplus_{j<i}L(j)\oplus\nabla L(j)
for
i>1
.

The algorithm works as follows: if
r^{M}=r^{N}
then
M\simeq N
; otherwise if
M_{n}
is zero then both
M
and
N
are supported on a smaller quiver. We can hence assume that
M_{n}
is not zero. We choose the maximal index
i
such that
r_{i,n}^{N}>r_{i-1,n}^{N}
; then
N=L(1)\oplus\nabla L(1)\oplus N(1)
where
L(1)=U_{i,n}=P_{i}
. Let
\iota:L\rightarrow M
be a generic isotropic embedding of
L
into
M
and we define
M(1)=\iota(L)^{\perp}/\iota(L)
. We then repeat this construction to the pair
M(1)
and
N(1)
and continue this way.
The algorithm stops when
r^{M(i)}=r^{N(i)}
.
