Self-Adjointness of Toeplitz Operators on the Segal-Bargmann Space
Wolfram Bauer, Lauritz van Luijk, Alexander Stottmeister, Reinhard F. Werner

Abstract
. We prove a new criterion that guarantees self-adjointness of Toeplitz operators with unbounded operator-valued symbols. Our criterion applies, in particular, to symbols with Lipschitz continuous derivatives, which is the natural class of Hamiltonian functions for classical mechanics. For this we extend the Berger-Coburn estimate to the case of vector-valued Segal-Bargmann spaces. Finally, we apply our result to prove self-adjointness for a class of (operator-valued) quadratic forms on the space of Schwartz functions in the Schrödinger representation.



1.
Introduction

Proving the self-adjointness of unbounded Hilbert space operators is a key problem for many applications of functional analysis in quantum theory.
For von Neumann, who founded the theory
[
25
]
, the principal distinction of self-adjoint operators over merely hermitian operators was the existence of a spectral resolution.
This property was significant to him as the creator of the mathematical formalism of quantum theory, not least because a partition of unity into projections was needed for a quantum-probabilistic interpretation as a quantum observable.
Equally important in modern applications is the role of self-adjoint operators as generators of dynamical evolution. According to the rules of quantum mechanics, this must be given by a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group and, hence, be generated by a self-adjoint operator.
A hermitian operator built as a sum of terms motivated by a physical model may very well fail this crucial property.
This is then a sign that the problem has not been sufficiently well defined, and a self-adjoint extension must be singled out, typically by specifying suitable boundary conditions.
Once self-adjointness is achieved, and so the generator can be exponentiated, the group property extends this to all times.
The counterpart of the self-adjointness problem for classical dynamical systems is, therefore, the
global
existence of the flow (possibly only for almost all initial values, as the Coulomb potential
V(x)=\lvert x\rvert^{-2}
shows). This connection is not just superficial and can be studied case by case.
Indeed, a folk “theorem” states that a Schrödinger operator with given potential is self-adjoint on its natural domain precisely if the Hamiltonian flow for the corresponding problem of classical mechanics exists for all times.
There are well-known counterexamples to this statement in either direction
[
22
, Ch. X.1]
. Nevertheless, there is some truth in it, so the question may be which additional regularity assumptions make it true. This paper establishes a criterion for self-adjointness directly motivated by a classical counterpart.

The basic connection between the classical and the quantum world is provided by a quantization
f\mapsto T_{f}
relating classical phase space functions
f
to quantum operators
T_{f}
, e.g., the classical Hamilton function to the quantum Hamiltonian.
Even though the natural stage for quantum mechanical operators
T_{f}
of systems with
n
canonical degrees of freedom is without a doubt the Schrödinger representation, i.e., the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
together with the standard representation of the canonical commutation relations in terms of position and momentum operators. We will, however, work in the unitarily equivalent Segal-Bargmann representation.
The main reason for this is that we will use the coherent-state quantization which takes on a more natural form in this representation, known as the
Berezin-Toeplitz quantization
.
It depends on a quantization parameter
t>0
, which plays the role of Planck’s constant.
Smaller values of
t
correspond to larger scales of
action
and the limit
t\to 0
(formally) is the classical limit.
A natural criterion to ensure global existence of the solutions to Hamilton’s equations is provided by the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, which requires the derivatives of the classical Hamiltonian function
f
to be globally Lipschitz continuous.
We will show that precisely the same assumption guarantees self-adjointness of the Berezin-Toeplitz operator
T_{f}
.
As it turns out, we can even weaken the assumption of Lipschitz continuity in that we only need the derivatives of a heat transformed symbol to have bounded oscillation.
This comes in handy, as the heat transformed symbol is generally better behaved.

In the following we denote by
T_{f}
the Toeplitz operator with symbol
f
acting on the Segal-Bargmann space
\mathcal{H}
of Gaussian square integrable analytic functions (s.
Section

2
).
Applying the well-known reproducing kernel function of
\mathcal{H}
we express
T_{f}
in an integral form and consider it on its natural domain of functions for which this integral again defines an element in the Segal-Bargmann space (see (
12
) for details).

Theorem 1
.

Fix a quantization parameter
t>0
.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\!\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable polynomially bounded symbol such that there is a time
s\in[0,\frac{t}{2})
for which the first-order derivatives of the heat-transformed symbol
\widetilde{f}^{(s)}
at time
s
have bounded oscillation, i.e., satisfy

\lvert\partial_{j}\widetilde{f}^{(s)}(z)-\partial_{j}\widetilde{f}^{(s)}(w)\rvert\lesssim 1+\lvert z-w\rvert,\quad j=1,\ldots,2n,

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Then the Toeplitz operator
T_{f}
is self-adjoint on its natural domain.

The symbol “
\lesssim
” means that the inequality “
\leq
” holds provided that the right-hand side is multliplied by some constant
c>0
, and
\widetilde{f}^{(s)}
is the heat transform of
f
after time
s
.
We will extend this theorem to allow for operator-valued symbols and use it to prove a self-adjointness criterion for (operator-valued) continuous quadratic forms on the space of Schwartz functions in the Schrödinger representation (see Theorems
14
and
20
).
One ingredient of the proof are the Berger-Coburn estimates
[
6
,
7
]
which we derive in the case of operator-valued symbols in
Section

4
.

Previous work on the self-adjointness of unbounded Toeplitz operators was done by J. Janas in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
Apart from considering the scalar-valued case only (i.e., no operator-valued symbols), his work differs from ours in several aspects.
In particular, Janas investigates many aspects of unbounded operators, e.g., closedness or computation of the adjoint, not just self-adjointness.
While Janas’ work often considers families of symbols with specific properties such as (anti-)analyticity, we are interested in classes of symbols that also serve as natural Hamiltonian functions for a classical mechanical system.
In
[
15
, Thm. 3.3]
it is proved (for
t=\frac{1}{2}
) that the Toeplitz operators with measurable real-valued symbols
f
are self-adjoint if

\lvert f(z)-f(w)\rvert\lesssim e^{\frac{1}{8t}\lvert z-w\rvert^{2}}

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
By noting that this inequality still holds if the right-hand side is replaced by
1+\lvert z-w\rvert
, we will show that
1
is a generalization of this result (see
17
).

Finally, we collect some examples and applications in
Section

6
.
Among others, these include results on the self-adjointness of relativistic and non-relativistic Schrödinger operators with operator-valued potentials, which are new to the authors best knowledge.

Notation

We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the Segal-Bargmann space with quantization parameter
t>0
,
which is defined as the closed subspace consisting of entire functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu)
, where

\,\textup{d}\mu(z)=(2\pi t)^{-n}e^{-\frac{\lvert z\rvert^{2}}{2t}}\,\textup{d}z.

The norm and inner product on
\mathcal{H}
are induced by
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu)
and will be denoted by
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
and
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
, respectively.
We will use these symbols for the scalar products and norms of all Hilbert spaces, since it will be clear from the context to which space the vectors belong. Nevertheless, we will sometimes append the space as an index for emphasis when that seems helpful.

\mathcal{H}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
K(z,w)=e^{\frac{\overline{w}\cdot z}{2t}}
where
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e.,
f(z)=\langle f,K(\,\cdot\,,z)\rangle
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
.
The normalized kernels are denoted by
k_{w}=\lVert K(\,\cdot\,,w)\rVert^{-1}K(\,\cdot\,,w)
.
The standard orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
is given by the monomials

e_{\nu}(z)=\frac{z^{\nu}}{\sqrt{\nu!(2t)^{\lvert\nu\rvert}}},\quad\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},

where
z^{\nu}=(z_{1})^{\nu_{1}}\cdots(z_{n})^{\nu_{n}}
,
\nu!=\nu_{1}!\cdots\nu_{n}!
and
\lvert\nu\rvert=\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{n}
.
Denote by
P:L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu)\to\mathcal{H}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}
.
The projection
P
can be expressed in terms of the reproducing kernel by

Pf(z)=\int K(z,w)f(w)\,\textup{d}\mu(w).

For a measurable function
f:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
, the
Toeplitz operator
acts on a suitable domain in
\mathcal{H}
by

(1)

T_{f}g=P(f\cdot g).

Using the reproducing kernel this leads to

(2)

T_{f}g(z)=\int K(z,w)f(w)g(w)\,\textup{d}\mu(w).

When denoting Toeplitz operators we will use “
z
” as a dummy variable, i.e.,
T_{f(z)}=T_{f}
.

The unitary
Weyl-operators
act by

W_{w}f(z)=k_{w}(z)\,f(z-w),

where
w,z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The reproducing kernel is connected to the Weyl-operators through
k_{z}=W_{z}1
.
The Weyl-operators are generated by unbounded Toeplitz operators with linear symbols

(3)

W_{w}=e^{\frac{1}{2t}(T_{w\cdot\overline{z}}-T_{\overline{w}\cdot z})}=e^{\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}}

where
\omega(z,w)=\operatorname{Im}(\overline{z}\cdot w)
.
The mapping
z\mapsto W_{z}
is a strongly continuous, projective unitary representation of the group
(\mathbb{C}^{n},+)
of phase space translations.
They satisfy the so-called
Weyl-relations
,

W_{w}W_{z}=e^{\frac{i}{2t}\omega(z,w)}W_{z+w}

and, hence, define a proper unitary representation of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{n}
.
The Weyl-operators implement phase space translations on
\mathcal{H}

[
9
]
.
To see this, we define
\alpha_{z}(A)=W_{z}AW_{-z}
and
\alpha_{z}f(w)=f(w+z)
for operators
A
on
\mathcal{H}
and functions
f
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Then it follows that

\alpha_{z}T_{f}=T_{\alpha_{z}f}.

The
Berezin transform
of an operator
A:D(A)\to\mathcal{H}
is
\widetilde{A}(z)=\langle Ak_{z},k_{z}\rangle
, provided
k_{z}\in D(A)
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
While the Toeplitz operator is a form of covariant quantization, the Berezin transform provides a covariant dequantization, i.e.,
\alpha_{z}\widetilde{A}=(\alpha_{z}A)^{\sim}
.
The Berezin transform of a Toeplitz operator
T_{f}
is the heat transform of the symbol
f
after time
t

[
7
]
.

By the
Schrödinger representation
we mean the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}
via the
Bargmann isometry
(cf.
[
11
, Chap. 1.6]
)

B:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The Bargmann isometry maps the standard basis
\{e_{\nu}\}
to the basis of (scaled) Hermite functions:

Be_{\nu}(x)=t^{-n/4}\psi_{\nu}(x/\sqrt{t})
,
where
\psi_{\nu}
is the
\nu
-th Hermite function.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a separable complex Hilbert space.
The
vector-valued Segal-Bargmann space

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
is the closed subspace of analytic functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu;\mathcal{K})
, the space of square integrable
\mathcal{K}
-valued functions with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu;\mathcal{K})}=\int\langle f(z),g(z)\rangle\,\textup{d}\mu(z)

for
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu;\mathcal{K})
.
The norm and inner product on
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
are, as in the scalar-valued case, induced by
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu;\mathcal{K})
and are again denoted
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
and
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
, respectively.
The space
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
can also be viewed as the Hilbert space tensor product
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K}
by setting
(f\otimes x)(z)\coloneqq f(z)x
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{V})
the algebra of bounded operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
.
For a weakly measurable (see
5
) symbol
f:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
, the Toeplitz operator
T_{f}
is defined on a suitable domain as in the scalar-valued case:

T_{f}g\coloneqq P(f\cdot g)
,
g\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
, where
(f\cdot g)(z)=f(z)g(z)
and
P:L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mu;\mathcal{K})\to\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.
The integral in (
2
) exists as a Bochner-integral in
\mathcal{K}
.
In the case that
f=\lambda\otimes A
, i.e.,
f(z)x=\lambda(z)Ax
,
x\in\mathcal{K}
, with
\lambda\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
, one has
T_{f}=T_{\lambda}\otimes A
.

Schwartz Elements

In

The space of Schwartz elements in

Especially for the latter it is convenient (for our purposes) that

Let

Remark 5

As we assume that the Hilbert space

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\langle g(z),v_{i}\rangle\langle f(z)v_{i},x\rangle,

is measurable for any

and some (countable) basis

\displaystyle\leq(\pi t)^{n}\int\lVert f(z-w)\rVert\Big|\partial^{\alpha}_{w}\overline{\partial}^{\beta}_{w}e^{-\frac{\lvert w\rvert^{2}}{4t}}\Big|\,\textup{d}w

\displaystyle\lesssim m(z)^{N}\!\int m(w)^{N+\lvert\alpha\rvert+\lvert\beta\rvert}e^{-\frac{\lvert w\rvert^{2}}{4t}}\,\textup{d}w\lesssim m(z)^{N}.

Proposition A.4 in
[
23
]
finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 6

If one is interested in finding a dense subspace

It is clear that the Weyl-operators

In fact, we can show that the action of the Weyl-operators is strongly smooth on
\mathcal{S}

Lemma 7
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{S}
the map

is in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

The product formula for Toeplitz operators with polynomial symbols (cf.

) implies that the topology of
\mathcal{S}
is induced by the equivalent family of semi-norms
q_{p}(f)\coloneqq\lVert T_{p}f\rVert
where

is a polynomial in
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\overline{z}_{1},\dots,\overline{z}_{n}
.
Note that

W_{w}^{*}T_{p}W_{w}=T_{p(z+w)}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}\frac{1}{\alpha!\beta!}\,{w^{\alpha}\overline{w}^{\beta}}\,T_{\partial^{\alpha}\overline{\partial}^{\beta}p}.

Furthermore, we have that
[T_{\omega(w,\,\cdot\,)},T_{p}]=\frac{t}{2i}T_{\overline{w}\cdot\overline{\nabla}p-w\cdot\nabla p}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle T_{p}\Big(\frac{1}{s}(W_{sw}-\mathds{1})-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\Big(W_{sw}W_{sw}^{*}T_{p}W_{sw}-T_{p}\Big)-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}T_{p}+\frac{i}{t}[T_{\omega(w,z)},T_{p}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{s}(W_{w}-\mathds{1})-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}\Big)T_{p}+\text{terms with $s,s^{2},\cdots,s^{\text{deg}(p)-1}$}.

Applying this to an element

and using

q_{p}\Big(\frac{1}{s}(W_{sw}f-f)-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}f\Big)=\Big\|\frac{1}{s}(W_{w}g-g)-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}g\Big\|+\mathbf{o}(s)\overset{w\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0

As anounced earlier, we use
\mathcal{S}
as a domain for quadratic forms.
Let
E
be a topological vector space.
A continuous
E
-valued quadratic form on
\mathcal{S}
is a separately continuous and sesquilinear map
A:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to E
.
The principle of uniform boundedness
[
21
, Thm. V.7]
and the fact that
\mathcal{S}
is a nuclear Fréchet space imply that separate continuity is equivalent to joint continuity.
We will not always mention the space
E
and the domain
\mathcal{S}
and simply refer to these as “continuous forms”.

For a continuous form
A
and a continuous operator
B:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
the commutator
[A,B](f,g)\coloneqq A(Bf,g)-A(f,Bg)
is well-defined as a continuous form (vector-valued whenever
A
is).
We introduce phase space derivatives of continuous forms to be the forms
\partial_{j}A
and
\overline{\partial}_{j}A
with

(5)

\partial_{j}A\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2t}[A,T_{\overline{z}_{j}}]\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\partial}_{j}A\coloneqq\frac{1}{2t}[A,T_{z_{j}}].

These are again continuous forms because of
2
.
Higher-order derivatives are defined iteratively
\partial^{\beta}\overline{\partial}^{\gamma}A=(\partial_{1})^{\beta_{1}}\dots(\overline{\partial}_{n})^{\gamma_{n}}A
, where
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
.
In fact, these derivatives commute with each other because
T_{z_{j}}
and
T_{\overline{z}_{j}}
commute with their commutator.

For a continuous form on
\mathcal{S}
, we define the continuous form
\alpha_{z}A
by
\alpha_{z}A(f,g)\coloneqq A(W_{-z}f,W_{-z}g)
and the Berezin transform

by
\widetilde{A}(z)\coloneqq A(k_{z},k_{z})=\alpha_{-z}A(1,1)
.
The suggestive definition of phase space derivatives of continuous forms is justified by the next result and the fact that the derivatives of a sufficiently regular function

on phase space coincide with the derivatives of

at

.

Corollary 8
.

Let
E
be a Fréchet space and let
A
be an
E
-valued continuous quadratic form on
\mathcal{S}
.
Then
z\mapsto\alpha_{z}A(f,g)
is a smooth map
\mathbb{C}^{n}\to E
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{S}
.
The derivatives are given by

\frac{\partial^{\lvert\beta\rvert+\lvert\gamma\rvert}}{\partial z^{\beta}\partial\overline{z}^{\gamma}}\alpha_{z}A(f,g)\big|_{z=0}=\partial^{\beta}\overline{\partial}^{\gamma}A(f,g),\quad\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}.

In particular, the Berezin transform
\widetilde{A}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to E
is smooth.

Proof.

For any continuous semi-norm
q
on
E
, pick a continuous semi-norm
p
on
\mathcal{S}
such that
q(A(f,g))\leq p(f)p(g)
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{S}
.
By
2
the commutator
\frac{i}{t}[A,T_{\omega(w,z)}]
is again a continuous
E
-valued form on
\mathcal{S}
.
Differentiability of
\alpha_{z}A(f,g)
now follows from applying the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\ q\Big(\frac{1}{s}\big(A(W_{sw}f,W_{sw}g)-A(f,g)\big)-\frac{i}{t}[A,T_{\omega(w,z)}](f,g)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ q\Big(A\Big(\frac{1}{s}(W_{sw}f-f)-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}f,W_{sw}g\Big)\Big)+q\Big(A\big(f,\frac{1}{s}(W_{sw}g-g)-\frac{i}{t}T_{\omega(w,z)}g\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{t}q\Big(A(T_{\omega(w,z)}f,W_{sw}g-g)\Big).

The first term is bounded by
p\big[(W_{sw}f-f)/s-(i/t)T_{\omega(w,z)}f\big]p(W_{tw}g)
which tends to zero as
s\to 0
.
The second and third term behave similarly.
∎

If

Lemma 9
.

Let

It is sufficient to consider the case

\displaystyle\partial_{j}T_{f}(g,h)=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}\langle T_{f}W_{-z}g,W_{-z}h\rangle\big|_{z=0}

5.
Self-Adjointness

Recall that we denote the heat transform at time
s
of a polynomially bounded function
f
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by
\widetilde{f}^{(s)}
, i.e.,

(10)

\widetilde{f}^{(s)}(z)=(2\pi s)^{-n/2}\!\int e^{-\frac{\lvert w\rvert^{2}}{2s}}f(z+w)\,\textup{d}w.

This makes sense as a Bochner-integral if
f
is
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
-valued, and we set
\widetilde{f}^{(0)}=f
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})_{h}
the (real) Banach space of hermitian bounded operators.

Let
(E,\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
be some normed space. A function
f:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to E
has
bounded oscillation

[
5
]
, if it is continuous and satisfies

(11)

\lVert f(z)-f(w)\rVert\lesssim 1+\lvert z-w\rvert,\quad\text{for all $z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}$}.

This can be seen to be equivalent to
\sup\{\lVert f(z)-f(w)\rVert:z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\lvert w\rvert<1\}<\infty
(cf.
[
3
]
).

Theorem 14
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})_{h}
be a weakly measurable polynomially bounded symbol such that there is a time
s\in[0,\frac{t}{2})
for which the first-order derivatives of
\widetilde{f}^{(s)}
have bounded oscillation.
Then
T_{f}
is self-adjoint on its natural domain

(12)

D(T_{f})=\bigl\{g\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}):\text{the integral \eqref{eq:toeplitz_kernel} exists $\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}$ and $T_{f}g\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})$}\bigr\}.

The algebraic tensor products
\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{K}
, and thus
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})
, are cores for
T_{f}
.

If
s=0
then the symbol
f
has to be continuously differentiable w.r.t. the weak operator topology on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
.
The spaces
\mathcal{P}
, and
\mathcal{E}
are defined in (
4
).
We divide the proof in two steps:
In the first step we treat the case
s=0
by use of the commutator theorem, and in the second step we use the operator-valued Berger-Coburn estimate in
10
to reduce the statement to the case
s=0
.

The main tool in the proof of
14
is the
commutator theorem
(cf.
[
22
, Thm. X.37]
).

Lemma 15

(Commutator Theorem)
.

Let
N\geq c\,\mathds{1}
,
c>0
, be a self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
with core
\mathcal{D}
.
Let
A:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{V}
be a symmetric operator, such that

(1)

\lVert Ax\rVert\lesssim\lVert Nx\rVert
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.

(2)

\lvert\langle Nx,Ax\rangle-\langle Ax,Nx\rangle\rvert\lesssim\langle Nx,x\rangle
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}
.

Then
A
is essentially self-adjoint,
D(N)\subset D(\overline{A})
and any core for
N
is also a core for the self-adjoint extension
\overline{A}
.

Proof of
14
. Step 1.

We prove the case
s=0
under the assumption that
f
is differentiable w.r.t. the weak operator topology on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
.

We will apply
15
with
N=N_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
where

7.
Outlook

We want to discuss the
problem of dynamical completeness
for the Toeplitz quantization.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable on phase space. Then
f
is said to be
classically complete
(up to a null-set of initial values) if the Hamiltonian dynamics generated by
f
, i.e., the flow on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
generated by the ODE

(14)

(\dot{x},\dot{\xi})=-J\nabla f(x,\xi),

where
J(x,\xi)=(\xi,-x)
, exists for all times and (almost) all initial values.
Existence of the flow for almost all initial values and all times is sufficient to define an isometric one-parameter group on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
where isometricity follows from Liouvilles theorem in symplectic geometry
[
1
, Prop. 3.3.4]
.
A symmetric operator
H
on a Hilbert space is said to be
quantum complete
if it is essentially self-adjoint.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be some quantization scheme which maps a classical Hamiltonian function
f
on phase space to a symmetric operator
\mathcal{Q}(f)
on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Examples are the Berezin-Toeplitz quantization
f\mapsto T_{f}
and the Weyl-quantization which sends a symbol
f
to its Weyl-pseudodifferential operator
op^{w}(f)
.
The problem of dynamical completeness asks whether quantum completeness of
\mathcal{Q}(f)
is equivalent to classical completeness of
f
.

For the Weyl-quantization well-known counterexamples in both directions exist already on the level of Schrödinger operators.
See, for instance, the examples on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
in
[
22
, Chap. X.1]
which can be easily extended to the whole real line by considering the potentials
V(\lvert x\rvert)
.

What makes these counterexamples work is the fact that the chosen potentials
V(x)
become “more singular” as
x\to\infty
but it is straightforward to see that the counterexamples fail if one considers heat-transformed potentials
\widetilde{V}^{(t)}(x)
instead, even for arbitrarily short times
t>0
.
This, however, is precisely what happens if one uses the Toeplitz quantization
T_{f}
with
f(x,\xi)=\xi^{2}+V(x)
instead of the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta+V(x)
because
T_{f}\equiv-\Delta+\widetilde{V}^{(t/2)}(x)+\frac{t}{2}\mathds{1}
for measurable and polynomially bounded
V
.
This leads us to the following conjecture:

Conjecture
.

For a reasonably regular Hamiltonian function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following are equivalent

(1)

The Hamiltonian function
f
is classically complete up to a null-set of inital values.

(2)

The Berezin-Toeplitz quantization
T_{f}
is quantum complete for all sufficiently small quantization parameters
t>0
.

14
implies that this is true for the class of differentiable symbols with Lipschitz continuous derivatives but, of course, this class consists only of classically complete functions.

It is not hard to generate counterexamples if one would ask for classical completeness to hold everywhere: For
n>1
one can pick
f\in L^{1}
such that classical completeness fails on a null-set. Still the corresponding quantization
T_{f}
is a hermitian trace-class operator and thus self-adjoint.
