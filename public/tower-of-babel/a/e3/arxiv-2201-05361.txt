Abstract
Finite-dimensional Hopf algebras admit a correspondence between so-called pairs in involution, one-dimensional anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules and algebra isomorphisms between the Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double. We extend it to general rigid monoidal categories and provide a monadic interpretation under the assumption that certain coends exist. Hereto we construct and study the anti-Drinfeld double of a Hopf monad. As an application the connection with the pivotality of Drinfeld centres and their underlying categories is discussed.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study the relationship between the Drinfeld centre of a monoidal category and a ‘twisted’ version of it, which arises in the study of Hopf cyclic cohomology.
Our approach splits into two parts.
First, we deploy general categorical tools in order to identify equivalences of the aforementioned categories with ‘invertible’ objects in a twisted centre.
Second, we take the monadic point of view and explain which of these equivalences translate into isomorphisms of monads generalising the Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double.
As a byproduct we show that a rigid category which admits these monads is pivotal if and only if the generalised double and anti-double are isomorphic.

The Hopf algebraic case

Our goal is best explained by first recalling the interactions between the various objects and categories in the setting of finite-dimensional Hopf algebras.
This is covered in greater detail in
[
28
]
.

A peculiarity of the Hopf cyclic cohomology, as defined by Connes and Moscovici
[
15
]
, is the lack of ‘canonical’ coefficients.
Originally, see
[
16
]
, modular pairs in involution were considered.
These consist of a group-like and a character implementing the square of the antipode by their respective adjoint actions.
Later, Hajac et.al. obtained a quite general source for coefficients in what they called the category of anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules,
[
30
]
.
Their name is due to the similarity with Yetter–Drinfeld modules:
Like their well-known ‘cousins’, they are simultaneously modules and comodules satisfying a compatibility condition between the action and coaction.
In general, they do not form a monoidal category but a module category over the Yetter–Drinfeld modules.
This is reflected by the fact that they can be identified with the modules over the anti-Drinfeld double, a comodule algebra over the Drinfeld double.
The special role of pairs in involution is captured by the following theorem due to Hajac and Sommerhäuser:

Theorem 1

(
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

For any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The Hopf algebra
H
admits a pair in involution.

(ii)

There exists a one-dimensional anti-Yetter–Drinfeld module over
H
.

(iii)

The Drinfeld double and anti-Drinfeld double of
H
are isomorphic algebras.

Furthermore, these pairs are of categorical interest as they give rise to pivotal structures on the Yetter–Drinfeld modules.
That is, they provide a natural monoidal isomorphism between each object and its double dual.

Twisted centres and pivotality

We want to reformulate this theorem in a categorical framework with an emphasis on pivotal structures.

First, let us discuss appropriate replacements for the concepts described above.
The role of the Hopf algebra is taken by a rigid monoidal category
\mathcal{C}
.
Roughly speaking, that means a category with a suitably associative and unital product in which every object has a left and right dual.
Due to the monoid-like nature of
\mathcal{C}
, we can study its bimodule categories.
Of special interest is the regular bimodule, whose actions are given by respectively ‘multiplying’ from the left or right.
Its centre
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
, called the Drinfeld centre of
\mathcal{C}
, provides us with an analogue of the category of Yetter–Drinfeld modules, see
[
38
, Chapter XIII]
.
Anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules were generalised in

[
31
]
to what one might call the anti-Drinfeld centre

\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
of
\mathcal{C}
.
In the context of topological quantum field theories this type of
category was studied for example in
[
25
]
and
[
19
]
.
As in the Hopf algebraic case, it is a module category over
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
.
An adaptation of pairs in involution are, what we will call, quasi-pivotal structures, studied for example in
[
57
]
.
They consist of an invertible object, which replaces the character, and, instead of a group-like element, a certain natural monoidal isomorphism.

The main observation needed to generalise Theorem
1
is that the anti-Drinfeld centre admits a ‘dual’.
In Proposition
39
this allows us to identify equivalences of
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
modules between
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
and
\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
with so-called
\mathcal{C}
-invertible objects in
\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
.
These are objects in
\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
whose image under the forgetful functor to
\mathcal{C}
is invertible.
Subsequently, we prove that these objects correspond to quasi-pivotal structures on
\mathcal{C}
and obtain the categorical version of Theorem
1
as Theorem
47
, which can be summarised as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a rigid monoidal category. The following are equivalent:

(i)

The category
\mathcal{C}
is quasi-pivotal.

(ii)

There exists a
\mathcal{C}
-invertible object in
\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
.

(iii)

The Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld centre of
\mathcal{C}
are equivalent module categories.

The pivotal structures of the Drinfeld centre
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
of a finite tensor category
\mathcal{C}
were studied by Shimizu in
[
57
]
.
We contribute to these results with the following observations:
the set
\Pic\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
of isomorphism classes of
\mathcal{C}
-invertible objects in
\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
forms a heap, see Lemma
41
.
That is, it behaves like a group but without a fixed neutral element.
Note that this provides a parallel with the aforementioned fact that Hopf cyclic cohomology has no canonical coefficients.
Equipping the set of pivotal structures
\Piv\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
of
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
with the same algebraic structure, we construct a heap morphism

\kappa\colon\Pic\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})\longrightarrow\Piv\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
.
In general, we cannot expect
\kappa
to be injective.
Instead, we show that invertible objects in the centre
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
which admit a ‘trivial’ braiding act nicely on
\Pic\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})
and the induced pivotal structures only depend on the orbit of this action.
The orbits themselves form a quotient heap and the induced morphism

\iota\colon\Pic\mathsf{A}({\mathcal{C}})/_{\sim}\longrightarrow\Piv\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}}),

is injective, see Theorem
56
.
In many cases, such as
\mathcal{C}
being a finite tensor category, it is moreover surjective.
However, by constructing a counterexample, we show in Theorem
71
that this is not true in general.

Reconstruction: Comodule monads

To reconcile our results with the initial Hopf algebraic formulation, we provide a monadic interpretation under the assumption that certain coends exist.

The starting point for our considerations is a Hopf monad
H\colon\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}
on a rigid, possibly pivotal, category
\mathcal{V}
which we think of as a replacement of finite-dimensional vector spaces.
Its modules form a rigid monoidal category
\mathcal{V}^{H}
.
Utilising the centralisers of Day and Street,
[
18
]
, Bruguières and Virelizier described in
[
11
]
the Drinfeld double
D(H)
of
H
.
It is obtained through a two-step process.
First, the central Hopf monad on
\mathcal{V}^{H}
is defined.
Then, the double
D(H)\colon\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}
arises by applying a variant of Beck’s theorem of distributive laws to it.
As in the classical setting, the modules of
D(H)
are isomorphic as a braided rigid monoidal category to the Drinfeld centre
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{V}^{H}})
.
By adapting the procedure outlined above for our purposes, we construct the anti-central monad and derive the anti-Drinfeld double
Q(H)\colon\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}
of
H
from it.
It is a comodule monad over
D(H)
in the sense of
[
2
]
which implements the ‘dual’ of the anti-centre
\mathsf{Q}({\mathcal{\mathcal{V}^{H}}})
as a module category.
Having all ingredients assembled, we show in Proposition
114
that certain module equivalences between
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{V}^{H}})
and
\mathsf{Q}({\mathcal{\mathcal{V}^{H}}})
materialise as isomorphisms between their associated monads.
Applying our general categorical results to
\mathcal{V}^{H}
and combining it with a monadic version of pairs in involution, we obtain in Theorem
115
an almost verbatim translation of Theorem
1
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
H
be a Hopf monad on a pivotal category
\mathcal{V}
that admits a Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double. The following are equivalent:

(i)

The Hopf monad
H
admits a pair in involution.

(ii)

There exists a module over
Q(H)
whose underlying object is
1\in\mathcal{V}
.

(iii)

The Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double of
H
are isomorphic as monads.

An immediate consequence of the above result is the observation that pivotal structures on
\mathcal{V}^{H}
equate to isomorphisms between the central and anti-central monads, see Corollary
116
.

2.1.1
Monoidal categories, functors, and natural transformations

Definition 4
.

A

a category
\mathcal{C}

({-}\otimes{-})\otimes{-}={-}\otimes({-}\otimes{-})\qquad\text{and}\qquad 1\otimes{-}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}={-}\otimes 1.

(2.1)

Many natural examples of monoidal categories, such as the category of vector spaces, are not strict.
That is, the associativity and unitality of the tensor product only hold up to (suitably coherent) natural isomorphisms.
However, we can compensate this by
Mac Lane’s strictification theorem
.
It states that any monoidal category is, in a ‘structure preserving manner’, equivalent to a strict one.
A proof is given for example in
[
20
, Theorem 2.8.5]
.
Thus, we shall only consider strict monoidal categories from now on, and will omit the prefix ‘strict’.

The next definition slightly extends the scope of

20

but is standard in the literature, see for example
[
3
, Chapter 3]

Definition 5
.

An
oplax monoidal functor
between monoidal categories
(\mathcal{C},\otimes,1)
and
(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\otimes^{\prime},1^{\prime})
is a functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

\Delta_{X,Y}\colon F(X\otimes Y)\longrightarrow F(X)\otimes^{\prime}F(Y),\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }X,Y\in\mathcal{C},

and a morphism
\varepsilon\colon F(1)\rightarrow 1^{\prime}

and
counitality

If the

(strong) monoidal
or

We think of an oplax monoidal functor

as a generalisation of a coalgebra.
To emphasise this point of view, we refer to
\Delta
and
\varepsilon

and

of

Assume

to be strong monoidal and an equivalence of categories.
Its quasi-inverse
G\colon\mathcal{D}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
can be turned into a monoidal functor such that the natural isomorphisms

This justifies calling

Definition 6

An

(2.2)

If

In case we want to emphasise that the underlying functors of an oplax monoidal natural transformation

For our purposes, we can restrict ourselves to the following situation.

An efficient means for computations in strict monoidal categories are

They consist of strings labelled with objects and vertices between the strings labelled with morphisms.
If two string diagrams can be transformed into each other, the morphisms that they represent are equal.
A more detailed description is given in

3
Heaps

Heaps can be thought of as groups without a fixed neutral element. Prüfer studied their abelian version under the name
Schar
in
[
52
]
.
Since then, the notion has been adapted to the non-abelian case, see
[
32
]
. Recently, their homological properties were studied in
[
23
]
; a generalisation towards a ‘quantum version’ of heaps is hinted at in
[
60
]
. We follow Section 2 of
[
8
]
for our exposition.

Definition 27
.

A
heap
is a set
G
together with a ternary operation

\langle{-},{-},{-}\rangle\colon G\times G\times G\longrightarrow G,

which we call the
heap operation
,
satisfying a generalised associativity axiom and the
Mal’cev identities
, of which we think as unitality axioms:

\displaystyle\langle g,h,\langle i,j,k\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle g,h,i\rangle,j,k\rangle,\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }g,h,i,j,k\in G,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\langle g,g,h\rangle=h=\langle h,g,g\rangle,\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }g,h\in G.

(3.2)

There are two peculiarities we want to point out.
First, our definition does, intentionally, not exclude the empty set from being a heap.
Second, due to a slightly different setup, an additional ‘middle’ associativity axiom is required in
[
32
]
.
However, as noted in
[
8
, Lemma 2.3]
, it is implied by the ‘outer’ associativity and the Mal’cev identities.

Definition 28
.

A map
f\colon G\longrightarrow H
between heaps is a
morphism of heaps
if

f\left(\langle g,h,i\rangle\right)=\langle f(g),f(h),f(i)\rangle,\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }g,h,i\in G.

(3.3)

The next lemma can be shown by mimicking the proof of its group theoretical version.

Lemma 29
.

A morphism of heaps
f\colon G\longrightarrow H
is an isomorphism if and only if it is bijective.

By forgetting its unit, any group defines a heap.
Conversely, any non-empty heap can be turned into a group by choosing a fixed element to act as unit, see
[
14
]
.

Lemma 30
.

Every group
(G,\cdot,e)
is a heap via

\langle{-},{-},{-}\rangle\colon G\times G\times G\longrightarrow G,\qquad\qquad\langle g,h,i\rangle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=g\cdot h^{-1}\cdot i.

A morphism of groups becomes a morphism of the induced heaps.

Lemma 31
.

A non-empty heap
H
with a fixed element
e\in H
can be considered as a group with unit
e
via the multiplication

{-}\cdot_{e}{-}\colon H\times H\longrightarrow H,\qquad g\cdot_{e}h\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\langle g,e,h\rangle.

The inverse of an element
g\in H
with respect to
\cdot_{e}
is given by
g^{-1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\langle e,g,e\rangle
.
A morphism of heaps is a morphism of the induced groups, provided it maps the fixed element of its source to the fixed element of its target.

More generally, if
\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid and
x,y\in\mathcal{G}
,
then
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
becomes a heap; the heap operation is given by

\langle f,g,h\rangle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=fg^{-1}h
.
The following example is a special case of this construction,
which will play a prominent role in our investigation.

Example 32
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
be two oplax monoidal endofunctors. The set

\Isospace_{\otimes}(F,G)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{\text{oplax monoidal natural isomorphisms from $F$ to $G$}\right\}

bears a heap structure, the heap operation being

\langle{-},{-},{-}\rangle\colon\Isospace_{\otimes}(F,G)^{3}\longrightarrow\Isospace_{\otimes}(F,G),\qquad\qquad\langle\phi,\psi,\xi\rangle=\phi\psi^{-1}\xi.

(3.4)

4
Pivotal structures and twisted centres

In this section, we study the relations between pairs in involution, anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules and isomorphisms between the Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double from a categorical point of view.
Our approach is representation theoretic in nature.
We consider variants of the regular bimodule of a rigid category
\mathcal{C}
with either the left or right action twisted by a strict monoidal endofunctor.
Their centres are canonically modules over the Drinfeld centre.
These
twisted centres
inherit a notion of duality which follows in close parallel to that of
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
.
Module functors between the Drinfeld and a twisted centre are determined by their value on the unit object.
A consequence of the above sketched duality is that module equivalences correspond to objects in the twisted centre, which behave as if they were invertible.
We gather these objects into the Picard heap of the twisted centre.
If we twist with the left double dual functor, we obtain a generalised version of the anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules, see
[
31
]
.
Its Picard heap has an alternative interpretation as quasi-pivotal structures; appropriate analogues of pairs in involution.
This observation leads us to the desired relations in categorical terms, given in Theorem
47
.

In
[
57
]
, Shimizu observed that quasi-pivotality of
\mathcal{C}
induces pivotality of
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
.
We recall his proof from the perspective of twisted centres and investigate how this construction is related to the so-called symmetric centre of
\mathcal{C}
.
This leads to an injective heap morphism from a quotient of the Picard heap of the generalised anti-Yetter–Drinfeld modules to the heap of pivotal structures of
\mathsf{Z}({\mathcal{C}})
.
We end the section, by constructing a category such that this morphism is not surjective.

In the following,
\mathcal{C}
denotes a strict rigid category.

Theorem 115
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a rigid monoidal category,
and suppose that
H\colon\mathcal{V}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}
is a Hopf monad
that admits a double
D(H)
and anti-double

.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The monoidal unit
1\in\mathcal{V}
lifts to a module over
Q(H)
.

(ii)

The Drinfeld double and anti-Drinfeld double of
H
are isomorphic as comodule monads.

(iii)

There is an isomorphism of monads
g\colon Q(H)\longrightarrow D(H)
.

Additionally, if
\mathcal{V}
is pivotal with pivotal structure
\phi
,
any of the above statements hold if and only if
H
and
\phi
admit a pair in involution.

Proof.

(i)\!\!\implies\!\!(ii)
: suppose

with
U^{Q(H)}(\omega)=1
.
As shown in Equation (
4.6
), it induces a functor of module categories

\omega\otimes{-}\colon\mathcal{V}^{D(H)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{Q(H)}.

Since
U^{Q(H)}(\omega)=1\in\mathcal{V}
, we can apply Proposition
114
and obtain that
Q(H)
and
D(H)
are isomorphic as comodule monads.

It immediately follows that
(ii)
implies
(iii)
; we proceed with
(iii)\!\!\implies\!\!(i)
: consider an isomorphism of monads
g\colon Q(H)\longrightarrow D(H)
.
It gives rise to a functor
G\colon\mathcal{V}^{D(H)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{Q(H)}
that, on objects, is defined by

G(M,\vartheta_{M})=(M,\vartheta_{M}g_{M}),\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }(M,\vartheta_{M})\in\mathcal{V}^{D(H)}.

We compose
G
with the inverse of the comparison functor
E^{(Q(H))}\colon\mathcal{V}^{Q(H)}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Q}({\mathcal{V}^{H}})
, defined in Equation (
6.13
), and see that there exists an object

1^{(Q)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=E^{(Q(H))}G(1)\in\mathsf{Q}({\mathcal{V}^{H}})

whose underlying object is the unit of
\mathcal{V}
.

Now let
(\mathcal{V},\phi)
be pivotal.
By Lemma
45
, lifts of
1\in\mathcal{V}
to the dual of the anti-center
\mathsf{Q}({\mathcal{V}^{H}})
are in correspondence with quasi-pivotal structures

(\beta,\rho_{\beta})
, where
\beta\in\Char(H)
.
By Proposition
113
such a quasi-pivotal structure exists if and only if
there exists a pair in involution for
H
and
\phi
.
∎

As a corollary, we can determine whether a category is pivotal in terms of monad isomorphisms between the central and anti-central monad.
For a category
\mathcal{C}

recall Definition
95
of its central Hopf monad,
and Definition

Corollary 116
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a rigid monoidal category.
If
\mathcal{C}
admits a central Hopf monad
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{C})

and an anti-central comodule monad
\mathfrak{Q}(\mathcal{C})

then it is pivotal if and only if
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{C})\cong\mathfrak{Q}(\mathcal{C})
as monads.

Proof.

We consider the identity
\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
as a Hopf monad.
Its Drinfeld and anti-Drinfeld double are
D(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{C})\rtimes\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}

From here it follows that

The proof is concluded by Theorem
