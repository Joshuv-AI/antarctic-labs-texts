Weight diagrams of finite dimensional highest weight 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( m | n ) \mathfrak{gl}(m|n) -modules
Matan Pinkas

Abstract
. Many properties of simple finite dimensional 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( m | n ) \mathfrak{gl}(m|n) -modules may be better understood by assigning weight diagrams to the highest weights with respect to a given base of simple roots. In this paper we consider bases that are compatible with the standard Borel subalgebra in 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( m | n ) 0 ¯ = 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( m ) × 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\bar{0}}=\mathfrak{gl}(m)\times\mathfrak{gl}(n) ; namely, the bases that differ from the distinguished base Σ dist \Sigma^{\text{dist}} of simple roots by a sequence of odd reflections. We examine the weight diagrams that arise from the highest weights of a simple module L ⁡ ( λ ) L(\lambda) with respect to such bases. Further, we provide combinatorial tools to describe all the weight diagrams of highest weights of L ⁡ ( λ ) L(\lambda) provided only with the the weight diagram of L ⁡ ( λ ) L(\lambda) with respect to distinguished highest weight λ \lambda . Finally, we study the maximal cardinality of incomparable sets of positive odd roots with respect to Σ dist \Sigma^{\text{dist}} which are orthogonal to some highest weight of L ⁡ ( λ ) L(\lambda) with respect to a base as above. We provide explicit formulas for this value, connecting it to the combinatorics of the weight diagrams. Based on this study, we respond to the work of M. Gorelik and Th. Heidersdorf by providing a counterexample to the Tail Conjecture appearing in [ GH23 ] .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The study of superalgebras and Lie superalgebras is the study of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded objects, with sign rules governing the commutation relations.

Such objects occur naturally when studying subjects such as exterior algebras of vector spaces, cohomology and tensor categories.

A systematic study of the structure and the representation theory of Lie superalgebras was initiated in the famous papers
[
K77
,
K78
]
by V. Kac.
Since then, the theory of Lie superalgebras has become an active field of study, see for example
[
S17
,
B14
,
BS12
,
M12
,
CW12
]

and the references therein.

As with classical Lie algebras, to study Lie superalgebras one needs a combinatorial framework including the notions of root systems, bases of simple roots and more. The goal of this paper is to contribute to this framework for the general linear Lie superalgebra.

1.2.
The general linear Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)

Lie superalgebras are a natural generalization of the notion of Lie algebras.

A vector space
V
with a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading
V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
is called a vector superspace. We denote the parity of a homogeneous vector
a\in V_{\bar{i}}
by
p(a):=i
.

We define the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
to be the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space
End(V)
together with the super-commutator

[a,b]=ab-(-1)^{p(a)p(b)}ba.

Taking the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded space
V=\mathbb{C}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we denote
\mathfrak{gl}(V)=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.

Much like the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
,
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
has a root system. One can consider different simple bases for this root system and study the highest weights of simple finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-modules with respect to these bases.

Since the ”super” setting adds parity into the picture, some differences arise. The root system of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
has even roots (corresponding to eigenvectors of parity
\overline{0}
) and odd root (corresponding to eigenvectors of parity
\overline{1}
).

Similarly to the theory of semisimple Lie algebras, we may consider the Weyl group corresponding to the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. This group is isomorphic to
S_{m}\times S_{n}
. It is generated by reflections with respect to the even roots of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, thus encoding the information about the even part of the Lie superalgebra.

However, not all the bases of the root system for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
are conjugate to one another via the action of the Weyl group. To remedy that, V. Serganova defined in
[
LSS86
, Appendix]
the notion of an odd reflection: that is, a transformation of the set of all roots which behaves as a reflection with respect to an odd root should (See Definition
2.2.5
). more details on odd reflections, see
[
CW12
, Sections 1.3, 2.4]
and
[
M12
, Section 3.5]
. We denote by
r_{\alpha}
the odd reflection with respect to a root
\alpha\in\Delta_{\bar{1}}
.

Consider the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. This Lie sub-superalgebra is purely even, and is just the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\bar{0}}=\mathfrak{gl}(m)\times\mathfrak{gl}(n)
. We write the natural basis of the dual space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
(the space of weights) as
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}
. Then the set of even roots of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is

\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}\,|\,1\leq i\neq j\leq m\}\cup\{\delta_{i}-\delta_{j}\,|\,1\leq i\neq j\leq n\}

and the set of odd roots is
\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j})\,|\,1\leq i\leq m,\,1\leq j\leq n\}
.

The distinguished base for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is defined to be

\Sigma^{\text{dist}}:=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1},\,\varepsilon_{m}-\delta_{1},\,\delta_{j}-\delta_{j+1}\,|\,1\leq i\leq m-1,\,1\leq j\leq n-1\}

The positive odd roots with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
are of the form
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
for some
i,j
, and we denote
\mathcal{R}:=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\,|\,1\leq i\leq m,\,1\leq j\leq n\}
. These will be called
right odd roots
.

Any base of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
can be obtained from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
by a sequence of even and odd reflections, but the set of all the bases which can be obtained from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
using
only odd reflections by right odd roots
is denoted by
\mathbb{S}
. For any base
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, the positive even roots with respect to the base
\Sigma
are exactly the positive even roots for the base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

1.3.
Definition of the weight diagram

As it turns out, a lot of information regarding a simple module over
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
may be encoded into what is known as a weight diagram of that module. Let us first explain how to construct this diagram.

Let
{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
denote the set of all integral dominant weights with respect to the distinguished base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
. This set is in bijection with the set of isomorphism classes of (finite-dimensional) integrable simple modules over
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, with the simple module corresponding to
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
denoted by
L(\lambda)
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and let
\Sigma
be any base of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. We consider the positive and the negative roots corresponding to
\Sigma
and denote by
\rho_{\Sigma}
half the difference between the sums of the even positive roots and the sums of the odd positive roots. We then denote by
\lambda_{\Sigma}
the highest weight of
L(\lambda)
with respect to the base
\Sigma
, and
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}:=\lambda_{\Sigma}+\rho_{\Sigma}
, with
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}
denoted by
\overline{\lambda}
for short.

This shifted highest weight
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
is then encoded by a very useful combinatorial tool, due to Brundan and Stroppel
[
BS12
]
as well as Musson and Serganova
[
MS11
]
.

The weight diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
of
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
is defined as a function on the integers, whose value in
p\in\mathbb{Z}
is a finite collection of symbols
\times,>,<,\circ
, subject to the following rules:

•

Let
k:=\sharp\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}|(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})_{i}=p\}
,
s:=\sharp\{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}|(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})_{-j}=-p\}
. Then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly
\min(k,s)
symbols
\times
.

•

If
k>s
then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly
k-s
symbols
>
.

•

If
k<s
then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly
s-k
symbols
<
.

•

D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains a (unique) symbol
\circ
iff it does not contain any other symbol (in such a case, we day that
p
is an empty position).

From the definition of
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, it is easy to see that
|s-k|\leq 1
, so the collection
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
may contain at most one symbol
>
or
<
. Furthermore, for
\Sigma=\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
, each
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
exactly one symbol (perhaps
\circ
).

This diagram can by interpreted visually by drawing the symbols corresponding to a
p\in\mathbb{Z}
at the position
p
on the real line.

For example, if we consider
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}_{3|3}
such that
\overline{\lambda}=(4\,2\,0\,|0\,-3\,-5)
. Then the diagram
D^{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}_{\lambda}
is given by

-1
0
1
2

A natural question that arises from the definition of weight diagrams is: given a integrable simple finite dimensional
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-module
L
, what are all possible weight diagrams of highest weights of
L
with respect to the different bases; that is, given
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
, can we describe all the possible diagrams
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}
?

This is a complex combinatorial question, but in this paper we attempt to give a satisfactory answer to this question when
\Sigma
is any base in
\mathbb{S}
.

1.4.
Main results

The partial order on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
given by
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
induces a partial order on
\mathcal{R}
:
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}-\delta_{j^{\prime}}
if
i\geq i^{\prime}
and
j\leq j^{\prime}
. A subset of
\mathcal{R}
is called
incomparable
if no two elements of this subset can be compared in the partial order.

Our first result describes the bases in
\mathbb{S}
via the right odd roots lying in each base.

Theorem 1

(See Lemma
3.2.4
, Theorem
3.2.9
)
.

(1)

There is a bijection between
\mathbb{S}
and the collection of incomparable subsets of
\mathcal{R}
. This bijection is given by sending
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
to
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
.

(2)

Let
B_{\Sigma}:=\mathcal{R}\setminus\{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}|\;\exists\beta\in\Sigma\text{ so that }\alpha\geq\beta\}
be the collection of all right odd roots which are not greater than some element of
\Sigma
.

Then
\Sigma
can be produced from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
by performing odd reflections with respect to every odd root in
B_{\Sigma}
, in some valid order.

To give a visualization of this statement, we interpret the partially ordered set

as a lattice of size

Understanding the result of performing odd reflections by right odd roots is the central question required to understand which weight diagrams correspond to a simple integrable finite-dimensional module
L
. If we denote by
\lambda
the highest weight of
L
with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
, we are interested in understanding the set of diagrams
\{D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}\}
.

This is done using a new combinatorial tool which we introduce in this paper: the
arrow diagram
of
\lambda
.

Given
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
, we define the arrow diagram of
\lambda
by drawing
m
arrows on the diagram
D^{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}_{\lambda}
according to the following rules:

•

The arrows start at positions
\overline{\lambda}_{m}<\overline{\lambda}_{m-1}<\ldots<\overline{\lambda}_{1}
.

•

The order in which we draw the arrows is that of the starting points (from left to right).

•

Each arrow ends at the first position
p
to the right of the starting point that has
D^{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}_{\lambda}(p)\in\{\circ,>\}
and such that
p
is not the endpoint of any previously drawn arrow.

We denote by
k_{i}
the position of the endpoint of the arrow starting at position
\overline{\lambda}_{i}
and by
M_{i}
the total number of
<
and
\times
symbols in
D^{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}_{\lambda}
to the left of position
k_{i}
.

Theorem 2

(See Proposition

.

Let

(1)

Let

That is, given

(2)

•

•

The indices

M_{i}-(k_{i}-\overline{\lambda}_{i}-1)\leq j\leq M_{i}

1.5.
The longtail of an integral dominant weight

Consider a symmetric bilinear form
(-|-)
on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
defined by

(\varepsilon_{i}|\varepsilon_{j}):=\delta_{i,j},\;(\varepsilon_{i}|\delta_{j}):=(\delta_{j}|\varepsilon_{i}):=0,\;(\delta_{i}|\delta_{j}):=-\delta_{i,j}.

Let
\nu\in span_{\mathbb{Z}}\{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m},\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
be an integral weight for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. We define
s(\nu)
to be the cardinality of the largest set of odd roots
S
such that

\forall\alpha,\beta\in S,\;\;(\alpha|\beta)=0,\,(\alpha|\nu)=0.

For
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
we define

longtail(\lambda):=\max_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}}s(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}).

Theorem 4

(See Corollary
6.5.3
, Theorem
6.6.10
, Corollary
6.6.12
)
.

The following values are equal to
longtail(\lambda)
:

(1)

The maximal cardinality of an incomparable subset
S\subset\mathcal{R}
such that
c_{\lambda}(\alpha)=1
for all
\alpha\in S
.

(2)

\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}}\sharp\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}|p\in[\overline{\lambda}_{i},k_{i}]\}
which is to say the long arrow of
\lambda
is the maximal number of arrows going over a single point in the arrow diagram of
\lambda
.

(3)

The maximal number of caps going over a single point in the cap diagram of
\lambda
, as defined in
[
BS12
]
.

The above theorem is a correction to the Tail Conjecture appearing in
[
GH23
]
(see
6.3.8
).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Vector superspaces and Lie superalgebras

In this section we follow
[
CW12
, Chapter 1]
with some adaptation of notation.

Definition 2.1.1
.

A
vector superspace

V
is a vector space together with
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading
V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
. The
dimension
of
V
is given by the pair
\dim V=(\dim V_{\bar{0}}|\,\dim V_{\bar{1}})
, and the
superdimension
of
V
is defined as
sdimV=\dim V_{\bar{0}}-\dim V_{\bar{1}}
.

The
parity
of a homogeneous
a\in V_{i}
is denoted by
p(a)=i
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Example 2.1.2
.

We denote by
\mathbb{C}^{m|n}
the super vector space with even subspace
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and odd subspace
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1.3
.

An associative
superalgebra

A
is a super vector space
A=A_{\bar{0}}\oplus A_{\bar{1}}
, with a multiplication operation
A\otimes A\to A
that satisfies
A_{i}A_{j}\subseteq A_{i+j}
for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and which is associative.

Definition 2.1.4
.

A
module

M
over a superalgebra
A
is a module in the traditional sense, that must also agree with the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-grading. That is,
M=M_{\bar{0}}\oplus M_{\bar{1}}
and
A_{i}M_{j}\subseteq M_{i+j}
for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.1.5
.

A
Lie superalgebra
is a vector superspace
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{1}}
with a binary bilinear operation
[\cdot,\cdot]
satisfying the following axioms for homogeneous
a,b,c\in\mathfrak{g}
:

(1)

Supersymmetry:
[a,b]=-(-1)^{p(a)\cdot p(b)}[b,a]
.

(2)

Super Jacobi identity:
[a,[b,c]]=[[a,b],c]+(-1)^{p(a)\cdot p(b)}[b,[a,c]]
.

Example 2.1.6
.

Any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie superalgebra with even part given by
\mathfrak{g}
and odd part equal to
\{0\}
.

Example 2.1.7
.

Let
A=A_{\bar{0}}\oplus A_{\bar{1}}
be an associative superalgebra. We can give it the structure of a superalgebra by defining the bracket by the supercommutator

[a,b]=ab-(-1)^{p(a)\cdot p(b)}ba

for homogeneous
a,b\in A
, and extending
[\cdot,\cdot]
bilinearly.

Definition 2.1.8

(The general linear Lie superalgebra)
.

Let
V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
be a super vector space. The algebra
\text{End}(V)
is naturally an associative superalgebra, and as in Example
2.1.7
we may equip it with the supercommutator to form a Lie superalgebra called the
general linear Lie superalgebra
. This superalgebra is denoted by
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
.

When
V=\mathbb{C}^{m|n}
we write
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n):=\mathfrak{gl}(V)
.

Observe that for a vector superspace
V=V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}
, we have:
\mathfrak{gl}(V)_{\bar{0}}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(V_{\bar{0}})\oplus\mathfrak{gl}(V_{\bar{1}})
and
\mathfrak{gl}(V)_{\bar{1}}\cong(V_{\bar{0}}\oplus V_{\bar{1}}^{*})\oplus(V_{\bar{0}}^{*}\oplus V_{\bar{1}})
.

Fixing a basis for
V_{\bar{0}}
and for
V_{\bar{1}}
that combine to a basis of
V
, elements of
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
can be realized as a superalgebra of
(m+n)\times(m+n)
matrices, with basis given by elementary matrices
E_{ij}
.

Definition 2.1.9
.

A
Cartan subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is defined as a Cartan subalgebra of the even subalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\bar{0}}\cong\mathfrak{gl}(m)\oplus\mathfrak{gl}(n)
(which in turn is defined as a subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\bar{0}}
that is both nilpotent and equal to its normalizer).

Note that Cartan subalgebras need not be unique, and there can (and often are) multiple Cartan subalgebras. Cartan subalgebras are conjugate under inner automorphisms.

Example 2.1.10
.

The set of all diagonal matrices is a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.

Note that the scope of this paper is limited to the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. That being said, all Lie superalgebras with a reductive even part and an invariant symmetric form have Cartan subalgebras.

Furthermore, all definitions and statements presented in this section can be stated for any Lie superalgebra equipped with a grading
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(-1)\oplus\mathfrak{g}(0)\oplus\mathfrak{g}(1)
where
\mathfrak{g}(0)=\mathfrak{g}_{\bar{0}}
is a reductive Lie algebra and
\mathfrak{g}(-1)
and
\mathfrak{g}(1)
are irreducible
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-modules.

Definition 2.1.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.
Consider the actions of the elements of
\mathfrak{h}
on
\mathfrak{g}
by the adjoint action (as in Example
2.1.20
). These can be simultaneously diagonalized, so there exists a decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
into eigenspaces

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\underset{\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}}{\bigoplus}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\text{.}

where
\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\{g\in\mathfrak{g}\,|\,\forall h\in\mathfrak{h}\,[h,g]=\alpha(h)g\}
.
We call
\alpha
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\neq 0
a
root
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
a
root space
.
The set of all roots
\Delta\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is called the
root system
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Roots can be assigned parity via the parity of their respective root spaces. As such
\Delta
can be split into even and odd parts denoted by
\Delta_{\bar{0}}
and
\Delta_{\bar{1}}
respectively.

Theorem 2.1.12
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. We have:

(1)

\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=1
, for
\alpha\in\Delta
.

(2)

[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
where
\mathfrak{h}
is considered to be
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
.

(3)

\Delta=-\Delta
,
\Delta_{\bar{0}}=-\Delta_{\bar{0}}
and
\Delta_{\bar{1}}=-\Delta_{\bar{1}}
.

Definition 2.1.13
.

Fix a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Functionals
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
are called
weights
.
Given a simple
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-module
M
, any subspace
V
of dimension one is a weight space in the sense that there exists
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
such that
\{v\in V|\,\forall h\in\mathfrak{h}\,h.v=\lambda(h)v\}
.

Notice that the set of roots
\Delta
is in fact a set of weights, that is to say
\Delta\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

Example 2.1.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
given by all diagonal matrices. For
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
, denote by
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\delta_{j}
the weights which are dual to the matrices
E_{i,\,i}\in\mathfrak{h}
and
E_{m+j,\,m+j}\in\mathfrak{h}
.

Then
\Delta_{\overline{0}}=\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})\}_{1\leq i,j\leq m}\cup\{\pm(\delta_{i}-\delta_{j})\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
,
\Delta_{\overline{1}}=\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j})\}_{1\leq i\leq m,\,1\leq j\leq n}
.

Defining
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\delta_{j}
as in Example
2.1.14
, any weight
\nu\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
is given by

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\delta_{j}.

For convenience we write
(a_{1},\,\dots,a_{m}\,|b_{1},\,\dots,b_{n})
as shorthand for
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\delta_{j}
and write
\nu_{k}:=a_{k}
and
\nu_{-k}:=b_{k}
.

Further, one may equip
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
with a symmetric bilinear form given by

(\varepsilon_{i}|\varepsilon_{j}):=\delta_{ij},\;(\delta_{j}|\varepsilon_{i}):=(\varepsilon_{i}|\delta_{j}):=-\delta_{ij}\;(\delta_{i}|\delta_{j}):=-\delta_{ij}

for all
i,j
.

Definition 2.1.15
.

A base of
\Delta
is a linearly independent set
\Sigma\subset\Delta
such that every root
\alpha\in\Delta
can be written as a linear combination of the elements of
\Sigma
, with integer coefficients, so that the coefficients are either all non-positive or all non-negative.

The following is a classical result appearing in
[
H72
, Chapter 10]
.

Lemma 2.1.16
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta^{+}\sqcup\Delta^{-}
be a decomposition of the root system such that

\alpha\in\Delta^{+}\,\Longleftrightarrow\,-\alpha\in\Delta^{-}.

Consider the simple roots with respect to this decomposition: these are roots that cannot be written as a sum of two positive roots with respect to this decomposition. The set of all simple roots is a base.

Conversely, given a base
\Sigma
, denote by
\Delta^{+}
the set of all roots which are linear combinations of elements in
\Sigma
with non-negative coefficients. Then
\Delta=\Delta^{+}\sqcup(\Delta\setminus\Delta^{+})
is a a decomposition of
\Delta
into sets of positive and negative roots, satisfying the requirement above.

Definition 2.1.17
.

Consider
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
with the Cartan subalgebra as described in
Example

2.1.14
and set of positive roots given by

\Delta^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}|1\leq i<j\leq m\}\cup\{\delta_{i}-\delta_{j}|1\leq i<j\leq n\}\cup\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}|i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\text{ and }j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}.

We call the base corresponding to
\Delta^{+}
the
distinguished base
and denote it by
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

Example 2.1.18
.

For
\mathfrak{gl}(2|2)
, we have:

\Delta=\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j})|1\leq i,j\leq 2\}
. The distinguished base is given by base

\Sigma^{\text{dist}}=\{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\}.

The set of positive and negative roots is given by the set of roots in
\Delta
which can we written as
\sum k_{i}\alpha_{i}
where
\alpha_{i}\in\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
and
k_{i}
are either all non-negative and all non-positive respectively.

As such, the set of positive roots with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
is

\Delta^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},\delta_{1}-\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}\}

and
\Delta^{-}=-\Delta^{+}
.

Definition 2.1.19
.

A
\mathfrak{g}
-module
M
is a vector superspace with a Lie superalgebra homomorphism
\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(M)
.

Example 2.1.20
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie superalgebra. The adjoint module of
\mathfrak{g}
is the superspace
\mathfrak{g}
; the action of
g\in\mathfrak{g}
on
h\in\mathfrak{g}
is given by
g.h=[g,h]
.

In this paper we consider only
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-modules that are finite dimensional and semisimple over
\mathfrak{h}
, with
\mathfrak{h}
acting integrably (that is to say, the action of
\mathfrak{h}
integrates to an algebraic action of the corresponding torus group).

Definition 2.1.21
.

We define a partial order on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by
\lambda\geq\mu
if
\lambda-\mu=\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}a_{\alpha}\alpha
where
a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Fact 2.1.22
.

Let
L
be a simple
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-module. As an
\mathfrak{h}
-module,
L=\oplus_{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}}V_{\lambda}
where
V_{\lambda}
is trivial for all except finitely many
\lambda
. Fixing any base
\Sigma
, there exists a single highest weight
\lambda
with respect to the partial order described in
Definition

2.1.21
. Such
\lambda
is called the highest weight of
L
with respect to
\Sigma
.

Moreover, any pair of simple
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-modules
M\not\cong N
have distinct highest weights. Therefore, we denote the simple
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-module of highest weight
\lambda
with respect to a base
\Sigma
by
L_{\Sigma}(\lambda)
.

When
\Sigma=\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
, we write
L(\lambda)
.

The highest weight
\lambda
of any integrable simple module
L
with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
satisfies the conditions:
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for any
i
, and
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
for
i<m
and
i\geq m+1
.

The weights satisfying the first of these conditions are called integral, and the weights satisfying the the second condition are called dominant. Any integral dominant weight of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is the highest weight of some integrable simple module
L
with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

Notation 2.1.23
.

•

Denote by
{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
the set of all
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
such that
\lambda
is integral dominant with respect to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

•

Denote
\Lambda^{+}:=\bigsqcup_{m,n\geq 0}{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
.

2.2.
Reflections

We now consider
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
.

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be some base of
\Delta
. We denote the Weyl vector associated with
\Sigma
by

\rho_{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{\bar{0}}^{+}}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\beta\in\Delta_{\bar{1}}^{+}}\beta.

We will usually write
\rho:=\rho_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}
.

Fact 2.2.2
.

For any
\alpha\in\Sigma
,
(\rho_{\Sigma}|\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha|\alpha)
.

This fact is
[
CW12
, Proposition 1.28]
.

Remark 2.2.3
.

When discussing the Weyl vector
\rho_{\Sigma}
, we will usually consider the Weyl vector shifted by
r(1\,\dots\,1|-1\,\dots\,-1)
for some
r\in\{0,1/2\}
, so that all the coordinates of
\rho_{\Sigma}
are integers (which need not always be true as the Weyl vector is a half sum). This shifted Weyl vector satisfies the above property as well.

Definition 2.2.4
.

An
even reflection by
\alpha\in\Delta_{\bar{0}}
is an automorphism
s_{\alpha}:\Delta\to\Delta
of the root system given by

s_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta-2\frac{(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.

The even reflections generate the Weyl group of the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
, which is isomorphic to
S_{m}\times S_{n}
.

This definition is the same as for root systems of Lie algebras. That being said, with parity coming into play, one may need to consider another type of reflection. This is because not every pair of bases
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
are conjugate with respect to the action of the Weyl group.

We now introduce the notion of an odd reflection, due to Serganova in
[
LSS86
]
.

Definition 2.2.5
.

An
odd reflection by
\alpha\in\Delta_{\bar{1}}
is an automorphism
r_{\alpha}:\Delta\to\Delta
given by

r_{\alpha}(\beta)=\begin{cases}-\alpha&\text{ if }\beta=\alpha\\
\beta+\alpha&\text{ if }\beta\neq\alpha\text{ and }(\beta,\alpha)\neq 0\\
\beta&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Fact 2.2.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a base. For any
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\bar{1}}
and
\beta\in\Sigma_{\bar{0}}
,
r_{\alpha}(\Sigma)
and
s_{\beta}(\Sigma)
are also bases of
\Delta
.

Remark 2.2.7
.

It is not immediately obvious why the odd reflection is a natural extension of the notion of even reflection. One way to see this connection is by considering the way even and odd reflections change the set of positive roots.

Given a base
\Sigma
and
\beta\in\Sigma_{\bar{0}}
, we have
\Delta^{+}_{s_{\beta}\Sigma}=\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}\setminus\{\beta\}\cup\{-\beta\}
.

Similarly, for
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\bar{1}}
we have
\Delta^{+}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}\setminus\{\alpha\}\cup\{-\alpha\}
.

We now consider how an odd reflection of a base affect the the highest weight of a given module with respect to this base.

Fact 2.2.8
.

Let
\Sigma
be a base for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Let
L
be a simple integrable (finite dimensional)
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
-module, and let
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\bar{1}}
. Let
\lambda
be the highest weight of the module
L
with respect to the base
\Sigma
and let
\lambda^{\prime}
be the highest weight of the module
L
with respect to the base
r_{\alpha}\Sigma
; that is,

L_{\Sigma}(\lambda)\cong L_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(\lambda^{\prime})
. Then
\lambda^{\prime}
is given by

\lambda^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\lambda-\alpha&\text{ if }(\lambda,\alpha)\neq 0\\
\lambda&\text{ if }(\lambda,\alpha)=0.\end{cases}

Notation 2.2.9
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and let
\Sigma
be a base for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. We denote by
\lambda_{\Sigma}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
the highest weight of
L(\lambda)
with respect to the base
\Sigma
; that is,
L_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}(\lambda)\cong L_{\Sigma}(\lambda_{\Sigma})
.

By
2.2.8
, the weights
\lambda_{\Sigma}
are always integral.

Fact 2.2.10
.

Let
\Sigma
be a base of
\Delta
. Then for
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\bar{0}}
we have

\rho_{s_{\alpha}\Sigma}=\rho_{\Sigma}-\alpha.

Similarly, for
\alpha\in\Sigma_{\bar{1}}
we have

\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=\rho_{\Sigma}+\alpha.

This fact appears as part of the proof of
[
CW12
, Proposition 1.28]
.

Definition 2.2.11
.

We say that a base
\Sigma

agrees with the even part of

\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
if
\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma,\bar{0}}=\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}},\bar{0}}
. Denote by
\mathbb{S}
the set of all bases that agree with the even part of
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

Remark 2.2.12
.

As Remark
2.2.7
states, even reflections change the set of positive even roots and while odd reflections change the set of positive odd roots. As such,
\mathbb{S}
is clearly the set of all bases that can be produced from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
by performing only odd reflections with respect to roots in
\Delta^{+}_{\overline{1}}
(the set of positive odd roots for the distinguished base).

Notation 2.3.1
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
.
We denote
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}:=\lambda_{\Sigma}+\rho_{\Sigma}
and we write
\overline{\lambda}:=\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}

Notation 2.3.2
.

Given

the set of

Remark 2.3.3
.

Note that

is itself an element of

and an integral weight, so we maintain the indexing convention described in Definition

Definition 2.3.4
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
.
The weight diagram of
\lambda
with respect to
\Sigma
is defined as a function on the integers, whose value in

is a finite collection of symbols
\times,>,<,\circ
. This function is subject to the following rules:

•

,

. Then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly
\min(k,s)
symbols
\times
.

•

If
k>s
then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly

symbols

.

•

If
k<s
then
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains exactly

symbols
<
.

•

D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
contains a (unique) symbol
\circ
iff it does not contain any other symbol (in such a case, we day that
p
is an empty position).

Remark 2.3.5
.

For
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, we have:
-1\leq s-k\leq 1
by

. That is to say, in diagrams

for

, the number of positive coordinates such that

and the number of negative coordinates such that
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})_{-j}=-p
, differ by 1 at most (for any

).

Notation 2.3.6
.

When not specified otherwise, we denote by
D_{\lambda}:=D^{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}_{\lambda}
the weight diagram of
\lambda
with respect to the distinguished base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
.

Remark 2.3.7
.

The weight diagram
D_{\lambda}
has at most one symbol corresponding to each

; that is to say, for any

,
D_{\lambda}(p)\in\{\circ,\times,>,<\}
.

Given
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
, one may visualize

by drawing the symbols corresponding to each integer at the integer points of the real line: that is, at every point

we draw the symbols
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}(p)
(expressions of the form
\times^{k}>
or
\times^{k}<
are drawn with the symbol
>
or
<
on the line and
k
symbols
\times
stacked vertically above it).

.

Consider the weight

\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}=(4\,4\,2\,|-1\,-2\,-4\,-4\,-5)

-shifted weight

Proposition 2.3.9
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}

2.4.
Cap diagrams

The cap diagrams are a combinatorial tool developed to study representations of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and similar representation theories. They were introduced by Brundan and Stroppel, see for example
[
BS12
]
.

Definition 2.4.1
.

The
cap diagram
of the weight diagram
D_{\lambda}
is defined as follows: for every symbol
\times
in position
p
in, we draw a cap from position
p
to position
q>p
if
D_{\lambda}(q)=\circ
and we have:

\displaystyle\forall\,p\leq r<q,\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\sharp\{p\leq s\leq r\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}>\sharp\{p\leq s\leq r\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}

\displaystyle\sharp\{p\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}=\sharp\{p\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}.

In other words, we draw a cap from position
p
to position
q
if for every
p\leq r<q
, the number of
\times
symbols in the segment
[p,r]
is greater than the number of
\circ
symbols in the same segment, and these numbers are equal in the segment
[p,q]
.

Remark 2.4.2
.

Note that the function

g^{(p)}_{\times}(r):=\sharp\{p\leq s\leq r\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}-\sharp\{p\leq s\leq r\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}

satisfies:

•

g^{(p)}_{\times}(p)=1
(since
D_{\lambda}(p)=\times
),

•

g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)<0
for
r>>l
(due to the fact that there are finitely many
\times
symbols and infinitely many
\circ
symbols in
D_{\lambda}
), and

•

|g^{(p)}_{\times}(r+1)-g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)|\leq 1
for any
r\geq p
.

So there exists
q\geq p
such that
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=0
and by the definition above, the minimal such
q
is the endpoint of the cap starting from
l
.

Moreover, the fact that
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=0
and
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q-1)>0
implies that
D_{\lambda}(i)=\circ
.

Lemma 2.4.3
.

A position
q\in\mathbb{Z}
is the endpoint of a cap iff there exists
p\in\mathbb{Z},p\leq q
such that

\displaystyle\forall\,p<r\leq q,\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}<\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}

\displaystyle\sharp\{p\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}=\sharp\{p\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}.

Proof.

Define

g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r):=\sharp\{r<s\leq q|D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}-\sharp\{r<s\leq q|D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}.

Notice that for
p<r\leq q
,

\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}<\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}\iff g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<0

Similarly, for
p\leq r<q
,

\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\times\}<\sharp\{r\leq s\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(s)=\circ\}\iff g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)>0

Furthermore,
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=g^{(q)}_{\circ}(p)
. So for the
\Rightarrow
direction it is sufficient to show that

\forall p<r\leq q,\;\;\;\;g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<0.

Likewise, for the
\Leftarrow
direction it is sufficient to show that

\forall p\leq r<q,\;\;\;\;g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)<0.

The value
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)+g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)
is precisely the number
\times
symbols in the segment
(p,q]
plus the number of
\circ
symbols in the same segment. Thus
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)+g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)
doesn’t depend on
\lambda
.

We now prove the implications in the statement of the lemma.

\Rightarrow:
Let
q
be the position of the endpoint of a cap starting at position
p
(thus
D_{\lambda}(q)=\circ
). Then by definition
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)>0
for
p<r<q
and
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=0
.
As such the number of
\times
symbols in the segment
[p,r]
is greater than the number of
\circ
symbols. Since
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=0
we deduce that
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)+g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)=0
. As such,
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<0
. Additionally we have:

D_{\lambda}(q)=\circ,\;g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)=-1<0.

\Leftarrow:
Let
q
and
p\leq q
be such that for
p<r\leq q
, we have:
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<0
. Recall that
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(p)=0
, and for the same reason
g^{(p)}_{\times}(q)=0
. We deduce that
D_{\lambda}(p)=\times
and
g^{(p)}_{\times}(p)>0
.

As
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)+g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)
is constant and equal to zero, and
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<0
for
p<r<q
, we conclude that
g^{(p)}_{\times}(r)>0
.

∎

From the above proof, the function
g^{(q)}_{\circ}
also satisfies properties similar to the ones of
g^{(p)}_{\times}
:

•

g^{(q)}_{\circ}(q)=-1
,

•

g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)<<0
for
r<<q
(due to the fact that there are finitely many
\times
symbols and infinitely many
\circ
symbols in
D_{\lambda}
), and

•

|g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r+1)-g^{(q)}_{\circ}(r)|\leq 1
for any
r\geq p
.

This immediately implies:

Corollary 2.4.4
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
D_{\lambda}(q)=\circ
. Assume that for some
p\in\mathbb{Z},p<q
we have:

\displaystyle\sharp\{p\leq r\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(r)=\times\}\geq\sharp\{p\leq r\leq q\,|\,D_{\lambda}(r)=\circ\}

Then position
q
is the end of some cap in
D_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

For
p
as in the statement of the Corollary, we have
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(p)>0
. Thus there exists
p\leq p^{\prime}\leq q
such that
g^{(q)}_{\circ}(p^{\prime})=0
, and the largest such
p^{\prime}
would be the beginning of a cap which ends at
q
.
∎

Remark 2.4.5
.

The cap diagram of a given weight diagram
D_{\lambda}
may be described in a more intuitive manner. Denote by
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}
the set of right endpoints of the caps, where
c_{i}
is the end of the cap starting at
\times_{i}
. The positions
c_{k},\dots,c_{1}
can be defined starting inductively by

c_{l}=\min\{p\in\mathbb{Z}|p>\times_{l},\,D_{\lambda}(p)=\circ\text{ and }p\neq c_{l^{\prime}}\text{ for }l^{\prime}>l\}

This description tells us how to draw a cap diagram effectively.
This is done by going over all
\times
symbols of the diagram from rightmost to leftmost and drawing a cap from the
\times
to the first empty position to the right of this
\times
symbol that is not the endpoint of a previously drawn cap.

3.
Description of bases in
\mathbb{S}

3.1.
Right odd roots

Let
m,n\geq 0
.

Definition 3.1.1
.

•

The odd positive roots for the choice of distinguished base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
will be called right odd roots. The set of right odd roots is denoted by
\mathcal{R}:={\Delta^{+}_{dist}}_{\bar{1}}
. Explicitly,
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}\implies\alpha=\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\text{ for some }1\leq i\leq m\text{ and }1\leq j\leq n
.

•

The set
\mathcal{R}
inherits the partial order on the weights induced by the base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
:
\alpha\leq\beta
iff
\beta-\alpha
is a linear combination of elements of
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
with non-negative integer coefficients. Explicitly,

\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{k}-\delta_{l}\iff i\geq k\text{ and }j\leq l.

•

Given a set
X\subset\mathcal{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
, we write
\alpha\geq X
when
\alpha\geq\beta
for all
\beta\in X
.

Remark 3.1.2
.

This order yields a lattice structure on
\mathcal{R}
. For example, for
\mathfrak{gl}(4|4)
the lattice looks as follows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{4}-\delta_{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{4}-\delta_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{4}-\delta_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{4}-\delta_{1}}

Definition 3.1.3
.

A subset
X\subset\mathcal{R}
is called
incomparable
if no two elements of
X
can be compared in the order above: that is,
\forall\alpha,\beta\in X
,
\alpha\not\geq\beta
,
\alpha\not\leq\beta
.

3.2.
Describing bases in
\mathbb{S}
using right odd roots

In what follows we will often use words over the alphabet
\{\varepsilon,\delta\}
with
m
copies of
\varepsilon
and
n
copies of
\delta
to represent bases in
\mathbb{S}
, which is possible due to the well-known lemma below (cf.
[
CW12
, Example 1.20]
).

Notation 3.2.1
.

•

For any word
w
as above, we will write
\varepsilon_{i}
for the
i
-th letter
\varepsilon
in
w
, read from left to right (respectively,
\delta_{j}
for the
j
-th letter
\delta
in
w
).

•

Given a word
w
, let
f_{w}(k)
denote the number of letters
\delta
in
w
standing between the
\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}
. We also set
f_{w}(0)
to be the number of letters
\delta
to the left of
\varepsilon_{1}
. Similarly, let
g_{w}(k)
denote the number of letters
\varepsilon
in
w
standing between the
\delta_{k}
and
\delta_{k+1}
. We also set
g_{w}(0)
to be the number of letters
\varepsilon
to the left of
\delta_{1}
. When it is clear which word
w
we are referring to, we will omit the subscript
w
from this notation.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

There exists a bijection between
\mathbb{S}
and the set of words over the alphabet
\{\varepsilon,\delta\}
with
m
copies of
\varepsilon
and
n
copies of
\delta
.

Proof.

Fixing a word
w
, we number the letters
\varepsilon
in
w
from left to right
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}
as before, and similarly for the letters
\delta
in
w
. Taking the differences between each two adjacent symbols gives a base
\Sigma_{w}
, and it is obviously in
\mathbb{S}
(due to the numbering of the symbols).

Vice versa, given a base
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, we define the corresponding word
w
by describing the function
f:\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}
corresponding to
w
: we set

f(0):=\sharp\{1\leq i\leq n:\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{i}\in\Delta^{-}_{\Sigma}\}

and for
k>0
, we set

f(k):=\sharp\{1\leq i\leq n:\varepsilon_{k}-\delta_{i}\in\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}\}-\sharp\{1\leq i\leq m:\varepsilon_{k+1}-\delta_{i}\in\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}\}.

These maps are clearly inverse to one another.

∎

Example 3.2.3
.

The base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
is given by the word
w_{dist}:=\varepsilon^{m}\delta^{n}=\delta^{f(0)}\overset{m}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\varepsilon\delta^{f(k)}
where
f(k)=0
for
k\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
and
f(m)=n
.

Lemma 3.2.4
.

There exists a map between
\mathbb{S}
and the set of incomparable subsets of
\mathcal{R}
given by
\Sigma\longmapsto\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
, and this map is a bijection.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
. Assume
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma
satisfy:
\alpha-\beta
is a linear combination with positive coefficients of the elements of
\Delta_{\overline{0},\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}^{+}
. Then
\alpha-\beta
is a combination with positive coefficients of the elements of
\Delta_{\overline{0},\Sigma}^{+}
, since
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
. But that contradicts the assumption that
\Sigma
is a base and its elements are linearly independent. Thus
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
is incomparable and the map is well-defined.

We now define the inverse map.
Let
S
be a set of incomparable roots. For any pair of roots
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
and
\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}-\delta_{j^{\prime}}
in
S
, we have:
i^{\prime}\neq i
, and
i<i^{\prime}\Rightarrow j<j^{\prime}
.
So we may write
S=\{\varepsilon_{i_{1}}-\delta_{j_{1}},\dots,\varepsilon_{i_{k}}-\delta_{j_{k}}\}
where
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}
, and
j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{k}
. We define the word
w_{S}
by defining the function
d:\{0,\ldots,n+1\}\to\{0,\ldots,m\}
:

d(j):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;0\leq j<j_{1}\\
i_{l}&\text{ if }\;j_{l-1}\leq j<j_{l}\text{ for }l\in\{2,\dots,k-1\}\\
i_{k}&\text{ if }\;j_{k}\leq j\leq n\\
m&\text{ if }\;j=n+1\end{cases}

It is obvious that
d
is an increasing function and we take the word
w_{S}=\varepsilon^{g(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}(\delta\varepsilon^{g(k)})

where
g(k):=d(k+1)-d(k)
,
k=0,\ldots,n
. Let
\Sigma_{S}\in\mathbb{S}
be the base corresponding to the word
w_{S}
.

All that remains is to verify that these maps are indeed inverse to each other. One direction is clear: if we set
S:=\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
for some
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, then the base we recover from
S
by the construction above is clearly
\Sigma
(it has the same word as
\Sigma
).

So we only need to check that for any incomparable set
S\subset\mathcal{R}
, we have:
\Sigma_{S}\cap\mathcal{R}=S
.
Consider the word
w_{S}
constructed above.
Observe that
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma_{S}\cap\mathcal{R}
if and only if
j=\min\{j^{\prime}:i=d(j^{\prime})\}
.
Thus
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma_{S}\cap\mathcal{R}
iff
(i,j)=(i_{l},j_{l})
for some
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, which implies
S\subset\Sigma_{S}\cap\mathcal{R}
.

∎

Example 3.2.5
.

Consider the root system of
\mathfrak{gl}(4|4)
and
X=\{\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{3}\}
, we know that
f(1)=f(4)=0
,
f(1)+f(2)=3-1=2
and
f(1)+f(2)+f(3)+f(4)=4
thus the word corresponding to the inverse of
X
is
\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}\varepsilon\delta^{2}\varepsilon
and thus the base is

\Sigma=\{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},\delta_{1}-\delta_{2},\delta_{2}-\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{3}-\delta_{3},\delta_{3}-\delta_{4},\delta_{4}-\varepsilon_{4}\}.

Recall that for any
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma
, then
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\in\mathbb{S}
. Here is how the words describing
\Sigma,r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
are related: take the word
w
corresponding to
\Sigma
. The word
w
corresponding to
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
is obtained by swapping the
\varepsilon_{i}
(from the left) with the symbol
\delta_{j}
standing to its right.

In other words, if the word corresponding to
\Sigma
is
w=\delta^{f(0)}\overset{m}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\varepsilon\delta^{f(k)}
then the word corresponding to
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
will be
w^{\prime}=\delta^{f^{\prime}(0)}\overset{m}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\varepsilon\delta^{f^{\prime}(k)}
where
f^{\prime}(i-1)=f(i-1)+1
,
f^{\prime}(i)=f(i)-1
and
f^{\prime}
agrees with
f
everywhere else.

Lemma 3.2.6
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma
. Then for any
\alpha\in(r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R})\setminus(\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R})
has
\alpha>\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma
.
Consider the word
w
corresponding to
\Sigma
, since
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma

\varepsilon_{i}
must be immediately to the left of
\delta_{j}
.

The letter immediately to the left of
\varepsilon_{i}
is either
\delta
or
\varepsilon
. Since
\delta
s and
\varepsilon
s are numbered by order of appearance from left to right, the letter to left of
\varepsilon_{i}
is either
\delta_{j-1}
or
\varepsilon_{i-1}
.

Similarly, the letter immediately to the right of
\delta_{j}
is either
\delta_{j+1}
or
\varepsilon_{i+1}
.

As the word corresponding to
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
is the same as
w
excepts
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\delta_{j}
swap places, we must simply consider the letters immediately to the left and right of
\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}
. As such we consider 4 possible cases:
\varepsilon_{i-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i+1}
,
\varepsilon_{i-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1}
,

\delta_{j-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i+1}
and
\delta_{j-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1}
.

In the first case,
\varepsilon_{i-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i+1}\mapsto\varepsilon_{i-1}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{i+1}
thus

r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R})\cup\{\varepsilon_{i-1}-\delta_{j}\}\setminus\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\}.

Notice that
\varepsilon_{i-1}-\delta_{j}>\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
.

In the second case,
\varepsilon_{i-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1}\mapsto\varepsilon_{i-1}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j+1}
thus

r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R})\cup\{\varepsilon_{i-1}-\delta_{j},\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j+1}\}\setminus\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\}.

Notice that
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j+1}>\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
.

In the third case,
\delta_{j-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i+1}\mapsto\delta_{j-1}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{i+1}
thus
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R})\setminus\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\}
.

And finally in the fourth case
\delta_{j-1}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1}\mapsto\delta_{j-1}\delta_{j}\varepsilon_{i}\delta_{j+1}
thus

r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R})\cup\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j+1}\}\setminus\{\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\}.

In all cases
\alpha\in r_{i,j}(\Sigma)\cap\mathcal{R}\setminus\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
has
\alpha>\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
.
∎

Definition 3.2.7
.

Given a sequence of right odd roots
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
, we say that the expression
r_{\alpha_{k}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
is valid means that for any
i=0,\ldots,k-1
we have:

\alpha_{i+1}\in r_{\alpha_{i}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}}).

Lemma 3.2.8
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
. Then there exists a sequence of right odd roots
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
such that the
r_{\alpha_{k}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma
.

Note that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for all indices
i\neq j
.

Proof.

Consider
w
the word corresponding to some
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and
f_{w}
the function correspond to
w
, which is to say,
w=\delta^{f_{w}(0)}\overset{m}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\varepsilon\delta^{f_{w}(k)}
.
Set
e(i):=\sum_{l=i}^{m}f_{w}(l)
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(n-e(i))
.

We prove the Lemma by induction of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma)
.

For the base case the only base that has
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma)=0
is
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
and there is the set of roots is
\varnothing
.

Assume the statement is correct for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma)=K
and let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma)=K
.
Let
w
be the word corresponding to
\Sigma
. Let
1\leq i\leq m
be the minimal index such that
f_{w}(i)>0
. By minimality there must exists at least one
\delta
between
\varepsilon_{m-i}
and
\varepsilon_{m-(i+1)}
. denote the
\delta
immediately to the left of
\varepsilon_{m-(i+1)}
by
\delta_{j}
. Consider
w^{\prime}
obtained from
w
by swapping
\delta_{j}
with
\varepsilon_{m-(i+1)}
.
w^{\prime}
corresponds to some base
\Sigma^{\prime}
which clearly has
\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathbf{\Delta}_{e}(\Sigma)-1
and we find that:

\varepsilon_{m-(i+1)}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma^{\prime},\,r_{(m-(i+1)),j}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\Sigma

This completes the proof of the induction step.
∎

Note that there may be many other sequences of right odd roots
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})
such that
r_{k}\circ\dots\circ r_{1}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma
. We will now show that all such sequences are in fact simply permutations of one another. That is to say, the
set

\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\}
is uniquely defined.

Theorem 3.2.9
.

Consider the map

B:\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow P(\mathcal{R})
(subsets of
\mathcal{R}
)
given by

B_{\Sigma}:=\mathcal{R}\setminus\{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}|\;\exists\beta\in\Sigma\text{ so that }\alpha\geq\beta\}

(in other words,
B_{\Sigma}
is a subset of
\mathcal{R}
such that the minimal elements in the complement
\mathcal{R}\setminus B_{\Sigma}
are precisely the elements of
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
).

Then the map
B
satisfies:

(1)

There exists an ordering
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
of the elements of
B_{\Sigma}
such that the expression
r_{\alpha_{k}}\circ\ldots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma_{dist})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma
;

(2)

Given any sequence
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
of right odd roots such that
r_{\alpha_{k}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma_{dist})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma
, we have:
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}=B_{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

We may use Lemma
3.2.8
to stipulate that such a sequence
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
exists, and by Part (2) it will be an ordering of the elements of
B_{\Sigma}
, proving Part (1).
Moving on to Part (2), we need only verify that given a sequence of right odd roots
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
such that
r_{\alpha_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})=\Sigma
, that
k=\sharp B_{\Sigma}
and that
\alpha_{i}\in B_{\Sigma}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Consider a base
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
corresponding to a word
w=\varepsilon^{g(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\delta\varepsilon^{g(k)}
. As stated before, a reflection by
\alpha\in\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
corresponds to a swap of a pair of letters
\varepsilon\delta\mapsto\delta\varepsilon
in
w
. Therefore, the number of reflections with respect to right odd roots required to produce
\Sigma
from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
is fixed and does not depend on the sequence of reflections: this number is

k=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(j)
, where
d(j):=m-\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}g(l)
is the number of letters
\varepsilon
to the right of the letter
\delta_{j}
in
w
.

We now construct a sequence
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
explicitly,
consider
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
corresponding to a word
w=\varepsilon^{g(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\delta\varepsilon^{g(k)}
.
Recall that
w_{dist}
, the word corresponding to
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
is given by
w_{dist}=\varepsilon^{m}\delta^{n}=\varepsilon^{g^{\prime}(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\delta\varepsilon^{g^{\prime}(k)}
where
g^{\prime}(0)=m
and
g^{\prime}(k)\equiv 0
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

Consider that performing reflections
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{1}
for
i=m,m-1,\dots,g(0)
on
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
in that order is valid and yields a base i.e.
r_{m,1}\circ\cdots\circ r_{g(0),1}=\Sigma_{1}
corresponding to word
w_{1}=\varepsilon^{g_{1}(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\delta\varepsilon^{g_{1}(k)}
where
g_{1}(0)=g(0)
and
g_{1}(1)=m-g(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)
. Note here that
d(1)
reflections have been performed.

Repeating this process by performing reflections by
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{2}
for
i=m,\dots,g(1)+g(0)
on
\Sigma_{1}
is once again valid and yields a base i.e.
r_{m,2}\circ\cdots\circ r_{g(0)+g(1),2}(\Sigma_{1})=\Sigma_{2}
which is given by word
w_{2}=\varepsilon^{g_{2}(0)}\overset{n}{\underset{k=1}{\prod}}\delta\varepsilon^{g_{2}(k)}
where
g_{2}(0)=g_{1}(0)=g(0)
and
g_{2}(1)=g_{1}(1)-(m-g(0)-g(1))=g(1)
and
g_{2}(2)=m-g(0)-g(1)
. Again note that
d(2)
additional reflections have been performed to get from
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
to
\Sigma_{2}
.

Further repeating these steps for every
1\leq j\leq n
yields a base
\Sigma_{n}
which has
w_{n}=w
one may verify that the number of reflections performed is
k=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(j)
.
By Lemmas
3.2.4
,
3.2.6
we obtain: for any
i=1,\ldots,k
,

\forall\beta\in r_{\alpha_{i}}\circ\ldots\circ r_{\alpha_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})\cap\mathcal{R},\;\;\;\;\text{ either }\;\beta>\alpha_{i}\;\;\text{ or }\;\;\beta,\alpha_{i}\;\text{ are incomparable}.

This means that for any
\beta\in\Sigma
, and any
1\leq i\leq k
, we have
\beta\not<\alpha_{i}
. Thus
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in B_{\Sigma}
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.2.10
.

Let
\Sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{S}
and let
\alpha\in\Sigma_{1}
. Let
\Sigma_{2}:=r_{\alpha}(\Sigma_{1})
. Then
B_{\Sigma_{2}}=B_{\Sigma_{1}}\cup\{\alpha\}
.

Proof.

By Part (1) of Theorem
3.2.9
, we deduce that there is some ordering of the elements of
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\}:=B_{\Sigma_{1}}
such that
r_{\beta_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ r_{\beta_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma_{1}
.
Therefore
r_{\alpha}\circ r_{\beta_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ r_{\beta_{1}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
is valid and equal to
\Sigma_{2}
.

By Part (2) we deduce that
B_{\Sigma_{2}}=B_{\Sigma_{1}}\cup\{\alpha\}
.
∎

3.3.
Visual interpretation of the set
B_{\Sigma}
and the set
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}

The set
B_{\Sigma}
gives us a way to describe a base in
\mathbb{S}
in terms of a set of right odd roots. This in itself is quite convenient, but we may further develop this set by giving it a visual interpretation.

4.
Tracking changes in the weight diagrams

4.1.
Applying a single right odd reflection

Definition 4.1.1
.

•

Given
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and
\alpha\in\Sigma
, we say that
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}

changes
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
if
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}\neq D_{\lambda}^{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
.

•

We say that
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}

changes the weight diagram of
\lambda
if there exists
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\alpha\in\Sigma
and
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\neq\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
.

In other words,
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
changes the weight diagram of
\lambda
iff there exists
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}\neq D_{\lambda}^{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
.

We will use the following straightforward observations, the first of them being just
Remark

2.3.10
:

Lemma 4.1.2
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, and let
1\leq i\leq m
,
1\leq j\leq n
.

(1)

Assume that the application
r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
is valid, i.e.
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma
. Denote:
p:=\left(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\right)_{i}
.

Then the odd reflection
r_{i,j}
changes the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
iff

p=\left(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\right)_{i}=-\left(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\right)_{-j}.

In that case, the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{r_{i,j}.\Sigma}
is given by moving a
\times
symbol in the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
from position
p
to the position
p+1
.

(2)

Assume the composition
r_{i,j+1}r_{i,j}(\Sigma)
is valid and
r_{i,j}
changes the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
.

Then the odd reflection
r_{i,j+1}
changes the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{r_{i,j}(\Sigma)}
if and only if

-\left(\overline{\lambda}_{r_{i,j}.\Sigma}\right)_{-(j+1)}=p+1.

Proof.

The first two statements follow from the fact that an odd reflection
r_{\alpha}
changes the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
for any
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
,
\alpha\in\Sigma
iff
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}|\alpha)=0
, and in that case,
\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\alpha}.\Sigma}=\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}+\alpha
.

The last statement follows from the fact that

\overline{\lambda}_{r_{i,j}.\Sigma}=\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}+\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
so
\left(\overline{\lambda}_{r_{i,j}.\Sigma}\right)_{i}=p+1
.

∎

Remark 4.1.3
.

In particular, in the setting of (

),
r_{i,j+1}
changes the diagram
D_{\lambda}^{r_{i,j}(\Sigma)}
iff
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}(p+1)
has either a
<
or a
\times
symbol.

Recall from Lemma

that any
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
can be obtained from the base
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
by applying several right odd reflections, and
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
is obtained from
\overline{\lambda}
by adding several right odd roots
\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}
for some

. Together with

, this implies:

Corollary 4.1.4
.

The diagram
D_{\lambda}^{\Sigma}
is obtained from

by moving several (perhaps zero)

symbols several positions to the right.

Accordingly,
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})_{i}\geq\overline{\lambda}_{i}
and
-(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})_{-j}\geq-(\overline{\lambda}_{-j})
for any

.

Proposition 4.1.5
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and let
\alpha
be a right odd root. For any pair
\Sigma^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\alpha\in\Sigma^{\prime}\cap\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
, we have:

\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\neq\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\prime}}\implies\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}\neq\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}.

In other words, if the odd reflection
r_{\alpha}
changes the weight diagram
D^{\Sigma}_{\lambda}
for some
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, then it does so for every
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
with
\alpha\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}
.

Recall that by
Lemma

4.1.2
, for any
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and any
\beta\in\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
, we have:

\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\beta}\Sigma}=\begin{cases}\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}&\text{ if }(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}|\beta)\neq 0\\
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}+\beta&\text{ if }(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}|\beta)=0\end{cases}

Thus
\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}\neq\overline{\lambda}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
iff
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma}|\beta)=0
.
Now, let
\Sigma^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
. We need to show that for any
\alpha=\varepsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}\in\Sigma^{\prime}\cap\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}
, the scalars

(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}|\beta)=(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}})_{i}+(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}})_{-j},\;\;(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}|\beta)=(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}})_{i}+(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}})_{-j}

are either both zero or both non-zero.

Recall from Theorem
3.2.9
that
\Sigma^{\prime}
and
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
can be obtained by performing a sequence of right odd reflections on
\Sigma^{\text{dist}}
; we may write
\Sigma^{\prime}=r_{\beta_{1}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\beta_{k}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
where
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\}=B_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
, and
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}=r_{\gamma_{1}}\circ\dots\circ r_{\gamma_{l}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
where
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{l}\}=B_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}
.

Thus the inequality
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}|\alpha)\neq(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}|\alpha)
may occur only if there was some
\beta\in B_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\triangle B_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}
(here
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference) such that
(\beta|\alpha)\neq 0
. Yet for
\beta=\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}-\delta_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{R}
, to have
(\beta|\alpha)\neq 0
we must have either

or

which means that either
\beta<\alpha
or
\beta>\alpha
.

By the definition of
B_{\Sigma}
in Theorem

, all the elements
\beta\in\mathcal{R},\beta<\alpha
must appear in both sets
B_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
and
B_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}
, while by the same theorem elements
\beta\in\mathcal{R},\beta>\alpha
appear neither in
B_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
nor in
B_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}
. This shows that
(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}|\alpha)\neq(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}|\alpha)
and the statement is proved.

∎

From the proof of

, we also obtain:

.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and let

. Then for any

such that

(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}|\beta)=0\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}|\beta)=0

4.2.
The arrow diagram

Definition 4.2.1
.

Given
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
with
\overline{\lambda}:=\lambda+\rho_{\Sigma^{\text{dist}}}
, we construct the
arrow diagram of
\lambda
by considering the weight diagram
D_{\lambda}
together with a set
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}
. The number
k_{i}
is defined recursively (from
k_{m}
to
k_{1}
) by

k_{i}:=\min\{c\in\mathbb{Z}\;|\;\forall 1\leq j\leq n,\forall i<i^{\prime}\leq m,\;c\geq\overline{\lambda}_{i},\,c\neq-\overline{\lambda}_{-j}\;\text{ and }\;c\neq k_{i^{\prime}}\}.

This set can be interpreted visually by iteratively drawing arrows above the diagram
D_{\lambda}
, each arrow beginning in a position with a symbol
>
or
\times
, and ending in a symbol
\circ
or
>
. At the
i
-th step of the process (
i=0,\ldots,m-1
), we draw an arrow from the position
\overline{\lambda}_{m-i}
to the first position to the right of
\overline{\lambda}_{m-i}
that does not contain a symbol
<
nor a symbol
\times
, nor is the end of a previously drawn arrow. The endpoint of the arrow originating from
\overline{\lambda}_{m-i}
is called
k_{m-i}
. We will refer to this arrow as the
i
-th arrow in the diagram
D_{\lambda}
.

Thus the arrows are drawn from left to right and numbered from right to left.

Additionally, we will use the following notation:

Definition 4.2.2
.

For
1\leq i\leq m
we denote

M_{i}:=\max\{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}|-\overline{\lambda}_{-j}<k_{i}\}.

The number
M_{i}
is the index
j
such that
-\overline{\lambda}_{-j}
is the position closest to the left of
k_{i}
such that
D_{\lambda}(\overline{\lambda}_{-j})\in\{\times,<\}
.
Another way to interpret
M_{i}
is as the number of the symbols
<
and
\times
that appear in
D_{\lambda}
to the left of the position
k_{i}
.

Remark 4.2.3
.

We remind the reader that the similar treatment of the symbols
\times
and
<
(respectively,
\times
and
>
) is due to the fact that the symbol
\times
is in fact a combination of the symbols
>
and
<
.

In order to proceed with the description of the set of right odd roots that change the diagram of

For any

Denote

Theorem 4.2.6
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
. For

we have:

(1)

For every
1\leq j<n
, we have
-\overline{\lambda}^{i}_{-j}<-\overline{\lambda}^{i}_{-(j+1)}
.

(2)

For
\overline{\lambda}_{i}=\overline{\lambda}^{i}_{i}\leq p<k_{i}

(3)

For every

(here we set

(4)

For every

, the odd reflection

changes the diagram of

if and only if

M_{i}+1-(k_{i}-\overline{\lambda}_{i})\leq j\leq M_{i}.

Note that the first statement means that for all

, we have:

for

. The last statement implies that
r_{i,j}
changes the diagram of

only if

(that is, only if the
i
-th arrow in

has distinct beginning and endpoint).

We prove the statements (

)-(

) for

.

For the base case consider

(

1
)-(

Step:

Proposition 5.2.12

6.
Counterexample and Correction to Tail Conjecture

The following section is intended as a response to
[
GH23
, Conjecture 6.3.1]
.

6.1.
Iso-sets

Definition 6.1.1
.

We call
S\subset\Delta_{1}
an iso-set if
S
forms a basis of an isotropic subspace in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, i.e. if
S
is linearly independent and
(S|S)=0
.

Remark 6.1.2
.

Let
S
be a subset
S\subset\Delta_{1}
such that
(\alpha|\beta)=0
for any
\alpha,\beta\in S
. The following conditions are equivalent:

•

The set
S
is an iso-set.

•

The set
S
is linearly independent in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

•

\forall\alpha\in S,\;\;-\alpha\notin S
.

•

There exists a base
\Sigma
(not necessarily in
\mathbb{S}
) such that
S\subset\Sigma
.

Remark 6.1.3
.

Let
S
be an iso-set. Then any
\alpha\in S
may be replaced by
-\alpha
, and we again obtain an iso-set of the same size as
S
: indeed, every
\beta\in S
satisfied:
(\beta|\alpha)=0
, so
(\pm\beta|\pm\alpha)=0
.

In particular, we may define:

Definition 6.1.4
.

Given an iso-set
S
, we may define the iso-set
||S||\subset\mathcal{R}
such that:

•

\forall\alpha\in S
either
\alpha\in||S||
or
-\alpha\in||S||
,

•

\forall\beta\in||S||
, exactly one of the roots
\pm\beta
belongs to
S
.

The following lemma is obvious:

Lemma 6.1.5
.

For any iso-set
S
,
\sharp S=\sharp||S||
.

Remark 6.1.6
.

In terms of the rectangular visualization of
\mathcal{R}
, an iso-set
S\subset\mathcal{R}
is any subset of
\mathcal{R}
where no two elements lie in the same ”row” or ”column” of the rectangle.

In particular, every incomparable set
S\subset\mathcal{R}
is an iso-set.

Example 6.1.7
.

In this example we consider the superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(2|2)
.

Let
S_{1}=\{\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{2}\}
. The set
S_{1}
is an iso-set, and
S_{1}\subset\mathcal{R}
. Further, we may define a base
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
S_{1}\subset\Sigma
: we take
\Sigma
to be the base given by the word
\varepsilon\delta\varepsilon\delta
. In fact, we have:
S_{1}=\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
.

Iso-sets need not be subsets of
\mathcal{R}
.
Consider
S_{2}=\{\delta_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\}\subset\Sigma
which is again a subset of the same base
\Sigma
as above. This is obviously an iso-set. Notice that
\delta_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
is not a right odd root, as such
S_{1}\not\subset\mathcal{R}
.

Finally, we may consider iso-sets that can not be embedded into any
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
(although this section focuses on iso-sets that can be embedded into such bases). For example if we consider the base

\Sigma=\{\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},-\varepsilon_{1}+\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}\}
(here
\Sigma=r_{\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{1}}\circ s_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}(\Sigma^{\text{dist}})
). Take
S_{3}=\{\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}\}
. Notice that
S_{3}
is an iso-set but
\varepsilon_{2}-\delta_{1}<\varepsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}
as such
S_{3}
by Lemma
3.2.4
does not correspond to a base in
\mathbb{S}
.

Let
\nu\in\Lambda_{m|n}
.

Definition 6.1.8
.

We define
s(\nu)
to be the cardinality of the largest iso-set that can be embedded into some
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and is orthogonal to
\nu
with respect to the pairing
(-|-)
.

It is not immediately obvious how to compute
s(\nu)
(as one may need to consider every single base). The following lemma simplifies this problem by implying one may consider only iso-sets composed of right odd roots.

Lemma 6.1.9
.

Let
S
be an iso-set. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a base
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
S\subset\Sigma
.

(2)

The set
||S||
can be embedded into some
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
.

(3)

The set
||S||
is incomparable.

Proof.

We first note that (2) and (3) are equivalent by Lemma
3.2.4
: by this Lemma, there is a bijection
\Sigma\longmapsto\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}
between bases
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and sets of incomparable right odd roots.

\lambda
.

Definition 6.5.1
.

For any
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
, denote by

C_{\lambda}:=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,c_{\lambda}(\alpha)=1\}

the set of right odd roots changing the diagram of
\lambda
.

Lemma 6.5.2
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
, and consider its CTD
c_{\lambda}
. Let
S\subset C_{\lambda}
be an incomparable set, and let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{R}=S
(see Lemma
3.2.4
)

Then
s(\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})\geq\sharp S
.

Proof.

Recall that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
,

c_{\lambda}(\alpha)=1\;\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\;\forall\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}\;\text{ such that }\alpha\in\Sigma,\;(\alpha|\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})=0.

As such it is immediately obvious that for any
\alpha\in S
,
(\alpha|\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})=0
. Since
S
is an incomparable set, we conclude that
s(\lambda_{\Sigma})\geq\sharp S
.
∎

The next corollary gives the first explicit way of computing
longtail(\lambda)
: it states that
longtail(\lambda)
is the size of the maximal incomparable subset of
\mathcal{R}
all of whose elements change the diagram of
\lambda
.

Corollary 6.5.3
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
. We have:

(4)

longtail(\lambda)=\max_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}}\sharp\Sigma\cap C_{\lambda}

Proof.

By
Lemma

6.5.2
, any incomparable subset of
C_{\lambda}
is of cardinality at most
longtail(\lambda)
. This shows that the left hand side of (
4
) is greater or equal to the right hand side.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}
, and let
S\subset\mathcal{R}
be an incomparable set of maximal cardinality such that
(S|\overline{\lambda}_{\Sigma})=0
. Then by Proposition
5.1.6
, every
\alpha\in S
has
c_{\lambda}(\alpha)=1
and therefore
S
is an incomparable subset of
C_{\lambda}
.

Finally, any incomparable subset
S\subset C_{\lambda}
can be written as
S=\Sigma^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}
for some
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
, proving that

\sharp S\leq\max_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}}\sharp\Sigma\cap C_{\lambda}

This proves that the right hand side in (
4
) is greater or equal to the left hand side, completing the proof.
∎

6.6.
Corrected Tail Conjecture: description in terms of arrow and cap diagrams

In this subsection, we will give explicit formulas for

in terms of arrow and cap diagrams of

Definition 6.6.1

Lemma 6.6.8

. Then

\sharp\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,|\,\overline{\lambda}_{i}\leq r<k_{i}\}=\sharp\{i\in\{1,\dots,a\}\,|\,\times_{i}\leq r<c_{i}\}

In other words, the number of arrows going over position
r
is the number of caps going over position
r
.

Proof.

Denote:

A:=\{\times_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq a,\,\times_{i}\leq r\},\;\;A^{\prime}:=\{\overline{\lambda}_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\,\overline{\lambda}_{i}\leq r\}

and

E:=\{c_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq a,\,c_{i}\leq r\},\;\;E^{\prime}:=\{k_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\,k_{i}\leq r\}.

The number of caps going over position
r
is

\sharp\{i\,|\,\times_{i}\leq r<c_{i}\}=\sharp A-\sharp E

(we consider the number of caps starting to the left of
r
or at
r
and subtract the number of caps ending to the left of
r
or at
r
).

The number of arrows going over position
r
is

\sharp\{i\,|\,\overline{\lambda}_{i}\leq r\leq k_{i}\}=\sharp A^{\prime}-\sharp E^{\prime}

(we consider the number of arrows starting to the left of
r
or at
r
and subtract the number of arrows ending to the left of
r
or at
r
).

Clearly we have:
A\subset A^{\prime}
. Recall from Lemma
4.2.19
that,

\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{a}\}=\{k_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\;D_{\lambda}(k_{i})=\circ\}

Hence
E\subset E^{\prime}
and

E^{\prime}\setminus E=\{k_{i}\leq r\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\;D_{\lambda}(k_{i})\text{ is }>\}=\{\overline{\lambda}_{i}\leq r\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\;D_{\lambda}(\overline{\lambda}_{i})\text{ is }>\}

The last equality is due to the fact that every symbol
>
in
D_{\lambda}
is the endpoint of some arrow. On the other hand,

\{\overline{\lambda}_{i}<r\,|\,1\leq i\leq n,\;D_{\lambda}(\overline{\lambda}_{i})\text{ is }>\}=A^{\prime}\setminus A

so
E^{\prime}\setminus E=A^{\prime}\setminus A
. This proves that
\sharp A-\sharp E=\sharp A^{\prime}-\sharp E^{\prime}
as required.
∎

The next statement is a direct corollary of
Theorem

6.6.10
:

Corollary 6.6.12
.

Let
\lambda\in{\Lambda^{+}_{m|n}}
and let
a:=atyp(\lambda)
. Let
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{a}\}
be the right endpoints of the caps in the cap diagram of
\lambda
. Then

longtail(\lambda)=\max_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\sharp\{i\in\{1,\dots,a\}|\times_{i}\leq r<c_{i}\}

That is to say,
longtail(\lambda)
is given by the maximal number of caps going over (strictly above) a single point in the cap diagram of
\lambda
.
