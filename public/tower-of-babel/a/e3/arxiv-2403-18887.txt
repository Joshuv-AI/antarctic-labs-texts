(2+1)D topological phases with RT symmetry: many-body invariant, classification, and higher order edge modes
Ryohei Kobayashi, Yuxuan Zhang, Yan-Qi Wang, Maissam Barkeshli

Abstract
It is common in condensed matter systems for reflection ( R R ) and time-reversal ( T T ) symmetry to both be broken while the combination R ​ T RT is preserved. In this paper we study invariants that arise due to R ​ T RT symmetry. We consider many-body systems of interacting fermions with fermionic symmetry groups G f = ℤ 2 f × ℤ 2 R ​ T G_{f}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{RT} , U ​ ( 1 ) f ⋊ ℤ 2 R ​ T U(1)^{f}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{RT} , and U ​ ( 1 ) f × ℤ 2 R ​ T U(1)^{f}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{RT} . We show that (2+1)D invertible fermionic topological phases with these symmetries have a ℤ × ℤ 8 \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{8} , ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} , and ℤ 2 × ℤ 4 \mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4} classification, respectively, which we compute using the framework of G G -crossed braided tensor categories. We provide a many-body R ​ T RT invariant in terms of a tripartite entanglement measure, and which we show can be understood using an edge conformal field theory computation in terms of vertex states. For G f = U ​ ( 1 ) f ⋊ ℤ 2 R ​ T G_{f}=U(1)^{f}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{RT} , which applies to charged fermions in a magnetic field, the non-trivial value of the ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} invariant requires strong interactions. For symmetry-preserving boundaries, the phases are distinguished by zero modes at the intersection of the reflection axis and the boundary. Additional invariants arise in the presence of translation or rotation symmetry.

A central result of this paper is that we can derive the properties of

Below we sketch the calculation; additional details are presented in Appendix

We employ the “cut-and-glue” argument
Qi et al. 2012

We then “glue” the cut back by turning on the interaction connecting the subsystems along the interface. This gluing interaction is regarded as the perturbation gapping out the counter-propagating CFT at the cut, and thought to correspond to the conformally invariant boundary condition of the CFT
Calabrese and Cardy 2007

If we had a bipartition of the 2d system instead of the tripartition, we would have been able to explicitly represent the boundary state of edge CFT at the cut using the Ishibashi state
Ishibashi 1989

However, one can still express the CFT state at the tripartition cut in terms of the path integral of the CFT

2\beta

. By solving these consistency conditions and equivalence relations, we can derive the classification given in Table
1
, as detailed in Appendix
II
.

Higher order edge modes
. If
c_{-}=C=0
, the boundary does not generically have dispersing edge modes with the symmetries in Table
1
, unless additional symmetries are present. If the boundary respects the
RT
symmetry, there will be an edge zero mode at the intersection point between the reflection axis and the boundary of the system. Thus such phases are analogous to “higher order topological phases”
Benalcazar et al. 2019
except the zero modes are not at corners. In the case of the
\nu=1
phase with
G_{f}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{f}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{RT}
, the intersection point localizes a Majorana zero mode. We can understand this in more detail for the model Hamiltonian
H_{2}
introduced above. The low energy edge theory consists of counterpropagating Majorana fermions. A mass term
m(x)
gaps the modes, but
RT
symmetry requires
m(x)=-m(-x)

Wang and Moore 2019
. The domain wall in the mass leads to a Majorana zero mode at
x=0

Read and Green 2000
.

Including translation and rotation symmetry
. In the presence of translation or rotation symmetry, the system acquires inequivalent reflection axes, and we can decorate each inequivalent reflection axis with a (1+1)D invertible phase. Therefore, we expect to get additional invariants, one for each inequivalent reflection axis. For example, for a square lattice, we have four inequivalent reflection axes, one of which is a glide reflection axis, and so we expect four inequivalent
RT
invariants. We leave a systematic classification for future work.

II.1
Generalities of fermionic symmetry groups

Here we introduce generic terminologies about fermionic symmetry groups. In general, the (0-form) symmetry group of a fermionic system
G_{f}
has the structure of the central extension of a bosonic symmetry group
G_{b}
by
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{f}
,

1\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{f}\rightarrow G_{f}\rightarrow G_{b}\rightarrow 1.

(21)

This central extension is specified by a
2
-cocycle
\omega_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{2}(G_{b},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
. More specifically, one can denote a general element in
G_{f}
as a pair
({\bm{g}},a)
, where
{\bm{g}}\in G_{b}
and
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{f}=\{0,1\}
, then the group law in
G_{f}
reads

({\bf g}_{1},a_{1})({\bf g}_{2},a_{2})=({\bf g}_{1}{\bf g}_{2},a_{1}+a_{2}+\omega_{2}({\bf g}_{1},{\bf g}_{2})).

(22)

We also define the homomorphism
s_{1}:G_{b}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. If
{\bf g}
is unitary,
s_{1}({\bf g})=0
, if
{\bf g}
is antiunitary,
s_{1}({\bf g})=1
.

II.2
Crystalline equivalence principle

In general, for a clean 2D system without any defects defined on the infinite plane, the spatial elements in
G_{b}
are specified by a map
(\vec{R},\rho_{s}):G_{b}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rtimes{O}(2)
where
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rtimes{O}(2)
is the group of continuous translations (
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
), rotations (
{SO}(2)
part of
{O}(2)
), and reflections (
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
part of
{O}(2)
) in two dimensions. Note that, the map
\vec{R}:G_{b}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and
\rho_{s}:G_{b}\rightarrow{O}(2)
. Having a non-trivial map
(\vec{R},\rho_{s})
implies that
G_{b}
acts as a spatial symmetry, and we include it as part of the symmetry data of the system.

The fermionic crystalline equivalence principle (fCEP) states that the classification of invertible fermionic topological phases with spatial symmetry
G_{f}
defined by the data
(G_{b},s_{1},\omega_{2},(\vec{R},\rho_{s}))
is one-to-one correspondence with the classification of invertible fermionic topological phases with an effective internal symmetry
G_{f}^{\rm eff}
that has data
(G_{b},s_{1}^{\rm eff},{\omega_{2}}^{\rm eff})
where
G_{b}
acts trivially on space. The terms
s_{1}^{\rm eff}
,
\omega_{2}^{\rm eff}
are conjectured to be determined by
s_{1},\omega_{2},\rho_{s}
through the equations
Manjunath et al. 2023

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=s_{1}+w_{1},

(23a)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}+w_{2}+w_{1}(s_{1}+w_{1}),

(23b)

where
w_{1}=\rho_{s}^{*}{\rm w}_{1,r}
,
w_{2}=\rho_{s}^{*}{\rm w}_{2}
are obtained by pulling back the Stiefel-Whitney classes
{\rm w}_{1,r}
,
{\rm w}_{2}
that generate
{\mathcal{H}}^{1}({O}(2),{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
,
{\mathcal{H}}^{2}({SO}(2),{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
.

Let us consider the examples
G_{b}={U}(1)\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT},{U}(1)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}
. In these cases,
RT
can be viewed as an effective anti-unitary reflection symmetry, which implies that

are both non-trivial. Since there is no effective spatial rotation in

,
we have
w_{2}=0
.
Thus we have the effective data:

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=s_{1}+w_{1}=0,

(24)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}+w_{2}+w_{1}(s_{1}+w_{1})=\omega_{2}.

The above result

implies that
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}
is treated as a unitary internal

symmetry under fCEP, and internal symmetry

has the same group structure as

.

\hat{\bf g}\cup w_{2}
is even under charge conjugation, so twisted and untwisted coboundary is the same. So

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{4}=(-1)^{n_{2}(n_{2}+w_{2})+kw_{1}^{2}w_{2}}e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{4}d(\hat{\bf g}\cup w_{2})}(-1)^{d(kw_{2}\cup_{2}(w_{1}w_{2}))}

(37)

Then, this
\mathcal{O}_{4}
has a trivialization
d\nu_{3}=\mathcal{O}_{4}
only if
n_{2}=kw_{1}^{2},kw_{1}^{2}+w_{2}
. These two data actually specifies the same phase due to the equivalence relations
Barkeshli et al. 2022
. Then we can find a solution

\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}_{3}=e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{4}(w_{1}^{3}+\hat{\bf g}\cup w_{2})}(-1)^{kw_{2}\cup_{2}(w_{1}w_{2})}

(38)

Summarizing, we have a solution with fixed even
c_{-}=2k
, given by

\displaystyle(n_{1},n_{2},\nu_{3})=\left(0,kw_{1}^{2},\nu_{3}^{(0)}+\nu_{3}^{\prime}\right),

(39)

where
\nu^{\prime}_{3}\in\mathcal{H}^{3}(O(2),U(1))
.

Classification of invertible phases with generic
c_{-}
:

Now let us consider the case where
c_{-}
is a generic integer. In that case we have
n_{1}=c_{-}w_{1}
, implying that the defect of the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}
symmetry bounds a Majorana zero mode when
c_{-}
is odd
Manjunath et al. 2023
.
While we do not try to provide complete solutions to the consistency equations
(n_{2},\nu_{3})
when
c_{-}
is odd, one can still find the classification of the invertible phases. Since the Chern insulator is consistent with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}
symmetry conjugating
U(1)^{f}
, there is one non-trivial phase carrying
c_{-}=1
.
Meanwhile, the previous analysis with
c_{-}=0
implies that all the non-trivial invertible phase with
c_{-}=0
are the bosonic SPT phases classified by
\mathcal{H}^{3}(O(2),U(1))={\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. This implies that the classification is generated by the Chern insulator
{\mathbb{Z}}
and bosonic SPT phases
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, which together form the whole classification
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
The invertible phases are labeled by
(c_{-},C,\nu)
with
\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
the index characterizing the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
classification of the bosonic SPT.

II.3.3
G_{f}=U(1)^{f}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}

Here let us compute the classification for
G_{f}=U(1)^{f}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{RT}
which is equivalent to the internal symmetry
U(1)^{f}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
under fCEP, with
\omega_{2}=w_{2}
(in this section,
w_{1}
is a non-trivial element in
\mathcal{H}^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}_{2},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
, and
w_{2}=w_{2}(U(1))
). Note that
c_{-}
has to be integer since we have
U(1)^{f}
symmetry. Below, let us derive the classification of invertible phases by computing the consistency conditions.

First of all, we have

dn_{2}=n_{1}\omega_{2}+c_{-}\omega_{2}\cup_{1}\omega_{2}.

(40)

Note that
\omega_{2}\cup_{1}\omega_{2}=\mathrm{Sq}^{1}w_{2}=0
, so we get
dn_{2}=n_{1}\omega_{2}
. In order to have a solution, we must have
n_{1}=0
.
n_{2}
is then closed and
n_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{2}({U}(1)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2},{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
, which take the value of
aw_{1}^{2}+b\omega_{2}
,
a,b\in\{0,1\}
. Here,
b=0,1
actually corresponds to the redundant labels for the invertible phase due to the equivalence relations
Barkeshli et al. 2022
, so we only need to consider the case
n_{2}=0
or
n_{2}=w_{1}^{2}
below.

Since
n_{1}=0
, the
\mathcal{O}_{4}
class is given by

{\mathcal{O}}_{4}=(-1)^{n_{2}\cup(n_{2}+\omega_{2})}e^{i\pi\frac{c_{-}}{4}{\mathcal{P}}(\omega_{2})}.

(41)

Let us first consider the case
c_{-}=0
and find solutions for
d\nu_{3}=\mathcal{O}_{4}
.

When
n_{2}=0
, we have
d\nu_{3}=0
and
\nu_{3}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{3}({U}(1)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2},{U}(1))={\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
labels the bosonic invertible phases, with the
{\mathbb{Z}}
from the integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE), one
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
generated by
w_{1}^{3}/2
, and the other
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
generated by
w_{1}\omega_{2}/2
. However, since for
(n_{2},\nu_{3})
we have the equivalence of phases
(0,0)\sim(db,b\omega_{2}/2)
, by taking
b=w_{1}
, one can see that
(n_{2},\nu_{3})=(0,w_{1}\omega_{2}/2)
is a trivial phase
Barkeshli et al. 2022
. This implies that the phases with
c_{-}=0,n_{2}=0
is labeled by
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

When
n_{2}=w_{1}^{2}
, since

\frac{1}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}+\omega_{2})=\frac{1}{2}w_{1}^{2}(w_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2})=\frac{dw_{1}}{4}\big(\frac{dw_{1}}{2}+\omega_{2}\big)=\frac{1}{4}d\big(w_{1}\frac{dw_{1}}{2}+w_{1}\omega_{2}\big),

(42)

then we have the solution of
\nu_{3}
as

\displaystyle\nu_{3}=\frac{1}{4}\big(w_{1}\frac{dw_{1}}{2}+w_{1}\omega_{2}\big)+\nu_{3}^{\prime}

(43)

with
\nu^{\prime}_{3}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{3}({U}(1)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2},{U}(1))
.

To obtain the classification of invertible phases with
c_{-}=0
, we further notice that the stacking rule of the phases with the above
(n_{2},\nu_{3})
can be non-trivial. Indeed, the stacking rule of the phases with
n_{2}=w_{1}^{2}
is found to be

\displaystyle(w_{1}^{2},\frac{1}{4}\big(w_{1}\frac{dw_{1}}{2}+w_{1}\omega_{2}\big))\times(w_{1}^{2},\frac{1}{4}\big(w_{1}\frac{dw_{1}}{2}+w_{1}\omega_{2}\big))=(0,\frac{1}{2}w_{1}^{3}),

(44)

which implies that two copies of the fermionic SPT phases with
n_{2}=w_{1}^{2}
is equivalent to a bosonic SPT phase. The generic formula for the stacking rule of fermionic invertible phases is found in
Barkeshli et al. 2022
.
This implies that for the phases with
n_{2}=0
labeled by
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
classification gets extended by the fermionic phase with
n_{2}=w_{1}^{2}
.
Therefore, the classification with
c_{-}=0
is given by
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
.

Additionally, we have the phase with
c_{-}=1
on the top of the
c_{-}=0
classification, so the whole classification of invertible phases is given by
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
, we have one
{\mathbb{Z}}
from
c_{-}
, one
{\mathbb{Z}}
from bosonic IQHE, and the other
{\mathbb{Z}}_{4}
comes from a fermionic SPT phase.

III
Numerical methods

III.1
Standard numerical procedure for entanglement Hamiltonian

In this section we discuss how to derive the entanglement Hamiltonian and compute the RT invariant from a free-fermion tight-binding Hamiltonian based on Ref.
Shiozaki et al. 2018
;
Cheong and Henley 2003
;
Borchmann et al. 2014
;
Peschel 2003
;
Peschel and Eisler 2009
;
Shapourian et al. 2017b
. Consider the tight-binding Hamiltonian given by:

H=\sum_{<ij>}-t_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}+\Delta_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}^{\dagger}+{\rm H.c.}.

(45)

where the fermionic operators satisfy the anticommutation relation:
\{f_{i},f_{j}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{ij}
. The reduced density matrix of such Hamiltonians in the subsystem
\text{A}\cup\text{B}
of the whole system
\text{E}={\rm A}\cup{\rm B}\cup{\rm C}
can be expressed in terms of a quadratic form:

\rho_{\text{AB}}={\rm Tr}_{\rm{C}}(\rho)=\frac{e^{-\hat{H}_{\text{AB}}}}{\mathcal{Z}},

(46)

where the entanglement Hamiltonian is defined as:

\hat{H}_{\text{AB}}=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}^{1}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}+h^{2}_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}^{\dagger}+{\rm H.c.},

(47)

and
{\mathcal{Z}}
is the normalization factor. We denote the number of the degrees of freedom (the product of the number of sites and the number of internal degrees of freedom per site) as
N_{\text{AB}}
.

The Hamiltonian described above can be expressed in the Nambu basis as:

\hat{H}_{\text{AB}}=\psi^{\dagger}{\mathcal{H}}\psi,

(48)

where
\psi=(f_{1},...,f_{N_{\text{AB}}},f^{\dagger}_{1},...f^{\dagger}_{N_{\text{AB}}})^{T}
. The matrix
{\mathcal{H}}
exhibits particle-hole symmetry, enabling it to be diagonalized as
{\mathcal{H}}=WDW^{\dagger}
, where
D={\rm diag}(E,-E)
,
E={\rm diag}(E_{1},\cdots,E_{N_{\text{AB}}})
with
E_{i}>0
,
\forall i
.
W
is the eigenvectors.
We can further define the correlation matrix
G
as:

G=\begin{pmatrix}\langle f_{i}f_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle&\langle f_{i}f_{j}\rangle\\
\langle f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}^{\dagger}\rangle&\langle f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}[1-C^{T}]_{ij}&[F^{\dagger}]_{ij}\\
F_{ij}&C_{ij}\end{pmatrix}

(49)

One can demonstrate that
G
can be represented as
G=WG_{c}W^{\dagger}
, where
G_{c}={\rm diag}(1-{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{F}})
, with
{\mathcal{F}}={\rm diag}(n_{f}(E_{1})...n_{f}(E_{N}))
with
n_{f}(x)=1/(1+e^{x/(k_{B}T)})
. In our numerics, we take the 0 temperature limit which fills every negative energy state.

The reduced density matrix can be formulated in the coherent state basis as follows:

\rho_{\text{AB}}=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}}\int d[\xi]d[\bar{\xi}]\exp\bigg{[}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in I}(\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})S_{ij}(\xi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}\bigg]\ket{\{ \xi_j \}_{j \in\text{AB}}}\bra{\{ \bar\xi_j \}_{j \in\text{AB}}},

(50)

where
(\xi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}
denotes the column vector of
(\xi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})
. The
S_{ij}
matrix is given by:

S_{ij}=\Gamma_{ij}+[i\sigma_{y}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N_{\text{AB}}\times N_{\text{AB}}}]_{ij}.

(51)

where
\sigma_{y}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right)
is the second Pauli matrix that mixes
\xi_{i}
and
\bar{\xi}_{i}
. The inverse of the matrix
\Gamma
can be formulated using correlators:

[\Gamma^{-1}]_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}[F^{\dagger}]_{ij}&[C^{T}]_{ij}\\
-C_{ij}&F_{ij}\end{pmatrix},

(52)

which is a
2N_{\text{AB}}\times 2N_{\text{AB}}
matrix.
The normalization factor
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}
can be evaluated as
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}={\rm Pf}[\Gamma]
.

III.2
Partial time reversal

We now proceed to calculate the partial time-reversal transformation of
\rho_{\text{AB}}
.
Consider the case where the time reversal
T=\mathcal{K}
. In this case, the partial time reversal of
\rho_{\text{AB}}
is equivalent to the partial transpose
\rho_{\text{AB}}^{T_{\text{A}}}
.
We begin by defining
\rho_{\text{AB}}^{T_{\text{A}}}
while specifying which region each site belongs to:

\rho_{\text{AB}}^{T_{\text{A}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}}\int d[\xi]d[\bar{\xi}]\exp\bigg{[}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\text{AB}}(\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})S_{ij}(\xi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}\bigg]\times\ket{\{ i \bar\xi_j \}_{j \in A}, \{\xi_j \}_{j \in B}}\bra{\{ i \xi_j \}_{j \in A}, \{ \bar\xi_j \}_{j \in B}}.

(53)

This transformation can be incorporated through the redefinition of the
S_{ij}
matrix after introducing the new variables
{\bf\xi}=U_{S}\chi
, yielding:

\rho_{\text{AB}}^{T_{\text{A}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}}\int d[\chi]d[\bar{\chi}]\exp\bigg{[}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in I}(\chi_{i},\bar{\chi}_{i})S_{ij}^{T_{\text{A}}}(\chi_{j},\bar{\chi}_{j})^{T}\bigg]\ket{\{ \chi_j\}_{j \in{\text{AB}}}}\bra{\{ \bar\chi_j\}_{j \in\text{AB}} },

(54)

where
S^{T_{\text{A}}}:=U_{S}^{T}SU_{S}
and
U_{S}=U_{S}^{T}
act as a permutation matrix defined by:

U_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-i{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{A}}&0\\
0&{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{B}}&0&0\\
-i{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{A}}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{B}}\end{pmatrix},

(55)

in the
(\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\text{A}},\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in\text{B}},\{\bar{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\text{A}},\{\bar{\xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\text{B}})
basis. Here,
{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{A}}
and
{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{B}}
are identity matrices acting on regions A and B, respectively.

III.3
Partial reflection

In a manner similar to the partial time reversal, the partial reflection can also be incorporated by redefining the
S
matrix. We apply the combined
RT
symmetry action on the
S
matrix as
S^{RT_{\text{A}}}:=U_{R}^{T}S^{T_{\text{A}}}U_{R}
, where

U_{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{\text{A}}&0&0&0\\
0&{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{B}}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathcal{R}_{\text{A}}^{*}&0\\
0&0&0&{\mathbb{I}}_{\text{B}}\end{pmatrix},

(56)

and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{A}}
denotes the reflection matrix acting within region A. For any
j
labeled by its position
x,y
and layer index
s
,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{A}}
is defined by its action
\mathcal{R}_{\text{A}}\xi_{x,y,s}=\xi_{-x,y,s}
.

III.4
Calculation of
Z_{RT}

To compute the topological invariant
Z_{RT}=\text{Tr}(\Xi)
, where
\Xi:=\rho_{\text{AB}}R_{\rm A}\rho_{\text{AB}}^{T_{\text{A}}}R_{\rm A}^{\dagger}
, we begin by multiplying the two density matrices and then performing a partial Gaussian integral as follows:

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}^{2}}\int d[\chi]d[\bar{\chi}]d[\xi]d[\bar{\xi}]e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in{\text{AB}}}(\chi_{i},\bar{\chi}_{i})S_{ij}(\chi_{j},\bar{\chi}_{j})^{T}}e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\text{AB}}(\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})S_{ij}^{RT_{\text{A}}}(\xi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}}\ket{\{ \chi_j\}_{j \in\text{AB}}}\bra{\{ \bar\chi_j \}_{j \in\text{AB}}}\ket{\{ \xi_j\}_{j \in\text{AB}}}\bra{\{ \bar\xi_j \}_{j \in\text{AB}}}

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{Q}}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho}^{2}}\int d[\chi]d[\bar{\xi}]e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\text{AB}}(\chi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})\tilde{S}_{ij}(\chi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}}\ket{\{\chi_j\}_{j \in\text{AB}}}\bra{\{\bar\xi_j\}_{j \in\text{AB}}},

where
\mathscr{Q}
is a normalization factor. The partial Gaussian integral can be further detailed as:

\mathscr{Q}e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\text{AB}}(\chi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})\tilde{S}_{ij}(\chi_{j},\bar{\xi}_{j})^{T}}=\int[d\bar{\chi}][d\xi]e^{\sum_{i,j\in I}(\chi_{i},\bar{\chi}_{i},\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})M_{ij}(\chi_{i}\bar{\chi}_{i},\xi_{i},\bar{\xi}_{i})^{T}}

(58)

with the kernel
M
defined as:

M=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}S&K\\
-K^{T}&S^{T_{A}}\end{pmatrix}=:\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|@{}c@{}|c@{}}\alpha_{1}&\quad\beta_{1}&\alpha_{2}\\
\hline\cr-\beta_{1}^{T}&\gamma&-\beta_{2}^{T}\\
\hline\cr\alpha_{3}&\beta_{2}&\alpha_{4}\end{array}\right),\quad K=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-{\mathbb{I}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(59)

The submatrix
K
arise from the inner product
\langle\{\bar{\chi}_{j}\}_{j\in I}|\{\xi_{j}\}_{j\in A}\rangle
. We decompose
M
into block matrices, with
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}\}
as
N_{\text{AB}}\times N_{\text{AB}}
matrices,
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
as
2N_{\text{AB}}\times N_{\text{AB}}
matrices, and
\gamma
as a
2N_{\text{AB}}\times 2N_{\text{AB}}
matrix. We also define
\alpha
and
\beta
from these block matrices:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}\end{pmatrix},\quad\beta=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(60)

where
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
are square matrices with the same sizes.

After performing the partial Gaussian integral,
\tilde{S}_{ij}
can be expressed as:

\tilde{S}_{ij}=[\alpha+\beta(\gamma)^{-1}\beta^{T}]_{ij}.

(61)

We also calculate the normalization factor
\mathscr{Q}=\text{Pf}[\gamma]
. The new
\tilde{\Gamma}
matrix is defined using Eq. (
51
)

\tilde{\Gamma}_{ij}=\tilde{S}_{ij}-[i\sigma_{y}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{N_{\text{AB}}\times N_{\text{AB}}}]_{ij}.

(62)

Finally, we obtain the
RT
invariant
Z_{RT}
by performing the Gaussian integral:

Z_{RT}={\rm Tr}[\Xi]=\frac{{\rm Pf}[\gamma]{\rm Pf}[\tilde{\Gamma}]}{{\rm Pf}[\Gamma]^{2}},

(63)

which gives the numerical value for Eq. (1) in the main text.
