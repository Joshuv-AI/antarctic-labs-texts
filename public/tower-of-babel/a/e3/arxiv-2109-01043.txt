Sparsity of integral points on moduli spaces of varieties
Jordan S. Ellenberg, Brian Lawrence, and Akshay Venkatesh

Abstract
. Let X X be a quasi-projective variety over a number field, admitting (after passage to C \mathbb{C} ) a geometric variation of Hodge structure whose period mapping has zero-dimensional fibers. Then the integral points of X X are sparse : the number of such points of height ≤ B \leq B grows slower than any positive power of B B . For example, homogeneous integral polynomials in a fixed number of variables and degree, with discriminant divisible only by a fixed set of primes, are sparse when considered up to integral linear substitutions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

By “geometric variation of Hodge structure”
we mean a direct summand of a VHS arising from a smooth projective family over

1.1.
Discussion of the proof

The reduction of Theorem
1.1
to Theorem
1.2
is relatively formal, and so we will focus
on the latter Theorem here.

The idea of the proof goes back to ideas initiated in the work of Bombieri-Pila
[
3
]
, Heath-Brown
[
15
]

(and, independently and in a different context, Coppersmith
[
7
]
):
One can show that an excess of rational (or integral) points on any
X
as above must lie on the zero-loci
of some auxiliary functions, i.e., must be covered by a certain collection of divisors.
Then we iterate the process, replacing
X
by these divisors, and covering the points by codimension-
2
subvarieties, etc.; since we have almost no control over what these subvarieties look like, it is crucial to have, as in Bombieri-Pila and Heath-Brown, that the basic bounds are
uniform
in the ambient variety. See Remark
2.7

for a quick review of this method.

Unfortunately, this process is quite lossy when executed in dimensions larger than
1
.
The problem is the usual one in studying rational points on higher dimensional varieties: Even if a variety
has high degree, it may contain subvarieties of much lower degree, such as hyperplanes.
For this reason, there are very few situations where one can achieve
B^{\epsilon}
-type bounds (see e.g.
[
23
]
for an example
in a different context).

The key point of our strategy here is to use the fact that the bounds of
[
4
]
improve
under étale covers, which are plentiful under the conditions on
\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow X
we have imposed; these covers can be used to raise the degree of intermediate subvarieties. To deploy this in a way suitable for an iterative argument, we use
the global invariant cycle theorem of Hodge theory to construct a cover
\widetilde{X}\rightarrow X

which remains nontrivial when restricted to any subvariety of
X
. In some sense, this strategy generalizes the approach of
[
10
]
, which used étale covers to slightly improve on Heath-Brown’s bounds in case
X
is a non-rational curve (i.e. a curve with interesting étale covers); in the case
\dim X=1
, of course, the issue of restriction of covers to subvarieties becomes trivial.

In the end, the core part of the proof is quite short – the main idea is the induction on dimension laid out in §
4.2
, with the key induction step being provided by Lemma
4.2
. The reader may want to
skip directly to these, and refer back as necessary. One of the reasons for the length of the text is
the technicalities
involved in making various results uniform over all subvarieties of
X
.

One way of thinking about the strategy executed here is in terms of “profinite repulsion” between low-height integral points. For simplicity of exposition we take
K=\mathbb{Q}
for this paragraph. For any prime
p
, the embedding of
X(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])
into
X(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
induces a topology on the former set, and the methods of Bombieri-Pila and Heath-Brown rely on an argument that low-height points tend to repel each other in this topology. In the cases considered in this paper, there is an alternate profinite topology on
X^{\circ}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])
afforded by the map

X^{\circ}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])\to H^{1}(G{Q},\pi_{1}(X^{\circ}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}))

and our approach can be thought of as exploiting the fact that in this topology, too, the low-height
S
-integral points repel each other. The two profinite repulsion phenomena working in tandem is what allows us to get a better upper bound on the number of low-height points. To be more precise: to say that two points
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
of
X^{\circ}(\mathbb{Z}[1/S])
are close together in the alternate profinite topology is to say that there is some high-degree cover
\widetilde{X}^{\circ}\to X^{\circ}
, with
\widetilde{X}^{\circ}
irreducible, such that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
both lie in the image of
\widetilde{X}^{\circ}(\mathbb{Q})
. Then the method of Heath-Brown is used to control the low-height points of
\widetilde{X}^{\circ}(\mathbb{Q})
, which is aided by the fact that this new variety has very high degree.

In the course of writing this paper we learned of the paper
[
5
]
of Brunebarbe, which uses the same basic idea of increasing the positivity of line bundles by passing to étale covers, in the context of a VHS with finite period map, in order to prove uniform algebro-geometric assertions about covers of varieties carrying such a VHS. Indeed
Brunebarbe gives a more analytic (and therefore effective) proof of a result (
[
5
, Theorem 1.13]
) of the type of Lemma
4.1
, whereas
our argument uses general finiteness statements of algebraic geometry. As in our paper, a crucial input to
[
5
]

is the invariant cycle theorem of Hodge theory.

Finally, let us mention some potential refinements of the result.

•

The reader will observe that the
geometricity
of the variation of Hodge structure is,
in fact, barely used at all in our proof. It permits us to phrase
the argument in quite an explicit way, but it should
be possible to entirely avoid it.

•

Indeed, the whole apparatus of variations of Hodge structures is used simply to provide examples of varieties
X^{\circ}
with the property that the image of
\pi_{1}(W^{\circ{\text{an}}})\to\pi_{1}(X^{\circ{\text{an}}})
has infinite order for every subvariety
W^{\circ}
of
X^{\circ}
. We note that this is essentially the same as Kollár’s condition of
large fundamental group
from
[
20
]
.
For an
X^{\circ}
which possessed this property for some reason unrelated to Hodge structures, the arguments here should work just as well to control its rational points. For example, abelian varieties do not admit variations of Hodge structure but they do have large fundamental group, so the arguments of this paper should in principle imply sparseness of rational points; but for abelian varieties, we know this already, by the Mordell-Weil theorem.

•

One might ask what happens for varieties
X^{\circ}
carrying a variation of
mixed
Hodge structures; it seems plausible that a theorem like the one proved here will still hold.

•

It would be interesting to refine our statement
O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon})
to a more precise upper bound. To do this would, at the very least, require that the inexplicit dependence on degree in Broberg’s bounds be made explicit. Questions in this vein have been the topic of much recent progress; see, e.g., the recent result of Castryck, Cluckers, Dittman, and Nguyen
[
6
, Theorem 2]
, which gives bounds with explicit polynomial dependence on degree.
The quantitative approach of
[
5
]
will also likely be needed in order to get effective lower bounds for the degrees of covers that arise in the argument.

be a locally closed subvariety, and

.

If we choose two different choices of good integral model as above, there exists a finite set

such that any

-integral point for the first model is

-integral

Finally, we fix some other notation to be used:
For any field

and any

,
we denote by

the preimage of

.
If the map

, characterized by the asymptotic formula

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\pi:\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow X

is a projective smooth morphism of algebraic varieties
over a subfield
\kappa\subset\mathbb{C}
, with

X
smooth,
and let
\mathcal{T}
be the tangent bundle of

.
Let

\Phi
be the period map
associated to the variation of Hodge structure

\mathsf{V}=R^{i}\pi_{*}\mathbb{Q}
on
(X)^{{\text{an}}}
,

Then there is a morphism

(3)

g\colon\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}

of vector bundles
over
X
(everything defined over
\kappa
) such that
the derivative of the period map along a tangent vector

vanishes if and only if

is the zero map from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of standard facts in Hodge theory, in particular the algebraicity of the Gauss–Manin connection (see
[
18
]
and
[
19
, §1]

The

gives a variation of Hodge structure

on

As a variation of Hodge structure,

is a vector bundle,
equipped with a flat connection and a filtration by algebraic subbundles
F^{p}H_{\text{dR}}
.
These data determine the period map
\widetilde{X^{\circ{\text{an}}}}\rightarrow D
,
where

is a flag variety classifying filtrations
of a fixed vector space of dimension
\operatorname{rank}H_{\text{dR}}

by subspaces of dimensions
\operatorname{rank}F^{p}H_{\text{dR}}
.

For each step

of the filtration, the connection on

defines an

-linear map

g_{p}\colon F^{p}H_{\text{dR}}\otimes\mathcal{T}\rightarrow H_{\text{dR}}/F^{p}H_{\text{dR}},

varies in

.
Combining the maps

over all

This map

More precisely, at every point, the tangent space to

is identified with a subspace of

and with this identification,

gives the differential of the period map.

Finally, we note that

is defined over
\kappa
by
[
18
]
and
[
19
, §1]
.

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic zero.

Suppose that
Y
is a proper
F

,

Writing

\deg_{g^{*}\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{Y})=(\deg g)\deg_{\mathcal{L}}(Y).

We will apply this only when

By the projection formula we have

Now

is isomorphic to

Example 2.5
.

As an example of “bad” examples for part (a): take
a
d
-dimensional variety, and adjoin to it a large set of disjoint points;
this modification does not affect the degree, and shows the need for irreducibility or at least
equidimensionality. Similarly, consideration of
embedded points shows that “reduced” is also important.

As an example of the situation in part (c), consider the plane curve defined by
x^{2}+y^{2}=0
.
This is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
but not geometrically irreducible;
it only has one rational point
(0,0)
, which is contained in a zero-dimensional, geometrically irreducible subvariety.
More generally, suppose
F
is a number field, and consider “the affine line over
F
, with the origin reduced to a
\mathbb{Q}
-point” – that is,
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Q}+TF[T])
.
If

, this scheme is irreducible, but geometrically reducible, and its only

-point is the origin.
(Taking

recovers the original example.)

Proof.

The first assertion of (a) follows from
[
1

That assertion applies as formulated to “special positive cycles” over an algebraically closed field;

Remark 2.7
.

Because Theorem
2.6
is so crucial, particularly its dependence on degree,
we briefly outline
where it comes from, taking
K=\mathbb{Q}
to simplify notation; this is not needed in the remainder of the paper.

One chooses a large integer
k

and embeds
V\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{e-1}

via a basis of sections of
\Gamma(V,\mathcal{O}(1)^{\otimes k})
. In fact, we can and do choose from
\Gamma(\mathbb{P}^{M},\mathcal{O}(1)^{\otimes k})
a set of monomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{e}
of degree
k
in the
M+1
-variables which freely span
\Gamma(V,\mathcal{O}(1)^{\otimes k})
.

Choose a “good” prime
p
and examine a collection of
e
points
P_{i}\in V(\mathbb{Q})

which all reduce to the same point modulo
p
of
\mathbb{P}^{M}
, and whose height is at most
H
. Expressing each
P_{i}
in coprime integer coordinates,
we can speak of the evaluation
f_{i}(P_{j})\in\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Consider

\Delta:=\det\left[f_{i}(P_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq e}\in\mathbb{Z}

which measures the volume of the
e
-simplex in
\mathbb{Z}^{e}
spanned by the
P_{i}
and the origin.
On the one hand,

\Delta
is bounded by a constant multiple of
H^{ke}
.

On the other hand,
\Delta
is highly divisible by
p
,
because the values of
f_{j}
modulo power of
p
are highly constrained,
and therefore there are many relations (mod
p^{k}
) between rows of
\Delta
.
To see this more formally, fix
r\geq 1
and let
denote by
V(\mathbb{Z}_{p})_{0}
the subset of
V(\mathbb{Z}_{p})

consisting of points with a given reduction modulo
p
. Set

M_{r}:=\{\mbox{functions: $V(\mathbb{Z}_{p})_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}$}\}.

Each
f_{j}
gives an element of
M_{r}
, by evaluation and reduction modulo
p^{r}
.
For
r=1
, all these functions (for varying
j
) lie in a
\mathbb{Z}/p
-module of rank one: the constant functions.
For
r=2
, these functions
depend only on the “constant term” and “derivative” of
f_{j}
,
and thereby
lie in a
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}
-submodule of
M_{2}
of rank
n+1
.
For
r=3
we get
\mathbb{Z}/p^{3}
-submodule of
M_{3}
of rank
(n+1)(n+2)/2
,
where the number comes from counting possible
Taylor expansions of
f_{j}
up to degree two.
Each such statement gives linear constraints on the rows of
f_{i}(P_{j})
,
and therefore leads to divisibility for
\Delta
.

Computing with this we find

v_{p}(\Delta)\gtrsim ke\cdot\frac{d^{1/n}}{1+1/n},

where

arises on the right-hand side
eventually through the asymptotic behaviour of
e=\dim\Gamma(V,\mathcal{O}(k))
,
cf. (
2
).
Choosing

so that

p^{v_{p}(\Delta)}
is larger than the size bound

then forces
\Delta=0
;
so the points
P_{i}
lie on a hyperplane of
\mathbb{P}^{e-1}
, i.e.
all the points of
V
with a fixed mod
p
reduction lie on a divisor.
So we produce
\approx p^{n}

divisors covering the points of height
\leq H
. The argument above is so flexible – in particular, using freedom to choose

–
that one can achieve bounds that are uniform in

.

Notice the crucial point: as the degree of
\mathcal{O}(1)
on

increases,
\mathcal{O}(1)^{\otimes k}
has more sections for a fixed

, giving
stronger divisibility for
\Delta
and thus stronger bounds.

Proof of Theorem

Construction of a suitable cover of

Our proof will involve an induction over higher and higher-codimension subvarieties of

,

\leq d

There are a finite group

of

and, moreover, extends to a finite étale cover of the good integral model

(b)

Let

Suppose that:

–

Let

\geq D

Proof.

Through the rest of this proof,

for the universal family.

We are going to find a cover

,
satisfying the assumptions of (b), and

In particular, removing the singular locus of

Take a geometric generic point

Let

We may assume without loss of generality that:

Situation:

(a)

Every geometric fiber of

(b)

Every fiber of
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow H

;

(c)

On each fiber

For (a), note that

Now note that

For (c) let

Note that:

•

•

•

It is reduced by smoothness,

The tangent bundle

Now, we may certainly assume there is some

In particular, there exists a point

So we proceed assuming ourselves to be in the

above.
We continue to write

for the smooth locus of

and

for the preimage of

in

By §

Let

The morphism

.
Now this morphism is the geometric generic fiber of a

(b)

The following result is the key inductive step.

Choose

that arises in the above fashion, i.e. to the
Zariski closure of any irreducible geometric component of
(f_{j}^{-1}V^{\prime})^{\circ}
that has a
K
-point.
Theorem
2.6
and our choice of
D
implies that the
set of rational points of
Q
of height
\leq cB^{e}

are supported on a set
of proper closed subvarieties of
Q
of degree
O_{d,\epsilon}(1)
with cardinality
\ll_{d,\epsilon}B^{2\epsilon}
. These subvarieties are defined over
K
and need not be geometrically irreducible.

For any such
Q
and any such proper subvariety
Y\subset Q
,
the scheme-theoretic image
f_{j}(Y)
under the finite map
f_{j}
is a proper subvariety
f_{j}(Y)\subset V
, in particular, of dimension
\leq n-1
.
Moreover,
f_{j}
restricts to a finite map
Y\rightarrow f_{j}(Y)
.
By Lemma
2.2
the
\mathcal{L}
-degree of
f_{j}(Y)

is no larger than the
f_{j}^{*}\mathcal{L}
-degree of
Y
, in particular,
O_{d,\epsilon}(1)
.

The number of maps
f_{j}
depends only on
d,\epsilon
,
and the number of
Q
arising is then at most
the number of
f_{j}

multiplied by the order of
G
, which is again
O_{d,\epsilon}(1)
.
Consequently,
the number of
Y
arising as in the prior paragraph
is
O_{d,\epsilon}(B^{2\epsilon})
, concluding the proof (after the obvious
scaling
\epsilon\leftarrow\epsilon/2
.)
∎

4.2.
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
1.2

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.
We use descending induction via Lemma
4.2
. The inductive statement is the following:

(\star)_{n}
: For every
n
with
0\leq n\leq\dim X
, there exists an integer
d_{n}
with the following property: for all
B>0
,
the
S
-integral points of
X^{\circ}
are covered by a collection of

O_{\epsilon}(B^{(\dim X-n)\epsilon})

irreducible subvarieties of
X
, all defined over
K
, each of which is either

–

\textrm{(a)}_{n}
: a subvariety of dimension
\leq n
and degree
\leq d_{n}
, or

–

(b)
: a geometrically irreducible subvariety that is contained in a single fiber of the period map.

The base case is given by
n=\dim X
, in which case, of course, the single subvariety
X\subseteq X
suffices.

The implication
(\star)_{n}\implies(\star)_{n-1}
follows from Lemma
4.2
:
Let
\mathcal{V}_{n}
be the collection of
n
-dimensional varieties in the statement of
(\star)_{n}
.
For each
V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, we will
construct a set of varieties covering all the integral points of
X^{\circ}
lying on
V
. We subdivide into cases:

•

V
is not geometrically irreducible.
In this case,
we take the set
\{V_{i}\}
of subvarieties given by
part (c) of Lemma
2.4
.
These varieties number at most

O_{n,d_{n}}(1)
and they have
dimension
\leq n-1
and degree
O_{n,d_{n}}(1)
.

•

V
is geometrically irreducible but
V^{\circ{\text{an}}}
is contained in a fiber of
\Phi
: then
we take the singleton set
\{V\}
.

•

V
is contained in
Z
; in this case
we can take the empty set
\emptyset
.

•

V
is geometrically irreducible and not contained in
Z
, and
V^{\circ{\text{an}}}
is not contained in a fiber of
\Phi
; then we may apply Lemma
4.2
to show
that integral points of height
\leq B
on
V
are covered by
O_{d_{n},\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon})
irreducible
K
-varieties of dimension
\leq n-1
and degree
O_{d_{n},n,\epsilon}(1)
.

We take
d_{n-1}
to be the largest of the implicit constants
O_{n,d_{n}}(1)
and
O_{n,d_{n},\epsilon}(1)

appearing in the above proof. Then, to sum up,
by
(\star)_{n}
we know that the
S
-integral points of
X^{\circ}
of height at most
B
are covered by
O_{\epsilon}(B^{(\dim X-n)\epsilon})
subvarieties
V

satisfying either
\textrm{(a)}_{n}
or (b),
and we know that for each of those
V
, the subset of those points lying on
V
is covered by
O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon})
subvarieties
satisfying either
\textrm{(a)}_{n-1}
or (b);
together, these facts yield
(\star)_{n-1}
.

We emphasize that this is the point in the argument where the uniformity in Broberg’s result is crucial. We have no control of the heights of the varieties making up the collection
\mathcal{V}_{n}
, and indeed these heights will grow with
B
; but since the implicit constants in Lemma
4.2
depend only on
X
and
\epsilon
, not on
V
, this lack of control does not present a problem.

The case
n=0
gives the Theorem.
∎
