On the group of homeomorphisms of the Basilica
Yury A.Neretin

Abstract




We show that the group of all homeomorphisms of the Basilica fractal
coincides with a group of transformations of a certain non-locally finite ribbon tree.
Also, we show that Basilica Thompson group defined by Belk and Forrest is dense in the group
of all orientation preserving homeomorphisms of the Basilica.

1. The Basilica.
The Basilica
\mathcal{B}
is a compact topological space obtained as a quotient of the circle
S={\mathbb{R}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
under the equivalence relation

(1)

\tfrac{3k+1}{3^{n}}\sim\tfrac{3k+2}{3^{n}},\qquad\text{where $k$, $n$ are nonnegative integers,}

In particular
1\sim 2=0
.
In other words, we write numbers in ternary system and identify
a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n-1}1\sim a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n-1}2
.
The reader can find nice pictures of the Basilica in papers

see also Fig. 1.

We say that a
separating point
of
\mathcal{B}
is a point corresponding a nontrivial (two-point) equivalence class.
Denote by
\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})
the set of separating points. If
x\in\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})
, then
\mathcal{B}\setminus x
consists of two components.
Otherwise,
\mathcal{B}\setminus x
is connected.

It is convenient
to think that our circle
S
is a boundary of a disk
D
, and equivalent points are connected by chords
[a\text{---}b]
(
leafs
), clearly they are disjoint.
Denote the set of all leafs by
L(\mathcal{B})
.
Leafs divide the disc into domains, say
Cantorgons
(cf.
[
4
]
), having a countable number of sides, an intersection of a Cantorgon with
S

is a totally disconnected (Cantor) set. The set of sides of a Cantorgon has a canonical cyclic order; as a cyclic ordered set this set
is equivalent to
{\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
(or to a countable dense subset of a circle, all such subsets are equivalent).

Remark.

The set of all leafs can be obtained as follows. Consider a circle of length 2. Its diameter
[0\text{---}1]
splits a circle into two arcs.
We divide each arc by a pair of points with ratios
1:1:1
and connect elements of each pair by a chord. We get 6 ends of chords and 6 arcs and divide arcs with ratios

1:1:1
, etc.

\boxtimes

Consider a Cantorgon
C
and contract each side to a point. Then we get a topological disc, its boundary

\eth(C)

is a topological circle equipped with a canonical orientation induced from
S
. Each circle
\eth(C_{j})
is homeomorphically embedded to
\mathcal{B}
,
we call such image a
component
of the Basilica.
Two components are disjoint or contain one joint point, which with necessity is a separating point. The union of all components is dense in the Basilica.

Clearly, homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{B}
send components to components.
We say that a homeomorphism of
\mathcal{B}

preserves orientation
if it is an orientation preserving map on each component.
Denote by
\operatorname{Homeo}(\mathcal{B})
the group of all homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{B}
, by
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})
the subgroup of orientation preserving homeomorphisms.

2. The tree of the Basilica.

Consider a graph
T
, whose vertices are enumerated by Cantorgons of
\mathcal{B}
and edges are enumerated by leafs; an edge contains a vertex
if the corresponding leaf is a side of the corresponding Cantorgon. Clearly,
T
is a tree and valences of all vertices are countable.
Denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(v)
the set of edges incident to a vertex
v
. As we have seen above, each set
\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(v)
has a canonical cyclic order.
Denote the tree equipped with this structure by
T(\mathcal{B})
.

Consider an automorphism
g
of the tree
T
(i.e., a bijection of the set of vertices to itself sending adjacent vertices to adjacent vertices).
We say that
g
is
orientation preserving
if for each vertex
v
the map
g
induces a cyclic order preserving map

\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(v)\to\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(gv)
. We say that
g

respects orientation
if each induced map
\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(v)\to\mathop{\mathrm{edge}}\nolimits(gv)
preserves or inverses the cyclic order.
We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
(resp.,
\operatorname{Aut}(T(\mathcal{B}))
) the groups of all orientation preserving (resp., orientation respecting)
automorphisms of
T(\mathcal{B})
(this construction is inside the formalism of Burger–Mozes groups, see
[
3

, Subsect. 3.2).

3. The statement of the paper.

Since a homeomorphism of the Basilica sends components to components and separating points to separating points,
we have a canonical homomorphism

\Pi:\operatorname{Homeo}(\mathcal{B})\to\operatorname{Aut}(T(\mathcal{B})),

sending
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})\to\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
. Since separating points are dense, our homomorphism is injective.

Theorem 1
.

a)

\Pi
establishes an isomorphism

\operatorname{Homeo}(\mathcal{B})\simeq\operatorname{Aut}(T(\mathcal{B}))
.

b)

\Pi
establishes an isomorphism
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})\simeq\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
.

c)
The Basilica Thompson group
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
is dense in
\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
.

4. The Basilica Thompson group
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
.

We say that a
diagram
is a finite collection
\Delta\subset L(\mathcal{B})
of leafs such that

1) for any
\eta\in L(\mathcal{B})\setminus\Delta

all elements of
\Delta
lie on one side of
\eta
.

2)
\Delta
contains the leaf
[0\text{---}1]
.

Leafs of
\Delta
divide the disk
D
into domains, say
cells
.
Our condition means
that for each cell all adjacent leafs are sides of one Cantorgon. We call ends of leafs
vertices of a diagram
,
and arcs of
S
between vertices
intervals of a diagram
.

We say that a diagram is
perfect

if it can be obtained by the following procedure.
We start with the diameter
[1-2]
. It splits
S
into two intervals. We divide one of intervals with ratios
1:1:1
by points
a
,
b
and draw the corresponding chord
[a-b]
.
Now a circle is split into 4 intervals. We choose one interval, divide it with ratios
1:1:1
and add the corresponding chord. Etc.
By the construction, intervals of a perfect diagram have forms

(2)

\bigl[\tfrac{3\alpha}{3^{a}},\tfrac{3\alpha+1}{3^{a}}\bigr],\qquad\bigl[\tfrac{3\beta+1}{3^{b}},\tfrac{3\beta+2}{3^{b}}\bigr],\qquad\bigl[\tfrac{3\gamma+2}{3^{c}},\tfrac{3\gamma}{3^{c}}\bigr].

We say that two perfect diagrams
\Delta_{1}
,
\Delta_{2}
are isomorphic if there is an orientation preserving bijective continuous map identifying

\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
. An isomorphism determines a bijective map on the set of vertices. We extend this map to each interval of
\Delta_{1}

in a linear way (all slopes have a form
3^{k}
). According Belk and Forrest
[
1
]
, such maps form a group—the
Basilica Thompson group

\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
, see
[
1
]
(for instance, it is generated by 4 explicit elements, Theorem 7.1), for further properties, see
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
, see also
[
2
]
on extensions of this construction. On the other hand, such a map respects the equivalence relation
(
1
)
and therefore determines a homeomorphism of the Basilica.

5. Correspondence with the initial model of the Basilica Thompson group.
The Basilica naturally arises as an object of
complex dynamics (as a Julia set, see, e.g.,
[
7
]
), a natural equivalence relation on a circle is defined in the following way. We consider a circle
S^{\diamond}={\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}

of length 1 and the corresponding disc. We start with chords
[\frac{1}{6}\text{---}(-\frac{1}{6})]
,
[\frac{1}{3}\text{---}\frac{2}{3}]
and apply the procedure
described in Remark in Sect.1 changing ratios
1:1:1
to
1:2:1
. In this way,
we get the same combinatorial picture and the same
topological object. Again, we consider perfect diagrams, their isomorphisms,
and linear maps on intervals (now all slopes have form
2^{k}
).

Let us describe the correspondence more precisely. First,
we pass to the circle
S^{\circ}={\mathbb{R}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}
with chords
[0\text{---}1]
,

[3/2\text{---}5/2]
(we rotate the circle
S^{\diamond}
and dilatate it).
The homeomorphism
\Theta:S\to S^{\circ}
is defined in the following way. Let
a
range in the set
\{0,1,2\}
. We define
functions
\varphi(a)
,
\psi(a)

by

\varphi(0)=\tfrac{1}{4},\,\varphi(1)=\tfrac{1}{2},\,\varphi(2)=\tfrac{1}{4},\qquad\psi(0)=0,\,\psi(1)=\tfrac{1}{4},\,\psi(2)=\tfrac{3}{4}.

Then for a ternary number
A=a_{0}.a_{1}a_{2}\dots
we set

\Theta(A)=\frac{\psi(a_{0})}{\varphi(0)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\prod_{p=0}^{n-1}\varphi(a_{p})\cdot\psi(a_{n})\Bigr).

This homeomorphism identifies the equivalence relations and identify the group

\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
defined above with the group from

6. Identification of
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})
and
\operatorname{Aut}_{+}T(\mathcal{B})
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Delta
be a diagram. Consider all cells
M_{j}
of
\Delta
adjacent to
>1
leafs
and the corresponding Cantorgons
C_{j}
. Then there is a finite collection of leafs

l_{1},l_{2},\dots
, such that each
l_{q}
is a side of some Cantorgon
C_{p}
and the diagram

\Delta\cup\{l_{1},l_{2},\dots\}
is perfect.

Proof.
Let a leaf
[a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n}1\text{---}a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n}2]

be contained in a perfect diagram
\Sigma
. It can appear as a result of cutting of the arc

[a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n},a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n}+3^{-n}]
with ratios
1:1:1
and therefore ends of this arc are vertices
of
\Sigma
, i.e., ends of arcs of
\Sigma
. Minimal ternary notation of these vertices has
\leqslant n
places,
therefore the corresponding leafs are longer than our initial leaf.

Consider the diagram
\Delta
. For each cell
M_{j}
we find an adjacent leaf
m_{j}
of minimal length and add to
\Delta
all
sides of the Cantorgon
C_{j}
whose lengths are larger than the length of
m_{j}
. We get a perfect diagram.

\square

Lemma 3
.

Let
r\in\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
,
let
P
be a finite subtree in
T(\mathcal{B})
.
Then there exists
g\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
such that
g\Bigr|_{P}=\Pi\circ r\Bigr|_{P}
.

Proof.
Without loss of generality we can assume that both
P
,
rP
contain
the edge corresponding the leaf
[0\text{---}1]
.

For each vertex of
P
the set of adjacent vertices is equipped with a cyclic order.
So
P
has a structure of a planar tree;
rP
is an isomorphic planar tree.
For each edge of
P
we draw the corresponding leaf and get a diagram, say
\Delta
.
Let
\Sigma
be the similar diagram for
rP
. These diagrams are combinatorially isomorphic.
Next, we extend them to perfect diagrams
\widetilde{\Delta}
,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
as in Lemma

2
. This means that we added additional leafs between each pair of neighbouring leafs of each cell
of
\Delta
(resp.
\Sigma
). Generally, diagrams
\widetilde{\Delta}
,
\widetilde{\Sigma}
are non-isomorphic since number of additional leafs
for corresponding pairs are different. However, adding more leafs to
\widetilde{\Delta}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}

we can get isomorphic perfect diagrams. It remains to take the corresponding
g\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
.

\square

Proof of statements b), c) of Theorem
1
.

Fix
r\in\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))

Consider a sequence of finite subtrees
P_{j}\subset T(\mathcal{B})
such that

\cup P_{j}=T(\mathcal{B})
. For each
P_{j}
, consider an element
g_{j}\in\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))

such that
g_{j}\Bigr|_{P_{j}}=\Pi\circ r\Bigr|_{P_{j}}
.

Then for each
x_{\alpha}\in\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})
the sequence
g_{j}(x_{\alpha})
is eventually constant, denote
its limit by
\rho(x_{\alpha})
.
For each
m
denote by
\Omega_{m}
the set of
x_{\alpha}\in\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})
such that

g_{j}x_{\alpha}=\rho(x_{\alpha})
for
j\geqslant m
. Clearly, we have
\cup\Omega_{m}=\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})=\cup\rho(\Omega_{m})
.
For this reason, the map
\rho:\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})\to\operatorname{Sep}(\mathcal{B})
is a bijection.

Next, sets
\mathcal{B}\setminus\Omega_{m}
,
\mathcal{B}\setminus\rho(\Omega_{m})
split into finite collection of connected
pieces and diameters of these pieces tend to 0 as
m\to\infty
. Elements
g_{j}
for
j\geqslant m
send
pieces to pieces. This implies uniform convergence of homeomorphisms
g_{j}
. We define
\rho
as
limit of
g_{j}
, the corresponding transformation of the tree
T(\mathcal{B})
is
r
.

Thus, we established that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
is dense in
\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})
and the homomorphism

\Pi:\operatorname{Homeo}_{+}(\mathcal{B})\to\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
is surjective. Continuity of
\Pi
is obvious. Banach’s bounded inverse theorem
remains true for Polish groups, see e.g.,
[
5
]
, Corollary 2.3.4. For this reason,
\Pi
is an isomorphism
of Polish groups.

\operatorname{Homeo}(\mathcal{B})
and

For any leaf
[A\text{---}A^{\prime}]=[a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n}1\text{---}a_{0}.a_{1}\dots a_{n}2]

\gamma_{A,A^{\prime}}
of
\mathcal{B}

it permutes

We can repeat the previous considerations for the countable group

and
\gamma_{A,A^{\prime}}

e_{A,A^{\prime}}
the corresponding edge of
T(\mathcal{B})
. Cutting it in a midpoint we splits the tree into two branches. The transformation

\gamma_{A,A^{\prime}}
is identical on one of branches and is a map changing cyclic ordering in each vertex of another branch. Now we can get the analogue
of Lemma
3
. Let

,

be a finite subtree. We can
choose a collection
(A_{1},A^{\prime}_{1})
, …,
(A_{k},A^{\prime}_{k})
such that

8. Final remarks.
a) I have not seen the groups
\operatorname{Aut}_{+}(T(\mathcal{B}))
and

\operatorname{Aut}(T(\mathcal{B}))
in literature. However, these objects are not as exotic as it may seem at first glance. In 1982 Olshanski

[
10
]
classified unitary representations of
the group of all automorphisms of a non-locally finite
Bruhat–Tits tree, in a certain sense this example is toy, but this work was an important test site for representation theory of infinite-dimensional groups. The group
of spheromorphisms of this tree is a nontrivial object for representation theory

[
9
]
.

The group of order preserving bijections of the set
{\mathbb{Q}}
is a standard example of Polish groups,
see, e.g.,
[
11
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
6
]
. It also admits explicit classification of unitary representations
[
14
]
(but in this case the list of representations is poor). On the other hand, dense cyclic orderings participate in the construction of ’Chinese restaurant process’ by Pitman, see, e.g.,

[
12
]
,
this process is a starting point for harmonic analysis related to
infinite symmetric groups.

b) Belk and Forrest
[
2
]
proposed a general construction of discrete groups of Thompson type acting on certain fractal
compact spaces. Apparently, there are statements similar to Theorem
1
for some of these spaces. Certainly,
this is so for the Doaudy rabbit known in complex dynamics. Another interesting possible example is the Airplane fractal defined in
[
2
]
, see also
[
13
]
.
Apparently, in this case we must consider the Bruhat–Tits
{\mathbb{R}}
-tree
(see, e. g.,
[
8
]
) of the field of formal Laurent series

{\mathbb{Q}}[[t,t^{1,2},t^{1/4},\dots]]
equipped with a ribbon structure.
Apparently, the group of homeomorphisms of the Airplane is isomorphic to the group of
homeomorphisms

of this
{\mathbb{R}}
-tree
respecting the cyclic ordering structure of germs of edges in vertices.

c) It is well-known that unitary representation of discrete groups are a dangerous topic. However, classification
[
10
]

of unitary representations of a non-locally finite Bruhat–Tits tree
contains a counterpart of the complementary series. Restricting it to
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{B})
we get unitary representations that are at least unusual and are spherical with respect to the stabilizer of a Cantorgon (this stabilizer is described in

[
1
]
, Sect. 6).
