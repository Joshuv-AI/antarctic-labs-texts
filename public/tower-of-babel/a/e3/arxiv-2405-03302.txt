The number of random 2-SAT solutions is asymptotically log-normal
Arnab Chatterjee, Amin Coja-Oghlan, Noëla Müller, Connor Riddlesden, Maurice Rolvien, Pavel Zakharov, Haodong Zhu

Abstract
. We prove that throughout the satisfiable phase, the logarithm of the number of satisfying assignments of a random 2-SAT formula satisfies a central limit theorem. This implies that the log of the number of satisfying assignments exhibits fluctuations of order n \sqrt{n} , with n n the number of variables. The formula for the variance can be evaluated effectively. By contrast, for numerous other random constraint satisfaction problems the typical fluctuations of the logarithm of the number of solutions are bounded throughout all or most of the satisfiable regime. MSc: 05C80, 60C05, 68Q87

Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

The quest for satisfiability thresholds has been a guiding theme of research into random constraint satisfaction problems
[
7
,
17
,
25
]
.
But once the satisfiability threshold has been pinpointed a question of no less consequence is to determine the distribution of the number of satisfying assignments within the satisfiable phase
[
35
]
.
Indeed, the number of solutions is intimately tied to phase transitions that affect the geometry of the solution space, which in turn impacts the computational nature of finding or sampling solutions
[
4
,
18
,
29
]
.
However, few tools are currently available to count solutions of random problems.
Where precise rigorous results exist (such as in random NAESAT or XORSAT), the proofs typically rely on the method of moments (e.g.,
[
6
,
27
,
43
,
44
]
).
Yet a necessary condition for the success of this approach is that the problem in question exhibits certain symmetries, which are absent in many interesting cases
[
7
,
21
]
.

The aim of the present paper is to shed a closer light on the number of satisfying assignments in random 2-SAT, the simplest random CSP that lacks said symmetry properties.
While the random 2-SAT satisfiability threshold has been known since the 1990s
[
20
,
32
]
, a first-order approximation to the number of satisfying assignments has been obtained only recently
[
5
]
.
This timeline reflects the computational complexity of the respective questions.
As is well known, deciding the satisfiability of a 2-CNF reduces to directed reachability, solvable in polynomial time
[
10
]
.

By contrast, calculating the number of satisfying assignmets

of a 2-CNF

[
48
]

Nonetheless, Monasson and Zecchina
[

]
put forward a delicate physics-inspired conjecture as to the exponential order of the number of satisfying assignments of random 2-CNFs.
Achlioptas et al.
[
5
]
recently proved this conjecture.
Their theorem provides a first-order, law-of-large-numbers approximation of the logarithm of the number of satisfying assignments.
The present paper contributes a much more precise result, namely a central limit theorem.
We show that throughout the satisfiable phase the logarithm of the number of satisfying assignments, suitably shifted and scaled, converges to a Gaussian.
This is the first central limit theorem of this type for any random CSP.

2.
Proof strategy

The main challenge towards the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to get a handle on the variance of
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
given satisfiability.
The key idea, inspired by spin glass theory
[
19
]
but novel to random constraint satisfaction, is to count the
joint
number of satisfying assignments of two correlated random formulas.
Once this is accomplished Theorem
1.1
will follow from the careful application of a general martingale central limit theorem.
To get acclimatised we first revisit the method of moments, the reasons it fails on random 2-SAT and the combinatorial interpretation of the law of large numbers (
1.1
).

2.1.
The method of moments fails

The default approach to estimating the number of solutions to a random CSP is the venerable second moment method
[
7
]
.
Its thrust is to show that the second moment of the number of solutions is of the same order as the square of the expected number of solutions.
If so then the moment computation together with small subgraph conditioning yields the precise limiting distribution of the number of solutions
[
24
,
45
]
.
However, this approach works only if the log of the number of solutions superconcentrates around the log of the expected number of solutions.

This necessary condition is not satisfied in random 2-SAT.
In fact, a straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}[Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})]

\displaystyle\sim\log 2+\frac{d}{2}\log(3/4).

(2.1)

The formula on the r.h.s. is displayed as the black dashed line in Figure
1
.
As can be verified analytically, this line strictly exceeds the function
\phi(d)
from (
1.1
) for any
0<d<2
.
Consequently, (
1.1
) implies that
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\leq\log{\mathbb{E}}[Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})]-\Omega(n)
w.h.p. In other words, the expected number of solutions
{\mathbb{E}}[Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})]
overshoots the typical number of solutions by an exponential factor w.h.p. ; cf. the discussion in
[
6
,
8
]
.

2.2.
Belief Propagation

Instead of the method of moments, the prescription of the physics-based work of Monasson and Zecchina
[
38
]
is to estimate
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
by way of the Belief Propagation (BP) message passing algorithm.
This approach was vindicated rigorously by Achlioptas et al.
[
5

As we will reuse certain elements of that analysis we dwell on BP briefly.
For a clause

\Phi

\partial a=\partial_{\Phi}a
be the set of variables that
a

Moreover, for

Analogously, let
\partial x=\partial_{\Phi}x
be the set of clauses in which variable

BP introduces ‘messages’ between clauses

More precisely, each such clause-variable pair

The messages are probability distributions on ‘true’ and ‘false’, which we represent by
\pm 1

Thus,
\mu_{x\to a}(\pm 1),\mu_{a\to x}(\pm 1)\geq 0

\mu_{x\to a}(1)+\mu_{x\to a}(-1)=\mu_{a\to x}(1)+\mu_{a\to x}(-1)=1

The messages get updated iteratively by an operator

Of course, for

First, that the local structure of the clause-variable incidence graph can be described by a Galton-Watson tree.

Since the proof of Theorem

To mimic the local structure of

2.3.
Approaching the variance

The proof of the formula (
1.1
) combines the Gibbs uniqueness property and the local convergence to the Galton-Watson tree with a coupling argument called the ‘Aizenman-Sims-Starr scheme’
[
5
]
.
Unfortunately, this combination does not seem precise enough to get a handle on the limiting distribution of
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
by a long shot.
Actually, it is anything but clear how even the
order
of the standard deviation of
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
could be derived along these lines.
One specific problem is that the rate of convergence of (
2.6
) diminishes as
d
approaches the satisfiability threshold.

To tackle this challenge we devise a combinatorial interpretation of
\log^{2}Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
.
A key idea, which we borrow from spin glass theory
[
19
]
, is to set up a family of correlated random formulas.
Specifically, given integers
M,M^{\prime}\geq 0
we construct a correlated pair
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M,M^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M,M^{\prime}))
of formulas on the variable set
V_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
as follows.
Let
(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\geq 1}
,
(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{i\geq 1}
be sequences of mutually independent uniformly random clauses on
V_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M,M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{M}\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{M^{\prime}}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M,M^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{M}\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{a}_{M^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}.

(2.7)

Thus, the two formulas share clauses
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{M}
.
Additionally, each contains another
M^{\prime}
independent clauses.
In particular,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(m,0)
,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(m,0)
are identical, while
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(0,m)
,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(0,m)
are independent.

Interpolating between these extreme cases offers a promising avenue for computing the variance:
given that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M,m-M)
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M,m-M)
are satisfiable for all
M
, we can write a telescoping sum

\displaystyle\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(m,0))

\displaystyle\cdot\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(m,0))-\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(0,m))\cdot\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(0,m))

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=1}^{m}\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M,m-M))\cdot\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M,m-M))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M-1,m-M+1))\cdot\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M-1,m-M+1)).

If we
could
take the expectation on the l.h.s. of (
2.8
), we would precisely obtain the variance of
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
.
Moreover, each summand on the r.h.s. amounts to a ‘local’ change of swapping a shared clause for a pair of independent clauses.
Yet we cannot just take the expectation of (
2.8
), because some
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}(M,m-M)
may be unsatisfiable.
To remedy this, we will replace
\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
by a tamer random variable with the same limiting distribution.
Its construction is based on the Unit Clause Propagation algorithm.

2.4.
Unit Clause Propagation

Employed by all modern SAT solvers as a sub-routine, Unit Clause Propagation is a linear time algorithm that tracks the implications of partial assignments.
The algorithm receives as input a 2-CNF
\Phi

These literals are deemed to be ‘true’.
The algorithm then pursues direct logical implications, thereby identifying additional ‘implied’ literals that need to be true so that no clause gets violated.
This procedure is outlined in Steps 1–2 of Algorithm

With the variance computation done, we have now overcome the greatest hurdle en route to Theorem

Discussion

The hunt for satisfiability thresholds of random constraint satisfaction problems was launched by the experimental work of Cheeseman, Kanefsky and Taylor
[
17
]
.
The 2-SAT threshold was the first one to be caught
[
20
,
32
]
.
Subsequent successes include the 1-in-

-SAT threshold
[
3
]
and the

-XORSAT threshold
[
27
,
43
]
.
Furthermore, Friedgut
[
30

proved the existence of non-uniform (i.e.,
n

The plot thickened when physicists employed a compelling but non-rigorous technique called the cavity method to ‘predict’ the exact satisfiability thresholds of many further problems, including the
k
-SAT problem for
k\geq 3

[
37
]
.
A line of rigorous work
[
6
,
8
,
23
]
culminated in the verification of this physics prediction for large
k

[
25
]
.

Even though the satisfiability threshold of random 2-SAT was determined already in the 1990s, the problem continued to receive considerable attention.
For example, Bollobás, Borgs, Chayes, Kim and Wilson
[
15
]
investigated the scaling window around the satisfiability threshold, a point on which a recent contribution by Dovgal, de Panafieu and Ravelomanana elaborates
[
26
]
.
Abbe and Montanari
[
2
]
made the first substantial step towards the study of the number of satisfying assignments that
\frac{1}{n}\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
converges in probability to a deterministic limit
\varphi(d)
for Lebesgue-almost all
d\in(0,2)
.
However, their techniques do not reveal the value
\varphi(d)
.
Moreover, Montanari and Shah
[
39
]
obtain a ‘law-of-large-numbers’ estimate of the number of assignments that satisfy all but
o(n)
clauses for
d<1.16
.
Finally, the aforementioned article of Achlioptas et al.
[
5
]
verifies the prediction from
[
38
]
as to the number of satisfying assignments for all
d<2
.
The main result of the present paper refines these results considerably by establishing a central limit theorem.

For random
k
-CNFs with
k\geq 3
an upper bound on the number of satisfying assignments can be obtained via the interpolation method from mathematical physics
[
42
]
.
This bound matches the predictions of the cavity method
[
36
]
.
However, no matching lower bound is currently known.
The precise physics prediction called the ‘replica symmetric solution’ has only been verified for ‘soft’ versions of random
k
-SAT where unsatisfied clauses are penalised but not strictly forbidden, and for clause-to-variable ratios well below the satisfiability threshold
[
39
,
41
,
47
]
.

Random CSPs such as random

-XORSAT or random

-SAT tend to be amenable to the method of moments
[
6
]
.
5
5

5

Formally, by ‘symmetry’ we mean that the empirical distribution of the marginals of random solutions converges to an atom; cf.
[
22
]
.

Therefore, more is known about their number of solutions.
For example, due to the inherent connection to linear algebra, the number of satisfying assignments of random
k
-XORSAT formulas is known to concentrate on a single value right up to the satisfiability threshold
[
11
,
27
,
43
]
.
Furthermore, in random
k
-NAESAT, random graph colouring and several related problems the logarithm of the number of solutions superconcentrates, i.e., has only bounded fluctuations for constraint densities up to the so-called condensation threshold, a phase transition that shortly precedes the satisfiability threshold
[
12
,
21
,
44
]
.
The same is true of random
k
-SAT instances with regular literal degrees
[
24
]
.
A further example is the symmetric perceptron
[
1
]
, where the number of solutions superconcentrates but the limiting distribution is a log-normal with bounded variance.
Going beyond the condensation transition, Sly, Sun and Zhang
[
46
]
proved that the number of satisfying assignments of random regular
k
-NAESAT formulas matches the ‘1-step replica symmetry breaking’ prediction from physics.

Apart from the superconcentration results for symmetric problems from
[

,
24
,
21
,
44
]
, the limiting distribution of the logarithm of the number of solutions has not been known in any random constraint satisfaction problem.
In particular, Theorem
1.1
is the first central limit theorem for this quantity in any random CSP.
We expect that the technique developed in the present work, particularly the use of two correlated random instances in combination with spatial mixing, can be extended to other problems.
The present use of correlated instances is inspired by the work of Chen, Dey and Panchenko
[
19
]
on the
p
-spin model from mathematical physics, a generalisation of the famous Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model.
That said, on a technical level the present use of correlated instances is quite different from the approach from
[
19
]
.
Specifically, while here we construct correlated 2-CNFs that share a specific fraction of their clauses and employ a martingale central limit theorem, Chen, Dey and Panchenko combine a continuous interpolation of two mixed
p
-spin Hamiltonians with Stein’s method.

A further line of work deals with central limit theorems for random optimisation problems.
Cao
[
16
]
provided a general framework based on the ‘objective method’
[
9

Preliminaries and notation

4.1.
Boolean formulas

A
2
-SAT formula
or
2-CNF
\Phi
consists of a finite set
V(\Phi)
of propositional variables and another set
F(\Phi)
of clauses.
Unless specified otherwise, we assume that each clause contains two distinct variables.

For a clause
a\in F(\Phi)
we denote by
\partial a=\partial_{\Phi}a
the set of variables that appear in clause
a
.
Similarly, for a variable
x\in V(\Phi)
let
\partial x=\partial_{\Phi}x
signify the set of clauses in which
x
appears.
Thus, the formula
\Phi
induces a bipartite graph on variables and clauses, the so-called
incidence graph
of
\Phi
.
Further, the shortest path metric on the incidence graph induces a metric on the variables and clauses of
\Phi
.
Accordingly, for a variable or clause
u
let
\partial^{\ell}u=\partial^{\ell}_{\Phi}u
be the set of all nodes at a distance precisely
\ell
from
u
.
Moreover, let
\partial^{\leq\ell}u=\partial^{\leq\ell}_{\Phi}u
be the sub-formula of
\Phi
obtained by deleting all clauses and variables at a distance greater than
\ell
from
u
.
In other words,
\partial^{\leq\ell}u
is the depth-
\ell
neighbourhood of
u
.

We encode the Boolean values ‘true’ and ‘false’ as
\pm 1
.
Accordingly, let
S(\Phi)\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{V(\Phi)}
be the set of satisfying assignments of
\Phi
and let
Z(\Phi)=|S(\Phi)|
.
Further,
\mathrm{sign}(x,a)=\mathrm{sign}_{\Phi}(x,a)\in\{\pm 1\}
denotes the sign with which variable
x
appears in clause
a
, i.e.,
\mathrm{sign}(x,a)=1
if
x
appears in
a
positively and
\mathrm{sign}(x,a)=-1
if
a
contains the negation
\neg x
.
Finally, for a literal
l\in\{x,\neg x\}
we let
|l|=x
denote the underlying Boolean variable.

Assuming
S(\Phi)\neq\emptyset
let

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\{\sigma\in S(\Phi)\}/Z(\Phi),

\displaystyle(\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{V(\Phi)})

(4.1)

be the uniform distribution on
S(\Phi)
.
We write
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Phi}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\Phi,x})_{x\in V(\Phi)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{V(\Phi)}
for a sample from
\mu_{\Phi}
, i.e., a uniformly random satisfying assignment of
\Phi
.

In contrast to
k
-SAT for
k\geq 3
, the 2-SAT problem can be solved in polynomial time.
This is because a 2-SAT instance is unsatisfiable if and only if it contains a peculiar sub-formula called a bicycle.
To be precise, let
\Phi
be a CNF with clauses of length one or two.
A
bicycle
of
\Phi
is an alternating sequence
l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}
of literals
l_{0},\ldots,l_{k}
and clauses
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in F(\Phi)
such that

BIC1:

l_{0}=l_{k}
,

BIC2:

l_{i}=\neg l_{0}
for some
0<i<k
and

BIC3:

a_{i}\equiv\neg l_{i-1}\vee l_{i}\equiv l_{i-1}\to l_{i}
.

(Observe that a clause
a
comprising only a single literal
l
is logically equivalent to
l\vee l\equiv\neg l\to l
.)
Hence, the bicycle consists of clauses that are logically equivalent to a chain of implications
l_{0}\to\neg l_{0}\to l_{0}
.

Fact 4.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

A CNF
\Phi
with clauses of lengths one or two is unsatisfiable iff
\Phi
contains a bicycle.

4.2.
Unit Clause Propagation

The
PUC
algorithm (Algorithm
1
) takes as input a CNF
\Phi
along with an initial set
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of literals.

PUC
outputs a set
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})\supseteq\mathcal{L}_{0}
of literals.
Let

\mathcal{V}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})=\{|l|:l\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})\}

be the set of underlying variables.
In addition to
\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
,
PUC
also outputs a partial assignment

\sigma=\sigma_{\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0}}:\mathcal{V}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})\to\{0,\pm 1\}

that sets each
x\in\mathcal{V}
either to a truth value
\pm 1
or to the dummy value
0
.
Let

\mathcal{V}_{0}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})=\{x\in\mathcal{V}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0}):\sigma_{\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0},x}=0\}

be the set of variables that receive the dummy value.
Finally, the algorithm identifies a set
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
of
conflict clauses
, i.e., clauses
a
such that
\partial a\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{0}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.

We make a note of a few basic facts about
PUC
.
These remarks apply to any CNF
\Phi
with clauses of length
at most
two.
To get started, we say that a literal
l^{\prime}
is
implication-reachable
from another literal
l
if there exists an alternating sequence
l=l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}=l^{\prime}
of literals
l_{i}
and clauses
a_{i}
of
\Phi
such that
a_{i}\equiv\neg l_{i-1}\vee l_{i}\equiv l_{i-1}\to l_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
We call this sequence an
implication chain
from
l
to
l^{\prime}
.
Observe that a unit clause (clause of length one) comprising a single literal
l
is equivalent to the implication
\neg l\to l\equiv l\vee l
.
Furthermore, if
l^{\prime}
is implication-reachable from
l
, then
\neg l
is implication-reachable from
\neg l^{\prime}
.
Indeed, if
l=l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}=l^{\prime}
is an implication chain from
l
to
l^{\prime}
, then its contraposition

\displaystyle\neg l^{\prime}=\neg l_{k},a_{k},\neg l_{k-1},\ldots,\neg l_{1},a_{1},\neg l

is an implication chain from
\neg l^{\prime}
to
\neg l
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Phi
be a CNF with clauses of length at most two and let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
be a set of literals of
\Phi
.
Then
\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
is the set of all literals
l^{\prime}
that are implication-reachable from a literal
l\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

Proof.

This is an easy induction on the length of the shortest implication chain from
l
to
l^{\prime}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Lemma
4.2
is that the order in which
PUC
proceeds is irrelevant.

Finally, for the sake of completeness, we carry out the proof of Fact
2.2
.

Proof of Fact
2.2
.

Fix some order
l_{1},\ldots,l_{\nu}
of the literals
\{x,\neg x:x\in V(\Phi)\}
.
Let
\sigma_{i}
be the assignments produced by
PUC
on input
(\Phi,\{l_{i}\})
.
We construct an assignment
\sigma:V(\Phi)\to\{0,\pm 1\}
by proceeding as follows for
i=1,\ldots,\nu
.
For each variable
x
such that
\{x,\neg x\}\cap\mathcal{L}(\Phi,l_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{1\leq h<i}\mathcal{L}(\Phi,l_{h})\neq\emptyset
let
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{i}(x)
.
We claim that for each clause
a\in\hat{\Phi}
there is a variable
x\in\partial a
such that
\sigma_{x}=\mathrm{sign}(x,a)
.
Indeed, it is not possible that
\sigma_{x}=0
for all
x\in\partial a
; for otherwise
a\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,l_{i})
for some
i
, and thus
a
would not be present in
\hat{\Phi}
.
Thus, there exists
x\in\partial a
such that
\sigma_{x}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
If
\sigma_{x}\neq\mathrm{sign}(x,a)
, then
PUC
would have included the second literal
k
that appears in
a
into the set
\mathcal{L}
.
Hence,
\sigma_{|k|}=\mathrm{sign}(|k|,a)
, because otherwise
a
would have been a contradiction and therefore omitted from
\hat{\Phi}
.
∎

4.4.

For a measurable subset
\Omega
of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
we let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
denote the space of all probability distributions on
\Omega

Moreover, for

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\Omega)
to be the set of all

\int_{\Omega}\|x\|_{2}^{p}{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)<\infty
.
We equip
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\Omega)
with the Wasserstein metric

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{p}}\right]^{1/p}

\displaystyle(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\Omega)),

(4.2)

where the infimum is taken over all pairs of random variables
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
that are defined on some common probability space such that
\boldsymbol{X}
has distribution
\mu
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
has distribution
\mu^{\prime}
.

The infimum in (
4.2
) is attained for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
.
Random vectors
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}
for which the infimum is attained are called
optimal couplings
.
Such optimal couplings exist for all
\mu,\mu^{\prime}

[
14
]
.

The spaces
(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\Omega),W_{p})
are complete metric spaces
[
14
]
.
Finally, convergence in
(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\Omega),W_{p})
implies weak convergence of the corresponding probability measures.

For a measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and a measurable function
f:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
from
\Omega
to another probability space
\Omega^{\prime}
we denote by
f(\rho)
the pushforward measure of
\rho
.
Thus, the measure
f(\rho)
that assigns mass
\rho(f^{-1}(A))
to measurable
A\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
.

Throughout the paper we let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{t}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to(0,1)^{2},\mathfrak{t}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\tanh(x_{1}/2)}{2}\\
\frac{1+\tanh(x_{2}/2)}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathfrak{l}:(0,1)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathfrak{l}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\log\frac{x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\\
\log\frac{x_{2}}{1-x_{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof of Proposition

In this section we estimate the difference between the number of satisfying assignments of the pruned random formula
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
and the original formula
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
.
We begin with a basic observation about the Unit Clause Propagation algorithm, and then estimate the number of clauses that the pruning process removes.
Apart from proving Proposition
2.3
, the considerations in this section also pave the way for the proof of the variance formula in Section

.

5.1.
Tracing Unit Clause Propagation

For a 2-CNF
\Phi
and a set of literals
\mathcal{L}_{0}
consider a set of conflict clauses
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
that
PUC
produces along with a set
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
of conflict variables.
Let
\Phi-\mathcal{C}
be the formula obtained from
\Phi
by deleting the clauses from
\mathcal{C}
.
Clearly
Z(\Phi)\leq Z(\Phi-\mathcal{C})
.
Conversely, the following lemma puts a bound on how much bigger
Z(\Phi-\mathcal{C})
may be.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
\Phi
is a satisfiable 2-CNF.
For any set
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of literals we have

\displaystyle Z(\Phi-\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0}))\leq 2^{|\mathcal{V}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})|\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})\not=\emptyset\}}Z(\Phi).

(5.1)

Towards the proof of Lemma
5.1
let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
be the final set of literals that
PUC
produces.
Moreover, let
\sigma:\mathcal{V}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
be the function that
PUC
outputs and let
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{x\in\mathcal{V}:\sigma_{x}=0\}
.
Further, let
\Phi_{0}
be a CNF with variable set
\mathcal{V}
that contains the following clauses:

(i)

any clause
a\in F(\Phi)
with
\partial a\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,

(ii)

a unit clause
l
for every literal
l
with
|l|\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\Phi
contains a clause
a\equiv l\vee l^{\prime}
with
|l^{\prime}|\not\in\mathcal{V}
.

Thus,
\Phi_{0}
contains clauses of length one or two.

Claim 5.2
.

The formula
\Phi_{0}
possesses a satisfying assignment
\tau
such that
\tau_{x}=\sigma_{x}\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}.

Proof.

Obtain a formula
\Phi_{1}
by adding to
\Phi_{0}
a unit clause
x
for every variable
x\in\mathcal{V}
with
\sigma_{x}=1
and a unit clause
\neg x
for every
x\in\mathcal{V}
with
\sigma_{x}=-1
.
Then we just need to show that
\Phi_{1}
is satisfiable.

Assume otherwise.
Then by Fact
4.1

\Phi_{1}
contains a bicycle
l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}
.
This bicycle is logically equivalent to an implication chain

\displaystyle l_{0}\to l_{1}\to\cdots\to\neg l_{0}\to\cdots\to l_{k-1}\to l_{k}=l_{0}.

(5.2)

The contraposition of this chain reads

\displaystyle\neg l_{0}=\neg l_{k}\to\neg l_{k-1}\to\cdots\to l_{0}\to\cdots\to\neg l_{1}\to\neg l_{0}.

(5.3)

Since
\Phi
is satisfiable, Fact
4.1
shows that the bicycle (
5.2
) cannot be contained in
\Phi
.
Therefore, the bicycle contains a unit clause
l_{i}\in F(\Phi_{1})\setminus F(\Phi)
for some
1\leq i\leq k
.
Hence, the constructions of
\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi_{1}
ensure that
l_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.
Indeed, letting
\mathcal{U}
be the the set of all literals
l_{i}
that appear in (
5.2
) as unit clauses, we obtain
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.

We claim that in fact
l_{0},\ldots,l_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.
To see this, pick any
0\leq j\leq k
such that
l_{j}
does not appear as a unit clause in
\Phi_{1}
.
Define
l_{-i}=l_{k-i}
for
0\leq i<k
and let
1-k\leq i<j
be the largest index such that
l_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Then
\Phi
contains the implication chain
l_{i}\to\cdots\to l_{j}
.
Therefore, Lemma
4.2
implies that
l_{j}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.
Analogously, considering the contraposition (
5.3
), we conclude that the negations of the literals
l_{0},\ldots,l_{k}
belong to
\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
.
In summary,

\displaystyle l_{0},\neg l_{0},\ldots,l_{k},\neg l_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0}).

(5.4)

But (
5.4
) implies that
|l_{0}|,\ldots,|l_{k}|\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
.
Consequently, none of these literals belongs to a unit clause
u\in F(\Phi_{1})\setminus F(\Phi_{0})
.
Furthermore, none of the literals
l_{i},\neg l_{i}
belongs to a unit clause
a\in F(\Phi_{0})\setminus F(\Phi)
.
This is because if
\Phi
contains a clause
l_{i}\vee l^{\prime}
or
\neg l_{i}\vee l^{\prime}
and
l_{i},\neg l_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})
, then
PUC
added
l^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{0})
as well.
Thus, we conclude that the bicycle (
5.2
) consists of clauses of
\Phi
only.
But by Fact
4.1
this contradicts the fact that
\Phi
is satisfiable.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Clearly, if
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})=\emptyset
, the statement is true.
Hence, assume that
\mathcal{C}(\Phi,\mathcal{L}_{0})\neq\emptyset
and let
\tau
be a satisfying assignment of
\Phi_{0}
from Claim
5.2
.
Consider a satisfying assignment
\chi
of
\Phi-\mathcal{C}
and let
\chi^{\prime}:V(\Phi)\setminus\mathcal{V}\to\{\pm 1\}
be the restriction of
\chi
to
V(\Phi)\setminus\mathcal{V}
.
We extend
\chi^{\prime}
to a satisfying assignment
\chi^{\prime\prime}
of
\Phi
by letting

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}_{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\{x\in\mathcal{V}\}\tau_{x}+\mathbb{1}\{x\not\in\mathcal{V}\}\chi^{\prime}_{x};

clearly,
\chi^{\prime\prime}
satisfies all clauses
a
such that
\partial a\cap\mathcal{V}=\emptyset
, because all these clauses are contained in
\Phi-\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover,
\chi^{\prime\prime}
satisfies all
a
such that
\partial a\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, because these clauses belong to
\Phi_{0}
.
Further, if
a=l\vee l^{\prime}
is a clause such that
|l|\in\mathcal{V}
but
|l^{\prime}|\not\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\tau_{|l|}=\sigma_{|l|}
.
Since
|l^{\prime}|\not\in\mathcal{V}
, this means that
\sigma_{x}=\mathrm{sign}(|l|,a)
, as otherwise
PUC
would have added

to
\mathcal{L}
.
Therefore,
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{|l|}=\tau_{|l|}=\sigma_{|l|}
satisfies
a
.
Since the map
\chi\mapsto\chi^{\prime}
only discards the values of the variables in
\mathcal{V}
, we obtain the bound (
5.1
).
∎

5.2.
Cycles in random formulas

To prove Proposition
2.3
we need a good estimate of the total number of clauses that will be removed from
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
to obtain
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
.
This estimate is provided by the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix any
\delta>0
.
With probability
1-o(n^{-1})
the number of literals
l
such that
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\left\{{l}\right\})\neq\emptyset
is smaller than
n^{\delta}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{N}
be the number of literals
l
such that
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\left\{{l}\right\})\neq\emptyset
.
We are going to show that for any fixed (i.e.,
n

\ell\geq 1
for large enough

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{N}-i+1)}\right]\leq\left({\ell\log^{3}n}\right)^{\ell}.

(5.5)

Providing
\ell\geq 2/\delta
and
n
is sufficiently large, Markov’s inequality then shows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{N}\geq n^{\delta}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{N}-i+1)\geq(n^{\delta}/2)^{\ell}}\right]\leq\left({\frac{2\ell\log^{3}n}{n^{\delta}}}\right)^{\ell}=o(n^{-1}),

which implies the assertion.

Thus, we are left to prove (
5.5
).
By symmetry it suffices to bound the probability of the event

\mathfrak{E}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})\not=\emptyset\}

that
PUC
will produce at least one conflict clause from each of the literals
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
; then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(\boldsymbol{N}-i+1)}\right]\leq(2n)^{\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}}\right].

(5.6)

In order to estimate the probability of
\mathfrak{E}
we are going to launch
PUC
from the initial set
\mathcal{L}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\}
.
While the order in which the literals and clauses are processed does not affect the ultimate outcome of
PUC
, for the present analysis we assume that
PUC
processes the literals one at a time, each time pursuing
all
the clauses
l\vee\neg l^{\prime}
that contain the negation of a specific
l^{\prime}
.
We also presume that the literals are processed in the same order as they get inserted into the set
\mathcal{L}
.
In other words,
PUC
proceeds in breadth-first-search order.
Let
\mathfrak{H}_{t}
be the history of the execution of
PUC
up to and including the point where the first
t
literals and their adjacent clauses have been explored.
Formally,
\mathfrak{H}_{t}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by these first
t
literals that get added to
\mathcal{L}
and their adjacent clauses.

Lemma
4.3
implies that with probability
1-o(n^{-1})
the set
\mathcal{L}
returned by
PUC
has size at most
L=\ell\log^{2}n
.
Let
\mathfrak{E}_{t}
be the event that at time
t
we explored a clause that contains two variables from
\mathcal{L}
and
|\mathcal{L}|\leq L
.
Moreover, let
\boldsymbol{S}=\sum_{t}\mathbb{1}\{\mathfrak{E}_{t}\}
.
Let
0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{\ell}\leq L
be distinct time steps.
Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}}\right]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{S}\geq\ell}\right]\leq\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathfrak{E}_{t_{i}}}\right]=\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}_{t_{i}}\Big|\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathfrak{E}_{t_{j}}}\right].

(5.7)

To bound the r.h.s. of (
5.7
) we will estimate the probability of
\mathfrak{E}_{t+1}
given the history
\mathfrak{H}_{t}
of the process up to time
t
, showing that for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}_{t+1}|\mathfrak{H}_{t}}\right]\leq{L\over n}.

(5.8)

In fact the probability that in step
t+1
we will run into already discovered variable is bounded by the probability that the literal explored during that step shares a clause with an already explored variable, which is bounded by
Lm/n\leq L/n
; for if more than
L
literals have been already explored the event
\mathfrak{E}_{t+1}
does not occur by definition.
Finally, because the event
\mathfrak{E}_{t}
is
\mathfrak{H}_{t}
-measurable, (
5.8
) implies

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}_{t_{i}}\Big|\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathfrak{E}_{t_{j}}}\right]\leq\left({L\over n}\right)^{\ell}.

Thus (
5.7
) gives
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{E}}\right]\leq{L\choose\ell}{L^{\ell}\over n^{\ell}}\leq\left({\mathrm{e}L^{2}\over\ell n}\right)^{\ell}
, which together with (
5.6
) implies (
5.5
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Lemma
4.5
shows that
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})=0}\right]=o(n^{-1/2})
.
Thus, we may condition on the event that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is satisfiable.
Furthermore, Lemma

shows that given that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is satisfiable we have

\displaystyle\log Z(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})-\log Z(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})\neq\emptyset\}|\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})|+\mathbb{1}\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x_{i}\})\neq\emptyset\}|\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x_{i}\})|.

(5.9)

Finally, Corollary
4.4
and Lemma
5.3
(applied with

) imply that with probability

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})\neq\emptyset\}|\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})|+\mathbb{1}\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x_{i}\})\neq\emptyset\}|\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x_{i}\})|

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\left({\left|{\left\{{x\in V_{n}:\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x\})\neq\emptyset}\right\}}\right|+\left|{\left\{{x\in V_{n}:\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x\})\neq\emptyset}\right\}}\right|}\right)\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|{\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{x_{i}\})|+|\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\neg x_{i}\})}\right|=o(n^{1/3}).

Thus, the assertion follows from (

∎

6.
Proof of Proposition
2.6

The proof of Proposition
2.6
is based on a combination of a coupling and a second moment argument.
As a first step we observe that we do not need to worry about trees of very high maximum degree.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
\ell\geq 0
there exists
L>0
such that for all
t\in[0,1]
with probability at least

Proof.

The construction of the tree
\boldsymbol{T}^{\otimes,\,(2\ell)}
in Section

ensures that every variable node has a Poisson number of clauses as offspring.
The mean of this Poisson variable is always bounded by
2d
.
Hence, Bennett’s inequality shows that for any

the probability that a specific variable has more than
L
offspring is bounded by
\exp(-L^{2}/(4d+L))
.
Thus, choosing
L
sufficiently large so that

and applying the union bound, we obtain the assertion (combined with the chain rule starting from the root).
∎

Thus, in the following we confine ourselves to trees
T
with a maximum degree bounded by a large enough number

.
First we are going to count the number of copies of such trees

in
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}(M,M^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}(M,M^{\prime}))
via the method of moments.
The following lemma estimates the first moment.

For any fixed integers

, any possible outcome

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{H}_{1}|\mathfrak{F}\cap\mathfrak{R}\cap\mathfrak{H}_{0}}\right]

9.2.
Belief Propagation on the Galton-Watson tree

The proof of Proposition

relies on the fact that Belief Propagation is ‘exact’ on trees.
The following fact, which is a direct consequence of
[
36
, Theorem 14.1]

Fact 9.2
.

Assume that the bipartite graph associated with the 2-CNF
\Phi
is a (finite) tree.
Let
z\in V(\Phi)
be a variable and let
\ell\geq 1
be an integer such that no variable or clause of
\Phi
has distance greater than
2\ell
from
z
.
Let

\displaystyle\mu_{x\to a}^{(0)}(s)

\displaystyle=\mu_{a\to x}^{(0)}(s)=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }x\in V(\Phi),\,a\in\partial x,\,s\in\{\pm 1\}.

Furthermore, obtain the messages
(\mu_{x\to a}^{(i+1)}(s),\mu_{a\to x}^{(i+1)}(s))_{x,a,s}
by applying the BP operator (
2.2
) to

(\mu_{x\to a}^{(i)}(s),\mu_{a\to x}^{(i)}(s))_{x,a,s}
.
Then for all
i\geq 2\ell
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}(\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}=s\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{a\in\partial z}\mu_{a\to z}^{(i)}(s)}{\prod_{a\in\partial z}\mu_{a\to z}^{(i)}(1)+\prod_{a\in\partial z}\mu_{a\to z}^{(i)}(-1)}.

(9.4)

As a preparation toward the proof of Proposition
2.7
we establish the following ‘univariate’ variant of the proposition.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
h=1,2
.
Let
\pi_{d,t,h}^{(\ell)}
be the distribution of the
h
-th component of a random vector with distribution

\ell

1/2

To go from
\ell-1
to

we exploit the fact that

by itself has the same distribution as the ‘plain’ Galton-Watson tree

from Section

itself, and these trees are mutually independent for all

.
Consequently, by induction we know that the marginal

implies that the marginals

The main goal of this section is to show that both the evaluation of the functional

Proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dist}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t}1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dist}}}{{=}}1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1}.

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{-\boldsymbol{d}_{-1}-\boldsymbol{d}_{-1}^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{-1}}(1+\boldsymbol{r}_{-1,i}(2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},-1,i,1}-1))\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{-1}^{\prime}}(1+\boldsymbol{r}_{-1,i}^{\prime}(2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},-1,i,1}^{\prime}-1))}{\sum_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}2^{-\boldsymbol{d}_{-s}-\boldsymbol{d}_{-s}^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{-s}}(1+\boldsymbol{r}_{-s,i}(2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},-s,i,1}-1))\prod_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{d}_{-s}^{\prime}}(1+\boldsymbol{r}_{-s,i}^{\prime}(2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},-s,i,1}^{\prime}-1))}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{d,t}^{\otimes}(\rho_{d,t})|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,1}}\right)}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,2}}\right)}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,1}}\right)}\right)}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,2}}\right)}\right)}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,1}}\right)}\right)}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,2}}\right)\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,2}}\right)}\right)}\right]^{1/2}.

As
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dist}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dist}}}{{=}}1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}
thanks to Claim
10.2
, we further get

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{d,t}^{\otimes}(\rho_{d,t})|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,1}}\right)}\right].

Next, recalling (
9.3
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},2,1}}\right)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\leq\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\left|{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)}\right|}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}>\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\left|{\log^{2}\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}\leq\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\log^{2}2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}>\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\left({\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}{\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)}-\log\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}{1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}}\right)}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}>\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}{\left({1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right)}\log\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\log^{2}\left({\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}{1-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\pi_{d,t},1,1}}}\right)}\right]=\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d,t},1,1}^{2}}\right].

However
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d,t},1,1}^{2}]<\infty
, since
\rho_{d,t}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}_{d,t}^{\otimes}(\rho_{d,t})<\infty
.

Moreover, it is easy to see that the distribution of the marginal sample
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d,t},1,1}
is independent of for any

. Call this distribution
\rho_{d}
and let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d}}
be a sample from

. Then the previous upper bound yields

\displaystyle\eta(d)^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\mathcal{B}_{d,t}^{\otimes}(\rho_{d,t})|\mathrm{d}t+|\mathcal{B}_{d}^{\otimes}(\rho_{d,0})|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d}}^{2}}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d}}^{2}}\right]\leq 2\left({\log^{2}2+\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rho_{d}}^{2}}\right]}\right)<\infty.

∎

11.
Proof of Proposition

We combine the results from the previous sections in order to analyse the variance process.
As a first step we derive a rough upper bound on the potential change in the number of satisfying assignments upon insertion of a single clause (Lemmas
11.1
and
11.6
).
Subsequently we derive a combinatorial formula for the squared martingle difference (Lemma
11.9
), which easily implies Lemma
2.4
.
The combinatorial formula puts us in a position to obtain an
L^{2}
-bound on the squared martingale difference (Lemma
11.10
).
With these ingredients in place, we complete the proof of Proposition
2.12
and of Theorem
1.1
in Section
11.5
.

11.1.
A pessimistic estimate

Let
\Phi,\Psi
be two 2-CNFs on the same set of variables such that
\Psi
is obtained from
\Phi
by adding a single clause
e
.
We are going to need a baseline estimate of the difference
|\log Z(\hat{\Phi})-\log Z(\hat{\Psi})|
.
The principal difficulty here is to assess the impact of the additional clause on the pruning operation.
The issue is that the extra clause may also cause additional pruning.
Indeed, while clearly

\displaystyle F(\hat{\Psi})\setminus\{e\}\subseteq F(\hat{\Phi}),

i.e., any clause
a\neq e
that survives pruning on
\Psi
also remains present in
\hat{\Phi}
, the pruned formula
\hat{\Psi}
may ironically end up having strictly fewer clauses than
\hat{\Phi}
.

To get a handle on the potential repercussions of pruning, let
\{v,v^{\prime}\}=\partial e
be the variables that appear in clause
e
.
Let
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)
be the set of all literals
l
such that
\{v,\neg v\}\cap\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\left\{{l}\right\})\not=\emptyset
.
Thus,
PUC
may reach
v
or
\neg v
once
l
is deemed true.
Observe that
v\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)
and
\neg v\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)
.
Define
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})
analogously.
Further, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)=\bigcup_{l\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})}\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\left\{{l}\right\}).

(11.1)

Thus,
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
contains all literals that
PUC
can reach by tracing the implications of a literal from
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})
.
The definition of the sets
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)
,
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})
ensures that

\displaystyle v,\neg v

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v),

\displaystyle v^{\prime},\neg v^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime}).

(11.2)

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
\Phi
be a 2-CNF formula.
Suppose that
\Psi
is obtained from
\Phi
by adding a single clause
e
.
Then

\displaystyle\left|{\log(Z(\hat{\Phi}))-\log(Z(\hat{\Psi}))}\right|\leq\left|{\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)}\right|\log 2.

(11.3)

As a first step towards the proof of Lemma
11.1
we observe that (
11.1
) can be rewritten as follows.

Claim 11.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)=\bigcup_{l\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})}\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\}).

(11.4)

Proof.

Clearly
\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})\supseteq\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\left\{{l}\right\})
for every literal
l
.
Hence, we just need to show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }l\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime}).

(11.5)

Hence, let
l\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})
and let
l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})
.
Then Lemma
4.2
shows that there exists an implication chain

\displaystyle l=l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}=l^{\prime}

(11.6)

comprising literals
l_{i}
and clauses
a_{i}\in F(\Psi)
such that
a_{i}\equiv l_{i-1}\to l_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
If
a_{i}\neq e
for all
i
, then the chain (
11.6
) is contained in
\Phi
and thus
l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\{l\})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
.
Otherwise let
1\leq j\leq k
be the largest index such that
a_{j}=e
.
Then
l_{j}
is one of the constituent literals of
a_{j}
and thus
l_{j}\in\{v,\neg v,v^{\prime},\neg v^{\prime}\}
.
Furthermore, the implication chain
l_{j},a_{j+1},l_{j+1},\ldots,a_{k},l_{k}=l^{\prime}
from
l_{j}
to
l^{\prime}
is contained in
\Phi
.
Therefore, (
11.2
) shows in combination with Lemma
4.2
and (
11.1
) that
l^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\Phi,\{l_{j}\})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
.
∎

We proceed to show that
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
contains the variables of all clauses
a\in F(\Phi)
on which the pruning processes run on
\Phi,\Psi
differ.

Claim 11.3
.

For any clause
a\in F(\hat{\Phi})\setminus F(\hat{\Psi})
we have
\partial a\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
.

Proof.

Consider a clause
a\in F(\Phi)
that was removed by pruning applied to
\Psi
but not by pruning applied to
\Phi
.
Let
w,w^{\prime}
be the constituent literals of
a
, i.e.,
a\equiv w\vee w^{\prime}
.
Then
PUC
(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})
added
a
to the set
\mathcal{C}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})
of conflict clauses for some literal
l
.
Hence,

\displaystyle w,\neg w,w^{\prime},\neg w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\}).

(11.7)

Consequently, Lemma
4.2
shows that for each literal
k\in\{\neg w,\neg w^{\prime}\}
,
PUC
(\Psi,\{l\})
traverses an implication chain

l_{0,k}=l,a_{0,k},l_{1,k},a_{1,k},\ldots,l_{j_{k},k}=k

of literals
l_{i,k}\in\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\left\{{l}\right\})
and clauses
a_{i,k}\equiv\neg l_{i,k}\vee l_{i+1,k}\equiv l_{i,k}\to l_{i+1,k}
for
0\leq i<j_{k}
.
Because
a\not\in\mathcal{C}(\Phi,l)
, at least one of these two sequences includes the clause
e
and thus at least one of
v,\neg v
and one of
v^{\prime},\neg v^{\prime}
.
Hence,
l\in\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v)\cup\mathcal{N}(\Phi,v^{\prime})
.
Therefore, combining (
11.4
) and (
11.7
), we conclude that
\partial a=\{|w|,|w^{\prime}|\}\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
.
∎

Let
\tilde{\Phi}
be the formula obtained from
\hat{\Phi}
by removing all variables
x\in V(\hat{\Phi})
such that
\{x,\neg x\}\cap\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)\neq\emptyset
along with their adjacent clauses.

Claim 11.4
.

For any
\tilde{\sigma}\in S(\tilde{\Phi})
there exists
\sigma\in S(\hat{\Psi})
such that
\sigma_{x}=\tilde{\sigma}_{x}
for all

.

Proof.

Let
\check{\Psi}
be a CNF with variables

V(\check{\Psi})=V(\hat{\Psi})\setminus V(\tilde{\Phi})=\{x\in V(\hat{\Phi}):\{x,\neg x\}\cap\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)\neq\emptyset\}.

The clauses of
\check{\Psi}
include all
a\in F(\hat{\Psi})
such that
\partial a\subseteq V(\check{\Psi})
.
Additionally, for every clause
a\in F(\hat{\Psi})
that contains exactly one literal
l
with
|l|\in V(\check{\Psi})
we include the literal
l
as a unit clause into
\check{\Psi}
.
In light of Claim
11.3
, to prove the assertion it suffices to show that
\check{\Psi}
is satisfiable.
For then we could extend any
\sigma\in S(\tilde{\Phi})
to a satisfying assignment of
\hat{\Psi}
by simply setting the variables

in accordance with a satisfying assignment of
\check{\Psi}
.

As in the proof of Fact

, to construct a satisfying assignment of
\check{\Psi}
we fix an order
l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}
of the literals
\mathcal{N}(\Phi,e)
.
Let
\sigma_{i}
be the assignment that
PUC
outputs on input
\Psi,\{l_{i}\}
.
Further, define a
\{0,\pm 1\}
-valued assignment
(\sigma_{x})_{x\in V(\check{\Psi})}
by letting
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{i,x}
for the least index
i
such that
\{x,\neg x\}\cap\mathcal{L}(\Psi,l_{i})\neq\emptyset
.

We claim that

\displaystyle\forall a\in F(\check{\Psi})\,\exists x\in\partial_{\check{\Psi}}a\,:\,\sigma_{x}=\mathrm{sign}(x,a)\kern 5.0pt;

(11.8)

thus, we can turn
\sigma
into a satisfying assignment of
\check{\Psi}
by assigning those variables
y

\sigma_{y}=0
arbitrarily.
To verify (

), we consider two cases separately.

Case 1:
|\partial_{\check{\Psi}}a|=2
:

then

.
Let

and let

be the smallest index such that

.
Also let

Suppose that

.
Finally, the case

Case 2:

and literals

be the least index such that

If

(\Psi,\{l_{i}\})

\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\{l_{i}\})

.
But
|l^{\prime}|\not\in V(\check{\Psi})
.
Hence,
\{l,\neg l\}\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\{l_{i}\})=\{l\}
and thus
\sigma_{|l|}=\sigma_{i,|l|}=\mathrm{sign}_{\Psi}(|l|,b)=\mathrm{sign}_{\check{\Psi}}(|l|,a)
.

Thus, in either case

satisfies clause

.
∎

In perfect analogy to the above let
\tilde{\Psi}

.

be a CNF with variables

.

.
Moreover, for every

that contains exactly one literal

as a clause to
\check{\Phi}
.
As in the proof of Claim

Proof of Lemma

11.2.
A tail bound

As a next step we are going to derive a bound on the r.h.s. of (
11.3
) on random formulas.
More specifically, obtain the formula
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime}
from
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
by deleting the last clause
\boldsymbol{a}_{m}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{N}^{\prime}=|\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{a}_{m})|
.

Lemma 11.6
.

There exists
c=c(d)>0
such that for all
t>c
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{N}^{\prime}>t^{2}}\right]\leq c\exp(-t/c).

As a first step we are going to estimate the size of the set
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},x_{1})
that contains all literals
l
such that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)\cap\{x_{1},\neg x_{1}\}\neq\emptyset
.

Claim 11.7
.

There exists
c_{1}=c_{1}(d)>0
such that for all
t>c_{1}
we have
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},x_{1})|>t}\right]\leq c_{1}\exp(-t/c_{1}).

Proof.

We use a classical branching process argument.
Let
{\mathcal{R}}
be the set of literals
l
such that
x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)
.
By symmetry it suffices to bound
|{\mathcal{R}}|
.

For every
l\in{\mathcal{R}}
there exists an alternating sequence
l=l_{0},a_{1},l_{1},a_{2},\ldots,l_{k}=x_{1}
of literals and clauses such that
a_{i}\equiv\neg l_{i-1}\vee l_{i}
.
Flipping the negations along this sequence yields a reverse sequence

l_{0}^{\prime}=\neg x_{1}=\neg l_{k},a_{1}^{\prime}=a_{k},l_{1}^{\prime}=\neg l_{k-1},\ldots,l_{k}^{\prime}=\neg l
such that
a_{i}^{\prime}\equiv\neg l_{i-1}^{\prime}\vee l_{i}^{\prime}
.
Hence,
{\mathcal{R}}
is precisely the set of literals
l
that are reachable from
x_{1}
via such an alternating sequence
l_{0}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,l_{k}^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, for any literal
l
the expected number of clauses
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
such that
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\equiv l\vee l^{\prime}
for some other literal
l^{\prime}
equals
m/2n\sim d/2
.
Therefore,
|{\mathcal{R}}|
is stochastically dominated by the progeny of a branching process with offspring
{\rm Po}(d/2)
.
Standard branching process tail bounds therefore yield the desired bound on
|{\mathcal{R}}|
.
∎

Claim 11.8
.

There exists
c_{2}=c_{2}(d)>0
such that for all
t>c_{2}
and for every literal
l\neq x_{1}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|>t\mid x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)}\right]\leq c_{2}\exp(-t/c_{2}).

Proof.

We combine a branching process argument with Bayes’ formula.
Specifically, because the formula
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime}
is random, the set
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)\setminus\{l\}
is random given its size.
Hence, for an integer
\ell
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)\mid|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|=\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell-1}{2n-1}.

(11.9)

Furthermore, the size
|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|
is stochastically dominated by the progeny of a branching process with offspring
{\rm Po}(d/2)
.
Therefore, there exists
c_{2}^{\prime}=c_{2}^{\prime}(d)>0
such that for all
t>c_{2}^{\prime}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|>t}\right]\leq c_{2}^{\prime}\exp(-t/c_{2}^{\prime}).

(11.10)

Moreover, for any
d>0
there exists
c_{2}^{\prime\prime}=c_{2}^{\prime\prime}(d)>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)}\right]\geq c_{2}^{\prime\prime}/n.

(11.11)

Hence, combining (
11.9
)–(
11.11
) with Bayes’ rule, we obtain for
\ell>c_{2}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|=\ell\mid x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},\ell)\mid|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|=\ell}\right]{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|=\ell}\right]}{{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)}\right]}\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}}{c_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\ell\exp(-\ell/c_{2}^{\prime}),

which implies the assertion.
∎

Proof of Lemma
11.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}(\ell)
be the event that there exists
l\in\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},x_{1})\setminus\{x_{1}\}
such that
|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|>\ell
.
Claim
11.7
implies that there exists
c_{3}=c_{3}(d)>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)}\right]\leq c_{3}/n.

(11.12)

Hence, by Claim
11.8
, (
11.12
) and the union bound,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathfrak{R}(\ell)}\right]\leq\sum_{l\neq x_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{x_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l),\,|\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},l)|>\ell}\right]\leq 2c_{2}c_{3}\exp(-\ell/c_{2}).

(11.13)

Furthermore, Claim
11.7
shows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\prime},x_{1})\setminus\{x_{1}\}>\ell}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\exp(-\ell/c_{1}).

(11.14)

Combining (
11.13
) and (
11.14

Theorem 12.1

(
[
33
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,i},\mathfrak{F}_{n,i})_{0\leq i\leq m_{n},n\geq 1}

\boldsymbol{X}_{n,i}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,i}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,i-1}

\displaystyle\text{in probability},

(12.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}\boldsymbol{X}_{n,i}^{2}=\eta^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in probability},

(12.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\max_{1\leq i\leq m_{n}}\boldsymbol{X}_{n,i}^{2}}\right]\qquad

\displaystyle\text{is bounded in }n.

(12.3)

\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,m_{n}}

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We apply Theorem
12.1

(\mathfrak{F}_{n,M})_{0\leq M\leq m_{n}}

2.8

(\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,M}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{Z}_{n,M}}\right])_{M}

Proposition
2.12

) of Theorem

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\max_{1\leq M\leq m_{n}}\boldsymbol{X}_{n,M}^{2}}\right]
