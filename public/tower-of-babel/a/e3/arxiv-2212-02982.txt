All projections of a typical Cantor set are Cantor sets
Olga Frolkina

Abstract
In 1994, John Cobb asked: given N > m > k > 0 N>m>k>0 , does there exist a Cantor set in ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} such that each of its projections into m m -planes is exactly k k -dimensional? Such sets were described for ( N , m , k ) = ( 2 , 1 , 1 ) (N,m,k)=(2,1,1) by L. Antoine (1924) and for ( N , m , m ) (N,m,m) by K. Borsuk (1947). Examples were constructed for the cases ( 3 , 2 , 1 ) (3,2,1) by J. Cobb (1994), for ( N , m , m − 1 ) (N,m,m-1) and in a different way for ( N , N − 1 , N − 2 ) (N,N-1,N-2) by O. Frolkina (2010, 2019), for ( N , N − 1 , k ) (N,N-1,k) by S. Barov, J.J. Dijkstra and M. van der Meer (2012). We show that such sets are exceptional in the following sense. Let 𝒞 ⁡ ( ℝ N ) \mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N}) be a set of all Cantor subsets of ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} endowed with the Hausdorff metric. It is known that 𝒞 ⁡ ( ℝ N ) \mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N}) is a Baire space. We prove that there is a dense G δ G_{\delta} subset 𝒫 ⊂ 𝒞 ⁡ ( ℝ N ) \mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N}) such that for each X ∈ 𝒫 X\in\mathcal{P} and each non-zero linear subspace L ⊂ ℝ N L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N} , the orthogonal projection of X X into L L is a Cantor set. This gives a partial answer to another question of J. Cobb stated in the same paper (1994). Keywords: Euclidean space, projection, Cantor set, dimension, Baire category theorem. MSC: 54E52, 54C25.

Introduction and
Statements of Main Results

Any topological space homeomorphic to
the standard middle-thirds Cantor set

\mathcal{C}\subset I=[0,1]

is called a Cantor set.
In 1884, G. Cantor
described a
continuous surjection of
\mathcal{C}
onto
I
;
it takes a point

\frac{x_{1}}{3}+\frac{x_{2}}{3^{2}}+\ldots\in\mathcal{C}

(where each

x_{i}\in\{0;2\}
)
into the point

\frac{x_{1}}{2}+\frac{x_{2}}{2^{2}}+\ldots\in I

[
6
, p. 255–256]
.
In
[
23
]
,
L. Zoretti
recalls
as a known fact
(“ce resultat bien connu etant acquis…”)
that
the graph
of any such surjection
is a Cantor set in plane
whose projection to
y
-axis coincides with
I
.
Taking an appropriate union of such sets,
he constructed
a Cantor set in plane
such that its projections
to a countable set of lines
contain segments

[
23
, p. 763]
.
Zoretti made an attempt
to describe a Cantor
set in plane all of whose projections
contain segments
[
23
, p. 763]

(see also
[
24
]
);
unfortunately it is not clear from the text why this set does not contain arcs.
(The purpose of Zoretti’s note was to
disprove a statement made by F. Riesz who wrote in
[
22
, p. 651]

that a projection of a closed
hereditarily disconnected subset of plane
to a straight line is again closed and
hereditarily disconnected.
F. Riesz attributed this proposition to R. Baire; this is incorrect because the work of Baire contained a different statement.)

L. Antoine
described
a Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

all of
whose projections coincide
with those of a regular hexagon

[
1
, p. 272,
9
; p. 273, fig. 2]
.
Other examples
of Cantor sets in plane all of whose projections
are segments
can be found in

[
7
, p. 124, Example]
,

[
8
, Prop. 1]
.

K. Borsuk
[
5
]
constructed a
Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geqslant 2
, such that its projection to every hyperplane contains an
(N-1)
-dimensional ball
(see
[
9
, Prop. 3.1]
for an alternative proof).
As a corollary,
for any
m\leqslant N-1

the projection of this set to each

m
-plane
has dimension
m
.
Extensions of this result can be found in

[
20
]
,

[
14
]
,

[
18
]
.
In contrast to the case of plane,
for
N\geqslant 3
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

there is no Cantor set
all of whose projections to
(N-1)
-planes
are convex bodies

[
7
, Thm. 3]
;
generalizations are obtained in

[
9
, Thm. 4.7]
,

[
2
, Thm. 1]
.

J. Cobb
[
7
, Thm. 1]
gave an example of a Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose projection to
every
2
-plane is
1
-dimensional.
He asked
[
7
, p. 126]
:
given
N>m>k>0
, does there exist a Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
each of its projections
into
m
-planes is exactly
k
-dimensional?
(As in
[
3
]
, call it the
case

(N,m,k)
of Cobb’s problem.)
For the cases
(N,m,m-1)
and
(N,N-1,k)
,
examples are given in

[
10
, Thm. 1]

and

[
3
, Thm. 1]
, respectively.
I recently remarked that
a well-known Antoine’s Necklace can serve as
an example of a
Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

all of whose projections are one-dimensional and
connected
[
11
]
; further,
each Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
can be moved
by a small
ambient isotopy
to an
(N,N-1,N-2)
-set

[
12
]
.

For a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
Cobb suggested a notion of

general position with respect to all projections

[
7
, p. 127]
.
In some sense,
the behaviour of such sets
under projections
is
as close as possible to that of embeddings;
all projections of such a Cantor set
are again Cantor sets.
In
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geqslant 2
,
Cobb
constructed
a Cantor set in
general position with respect to all projections

[
7
, Thm. 5]
.
Cobb
asked
[
7
, p. 128]
:
“Cantor sets that raise dimension under all projections
and those in general position with respect to all projections are
both dense in the Cantor sets in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
—
which (if either) is more common, in the sense of
category or dimension or anything?”
Our main result provides a partial answer:
all projections
of a typical Cantor set in

\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geqslant 1
,
are Cantor sets.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
N\geqslant 1
,
in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

there is
a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{P}

such that
for each
X\in\mathcal{P}
and
each non-zero linear subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the orthogonal projection
of
X
into
L
is a Cantor set.

Here, the set

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of all Cantor sets in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

is endowed with the Hausdorff metric;
it is
a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
of the space

\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of all non-empty
compact subsets
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

(in
[
13
, p. 1089]
,
this fact is called “well known”;
for a proof, see

[
19
, Lemma 1]
or
[
13
, Lemma 2.1, Remark 2.2]
).
Thus
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is a Baire space.

By the trivial projection we mean the projection into
the subspace
\{0\}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

For
N\geqslant 1
,
there is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

such that each
X\in\mathcal{A}

is a Cantor set
all of whose non-trivial projections are Cantor sets.

Denote by
\operatorname{Emb}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{R}^{N})

the space of all embeddings of the
Cantor set
\mathcal{C}

into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
endowed
with the distance

\rho(f,g)=\sup\{d(f(x),g(x))\mid x\in\mathcal{C}\}
;
it is a Baire space

[
16
, Thm. V 2]
.
The evaluation map

\mathcal{E}:\operatorname{Emb}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,

f\mapsto f(\mathcal{C})

is an open continuous surjection
(Lemma
5.3
);
hence Theorem
1.1

implies

Corollary 1.3
.

For
N\geqslant 1
,
in
\operatorname{Emb}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{R}^{N})

there is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset

\mathcal{B}

such that
for each
f\in\mathcal{B}
,
any non-trivial
projection
of
f(\mathcal{C})

is a Cantor set.

Cantor sets are exactly
non-empty metric zero-dimensional
perfect compacta

[
17
, 7.4]
.
Thus
Theorem
1.1
follows
from three
statements
(here
p_{L}X
denotes the orthogonal projection of
X

into
L
):

Statement 1.4
.

For
N\geqslant 1
,
let

\mathcal{D}
be
the set of all

X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

such that

|p_{L}X|=1

for some non-zero linear subspace

L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Then
\mathcal{D}

is closed and nowhere dense in

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Statement 1.5
.

For
N\geqslant 1
, let
\mathcal{I}

be the set of all

X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

such that
for some non-zero linear subspace

L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
holds:

|p_{L}X|>1
and
p_{L}X
has an isolated point.
Then
\mathcal{I}

is a meager
F_{\sigma}
subset
of

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Statement 1.6
.

For
N\geqslant 1
,
there is
a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

\dim p_{L}X=0

for each
X\in\mathcal{Z}
and
each linear subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

To obtain Theorem
1.1
, put

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{I}
.

Remark 1.7
.

We restrict ourselves by orthogonal projections only for simplicity of exposition and
notation.
In fact, suppose that

\mathbb{R}^{N}=L_{1}\oplus L_{2}

and take any set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The projection of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

into
L_{1}
parallel to
L_{2}

maps
X
to a set
affinely homeomorphic to the image
of
X
under the orthogonal projection
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
into
L_{2}^{\bot}
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a non-empty topological space.
A set
A\subset X
is called

meager in
X
, or
of first category in
X
,
if it is the union of countably many
nowhere dense subsets of
X
.
A set
A
that is not of first category in
X
is said to be

non-meager in
X
or

of second category in
X
.
A set
A\subset X
is called
comeager in
X

or

residual in
X

if it is the complement of a meager set;
equivalently, if
it contains the intersection
of a countable family of dense open sets.
If the space
X
is of first category (in itself),
then each of its subsets is of first category
in
X
;
hence the two last definitions make sense only
if
X
is of second category (in itself).

A
Baire space

is a topological space
X
with the property:
every non-empty open set in
X
is of second category in
X
;
equivalently, every comeager set is dense in
X
;
equivalently,
for each countable family
\{U_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}

of open dense subsets of
X
, their intersection

\bigcap\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}U_{n}

is dense in
X
.
Any Baire space is of
second category in itself.

Baire’s Theorem
states that

a completely metrizable space is Baire

(see e.g.
[
17
, 8.4]
for a proof).
A
G_{\delta}
subset of a completely metrizable
space is itself completely metrizable

[
17
, 3.11]
.

For a non-empty Baire space
X

and its subset
P
,
we say that

a generic (or typical) element of
X
is in
P

or

most elements of
X
are in
P

if
P
is comeager in
X
;
this makes sense since in such a space,
it is impossible that
“most elements are in
P
”
and “most elements are in
X-P
”
simultaneously.
Any dense
G_{\delta}
subset of a Baire space
X
is comeager in
X
.

Notation

For a linear subspace
L
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
denote by
p_{L}
the orthogonal projection
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
into
L
.
A projection
p_{L}
is called non-trivial if

L\neq\{0\}
.

The Euclidean distance
between points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is denoted by
d(x,y)
;
in general, for any
non-empty subsets
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
we define

d(X,Y)=\inf\{d(x,y)\mid x\in X,\ y\in Y\}
.
For a non-empty set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
let
O(X,\varepsilon)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid d(y,X)<\varepsilon\}
.
In particular,
O(a,\varepsilon)

is the open ball with center
a

and radius
\varepsilon
.
By
B(a,\varepsilon)
we denote
the closed ball with center
a

and radius
\varepsilon
.
(The dimension
N
of the ambient space
is not reflected in the notation

O(a,\varepsilon)
and
B(a,\varepsilon)
;
it is usually clear from the context.)

The Hausdorff distance between
non-empty compacta
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

is
d_{H}(X,Y)=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\mid X\subset O(Y,\varepsilon)\ \text{and}\ Y\subset O(X,\varepsilon)\}
.
(Equivalently,

d_{H}(X,Y)<\varepsilon

iff

X\subset O(Y,\varepsilon)

and

Y\subset O(X,\varepsilon)
.)

By
G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})

we denote the Grassman space
consisting
of all
\ell
-dimensional linear subspaces of

\mathbb{R}^{N}
;
the distance
between
L_{1}
and
L_{2}

is defined
as the Hausdorff distance

d_{H}(L_{1}\cap S,L_{2}\cap S)
, where
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the unit sphere

[
15
]
.
The space
G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})
is compact

[
4
, Cor. 6.2, Cor. 6.7]
.

The space

\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of all non-empty
compact subsets
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

is endowed with the Hausdorff metric
(equivalently, one may use the Vietoris topology

[
17
, 4.F, 4.21]
);
this space is complete

[
17
, 4.25]
.

Any topological space
homeomorphic to the usual middle-thirds Cantor set
is called a Cantor set.
(These are exactly non-empty metric zero-dimensional perfect compacta

[
17
, 7.4]
.)

The set

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

of all Cantor sets in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

is
a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

(in
[
13
, p. 1089]
,
this fact is called “well known”;
the proof can be found in

[
19
, Lemma 1]
or
[
13
, Lemma 2.1, Remark 2.2]
).
Hence
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a Baire space.
Although the Hausdorff metric
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is not complete,
we may — and will — use it to prove
purely topological statements (concerning e.g.
openness, nowhere density, etc.).

By
\operatorname{Emb}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{R}^{N})
we denote
the space of all embeddings of the
Cantor set
\mathcal{C}

into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
with the distance

\rho(f,g)=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{C}}d(f(x),g(x))
;
it is a Baire space.

For a topological manifold-with-boundary

M
,
denote by

\operatorname{Int}M

and
\partial M

the interior and the boundary of
M
, correspondingly.

A system of points

x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

is said to be
in general position
,
if each of its subsystems
of
k+1
points
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}

(
k\leqslant N
) is affinely independent,
see
[
21
]
.
Any finite system of points of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

can be brought into general position
by an arbitrarily small displacement

[
21
, Thm. 2]
.
For any finite system of points

x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}

of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in general position,
there exists an
\varepsilon>0

such that

d(x_{i},y_{i})<\varepsilon
,
i=0,\ldots,m

implies that the system

y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}

is in general position
[
21
, Thm. 1]
.

Finally,
|A|
denotes the cardinality of a set
A
.

3
Proofs of Statements
1.4

and
1.5

We start with two useful lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
.
1) For each
linear subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

and each pair of non-empty compact sets
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

we have

d_{H}(p_{L}X,p_{L}Y)\leqslant d_{H}(X,Y)
.

2) Let
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}L_{n}=L
in the space
G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Then

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X,p_{L}X)=0

for each non-empty compactum
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

3) For each

\ell=0,\ldots,N

the map

\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\quad(X,L)\mapsto p_{L}X

is continuous.

Proof.
1)
For each
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

holds

d(p_{L}x,p_{L}y)\leqslant d(x,y)
;
this implies the proposition.

2) The statement can be easily verified when

X
is
a closed

N
-dimensional
ball
B(a,r)
.
In general case, for each

\varepsilon>0

take a finite family

B_{1},\ldots,B_{t}

of closed balls
such that

X\subset\cup_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}\subset O\left(X,{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)
.
There exists an integer
n_{0}
such that

d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}B_{i},p_{L}B_{i})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

for each

n\geqslant n_{0}
and each

i=1,\ldots,t
.
Hence

p_{L_{n}}B_{i}\subset O\left(p_{L}B_{i},{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)

and

p_{L}B_{i}\subset O\left(p_{L_{n}}B_{i},{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)
;
this implies

p_{L_{n}}X\subset p_{L_{n}}\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}\right)=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}p_{L_{n}}B_{i}\subset\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}O\left(p_{L}B_{i},{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)=O\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}p_{L}B_{i},{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)=

=O\left(p_{L}\left(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}\right),{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)\subset O\left(p_{L}\left(O\left(X,{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)\right),{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)\subset O(p_{L}X,{\varepsilon}).

Similarly,

p_{L}X\subset O(p_{L_{n}}X,\varepsilon)
.
We get

d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X,p_{L}X)<\varepsilon
.

3) Suppose that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(X_{n},L_{n})=(X,L)
in

\mathcal{K}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
We have

d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X_{n},p_{L}X)\leqslant d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X_{n},p_{L_{n}}X)+d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X,p_{L}X)\leqslant

\leqslant d_{H}(X_{n},X)+d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X,p_{L}X);

this tends to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
.
For
any
\varepsilon>0

and any non-empty compactum

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

there exists a finite set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

in general position
such that

d_{H}(K,A)<\varepsilon

and

|A|\geqslant N+1
.

Proof.
Let
F\subset K

be
a finite
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
-net
of
K
;
we may assume that
|F|\geqslant N+1
.
Moving each point of
F

at a distance
less than
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

from its original position,
construct
a set

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

such that
|F|=|A|
,

A
is in general position,
and
d_{H}(A,F)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
We have

d_{H}(K,A)\leqslant d_{H}(K,F)+d_{H}(F,A)<\varepsilon
;
thus
A
is the desired set.

Proof of Statement
1.4
.

Closedness.

Take

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

such that

d_{H}(X_{n},X)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}

let
L_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an

\ell
-dimensional linear subspace
such that

|p_{L_{n}}X_{n}|=1
.
The Grassman space

G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})

is compact;
we may therefore assume
(passing to a subsequence if necessary)
that
there exists an
L\in G(\ell,\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

L_{n}\to L
as
n\to\infty
.
By 3) of Lemma
3.1

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{H}(p_{L_{n}}X_{n},p_{L}X)=0
.
Recall that
|p_{L_{n}}X_{n}|=1
;
we easily get
|p_{L}X|=1
.

Nowhere density.

Take any
X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

and any
\varepsilon>0
.
By Lemma
3.2

there exists a finite set
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

in general position
such that

d_{H}(X,A)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

and

t=|A|\geqslant N+1
.

Take a number
r>0

such that:

r<\frac{1}{2}\min\{d(x,y)\mid x,y\in A;x\neq y\}
;
r<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
;
and for any choice of points

b_{1}\in O(a_{1},r),\ldots,b_{t}\in O(a_{t},r)

the set
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}

is in general position.

For each
i=1,\ldots,t

take a Cantor set

K_{i}\subset O(a_{i},r)
.
Let
K=K_{1}\cup\ldots\cup K_{t}
;
this is a Cantor set.
We have

d_{H}(K,A)<r<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
hence

d_{H}(X,K)\leqslant d_{H}(X,A)+d_{H}(A,K)<\varepsilon
.

Choose a positive number
\delta
such that

\delta<\min\limits_{i=1,\ldots,t}d\left(K_{i},\partial(O(a_{i},r))\right)
.
Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

be any Cantor set
with
d_{H}(Y,K)<\delta
.
Let us show that

Y\notin\mathcal{D}
.
Suppose that
|p_{L}Y|=1

for some non-zero linear subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof of Statement

We need some auxiliary results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{q}\}
be
a set of pairwise distinct points in

\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
{D\geqslant 1}
.
Suppose that

-dimensional

,
where

is a positive integer.
Then there exists an integer

and
an ordered set
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{s})

of pairwise distinct integers such that:

for each

By assumption, there exist points

Take any
i_{1},\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,q\}

i_{1}
and

Moreover, we have

Take the sequence of sets

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{\alpha\};

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}\mid B(p_{j},\delta)\cap B(p_{\alpha},\delta)\neq\emptyset\};

\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}\mid\exists m\in\mathcal{A}_{1}:B(p_{j},\delta)\cap B(p_{m},\delta)\neq\emptyset\};

\mathcal{A}_{3}=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}\mid\exists m\in\mathcal{A}_{2}:B(p_{j},\delta)\cap B(p_{m},\delta)\neq\emptyset\};

Since

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{A}_{n+1}=\mathcal{A}_{n+2}=\ldots

Connectedness of

The index

.

Take the minimal integer
m_{1}

such that

i_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{m_{1}}

By the definition of

\mathcal{A}_{m_{1}}

there exists

we have

i_{2}\neq i_{1}
.
Let
m_{2}
be the minimal possible
integer

with the property

i_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{m}
.
Continue in similar way.
Find an index
i_{3}\in\mathcal{A}_{m_{2}-1}

i_{3}\neq i_{2}

and
i_{3}\neq i_{1}

Denote by

The ordered set

We have

\delta<\frac{1}{3}\min\{d(X_{i},X_{j})\mid i,j=1,\ldots,s;\ i\neq j\}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

The neighborhoods

O(X_{i},\delta)
,
i=1,\ldots,s

are pairwise disjoint,
and

\operatorname{diam}O(X_{i},\delta)\leqslant\operatorname{diam}X_{i}+2\delta<\varepsilon

Let

We have

K\subset O(f(\mathcal{C}),\delta)=O(X_{1},\delta)\sqcup\ldots\sqcup O(X_{s},\delta)

.

Now take any Cantor set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

such that

d_{H}(f(\mathcal{C}),K)<\delta
.
For each

\mathcal{C}=f^{-1}(X_{1})\sqcup\ldots\sqcup f^{-1}(X_{s})

For any

h_{i}:f^{-1}(X_{i})\cong K_{i}
.
Define
an embedding

g:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

g|_{f^{-1}(X_{i})}=h_{i}

we get

\mathcal{C}=f^{-1}(X_{1})\sqcup\ldots\sqcup f^{-1}(X_{s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g=h_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup h_{s}}}{{\to}}K_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup K_{s}=K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}.

We have

g(\mathcal{C})=K
.
The inclusions

f(f^{-1}(X_{i}))\cup g(f^{-1}(X_{i}))=X_{i}\cup K_{i}\subset O(X_{i},\delta),\quad i=1,\ldots,s

together with the inequality

\operatorname{diam}O(X_{i},\delta)<\varepsilon

imply that

\rho(g,f)<\varepsilon
.
Thus
g
is the required embedding.
