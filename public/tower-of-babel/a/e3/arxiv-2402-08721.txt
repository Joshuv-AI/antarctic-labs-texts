Beyond unital noise in variational quantum algorithms: noise-induced barren plateaus and limit sets
Phattharaporn Singkanipa, Daniel A. Lidar

Abstract
Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) hold much promise but face the challenge of exponentially small gradients. Unmitigated, this barren plateau (BP) phenomenon leads to an exponential training overhead for VQAs. Perhaps the most pernicious are noise-induced barren plateaus (NIBPs), a type of unavoidable BP arising from open system effects, which have so far been shown to exist for unital noise maps. Here, we generalize the study of NIBPs to more general completely positive, trace-preserving maps, investigating the existence of NIBPs in the unital case and a class of non-unital maps we call Hilbert-Schmidt (HS)-contractive. The latter includes amplitude damping. We identify the associated phenomenon of noise-induced limit sets (NILS) of the VQA cost function and prove its existence for both unital and HS-contractive non-unital noise maps. Along the way, we extend the parameter shift rule of VQAs to the noisy setting. We provide rigorous bounds in terms of the relevant variables that give rise to NIBPs and NILSs, along with numerical simulations of the depolarizing and amplitude-damping maps that illustrate our analytical results.

VQA and PSR

We adopt the variational quantum algorithm (VQA) framework of Ref.

2.2
Nice operator basis and Schatten
p
-norms

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
d<\infty
. The space of bounded linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Let
\mathcal{M}(d,\mathbb{F})
denote the vector space of
d\times d
matrices with coefficients
in
\mathbb{F}
, where
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. For our purposes it suffices to identify
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\mathcal{M}(d,\mathbb{C})
. The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product is
\langle A,B\rangle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)
for any two operators
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

We define a “nice operator basis” as a set
\{F_{j}\}_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, where
F_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I
,
\mathrm{Tr}(F_{j})=0

\forall j\geq 1
, that in addition satisfies the following properties:

F_{j}=F_{j}^{\dagger},\ \ \langle F_{j},F_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(F_{j}F_{k})=\delta_{jk}\quad\forall j,k.

(7)

The normalized Pauli strings
\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}P_{j}\}_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}
(where
d=2^{n}
) are a convenient explicit choice for the nice operator basis.
Another convenient choice is the set of generalized
d\times d
Gell-Mann matrices
[
53
,
54
]
, normalized such that
\mathrm{Tr}(F_{j}F_{k})=\delta_{jk}
is satisfied.

Let
|A|\equiv\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
. Recall that the Schatten
p
-norm
\|A\|_{p}
is given, for
1\leq p<\infty
, by the
p
-norm of the singular values
\sigma_{i}
of the operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
:

\|A\|_{p}=\mathrm{Tr}(|A|^{p})^{1/p}=(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{p})^{1/p}.

(8)

\|A\|_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}(|A|)
is the trace norm (sum of the singular values),
\|A\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(|A|^{2})}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt or Frobenius norm, and
\|A\|_{\infty}
is the operator norm (largest singular value). Without risk of confusion, we use
\|A\|
to denote
\|A\|_{\infty}
and also use
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
to denote the Euclidian norm (i.e.,

-norm) of any
vector

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{H}
from hereon.

2.3
The coherence vector

Quantum states are represented by density operators
\rho\in\mathcal{B}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
(positive trace-class operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
) with unit trace:
\mathrm{Tr}\rho=1
.
Elements of
\mathcal{B}[\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})]
, i.e., linear transformations
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, are called superoperators, or maps.

Complete positivity of a superoperator
\mathcal{N}
is equivalent to the statement that
\mathcal{N}
has a Kraus representation
[
55
]
:
\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

\mathcal{N}(X)=\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}XK_{\alpha}^{\dagger},

(9)

where the
\{K_{\alpha}\}
are called Kraus operators. When they satisfy
\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}K_{\alpha}=I
, the map
\mathcal{N}
is trace-preserving.

The density operator can be expanded in an arbitrary nice operator basis as

\rho=\frac{1}{d}I+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}-1}v_{j}F_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}F_{0}+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{F},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{F}=\{F_{1},\dots,F_{d^{2}-1}\}^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{d^{2}-1}\}
is called the
coherence vector
.

We summarize two well-known facts about the coherence vector. First, the purity

P\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{2}=\langle\rho,\rho\rangle=\|\rho\|_{2}^{2}

(11)

is bounded by

\displaystyle 0\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=\left(P-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{1/2}<1\ .

(12)

See
Appendix

A
for a proof.

Second,
let
\mathcal{M}(d,F)
denote the vector space of
d\times d
matrices with coefficients in the field
F
.

Proposition 1
.

The CPTP map
\rho^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(\rho)
is equivalent to the affine coherence vector transformation

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=M\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{c},

(13)

where
M\in\mathcal{M}(d^{2}-1,\mathbb{R})
and
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}-1}
have elements given by

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle F_{i},\mathcal{N}(F_{j})\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(F_{i}K_{\alpha}F_{j}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger})

(14a)

\displaystyle c_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\langle F_{i},\mathcal{N}(I)\rangle=\frac{1}{{d}}\sum_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(F_{i}K_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}^{\dagger})\ .

(14b)

See
Appendix

B
for a proof.

The Gell-Mann matrices reduce to the standard Pauli matrices for
d=2
, normalized such that
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}/\sqrt{2}=(\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z})/\sqrt{2}
.
Therefore, in the case of single qubit, we can write
\rho
in the well-known form

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(I+\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}F_{0}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{F},

(15)

where
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}/\sqrt{2}
.
Note that
\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\|\leq 1
, which is the convention for the Bloch sphere representation. We avoid this normalization and instead use the nice operator basis convention from here on, even for
d=2
, so that
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
[
Eq.

12
].

2.4
Unital maps

A unital map
\mathcal{N}
is defined as satisfying

, hence

Lemma 1

Unital CPTP maps are purity non-increasing:

, where
P
and
P^{\prime}
are, respectively, the purity of
\rho

\rho^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(\rho)
. Equality holds for all

See
Appendix

C

Lemma 2
.

For unital CPTP maps
\mathcal{N}
we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(16a)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle=\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|.

(16b)

Equality in
Eq.

holds for all
\boldsymbol{v}
iff
\mathcal{N}
is unitary, in which case

is norm-preserving (hence orthogonal).

Proof.

To prove

, note that it follows from

that

.

To prove

, note that from

12
and

we have
\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|=\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\|=\sqrt{P^{\prime}-1/d}

. If

2.5
Non-unital maps

From here on, when we consider non-unital noise maps we restrict our analysis to the following class, which are contractive under the Hilbert-Schmidt (HS) norm:

Definition 1
.

A (finite-dimensional) map
\mathcal{N}
is called
HS-contractive
if
\exists r<1
s.t. for all states
\rho_{1}\neq\rho_{2}
we have
\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})\|_{2}\leq r\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{2}
.

This definition of contractivity is different from the standard one for CPTP maps, which are well known to be contractive under the trace norm, i.e.,
\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})\|_{1}\leq\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{1}
for any pair of states
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
and any CPTP map
\mathcal{N}
, including all non-unital maps
[
56
]
. Other notions of contractivity also exist, such as for general positive maps between matrix spaces that include the zero element, in which case a non-unital map is always non-contractive
[
57
]
; see
Appendix

D
for details.

Lemma 3
.

A map is HS-contractive if and only if its matrix
M
satisfies
\|M\|<1
.

Proof.

In terms of a nice operator basis and the corresponding coherence vector, we can write
\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{2}=\|(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\|_{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
and using the properties of
\{F_{i}\}
in
Eq.

7
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
.
Let
\rho^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(\rho)
and
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=M\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{c}
. We can write

\|\rho^{\prime}_{1}-\rho^{\prime}_{2}\|_{2}=\|(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\|_{2}=\|(M\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-M\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\|_{2}=\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|
.

(
\Rightarrow
) By definition of
\mathcal{N}
being HS-contractive we have
\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|=\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})\|_{2}\leq r\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{2}=r\|\boldsymbol{v}\|<\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
,
\forall\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. This is true in particular for the vector
\boldsymbol{v}
that achieves the supremum in
\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|/\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=\|M\|
, which implies that
\|M\|<1
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Let
r=\|M\|
. By assumption,
r<1
. We have
\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq\|M\|\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=r\|\boldsymbol{v}\|
for any
\boldsymbol{v}
. Taking two arbitrary density matrices
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
with respective coherence vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
and applying this to
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, we obtain
\|\mathcal{N}(\rho_{1})-\mathcal{N}(\rho_{2})\|_{2}\leq r\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{2}
.
∎

Using the polar decomposition, let us decompose
M
in
Eq.

14
as
M=OS
, where
O
is orthogonal and
S=|M|
is positive semidefinite. Let
\sigma_{\max/\min}(M)
denote the largest/smallest singular value of
M
(the largest/smallest eigenvalue of

). We interpret

as a rotation,

as a dilation, and

[

13
] as an affine shift.

Lemma 4
.

For HS-contractive non-unital CPTP maps
\mathcal{N}
, we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{0}

(17a)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{c}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{1-1/d}

(17b)

\displaystyle\|M\|<1

(17c)

\displaystyle\|M\boldsymbol{v}\|<\|\boldsymbol{v}\|,\forall\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}.

(17d)

Lemma 5

Any single-qubit (

Corollary 1

If
\mathcal{N}
is an HS-contractive non-unitary CPTP map and

is unitary, with corresponding coherence vector transformations

3

The PSR given in

This section presents a brief rederivation for the noiseless setting, which we then adapt to accommodate scenarios involving control noise and random unitary noise. We also bound the gradient of the cost function in both of these cases.

3.1
The noiseless PSR case

For simplicity (and w.l.o.g., but at the expense of increasing the circuit depth), let us assume that each of the gate Hamiltonians

[

4

\exp(-\frac{i}{2}\theta_{lm}H_{lm})=\exp(-\frac{i}{2}\theta_{\mu}P_{j(\mu)})\equiv U(\theta_{\mu}),

(19)

where
\mu=(l,m)
is the location of the gate in the circuit (the
m
’th gate in the
l
’th layer), we wrote

since the Pauli string type depends on the location
\mu
, and we dropped the subscript

on

since, in the calculation below, the type of Pauli string will not matter. From now on we sometimes also write

Recall

1

\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a},\theta_{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b}\}

and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a}

Anticipating the derivative with respect to

, we rewrite the cost function as follows:

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\mathrm{Tr}[H\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta})(\rho_{0})]

(20a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[H\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{a})\circ\mathcal{U}(\theta_{\mu})\circ\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{b})(\rho_{0})]

(20b)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}(\theta_{\mu})(\tilde{\rho})],

(20c)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{H}

3.2
Control noise

Now consider adding a small perturbation to the ideal Pauli generator of a gate, i.e.,

In analogy to

where

We may now write the noisy version of the gate as:

where the prime indicates the presence of noise.
Note that this noise model includes both under/over-rotation and axis-angle errors. In the former case

Using

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})(\tilde{\rho})]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})([P_{j(\mu)}+A_{j(\mu)},\tilde{\rho}])].

(27b)

Expanding

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}=-\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})([P_{j}+A_{j},\tilde{\rho}])]

(28a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})([P_{j},\tilde{\rho}])]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}a_{jk}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})([P_{k},\tilde{\rho}])]

(28b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})\left(\mathcal{U}_{j}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(\tilde{\rho})-\mathcal{U}_{j}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(\tilde{\rho})\right)]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}a_{jk}\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{H}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\theta_{\mu})\times

(28c)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left(\mathcal{U}_{k}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(\tilde{\rho})-\mathcal{U}_{k}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(\tilde{\rho})\right)],

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{+}_{j(\mu)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-}_{j(\mu)},

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{j(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{j(\mu)}^{+}-\tilde{\rho}_{j(\mu)}^{-}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j(\mu)}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}.

Substituting

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}

3.3
Random unitary noise

The noise model in

3.2

\sum_{k=0}^{d^{2}-1}a_{jk}P_{k}\otimes B_{k}+H_{B}

\{B_{k}\}

\{p_{k},U_{k}\}

U_{k}=\exp(-i\theta_{\mu,k}P_{k}/2)

\sum_{k\neq j}p_{k}\ll p_{j}

).

For simplicity, we may assume that
\theta_{\mu,k}=\theta_{\mu}
, since the case
\theta_{\mu,k}=\theta_{\mu}+(\Delta\theta)_{k}
was already accounted for in
Section

3.2

U_{k}
is implicitly
U_{k}(\theta_{\mu})
, which has the same angle dependence as
U_{l}(\theta_{\mu})
for
k\neq l
.

This noise model can be written in the Kraus representation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}U_{k}XU_{k}^{\dagger}

(33a)

\displaystyle=p_{j}\mathcal{U}(X)+\sum_{k\neq j}p_{k}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}(X),

(33b)

where
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}(X)=U_{k}XU_{k}^{\dagger}
for
k\neq j
and
\{\sqrt{p_{k}}U_{k}\}
are the Kraus operators.

Using a similar approach as in the derivation of

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq p_{j(\mu)}\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}\right|

(34)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\neq j}p_{k(\mu)}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k(\mu)}\|\|\boldsymbol{h}\|.

Details are given in
Appendix

F
.
The conclusion regarding this bound is similar to the case discussed in
Section

3.2
.

3.4
Bounds on
\|\boldsymbol{h}\|

Both
Eqs.

32
and
34
involve the Hamiltonian norm
\|\boldsymbol{h}\|
, so we next bound this quantity.

Consider the scaling of the HS norm of the problem Hamiltonian. Writing such Hamiltonians as
H=\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}h_{j}F_{j}
, we have
\|H\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}h_{j}^{2}=h_{0}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{h}\|^{2}
. In practice, we choose the nice operator basis
\{F_{j}\}
as the normalized Pauli basis,
\{P_{j}\}/\sqrt{d}
.

Assume that the Pauli strings are ordered by Hamming weight
k
.
We say that
H
is
K
-local if
h_{j}=0
for
j>K
with
K
a constant independent of
n
. The number of non-zero
h_{j}
terms in
\boldsymbol{h}
is at most
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\binom{n}{k}
, and a crude upper bound for
K\leq n/2
(which holds in the
K
-local case) is

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\binom{n}{k}

\displaystyle\leq K\max_{k\in[0,K]}\binom{n}{k}=K\binom{n}{K}

(35a)

\displaystyle=K\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-K+1)}{K!}

(35b)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{K}}{(K-1)!}.

(35c)

Thus,

\|\boldsymbol{h}\|\leq\frac{h}{\sqrt{(K-1)!}}n^{K/2},\quad h\equiv\max_{j>0}h_{j}.

(36)

4
Cost Function Concentration and Noise-Induced Limit Sets

Under the unital map setting of generalized Pauli noise, Ref.
[
31
]
has shown that the cost function concentrates. We now extend this to the action of HS-contractive non-unital noise maps and show furthermore that a new phenomenon of noise-induced limit sets arises in this context.

4.1
Cost function concentration under non-unital noise

Expanding
H
in a nice operator basis, we have:

H=\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}h_{j}F_{j}=h_{0}F_{0}+\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{F},

(37)

so that
\boldsymbol{h}
collects the coordinates of the traceless component of
H
. The cost function can be written as:

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}.

(38)

We already showed that when the noise is part of the gate, the gradient of the noisy cost function is bounded as in
Eqs.

32
and
34
. We now consider noise between gate applications, which we model as a concatenation of non-unitary CPTP maps
\mathcal{N}_{l}
after applying all the noisy gates in the
l
’th layer:

\rho_{l+1}=[\mathcal{N}_{l+1}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l+1})](\rho_{l})\quad\forall l\geq 0.

(39)

I.e., for the evolution of a circuit of depth
L
, with the initial state
\rho_{0}
, we have

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\equiv\rho_{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=[\mathcal{N}_{L}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{L})\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})](\rho_{0}),

(40)

where, as before
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, and
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})
denotes the noisy unitary superoperator in the
l

Eq.

26

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{l}
be the coherence vector corresponding to
\rho_{l}
in
Eq.

39

Let us denote the transformed coherence vector after
\mathcal{N}_{l}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})

(41)

with
O_{l}
the orthogonal rotation corresponding to
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})
, and

the rotation+dilation and
\boldsymbol{c}_{l}
the affine shift corresponding to
\mathcal{N}_{l}
(see
Section

2.5

is norm-preserving

\boldsymbol{c}_{l}
is either zero or non-zero, depending on whether
\mathcal{N}_{l}
is unital or non-unital, respectively (
2
and
4

The dilation part of
M_{l}
contracts the vector it acts on. Let

be the contractivity factor associated with
\Omega_{l}
, i.e., for any vector
\boldsymbol{v}
,

\|\Omega_{l}\boldsymbol{v}\|=q_{l}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|,\quad 0\leq q_{l}<1.

(42)

Expanding the recursion given by
Eq.

41

\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
corresponding to
\rho_{0}
, we obtain, with
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\equiv\boldsymbol{c}_{1}
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{j}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\boldsymbol{d}_{j}\ ,\qquad\quad 1\leq j\leq L\

(43a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{j}\cdots\Omega_{2}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}+\Omega_{j}\cdots\Omega_{3}\boldsymbol{c}_{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Omega_{j}\boldsymbol{c}_{j-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{j}

(43b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{j-1}\left(\prod_{s=j}^{r+1}\Omega_{s}\right)\boldsymbol{c}_{r}+\boldsymbol{c}_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq L.

(43c)

Note that
\boldsymbol{d}_{j}
is entirely a property of the maps and does not depend on the system state
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
. In other words,
\boldsymbol{d}_{j}
contains no useful information about the state of the computation carried out by the circuit. Using
Eq.

43

at the end of the circuit as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{L}=\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\boldsymbol{d}_{L},

(44)

where
\boldsymbol{d}_{L}
arises from the noise map combined with the unitary VQA map.
Substituting this into
Eq.

38

\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{v}_{L}
, we have:

\displaystyle C(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)+\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}.

(45)

Grouping together the terms that do not get contracted over the layers of the VQA circuit, we find:

\displaystyle|C(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)-\boldsymbol{d}_{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|

\displaystyle=q^{L}|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|

(46a)

\displaystyle\leq q^{L}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|\|\boldsymbol{h}\|

(46b)

\displaystyle\leq q^{L}\|\boldsymbol{h}\|,

(46c)

where

q^{L}\equiv q_{1}\cdots q_{L}\ ,\quad 0\leq q_{l}<1\ \forall l.

(47)

Here

is the contractivity factor associated with
\Omega_{l}
, so that the effective contractivity factor

. Moreover, recall from

\|\boldsymbol{h}\|\leq\frac{h}{\sqrt{(K-1)!}}n^{K/2}
, where
K

. We have thus proven that the cost function landscape concentrates:

Theorem 1
.

The cost function of HS-contractive non-unital maps concentrates for any VQA circuit with greater than logarithmic depth, i.e., if
L\in\omega[\log(n)]
then
1
1

1

\displaystyle|C(\boldsymbol{\theta})-C_{L}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h}{\sqrt{(K-1)!}}n^{K/2}q^{L}

(48a)

\displaystyle C_{L}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}

4.2
Noise-induced limit set

While
1
shows that the cost function concentrates on
C_{\infty}=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)+\boldsymbol{d}_{\infty}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}
, this does not mean that it tends to a fixed point. The reason is that the vector
\boldsymbol{d}_{L}
is affected by the unitary transformations in the VQA circuit, which rotate it, and by noise in the circuit, which rotates and contracts it. The rotations prevent convergence on a fixed point, and instead
C_{\infty}
lies in an interval. We call this interval the
noise-induced limit set
(NILS). Our next result characterizes the NILS.

We can rewrite
Eq.

43c
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{L}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{2}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}+\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{3}\boldsymbol{c}_{2}+\cdots

(49a)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Omega_{L}\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{L}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{L-1}\Theta_{1}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}+p_{2}^{L-2}\Theta_{2}\boldsymbol{c}_{2}+\cdots

(49b)

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{L-1}\Theta_{L-1}\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{L},

where
\Pi_{l=i+1}^{L}\Omega_{l}\equiv p_{i}^{L-i}\Theta_{i}
, i.e., combining the transformations of
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
into an overall rotation
\Theta_{i}
and an overall contraction
p_{i}^{L-i}
, where
0\leq p_{i}<1,\forall i
. Let
p\equiv\max_{i}p_{i}
, a noise contraction factor. Then:

Proposition 2
.

|\boldsymbol{d}_{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|\leq\frac{1-p^{L}}{1-p}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{h}\|}{\sqrt{1-1/d}}\equiv\Lambda_{L}\leq\Lambda_{\infty}.

(50)

The proof is given in
Appendix

G
.

Taking the limit
L\to\infty
, we can identify the
noise-induced limit set
(NILS) that the cost function concentrates in:

Corollary 2
.

C_{\infty}\in[\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)-\Lambda_{\infty},\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)+\Lambda_{\infty}]\equiv\mathrm{NILS}.

(51)

where

\Lambda_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1-p}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{h}\|}{\sqrt{1-1/d}}.

(52)

Proof.

It follows from
Eq.

50
that
-\Lambda_{\infty}\leq\boldsymbol{d}_{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}\leq\Lambda_{\infty}
; adding
\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)
to all sides gives
-\Lambda_{\infty}+\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)\leq C_{L}\leq\Lambda_{\infty}+\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)

\forall L
.
∎

Thus, the NILS is an interval dictated by the dimension of the system, the problem Hamiltonian, and the noise through the contraction factor
p
. Note again, that
C_{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{\infty}
are notations we have chosen and are not meant to suggest that a limit should exist as a point. As we have shown, the limit is an
interval
that we call a noise-induced limit set.

4.3
The unital case

Since the unital case is the case for which
\boldsymbol{d}_{L}=\boldsymbol{0}
[all the shift vectors
\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
vanish in
Eq.

43c
], we have:

Corollary 3
.

The cost function of unital maps concentrates super-polynomially for any VQA circuit with logarithmic depth, i.e., if
L=\omega[\log(n)]
, and exponentially for any VQA circuit with at least linear depth, i.e., if
L=\Omega(n)
.

The latter statement recovers the concentration result for unital maps of Ref.
[
31
, Lemma 1]
, which holds “whenever the number of layers
L
scales linearly with the number of qubits”, i.e., if
L=\omega(n)
in our notation.

Note that the NILS in the case of HS-contractive non-unital noise is worse for VQA circuit performance than unital noise since knowledge of
C_{L}
requires a precise characterization of each of the intermediate non-unital maps (to determine
\boldsymbol{d}_{L}
), whereas in the unital case, the NILS is determined purely by the target Hamiltonian
H
, and becomes a fixed point:

C^{\text{unital}}_{\infty}=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(H)=\langle H\rangle_{I/d},

(53)

i.e., the expectation value of the Hamiltonian with respect to the fully mixed state.

Next, we discuss the NIBP phenomenon for unital and HS-contractive non-unital maps and show that it appears for the former (in agreement with Ref.
[
31
]
) but not for the latter.

5
NIBP via parameter shift rules

From now on, when we use
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we refer to the cost function in the presence of a noise map. In this section, we bound
|\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\partial\theta_{\mu}|
, the magnitude of the cost function gradient, and show that it is exponentially small in the circuit depth
L
for both unital and non-unital noise, for
n\geq 1
qubits. We interchangeably use both
lm
and
\mu
to denote the gate location.

Our proof in the unital case is simpler and more general than that of Ref.
[
31
]
, but the HS-contractive non-unital case is our main new result. The bound we find has implications for the many applications of VQA where the goal is to learn the optimal parameters, i.e., when one is primarily concerned with trainability, and hence the gradient is a key quantity of interest.

At this point, it is useful to provide a formal definition of noise-induced barren plateaus. The following definition is inspired by Ref.
[
31
, Theorem 1]
:

Definition 2
.

A cost function
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
exhibits a noise-induced barren plateau (NIBP) if the magnitude of its gradient,
\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}\right|
, decays exponentially as a function of the circuit depth
L
for all
L
larger than some constant
L_{0}\geq 1
, independently of
l
and
m
, even for constant-width circuits.

Thus, NIBPs flatten the entire control landscape independently of the location
\mu=(l,m)
of the gate in the circuit at which the derivative is taken. Moreover, we impose the condition that the result holds even for constant-width circuits in order to preclude a measure concentration-type argument, which is typical of noise-free barren plateaus
[
24
]
. In this sense, NIBPs are distinct from the latter, for which the global minimum can be embedded inside a deep, narrow valley in the control landscape
[
26
]
.

Using the PSR [
Eq.

5
], and choosing the target operator to be a Hamiltonian
H
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[H(\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{+})-\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{-}))],

(54)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{\pm}\equiv\boldsymbol{\theta}\pm\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{\pi/2}
. We use
Eq.

10
to write

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{\pm})=\frac{1}{d}I+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}({v}^{\pm}_{\mu})_{i}F_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}F_{0}+\boldsymbol{v}^{\pm}_{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\ ,

(55)

so that

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mu}^{\pm})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}F_{0}+\boldsymbol{v}_{\mu}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}.

(56)

Let

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L}\equiv\boldsymbol{v}^{+}_{\mu}-\boldsymbol{v}^{-}_{\mu},

(57)

where we added the
L
subscript as a reminder that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L}
corresponds to the difference between two states obtained at the end of the circuit. Using
Eq.

37
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(H\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{F})

(58a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L})_{i}\mathrm{Tr}(HF_{i})

(58b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L})_{i}h_{i}.

(58c)

We thus arrive at the key result that the magnitude of the cost function gradient can be written simply as:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}\right|=\frac{1}{2}\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}\right|,

(59)

which expresses the cost function gradient in terms of the overlap of the difference between two coherence vectors with the coordinates of the traceless component of the target Hamiltonian.

5.1
NIBP in the unital case

We now prove the existence of an NIBP for unital maps, in agreement with Ref.
[

]
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the maps
\{\mathcal{N}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}

Using

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mu}^{L}\|

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{v}^{+}_{\mu}-\boldsymbol{v}^{-}_{\mu}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|(p^{L}+q^{L})\leq 2r^{L}

The noise channel in

19

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial C^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{\mu}}\right|_{\text{ctrl}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{h}\|r_{\text{c}}^{L}\ ,\quad\ 0\leq r_{\text{c}}<1.

If

for all

When

if

∎

Note that if

5.2
The non-unital case

Now assume that at least one of the maps
\mathcal{N}_{l}
in
Eq.

40
is non-unital. We will show that in contrast to the unital case, in the non-unital case
\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{lm}}\right|
can be lower-bounded by a quantity that is non-vanishing even for arbitrarily deep circuits. This means that there is no guarantee of an NIBP in the non-unital case.

Recall
Eq.

41
. The effect of shifting
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{lm}^{\pm}
at a single location
\mu=(l,m)
in the circuit is that in layer
l
, and only in this layer, we have two different unitaries
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\pm}_{lm})
, and correspondingly two different orthogonal rotations
O_{lm}^{\pm}
:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\pm,l}_{lm}=\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{v}_{l-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{l}\ ,\quad\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}\equiv M_{l}O_{lm}^{\pm}.

(70)

Note that prior to this location, i.e., for all
l^{\prime}<l
, the bifurcation into the two paths labeled
\pm
has not yet happened, which is why
\boldsymbol{v}_{l-1}
does not carry a
\pm
label.

5.2.1
No guarantee of an NIBP: an example

As a simple example that demonstrates why there is no guarantee of an NIBP in the non-unital case,
assume that all
\mathcal{N}_{l}
are unital except for the last two, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}_{l}
is non-unital only for
l=L-1
and
l=L
. Writing the last two coherence vectors explicitly then gives:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{Lm}^{\pm,L}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{Lm}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{v}_{L-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{L}

(71a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{L-1}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{L-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{L-2}+\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1},

(71b)

and substituting
Eq.

71b
into
Eq.

71a
yields:

\boldsymbol{v}_{Lm}^{\pm,L}=\Omega_{Lm}^{\pm}\Omega_{L-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{L-2}+\Omega_{Lm}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{L}.

(72)

The term
\Omega_{Lm}^{\pm}\Omega_{L-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{L-2}
is identical to the terms that appear in the unital case, so we know from the proof of
2
that its norm is
O\left(e^{-L}\right)
; therefore, for simplicity, let us neglect it entirely.
Subtracting then yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{L}_{Lm}=\boldsymbol{v}_{Lm}^{+,L}-\boldsymbol{v}_{Lm}^{-,L}=M_{L}(O_{Lm}^{+}-O_{Lm}^{-})\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}.

(73a)

We know from
4
that
0<\|\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}\|<1
. Thus, the vector
(O_{Lm}^{+}-O_{Lm}^{-})\boldsymbol{c}_{L-1}
has a non-zero
L
-independent norm determined by the two different rotations
O_{Lm}^{\pm}
. Applying
M_{L}
to it can rescale its norm by, at most, a constant (
L
-independent) factor. Thus, the argument leading to the exponentially small upper bound on
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{L}_{Lm}\|
in
Eq.

63a
does not hold in this case. Instead, we now have, from
Eq.

59
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{Lm}}\right|=\frac{1}{2}\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{L}_{Lm}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}\right|.

(74)

It follows from standard Levy’s lemma-type arguments that two randomly chosen unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
have overlap
\sim 1/\sqrt{D}
(see, e.g., Ref.
[
58
]
for a variety of intuitive arguments). In our case, there is no
a priori
relation between
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{L}_{Lm}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
that would compel them to lie in some joint smaller-dimensional subspace, and since the Hamiltonian is
K
-local, the effective dimension
D
of
\boldsymbol{h}
is
\sum_{k=1}^{K}{n\choose k}=O(n^{K})
as discussed in
Section

3.4
.
This polynomially small overlap is, however, determined by the circuit width
n
rather than its depth
L
, so it will not be an NIBP as per
2
. See
Appendix

H
for a proof sketch and simulation results. Of course, any circuit for which width and depth are related will impose a barren plateau-type result via
Eq.

74
that does depend on
L
; this type of noise-free barren plateau is the one first demonstrated in Ref.
[
24
]
.

5.2.2
No guarantee of an NIBP: general argument

We now show that the example above can be generalized, in particular without assuming that
l=L
. Specifically:

Theorem 3
.

Assume that a circuit described by
Eq.

40
contains any sequence of HS-contractive non-unital maps
\{\mathcal{N}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l-1}
for which
\max_{1\leq i\leq l-1}\sigma_{\max}(M_{i})\in[0,\mu)
, where
\mu\in(0,1/2]
,
l\geq 3
, and
\sigma_{\max}(M_{i})
is the largest singular value of
M_{i}
, the real rotation+dilation matrix associated with the map
\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
Assume in addition that the last maps
\{\mathcal{N}_{i}\}_{i=l}^{L}
in the circuit are all HS-contractive non-unital,
L-l=O(1)
for large

, and
\{\sigma_{\min}(M_{i})>c\}_{i=l}^{L}
, where
c>0
and
\sigma_{\min}(M_{i})
denotes the smallest singular value of
M_{i}
.
The cost function of such a circuit does not exhibit an NIBP.

Before we present the proof, we remark that
1
concerns the behavior of the
cost function
in circuits deeper than logarithmic depth (
L\in\omega[\log(n)]
), while
3
concerns the behavior of
derivative of the cost function
in the last few layers of deep circuits (large
L
). The class of deep circuits in
3
is included in the class considered in
1
since deep circuits satisfy
L\in\omega[\log(n)]
. The concentration of the cost function in
1
does not prevent the non-concentration of the derivative in
3
. This is because the cost function in
1
concentrates within a
finite interval
(the NILS; see
Section

4.2
), which means that a pair of cost functions
C(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})
and
C(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})
for circuits of the same depth

can have a finite difference. Using PSRs, this allows for scenarios where the derivative of the cost function in
3
remains finite, i.e., non-concentrated.

Proof.

We start from
Eq.

43
.
There is no additional bifurcation after the
l
’th layer, so that using
Eq.

70
, and the recursion given by
Eq.

41
again, we obtain for
1\leq j\leq L-l
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{lm}^{\pm,l+j}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{l+j}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{\pm,l}_{lm}+\boldsymbol{d}_{l+j}

(75a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{l+j}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{l+j}\cdots\Omega_{l+2}\boldsymbol{c}_{l+1}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Omega_{l+j}\boldsymbol{c}_{l+j-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{l+j}.

(75b)

Note that it follows from
Eq.

17b

43a

75a

\|\boldsymbol{d}_{j}\|<1

Combining

43

75

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{lm}^{\pm,L}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{\pm,l}_{lm}+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}

(76a)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}(\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{v}_{l-1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{l})+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}

(76b)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}[\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}(\Omega_{l-1}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1})+\boldsymbol{c}_{l}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}

(76c)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}\Omega_{l-1}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{lm}^{\pm,L}}

(76d)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}\Omega_{lm}^{\pm}\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{lm}^{\pm,L}}+\underbrace{\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}\boldsymbol{c}_{l}+\boldsymbol{d}_{L}}_{\boldsymbol{f}_{L}}.

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{lm}^{L}\equiv\boldsymbol{w}_{lm}^{+,L}-\boldsymbol{w}_{lm}^{-,L}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{L}_{lm}\equiv\boldsymbol{e}_{lm}^{+,L}-\boldsymbol{e}_{lm}^{-,L}

76

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}^{L}_{lm}\equiv\boldsymbol{v}_{lm}^{+,L}-\boldsymbol{v}_{lm}^{-,L}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{L}_{lm}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{L}_{lm}.

(77)

We will show that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{L}_{lm}
can be neglected but
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{L}_{lm}
cannot. First:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{lm}^{L}\|

\displaystyle=\|\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}(\Omega_{lm}^{+}-\Omega_{lm}^{-})\Omega_{l-1}\cdots\Omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|

(78a)

\displaystyle\leq\|\Omega_{L}\cdots\Omega_{l+1}(\Omega_{lm}^{+}-\Omega_{lm}^{-})\Omega_{l-1}\cdots\Omega_{1}\|\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|

(78b)

\displaystyle<\|\Omega_{L}\|\cdots\|\Omega_{l+1}\|\|\Omega_{lm}^{+}-\Omega_{lm}^{-}\|\|\Omega_{l-1}\|\cdots\|\Omega_{1}\|

(78c)

\displaystyle<2p_{L}\cdots p_{1}

(78d)

where the second line follows by definition of the operator norm, in the third line we used submultiplicativity and
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|<1

We may thus write

\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{lm}^{L}\|<2p^{L}\ ,\quad p<1,

(79)

Next, let us consider
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{L}_{lm}

43c
for

\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}=\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}+\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\prod_{i=l-1}^{l^{\prime}+1}\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\ ,\quad l\geq 3\ ,

Next, we lower-bound

\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\neq\boldsymbol{0}

\{\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}\}_{j=1}^{l-2}

\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\|=\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\|>0

To make the argument more general, we note that it follows from the triangle inequality
\|A+B\|\geq|\|A\|-\|B\||
that:

\displaystyle\|\sum_{k=1}A_{k}\|

\displaystyle\geq|\|A_{1}\|-\|\sum_{k=2}A_{k}\||

(81a)

\displaystyle\geq\|A_{1}\|-\|\sum_{k=2}A_{k}\|\geq\|A_{1}\|-b,

(81b)

where

In the context of bounding
\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\|

A_{1}\equiv\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\ ,\quad\{A_{k}\}_{k=2}\equiv\left\{\prod_{i=l-1}^{l^{\prime}+1}\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\right\}_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}.

(82)

Using the polar decomposition
M_{i}=V_{i}S_{i}
with
V_{i}

S_{i}=\sqrt{M_{i}^{\dagger}M_{i}}

\displaystyle\|\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}\|=\|V_{i}S_{i}O_{i}\boldsymbol{c}\|=\|S_{i}O_{i}\boldsymbol{c}\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\lambda_{i,1}\|O_{i}\boldsymbol{c}\|=\lambda_{i,1}\|\boldsymbol{c}\|,

\|\boldsymbol{c}\|<1

(84)

Thus, letting

\tilde{\lambda}_{l}\equiv\max_{1\leq i\leq l-1}\lambda_{i,1}\ ,\quad\tilde{c}_{l}\equiv\max_{1\leq l^{\prime}\leq l-1}\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\|\ ,

\displaystyle\|\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\prod_{i=l-1}^{l^{\prime}+1}\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\|\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\|\prod_{i=l-1}^{l^{\prime}+1}\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\|

(86a)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\prod^{l-1}_{i=l^{\prime}+1}\lambda_{i,1}\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\|\leq\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\|\tilde{\lambda}_{l}^{l-l^{\prime}-1}

(86b)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\tilde{c}_{l}r_{l}\ ,\qquad r_{l}\equiv\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{l}-\tilde{\lambda}_{l}^{l-1}}{1-\tilde{\lambda}_{l}}\ ,\quad l\geq 3,

(86c)

where in the first inequality in
Eq.

86b

l^{\prime}+1\leq l-1
. Using
Eqs.

80
,
81
and
82

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\|

\displaystyle\geq|\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\|-\|\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l-2}\prod_{i=l-1}^{l^{\prime}+1}\Omega_{i}\boldsymbol{c}_{l^{\prime}}\||

(87a)

\displaystyle\geq\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\|-\tilde{c}_{l}r_{l}.

(87b)

We defined
\tilde{c}_{l}
so that
\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\|
is included in the maximum in

\tilde{c}_{l}=\|\boldsymbol{c}_{l-1}\|
and

for
\tilde{\lambda}_{l}

Thus, we may conclude that a sufficient condition for
\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\|>C

\{\mathcal{N}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l-1}

\mathcal{N}_{i}

Next, from

\displaystyle d_{l}

\displaystyle=2\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\||\sin(\theta/2)|

(88a)

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{(O_{lm}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1})\cdot(O_{lm}^{-}\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1})}{\|\boldsymbol{d}_{l-1}\|^{2}}\ .

(88b)

The remaining transformations prescribed by

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\min}(M_{l})\cdots\sigma_{\min}(M_{L})d_{l}-2p^{L}.

(90b)

Therefore, as long as

As a physical example of a HS-contractive non-unital map that prevents an NIBP, consider an amplitude-damping map. It suffices to consider the simple case of the amplitude-damping map for a qubit coupled to a zero-temperature bath. The Kraus operators are

6.2
Cost function behavior

Our results indicate that unital noise is subject to both NIBP and other forms of BP, and that, conversely, HS-contractive non-unital noise may potentially evade NIBP despite experiencing a comparable degree of other BPs. The primary question within the framework of VQA pertains to its efficacy in determining the ground state of a specific Hamiltonian. 7
Conclusions

This work expands the study of NIBPs to incorporate arbitrary unital and HS-contractive non-unital noise. Using a generalization of the parameter shift rule that includes noise, we have derived upper bounds for the scaling of the magnitude of the cost function gradient with respect to circuit width
n
, circuit depth
L
, and noise strength. In the unital case, we have shown that the onset of an NIBP occurs already for circuits of logarithmic depth (
2
). In contrast, in the HS-contractive non-unital case, we have shown that VQA circuits need not necessarily exhibit an NIBP. This is true, in particular, when a constant number of final layers in a VQA circuit are subject to HS-contractive non-unital noise (
3
).

We found that both unital and HS-contractive non-unital circuits exhibit a phenomenon we call a noise-induced limit set (NILS), whereby the cost function concentrates on a fixed value for circuits of greater than logarithmic depth. In the unital case, this is given by the expectation value of the problem Hamiltonian with respect to the fully mixed state [
Eq.

53
], but in the HS-contractive non-unital case, the fixed value is determined by the parameters of the noise map as well [
Eq.

48c
].

Our results are validated with numerical simulations for the depolarizing and amplitude-damping maps.
Interesting open questions we do not address here are whether HS-contractive (
\|M\|<1
) or non-HS-contractive
(\|M\|\geq 1)
non-unital noise maps appear more often in practical scenarios, how to characterize them according to a given set of Kraus operators, and what the measure of HS-contractive non-unital maps is in the space of all non-unital maps.

Overall, our work shows that NIBPs present a significant challenge for VQAs, even after mitigation of the standard (noiseless) BP problem. A combination of error suppression, mitigation, and correction methods will be necessary to realize the promise of VQAs, just as is the case for other quantum algorithms running on noisy quantum computers.

To prove that a single-qubit non-unital map is always HS-contractive, we will show that it satisfies

3

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{M}
be a coherence vector satisfying
\|M\|=\|M\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|/\|\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|
. Without loss of generality we can assume
\boldsymbol{v}_{M}
corresponds to a pure state: if it does not, we can normalize it so that
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|=1/\sqrt{2}
.
We will prove the claim by contradiction and assume that
\|M\|\geq 1
, which implies
\|M\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|=\|M\|\|\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|\geq 1/\sqrt{2}

(M\boldsymbol{v}_{M})\cdot\boldsymbol{c}\geq 0

\boldsymbol{v}_{M}

-\boldsymbol{v}_{M}

\|M\boldsymbol{v}_{M}+\boldsymbol{c}\|>\|M\boldsymbol{v}_{M}\|\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
. However,

is a valid quantum state which has
\|M\boldsymbol{v}_{M}+\boldsymbol{c}\|\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
. Hence, by contradiction,
\|M\|<1
.

Using
3

Appendix H
Dependence on circuit width

In
Eq.

74
, we considered the overlap of two randomly chosen
D
-dimensional vectors, which suggests that the gradient of the cost function scales as
1/\sqrt{D}
. This phenomenon was initially discussed in Ref.
[
24
]
and is the original (noise-free) barren plateau (BP). To rederive it, we follow the approach of Ref.
[
67
]
.

Consider two normalized
D
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
, and choose each component of
\boldsymbol{h}
uniformly from the surface of a normalized
D
-Ball, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=\|\boldsymbol{h}\|=1
. Without loss of generality, we can construct
\boldsymbol{h}
such that its elements
h_{i}
are chosen randomly from
\{-1,1\}/\sqrt{D}
, for
i\in[1,D]
.
The expectation value of the inner product of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
is
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i}v_{i}h_{i}]=0
. The variance of their inner product is
\sigma^{2}[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i,j}v_{i}h_{i}v_{j}h_{j}]-\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}]^{2}=\sum_{i,j}v_{i}v_{j}\mathbb{E}[h_{i}h_{j}]=\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}/D=1/D
. The second to last equality uses
h_{i}^{2}=1/D
and
\mathbb{E}[h_{i}h_{j}]=0
for
i\neq j
.

The Chernoff bound states that
\mathrm{Pr}(|X|>\epsilon)<\exp(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma[X]^{2}})
. Applying this bound to
Eq.

74
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}\left(2\left|\frac{\partial C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{Lm}}\right|>\epsilon\right)=\mathrm{Pr}(|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|>\epsilon)<e^{-D\epsilon^{2}}
. Substituting
\epsilon=1/\sqrt{D}
, we obtain
\mathrm{Pr}(|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|>1/\sqrt{D})<1/e
, ensuring that it is likely that
|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{h}|
stays below
1/\sqrt{D}
.

Note that since our
n
-qubit VQA Hamiltonian is
2
-local, the effective dimension
D
of
\boldsymbol{h}
is
\sum_{k=1}^{2}{n\choose k}=(n^{2}+n)/2
as discussed below
Eq.

74
. Thus, this result can be interpreted as stating that the cost function gradient scale as the inverse of the number of qubits (circuit width).

Next, we examine whether this dependence on circuit width can be observed in numerical simulations of the same type as discussed in
Section

6
. We again employ a set of
50
randomly chosen
n
-qubit Hamiltonians, with
2\leq n\leq 9
. As stated in
Algorithm

1
, we set
\|H\|_{2}=1
; this ensures that
\|\boldsymbol{h}\|\leq 1
, i.e., does not grow with the effective dimension
D
of
\boldsymbol{h}
.

Fig.

4
shows the result of the simulation.
The two types of noise we simulated exhibit similar patterns, with amplitude-damping having a larger gradient magnitude and variance. Our simulation results exhibit a discernible trend along the dashed-dotted gray line denoted as
\|\boldsymbol{h}\|/\sqrt{D}
. The magnitude of the gradient (top row) appears to approach the scaling
1/\sqrt{(n^{2}+n)/2}
as the noise probability increases.
We emphasize that this does not constitute an upper bound for the magnitude; rather, it represents an expected value obtained through averaging over a large number of randomly generated vectors.

Appendix J
Example of non-unital channel outside
3

We expand on the example of a non-unital channel with
\sigma_{\max}(M)>0
and
\sigma_{\min}(M)=0
, which was mentioned below the proof of
3
. This kind of channel is expected to avoid NIBPs using a Levy’s lemma-type argument.

Consider a composite of a bit flip (BF) channel followed by an amplitude damping (AD) channel. The Kraus operators of an AD channel are given in
Eq.

92
, while those of a BF channel are

K_{0}=\sqrt{p}I,\quad K_{1}=\sqrt{1-p}X,

(115)

where
1-p
is the probability that a bit flip occurs. We can also find, using
Eq.

14
, that
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{0}
(as is true for all unital channels) and
M=\text{diag}(1,2p-1,2p-1)
. When
p=1/2
, we have
\sigma_{\max}(M)=1
and
\sigma_{\min}(M)=0
.

Recall that a composition
\Phi(\rho)=\Psi_{2}(\Psi_{1}(\rho))
of two CP maps
\Psi_{1}(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha}J_{\alpha}\rho J_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
and
\Psi_{2}(\rho)=\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}\rho K_{\beta}^{\dagger}
is:

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{\gamma}L_{\gamma}\rho L_{\gamma}^{\dagger},

(116)

where
\gamma=(\alpha,\beta)
and
L_{\gamma}=K_{\beta}J_{\alpha}
are the Kraus operators of the composite channel.

Using
\Psi_{1}
as a BF channel with
p=1/2
and
\Psi_{2}
as an AD channel in
Eq.

92
, we can construct
L_{\gamma}
as

\displaystyle L_{(0,0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&0\\
0&\sqrt{(1-p)/2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle L_{(1,0)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{p/2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

(117)

\displaystyle L_{(0,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/\sqrt{2}\\
\sqrt{(1-p)/2}&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle L_{(1,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p/2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

This set of Kraus operators corresponds to
M=\text{diag}(\sqrt{1-p},0,0)
and
\boldsymbol{c}=(0,0,p)
. We have composed a non-unital channel with
\sigma_{\max}(M)=\sqrt{1-p}
and
\sigma_{\min}(M)=0
.

Alternatively, we can derive this using the transformation of the coherence vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, by a BF channel with
p=1/2
followed by an AD channel:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\rightarrow M_{\text{AD}}M_{\text{BF}}(\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{c}_{\text{AD}}

(118)

\displaystyle=M\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{c},

where
M=M_{\text{AD}}M_{\text{BF}}
and
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{c}_{\text{AD}}
as expected.

Using
Z
in place of
X
in
Eq.

115
to first construct a dephasing channel with
p=1/2
would also yield
\sigma_{\min}(M)=0
, as would other unital channels at special values of their parameters.
