Quantification of the Fourth Moment Theorem for Cyclotomic Generating Functions
Benedikt Rednoß, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
This paper deals with sequences of random variables X n X_{n} only taking values in { 0 , … , n } \{0,\ldots,n\} . The probability generating functions of such random variables are polynomials of degree n n . Under the assumption that the roots of these polynomials are either all real or all lie on the unit circle in the complex plane, a quantitative normal approximation bound for X n X_{n} is established in a unified way. In the real rooted case the result is classical and only involves the variances of X n X_{n} , while in the cyclotomic case the fourth cumulants or moments of X n X_{n} appear in addition. The proofs are elementary and based on the Stein–Tikhomirov method. Keywords . Berry–Esseen bound, central limit theorem, cyclotomic polynomial, fourth moment theorem, generating function, real rooted polynomial, Stein–Tikhomirov method MSC . 05A15, 60F05

Introduction and Results

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a natural number and
X_{n}
a random variable that only takes integer values in
\{0,\dots,n\}
.
We abbreviate its expectation
\mathbb{E}X_{n}
by
\mu_{n}
,
its variance
\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})
by
\sigma_{n}^{2}

and its fourth cumulant
\operatorname{Cum}_{4}(X_{n})=\mathbb{E}X_{n}^{4}-3(\mathbb{E}X_{n}^{2})^{2}
by
\kappa_{n}
. In what follows we shall implicitly assume that
\sigma_{n}^{2}>0
to avoid discussion of degenerate cases.
Moreover, by
g_{n}\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
we denote the (probability) generating function of
X_{n}
, that is,

g_{n}(z):=\mathbb{E}[z^{X_{n}}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=k)z^{k},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Clearly,
g_{n}
is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to
n
.
The complex roots of
g_{n}
will be denoted by
\varrho^{(n)}_{1},\dots,\varrho^{(n)}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, where we do not necessarily assume them to be pairwise distinct.

Starting with the seminal work of Harper
[
6
]
it has become an active and fruitful direction of research to deduce probabilistic limit theorems for the sequence of random variables
X_{n}
as
n\to\infty
from information about the location of the zeros
\varrho^{(n)}_{1},\dots,\varrho^{(n)}_{n}
of
g_{n}
.
Thereby the following two cases have attracted particular interest:

(R)

g_{n}
is
real rooted
, that is, all roots
\varrho^{(n)}_{1},\dots,\varrho^{(n)}_{n}
are non-positive real numbers;

(C)

g_{n}
is
cyclotomic
, meaning that all roots
\varrho^{(n)}_{1},\dots,\varrho^{(n)}_{n}
are located on the unit circle in the complex plane.
In this case, one also says that
g_{n}
is
root unitary
.

It is a remarkable observation that a large number of random variables arising in problems having their origin in statistical mechanics or in algebraic and probabilistic combinatorics fall into these two classes.
For example, the number of descents or the number of cycles in a uniform random permutation in finite Coxeter groups (such as the classical groups of type
A
,
B
or
D
) lead to real rooted generating functions. Also, the matching polynomial associated with an arbitrary graph is real rooted by the celebrated Heilmann–Lieb theorem
[
8
]
. As special cases this yields real rootedness of the monomer-dimer partition function, the
r
-Touchard polynomials or the
r
-Lah number polynomials, for example. We also mention that real rootedness of a polynomial has deep connections with unimodality and log-concavity properties of its coefficient sequence. On the other hand, cyclotomic generating functions arise in the context of the number of inversions in a uniform random permutation in finite Coxeter groups and are prevalent in the theory of polynomials whose coefficients are quotients of so-called
q
-integers. Examples include the
q
-Catalan or the
q
-Narayana numbers, the
q
-analogues of the Hook length formula for standard Young tableaux or the
q
-analogue of the Weyl dimension formula for highest weight modules of semi-simple Lie algebras. Limit theorems for sequences of random variables under assumption
(R)
or
(C)
can be found in
[
1
]
or
[
9
]
, respectively.
For more examples, background material and further references we refer the reader to the survey articles
[
2
,
3
,
12
]
.

Under assumption
(R)
it is a folklore result in probability theory that the sequence of random variables
X_{n}
satisfies a central limit theorem if and only if the variances
\sigma_{n}^{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\infty

\displaystyle\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(1)

see
[
1
,
3
,
12
]
.
On the other hand, in the framework of set-up
(C)
it was shown in
[
9
]
that a necessary and sufficient condition for
X_{n}
to satisfy a central limit theorem is that the fourth cumulants
\kappa_{n}
satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}\to 0

\displaystyle\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(2)

The goal of this article is to provide an elementary and unified approach to the following Berry-Esseen type quantitative central limit theorem under assumption
(R)
or
(C)
. It is an important observation that the bounds we obtain match the necessary and sufficient conditions presented in (
1
) and (
2
).
In what follows we let

\displaystyle F(w):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{w}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,\textrm{d}x,\qquad w\in\mathbb{R},

be the distribution function of a standard normal random variable.

Theorem 1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
X_{n}
be a random variable only taking values in
\{0,\dots,n\}
.
Let
\mu_{n}=\mathbb{E}X_{n}
,
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(X_{n})>0
,
\kappa_{n}=\operatorname{Cum}_{4}(X_{n})
and
g_{n}
be the generating function of
X_{n}
. Then, there are absolute constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
such that the following assertions are valid.

(1)

If
g_{n}
is real rooted as defined in
(R)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{X_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq w\Big)-F(w)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma_{n}}.

(2)

If
g_{n}
is cyclotomic as defined in
(C)
, one has that

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{X_{n}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq w\Big)-F(w)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\sigma_{n}}.

In principle, the result of Theorem
1
is known and can be regarded as a special case of the main result of
[
11
]
.
In that paper, the roots of the generating function are assumed to be outside a circle of radius
d_{n}\in(0,1)
around
1
or outside a cone pointed at the origin with central angle
\delta_{n}\in(0,\pi]
, see Figure
1
.
Under these assumptions, Berry–Essen type bounds of order
\log n/(d_{n}\sigma_{n})
or
1/(\delta_{n}\sigma_{n})
, respectively, were derived.
Moreover, for random variables whose generating functions are of the form

\displaystyle g_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(z)}{f(1)}\qquad\text{with}\qquad f(z)=\prod_{j}\frac{1-z^{b_{j}}}{1-z^{a_{j}}},\quad a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{N},

(3)

a quantitative central limit theorem was obtained in
[
7
]
, among other results, by the method of cumulants.
In this case, the rate of convergence was shown to be of the order
M_{n}/\sigma_{n}
, where
M_{n}:=\max(b_{j}-a_{j})
.
Even though it is known from
[
9
]
that in the cyclotomic case

the behaviour of the fourth cumulant regulates whether or not a central limit theorem holds, see (
2
),
our result is the first to provide a Berry–Esseen type bound including the fourth cumulant. We remark in this context that for all examples treated in
[
2
,
7
,
9
]
our result leads to a speed of convergence of the same order and differs only by an absolute constant.
Our computation we present in Section

below can in principle be combined with the main result in
[
11
]
to derive a result similar to Theorem
1
. However, the computations and arguments that are carried out in
[
11
]
require substantial theoretical and technical background, though. For this reason, we believe that a self-contained and elementary proof of Theorem
1
is of independent interest.

As we already pointed out above, our contribution is an elementary and unified route to Theorem
1
,
which is based on the so-called Stein–Tikhomirov method.
The classical Stein’s method for normal approximation is based on the observation
that a real-valued random variable
Z
is standard normally distributed
if and only if

\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(Z)-Zf(Z)]=0

(4)

for all sufficiently regular functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
If
Z
is not standard normally distributed, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(Z)-Zf(Z)]
can be used to quantify how ‘close’ the distribution of
Z
is to the standard normal distribution.
Eventually, this leads to bounds for common probability distances,
such as the Kolmogorov distance or the Wasserstein distance.
For a detailed introduction to Stein’s method, see
[
4
]
.
Tikhomirov
[
14
]
combined this core idea of Stein’s method with the more traditional theory of characteristic functions.
Choosing for fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
the function
f
in (
4
) as
f(x)=e^{itx}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, leads to the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle t\Psi_{Z}(t)+\Psi_{Z}^{\prime}(t)=0

(5)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\Psi_{Z}(t):=\mathbb{E}[e^{itZ}]
denotes the characteristic function of
Z
.
The differential equation in (
5
) indeed characterizes the characteristic function of the standard normal distribution, which is
e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
This motivates the idea of evaluating the left-hand side of (
5
) even if
Z
is not exactly standard normally distributed,
in order to derive a non-uniform bound for the difference
|\Psi_{Z}(t)-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}|
of characteristic functions.
This bound can then be combined with Esseen’s classical smoothing lemma, which states that

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}(Z\leq w)-F(w)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\bigg|\frac{\Psi_{Z}(t)-e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}}{t}\bigg|\,\textrm{d}t+\frac{12\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi^{3}}T}

for all
T>0
, see
[
5
, Lemma 2 in Chapter XVI.3]
. Many important aspects of this strategy are carried out rather implicitly in the original paper
[
14
]
,
but a lemma summarizing and partly generalizing the essential ingredients of this approach is the content of
[
13
, Lemma 2.3]
. A version of it can be rephrased as follows:
Suppose there are continuous functions
a,b\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
and non-negative constants
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},C\in\mathbb{R}

satisfying
A_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
,

\Psi_{Z}^{\prime}(t)=-t(1+a(t))\Psi_{Z}(t)+b(t)
,

|a(t)|\leq A_{0}+A_{1}|t|
, and

|b(t)|\leq B_{0}+B_{1}|t|+B_{2}t^{2}

for all
t\in(-C,C)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}(Z\leq w)-F(w)\big|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}A_{0}+\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}}A_{1}+\sqrt{\pi}B_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\pi}\big(1+2\max\{0,-\log 2s\}\big)B_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{\pi s}B_{2}+\frac{24s}{\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}

(6)

for all
s\geq\max\big\{\frac{2A_{1}}{1-2A_{0}},\frac{1}{C}\bigr\}
. In fact, this follows from Lemma 2.3 in
[
13
]
by realizing that the assumptions on
a(t)
and
b(t)
in said lemma actually are only required to hold in an interval
(-C,C)
around zero and not necessarily for the whole real line.

The Berry-Esseen type bound (
6
) is the starting point of our proof of Theorem
1
, which in turn is the content of the remaining parts of this paper. More precisely, in Section
2
we collect some preliminary computations and introduce some further notation. The proof of Theorem
1
is the content of the final Section
3
.

3.1
Common Ground

We use the same notation as in the previous section and let
X
be a random variable taking values in
\{0,\ldots,n\}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}>0
stand for its expectation and variance, respectively.
Let
\widehat{X}
be the standardization of
X
, that is,

\widehat{X}=\frac{1}{\sigma}(X-\mu).

We write
\widehat{\Psi}
for the characteristic function of
\widehat{X}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(t)

\displaystyle=\Psi\Bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,e^{-i\frac{\mu}{\sigma}t}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\Psi^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,e^{-i\frac{\mu}{\sigma}t}-i\frac{\mu}{\sigma}\,\Psi\Bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,e^{-i\frac{\mu}{\sigma}t}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using the function
q
defined by (
10
) we find

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\Bigl(q\Bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\Bigr)-i\mu\Bigr)\,\widehat{\Psi}(t)

for
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma\Phi
with
\sigma\Phi=\{\sigma\varphi:\varphi\in\Phi\}
.

Following the Stein–Tikhomirov method as explained in Section
1
, we are interested for
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma\Phi
in the differential expression

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{\prime}(t)+t\widehat{\Psi}(t)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{q\bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\bigr)-i\mu}{\sigma}+t\biggr)\,\widehat{\Psi}(t)=\alpha(t)\,\widehat{\Psi}(t)

with the continuous function

\displaystyle\alpha(t):={}

\displaystyle\frac{q\bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\bigr)-i\mu}{\sigma}+t.

We aim to show that under the assumptions
(R)
or
(C)
on the location of the roots of
g
there are constants
A>0
and
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|\alpha(t)|

\displaystyle\leq A\,t^{2}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad t\in(-C,C).

(17)

We can then apply the Stein–Tikhomirov bound (
6
) with the functions
a(t)
and
b(t)
there given by
-ta(t)=\alpha(t)
and
b(t)\equiv 0
.
This leads to
A_{0}=B_{0}=B_{1}=B_{2}=0
and
A_{1}=A
and with the choice
s=\max\bigl\{2A,\frac{1}{C}\bigr\}
, (
6

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{X}\leq w)-F(w)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}}\,A+\frac{24}{\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\max\Bigl\{2A,\frac{1}{C}\Bigr\}.

(18)

In order to verify (
17
)
we use (

we use (
13
) to see that

t=-\frac{1}{\sigma}\cdot q^{\prime}(0)\cdot\frac{t}{\sigma}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{q\bigl(\frac{t}{\sigma}\bigr)-q(0)-q^{\prime}(0)\cdot\frac{t}{\sigma}}{\sigma}

for
t\in\bigl(-\frac{\sigma\delta}{2},\frac{\sigma\delta}{2}\bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma\Phi
.
This equals the remainder term of a Taylor polynomial, which implies that
\alpha(t)
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle|\alpha(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sigma}\sup_{s\in(-\frac{\sigma\delta}{2},\frac{\sigma\delta}{2})}\Bigl|q^{\prime\prime}\Bigl(\frac{s}{\sigma}\Bigr)\Bigr|\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{3}}\sup_{s\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})}|q^{\prime\prime}(s)|

(19)

for all
t\in(-\frac{\sigma\delta}{2},\frac{\sigma\delta}{2})
.

To bound
q^{\prime\prime}(s)
, we recall from (
11
) that

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}{(1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is})^{2}}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}\biggr)

for
s\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})
.
Now, take a root
\varrho\in R
with polar representation
\varrho=re^{i\varphi}
.
Then,

\displaystyle(1-\varrho e^{-is})^{2}e^{i(s-\varphi)}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{i(s-\varphi)}-2r+r^{2}e^{i(\varphi-s)}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1+r^{2})\cos(\varphi-s)-2r-i(1-r^{2})\sin(\varphi-s)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(1+r)^{2}\bigl(1-\cos(\varphi-s)\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}(1-r)^{2}\bigl(1+\cos(\varphi-s)\bigr)

\displaystyle-i(1-r^{2})\sin(\varphi-s)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[(1+r)\sqrt{1-\cos(\varphi-s)}-i\cdot\operatorname{sn}_{\varphi-s}\cdot(1-r)\sqrt{1+\cos(\varphi-s)}\Bigr]^{2},

(20)

where
\operatorname{sn}_{x}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
denotes for
x\in\mathbb{R}
the sign of
\sin(x)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|1-\varrho e^{-is}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\bigl|(1-\varrho e^{-is})^{2}e^{i(s-\varphi)}\bigr|}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+r)^{2}\bigl(1-\cos(\varphi-s)\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}(1-r)^{2}\bigl(1+\cos(\varphi-s)\bigr)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1+r)\sqrt{1-\cos(\varphi-s)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1+r)\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\bigl||\varphi|-|s|\bigr|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}(1+r)|\varphi|,

where we used that

1-\cos(x)\geq\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}x^{2}
for
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
and that

\bigl||\varphi|-|s|\bigr|\geq\frac{|\varphi|}{2}

since
|s|<\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\frac{|\varphi|}{2}
.
Recalling the representation (
11
) of
q^{\prime\prime}
we can now estimate separately both factors appearing there.
The first factor satisfies

\displaystyle\biggl|\frac{-\varrho e^{-is}}{(1-\varrho e^{-is})^{2}}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\pi^{2}r}{(1+r)^{2}\,\varphi^{2}}\leq\frac{2\pi^{2}r}{(1+r)^{2}\,\bigl(1-\cos(\varphi)\bigr)},

(21)

where we used that
1-\cos(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
for
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
For the second factor we get

\displaystyle\biggl|1-\frac{2}{1-\varrho e^{-is}}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{4\pi}{(1+r)\,|\varphi|}\leq 1+\frac{4\pi}{(1+r)\,\delta},

where we used that
\delta\leq|\varphi|
by definition of
\delta
.
As an intermediate result, we get

\displaystyle|q^{\prime\prime}(s)|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|i\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}{(1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is})^{2}}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}\biggr)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{2\pi^{2}r_{k}}{(1+r_{k})^{2}\,\bigl(1-\cos(\varphi_{k})\bigr)}\biggl(1+\frac{4\pi}{(1+r_{k})\,\delta}\biggr)

for
s\in\bigl(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2}\bigr)
.
We emphasize that up to here all estimates hold for every possible root
\varrho
, regardless of its location.
To proceed, we shall now specialize to the set-up of
(R)
or
(C)
.

Specialization

If a root
\varrho
of

is real and negative or zero or if it satisfies

, then

\displaystyle\biggl|\frac{-\varrho}{(1-\varrho)^{2}}\biggr|

\displaystyle=\frac{-\varrho}{(1-\varrho)^{2}},

compare with (

), (

) and with Figure

Further, in this case, (

) for

reduces to

\displaystyle(1-\varrho)^{2}e^{-i\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+r)^{2}\bigl(1-\cos(\varphi)\bigr).

Together with (

\displaystyle\biggl|\frac{-\varrho e^{-is}}{(1-\varrho e^{-is})^{2}}\biggr|

so that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})}|q^{\prime\prime}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\in(-\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2})}\Biggl|i\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}{(1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is})^{2}}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{1-\varrho_{k}e^{-is}}\biggr)\Biggr|

.
Finally, (

) leads to the bound

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{X}\leq w)-F(w)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}}\,\frac{5\pi^{3}}{2\sigma\delta}+\frac{24}{\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\max\biggl\{\frac{5\pi^{3}}{\sigma\delta},\frac{2}{\sigma\delta}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}}{\sigma\delta},

(22)

with a constant
