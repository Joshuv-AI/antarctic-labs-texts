Abstract
Linear fusion is a cornerstone of estimation theory. Optimal linear fusion was derived by Bar-Shalom and Campo in the 1980s. It requires knowledge of the cross-covariances between the errors of the estimators. In distributed or cooperative systems, these cross-covariances are difficult to compute. To avoid an underestimation of the errors when these cross-covariances are unknown, conservative fusions must be performed. A conservative fusion provides a fused estimator with a covariance bound which is guaranteed to be larger than the true (but not computable) covariance of the error. Previous research by Reinhardt et al. proved that, if no additional assumption is made about the errors of the estimators, the minimal bound for fusing two estimators is given by a fusion called Covariance Intersection (CI). In practice, the errors of the estimators often have an uncorrelated component, because the dynamic or measurement noise is assumed to be independent. In this context, CI is no longer the optimal method and an adaptation called Split Covariance Intersection (SCI) has been designed to take advantage from these uncorrelated components. The contribution of this paper is to prove that SCI is the optimal fusion rule for two estimators under the assumption that they have an uncorrelated component. It is proved that SCI provides the optimal covariance bound with respect to any increasing cost function. To prove the result, a minimal volume that should contain all conservative bounds is derived, and the SCI bounds are proved to be the only bounds that tightly circumscribe this minimal volume.

Linear Estimation, Conservative Fusion, Split Covariance Intersection

1
Introduction

Fusion is one of the fundamental elements in estimation theory. It is the process of combining different pieces of information into a more accurate one. The problem of optimal fusion have been studied for decades
[
6
]
, and especially when the fused estimator is searched for as a linear combination of the estimators. The optimal linear fusion of two estimators was first proposed by Bar-Shalom and Campo
[
6
]
. They underlined the importance of the cross-covariance term. Since then, extensions for the fusion of any number of estimators have been derived, see e.g.,
[
23
]
. To perform the optimal linear fusion, the full knowledge of the second order moments of the estimators are required. In particular, the knowledge of the covariances of each estimator is not enough, but the knowledge of the cross-covariances between each pair of estimators is also necessary. This requirement may be prohibitive for some applications such as networked estimation or cooperative estimation.

Several strategies have been proposed to perform the fusion when the cross-covariances are unknown and cannot be computed. The most simple is to assume the estimators uncorrelated, then to apply the optimal scheme proposed by Bar-Shalom and Campo. However, this naive strategy may result in an underestimation of the error of estimation, see e.g.,
[
5
]
, and should therefore be avoided. Furthermore, as
[
32
]
points out, considering any particular possible cross-covariance to apply the optimal scheme results similarly to an underestimation of the error. As a consequence, the whole set of
admissible
cross-covariances should be considered when designing the fusion. In this case, the covariance of the error of the resulting fused estimator cannot be computed as each cross-covariance would produce a different fused covariance. Instead, a
conservative
bound is searched for to ensure that the error of estimation is not underestimated. This bound should be greater than the covariance of the fused estimator for all admissible cross-covariances. The optimal linear fusion problem then consists in finding a fused estimator having the smallest conservative bound. This problem can be formulated as a general optimization problem
[
10
]
. To fuse two estimators without any additional information on their cross-covariance, Covariance Intersection (CI)
[
14
]
is proved to give the optimal bound
[
28
]
. CI provides a bound whose associated precision matrix is a convex combination of the precision matrices of both estimators. This fusion is very conservative as it does not consider any assumption on the nature of the (unknown) correlation. Hence, it encompasses the extreme case of totally correlated estimators. The set of admissible cross-covariances is the largest set possible: it contains all cross-covariances generating a positive semi-definite centralized covariance. If this set can be reduced under additional assumptions, better bounds have been derived. For example, if the estimators to be fused are known to share a common estimate, Ellipsoidal Intersection
[
30
]
have been proposed, but it is not conservative. An alternative fusion scheme called Inverse Covariance Intersection is proved to provide, in this case, a conservative bound better than CI
[
26
]
. Another classical example is when the estimators are known to have an uncorrelated component (generally independent). In this case, an extension of CI called Split Covariance Intersection (SCI)
[
15
]
also provides a better result. SCI has been applied to a great variety of problems, e.g., SLAM
[
16
]
, cooperative localization
[
21
]
, or cooperative perception
[
24
]
. There are three main reasons for the success of SCI. Firstly, it is particularly adapted for cooperative and distributed systems as the dynamical noise and the measurement noise are often assumed independent from the errors of estimation. Second, it provides a conservative bound without knowing the cross-covariance but unlike CI, it takes benefit from the independent part and therefore generates a less conservative bound. Third, it is the natural extension of CI which is known to provide the minimal bound of a fusion and achieves empirically good performances. However, to the best of our knowledge, the optimality of the SCI fusion has not yet been demonstrated.

In this paper, we focus on the linear fusion of two estimators in the situation addressed by SCI. We assume that the estimators are the sum of two components: one
correlated
and the other
uncorrelated
. In this context, we prove that SCI is the optimal fusion scheme in the sense that it provides the minimal bound with respect to any increasing cost function. To prove this result, we introduce a minimal volume that all conservative bounds must contain. Then, we prove that the SCI bounds are the only bounds that tightly circumscribe this volume.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, Section
2
proposes an overview of the Optimal Linear Fusion Problem and of different fusion schemes. Then, the problem of optimal fusion under split covariances and our main result, the optimality of SCI, are introduced in Section
3
. To solve this problem, Section
4
introduces and characterizes the minimal volume that must contain all conservative bounds. Section
5
studies the tightness around this minimal volume. Section
6
proves our main result. A discussion is proposed in Section
7
. Finally, Section
8
gives some perspectives.

Background: Optimal Linear fusion

Consider a random state
\underline{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and two unbiased estimators
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}
of
\underline{\bm{x}}
. The errors of estimation are denoted as
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}-\underline{\bm{x}}
and
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}-\underline{\bm{x}}
, and their covariances and cross-covariance are denoted as
\bm{C}_{A}=\mathrm{E}[\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}^{\intercal}]
,
\bm{C}_{B}=\mathrm{E}[\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}^{\intercal}]
, and
\bm{C}_{AB}=\mathrm{E}[\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}^{\intercal}]
. A linear fusion consists in creating a new unbiased estimator
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{F}
(
F
for
fused
) as a linear combination of
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}
. It depends on two gains
\bm{K}_{A}
and
\bm{K}_{B}
, and is defined as:

\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{F}(\bm{K})=\bm{K}_{A}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}+\bm{K}_{B}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B},

(1)

where
\bm{K}=(\bm{K}_{A},\bm{K}_{B})
for compactness. When there is no ambiguity on the gains, we simply denote
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{F}
. The unbiasedness of
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{F}(\bm{K})
imposes that the gains satisfy:

\bm{K}_{A}+\bm{K}_{B}=\bm{I}.

(
C_{1}
)

The error of the fused estimator is defined as
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{F}=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{F}-\underline{\bm{x}}=\bm{K}_{A}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}+\bm{K}_{B}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}
, and its covariance matrix is:

\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K})=\bm{K}_{A}\bm{C}_{A}\bm{K}_{A}^{\intercal}+\bm{K}_{A}\bm{C}_{AB}\bm{K}_{B}^{\intercal}\\
+\bm{K}_{B}\bm{C}_{AB}^{\intercal}\bm{K}_{A}^{\intercal}+\bm{K}_{B}\bm{C}_{B}\bm{K}_{B}^{\intercal}.

(2)

The covariance
\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K})
represents the mean squared error (MSE) matrix of the fused estimator. The objective of the optimal fusion is to minimize the error of estimation, i.e., to minimize some cost function on
\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K})
, e.g., its trace or its determinant.
If the covariances
\bm{C}_{A}
and
\bm{C}_{B}
and the cross-covariance
\bm{C}_{AB}
are known, the optimal fusion is well-known. It was solved by Bar-Shalom and Campo
[
6
]
and is defined as:

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{F}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{A}-(\bm{C}_{A}-\bm{C}_{AB})\bm{R}^{-1}(\bm{C}_{A}-\bm{C}_{BA}),

(3a)

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\bm{C}_{B}-\bm{C}_{AB}^{\intercal})\bm{R}^{-1},

(3b)

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\bm{C}_{A}-\bm{C}_{AB})\bm{R}^{-1},

(3c)

where
\bm{R}=\bm{C}_{A}+\bm{C}_{B}-\bm{C}_{AB}-\bm{C}_{AB}^{\intercal}
. In this case,
\bm{C}_{F}^{*}
is the minimum in the Loewner ordering sense over the set of possible covariance matrices. This means that for any other gain
\bm{K}
satisfying (

C
1

),
\bm{C}_{F}^{*}\preceq\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K})
. As a consequence,
\bm{C}_{F}^{*}
is the optimum for all increasing cost functions. As a particular case, if the estimators are uncorrelated, i.e.,
\bm{C}_{AB}=\bm{0}
, then
\bm{C}_{F}^{*}=(\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}+\bm{C}_{B}^{-1})^{-1}
. The precision matrix of the optimal fused estimator is in this case the sum of the precision matrices of the two estimators, which corresponds to the classical information form of the Kalman Filter, see e.g.,
[
4
]
.

If the covariances
\bm{C}_{A}
and
\bm{C}_{B}
are known but the cross-covariance
\bm{C}_{AB}
is unknown (and cannot be estimated), then the MSE matrix of the fused estimator should be seen as a function of
(i)
the gains and
(ii)
the cross-covariance:
\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K},\bm{C}_{AB})
. The set of cross-covariances to consider in the fusion is called the set of
admissible cross-covariances
and is denoted as
\mathcal{A}
. Without any additional information, this set equals to:

\bar{\mathcal{A}}:=\left\{\bm{C}_{AB}\ |\ \begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{A}&\bm{C}_{AB}\\
{\bm{C}_{AB}}^{\intercal}&\bm{C}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\right\}.

(4)

The set
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is the most general and largest set possible, it simply imposes that
\bm{C}_{AB}
is indeed a cross-covariance matrix. With additional assumptions on the cross-covariances
\bm{C}_{AB}
, the set
\mathcal{A}
is generally smaller

.

As each admissible

, the

This problem was proposed in a more general form in
[
10
]
to unify the formulations of different fusions. The number of variables of in (

) is

The first and most famous conservative bound, proposed by Uhlmann and Julier
[

,
14
]
, is CI. This fusion rule is conservative for the whole set
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
. It is defined (in a information form) as:

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\text{CI}}(\omega)^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{CI}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}+(1-\omega)\bm{C}_{B}^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B},

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\text{CI}}^{-1}(\omega)

\bm{C}_{A}
and
\bm{C}_{B}
. The bounds generated by CI are illustrated in Figure

. As the parameter
\omega
should be chosen to minimize the cost function

is known is that the optimal bound depends on the cost function

The CI fusion rule can be extended to any number of estimators by considering a convex combination of their precision matrices. The optimization of the weights becomes harder and several techniques have been developed to speed up the optimization, at a cost of suboptimality
[
25
,
11
,
33
,
9
]
. The good performances of CI have first been described in
[
8
]
: CI was proved to provide the optimal bound for the fusion of two estimators if the cost function is the trace and the whole set
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is considered. More recently, this optimality was extended to any increasing cost function
J
in
[
28
]
. Without any other assumption on the set of admissible cross-covariance matrices, any other bound is therefore either worst or non-conservative. As a consequence, CI have been applied to several estimation problems
[
5
,
16
,
12
,
19
]
.

Considering the whole set
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
assumes that the errors
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}
may be totally correlated. It is usually a very pessimistic assumption. In practice, this assumption can often be weaken and specific fusion rules have been derived for specific situations. In
[
2
]
, a conservative fusion was proposed if only one component of the cross-covariance is unknown. No proof of optimality of the bound was however given. An extension to several components was then presented in
[
3
]
. Another well-studied case is if the correlation between the errors
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}
is known to be bounded. In
[
34
]
, the authors considered a generalization of the Pearson’s correlation coefficient: the largest eigenvalue of the correlation matrix:
\bm{C}_{A}^{-1/2}\bm{C}_{AB}\bm{C}_{B}^{-1/2}
. They assumed that this correlation coefficient is bounded by some value
\rho\in[0,1]
. They proved that, in this case, the set admissible of cross-covariances
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is reduced to:

\mathcal{A}_{\rho}:=\left\{\bm{C}_{AB}\ |\ \begin{bmatrix}\rho\bm{C}_{A}&\bm{C}_{AB}\\
{\bm{C}_{AB}}^{\intercal}&\rho\bm{C}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\right\}.

(7)

The authors proposed a family of bounds for this set, and proved that the optimal bound is in the family when considering the trace as a cost function. They also proposed an algorithm to find the optimal bound within the family. In Section
7
, their family is proved to be in fact optimal for any increasing cost function as it is a particular case of SCI. A more general context is to assume that the estimators
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}
are the sum of two components: one whose error may be correlated to an unknown degree (with the error of the other first component), and the second whose error is uncorrelated with everything else. In this situation, the covariances
\bm{C}_{A}
and
\bm{C}_{B}
are split into a correlated component, denoted as
\bm{P}_{A}
and
\bm{P}_{B}
, and an uncorrelated component, denoted as
\bm{Q}_{A}
and
\bm{Q}_{B}
:
\bm{C}_{A}=\bm{P}_{A}+\bm{Q}_{A}
and
\bm{C}_{B}=\bm{P}_{B}+\bm{Q}_{B}
. The unknown cross-covariance
\bm{C}_{AB}
corresponds in this case to
\bm{P}_{AB}
: the cross-covariance between the errors of the first components. Consequently, the set of admissible cross-covariances is reduced to:

\mathcal{A}_{\text{Split}}:=\left\{\bm{C}_{AB}\ |\ \begin{bmatrix}\bm{P}_{A}&\bm{C}_{AB}\\
{\bm{C}_{AB}}^{\intercal}&\bm{P}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\right\}.

(8)

To exploit this particular situation, SCI was designed as an adaptation of CI
[
15
]
. Its fusion rule is conservative for the set
\mathcal{A}_{\text{Split}}
, and is also parameterized by an
\omega\in[0,1]
as follows:

\bm{B}_{\text{SCI}}(\omega)^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{SCI}}(\omega)=\omega(\bm{P}_{A}+\omega\bm{Q}_{A})^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}\\
+\bar{\omega}(\bm{P}_{B}+\bar{\omega}\bm{Q}_{B})^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B},

(9a)

\bm{B}_{\text{SCI}}^{-1}(\omega)=\omega(\bm{P}_{A}+\omega\bm{Q}_{A})^{-1}+\bar{\omega}(\bm{P}_{B}+\bar{\omega}\bm{Q}_{B})^{-1},

(9b)

where
\bar{\omega}=1-\omega
. SCI takes benefit from the uncorrelated components to reduce the bounds and provide a better bound than CI. A comparison between CI and SCI is proposed in Figure
1
, it can be observed that SCI bounds are smaller. The splitting assumption of the covariances appears in a variety of contexts for the filtering of distributed and cooperative systems. For example, the integration of the SCI fusion into linear filtering is introduced in
[
22
]
in the context of cooperative localization. In that scenario, the independent components of the covariances come e.g., from the dynamic noise of the agents which is assumed independent. SCI have also been applied to several other problems
[
7
,
21
,
27
,
24
]
. Despite SCI’s good performance, which makes it widely used in practice, and the fact that SCI is the natural extension of CI, SCI has not been proven to provide the optimal bound for the set
\mathcal{A}_{\text{Split}}
. This is the goal of this paper.

and two unbiased estimators
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}
and
\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}
of
\underline{\bm{x}}
. The errors of estimation are denoted as
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}-\underline{\bm{x}}
and
\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B}-\underline{\bm{x}}
, and their covariances and cross-covariance are denoted as
\bm{C}_{A}=\mathrm{E}[\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{A}^{\intercal}]
,
\bm{C}_{B}=\mathrm{E}[\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}\underline{\bm{\tilde{x}}}_{B}^{\intercal}]

\displaystyle=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A,1}+\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A,2},

\displaystyle=\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B,1}+\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B,2}.

The condition “

\bm{C}_{F}(\bm{K,\bm{P}_{AB}})\preceq\bm{B}_{F}

Main result: Minimal bound

The main result is that the bounds which are solution of Problem

Theorem 2
.

Let
(\bm{K},\bm{B}_{F})
define a conservative fusion.
(\bm{K},\bm{B}_{F})

A solution of Problem

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bar{\omega}^{*}\bm{B}_{\text{SCI}}(\omega^{*})(\bm{P}_{B}+\bar{\omega}^{*}\bm{Q}_{B})^{-1},

g(\bm{x})\geq\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)\bm{x}
, so
g(\bm{x})\geq\max_{\omega}\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)\bm{x}
. According to Theorem
8
, for all
\bm{x}
, the bound
g(\bm{x})
is reached at some
\omega_{0}
. Consequently,
g
can be re-expressed as:

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})=\max_{\omega\in[0,1]}\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)\bm{x}.

Geometrically, the set
\mathcal{V}^{*}
can also be re-expressed as:

\mathcal{V}^{*}=\left\{\bm{x}\ |\ g(\bm{x})\leq 1\right\}=\bigcap_{\omega\in[0,1]}\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)).

(27)

Thus, the minimal set
\mathcal{V}^{*}
is also characterized by the intersection of the ellipsoids induced by SCI bounds. As a consequence,
\mathcal{V}^{*}
is not only a volume common to all ellipsoids associated with conservative bounds, but it is also the largest volume common to these ellipsoids.

The common volume
\mathcal{V}^{*}
of the conservative bounds is now characterized by SCI bounds. The next section shows that SCI bounds generate also the smaller ellipsoids containing this volume.

5
Tightness of SCI bounds over
\mathcal{V}^{*}

An ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\bm{P})
is said to
tightly circumscribe

\mathcal{V}^{*}
, if for any other ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q})
,
\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q})\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{P})
implies
\bm{P}=\bm{Q}
. In other words, there is no ellipsoid smaller than
\mathcal{E}(\bm{P})
that contains
\mathcal{V}^{*}
. In this section, we characterize the ellipsoids that tightly circumscribe the set
\mathcal{V}^{*}
. The main result is the following theorem. Its proof is inspired by the proof of Kahan for the intersection of ellipsoids
[
17
]
. It is a proof by exhaustion whose cases have been adapted to SCI.

Theorem 9
.

If
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B})
tightly circumscribes
\mathcal{V}^{*}
, then there exists
\omega_{1}\in[0,1]
such that
\bm{B}=\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{B}
be a bound whose ellipsoid tightly circumscribes
\mathcal{V}^{*}
and denote
\bm{H}=\bm{B}^{-1}
its precision matrix. Let:

\phi=\min_{\bm{x}\neq 0}\frac{g(\bm{x})}{\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}\bm{x}}.

By using the definition of
g
:

\phi=\min_{\bm{x}\neq 0}\min_{\bm{P}_{AB}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{Split}}}\frac{\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{M}_{F}^{*}(\bm{P}_{AB})\bm{x}}{\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}\bm{x}}.

As
\phi
is the minimum over a compact (e.g.,
\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}\bm{x}=1
), it is achieved at some vector
\bm{z}
and some cross-covariance
\bm{P}_{AB}^{*}
. Furthermore, by construction
\phi\geq 1
, and
\phi=1
otherwise,
\mathcal{E}(\frac{1}{\phi}\bm{B})
would be a smaller ellipsoid than
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B})
containing
\mathcal{V}^{*}
. By Theorem
8
, there exists a unique
\omega_{0}\in[0,1]
such that
g(\bm{z})=\bm{z}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{0})\bm{z}
. Assume for the time being, that the following property is true for every
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{\intercal}(\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{0})-\bm{H})\bm{y}\geq 0.

(
\mathcal{C}_{y}
)

This is equivalent to
\bm{H}\preceq\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{0})
. Then, as SCI bounds are conservative,
\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{0}))
, and the tightness implies that
\bm{B}=\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{0})
which concludes the proof. To prove Theorem

C

The reverse is not always true: a SCI bound may not be tight. An example has already been seen in Figure

: the SCI bound for
\omega=0
corresponding to
\bm{C}_{B}
is not tight. However, if
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))

touches

\mathcal{V}^{*}
, then the bound is tight as claimed in the following theorem.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\omega_{1}\in[0,1]
,
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
tightly circumscribes
\mathcal{V}^{*}
if and only if there exists
\bm{x}\neq 0
such that
g(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}
.

Proof.

As SCI bounds are conservative, for all
\bm{x}
,
\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}\leq g(\bm{x})
. If
\forall\bm{x}\neq 0
,
\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}<g(\bm{x})
, consider
\phi=\min_{\bm{x}\neq 0}g(\bm{x})/\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}
. As
\phi
is the minimum over a compact, it is reached at some vector
\bm{z}\neq 0
. Since
\bm{z}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)\bm{z}<g(\bm{z})
,
\phi>1
. Then, it can be verified that
\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\frac{1}{\phi}\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
. Thus,
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
does not tightly circumscribe
\mathcal{V}^{*}
.

Conversely, assume that there exists
\bm{x}\neq 0
such that
g(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}
and consider another ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B})
such that
\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{B})\subseteq\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
. We can chose
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B})
tight and Theorem
9
states that there exists
\omega_{2}\in[0,1]
such that
\bm{B}=\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{2})
. Let us prove that
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
. By assumption
g(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\bm{x}\leq\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{2})\bm{x}\leq g(\bm{x})
, so
g(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{2})\bm{x}
. As the function
h:\omega\mapsto\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)\bm{x}
is strictly concave on
[0,1]
by Lemma
6
, it reaches its maximum exactly once on
[0,1]
. Furthermore, as SCI is conservative,
\forall\omega\in[0,1]
,
h(\omega)\leq g(\bm{x})=h(\omega_{1})=h(\omega_{2})
. Thus,
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
, and
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
tightly circumscribes
\mathcal{V}^{*}
.
∎

6
Proof of the main result

Thanks to Theorem
9
, we are now in a position to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
\omega^{*}\in\arg\min_{\omega\in[0,1]}J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))
. Let us prove that for any conservative bound
\bm{B}_{F}
,
J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega^{*}))\leq J(\bm{B}_{F})
.

Let a pair
(\bm{K},\bm{B}_{F})
define a conservative fusion. According to Lemma
5
, the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(\bm{B}_{F})
contains
\mathcal{V}^{*}
.
If the inclusion is tight, according to Theorem
9
, there exists
\omega_{1}\in[0,1]
such that
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})=\bm{B}_{F}
. In this case,
J(\bm{B}_{F})=J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))
. If the inclusion is not tight, there exists a smaller ellipsoid which circumscribes tightly
\mathcal{V}^{*}
and then there exists
\omega_{1}\in[0,1]
such that
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\preceq\bm{B}_{F}
and
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\neq\bm{B}_{F}
. In this case, since
J
is increasing,
J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))<J(\bm{B}_{F})
. In both cases as
\omega^{*}\in\arg\min_{\omega\in[0,1]}J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))
,
J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega^{*}))\leq J(\bm{B}_{F})
. Hence
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega^{*})
reaches the minimum.

Furthermore,
(\bm{K},\bm{B}_{F})
is also solution of Problem
1
if and only if
J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega^{*}))=J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1}))=J(\bm{B}_{F})
. By definition, the first equality is equivalent to
\omega_{1}\in\arg\min_{\omega\in[0,1]}J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))
, and as
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})\preceq\bm{B}_{F}
, the second equality is equivalent to
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega_{1})=\bm{B}_{F}
.
∎

7
Discussion

The most important implication of Theorem
2
is the drastic simplification of Problem
1
. It can be reformulated as:

Problem 12

(New Optimal Fusion with Split Covariances)
.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\minimize\limits_{\omega}&J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))\\
\subject&0\leq\omega\leq 1\end{array}\right.

where
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega)
is given by (
9
).

As a consequence, instead of optimizing for
O(n^{2})
unknowns, there is now only one unknown lying on a segment. Such optimization becomes trivial for modern solvers. Even a linear search would be efficient. In addition, the function
\omega\mapsto J(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))
has been proven to be convex if the cost function is the determinant
[
20
]
, making the optimization process even faster. It is also convex for the trace as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

The function
\omega\mapsto\trace(\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(\omega))
is convex on
[0,1]
.

For low dimensions,
n\leq 4
,
[
29
]
proposes closed-formed solutions for the parameters
\omega
minimizing the trace and the determinant of the CI bounds. These solutions may be adapted with SCI to speed-up even more the optimization.

Geometrically, the optimality of the SCI fusion have been proved using the tightness over the minimal set
\mathcal{V}^{*}
. Therefore, the optimality of the SCI fusion does not depend on the (increasing) cost function. However, similarly to the CI fusion, the optimal bound generally depends on the cost function: e.g., optimizing the trace or the determinant results generally in different optimal bounds. A notable difference with CI is that, except in the trivial cases where
\bm{C}_{A}\preceq\bm{C}_{B}
or
\bm{C}_{B}\preceq\bm{C}_{A}
, all CI bounds tightly circumscribe the minimal volume
[
17
]
(in the case of CI, it is the intersection of the ellipsoids
\mathcal{E}(\bm{C}_{A})
and
\mathcal{E}(\bm{C}_{B})
). This implies that all CI bounds reach the minimal bound for some cost function. For SCI, this is not the case as stated in Theorem
11
. For example, if
\bm{P}_{A}=\bm{P}_{B}=\bm{Q}_{A}=\bm{Q}_{B}=\bm{I}
, it can be proved (it is not detailed here) that there is only one tight SCI bound: for
\omega=1/2
. In this case,
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{SCI}}(1/2)
is the minimal bound for all increasing cost functions.

SCI provides the optimal fusion bound when the estimators are split into a correlated (but to an unknown degree) component and an uncorrelated component. Therefore, by considering the limit cases in which one of these two components is null, we rediscover two well-known optimal fusions. If there is no uncorrelated component, i.e., if
\bm{Q}_{A}=\bm{Q}_{B}=\bm{0}
, then the SCI equations (
9
) become the CI equations (
6
), and the admissible set
\mathcal{A}_{\text{Split}}
becomes
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
. CI has indeed been proven to provide the optimal bound for the set
\bar{\mathcal{A}}

[
28
]
; in fact, this was the original motivation of this work. On the other hand, if the correlated components are null, i.e., if
\bm{P}_{A}=\bm{P}_{B}=\bm{0}
, the only admissible cross-covariance is
\bm{P}_{AB}=\bm{0}
and the admissible set
\mathcal{A}
is a singleton. In this case, the SCI equations (
9
) becomes the well-known Information Filter (IF):

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\text{IF}}^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{IF}}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{A}+\bm{C}_{B}^{-1}\underline{\bm{\hat{x}}}_{B},

(28a)

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\text{IF}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}+\bm{C}_{B}^{-1}.

(28b)

This corresponds to the application of the Bar-Shalom-Campo formula with
\bm{C}_{AB}=\bm{0}
. The bound
\bm{C}_{\text{IF}}
is actually the
true
covariance of the error of the fused estimator. SCI is therefore a more general optimal fusion rule than both CI and the information filter.

(29b)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{F}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{\rho+\omega(1-\rho)}\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}+\frac{\bar{\omega}}{\rho+\bar{\omega}(1-\rho)}\bm{C}_{B}^{-1},

(29c)

with
\omega\in[0,1]
. By defining
\gamma=\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}
, or equivalently letting
\omega=\frac{1}{1+\gamma}
, (
29
) becomes:

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{A}

\displaystyle=(1+\gamma\rho)^{-1}\bm{B}_{F}\bm{C}_{A}^{-1},

(30a)

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{B}

\displaystyle=(1+\gamma^{-1}\rho)^{-1}\bm{B}_{F}\bm{C}_{B}^{-1},

(30b)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{F}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1+\gamma\rho)^{-1}\bm{C}_{A}^{-1}+(1+\gamma^{-1}\rho)^{-1}\bm{C}_{B}^{-1}.

(30c)

The bounds defined by (
30
) was proposed in
[
34
]
and proved to provide the minimal bound when considering the trace as a cost function. Theorem
2
now adds that (
30
) also provides the minimal bound when considering any increasing cost function.
Figure
5
presents the evolution of the minimum volume
\mathcal{V}^{*}
as the maximum correlation
\rho
ranges from
0
to
1
. As it can been seen, if
\rho=0
, then
\mathcal{V}^{*}
is an ellipse: it is
\mathcal{E}(\bm{C}_{\text{IF}})
. On the other hand if
\rho=1
, then
\mathcal{V}^{*}
corresponds to the intersection of
\mathcal{E}(\bm{C}_{A})
and
\mathcal{E}(\bm{C}_{B})
as in CI. As the maximum correlation
\rho
increases, the set
\mathcal{V}^{*}
also increases what illustrates why the information filter gives smaller bound than SCI and than CI: the less information, the greater the minimal volume
\mathcal{V}^{*}
, and the larger the bound.

8
Conclusion

The SCI fusion has been proven to reach the minimum covariance bound for the fusion of two estimators having split covariances. SCI can be adapted to any number of estimators by extending the convex combination of precision matrices. In this case, it still defines a conservative fusion
[
15
]
. Unfortunately, it has been demonstrated, in e.g.,
[
28
,
1
]
, that CI is not optimal for more than two estimators. The same arguments also apply to SCI.
As a consequence, SCI and CI are both suboptimal when applied with sequential inputs. The definition of recursive filters, such as the well-known Kalman filter, is therefore guaranteed to provided suboptimal bounds. Future work should focus on the research of the optimal fusion for more than two estimators. This challenging task is related to the problem of circumscribing an ellipsoid to the intersection of several ellipsoids. This problem is open since at least the work of Kahan
[
17
]
.

A.5
Proof of Lemma

Recall that we note
