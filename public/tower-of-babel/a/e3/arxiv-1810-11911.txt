Probabilistic Multilevel Clustering via Composite Transportation Distance


Abstract
We propose a novel probabilistic approach to multilevel clustering problems based on composite transportation distance, which is a variant of transportation distance where the underlying metric is Kullback-Leibler divergence. Our method involves solving a joint optimization problem over spaces of probability measures to simultaneously discover grouping structures within groups and among groups. By exploiting the connection of our method to the problem of finding composite transportation barycenters, we develop fast and efficient optimization algorithms even for potentially large-scale multilevel datasets. Finally, we present experimental results with both synthetic and real data to demonstrate the efficiency and scalability of the proposed approach.

Introduction

Clustering is a classic and fundamental problem in machine
learning. Popular clustering methods such as K-means and the EM algorithm
have been the workhorses of exploratory data analysis. However, the underlying model for such methods is a simple flat partition or a mixture model, which do not capture multilevel structures (e.g.,
words are grouped into documents, documents are grouped into corpora) that arise in many applications in the physical, biological or cognitive sciences. The clustering of multilevel structured data calls for novel methodologies beyond classical clustering.

One natural approach for capturing multilevel structures is to use a hierarchy in which data are clustered locally into groups, and those groups are partitioned in a “global clustering.” Attempts to develop algorithms of this kind can be roughly classified
into two categories. The first category makes use of probabilistic models, often based on Dirichlet process priors. Examples in this vein include the Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP)
[
18
]
,
Nested Dirichlet Process (NDP)
[
15
]
, Multilevel Clustering
with Context (MC
2
)
[
11
]
, and Multilevel Clustering
Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (MLC-HDP)
[
21
]
. Despite the
flexibility and solid statistical foundation of these models, they have seen limited application to large-scale datasets, given concerns about the computational scaling of the sampling-based algorithms that are generally used for inference under these models.

A second category of multilevel methods is based on tools from optimal transport theory, where algorithms such as Wasserstein barycenters provide scalable computation
[
3
,
4
]
. These methods trace their origins to a seminal paper by
[
14
]
which established a connection between the K-means algorithm and the problem of determining a discrete probability measure that is close in Wasserstein distance
[
19
]
to the empirical measure of the data. Based on this connection, it is possible to use Wasserstein distance to develop a combined local/global multilevel clustering method
[
5
]
.

The specific multilevel clustering method proposed in
[
5
]
has, however, its limitations. Most notably, as that method uses K-means as a building block, it is only applicable to continuous data. When being used to cluster discrete data, it yields poor results. In this work, we make use of a novel form of transportation distance, which is termed as
composite transportation distance

[
12
]
, to overcome this limitation, and to provide a more general multilevel clustering method. The salient feature of composite transportation distance is that it utilizes Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence as the underlying metric of optimal transportation distance, in contrast to the standard Euclidean metric that has been used in optimal transportation approaches to clustering to date.

In order to motivate our use of composite transportation distance, we start with a one- level structure data in which the data are generated from a finite mixture model, e.g., a mixture of (multivariate) Gaussian distributions or multinomial distributions. Unlike traditional estimators such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), the high-level idea of using composite transportation distance is to determine optimal parameters to minimize the KL cost of moving the likelihood from one cluster to another cluster. Intuitively, with such a distance, we can employ the underlying geometric structure of parameters to perform efficient clustering with the data. Another advantage of composite transportation distance is its flexibility to generalize to multilevel structure data. More precisely, by representing each group in a multilevel clustering problem by an unknown mixture model (local clustering), we can determine the optimal parameters, which can be represented as local (probability) measures, of each group via optimization problems based on composite transportation distance. Then, in order to determine global clustering among these groups, we perform a composite transportation barycenter problem over the local measures to obtain a global measure over the space of mixture models, which serves as a partition of these groups. As a result, our final method, which we refer to as
multilevel composite transportation
(MCT), involves solving a joint optimal transport optimization problem with respect to both a local clustering and a global clustering based on the cost matrix encoding KL divergence among atoms. The solution strategy involves using the fast computation method of Wasserstein barycenters combined with coordinate descent.

In summary, our main contributions are the following: (i) A new optimization formulation for clustering based on a variety of multilevel data types, including both continuous and discrete observations, based on composite transportation distance; (ii) We provide a highly scalable solution strategy for this optimization formulation; (iii) Although our approach avoids the use of the Dirichlet process as a building block, the approach has much of the flexibility the hierarchical Dirichlet process in its ability to share atoms among local clusterings. We thus are able to borrow strength among clusters, which improves statistical efficiency under certain applications, e.g., image annotation in computer vision.

3
formulates the multilevel composite transportation optimization model, while Section
4
presents simulation studies with both synthetic and real data. Finally, we conclude the paper with a discussion in Section
5

2.3
Composite transportation barycenter for mixtures of exponential families

In this section, we consider a problem of finding composite transportation barycenters for a collection of mixtures of exponential family. For

\displaystyle\underset{{}_{\mathbf{w}_{L},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}}}{\text{argmin}\ }

to denote a finite mixture with at most
L

P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}
and

\displaystyle\underset{{}_{\mathbf{w}_{L},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}}}{\text{argmin}\ }

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}\min_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\in\Pi\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\mathbf{w}_{L}\right)}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j},\boldsymbol{M}^{j}\right\rangle,

\boldsymbol{M}^{j}=(M_{uv}^{j})
satisfy

1\leq j\leq J
, which has the closed form defined in Eq. (

3
Probabilistic clustering with multilevel structural data

Assume that we have
J
groups of independent data,
X_{j,i}
, where
1\leq j\leq J
and
1\leq i\leq n_{j}
; i.e., the data are presented in a two-level grouping structure. Our goal is to find simultaneously the local clustering for each data group and the global clustering across groups.

3.1
Multilevel composite transportation (MCT)

To facilitate the discussion, for each
1\leq j\leq J
, we denote the empirical measure associated with group
j
as

\displaystyle P_{n_{j}}^{j}:=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\delta_{X_{j,i}}.

Additionally, we assume that the number of local and global clusters are bounded. In particular, we allow local group
j
to have at most
K_{j}
clusters, which can be represented as a mixture of exponential families
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j},\Theta_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}
, while we have at most
\boldsymbol{C}
global clusters among
J
given groups. Here, each global cluster can be represented as a finite mixture distribution
Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}
with at most
L
clusters, where
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}=(w_{1}^{m},\ldots,w_{L}^{m})
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}=(\psi_{1}^{m},\ldots,\psi_{L}^{m})
are global weights and atoms for
1\leq m\leq\boldsymbol{C}
, respectively.

3.1.1
Local clustering and global clustering

With the local clustering, we perform composite transportation distance optimization for group
j
, which can be expressed as in (
5
). More precisely, this step can be viewed as finding optimal local weights
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j}
and local atoms
\Theta_{K_{j}}^{j}
to minimize the composite transportation distance
\widehat{W}(P_{n}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}})
for all
1\leq j\leq J
. Regarding the global clustering with
J
given groups, we can treat the finite mixture probability distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}
of each group as observations in the space of distributions over probability distributions. Thus we achieve a clustering of these distributions by means of an optimization with the following composite transportation distance on the space of finite mixtures of finite mixtures:

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}}\widehat{W}\left(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\right),

where we denote
\boldsymbol{P}:=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}:=\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}b_{m}\delta_{Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}}
.

3.1.2
MCT formulation

Since the finite mixture probability distributions
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}
in each group are unobserved, we determine them by minimizing the objective cost functions in the local clustering and global clustering simultaneously. In particular, we consider the following objective function:

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)+\zeta\widehat{W}\left(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\right),

(9)

where
\zeta>0
serves as a penalization term between the global cluster and local cluster. We call this problem
Multilevel Composite Transportation (MCT)
.

3.2
Regularized version of MCT

To obtain a favorable computation profile with MCT, we consider a regularized version of the composite transportation distances in both the local and global structures. To simplify the discussion, we denote
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\in\Pi(\frac{1}{n_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{j}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j})

\displaystyle\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\in\Pi(\frac{1}{n_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{j}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j})}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j},\boldsymbol{M}^{j}\right\rangle,

\boldsymbol{M}^{j}

P_{n_{j}}^{j}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)-\lambda_{l}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}),

5
Discussion

We have proposed a probabilistic model that uses a novel composite transportation distance to cluster data with potentially complexed hierarchical multilevel structures. The proposed model is able to handle both discrete and continuous observations. Experiments on simulated and real-world data have shown that our approach outperforms competing methods that also target multilevel clustering tasks. Our developed model is based on the exponential family assumption with data distribution and thereby applies naturally to other data types; e.g., a mixture of Poisson distributions
[
8
]
. Finally, there are several possible directions for extensions from our work. First, it is of interest to extend our approach to richer settings of hierarchical data similar to those considered in MC
2

[
11
]
; e.g., when group-level context is available in the data. Second, our method requires knowledge of the upper bounds with the numbers of clusters both in local and global clustering. It is of practical importance to develop methods that are able to estimate these cardinalities efficiently.

A.1
Update weights

Our strategy for updating weights
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K}
in the above objective function relies on solving the

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{M}\right\rangle-\lambda\mathbb{H}\left(\boldsymbol{\pi}\right)

\boldsymbol{\pi}\boldsymbol{1}_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{ij}M_{ij}+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{ij}\left(\log\pi_{ij}-1\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{ij}-\frac{1}{n}\right).

By taking the derivative of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\pi_{ij}
and setting it to zero, the following equation holds

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\pi_{ij}}

\displaystyle=M_{ij}+\lambda\log\pi_{ij}+\kappa_{i}=0.

The above equation leads to

\displaystyle\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{-M_{ij}-\kappa_{i}}{\lambda}\right)=\left(f(X_{i}|\theta_{j})\right)^{1/\lambda}\exp\left(\frac{-\kappa_{i}}{\lambda}\right).

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{-\kappa_{i}}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(f(X_{i}|\theta_{j})\right)^{1/\lambda}}.

Governed by the previous equations, we find that

\displaystyle\pi_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\frac{\left(f(X_{i}|\theta_{j})\right)^{1/\lambda}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(f(X_{i}|\theta_{j})\right)^{1/\lambda}}.

(16)

\displaystyle\omega_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)+\zeta\widehat{W}\left(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\right)-R\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{a}\right),

where
R\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{a}\right)=\lambda_{l}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j})+\zeta\lambda_{a}[\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{a})+\lambda_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m})]
is a combination of all regularized terms for the local and global clustering. Here, for the simplicity of our argument, we choose
\zeta=1
to derive our learning updates. In the above objective function,
\boldsymbol{P}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}b_{m}\delta_{Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}}
. We summarize below the notations for our algorithm development.

Variables of local clustering structures

•

Local transportation plans for group
j
:
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}=\left\{\pi_{uv}^{j}\right\}_{u,v}\in\Pi(\frac{1}{n_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{j}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j})

s.t.
\sum_{v}\pi_{uv}^{j}=\frac{1}{n_{j}}
, and
\sum_{u}\pi_{uv}^{j}=\omega_{v}^{j}
,

•

Local atoms for local group
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}=\left\{\theta_{k}^{j}\right\}_{k=1}^{K_{j}}

and their local mixing weights
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{j}=\left\{\omega_{k}^{j}\right\}_{k=1}^{K_{j}}
.

Variables of assignment group to barycenter

•

Global transportation plan
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{jm})\in\Pi(\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{1}_{J},\boldsymbol{b})
between
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}
.

Variables of global clustering structures

•

Partial global transportation plans between local measure
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}
and global measure
Q_{\mathbf{w}^{m},\Psi_{k}^{m}}^{m}
:
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}=\left\{\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\right\}_{k,l}
where

\sum_{l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}=\omega_{k}^{j}
and
\sum_{l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}=w_{l}^{m}

for all
1\leq j\leq J
and
1\leq m\leq\boldsymbol{C}
.

•

Global atoms for global measure
\Psi_{L}^{m}=\left\{\psi_{l}^{m}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
.
and global mixing weights
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}=\left\{w_{l}^{m}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
where

w_{l}^{m}=\sum_{k}\tau_{kl}^{jm}
for any
j
.

C.1
Local clustering updates

As being mentioned in the main text, to obtain updates for local weights
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j}
and local atoms
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}
, we solve the following regularized composite transportation barycenter problem

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)-\lambda_{l}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j})+\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\widehat{W}(P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j},Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\Psi_{L}^{m}}^{m})-\lambda_{g}\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}).

The above objective function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\in\Pi(\frac{1}{n_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{j}},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j})}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j},\boldsymbol{M}^{j}\right\rangle-\lambda_{l}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j})+\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}\in\Pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m})}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j,m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 240.00037pt-\lambda_{g}\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m})

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{M}^{j}
is the cost matrix between
P_{n_{j}}^{j}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}
that has a formulation as

\displaystyle[M^{j}]_{uv}=-\log f(X_{j,u}|\theta_{v}^{j}),

for
1\leq u\leq n_{j}
and
1\leq v\leq K_{j}
. Additionally, the cost matrix
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j,m}
has the following formulation

\displaystyle\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}

\displaystyle=A\left(\theta_{k}^{j}\right)-A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)-\left\langle\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right),\left(\theta_{k}^{j}-\psi_{l}^{m}\right)\right\rangle.

Update local weights:

The idea for obtaining the local solutions of above objective function is similar to that in Section
A
.

Update local atoms:

Given the updates for local weight
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j}
, to obtain the update equation
for local atoms
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}
, we optimize the following
objective function

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}-\sum_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\sum_{v=1}^{K_{j}}

\displaystyle\pi_{uv}^{j}\log f(X_{j,u}|\theta_{v}^{j})+\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\sum_{v,l}\tau_{vl}^{j,m}\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}.

(21)

Since
f(x|\theta)
is an exponential family distribution with natural
parameter
\theta
, we can represent it as

\displaystyle f(x|\theta)

\displaystyle=h(x)\exp\left(\left\langle T\left(x\right),\theta\right\rangle-A\left(\theta\right)\right),

where
A\left(\theta\right)
is the log-partition function which
is convex. Given that formulation of
f(x|\theta)
, our objective
function (
17
) is equivalent to minimize
the following objective function

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\sum_{v=1}^{K_{j}}\pi_{uv}^{j}\left(\left\langle T\left(X_{j,u}\right),\theta_{v}^{j}\right\rangle-A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)\right)+\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\sum_{v,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\biggr[A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)-A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 240.00037pt-\left\langle\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right),\left(\theta_{v}^{j}-\psi_{l}^{m}\right)\right\rangle\biggr].

By direct computation,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}
has the following partial derivative
with respect to
\theta_{v}^{j}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}}{\partial\theta_{v}^{j}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\pi_{uv}^{j}\left(T(X_{j,u})-\nabla A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)\right)+\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\tau_{vl}^{j,m}\left[\nabla A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)-\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\pi_{uv}^{j}T(X_{j,u})+\omega_{v}^{j}\nabla A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)+\sum_{m=1}^{C}a_{jm}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\tau_{vl}^{j,m}\left(\nabla A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)-\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)\right),

where in the last equality,
we use the identity
\sum_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\pi_{uv}^{j}=\omega_{v}^{j}
.

Given the above partial derivatives, we can update the atoms
\theta_{v}^{j}
to be the solution of the following
equation

\displaystyle\nabla A\left(\theta_{v}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{C}a_{jm}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\tau_{vl}^{j,m}\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)+\sum\limits_{u=1}^{n_{j}}\pi_{uv}^{j}T(X_{j,u})}{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{C}a_{jm}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\tau_{vl}^{j,m}+\omega_{v}^{j}}

(22)

C.2
Computing global transportation plan

Given the updates for local weights
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j}
and local atoms
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}
for
1\leq j\leq J
, we now develop an update on for global transportation plan
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{jm})
between
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}
. Our strategy for the update relies on solving the following objective function

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}}\sum_{j,m}a_{jm}\widehat{W}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j},Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}\right)-\lambda_{a}\mathbb{H}\left(\boldsymbol{a}\right).

where
\boldsymbol{a}
in the above infimum satisfies the constraint
\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{1}_{K}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
. By means of Lagrangian multiplier, the above objective function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{global}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m}a_{jm}\widehat{W}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j},Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}\right)-\lambda_{a}\mathbb{H}\left(\boldsymbol{a}\right)+\kappa_{a}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}-\frac{1}{J}\right),

The function
\mathcal{F}_{\text{global}}
has the partial derivative with respect to
a_{jm}
as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\text{global}}}{\partial a_{jm}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}\left(P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j},Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}^{m}\right)+\lambda_{a}\log a_{jm}+\kappa_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}+\lambda_{a}\ln a_{jm}+\kappa_{a}.

Setting the above derivative to 0 and invoking the constraint that
\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}=\frac{1}{J}
,
we find that

\displaystyle a_{jm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\frac{\exp\left(-\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}\right)^{1/\lambda_{a}}\,}{\sum_{m}\exp\left(-\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}\right)^{1/\lambda_{a}}}

(23)

for
1\leq j\leq J
and
1\leq m\leq L
.

C.3
Global clustering updates

Given the updates with local weights and atoms as well as the global transportation plan, we are now ready to develop an update for global weights
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}
and global atoms
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}
for
1\leq m\leq\boldsymbol{C}
. In particular, the objective
function for updating these global parameters are as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\widehat{W}(P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j},Q_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\Psi_{L}^{m}}^{m})-\lambda_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}\mathbb{H}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m})

The above objective function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=1}^{\boldsymbol{C}}a_{jm}\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}\in\Pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m})}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j,m}\right\rangle+\lambda_{g}\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\left(\log\tau_{kl}^{j,m}-1\right)\biggr)

Given the above objective function, for each
m
, to update the global weights
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}
and global atoms
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}
, we consider the following composite transportation barycenter

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{jm}\inf\limits_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}\in\Pi(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m})}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{j,m}\right\rangle+\lambda_{g}\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\left(\log\tau_{kl}^{j,m}-1\right)\biggr).

Update global weights:

Given the above objective function, the idea for updating the global weights
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}
is similar to Algorithm 1 in
[
4
]
.

Update partial transportation plans:

Once global weights are obtained, we can use Algorithm 3 in
[
4
]
to update the optimal partial transportation plans
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j,m}
between local measure
P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}
and global measure
Q_{\mathbf{w}^{m},\Psi_{k}^{m}}^{m}
.

Update global atoms:

With the updates for the global weight
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m}
, to obtain the update equation
for global atoms
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m}
, we minimize the following
objective function

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{p-global}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{jm}\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\gamma_{kl}^{j,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{jm}\sum_{k,l}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\biggr(A\left(\theta_{k}^{j}\right)-A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)-\left\langle\nabla A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right),\theta_{k}^{j}-\psi_{l}^{m}\right\rangle\biggr).

Taking the derivative of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{p-global}}
with respect to
\psi_{l}^{m}
and setting it to zero, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\text{p-global}}}{\partial\psi_{l}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{jm}\sum_{k}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\nabla^{2}A\left(\psi_{l}^{m}\right)\left(\psi_{l}^{m}-\theta_{k}^{j}\right)=0.

Since the log-partition function
A\left(\cdot\right)
is convex,

\nabla^{2}A\left(\psi_{v}\right)
is a positive-semidefinite matrix.
Therefore, we can choose
\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{jm}\sum_{k}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\left(\psi_{l}^{m}-\theta_{k}^{j}\right)=0
,
which means that

\displaystyle\psi_{l}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}a_{jm}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}\theta_{k}^{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}a_{jm}\tau_{kl}^{j,m}}.

C.4
Proof for local convergence of Algorithm
2

Equipped with the above updates with local and global parameters of regularized MCT, we are ready to demonstrate the convergence of Algorithm
2
to local solution of objective function (
12
) of regularized MCT in Theorem
2
. To simplify the argument, we only provide proof sketch for this theorem.

In particular, we denote
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}
and

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}
as the updates of local weights and
local atoms in step
t
of Algorithm
2

for
t\geq 0
. Similarly, we denote
\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}
as the updates of global weights and global atoms at step
t
. Furthermore, we denote

\displaystyle g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}\})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\widehat{W}\left(P_{n_{j}}^{j},P_{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j}}^{j}\right)+\zeta\widehat{W}\left(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 140.00021pt-R\left(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{a}\right).

Then, according to local clustering updates step, we would have

\displaystyle g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}\})\geq g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}\}).

On the other hand, invoking the global clustering updates step, we achieve

\displaystyle g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}\})\geq g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t+1)}\})

Governed by the above results, for any
t\geq 0
, the following holds

\displaystyle g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t)}\})\geq g(\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{K_{j}}^{j,(t+1)}\},\{\mathbf{w}_{L}^{m,(t+1)}\},\{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}^{m,(t+1)}\}).

As a consequence, we achieve the conclusion of Theorem
2
.
