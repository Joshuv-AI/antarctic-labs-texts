On the squarefree values of a 4 + b 3 a^{4}+b^{3}
Gian Cordana Sanjaya, Xiaoheng Wang

Abstract
In this article, we prove that the density of integers a , b a,b such that a 4 + b 3 a^{4}+b^{3} is squarefree, when ordered by max ⁡ { | a | 1 / 3 , | b | 1 / 4 } {\rm max}\{|a|^{1/3},|b|^{1/4}\} , equals the conjectured product of the local densities. We show that the same is true for polynomials of the form β ​ a 4 + α ​ b 3 \beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3} for any fixed integers α \alpha and β \beta . We give an exact count for the number of pairs ( a , b ) (a,b) of integers with max ⁡ { | a | 1 / 3 , | b | 1 / 4 } < X {\rm max}\{|a|^{1/3},|b|^{1/4}\}<X such that β ​ a 4 + α ​ b 3 \beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3} is squarefree, with a power-saving error term.

Introduction

A classical question in analytic number theory is to determine the probability that a given polynomial
F
with integer coefficients takes squarefree values when evaluated at random integers. The simplest case of one-variable and degree-one asks for the probability that a random integer is squarefree, which is well-known to be
6/\pi^{2}
. The one-variable degree-two case can also be solved by elementary methods. The one-variable degree-three case was solved by Hooley
[
14
]
. For homogeneous polynomials of two variables, the question is known up to degree 6 due to Greaves
[
8
]
. For non-homogeneous polynomials of two variables that factor completely into a product of linear factors over some extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
, the question is known also up to degree 6 due to Hooley
[
13
]
. Very recently, Kowalski
[
16
]
proved the case where
F
is a sum of at least
3
cubic polynomials in different variables. The cases when
F
is the discriminant of monic polynomials or when
F
is the discriminant of general polynomials were solved by Bhargava-Shankar-Wang
[
4
,
5
]
.

Conditionally, Granville
[
7
]
showed that the one-variable case in general follows from the
abc
-conjecture and a bound on the error term was later obtained by Murty-Pasten
[
17
]
. Poonen
[
18
]
generalized it to the multi-variable case where the variables are growing to infinity one by one.

Very little is known otherwise. In most cases, even the infinitude of squarefree values is open—the most famous example being
a^{4}+2.
There is a handful of results on the number of prime values taken by polynomials: Friedlander-Iwaniec
[
6
]
for
a^{4}+b^{2}
; Heath-Brown
[
10
]
for
a^{3}+2b^{3}
and its generalization by Heath-Brown-Moroz
[
12
]
for irreducible binary cubic forms; and Health-Brown-Li
[
11
]
for
a^{2}+p^{4}
where
p
is a prime.

In this paper, we consider for the first time the polynomial
a^{4}+b^{3}
. Our method in fact allows us to consider all polynomials of the form
\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3}
for any fixed integers
\alpha
and
\beta
. We prove:

Theorem 1.1

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be fixed nonzero integers such that
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)
is squarefree. Let

N(X;\alpha,\beta)=\#\{(a,b)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\colon{\rm max}\{|a|^{1/3},|b|^{1/4}\}<X,\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3}\mbox{ is squarefree}\}.

For any positive integer
m
, let
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(m)=\#\{(a,b)\bmod{m}\colon m\mid\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3}\}
and let

C(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{p}(1-\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(p^{2})p^{-4}).

Then

N(X;\alpha,\beta)=C(\alpha,\beta)X^{7}+O_{\epsilon}(X^{6.992+\epsilon}).

The implied constant depends on
\alpha
and
\beta
.

The case
\alpha=256
and
\beta=-27
is of special importance since
256b^{3}-27a^{4}
is the discriminant of the quartic polynomial
x^{4}+ax+b.
An elementary calculation shows that
\rho_{256,-27}(p^{2})
equals
p^{3}
for
p=2,3
; and equals
2p^{2}-p
for
p\geq 5
. Therefore, we have:

Theorem 1.2

When pairs
(a,b)
of integers are ordered by
H(a,b)={\rm max}\{|a|^{1/3},|b|^{1/4}\}
, the density of quartic polynomials of the form
x^{4}+ax+b
having squarefree discriminant exists and is equal to

\frac{1}{3}\prod_{p\geq 5}(1-\frac{2}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{p^{3}})

which is approximately
28.03\%
.

It is easy to see that the Euler product
C(\alpha,\beta)
gives an upper bound for the desired density, if it exists, by applying the Chinese Remainder Theorem to more and more primes. As is standard in sieve theory, to demonstrate the lower bound, a “tail estimate” is required to show that there are not too many pairs
(a,b)
of integers such that
\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3}
is divisible by
m^{2}
for some squarefree integer
m
. More precisely, we prove:

Theorem 1.3

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be fixed nonzero integers such that
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)
is squarefree. For any squarefree integer
m
, let

N_{m}(X;\alpha,\beta)=\#\{(a,b)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\colon|a|\leq X^{3},|b|\leq X^{4},m^{2}\mid\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3}\}.

Then for any positive real number
M
and
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>M\\
m\;\mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}N_{m}(X;\alpha,\beta)=O_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{X^{7+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{M}}\right)+O_{\epsilon}(X^{6.992+\epsilon})

(1)

The implied constants depend on
\alpha
and
\beta
.

We note that since the exponents
3
and
4
are coprime, it is enough to prove Theorem
1.3
for one choice of
\alpha
,
\beta
. Indeed, we have

-256\cdot 27\cdot\alpha^{8}\beta^{3}(\beta a^{4}+\alpha b^{3})=256(-3\alpha^{3}\beta b)^{3}-27(4\alpha^{2}\beta a)^{4},

which implies that,

N_{m}(X;\alpha,\beta)\leq N_{m}(c_{\alpha,\beta}X;\,256,-27)

for some constant
c_{\alpha,\beta}
depending only on
\alpha,\beta.
Hence the power saving bound (
1
) for
\alpha=256
and
\beta=-27
implies it for all other
\alpha
and
\beta.
We simplify notation by writing
\Delta(a,b)
for
256b^{3}-27a^{4}
.

For any prime
p
and pair
(a,b)
of integers such that
p^{2}\mid\Delta(a,b)
, we say
p^{2}

strongly divides

\Delta(a,b)
if
p^{2}\mid\Delta(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
for any integers
a^{\prime}\equiv a\pmod{p}
and
b^{\prime}\equiv b\pmod{p}
; otherwise, we say
p^{2}

weakly divides

\Delta(a,b)
. Note in this case, for
p\geq 5
,
p^{2}
strongly divides
\Delta(a,b)
if and only if
p\mid a
and
p\mid b.
For any squarefree integer

, let
{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(1)}
(respectively
{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(2)}
) denote the set of pairs
(a,b)
of integers such that
p^{2}
strongly divides (respectively weakly divides)
\Delta(a,b)
for every prime

. Then we prove:

Theorem 1.4

For any positive real number
M
and
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\rm(a)}\quad\#\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>M\\
m\;\mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\{(a,b)\in{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(1)}\colon H(a,b)<X\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\Big(\frac{X^{7}}{M}\Big)+O\Big(X^{4}\log X\Big);

(2)

\displaystyle{\rm(b)}\quad\#\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>M\\
m\;\mathrm{squarefree}\end{subarray}}\{(a,b)\in{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(2)}\colon H(a,b)<X\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{\epsilon}\Big(X^{6.992+\epsilon}\Big)+O_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{X^{7+\epsilon}}{M}\right),

(3)

where the implied constants are independent of
M
and
X
.

We now briefly describe our methods. Theorem
1.4
(a) is immediate with the first term counting the contribution from
a\neq 0
and the second term counting the contributiom from
a=0
. We devote the rest of the paper to proving Theorem
1.4
(b). We follow the strategy of
[
4
]
to embed
{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(2)}
into the space
W
of
4\times 4
symmetric matrices. More precisely, let
A_{0}
denote the
4\times 4
matrix with
1
’s on the anti-diagonal and
0
’s elsewhere. The group

G={\rm PSO}(A_{0})={\rm SO}(A_{0})/\langle\pm I\rangle
acts on
W
via the action
g\cdot B=gBg^{t}
for
g\in G

and
B\in W
. Define the
invariant polynomial
of an element

B\in W
by

f_{B}(x)=\det(A_{0}x-B).

Then
f_{B}
is a monic quartic polynomial. We extend the definition
H(a,b)
to arbitrary monic quartic polynomials by

H(x^{4}+c_{1}x^{3}+c_{2}x^{2}+c_{3}x+c_{4})={\rm max}\{|c_{1}|,|c_{2}|^{1/2},|c_{3}|^{1/3},|c_{4}|^{1/4}\}.

Define the discriminant and height of an element
B\in W
by the discriminant and height of
f_{B}
, respectively. We then construct a map

\sigma_{m}:{\mathcal{W}}_{m}^{(2)}\rightarrow\frac{1}{4}W({\mathbb{Z}})

with
f_{\sigma_{m}(a,b)}=x^{4}+ax+b
as in
[
4
]
, where
\frac{1}{4}W({\mathbb{Z}})
is the lattice of elements

whose coefficients have denominators dividing
4
.
It thus remains to count
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits in
\frac{1}{4}W({\mathbb{Z}})
that intersect the image of
\sigma_{m}
for some squarefree
m>M
, and have height bounded by
X
.

The space

has several subspaces:
W_{00}
consisting of
B\in W
whose
(1,1)
- and
(1,2)
-entries are
0
;
W_{01}
consisting of
B\in W
whose
(1,1)
- and
(1,3)
-entries are
0
; and
W_{0}
consisting of
B\in W
whose
(1,1)
-entry is 0. The map

in fact lands in

, and so are guaranteed to be

in the sense of
[

]
. We obtain a bound of
O_{\epsilon}(X^{7+\epsilon}/M)
for the distinguished cusps

and

and a bound of

for the “thick” cusp

.

The main novelty of this paper is on counting the distinguished orbits in the main body. We use the circle method to handle the condition that the invariant polynomials have vanishing
x^{2}
-coefficients, combined with the Selberg sieve to impose the distinguished condition to obtain the desired power saving.

We remark that Heath-Brown’s result
[
9
]
on
k
-free values of the polynomial

specializes to squarefree values of the cubic polynomial

where

is a constant. A major observation of
[
9
]
is that counting triples
(n,s,t)
with

when

and

are large and

is fixed, is akin to counting points close to the projective curve

. The bigger

is, which in our case can be as big as

, the worse the estimate gets. As such, we cannot patch the results of
[

]
together to prove Theorem
1.1
. One does immediately obtain the infinitude of squarefree values.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section

(4)

(5)

Let
U\simeq{\mathbb{A}}^{2}\backslash\{\Delta=0\}

Proposition 2.2

The number of elements

with

such that

is either reducible over
{\mathbb{Q}}
or factors as

.

Throughout this proof, we use repeatedly the classical result that the sum
\sum_{|n|<X}d(n)
of the divisor function is

and that the sum
\sum_{|n|<X}\tau_{3}(n)
of the triple-divisor function is

See for example
[

Suppose first

{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then there are

of them with

. Suppose now

Next we consider the case where

Finally, we consider the case where

such

that factors into conjugate quadratic polynomials over some quadratic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

of any stabilizer with invariant polynomial

B\in W({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
with
f_{B}=f
equals
\#G({\mathbb{F}}_{p})=p^{2}(p^{2}-1)^{2}.
There are
p^{2}+O(p)
polynomials
f\in U({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
and so a total of
p^{8}+O(p^{7})
elements
B\in W({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
with
f_{B}\in U({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
. Moreover, for any
f\in U({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
, there is at least one
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-distinguished orbit with stabilizer having size at most
4
. Hence, we have the upper bound
d_{p}\leq\frac{3}{4}p^{8}+O(p^{7})
.

Consider next quartic polynomials of the form

g_{a,b}(x):=(x-a)(x-b)(x^{2}+(a+b)x+(a^{2}+ab+b^{2}))\in U({\mathbb{F}}_{p}).

Since
g_{a,b}(x)
has a linear factor, there is only one distinguished orbit with invariant
g_{a,b}
. Moreover, we have
2\leq\#J_{g_{a,b}}[2]({\mathbb{F}}_{p})\leq 4
. Hence, there is at least one non-distinguished orbit of size at least
|G({\mathbb{F}}_{p})|/4.
It remains to count the number of such
g_{a,b}(x)
with nonzero discriminant, which is equivalent to requiring that
a\neq b
, that
a
is not a root of the quadratic factor, and that the quadratic factor has nonzero discriminant. In other words, we have
a\neq b
,
3(a+b/3)^{2}+(2/3)b^{2}\neq 0
and
3(a+b/3)^{2}+(8/3)b^{2}\neq 0
. Given any
b
, there are at least
p-5
choices for
a
. Finally, given any
g_{a,b}
with nonzero discriminant, we see that
g_{a,b}=g_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
if and only if
x^{2}+(a+b)x+(a^{2}+ab+b^{2})=(x-a^{\prime})(x-b^{\prime})
or
(x-a)(x-b)=(x-a^{\prime})(x-b^{\prime})
, as any other possibility contradicts
\Delta(g_{a,b})\neq 0
. Hence, there are at least
p(p-5)/4
quartic polynomials with nonzero discriminant of the form
g_{a,b}
for some
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}.
Therefore, we have at least
\frac{1}{16}p^{8}+O(p^{7})
non-distinguished elements
B
in
W({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
with
f_{B}\in U({\mathbb{F}}_{p}).

\Box

2.2

Fix

We claim that

is some polynomial with integer coefficients. Suppose for a contradiction that

Hence,

for any monic quartic polynomial

congruent to

. Now for any
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with

and

weakly divides

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, there exists an integer

B(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&m&0\\
0&1&-c_{1}/2&0\\
m&-c_{1}/2&c_{1}^{2}/4-c_{2}&-c_{3}/2\\

A direct computation shows that

We now set

Then

. Note in fact that the image of
\sigma_{m}
lies inside
W_{00}({\mathbb{Q}})
and the
(1,3)
-entry of any element in the image of
\sigma_{m}
is
m
. We summarize the above in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.4

Let

be any squarefree integer. There is a map

(respectively

-equivalent to some element in

p
in absolute value.

Lemma 4.5

If
\gcd(r,m)=1
, then
\displaystyle c_{q}(a;r,m,B_{0})=\frac{1}{m^{9}}c_{q}(a;r)

Otherwise

Proof:

We consider the case
\gcd(r,m)=1

Let

be any integer such that

For any integer

\frac{a}{rm}n\equiv\frac{a\bar{m}}{r}n\pmod{1}

Suppose now
B_{1},B^{\prime}\in V({\mathbb{Z}})

and

Then
q(B_{1})\equiv q(B_{0})\equiv 0\pmod{m}

e\left(\frac{a}{rm}q(B_{1})\right)=e\left(\frac{a\bar{m}}{r}q(B_{1})\right)=e\left(\frac{a\bar{m}}{r}q(B^{\prime})\right).

Since
m
and
r
are coprime, we have by the Chinese Remainder Theorem,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1}\bmod{rm}\\
B_{1}\equiv B_{0}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a}{rm}q(B_{1})\right)=\sum_{B^{\prime}\bmod{r}}e\left(\frac{a}{r}q(B^{\prime})\right).

Dividing by

gives us
\displaystyle c_{q}(a;r,m,B_{0})=\frac{1}{m^{9}}c_{q}(a;r)
.

Now, we consider the case
\gcd(r,m)>1
.
Suppose that
p

.
We rewrite the sum as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1}\bmod{rm}\\
B_{1}\equiv B_{0}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{a}{rm}q(B_{1})\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B^{\prime}\bmod{rm/p}\\
B^{\prime}\equiv B_{0}\bmod{m}\end{subarray}}\sum_{B_{0}^{\prime}\bmod{p}}e\left(\frac{a}{rm}q\left(\frac{rm}{p}B_{0}^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\right)\right).

Given

, we write
\langle v,w\rangle=q(v+w)-q(v)-q(w)
for the associated bilinear form.
Hence, we have

q\left(\frac{rm}{p}B_{0}^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\right)=q(B^{\prime})+\frac{rm}{p}\langle B^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{r^{2}m^{2}}{p^{2}}q(B_{0}^{\prime}).

Since

, the inner sum equals

\sum_{B_{0}^{\prime}\bmod{p}}e\left(\frac{a}{rm}\left(q(B^{\prime})+\frac{rm}{p}\langle B^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\right)=e\left(\frac{a}{rm}q(B^{\prime})\right)\sum_{B_{0}^{\prime}\bmod{p}}e\left(\frac{a}{p}\langle B^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime}\rangle\right).

Since

is nonzero modulo

and

is non-degenerate modulo

, the linear form
\langle B^{\prime},*\rangle:V({\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is nonzero.
Moreover,
a
is coprime to

and
a
is coprime to

.
Therefore, the above exponential sum vanishes and as a result,

\Box

|S_{\mathcal{B}}(\alpha;m,B_{0})|^{2}\ll\prod_{(i,j)}\sum_{b_{ij}\in I_{ij}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm min}\left\{\frac{X_{ij}}{m},||\frac{a_{ij}}{r_{ij}}b_{ij}||^{-1}\right\}.

Now if
r_{ij}>4X_{ij}/m
, then

\sum_{b_{ij}\in I_{ij}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm min}\left\{\frac{X_{ij}}{m},||\frac{a_{ij}}{r_{ij}}b_{ij}||^{-1}\right\}\leq\frac{X_{ij}}{m}+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lceil 2X_{ij}/m\rceil}\frac{r_{ij}}{\ell}\ll\frac{X_{ij}}{m}+r_{ij}\log X_{ij}.

(30)

If
r_{ij}\leq 4X_{ij}/m
, then

\sum_{b_{ij}\in I_{ij}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm min}\left\{\frac{X_{ij}}{m},||\frac{a_{ij}}{r_{ij}}b_{ij}||^{-1}\right\}\ll\frac{X_{ij}}{m}\frac{4X_{ij}/m}{r_{ij}}+\frac{4X_{ij}/m}{r_{ij}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lfloor r_{ij}/2\rfloor}\frac{r_{ij}}{\ell}\ll\frac{X_{ij}^{2}}{rm}+\frac{X_{ij}}{m}\log X_{ij}.

(31)

Combining (
30
) and (
31
) then gives

\sum_{b_{ij}\in I_{ij}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}{\rm min}\left\{\frac{X_{ij}}{m},||\frac{a_{ij}}{r_{ij}}b_{ij}||^{-1}\right\}\ll\frac{X^{2(1+2\delta)}}{rm}+r_{2}\,\log X.

Raising it to the power
9
and taking square root give

S_{\mathcal{B}}(\alpha;m,B_{0})\ll\frac{X^{9(1+2\delta)}}{r^{9/2}m^{9/2}}+r_{2}^{9/2}\,\log^{9/2}X.

Finally, integrating over the minor arc gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{m}}S_{\mathcal{B}}(\alpha;m,B_{0})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1}<r\leq r_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a<r\\
(a,r)=1\end{subarray}}\int_{|\theta|\leq\frac{1}{rr_{2}}}\left(\frac{X^{9(1+2\delta)}}{r^{9/2}m^{9/2}}+r_{2}^{9/2}\,\log^{9/2}X\right)d\theta

(32)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1}<r\leq r_{2}}\left(\frac{X^{9(1+2\delta)}}{r_{2}r^{9/2}m^{9/2}}+r_{2}^{7/2}\log^{9/2}X\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{X^{9(1+2\delta)}}{r_{2}r_{1}^{7/2}m^{9/2}}+r_{2}^{9/2}\log^{9/2}X,

where the last bound follows from
r_{2}\gg X^{1+2\delta}.

4.1.3
Proof of Theorem 4.2

We are ready to prove Theorem
4.2
.
By (
27
) and (
32
), we have

N_{q}({\mathcal{B}};m,B_{0})=\mathfrak{S}_{m}(q)\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}({\mathcal{B}};q)+O\left(\frac{X^{7}}{r_{1}^{5/2}m^{8}}+\frac{r_{1}r_{2}^{7/2}}{m^{9/2}}+\frac{r_{1}^{2}X^{8(1+2\delta)}}{r_{2}m^{8}}+\frac{X^{9(1+2\delta)}}{r_{2}r_{1}^{7/2}m^{9/2}}+r_{2}^{9/2}\log^{9/2}X\right),

where
\mathfrak{S}_{m}(q)
is defined in (
22
).
Since
X\gg m^{3}
and
\delta<0.01
, we may pick

4.2

For any prime

{\mathbb{F}}_{p}

-distinguished in the sense of Section
2.1
. Then any element

that is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-distinguished is
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-reducible for all primes
p

We now apply the Selberg sieve to prove Theorem
4.1
.
We follow the setup as in
[
19
, §3]
.
Let
z

.
Let
P

p

, let
a_{m}

•

;

•

B

{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-reducible;

•

p\mid m

B

{\mathbb{F}}_{p}

, we set

.
Then, applying the Selberg sieve will give us the count for

which is the number of elements

with

-reducible for all primes

.

For any squarefree

such that

and

is not
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-reducible for any

. Recall that for any prime
p
, we defined

for the number of

Thus, by Proposition
2.3
and Theorem

\displaystyle\sum_{n\equiv 0\bmod{m}}a_{n}

\displaystyle=
