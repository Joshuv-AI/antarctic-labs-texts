The sudden death of quantum advantage in correlation generations
Weixiao Sun 1, Fuchuan Wei 2,3, Yuguo Shao 2,3, Zhaohui Wei 2,4,

Abstract
As quantum error corrections still cannot be realized physically, quantum noise is the most profound obstacle to the implementations of large-scale quantum algorithms or quantum schemes. It has been well-known that if a quantum computer suffers from too strong quantum noise, its running can be easily simulated by a classical computer, making the quantum advantage impossible. Generally speaking, however, the dynamical process that how quantum noise of varying strengths from 0 to a fatal level impacts and destroys quantum advantage has not been understood well. Undoubtedly, achieving this will be extremely valuable for us to understand the power of noisy intermediate-scale quantum computers. Meanwhile, correlation generation is a precious theoretical model of information processing tasks in which the quantum advantage can be precisely quantified. Here we show that this model also provides us a valuable insight into understanding the impact of noise on quantum advantage. Particularly, we will rigorously prove that when the strength of quantum noise continuously goes up from 0, the quantum advantage gradually declines, and eventually fades away completely. Surprisingly, in some cases we observe an unexpected phenomenon we call the sudden death of the quantum advantage, i.e., when the strength of quantum noise exceeds a certain point, the quantum advantage disappears suddenly from a non-negligible level. This interesting phenomenon reveals the tremendous harm of noise to quantum information processing tasks from a new viewpoint.

In this paper, we will focus on a simpler sampling model than sampling from the outputs of noisy random quantum circuits, in which two separated parties, Alice and Bob, cooperate and try to sample two separated outcomes according to a target joint probability distribution
[

]
. This task can be fulfilled by either a quantum scheme or a classical one. In the former, Alice and Bob can realize the sampling by locally measuring a shared quantum seed state without any communications, and then outputting the corresponding outcomes; in the latter, Alice and Bob can replace the shared quantum state with a public randomness, and the other actions allowed are local classical operations only. A crucial feature of this elegant model is that a quantum scheme can enjoy a remarkable advantage over any classical one, furthermore, in the noiseless case the smallest computational costs needed for both scenarios, which are measured by the sizes of the shared quantum states and the public randomness respectively, can be characterized exactly

44
,
45

. In other words, the quantum advantage in this model can be precisely quantified. Based on this key fact, here we show that this model also allows us to rigorously analyze the dynamical process that how noise of continuously increasing strengths impacts the quantum advantage. Specifically, on an arbitrary target correlation, we first define a measure for the quantum advantage in this model, then we prove that when the strength of noise continuously goes up from zero, the quantum advantage gradually decreases, and eventually fades away completely. Surprisingly, we will report an unexpected phenomenon we call the
sudden death of quantum advantage
, i.e., in some cases when the strength of noise reaches a certain point, the quantum advantage disappears suddenly from a non-negligible level. This interesting phenomenon brings us a new viewpoint to appreciate the harm of noise in quantum information processing tasks.

II
The Setting

Suppose two separate parties, Alice and Bob, want to sample two outcomes
x\in[m]\equiv\{1,2,...,m\}
and
y\in[n]
respectively according to a discrete joint probability distribution
P=[P(x,y)]_{x\in[m],y\in[n]}
, i.e., the probability that Alice outputs
x
and Bob outputs
y
is
P(x,y)
. If this is the case, we say Alice and Bob generate
P
, and
P
is also called a classical correlation. We can write
P
as an
m\times n
nonnegative matrix (A nonnegative matrix is a matrix with all the entries nonnegative), and without loss of generality in the current work we assume that
m=n
for simplicity.
When generating
P
, we suppose that any communication between Alice and Bob is not allowed. Then if the target
P
is not a product distribution, that is,
\operatorname{rank}(P)>1
, they need to share some initial resource to generate
P
. If Alice and Bob choose to generate
P
with a classical protocol, the initial shared resource can be another shared classical correlation
P^{\prime}
, which is called a
seed
correlation. In this case, Alice and Bob can perform local classical operations on
P^{\prime}
to generate
P
. Similarly, they can also generate
P
with a quantum protocol, in which the initial shared resource can be a shared quantum state
\rho
called a
seed
state. On this state, Alice and Bob perform proper local positive operator-valued measurements (POVM) on
\rho
and output the corresponding outcomes to obtain
P
.
We define the size of
P^{\prime}
or
\rho
to be half of the total number of bits or qubits needed to record
P^{\prime}
or
\rho
, and denote it as
\operatorname{size}(P^{\prime})
or
\operatorname{size}(\rho)
respectively.
Please see Refs.
[
44
,
45
]
for more details on this model of correlation generations.

For a given target
P
, we are concerned about the minimum value of
\operatorname{size}(P^{\prime})
(or
\operatorname{size}(\rho)
) for
P^{\prime}
(or
\rho
) that can generate
P
, which is called the
classical (quantum) correlation complexity
of
P
, denoted
\mathrm{R}(P)
(or
\mathrm{Q}(P)
), correspondingly.
It turns out that
\mathrm{R}(P)
and
\mathrm{Q}(P)
have been fully characterized
[
44
,
45
]
. Specifically, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(P)=\left\lceil\log_{2}\operatorname{rank}_{+}(P)\right\rceil

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}(P)

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\log_{2}\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(P)\right\rceil.

For a nonnegative matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
,
\operatorname{rank}_{+}(P)
is the nonnegative rank defined to be the minimum integer
r
such that
P
can be expressed as the sum of
r
nonnegative rank-
1
matrices.

\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(P)
is the positive semidefinite rank (PSD-rank) defined to be the minimum integer
r
such that there exist
r\times r
positive semidefinite matrices
C_{x},D_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
satisfying
P(x,y)=\tr\left(C_{x}D_{y}\right)
for all
x
and
y
, and this factorization is called a
PSD decomposition

[
46
,
47
]
. Although both of these two ranks are NP-hard to compute
[
48
,
49
]
, this problem provides us with a precious model on which the quantum advantage can be quantified precisely. Indeed, it has been known that there exists such a classical correlation
P
that
\operatorname{rank}_{+}(P)\gg\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(P)

[
46
]
, implying that quantum schemes can have a remarkable advantage over classical ones in generating correlations.

In the quantum protocol, however, the states that Alice and Bob share may suffer from noise, due to various experimental imperfections, which may affect the above quantum advantage seriously. Analyzing this kind of affection is the major motivation of the current paper. For simplicity, here we suppose that all quantum noise is concentrated on the preparations and the quantum memories for initial seed states, and assume that all the other quantum operations are noiseless. Meanwhile, we assume that classical protocols are always noiseless. More specifically, in a quantum protocol we assume that the seed state
\rho
suffers from a bipartite quantum noise of the form
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, i.e., each of the two subsystems of
\rho
goes through a global depolarizing noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
with a strength
\lambda
, which keeps the subsystems unchanged with probability
1-\lambda
and replaces it with the maximally mixed state with probability
\lambda
. We would like to point out that this kind of noise can appear naturally in real-life quantum systems. For example, Ref.
[
26
]
shows that under certain conditions the overall effect of local quantum errors in random quantum circuits can be approximated by global white noise. For simplicity, here we assume that all the other quantum operations are noiseless. When the strength
\lambda=0
, as mentioned a quantum scheme for correlation generations may exhibit a remarkable quantum advantage over its classical counterparts, while when
\lambda=1
, the initial seed state degrades into the white noise completely, making quantum advantage impossible (Alice and Bob can only produce a product distribution). In the rest of the paper, we will characterize the process that as the noise strength
\lambda
continuously increases from
0
to
1
, how the advantage of quantum schemes over classical ones gradually disappears.

If a classical correlation
P
can be generated by
\rho
under the noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, we denote this fact to be
\rho\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
.
Under such a noise channel, we define the
quantum advantage
in generating a classical correlation P, denoted
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(P)
, as the ratio between the lowest classical cost of generating
P
and the smallest size of
\rho
such that
\rho\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
. Formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(P)=\frac{\mathrm{R}(P)}{\inf_{\rho}\textup{size}(\rho)},

where the infimum is taken over all
\rho
such that
\rho\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
. It can be seen that this definition is consistent with our intuition on quantum advantage: If
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(P)
is very large, say
10
, then the classical cost of generating
P
is at least
9
times higher than that of the optimal noisy quantum protocol that suffers from the noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
.

Before proceeding, we first analyze the quantum advantage of noiseless quantum protocols in generating classical correlations. It has been known that for an arbitrary classical correlation
P
, the minimum quantum seed that can generate
P
can always be chosen as a pure quantum state
[
50
]
. Then we have the following conclusion, which shows that noiseless quantum protocols enjoy a remarkable quantum advantage in generating classical correlations.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
is a bipartite entangled pure quantum state on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
Then, there always exists a family of classical correlation
\{P_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
such that
\rho\xrightarrow{0}P_{m}
for all
m
, and
\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\operatorname{lim}}\mathcal{S}_{0}(P_{m})=\infty
.

The proof for Theorem
1
can be seen in Appendix
A
. Here we sketch the main idea for the proof. For
m
distinct real numbers
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{m}
, we define a so-called
m\times m
Euclidean distance matrix
\mathrm{EDM}_{m}
by
\mathrm{EDM}_{m}(x,y)=\frac{(\alpha_{x}-\alpha_{y})^{2}}{\sum_{ij}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}}
. It has been known that
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(\mathrm{EDM}_{m})=2
and
\operatorname{rank}_{+}(\mathrm{EDM}_{m})\geq 2\sqrt{m}-2

[
51
]
. Then we call
\mathrm{EDM}_{m}^{\prime}\equiv L\cdot\mathrm{EDM}_{m}\cdot R
a modified Euclidean distance matrix, where
L
and
R
are two
m\times m
diagonal matrices with positive diagonal entries. We can prove that for any bipartite entangled pure state
\ket{\psi}
and any integer
m
,
\ket{\psi}
can always generate a class correlation
P_{m}
such that some submatrix of
P_{m}
is an
m\times m
modified Euclidean distance matrix
\mathrm{EDM}_{m}^{\prime}
. Then it can be seen

\mathcal{S}_{0}(P_{m})\geq\lceil\log_{2}(2\sqrt{m}-2)\rceil/\operatorname{size}(\rho)=\Omega(\log(m))
. Since
m
can be chosen arbitrarily, the proof is completed.

III
The Impact of Noise on Quantum Advantage

We now move to the case of noisy quantum protocols that generate classical correlations, and try to find out how noise affects quantum advantage. Recall that the noise model we choose is that the global depolarizing noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(\rho)=(1-\lambda)\rho+\lambda\tr(\rho)I_{d}/d
acts independently on the subsystems of Alice and Bob respectively, which transfers a noiseless seed state
\sigma
to
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(\sigma)
.

III.1
The case of strong noise: the destruction of reachability

Let us first consider the scenario of strong quantum noise. It turns out that for a classical correlation
P
, if the strength of quantum noise is higher than a certain value of
\lambda
, which we call the threshold, any quantum protocols will fail in generating
P
. Denote
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\sum_{x=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{ s_x}
as the one-norm of a vector
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})
. For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, denote
D(\mathcal{H})
be the set of density operators on
\mathcal{H}
. The following proposition can be utilized to identify this threshold. Note that if
\sigma\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
for some
\lambda>0
, then every entry of
P
is positive. Thus we always assume
P\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}
below.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{>0}
is a correlation and
\lambda<1
is a noise strength. Then there exists a dimension
d
and a quantum state
\sigma\in D(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})
making
\sigma\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
, if and only if there exists
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}),\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\equiv P(x,y)-\lambda s_{x}\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\sum_{b}P(x,b)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}\geq 0\

(1)

holds for all
x,y
.

The proof for Proposition
1
can be found in Appendix
B
.
We sketch the main idea of the proof here.
If there exist POVMs
\{E_{x}\}
and
\{F_{y}\}
such that

\displaystyle P(x,y)=

\displaystyle\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\E_\lambda\otimes\E_\lambda(\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)+\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\tr(F_y\sigma_B)

\displaystyle+\lambda(1-\lambda)\tr(E_x\sigma_A)\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}+\lambda^{2}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}.

Take
s_{x}=\tr(E_x)/d
and
t_{y}=\tr(F_y)/d
and rearrange the terms, we can see that
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)=(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)\geq 0
.
On the other hand, if Eq.(
1
) holds, we can first find a noiseless quantum protocol with a seed state
\sigma^{\prime}
, and POVMs
\{E_{x}^{\prime}\}
and
\{F_{y}^{\prime}\}
to generate the correlation
\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}
. Then by enlarging the quantum systems of Alice and Bob to allow more freedom, we can construct a new quantum protocol that can generate
P
exactly under the noise.

We now show how Proposition
1
allows us to characterize the region of
\lambda
that a given classical correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}
is reachable for quantum protocols. For this, we denote
\Lambda(P)
as this region, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Lambda(P)=\left\{\lambda:\text{ there exists a quantum state }\rho\text{ such that }\rho\xrightarrow{\lambda}P\right\}.

(2)

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda(P)
, according to Proposition
1
there exists
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
, such that

\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}
defined in Eq.(
1
) is a nonnegative matrix.
Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=2\lambda s_{x}t_{y}-s_{x}\sum_{a}P(a,y)-t_{y}\sum_{b}P(x,b)

(3)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(s_{x}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)+t_{y}\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)\leq 0.

Thus for any
0\leq\lambda^{\prime}\leq\lambda
we have
\hat{P}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
, which implies
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda(P)
. Therefore,
\Lambda(P)
is an interval. That is, the continuous increase of
\lambda
will destroy the reachability of quantum protocols for
P
, which will never come back.

Proposition
1
fully characterizes the reachability of noisy quantum protocols in generating correlations. However, for a given
P
, finding out whether suitable
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
can be found to satisfy the conditions in the Proposition
1
is very challenging, making it difficult to determine whether a correlated
P
can be generated quantumly under the noise strength
\lambda
.
Because of this, below we provide an easy-to-handle necessary condition for the reachability of noisy quantum protocols. Specifically, for a given classical correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\geq 0}
, there exists a quantum state
\sigma\in D(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})
satisfying
\sigma\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
, only if

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\max_{\varphi\in S_{n}}\sum_{x=1}^{n}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,\sum_{b=1}^{n}P(x,b)\sum_{a=1}^{n}P(a,\varphi(x))-P(x,\varphi(x))\right\}},

(4)

where
S_{n}
is a symmetric group of degree
n
. The proof can be found in Appendix
C
.

Note that
\sum_{xy}\big(\sum_{b}P(x,b)\sum_{a}P(a,y)-P(x,y)\big)=0
. Then if
\operatorname{rank}(P)\geq 2
, there always exist
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{b}P(x_{0},b)\sum_{a}P(a,y_{0})-P(x_{0},y_{0})>0.

Thus the upper bound is always strictly less than
1
. That is to say, for any
P
with
\operatorname{rank}(P)\geq 2
, reachability will always be destroyed before
\lambda
reaches
1
.

III.2
The case of weak noise: the costs of noisy quantum protocols

For a given nontrivial classical correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
whose
\textup{rank}\geq 2
,
we now focus on the interval
\Lambda(P)
, that is, the region of
\lambda
that
P
is reachable for noisy quantum protocols. In such a situation, the most important problem is how the cost of generating
P
increases as the noise strength grows.

We denote
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)
as the minimal local dimension for the seed states that can generate
P
under the noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, i.e., the smallest number
d
such that there exists
\sigma\in D(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})
satisfying
\sigma\xrightarrow{\lambda}P
. Then the size of the minimum seed state can be written as
\lceil\log_{2}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)\rceil
.

Recall that the cost of the optimal noiseless quantum protocol generating
P
can be fully characterized as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}(P)

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\log_{2}\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(P)\right\rceil,

where
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}(P)
is the PSD-rank of
P

[
45
]
. Inspired by the result above, we connect the cost of noisy quantum protocols in generating correlations to a modified version of PSD-rank. Specifically, we define
\operatorname{rank}_{\textup{psd}}^{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}(P)
to be the smallest
r
for which there exist
r\times r
PSD matrices
\{C_{x}\},\{D_{y}\}
such that
P(x,y)=\tr(C_xD_y)
, satisfying that both

\displaystyle C_{x}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(C_x\left(\sum_{k=1}^nC_k\right)^{-1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{k}

and

\displaystyle D_{y}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(D_y\left(\sum_{k=1}^nD_k\right)^{-1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{k}

are PSD matrices. In this context, we say
\{C_{i}\},\{D_{j}\}
is an
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
-PSD factorization of
P
.
Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)\leq\operatorname{rank}_{\textup{psd}}^{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}(P),

(5)

which will be proved in Appendix
D
. Particularly, if we restrict seed states to be pure states, the above inequality holds with equality.

Meanwhile, it turns out that Proposition
1
also helps to bound
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)
, which allows us to obtain the following upper and lower bounds for
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)
.
For a given classical correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}
and
\lambda\geq 0
, if there exists
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)
defined in Eq.(
1
) is nonnegative for all
x,y
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)

\displaystyle\leq\rank_{\textup{psd}}(\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}})\left\lceil\frac{1}{\min_{x,y}\{s_{x},t_{y}\}}\right\rceil,

(6)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(\inf_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}\max_{x,y}\left\{\frac{\sum_{b}P(x,b)}{s^{\prime}_{x}},\frac{\sum_{a}P(a,y)}{t^{\prime}_{y}}\right\}-\lambda\right),

(7)

where
\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}'}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}'}_{1}=1
satifying that
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq 0
. The proofs for these bounds can be found in Appendix
E
.

III.3
The decay process of quantum advantage

Now we are ready to analyze how quantum advantage is affected when the strength of noise continuously grows up. We will demonstrate our techniques using specific examples.

Define
A_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
to be the correlation with the entries given by

\displaystyle A_{m}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{(1-q)^{2}}{2}&\frac{q(1-q)}{2m}&\cdots&\frac{q(1-q)}{2m}\\
\frac{q(1-q)}{2m}&&&\\
\vdots&&\frac{1+q^{2}}{2}B_{m}&\\
\frac{q(1-q)}{2m}&&&\end{array}\right),

where

,

is a parameter, and
B_{m}
is an
m\times m
correlation with the entries being

\displaystyle B_{m}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(1-k\cos(2\pi\frac{x-1+y-1}{m})\right)

for
x,y\in[m]
. Note that if

. In fact, the correlation

, as a matrix, is the slack matrix of two concentric regular polygons, as shown in Fig.

. More details about this geometric interpretation can be found in

, where

It can be shown that in the absence of noise,

In fact, in Appendix

, then
\operatorname{rank}_{+}(A_{m})>\log_{2}(m/2)
, whereas
\operatorname{rank}_{\textup{psd}}(A_{m})\leq 3
. As a result, it indicates that

\displaystyle\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\mathcal{S}_{0}(A_{m})=\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{\mathrm{R}(A_{m})}{\operatorname{rank}_{\textup{psd}}(A_{m})}=\infty.

(12)

We then turn to noisy quantum protocols that generate
A_{m}
. Recall that
\lceil\log_{2}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(A_{m})\rceil
is the smallest size of a seed state that can generate

under the noise channel

Theorem 2
.

As the noise strength
\lambda
approaches
q
, the quantum advantage in generating
A_{m}
decreases gradually to
0
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=q-\epsilon
, where
0<\epsilon<q
. And still use the notation

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)=A_{m}(x,y)-\lambda s_{x}\sum_{a=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\sum_{b=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(x,b)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}.

According to the Proposition
1
, there exists
\rho
such that
\rho\xrightarrow{q-\epsilon}P
if and only if there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m+1}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
.

First, we prove that for any
0<\lambda<q
,
A_{m}
can always be generated by some quantum state. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{t}=\big(\eta,(1-\eta)/m,(1-\eta)/m,\cdots,(1-\eta)/m\big),

where
\eta=\min\left\{\frac{1-q}{2(q-\epsilon)},\frac{\epsilon(1+q)}{2q(q-\epsilon)}\right\}
. Note that
B_{m}(x,y)\geq\frac{1-k}{m^{2}}
. We have

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(1,1)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-q)^{2}}{2}-(q-\epsilon)\eta(1-q)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(1,y)=

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,1)\geq\frac{q(1-q)}{2m}-(q-\epsilon)\eta\frac{1+q}{2m}-(q-\epsilon)\frac{1-\eta}{m}\frac{1-q}{2}\geq 0,\ 2\leq x,y\leq m+1,

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1+q^{2}}{2}\frac{1-k}{m^{2}}-2(q-\epsilon)\frac{1-\eta}{m}\frac{1+q}{2m}+(q-\epsilon)^{2}\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1+q^{2}}{2}\frac{1-k}{m^{2}}-2q\frac{1}{m}\frac{1+q}{2m}+q^{2}\frac{1}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\frac{(1+q^{2})(1-k)}{2}-q\right)=0,\ 2\leq x,y\leq m+1.

That is, when
0<\lambda<q
,
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
for any
x,y
, which means there exists a quantum state
\rho
such that
\rho\xrightarrow{\lambda}A_{m}
.

By Eq.(
6
), if

\epsilon
is small enough, we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(A_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\rank_{\textup{psd}}(\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}})\left\lceil\frac{1}{\min_{x,y}\{s_{x},t_{y}\}}\right\rceil\leq m\left\lceil\frac{2q(q-\epsilon)}{\epsilon(1+q)}\right\rceil=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}).

Second, we now show that when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, for any
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m+1}
satisfying
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
and
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y
, we must have
\min\{s_{1},t_{1}\}\rightarrow 0
. In fact, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{m+1}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(i,1)+\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(1,j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{m+1}A_{m}(i,1)-\sum_{i=2}^{m+1}\lambda s_{i}\sum_{a=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(a,1)-\sum_{i=2}^{m+1}\lambda t_{1}\sum_{b=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(i,b)+\sum_{i=2}^{m+1}\lambda^{2}s_{i}t_{1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}A_{m}(1,j)-\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}\lambda s_{1}\sum_{a=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(a,j)-\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}\lambda t_{j}\sum_{b=1}^{m+1}A_{m}(1,b)+\sum_{j=2}^{m+1}\lambda^{2}s_{1}t_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q(1-q)}{2}-\lambda(1-s_{1})\frac{1-q}{2}-\lambda t_{1}\frac{1+q}{2}+\lambda^{2}(1-s_{1})t_{1}+\frac{q(1-q)}{2}-\lambda s_{1}\frac{1+q}{2}-\lambda(1-t_{1})\frac{1-q}{2}+\lambda^{2}s_{1}(1-t_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{q(1-q)}{2}-\lambda(1-s_{1})\frac{1-q}{2}-\lambda t_{1}\frac{1+q}{2}+q^{2}(1-s_{1})t_{1}+\frac{q(1-q)}{2}-\lambda s_{1}\frac{1+q}{2}-\lambda(1-t_{1})\frac{1-q}{2}+q^{2}s_{1}(1-t_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2q^{2}s_{1}t_{1}+\epsilon-\epsilon(1-s_{1}-t_{1})q

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-2q^{2}s_{1}t_{1}+\epsilon(1+q).

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m+1}
and
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
, in order to let
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
, we must have

\displaystyle\min\{s_{1},t_{1}\}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon(1+q)}{2q^{2}}}.

Then according to Eq.(
7
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(A_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(\inf_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\max_{x,y}\left\{\frac{\sum_{b}A_{m}(x,b)}{s_{x}},\frac{\sum_{a}A_{m}(a,y)}{t_{y}}\right\}-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left(\inf_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\max\left\{\frac{\sum_{b}A_{m}(1,b)}{s_{1}},\frac{\sum_{a}A_{m}(a,1)}{t_{1}}\right\}-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-q+\epsilon}\left(\frac{1-q}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2q^{2}}{\epsilon(1+q)}}-q+\epsilon\right)=\Omega(\epsilon^{-1/2}).

In conclusion, when
\epsilon
is small, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(A_{m})=\frac{\mathrm{R}(A_{m})}{\lceil\log_{2}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(A_{m})\rceil}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\log\epsilon^{-1}}\right).

In other words, when
\lambda\rightarrow q
,
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(A_{m})\rightarrow\infty
, implying that
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(A_{m})\rightarrow 0
.
This means that when
\lambda
approaches
q
from the left, although
A_{m}
is always reachable for noisy quantum protocols, their costs will continuously increase without bound, implying that the noise with a strength around
q
will completely destroy the remarkable quantum advantage of the noiseless quantum protocol generating
A_{m}
.
∎

III.4
The sudden death of quantum advantage

We have shown that
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(A_{m})
decays gradually to
0
as the noise rate
\lambda
grows. Surprisingly, if we instead focus on
B_{m}
, a subcorrelation of
A_{m}
, we will observe an unexpected phenomenon: as
\lambda
continuously increases from
0
and exceeds a certain point,
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(B_{m})
suddenly drops to
0
from a non-negligible value. We call this kind of phenomenon the sudden death of quantum advantage in correlation generation.

Theorem 3
.

Let
0<k<1
. For any integer
m
and the classical correlation
B_{m}
defined in Eq.(
III.3
), we have

1.

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(B_{m})=\Omega\left(\log\log(m)\right)
,
if

0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{k}
;

2.

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(B_{m})=0
,
if

\lambda>1-\sqrt{k}
.

Therefore, the quantum advantage in generating
B_{m}
experiences sudden death when the noise strength exceeds
\lambda=1-\sqrt{k}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi(1)=1,\varphi(x)=m+2-x
. We have
B_{m}(x,\varphi(x))=(1-k)/m^{2}
for all
x
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{b}B_{m}(x,b)\sum_{a}B_{m}(a,\varphi(x))-B_{m}(x,\varphi(x))=\frac{k}{m^{2}}.

By Eq.(
4
), if
B_{m}
can be generated under noise
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, we must have

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\sum_{x=1}^{m}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,\sum_{b}B_{m}(x,b)\sum_{a}B_{m}(a,\varphi(x))-B_{m}(x,\varphi(x))\right\}}=1-\sqrt{k}.

Thus for
\lambda>1-\sqrt{k}
,
B_{m}
is not reachable for any noisy quantum protocols, meaning that
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(B_{m})=\frac{\mathrm{R}(B_{m})}{\infty}=0
.

For
0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{k}
, we selcet the
2\times 2
hermitian matrices
\{C_{x}\}
and
\{D_{y}\}
introduced in Lemma
F.1
of Appendix
F
as a PSD decomposition of
B_{m}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle C_{x}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(C_x\Big(\sum_{k=1}^nC_k\Big)^{-1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{k}=C_{x}-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}m}I,

\displaystyle D_{y}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(D_y\Big(\sum_{k=1}^nD_k\Big)^{-1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{k}=D_{y}-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}m}I.

Note that the minimal eigenvalues of
C_{x}
and
D_{y}
are all
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}m}(1-\sqrt{k})
, which means both of
C_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}m}I
and
D_{j}-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2}m}I
are PSD matrices, thus
\{C_{x}\}
and
\{D_{y}\}
form an
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
-PSD factorization of
B_{m}
. By Eq.(
5
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(B_{m})\leq\operatorname{rank}_{\textup{psd}}^{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}(B_{m})\leq 2.

Since
B_{m}
is not a product distribution, we have
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(B_{m})=2
. That is, if
0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(B_{m})=\frac{\mathrm{R}(B_{m})}{\lceil\log_{2}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(B_{m})\rceil}=\mathrm{R}(B_{m}),

which is a constant independent of
\lambda
. By Lemma
F.1
in Appendix
F
, we have
\mathrm{R}(B_{m})\geq\lceil\log_{2}\log_{2}(m/2)\rceil
, which completes the proof.
∎

For any classical correlation
P
, when the sudden death of quantum advantage happens to its generation,
\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)
will be upper bounded for any
\lambda\in\Lambda(P)
, which means that
\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)<\infty
. To figure out when such a phenomenon occurs, we provide two equivalent conditions as below.

Theorem 4
.

For any correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}
, the following statements are equivalent to each other:

1.

The quantum advantage in generating
P
experiences the sudden death when
\lambda
continuously goes up from
0
.

2.

\Lambda(P)
is a right closed interval.

3.

For any
\lambda>0
, if there exist non-negative vectors
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that

\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}

defined in Eq.(
1
) is a nonnegative matrix, then
P
can be generated quantumly under the noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
.

The proof for Theorem
4
is deferred to Appendix
G
. As a simple application of this theorem, we now use it to reexamine the correlation
B_{m}
. According to the proof for Theorem
3
we have that
\Lambda(B_{m})=\big[0,1-\sqrt{k}\big]
, which is right-closed. Then Theorem
4
immediately implies that the quantum advantage in generating
B_{m}
suffers from the sudden death.

IV
Discussion

Based on an elegant sampling model in which quantum advantage has been identified and even quantified, we mathematically characterize the dynamical process that noise of increasing strength gradually suppresses and even destroys the quantum advantage. We also report a surprising phenomenon that under the impact of noise, sudden death may happen to the quantum advantage in this model. To our knowledge, this is the first quantum protocol that sudden death is exhibited to quantum advantage, implying that in different quantum information processing tasks the impact of noise may have different behaviors. Our work suggests that, to fully understand the power of NISQ computers, we need to make further efforts to characterize the nature of quantum noise for different computational models of these computers, especially the quantum circuit model.

\displaystyle+\lambda(1-\lambda)\tr(E_x\sigma_A)\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}+\lambda^{2}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)+\lambda\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\left(\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}\left(\sum_{b}P(x,b)-\lambda\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\right)+\lambda^{2}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)+\lambda\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\sum_{a}P(a,y)+\lambda\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}\sum_{b}P(x,b)-\lambda^{2}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d},

where
\sigma_{A}=\tr_{B}(\sigma)
and
\sigma_{B}=\tr_{A}(\sigma)
are the reduced density matrices.
If we can take
s_{x}=\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}
,
t_{y}=\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}
, then

\displaystyle P(x,y)-\lambda s_{x}\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\sum_{b}P(x,b)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}=(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)\geq 0.

With the properties of POVM, we have
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, and
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\sum_{x}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}=\frac{\tr(I_d)}{d}=1
. Similarly, it holds that
\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Denote that

\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)=P(x,y)-\lambda s_{x}\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\sum_{b}P(x,b)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y},

which naturally satisfies
\sum_{xy}\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)=1
.

Hence,
\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}
can be regarded as a correlation. Based on the results in Ref.
[
45
]
, we know that there exist POVMs
\{E_{x}^{\prime}\}
and
\{F_{y}^{\prime}\}
, and a quantum state
\sigma^{\prime}\in D(\mathbb{C}^{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d^{\prime}})
such that
\tr(E_x'\otimes F_y'\sigma')=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)
, where
d^{\prime}=\operatorname{rank_{psd}}(\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}})
.

Take
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

\displaystyle d^{\prime}ks_{x}-\tr(E_x')\geq 0,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}kt_{y}-\tr(F_y')\geq 0,

for all
x,y
. Denote
d=d^{\prime}k
and
\sigma=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\sigma^{\prime}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}
, where
\outerproduct{0}{0}\in D(\mathbb{C}^{k})
. Let

\displaystyle E_{x}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes E_{x}^{\prime}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}ks_{x}-\tr(E_x')}{k-1}\right)(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0})\otimes\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}},

\displaystyle F_{y}

\displaystyle=F_{y}^{\prime}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}kt_{y}-\tr(F_y')}{k-1}\right)\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}}\otimes(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0}),

which are PSD matrices. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{x}E_{x}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\sum_{x}E_{x}^{\prime}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}k\sum_{x}s_{x}-\tr(\sum_xE_x')}{k-1}\right)(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0})\otimes\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes I_{d^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}k-d^{\prime}}{k-1}\right)(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0})\otimes\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}}=I_{k}\otimes I_{d^{\prime}}=I_{d},

\displaystyle\sum_{y}F_{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}F_{y}^{\prime}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}k\sum_{b}t_{b}-\tr(\sum_yF_y')}{k-1}\right)\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}}\otimes(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0})

\displaystyle=I_{d^{\prime}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}k-d^{\prime}}{k-1}\right)\frac{I_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\prime}}\otimes(I_{k}-\outerproduct{0}{0})=I_{d^{\prime}}\otimes I_{k}=I_{d},

thus
\{E_{x}\}
,
\{F_{y}\}
are valid POVMs.
We also have

\displaystyle\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)

\displaystyle=\tr(\ketbra{0}{0}\otimes E_x'\otimes F_y'\otimes\ketbra{0}{0}\cdot\ketbra{0}{0}\otimes\sigma'\otimes\ketbra{0}{0}+0+0+0)=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y),

\displaystyle\tr(E_x)

\displaystyle=\tr(E_x')+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}ks_{x}-\tr(E_x')}{k-1}\right)(k-1)=d^{\prime}ks_{x}=ds_{x},

\displaystyle\tr(F_y)

\displaystyle=\tr(F_y')+\left(\frac{d^{\prime}kt_{y}-\tr(F_y')}{k-1}\right)(k-1)=d^{\prime}kt_{y}=dt_{y}.

Finally, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{b}P(x,b)-\lambda s_{x}\right).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\Tr(E_x\otimes F_y\E_{\lambda}\otimes\E_\lambda(\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}\tr(E_x\otimes F_y\sigma)+\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\tr(F_y\sigma_B)+\lambda(1-\lambda)\tr(E_x\sigma_A)\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}+\lambda^{2}\frac{\tr(E_x)}{d}\frac{\tr(F_y)}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)+\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}s_{x}\left(\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\right)+\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}t_{y}\left(\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)+\lambda s_{x}\left(\sum_{a}P(a,y)-\lambda t_{y}\right)+\lambda t_{y}\left(\sum_{b}P(x,b)-\lambda s_{x}\right)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}=P(x,y),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
The proof for Eq.(
4
)

We restate the conclusion in Eq.(
4
): For a given classical correlation

, there exists a quantum state
\sigma\in D(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda\leq 1-\max_{\varphi\in S_{n}}\sum_{x=1}^{n}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,\sum_{b}P(x,b)\sum_{a}P(a,\varphi(x))-P(x,\varphi(x))\right\}},

Proof.

By Proposition
1
, there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1

\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,b)=P(a,b)-\lambda s_{a}\sum_{a}P(a,b)-\lambda t_{b}\sum_{b}P(a,b)+\lambda^{2}s_{a}t_{b}\geq 0.

(15)

Then, summing
a
and
b
separately yields

\displaystyle\sum_{a}P(a,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)+\lambda t_{y},

\displaystyle\sum_{b}P(x,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)+\lambda s_{x}.

Thus by replacing
\sum_{a}P(a,y)
and
\sum_{b}P(a,y)
in Eq.(
15
),

can be expressed as

\displaystyle P(x,y)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)+\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}s_{x}\left(\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\right)+\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}t_{y}\left(\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)+\lambda^{2}s_{x}t_{y}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)-\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)+\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)+\lambda t_{y}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)+\lambda s_{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)-\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)+\sum_{a}P(a,y)\sum_{b}P(x,b).

x,y

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,b)-\left(P(x,y)-\sum_{a}P(a,y)\sum_{b}P(x,b)\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\left(P(x,y)-\sum_{a}P(a,y)\sum_{b}P(x,b)\right).

Note that
\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,y)\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\geq 0
and
\sum_{a,b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,b)=(1-\lambda)^{2}
. Then for any permutation
\varphi\in S_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{x}\left(\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,\varphi(x))+\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\sum_{x}\sqrt{\sum_{a}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(a,\varphi(x))\sum_{b}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,b)}

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{x}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,-\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\left(P(x,\varphi(x))-\sum_{a}P(a,\varphi(x))\sum_{b}P(x,b)\right)\right\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1-\lambda}\sum_{x}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,\sum_{a}P(a,\varphi(x))\sum_{b}P(x,b)-P(x,\varphi(x))\right\}}.

This means that

\displaystyle\lambda\leq 1-\sum_{x=1}^{n}\sqrt{\max\left\{0,\sum_{b}P(x,b)\sum_{a}P(a,\varphi(x))-P(x,\varphi(x))\right\}}.

The proof for Eq.(

r\times r
PSD matrices
\{C_{i}\},\{D_{j}\}
is an
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
-PSD factorization of

\{C_{i}\},\{D_{j}\}

We assume that

H

\widetilde{D_{j}}={(H^{\dagger})}^{-1}D_{j}H^{-1}

[
54

\{\widetilde{C_{i}}\},\{\widetilde{D_{j}}\}

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}

\{\widetilde{C_{i}}\},\{\widetilde{D_{j}}\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{C_{i}}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(\widetilde{C_i}\left(\sum_{k=1}^n\widetilde{C_k}\right)^{-1})\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{C_{k}}

\tr(\Lambda^2)=1

\displaystyle M_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\sqrt{\Lambda^{-1}}\left(C_{i}^{T}-\frac{\lambda}{r}\tr(C_i\Lambda^{-1})\Lambda\right)\sqrt{\Lambda^{-1}},

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle|\psi_{\Lambda}\rangle

\displaystyle P_{ij}

Lemma F.1

(Part 1)

.

A lower bound for
\textup{rank}_{+}(B_{m})
is derived from
\textup{rank}_{+}^{*}(B_{m})
, known as the restricted nonnegative rank. This quantity represents the minimum number
k
of vertices of a convex polygon
T\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
R_{\mathrm{in}}\subset T\subset R_{\mathrm{out}}
, where
R_{\mathrm{out}}
and
R_{\mathrm{in}}
denote the polygons described in Eq.(
18
) and (
19
) respectively. More details can be found in
[
53
]
.

A necessary condition for an
l
-gon
T
to be put between
R_{\mathrm{in}}
and
R_{\mathrm{out}}
is
R_{\mathrm{in}}^{C}\subset T\subset R_{\mathrm{out}}^{C}
, where
R_{\mathrm{out}}^{C}
denotes the circumscribed circle of
R_{\mathrm{out}}
with a radius of
\frac{1}{\cos{\frac{\pi}{m}}}
, and
R_{\mathrm{in}}^{C}
denotes the inscribed circle of
R_{\mathrm{in}}
with a radius of
k\cos{\frac{\pi}{m}}
. Therefore, every angle of such a
T
is at least
2\arcsin(k\cos^2\frac{\pi}{m})
, while the smallest angle of any
l
-gon is at most
\pi-\frac{2\pi}{l}
. Consequently, it must hold that
k\leq\frac{\cos(\pi/l)}{\cos^{2}(\pi/m)}
. In other words, if
k>\frac{\cos(\pi/l)}{\cos^{2}(\pi/m)}
, no
l
-gon can be put between
R_{\mathrm{in}}
and
R_{\mathrm{out}}
. Thus,
\textup{rank}_{+}^{*}(B_{m})>l
.
According to Theorem 6 in
[
53
]
, we have that
\rank_{+}(B_{m})\geq\log_{2}(\rank^{*}_{+}(B_{m}))>\log_{2}(l)
.

By taking
l=m/2
, it follows that
\rank_{+}(B_{m})>\log_{2}(l)=\log_{2}(m/2)
when
k>\frac{\cos(\pi/l)}{\cos^{2}(\pi/m)}=\frac{\cos(2\pi/m)}{\cos^{2}(\pi/m)}
, where
\frac{\cos(2\pi/m)}{\cos^{2}(\pi/m)}<1
.
Thus for any
m\geq 3
, we can always choose
k
close enough to
1
such that
\rank_{+}(B_{m})>\log_{2}(m/2)
.

(Part 2)

We can take

Appendix G
The mathematical characterization for sudden death

We prove the following result, which is equivalent to Theorem
4
.

Theorem G.2
.

For any correlation
P\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n\times n}
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)=\infty
.

2.

\Lambda(P)
is a right open interval.

3.

There exists
\lambda>0
, such that there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)
defined in Eq.(
1
) is nonnegative for all
x,y
. However,
P
is not reachable for any quantum protocols under noise
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

“
1\Rightarrow 2
”: If
\Lambda(P)
is right closed, we choose
\lambda_{u}=\sup\Lambda(P)\in\Lambda(P)
. Thus there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
. According to Eq.(
6
),
\mathrm{C}_{{\lambda_{u}}}(P)
has an upper bound.

Since for any
\lambda\leq\lambda_{u}
, it holds that
\mathrm{C}_{{\lambda}}(P)\leq\mathrm{C}_{{\lambda_{u}}}(P)
, i.e., Alice and Bob can simulate strong noise with weak noise by introducing more noise. Then we have that
\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{{\lambda}}(P)
has an upper bound, which contradicts with
\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)=\infty
.

“
2\Rightarrow 3
”: Let

\displaystyle ST_{\lambda}=\left\{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\right|\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\geq 0,\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1\}.

Note that in this definition we allow the entries of
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
to be
0
, thus

ST_{\lambda}
is closed and
ST_{\lambda}\subset\left\{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\right|\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1\}
which is a compact set. And also
\forall\ 0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda(P)
,
ST_{\lambda}\supseteq ST_{\lambda^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, which is directly from Eq.(
3
). So we have
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}ST_{\lambda}\neq\emptyset
.

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda(P)
and
(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime})\in\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}ST_{\lambda}
, we always have
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y
. Then
\hat{P}_{\sup\Lambda(P)}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\sup\Lambda(P)}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq 0
.
That is, for
\lambda_{u}=\sup\Lambda(P)
, there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
such that
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
. However, since
\sup\Lambda(P)\notin\Lambda(P)
, there do not exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
with
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
satisfying that
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
.

“
3\Rightarrow 2
”: Suppose that
\lambda_{u}
satisfies Statement 3 and
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y
, where
\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
satisfy
\norm{ \mathbf{s}_u}_{1}=\norm{ \mathbf{t}_u}_{1}=1
. We prove that for any
\epsilon>0
,
\lambda_{u}-\epsilon\in\Lambda(P)
.

For any
x,y
, if
(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}=(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}=0
, then
\hat{P}_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)=P(x,y)>0
.
And if
(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}=0,(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda=\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}=-(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}\sum_{b}P(x,b)<0.

Thus
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)>\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)\geq 0
.
The case that
(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}>0
and
(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}=0
is similiar.

Lastly, if
(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}>0,(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda=\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}=\frac{\partial\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)}{\partial\lambda}|_{\lambda=\lambda_{u}}-2\epsilon(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}\leq-2\epsilon(\mathbf{s}_{u})_{x}(\mathbf{t}_{u})_{y}<0.

Thus
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)>\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)\geq 0
.

In conclusion, we always have
\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)>0
. Let
w=\underset{x,y}{\min}\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)>0
. Note that

\displaystyle\left|\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)-\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\right|\leq 3\left|s_{x}-s^{\prime}_{x}\right|+3\left|t_{y}-t^{\prime}_{y}\right|

for any
\lambda,\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime},x,y
. Then for any
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
that satisfy
\norm{\mathbf{s}- \mathbf{s}_u}_{2}\leq\frac{w}{6}
and
\norm{\mathbf{t}- \mathbf{t}_u}_{2}\leq\frac{w}{6}
(such
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}
exist), we have

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}-\epsilon}^{\mathbf{s}_{u},\mathbf{t}_{u}}(x,y)-w\geq 0.

Thus
\lambda_{u}-\epsilon\in\Lambda(P)
, which means
\Lambda(P)=\left[0,\lambda_{u}\right)
.

“
2\Rightarrow 1
”: Let
\lambda_{u}=\sup\Lambda(P)
. Suppose
\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)<d
for some constant
d
. Then from Eq.(
3
), for any
\lambda\in\Lambda(P)
, there exist
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
such that
\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1
and
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y
, where
s_{x}\geq\frac{\sum_{b}P(x,b)}{d(1-\lambda)+\lambda}
and
t_{y}\geq\frac{\sum_{a}P(a,y)}{d(1-\lambda)+\lambda}
, as shown in Eq.(
17
). Let

\displaystyle v=\min_{x,y}\left\{\frac{\sum_{b}P(x,b)}{d(1-\lambda_{u})+\lambda_{u}},\frac{\sum_{a}P(a,y)}{d(1-\lambda_{u})+\lambda_{u}}\right\}.

We have
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq v}^{n}
. Again let

\displaystyle ST_{\lambda}=\left\{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq v}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq v}^{n}\right|\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}}\geq 0,\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1\}.

Note that
ST_{\lambda}
is closed and
ST_{\lambda}\subset\left\{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq v}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq v}^{n}\right|\norm{\mathbf{s}}_{1}=\norm{\mathbf{t}}_{1}=1\}
which is compact. And for any
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda(P)
,
ST_{\lambda}\supseteq ST_{\lambda^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, which is directly from Eq.(
3
). So we have
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}ST_{\lambda}\neq\emptyset
.

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda(P)
and
(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime})\in\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}ST_{\lambda}
, we always have
\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y
. Then

\hat{P}_{\lambda_{u}}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)=\hat{P}_{\sup\Lambda(P)}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\sup\Lambda(P)}\hat{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq 0.

(21)

Thus
\sup\Lambda(P)\in\Lambda(P)
, which contradicts
\Lambda(P)
is right open. Thus we have
\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda(P)}\mathrm{C}_{\lambda}(P)=\infty
.
∎
