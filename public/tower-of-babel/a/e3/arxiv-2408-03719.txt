Two Bijections on NBC Subsets


Abstract
We establish two explicit bijections: from acyclic reorientations of an oriented matroid to no broken circuit (NBC) subsets of its underlying matroid, and from regions of a real hyperplane arrangement to its affine NBC subsets. Keywords: Oriented matroid, hyperplane arrangement, broken circuit, acyclic orientation Mathematics Subject Classifications: 05B35, 52C35

Introduction

In order to solve the long-standing four colour problem, Birkhoff
[
1
]
first introduced the chromatic polynomial of planar graphs in 1912. Twenty years later, Whitney
[
24
]
defined the chromatic polynomial for finite graphs and gave a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of the chromatic polynomial. This interpretation is called Whitney’s broken circuit theorem. In 1973, applying Whitney’s broken circuit theorem, Stanley
[
22
, Proposition 1.1]
obtained that the number of acyclic orientations of a finite graph
G
equals the number of no broken circuits of the edge set
E(G)
, called Stanley’s theorem.

As an extension of oriented graphs, in 1978 Bland and Las Vergnas
[
4
]
introduced the concept of oriented matroids.
Let
\mathcal{M}=\big(E,\mathcal{C}\big)
be an oriented matroid on the ground set
E
. For
A\subseteq E
, denote by
{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{M}=\big(E,{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{C}\big)
the
reorientation
of
\mathcal{M}
by reversing signs on
A
. By a slight abuse of language, we will usually say that
A\subseteq E
is an
acyclic reorientation
of
\mathcal{M}
if
{}_{-A}\mathcal{M}
is acyclic (i.e., containing no positive circuits). Denote by
A(\mathcal{M})
the collection of subsets
A
of
E
such that
A
is an acyclic reorientation of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose the underlying matroid of
\mathcal{M}
is
\underline{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\underline{\mathcal{C}})
and the ground set
E
is totally ordered by
\prec
. A
broken circuit
of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
is a subset of
E
obtained from a member of
\underline{\mathcal{C}}
by deleting its maximal element with respect to
\prec
.
A subset
A\subseteq E
is an
NBC subset
of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
if
A
contains no broken circuits of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
. Denote by
{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
the collection of NBC subsets of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
. As a generalization of the chromatic polynomial, in 1964 Rota
[
20
]
introduced the characteristic polynomial for matroids (or combinatorial geometries) and obtained a matroid version of Whitney’s broken circuit theorem, see
[
3
,
6
,
15
,
21
,
23
]
for details. In 1980, Stanley’s theorem was extended to oriented matroids by Las Vergnas
[
17
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
17
]
, Theorem 3.1)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is an oriented matroid and
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
its underlying matroid, then

|A(\mathcal{M})|=|{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})|.

A
hyperplane arrangement

\mathcal{A}
is a finite collection of (affine) hyperplanes in an
n
-dimensional vector space
V
.
In particular,
\mathcal{A}
is called a
linear arrangement
if all hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
pass through the origin. Given a total ordering
\prec
on
\mathcal{A}
, an
affine circuit
is a smallest subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{A}
such that
\cap\mathcal{B}:=\bigcap_{H\in\mathcal{B}}H\neq\emptyset
and
\dim(\cap\mathcal{B})+|\mathcal{B}|=n+1
. An
affine broken circuit
is a subset of
\mathcal{A}
obtained from an affine circuit by removing its maximal element with respect to
\prec
. A subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{A}
is called an
affine NBC subset
if
\mathcal{B}
contains no affine broken circuits and
\cap\mathcal{B}\neq\emptyset
. Let
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})
be the collection of all affine NBC subsets of
\mathcal{A}
. When
\mathcal{A}
is a linear arrangement, ‘affine circuit’, ‘affine broken circuit’ and ‘affine NBC subset’ are referred to as ‘circuit’, ‘broken circuit’ and ‘NBC subset’ respectively. In 1992, Orlik-Terao
[
18
]
extended Whitney’s broken circuit theorem to hyperplane arrangements, that is, for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, the absolute value of the coefficient of
t^{n-k}
in the characteristic polynomial
\chi(\mathcal{A},t)
of
\mathcal{A}
equals the number of all affine NBC
k
-subsets of
\mathcal{A}
. When
V
is a real vector space, the complement
V-\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{A}}H
consists of finitely many connected components, called the
regions
of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
be the collection of all regions of
\mathcal{A}
. In 1975, Zaslavsky
[
26
]
showed that the number of all regions of
\mathcal{A}
is exactly equal to
|\chi(\mathcal{A},-1)|
. Below is an easy consequence of Zaslavsky’s formula and Whitney’s broken circuit theorem for hyperplane arrangements, which is a version of Stanley’s theorem for real hyperplane arrangements.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
26
,
18
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a hyperplane arrangement in a real vector space, then

|\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})|=|{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})|.

Bijective proofs for
Theorem 1.1
and
Theorem 1.2
have been well studied over the past 25 years. By this time there were many important bijections constructed inductively by the deletion-contraction operation of hyperplane arrangements or oriented matroids as well. For example, Jewell-Orlik
[
16
]
established the bijection from
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
to
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})
, which was reworked by Delucchi
[
7
]
in classical settings of hyperplane arrangement complexes. In a series of papers
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, Gioan and Las Vergnas made a systematic study on a framework to provide bijections for acyclic orientations of graphs and matroids, regions of hyperplane arrangements, no broken circuits, etc.. The first chapter of Gioan’s thesis
[
9
]
provides a deletion-contraction framework for oriented matroids whose specification are available for hyperplane arrangements. This framework is detailed precisely in
[
14
]
for graphs. All these are developed in the celebrated “active bijection” theory, initially defined in
[
9
]
and detailed in
[
13
]
. Indeed, the refined active bijection produces bijections from acyclic reorientations or regions to no broken circuits. Furthermore, the canonical active bijection available for oriented matroids has the advantage of preserving Tutte polynomial activities and being substantially independent of the initial orientation.

Note that the bijection in
[
16
,
7
]
are defined by the deletion-contraction operation, which means the correspondence are not characterized specifically, but built step by step in a recursive way. A bijection for graphs by Blass and Sagan
[
5
]
in 1986 is particularly noteworthy. In the Blass-Sagan’s algorithm, the correspondence of each step is stated in a closed form and its domain (or its range as well) is characterized explicitly. In this paper, the Blass-Sagan’s bijection available for graphs will be extended to oriented matroids and real hyperplane arrangements, which gives bijective proofs for
Theorem 1.1
and
Theorem 1.2
respectively. As a straightforward generalization, our bijection will be established more explicitly by introducing the pairings of NBC subsets and acyclic reorientation sets of oriented matroids (or regions of real hyperplane arrangements), see
Subsection 2.2
and
Section 3
for details.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section 2
is devoted to establishing a bijection between all acyclic reorientations of an oriented matroid and its NBC subsets. Applying this bijection to real hyperplane arrangements,
Section 3
gives a bijection from all regions of a real hyperplane arrangement to its affine NBC subsets.

2
Bijection from acyclic reorientations to NBC subsets

This section aims to establish an explicit bijection from acyclic reorientations of an oriented matroid to no broken circuit (NBC) subsets of its underlying matroid.

2.1
Basics on oriented matroids

We start with some basics on matroids and oriented matroids following from
[
19
,
2
]
. In 1935, Whitney
[
25
]
introduced matroids to capture abstract properties of vector dependences and graph cycles. A
matroid

M=(E,C)
is an ordered pair of a finite set
E
and a collection
C
of subsets of

E
satisfying the following three properties:

(C1)

\emptyset\notin C
;

(C2)

if
X,Y\in C
and
X\subseteq Y
, then
X=Y
;

(C3)

if
X,Y
are distinct members of
C
and
e\in X\cap Y
, then there is a member
Z\in C
such that
Z\subseteq X\cup Y-\{e\}
.

Members of
C
are called circuits of
M
and
E
is called the ground set of
M
. In particular, a single element of
E
is a loop if itself is a circuit. A matroid is said to be loopless if it contains no loops. A subset of
E
is independent if it contains no circuits of
M
and dependent otherwise. Additionally, a subset
X
of
E
is called a flat of
M
if for any element
e\in E-X
, there is no circuit
Y
such that
e\in Y\subseteq X\cup\{e\}
. Let
M=(E,C)
be a matroid on the ground set
E
and
X
a subset of
E
. The deletion
M\backslash X=(E-X,C\backslash X)
is a matroid on the ground set
E-X
with the circuit set

C\backslash X:=\{Y\mid Y\in C\mbox{\rm~and~}Y\subseteq E-X\}.

The contraction
M/X=(E-X,C/X)
is a matroid on the ground set
E-X
with the circuit set

C/X:={\rm min}\{Y-X\mid Y\in C\mbox{\rm~and~}Y-X\neq\emptyset\},

where ‘
{\rm min}
’ means the inclusion-minimal non-empty sets. In addition, a minor of
M
is a matroid obtained from
M
by a sequence of deletions and contractions.

To equip matroids with orientations, in 1978 Bland and Las Vergnas
[
4
]
introduced the concept of oriented matroids. For any two disjoint subsets
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
of a finite set
E
, the paring
X=(X^{+},X^{-})
is said to be a signed subset of
E
and
X^{+}
(resp.
X^{-}
) is the set of positive (resp. negative) elements of
X
. The support of
X
is
\underline{X}=X^{+}\cup X^{-}
, and the opposite of
X
is
-X=(X^{-},X^{+})
. An
oriented matroid

\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
is an ordered pair of a finite set
E
and a collection
\mathcal{C}
of signed subsets of
E
satisfying:

(
\mathcal{C}1
)

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{C}
;

(
\mathcal{C}2
)

\mathcal{C}=-\mathcal{C}
;

(
\mathcal{C}3
)

if
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
and
\underline{X}\subseteq\underline{Y}
, then
X=Y
or
X=-Y
;

(
\mathcal{C}4
)

if two distinct members
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
,
X\neq-Y
and
e\in X^{+}\cap Y^{-}
, then there is
Z\in\mathcal{C}
such that
Z^{+}\subseteq X^{+}\cup Y^{+}-\{e\}
and
Z^{-}\subseteq X^{-}\cup Y^{-}-\{e\}
.

Members of
\mathcal{C}
are called signed circuits of
\mathcal{M}
and
E
is called the ground set of
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\underline{\mathcal{C}}=\{\underline{X}\mid X\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Then the ordinary matroid
\underline{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\underline{\mathcal{C}})
is said to be the underlying matroid of
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\mathcal{M}=\big(E,\mathcal{C}\big)
be an oriented matroid on the ground set
E
. A signed circuit
X=(X^{+},X^{-})\in\mathcal{C}
is called positive if
X^{-}=\emptyset
. An oriented matroid is said to be acyclic if it contains no positive circuits. For
A\subseteq E
,
{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{M}=\big(E,{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{C}\big)
is a reorientation of
\mathcal{M}
, where
{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{C}
consists of signed circuits
{}_{-A}X=({}_{-A}X^{+},{}_{-A}X^{-})
with
{}_{-A}X^{+}=(X^{+}-A)\cup(X^{-}\cap A)
and
{}_{-A}X^{-}=(X^{-}-A)\cup(X^{+}\cap A)
. Our first result is to establish a bijection between acyclic reorientations of
\mathcal{M}
and NBC subsets of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
. The deletion and contraction operations of ordinary matroids can be naturally extended to oriented matroids. More precisely, for
X\subseteq E
, the deletion
\mathcal{M}\backslash X=(E-X,\mathcal{C}\backslash X)
has the signed circuit set

\mathcal{C}\backslash X:=\{Y\mid Y\in\mathcal{C}\mbox{\rm~and~}\underline{Y}\subseteq E-X\},

and the contraction
\mathcal{M}/X=(E-X,\mathcal{C}/X)
has

\mathcal{C}/X:={\rm min}\{Y\backslash X\mid Y\in\mathcal{C}\mbox{\rm~and~}\underline{Y}-X\neq\emptyset\},

where
Y\backslash X=(Y^{+}-X,Y^{-}-X)
. Likewise, a minor of
\mathcal{M}
is obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by a sequence of deletions and contractions. Note from
[
2
, 3.3.3 Proposition]
that for disjoint subsets
X,Y
of
E
,

\mathcal{M}\backslash X/Y=\mathcal{M}/Y\backslash X,

i.e., the minor of
\mathcal{M}
does not depend on the ordering of deletion and contraction operations. Clearly,
\underline{\mathcal{M}\backslash X/Y}=\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash X/Y
.

2.2
Bijection for oriented matroids

Given an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
with
E=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
. Recall

A(\mathcal{M})=\{A\subseteq E\mid{{}_{-A}}\mathcal{M}{\rm~is~acyclic}\}\;\mbox{\rm~and~}\;{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})=\{N\subseteq E\mid N{\rm~is~NBC~subset~of~}\underline{\mathcal{M}}\}.

In this section, the Blass-Sagan’s algorithm will be extended to a bijection from
A(\mathcal{M})
to
{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. In the Blass-Sagan’s algorithm, the key ingredient is the set
\mathscr{D}_{k}
of all possible results of its first
k
steps for
k=1,\ldots,m
. Likewise, below we will introduce the key set
\mathscr{N}_{k}
and the bijection
\psi_{k}:\mathscr{N}_{k-1}\to\mathscr{N}_{k}
whose composition will automatically lead to a bijection from
A(\mathcal{M})
to
{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. Note that if
\mathcal{M}
has loops, then
\mathcal{M}
has no acyclic reorientations and
{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})=\emptyset
. We may assume that
\mathcal{M}
is loopless and the ground set
E
is totally ordered by
e_{1}\prec e_{2}\prec\cdots\prec e_{m}
. For
k=0,1,\ldots,m
, let
E_{k}=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k}\}
. Note that if
N_{k}\subseteq E_{k}
and
N_{k}^{c}:=E_{k}-N_{k}
, then the minor
\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}
has the ground set
E-E_{k}
. Define
\mathscr{N}_{k}
to be the set of pairings
(N_{k},A_{k})
with
N_{k}\subseteq E_{k}
,
A_{k}\subseteq E-E_{k}
satisfying

(I)

N_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
,

(II)

\mathcal{M}_{k}:={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}\big)
is acyclic.

The map
\psi_{k}:\mathscr{N}_{k-1}\to\mathscr{N}_{k}
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\psi_{k}\big(N_{k-1},A_{k-1}\big)=\begin{cases}\big(N_{k-1}\cup\{e_{k}\},A_{k-1}\big)&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\notin A_{k-1}\mbox{\;and\;}{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\mbox{\;is acyclic};\\
\big(N_{k-1},A_{k-1}\big)&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\notin A_{k-1}\mbox{\;and\;}{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\mbox{\;is not acyclic};\\
\big(N_{k-1},A_{k-1}-\{e_{k}\}\big)&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\in A_{k-1}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

From
Lemma 2.3
,
Lemma 2.4
and
Lemma 2.5
, we will see that
\psi_{k}
is well-defined, injective and surjective respectively, i.e., each
\psi_{k}
is a bijection for
k=1,2,\ldots,m
. It follows that the composition
\psi:=\psi_{m}\circ\psi_{m-1}\circ\cdots\circ\psi_{1}
is a bijection from
\mathscr{N}_{0}
to
\mathscr{N}_{m}
. Note

\mathscr{N}_{0}=\big\{(\emptyset,A_{0})\mid A_{0}\in A(\mathcal{M})\big\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad\mathscr{N}_{m}=\big\{(N_{m},\emptyset)\mid N_{m}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})\big\}.

Immediately, the map
\psi
induces a bijection from
A(\mathcal{M})
to
{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. The example below is a glimpse of this bijection.

Example 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
is an oriented matroid with the ground set

E=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
and
\mathcal{C}=\{\pm X_{i}\mid i=1,2,3,4\}
, where

X_{1}=(e_{1},e_{2}e_{3}),\quad X_{2}=(e_{1}e_{2},e_{4}),\quad X_{3}=(e_{1},e_{3}e_{4}),\quad X_{4}=(e_{2}e_{3},e_{4}).

Without causing confusion, we use the sequence
e_{i}e_{j}\cdots
to denote the set
\{e_{i},e_{j},\ldots\}
. By routine analyses, the set of all acyclic reorientations of
\mathcal{M}
is

A(\mathcal{M})=\{\emptyset,\,e_{2},\,e_{3},\,e_{1}e_{3},\,e_{2}e_{4},\,e_{1}e_{2}e_{4},\,e_{1}e_{3}e_{4},\,e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}e_{4}\}.

It is clear that the underlying matroid
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
is the uniform matroid of rank 2 and size 4. Given the total order

To prove that
\psi_{k}
is a bijection, we need the following proposition

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{C})
be an oriented matroid,
e\in E
, and
(e,\emptyset)\notin\mathcal{C}
. If
\mathcal{M}/\{e\}
is acyclic, then
\mathcal{M}
is acyclic.

Proof.

For convenience, we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}/\{e\}
. It follows that the set of signed circuits of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}={\rm min}\big\{X\backslash\{e\}\mid X\in\mathcal{C}\mbox{\rm~and~}\underline{X}-\{e\}\neq\emptyset\big\}.

From the axiom
(\mathcal{C}3)
on signed circuits, we have an easy fact that for any
X\in\mathcal{C}
with
\{e\}\subsetneqq\underline{X}
, we have
X\backslash\{e\}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is not acyclic, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}
has a positive circuit
Y_{0}=(\underline{Y_{0}},\emptyset)
. The above fact implies that
e\notin\underline{Y_{0}}
. Otherwise,
Y_{0}\backslash\{e\}
is a positive circuit of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, a contradiction with
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
being acyclic. Let

\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}:=\big\{Y\in\mathcal{C}\mid e\in\underline{Y}\mbox{\rm~and~}\underline{Y}-\{e\}\subseteq\underline{Y_{0}}\big\}.

Firstly, we claim
\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}\neq\emptyset
. Since
Y_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
and
e\notin\underline{Y_{0}}
, the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
implies that there is a signed circuit
Y_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
Y_{1}\backslash\{e\}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\underline{Y_{1}}-\{e\}\subseteq\underline{Y_{0}}
. It remains to prove
e\in\underline{Y_{1}}
. If not, we have
Y_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\underline{Y_{1}}\subseteq\underline{Y_{0}}
. The axiom
(\mathcal{C}3)
on signed circuits of
\mathcal{M}
implies
Y_{1}=Y_{0}
or
-Y_{0}
, i.e.,
Y_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, a contradiction with
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
being acyclic.

Secondly, we claim that
\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
contains a signed circuit with only one negative element
e
. Note that
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}\Leftrightarrow-Y\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
. We may assume
Y_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
and
e\in Y_{1}^{-}
. Clearly, the claim is true if
Y_{1}^{-}=\{e\}
. If
Y_{1}^{-}
contains a distinct element
f\neq e
, obviously we have
f\in Y_{0}^{+}
. The axiom
(\mathcal{C}4)
implies that there exists a signed circuit
Y_{2}
of
\mathcal{M}
satisfying

Y_{2}^{+}\subseteq Y_{0}^{+}\cup Y_{1}^{+}-\{f\}=\underline{Y_{0}}-\{f\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad Y_{2}^{-}\subseteq Y_{0}^{-}\cup Y_{1}^{-}-\{f\}=Y_{1}^{-}-\{f\},

which means
\underline{Y_{2}}-\{e\}\subsetneqq\underline{Y_{0}}
. Applying the axiom
(\mathcal{C}3)
again, we have
e\in\underline{Y_{2}}
. Consequently, we obtain
Y_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
with less negative elements than
Y_{1}
. Continuing this process, after finitely many steps it will produce a signed circuit in
\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
with only one negative element
e
. So the claim is verified.

Now we may assume
Y=(Y^{+},e)\in\mathcal{C}_{e,\scriptscriptstyle Y_{0}}
. The assumption
(e,\emptyset)\notin\mathcal{C}
implies
Y^{+}\neq\emptyset
. From the fact stated at the very beginning, we have
Y\backslash\{e\}=(Y^{+},\emptyset)\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, a contradiction with
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
being acyclic. This completes the proof.
∎

2.3
Proofs on the bijection
\psi_{k}

Lemma 2.3
.

\psi_{k}
is well-defined.

Proof.

Given a paring
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
and let
(N_{k},A_{k})=\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})
. From (
2.1
), we have
N_{k}\subseteq E_{k}
and
A_{k}\subseteq E-E_{k}
clearly. To prove
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
, it is enough to show that
N_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
and
\mathcal{M}_{k}:={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}\big)
is acyclic. We proceed the proof in the following three cases.

Case 1. If
e_{k}\notin A_{k-1}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic, then

N_{k}=N_{k-1}\cup\{e_{k}\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k}=A_{k-1}.

Since
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
, we have
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
which implies
N_{k}
contains no circuits of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
. Suppose
N_{k}
contains broken circuits of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,
N_{k}\notin{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. There is a circuit
X\in\underline{\mathcal{C}}
with the maximal element
e_{l}
such that
X\subseteq N_{k}\cup\{e_{l}\}
. Moreover, we have
l>k
and
e_{k}\in X
. Otherwise if
l\leq k
(or
e_{k}\notin X
), then
N_{k-1}
contains the broken circuit
X-{e_{l}}
which contradicts
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. Recall the definitions of deletion and contraction operations that the set of circuits of
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}=\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}/N_{k-1}
is

{\rm min}\big\{Y-N_{k-1}\mid Y\in\underline{\mathcal{C}},\,Y\subseteq E-N_{k-1}^{c}\mbox{\rm~and~}Y-N_{k-1}\neq\emptyset\big\}.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic, neither
e_{k}
nor
e_{l}
is a loop of
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}
. It follows that
X-N_{k-1}=\{e_{k},e_{l}\}
is a circuit of
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}
. Then either
(e_{l}e_{k},\emptyset)
or
(e_{l},e_{k})
is a signed circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
, which is impossible since both
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
are acyclic. So we have
N_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. It remains to show that
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is acyclic. Since
\mathcal{M}_{k}={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}\big)
,
N_{k}=N_{k-1}\cup\{e_{k}\}
, and
A_{k}=A_{k-1}
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}/\{e_{k}\}
, which means that the set of signed circuits of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is

{\rm min}\big\{Y\backslash\{e_{k}\}\mid Y{\rm~is~a~signed~circuit~of~}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\mbox{\rm~and~}\underline{Y}-\{e_{k}\}\neq\emptyset\big\}.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is not acyclic, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{k}
has a positive circuit, say
Z=(\underline{Z},\emptyset)
. Then
Z=Y\backslash\{e_{k}\}
for some signed circuit
Y
of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic, which implies
e_{k}\in\underline{Y}
. So we have
Y=(\underline{Z},e_{k})
clearly, i.e., the oriented matroid
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
has a positive circuit
{}_{-e_{k}}Y=\big(\underline{Z}\cup\{e_{k}\},\emptyset\big)
, contradicting that
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic. So we have
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
in this case.

Case 2. If
e_{k}\notin A_{k-1}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is not acyclic, then

N_{k}=N_{k-1}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k}=A_{k-1}.

It follows that
\mathcal{M}_{k}={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}\big)=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}
and each positive circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is also a positive circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
contains no positive circuits, so does
\mathcal{M}_{k}
. Namely,
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is acyclic. Clearly, we have
N_{k}=N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. So
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
.

Case 3. If
e_{k}\in A_{k-1}
, then

N_{k}=N_{k-1}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k}=A_{k-1}-\{e_{k}\}.

Note that the element
e_{k}
is not in the ground set of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
, which implies
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}
. By similar arguments as Case 2, we have
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

\psi_{k}
is injective.

Proof.

If
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})=(N_{k},A_{k})
, the definition (
2.1
) implies
N_{k-1}=N_{k}-\{e_{k}\}
and
A_{k}=A_{k-1}-\{e_{k}\}
.
Suppose there is another element
(N_{k-1}^{\prime},A_{k-1}^{\prime})
satisfying
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1}^{\prime},A_{k-1}^{\prime})=(N_{k},A_{k})
. Obviously,
N_{k-1}^{\prime}=N_{k-1}=N_{k}-\{e_{k}\}
and

A_{k}=A_{k-1}-\{e_{k}\}=A_{k-1}^{\prime}-\{e_{k}\}.

Suppose
A_{k-1}\neq A_{k-1}^{\prime}
and assume
e_{k}\in A_{k-1}
,
e_{k}\notin A_{k-1}^{\prime}
, i.e.,
A_{k-1}=A_{k}\sqcup\{e_{k}\}
and
A_{k-1}^{\prime}=A_{k}
. Notice
e_{k}\in A_{k-1}
corresponds to the 3rd case in (
2.1
), which implies
N_{k-1}=N_{k}
and
(N_{k},A_{k})=(N_{k-1}^{\prime},A_{k-1}^{\prime})
. From the 2nd case of (
2.1
), the oriented matroid

{}_{-A_{k-1}^{\prime}\sqcup\{e_{k}\}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash(N_{k-1}^{\prime})^{c}/N_{k-1}^{\prime}\big)={}_{-A_{k-1}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}/N_{k-1}\big)

is not acyclic, a contradiction with
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
being acyclic.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

\psi_{k}
is surjective.

Proof.

We will proceed the proof in 3 cases to show that for any
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
, there is a paring
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
satisfying
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})=(N_{k},A_{k})
. From the definition of
\mathscr{N}_{k}
, we have that
N_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
and
\mathcal{M}_{k}:={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k}^{c}/N_{k}\big)
is acyclic.

Case 1. When
e_{k}\in N_{k}
, take

N_{k-1}=N_{k}-\{e_{k}\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k-1}=A_{k}.

Obviously, we have
N_{k-1}\subseteq E_{k-1}
and
A_{k-1}\subseteq E-E_{k-1}
. Moreover,
N_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
implies
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
. We claim that
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}:=\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}/N_{k-1}
is loopless. Indeed, exercise 8 in
[
19
, P. 105]
states that
\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}/N_{k-1}
is loopless if and only if
N_{k-1}
is a flat of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}
. Suppose
N_{k-1}
is not a flat of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N^{c}_{k-1}
. Then for some element
e_{l}\notin E_{k-1}
, there is a circuit
Y
of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}
such that
e_{l}\in Y\subseteq N_{k-1}\cup\{e_{l}\}
. It implies that
N_{k-1}
contains a broken circuit
Y-\{e_{l}\}
of
\underline{\mathcal{M}}
, a contradiction with
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{M})
, which proves the claim. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}/\{e_{k}\}
and
(e_{k},\emptyset)
is not a signed circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
since
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}
is loopless. Immediately from
Proposition 2.2
,
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic. Likewise, we have
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic since
\big({}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\big)/\{e_{k}\}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}/\{e_{k}\}=\mathcal{M}_{k}
.
So we obtain
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
and
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})=(N_{k},A_{k})
from the definition of
\psi_{k}
, see (
2.1
). It completes the proof.

To proceed the other two cases, let

\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}:={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash(E_{k-1}-N_{k})/N_{k}\big).

Case 2. When
e_{k}\notin N_{k}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}
is not acyclic, take

N_{k-1}=N_{k}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k-1}=A_{k}.

Clearly, we have
N_{k-1}\subseteq E_{k-1}
,
A_{k-1}\subseteq E-E_{k-1}
. Applying similar arguments as Case 1, we can obtain that
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
and
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}
is loopless. Note
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}:={}_{-A_{k-1}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash N_{k-1}^{c}/N_{k-1}\big)
. We have

\mathcal{M}_{k-1}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}={}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}.

Immediately, we have
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is not acyclic. Let
Y=(\underline{Y},\emptyset)
be a positive circuit of
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
. The assumption that
\mathcal{M}_{k}={}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}
is acyclic implies
e_{k}\in\underline{Y}
. Next we show that
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is acyclic by contradiction. Suppose
Z=(\underline{Z},\emptyset)
is a positive circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
. Again we have
e_{k}\in\underline{Z}
since
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}
is acyclic. Moreover,
\underline{Z}-\{e_{k}\}\neq\emptyset
since
\underline{\mathcal{M}_{k-1}}
is loopless. Let
Y_{1}={}_{-e_{k}}Z=(Y_{1}^{+},e_{k})
. Now we have two signed circuits
Y
and
Y_{1}
of
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
satisfying
Y\neq-Y_{1}
and
e_{k}\in Y^{+}\cap Y_{1}^{-}
. The axiom
(\mathcal{C}4)
yields to a signed circuit
Y_{2}
with
Y_{2}^{-}\subseteq Y^{-}\cup Y_{1}^{-}-\{e_{k}\}=\emptyset
, i.e., indeed a positive circuit of
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
. Since
e_{k}\notin\underline{Y_{2}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{k}={}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}\backslash\{e_{k}\}
,
Y_{2}
is also a positive circuit of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is acyclic. So we have shown that
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
is not acyclic. From the definition of
\psi_{k}
, we have
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})=(N_{k},A_{k})
.

Case 3. When
e_{k}\notin N_{k}
and
{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}
is acyclic, take

N_{k-1}=N_{k}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad A_{k-1}=A_{k}\cup\{e_{k}\}.

Immediately, we have
N_{k-1}\subseteq E_{k-1}
,
A_{k-1}\subseteq E-E_{k-1}
,
N_{k-1}\in{\rm NBC}(\underline{\mathcal{M}})
, and
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}={}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}
, which implies
(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})\in\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
and
\psi_{k}(N_{k-1},A_{k-1})=(N_{k},A_{k})
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The arguments of
Lemma 2.5
provide a direct way to obtain the inverse map of
\psi_{k}
. More precisely, the inverse
\psi_{k}^{-1}:\mathscr{N}_{k}\to\mathscr{N}_{k-1}
is defined as follows. For any
(N_{k},A_{k})\in\mathscr{N}_{k}
, let

\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}={}_{-A_{k}}\big(\mathcal{M}\backslash(E_{k-1}-N_{k})/N_{k}\big)

and define

\psi_{k}^{-1}(N_{k},A_{k})=\begin{cases}\big(N_{k}-\{e_{k}\},A_{k}\big),&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\in N_{k};\\
\big(N_{k},A_{k}\big),&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\notin N_{k}\mbox{\;and\;}{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}\mbox{\;is not acyclic};\\
\big(N_{k},A_{k}\cup\{e_{k}\}\big),&\mbox{if\;}e_{k}\notin N_{k}\mbox{\;and\;}{}_{-e_{k}}\mathcal{M}_{k-1}^{\prime}\mbox{\;is acyclic}.\end{cases}

3
Bijection from regions to affine NBC subsets

In this section, the bijection (
2.1
) for oriented matroids will be specialized for real hyperplane arrangements to establish a bijection between regions and NBC subsets.

3.1
Bijection for real linear arrangements

In order to be compatible with matroid notations, in this section we make the abuse of the notation
\mathcal{A}
to denote a multi-arrangement, i.e., a multi-set of hyperplanes. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a linear multi-arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is well known that there is a natural way to define an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
such that all regions of
\mathcal{A}
are one-to-one corresponding to acyclic reorientations of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
. More precisely, given a finite set
E
and for each
e\in E
, take
\bm{\alpha}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define

H_{e}:\bm{\alpha}_{e}\cdot\bm{x}=0
. This defines a linear multi-arrangement
\mathcal{A}=\{H_{e}\mid e\in E\}
. For each hyperplane
H_{e}:\bm{\alpha}_{e}\cdot{\bm{x}}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the positive and negative sides of
H_{e}
can be defined as

H_{e}^{+}:\bm{\alpha}_{e}\cdot{\bm{x}}>0\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad H_{e}^{-}:\bm{\alpha}_{e}\cdot{\bm{x}}<0

respectively. Recall from
Section 1
that if
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}:=\{H_{e}:e\in S\}
, for some subset
S\subseteq E
, is a circuit of
\mathcal{A}
, there is a linear relation
\sum_{e\in S}\lambda_{e}\bm{\alpha}_{e}=0
with
\lambda_{e}\neq 0
. Associated with the circuit
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, we have a signed set
X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=(X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{+},X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{-})
satisfying

X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{+}=\big\{e\in S:\lambda_{e}>0\big\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{-}=\big\{e\in S:\lambda_{e}<0\big\}.

Then we define the oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=(E,\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the collection of signed sets
X_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
for those
S\subseteq E
so that
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
is a circuit of
\mathcal{A}
. The deletion
\mathcal{A}\backslash H_{e}
is a multi-arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by deleting
H_{e}
from
\mathcal{A}
and the contraction
\mathcal{A}/H_{e}
is a multi-arrangement in
H_{e}
consisting of hyperplanes
H\cap H_{e}
for all
H\in\mathcal{A}\backslash H_{e}
. With these definitions, it is easily seen that

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}/H_{e})=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})/\{e\}\quad\mbox{\rm~and~}\quad\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}\backslash H_{e})=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\{e\},

where the positive (negative) sides of
\mathcal{A}\backslash H_{e}
and
\mathcal{A}/H_{e}
are inherited from
\mathcal{A}
. More generally, for any two disjoint subsets
S
and
T
of
E
, we have

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}\backslash\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}/\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle T})=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\backslash S/T.

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
share the same total order, it is clear that for
S\subseteq E
, we have

S\in{\rm NBC}\big(\underline{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})}\big)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\in{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A}).

(3.1)

Note that with the choices of normal vectors
\pm{\bm{\alpha}}_{e}
, we have distinguished oriented matroids. In fact, for
A\subseteq E
and each
e\in A
, choosing
-{\bm{\alpha}}_{e}
as the normal vector of
H_{e}
, then we obtain the oriented matroid
{}_{-A}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
, a reorientation of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
by
A
. Recall that a region
\Delta\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
is a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}-\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{A}}H
, which implies all points of
\Delta
lie in the same side of
H_{e}
for each
H_{e}\in\mathcal{A}
. Let

A(\Delta)
be a subset of
E
defined by

A(\Delta)=\{e\in E\mid\Delta\subseteq H_{e}^{-}\}.

It is clear from
[
2
]
that the reorientation

{}_{-A(\Delta)}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is acyclic. Moreover, the map

A:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})\to A(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})),\quad\Delta\mapsto A(\Delta)

(3.2)

is a bijection. In
Subsection 2.2
, we have established a bijection from
A(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))
to
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))
. With the obvious correspondence between
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))
and
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})
,
the composition of these two bijections will lead to a bijection from
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
to
{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})
, which is our main result in this section and will be formulated precisely as follows.

Suppose
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
is totally ordered by
e_{1}\prec\cdots\prec e_{m}
, and
\mathcal{A}=\big\{H_{e_{i}}:\bm{\alpha}_{e_{i}}\cdot{\bm{x}}=0,\;e_{i}\in E\big\}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\big\{H_{e_{i}}\mid i\in[k]\big\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}:=\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{A}_{k}
. For some
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}/\mathcal{B}
is a hyperplane arrangement in the affine subspace
\cap\mathcal{B}=\cap_{H\in\mathcal{B}}H
defined by

\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}/\mathcal{B}:=\big\{(\cap\mathcal{B})\cap H_{i}\mid H_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c},\;(\cap\mathcal{B})\cap H_{i}\neq\cap\mathcal{B}\mbox{\rm~and~}\emptyset\big\}.

For
k=0,\ldots,m
, let
\mathscr{B}_{k}
be the collection of pairings of NBC subsets and regions of
\mathcal{A}
defined by

\mathscr{B}_{k}=\big\{(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k})\mid\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{k},\,\mathcal{B}_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})\mbox{\rm~and~}\Delta_{k}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}/\mathcal{B}_{k})\big\}.

In particular,
\mathscr{B}_{0}=\big\{(\emptyset,\Delta_{0})\mid\Delta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})\big\}
and
\mathscr{B}_{m}=\big\{(\mathcal{B}_{m},\cap\mathcal{B}_{m})\mid\mathcal{B}_{m}\in{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})\big\}
. The bijection
\phi_{k}:\mathscr{B}_{k-1}\to\mathscr{B}_{k}
is defined as follows. For any
(\mathcal{B}_{k-1},\Delta_{k-1})\in\mathscr{B}_{k-1}
, let

3.3
Extension to real hyperplane arrangements

In this subsection, the bijection in
Subsection 3.1
will be extended to real hyperplane arrangements via the coning operation. Let
\mathcal{A}=\big\{H_{i}:\bm{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\bm{x}=b_{i}\mid i\in[m]\big\}
be a real hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then its
coning

\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
is a linear arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
defined by

\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}=\big\{\mbox{\rm c}H_{i}:\bm{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\bm{x}=b_{i}x_{n+1}\mid i\in[m]\big\}\sqcup\{K_{0}:x_{n+1}=0\}.

Let
K_{1}:x_{n+1}=1
. It is clear that

\mathcal{A}=\big\{\mbox{\rm c}H_{i}\cap K_{1}\mid i\in[m]\big\},

i.e., the hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is nothing but the restriction of
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
on
K_{1}
. It follows that

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})=\{\Delta\cap K_{1}\mid\Delta\in\mathcal{R}(\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A})\mbox{\rm~and~}\Delta\cap K_{1}\neq\emptyset\}.

Then the bijection of
Subsection 3.1
can be applied to the linear arrangement
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
to obtain the bijection for hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
. Suppose
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
is totally ordered by
\mbox{\rm c}H_{1}\prec\mbox{\rm c}H_{2}\prec\cdots\prec\mbox{\rm c}H_{m}\prec K_{0}
and
\mathcal{A}
is ordered by
H_{1}\prec H_{2}\prec\cdots\prec H_{m}
. Note that for any subset
S\subseteq[m]
,
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
is an affine NBC subset of
\mathcal{A}
if and only if both
(\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A})_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and
(\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A})_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\cup\{K_{0}\}
are the NBC subsets of
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
. More precisely, the bijection from regions of
\mathcal{A}
to its affine subsets is obtained from the bijection of
Subsection 3.1
as follows. For each
\Delta_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
, there is a unique
\Delta\in\mathcal{R}(\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A})
with
\Delta_{1}=\Delta\cap K_{1}
, which is by the bijection of
Subsection 3.1
sent to an NBC subset of
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{A}
, denoted
\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{B}
. Then the set
\{H_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\mid\mbox{\rm c}H_{i}\in\mbox{\rm c}\mathcal{B}\}
is automatically an affine NBC subset of
\mathcal{A}
, which establishes the bijection. To state the bijection explicitly, we need to introduce the set
\mathscr{B}_{k}
and the bijection
\phi_{k}:\mathscr{B}_{k-1}\to\mathscr{B}_{k}
for
\mathcal{A}
, which can be defined exactly in the same manner as (
3.3
). Indeed, all notations and definitions for linear arrangements in (
3.3
) are also available for hyperplane arrangements. The following remark is the inverse of
\phi_{k}
in (
3.3
) for hyperplane arrangements
\mathcal{A}
, whose composition gives a bijection from affine NBC subsets of
\mathcal{A}
to its regions.

Remark 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\big\{H_{i}:\bm{\alpha}_{i}\cdot\bm{x}=b_{i}\mid i\in[m]\big\}
be a hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the total order
H_{1}\prec\cdots\prec H_{m}
. Recall from
Subsection 3.1
that

\mathscr{B}_{k}
is the collection of pairings of NBC subsets and regions of
\mathcal{A}
defined by

\mathscr{B}_{k}=\big\{(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k})\mid\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{k},\,\mathcal{B}_{k}\in{\rm NBC}(\mathcal{A})\mbox{\rm~and~}\Delta_{k}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}/\mathcal{B}_{k})\big\}.

For any paring
(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k})\in\mathscr{B}_{k}
, let
\Delta_{k}^{\prime}
be the unique region in
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{c}/(\mathcal{B}_{k}-\{H_{k}\})
with
\Delta_{k}\subseteq\Delta_{k}^{\prime}
.
The inverse map
\phi_{k}^{-1}:\mathscr{B}_{k}\to\mathscr{B}_{k-1}
is defined to be

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k})=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{B}_{k}-\{H_{k}\},\Delta_{k}^{\prime}\cap H_{k}^{+})&\mbox{if\;}\Delta_{k}\subseteq H_{k};\\
(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k})&\mbox{if\;}\Delta_{k}\cap H_{k}=\emptyset;\\
(\mathcal{B}_{k},\Delta_{k}^{\prime}\cap H_{k}^{-})&\mbox{if\;}\Delta_{k}\cap H_{k}\neq\emptyset,\;\Delta_{k}\nsubseteq H_{k}.\end{cases}

One should observe that the bijections
\phi_{k}
directly give rise to an extended version
Theorem 3.2
of
Theorem 1.2
, which was first proved in
[
8
]
by induction on the number of hyperplanes. In particular, this coincides with
Theorem 1.2
at
k=m
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
8
]
, Theorem 4.2)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}\}
