The Attractor of the Replicator Dynamic in Zero-Sum Games
Oliver Biggar, Iman Shames

Abstract
In this paper we characterise the long-run behaviour of the replicator dynamic in zero-sum games (symmetric or non-symmetric). Specifically, we prove that every zero-sum game possesses a unique global replicator attractor, which we then characterise. Most surprisingly, this attractor depends only on each player’s preference order over their own strategies and not on the cardinal payoff values, defined by a finite directed graph we call the game’s preference graph . When the game is symmetric, this graph is a tournament whose nodes are strategies; when the game is not symmetric, this graph is the game’s response graph. We discuss the consequences of our results on chain recurrence and Nash equilibria.

Introduction

Learning in the presence of other learning agents is an increasingly fundamental topic in modern machine learning, motivated by its role at the core of cutting-edge techniques like
learning from self-play

(
Silver et al. 2016
;
Silver et al. 2018
)
and
Generative Adversarial Networks

(
Goodfellow et al. 2020
)
. The challenge of these systems is analysing their collective behavior, which is where learning theory intersects with game theory. To quote
Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998
, “a major task of game theory [is] to describe the dynamical outcome of model games described by strategies, payoffs and adaptive mechanisms." That is, when agents learn collectively, what do they learn?

In online learning, the best-known approaches use variants of the Multiplicative Weights Update algorithm (MWU)
(
Arora et al. 2012
)
. To achieve the ‘no-regret’ property, these algorithms typically decrease the step size as more samples are observed. In the long-run, as the step size becomes small, the behavior of a collection of MWU-playing agents converges to the flow of a famous differential equation: the
replicator dynamic

(
Taylor and Jonker 1978
)
. This model was originally inspired by biological models of evolution
(
Smith and Price 1973
)
, and is a central object of study in
evolutionary game theory
, the subfield of game theory which focuses on dynamic processes. Since its discovery, the replicator has been extensively analysed by biologists, mathematicians, economists and computer scientists
(
Hofbauer and Sigmund 2003
;
Sandholm 2010
;
Kleinberg et al. 2011
)
. Indeed, just as MWU is the flagship algorithm in online learning, the replicator is the flagship dynamic in evolutionary game theory
(
Sandholm 2010
)
.

Describing the ‘dynamical outcome’ of games under the replicator dynamic (and hence MWU) involves answering a basic question:
to which strategy profiles do we converge over time?
In dynamical systems, a system’s long-run behavior is defined by its
attractors

(
Strogatz 2018
)
. Attractors are sets of points which are
invariant
(points inside the set remain there for all time),
asymptotically stable
(points in some neighbourhood converge to the set) and
minimal
(they do not contain a smaller set with the first two properties). Understandably, identifying the attractors of the replicator is one of the core questions of evolutionary game theory, and increasingly also algorithmic game theory
(
Zeeman 1980
;
Hofbauer and Sigmund 2003
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
;
Omidshafiei et al. 2019
;
Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis et al. 2020
, see Section
2
)
. The broadest takeaway of this line of work is that a strategy profile is an attractor
if and only if
it is a
strict (pure) Nash equilibrium

(
Sandholm 2010
)
. However, this result only describes the simplest attractors—those which contain only a single point. Most games—especially zero-sum games—don’t have any pure Nash equilibria, and most attractors contain more than one point! Instead, the trajectories of the replicator in zero-sum games are typically periodic
(
Mertikopoulos et al. 2018
)
, and under MWU they are often
chaotic

(
Cheung and Piliouras 2019
;
Cheung and Piliouras 2020
)
.

We conclude that, despite four decades of research,
the attractors of the replicator remain largely unknown
, even in zero-sum games, arguably the best-studied special case
(
Akin and Losert 1984
;
Hofbauer 1996
;
Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998
;
Hofbauer and Sigmund 2003
;
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
, see Section
2
)
. This is what we achieve in this paper: we characterise the attractors of the replicator dynamic in every zero-sum game. Beyond this result, our concepts and techniques shed new light on equilibria, the graph structure of games
(
Biggar and Shames 2023a
;
Biggar and Shames 2023b
)
and the modelling of payoffs/losses in economics and machine learning.

Related Work

Long-run stability of strategy profiles under the replicator is a fundamental topic in evolutionary game theory, especially with regard to classical solution concepts, such as Nash equilibria and
evolutionarily stable strategies
. See
Sandholm 2010
for a summary. A particularly relevant work is that of
Eshel et al. 1983
, who studied zero-sum games under the replicator, proving a crucial
volume conservation
property, analysed in depth by
Hofbauer 1996
. Many papers since
(such as
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
;
Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis et al. 2020
;
Biggar and Shames 2023b
, and this paper)
have used this property to bound asymptotically stable sets of the replicator.
Zeeman 1980
performed an early study of replicator attractors, suggesting that the qualitative behaviour of the replicator can be split into a finite number of classes; we extend this by showing that in zero-sum games the qualitative behaviour is defined by the preference graph alone.
Ritzberger and Weibull 1995
showed that when a
subgame
is closed under “weakly better responses", then it is asymptotically stable under the replicator. The preference graph is defined by the weakly better responses, and so this follows as a special case of our result when a connected component of the preference graph is a subgame. Finally, one of the most famous of these classical results proves that the
time-average
of the replicator (and MWU) converges to the Nash equilibrium in zero-sum games
(
Freund and Schapire 1999
;
Hofbauer et al. 2009
)
. However, the time-average behavior is distinct from the day-to-day or
last-iterate
behavior
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
)
.

Despite these efforts, a general negative conclusion of this line of work is that the replicator doesn’t converge to mixed equilibria
(
Sandholm 2010
)
, and moreover no dynamic can converge to equilibria in all games
(
Hart and Mas-Colell 2003
;
Benaïm et al. 2012
)
. Instead, its behavior can be chaotic
(
Sato et al. 2002
)
. Further, finding equilibria is generally computationally intractable
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
)
. Consequently, the algorithmic game theory and learning communities have increasingly shifted towards new notions of dynamical outcome which can predict the day-to-day behavior of computational agents in games
(
Kleinberg et al. 2011
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
)
.

To this end,
sink chain components
were recently proposed
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
)
as the outcome of dynamic games, with the replicator used as the motivating example. Sink chain components are built on a concept called
chain recurrence
(Definition
5.1
), a generalisation of periodicity which forms the foundation of the Fundamental Theorem of Dynamical Systems
(
Conley 1978
)
. Crucially, chain components are grounded in computational considerations. To quote
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
, informally, a “point
x
is chain recurrent if, whenever Alice starts at
x
, Bob can convince her that she is on a cycle by manipulating the round off error of her computer—no matter how much precision Alice brings to bear.” Interestingly, this computational solution concept brings us back to the classical notion of an attractor: when a replicator attractor exists, it is a sink chain component (Lemma
5.2
). Thus, finding the attractors of the replicator dynamic is motivated not only by dynamical systems but also computer science: the attractors give us the strongest prediction of long-run behavior which is consistent with reliable computation.

This chain recurrence approach has inspired a number of new results on games and the replicator dynamic
(
Omidshafiei et al. 2019
;
Biggar and Shames 2023a
;
Biggar and Shames 2023b
)
. In zero-sum games, when a
fully-mixed
Nash equilibria exists, the behavior is essentially unpredictable: the sink chain component is the whole game
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2016
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2018
)
. Under MWU, we observe chaotic behavior in these games
(
Cheung and Piliouras 2019
;
Cheung and Piliouras 2020
)
. Further, when a fully-mixed NE does not exist,
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
showed that all fully-mixed strategy profiles converge to the subgame containing the equilibrium, called the
essential subgame
, and within this subgame all profiles are chain recurrent
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2016
)
. Surprisingly, however, these results do not characterise the chain components of zero-sum games—the essential subgame is generally
not
a sink chain component. The reason is that the convergence to the essential subgame is not
uniform
, and so the essential subgame is typically
not
asymptotically stable (see Section
5
), one of the defining properties of attractors and sink chain components
(
Alongi and Nelson 2007
)
and a key property for predicting learning processes
(
Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis et al. 2020
;
Omidshafiei et al. 2019
)
. Instead, interior profiles which are
arbitrarily close
to the essential subgame may move far away before returning. The question remains unanswered: what are the replicator sink chain components/attractors of zero-sum games?

The story of predicting the replicator seems generally negative: the replicator may be chaotic, may not converge, or may only converge in time-average or non-uniformly. Our results tell a different, more positive story: we characterise the attractor/sink chain component of the replicator, which, while larger than the essential subgame, is the smallest outcome which is consistent with computation, in the sense of chain recurrence. What’s more, being defined by discrete preferences, the solution is natural and robust. As an example, in Figure
2
, shifting the payoff for the profile
(a,c)
from
(3,-3)
to
(4,-4)
moves the essential subgame from
\{b,c\}\times\{b,c\}
to
\{a,c\}\times\{b,c\}
. However, all preferences remain unchanged, so the graph and hence the attractor do not change. Shifting focus from the Nash equilibrium also gives a new perspective on chain recurrence. Prior approaches
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2016
;
Mertikopoulos et al. 2018
)
suggested a connection between equilibria and chain recurrence in zero-sum games. We find instead that chain recurrence in zero-sum games is
entirely
defined by the preference graph (Lemma
5.3
). The previous findings are now explained by a non-trivial connection between equilibria and the preference graph: the existence of a fully-mixed equilibrium implies strong connectedness of the preference graph (Lemma
5.4
). See Section
5
.

3
Preliminaries

In game theory, a game is defined by a triple consisting of the
players
,
strategy sets
for each player, and
payoffs
. A combination of strategies for each player is called a
strategy profile
or simply a profile, and for each profile there is a real-valued payoff to each player. In this paper we focus on two-player games, where we denote the players by the integers 1 and 2 and their strategy sets by
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. The strategy names are simply labels, so we assume
S_{1}=[n]:=\{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|\ i<n\}
and
S_{2}=[m]:=\{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|\ i<m\}
. The profiles are the pairs
S_{1}\times S_{2}
. We call this an
n\times m
game. An
n\times m
game is defined by a pair
(A,B)
of matrices,
A\in\real^{n\times m}
and
B\in\real^{m\times n}
, representing the payoffs to players 1 and 2 respectively. A game is
symmetric
if
A=B
. Intuitively, a game is symmetric if the payoff is determined solely by the choice of strategies, and not identity of the player. We focus on
zero-sum games
, which we represent by a single matrix
M\in\real^{n\times m}
, implicitly assumed to be the payoffs for the first player, which defines the game
(M,-M^{T})
. That is, in a profile
(s_{1},s_{2})
, player 1 receives
M_{s_{1},s_{2}}
and player 2 receives
(-M^{T})_{s_{2},s_{1}}=-M_{s_{1},s_{2}}
. A zero-sum game is also a symmetric game if
M=-M^{T}
, that is,
M
is
anti-symmetric
. Thus, there is a natural one-to-one correspondence between anti-symmetric real matrices and symmetric zero-sum games.
A
subgame
of a game is formed by choosing subsets
T_{1}\subseteq S_{1}
and
T_{2}\subseteq S_{2}
of each player’s strategy sets and restricting the game to the profiles in
T_{1}\times T_{2}
. We typically represent a subgame by its product set of profiles
T_{1}\times T_{2}
.

A
mixed strategy
is a distribution over a player’s strategies, and a
mixed profile
is an assignment of a mixed strategy to each player. We sometimes refer to a profile as a
pure profile
to distinguish it from a mixed profile. If
x
is a mixed strategy, and
s
a strategy, we write
x_{s}
for the
s
-entry of
x
. Like profiles, we denote mixed profiles by pairs
(x,y)
where
x
and
y
are mixed strategies for the first and second player, respectively. The
support
of a mixed strategy
x
, written
\supp(x)
, is the set of strategies
s
where
x_{s}
is non-zero. The
support
of a mixed profile
(x,y)
is the Cartesian product
\supp(x)\times\supp(y)
, the set of profiles whose strategies are in the support of
x
and
y
respectively. As distributions over a finite set, mixed strategies can be naturally embedded in the standard probability simplex in Euclidean space, by choosing some arbitrary ordering of the strategies in
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. We denote these spaces by
\Delta(S_{1})
and
\Delta(S_{2})
. The set of mixed profiles is the product
\Delta(S_{1})\times\Delta(S_{2})
, which we call the
strategy space
of the game, and we refer to mixed profiles as ‘points’ in strategy space. The strategy space is also naturally embedded in Euclidean space, so we can talk about geometric properties of sets of mixed profiles. The payoffs extend to mixed profiles using expectation. In a zero-sum game
(M,-M^{T})
, the
expected payoff
of a mixed profile
(x,y)
is
y^{T}Mx
to player 1 and
x^{T}(-M^{T})y=-(y^{T}Mx)
to player 2.

In evolutionary game theory, we think of an
n\times m
game as a pair of ‘populations’, with the strategies as ‘types’ within each population. A mixed strategy
x
represents the distribution of types in the population. Symmetric games, called
single-population games
in this context, are viewed as having a a single underlying population, with types from the strategy set
S
. See
Sandholm 2010
. Viewed as a two-player game, all mixed profiles in a single-population game are of the form
(x,x)
, because there is only one underlying population. This leads to an important terminology convention:
in symmetric games, (mixed) profiles are the same as (mixed) strategies
. Intuitively, this is because a profile is a choice of strategy for each player, and symmetric games have ‘only one player’. The strategy space of the game consists of the symmetric profiles
(x,x)
in
\Delta(S)\times\Delta(S)
. We write the mixed profile
(x,x)
as the mixed strategy
x
, and write simply
\Delta(S)
for the strategy space of the symmetric game.

3.2
Dynamical Systems and the Replicator

The replicator dynamic is a continuous-time dynamical system
(
Sandholm 2010
)
, defined by an ordinary differential equation. Let
x\in\Delta(S_{1})
and
y\in\Delta(S_{2})
be mixed strategies, and let
s\in S_{1}
and
t\in S_{2}
be pure strategies. Then, for a (non-symmetric) zero-sum game
M
we have

Definition 3.3

(Non-Symmetric Zero-Sum Replicator Equation)
.

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{s}

\displaystyle=x_{s}((My)_{s}-x^{T}My)

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=-y_{t}((M^{T}x)_{t}-x^{T}My)

In a symmetric game
M
, the replicator is defined a similar way:

\dot{x}_{s}=x_{s}((Mx)_{s}-x^{T}Mx)

If
M
is also zero-sum, then
x^{T}Mx=0
(by anti-symmetry), and this reduces to

Definition 3.4

(Symmetric Zero-Sum Replicator Equation)
.

\dot{x}_{s}=x_{s}(Mx)_{s}

Note that the symmetric and non-symmetric replicator, while similar, are distinct equations with different properties. The solutions to these equations define a
flow

(
Sandholm 2010
)
on the strategy space of the game, which is a function
\phi:X\times\real\to X
that is a continuous group action of the reals on
X
. We call these the symmetric or non-symmetric
replicator flow
, respectively. The forward orbit of the flow from a given point is called a
trajectory
of the system. A set of points
Y
is called
invariant
under
\phi

\phi(Y,t)=Y
for any
t\in\real
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
X
be a compact space,
\phi
a flow on
X
and
A
a compact subspace of
X
. If there is a neighbourhood

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{x\in U}\inf_{y\in A}\mathbf{d}(\phi(x,t),y)=0

under any metric

is
asymptotically stable
. If
A
is also invariant under
\phi

There are some differences in terminology in the literature to be wary of. What we call attracting sets are sometimes called attractors

;
Sandholm 2010
;
Biggar and Shames 2023b
)
, in which case what we call an attractor is a

The set of points which approach an attractor

\intr(X)

In this section we prove Theorem
4.3
, which characterises the attractor of the replicator in zero-sum games. We begin by noting that any mixed profile
x
naturally defines a distribution over profiles, with
x_{p}
denoting the mass on a profile
p
. If the game is symmetric, profiles and strategies are the same and so this is trivial. In a non-symmetric game,
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
is a pair of mixed strategies, and the distribution is defined by the
product
, with the mass on a profile
p=(p_{1},p_{2})
defined by

x_{p}:={x_{1}}_{p_{1}}{x_{2}}_{p_{2}}

(1)

This distribution over profiles is used to define an important concept: the
content
of a set of profiles.

Definition 4.1

(
Biggar and Shames 2023b
)
.

Let
H
be a set of profiles in a game. The
content
of
H
, denoted
\content(H)
, is the set of all mixed profiles
x
where all profiles in the support of
x
are in
H
.

Equivalently,
x\in\content(H)
if and only if
x_{H}:=\sum_{h\in H}x_{h}=1
, that is,
x
defines a distribution whose mass is entirely distributed over profiles in
H
. The content is a union of subgames, and so is an invariant set under the replicator
Sandholm 2010
. An example is shown in Figure
2
.
We will show that the unique global attractor is the content of the unique sink component of the preference graph. Uniqueness follows from graph structure, established originally in
Biggar and Shames 2023a
.

Lemma 4.2

(Uniqueness)
.

The preference graph of a zero-sum game has a unique sink component.

The proof of Lemma
4.2
can be found in the appendix. Now we can prove our main theorem.

Theorem 4.3

(The Attractor of the Replicator)
.

In a (symmetric or non-symmetric) zero-sum game
M
, the content of the unique sink component
H
of its preference graph is the unique global attractor of the (respectively symmetric or non-symmetric) replicator dynamic.

Proof.

Proving Theorem
4.3
requires showing (i)
\content(H)
is an invariant set (
invariance
), (ii) every attracting set contains
\content(H)
(
minimality
) and (iii)
\content(H)
is asymptotically stable and its basin of attraction contains
\intr(X)
(
global asymptotic stability
). We establish (i) and (ii) in Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma 4.4

(Invariance and Minimality)
.

If
H
is the sink component of the preference graph of a (symmetric or non-symmetric) zero-sum game
M
, then
\content(H)
is invariant under the replicator. Further, for any attracting set
A
,
\content(H)\subseteq A
.

Lemma
4.4
is a streamlined presentation of existing results
(
Biggar and Shames 2023b
, particularly Theorem 5.2 of)
, so we defer its proof to the appendix. The challenge and main contribution of Theorem
4.3
lies in (iii): showing global asymptotic stability of
\content(H)
. We do this by demonstrating that
x_{H}
, the total mass on the sink component
H
, increases over time (Lemmas
4.5
and
4.9
). That is,
\dot{x}_{H}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{H}=\sum_{h\in H}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{h}=\sum_{h\in H}\dot{x}_{h}>0
, for any
x\in\intr(X)
. The function
x_{H}
is a natural choice, because
x_{H}
is uniformly continuous, bounded in
[0,1]
and
x_{H}=1
if and only if
x\in\content(H)
, so
x_{H}
can be thought of as a metric for the distance between
x
and the content. Showing
\dot{x}_{H}>0
requires different arguments for the symmetric and non-symmetric cases. The symmetric case is straightforward, and we complete it in Lemma
4.5
below.

Lemma 4.5
.

In a symmetric zero-sum game
M
, under the symmetric replicator (Definition
3.4
), if
x_{H}\in(0,1)
then
\dot{x}_{H}>0
.

Proof.

First,
\dot{x}_{H}=\sum_{h\in H}x_{h}(Mx)_{h}
. As
M
is anti-symmetric, for any profiles
h
and
q
we have
x_{h}x_{q}M_{h,q}+x_{q}x_{h}M_{q,h}=0
. Hence,

\dot{x}_{H}=\sum_{q\in S}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}M_{h,q}=\sum_{q\in H}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}M_{h,q}+\sum_{q\not\in H}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}M_{h,q}=\sum_{q\not\in H}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}M_{h,q}

For any
q\notin H
and
h\in H
, as
H
is a sink component, there is an arc
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
in the preference graph with strictly positive weight. That is,
M_{h,q}>0
. Hence the summands are nonnegative. Finally, because
x_{H}\in(0,1)
, there must exist a
q\not\in H
and an
h\in H
such that
x_{q}x_{h}>0
. Thus,
\dot{x}_{H}>0
.
∎

The more challenging case of the proof is showing that
\dot{x}_{H}>0
in non-symmetric games (Lemma
4.9
). We solve this using Theorem
4.8
, which establishes that we can embed the flow of the non-symmetric replicator on our non-symmetric game
M
into the flow of the symmetric replicator on a larger symmetric game,
M
’s
von Neumann symmetrisation
.

Definition 4.6

(Von Neumann Symmetrisation)
.

Let
M
be a zero-sum game, with
M\in\real^{n\times m}
. The
von Neumann symmetrisation
of
M
, written
\mathcal{S}_{M}
, is defined as the following
(nm)×(nm)
matrix, which we index by profiles
p=(p_{1},p_{2})
and
q=(q_{1},q_{2})
:

(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{p,q}=M_{p_{1},q_{2}}-M_{q_{1},p_{2}}.

The strategy space of
\mathcal{S}_{M}
is
S_{1}\times S_{2}
. That is, the
strategy profiles
of the original game
M
become
strategies
of the von Neumann symmetrisation. Mixed profiles likewise become
mixed strategies
, using the production distribution as in equation (
1
):
x_{p}:={x_{1}}_{p_{1}}{x_{2}}_{p_{2}}
.

This construction appeared first in
Gale et al. 1950
, who attributed it to John von Neumann, hence the name. It possesses an intuitive explanation: it results from holding two plays of the original game
M
, with each player taking the role of the row player 1 in one play and player 2 in the other
(
Gale et al. 1950
)
. The von Neumann symmetrisation has two important properties: (1) it is anti-symmetric, and so is a symmetric zero-sum game, and (2) it can be viewed as an extension of the weight matrix
W
, previously only defined on comparable profiles, to a relation over all profiles. That is, if
p
and
q
are comparable then
(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{p,q}=W_{p,q}
. More generally,

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
p=(p_{1},p_{2})
and
q=(q_{1},q_{2})
be profiles. Then:

(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{p,q}=W_{p,(p_{1},q_{2})}+W_{p,(q_{1},p_{2})}=W_{(p_{1},q_{2}),q}+W_{(q_{1},p_{2}),q}.

In Theorem
4.8
, we establish a novel link between the replicator flow on a zero-sum game and its von Neumann symmetrisation.

Theorem 4.8

(Symmetrising the Replicator Dynamic)
.

Let
M
be a non-symmetric zero-sum game. Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
be a mixed profile and
p=(p_{1},p_{2})
a pure profile. Write
x_{p}:={x_{1}}_{p_{1}}{x_{2}}_{p_{2}}
as in equation (
1
). Then, under the non-symmetric replicator (Definition
3.3
),

\dot{x}_{p}=x_{p}(\mathcal{S}_{M}x)_{p}.

In English, Theorem
4.8
states that the flow of the non-symmetric replicator (Definition
3.3
) on
M
embeds (on the subspace of product distributions) in the flow of the symmetric replicator (Definition
3.4
) on
\mathcal{S}_{M}
. The proofs of Theorem
4.8
and Lemma
4.7
are straightforward and can be found in the appendix. Using Theorem
4.8
, we can complete the proof of Theorem
4.3
on non-symmetric games by showing that
x_{H}
is increasing in
x_{H}\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 4.9
.

In a non-symmetric zero-sum game
M
, under the non-symmetric replicator (Definition
3.3
), if
x_{H}\in(0,1)
then
\dot{x}_{H}
> 0.

Proof.

If
h=(h_{1},h_{2})
is a pure profile, then by Theorem
4.8
,
\dot{x}_{h}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}({x_{1}}_{h_{1}}{x_{2}}_{h_{2}})=x_{h}(\mathcal{S}_{M}x)_{h}
. Because
\mathcal{S}_{M}
is symmetric, by the same argument as in Lemma
4.5
, we can show that

\dot{x}_{H}=\sum_{q\not\in H}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}.

Now pick some profiles
q\not\in H
and
h\in H
with
x_{q}x_{h}>0
. As in Lemma
4.5
, because
x_{H}\in(0,1)
, at least one such pair exist. We will show that the sum above is strictly positive. Firstly, observe that if
q
and
h
are comparable, the arc
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
of the preference graph of
M
goes from
q
to
h
with strictly positive weight, because
q\not\in H
and
h\in H
, and so
(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}=W_{h,q}>0
. Otherwise, suppose that
q=(q_{1},q_{2})
and
h=(h_{1},h_{1})
are not comparable, and let
a=(q_{1},h_{2})
and
b=(h_{1},q_{2})
. We have the following three cases:

1.

a,b\in H
. Then the arcs
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
are directed towards
a
and
b
because
q
is outside the sink component
H
. By Lemma
4.7
,
(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}=W_{a,q}+W_{b,q}>0
.

2.

a,b\not\in H
.
Then the arcs
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
are directed towards
h
because
a
and
b
are outside the sink component. By Lemma
4.7
,
(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}=W_{h,a}+W_{h,b}>0
.

3.

a\in H
,
b\not\in H
(the case
b\in H
,
a\not\in H
is identical). The sum
\sum_{q\not\in H}\sum_{h\in H}x_{q}x_{h}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}
includes the terms
x_{q}x_{h}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}
and
x_{b}x_{a}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{a,b}
. However,
x_{q}x_{h}=({x_{1}}_{q_{1}}{x_{2}}_{q_{2}})({x_{1}}_{h_{1}}{x_{2}}_{h_{2}})=({x_{1}}_{q_{1}}{x_{2}}_{h_{2}})({x_{1}}_{h_{1}}{x_{2}}_{q_{2}})=x_{a}x_{b}

by Lemma
4.7
. Since
a
and

are inside the sink component and
q
and
b
are outside, and the arcs
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
must be directed into the component, so

x_{q}x_{h}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{h,q}+x_{b}x_{a}(\mathcal{S}_{M})_{a,b}>0
.

Overall, we conclude that
\dot{x}_{H}>0
.
∎

Finally, asymptotic stability of
\content(H)
follows easily from the fact that

is uniformly continuous. Pick any

. Then for any

with
x_{H}\in[\alpha,\beta]
,
\dot{x}_{H}>\epsilon
for some

(uniform continuity), so after some finite time

shows that
\content(H)
is asymptotically stable.
∎

5
Chain Recurrence and Nash Equilibria

In this section we discuss some consequences of Theorem
4.3
. The first concerns chain recurrence, which is defined by
(\epsilon,T)
-chains.

Definition 5.1

(
(\epsilon,T)
-Chains,
Alongi and Nelson 2007
)
.

Let
\phi
be a flow on a compact metric space
X
, with
x
and
y
in
X
. An
(\epsilon,T)
-chain
from
x
to
y
is a finite sequence of points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
with
x=x_{1}
and
y=x_{n}
, and times
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in[T,\infty)
such that
\mathbf{d}(\phi(x_{i},t_{i}),x_{i+1})<\epsilon
. If there is an
(\epsilon,T)
-chain from
x
to
y
for
all

\epsilon>0
and
T>0
we say there is a
pseudo-orbit
from
x
to
y
.

A point is called
chain recurrent
if it has a pseudo-orbit to itself. Two points are
chain equivalent
if there are pseudo-orbits between them in both directions, and equivalent chain recurrent points are grouped in topologically connected components called
chain components

(
Alongi and Nelson 2007
)
. Reachability under pseudo-orbits provides an ordering on the chain components, and sink chain components are those which are minimal in this order. Sink chain components have been increasingly studied in algorithmic game theory
(
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2016
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2018
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2019
)
. A connection between the preference graph and chain components of the replicator was demonstrated by
Biggar and Shames 2023b
, who proved that replicator sink chain components always exist. However, sink chain components have not generally been characterised. Attractors, when they exist, are sink chain components, so our results present the first characterisation of sink chain components of zero-sum games.

Lemma 5.2

(Folklore, see the appendix)
.

In any flow, every attractor is a sink chain component.

Lemma 5.3
.

The content of the sink component of the preference graph is the unique sink chain component of a zero-sum game.

Lemma
5.3
follows directly from Theorem
4.3
combined with Lemma
5.2
. The proof of both lemmas can be found in the appendix. In zero-sum games, previous state-of-the-art results on chain recurrence made use of the Nash equilibrium
(
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
;
Papadimitriou and Piliouras 2016
;
Mertikopoulos et al. 2018
)
. This line of inquiry established first (1) that all points in the essential subgame are contained within the sink chain component. Secondly (2), if the essential subgame is not the whole game, all interior starting points converge to the essential subgame. This suggests a connection between chain recurrence and equilibria in zero-sum games. Lemma
5.3
comes to a different conclusion; it proves that sink chain components are characterised solely by the preference graph. Lemma
5.4
resolves this seeming discrepancy:
chain components are determined by the preference graph, but the presence of an equilibrium in a subgame forces some structure on the induced preference graph of that subgame—in particular, it must be strongly connected and contained within the sink component of the whole game’s preference graph.

Lemma 5.4
.

In a zero-sum game, any subgame with a Nash equilibrium in its interior must (i) be contained within the sink component of the preference graph and (ii) the subgraph which this subgame induces in the preference graph must be strongly connected.

Proof.

(i) Assume the attractor is not the whole game, in which case it is on the boundary. By Theorem
4.3
, all interior points are in the basin of attraction, and by Theorem 3.4 of
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
, trajectories starting from interior points converge to the essential subgame in the limit, and so the essential subgame must be within the attractor. (ii) Each subgame is independent, so we can assume we are working with the whole game and the equilibrium
x
is fully-mixed. For contradiction, assume the preference graph is not strongly connected. Consequently, there is an attractor
A
on the boundary of the strategy space (Theorem
4.3
). The point
x
is in the basin of attraction of
A
, so
x
converges to
A
, which contradicts the fact that
x
is a Nash equilibrium, which are fixed points under the replicator
(
Sandholm 2010
)
.
∎

This Lemma rephrases our understanding of equilibria and chain recurrence, highlighting the key role of the preference graph. As an example, Lemma
5.3
implies that sink chain component is the whole game
if and only if
the preference graph is strongly connected. When a fully-mixed equilibrium exists, the preference graph must be connected, and so the sink chain component is the whole game. Most interestingly, even though we prove Lemma
5.4
using the replicator dynamic, this lemma is a
purely game-theoretic result
which only relates equilibria and the preference graph. This connection between the preference graph and equilibria is useful for analysing games. For instance, examining Figure
2
we find the Nash equilibrium is
((0,0.5,0.5),(0,0.5,0.5))
(both players play
b
and
c
half the time), which has support
\{b,c\}\times\{b,c\}
. As Lemma
5.4
predicts, this is within the attractor and its induced preference graph (a 4-cycle) is strongly connected.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper we gave the first characterisation of the unique attractor of the replicator in zero-sum games, thereby describing the long-term behaviour of the dynamic in these games. As a secondary result, we have demonstrated the importance of the preference graph as a tool for analysing game dynamics. In particular, in the long run, only the
preferences
dictates the outcome of the game. This surprising conclusion has potentially significant consequences for modelling strategic interactions. In game theory—especially evolutionary game theory—precise knowledge of utilities is difficult to achieve; our results show that modelling only the preferences for each player is sufficient to characterise the attractor of the replicator dynamic.

An important goal for future research is to characterise the attractors of the replicator in
all
games, not just zero-sum ones. Currently, the attractors of the replicator have been characterised in only a few classes of games, such as potential games and
2\times n
games without dominated strategies
(
Biggar and Shames 2023b
)
, as well as some individual games, like the Asymmetric Cyclic Matching Pennies game from
(
Kleinberg et al. 2011
)
. Our paper adds zero-sum games to this list of solved classes.

Lemma A.1

(Lemma

Proof.

Lemma A.2

(Lemma
4.4
)
.

If
H
is the sink component of the preference graph of a (symmetric or non-symmetric) zero-sum game

\content(H)\subseteq A

Proof.

This proof is largely the same as Theorem 5.2 of
Biggar and Shames 2023b
, with the addition of the symmetric case.
(
Invariance
:) Observe that if
x\in\content(H)
, then the support of
x
is contained in
H

\Delta(\supp(x))

\Delta(\supp(x))\subseteq\content(H)

\content(H)
is a union of subgames. By Theorem 5.4.7 of

(

Sandholm 2010
, no asymptotically stable set can exist in the interior of the strategy space of a symmetric or non-symmetric zero-sum game. Subgames have the same properties as the whole game under the replicator, so the same is true of all subgames. Dually, no repelling set can exist in the interior of any subgame.

(
Claim
: every attracting set contains a profile.)
This follows by induction, using the fact that the replicator dynamic on a subgame has the same properties as on the whole game. In the whole game, an asymptotically stable set intersects the boundary. This intersection with the boundary must also be asymptotically stable in any subgame it intersects on the boundary, and so it intersects the boundary of this smaller subgame, and so on. We conclude that such a set contains a pure profile, the smallest possible subgame. This claim generalises to other dynamics—see
Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis et al. 2020
, Theorem 4.5.

(

An arc

and

are in the support. The (symmetric or non-symmetric) replicator reduces to
\dot{x}_{p}=x_{p}(1-x_{p})W_{q,p}
on this subgame, where

(for the player for which these profiles are comparable). If
p
is contained in an asymptotically stable set, then

must also be contained in this set, because points near

.

(

Let

be a subgame, where the pure profiles in

Theorem A.5

Proof.

The two-population replicator dynamic (written for player 1, the player 2 case is similar) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\dot{x_{1}}_{s}

\displaystyle={x_{1}}_{s}((Mx_{2})_{s}-x_{1}^{T}Mx_{2})

\displaystyle(\text{Definition~\ref{def: nonsymmetric replicator}})

\displaystyle\dot{x_{1}}_{s}/{x_{1}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in S_{1}}{x_{1}}_{t}((Mx_{2})_{s}-(Mx_{2})_{t})

\displaystyle(\text{as}\ \sum_{t\in S_{1}}{x_{1}}_{t}=1)

Lemma A.6

(Lemma
5.2
)
.

In any flow, every attractor is a sink chain component.

Proof.

We first show that all points in the attractor are chain recurrent. Attracting sets are closed under intersection
[
Kalies et al. 2021
,
Biggar and Shames 2023b
]
, so an attractor cannot overlap any other attracting set—that would contradict the minimality of the attractor.
Conley 1978
showed that points are chain recurrent if, for each attracting set
A
, the point is contained in either
A
or its dual repelling set
A^{*}
. The attractor is compact, invariant, and no attracting set overlaps it, so an attracting or repelling set must contain all points in the attractor. Hence all points are chain recurrent.

Pseudo-orbits cannot leave attracting sets
[
Akin and Losert 1984
]
. Consequently, no point outside the attractor is chain equivalent to a point inside it, and all points in the attractor are chain equivalent, so it is a chain component. It is a
sink
chain component because no pseudo-orbits leave the set.
∎

Corollary A.7
.

The content of the sink component of the preference graph is the unique sink chain component of a zero-sum game.

Proof.

By Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 3.3, the content of the sink component is a sink chain component. Uniqueness follows for the same reason as in Theorem 3.3: distinct sink chain components are disjoint, but every sink chain component contains the content
[
Biggar and Shames 2023b
]
.
