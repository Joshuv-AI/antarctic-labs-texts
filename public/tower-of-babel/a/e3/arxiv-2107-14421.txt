Abstract
. The principal ratio of a connected graph G G , γ ⁡ ( G ) \gamma(G) , is the ratio between the largest and smallest coordinates of the principal eigenvector of the adjacency matrix of G G . Over all connected graphs on n n vertices, γ ⁡ ( G ) \gamma(G) ranges from 1 1 to n c ​ n n^{cn} . Moreover, γ ⁡ ( G ) = 1 \displaystyle{\gamma(G)=1} if and only if G G is regular. This indicates that γ ⁡ ( G ) \gamma(G) can be viewed as an irregularity measure of G G , as first suggested by Tait and Tobin (El. J. Lin. Alg. 2018). We are interested in how stable this measure is. In particular, we ask how γ \gamma changes when there is a small modification to a regular graph G G . We show that this ratio is polynomially bounded if we remove an edge belonging to a cycle of bounded length in G G , while the ratio can jump from 1 1 to exponential if we join a pair of vertices at distance 2 2 . We study the connection between the spectral gap of a regular graph and the stability of its principal ratio. A naive bound shows that given a constant multiplicative spectral gap and bounded degree, the ratio remains polynomially bounded if we add or delete an edge. Using results from matrix perturbation theory, we show that given an additive spectral gap greater than ( 2 + ϵ ) ​ n (2+\epsilon)\sqrt{n} , the ratio stays bounded after adding or deleting an edge.



1.
Introduction

It is known that the adjacency matrix of every connected graph has a simple largest eigenvalue, and this eigenvalue has an eigenvector with all-positive coordinates, called the principal eigenvector of the graph. It is natural to study how this eigenvector reflects the structure of the graph. Our goal is to obtain asymptotic information as the number of vertices approaches infinity in a family of graphs.

Cioabă and Gregory
[
6
]
defined the
principal ratio
of the graph
G
,
\gamma(G)=q_{\max}/q_{\min}
, to be the ratio between the largest and smallest coordinates of the principal eigenvector
\mathbf{q}
. This ratio is
1
for regular graphs, while it can grow at factorial rate (i.e.,
\gamma(G)>n^{cn}
for some positive constant
c
)
[
6
]
. Since
\gamma(G)\geq 1
where equality holds if and only if
G
is regular, it is natural to think of
\gamma(G)
as a measure of the irregularity of
G
. This view was suggested by Tait and Tobin
[
8
]
.

A basic observation is that, given a connected graph
G
with largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
and diameter
D
, the principal ratio satisfies

(1.1)

\gamma(G)\leq\lambda_{1}^{D}.

We are interested in the stability of
\gamma
, i.e., how a slight change of
G
influences
\gamma(G)
. In particular, given a
d
-regular graph
G
, we ask how
\gamma(G)
changes from the constant
1
if we add or remove one edge in
G
. (We call the resulting graphs
G+e
and
G-e
, respectively.) We always assume the edge we remove will not disconnect
G
(i.e., the edge
e
is not a bridge), so that the principal eigenvector of
G-e
is defined.

In Section
4
, we study the cases where the edge we add to or remove from a regular graph is between vertices at bounded distance. We show the following.

•

\gamma(G+e)
can jump to exponential in
n
when the degree is bounded [Theorem
4.3
]. In our example,
e
connects two vertices at distance
2
in
G
. We make use of Chebyshev polynomials in proving this result. We recall some properties of Chebyshev polynomials and show their connection with the principal eigenvectors of certain graphs in Sec.
3.2
.

It is easy to infer from (
1.1
) that boundedness of the degree is necessary here (see Cor.
3.4
and Fact
3.5
).

•

If we remove an edge belonging to a cycle of bounded length in
G
, then
\gamma(G-e)
is always polynomially bounded regardless of the degree [Theorem
4.12
].

We also study the relevance of the spectral gap to the stability of
\gamma(G)
for regular graphs. In Section
5
, based on (
1.1
), we note the following.

•

\gamma(G\pm e)
is always polynomially bounded in
n
when
G
is a bounded-degree expander graph, i.e., when the degree is bounded and the spectral gap of
G
is bounded away from zero [Observation
5.3
].

In Section
6.2
, we put this problem in the more general context of perturbations of matrices. By adapting theorems and proofs from Stewart and Sun’s book
[
3
]
to our special case, we show the following.

•

If the additive spectral gap is greater than
(2+\epsilon)\sqrt{n}
, then
\gamma(G\pm e)
is bounded [Theorem
6.1
].

This result does not follow from (
1.1
). Indeed, in Section
6.1
we construct graphs with degree of order
n^{(2+t)/3}
and additive spectral gap of order
n^{t}
, having diameter of order
n^{(1-t)/3}
, for any constant
0<t<1
. Similar applications of matrix perturbation theory in link analysis for networks can be found in
[
5
]
.

The main technical results of this paper are the first and the fourth bullet points.

\left\{\left\{2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{r}\right)\bigg|\>j=0,1,\dots,r-1\right\}\right\}.\qed

2.3.
The adjacency operator, principal eigenvector and eigenvalue

We write
A_{G}
to denote the adjacency matrix of a graph
G
. We note that
A_{G}
is a real symmetric matrix, so its eigenvalues are real. We refer to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
A_{G}
as the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the graph
G
, respectively. We write
\lambda_{1}(G)\geq\lambda_{2}(G)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(G)
to denote the eigenvalues of
G
.

Fact 2.6
.

For every connected graph
G
, the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(G)
is simple, and it has a unique-up-to-scaling all-positive eigenvector (the principal eigenvector). ∎

We write
\mathbf{q}(G)
for the principal eigenvector of
G
scaled to have
\ell^{2}
norm
1
. Let
q_{i}(G)
denote the coordinate corresponding to vertex
i
in
\mathbf{q}(G)
. We write
q_{\max}(G)
and
q_{\min}(G)
for the maximum and minimum coordinates of
\mathbf{q}(G)
, and
v_{\max}(G)
and
v_{\min}(G)
for corresponding vertices. Recall that the principal ratio of
G
is defined as

(2.7)

\gamma(G):=\frac{q_{\max}(G)}{q_{\min}(G)}.

In the rest of Sec.
2.3
, we fix the graph
G
and omit it from notations.

Observation 2.8
.

Given
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, the
i
-th coordinate of
A\mathbf{y}
is given by

.

(A\mathbf{y})_{i}=\sum_{j:i\sim j}y_{j}.\qed

Corollary 2.9
.

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
is an eigenvector of
A
to eigenvalue
\rho
if and only if

.

\rho y_{i}=\sum_{j:j\sim i}y_{j}.\qed

Recall that
\mathbf{j}
denotes the all-ones vector.

Corollary 2.10
.

\mathbf{j}
is an eigenvector of
A
if and only if
G
is regular. ∎

Fact 2.11
.

If
G
is connected and
\mathbf{y}
is an eigenvector to eigenvalue
\lambda
with all-positive coordinates, then
\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of
A
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{1}
is simple, any eigenvector
\mathbf{y}
to an eigenvalue other than
\lambda_{1}
must be orthogonal to the principal eigenvector.
∎

Fact 2.12
.

For a connected
d
-regular graph
G
,
\lambda_{1}=d
. ∎

Let
\Delta
denote
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\deg(i)
, the maximum degree of
G
.

Fact 2.13
.

For every graph
G
, we have
\lambda_{1}\leq\Delta
. For connected graphs, equality holds if and only if
G
is regular. ∎

We denote the quadratic mean of the degrees of vertices by

(2.14)

\deg_{\qavg}:=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\deg(i)^{2}}{n}}.

Fact 2.15
.

For every graph
G
, we have
\lambda_{1}\geq\deg_{\qavg}
.

Proof.

Immediate using Rayleigh’s principle applied to
A^{2}
and the
\mathbf{j}_{n}
vector.
∎

2.4.
Spectral gaps

For a graph
G
, we call the quantity
\lambda_{1}(G)-\lambda_{2}(G)
the
additive spectral gap
of
G
, and the quantity
(\lambda_{1}(G)-\lambda_{2}(G))/\lambda_{1}(G)
the
multiplicative spectral gap
of
G
.

We write
L_{G}
for the Laplacian of
G
, defined as the
n\times n
matrix

L_{G}=\degag(\deg(1),\deg(2),\dots,\deg(n))-A_{G}.

L_{G}
is positive semidefinite;
\mathbf{j}
is an eigenvector of
L_{G}
to eigenvalue zero. We write
\delta(G)
for the smallest eigenvalue of
L_{G}
restricted to the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{j}
. We note that
\delta(G)=0
if and only if
G
is disconnected.
\delta(G)
is the
algebraic connectivity
of the graph
G
, as first defined by Fiedler
[
1
]
.

For a graph
G
, let
f_{G}
be the characteristic polynomial of the adjacency matrix, and
g_{G}
the characteristic polynomial of the Laplacian.
When
G
is a
d
-regular graph, we have

(2.16)

f_{G}(t)=g_{G}(d-t).

It follows that

(2.17)

\delta(G)=d-\lambda_{2}(G)

is the additive spectral gap of
G
, and

(2.18)

\frac{\delta(G)}{d}=1-\frac{\lambda_{2}(G)}{d}

is the multiplicative spectral gap of
G
. We shall discuss spectral gaps for regular graphs only.

In all notation, we omit the graph
G
when it is clear from context.

2.5.
Rates of growth

By a
family of graphs
, we mean an infinite set of non-isomorphic finite graphs.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{G_{1},G_{2},\dots\}
be a family of graphs, and let
n_{i}
be the number of vertices of
G_{i}
.
We say
\gamma(\mathcal{G})
is
polynomially bounded
if
\displaystyle{\gamma(G_{i})<n_{i}^{c}}
for some constant
c
and all sufficiently large
i
. We say
\gamma(\mathcal{G})
is
exponential
if
\displaystyle{\gamma(G_{i})\geq a^{n_{i}}}
for some constant
a>1
and all sufficiently large
i
. We say
\gamma(\mathcal{G})
has
factorial growth
if
\displaystyle{\gamma(G_{i})\geq n_{i}^{cn_{i}}}
for some positive constant
c
and all sufficiently large
i
.

3.
Preliminary results about the principal eigenvector

As previously introduced,
G
will always denote a connected graph. We write
\lambda_{1}(G)
for the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix of
G
. We use
\mathbf{q}(G)
to mean the principal eigenvector of the adjacency matrix, the all-positive eigenvector to
\lambda_{1}(G)
. We assume
\mathbf{q}(G)
is scaled to have
\ell^{2}
norm
1
unless otherwise stated. We write
\gamma(G)
for the principal ratio
q_{\max}(G)/q_{\min}(G)
of
G
. We omit
G
from these notations when the graph is clear from context.

3.1.
Observations and naive bounds on the ratio

First, we note that
\mathbf{q}
reflects the symmetries of
G
.

Fact 3.1
.

The principal eigenvector
\mathbf{q}
is constant on orbits of
\aut(G)
, the automorphism group of graph
G
. ∎

Next we note some basic bounds on the ratio between the coordinates of
\mathbf{q}
.

Observation 3.2
.

For two vertices
i,j
in
G
, let
\dist(i,j)=k
. Then

\frac{q_{j}}{q_{i}}\leq\lambda_{1}^{k}.

Proof.

If
\dist(i,j)=0
, then
q_{j}/q_{i}=1=\lambda_{1}^{0}
.
If
\dist(i,j)=1
, then by Corollary
2.9
and since all
q_{w}
are positive,
\lambda_{1}q_{i}=\sum_{w:w\sim i}q_{w}\geq q_{j}
. Now, suppose
k\geq 2
and
\displaystyle{q_{w}/q_{i}\leq\lambda_{1}^{k-1}}
for all vertices
w
at distance
k-1
from
i
. We know
j
is adjacent to at least one vertex
w
at distance
k-1
from
i
.
Then

\frac{q_{j}}{q_{i}}=\frac{q_{j}}{q_{w}}\cdot\frac{q_{w}}{q_{1}}\leq\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{1}^{k-1}=\lambda_{1}^{k}.\qed

Recall that
D
denotes the diameter of the graph.

Corollary 3.3
.

For a connected graph
G
of diameter
D
,

.

\gamma\leq\lambda_{1}^{D}\leq\Delta^{D}\leq(n-1)^{D}.\qed

Corollary 3.4
.

If
D
is bounded for some family
\mathcal{G}
of graphs , then
\gamma(\mathcal{G})
is polynomially bounded in
n
. ∎

Since
D
is relevant in bounding the ratio, we introduce a bound on

for regular graphs.

Fact 3.5
.

Let
G
be a connected
d
-regular graph. Then
\displaystyle{D\leq\frac{3n}{d}}
.

Proof.

Pick
v_{0},v_{D}
in
G
so that
\dist(v_{0},v_{D})=D
. Let
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{D}
be a shortest path from
v_{0}
to
v_{D}
. Any
v_{i},v_{j}
with
|i-j|\geq 3
cannot have any common neighbors, since otherwise the path will not be a shortest path. Thus

d\left\lceil\frac{D}{3}\right\rceil\leq n.

Therefore
D\leq 3n/d
.
∎

We know
D(G+e)\leq D(G)
for any
e\in\overline{G}
. The following result shows that
D(G+e)
, and consequently, also
D(G-e)
(if still connected), cannot differ from

by more than a factor of
2
.

Fact 3.6
.

For a connected graph
G
with
D(G)=D
and
e\in E(\overline{G})
,

D(G+e)\geq\frac{1}{2}D(G).

Proof.

(Notation: By
\dist_{l}(x,y)
, where
l
is a path, we mean the distance between
x
and
y
along the path.)

We need to prove that there is a pair of vertices at distance at least
\frac{D}{2}
in
G+e
.
Let
u,v\in V(G)
be such that
\dist_{G}(u,v)=D
. If
\dist_{G+e}(u,v)=D
, we are done. Otherwise, let
p
be a shortest path between
u
and
v
in
G
, and
q
a shortest path between
u
and
v
in
G+e
.
Then
e
must be on
q
. Denote by
x
the endpoint of
e
which is closer to
u
on
q
, and by
y
the other endpoint.
Pick the middle vertex
w
of

with
\dist_{p}(u,w)=\lceil\frac{D}{2}\rceil
. If
\dist_{G+e}(u,w)=\dist_{G}(u,w)=\lceil\frac{D}{2}\rceil
, we are done. Otherwise, any shortest path
r
in
G+e
from
u
to
w
must pass through edge
e
. Suppose we go along
r
from
u
to
w
. By the optimality of
q
, we may assume that
r
and
q
overlap from
u
to
y
.
Now we look at the vertex
w^{\prime}
adjacent to
w
on
p
which is closer to
u
than to
v
. We have

(3.7)

\dist_{G}(w^{\prime},v)=\dist_{p}(w^{\prime},v)=\left\lfloor\frac{D}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

We claim that there is a shortest path in
G+e
from
w^{\prime}
to

that does not pass through

. Let

be a shortest path in
G+e
from
w^{\prime}
to

that passes through

. By the optimality of

, we may assume that

and

overlap from

to

.
Then by the optimality of

,

\dist_{s}(x,w^{\prime})+\dist_{G+e}(w^{\prime},w)\geq\dist_{r}(x,w)=\dist_{G+e}(x,y)+\dist_{r}(y,w),

that is,

\dist_{s}(x,w^{\prime})\geq\dist_{r}(y,w).

Therefore

\dist_{s}(w^{\prime},y)=\dist_{s}(w^{\prime},x)+\dist_{G+e}(x,y)\geq\dist_{G+e}(w^{\prime},w)+\dist_{r}(w,y).

Thus

is equivalent to a path that does not pass through

in

. As a result, a shortest path between

and
v
in

is also available in

. Then by (

),

\dist_{G+e}(w^{\prime},v)=\dist_{G}(w^{\prime},v)=\left\lfloor\frac{D}{2}\right\rfloor+1.\qed

3.2.
Chebyshev polynomials and principal eigenvectors

The Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
,
T_{n}
, can be characterized by the recurrence

(3.8)

T_{n+1}(t)=2t\cdot T_{n}(t)-T_{n-1}(t),

with initial values
T_{0}(t)=1
and
T_{1}(t)=t
.

The Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind
,
U_{n}
, can be characterized by the same recurrence

(3.9)

U_{n+1}(t)=2t\cdot U_{n}(t)-U_{n-1}(t),

with initial values
U_{0}(t)=1
and
U_{1}(t)=2t
.

Fact 3.10
.

When
|t|\geq 1
, the explicit formula for
T_{n}
is

(3.11)

4.2.
Removing an edge

Let

.

We show that in the case of removing the edge

is bounded,

is polynomially bounded for all

. We make use of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.11

(Cioabă, Gregory, Nikiforov
[
7
]
)
.

If

is a connected nonregular graph with
n
vertices, diameter

, and maximum degree

\Delta-\lambda_{1}(G)\geq\frac{1}{n(D+1)}.

Theorem 4.12
.

For a connected
d
-regular graph

and an edge

\dist_{G-e}(1,2)<c
where

\gamma(G-e)
is polynomially bounded in

.

Lemma 4.13
.

q_{\min}(G-e)
is either
q_{1}
or
q_{2}
.

Proof.

If
q_{\min}
corresponds to some vertex
j
with degree

, then the average of the coordinates corresponding to the neighbors of

would be

\frac{\lambda_{1}(G-e)q_{j}}{d}<\frac{\lambda_{1}(G-e)q_{j}}{\lambda_{1}(G-e)}=q_{j},

which is a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.12
.

Without loss of generality suppose
q_{1}=q_{\min}
. Then summing
n

\lambda_{1}(G-e)q_{i}=\sum_{j:j\sim i}q_{j}
, we have

\lambda_{1}(G-e)\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}=(d-1)q_{1}+(d-1)q_{2}+dq_{3}+\cdots+dq_{n}=d\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)-q_{1}-q_{2}.

Therefore by Theorem
4.11
,

\frac{q_{1}+q_{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}}=d-\lambda_{1}(G-e)\geq\frac{1}{n(D(G-e)+1)}\geq\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

By Observation
3.2
,
q_{2}\leq\lambda_{1}(G-e)^{c}q_{1}
. Therefore

\gamma(G-e)<\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}}{q_{1}}\leq n^{2}\left(1+\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}}\right)\leq n^{2}(1+\lambda_{1}(G-e)^{c})<n^{2}(1+d^{c}).\qed

5.
Stability of the ratio: multiplicative spectral gap

We use a known bound on the diameter of a graph in terms of the spectral gap to show that
\gamma(G\pm e)
is polynomially bounded for bounded-degree expanders.

Definition 5.1
.

For
0<\epsilon<1
, a regular graph of degree
d
is an
\epsilon
-expander if
\displaystyle{\lambda_{2}\leq(1-\epsilon)d}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle{\delta\geq\epsilon d}
.

We note that this definition implies that an expander graph is connected.

Theorem 5.2

(N. Alon, V. D. Milman
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
vertices with maximum degree
\Delta
and let
\delta
denote the smallest positive eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix. Then

D(G)\leq 2\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta}{\delta}}\log_{2}n\right\rfloor.

Corollary 5.3
.

For
d
-regular
\epsilon
-expander graphs
G
, we have

(5.4)

\gamma(G+e)\leq n^{4\sqrt{2/\epsilon}\log_{2}(d+1)}.

In particcular, for expander graphs
G
with bounded degree,
\gamma(G\pm e)
is polynomially bounded in
n
.

Proof.

By definition,

\frac{\Delta(G)}{\delta(G)}=\frac{d}{\delta(G)}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

Then

D(G)\leq 2\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{2}{\epsilon}}\log_{2}n\right\rfloor.

By Fact
3.6
,

D(G\pm e)\leq 4\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{2}{\epsilon}}\log_{2}n\right\rfloor.

Since
\gamma\leq\Delta^{D}
(Corollary
3.3
),

\displaystyle\gamma(G\pm e)

\displaystyle\leq(d+1)^{4\left\lfloor\sqrt{2/\epsilon}\log_{2}n\right\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leq(d+1)^{4\sqrt{2/\epsilon}\log_{2}n}

\displaystyle=2^{4\sqrt{2/\epsilon}\log_{2}n\log_{2}(d+1)}

\displaystyle=n^{4\sqrt{2/\epsilon}\log_{2}(d+1)}.\qed

6.
Stability of the ratio: additive spectral gap

We show that a large (
(2+\epsilon)\sqrt{n}
) additive spectral gap implies that
\gamma(G\pm e)
is bounded. Specifically, we prove the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a connected
d
-regular graph. If the additive spectral gap
\delta=d-\lambda_{2}
of
G
satisfies
\displaystyle{\delta>\frac{2}{c}\sqrt{n}}+2
for some value
0<c<1
,
then

\gamma(G\pm e)<\frac{1+c}{1-c}.

To motivate this result, we first point out that graphs with such an additive spectral gap are not necessarily expanders. Indeed, when
d
is greater than
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, the multiplicative spectral gap of graphs with a
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
additive spectral graph will go to zero. Moreover, the diameter of graphs with such an additive spectral gap can still grow quite fast (polynomially in
n
), approaching the upper bound derived from Theorem
5.2
.

6.1.
Existence of graphs with large additive spectral gap and large diameter

Proposition 6.2
.

For a regular graph with additive spectral gap
\delta
, the diameter
D
is
O((n/\delta)^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})
.

Proof.

Let the regular graph have degree
d
.
From Theorem
5.2
, we know that

(6.3)

D^{2}\leq c\frac{d}{\delta}(\log n)^{2}

where
c
is some constant.
By Fact
3.5
, we also have

(6.4)

D\leq\frac{3n}{d}.

Multiplying (
6.3
) and (
6.4
), we have

(6.5)

D\leq(3c)^{1/3}\left(\frac{n}{\delta}\right)^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3}.\qed

Corollary 6.6
.

For a regular graph with an
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
additive spectral gap, the diameter is
O(n^{1/6}(\log n)^{2/3})
.

We show that this bound is nearly tight.

Proposition 6.7
.

There are connected regular graphs with diameter
(1/2)n^{1/6}
and an additive spectral gap of
cn^{1/2}
where
c=2\pi^{2}(1+O(n^{-1/3}))
.

We prove a more general statement.

Proposition 6.8
.

For any constant
0<t<1
, there are connected regular graphs with diameter
(1/2)n^{(1-t)/3}
and an additive spectral gap of
cn^{t}
where

c=2\pi^{2}(1+O(n^{(2t-2)/3}))
.

Proof.

Recall the lexicographic product and its properties in Definition
2.22
, Observation
2.23
, and Fact
2.5
.
Let
G:=C_{r}\circ\overline{K}_{s}
where
r\cdot s=n
. Then
G
is a connected regular graph of degree
2s
and diameter
\lfloor r/2\rfloor
. The adjacency matrix of
G
can be expressed as

(6.9)

A_{G}=A_{C_{r}}\otimes J_{s}+I_{r}\otimes\mathbf{0}=A_{C_{r}}\otimes J_{s}.

Since

(6.10)

\spec(J_{s})=\{\{s,0^{s-1}\}\},

we have

(6.11)

\spec(G)=\{\{s\lambda_{1}(C_{r}),s\lambda_{2}(C_{r}),\dots,s\lambda_{r}(C_{r}),0^{(s-1)r}.\}\}

Thus

\delta(G)=s(\lambda_{1}(C_{r})-\lambda_{2}(C_{r})).

By Fact
2.3
,

\lambda_{1}(C_{r})-\lambda_{2}(C_{r})=2\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{r}\right)\right)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{r^{2}}+\epsilon

where
\displaystyle{|\epsilon|<\frac{2^{5}\pi^{4}}{4!r^{4}}}
. We take
\displaystyle{r=n^{(1-t)/3}}
and
\displaystyle{s=n^{(2+t)/3}}
. Then

\delta(G)=4\pi^{2}n^{t}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\right)=4\pi^{2}n^{t}\left(1+O(n^{(2t-2)/3})\right).\qed

6.2.

Notation 6.12

In the context of adding an edge

6.2.1.
Rotating the eigenbasis

We know
\mathbf{x}
is not an eigenvector of

. We want to know how close
\mathbf{x}
is to the principal eigenvector of

, in the sense that we want to find a vector
\mathbf{v}
with small norm such that
\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}\|}}
is the unit principal eigenvector of

.

Proposition 6.15
.

Let
\mathbf{p}
be a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Let
U=(\mathbf{x}\;Y)\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be orthogonal, where
\mathbf{x}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
Y
is an
n\times(n-1)
matrix.
Define
\widetilde{U}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
as
\displaystyle{\widetilde{U}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\;\widetilde{Y})}
, where

(6.16)

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}+Y\mathbf{p}}{\sqrt{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{Y}=(Y-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{p}^{T})(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}.

Then
\widetilde{U}
is an orthogonal matrix.

Proof.

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{p}^{T}Y^{T}Y\mathbf{p}}{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}=\frac{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}=1.

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}^{T}\widetilde{Y}

\displaystyle=(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}(Y^{T}-\mathbf{p}\mathbf{x}^{T})(Y-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{p}^{T})(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=I_{n-1}.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\widetilde{Y}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathbf{x}^{T}+\mathbf{p}^{T}Y^{T})(Y-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{p}^{T})(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-\mathbf{p}^{T}+\mathbf{p}^{T})(I_{n-1}+\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T})^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1+\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore

\widetilde{U}^{T}\widetilde{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\widetilde{Y}\\
\widetilde{Y}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}&\widetilde{Y}^{T}\widetilde{Y}\end{pmatrix}=I_{n}.\qed

6.2.2.
Rotating

Notation 6.17
.

.

Since

\displaystyle{|e_{11}|,\|e_{21}\|,\|E_{22}\|\leq 1}

Proposition 6.19

(6.20)

((d+e_{11})I_{n-1}-(L+E_{22}))\mathbf{p}=e_{21}-\mathbf{p}e_{21}^{T}\mathbf{p},

Proof.

(

is an orthogonal matrix,

∎

6.2.3.
Finding small

Notation 6.21

We define

Proposition 6.22

If

(6.23)

Proof.

is symmetrix. Recall that
\|E_{22}\|\leq 1
. By Rayleigh’s principle and Observation
6.18
,

\displaystyle\min\spec(M)

\displaystyle=\min_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\mathbf{x}^{T}((d+e_{11})I_{n-1}-L-E_{22})\mathbf{x}

Proposition 6.24
.

We write (

(6.25)

Proof.

We want to find a solution with small norm to the non-linear equation (

We define a sequence of vectors

\mathbf{p}_{0}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{p}_{i}=M^{-1}(e_{21}-\mathbf{p}_{i-1}e_{21}^{T}\mathbf{p}_{i-1}),\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 1.

\xi_{0}=0,\quad\xi_{i}=\frac{\eta(1+\xi_{i-1}^{2})}{\theta},\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 1.

\phi(\xi)=\frac{\eta(1+\xi^{2})}{\theta}.

. For any

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{i-1}\|

\displaystyle=|M^{-1}(\mathbf{p}_{i-1}e_{21}^{T}\mathbf{p}_{i-1}-\mathbf{p}_{i-2}e_{21}^{T}\mathbf{p}_{i-2})
