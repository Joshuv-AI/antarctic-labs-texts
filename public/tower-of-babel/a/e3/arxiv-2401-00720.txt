Abstract
. In this paper, we obtain an improved upper bound involving the systole and area for the volume entropy of a Riemannian surface. As a result, we show that every orientable and closed Riemannian surface of genus g ≥ 18 g\geq 18 satisfies Loewner’s systolic ratio inequality. We also show that every closed orientable and nonpositively curved Riemannnian surface of genus g ≥ 11 g\geq 11 satisfies Loewner’s systolic ratio inequality. Keywords: Systole; Loewner’s inequality; entropy. MSC2020: 53C23, 37C35.



1.
Introduction

Unless otherwise stated, all surfaces we consider in this paper are assumed to be orientable and closed, endowed with Riemannian metrics.

Let
M
be a surface of genus
g\geq 1
. Denote the
systole
of
M
by
\operatorname{sys}(M)
. By definition,
\operatorname{sys}(M)
is the length of shortest non-contractible closed geodesics on
M
. Denote the area of
M
by

\operatorname{Area}(M)
. We say that the surface
M
is
Loewner
if the systolic ratio satisfies the inequality

(1)

\frac{\operatorname{sys}^{2}(M)}{\operatorname{Area}(M)}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}.

Around 1949,
Loewner (ref.
[
11
]
) first proved that every surface of genus
1
satisfies the inequality (
1
).
Gromov
[
4
]
showed that, for any surface of genus
g
,

\frac{\operatorname{sys}^{2}(M)}{\operatorname{Area}(M)}<\frac{64}{4\sqrt{g}+27}.

Thus all surfaces of genus
g\geq 50
is Loewner.
For details on history of Loewner’s inequality and related developments in the systolic geometry,
we refer to the book of Katz
[
9
]
.
See also Guth
[
5
]
.

Problem 1.1
.

Whether or not all surfaces of genus at least
1

are Loewner?

For surfaces of genus
2
and genus
g\geq 20
, the answer is affirmative, shown by Katz and Sabourau
[
7
,
8
]
.

This aim of this short note is to prove

Theorem 1.2
.

Every closed Riemannian surface of genus
g\geq 18
is Loewner.

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
uses the argument in
[
7
]
. We improve the upper bound for the volume entropy, which gives a better bound on the genus when the surface is not Loewner.

For
3\leq g\leq 17
, the problem remains unsolved. In Proposition
2.2
, we show that all surfaces of
of genus at least
17
satisfying

(2)

\operatorname{inj}(M)=\operatorname{sys}(M)/2

are Loewner. The hypothesis (
2
) is true for nonpositively curved surfaces, see

4.2
.
We can do even better for nonpositively curved surfaces using the method of Gromov and Bishop-Günther volume comparison theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Every closed nonpositively curved Riemannnian surface of genus

is Loewner.

2.
An upper bound for the entropy

2.1.
Entropy

Let
M
be a surface of genus
g
.
Let
\widetilde{M}
be the universal cover of
M
.
The
volume entropy
of
M
is defined by

h(M)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}\frac{\log\operatorname{Area}(B(\tilde{x}_{0},R))}{R}.

The limit exists and does not depend on the choice of
\tilde{x}_{0}\in\widetilde{M}
(see
[
10
]
).

By a classical result of Katok
[
6
]
, we have

h(M)^{2}\geq\frac{2\pi(2g-2)}{\operatorname{Area}(M)}.

The following equivalent definition of
h(M)
is very useful.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
N(T)
be the number of homotopy classes of non-contractible closed curves on
M
, based at some fixed point
x_{0}\in M
and which can be represented by loops of length at most
T
. Then

h(M)=\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\log N(T)}{T}.

See
[
7
, Lemma 3.6]
for the proof.

Proof of Theorem

Let
(M,g)
be a surface of genus

.

\operatorname{Area}(B(x,R))\geq\frac{1}{2}(2R-\rho(x))^{2}.

The function

For an arbitrary

\operatorname{sys}(\overline{M})=\operatorname{sys}(M).

This is proved by Gromov

. Note that

Assume

\frac{\operatorname{sys}(M)^{2}}{\operatorname{Area}(M)}>\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}.

Proof of Theorem

Assume that
M
is nonpositively curved. By the Bishop-Günther volume comparison theorem (ref
[
1
, Theorem III.4.2]
), we have for all

and

\operatorname{Area}(B(q,R))\geq\pi R^{2}.

Note that

is the area of the disk of radius

in the Euclidean plane.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
M
be a compact Riemannian manifolds. Then
the injectivity radius of
M
equals the minimium of the
conjugate radius and half the length of a shortest nontrivial closed geodesic on

M
.

Proof.

This is a corollary of Cheeger-Ebin
[
2
, Lemma 5.6]
. We give a proof for the sake of completeness.

For
p\in M
,
denote the injectivity radius and conjugate radius
at
p
by
\operatorname{inj}_{p}(M)
and
\operatorname{conj}_{p}(M)
,
respectively.
And denote the shortest length of closed nontrivial geodesic on

M
by
\ell(M)
. It is obvious that
\operatorname{inj}_{p}(M)\leq\operatorname{conj}_{p}(M)
and thus
\operatorname{inj}(M)\leq\operatorname{conj}(M).

Suppose
\operatorname{inj}(M)<\operatorname{conj}(M)
. Assume that at some
p\in M
,
\operatorname{inj}_{p}(M)=\operatorname{inj}(M)
.
By Cheeger-Ebin
[
2
, Lemma 5.6]
, there is a geodesic loop starting and ending at
p
, with length
2\operatorname{inj}_{p}(M)=2\operatorname{inj}(M)
. Let
q\in\gamma
that separates
\gamma
into two segments with the same length
\operatorname{inj}(M)
. Note that
p
and
q
are not conjugate since
\operatorname{inj}_{p}(M)<\operatorname{conj}(M)
. The existence of two geodesic segments between
p
and
q
implies that
\operatorname{inj}_{q}(M)=\operatorname{inj}(M)
. If we look at
\gamma
as a loop starting and ending at

q
, then
\gamma
is also smooth at
p
by using Cheeger-Ebin
[
2
, Lemma 5.6]
again. Thus
\gamma
is a smooth nontrivial closed geodesic.
This shows that
\operatorname{inj}(M)\geq\frac{\ell(M)}{2}
.

Therefore,

\operatorname{inj}(M)=\min\{\operatorname{conj}(M),\ell(M)/2\}.

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Under the assumption that
M
is nonpositively curved, we have

(5)

\operatorname{inj}(M)=\frac{\operatorname{sys}(M)}{2}.

Proof.

Since
M
is nonpositively curved, there is no conjugate points on
M
.
Moreover, any (nontrivial) closed geodesic
\gamma
on
M
is not contractible. Otherwise,

\gamma
lifts to a closed geodesic on the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
. This contradicts the fact that on a Cartan-Hadamard manifold, any two points are connected by a unique geodesic.

Now (
5
) is an immediate corollary of Lemma
4.1
.

∎

Note that for a nonpositively curved surface
M
, we already know that the area of
M
is at least
\pi\operatorname{inj}(M)^{2}=\frac{\pi}{4}\operatorname{sys}(M)^{2}.
Thus
\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)}{\operatorname{sys}(M)^{2}}\geq\frac{\pi}{4},
while
\frac{\pi}{4}
is strictly less than
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
. One cannot conclude the Loewner property directly from the nonpositively curved condition. This is different from the case of hyperbolic metric, where we have a better lower bound of the area of
M
to ensure it is Loewner.

.

Proof.

We will use the argument of Gromov

[
4

.

Fix an arbitrary point

. Choose some

p_{0}\in M\setminus B(x,\operatorname{sys}(M)/2)
and choose
\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{2g}
in
M\setminus B(x,\operatorname{sys}(M)/2)
as a canonical basis of of
\pi_{1}(M,p_{0})
. Denote

\Gamma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{2g}\gamma_{i}.

We consider
\Gamma
as a connected graph.
The Betti number of a connected graph is given by

1+E-V
where
E
is the number of edges and
V
is the number of vertices. Thus the Betti number of
\Gamma
,
denoted by
b_{1}(\Gamma)
, is equal to
2g
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a maximal set of centers on
\Gamma
of disjoint disks of radius
\operatorname{sys}(M)/8
such that

\{B(q,\operatorname{sys}(M)/4)\ |\ q\in\mathcal{S}\}

forms a cover of
\Gamma
. We assume that
p_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
.

We first construct a covering of each
\gamma_{i}
as following. Denote
\gamma=\gamma_{i}
for simplicity.

Start with
p_{0}=\gamma(0)
. Set
q_{0}=p_{0}
.
Let
t_{1}
be the smallest positive number such that
d(\gamma(t_{1}),p_{0}=q_{0})<\operatorname{sys}(M)/4
does not hold. Define
p_{1}=\gamma(t_{1})
and thus
d(p_{1},q_{0})=\operatorname{sys}(M)/4
. Denote by

\overline{p_{0}p_{1}}
the geodesic connecting
p_{0}

to
p_{1}
and so on. Since

\gamma([0,t_{1}])\cup\overline{p_{0}p_{1}}
is contained in

B(p_{0},\operatorname{sys}(M)/4)
,
the path
\gamma([0,t_{1}])
is homotopic to
\overline{p_{0}p_{1}}
.

Choose
q_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
d(p_{1},q_{1})<\operatorname{sys}(M)/4.
Let
t_{2}
be the smallest positive number such that
d(\gamma(t_{2}),q_{1})<\operatorname{sys}(M)/4
does not hold. Define
p_{2}=\gamma(t_{2})
and thus
d(p_{2},q_{1})=\operatorname{sys}(M)/4
. Then the path
\gamma([t_{1},t_{2}])
is homotopic to
\overline{p_{1}p_{2}}.

Repeat this process, we obtain a sequence of points
p_{0}=\gamma(0),\cdots,p_{r}=\gamma(t_{r}),p_{r+1}=p_{0}=\gamma(l)
on
\gamma
and
q_{0},q_{1},\cdots,q_{r},q_{r+1}=q_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
satisfying:

d(p_{i},q_{i})<\operatorname{sys}(M)/4,\quad d(p_{i+1},q_{i})=\operatorname{sys}(M)/4.

And for
t\in[t_{r},l]
,
d(\gamma(t),p_{r})<\operatorname{sys}(M)/4.
So the path
\gamma([t_{r},l])
is homotopic to
\overline{p_{r}p_{0}}.

Then the triangle formed by
p_{k},q_{k},p_{k+1}
has length less than
2(\operatorname{sys}(M)/4+\operatorname{sys}(M)/4)=\operatorname{sys}(M)
and thus is null-homotopic. Also, the triangle formed by
p_{k+1},q_{k},q_{k+1}
has length less than
2(\operatorname{sys}(M)/4+\operatorname{sys}(M)/4)=\operatorname{sys}(M)
and thus is null-homotopic. Together, we obtain that the circle
\overline{p_{k}q_{k}}\cup\overline{q_{k}q_{k+1}}\cup\overline{p_{k+1}q_{k+1}}\cup\overline{p_{k}p_{k+1}}

is homotopically trivial. Thus the curve
\gamma
can be homotopic to a piecewise geodesic connected by points

q_{0},q_{1},\cdots,q_{r},q_{r+1}=q_{0}
.

For each
\gamma_{i}
, we obtain that it is homotopic to a piecewise geodesic connected by points
p_{0}^{(i)},p_{1}^{(i)},\cdots,p_{r+1}^{(i)}=p_{0}^{(i)}
lying in
\gamma_{i}
and also a piecewise geodesic connected by points
q_{0}^{(i)},q_{1}^{(i)},\cdots,q_{r+1}^{(i)}=q_{0}^{(i)}
lying in
\mathcal{S}.
Note that
p_{0}^{(i)}=q_{0}^{(i)}=q_{0}.

Therefore, we obtain an abstract connected graph
\Gamma^{\prime}
with vertices
q_{k}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}
appeared above,
q,q^{\prime}
has an edge if and only if they appear as
q=q_{k}^{(i)},q^{\prime}=q_{k+1}^{(i)}
for some
i
. There is a (not necessarily injective) map from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma^{\prime}
, taking
p_{k}^{(i)}
to
q_{k}^{(i)}
and the edge
\overline{p_{k}^{(i)}p_{k+1}^{(i)}}
to
\overline{q_{k}^{(i)}q_{k+1}^{(i)}}.
Denote the image of
\gamma_{i}
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
by
\sigma_{i}.
So
\sigma_{i},i=1,\cdots,2g,
are different nontrivial loops based at
q_{0}
.

Moreover, there is a natural map
\iota:\Gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow M
by the natural map taking the edge between
q_{k},q_{k+1}
to a fixed geodesic between
q_{k},q_{k+1}
.
Note that by our construction, for
i=1,\cdots,2g
,
\gamma_{i}
is homotopic to
\iota(\sigma_{i}).

Suppose there is a loop
\sigma_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{l}}^{n_{l}}
is homotopically trivial in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. It is clear that
\iota(\sigma_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{l}}^{n_{l}})\in\pi_{1}(M)
is also homotopically trivial. Since
\gamma_{i}
is homotopic to
\iota(\sigma_{i})
, we obtain
\gamma_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{i_{l}}^{n_{l}}
is also homotopically trivial.

Therefore,
\pi_{1}(\Gamma^{\prime})
has at least
2g
generators and thus the Betti number of
\Gamma^{\prime}
,
b_{1}(\Gamma^{\prime})
, is not less than
2g
.

Let
N
denote the number of vertices in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. So
N\leq|\mathcal{S}|
. Denote the number of edges in
\Gamma^{\prime}
by
K
. Then
K\leq\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
. So

\displaystyle 2g\leq b_{1}(\Gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N(N-1)}{2}-N+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(|\mathcal{S}|-1)(|\mathcal{S}|-2)}{2}.

Note that

\operatorname{Area}(B(p,\operatorname{sys}(M)/8))\geq\pi\left(\operatorname{sys}(M)/8\right)^{2},\quad\operatorname{Area}(B(p,3\operatorname{sys}(M)/8))\geq\pi\left(3\operatorname{sys}(M)/8\right)^{2}.

Since the disjoint balls
B(q,\operatorname{sys}(M)/8)(q\in\mathcal{S})
lie in
M\setminus B(x,3\operatorname{sys}(M)/8)
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)-\operatorname{Area}(B(x,\frac{3}{8}\operatorname{sys}(M)))}{\pi(\operatorname{sys}(M)/8)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)-\pi(\frac{3}{8}\operatorname{sys}(M))^{2}}{\pi(\operatorname{sys}(M)/8)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{64}{\pi}\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)}{\operatorname{sys}(M)^{2}}-9.

Assume that
M
is not Loewner, then
\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)}{\operatorname{sys}(M)^{2}}<\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.

The above inquality of
|\mathcal{S}|

implies that
|\mathcal{S}|\leq\frac{32\sqrt{3}}{\pi}-9=8.64252
and thus
|\mathcal{S}|\leq 8
.

Using
2g\leq(|\mathcal{S}|-1)(|\mathcal{S}|-2)/2
, we obtain

g\leq\frac{(|\mathcal{S}|-1)(|\mathcal{S}|-2)}{4}=10.25.

Therefore, if
M
is not Loewner, then
g\leq 10.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Gromov
[
4
, Proposition 5.3.A]
proved
the same inequality

2g\leq\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{2},

where the graph
\Gamma
is given by a short basis of the homology group. Then he obtain a lower bound for

\frac{\operatorname{Area}(M)}{\operatorname{sys}^{\prime}(M)^{2}}
, where
\operatorname{sys}^{\prime}(M)
denotes the homological systole.
