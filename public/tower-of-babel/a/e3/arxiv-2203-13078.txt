Abstract
. This paper explores the complexity associated with solving the inverse Sturm-Liouville problem with Robin boundary conditions: given a sequence of eigenvalues and a sequence of norming constants, how many limits does a universal algorithm require to return the potential and boundary conditions? It is shown that if all but finitely many of the eigenvalues and norming constants coincide with those for the zero potential then the number of limits is zero, i.e. it is possible to retrieve the potential and boundary conditions precisely in finitely many steps. Otherwise, it is shown that this problem requires a single limit; moreover, if one has a priori control over how much the eigenvalues and norming constants differ from those of the zero-potential problem, and one knows that the average of the potential is zero, then the computation can be performed with complete error control. This is done in the spirit of the Solvability Complexity Index. All algorithms are provided explicitly along with numerical examples.

1.1.
Classical Sturm-Liouville inverse problem

The history of the one-dimensional inverse spectral problem for the Sturm-Liouville equation in Liouville normal form goes back to the work
of Ambarzumjan
[
1
]

can give the eigenvalues
\{n^{2}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

for Neumann boundary conditions. Borg
[

obtained the first general results, for recovery of the potential

from
two spectra (belonging to different boundary conditions), subject to a technical restriction removed by Levinson

[
23
]
. Marchenko’s 1950 paper
[
28
]

of the potential
q(x)
by the so-called spectral function
\rho(\lambda)
and allowed treatment of problems on a semi-axis, marked the
start of a period of intense research in the Soviet school, culiminating in the Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko theory and associated
integral equation; overviews of this work may be found in the monographs of Levitan
[
24
]
, Marchenko
[
26
]
, and Freiling and Yurko
[
19
]
. We also mention the classical text by Pöschel and Trubowitz
[
31
]
.

Compared to research on numerical algorithms for forward problems, numerical work on inverse problems was sparse. Despite
the local stability results of Ryabushko
[
34
]
and McLaughlin
[
29
]
, the inverse problem is well known
to be ill-conditioned, a fact which is reflected in the rather weak norm in which Marletta and Weikard
[
27
]

estimate errors in

arising from errors in finite spectral data. However by the early 1990s, computing power had reached a
level which allowed Andersson
[
2
]
and Rundell and Sacks
[
33
]
to propose algorithms which could run
on desktop machines. The results in
[
33
]
show clearly that smoother potentials are recovered more accurately,
something which is explained precisely by the results of Savchuk and Shkalikov
[
35
]
. Nevertheless, the inverse
problem appears to be intrinsically more computationally demanding, especially if one uses an approach which requires the
solution of many ‘trial’ forward problems in order to approximate the solution of the inverse problem. One might
easily be led by this reasoning to suspect that whatever the complexity of the forward problem (as defined below in Definition
1.12
), the complexity of the inverse problem
should be greater by at least
+1
. Our main result, Theorem
1.1
, shows that this is not true.

Part of the key to obtaining this unexpectedly optimistic result for the inverse problem with full spectral data is the availability
of an algorithm which, for
finite spectral data
, recovers a potential

fitting the finite data by solving a finite system of linear
algebraic equations whose coefficients and right hand side are expressed explicitly in terms of elementary functions of the independent
variable

values for the free problem

1.6
)
below) for the Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko equation (

) by truncating the infinite sum which defines it. In essence, this approach was proposed by McLaughlin and Handelman

[
30
]
as a method for creating, from a known Schrödinger equation, a new equation with finitely many different
eigenvalues and norming constants, although no numerical results were presented there.

The proof of Theorem

data algorithm to demonstrate that none of its finitely many steps requires any limiting procedures. Part (i) depends on showing that
the finite-data potentials converge, in suitable topologies, as the data set expands to a full data set. Part (ii) is the most

The Sturm-Liouville problem (

We now follow

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F(x,y)&=\alpha_{0}^{-1}\cos(\lambda_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}x)\cos(\lambda_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}y)-\pi^{-1}\\

\displaystyle q(x)=2\frac{d}{dx}K(x,x).

Moreover, one has

\displaystyle\phi(x,\lambda)=\cos(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}x)+\int_{0}^{x}K(x,t)\cos(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}t)\,dt

and the boundary conditions can be reconstructed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} h&=K(0,0)=-F(0,0),\\

where the expression for

1.2.
The Solvability Complexity Index Hierarchy

The Solvability Complexity Index (SCI) Hierarchy addresses questions which are at the nexus of pure and applied mathematics, as well as computer science. Specifically, it provides a classification of the complexity of problems that can only be computed as the limit of a sequence of approximations. This classification considers how many independent limits are required to solve a problem (for instance, computing the spectrum of elements in
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}))
requires three independent limits) and whether one can control the approximation errors.
These broad topics are addressed in the sequence of papers
[
20
,
5
,
6
]
.
Research related to this theory has gathered pace in recent years. We point out
[
17
,
13
,
16
]
where some of the theory of spectral computations has been further developed;
[
32
]
where this has been applied to certain classes of unbounded operators;
[
4
,
15
]
where solutions of PDEs were considered;
[
9
]
where we considered periodic spectral problems;
[
8
,
7
]
where we considered resonance problems; and
[
18
,

]
where the authors give further examples of how to perform certain spectral computations with error bounds. Let us summarize the main definitions of the SCI theory.

Definition 1.5

(Computational problem)
.

A
computational problem
is a quadruple
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
, where

(i)

\Omega
is a set, called the
primary set
,

(ii)

\Lambda
is a set of complex-valued functions on
\Omega

,

(iii)

\mathcal{M}
is a metric space,

(iv)

\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is a map, called the

.

Definition 1.6

(General algorithm)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
be a computational problem. A

is a mapping
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
such that for each

(i)

there exists a finite (non-empty) subset

(ii)

(iii)

for every

Definition 1.7

(Tower of general algorithms)
.

Let

be a computational problem. A

Definition 1.9

(Tower of arithmetic algorithms)

Given a computational problem
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})

for
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.15
(
iii
): finite spectral data

In this section we prove Theorem
1.15
(
iii
) dealing with the case of finite spectral data. Following immediately from the finite sum expression (
1.10
) for
F
, for any
x,y\in[0,\pi]
the values
F(x,y)
can be computed using a finite number of arithmetic operations.
We refer the reader to Remark
1.16
regarding the evaluation of trigonometric functions.

The first step of the proof is to compute
K(x,y)
by solving the integral equation (
1.7
). To this end, we consider
x
as a fixed parameter and solve (
1.7
) as an equation in
y
. Let us introduce the following notation.

Notation 2.1
.

Define

•

Right-hand side:
f_{x}(y):=-F(x,y)
,

•

Solution:
u_{x}(y):=K(x,y)
,

•

Integral kernel:
k(t,s):=-F(s,t)
.

This transforms (
1.7
) into the more familiar-looking form

(2.1)

\displaystyle u_{x}(y)-\int_{0}^{x}k(y,s)u_{x}(s)\,ds=f_{x}(y).

This is a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind whose kernel is of the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle k(t,s)=\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}A_{i}(t)B_{i}(s)

(cf. (
1.10
)). A concrete choice of
A_{i},B_{i}
that satisfy (
2.2
) is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l@{}}A_{i}(s)=-B_{i}(s)=\alpha_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cos(\lambda_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}s)&\text{ for }i=0,\dots,N,\\
A_{i}(s)=B_{i}(s)=\big(\frac{2}{\pi}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cos((i-N)s)&\text{ for }i=N+1,\dots,2N,\\
A_{2N+1}(s)=B_{2N+1}(s)\equiv\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}.&\end{array}\right.

As detailed in
[
3
, Ch. 2]
, making the ansatz
u_{x}(y)=f_{x}(y)+\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}c_{i}A_{i}(y)
and plugging it into (
2.1
) yields the finite linear system

(2.6)

\displaystyle c_{i}-\sum_{j=0}^{2N+1}\langle A_{j},B_{i}\rangle c_{j}=\langle f_{x},B_{i}\rangle,

where

\langle A_{j},B_{i}\rangle=\int_{0}^{x}A_{j}(s)B_{i}(s)\,ds\quad\text{and}\quad\langle f_{x},B_{i}\rangle=\int_{0}^{x}f_{x}(s)B_{i}(s)\,ds.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that both integrals can be calculated
analytically
using (
2
) and elementary rules for integration. The result of these integrations will always be a polynomial of degree 2 in
x
,
\cos(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\cos(jx)
,
\sin(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\sin(jx)
for
j=0,\dots,N
, whose coefficients can be computed from the
\lambda_{n}
,
\alpha_{n}
.

Next, we apply two classical results to show that (
2.6
) is uniquely solveble. To simplify notation we denote the
(2N+2)\times(2N+2)
matrix with entries
\langle A_{j},B_{i}\rangle
by
\mathfrak{A}
, the vector with entries
\langle f_{x},B_{i}\rangle
by
\mathfrak{b}
and the identity matrix by
I
. The linear system (
2.6
) becomes

(I-\mathfrak{A})c=\mathfrak{b}.

We note that a similar approach to inverse problems has been used in
[
30
]
, however not in the context of the SCI Hierarchy.
To avoid confusion in the sequel, we introduce the integral operator
\kappa_{x}:L^{2}([0,x])\to L^{2}([0,x])
defined as

(2.7)

(\kappa_{x}u)(t):=\int_{0}^{x}k(t,s)u(s)\,ds.

Lemma 2.3
.

The matrix
I-\mathfrak{A}
is invertible, and hence the system (
2.6
) has a unique solution for every
x\in[0,\pi]
.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Th. 2.1.2]
the system (
2.6
) is nonsingular if the operator
\mathrm{Id}-\kappa_{x}:L^{2}([0,x])\to L^{2}([0,x])
is invertible, and by
[
24
, Th. 2.3.1]
this operator is indeed invertible for all
x\in[0,\pi]
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The solutions
(c_{0},\dots,c_{2N+1})
of (
2.6
) are rational functions of degree
4N+6
in
x
,
\cos(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\cos(jx)
,
\sin(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\sin(jx)
for
j=0,\dots,N
. They can be computed symbolically in finitely many arithmetic operations from
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}\cup\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
the system (
2.6
) is solvable and its solution is given by
(I-\mathfrak{A})^{-1}\mathfrak{b}
. By Remark
2.2
the entries of
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{b}
can be calculated explicitly from
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}\cup\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
as polynomials of degree 2 in
x,\cos(\dots),\sin(\dots)
. But the entries of
(I-\mathfrak{A})^{-1}
can be calculated in finitely many arithmetic operations from the entries of
\mathfrak{A}
by the formula

(2.8)

\displaystyle M^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(M)}\operatorname{adj}(M),

where
\operatorname{adj}(M)=(-1)^{i+j}[M]_{ij}
and
[M]_{ij}
denotes the
ij
-th minor of
M
. The result is a rational function in
x,\cos(\dots),\sin(\dots)
of degree
4N+4
.
Finally, the product
(I-\mathfrak{A})^{-1}\mathfrak{b}
is computed in finitely many operations on the entries of
(I-\mathfrak{A})^{-1}
and
\mathfrak{b}
and yields a rational function of degree
4N+6
.
Hence, each
c_{i}
has a representation as a rational function in
x
,
\cos(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\cos(jx)
,
\sin(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\sin(jx)
for
j=0,\dots,N
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the denominator in the rational function expression for
c_{j}
is never zero. Indeed, by (
2.8
) the denominator is given by
\det(I-\mathfrak{A})
, which is guaranteed to be nonzero for all
x\in[0,\pi]
by Lemma
2.3
.

To emphasize the dependence of the
c_{j}
on
x
, we will sometimes write
c_{j}=c_{j}(x)
in the following. Having obtained a computable solution
(c_{0},\dots,c_{2N+1})
of (
2.6
), we recall our ansatz for
u_{x}
:

(2.9)

\displaystyle u_{x}(y)=f_{x}(y)+\sum_{i=0}^{2N+1}c_{i}(x)A_{i}(y).

By construction
u_{x}
satisfies (
2.1
) for every
x\in[0,\pi]
. By Lemma
2.4
and (
2
) the right hand side of (
2.9
) is given symbolically as a rational function in
x
,
\cos(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\cos(jx)
,
\sin(\lambda_{j}x)
,
\sin(jx)
for
j=0,\dots,N
, and likewise for
y
. In particular, the derivative

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle:=2\frac{d}{dx}u_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=2\frac{d}{dx}K(x,x)

is a rational function again and can be computed symbolically as a function of
x
. Moreover, once
K(x,y)=u_{x}(y)
has been computed, the boundary conditions
h,H
can be reconstructed using (
1.9
). Indeed,
h
is given by

\displaystyle h=K(0,0),

and we claim that
H
is given by

\displaystyle H=-K(\pi,\pi).

To compute
H
, recall that for
n>N
one has

\displaystyle H=-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\pi,n^{2})}{\phi(\pi,n^{2})}.

These can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(\pi,n^{2})

\displaystyle=\cos(n\pi)+\int_{0}^{\pi}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}+\int_{0}^{\pi}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt,

and

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(\pi,n^{2})

\displaystyle=-n\sin(n\pi)+K(\pi,\pi)\cos(n\pi)+\int_{0}^{\pi}\partial_{x}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}K(\pi,\pi)+\int_{0}^{\pi}\partial_{x}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt.

Since the quotient
\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\pi,n^{2})}{\phi(\pi,n^{2})}
is independent of
n
by
[
24
, Th. 2.10.5]
, one has

\displaystyle H=-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\pi,n^{2})}{\phi(\pi,n^{2})}=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\pi,n^{2})}{\phi(\pi,n^{2})}.

Moreover, by the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma one has

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\partial_{x}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

We therefore conclude that

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}K(\pi,\pi)+\int_{0}^{\pi}\partial_{x}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt}{(-1)^{n}+\int_{0}^{\pi}K(\pi,t)\cos(nt)\,dt}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=-K(\pi,\pi).

Thus,
H
can be computed in finitely many arithmeric operations and the proof is complete.
∎

3.1.

Lemma 3.3

Lemma 3.4
.

The operator

is bounded from

Proof.

Fix

Using Hölder’s inequality again, this implies

\displaystyle\|u_{x}\|_{L^{p}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{x}\|_{L^{p}([0,x])}+\pi^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{p}}\|k\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}

.

For

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\left\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\right\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}<+\infty.

Follows from continuity of the map

Lemma 3.6
.

For
p\in[2,+\infty)
one has

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\left\|(I-\kappa_{x,N})^{-1}\right\|_{L^{p}([0,x])\to L^{p}([0,x])}<+\infty.

Proof.

For brevity we denote
\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}([0,x])\to L^{p}([0,x])}
. By Lemma
3.1
one has the bound

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|(I-\kappa_{x,N})^{-1}\|\leq\frac{\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|}{1-\|\kappa_{x}-\kappa_{x,N}\|\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|}

\displaystyle\|\kappa_{x}u-\kappa_{x,N}u\|_{L^{p}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\left|\int_{0}^{x}(k(t,s)-k_{N}(t,s))u(s)\,ds\right|^{p}\,dt

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\int_{0}^{x}\|k(t,\cdot)-k_{N}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}^{p}\,dt

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\|\kappa_{x}-\kappa_{x,N}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leq\|k-k_{N}\|_{L^{p}([0,\pi];L^{p^{\prime}}([0,\pi]))}

After this preparation, we are ready to prove convergence of

Proposition 3.7
.

For all

and for all

Let

,

\displaystyle=f_{x,N}-f_{x}+\int_{0}^{x}\big(k_{N}(\cdot,s)u_{x,N}(s)-k(\cdot,s)u_{x}(s)\big)\,ds

\displaystyle=f_{x,N}-f_{x}+\int_{0}^{x}(k_{N}(\cdot,s)-k(\cdot,s))u_{x,N}(s)+k(\cdot,s)(u_{x,N}(s)-u_{x}(s))\,ds

\displaystyle(I-\kappa_{x})(u_{x,N}-u_{x})=f_{x,N}-f_{x}+\int_{0}^{x}(k_{N}(\cdot,s)-k(\cdot,s))u_{x,N}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle u_{x,N}(x)-u_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{x,N}(x)-f_{x}(x)+\int_{0}^{x}\big(k_{N}(x,s)u_{x,N}(s)-k(x,s)u_{x}(s)\big)\,ds

for every

\displaystyle\leq|f_{x,N}(x)-f_{x}(x)|

\displaystyle|K_{N}(x,x)|

\displaystyle\leq|F_{N}(x,x)|+\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\|F_{N}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\|(I-\kappa_{x,N})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|F_{N}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{F}\bigg(1+\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{F}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\|(I-\kappa_{x,N})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\bigg).

&\leq 2\|K(\cdot,\cdot)-K_{N}(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}([0,\pi])}\\
&\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0.\end{split}

|\cos(z)|,|\sin(z)|,|\sinh(z)|\leq\cosh(|z|)

t=2\pi
, using
\frac{1}{n+1}<\frac{1}{n}
and using Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle|\Phi(t)-\Phi_{N}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(2\pi M)\left[8\pi\|\kappa\|_{\ell^{\infty}}^{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\|\tilde{\kappa}\|_{\ell^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\pi\Big\|\frac{\kappa}{\alpha}\Big\|_{\ell^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\cosh(2\pi M)\bigg(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[8\pi\|\kappa\|_{\ell^{\infty}}^{2}\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\tilde{\kappa}\|_{\ell^{2}}+2(2+\pi\|\tilde{\kappa}\|_{\ell^{\infty}})\|\kappa\|_{\ell^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(2\pi M)\bigg(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\Big(\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{6}}+2\pi\Big)M+5\right]M

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cosh(2\pi M)\left[\Big(\frac{8\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{6}}+2\pi\Big)M+5\right]M

The proof is concluded by noting that
\|F-F_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}=\|\Phi-\Phi_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}([-\pi,2\pi])}
.
∎

Lemma
4.4
implies explicit bounds on the terms
\|f_{x,N}-f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])}
and
\|k_{N}-k\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}
in (
4.6
).
Next we apply the theory from Section
4.1
. The following lemma shows that Hypothesis
4.1
is satisfied in our situation.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
t\in[0,\pi]
define

(4.10)

f_{n}(t):=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cos(nt)\quad\text{and}\quad g_{n}(t):=\alpha_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cos(\lambda_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}t).

Then the families
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfy Hypothesis
4.1
with
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}([0,\pi])
. In particular:

(i)

For all
J\in\mathbb{N}
one has

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Omega_{J}=\bigg(\sum_{n=J+1}^{\infty}\|g_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{C_{\Omega}}{J},

where
C_{\Omega}=M\pi\cosh(M\pi)\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\big(1+(2\pi)^{2}M^{2}+(2+\pi M)^{2}\big)}
.

(ii)

Let
J\geq M
and let
j\leq J<k
, then

\displaystyle|\langle g_{j},f_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}|\leq\frac{c}{k^{2}-(j+\frac{M}{j+1})^{2}}

where
c=(\frac{\pi}{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{2}{\pi}+M)^{\frac{1}{2}}M(1+M)\cosh(M\pi)
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows immediately from the term-wise inequality

\displaystyle\|g_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}^{2}\leq\frac{\cosh(M\pi)^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\frac{3\pi^{2}}{2}\Big(|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|^{2}+(2\pi)^{2}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}}+(2+\pi M)^{2}|\kappa_{n}|^{2}\Big),

which is proved by a calculation similar to the ones found within the proof of Lemma
4.4
. We give the details below.
For notational convenience, denote
\alpha_{n}^{0}:=\frac{\pi}{2}
for
n>0
and
\alpha_{0}^{0}:=\pi
. By trigonometric identities we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g_{n}(t)-f_{n}(t)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\cos\bigl(\lambda_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}t\bigr)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\cos(nt)\\
&=\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\cos\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\bigg]\cos(nt)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\sin(nt)\sin\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr).\end{aligned}

Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}+\frac{\tilde{\kappa}_{n}}{n+1}}=\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\tilde{\kappa}_{n}}{n+1}}

and hence

(4.13)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}-\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg|\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\tilde{\kappa}_{n}}{n+1}}-1\bigg|\leq\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}=\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}.

Taking absolute values in (
4.12
) and using (
4.13
) we have

\displaystyle|g_{n}(t)-f_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\cos\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\bigg|+\bigg|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\sin(nt)\sin\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\biggr|\biggl|\cos\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)\biggr|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\biggl|\cos\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)-1\biggr|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}}\biggl|\sin\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n+1}t\Bigr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(M|t|)\left\{\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}t^{2}+\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{n}^{0}}}+\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}\bigg]\frac{|\kappa_{n}|}{n+1}|t|\right\},

where in the last line the bounds
|f(z)-f(0)|\leq|z|\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{|z|}(0))}
and
|\cos(z)|,|\sin(z)|,|\sinh(z)|\leq\cosh(|z|)
were used. Setting
t=\pi
and focusing on
n\geq 1
(i.e.
\alpha_{n}^{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}
) we obtain

\displaystyle|g_{n}(t)-f_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(M\pi)\left\{\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}+\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\pi^{2}+\bigg[\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}\bigg]\frac{|\kappa_{n}|}{n+1}\pi\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(M\pi)\left\{\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|}{n+1}+\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\pi^{2}+\bigg[\Big(\frac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}M\bigg]\frac{|\kappa_{n}|}{n+1}\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cosh(M\pi)}{n+1}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|+2\pi\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{2}}{n+1}+[2+\pi M]|\kappa_{n}|\Big)

Squaring both sides and using the inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
finally yields

\displaystyle|g_{n}(t)-f_{n}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\cosh(M\pi)^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\frac{3\pi}{2}\Big(|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|^{2}+(2\pi)^{2}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}}+[2+\pi M]^{2}|\kappa_{n}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\|g_{n}-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\cosh(M\pi)^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}\frac{3\pi^{2}}{2}\Big(|\tilde{\kappa}_{n}|^{2}+(2\pi)^{2}\frac{|\kappa_{n}|^{4}}{(n+1)^{2}}+[2+\pi M]^{2}|\kappa_{n}|^{2}\Big).

Now we prove (ii). For brevity, denote
\rho_{n}:=\lambda_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then we compute

\displaystyle\langle g_{j},f_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(\rho_{j}t)\cos(kt)\,dt

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rho_{j}\sin(\rho_{j}\pi)}{\rho_{j}^{2}-k^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j+k}\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rho_{j}\sin(\frac{\kappa_{j}}{{j+1}}\pi)}{\rho_{j}^{2}-k^{2}}.

Thus

\displaystyle|\langle g_{j},f_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rho_{j}}{|k^{2}-\rho_{j}^{2}|}\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{{j+1}}\Bigl|\cosh\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{j}}{{j+1}}\pi\Bigr)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\rho_{j}}{{j+1}}\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{|k^{2}-\rho_{j}^{2}|}\cosh\Bigl(\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{{j+1}}\pi\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{j+\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{j+1}}{{j+1}}\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{|k^{2}-(j+\frac{\kappa_{j}}{{j+1}})^{2}|}\cosh(|\kappa_{j}|\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha_{j}\bigg)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+M)\frac{|\kappa_{j}|}{|k^{2}-(j+\frac{M}{{j+1}})^{2}|}\cosh(M\pi).

Note that as soon as
J\geq M
one has
J+1>j+\frac{M}{j+1}
for all
j\leq J
. Therefore, since
k\geq J+1
, the denominator in the last term above is always nonzero.
∎

The following proposition now follows immediately from Lemma
4.5
and Proposition
4.3
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be as in (
4.10
). There exists a constant
C_{M}^{(2)}
, which can be computed in finitely many arithmetic operations from the information in
\Lambda
, such that for all
u\in L^{2}([0,\pi])

(4.14)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}^{2}\leq C_{M}^{(2)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\langle u,g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}\big|^{2}.

Proof.

Lemma
4.5
shows that Hypothesis
4.1
is satisfied by
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
\mu=M
and
\eta=c
. Moreover, (
4.11
) shows that whenever
J>\exp\big(\frac{C_{\Omega}}{4c}\big)

\displaystyle\delta_{J}

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{4c\frac{\log(J)}{J},\Omega_{J}\Big\}=4c\frac{\log(J)}{J}.

Computability of
C_{M}^{(2)}
follows from Proposition
4.3
and the fact that the matrix elements
T_{ij}
in (
4.2
) are given by scalar products
\langle g_{i},f_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}
, which can be calculated symbolically for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(recall that the
g_{i}
’s and
f_{j}
’s are all cosines).
∎

Next we prove a computable bound on the operator norm of
(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
C_{M}^{(2)}
be defined as in Lemma
4.6
. Then for all
x\in[0,\pi]
one has

(i)

\displaystyle\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])\to L^{2}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(2)},

(ii)

\displaystyle\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])\to L^{\infty}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi\|k\|_{\infty}C_{M}^{(2)}.

Proof.

We first prove (i). To this end, let
f_{x}\in L^{2}([0,x])
and let
u_{x}=(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}f_{x}
be the solution to the integral equation (
2.1
), which we rewrite here for convenience:

(4.15)

\displaystyle u_{x}-\int_{0}^{x}k(\cdot,s)u_{x}(s)\,ds=f_{x}.

Testing this equation with
u_{x}
and using Parseval’s identity (as detailed in the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 1.5.7]
) gives

(4.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\langle u_{x},g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,x])}\big|^{2}=\langle f_{x},u_{x}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,x])},

where
g_{n}(t):=\alpha_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cos\big(\lambda_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}t\big)
.
Combining (
4.16
) and (
4.14
) we define
u_{x}^{0}(y):=u_{x}(y)
for
y\leq x
and
u_{x}^{0}(y):=0
for
y>x
and compute

\displaystyle\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}^{2}=\|u_{x}^{0}\|_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(2)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\langle u_{x}^{0},g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,\pi])}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=C_{M}^{(2)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\langle u_{x},g_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,x])}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=C_{M}^{(2)}\langle f_{x},u_{x}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(2)}\|f_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])},

which implies

\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}\leq C_{M}^{(2)}\|f_{x}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}.

To prove (ii), we use the regularizing properties of
\kappa_{x}
, namely if
f_{x}\in L^{\infty}([0,x])
, then by (
4.15
) and Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle|u_{x}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{x}(y)|+\left|\int_{0}^{x}k(y,s)u_{x}(s)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\|k\|_{\infty}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\|k\|_{\infty}C_{M}^{(2)}\|f_{x}\|_{L^{2}}

which implies

\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi\|k\|_{\infty}C_{M}^{(2)}\right)\|f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We can now finalize the proof of Theorem
1.15
(
ii
). Recall that our starting point was eq. (
4.6
),

\displaystyle\|u_{x,N}-u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Big(\|f_{x,N}-f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])}+\pi\|k_{N}-k\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}\|u_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}\Big),

where we wanted to bound each term. First, we note that from (
4.9
) follows the uniform bound
\|k\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{M}^{(1)}
. Combining this bound with Lemmas
4.4
and
4.7
we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(2)},

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)},

(4.19)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,\pi]}\|f_{x,N}-f_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(1)}N^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(4.20)

\displaystyle\|k_{N}-k\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}^{(1)}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Moreover, using Lemma
3.1
and (
4.9
) again, we have

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|u_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}&\leq\|(I-\kappa_{x,N})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])\to L^{2}([0,x])}\|f_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}}{1-\|\kappa_{x}-\kappa_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}}\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)},\end{aligned}

where the last line holds if
\|\kappa_{x}-\kappa_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}<\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}^{-1}
. But this last condition can be ensured by choosing
N
large enough: by (
4.18
)-(
4.20
) the choice

N>\pi(C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)})^{2}
is sufficient, where we remind that this constant is explicitly computable. For the sake of definiteness, let us assume from now on that
N>N_{0}
, where

\displaystyle N_{0}:=2\pi\big(C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\big)^{2}.

This choice ensures
\|\kappa_{x}-\kappa_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|(I-\kappa_{x})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}<1/2
.
Inserting the necessary bounds into (
4.21
) we obtain

(4.22)

\displaystyle\|u_{x,N}\|_{L^{2}([0,x])}

\displaystyle<2\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\quad\text{ for }N>N_{0}.

Using (
4.18
)-(
4.20
) and (
4.22
) in (
4.6
) we have that for all
N>N_{0}

\displaystyle\|u_{x,N}-u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,x])}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(C_{M}^{(1)}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}2\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)

\displaystyle=C_{M}^{(1)}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(1+2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big).

Taking the supremum over
x\in[0,\pi]
and reverting back to the classical notation, we have shown

(4.23)

\displaystyle\|K_{N}-K\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\pi]^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(1+2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big).

Now the calculation (
3.6
) implies

(4.24)

\displaystyle\|q_{N}-q\|_{W^{-1,\infty}([0,\pi])}

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(1+2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big),

It remains to prove error bounds for the boundary conditions
h
,
H
. To this end, note that
h=K(0,0)
. Thus, with
h_{N}:=K_{N}(0,0)
, eq. (
4.23
) implies

(4.25)

\displaystyle|h-h_{N}|

\displaystyle\leq N^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(1+2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big).

To compute
H
, recall our assumption
\omega=0
, hence by (
1.4
) we have

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-h-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}q(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-h-K(\pi,\pi).

Hence we may define the computable approximation
H_{N}:=-h_{N}-K_{N}(\pi,\pi)
. Then by (
4.23
) we have

\displaystyle|H-H_{N}|

\displaystyle\leq|h-h_{N}|+|K(\pi,\pi)-K_{N}(\pi,\pi)|

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}\Big(\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}+\pi C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big)\Big(1+2\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}C_{M}^{(1)}C_{M}^{(2)}\Big).

Together, (
4.24
), (
4.25
) and (
4.2
) imply that the algorithm

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}(\lambda,\alpha):=(q_{N},h_{N},H_{N})

achieves explicit error control with convergence rate
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and computable constants
C_{M}^{(1)}
,
C_{M}^{(2)}
. We immediately conclude that
(\Omega_{0,M},\Lambda,\mathcal{M}_{\infty},\Xi_{\infty})\in\Delta_{1}^{A}
. Showing that
(\Omega_{0,M},\Lambda,\mathcal{M}_{p},\Xi_{p})\in\Delta_{1}^{A}
for

follows immediately, using Hölder’s inequality.
∎
