Stochastic Voronoi Tessellations as Models for Cellular Neighborhoods in Simple Multicellular Organisms
Anand Srinivasan, Steph S.M.H. Höhn, Raymond E. Goldstein

Abstract
Recent work on distinct multicellular organisms has revealed a hitherto unknown type of biological noise; rather than a regular arrangement, cellular neighborhood volumes, obtained by Voronoi tessellations of the cell locations, are broadly distributed and consistent with gamma distributions. We propose an explanation for those observations in the case of the alga Volvox , whose somatic cells are embedded in an extracellular matrix (ECM) they export. Both a solvable one-dimensional model of ECM growth derived from bursty transcriptional activity and a two-dimensional “Voronoi liquid” model are shown to provide one-parameter families that smoothly interpolate between the empirically-observed near-maximum-entropy gamma distributions and the crystalline limit of Gaussian distributions governed by the central limit theorem. These results highlight a universal consequence of intrinsic biological noise on the architecture of certain tissues.

A biological interpretation of the Voronoi assumption is that the polygonal boundaries of each
cellular neighborhood are the colliding fronts of isotropically produced ECM
material exported from cells.
Inverting the typical modelling procedure by
assuming
that the Voronoi rule holds
at some temperature
\beta^{-1}

distributional parameters
using standard maximum-likelihood estimators for Gibbs point processes

From the estimated temperature, for example, one can invert the

steps in growth.
Such estimators are critical for elastic models of tissues, where noise in
individual cellular configurations co-exists with stable geometric properties of the population.
The stochastic Voronoi models we have presented here reproduce aspects of configurational noise, such as
the empirically observed gamma distributions, and simultaneously provide a formal framework—an ensemble—within which
to infer features of random finite configurations of cells such as interaction strength and preferential geometry.

As a purely
mathematical construct, a Voronoi tessellation makes no reference to
microstructure around cells, and it thus plays a role for tissues
analogous to the random walk model of
polymers and the hard sphere model of fluids.
Yet,
each

boundaries are composed of
denser material within the larger ECM

. Dimly visible in

motion tracking of cells and growth of compartments during development.
The strong correlation observed

the associated Voronoi partitions
will enable tests of the connection hypothesized here between properties of
stochastic ECM generation at the
single cell level and population-level statistics.

Lemma S2

(Beta-gamma convergence)
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{m}{\alpha}Z_{2,m}\overset{\text{cdf}}{\ \sim\ }\mathrm{P}\left[Z_{2,m}\leq\frac{z\alpha}{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(2,m)}\int_{0}^{\frac{z\alpha}{m}}t(1-t)^{m-1}dt\qquad z\in\left[0,\frac{m}{\alpha}\right],

with the change of variables

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(2,m)}\int_{1-\frac{\alpha z}{m}}^{1}(1-s)s^{m-1}ds

(S15)

\displaystyle=m(m+1)\left[\frac{1}{m}s^{m}-\frac{1}{m+1}s^{m+1}\right]_{1-\frac{z\alpha}{m}}^{1}

\displaystyle=1-\left((m+1)\left(1-\frac{z\alpha}{m}\right)^{m}-m\left(1-\frac{z\alpha}{m}\right)^{m+1}\right)

Lemma S3

(Memoryless characterization of the exponential)
.

The only continuous random variable

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}[X>y]

By Bayes’ theorem, (

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}[X>x+y]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}[X>x]\mathrm{P}[X>y]\ \Leftrightarrow\ 1-F(x+y)=(1-F(x))(1-F(y)).

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{G(z)G(h)-G(z)G(0)}{h}=G(z)G^{\prime}(0).

\displaystyle=\frac{G^{\prime}(w)}{G(w)}=\frac{d}{dw}\log G(w).

Lemma S4

(Maximum-entropy characterization of the exponential)
.

The only nonnegative continuous random variable
X
with density

which maximizes the entropy with fixed mean

Proof.

By hypothesis,
f_{X}
is a critical point of the functional

\displaystyle J[f]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}L(f(x),\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})dx,

(S25)

with Lagrange multipliers

and
\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle L(f(x),\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})

\displaystyle=f(x)\log f(x)+\lambda_{0}f(x)+\lambda_{1}xf(x).

(S26)

Since for all test functions

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle DJ[f],\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial L}{\partial f(x)}\varphi(x)dx,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f(x)}=1+\log f(x)+\lambda_{0}+x\lambda_{1},

hence

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-1-\lambda_{0}-x\lambda_{1})dx=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\exp(-1-\lambda_{0}),

where

Lemma S6

Definition S1

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}[N(E)<\infty]

\displaystyle=1.

Formally,

(see e.g. 5.1

or Chapter 9

Definition S2

(Simple point process)
.

A

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{x_{i}}

(Non-atomic point process)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}[N(\{x\})>0]

These definitions are sufficient to define and analyze the Poisson point process.

Definition S4

\displaystyle N(E)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{pdf}}{\ \sim\ }\frac{\Lambda(E)^{k}\exp(-\Lambda(E))}{k!}.

Example III.1

(Stationary Poisson process)

(Independent scattering / complete independence)

Immediately, we see that Poisson point processes are simple and finite if the intensity measure

V.4
Stochastic gradient flow on the torus

Fix now

It is shown in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle\dot{x_{i}}

\displaystyle=2\left|D_{i}\right|(\mu_{i}-x_{i}).

(S89)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot p\nabla U+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta p.

Since

VI.1
Fitting ellipsoids in
d
dimensions

Let
x,v
be vectors in some
d
-dimensional basis.
The equation of a
(d-1)
-sphere
S
centered at
v
is

\displaystyle\left\lVert x-v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=1\quad\forall x\in S.

(S96)

Applying a rotation
P
(an orthogonal matrix) of the sphere onto a set of principal axes and stretching along those axes by
\Lambda
(a positive diagonal matrix), one generalizes (
S96
) to an ellipsoid
E
via a symmetric positive-definite matrix
M=P\Lambda P^{\intercal}
defining a generalized inner product in which
x
satisfies

\displaystyle(x-v)^{\intercal}M(x-v)

\displaystyle=1\quad\forall x\in E.

(S97)

The elliptic radii
r_{i}
are then
r_{i}=\Lambda_{ii}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and the elliptic axes are the columns of
P
.

VI.1.1
The minimum volume bounding ellipsoid

Given the volume

V=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}\propto\sqrt{\det(M^{-1})},

(S98)

it follows that maximizing
\log\det(M)
minimizes
V
. Hence for a given dataset
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the following convex program computes the minimum-volume bounding ellipsoid:

\displaystyle\sup_{M,v}\

\displaystyle\log\det(M)

(S99)

subject to

\displaystyle(x_{i}-v)^{\intercal}M(x-v_{i})\leq 1\quad\forall i

(S100)

\displaystyle M>0.

(S101)

Here,
M>0
in the sense of linear matrix inequality (LMI), i.e.
M
is constrained to lie in the positive-definite cone.
However, the offset
x_{i}-v
produces a variable-product constraint which is not disciplined convex (DCP). The following reparametrization uses the invertibility of
M
to convert the constraint to linear least squares:

\displaystyle\sup_{A,b}\

\displaystyle\log\det(A)

(S102)

subject to

\displaystyle\left\lVert Ax_{i}-b\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1\quad\forall i

(S103)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle>0

(S104)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=A^{2}=A^{\intercal}A

(S105)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=A^{-1}b.

(S106)

DCP solvers such as CVXOPT
CVXPY
solve this problem. We further reduce the problem size by considering the subset of
X
lying on the convex hull, computed in
O(n\log n)
.

VI.1.2
\ell^{2}
-minimal projection to ellipsoids

Given a representation of an ellipsoid as
(M,v)
in the same basis as
x
, define the following convex program:

\displaystyle\inf_{Y}\

\displaystyle\left\lVert Y-X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(S107)

subject to

\displaystyle\left\lVert A(x_{i}-v)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=1\quad\forall i

(S108)

\displaystyle A^{\intercal}A=A^{2}=M,

(S109)

with
A
computed by (Hermitian) eigendecomposition of
M
.
Then
Y
is the minimum-
\ell^{2}
-distance projection of
X
onto
(M,v)
. This problem is not DCP; however, since there are no matrix cone constraints, we can simply use non-DCP solvers compatible with nonlinear constraints, such as SLSQP
SLSQP
. The constraint Jacobian for (
S108
) is
2M(x_{i}-v)
.

VI.2
Fitting hyperplanes in
d

The equation of a hyperplane

\displaystyle n\cdot(x-v)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall x\in H

(S110)

for
n,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that

is a unit normal to

\displaystyle n\cdot x-b

\displaystyle=0,

(S111)

we see that

is the distance from the origin to

\displaystyle d(y,H)

\displaystyle=\left|n\cdot(y-v)\right|=\left|n\cdot y-b\right|.

Now, let

\displaystyle\inf_{n,v}\

\displaystyle\ \left\lVert(X-v)n\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(S113)

subject to

\displaystyle\ \left\lVert n\right\rVert_{2}=1.

(S114)

The cost is bi-convex in the parameters

For fixed

\displaystyle 0

A common method to estimate

By (

(S117)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert}\begin{bmatrix}v&v\times n\end{bmatrix},\quad v=u\times n,

\displaystyle Y_{H}
