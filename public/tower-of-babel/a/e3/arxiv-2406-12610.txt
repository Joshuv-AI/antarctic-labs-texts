Modified difference ascent sequences and Fishburn structures
Giulio Cerbai, Anders Claesson, Bruce E. Sagan

Abstract
Ascent sequences and their modified version play a central role in the bijective framework relating several combinatorial structures counted by the Fishburn numbers. Ascent sequences are positive integer sequences defined by imposing a bound on the growth of their entries in terms of the number of ascents contained in the corresponding prefix, while modified ascent sequences are the image of ascent sequences under the so-called hat map. By relaxing the notion of ascent, Dukes and Sagan have recently introduced difference ascent sequences. Here we define modified difference ascent sequences and study their combinatorial properties. Inversion sequences are a superset of the difference ascent sequences and we extend the hat map to this domain. Our extension depends on a parameter which we specialize to obtain a new set of permutations counted by the Fishburn numbers and characterized by a subdiagonality property.

Introduction

Fishburn structures is a collective term for combinatorial objects
counted by the Fishburn numbers. These numbers appear as sequence A22493
in the OEIS
[
OEI
]
and the
n
th Fishburn number is defined as the
coefficient of
x^{n}
in the series

\sum_{n\geq 0}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(1-(1-x)^{k}\bigr).

This generating function first appeared 2001 in a paper by
Zagier
[
Zag01
]
concerned with bounds on the dimension of the
space of Vassiliev’s knot invariants. Eight years later, Bousquet-Mélou,
Claesson, Dukes and Kitaev
[
BMCDK10
]
proved that this series also
enumerates unlabeled interval orders, thus resolving a long standing open
problem. Peter C. Fishburn pioneered the study of interval
orders
[
Fis70a
,
Fis70b
,
Fis85
]
and it is in honor of him Claesson
and Linusson
[
CL11
]
named the coefficients of Zagier’s series.

Bousquet-Mélou et al.
[
BMCDK10
]
laid the foundation of a
bijective framework relating interval orders, Stoimenow matchings, and
Fishburn permutations, defined by avoidance of a single bivincular
pattern of length three. To link these objects, as well as to count
them, they introduced an auxiliary set of sequences that embody their
recursive structure more transparently, the ascent sequences.
They defined them as certain nonnegative integer sequences whose
growth of their entries is bounded by the number of ascents contained
in the corresponding prefix. Research into Fishburn structures
(sparked by the work of Bousquet-Mélou et al.) has
blossomed over the last 15 years. The structures studied are mostly the
ones previously mentioned but also include Fishburn
matrices
[
Fis70b
,
DP10
]
, descent correcting
sequences
[
CL11
]
and inversion sequences avoiding the covincular
pattern

Recently, Cerbai and Claesson
[
CC23a
]
introduced Fishburn
trees and Fishburn covers to obtain simplified versions of the existing
bijections.

The bijection relating ascent sequences with Fishburn permutations is easy
to describe. Ascent sequences encode the recursive construction
of Fishburn permutations by insertion of a new maximum element.
On the other hand, their relation with
(\mathbf{2\hskip-2.29994pt+\hskip-2.29994pt2})
-free posets is better
expressed in terms of a modified version, that is, their bijective image
under the hat map. Roughly speaking, the hat map goes through the ascent tops
of a given ascent sequence; at each step it increases by one all the entries
in the corresponding prefix that are currently greater than or equal to the current ascent top.
Modified ascent sequences interact better with Fishburn trees too, as they
are simply obtained by reading the labels of Fishburn trees with the
in-order traversal. Further, Fishburn trees arise from the max-decomposition
of modified ascent sequences.
In fact, even though they only appeared marginally in the original
paper
[
BMCDK10
]
, modified ascent sequences have recently assumed
a key role in the understanding of Fishburn structures
[
CC23b
,
CC23a
,
Cera
,
Cerb
]
.

In 2023, Bényi, Claesson and Dukes
[
BCD23
]
generalized ascent
sequences to weak ascent sequences. They are defined analogously to the
classical case, but (strict) ascents are replaced with weak ascents.
In the spirit of the original framework, the authors provided
bijections with several classes of matrices, posets and permutations.
Among them, weak ascent sequences encode the active site
construction of weak Fishburn permutations, a superset of Fishburn
permutations defined by avoidance of a single bivincular pattern of
length four.

By relaxing the bound on the growth of the rightmost entry further,
that is, by replacing ascents or weak ascents with difference
d
ascents, Dukes and
Sagan
[
DS23
]
arrived at
d
-ascent sequences.
This allowed them to generalize the ascent and weak ascent constructions
whose corresponding combinatorial objects now depended on the paramenter
d
.
They also provided natural injections from
d
-ascent sequences to various structures, for example,
permutations avoiding a bivincular pattern of length
d+3
, leaving
the problem of improving these maps to bijections open.
This was done very recently by Zang and Zhou
[
ZZ
]
, who
introduced what we will call
d
-Fishburn permutations (they used the term
d
-permutations) and proved that their
recursive structure is embodied by
d
-ascent sequences in the same
way as ascent sequences encode Fishburn permutations.

In this paper, we generalize the hat map to
d
-ascent sequences,
obtaining modified
d
-ascent sequences in the process.
We present a recursive construction of modified
d
-ascent sequences
and use it to study their combinatorial properties.
Our framework is in fact more flexible: it extends to inversion sequences,
a superset of
d
-ascent sequences. Further, our definition of the
hat map depends on a parameter whose specific choices lead to
interesting examples.
Fishburn permutations are obtained
[
BMCDK10
]
by applying the
Burge transpose
[
CC23b
]
to modified ascent sequences,
and we prove that the same construction holds for modified

d
-ascent sequences and
d
-Fishburn permutations.
Finally, we initiate the study of pattern avoidance on
d
-Fishburn
permutations.

We start by giving the necessary tools and definitions in
Section
2
.

In Section
3
, we introduce the
d
-hat map and use it to
define the set of modified
d
-ascent sequences. We then provide a
recursive description of modified
d
-ascent sequences and show in
Proposition
3.3
that they are Cayley permutations
whose set of indices of left-most copies is equal to the
d
-ascent set of the
unmodified sequence.

Section
4
is devoted to the study of
certain properties of the
d
-hat map. Our main result,
Corollary
4.7
, shows that
d
-hat is injective on
modified
d
-ascent sequences. We then consider which statistics
are preserved by
d
-hat in Section
4.2
.

In Section
5
, we define modified inversion sequences
and the
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
map. We show that, under
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
, a permutation
corresponds bijectively to the inversion sequence recording its
recursive construction by insertion of a new rightmost maximum value.

This approach is pushed further in Section
6
. We restrict the

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
map to ascent sequences and weak descent sequences,
characterizing the corresponding sets of permutations as those that are
subdiagonal in a certain sense.

In Section
7
, we prove that
d
-Fishburn permutations
can be obtained as the bijective image of
d
-ascent sequences
under the composition of the
d
-hat map with the Burge transpose,
lifting a classical result by Bousquet-Mélou et al.
[
BMCDK10
]

to any
d\geq 0
.

In Section
8
, we enumerate
d
-Fishburn permutations
avoiding
231
using a bijection with certain Dyck paths and
the cluster method.

Section
9
contains some final remarks and
suggestions for future work.

2
Preliminaries

For any nonnegative integer number
n
, let
\End_{n}
be the set of
endofunctions
,

\alpha:[n]\to[n]
, where
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We sometimes
identify an endofunction
\alpha
with the word
\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
, where

a_{i}=\alpha(i)
for each
i\in[n]
. We will use the convention that Greek
letters will usually be used for sequences and the corresponding Roman
letters will be used for their elements
so, for example,
a_{i}
will be
the
i
th element of
\alpha
unless otherwise indicated.
Let
\End=\cup_{n\geq 0}\End_{n}
. In general, given a definition of
E_{n}

(of elements of size
n
) we let
E=\cup_{n\geq 0}E_{n}
. Or, conversely,
given a set
E
whose elements are equipped with a notion of size, we
will denote by
E_{n}
the set of elements in
E
that have size
n
.

A
Cayley permutation
is an endofunction
\alpha
where

\img\alpha=[k]
, for some
k\leq n
. In other words,
\alpha
is a Cayley
permutation if it contains at least one copy of each integer between
1

and its maximum element. The set of Cayley permutations of length
n
is
denoted by
\Cay_{n}
. For example,
\Cay_{1}=\left\{1\right\}
,

\Cay_{2}=\left\{11,12,21\right\}
and

\Cay_{3}=\left\{111,112,121,122,123,132,211,212,213,221,231,312,321\right\}.

There is a well-known one-to-one correspondence between ordered
set partitions and Cayley permutations: The Cayley permutation

\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
encodes the ordered set partition if
[n]
into subsets
B_{1}\ldots B_{k}
where
k=\max\alpha

and
i\in B_{a_{i}}
for every
i\in[n]
.

An endofunction
\alpha\in\End_{n}
is an
inversion sequence
if

a_{i}\leq i
for each
i\in[n]
. We let
\I_{n}
denote the set of inversion
sequences of length
n
. For example,

\I_{1}=\{1\},\quad\I_{2}=\{11,12\},\quad\I_{3}=\left\{111,112,113,121,122,123\right\}.

Let
\alpha:[n]\to[n]
be an endofunction. We call
i\in[n]
an

ascent
of
\alpha
if
i=1
or
i\geq 2
and

a_{i}>a_{i-1}.

We define the
ascent set
of
\alpha
to be

\Asc\alpha=\{i\in[n]\mid\text{$i$ is an ascent of $\alpha$}\}

and

\asc\alpha=\#\Asc\alpha

where, for any set
S
,
\#S
denotes the cardinality of
S
.
Note that our conventions differ
from some others in the literature in that we are taking the indices
of ascent tops, rather than bottoms, and that
1
is always an ascent
which is done for the purpose of simplifying the definition of an ascent sequence.
It will sometimes be convenient to order
\Asc\alpha
and other similar sets below increasingly to obtain the
ascent list

\Asc\alpha=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}),

where
k=\asc\alpha
. Our notation will not distinguish between the set and its sequence.

From now on, let
\alpha_{i}=a_{1}\ldots a_{i}
denote the prefix of
\alpha
of length
i
. Call
\alpha
an
ascent sequence
if for all
i\in[n]
we have

a_{i}\leq 1+\asc\alpha_{i-1}.

Note that when
i=1
we have
a_{1}\leq 1+\asc\epsilon=1
, where
\epsilon

denotes the empty sequence. Since the entries of
\alpha
are positive
integers, this forces
a_{1}=1
.
Let
\Ascseq_{0}
be the set of ascent sequences and let
\Ascseq_{0,n}

denote the set of ascent sequences of length
n
. For instance,

\Ascseq_{0,3}=\{111,112,121,122,123\}.

Clearly, every
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0,n+1}
is of the form
\alpha=\beta a
,
where
\beta\in\Ascseq_{0,n}
and
1\leq a\leq 1+\asc\beta
.
Note that
\Ascseq_{0,n}\subseteq\I_{n}
. On the other hand, some ascent
sequences are not Cayley permutations, the smallest example of which is

12124
. Note also that we depart slightly from the original definition
of ascent sequences
[
BMCDK10
]
and other papers on the topic
in that our sequences use the positive, rather than nonnegative, integers.
The reason is that we want to bring all the
families of sequences considered in this paper under the umbrella of
endofunctions of
[n]
so as to relate them with Cayley permutations and inversion sequences.

The set
\Modasc_{0}
of
modified ascent sequences

[
BMCDK10
]

is the bijective image of
\Ascseq_{0}
under the
\alpha\mapsto\hat{\alpha}
mapping,
defined as follows. Given an ascent sequence
\alpha
, let

M(\alpha,j)=\alpha^{+},\text{ where }\alpha^{+}(i)=a_{i}+\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i<j$ and $a_{i}\geq a_{j}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and extend the definition of
M
to multiple indices
j_{1}
,
j_{2}
,…,
j_{k}

by

M(\alpha,j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k})=M\bigl(M(\alpha,j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1}),j_{k}\bigr).

Then

\hat{\alpha}=M(\alpha,\Asc\alpha),

where in this context
\Asc\alpha
is the ascent list of
\alpha
.
For example, if
\alpha=121242232
, then
\Asc\alpha=(1,2,4,5,8)
and we get
the following where at each stage the entry governing the modification
is underlined while the entries which are modified italicized:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=121242232

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1)

\displaystyle=\underline{1}21242232

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2)

\displaystyle=1\underline{2}1242232

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,4)

\displaystyle=1\mathbf{3}1\underline{2}42232

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,4,5)

\displaystyle=1312\underline{4}2232

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,4,5,8)

\displaystyle=1\mathbf{4}12\mathbf{5}22\underline{3}2=\hat{\alpha}

More informally, to determine
\hat{\alpha}
, we scan the ascents of
\alpha

from left to right; at each step, every element strictly to the left of
and weakly larger than the current ascent top is incremented by one.
The construction described above can easily be inverted
since
\Asc\alpha=\Asc\hat{\alpha}
.
Thus the
mapping
\Ascseq_{0}\rightarrow\Modasc_{0}
by
\alpha\mapsto\hat{\alpha}
is a bijection.

It is easy to turn this into a definition of
\Modasc_{0}
which is recursive
by length and will be given later (see Definition
3.2
).
Finally, in
[
CC23b
]
it was proved that

\Modasc_{0}=\{\alpha\in\Cay\mid\Asc\alpha=\nub\alpha\},

(1)

where

\nub\alpha=\{\min\alpha^{-1}(j)\mid 1\leq j\leq\max\alpha\}

is the set of positions of leftmost copies.
The term “nub” comes from a Haskell function that removes duplicate
elements from a list, keeping only the first occurrence of each
element. One may also think of nub as a short for “not used before.”
Interestingly, the nub (under the name “sequence of first occurrences”)
has recently appeared in an entirely different context as part of the
work of Liang and Sagan
[
LS
]
on proving log-concavity and
log-convexity results using distributive lattices.

In the above pair of forbidden Cayley-mesh patterns, the
leftmost one indicates an ascent that is not a leftmost
copy; and the one on the right stands for a leftmost
copy that is not an ascent.
Unlike
\Ascseq_{0}
, not every modified ascent sequence is
an inversion sequence. For instance, the modified ascent sequence
of
\alpha=1212
is
\hat{\alpha}=1312
.

Dukes and Sagan
[
DS23
]
have recently introduced
difference
d

ascent sequences
. Let
\alpha\in\End_{n}
. Given a nonnegative number
d\geq 0
,
we call
i\in[n]
a
d
-ascent
if

i=1
or
i\geq 2
and

a_{i}>a_{i-1}-d.

As with ordinary ascents, we have the
d
-ascent set
(or
list
)

\Asc_{d}\alpha=\{i\in[n]\mid\text{$i$ is a $d$-ascent of $\alpha$}\}.

and
d
-ascent number

\asc_{d}\alpha=\#\Asc_{d}\alpha.

Note that a
0
-ascent is simply an ascent, while a
1
-ascent is what
is called a
weak ascent
:

a_{i}>a_{i-1}-1\,\iff\,a_{i}\geq a_{i-1}.

The analogue of the definition of an ascent sequence in the weak case
is as expected.
Call
\alpha
a
d
-ascent sequence
if for all
i\in[n]
we have

a_{i}\leq 1+\asc_{d}\alpha_{i-1}.

Once again, the above restriction forces
a_{1}=1
. From now on, denote by

\Ascseq_{d,n}
the set of
d
-ascent sequences of length
n
.
Clearly, for
d=0
we recover the set of ascent sequences,
while for
d=1

we obtain the set of weak ascent sequences of Bényi
et al.
[
BCD23
]
.
Note also that
\Asc_{d}\alpha\subseteq\Asc_{d+1}\alpha
for each
d
,
from which the chain of containments

\Ascseq_{0}\subseteq\Ascseq_{1}\subseteq\Ascseq_{2}\subseteq\Ascseq_{3}\subseteq\cdots

(2)

follows immediately.

We now connect
d
-ascent sequences and inversion sequences.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

\I=\bigcup_{d\geq 0}\Ascseq_{d}.

Proof.

We will prove that each side of the equality is contained in the other.
We first show that
\Ascseq_{d}\subseteq\I
for all
d\geq 0
which will give one
of the desired inclusions. If
\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\Ascseq_{d}
then
a_{1}=1

as required for an inversion sequence. For
i\geq 2
, we have

a_{i}\leq 1+\asc_{d}\alpha_{i-1}\leq 1+(i-1)=i.

Thus
\alpha\in\I
.

For the other direction, it suffices to show that
\I_{n}\subseteq\Ascseq_{n,n}
.
So take
\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\I_{n}
. We have
a_{1}=1
as
needed. And for
i\geq 2
we have
a_{i-1}\leq i-1\leq n-1
. Hence

a_{i}>-1\geq a_{i-1}-n
. Thus every index
i\geq 2
is an
n
-ascent and so

a_{i}\leq i=1+\asc_{n}\alpha_{i-1}

showing that
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{n,n}
.
∎

We can now calculate the cardinality of certain
\A_{d,n}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
d\geq 0
we have

\#\A_{d,n}=\begin{cases}n!&\text{if $n\leq d+2$,}\\
(d+3)!-d!&\text{if $n=d+3$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

By the previous lemma
\A_{d,n}\subseteq\I_{n}
. Since
\#I_{n}=n!
, to prove
the first statement of the proposition, it suffices to show that if

n\leq d+2
then every inversion sequence of length
n
is a
d
-ascent
sequence.

Let
\alpha\in\I_{n}
where
n\leq d+2
. We claim that for every proper
prefix
\alpha_{i}
,
i\leq d+1
, we have
\Asc_{d}\alpha_{i}=[i]
.
Indeed, consider any element
a_{j}\in\alpha_{i}
. Then, since
\alpha
is an
inversion sequence,

a_{j-1}\leq j-1\leq i-1\leq d.

Also
a_{j}\geq 1
. So
a_{j-1}-a_{j}\leq d-1<d
, which forces
j\in\Asc_{d}\alpha_{i}
,
proving the claim. Now, for all
a_{k}\in\alpha
we have

a_{k}\leq k=1+\asc_{d}\alpha_{k-1},

hence
\alpha
is a
d
-ascent sequence, as desired.
To prove the second part of the proposition, we just need to show
that when
n=d+3
there are exactly
d!
elements of
\I_{d+3}
which
are not
d
-ascent sequences.

Let
\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{d+3}
be such a sequence. We show that the last
three entries of
\alpha
are

a_{d+1},a_{d+2},a_{d+3}=d+1,1,d+3,

while the prefix
\beta=a_{1}\ldots a_{d}
can be any inversion sequence of
size
d
. If we had
\Asc_{d}(\beta a_{d+1}a_{d+2})=[d+2]
, then, using an
argument like that of the previous paragraph, we would have
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
,
which is a contradiction. On the other hand, it follows from the proof
of the first part that
\Asc_{d}(\beta a_{d+1})=[d+1]
. So it must be that

d+2\not\in\Asc_{d}(\beta a_{d+1}a_{d+2})
, i.e.
a_{d+2}\leq a_{d+1}-d
.
Together with the fact that
a_{d+1}\leq d+1
and
a_{d+2}\geq 1
, this
forces

a_{d+1}=d+1\quad\text{and}\quad a_{d+2}=1.

Now, since we assumed that
\alpha
is not a
d
-ascent sequence, but we know that its
prefix
\beta a_{d+1}a_{d+2}
is, it must be that

a_{d+3}>\asc(\beta a_{d+1}a_{d+2})+1=d+2.

Since
\alpha\in\I_{d+3}
we also have
a_{d+3}\leq d+3
. It follows that
there is only one choice for the last element of
\alpha
, namely
a_{d+3}=d+3
.
In the end, we have
\alpha=\beta(d+1)1(d+3)
, where
\beta
is any inversion
sequence of size
d
. Since there are
d!
choices for such
\beta
,
the proposition is proved.
∎

3
Modified
d
-ascent sequences

We wish to extend the hat map
\alpha\mapsto\hat{\alpha}
, originally defined on

\Ascseq_{0}
, to the set
\A_{d}
.
Let
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
, for some
d\geq 0
.
The
d
-hat
of
\alpha
is defined as

\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=M\bigl(\alpha,\Asc_{d}\alpha\bigr),

where
\Asc_{d}\alpha
is the
d
-ascent list of
\alpha
.
To illustrate, suppose
d=2
. Then it is easy to check that

\alpha=12131532
is a
2
-ascent sequence with

\Asc_{2}(\alpha)=(1,2,3,4,6,8).

So, using the same notation as for the example computing
\hat{\alpha}
in the ascent sequence case,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=12131532

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1)

\displaystyle=\underline{1}2131532

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2)

\displaystyle=1\underline{2}131532

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,3)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{2}\mathbf{3}\underline{1}31532

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,3,4)

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{4}1\underline{3}1532

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,3,4,6)

\displaystyle=24131\underline{5}32

\displaystyle M(\alpha,1,2,3,4,6,8)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{35}1\mathbf{4}1\mathbf{64}\underline{2}=\hatt_{2}(\alpha).

The
d
-hat map is a natural generalization of the hat map, obtained
by replacing ascents with
d
-ascents. As a special case, we have

\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha)=\hat{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}
.
More generally, to compute
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
scan the
d
-ascents of

\alpha
from left to right; at each step, increment by one every element
strictly to the left of and weakly larger than the current
d
-ascent top.
From now on, given
d\geq 0
, we let

\Modasc_{d}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\Ascseq_{d})

denote the set of
modified
d
-ascent sequences
.

Let us set up some standard notation we shall use throughout the rest
of this paper. We will consider
d
-ascent sequences
\alpha=\beta a
,
where
a
is the last letter of
\alpha
and
\beta
is a
d
-ascent
sequence of size one less than
\alpha
. If
d
is clear from context,
we let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
and
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
. We also use “
+
” as a
superscript that denotes the operation of adding one to the entries

c\geq a
of a given sequence, where
a
is a threshold determined by
the context. For instance, we denote by
\hat{\beta}^{+}a
the sequence obtained
by adding one to each entry of
\hat{\beta}
that is greater than or equal to
a
. Clearly, letting
b
denote the last letter of
\beta
,
by definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
we have for every
n\geq 1
and

\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d,n}

\hat{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{\beta}a&\mbox{if $a\leq b-d$,}\\
\hat{\beta}^{+}a&\mbox{if $a>b-d$.}\end{array}\right.

(3)

Finally, we will denote
the entries of the above sequences by

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n},&&\hat{\alpha}=a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{n},\\
\beta=b_{1}\ldots b_{n-1},&&\hat{\beta}=b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{n-1},&&\hat{\beta}^{+}=b^{\prime\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime\prime}_{n-1},\end{array}

(4)

where
n
is the size of
\alpha
. The behavior of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
on the last two
letters of
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
is described more explicitly in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\Ascseq_{d}
, for some
d\geq 0
and
n\geq 2
. Let

\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\hat{\alpha}=a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{n}
. Then

a^{\prime}_{n-1},a^{\prime}_{n}=\begin{cases}a_{n-1}+1,a_{n}&\text{if $a_{n-1}-d<a_{n}\leq a_{n-1}$;}\\
a_{n-1},a_{n}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We use induction on the size of
\alpha
. Let
\alpha=\beta a_{n}
.
The last element of
\hat{\alpha}
is
a_{n}
by definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
.
Similarly, the last letter of
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
is
a_{n-1}
.

Suppose initially that
a_{n}>a_{n-1}-d
. Then
n
is a
d
-ascent and
so
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a_{n}
. Now, if
a_{n-1}\geq a_{n}
then
a^{\prime}_{n-1}=a_{n-1}+1

and
\hat{\alpha}
ends with
a_{n-1}+1,a_{n}
. Otherwise, if
a_{n-1}<a_{n}
then
a_{n-1}
will not be incremented and
\hat{\alpha}
ends with
a_{n-1},a_{n}
.

Finally, if
a_{n}\leq a_{n-1}-d
then
n
is not a
d
-ascent.
So in this case
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a_{n}
and the last two elements
are
a_{n-1},a_{n}
again.
∎

Our next goal is to provide a recursive definition of
\Modasc_{d}

which does not depend on constructing
\Ascseq_{d}
first.
In the classical case, such a
definition of
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}
is as follows
[
CC23b
]
, where
we use
\hat{\alpha}
and
\hat{\beta}
to denote generic elements of
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}
.
Note that this definition permits the computation of an element
\hat{\alpha}
in
\hat{\A}_{0}
directly from a given
\hat{\beta}
in
\hat{\A}_{0}
without needing to know
\alpha
itself.

Definition 3.2
.

We have
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0,0}=\{\epsilon\}
and
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0,1}=\{1\}
.
Let
n\geq 2
. Then every
\hat{\alpha}\in\hat{\Ascseq}_{0,n}
is of one of two forms
depending on whether the last letter forms an ascent with the
penultimate letter:

•

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
and
1\leq a\leq b
, or

•

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
and
b<a\leq 1+\asc\hat{\beta}
,

where
\hat{\beta}\in\hat{\Ascseq}_{0,n-1}
and the last letter of
\hat{\beta}
is
b
.

We wish to highlight a detail that explains why the definition given above
is consistent with letting
\hat{\alpha}=M\bigl(\alpha,\Asc\alpha\bigr)
.
Given
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}
, to compute
\hat{\alpha}
we increase entries in the
current prefix if and only if we encounter an ascent of
\alpha
. On the
other hand, Definition
3.2
is stated directly in terms
of the ascents of the modified sequence, i.e. in terms of
\Asc\hat{\beta}
.
Since it is known
[
BMCDK10
]
that

\Asc\alpha=\Asc\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha),

(5)

i.e. the ascent set is preserved under the hat map, these two
approaches are in fact equivalent.

In the same spirit, we wish to give a recursive definition of
\Modasc_{d}
.
The problem in generalizing Definition
3.2
is that
in general the
d
-ascent set, as well as its cardinality, is not preserved
under
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
. For instance, for
d=1
we have
\mathrm{hat}_{1}(11)=21
and

\{1,2\}=\Asc_{1}(11)\neq\Asc_{1}(21)=\{1\}.

A suggestion for an alternative approach comes from the classical
case
d=0
. Let
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}
and let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha)
.
Then (see
[
CC23b
, Theorem 7.3]
and
[
BMCDK10
, Section 4.1]
,
respectively),

\Asc\alpha=\nub\hat{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\asc\alpha=\max\hat{\alpha}.

(6)

In fact, the corresponding equalities hold for every
d\geq 0
,
as we show in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given
d\geq 0
, let
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
and let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
.
Then
\hat{\alpha}
is a Cayley permutation with

\Asc_{d}\alpha=\nub\hat{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\asc_{d}\alpha=\max\hat{\alpha}.

Proof.

We use induction on the size of
\alpha
. It is easy to see that the
statement holds if
\alpha
has length zero or one.
Let
n\geq 2
and let
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d,n}
. As usual, let
\alpha=\beta a
,
for some
\beta\in\Ascseq_{d,n-1}
and
1\leq a\leq 1+\asc_{d}\beta
.
By induction,
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
is a Cayley permutation with

\Asc_{d}\beta=\nub\hat{\beta}
and
\asc_{d}\beta=\max\hat{\beta}
.
Following the definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
, we consider two possibilities
according to whether or not
a
forms a
d
-ascent with the last
letter
b
of
\beta
.

•

Suppose
a\leq b-d
. Then
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
. Note that

\hat{\alpha}\in\Cay_{n}
since
\hat{\beta}\in\Cay_{n-1}
and

a\leq b-d\leq\max\beta
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Asc_{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\Asc_{d}\beta=\nub\hat{\beta}=\nub\hat{\alpha}

and

\displaystyle\asc_{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\asc_{d}\beta=\max\hat{\beta}=\max\hat{\alpha}.

•

Suppose
a>b-d
. Then
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
. Once again, it is
easy to see that
\hat{\alpha}\in\Cay_{n}
as follows.

First note that by the definition of
d
-ascent sequence and induction we have

a\leq\asc_{d}\beta+1=\max\hat{\beta}+1.

If
a=\max\hat{\beta}+1
,
then

\hat{\beta}^{+}=\hat{\beta}
and

\img\hat{\alpha}=\img\hat{\beta}\cup\{a\}=[\max\hat{\beta}+1]=[\max\hat{\alpha}].

On the other hand, if
a\leq\max\hat{\beta}
, then the
only gap created in
\hat{\beta}^{+}
(by lifting the entries
c\geq a
)
is filled by
a
. More formally,

\displaystyle\img\hat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\img\hat{\beta}^{+}\cup\{a\}

\displaystyle=\{1,2,\dots,a-1\}\cup\{a+1,a+2,\dots,\max(\hat{\beta})+1\}\cup\{a\}

\displaystyle=[\max\hat{\beta}+1]

\displaystyle=[\max\hat{\alpha}].

Finally,

\displaystyle\Asc_{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\Asc_{d}\beta\uplus\{n\}

\displaystyle=\nub\hat{\beta}\uplus\{n\}

\displaystyle=\nub\hat{\beta}^{+}\uplus\{n\}

\displaystyle=\nub\hat{\alpha},

where the last equality follows since
a
is a leftmost copy in
\hat{\alpha}
, and

\displaystyle\asc_{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\asc_{d}\beta+1=\max\hat{\beta}+1=\max\hat{\alpha}.

This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

The equality
\asc_{d}\beta=\max\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
proved in
Proposition
3.3
leads us to the following
recursive definition of modified
d
-ascent sequences, where we use
the same notational conventions as in Definition
3.2
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 0
be a nonnegative integer. Let
\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,0}=\{\epsilon\}

and
\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,1}=\{1\}
. Suppose
n\geq 2
. Then every

\hat{\alpha}\in\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,n}
is of one of two forms depending on whether the
last letter forms a
d
-ascent with the penultimate letter:

•

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
and
1\leq a\leq b-d
, or

•

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
and
b-d<a\leq 1+\max\hat{\beta}
,

where
\hat{\beta}\in\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,n-1}
and the last letter of
\hat{\beta}
is
b
.

The reader will immediately realize that the previous definition is
obtained by replacing
\asc\hat{\beta}
with
\max\hat{\beta}
in
Definition
3.2
. When
d=0
, the two definitions
are equivalent by equation (
6
).
Modified
d
-ascent sequences are built recursively by
insertion of a new rightmost entry
a
, which is at most equal to one plus
the current maximum; the parameter
d
determines the cases where the
prefix is rescaled (by adding one to each entry
c\geq a
).
For convenience, the analogous definitions of
\Ascseq_{d}
and
\Modasc_{d}

are illustrated below:

\displaystyle(\Ascseq_{d})\quad\alpha

\displaystyle=\beta a,\ 1\leq a\leq 1+\asc_{d}\beta.

\displaystyle(\Modasc_{d})\quad\hat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{\beta}a,\ 1\leq a\leq b-d;\\
\hat{\beta}^{+}a,\ b-d<a\leq 1+\max\hat{\beta}.\end{cases}

The equality
\asc_{d}\beta=\max\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
acts as a bridge between the
two definitions.

Let us end this section with a remark. In general, the set
\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,n}

is not included in
\hat{\Ascseq}_{d+1,n}
. For instance, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\Ascseq}_{0,2}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{hat}_{0}(11),\mathrm{hat}_{0}(12)\}=\{11,12\}

and

\displaystyle\hat{\Ascseq}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{hat}_{1}(11),\mathrm{hat}_{1}(12)\}=\{21,12\}.

4
Properties of
d
-hat

We devote this section to the study of several aspects related
to the
d
-hat map just introduced. Recall that
\hatt_{d}
is a map whose domain is the set of
d
-ascent sequences.

Recall from Proposition
3.3
that

\nub\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\Asc_{d}\alpha
. When
d=0
, using equation (
5
)
we obtain the equality

\nub\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha)=\Asc\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha)

characterizing
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}
as a subset of
\Cay
(see
equation (
1
)). Since we have established in
Proposition
3.3
that
\Modasc_{d}\subseteq\Cay
for every

, a natural question arises:

Is there an analogous equality characterizing
\Modasc_{d}

when
d\geq 1
?

As mentioned before Proposition
3.3
, the equality

\Asc_{d}\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\Asc_{d}\alpha
does not hold for
d\geq 1
. However, we
show in Proposition
4.3
that one inclusion holds.
First, a simple lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\beta\in\End
and let
\beta^{+}
be the result of increasing every element of
\beta
which is at least
a
by
1
for some
a\geq 0
.
Then for all
d\geq 0

\Asc_{d}\beta^{+}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\beta\quad\text{and}\quad\Asc\beta^{+}=\Asc\beta.

Proof.

Let
\beta=b_{1}\ldots b_{n}
and
\beta^{+}=b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{n}
, where
b^{\prime}_{i}=b_{i}
,
if
b_{i}<a
, and
b^{\prime}_{i}=b_{i}+1
, if
b_{i}\geq a
. Note that the first
position
i=1
is a
d
-ascent by definition. On the other hand, let
i\geq 2

and suppose that
i\in\Asc_{d}\beta^{+}
. We show that
i\in\Asc_{d}\beta
.
For a contradiction, suppose that
i\notin\Asc_{d}\beta
. More explicitly, we
have

\displaystyle i\notin\Asc_{d}\beta

\displaystyle\iff b_{i}\leq b_{i-1}-d;

\displaystyle i\in\Asc_{d}\beta^{+}

\displaystyle\iff b^{\prime}_{i}>b^{\prime}_{i-1}-d.

Comparing the two inequalities forces
b^{\prime}_{i}=b_{i}+1
and
b^{\prime}_{i-1}=b_{i-1}
. Therefore, we have

b_{i-1}<a\leq b_{i}
and

b_{i}\leq b_{i}+d\leq b_{i-1}<a\leq b_{i},

which gives us the desired contradiction.

By the previous part of the proposition (and since an ascent is a

0
-ascent), to prove the remaining equality
\Asc\beta^{+}=\Asc\beta
we only
need to show that
\Asc\beta^{+}\supseteq\Asc\beta
.
Let
i\in\Asc\beta
. If
i=1
, then
i\in\Asc\beta^{+}
. If instead
i\geq 2
,
then
b_{i}>b_{i-1}
and thus
b_{i}
will be increased in
\beta^{+}
if
b_{i-1}

is increased. In any case, we have
b^{\prime}_{i}>b^{\prime}_{i-1}
, hence
i\in\Asc\beta^{+}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 0
. Suppose that
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
and let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
.
Then

\Asc_{d}\hat{\alpha}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\alpha.

Proof.

We use induction on the size
n
of
\alpha
, taking the case
n\leq 1
for
granted. Assume
n\geq 2
. Let
\alpha=\beta a
, where
\beta\in\Ascseq_{d,n-1}

and
1\leq a\leq 1+\asc_{d}\beta
, and let
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
. As usual, we
consider two cases according to whether or not the last letter
b

of
\beta
forms a
d
-ascent with
a
.

Suppose first that
1\leq a\leq b-d
. Then
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
, where

\Asc_{d}\hat{\beta}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\beta
by induction. Now by Definition
3.4

\Asc_{d}\hat{\alpha}=\Asc_{d}\hat{\beta}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\beta=\Asc_{d}\alpha.

Otherwise, suppose that
b-d<a\leq 1+\asc_{d}\beta
. Then

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
.
Now using Lemma
4.1
and induction we have

\Asc_{d}\hat{\alpha}=\Asc_{d}\hat{\beta}^{+}\cup\{n\}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\hat{\beta}\cup\{n\}\subseteq\Asc_{d}\beta\cup\{n\}=\Asc_{d}\alpha.

This completes the demonstration.
∎

Combining Proposition
3.3
and Corollary
4.2
immediately gives the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 0
. We have, for any
\hat{\alpha}\in\Modasc_{d}
,

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\Asc_{d}\hat{\alpha}\subseteq\nub\hat{\alpha}.}\hfill\qed

4.1
Injectivity of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}

Our next goal is to prove that
d
-hat is injective on
\Ascseq_{d}
for
every
d\geq 0
. Let
\alpha\in\I
be an inversion sequence.
By Lemma
2.1
,
the quantity

\mind\alpha=\min\{d\geq 0\mid\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}\}

is a nonnegative integer for every
\alpha
. Furthermore, by
equation (
2
) if
\alpha
is a
d
-ascent sequence for
some
d
, then it is a
k
-ascent sequence for every
k\geq d
.
It is natural to study the set

H(\alpha)=\{\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)\mid d\geq\mind\alpha\}

of all the (meaningful)
d
-hats of
\alpha
. Note that

H(\alpha)\subseteq\Cay
by Proposition
3.3
.
Next, we show that
H(\alpha)
is finite.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\I_{n}
. Then
\mind\alpha\leq n
. Further,
we have
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{n}(\alpha)
for each
d\geq n-1
.

Proof.

Recall from the proof of Lemma
2.1
that

\I_{n}\subseteq\Ascseq_{n,n}
. The inequality
\mind\alpha\leq n

follows immediately. Finally, let
d\geq n-1
. Then

\Asc_{d}\alpha=\Asc_{n-1}\alpha=[n]

and the equality
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{n-1}(\alpha)
follows directly from
the definition of
d
-hat.
∎

By Lemma
4.4
, we have

H(\alpha)=\left\{\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)\mid\mind\alpha\leq d\leq|\alpha|\right\},

from which the following corollary is obtained immediately.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\alpha
be an inversion sequence. Then
H(\alpha)
is finite.
∎

Let us now prove that the sets
H(\alpha)
are disjoint. The injectivity
of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
over
\Ascseq_{d}
will immediately follow as a corollary.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\alpha
and
\sigma
be inversion sequences and suppose that

H(\alpha)\cap H(\sigma)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\alpha=\sigma
.

Proof.

We use induction on the size. The statement clearly holds for inversion
sequences of size
n\leq 1
, so suppose
n\geq 2
. Let
\alpha
and
\sigma

be in
\I_{n}
, with
H(\alpha)\cap H(\sigma)\neq\emptyset
. If

\gamma\in H(\alpha)\cap H(\sigma)
, then

\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\sigma)=\gamma,

for some
d\geq\mind\alpha
and
k\geq\mind\sigma
. We prove that
\alpha=\sigma
.
Denote by
y
the last letter of
\gamma
. Note that the last letters
of
\alpha
and
\sigma
are equal to
y
as well. That is, we have

\alpha=\beta y
and
\sigma=\tau y
, where
\beta
and
\tau
denote
the corresponding prefixes of
\alpha
and
\sigma
.
We consider two cases, according to whether or not
y
is a leftmost
copy in
\gamma
.

Initially, suppose that
n\notin\nub\gamma
. Recall by
Proposition
3.3
that

\Asc_{d}\alpha=\nub\gamma=\Asc_{k}\sigma.

In particular, the last position
n
is neither a
d
-ascent
in
\alpha
, nor a
k
-ascent in
\sigma
. By definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}

and
\mathrm{hat}_{k}
, we have, respectively,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)y

and

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\sigma)=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)y.

This forces

\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)
so that

H(\beta)\cap H(\tau)\neq\emptyset
.
By induction, we have

\beta=\tau
and consequently

\alpha=\beta y=\tau y=\sigma.

Finally, suppose that
n\in\nub\gamma
. The proof is similar
to the previous case, the difference being that here the last position
is a
d
-ascent in
\alpha
, as well as a
k
-ascent in
\sigma
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)^{+}y

and

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\sigma)=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)^{+}y,

and thus
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)^{+}=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)^{+}
. Since both
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)^{+}

and
\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)^{+}
are obtained by rescaling entries
c\geq y
,
we have
\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)=\mathrm{hat}_{k}(\tau)
, and we can finish the proof as in
the previous case.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

For each
d\geq 0
, we have a bijection
\mathrm{hat}_{d}:\Ascseq_{d}\to\Modasc_{d}
.
∎

4.2
Statistics preserved by
\mathrm{hat}_{d}

Let us now turn our attention to which statistics are preserved
by
d
-hat. Define the
weak descent set
of
\alpha
to be

\wDes\alpha=\{i\geq 2\mid a_{i}\leq a_{i-1}\}

We also say that
i
is a
right-left minimum index
of
\alpha
if

a_{i}<a_{j}
for all
i<j\leq n
. Further, the set of
right-left
minima pairs
is

\rlMinP\alpha=\{(i,a_{i})\mid\text{$i$ is a right-left minimum index of $\alpha$}\}.

The following lemma will be useful.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\alpha=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}=\beta a_{n}
where
n\geq 1
. Then

\rlMinP(\alpha)=\rlMinP(a_{1}\ldots a_{k})\uplus\{(n,a_{n})\}

where
k\geq 1
is the largest right-left minimum index of
\beta
such
that
a_{k}<a_{n}
. If no such index exists then we let
k=0
so that

\rlMinP(a_{1}\ldots a_{k})=\rlMinP(\emptyset)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider what happens in passing from
\rlMinP\beta
to

\rlMinP\alpha
. Of course,
(n,a_{n})
becomes a right-left minimum
pair in
\rlMinP\alpha
since
a_{n}
is the last element of the sequence.
Furthermore, any right-left minimum values
a_{i}
of
\rlMinP\beta

with
a_{i}>a_{n}
will now have a smaller element to their right and so
it will be removed in the transition to
\rlMinP\alpha
. The remaining
pairs of
\rlMinP\beta
will be preserved in
\rlMinP\alpha
.
This is equivalent to our claim.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\I_{n}
. We have the following for all
\gamma\in H(\alpha)
:

(a)

\Asc\gamma=\Asc\alpha
.

(b)

\wDes\gamma=\wDes\alpha
.

(c)

\rlMinP\gamma=\rlMinP\alpha
.

Proof.

(a) We induct on
n
where the case
n\leq 1
is trivial.
Let
\alpha=\beta a
. Pick a
d
for which
\alpha
is a
d
-ascent sequence and
let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
and
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta)
. We follow our usual conventions (
3
) and (
4
)
and denote by
b
,
b^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime\prime}
the last letter of
\beta
,
\hat{\beta}

and
\hat{\beta}^{+}
, respectively. Note that
b^{\prime}=b
by Lemma
3.1
.
By induction, we have

\Asc\hat{\beta}=\Asc\beta.

There are now three cases.
First suppose that
a\leq b-d
, so that
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
. From this, the
induction hypothesis, and the fact that
a\leq b=b^{\prime}
we obtain

\Asc\hat{\alpha}=\Asc(\hat{\beta}a)=\Asc\hat{\beta}=\Asc\beta=\Asc(\beta a)=\Asc\alpha.

For the next two cases we will have
a>b-d
so that
n
is a
d
-ascent
and
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
. If
a\leq b
, then

b^{\prime\prime}=b^{\prime}+1=b+1>a.

Thus, using Lemma
4.1
,

\Asc\hat{\alpha}=\Asc(\hat{\beta}^{+}a)=\Asc\hat{\beta}^{+}=\Asc\hat{\beta}=\Asc\beta=\Asc\beta a=\Asc\alpha.

Finally, suppose that
a>b
. Then

b^{\prime\prime}=b^{\prime}=b<a

and, in a similar manner to the first case,

\Asc\hat{\alpha}=\Asc\hat{\beta}^{+}\cup\{n\}=\Asc\beta\cup\{n\}=\Asc\alpha,

proving the first item.

(b) Directly from the definitions, for all inversion sequences
\alpha

of length
n
we have
\Asc\alpha\uplus\wDes\alpha=[n]
. So this part
follows immediately from (a).

(c) By induction

\rlMinP\hat{\beta}=\rlMinP\beta.

Again, we begin with the case
a\leq b-d
so that
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
.
By induction and the fact that both
\alpha
and
\hat{\alpha}

end in
a
, we see that the index
k
in Lemma
4.8
will be the
same for both
\alpha
and
\hat{\alpha}
. Thus, using the same lemma and the
inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle\rlMinP\hat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\rlMinP(b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{k})\uplus\{(n,a_{n})\}

\displaystyle=\rlMinP(b_{1}\ldots b_{k})\uplus\{(n,a_{n})\}

\displaystyle=\rlMinP\alpha.

Now consider what happens when
a>b-d
and
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
.
We must relate
\rlMinP\hat{\beta}
and
\rlMinP\hat{\beta}^{+}
. By the way
\hat{\beta}^{+}

is constructed from
\hat{\beta}
we see that every pair
(i,b^{\prime}_{i})\in\rlMinP\hat{\beta}

is either replaced by
(i,b^{\prime}_{i}+1)\in\rlMinP\hat{\beta}^{+}
if
b^{\prime}_{i}\geq a
or
remains as
(i,b^{\prime}_{i})
if
b^{\prime}_{i}<a
. In particular,
\hat{\beta}^{+}a
and
\hat{\beta}a
will have the same index
k
from Lemma
4.8
.
Moreover, due to our choice of
k
,

\rlMinP(b^{\prime\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime\prime}_{k})=\rlMinP(b^{\prime}_{1}\ldots b^{\prime}_{k})=\rlMinP(b_{1}\ldots b_{k}).

The proof is now completed in a manner similar to the first case.
∎

Recall from Lemma

We shall define the set

By Proposition

\alpha

\displaystyle\mathtt{for}\;i=i_{k},

\displaystyle\dots,i_{1}:

\displaystyle=1,\dots,i-1:

\displaystyle\mathtt{if}\;g_{j}>g_{i}\;\mathtt{then}\;g_{j}:=g_{j}-1.

The output of the above procedure is the desired ascent sequence
\alpha
.

\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}
, we have
\Asc\alpha=\Asc\gamma
.
The previous algorithm goes through the

-ascents of

is obtained from

We leave the details to the reader.

\displaystyle\mathrm{hat}_{\max}:\I\;

\displaystyle\alpha\;

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)

We start with a simple lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\I_{n}
. Suppose that
\alpha=\beta a
, for some
\beta\in\I_{n-1}

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\beta)^{+}a.

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{n}(\beta a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{n-1}(\beta)^{+}a

\Asc_{n}\alpha=[n]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\beta)^{+}a

(by the definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\I
. Then
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)\in\Sym
.

Proof.

We use induction on the size
n
of
\alpha
, where the case

n\geq 2
. Let
\alpha=\beta a
, for some
\beta\in\I_{n-1}

and

. By Lemma
5.3
, we have

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)=\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\beta)^{+}a
, which is clearly a permutation

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\beta)\in\Sym_{n-1}

∎

Corollary 5.5
.

We have a size-preserving bijection
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}:\I\to\Sym
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.6
and Lemma

, the map

is injective for every
n\geq 0
. The theorem
follows since it is well known that
\I_{n}
and
\Sym_{n}
are equinumerous.
∎

6
Subdiagonal permutations

Recall from Subsection
5.1
that an inversion sequence
\alpha

encodes the construction of
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)
by insertion of a new
rightmost entry. In this section, we restrict
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
to the
set of ascent sequences and characterize the resulting set of permutations
which have the following subdiagonal property.

Any permutation
\pi\in\Sym
factors
uniquely by maximal increasing runs
as
\pi=B_{1}B_{2}\ldots B_{k}
, where
k=n+1-\asc\alpha
.
We say that

\pi
is

\displaystyle\text{\emph{ir-superdiagonal}},

\displaystyle\text{if $c\geq i$ for each $c\in B_{i}$};

\displaystyle\text{\emph{ir-subdiagonal}},

\displaystyle\text{if $c\leq n+1-i$ for each $c\in B_{i}$},

where the prefix “ir” denotes that
\pi
is decomposed by “increasing
runs”. Clearly, two analogous notions are obtained by replacing
maximal increasing runs with maximal decreasing runs; that is, if

\pi=C_{1}\ldots C_{k}
, where now the blocks
C_{i}
are maximally decreasing,
we say that
\pi
is

\displaystyle\text{\emph{dr-superdiagonal}},

\displaystyle\text{if $c\geq i$ for each $c\in C_{i}$};

\displaystyle\text{\emph{dr-subdiagonal}},

\displaystyle\text{if $c\leq n+1-i$ for each $c\in C_{i}$}.

It is easy to see that
\pi
is ir-subdiagonal if and only if its
complement is dr-superdiagonal; similarly, it is dr-subdiagonal if and only
if its complement is ir-superdiagonal.
So it is no restriction to only consider subdiagonal permutations, denoted by

\displaystyle\irsub

\displaystyle=\{\pi:\pi\text{ is ir-subdiagonal}\};

\displaystyle\drsub

\displaystyle=\{\pi:\pi\text{ is dr-subdiagonal}\}.

In the following two subsections, we shall prove that

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\Ascseq_{0})=\irsub\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\wD)=\drsub,

where
\wD
denotes the set of weak descent sequences, defined later.
As a result of what was observed
in Subsection
5.1
, ascent
sequences encode the recursive construction of ir-subdiagonal
permutations by successive insertions of a new rightmost entry.
And weak descent sequences encode dr-subdiagonal permutations
in the same way. This construction is reminiscent of the way
ascent sequences encode Fishburn permutations
[
BMCDK10
]
,
the difference being that in the case of Fishburn permutations a
new maximum is inserted at each step. Note that we have not been able to find
bivincular patterns characterizing
\irsub
and
\drsub
.
Finally, we define an isomorphism between
two generating trees for weak descent sequences and
primitive
ascent sequences
, defined as those ascent sequences that have no
flat steps.

6.1
ir-subdiagonal permutations

Throughout this section, we let
\pi=B_{1}\ldots B_{k}
be the decomposition
of a given permutation
\pi
into maximal increasing runs.
If
c
is an entry of
B_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq k
, we let
\indx_{\pi}(c)=j

denote the index of the block of
\pi
that contains
c
.
Letting
\pi=p_{1}\ldots p_{n}

and
\pi_{i}=p_{1}\ldots p_{i}
, it is easy to see that

\pi\in\irsub\iff p_{i}\leq|\pi|+1-\indx_{\pi}(p_{i})

for each
i
, where

\indx_{\pi}(p_{i})=i+1-\asc\pi_{i}.

(8)

The next lemma shows that ir-subdiagonal permutations and ascent
sequences share a similar recursive structure.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\pi=p_{1}\ldots p_{n}
and
a\in[n+1]
. Then

\pi^{+}a\in\irsub\text{ if and only if }\pi\in\irsub\text{ and }a\leq 1+\asc\pi.

Proof.

We will prove the reverse implication as the forward one is similar.
We start by showing that entries in the prefix
\pi^{+}
satisfy the
subdiagonality constraint in
\pi^{+}a
.
Suppose that
\pi=B_{1}\ldots B_{k}
is the increasing run decomposition of
\pi
so that

\max B_{i}\leq n-i+1
for
i\in[k]
since
\pi\in\irsub
. It follows that

\pi^{+}=B_{1}^{+}\ldots B_{k}^{+}
is the increasing run decomposition of
\pi^{+}
and

\max B_{i}^{+}\leq\max B_{i}+1\leq(n+1)-i+1.

So
\pi^{+}
satisfies the ir-subdiagonal restrictions as the initial factor of
\pi^{+}a
.

There remains to show that
a
also satisfies the ir-subdiagonal restriction. There will be two cases depending on its size relative to
p_{n}
.
Suppose first that
a\leq p_{n}
. Then we have the increasing run decomposition
\pi^{+}a=B_{1}^{+}\ldots B_{k}^{+}B_{k+1}
where
B_{k+1}=a
,
and the desired inequality is
a\leq(n+1)-(k+1)+1
. But since
p_{n}\in B_{k}
we have
p_{n}\leq n-k+1
which, combined with
a\leq p_{n}
, finishes this case.
If
a>p_{n}
then our increasing run decomposition is
\pi^{+}a=B_{1}^{+}\ldots B_{k-1}^{+}B_{k}^{\prime}
where
B_{k}^{\prime}=B_{k}^{+}a
. Since
B_{k}
is the last run of
\pi
we have
k=n-\asc\pi+1
.
In this case we want
a\leq(n+1)-k+1
. But by the equation for
k
and inequality for
a
assumed in this direction

(n+1)-k+1=(n+1)-(n-\asc\pi+1)+1=1+\asc\pi\geq a

which finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\I
and let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)
. Then

\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}\iff\hat{\alpha}\in\irsub.

Therefore,
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
restricts to a bijection from
\Ascseq_{0}

to
\irsub
.

Proof.

We use induction on the size of
\alpha
where the result is clear for
size at most one. Let
\alpha=\beta a
,
for some
\beta\in\I_{n}
. By Lemma
5.3
, we have

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
. Using induction, we have that
\beta\in\Ascseq_{0}

if and only if
\hat{\beta}\in\irsub
. Now using Theorem
4.9

we have

\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}\iff\ \beta\in\Ascseq_{0}\text{ and }a\leq 1+\asc\beta=1+\asc\hat{\beta}.

But, by the lemma just proved, the inequality is equivalent
to
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a\in\irsub
as desired.
∎

We have just shown that the set
\irsub

of ir-subdiagonal permutations is the bijective image of the
set
\Ascseq_{0}
of ascent sequences under
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
.
Furthermore, by Proposition
5.6
primitive ascent sequences are in bijection with
ir-subdiagonal permutations avoiding
\mathfrak{a}
.
The next corollary follows immediately.

Corollary 6.3
.

For each
n\geq 0
, the number of ir-subdiagonal permutations of size
n

is equal to the
n
th Fishburn number, that is, the number of ascent sequences of length
n
. Furthermore, the number of
ir-subdiagonal permutations avoiding
\mathfrak{a}
is equal to the
number of primitive ascent sequences (see also A138265
[
OEI
]
).
∎

6.2
dr-subdiagonal permutations and weak descent sequences

Recall that the set of weak descents of
\alpha\in\End
is

\wDes\alpha=\{i\geq 2\mid a_{i}\leq a_{i-1}\}

Note that
[n]=\wDes\alpha\uplus\Asc\alpha
for every
\alpha\in\End_{n}
;
that is, every
i\in[n]
is either a weak descent or a strict
ascent. The set
\wD
of
weak descent sequences
is defined as

\wD_{n}=\{\alpha\in\I_{n}\mid a_{1}=1\text{ and }a_{i}\leq 1+\wdes\alpha_{i-1}\text{ for each }i\in[n]\},

where
\wdes\alpha=|\wDes\alpha|
.

The next result is a counterpart of Theorem
6.2
and
states that
\alpha\in\I
is a weak descent sequence if and only if

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)
is dr-subdiagonal. Its proof is obtained by simply
replicating the steps of Lemma
6.1
and
Theorem
6.2
, and is thus omitted.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\pi=p_{1}\ldots p_{n}
and
a\in[n+1]
. Then

\pi^{+}a\in\drsub\text{ if and only if }\pi\in\drsub\text{ and }a\leq 1+\wdes\pi.

Furthermore, if
\alpha\in\I
and
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\alpha)
,
then

\alpha\in\wD\iff\hat{\alpha}\in\drsub

and
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
restricts to a bijection from
\wD
to
\drsub
.
∎

We wish to prove that weak descent sequences (and thus dr-subdiagonal
permutations) are equinumerous with primitive ascent sequences.
A generating tree for ascent sequences is encoded by the following
generating rule, where the pair
(a,\ell)
keeps track of the number
of ascents,
a
, and the last letter,
\ell
:

\begin{cases}\text{Root: }(1,1)\\
(a,\ell)\longrightarrow(a,1)(a,2)\ldots(a,\ell-1)(a,\ell)(a+1,\ell+1)(a+1,\ell+2)\ldots(a+1,a+1).\end{cases}

The above rule encodes the standard construction of ascent sequences
by insertion of a new rightmost entry. The root
(1,1)
corresponds
to the only ascent sequence of size one, namely the single letter
word
1
. Further, if
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{0}
has
a
ascents and last
letter
\ell
, then it produces
a+1
children by insertion of a
new rightmost entry
i\in[a+1]
. If
i\leq\ell
, then the number of
ascents remains the same; otherwise, if
i>\ell
, then a new ascent
is created. To obtain a generating rule for primitive ascent
sequences, we remove the child
(a,\ell)
corresponding to a
flat step and obtain:

\Omega:\begin{cases}\text{Root: }(1,1)\\
(a,\ell)\longrightarrow(a,1)(a,2)\ldots(a,\ell-1)(a+1,\ell+1)(a+1,\ell+2)\ldots(a+1,a+1).\end{cases}

A generating rule for weak descent sequences that uses the number of
weak descents
w
and the last letter
u
as parameters is now
obtained similarly as:

\Theta:\begin{cases}\text{Root: }(0,1)\\
(w,u)\longrightarrow(w+1,1)(w+1,2)\ldots(w+1,u)(w,u+1)(w,u+2)\ldots(w,w+1).\end{cases}

In this case, inserting
i\in[w+1]
creates a new weak descent if and
only if
i\leq u
.

To show that primitive ascent sequences and weak
descent sequences are equinumerous, we shall give a bijection between
the generating trees encoded by the rules
\Omega
and
\Theta
.
Namely, we show that
\Omega
and
\Theta
are equivalent under
the linear transformation

\begin{cases}w=a-1\\
u=a-\ell+1\end{cases}\iff\;\;\,\begin{cases}a-w=1\\
\ell+u=a+1.\end{cases}

(9)

Indeed, the root
(a,\ell)=(1,1)
is mapped to
(w,u)=(0,1)
.
Further, assume that
(a,\ell)
is mapped to
(w,u)
, i.e. that
w=a-1
and
u=a+1-\ell
.
Then the children of
(a,\ell)
are mapped bijectively to the children
of
(w,u)
, since

\displaystyle(a,1)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a-1,a)=(w,w+1)

\displaystyle(a,2)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a-1,a-1)=(w,w)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle(a,\ell-1)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a-1,a-\ell+2)=(w,u+1)

\displaystyle(a+1,\ell+1)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a,a-\ell+1)=(w+1,u)

\displaystyle(a+1,\ell+2)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a,a-\ell)=(w+1,u-1)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle(a+1,a+1)

\displaystyle\,\mapsto\,(a,1)=(w+1,1).

As a result, we obtain a bijection between the generating tree of
primitive ascent sequences,
encoded by
\Omega
, and the generating tree of
\wD
,
encoded by
\Theta
. The next result follows immediately.

Corollary 6.5
.

For each
n\geq 0
, the number of weak descent sequences of size
n

is equal to the number of primitive ascent sequences
of size
n
.
∎

We wish to give an alternative definition of
d
-Fishburn
permutations that is reminiscent of the classical case
d=0
.
We say that a permutation
\pi
contains the
d
-Fishburn pattern
,
\fishpattern_{d}
, if it contains an occurrence

p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{j}
of
\fishpattern
where
p_{i}
is
d
-inactive.
The other two elements
p_{i+1}
and
p_{j}
can be either
d
-active
or
d
-inactive.
With a slight abuse, we will use the suggestive notation

\Sym(\fishpattern_{d})

to denote the set of permutations that do not
contain
\fishpattern_{d}
.

Proposition 7.1
.

For every
d\geq 0
,

\F_{d}=\Sym(\fishpattern_{d}).

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\Sym
. We show that
\pi
contains

\fishpattern_{d}
if and only if
\Ascbot\pi\not\subseteq\Act_{d}\pi
.
Initially, suppose that
\pi
contains an occurrence

p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{j}
of
\fishpattern_{d}
. Then
p_{i}\in\Ascbot\pi

and
p_{i}
is not
d
-active. Thus,
\Ascbot\pi\not\subseteq\Act_{d}\pi
,
as wanted. On the other hand, suppose that

\Ascbot\pi\not\subseteq\Act_{d}\pi
. That is, there is an entry
p_{i}

such that
p_{i}\in\Ascbot\pi
and
p_{i}\notin\Act_{d}\pi
.
Note that
p_{i}<p_{i+1}
. Further, since
p_{i}\notin\Act_{d}\pi
,
by definition of
d
-active site we have
p_{i}-1=p_{j}
, for some
j>i
.
Finally, the triple
p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{j}
is an occurrence of

\fishpattern_{d}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Zang and Zhou proved that
\F_{0}
coincides with the set of Fishburn
permutations, while
\F_{1}
is equal to the set of weak Fishburn
permutations introduced by Bényi et al.
[
BCD23
]
.
Further, they showed that
\F_{d}
is tree-like in the following sense.

Consider a set
\Pi
of permutations. As usual, let

\Pi_{n}=\Pi\cap\Sym_{n}
. Say that
\Pi
is
tree-like
if

\Pi_{0}=\{\epsilon\}
(where
\epsilon
is the empty permutation) and, for
n\geq 1
,
every
\pi\in\Pi_{n}
is obtained by inserting
n
into a site of some

\rho\in\Pi_{n-1}
, called the
parent
of
\pi
. The spaces
between letters of
\rho
into which
n
can be inserted are called
the
active sites with respect to
\Pi
, and all other sites
of
\rho
are said to be
inactive
.
Active sites are labeled
1,2,\ldots
from left to right. The active sites
of
\pi\in\F_{d}
are called
d
-active sites
and are the site before

\pi
as well as the sites which lie just after a
d
-active element.

Finally, Zang and Zhou generalized the classical encoding of Fishburn
permutations by ascent sequences to
d
-Fishburn permutations and

d
-ascent sequences; that is, they defined bijections

\Phi_{d}:\Ascseq_{d}\longrightarrow\F_{d}

by letting, recursively,

•

\Phi_{d}(\epsilon)=\epsilon
, and

•

for
n\geq 1
if
\alpha=\beta a\in\A_{d,n}
then
\Phi_{d}(\alpha)
is the
result of inserting
n
into the active site of

\Phi_{d}(\beta)
labeled
a
.

7.1
Burge transpose and Fishburn permutations

The set of
Burge words
is defined as

\Bur_{n}=\left\{\binom{u}{\alpha}\,\big\rvert\,u\in\WI_{n},\;\alpha\in\Cay_{n},\;\wDes(u)\subseteq\wDes(\alpha)\right\},

where
\WI_{n}
is the subset of
\Cay_{n}
consisting of the weakly
increasing Cayley permutations. We define a transposition operation

T
on
\Bur_{n}
as follows
[
CC23b
]
.
To compute the
Burge transpose

w^{T}
of

w=\binom{u}{\alpha}\in\Bur_{n}
, turn each column of
w
upside down
and then sort the columns in ascending order with respect to the
top entry, breaking ties by sorting in weakly decreasing order with
respect to the bottom entry. Observe that
T
is an involution
on
\Bur_{n}
. Now, let
\identity_{n}=12\ldots n
be the identity
permutation. Since
\identity_{n}
has no weak descents,

\binom{\identity_{n}}{\alpha}
is a Burge word for every

\alpha\in\Cay_{n}
. Thus, for any
\alpha\in\Cay_{n}
, we can always
pick
\identity_{n}
as the top row, and we get a
map
\burget:\Cay_{n}\to\Sym_{n}
, defined by

\binom{\identity}{\alpha}^{\!T}=\binom{\sort(\alpha)}{\burget(\alpha)},

for any
\alpha\in\Cay
, where
\sort(\alpha)
is obtained by sorting the
entries of
\alpha
in weakly increasing order.
If
\pi\in\Sym
is a permutation, then
\burget(\pi)=\pi^{-1}

(and thus
\burget
is surjective).
Note that the map
\burget
was originally
[
CC23b
]
denoted
by the letter
\gamma
.

One of the main advantages of modified ascent sequences and the
Burge transpose is that they give a non-recursive description
of the bijection
\Phi_{0}:\Ascseq_{0}\to\F_{0}
.
Indeed
[
BMCDK10
, Corollary 9]
, if
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{0}(\alpha)
is the
modified ascent sequence of
\alpha
, then
\burget(\hat{\alpha})
is the
Fishburn permutation corresponding to
\alpha
under
\Phi_{0}
. With
the ascent sequence
\alpha=121242232
of the example before Lemma
2.1
, we obtain
\hat{\alpha}=141252232
and

\displaystyle\binom{\identity}{\hat{\alpha}}^{\!T}

\displaystyle=\binom{1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7\ 8\ 9}{1\ 4\ 1\ 2\ 5\ 2\ 2\ 3\ 2}^{\!T}

\displaystyle=\binom{1\ 1\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5}{3\ 1\ 9\ 7\ 6\ 4\ 8\ 2\ 5}=\binom{\sort(\hat{\alpha})}{\burget(\hat{\alpha})},

where
\burget(\hat{\alpha})=\Phi_{0}(\alpha)
.

We wish to use the
d
-hat map to generalize the above construction
to every
d\geq 0
. That is, we shall prove that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Ascseq_{d}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\F_{d}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{\Ascseq}_{d}}
\Phi_{d}
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
\burget

(10)

commutes for every
d\geq 0
and that all the arrows are size-preserving
bijections. To this end, it will be convenient to let

\img_{d}=\burget\bigl(\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\Ascseq_{d})\bigr).

Our proof of the commutativity of diagram (
10
) proceeds
in the following steps: First we show that
\burget
is injective so that
the composition with
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
is an injective map. Next we demonstrate
that
\img_{d}
has a tree-like structure and describe the active sites
of its permutations. As a corollary, we obtain
\img_{d}=\F_{d}
.
The equality
\Phi_{d}=\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}
then follows by showing
that
\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}
has a recursive description that is identical to
the one given for
\Phi_{d}
in terms of active sites.

Let us start by proving that
\burget
is injective.
For the rest of this section, given a
d
-ascent sequence
\alpha
,
we let
\hat{\alpha}=\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\alpha)
denote the
d
-hat of
\alpha
.

Proposition 7.2
.

For all
d,n\geq 0
, the map
\burget:\hat{\Ascseq}_{d,n}\rightarrow\Sym_{n}
is injective.

Proof.

We fix
d\geq 0
and use induction on
n
. Let us set up the following
notation for the rest of the proof. We will consider two
d
-ascent
sequences
\alpha
and
\omega
in
\Ascseq_{d,n}
, where

\alpha=\beta a\quad\text{and}\quad\omega=\tau w,

for some
\beta
and
\tau
in
\Ascseq_{d,n-1}
. We also let
a
,
b
,
w
and
t

denote the last letter of
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\omega
and
\tau
, respectively.
By definition of
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{\beta}a&\text{if $a\leq b-d$};\\
\hat{\beta}^{+}a&\text{if $a>b-d$};\\
\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{\tau}w&\text{if $w\leq t-d$};\\
\hat{\tau}^{+}w&\text{if $w>t-d$}.\\
\end{cases}

We assume
\hat{\alpha}\neq\hat{\omega}
and prove that
\burget(\hat{\alpha})\neq\burget(\hat{\omega})
.

Assume first that
\beta\neq\tau
. Since the map
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
is injective
by Corollary
4.7
, we have
\hat{\beta}\neq\hat{\tau}
and
by induction,
\burget(\hat{\beta})\neq\burget(\hat{\tau})
.
Note that sorting
\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}
and
\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}^{+}}^{T}

yields the same permutation in the lower row and similarly for
\hat{\tau}

and
\hat{\tau}^{+}
. So, whichever case of the
\mathrm{hat}_{d}
map we are in, we will
be placing
n
into the two different permutations
\burget(\hat{\beta})

and
\burget(\hat{\tau})
to compute
\burget(\hat{\alpha})
and
\burget(\hat{\omega})
.
This must result in distinct permutations, as desired.

Now assume that
\beta=\tau
. Since
\alpha\neq\omega
it must be that

a\neq w
and we can assume, without loss of generality, that
a<w
.
If
a\leq b-d
, then
\binom{\id}{\hat{\alpha}}^{T}
is computed from

\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}
by placing the column
\binom{a}{n}
at the
beginning of the list of columns with top entry
a
. Note that such
columns must exist because of the given inequality.
If
a>b-d
then
\binom{\id}{\hat{\alpha}}^{T}
is computed from

\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}^{+}}^{T}
by inserting the column
\binom{a}{n}
between
the columns with top entry
a-1
and those with top entry
a+1
(if
there are any of the latter). In this case there will be no other columns
with top entry
a
. It is now a simple matter of checking to show that
in all possible cases the fact that
a<w
will force
n
to be
in
\burget(\hat{\alpha})
strictly to the left of its appearance
in
\burget(\hat{\omega})
. This completes the proof of injectivity.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

For every

, the map
\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}:\A_{d}\rightarrow\img_{d}
is

Proof.

Our claim follows directly from Corollary

,
Proposition

Lemma 7.4
.

For all
d\geq 0
, the set
\img_{d}
is tree-like.

Proof.

Clearly
\epsilon\in\img_{d,0}
, so let
n\geq 1
.
Pick
\pi\in\img_{d,n}
and suppose
\pi=\burget(\hat{\alpha})
,
for some
\alpha\in\Ascseq_{d}
. Suppose that
\alpha=\beta a
and consider

\rho=\burget(\hat{\beta})
. We claim that
\pi
is obtained
by inserting
n
in some site of
\rho
which will prove the theorem.
As usual, there are two cases depending on whether
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
or

\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a
.

Suppose first that
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}a
. As in the proof of
Proposition
7.2
,
\binom{\id}{\hat{\alpha}}^{T}
is obtained from

\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}
by inserting the column

at

is obtained from
\rho=\burget(\hat{\beta})
by

in the corresponding site, which proves the claim

Consider the second case. Here
\binom{\id}{\hat{\alpha}}^{T}
is obtained
from
\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}^{+}}^{T}
by inserting the column
\binom{a}{n}

between the columns with upper entry

and those with upper
entry

, there being no columns with upper entry
a
.
But since
\hat{\beta}
and
\hat{\beta}^{+}
are order isomorphic, it follows again
that insertion of

in the corresponding site of
\rho
yields
\pi
.
∎

Since
\img_{d}
is tree-like, it is natural to want a description of
the active sites of
\rho\in\img_{d}
. By Corollary
7.3
,
the map
\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}:\A_{d}\rightarrow\img_{d}
is a bijection.
If
\beta=(\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d})^{-1}(\rho)
then one could
consider all
d
-ascent sequences of the form

for

.
Computing the permutations
\pi=\burget(\hat{\alpha})
for each

and comparing with
\rho
would accomplish this task. But it would
be nice to have a description of the active sites which can be read off from

To this end, given
\rho\in\img_{d}
, let
\beta\in\Ascseq_{d}
be its preimage. Now

\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}=\binom{\sort(\hat{\beta})}{\rho}.

We shall use the active sites of

to define a labeling of the sites
of
\sort(\hat{\beta})
by letting a site of
\sort(\hat{\beta})
be active if and only

In this context, active sites refer to the tree-like structure of

established in Lemma

, and should not be confused
with the active sites with respect to
\F_{d}
. We will first describe
the active sites of

. To do so we need the concept of a

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose
\beta\in\Ascseq_{d,n-1}
.

(a)

The elements of the runs of
\sort(\hat{\beta})
are

1,2,\ldots,\asc_{d}\beta
from left to right.

(b)

The active sites of
\sort(\hat{\beta})
are the sites before,
after, or between its runs.

(c)

The number of active sites of
\burget(\hat{\beta})
is equal
to
\asc_{d}\beta+1
.

Proof.

(a)
This follows directly from Proposition
3.3
.

(b)
Suppose
\alpha=\beta a
where

.
If
a
does not create a
d
-ascent then
\binom{\id}{\hat{\alpha}}^{T}

is obtained from
\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}
by inserting the column

\binom{a}{n}
at the beginning of the run of
a
’s in
\sort(\hat{\beta})
.
This will be the site before
\sort(\hat{\beta})
or the site between the run
of
(a-1)
’s and the run of
a
’s.
Now suppose
a
does cause a

-ascent so that
\hat{\alpha}=\hat{\beta}^{+}a

Note that the runs of
\sort(\hat{\beta}

\sort(\hat{\beta}^{+})

Now

\binom{\id}{\hat{\beta}}^{T}

’s in

and

Since

.

(c)
From Item

.

Furthermore, a site of
\pi\in\F_{d,n}=\img_{d,n}
is
\F_{d}
-active
if and only if it is
\img_{d}
-active.

Proof.

We use induction on
n
, where the claim holds for
n\leq 1
.
Let
n\geq 2
and assume that
\F_{d,n-1}=\img_{d,n-1}
.
By induction, given
\rho\in\F_{d,n-1}=\img_{d,n-1}
, a site of
\rho

is
\F_{d}
-active if and only if it is
\img_{d}
-active.
Since both
\img_{d}
and
\F_{d}
are tree-like, by Lemmas
7.4

and
7.8
, respectively, the equality
\F_{d,n}=\img_{d,n}

follows immediately.

Let us now consider a permutation
\pi\in\F_{d,n}=\img_{d,n}
.
We have to show that a site of
\pi
is
\F_{d}
-active if and only if
it is
\img_{d}
-active.
The site before the leftmost entry is active in both cases by item (b)
of Lemma
7.5
and by Lemma
7.8
.
Now, let
\rho\in\F_{d,n-1}=\img_{d,n-1}
be the parent of
\pi
.
By Lemma
7.7
each common site is
\img_{d}
-active
in
\pi
if and only if it is
\img_{d}
-active in
\rho
; and
the new site before
n
is
\img_{d}
-active.
Similarly, by definition of
d
-active entry and Lemma
7.8
,
each of these sites is
\F_{d}
-active in
\pi
if and only if it is

\F_{d}
-active in
\rho
, and
n
is always placed in an
\F_{d}
-active site
which is directly after an
\F_{d}
-active element.
Since by induction
\F_{d}
-active and
\img_{d}
-active sites of
\rho

coincide, the desired claim holds for every common site, as well as for
the new site before
n
.

To finish the proof of the theorem, we only need to consider
the new site after
n
. Using the same notation as in
Lemma
7.7
, let
s
and
t
be the number of

\img_{d}
-active sites before
n
in
\pi
and before
n-1
in
\rho
,
respectively. By this lemma, the site after
n
is
\img_{d}
-active
if and only if
s>t-d
. If
n
appears to the right of
n-1
in
\pi
,
then
n
is
\F_{d}
-active. Moreover, we have
s\geq t+1
since the site
before
n
is
\img_{d}
-active. Thus

s\geq t+1>t\geq t-d

and the site after
n
is
\img_{d}
-active, as desired.
On the other hand, suppose that
n
appears to the left of
n-1
.
Write

\pi=g_{1}\ldots g_{i}\;n\;g_{i+1}\ldots g_{j}\;(n-1)\;g_{j+1}\ldots g_{n-1},

for some
i\leq j
.
We have

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\img_{d}$-active sites before $(n-1)$ in $\rho$}

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\F_{d}$-active sites before $(n-1)$ in $\rho$}

by induction, and

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\img_{d}$-active sites before $n$ in $\pi$}

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\img_{d}$-active sites before $g_{i+1}$ in $\rho$}

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\F_{d}$-active sites before $g_{i+1}$ in $\rho$}

where the last step is again by induction. Therefore,

\displaystyle t-s

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\F_{d}$-active sites between $g_{i+1}$ and $n-1$ in $\rho$}

\displaystyle=\text{$\#$ $\F_{d}$-active entries in $g_{i+1}\ldots g_{j}$ in $\rho$},

where at the last step we used Lemma
7.8
.
Finally, by Lemma
7.7
, the site after
n
is
\img_{d}
-active if and only if

s>t-d
. Rearranging terms gives

which is equivalent to

being a
d
-active element by the definition of
d
-active entries. In turn, this is equivalent to the site after
n
being
\F_{d}
-active by Lemma
7.8
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.10
.

For any
d\geq 0
,

\Phi_{d}=\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}.

Proof.

We have established in Theorem
7.9
that the maps
\Phi_{d}

and
\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}
have the same image
\F_{d}=\img_{d}
.
Let us prove inductively that
\Phi_{d}=\burget\circ\,\mathrm{hat}_{d}
.
Let
\alpha=\beta a\in\Ascseq_{d,n}
, where
\beta\in\Ascseq_{d,n-1}
and

. By induction, we have

\Phi_{d}(\beta)=\burget(\mathrm{hat}_{d}(\beta))=:\rho.

Again by Theorem

, a site of
\rho
is
\F_{d}
-active if
and only if it is
\img_{d}
-active. Moreover, the last letter of

is inserted both

∎

8
Pattern avoidance in
\F_{d}

The introduction and characterization of the
d
-Fishburn permutations
opens the door to pattern avoidance results parameterized by
d
. As an
illustration, we shall study one such instance in some depth, namely
the case of
d
-Fishburn permutations avoiding the classical pattern
213
.
First, recall the bivincular pattern

\sigma_{d}=(d+2)|(d+3)12\dots d\overline{(d+1)}.

Zang and Zhou
[
ZZ
, Theorem 2.4]
proved that

\F_{d}\subseteq\Sym(\sigma_{d})

(11)

for every
d\geq 0
, where for
d=0,1

equality holds.

Proposition 8.1
.

We have
\F_{d}(213)=\Sym(\sigma_{d},213)
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\F_{d}(213)\subseteq\Sym(\sigma_{d},213)
follows
from (
11
). For the same reason, if
d\leq 1
we obtain the
desired equality.
Now let
d\geq 2
. We shall prove the remaining inclusion

\Sym(\sigma_{d},213)\subseteq\F_{d}(213)
. Let
\pi\in\Sym_{d}(\sigma,213)
.
For a contradiction, suppose that
\pi\notin\F_{d}
. That is,

\pi
contains an occurrence
p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{j}
of
\fishpattern

where
p_{i}
is not a
d
-active element.
Since
p_{i}
is not
d
-active, there are at least
d
entries

p_{u_{1}},\dots,p_{u_{d}}
,
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{d}
, between
p_{i+1}

and
p_{j}
that are smaller than
p_{j}
(and
d
-active).
Further, since
d\geq 2
, these must be in increasing order or else
they would create an occurrence of
213
with
p_{j}
.
Thus we have obtained an occurrence
p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{u_{1}}\ldots p_{u_{d}}p_{j}

of
\sigma_{d}
, which is impossible.
∎

In order to enumerate
\F_{d}(213)
, we show that
\Sym_{n}(\sigma_{d},213)

is in bijection with the set of Dyck paths of semilength
n
that do
not contain
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
as a factor.
Let us start by defining a bijection
\phi
from
\Sym_{n}(213)
to Dyck
paths of semilength
n
. It is simply a tilted version of what is
sometimes called
[
CK08
]
the
standard bijection
from

132
-avoiding permutations to Dyck paths. Any non-empty
permutation
\pi\in\Sym(213)
decomposes uniquely as

\pi=p_{1}LR,

where all the entries in
L
are larger than
p_{1}
, and all the entries
in
R
are smaller than
p_{1}
. Then
\phi
is defined recursively
by mapping the empty permutation to the empty path and letting

\phi(\pi)=\phi(p_{1}LR)=\mathtt{U}\phi(L)\mathtt{D}\phi(R),

where here we abuse notation and use the same letter
L
for the
permutation that is order isomorphic to
L
. Under the bijection
\phi
,
the value of the first letter determines the first return to the

x
-axis.

We show that
\phi
restricts to a bijection from
\Sym(\sigma_{d},213)
to
Dyck paths avoiding
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
as a factor, for every
d\geq 0
.
First a lemma whose easy proof is omitted.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\pi\in\Sym_{n}(213)
and let
\rho=\phi(\pi)

be the corresponding Dyck path. Then

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{k}\;\iff\;\mathtt{U}^{k}\text{ is a prefix of $\rho$.}}\hfill\qed

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
\pi\in\Sym_{n}(213)
and let
\rho=\phi(\pi)

be the corresponding Dyck path. Then, for any
d\geq 0
,

\pi\text{ contains }\sigma_{d}\;\iff\;\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}\text{ is a factor of $\rho$.}

Proof.

We use induction on
n
, where
n=0
and
n=1
are trivial. Assume our
claim holds for
n-1
where
n\geq 2
and let
\pi=p_{1}LR\in\Sym_{n}(213)
. Initially, suppose that
\pi
contains an occurrence

p_{i}p_{i+1}p_{u_{1}}\ldots p_{u_{d}}p_{j}
of
\sigma_{d}
. If either

p_{j}\in L
or
p_{i}\in R
, then we can conclude that
\rho
contains
a factor
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
by induction.
Otherwise, since entries in
L
are larger than entries in
R
, it must
necessarily be that
p_{i+1}\in L
while
p_{u_{1}}
is contained in
R
.
Moreover, since
p_{j}=p_{i}-1
, we have
i=1
and
p_{j}
is the largest
entry in
R
.
Now, since
L
is not empty, the path
\mathtt{U}\phi(L)\mathtt{D}
ends with
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}
.
Furthermore, since
\pi
avoids
213
, all the entries preceding
p_{j}

in
R
are in increasing order. Taking
p_{j}
into account, (at least)
the first
d+1
entries of
R
are in increasing order. Using
Lemma
8.2
, it follows that
\phi(R)
starts with
\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
.
Hence the last two steps of
\mathtt{U}\phi(L)\mathtt{D}
form a factor
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}

with the first
d+1
steps of
\phi(R)
, as wanted.

On the other hand, suppose that
\rho
contains a factor
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
.
We will show that
\pi
contains
\sigma_{d}
. Similarly to the argument in
the previous paragraph, if the whole factor
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
is
contained in either
\phi(L)
or
\phi(R)
, then we can conclude the proof by
induction. Otherwise, it must be that the last two steps
of
\mathtt{U}\phi(L)\mathtt{D}
are
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}
and the first
d+1
steps of
\phi(R)

are
\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
. Since
\phi(L)
is not empty, we have
p_{1}<p_{2}
.
Using Lemma
8.2
once again, we have that the
first
d+1
entries of
R
, say
p_{u_{1}},\dots,p_{u_{d}},p_{u_{d+1}}
,
are in increasing order. Finally, the maximum entry of
R
is equal
to
p_{1}-1
, and we obtain the desired occurrence

p_{1}p_{2}p_{u_{1}}\ldots p_{u_{d}}(p_{i}-1)
of
\sigma_{d}
in
\pi
.
∎

For any fixed
d\geq 0
, we shall derive a generating function for the
numbers
\#\F_{d,n}(213)
. By the preceding proposition we can achieve
this by counting Dyck paths having no
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
factor. In fact, we
shall derive a generating function for the distribution of the number of

\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
factors over Dyck paths. Let us start with the case

d=0
. In the spirit of the cluster method
[
GJ79
,
Wan11
]
,
consider Dyck paths in which a subset of the
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factors have been
marked. For instance,

\rho=\mathtt{U}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}\underline{\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}\underline{\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}}\mathtt{D}

has three
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factors, two of which have been marked
(underlined). Let us encode
\rho
as a word
\rho^{\prime}
over the alphabet

\{\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\}
by replacing each marked
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factor with a

\mathtt{D}^{\prime}
. In our example we have

\rho^{\prime}=\mathtt{U}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\mathtt{D}.

Note that
\rho^{\prime}
represents a marked Dyck path if and only if
\rho^{\prime}

itself is a Dyck path, when interpreting
\mathtt{D}^{\prime}
as
\mathtt{D}
, and the height
at which any
\mathtt{D}^{\prime}
step starts is at least two.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\rangle
be the formal sum of
Dyck paths with two sorts of down steps,
\mathtt{D}
and
\mathtt{D}^{\prime}
. By the
usual first return decomposition
\mathcal{P}_{0}
satisfies

\mathcal{P}_{0}=1+\mathtt{U}\mathcal{P}_{0}\mathtt{D}\mathcal{P}_{0}+\mathtt{U}\mathcal{P}_{0}\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{0}.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\rangle
be the formal sum of the subset
of the paths encoded in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
defined by requiring that the height
at which any
\mathtt{D}^{\prime}
step starts is at least two. Then

\mathcal{Q}_{0}=(\mathtt{U}\mathcal{P}_{0}\mathtt{D})^{*},

where we use the (Kleene star) convention

\mathcal{F}^{*}=1+\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{F}^{2}+\cdots
. Define the map

\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\rangle\to\mathbb{Q}[q,x]
by

\mathtt{U}\mapsto x
,
\mathtt{D}\mapsto 1
,
\mathtt{D}^{\prime}\mapsto qx
and extending by
linearity. Now, letting
P_{0}(q,x)=\varphi(\mathcal{P}_{0})

and

, we get the functional equations:

\displaystyle P_{0}(q,x)

\displaystyle=1+xP_{0}(q,x)^{2}+qx^{2}P_{0}(q,x)^{2};

\displaystyle Q_{0}(q,x)

\displaystyle=1/(1-xP_{0}(q,x)).

Note that

\sum_{\rho}\,(1+q)^{\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}(\rho)}\,x^{|\rho|}\,=\,Q_{0}(q,x),

where the sum ranges over all Dyck paths,
|\rho|
is the semilength of

\rho
, and
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}(\rho)
is short for the number of
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factors
in
\rho

counts Dyck paths with
respect to semilength and number of marked

factors, but so does
the left-hand side: For each of the
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factors there is a choice to
be made, mark it (with a

Q_{0}(q-1,x)\,=\,\sum_{\rho}\,q^{\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}(\rho)}\,x^{|\rho|}

(12)

In particular,
Q_{0}(-1,x)
is
the generating function for Dyck paths with no
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}
factors.

A similar analysis applies when

\rho

\rho^{\prime}

\mathtt{U}^{\prime}

\mathtt{U}^{d-1}

\mathtt{U}^{\prime}

Let
\mathcal{P}_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathtt{U},\mathtt{U}^{\prime},\mathtt{D}\rangle

\mathtt{U}
and
\mathtt{U}^{\prime}

\mathcal{P}_{d}=1+\mathtt{U}\mathcal{P}_{d}\mathtt{D}\mathcal{P}_{d}+\mathtt{U}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathtt{D}\mathcal{P}_{d})^{d-1}.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathtt{U},\mathtt{U}^{\prime},\mathtt{D}\rangle

of the paths encoded in

Define

\mathtt{U}\mapsto x
,
\mathtt{D}\mapsto 1
and
\mathtt{U}^{\prime}\mapsto qx^{d+1}

P_{d}(q,x)=\varphi(\mathcal{P}_{d})

\displaystyle P_{d}(q,x)

\displaystyle=1+xP_{d}(q,x)^{2}+qx^{d+1}P_{d}(q,x)^{d-1};

\displaystyle Q_{d}(q,x)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{x}{1-xP_{d}(q,x)}}.

By following the same line of reasoning as were used to demonstrate

Proposition 8.4
.

For any
d\geq 0
,

\sum_{\rho}\,q^{\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}(\rho)}\,x^{|\rho|}\,=\,Q_{d}(q-1,x),

where the sum ranges over all Dyck paths,
|\rho|
is the semilength
of
\rho
, and
\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}(\rho)
is short for the number of

\mathtt{D}\mathtt{D}\mathtt{U}^{d+1}
factors in
\rho
.
∎

The transport of patterns between Fishburn permutations and modified
ascent sequences developed by the first two authors
[
CC23b
]

applies to

-Fishburn permutations and modified
d
-ascent sequences as well.
Call two Cayley permutations
\alpha
and
\beta

equivalent
if

\burget(\alpha)=\burget(\beta)
, and let
[\Cay]
denotes the set of equivalence classes over
\Cay
defined this way. Moreover, an element
[\alpha]

of
[\Cay]
contains
[\rho]
if
\alpha^{\prime}
contains
\rho^{\prime}
for
some
\alpha^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
and
\rho^{\prime}\in[\rho]
.
We denote by

[\Cay][\rho]
the
set of classes that avoid
[\rho]
.
By the transport theorem on equivalence classes of Cayley
permutations
[
CC23b
, Theorem 4.9]
, the Burge transpose induces
a bijection

\burget:[\Cay][\rho]\to\Sym\bigl(\burget(\rho)\bigr).

Since each equivalence
class contains at most one modified ascent sequence
and
\burget(\hat{\Ascseq}_{0})=\F_{0}
, we obtain a size-preserving bijection

\burget:\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}[\rho]\to\F_{0}\bigl(\burget(\rho)\bigr),

where
\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}[\rho]

avoiding every pattern in
[\rho]
. Equivalently
[

, Theorem 5.1]
,
for every permutation
\tau

\burget:\hat{\Ascseq}_{0}(B_{\tau})\to\F_{0}(\tau),

where

is the Fishburn basis of

.
A constructive procedure to compute

was given in the
same reference.

Now we have proved in Proposition

that the map
\burget

is injective on
\Modasc_{d}
for every
d\geq 0
. Therefore, each
equivalence class of Cayley permutations contains at most one
modified
d
-ascent sequence. Since
\burget(\Modasc_{d})=\F_{d}
,
we obtain the following transport theorem.

Theorem 8.6
.

For any
d\geq 0
and permutation
\tau
,

\burget:\Modasc_{d}(B_{\tau})\longrightarrow\F_{d}(\tau)

is a size-preserving bijection, where
B_{\tau}
is the Fishburn basis
of
\tau
,
\Modasc_{d}(B_{\tau})
is the set of modified
d
-ascent
sequences avoiding every pattern in
B_{\tau}
, and
F_{d}(\tau)
is the
set of
d
-Fishburn permutations avoiding
\tau
. In particular,

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\#\F_{d,n}(\tau)=\#\Modasc_{d,n}(B_{\tau}).}\hfill\qed

For instance,
B_{213}=\{112,213\}
and by combining Theorems
8.5

and
8.6
we get the following result.

Corollary 8.7
.

For any
d\geq 0
,

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\Modasc_{d}(112,213)}x^{|\alpha|}\,=\,Q_{d}(-1,x).}\hfill\qed

It would be interesting to make a deeper study of pattern avoidance in
d
-Fishburn permutations
and (modified)
d
-ascent sequences.

9
Final remarks

It would be desirable to have a better understanding of
\Modinv
.
Computer calculations show that the first few terms of
the sequence
|\Modinv_{n}|
, starting from
n=0
, are

1,1,3,10,43,224,1396,10136,84057.

We also recall the open problem from Section
5
.

Problem 9.1
.

Find a characterization of which Cayley permutations lie in
\Modinv
,
perhaps similar to that of
\Ascseq_{0}
in equation (
1
).

There are many properties of the bijection
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
which remain
to be investigated.
In Section
6
, we characterized the image of
\Ascseq_{0}

under this map. It is natural to ask which sets of permutations are obtained
by restricting
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
to the set
\Ascseq_{0}(p)
of ascent sequences which
avoid a pattern
p
. In this regard, we have several conjectures.

Conjecture 9.2
.

The map
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}
restricts to the following bijections.

(a)

\Ascseq_{0}(123)\longrightarrow\Sym(123,213)
,

(b)

\Ascseq_{0}(112)\longrightarrow\Sym(213,312)
,

(c)

\Ascseq_{0}(121)\longrightarrow\Sym(213,231)
,

(d)

\Ascseq_{0}(213)\longrightarrow\Sym(213,45123)
.

We note that the enumeration of
\Ascseq_{0}(p)
, for

p\in\{111,211,221,231,312\}
, is currently open.

One could also hope to find analogues of the characterization of

\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\Ascseq_{0})
in terms of ir-subdiagonal permutations for
larger
d
.

Question 9.3
.

What can we say about
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\Ascseq_{d})
, for
d>0
?
Since
\Ascseq_{0}\subseteq\Ascseq_{d}
, can we describe
\mathrm{hat}_{\max}(\Ascseq_{d})

by a similar notion of subdiagonality?

The approach adopted in Section
6
can be generalized as
follows. Let
U\subseteq\I
be any subset of
\I
. Given any
\alpha\in U
,
choose uniquely a nonnegative integer
d_{\alpha}
, with
d_{\alpha}\geq\mind\alpha
.
By Proposition
4.6
, we obtain an injection

\displaystyle\{(\alpha,d_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\in U}\;

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;\Modinv

\displaystyle(\alpha,d_{\alpha})\;

\displaystyle\longmapsto\;\mathrm{hat}_{d_{\alpha}}(\alpha).

What other choices of
U
and
d_{\alpha}
give interesting examples?
A natural choice consists in using
d_{\alpha}=\mind\alpha
. Can we
describe the corresponding subset of
\Modinv
?
Conversely, what sets of permutations
T\subseteq\Sym
can
be pulled back to interesting sets of pairs

\{(\alpha,d_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\in U}
?
