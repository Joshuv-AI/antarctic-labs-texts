A comparative analysis of influenza and COVID-19: Environmental-ecological impacts, socioeconomic implications, and future challenges
Authors: Yongman Guo, Kuiying Gu, Paul A Garber, Ruiling Zhang, Zijian Zhao, Lei Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We systematically reviewed the impacts of climate change, environmental, and socioeconomic factors on influenza and COVID-19 prevalence.•We analyzed and assessed the impacts of COVID-19 pandemic on the occurrence and epidemic of influenza.•We summarized lessons and implications to refine public health strategies, enhance pandemic preparedness, and strengthen healthcare system resilience.Keywords: Influenza, Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), Epidemiological characteristics, Influencing factors, Public health challenges

Introduction

Influenza, commonly known as the flu, is caused by influenza viruses and has been a recurring seasonal health challenge for decades [1]. It spreads in a similar manner to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and presents symptoms such as fever, cough, fatigue, and body aches. Complications can include pneumonia, especially in high-risk groups such as the elderly, the very young, and the immunocompromised [2], [3]. At the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, many countries implemented strict public health measures aimed at preventing its spread. These measures had an unintended impact on the circulation of other respiratory viruses, including influenza. However, following the relaxation of these measures, there was a resurgence in influenza circulation in late 2021 [4]. The interaction between the COVID-19 pandemic and the dynamics of influenza virus spread serves as a reminder of the complex interplay between infectious diseases and measures taken to control them [5], [6], [7]. At present, multiple challenges to global public health security are emerging. Climate change, environmental degradation, and the threat of potential infectious disease X have forced national governments and international institutions to improve their ability to respond to health emergencies. As we navigate these challenges, it is essential to address the unique characteristics of individual diseases and prioritize cooperative efforts for effective mitigation and response strategies.

We reviewed the history of influenza epidemics and found that, in addition to climate change and socioeconomic impacts on the occurrence and prevalence of influenza, the COVID-19 pandemic reduced the incidence of influenza. Is the outbreak of COVID-19 causing a decrease in incidences of influenza, or are prevention and control policies for COVID-19 affecting influenza? Given the unique circumstances of the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on healthcare, society, and the global economy, further investigation is needed to better understand the dynamics of how influenza and COVID-19 interact.

We utilized a systematic literature review to investigate the combined influence of climate change, environmental, and social factors on respiratory disease epidemics. We conducted a comprehensive search of electronic databases (e.g., PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) using a combination of keywords related to climate change, environmental change, social development, COVID-19, and influenza. The search strategy included Boolean operators and relevant Medical Subject Headings. More than 150 highly cited articles were published after 2000 and 69 were analyzed and summarized after screening (Fig. 1).

According to the theme and main purpose, here, we comprehensively and systematically review the differences and epidemiological connections between influenza and COVID-19 from the perspectives of epidemiological characteristics, ecological and socioeconomic impacts, and future challenges and implications. Systematic interdisciplinary research that considers the interactive effects of these healthcare challenges is crucial for developing more accurate and effective strategies for managing concurrent health threats. As we continue to learn from these experiences, we can better prepare for potential future scenarios and develop more robust and specific responses to mitigate the impacts of multiple health threats.

Epidemiological characteristics

Current status of influenza and COVID-19 epidemiology

Influenza epidemics have affected the global population for more than a century, causing significant morbidity, mortality and economic burdens each year [8]. Seasonal epidemic outbreaks of influenza are estimated to affect up to one billion people each year, causing three to five million severe cases and up to 650,000 deaths annually [9]. The highest mortality rates are reported in Sub-Saharan Africa (2.8 – 16.5 per 100,000 individuals) and Southeast Asia (3.5 – 9.2 per 100,000 individuals) [10]. In the United States, between 2010 and 2020, the flu resulted in 9-41 million illnesses, 140,000 – 710,000 hospitalizations, and 12,000 – 52,000 deaths annually [11]. In China, influenza resulted in 84 – 144 million illnesses between 2011 and 2019 [12]. Over the same period, the number of cases of influenza-related respiratory deaths in China averaged 88,100 per year, accounting for 8.2 % of reported deaths from respiratory diseases [13]. As influenza tends to be a seasonal illness, it can stress and overwhelm the healthcare system both physically and economically. Unlike non-respiratory infectious diseases, the entire population is susceptible to influenza, with high-risk groups such as infants, pregnant women, the elderly, and individuals with chronic conditions being more prone to developing severe complications and facing a greater risk of hospitalization and death after following infection [12].

COVID-19 was characterized as a pandemic in January 2020 and lasted until May 2023, when the World Health Organization (WHO) declared that the global health emergency was over [14]. As of August 2, 2023, 768,983,095 confirmed cases of COVID-19, including 6,953,743 deaths, have been reported to the WHO globally. The three countries with the greatest number of COVID-19 deaths were the United States (1,177,223), Brazil (702,116), and India (533,472) [15]. COVID-19 is highly contagious and can spread rapidly within communities, leading to large-scale outbreaks and the exponential growth of infected individuals. Lockdowns, travel restrictions, and business closures aimed at controlling this virus have caused significant economic disruptions, job losses, and societal strain [16]. However, COVID-19 has been much more than a health crisis. It not only causes severe social and economic upheaval but also leaves millions of people experiencing loneliness, isolation, anxiety, and depression [14]. At the same time, critical uncertainties about virus evolution persist, which makes it difficult to predict future transmission dynamics or seasonality of unknown X-diseases, including respiratory diseases that are annual epidemics across the globe.

The effects of environment and climate

Ambient air pollution, including PM10, PM2.5, and SO2, can exacerbate the spread of influenza and other respiratory viruses [17]. On a global scale, influenza seasons differ between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres due to different climatic conditions [5], [18]. In temperate climates, seasonal epidemics occur mainly during winter, while in tropical regions, influenza may occur throughout the year, causing more irregular outbreaks [19]. Due to the impact of climate change, the frequency, severity, and intensity of extreme weather events such as heatwaves, droughts, wildfires, hurricanes, and floods are expected to increase [20]. Moreover, climate-induced environmental changes can affect the migration of animal species and their preferred habitats, which is expected to enhance the global spread of pathogens and facilitate cross-species transmission [21]. These changes include shifts in the movement patterns of migratory birds, which are potential carriers of influenza [21]. From the perspective of human immunity, extremely hot or cold environments can weaken the immune system, increasing individual susceptibility to infection [22]. Extreme weather events can also alter human behavior, leading to increased indoor crowding or migration to shared public spaces [23], which can facilitate transmission and increase the likelihood of outbreaks. Moreover, low humidity (80 % relative humidity) and high temperatures (30 ℃) can prevent aerosol transmission of seasonal human influenza viruses [24].

In terms of the COVID-19 pandemic, scientists continue to focus on the origin of the coronavirus and the multiple factors that drive severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) mutations [25]. Land-use changes, deforestation, and wildlife trading have increased the risk of zoonotic disease spillover, and climate change has long been identified as a critical driver of these risks [26]. However, studies examining the links between climatic factors and COVID-19 have yielded inconclusive results. Some have shown that the persistence of SARS-CoV-2 on surfaces or in the air is sensitive to temperature, humidity, and ultraviolet light and that hot, dry places may transmit less, with viral spread reduced in summer compared to winter [27], [28]. In contrast, N. Ali and F. Islam [29] demonstrated that both short-term and long-term exposure to air pollution, especially PM2.5 and NO2, may contribute significantly to increased rates of COVID-19 infection and mortality.

While climate change and environmental conditions can influence COVID-19 transmission, the specific impacts and outcomes are not reflected consistently around globe. We systematically compared the effects of multiple environmental-climatic factors on the transmission of influenza and COVID-19, and found a strong positive correlation between poor air quality and increased transmission rates of both influenza and COVID-19 (Table 1). After this result, we will conduct a multiple regression model to assess the combined impact of these factors on disease transmission rates in the future. Climatic factors showed mixed and variable results on influenza and COVID-19 (Table 1), which means that such diseases are more prone to relapse and outbreak in the face of climate change. Therefore, given the complex and interconnected nature of environmental-climatic change and infectious disease, understanding the potential impacts of such factors on respiratory diseases and disease X requires interdisciplinary research involving epidemiology, climatology, ecologists, and public health.

Social and economic influences

While environmental and climatic factors influence the transmission and severity of both influenza and COVID-19, they constitute a complex puzzle. Socioeconomic factors can have a direct bearing on influenza prevention measures in relation to the quality of healthcare and the response of healthcare systems [30], [31]. Patterns of population movement and the age structure of the population play significant roles in the spread and transmission of infectious diseases [32], and socioeconomic factors are more likely to explain the differences in the impact of infectious diseases between different geographical locations [33]. For instance, data from China on real-time population flows between cities indicate that limiting the movement of people living in high-risk areas effectively prevented the spread of large-scale viral outbreaks [34]. Additionally, higher population density in urban areas facilitates the rapid spread of influenza due to close proximity and increased interaction [35]. Furthermore, housing conditions, socialization habits, and risk perception have potential implications for exposure and transmission [36].

Economic investment is critical to influenza prevention and related public health improvements, while economic inequalities within and between countries can strongly influence societies’ resilience to health crises [37]. Individuals with lower economic status generally have limited access to healthcare, which can delay diagnosis and treatment [38]. Similarly, well-functioning healthcare systems require large financial investments, and research and development into vaccine creation and production require appropriate infrastructure and the human capital of well-trained scientists. In this regard, COVID-19 has provided evidence that economic inequalities tend to erode the resilience of even robust healthcare systems [37]. Many wealthy and highly industrialized countries were unable to protect their citizens from high rates of COVID-19 transmission and mortality. For example, the United States, Brazil, and Mexico account for one-third (32 %) of the world’s reported COVID-19 deaths [39], despite having relatively high levels of per capita income [40]. Although these factors are likely to affect the spread of influenza, countries that experienced high levels of COVID-19 cases and deaths generally prioritized and accelerated vaccination to achieve high population immunity, implemented travel bans and quarantine requirements, and tested travelers to prevent the introduction of new variants. These actions were also effective in countries and regions with high influenza infection rates [41].

Social and economic factors vary widely across regions and populations, making it challenging to generalize predictions about disease transmission dynamics and outcomes. Better healthcare infrastructure is associated with lower transmission rates for COVID-19 but less so for influenza, while social factors show direct correlation with the infection rates for both diseases (Table 1). Meanwhile, surveillance data on infectious disease transmission may be incomplete, inconsistent, or lacking in certain regions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries with limited surveillance and healthcare infrastructure. Poor data quality can hinder the accuracy and reliability of predictive models and public health interventions. In addition, social and economic factors interact with biological, environmental, and behavioral determinants of health in complex ways, making it difficult to isolate their unique contributions to disease transmission. Therefore, multifactorial models that account for the interplay between different determinants are needed to improve predictions and guide effective public health interventions.

The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on influenza

As COVID-19 evolved into a global pandemic and mitigation strategies faced increased pressure to flatten the curve of virus transmission, an increasing number of countries turned to nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) to control it [34]. These NPIs mainly include hygiene and distancing measures, such isolating ill individuals, quarantining exposed individuals, travel restrictions, school and workplace closures, cancelling mass gatherings, and increased handwashing and masking. These actions helped contain the spread and reduce the size of the COVID-19 pandemic [42]. NPIs and behavioral changes designed to mitigate COVID-19 appear to have also affected the transmission dynamics of influenza and other respiratory diseases [43]. By comparing influenza patterns from 2019 to 2020 with those of 2011 to 2019, L. Feng et al. [44] reported that COVID-19 outbreaks and related NPIs may have reduced influenza in China and the United States by 79.3 % and 67.2 %, respectively. The data from the Pan American Health Organization’s (PAHO) FluNet revealed the positive infection rates of influenza decreased significantly after COVID-19 epidemic (Fig. 2) [45].

Influenza viruses have a short interval of two to four days in successive patients, with viral excretion peaking during the first one to three days of illness [46]. This means that there is limited time to effectively implement isolation and quarantine measures. This led some to the assumption that NPIs would not be effective in controlling influenza viruses [47]. Interestingly, H. Markel et al. [48] reported that the effectiveness of NPIs was not based on their introduction but rather on the time of adoption, duration, and choice or combination of NPIs, which suggests a strong association between early, sustained, and layered application of NPIs and mitigating the consequences of influenza epidemics. Moreover, substantial asymptomatic infection associated with influenza has created difficulties in finding patients to initiate nonpharmaceutical treatments [49]. In addition, both England and the United States indicated a marked reduction in vaccination coverage during the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic [50], and the roll-out of effective COVID-19 vaccines in 2021 led to a decline of 2 % – 4 % in global basic pediatric vaccination coverage and flu vaccination coverage [51]. As healthcare systems around the world have redirected resources and the capacity to manage COVID-19, this shift likely led to changes in how influenza cases were diagnosed, treated, and reported, leading to the misdiagnosis of some COVID-19 cases as influenza [52] or other febrile illnesses [53]. Given the waning population immunity against influenza and reduced genetic diversity of circulating influenza viruses [54], predictions of future influenza outbreaks remain unclear, creating challenges for the development of preemptive control strategies. More importantly, whether future infectious disease X will have an impact on respiratory diseases like influenza requires a comprehensive analysis of the pathogen’s unique characteristics and environmental, social, and public health factors.

Challenges and prospects

Future respiratory diseases

The world continues to be confronted by longstanding, emerging, and reemerging infectious disease threats. Intense viral outbreaks are increasing rapidly in response to factors such as population growth, climate change, and frequent contact between humans and disease-causing animals [55]. The maximum infectivity of some seasonal infectious diseases, such as influenza, may increase due to impending climate and environmental changes [56]. The increase in global temperature, alterations in precipitation patterns, and increasing frequency of extreme weather events are hallmark manifestations of climate change. In parallel, climate change also alters infectious disease dynamics in migratory species, such as endothermic birds, insects, and Chinook salmon, with altered migration routes increasing contact between otherwise geographically separated host species, leading to novel pathogen introductions and increasing disease risks for humans and wildlife [19], [57]. The public, however, tends to downplay the seriousness of influenza owing to its seasonal nature and conflation with the common cold. This puts humans at greater risk of new influenza pandemics. Importantly, new variants of COVID-19 may result in a rise in new infections and become dominant in some countries and / or globally due to its advantage of spread and immune escape characteristics [58]. The coinfection and overlap of the new coronavirus strain and influenza have the potential to cause a serious disease burden and strain medical resources, especially in countries with limited healthcare resources.

Despite the lessons learned from the influenza and COVID-19 pandemics, the world remains unprepared for the next influenza or disease X pandemic. The US National Academy of Medicine’s Advancing Pandemic and Seasonal Influenza Vaccine Preparedness and Response Initiative provides an in-depth description of the actions needed [8]. The Initiative was launched in 2020 to collect, understand, and leverage lessons learned from COVID-19 to ensure robust and effective preparedness for the next pandemic [8]. Although the initiative’s reports offer specific recommendations, policy implementation across many countries remains inadequate, with significant lacks in local influenza infection surveillance data, strain typing, and information on the timing of symptom onset, contact history, and regions visited.

Prevention and control of future respiratory diseases

At the local level, individual actions have been shown to have a substantial impact on reducing the transmission of respiratory diseases, particularly influenza and COVID-19 [59]. In particular, the implementation of personal NPIs was found to effectively reduce influenza and COVID-19 transmission among school-age children [60]. These measures, if continued and strengthened throughout the year (especially ahead of the influenza seasons), have the potential to reduce infection rates, hospitalizations, and deaths.

At the macroscale, national governments and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention need to increase public awareness and education campaigns about influenza and COVID-19 prevention, symptoms, early treatment, and timely healthcare-seeking behaviors. In addition, strengthening global and regional surveillance systems for infectious diseases and pathogens, as well as strengthening cooperation and information sharing among countries, represents an important line of defense in prompt detection and response to outbreaks. Early warnings, surveillance and reporting, variant tracking, access to clinical care at symptom onset, the administration of vaccine boosters to high-risk groups, ventilation improvements, and regular communication offer the best preventative measures [61].

Vaccination is a critical public health tool for preventing both influenza and COVID-19 transmission. However, current vaccines are largely limited in offering homosubtypic immunity, depend on accurate forecasting of anticipated seasonal strains, and offer little protection against emergent viruses [62]. In some areas, vaccine access is extremely limited, and citizens may mistrust government efforts to promote vaccinations. For example, despite campaigns to improve vaccine awareness, access, and acceptance in the United States, the use of both seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines remains suboptimal [63]. This complicates the ability of scientists and researchers to study vaccine effectiveness and create universal and long-lasting protection, such as research into the potential of humanized monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) [64]. Increasing vaccination coverage among the population is also crucial for vaccine effectiveness and short-term efficacy. Efforts should focus on improving vaccine availability, accessibility, and public education about the importance of vaccination, especially for high-risk groups.

Conclusions and recommendations

Although we must be prepared to address the next influenza or disease X pandemic, we need to remain aware of the uncertainty and variability of viruses and pathogens. Moreover, a key issue in accurately predicting future incidences of infectious diseases and carrying out early and effective prevention measures is quantifying the long-range dissemination of other infectious diseases [65]. Our understanding of the influenza and COVID-19 epidemic has been limited by the lack of high-quality data on individual viral characteristics, such as virus identification and classification, genomic data, geographic location, and clinical or epidemiological data. The critical questions we must track include: (1) What is the timing of infection detection and confirmation? (2) Where is the primary location of disease onset? (3) What is the local history of chronic diseases, healthcare accessibility, and timing and dose of vaccination?

To answer these questions, researchers must collect and analyze data on individual-level characteristics based on demographic information and contact tracing data. These are crucial areas for future data collection, as pandemic influenza will likely be spread through both child and adult social networks. Disease dissemination is a complex function of host movement, filtered by the age structure of the population and susceptibility [66].

By combining long-term historical infectious disease surveillance data, researchers could develop early warning systems to identify potential outbreaks or pandemics before they escalate by identifying patterns and trends in historical data. In an era of rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI), harnessing AI technology could effectively help identify patterns in data to inform public health interventions and strategies for preventing the spread of influenza and coronavirus [67]. For example, AI algorithms can analyze social media data to track the spread of outbreaks and predict future hotspots.

Furthermore, governments should be prepared for compound events, as climate extremes are likely to trigger not only the next influenza pandemic but also the emergence of unknown infectious diseases. The evolutionary processes that generate new pandemic strains are unpredictable and involve cross-species transmission (for instance, from swine or migratory birds to humans). Theoretically, this can occur during any season of the year [68]. Mammalian contact risk, the potential adaptation of avian influenza to human transmission, and the variety of settings in which spillover may occur demand a One Health approach for both surveillance and mitigation. A One Health approach to surveillance would include a dedicated focus on pathogens in wildlife, livestock, and animals sold at markets [69].

A key area for future research is whether the combined rapid evolution of infectious viruses and intrinsic differences in viral transmissibility drive the accelerated geographical spread of influenza epidemics. We are at a critically opportune period to launch well-designed research studies into the interplay between seasonal disease transmission, immunity, wildlife-domestic animal-human contact, and infection. Continuous monitoring and exploration of the transmission and evolution of pathogens in animal populations known to be reservoirs may reveal important patterns in infectious disease mutation and cross-species transmission. However, there is currently a lack of understanding of the mechanisms underlying infectious disease occurrence and spillover. We must continually assess and update our pandemic workflows to stay ahead of the next deadly strain. With our joint efforts, countless lives can be saved when the next pandemic occurs.
