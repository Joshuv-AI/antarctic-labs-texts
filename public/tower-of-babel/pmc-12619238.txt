The relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement of medical university teachers: the mediating role of self-efficacy
Authors: Dandan Xie, Dongdong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to explore the relationships among paternalistic leadership, self-efficacy and work engagement among teachers in medical universities, with a focus on the mediating role of self-efficacy. Six hundred and fifty teachers were randomly selected from three medical universities and completed three validated scales: the Paternalistic Leadership Scale, the General Self-Efficacy Scale, and the Work Engagement Scale, and 606 valid questionnaires were collected (93.23% response rate). Pearson correlation, CFA, SEM, ML and Bootstrap method were employed with SPSS 28.0 and AMOS 28.0 software to verify the hypotheses proposed in this study. The results revealed that paternalistic leadership significantly positively influenced work engagement (β = 0.550, p < 0.01) and self-efficacy (β = 0.525, p < 0.01). Moreover, self-efficacy not only positively predicted work engagement (β = 0.601, p < 0.01) but also mediated the relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement. These findings highlight that enhancing specific dimensions of paternalistic leadership and fostering teachers’ self-efficacy are effective strategies for improving teachers’ work engagement in medical education contexts.

Introduction

Existing research has established that managerial leadership styles significantly influence teachers’ work engagement [1]. However, leadership effectiveness is deeply embedded in cultural contexts [2]. Paternalistic leadership—a blend of authority, benevolence, and moral integrity—is prevalent in Chinese societies, where collectivism and hierarchical relationships dominate [3]. While studies suggest this leadership style enhances corporate employee performance [4], its impact on faculty in higher education, particularly in medical institutions, remains underexplored [5]. Moreover, findings on paternalistic leadership’s influence on work engagement are inconsistent [6], warranting further investigation.

The unique demands of medical education amplify the need for faculty work engagement. Medical training spans extended periods (5–6 years for undergraduates, plus residency) and requires mastery of both theoretical knowledge and complex clinical skills [7]. Faculty must continually adapt to advancements in pedagogy and healthcare, often under significant time and emotional pressures [8]. Engaged educators—those with sustained energy, dedication, and absorption in their roles—are critical to maintaining educational quality and student outcomes [9]. Yet, medical faculty face high burnout rates, with disengagement directly compromising teaching effectiveness and institutional performance [10]. Thus, understanding how leadership fosters engagement in this context is both theoretically and practically urgent.

This study examines whether paternalistic leadership directly affects work engagement among medical university faculty and if self-efficacy mediates this relationship. While prior research has linked leadership styles to teacher engagement, paternalistic leadership—rooted in Chinese culture—has rarely been studied in education. This gap is addressed by focusing on higher education, where faculty, especially in medical universities, are underrepresented. Most studies on paternalistic leadership involve corporate employees, and teacher engagement research often targets primary/secondary educators. By analyzing medical faculty, this study highlights paternalistic leadership’s role in higher education management. Although self-efficacy is well-researched, its connection to paternalistic leadership and work engagement remains unclear. This study introduces self-efficacy as a mediator, advancing understanding of how paternalistic leadership influences engagement in academia.

Theoretical review and research hypotheses

Concepts of paternalistic leadership, work engagement, and self-efficacy

The concept of systematic paternalistic leadership originated from Silin’s study of Taiwanese enterprises, where leadership behaviors diverged markedly from Western practices. Leaders in this context were likened to “parents,” exhibiting centralized authority and pronounced hierarchical distance [11]. Redding expanded this observation to Chinese enterprises in Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia, identifying key features such as mandatory obedience, rigid hierarchies, and leaders serving as moral exemplars [12]. Westwood later proposed a “paternalistic head” model for Chinese family businesses, emphasizing didactic leadership, opacity in intentions, and a preoccupation with power and reputation [13]. Building on these foundations, Zheng Boxun and colleagues formalized a three-dimensional model of paternalistic leadership—authoritarian, benevolent, and moral leadership—defining it as a style blending paternalistic care with rigid authority in human-centric governance [14]. Authoritarian leadership relies on control, top-down communication, and demands absolute obedience. Benevolent leadership treats subordinates as family, offering flexibility and holistic support. Moral leadership emphasizes leaders’ ethical self-discipline, role modeling, and normative guidance [14]. This study adopts Zheng’s framework given its empirical validation in similar cultural contexts.

Work engagement, a central construct in positive psychology, has evolved significantly in conceptualization. Early definitions by Kahn [15] framed engagement as the investment of physical, cognitive, and emotional energy into work roles, juxtaposed with disengagement (akin to burnout). Maslach and Leiter [16] later positioned engagement as the antithesis of burnout, characterized by energy, involvement, and efficacy. Rothbard [17] further differentiated engagement from mere participation by highlighting its dual components of attention (cognitive focus) and absorption (emotional attachment), thus enriching the understanding of its psychological dimensions. The most widely cited definition by Schaufeli and Bakker [18] describes engagement as a positive, fulfilling state marked by vigor (high energy and resilience), dedication (enthusiasm and meaning), and absorption (deep focus). Critically, contemporary research underscores the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model (Bakker, 2023), which identifies job resources (e.g., autonomy, feedback, leadership support) as primary drivers of engagement, while excessive job demands deplete it. This model clarifies how leadership styles—particularly paternalistic leadership—may function as contextual resources that foster or hinder engagement [19].

Self-efficacy, rooted in Bandura’s social cognitive theory, refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to mobilize resources and execute tasks successfully [20]. Stajkovic and Luthans [21] refined this as a dynamic, task-specific confidence that influences behavior. In organizational settings, self-efficacy mediates outcomes like performance, innovation, and well-being [22], with antecedents including leadership styles, organizational climate, and goal orientation [23]. Its role as a mediator aligns with the construct’s theoretical nature—operating at the intersection of cognition and action.

The relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement

Through a review of the literature on teacher work engagement, it has been observed that some scholars have explored how teachers’ perceptions of leadership styles influence their work engagement, identifying leadership style perception as one of the factors that affect work engagement [24]. Research on primary and secondary school teachers has revealed that transformational leadership has a significant positive impact on teachers’ work engagement [25]. While studies on the impact of paternalistic leadership on work engagement are relatively few, discrepancies still exist in the findings. For instance, Wei Lei [26] found that benevolent leadership had a significantly positive effect on work engagement, while authoritarian leadership could positively predict the vigor and dedication dimensions of work engagement, but moral leadership did not significantly predict work engagement. However, according to the study by Pellegrini and Scandura [27], both benevolent leadership and moral leadership were found to significantly and positively predict employees’ work engagement. Research by Aycan [28] indicated that high levels of benevolent leadership and low levels of authoritarian leadership effectively reduced employees’ work alienation, which in turn decreased job satisfaction and organizational commitment, ultimately reducing work engagement.

From a literature review, it is evident that some scholars have explored how teachers’ perceptions of leadership styles influence their work engagement; however, the majority of existing research on paternalistic leadership has focused on corporate employees, with relatively few studies examining paternalistic leadership in higher education institutions, especially in medical universities. Furthermore, the conclusions regarding the impact of paternalistic leadership on work engagement are inconsistent. Moreover, the cultural context is crucial. Paternalistic leadership is deeply rooted in Chinese cultural traditions, such as Confucianism, which emphasizes hierarchy, benevolence, and moral integrity [29]. In the context of Chinese medical universities, which often have strong hierarchical structures, the moral and benevolent dimensions of paternalistic leadership may be perceived as particularly legitimate and motivating resources, thereby fostering work engagement more effectively than in Western contexts. Authoritarian leadership, while often negative, might be more tolerated or even expected in certain situations within this cultural and institutional setting, though its effects are likely complex. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms by which paternalistic leadership affects work engagement among university faculty, especially in medical universities, is both theoretically and practically significant. Thus, this study proposes Hypothesis 1: All three dimensions of paternalistic leadership have significant positive impacts on work engagement of teachers in medical universities.

The relationship between paternalistic leadership and self-efficacy

Ye Yujian et al. [30], drawing from the perspective of different leadership styles, found that charismatic leadership behaviors significantly promoted the enhancement of employees’ self-efficacy, which in turn further improved employees’ organizational citizenship behaviors. Meng Hui et al. [31] observed that transformational leaders could enhance employees’ job performance and overall job satisfaction by positively influencing their self-efficacy. Bandura [32] found that leadership behaviors that emphasize support and fairness could significantly enhance employees’ self-efficacy. Across multiple studies conducted in various countries and at different stages, especially those examining the influence of principal leadership on teacher development, it has been consistently demonstrated that transformational leadership among school principals positively impacts teachers’ self-efficacy [33, 34].

Scholars who have specialized in the study of teacher self-efficacy over the years have identified leadership characteristics that contribute to the development of teacher self-efficacy. These include cultivating a school culture that emphasizes instructional support, providing professional development opportunities, encouraging innovation among teachers, and promoting mutual learning and collaboration [35]. Notably, the moral leadership and benevolent leadership dimensions of paternalistic leadership similarly emphasize supportive and inclusive behaviors, continuously offering robust and flexible support for teachers’ instructional activities and collaboration. In the Chinese context, where leaders are often viewed as moral exemplars and paternal figures, their support and encouragement (benevolent leadership) and their demonstration of integrity and self-discipline (moral leadership) may be particularly powerful sources of efficacy beliefs for teachers, as they align with cultural expectations of virtuous leadership.

Although previous research has recognized that leadership style can impact teachers’ self-efficacy [36], relatively little attention has been paid to the specific predictive effects of paternalistic leadership practices on teachers’ self-efficacy. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: All three dimensions of paternalistic leadership have significant positive impacts on self-efficacy of teachers in medical universities.

The relationship between Self-Efficacy and work engagement

Previous studies have indicated that self-efficacy is a significant predictor of work engagement [37]. According to self-efficacy theory [20], the stronger an individual’s confidence in successfully completing a task, the more this positive psychological cue directly promotes higher levels of work engagement. This relationship is also central to the JD-R model, where personal resources like self-efficacy are key drivers of engagement [19].

Luthans et al. [38] found that higher levels of psychological capital are associated with stronger self-efficacy among employees; moreover, both high psychological capital and high self-efficacy directly lead to enhanced work engagement and improved work performance. Xanthopoulou et al. [39], through empirical research, concluded that high self-efficacy increases individuals’ investment in their work, greatly motivating them to exert greater vigor, display a more focused attitude, and demonstrate a stronger spirit of dedication. Wang Ding [40] and colleagues, focusing on knowledge-based employees, conducted a comparative study from the dual perspectives of self-efficacy and work engagement and found that various dimensions of self-efficacy were positively correlated with multiple dimensions of work engagement.

Similarly, Simbula and Guglielmi [41], using Italian teachers as research participants, conducted a three-wave longitudinal study that demonstrated a significant positive relationship between teacher self-efficacy and work engagement. Li Yongzhou [42] studied the relationship between the self-efficacy of R&D employees and their work engagement and found a significant positive correlation, indicating that higher self-efficacy enhances work enthusiasm and initiative. Li Kuiliang [43] explored the relationship between self-efficacy and work engagement among university counselors and found that self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between professional competence and work engagement.

The above research examining the relationship between self-efficacy and work engagement consistently shows that self-efficacy can stimulate employees’ proactive behavior and predict their level of work engagement. Furthermore, self-efficacy often appears as a mediating variable, which is closely related to its theoretical nature—being a purely cognitive concept, it governs individuals’ behaviors and resulting outcomes under its influence. Based on the above, the present study proposes Hypothesis 3: Self-efficacy has a significant positive impact on work engagement and mediates the relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement (Fig 1).

Research methods

Research respondents

A total of 650 faculty and staff members (full-time teachers and administrative staff) from three medical universities were included in this cross-sectional, multi-center study. The sample size of 650 questionnaires was determined based on a power analysis (assuming 80% power, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution) to ensure statistical robustness, accounting for an anticipated 10% non-response/invalid rate. Participants were randomly selected through the university administration using a computer-generated randomization list stratified by department and job type (teacher/administrative staff) to ensure proportional representation.

On-site data collection was conducted by trained research assistants at designated university locations (e.g., staff lounges or meeting rooms) during scheduled sessions. Participants completed paper-based questionnaires in private booths to ensure anonymity; no identifying information was collected, and questionnaires were immediately sealed in opaque envelopes upon completion. To minimize selection bias, eligibility criteria included full-time employment with ≥ 1 year of service. Participants provided informed consent, were briefed on risks/benefits, and could withdraw anytime without penalty. Generalizability may be limited to similar medical institutions due to the homogeneous professional background of respondents.

Research instruments

The questionnaire used in this study included three mature and published questionnaires: Paternalistic Leadership Scale compiled by Professor Zheng Boxun et al. [14]; Work Engagement Scale developed by Schaufeli et al. [44]. and General Self-Efficacy Scale developed by Schwarzer et al. [45]. All questionnaires were administered in Chinese. The questionnaire featured a total of 34 items, which were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = uncertain, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree).

Paternalistic leadership scale

This study adopted a short version of the Paternalistic Leadership Scale, compiled by Professor Zheng Boxun et al. [14], Taiwan University. The scale includes three dimensions, namely, the authoritarian leadership (5 items), benevolent leadership (5 items) and moral leadership (5 items) dimensions. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale is 0.801, and KMO value is 0.858, indicating good reliability and validity.

Work engagement scale

The Work Engagement Scale developed by Schaufeli et al. was used in this study [44]. The scale consists of three dimensions: vigor (3 items), dedication (3 items), and absorption (3 items). Higher scores reflect a greater level of work engagement. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale is 0.665, KMO value is 0.722, and considering the large sample size and the relatively small number of items, the reliability and validity remain acceptable.

General self-efficacy scale

This study adopted the General Self-Efficacy Scale developed by Schwarzer et al. [45]. The scale consists of 10 items. Higher scores indicate a higher level of self-efficacy. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale is 0.746, KMO value is 0.779, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data processing

Regarding the handling of missing data, questionnaires with more than 20% missing values were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining datasets with minor random missing values, mean imputation was employed.

In this study, data analysis was conducted using SPSS 28.0 and AMOS 28.0 software. First, reliability analysis was performed using Cronbach’s α coefficient to examine the internal consistency of each scale. Second, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to explore the relationships among variables, providing preliminary evidence for subsequent hypothesis testing. Third, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model, including composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and discriminant validity. Subsequently, a structural equation model (SEM) was constructed to examine the path relationships among variables. Parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood (ML) method, and model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices (χ²/df, GFI, AGFI, RMR, CFI, NFI, RFI, TLI, IFI). Finally, the Bootstrap method for testing mediating effects (with 5,000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval) was applied to verify the mediating role of self-efficacy between paternalistic leadership and work engagement. This method provides robust testing of mediation effects without relying on normal distribution assumptions.

Analysis results

Demographic characteristics

Of the 650 questionnaires distributed, 606 were valid (93.23% response rate). The sample consisted of 373 male respondents (61.55%) and 233 female respondents (38.45%), with a male to female ratio of 1.6:1. In terms of professional roles, 365 participants were full-time teachers (60.23%) and 241 were administrative staff (39.77%). This indicates that the majority of the participants in this study were full-time faculty members.

Reliability analysis

This study conducted reliability analysis for all variables using SPSS 28.0, with results showing in Table 1 that the Cronbach’s α coefficients for all variables exceeded the acceptable threshold of 0.7. Specifically, the three dimensions of paternalistic leadership - authoritarian leadership, benevolent leadership, and moral leadership - demonstrated α coefficients of 0.881, 0.871, and 0.866 respectively, indicating excellent internal consistency of the paternalistic leadership scale. Among the three dimensions of work engagement, the α coefficients for vigor, dedication, and absorption were 0.757, 0.780, and 0.835 respectively, all meeting acceptable reliability standards. The self-efficacy scale, comprising 10 items, achieved an exceptionally high α coefficient of 0.919, demonstrating outstanding reliability.

The structural equation modeling (SEM) path coefficient test results revealed that authoritarian leadership (β = 0.194, C.R. = 4.607, p < 0.001), benevolent leadership (β = 0.337, C.R. = 7.543, p < 0.001), and moral leadership (β = 0.235, C.R. = 5.443, p < 0.001) all showed positive effects on self-efficacy.

Regarding the direct effects of leadership styles on work engagement, authoritarian leadership had no significant influence (β = 0.061, C.R. = 1.257, p = 0.209), whereas benevolent leadership (β = 0.164, C.R. = 3.125, p < 0.01) and moral leadership (β = 0.233, C.R. = 4.525, p < 0.001) showed significant positive effects. Additionally, self-efficacy demonstrated a significant positive predictive effect on work engagement (β = 0.337, C.R. = 5.876, p < 0.001).

Mediation effect analysis

To further examine the mediating role of self-efficacy between paternalistic leadership and work engagement, this study employed the Bootstrap method for mediation effect testing with 5,000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval. This method not only test mediation significance but also quantify the point estimate and variability range of indirect effects, offering superior accuracy over traditional methods [46].

The results in Table 8 demonstrated that self-efficacy played significant mediating roles across all three leadership styles. Specifically, the indirect effect of authoritarian leadership on work engagement through self-efficacy was 0.065 (95% CI = [0.033, 0.113], p < 0.01), with the confidence interval excluding zero, indicating a significant mediation effect. Benevolent leadership showed the strongest indirect effect on work engagement via self-efficacy (0.096, 95% CI = [0.057, 0.151], p < 0.01). Moral leadership also exhibited a significant indirect effect (0.054, 95% CI = [0.028, 0.090], p < 0.01).

Discussion

This study set out to investigate the relationships between paternalistic leadership, self-efficacy, and work engagement among teachers in Chinese medical universities.

The results demonstrate that: (1) Both benevolent and moral leadership have a significant positive impact on teachers’ work engagement, while authoritarian leadership shows no significant direct effect. (2) All three dimensions of paternalistic leadership—authoritarian, benevolent, and moral—positively predict teachers’ self-efficacy. (3) Self-efficacy not only directly enhances work engagement but also acts as a mediating variable through which paternalistic leadership influences engagement.

Paternalistic leadership and work engagement

Our findings confirm that benevolent and moral leadership significantly effect work engagement, whereas authoritarian leadership exhibits no significant direct effect. This aligns with prior research [47], reinforcing that leaders who demonstrate care (benevolence) and ethical integrity (morality) strengthen employees’ emotional and cognitive investment in their work. However, the non-significant effect of authoritarian leadership contrasts with the study [48] that found it reduces engagement by fostering alienation. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors: In high power-distance cultures, authoritarian leadership may be more accepted, mitigating its negative effects, however, when it suppresses autonomy, engagement may still suffer due to reduced intrinsic motivation [49]. Benevolent and moral leadership may buffer authoritarianism’s drawbacks. Employees may accept stricter directives if they perceive their leader as fair and caring [50]. In structured environments (e.g., military, healthcare), authoritarian leadership may not hinder engagement if employees value clarity over autonomy [51].

We posit that the non-significant role of authoritarian leadership in our context can be explained by the unique organizational structure of universities. Unlike in corporate hierarchies, university administrators often hold concurrent academic positions and are peers to the faculty they lead. This collegial relationship may dilute the perceived authority and impact of top-down, controlling behaviors [49]. Faculty members, as highly educated professionals, value autonomy and are less responsive to coercive influence. Instead, they are more motivated by leaders who provide support (benevolence) and embody ethical principles (morality) [52].

Paternalistic leadership and self-efficacy

A notable finding of this study is that all three dimensions of paternalistic leadership, including authoritarian leadership, positively predict teachers’ self-efficacy. While the positive effects of benevolent and moral leadership align with mainstream theory, the positive effect of authoritarian leadership warrants a culturally-nuanced explanation.

Benevolent leadership enhances self-efficacy by providing emotional support and a safety net, which reduces anxiety and bolsters individuals’ confidence in tackling challenges [20]. Moral leadership boosts self-efficacy by modeling high standards of conduct and ensuring fair treatment, which strengthens employees’ organizational identification and belief in a just environment where their efforts will be rewarded [53]. The positive impact of authoritarian leadership, however, can be understood through the lens of the Chinese cultural context, which is characterized by high power distance and collectivism [54]. As Aycan highlights, in hierarchical societies, authoritarian leadership may function effectively by providing guidance and structure, which employees interpret as reducing uncertainty—thereby enhancing their confidence (self-efficacy) [28]. Similarly, Pellegrini and Scandura emphasize that in collectivist cultures, authoritarian leaders are often perceived as “protective” rather than oppressive, fostering trust and a sense of competence among subordinates who expect strong direction [27].

For faculty navigating the complex and high-pressure environment of medical education, a leader’s authoritative directives can clarify goals and expectations, thereby increasing perceived control and competence—key sources of self-efficacy [20]. This suggests that in specific contexts, the “clarity” provided by authoritarianism can paradoxically foster confidence, a finding supported by recent research in Chinese educational settings [49].

Mediating role of self-efficacy

Our study confirms that paternalistic leadership not only directly influences work engagement but also indirectly enhances engagement levels by boosting self-efficacy. This validates self-efficacy’s role as a psychological mechanism that mediates the relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement in medical education contexts. This aligns with a substantial body of research across various professions [49], including Bandura’s assertion that leadership behaviors serve as key sources of efficacy beliefs through modeling, feedback, and emotional support [55]. Similarly, Luthans et al. found that self-efficacy, as part of psychological capital, mediates the link between leadership and proactive work behaviors [38].

The mechanism can be explained by Social Cognitive Theory [56]. Paternalistic leaders, through their actions, serve as powerful sources of psychological influence. Benevolent leaders provide verbal persuasion and create a positive emotional state; moral leaders offer credible vicarious learning experiences; and even authoritarian leaders may provide clear performance feedback. These influences may collectively enhance an individual’s belief in their capabilities (i.e., self-efficacy). According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals with high self-efficacy perceive themselves as possessing a key personal resource [57]. They are more likely to invest this resource into their work (i.e., higher engagement) because they are confident of achieving valued outcomes and avoiding resource loss. Conversely, those with low self-efficacy may withhold effort as a defensive strategy against potential failure. Thus, self-efficacy acts as the critical psychological bridge translating leadership influence into motivated action, a process further validated in recent studies on academic professionals [58].”

Research limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the relationship among paternalistic leadership, self-efficacy, and work engagement among teachers in medical universities. However, several limitations should be noted.

First, while our focus on China’s unique cultural context has enabled deep contextual understanding of paternalistic leadership phenomena, this same specificity creates boundaries for generalizability. The findings may be particularly influenced by cultural characteristics such as high power distance and collectivism that shape leadership expectations in Chinese universities. Future research should move beyond simple cross-national replications to address three key methodological challenges: (1) conducting measurement equivalence tests for our constructs across cultures, as direct translation of Chinese scales may not fully capture paternalistic leadership dimensions elsewhere; (2) employing established cultural frameworks to systematically compare how paternalistic leadership operates differently across cultural contexts; and (3) treating culture itself as a boundary condition or moderator to empirically test its contingent effects.

Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases such as social desirability and recall errors. To mitigate this, future studies should employ mixed-method approaches that combine quantitative surveys with qualitative methods (e.g., interviews, focus groups) to both validate self-reports and provide richer contextual understanding of the leadership dynamics observed.

Third, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inferences and temporal analysis. Longitudinal studies tracking teachers across different career stages would better reveal how paternalistic leadership and self-efficacy dynamically influence work engagement over time, while also helping to account for potential cohort effects in leadership perceptions.

Practical implications

The relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement in medical universities offers several valuable insights into the role of leadership styles in influencing the professional dynamics of academic staff. The following implications are derived from our findings within the specific cultural context of this study and should be considered with the understanding that their applicability to other cultural settings may require further validation and adaptation.

Cultivating benevolent and moral leadership

This study suggests that benevolent and moral leadership contribute to higher work engagement. Universities could consider fostering supportive leadership through structured mentorship programs, where senior faculty guide junior colleagues in career development, research, and teaching methodologies. Recognition mechanisms, such as acknowledgments in faculty meetings or notes of appreciation, may reinforce a sense of value among staff [59]. Flexible work arrangements, including hybrid teaching models or adjusted office hours, could also be implemented to accommodate diverse professional and personal needs.

Ethical role modeling may further support engagement. Transparent decision-making processes, such as faculty involvement in curriculum revisions or research funding allocations, could enhance perceptions of fairness [27]. Department heads might benefit from ethics training focused on integrity, impartiality, and conflict-of-interest management. Additionally, anonymous reporting channels for unethical conduct, along with clear consequences for violations, could strengthen accountability.

Leadership development programs could incorporate training on empathy and servant leadership, emphasizing active listening, emotional intelligence, and responsiveness to faculty needs [60]. Case-based learning, such as simulations of faculty disputes or workload management, may enhance practical skills. Peer leadership discussions, where leaders exchange challenges and strategies, could further refine leadership approaches.

Mitigating authoritarian leadership tendencies

Universities can encourage participative leadership by involving faculty in decision-making through committees for curriculum development or research strategy. Open forums, such as regular town hall meetings, could provide opportunities for faculty to express concerns and suggest improvements. Before implementing major policies, gathering faculty input through surveys or focus groups may help align decisions with staff needs.

Reducing excessive control may also be beneficial. Granting faculty autonomy in teaching methods—such as allowing experimentation with flipped classrooms or case-based learning—could foster innovation. Decentralizing research leadership, enabling department heads to allocate resources based on faculty expertise, might further enhance motivation. Monitoring leadership effectiveness through anonymous perception surveys and 360-degree feedback could help identify authoritarian tendencies, with targeted coaching offered to leaders scoring low on benevolence metrics.

Strengthening faculty Self-Efficacy

Given that self-efficacy appears to influence work engagement, universities could implement measures to enhance faculty confidence. Professional development opportunities, such as workshops on modern pedagogies, grant-writing training, and statistical analysis seminars, may improve teaching and research skills. Cross-disciplinary collaborations, such as joint projects between medical and engineering departments, could expand expertise and encourage innovation.

Peer mentoring networks, including junior-senior faculty pairings and teaching communities of practice, may provide structured guidance and constructive feedback. Recognizing achievements through merit-based awards, transparent career advancement pathways, and public acknowledgments in newsletters or institutional communications could reinforce faculty motivation.

Institutionalizing supportive policies for sustained engagement

To sustain the potential benefits of paternalistic leadership and self-efficacy, universities could institutionalize supportive policies by integrating them into organizational practices. This may include allocating sufficient resources for teaching tools, research support, and mental health services to mitigate burnout. Flexible work arrangements, such as accommodations for family responsibilities or research sabbaticals, might further reinforce benevolent leadership at an institutional level.

Longitudinal monitoring of work engagement and self-efficacy metrics could be conducted periodically, with data used to refine leadership training programs and optimize resource allocation for sustained effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study highlights that paternalistic leadership can significantly influence teachers’ self-efficacy and work engagement in medical universities, and self-efficacy plays a mediating role in the relationship between paternalistic leadership and work engagement. By prioritizing leadership development programs, fostering a supportive work environment, implementing targeted interventions to enhance self-efficacy, and enhancing self-efficacy and job fit, medical universities can create an academic culture that promotes both faculty well-being and professional growth. This study offers a new perspective for understanding the impact of paternalistic leadership on subordinates in the field of medical education, and provides important theoretical and practical references for addressing issues related to work engagement among faculty and staff in medical education institutions.
