The Progenitor of the Type II-Plateau SN 2025pht in NGC 1637: The Dustiest, Most Luminous Red Supergiant So Far? Facilities: HST (WFPC2, WFC3), JWST(NIRCam, MIRI), Shane (Kast Double spectrograph) AstroDrizzle ( STSCI Development Team 2012 ) , Dolphot ( A. Dolphin 2016 ) , DUSTY ( Z. Schuyler D. Van Dyk, Tamás Szalai, Gagandeep S. Anand, Thomas G. Brink, Noah Zimmer, Dan Milisavljevic, Ori D. Fox, Jacob E. Jencson, WeiKang Zheng, Alexei V. Filippenko

Abstract
We provide a characterization of the red supergiant (RSG) progenitor candidate for the nearby Type II-plateau supernova (SN) 2025pht in NGC 1637. The star was first detectable in 2001 by the Hubble Space Telescope ( HST ) and then again in a dozen bands by the James Webb Space Telescope ( JWST ) in 2024. This “quasi-snapshot” of the star’s nature almost immediately prior to explosion is unprecedented. The RSG varied in brightness, and we posit that it could have been a pulsating variable, possibly with a long period of ∼ 660 \sim 660 days. The largest uncertainty is the host-galaxy distance, which we establish to be 10.73 ± 1.76 10.73\pm 1.76 Mpc. The star was also heavily extinguished by interstellar dust internal to the host, with visual extinction A V ​ ( host ) ≈ 1.7 A_{V}({\rm host})\approx 1.7 mag (total A V ​ ( tot ) ≈ 1.8 A_{V}({\rm tot})\approx 1.8 mag). Dust radiative-transfer modeling reveals the star’s circumstellar medium to be quite dusty and silicate-rich, yielding a bolometric luminosity log ⁡ ( L bol / L ⊙ ) = 5.08 ± 0.16 \log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})=5.08\pm 0.16 and a cool effective temperature T eff = 2100 T_{\rm eff}=2100 –2500 K. The available HST optical data had no bearing on the shape of the candidate’s observed spectral energy distribution — for the first time, without the archival JWST observations we would not have been able to detect and characterize the candidate at all. The SN 2025pht progenitor candidate, although quite similar to that of SN 2023ixf, may be the most luminous candidate identified to date.

I
Introduction

Core-collapse supernovae (SNe) are thought to arise from stars with initial masses
M_{\rm ini}\gtrsim 8\ M_{\odot}
. Single stars in this mass range are further thought to reach the end of their lives in the red supergiant (RSG) phase. The SNe that result from the explosions of such stars, with massive extended hydrogen-rich envelopes, should be observed as the H-rich Type II SNe, specifically, the SNe II-plateau, or II-P. Indeed, strong observational evidence exists that SN II-P progenitors are RSGs, specifically through direct identification and characterization of the progenitor stars in pre-explosion images
(
S. J. Smartt et al. 2009
;
S. J. Smartt 2015
;
S. D. Van Dyk 2017
)
.

S. D. Van Dyk 2025
recently reviewed RSGs as SN progenitors. The conclusions, based on the cases to date, were that low-luminosity SNe II-P arose from progenitor RSGs in a relatively low range of bolometric luminosity
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})\approx 4
–4.7
(
S. M. Lisakov et al. 2018
, see also, e.g.,)
, and that these stars can generally be described into the near-infrared by a bare stellar atmosphere model, typically of effective temperature
T_{\rm eff}\approx 3400
–3700 K
(
E. M. Levesque et al. 2005
;
E. M. Levesque 2017
, see also, e.g.,)
. Likely all RSGs at the pre-SN stage are embedded to some extent in circumstellar material (CSM), established via mass loss from the star. For RSGs with
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})\gtrsim 4.7
the CSM may become more massive and dense, and therefore of a higher dust content, as evinced, for instance, by the characterization and modeling of Galactic RSGs
(
P. Massey et al. 2005
;
T. Verhoelst et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
. Recent higher-luminosity SN II-P events, such as SN 2012aw
(
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2012
;
C. S. Kochanek et al. 2012
;
M. Fraser et al. 2012
)
, SN 2017eaw
(
C. D. Kilpatrick & R. J. Foley 2018
;
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2019
;
L. Rui et al. 2019
)
, and SN 2023ixf
(
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2023
;
J. E. Jencson et al. 2023
;
D. Xiang et al. 2024
;
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, have exemplified the premise that some SNe may arise from higher-luminosity, dusty RSGs. Moreover, SN progenitors appear to be limited to stars with
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})\lesssim 5.2
, although the limit to known RSG luminosities appears to be
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})\approx 5.7

(
R. M. Humphreys & K. Davidson 1979
;
R. M. Humphreys 2025
)
, leading to the so-called “RSG problem”
(
S. J. Smartt et al. 2009
;
J. J. Walmswell & J. J. Eldridge 2012
;
B. Davies & E. R. Beasor 2020
;
C. S. Kochanek 2020
;
E. R. Beasor et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

Many known RSGs in Local Group host galaxies are semiregular, pulsational, long-period variables (LPVs, with periods of hundreds to thousands of days;
L. L. Kiss et al. 2006
;
M. Yang & B. W. Jiang 2011
;
M. Yang & B. W. Jiang 2012
;
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2018
;
Y. Ren et al. 2019
). In fact, the progenitor candidate of SN 2023ixf in Messier 101 was convincingly shown to be a variable with fundamental period
P\approx 1000
–1100 days
(
J. E. Jencson et al. 2023
;
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2023
)
.
The particular subtype of SN II could possibly be a manifestation of the phase of the light curve, and the extent of the stellar envelope, at which the explosion occurs
(
V. A. Bronner et al. 2025
)
.
However, pulsations in RSGs, especially, nonradial pulsations and overtone modes, could drive instabilities at late evolutionary stages, leading to enhanced mass loss and ejections
(
A. Suzuki & T. Shigeyama 2025
;
S. Sengupta et al. 2025
)
, possibly even via a “superwind”
(
S.-C. Yoon & M. Cantiello 2010
)
, altering the CSM that the SN shock encounters at explosion
(
J. A. Goldberg et al. 2020
)
.
As the star loses mass before explosion, the luminosity-to-mass ratio will increase, further driving pulsational amplitudes
(
E. Laplace et al. 2025
)
.
Abrupt, massive outbursts from the star
\lesssim 1
yr before its demise could account for the optical appearance of the resulting SN
(
B. Davies et al. 2022
)
, specifically at or near shock breakout
(
L. Dessart & W. V. Jacobson-Galan 2025
)
.

Here we present a characterization of the progenitor of SN 2025pht in the nearby spiral host galaxy NGC 1637.
K. Z. Stanek 2025
reported its discovery as a new transient on 2025 June 29 (UTC dates are used throughout this paper), and
J. Strader 2025
later classified it from an optical spectrum obtained on July 3 as SN II-P. Little else has been reported on the SN, other than that covered by
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
. D. Hodges added, also on July 3, a comment to the Transient Name Server online page for the SN
1
1

1

https://www.wis-tns.org/object/2025pht
that, based on its position reported by
K. Z. Stanek 2025
, the SN was very near an apparent blue supergiant, which was presented as a possible progenitor candidate. Subsequently, on July 16,
I. Pérez-Fournon et al. 2025
reported a position for the SN measured from ground-based Las Cumbres Observatory imaging, RA=
04^{\rm hr}41^{\rm m}28{\fs}872
, Dec =
-02\arcdeg 51^{\prime}55{\farcs}84
(J2000;
\pm 0{\farcs}08
), that is
0{\farcs}57
from the
K. Z. Stanek 2025
position. The new position corresponded to a source detected in images obtained by the
James Webb Space Telescope
(
JWST
), which made it potentially more likely to be a luminous red star and plausibly a better progenitor candidate, given the SN type, than the one suggested by Hodges.

We present our analysis of this progenitor candidate, based on the broad assumption that the
I. Pérez-Fournon et al. 2025
identification is correct. (
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
have subsequently demonstrated via precise relative astrometry that this star is indeed the best progenitor candidate.) We will show that the star itself was behind a substantial curtain of internal host extinction. What we find, based on the pre-SN observational evidence, is that the star is highly likely to have been a luminous RSG, with a significant amount of CSM dust.

We note that the host galaxy of SN 2025pht was also the host of the well-studied SN II-P 1999em
(
M. Hamuy et al. 2001
;
D. C. Leonard et al. 2002
;
A. Elmhamdi et al. 2003
;
V. P. Utrobin 2007
, e.g.,)
. The progenitor of that SN was not detected in pre-SN ground-based optical imaging
(
S. J. Smartt et al. 2002
)
.
The inclination for the host galaxy, of type SBc, is
i\approx 39\arcdeg
(from axial ratio
b/a=0.78
; position angle
PA=33\arcdeg
;
T. H. Jarrett et al. 2003
, via the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database, NED
2
2

2

https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/
). The host is a rather small spiral, with, respectively, major and minor axis (isophotal) diameters of
153{\farcs}2
and
119{\farcs}5

(
T. H. Jarrett et al. 2003
)
, which are
\sim 8.0
and
\sim 6.2
kpc, respectively, for the distance we adopt below. We also adopt a host-galaxy redshift
z=0.002392
, via NED.

II

III

IV

. Note that we made no further adjustments in time for the SN 2025pht curves, other than assuming our estimate of the explosion epoch, as we had inferred above from the spectral comparison. So, our choice of explosion epoch appears to be consistent between both the spectroscopic and photometric properties of the SN.

For SN 2025pht the photometry, then, commenced
\sim 20
days after explosion, missing the peak, and followed a plateau in each band, much like the comparison SNe. The similarity in the post-peak inflection in the
B

\sim 78

\sim 100

(

. The post-plateau behavior of SN 2025pht appears to display a rather flat exponential tail, especially in
B

B
and
I
. SN 2025pht photometrically most resembles SN 2008M at late times, although less so at earlier times along the plateau (we also only have data in

If the peak brightness followed the same trends as the comparison SNe, SN 2025pht would have had a maximum brightness of
V\approx 13.4
mag. Assuming the visual extinction and distance we have estimated below (in Sections

and
III.5

M_{V}\approx-18.6
mag, which is remarkably luminous.

III.3
Was The Progenitor Candidate Variable?

The progenitor candidate was clearly detected in
HST
F814W in 2001; see
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
. Examining Table
2
, it is evident that the star varied significantly in F814W brightness between 2001 August 12 and October 31 (the star was not detected at any time, either in 2001 or 2024, in F555W). In Figure
4
, panel (a), we show the F814W light curve during this time period. Visually one can see that the star was variable. However, clearly not enough data exist in this band (or at any other available wavelength, that we are aware of) to perform a rigorous period and amplitude analysis, similar to what, e.g.,
J. E. Jencson et al. 2023
and
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2023
undertook for the SN 2023ixf progenitor candidate. However, we applied a very simple sinusoidal function with various values of the period and performed a reduced
\chi^{2}
fit to the data points.

We further imposed the restriction that the model could not violate the upper limit on detection at F814W in 2024, and, to a lesser extent, that the model was in reasonable agreement with the brightnesses in the three NIRCam bands, F150W, F187N, and F335M, in which the host galaxy was observed by both
JWST
programs. For the latter we considered the ratios of root-mean-square (RMS) amplitudes in the infrared, relative to the optical (
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2023
, for the SN 2023ixf progenitor candidate) and applied those to this star (we note that wavelength-dependent phase lags, by
\sim 0.1
–0.15, may also exist for variability in the infrared, relative to the optical, as
B. J. Smith et al. 2002
found for Mira variables).

We found that this simple model met these requirements for sinusoids with periods of
\sim 470
and of
\sim 660
days, which both appear to provide a plausible representation of the F814W variability in 2001 and the NIRCam data; see Figure
4
. The inferred peak amplitude at F814W would then be
\sim 1.3
mag, and therefore, the RMS amplitude is
\sim 0.9
mag, which is consistent with the star having been an LPV
(
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.

We therefore consider it compelling to conclude that the SN 2025pht progenitor candidate could have been a pulsational variable star. Admittedly, this is somewhat contrived and a bit fanciful, and we likely have already overinterpreted what minimal data we had at hand; note that, for this reason, we have not included any further complexity to the model, such as nonradial pulsations or higher-order overtones. Yet, the agreement between both the optical and
JWST
light curves, such as they are, is at least self-consistent.

III.4
Reddening To the Supernova

The next step in the analysis is to estimate the total line of sight reddening to SN 2025pht, and then extrapolate this value to the total reddening to the progenitor candidate. We note first that the Galactic foreground visual extinction in the direction of the SN is
A_{V}=0.111
mag (
E(B-V)=0.036
mag;
E. F. Schlafly & D. P. Finkbeiner 2011
, via NED).

We have attempted to estimate the reddening via several different methods. (1) From the equivalent width (EQW) of the Na
i
D feature at the host-galaxy redshift in all of the SN 2025pht spectra (see Section
A.1
for details); (2) a comparison of the SN 2025pht spectra in their entirety with those of similar SNe II-P (see Section
A.2
for details); and (3) via the
B-V
color (see Section
A.3
for details).

The Na
i
D EQW has been empirically associated with the amount of reddening along a line of sight (
U. Munari & T. Zwitter 1997
;
M. Turatto et al. 2003
;
D. Poznanski et al. 2012
; however, also see
M. M. Phillips et al. 2013
). We estimated, based on
M. M. Phillips et al. 2013
, that the Na
i
D EQW in the July 31 spectrum, when the feature is strongest, corresponds to
A_{V}=0.70\pm 0.29
mag. However, as those authors also emphasized and cautioned, the relation between EQW and
A_{V}
becomes insensitive to dust extinction for EQW
\gtrsim 1
Å. Regardless, we consider that the presence of the Na
i
D feature at all observed epochs qualitatively implies that a significant contribution to the SN reddening is in the host-galaxy reference frame.

The comparison we then undertook was between the
J. Strader 2025
and Lick spectra (Figure
2
) and those of SN 2007od
(
C. P. Gutiérrez et al. 2017
)
, SN 2008M (also
C. P. Gutiérrez et al. 2017
), and SN 2013ej
(
G. Dhungana et al. 2016
)
, which either were not host-reddened or could be corrected for host reddening reasonably well. From the SN 2025pht spectra for all four dates, taken at face value, the average amount of additional (“addl”) reddening required for the comparison spectra to fit the spectral shape for SN 2025pht,
E(B-V)_{\rm addl}
, is
0.55\pm 0.05
mag.

Lastly, we considered the
B-V
color of SN 2025pht, and compared it to that of SN 2007od
(
C. Inserra et al. 2011
;
J. P. Anderson et al. 2024
)
, SN 2008M
(
J. P. Anderson et al. 2024
)
, SN 2009bw
(
C. Inserra et al. 2012
)
, SN 2013ej
(
S. Valenti et al. 2014
;
S. Bose et al. 2015
;
F. Huang et al. 2015
;
G. Dhungana et al. 2016
;
F. Yuan et al. 2016
)
, and SN 2023ixf
(
W. Zheng et al. 2025
)
. Although
T. de Jaeger et al. 2018
argued that significant dispersion exists across SN II intrinsic color, independent of any host-galaxy reddening, and therefore no true “color template” can possibly be constructed, we have fit the colors of these comparison SNe to that of SN 2025pht. To fit the SN 2025pht
B-V
color the average for the five comparison SNe is
E(B-V)_{\rm addl}=0.54\pm 0.12
mag, quite consistent with the estimate from the spectral comparison.

None of these methods is particularly foolproof. Ultimately, though, we considered the average color excess across both the spectral and color-curve comparisons and assume hereafter that
E(B-V)_{\rm host}=E(B-V)_{\rm addl}=0.54\pm 0.08
mag. Assuming
R_{V}=3.1
typical for interstellar dust, the visual extinction
A_{V}({\rm host})=1.69\pm 0.25
mag. (We note that the value of
A_{V}[{\rm host}]
appears to be clearly underestimated by the Na
i
D EQW technique.) Together with the Galactic
A_{V}
, this is
A_{V}({\rm tot})=1.80\pm 0.25
mag. The SN has evidently experienced a considerable amount of interstellar dust extinction along our line of sight.

We hereafter assume that this total extinction to the SN is applicable to the progenitor candidate as well. With regard to the candidate photometry, the
HST
bands are, of course, affected the most by this high total extinction, from
A_{\lambda}\approx 1.09
to 3.63 mag. The
JWST
bands are far less affected, from
A_{\lambda}\approx 0.07
to
\sim 0.38
mag, with the least extinction in MIRI F770W (
A_{\lambda}\approx 0.03
). For the optical through NIRCam bands we have assumed the
J. A. Cardelli et al. 1989
reddening law. For the F2100W band we assumed the reddening law from
M. Xue et al. 2016
.

III.5
Distance to the Host Galaxy

A number of distance estimates exist for the host galaxy, NGC 1637. Most of these are based on measurements associated with SN 1999em.
M. Hamuy et al. 2001
,
D. C. Leonard et al. 2002
, and
A. Elmhamdi et al. 2003
all established estimates using the expanding photosphere method (EPM) and arrived at quite similar values:
7.5\pm 0.5
,
8.2\pm 0.6
, and
7.83\pm 0.3
Mpc, respectively.

E. Baron et al. 2004
employed a spectral-fitting “expanding atmosphere method” and found a substantially larger
12.5\pm 1.8
Mpc.

M. I. Jones et al. 2009
recomputed the EPM distance assuming two different sets of SN II atmosphere models:
9.3\pm 0.5
Mpc from the
R. G. Eastman et al. 1996
models and (a quite different)
13.9\pm 1.4
Mpc using the
L. Dessart & D. J. Hillier 2005
models.
Via quantitative spectroscopic analysis,
L. Dessart & D. J. Hillier 2006
arrived at a distance of
11.5\pm 1.0
Mpc.

F. Olivares 2008
applied the standard candle method (SCM) to SN 1999em and found
10.0\pm 1.3
Mpc.

D. C. Leonard et al. 2003
measured a Cepheid-based distance of
11.7\pm 1.0
Mpc from the
HST
WFPC2 observations of NGC 1637 in 2001 listed in Table
2
. Note that
A. Saha et al. 2006
measured, from a similar technique employing the same data,
d=12.02\pm 0.39
Mpc, which agrees with the
D. C. Leonard et al. 2003
measurement to within the uncertainties.

We estimated another SCM distance to SN 1999em adopting the calibration by
J. Polshaw et al. 2015
, which is anchored to the megamaser galaxy NGC 4258 (M106); see Section
B.1
. Our result is
d=10.45\pm 2.32
Mpc, based on the expansion velocity from the Fe
ii

\lambda
5169 absorption line feature, and
d=9.86\pm 1.82
Mpc, from the weighted average of the velocities from Fe
ii

\lambda
5169,
\lambda
4629,
\lambda
4924, and
\lambda
5018 lines. These distance estimates agree with each other to within the (large) uncertainties. They also agree with the SCM distance by
F. Olivares 2008
, again, to within the uncertainties. At the very least, we have provided a cross-check on the previous estimate, employing a different SCM calibration.

We also measured a distance estimate to the host galaxy from SCM for SN 2025pht,
d=8.65\pm 0.88
Mpc. See Section
B.2
. This SCM distance is on the comparatively low side, although it agrees to within the uncertainties with the SCM distances from SN 1999em both in this study and by
F. Olivares 2008
. It also agrees with the early EPM distances to SN 1999em as well.

We also have exploited the fact that we have
JWST
photometry not only for the progenitor candidate, but for the entire host galaxy that was encompassed on the NIRCam detectors. This is especially relevant for measuring distances employing a relatively new technique, based on the presence of the J-region asymptotic giant branch (AGB), dominated by carbon-rich AGB stars and best detectable in the near-infrared
(
B. F. Madore & W. L. Freedman 2020
;
W. L. Freedman & B. F. Madore 2020
;
A. J. Lee et al. 2025
;
S. Li et al. 2024
;
S. Li et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, which agrees well with other distance indicators, such as the tip-of-the-red-giant branch (TRGB) method (
W. L. Freedman et al. 2025
; although see
G. S. Anand 2025
); details are given in Section
B.3
. From this technique we found a distance
d=11.38\pm 0.58
Mpc.

In summary, we consider all of these various distance estimates taken together, as shown in Figure
5
. They span a large range, as can be seen. The three early SN 1999em-based EPM estimates are all on the low end of the range. Further later considerations of SN 1999em as a distance indicator resulted in somewhat larger distances, including the SCM estimates both by
F. Olivares 2008
and this work. Our attempt here at an SCM distance for SN 2025pht is statistically consistent with those for SN 1999em; however, taken on its own, it is more indicative of a shorter distance. The two estimates based on stars in the host galaxy, and not on either SN, tend to indicate a longer distance. For lack of any discrimination regarding which estimates are necessarily superior, we establish a weighted mean from all of these estimates of
10.73\pm 1.76
Mpc (
\mu=30.15\pm 0.36
mag), which we adopt hereafter. Note that
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
assumed
d=12.03\pm 0.39
Mpc
(
A. Saha et al. 2006
)
; however, their distance agrees with our adopted value to within our more conservative uncertainty.

The SN-based techniques are likely to be the less certain, since individual SNe II-P can vary one from the other, and the calibrations of these distance indicators are less well established. The galaxy-based indicators are likely to be more reliable and should probably carry more weight. It is interesting that the
D. C. Leonard et al. 2003
Cepheid-based measurement and that from the JAGB conducted here agree with each other well to within their respective uncertainties. These two estimates also are in good agreement with the overall weighted mean.

As a final note here, it should be possible in principle to consider the extensive WFPC2 data from programs GO-9042 and GO-9155 in F555W and F814W, i.e., in
\sim I
and
\sim(V-I)
, to isolate the TRGB in these data
(
M. G. Lee et al. 1993
;
W. L. Freedman et al. 2019
;
G. S. Anand et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.
Additionally, we could consider the WFC3 data from GO-17502 in these same bands, to take advantage of the superior sensitivity and resolution.
Unfortunately, as we show in Section
B.4
, the resulting color-magnitude diagram (CMD) from both datasets is not deep enough and is too confused to reveal the TRGB.
Furthermore,
M. J. B. Newman et al. 2024
provided calibrations in
JWST
NIRCam bands for the TRGB; however, again unfortunately, none of the combinations of bands presented there are represented in the available archival data here.

The three bands in common between the two
JWST

<0.05

We also show in Figure

PHOENIX

, at a temperature of 2300 K. This model obviously does not represent the observed SED at all well. So, clearly what we are seeing in the case of the SN progenitor candidate is emission from dusty CSM, which was reprocessing the (primarily ultraviolet through near-infrared) light of the central stellar photosphere via dust radiative transfer into the mid-infrared and, assumedly, even longer wavelengths.

For that reason, we have generated models for the SED using

;
M. Elitzur & Ž. Ivezić 2001

(
V. Ossenkopf et al. 1992
)

S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
argued that, although the observed SEDs of some Galactic RSGs can be reproduced with the addition of a small mass fraction (
\lesssim

(
T. Verhoelst et al. 2009
)
, an assumption of pure, or near-pure, C-rich dust in this modeling is unjustified. We have also assumed a “modified Mathis–Rumpl–Nordsieck”
(
J. S. Mathis et al. 1977
, MRN;)
dust-grain size distribution (i.e., index
q=3.5
and grain size range
a({\rm min})=0.005
to
a({\rm min})=0.25\ \mu
m). The input photospheric SEDs at the center of the model shell are the
PHOENIX
models. The shell has an inner radius
R_{\rm in}
, at which the dust grains have condensed, and the dust density decreases
\propto r^{-2}
(assuming a constant mass-loss rate from the star,
\dot{M}
) to an outer radius
R_{\rm out}=1000\ R_{\rm in}
(i.e., we assumed
DUSTY
density type=1).

The models are then determined solely by the stellar photospheric temperature (
T_{\rm phot}
), the temperature at the inner radius of the dust sphere (
T[R_{\rm in}]
), and the dust optical depth (
\tau_{V}
) at 0.55
\mu
m. We applied a reduced chi-squared fitting,
\chi_{\rm red}^{2}<1
, of a relatively coarse grid of models to the star’s SED. (Given the size of the uncertainties in each of the
JWST
bands, we could not see a justification for generating a finer grid.) The SED is essentially unconstrained at short wavelengths (
<1.5\ \mu{\rm m}
). We found the allowed ranges in the input parameters to be limited to
T_{\rm phot}=2100
–2500 K,
T(R_{\rm in})=1000
–1200 K, and
\tau_{V}=11
–14. We show in Figure
6
a number of the allowed models. Although these models do not precisely follow the shape of the observed SED at (for example) F164N and F187N, it is our view that they overall provide quite satisfactory representations of the data to within the uncertainties.

In the figure we also show the upper limit from
Dolphot
at F2100W. Given that limit, models that nominally fit the shorter-wavelength data could be subsequently eliminated. If we imposed the stricter upper limit in that band from
space_phot
, as can be seen in the figure, many (although not all) of the model SEDs shown would further violate the limit; however, those that remain are still within the allowed range of parameters.

Interestingly, the
HST
data provide no meaningful constraints on the nature of the observed SED, particularly at F814W. In this case of SN 2025pht, the progenitor candidate SED is, for the first time, entirely defined by
JWST
observations obtained fortuitously not long before explosion. Also for the first time, the SED extends to the red beyond (
Spitzer
) 4.5

We also note that we could add no more than
\sim 4

(
T. Preibisch et al. 1993

The best-fit model SED had
T_{\rm phot}=2300
K,
T(R_{\rm in})=1100
K, and
\tau_{V}=12
. Integrating over that model (from 0.25 to 121,000

,
R_{\rm in}\approx(6.64\pm 1.13)\times 10^{14}
cm, or
\sim 4.4\,R_{\rm eff}
— although as the
DUSTY

T_{\rm eff}>2508
K, and should therefore be taken with a grain of salt (… or dust). From the best-fit model the visual extinction along the line of sight from CSM dust would be
A_{V}({\rm CSM})\approx 7.6
mag (for comparison, we estimated in Section
III.4
that
A_{V}[{\rm host}]=1.69
mag).

The range of apparently very cool input photospheres could be a result of the high dust obscuration by the CSM —
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
pointed to the example of the extremely dusty star VY Canis Majoris, for which interferometric observations indicate a far warmer photosphere

\sim 3490

than does SED modeling (
\sim 2800

M. Harwit et al. 2001

V. Ossenkopf et al. 1992
.) It is also difficult to envision how the reprocessed light from a 3500 K photosphere could behave in this same manner.

We note that, should the photospheric temperature actually be closer to 3500 K, then
R_{\rm eff}\approx 941\ R_{\odot}
, which is thought to be more typical for RSGs, based on previous estimates for these stars across nominally a broad luminosity range. Furthermore, then, for the dust shell,
R_{\rm in}\approx 10.1\ R_{\rm eff}
(the temperature warning from
DUSTY
would not apply in this case).

V
Discussion and Conclusions

A full analysis of SN 2025pht itself is beyond the scope of this work; however, we can say that we find it likely that it is a short-plateau Type II-P event. Such SNe are also quite luminous, surrounded by dense CSM resulting from high mass loss, and may generally arise from high-mass progenitor stars (
D. Hiramatsu et al. 2021
; although see
R. S. Teja et al. 2022
). We would likely expect these SNe, given the massive CSM set up prior to explosion, to exhibit sustained emission at late times as a result of SN shock-CSM interaction.

We have also inferred that the star that gave rise to SN 2025pht was,
at least in 2024
, a luminous RSG surrounded by dusty CSM. The star is also the candidate, to our knowledge, that is most extinguished by interstellar dust within the host galaxy. Without the archival
JWST
observations we would not have been able to fully characterize (or even detect) the candidate in the first place. The only information that we would have had, based solely on the
HST
F814W data, was that the star was variable in brightness, possibly as an LPV. However, that alone would not have provided any further indications regarding the properties of the star.

The star is among the most luminous (if not
the
most luminous) RSG, at
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})=5.08\pm 0.16
, that has been identified so far as an SN II-P progenitor candidate. At the upper boundary of this estimate, this pushes somewhat against the ceiling set by the RSG problem. It also may well be the dustiest, or certainly among the dustiest, progenitor. See Figure
8
, in which we compare the SN 2025pht progenitor candidate observed SED with the dusty, luminous progenitor SEDs of other SNe II-P, specifically SN 2012aw (
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2012
; also
C. S. Kochanek et al. 2012
;
M. Fraser et al. 2012
), SN 2017eaw (
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2019
; also
C. D. Kilpatrick & R. J. Foley 2018
;
L. Rui et al. 2019
), and SN 2023ixf (
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
; also, e.g.,
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2023
;
J. E. Jencson et al. 2023
;
D. Xiang et al. 2024
;
C. L. Ransome et al. 2024
;
Y.-J. Qin et al. 2024
). The SN 2025pht candidate SED differs notably from the SN 2017eaw one; however, it is similar in shape and overall luminosity with that for SN 2023ixf (despite the apparent lack of similarity in the spectra and light curves of SN 2025pht and this SN), although the latter does not extend past 4.5
\mu
m. We do not really know the overall shape of the SN 2012aw progenitor candidate SED, since it only extended to about 2
\mu
m. That our measured SED for the SN 2025pht candidate is overall similar to these other SN progenitors, particularly to that of SN 2023ixf, provides us with some satisfaction in the veracity of our analysis here.

From
DUSTY
modeling of the SN 2023ixf progenitor candidate, following similar input assumptions,
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
arrived at best-fitting models with ranges
T_{\rm eff}=2300
–2700 K,
T(R_{\rm in})=900
–1300 K, and
\tau_{V}=9
–11, which are quite similar to what we found here for the SN 2025pht candidate. (Could the inferred cooler-than-expected
T_{\rm eff}
in both cases be an evolutionary state prior to explosion?) Yet, the two SNe appear to have noteworthy differences in their properties and evolution, even if the two RSG progenitors may have had similarities in their nature, possibly pointing to overall differences in mass-loss histories, progenitor environments, and interactions with a possible binary companion. Since SN 2025pht was discovered
\sim 14
days post-explosion, and the first spectrum was obtained at
\sim 18
days, any flash-ionization features that may have appeared at early times were no longer detectable. So, there is no way of determining the nature of the CSM immediate to the progenitor and early shock interaction with it, and to compare that with (for example) the early evolution of SN 2023ixf (in which the flash features persisted for only
\sim 7
days, implying a shock radius of
\sim 6\times 10^{14}
cm;
W.
Jacobson-Galán 2025
, and references therein).

Although we are in agreement with
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
that the star was likely highly luminous and enshrouded in dusty CSM, our major results differ significantly, in that we find compelling evidence that the internal host-galaxy extinction is quite high, that the star’s
T_{\rm eff}
was cooler (2100–2500 K vs. 2760–3240 K), and, more fundamentally, that the dust composition was oxygen-rich, as we would expect for RSGs, rather than carbon-rich. Why would this one RSG be so inherently different from the hundreds of known RSGs in the Local Group? Ultimately, differences in photometric measurement and approach to the modeling has led to our
L_{\rm bol}
inference being somewhat higher (up to
\log[L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot}]=5.24
), although the two values agree to within the uncertainties in each.

Following
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2023
, it would be intriguing to examine whether our cursory attempt at an estimate of a period of variability for the SN 2025pht progenitor candidate (Section
III.3
) is consistent with the bolometric luminosity we have estimated (Section
IV
). The variability period for RSGs can be empirically correlated with their luminosity (a “period-luminosity relation”), typically in a given photometric bandpass
(
M. Yang & B. W. Jiang 2011
;
M. Yang & B. W. Jiang 2012
;
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2018
;
Y. Ren et al. 2019
)
, such as
K_{s}
. For the progenitor candidate, the absolute
K_{s}
brightness is
M_{K_{s}}\approx-10.8
mag (based on the F212N and F277W measurements). Recall that our nominal estimates of the star’s period are
\sim 470
and
\sim 660
days. If we consider the period-luminosity relations from
M. Yang & B. W. Jiang 2011
,
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2018
, and
Y. Ren et al. 2019
, they would all predict that
M_{K_{s}}\approx-10.3
and
\approx-10.8
mag, respectively. The former is significantly less luminous than the actual value. However, the latter period results in a luminosity that is far more consistent with the value we have inferred from the SED modeling. This is reassuring, and tends to indicate that the higher value for the period,
\sim 660
days, is more likely, if the star was actually an LPV.

The
DUSTY
models provide a good representation of the
JWST
observations from 2024. However, they are potentially insufficient at optical wavelengths. Via synthetic photometry, the observed brightness at WFC3 F814W of the best-fitting model would be
\sim 26.8
mag (Vega), whereas the estimated upper limit in this band is
<25.6
mag,
\sim 1.2
mag brighter. (The synthetic brightnesses in the
JWST
bands agree with the observations on average by
\sim 0.12
AB mag.)
The star, however, was observable at F814W in 2001. We had developed in Section
III.3
a plausible, yet grossly oversimplified, pulsational variability model, that was consistent with the F814W upper limit in 2024. Possibly the amplitude of the variability was actually more exaggerated than what we inferred with the model. Possibly much of the dust shell, the evidence for which was observed in 2024, was set up through enhanced mass loss sometime in the intervening
\sim 23
yr, and therefore simple, (semi-)regular variability, potentially seen in 2001, no longer applied as it had before. Possibly it never did. Possibly the star had been in its observed state in 2024 for a number of years or decades. We just do not know.

If all we had available to us, photometrically, was the brightest F814W magnitude for the star from 2001, and we relied exclusively on a bolometric correction (
BC
), e.g.,
BC({\rm F814W})=0.0
mag
(
B. Davies & E. R. Beasor 2018
)
, then we would have arrived at a bolometric magnitude
M_{\rm bol}\approx-6.4
mag. Assuming
M_{\rm bol}({\odot})=+4.74
mag
(
E. E. Mamajek et al. 2015
)
, then
\log(L_{\rm bol}/L_{\odot})\approx 4.5
, which would be a immense underestimate, compared to what we have determined here from detailed modeling of the infrared SED of this star. This should be taken as a cautionary warning (likely not the first time this has been issued) that the optical, even in the red, poorly exemplifies the actual emitted light from such a star. Additionally, of course, we know that the star was significantly less luminous at F814W in 2024, when all of the
JWST
data were obtained, owing to the star’s inferred variability.

We can obtain an estimate of the mass-loss rate,
\dot{M}
, from the SN 2025pht progenitor candidate based on
DUSTY
modeling. Here we assumed the
DUSTY
density type = 4, i.e., an analytical approximation for radiatively driven winds with the relative shell thickness of
10^{4}\ R_{\rm in}
(instead of
10^{3}\ R_{\rm in}
), for the best-fitting model (
T_{\rm phot}=2300
K,
T(R_{\rm in})=1100
K, and
\tau_{V}=12
). The spectral flux output from this model re-run only differed from the original output, on average, by
\sim 3.5
%. Following
K. Antoniadis et al. 2024
, and assuming Galactic values for the gas-to-dust ratio of 200 and the bulk density of 3 g cm
-3
, and scaling by
L_{\rm bol}
of the best-fit model, we found that
\dot{M}\approx 4.8\times 10^{-5}\ M_{\odot}\ {\rm yr}^{-1}
. This is generally somewhat higher than what has been found for Galactic RSGs at the luminosity of this star, e.g., by
E. R. Beasor et al. 2020
and
R. M. Humphreys et al. 2020
.

Although
A. Kozyreva et al. 2025
called for a precursor event for SN 2023ixf, roughly analogous to what
B. Davies et al. 2022
proposed — though observational evidence has pointed away from such an event in this case
(
Y. Dong et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
— the situation for the SN 2025pht progenitor candidate is comparatively unconstrained. We are not aware of any other available pre-SN data with sufficient spatial resolution that might provide us with clues as to the star’s nature during that decades-long time interval between 2001 and 2024.

Furthermore, the interplay between pulsations and interactions with a binary companion could affect the pre-SN envelope and CSM structure
(
E. Laplace et al. 2025
, and references therein)
. However, the uncertainties that we have needed to place on the ensemble of SED measurements for the progenitor candidate preclude us here from a meaningful constraint on a companion star
(see, e.g.,
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2024
, in the case of SN 2023ixf)
.

It should now go without saying that the candidate needs to be confirmed as the actual progenitor (e.g.,
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2023
, and references therein), through, say, future
JWST
observations to determine whether the star has disappeared. This could be challenging, though, since we are expecting excess late-time emission from long-term CSM interaction. Additionally, we expect dust formation for SNe showing evidence for interaction
(
A. Sarangi & I. Cherchneff 2015
;
A. Sarangi & J. D. Slavin 2022
, e.g.,)
, which will manifest itself with increased flux in
JWST
bands and thwart efforts to determine that the candidate has vanished.

This star, and other stars similar to it, could possibly go completely undetected in bulk searches for RSGs in galaxies.
S. K. Sarbadhicary et al. 2025
recently completed a study in which they isolated nearly 100,000 candidate RSGs across several nearby galaxies. Their selection algorithm consisted of a
HST
+
JWST
color and luminosity “wedge.” The SN 2025pht progenitor candidate had color
({\rm F814W}-{\rm F200W})_{0}\gtrsim 3.5
mag and luminosity
M_{\rm F200W}\approx-8.7
mag, which would have placed the star not only outside of the wedge boundaries, but also potentially beyond the nominal detection limits of their galaxy survey, at least in this filter combination. Complicating detection further, of course, was the significant host-galaxy reddening in this case. Possibly other color spaces, based on
JWST
bands alone, would provide better means of selecting these especially dusty and luminous RSGs.

In the case of SN 2025pht we have been extraordinarily fortunate that we have photometry with exceptional wavelength coverage, especially in the near- to mid-infrared, of the progenitor candidate, which are contemporaneous, or nearly-contemporaneous — what we earlier termed a “quasi-snapshot,” which reveals the star’s SED in extraordinary detail. This is astoundingly rare, to date, although progressively more
JWST
programs are covering in various bands a number of nearby galaxies, which could be potential SN hosts. Though we should appreciate the current stroke of luck, it is possible that this could occur again soon. However, what could well be lacking is sampling of the star’s variability. We know that RSGs can be LPVs and, in fact, that their colors vary with their pulsations (e.g.,
Y. Ogane et al. 2022
in the particular case of Betelgeuse; the SN 2023ixf progenitor candidate, for instance, varied in
H-K
by
\sim 0.68
mag over just
\sim 34
days,
M. D. Soraisam et al. 2023
). Possibly, archival
HST
data at one or more epochs would also exist, as in the current case. Potentially, through a combination of time-series coverage of nearby hosts via the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, together with the superb sensitivity and spatial resolution of
JWST
, and the wide-field, near-infrared coverage of the upcoming
Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
, the community may be able to gather more meaningful and instructive statistics on the nature of SN II-P progenitors.

B.1
Another SCM Distance Estimate for SN 1999em

Following
J. Polshaw et al. 2015
, the SCM is based on the
I
-band brightness and the Fe
ii
expansion velocity at age 50 days. It appears that the SCM described by
F. Olivares 2008
was based on 30 days. We estimated the explosion epoch from the
M. I. Jones et al. 2009
EPM value, for which those authors found
t_{0}
= JD 2,451,476.3
\pm 1.1
and 2,451,474.0
\pm 2.0
using the E96 and D05 models, respectively. These values for the explosion epoch agree to within the uncertainties; taking the average,

t_{0}
= 2,451,475.2, MJD 51474.7, which is 1999 Oct 23.7. This agrees with what
A. Elmhamdi et al. 2003
had found, 1999 Oct 24.5. Adding 50 days to this yields MJD 51524.7 (1999 Dec 12.7).

We estimated that the
I
magnitude on day 50 is
I_{50}=13.29\pm 0.03
, which is a weighted average of the photometry around this date from
M. Hamuy et al. 2001
,
D. C. Leonard et al. 2002
, and
A. Elmhamdi et al. 2003

D. C. Leonard et al. 2002
spectrum of SN 1999em from 1999 December 12 from WISeREP. After correcting the spectrum for the host-galaxy redshift, we measured the velocity at Fe
ii

\lambda
5169 on day 50,

3810\pm 270
km s
-1
. We also estimated a photospheric velocity,
v_{50}({\rm phot})
, which is the weighted average of
v_{50}(5169)
together with the velocities at Fe
ii

\lambda
\lambda
4629, 4924, and 5018: a similar
3620\pm 120
km s
-1
.

The remaining quantity to be determined is the total extinction

.
M. I. Jones et al. 2009
assumed that the host-galaxy visual extinction is
A_{V}=0.24\pm 0.14
mag, whereas
F. Olivares 2008
, from
L. Dessart & D. J. Hillier 2006

A_{V}=0.31\pm 0.16

A_{V}=0.27\pm 0.15

R_{V}=3.1
,
A_{I}({\rm host})=0.17\pm 0.09
mag. Including the Galactic foreground
A_{I}({\rm Gal})=0.061
mag
(
E. F. Schlafly & D. P. Finkbeiner 2011
)
leads to

Based on
v_{50}(5169)
we found that
d=10.45\pm 2.32
Mpc, and
from
v_{50}({\rm phot})
,
d=9.86\pm 1.82
Mpc, which agree with each other to within the (large) uncertainties. These values also agree with the SCM distance by
F. Olivares 2008
, again, to within the uncertainties. At the very least, we have provided a cross-check on the previous estimate, employing a different SCM calibration. Note that
J. Polshaw et al. 2015
based their calibration of SCM on a value of
H_{0}=73.8

-1
Mpc

B.3
A JAGB Distance For the Host Galaxy

Following
S. Li et al. 2024
;
S. Li et al. 2025
, we considered
Dolphot
photometry across all chips in F150W and F277W, using the “warmstart” option with the F150W output as the input star list for the F277W photometry. We subsequently filtered the merged star list in both bands with the following photometric quality cuts for the
Dolphot
output parameters: crowding
<0.5
, sharpness
2

\leq 0.01
, object type
\leq 2
, signal-to-noise ratio
S/N\geq 5
, and photometry quality flag
\leq 2

(
J. T. Warfield et al. 2023
;
G. S. Anand et al. 2024
)
. We then corrected both bands for Galactic foreground extinction. The resulting color-magnitude diagram (CMD) is shown in Figure
15
.

Next, following
A. J. Lee et al. 2025
, we applied a series of concentric elliptical masks (at a position angle of 20°), centered on the galactic nucleus, with progressively increasing major and minor axes, to mitigate against the effects of crowding and interstellar dust in the inner parts of the host, and establish a convergence in the mode brightness of the JAGB. We isolated the JAGB in the CMD as stars in the color range
1.0\leq({\rm F150W}-{\rm F277W})_{0}\leq 1.5
mag
(
S. Li et al. 2024
)
. Figure
14
displays this modal brightness at F150W as a function of semimajor axis.

A number of factors determine the uncertainty in the distance from this method. The statistical uncertainty includes an uncertainty in the mode of 0.01 mag; uncertainty due to the choice of smoothing scale of 0.04 mag (established by increasing and decreasing by 0.05 mag in the GLOESS smoothing scale); and, a “convergence” error of 0.02 mag, following, e.g.,
A. J. Lee et al. 2025
. All together those add up in quadrature to 0.05 mag.
The systematic uncertainty includes several individual terms, including an uncertainty in the
Dolphot
aperture corrections of 0.02 mag; uncertainty in the foreground extinction of 0.01 mag; miscellaneous systematics in
Dolphot
measurements (e.g., empirical point-spread-function fitting adjustments, etc.) of 0.02 mag; an average crowding correction, based on values from samples in
S. Li et al. 2024
;
S. Li et al. 2025
, adopted to be 0.04 mag;
and an additional systematic zero-point uncertainty, composed of many individual terms, such as a geometric distance uncertainty of
0.03 mag, “methodological” variations, of how the analysis is conducted, as pointed out by the SH0ES collaboration
(
A. G. Riess et al. 2022
)
, of 0.06 mag, a statistical measurement uncertainty of the JAGB feature in M106 of 0.02 mag, aperture correction uncertainties in the M106 measurement of 0.02 mag, and uncertainties due to smoothing scales of 0.04 mag.
Adding these systematic uncertainties in quadrature with the uncertainties in the JAGB magnitude results in the total 0.12 mag uncertainty in the distance modulus. Lastly, we caution that the JAGB technique is relatively new, and there are ongoing investigations into the potential metallicity dependencies of the JAGB feature
(
S. Goldman et al. 2025
)
. Our uncertainty budget should be considered as a lower limit on the true uncertainty of this method.

B.4
Host-Galaxy TRGB Distance Investigation

We considered all of the available
HST
WFPC2 data in both F555W and F814W, across all four chips of the instrument covering the host galaxy, to investigate whether it would be possible to detect the TRGB. The data from GO-9155 consisted of 12 epochs of WFPC2 F555W and 7 epochs of WFPC2 F814W images, with 1100 s exposure times for each frame. We also included the pair of 460 s exposures in F814W from GO-9042. The total depths of the CMD were ostensibly, then, a very deep 13,200 s and 8160 s in F555W and F814W, respectively. All of the frames were then run through
Dolphot
. We adopted the same quality filtering scheme for these
HST
data as we did in Section
B.3
for the
JWST
data.

We also considered the WFC3 data from GO-17502 in F555W and F814W with total exposure times 625 and 749 s, respectively. Although the depth is not nearly comparable to that afforded by the WFPC2 data, we could stand to gain with the higher WFC3/UVIS sensitivity and resolution. Again, these data were processed with
Dolphot
and a similar post-processing filtering was applied on the output source detections.

We show the resulting CMD in Figure
16
. As one can see, the WFPC2 photometry reached as deep as
{\rm F814W}_{0}\sim 26.5
mag, whereas the WFC3 observations only reach
\sim 25.8

mag. We also show the inferred levels of the TRGB, if the host distance is the JAGB value and also for the weighted mean of all the distance estimates, assuming the absolute brightness of the TRGB is
M_{I}=-4.05
mag
(
W. L. Freedman 2021
)
. As can be seen, neither the WFC3 nor the WFPC2 results are sufficiently deep to have reached the TRGB. (Of course, if the actual host distance is lower than these two estimates, then the TRGB may have been detectable, although the tip is not readily obvious in the results shown.)
