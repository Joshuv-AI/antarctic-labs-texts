The Immunometabolic Bridge: Targeting Metainflammation to Prevent the Transition from Obesity to Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Mingqian Wu, Yubo Han, Li Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity and cardiovascular disease are linked by more than excess adiposity. They are connected through a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state that determines who progresses from risk to overt disease. We propose that metainflammation, arising from adipose tissue dysfunction and metabolic surplus, constitutes a key immunometabolic bridge between preclinical obesity and clinical cardiovascular disease. A central tenet of this framework is that metainflammation acts as a contributory driver of this transition; however, its pathogenic potential is realized only within a permissive metabolic environment marked by dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, and genetic susceptibility. The intensity and persistence of this inflammatory state are governed by three interdependent mechanisms: immune cell heterogeneity within adipose tissue, mitochondrial dysfunction with associated redox stress, and cellular senescence driving a pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype—all sustained by impaired resolution pathways. Epidemiological and genetic evidence supports this dual requirement, demonstrating that inflammation requires a conducive metabolic milieu to precipitate clinical events. This conceptual framework translates directly into a tiered clinical strategy: identifying individuals with a high-inflammatory preclinical obesity phenotype, followed by a stepped intervention approach—from lifestyle modification and statins, highlighting their pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects, to dual-benefit GLP‑1 receptor agonists, and ultimately to senolytic therapies that target the senescent cell root cause. Screening biomarkers such as hsCRP and IL‑6 can help identify high‑risk individuals. By redefining the trajectory from preclinical to clinical obesity as an immunometabolic continuum and providing actionable strategies to dismantle this bridge before end-organ damage, this framework offers a paradigm shift in the primary prevention of obesity-related cardiovascular disease.

Introduction

Obesity has reached pandemic proportions, with nearly 2 billion adults projected to be affected by 2035.1 Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death globally, and obesity‑attributable CVD accounts for a growing share of this burden. Traditional BMI‑centred paradigms, however, fail to explain two key observations: the marked heterogeneity in cardiovascular outcomes among individuals with comparable adiposity, and the substantial time lag between obesity onset and manifest CVD. These observations challenge the notion that adiposity alone is sufficient to cause CVD and point instead to the existence of modifying factors that influence individual susceptibility. A plausible explanation is that a permissive factor, such as a chronic low‑grade inflammatory state, interacts with the necessary condition of excess adiposity to accelerate disease progression, thereby determining both the timing and severity of clinical events.2

A landmark advance came in January 2025, when the Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology Commission3 proposed a new diagnostic framework distinguishing preclinical obesity, defined as excess adiposity without organ dysfunction, from clinical obesity, characterised by objective organ damage. This distinction is conceptually important because it separates the state of having excess adiposity from the state of suffering its pathological consequences, thereby creating a window of opportunity for early intervention. Importantly, the “preclinical” phase should not be viewed merely as a passive window but as a state of active compensation, where the body attempts to maintain metabolic homeostasis despite increasing inflammatory and mechanical stress. By reframing obesity as a chronic disease with a definable preclinical phase, the Commission sets the stage for understanding the drivers of progression from risk to manifest disease.4

This perspective advances the central thesis that metainflammation, the chronic low‑grade inflammation arising from metabolic surplus and adipose tissue dysfunction, constitutes a key immunometabolic bridge connecting preclinical obesity to clinical CVD.5 This conceptualisation builds upon the understanding that immune responses and metabolic pathways are linked through evolutionarily conserved interactions; the maintenance of this delicate equilibrium is fundamental to health, whereas its disruption underlies the pathogenesis of obesity, diabetes, and other chronic diseases. We refine the concept into a testable hypothesis: metainflammation is a key contributory driver, though not sufficient, of this transition; it operates within a permissive metabolic environment encompassing dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance, and genetic susceptibility that collectively governs whether inflammatory signals translate into clinical events. In this framework, inflammation provides the requisite pathogenic impetus (the “spark”), while the metabolic milieu determines the threshold at which that impetus culminates in overt organ damage (the “fuel”). By conceptualising the transition from preclinical to clinical obesity as an immunometabolic continuum rather than a binary switch, this model reconciles the observed heterogeneity in cardiovascular outcomes and offers a mechanistic rationale for targeted prevention strategies. As a Perspective article, this work provides a narrative conceptual synthesis.

Immune Heterogeneity in Adipose Tissue

Single‑cell transcriptomic studies have revealed that adipose tissue macrophages exist on a spectrum of activation states rather than the simplistic M1/M2 dichotomy.6 In metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), specific mesenchymal‑like mesothelial cells in visceral fat correlate with preserved metabolic function, whereas in metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO), pro-inflammatory macrophage subpopulations dominate.6 This cellular heterogeneity provides a mechanistic basis for the variable inflammatory set point observed in clinical practice. Genetic variants, including PNPLA3 in hepatic steatosis and TCF7L2 in insulin resistance, further modulate this inflammatory tone and influence who develops high‑inflammation phenotypes. Specifically, PNPLA3 variants promote a pro‑inflammatory secretome in visceral adipose depots, thereby enhancing adipocyte‑immune crosstalk, while TCF7L2 variants may alter adipose tissue macrophage polarisation via Wnt signalling pathways.

Mitochondrial Dysfunction as an Amplifier

Adipocyte and macrophage mitochondria are central metabolic hubs whose dysfunction fuels inflammation. In obesity, mitochondrial stress leads to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and release of mitochondrial damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and perpetuate IL‑1β production.7,8 This creates a feed forward loop: inflammation impairs mitochondrial function, and dysfunctional mitochondria drive further inflammation. The degree of mitochondrial impairment may therefore explain part of the inter‑individual variation in inflammatory burden.

Cellular Senescence and the SASP: A Root Cause

A transformative insight is that senescent cells accumulate in adipose tissue with ageing and obesity and secrete a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) rich in pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL‑6, IL‑1β, and TNF α, as well as chemokines.9 The SASP propagates inflammation to neighbouring cells, turning a local stress response into a sustained systemic signal. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction is a primary driver of the SASP: damaged mitochondria release DAMPs that directly activate the SASP programme, unifying these two processes under a common root cause. Preclinical studies in nonhuman primates demonstrate that intermittent administration of the senolytic combination dasatinib plus quercetin reduces senescence marker gene expression in adipose tissue, decreases circulating inflammatory markers such as PAI‑1 and MMP‑9, and improves immune cell profiles with significant anti-inflammatory shifts.10 Importantly, these effects were accompanied by reduced microbial translocation biomarkers, suggesting improved intestinal barrier function.10 This positions cellular senescence as both a key mechanistic pillar and a promising therapeutic target.

Failed Resolution of Inflammation

Beyond the three core pillars, several modifiable factors further modulate the immunometabolic bridge. In healthy individuals, acute inflammation is actively terminated by specialised pro‑resolving mediators (SPMs) such as resolvins and protectins. In obesity, these resolution pathways are impaired, allowing inflammation to persist as a low-grade chronic state. The failure of resolution, together with ongoing senescence, explains why metainflammation does not spontaneously subside even after weight stabilisation. These pillars, encompassing immune heterogeneity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and failed resolution driven partly by cellular senescence, collectively determine the magnitude and persistence of the inflammatory bridge, explaining the variable risk observed in clinical practice.

Beyond these core mechanisms, several endogenous and nutritional factors modulate the immunometabolic bridge. Vitamin D exerts pleiotropic immunomodulatory effects, including downregulation of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6, TNF‑α) and upregulation of anti‑inflammatory pathways via the vitamin D receptor on immune cells.11 Low vitamin D levels are consistently associated with higher hsCRP and increased cardiometabolic risk. The gut microbiome influences systemic inflammation through bacterial metabolites such as short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which promote regulatory T‑cell differentiation and strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, thereby reducing microbial translocation and endotoxaemia.12 Antioxidants (eg, polyphenols, vitamin C, vitamin E) counteract oxidative stress and can lower circulating inflammatory markers, although their clinical efficacy in obesity‑related CVD remains under investigation.13

From Bridge to Disease: Arguing for Causality

Having established the mechanistic underpinnings, we now examine whether inflammation truly drives the progression from preclinical to clinical CVD. We structure this argument around two interlocking claims: that inflammation is a contributory driver, and that it operates within a permissive metabolic environment.

Evidence for Inflammation as a Contributory Driver

If inflammation is a major causal contributor, then reducing it should lower CVD risk independently of other risk factors. The CANTOS trial14 provides strong supportive evidence for a causal contribution: patients with prior myocardial infarction and residual inflammatory risk, defined as hsCRP of at least 2 mg per L, randomised to canakinumab, an IL‑1β neutralising antibody, experienced a 15% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events despite no change in lipids. This proof of concept demonstrates that targeting a proximal inflammatory mediator directly reduces clinical events, establishing causality. However, the concomitant increase in fatal infections with canakinumab underscores the need for more precise immunomodulation rather than broad immunosuppression.

Mendelian randomisation (MR) studies complement trial data by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, overcoming confounding and reverse causation. A comprehensive MR study examining the causal relationship among immune cells, plasma metabolites, and coronary atherosclerosis identified nine immune cell phenotypes with significant causal links to coronary artery disease, including specific T‑cell and monocyte populations.15 Mediation analysis revealed that the metabolite 3‑hydroxy‑2‑ethylpropionate mediated the effect of IgD+ CD24+ B cells on coronary atherosclerosis, with a mediating effect of 0.961, while trans‑2‑hexenoylglycine regulated IgD+ CD24+ B cells with a mediation ratio of 16.7%.15 Another MR study integrating transcriptomics identified two key inflammatory genes, HIF1A and TNFAIP3, with causal relationships to coronary artery disease, providing genetic evidence for inflammation as a causal driver.16 An MR study of IL‑6 receptor blockade demonstrated protective effects on coronary heart disease (odds ratio 0.757) and myocardial infarction (odds ratio 0.840), further supporting the causal role of IL‑6 signalling in atherosclerotic disease.17 These genetic data reinforce the causal role of inflammation and identify specific molecular nodes that could serve as therapeutic targets.

The Permissive Role of the Metabolic Milieu

If inflammation were sufficient, then everyone with elevated inflammatory markers would develop CVD, which is not the case. A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies18 published between 2013 and 2023, comprising 6,816 subjects, demonstrated that elevated hsCRP levels predict subsequent cardiovascular events with an odds ratio of 1.99 (95% CI: 1.36–2.94, p = 0.0004), indicating that affected individuals are nearly twice as likely to suffer cardiovascular events. Complementing this, a large‑scale prospective cohort study19 of 8,050 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention found that elevated hsCRP (>3 mg/L) independently predicts major adverse cardiovascular events at 3 years, with hazard ratios of 1.31 (95% CI 1.05–1.62) in non‑diabetic patients and 1.68 (95% CI 1.29–2.19) in diabetic patients after multivariable adjustment. This effect size is substantial, yet it also implies that other factors contribute significantly to excess risk, particularly as the meta-analysis included heterogeneous populations and outcomes, while the cohort study focused on patients with established coronary artery disease requiring revascularization. The UK Biobank 2025 analysis20 of 112,866 participants with preclinical or clinical obesity provides direct support for the “contributory plus permissive” model. Over 13.45 years of follow‑up, 21,601 incident CVD cases occurred. The strongest predictor was the triglyceride glucose index combined with waist circumference (TyG WC), a composite measure integrating metabolic parameters including triglycerides and glucose with anthropometric waist measurement, which yielded a hazard ratio of 1.41 when comparing extreme quartiles.20 Importantly, mediation analyses confirmed that inflammatory biomarkers partially mediated the association, but residual direct effects remained, consistent with a model where inflammation is a key driver yet requires a permissive metabolic background to exert its full effect. Thus, the clinical translation of the immunometabolic bridge depends on both the height of the inflammatory fire and the presence of metabolic fuel. This dual requirement guides our approach to risk stratification and intervention.

Clinical Implications: A Roadmap for Identification and Intervention

The framework of a contributory inflammatory driver operating within a permissive metabolic environment directly translates into a clinical roadmap. We propose a tiered strategy to identify individuals with high inflammatory preclinical obesity, the target population for early intervention, and outline evidence-based interventions that range from established therapies to emerging root cause approaches.

Tiered Screening: Identifying the High Inflammatory Phenotype

Table 1 summarises a pragmatic, three‑tier screening strategy that balances feasibility, cost, and predictive information. Tier 1 uses universally available markers: high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hsCRP) and the TyG WC index. These inexpensive tools can be applied in primary care to flag individuals with elevated inflammatory metabolic risk. Tier 2 is reserved for those with discordant or high-risk Tier 1 results; it includes interleukin 6 (IL‑6) and the adiponectin–leptin ratio, which provide mechanistic insight and help refine risk prediction. Genetic studies support IL‑6 as a causal mediator in atherosclerotic disease,17,21 while the adiponectin–leptin ratio has been shown to correlate inversely with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome components.22,23 Tier 3 markers, including soluble ST2 and Netrin 1, are currently research tools but may enter clinical practice as evidence accumulates. Soluble ST2 has demonstrated prognostic value for cardiovascular events and mortality,24,25 and emerging evidence supports the Netrin 1‑adiponectin axis as an immunometabolic marker set in early insulin‑resistant states.26 By implementing this stepwise approach, clinicians can identify the “high inflammatory preclinical obesity” phenotype, the group most likely to benefit from targeted anti-inflammatory therapy. Evidence-Based Interventions: From Foundation to Frontier

Foundational interventions remain the cornerstone. Lifestyle modification achieving 5% to 10% weight loss reduces inflammatory markers by 30% to 50%. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 randomised controlled trials including 2,752 participants demonstrated that exercise training significantly increased adiponectin levels and reduced fasting insulin, fasting glucose, insulin resistance measured by HOMA‑IR, and triglyceride levels in overweight and obese adults.27 Aerobic and resistance training showed the most consistent beneficial effects on inflammatory and cardiometabolic markers.27 Statins provide dual benefit: potent LDL lowering plus well documented anti-inflammatory effects, reducing hsCRP independently of cholesterol reduction. The JUPITER trial28 proved that targeting inflammation with statins in individuals with elevated hsCRP but normal LDL reduces cardiovascular events. Notably, the greatest benefit in JUPITER was observed in those who achieved both LDL cholesterol reduction and hsCRP reduction, supporting the “dual requirement” model that inflammation and metabolic risk factors act synergistically.

Dual benefit therapies offer additional leverage. GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, reduce weight and improve glycaemia while exerting direct anti-inflammatory effects on immune cells and vascular endothelium. A recent pharmacological review highlights that GLP‑1 receptor agonists reduce systemic and tissue‑specific inflammation through inhibition of nuclear factor kappa‑B signalling, reduction of proinflammatory cytokines, and modulation of immune cell activity including macrophages and microglia.29 These effects translate into improved insulin sensitivity and endothelial function, reduced hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction‑associated steatotic liver disease, and mitigation of atherosclerosis progression.29 The SELECT trial30 demonstrated that semaglutide reduces major adverse cardiovascular events by 20% in individuals with obesity and established CVD, an effect only partially explained by weight loss, consistent with weight‑independent anti-inflammatory actions. A large meta‑analysis of cardiovascular outcome trials31 confirmed that GLP‑1 receptor agonist treatment reduces major adverse cardiovascular events by 12% in patients with type 2 diabetes, with consistent benefits across key subgroups including those with and without established cardiovascular disease.

Preclinical studies in nonhuman primates demonstrate that six months of intermittent dasatinib plus quercetin exposure is safe, reduces adipose tissue senescence markers, improves immune cell profiles with anti-inflammatory shifts, decreases microbial translocation biomarkers, and when combined with caloric restriction, dramatically reduces glycosylated haemoglobin A1c.10 A Phase 2 clinical trial (NCT06240403) is currently investigating digoxin as a senolytic agent to reduce microvascular endothelial cell senescence and improve subcutaneous adipose tissue function in patients with heart failure and type 2 diabetes, with primary completion expected in 2028. Should these findings be confirmed, senolytics could complement existing therapies by providing durable remission of inflammation rather than continuous suppression. Other investigational approaches include NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors and SPM analogues that enhance resolution of inflammation. Marine‑derived omega‑3 fatty acids at high doses exceeding 2000 mg per day produce substantial triglyceride reductions and may have anti-inflammatory effects, although consistent benefits on other metabolic syndrome components remain to be established.32

Strengths and Limitations of the Proposed Framework

This conceptual framework has several strengths. First, it integrates recent mechanistic advances (single-cell omics, senescence biology) with large-scale clinical trial evidence from CANTOS, SELECT, and JUPITER and the 2025 Lancet Commission diagnostic shift. Second, it provides an actionable, tiered roadmap for primary prevention, moving beyond BMI-centric paradigms. Third, the “immunometabolic bridge” metaphor offers an intuitive and teachable model for both researchers and clinicians.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Heterogeneity in inflammatory set points across individuals is not fully captured by current biomarkers; genetic and epigenetic factors may define distinct endotypes that respond differently to therapy. Cost-effectiveness of advanced biomarkers and tiered screening in diverse healthcare settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, remains to be fully evaluated. Long-term safety of sustained immunomodulation (eg, chronic IL-1β blockade, senolytics) requires further investigation, particularly regarding infection risk and off-target effects. Senolytic therapies are still experimental in humans; their efficacy and safety in the context of preclinical obesity are not yet established.

The Inflammation Threshold: A Key Unanswered Question

A critical gap in translating this roadmap is defining the therapeutic target: how much must inflammation be reduced to achieve clinical benefit? The CANTOS trial suggested that on treatment hsCRP below 2 mg per L is associated with greater risk reduction. Whether this threshold applies to primary prevention in preclinical obesity, and whether relative reduction, for example, 30% from baseline, or absolute level is the better guide, remains unknown. Future trials must incorporate serial inflammatory monitoring to establish quantitative targets that inform treatment goals.

Other challenges include individual heterogeneity, encompassing genetic and epigenetic determinants of inflammatory set points, long‑term safety of sustained immunomodulation, and cost effectiveness of tiered screening. Nevertheless, the convergence of mechanistic insight and clinical trial evidence positions us to move towards precision immuno‑cardiovascular prevention.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

We have argued that metainflammation is a key contributory driver operating within a permissive metabolic milieu for the transition from preclinical to clinical obesity-related CVD. Mechanistically, immune heterogeneity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence, particularly the SASP, determine the magnitude and persistence of the inflammatory bridge. The contributory role of inflammation is supported by randomised trials including CANTOS and by Mendelian randomisation studies, while its permissive dependence on metabolic stressors is illustrated by the effect size from meta-analysis and the predictive superiority of composite indices like TyG WC. Clinically, this framework yields an actionable roadmap: tiered screening to identify the high‑inflammatory preclinical obesity phenotype, followed by a stepped intervention approach that begins with lifestyle modification and statins, incorporates dual‑benefit GLP-1 agonists, and looks forward to senolytics that target the root senescent cell source. The central unanswered question, the precise inflammation‑reduction threshold required for benefit, must be addressed in future trials. Importantly, senolytic therapies remain experimental and require stronger human clinical evidence before they can be recommended for primary prevention.

To realise the promise of this paradigm, we call for: first, international immuno cardiovascular cohorts integrating deep phenotyping of inflammation, metabolism, and senescence; second, randomised controlled trials of immunomodulatory interventions specifically in high inflammatory preclinical obesity, including so‑called primary immuno‑prevention trials targeting Stage A or B heart failure or subclinical atherosclerosis; and third, continued development of biomarkers and therapies that enable precision targeting of the bridge. By dismantling the immunometabolic bridge before organ damage occurs, we can substantially reduce the global burden of obesity‑related CVD by 2030.
