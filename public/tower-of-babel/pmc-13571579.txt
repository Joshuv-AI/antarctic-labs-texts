Evaluation of LLMs for Automated Dental-to-Medical Consult Letter Generation: Comparative Study
Authors: Dareen Eom, Kyeol Koh, Howon Chung, Kwangsoo Kim, Sang-Hwa Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsFor dental patients with systemic diseases, multidisciplinary care via referral letters is essential for clinical safety. This study evaluated the clinical feasibility and quality of dental-to-medical referral letters generated by Large Language Models (LLMs).MethodsUsing 153 paired datasets, three LLMs – Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5 and MedGemma – generated referral letters via a standardised prompting framework. Two dental clinicians performed a blinded assessment across nine sub-criteria covering content, style and correctness. GPT-5 also performed an automated evaluation. Statistical significance was analysed using Friedman and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction.ResultsIn the human clinical evaluation, Gemini-2.5-Pro achieved the highest overall performance (TOTAL mean SCORE: 2.9487 with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval [CI] of 2.9345-2.9606), significantly outperforming GPT-5 (2.8818 with 95% CI of 2.8613-2.9008) and MedGemma (2.8613 with 95% CI of 2.8338-2.8865) (P < .05). While GPT-5 showed high clarity in systemic problem descriptions, MedGemma excelled in structural adherence. The overall performance difference between GPT-5 and MedGemma was not statistically significant (P = .3519). Inter-rater reliability was strong in the prompt search dataset (QWK: 0.7175) but lower in the independent test dataset (QWK: 0.1505), reflecting the subjective nature of clinical document assessment; therefore, final human scores were reported as average ratings from two blinded clinicians with bootstrap confidence intervals. Notably, GPT-5’s self-evaluation results deviated from human judgment, exhibiting potential model-based bias.ConclusionThis preliminary study suggests the potential of Gemini-2.5-Pro for automated consult letter generation, though the low test-set inter-rater reliability (QWK: 0.1505) highlights the subjective complexity of clinical text. To integrate these assistive prototypes into routine clinical application, more systematic studies are required.Clinical relevanceIntegrating LLMs offers a promising approach to assisting with dental-to-medical referrals, presenting a potential future benefit of reducing administrative workloads and enabling healthcare providers to focus on safe, empathetic patient care.Key words: Artificial intelligence, Large language models, Dental records, Referral and consultation

Introduction

Medically compromised patients are frequently encountered in dental clinics. For these individuals, invasive dental procedures – such as tooth extraction or implant placement – carry potential risks related to their systemic conditions and medication regimens. Consequently, ensuring patient safety has become a paramount concern in daily dental practice. To manage these risks, dentists must thoroughly evaluate oral health and systemic history, identifying the need for multidisciplinary care via referral letters.1

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have expanded possibilities for supporting the drafting of clinical documents, such as discharge summaries and clinical notes, allowing clinicians to focus more on patient interaction.2, 3, 4 While closed-source models like GPT and Gemini show superior performance, their adoption is often limited by privacy concerns, making open-source models like MedGemma viable alternatives.5, 6, 7 In dental medicine, the potential applications and limitations of LLMs have also been increasingly discussed.8 However, despite these advancements, existing research in dentistry has primarily focused on diagnostic reasoning, clinical question answering or image interpretation rather than the generation of referral letters. For instance, recent studies have demonstrated the diagnostic potential of LLMs in oral medicine, including oral lesion assessment and rank-based differential diagnosis compared with human experts.9,10 In addition, LLMs and multimodal LLMs have been evaluated for clinical anamnesis and diagnostic reasoning in oral and maxillofacial disorders, orthopantomography analysis and dental licensing examination tasks involving clinical image interpretation.11, 12, 13

Despite this progress in AI-driven diagnostics, a significant gap remains in the automated generation of complex dental-to-medical consult letters. Unlike standardised medical records, dental records are often heterogeneous, comprising a mixture of structured data and free-text from medical questionnaires. While prior studies have examined text analysis for diagnostic support, empirical evidence regarding the generation of referral letters – which requires clinical reasoning and contextual summarisation of both dental needs and systemic risks – is still limited.14, 15, 16 This study addresses this gap by providing, to our knowledge, one of the first comparative evaluation of LLMs specifically for dental-to-medical referral documentation.

The primary purpose of this research is to evaluate the clinical feasibility and quality of dental-to-medical consult letters generated by three distinct LLMs – GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro and MedGemma. By utilising a dataset of 153 real-world patient records, we aim to determine which model best maintains clinical accuracy and clarity while adhering to necessary administrative structures. Furthermore, we investigate the reliability of LLM-based self-evaluation compared to human clinical judgment to ensure the objectivity of AI-assisted workflows in dentistry.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki for studies involving human participants. Ethical clearance was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, College of Medicine, The Catholic University of Korea (approval no. PC24RISI0198/PC23RISI0121). The IRB approval was granted under the strict condition that the study exclusively utilise text-only data extracted from the dental health questionnaires and initial visit records, which were completely de-identified by an independent, non-affiliated individual after being retrieved by the Medical Records Department. The IRB also determined that the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Data collection and processing

Following institutional approval by both the IRB and the Data Review Committee, the Medical Records Department retrieved the records of individuals who attended the Department of Dentistry at Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital and completed dental health questionnaires between December 28, 2022, and July 20, 2023. To maintain data security and patient anonymity, a non-affiliated individual with no conflict of interest performed the de-identification process, which was later confirmed by the Medical Records Department. A total of 894 dental health questionnaires were obtained and stored in PDF format for subsequent data processing. Responses from the binary (Yes/No) questions and the free-text fields were extracted using customised Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and Optical Mark Recognition (OMR) algorithms. All extracted information underwent a complete manual verification process to ensure precision prior to analysis. To establish the final comparative dataset, these 894 questionnaires were cross-referenced and merged with the patients' initial clinical records using unique patient identifiers. Records were excluded if they lacked a corresponding EMR clinical chart or contained incomplete medical history. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed due to the retrospective nature of the study; instead, a non-probability consecutive sampling approach was employed to capture all eligible, fully matched patient record sets within the designated timeframe. This selection process aimed to prevent data omission and maximise statistical power for model comparison, ultimately yielding a final analytical sample of 153 paired datasets (n = 153).

Two dental clinicians annotated four types of labels for each patient questionnaire by reviewing the 'Yes / No' checkboxes and the accompanying free-text responses: Need for invasive treatment (Yes / No), Need for collaborative consultation (Yes / No), Consulting department (Not applicable / Cardiology / Neurology / Endocrinology / Pulmonology / Oncology or Haematology / Others) and Precautions (Not applicable / Haemorrhage / Infection / Others). The first two labels were treated as binary classification tasks, while the latter two were multi-label classification tasks. Each 'Yes / No' and free-text item was annotated separately; afterward, for binary labels, any occurrence of 'Yes' among the items was consolidated as 'Yes' for the entire questionnaire and for multi-label categories, all labels were merged to produce a single aggregated label per questionnaire. During annotation, cases that were deemed 'possible' were also explicitly marked and included for later use.

For the initial visit dental record, the free-text sections were extracted from the PDF files and merged with the questionnaire data using a shared patient identifier. As illustrated in Supplemental Figure 1, a collaborative consultation referral letter is typically completed for patients who require invasive treatment, need interdepartmental consultation and require systemic disease management. Therefore, a data exclusion process was applied to retain only such relevant cases. Specifically, based on the annotated labels in the questionnaire data, records labelled as 'possible' or 'No' for Need for medical consultation were first excluded, keeping only those labelled as 'Yes'. Next, records where both Need for medical consultation and Need for invasive treatment were labelled as 'No' were removed. Subsequently, the free-text content of the remaining initial visit charts was manually reviewed to further exclude cases where collaboration was determined to be unnecessary.

After this exclusion process, a total of 153 pairs of questionnaires and charts were included in the final dataset. The final dataset was randomly split into three subsets: 6 pairs for few-shot examples, 30 pairs for prompt tuning and 117 pairs for the test set. For the few-shot example subset, two clinicians independently reviewed each case and manually drafted appropriate referral letters in advance, which were later used as exemplar references during model prompting. The data used in this study, including questionnaires and clinical charts, were primarily written in Korean, with some medical terms documented in English, resulting in a mixed Korean–English format.

Experiments

This study compared the performance of LLMs in generating referral letters based on information extracted from patient questionnaires and initial charts. Figure 1 presents an overview of the experimental workflow, illustrating how the contents of the questionnaire and the initial chart data were incorporated into the prompt, which LLMs were used to generate the referral letters, and how their outputs were evaluated.

Experiment overview.

In the Questionnaire dataset, two clinicians annotated four labels, which were used as patient information. To generate referral letters, the prompt included example referral letters, patient information and the initial clinical record, instructing the model to produce a new referral letter. This prompt was provided to various LLMs to generate referral letters, which were then evaluated and compared through both human evaluation and GPT-5 evaluation.

In the questionnaire dataset, two clinicians annotated four label types, which were used as patient information. To generate referral letters, the prompt included example referral letters, patient information and the initial chart, instructing the model to produce a new referral letter. This prompt was provided to various LLMs to generate referral letters, which were then evaluated and compared using both human evaluation and GPT-5-based evaluation.

To generate the dental-to-medical referral letters, a structured prompting framework was developed and evaluated using the questionnaire and initial clinical record datasets. In the prompt construction phase, four clinical label types annotated by two dental clinicians (need for invasive treatment, need for medical consultation, consulting department and precautions) were extracted from the Questionnaire dataset to serve as standardised patient information. This patient information was combined with the raw text from the initial clinical records and explicit instructions directing the models to synthesise a professional referral letter. To enhance model comprehension through in-context learning, several sample triads – comprising patient information, initial clinical records and corresponding ideal referral letters – were selected from a dedicated training subset and embedded as few-shot examples.

To identify the optimal prompt configuration, four distinct prompt variants were developed by systematically altering the instructional phrasing and the composition of few-shot examples. These variants were validated using a dedicated prompt search dataset across three large language models (LLMs): two closed-source models (GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Pro17) and one open-source model (MedGemma18). Prompt performance was quantified via blinded human clinical evaluation. The final prompt framework was selected based on its high consensus, model performance and inter-rater reliability during the tuning phase (quadratic weighted kappa = 0.7175; Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.7178; P < .01). The final prompt is provided in Supplemental Table 1, and the prompt format, representative examples and evaluation procedure are detailed in Supplemental Table 2.

The finalised prompt framework (Supplemental Table 1) was subsequently deployed to generate referral letters for the independent test dataset (n = 153) using the same triad of LLMs. To rigorously benchmark model performance, the generated outputs underwent both blinded human expert evaluation by two independent dental clinicians and automated evaluation via GPT-5. Inter-rater reliability for the final test evaluations was systematically calculated using Cohen's kappa and percent agreement to ensure evaluation validity. Statistical differences in scores across models were analysed using Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction, with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated to evaluate the precision and reproducibility of the model performance estimates.

Evaluation

To assess the clinical quality of the generated referral letters, standardised evaluation criteria were established by adapting validated frameworks from medical document evaluation.19, 20, 21, 22 As shown in Table 1, Clarity and Accuracy were independently evaluated across three critical text components: Treatment Plan, Systemic Problem and Reason for Referral. Additionally, global stylistic and structural attributes – including Frame, Conciseness and Correctness – were assessed for the entire text. Each criterion was rated on a 3-point scale (1-3), and the final score (TOTAL mean SCORE) was computed as the average of all category scores.

The Evaluation Item section specifies the criteria used for assessment. The Text Component section consists of three essential parts that should be included in a referral letter, and each part is evaluated separately according to the corresponding evaluation items.

These evaluation criteria were uniformly applied across both the initial prompt exploration phase and the final independent test dataset (n = 153). While the prompt search phase was conducted unblinded, the evaluation of the final test dataset employed a strict double-blind protocol: all model identifiers and prompt variants were completely masked, and samples were randomised to eliminate evaluation bias. Two independent dental clinicians performed the evaluations. To establish the scientific validity of the human assessment and address potential variance, inter-rater reliability was rigorously quantified using the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) and Spearman correlation coefficients for each sub-criterion and the TOTAL mean SCORE. Discrepancies between the two raters were resolved through a predefined consensus protocol, and the final human evaluation scores were computed as the average of both raters' assessments.

Various studies have explored the use of LLMs as evaluators (LLM-as-a-judge) to assess generated texts. For convenience and consistency, our study also incorporated an LLM-based evaluation process for the generated referral letters. The evaluation prompt was constructed based on the criteria described in Table 1, which is presented in Supplemental Table 3. The model generated evaluation outputs containing textual scores, from which we extracted numerical ratings to compute evaluation results. As the evaluation model, we employed GPT-5, a representative model from the GPT series that has been widely used for automatic evaluation in previous studies.

After completing both the human evaluation and the GPT-5-based evaluation, we analysed the distribution of the TOTAL mean SCORE. To determine whether the average and distribution of scores between any two of the three models differed significantly, we conducted Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction for all model pairs.

Experimental settings

Both GPT-5 and Gemini-2.5-Pro models were accessed via their respective APIs for inference. For GPT-5, we used the latest version, gpt-5-2025-08-07, which was consistently employed for both the referral letter generation and evaluation tasks. This model did not allow manual adjustment of parameters such as temperature or top_p, and thus it was used with its default configuration. For Gemini-2.5-Pro, we used the gemini-2.5-Pro model (latest update: June 2025; knowledge cutoff: January 2025). The model was configured with temperature = 0 and top_p = 1 to ensure greedy decoding.

The MedGemma model (google/medgemma-27b-it) was downloaded from Hugging Face and loaded in bfloat16 precision. Sampling was disabled by setting do_sample = False to enable greedy decoding. Loading MedGemma and inference were performed using two NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48 GB) GPUs.

During referral letter generation, the maximum new output length was fixed at 5000 tokens for all three models. For evaluation using GPT-5, this parameter was not explicitly specified, and the default value was applied.

Discussion

Favourable performance of Gemini-2.5-Pro in dental to medical consult letter generation despite rater variability

For the prompt-search dataset, inter-rater reliability was assessed using QWK and Spearman correlation. The results indicated strong agreement and a statistically significant correlation between evaluators’ scores, as summarised in Table 4. This relatively high consistency is likely attributable to the small number of few-shot examples (n = 6) and the prior calibration discussion between the two clinicians when defining evaluation criteria. When the final evaluation was conducted under a blinded setting, inter-rater agreement decreased, showing only slight agreement in QWK and a very weak but statistically significant positive correlation in Spearman’s coefficient, as shown in Table 5. This reduction in agreement appears to stem from differences in evaluators’ emphasis on key content elements and their subjective judgment regarding penalty criteria across the larger set of 117 test samples. Although the test dataset exhibited a noticeably lower inter-rater agreement compared to the prompt search dataset – reflecting the increased complexity and subjective variability inherent in evaluation under a fully blinded protocol – the positive Spearman correlation remained statistically significant (P < .01). To ensure the reproducibility and precision of these clinical ratings despite the lower rater consensus, 95% CIs were systematically calculated for all final pooled metrics.

Nevertheless, both evaluators independently – and also when their scores were averaged – consistently identified Gemini-2.5-Pro as the best-performing model. This favourable performance pattern was statistically significant across evaluation metrics, as demonstrated in Table 2 and Figure 2. Even in the presence of limited inter-rater agreement, this suggests that Gemini-2.5-Pro showed the most favourable performance under the present evaluation framework, rather than a finding dependent solely on one evaluator’s scoring tendency.

The observed discrepancy in inter-rater reliability within the test dataset highlights an important nuance in human expert evaluation. The two human evaluators did not process the provided medical information symmetrically, exhibiting inter-rater variability in the clinical weight and trust they assigned to clinician-authored initial medical records relative to patient-self-reported questionnaires. Because individual experts may vary slightly in how they prioritise these distinct data sources, subjective human scores naturally diverge. Conversely, AI models ingest and process all textual components flatly without such inherent hierarchical weighting. This fundamental difference in data interpretation between human experts and AI models, alongside variations in individual expert focus, likely contributed to the lower inter-rater reliability.

Additional analyses of evaluator-specific scoring patterns further support this interpretation. As shown in Supplemental Tables 6 and 7, human evaluator 2 tended to assign higher scores than human evaluator 1 in the Clarity domain, whereas human evaluator 2 assigned relatively lower scores in the Accuracy domain. These findings suggest that the two evaluators may have differed in the aspects of referral letter quality they emphasised during scoring. However, the magnitude of disagreement was not substantial. The highest proportion of score differences exceeding one point was observed for the 'reason for referral' item in the Clarity domain, accounting for only 6.60% of cases. Therefore, although the evaluators showed different scoring tendencies, large discrepancies between their ratings were uncommon.

The robustness of Gemini-2.5-Pro’s more favourable performance pattern was also supported by the criterion-level results. As shown in Supplemental Table 8, the best-performing model for each criterion was consistent across human evaluator 1, human evaluator 2, and the overall averaged scores. In addition, the bootstrap 95% CIs showed clear separation, with the lower bound for Gemini-2.5-Pro exceeding the upper bounds for the other models. Together with the statistical comparisons shown in Figure 2, these findings indicate that Gemini-2.5-Pro showed consistently favourable performance under the present evaluation framework, rather than a pattern driven by a single evaluator or a specific scoring tendency.

The use of a three-point rating scale was intended to balance clinical interpretability and rater consistency. Although a more granular scale might capture finer differences between model outputs, it could also increase subjective variability and further reduce inter-rater agreement, particularly given the limited number of expert evaluators available in this study. Because multiple evaluation criteria were assessed, the total mean score was expected to retain sufficient variability for comparing model performance. Furthermore, as the final test evaluation was conducted under a blinded protocol, we considered it appropriate not to perform additional rater calibration or consensus scoring during the final evaluation phase. Instead, the final results were reported using the averaged scores of the two evaluators, allowing the overall assessment to incorporate both evaluators’ perspectives, including their different emphases on clarity and accuracy. In addition, a formal minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the adapted 3-point medical document evaluation scale has not been firmly established in the literature. Although the observed score differences were numerically small, the consistent direction of the results and separated bootstrap confidence intervals suggest that Gemini-2.5-Pro produced more favourable outputs under the present evaluation framework. However, the clinical significance of these differences requires further validation using outcome-oriented safety metrics. Therefore, while further studies are warranted to validate the precise MCID for LLM-generated clinical texts, the statistically significant difference observed for the top-performing model suggests more favourable performance under the present evaluation framework, particularly in reducing the risk of critical omissions in multidisciplinary communication.

MedGemma as a practical alternative to GPT-5

When comparing the two models excluding Gemini-2.5-Pro, the averaged scores of the two evaluators indicated that GPT-5 achieved slightly higher overall ratings than MedGemma, as summarised in Table 2. However, results from both the paired t-test and KS test showed that neither the mean nor the score distribution differed significantly between the two models, as illustrated in Figure 2. This finding suggests that the performance of GPT-5 and MedGemma in referral letter generation is statistically comparable. Therefore, in situations where the use of the closed-source GPT-5 model is restricted due to privacy or data security concerns, MedGemma can serve as a practical and reliable alternative.

As shown in the human evaluation results in Table 2, MedGemma tended to receive slightly lower ratings than GPT-5 in the Clarity category, whereas its performance in Accuracy, Frame and particularly Conciseness was comparable to or even better than that of GPT-5. These results suggest that the choice between the two models may depend on which evaluation aspects are prioritised in clinical referral letter generation. Moreover, with further refinement of prompt design, the open-source MedGemma model may even outperform GPT-5 in generating more appropriate and contextually relevant referral letters.

Additional subgroup analyses in Supplemental Table 9 suggested that the relative performance of MedGemma and GPT-5 differed by referral department. This finding indicates that, when no specific deployment constraints exist, model selection could be tailored to the target referral department. However, hallucinations occurred more frequently in both MedGemma and GPT-5 than in Gemini-2.5-Pro. To provide a more concrete illustration of these clinical risks beyond average numerical scores, a representative patient case and its corresponding model-generated referral letters from Supplementary Table 10 were systematically analysed. This case analysis highlights three primary error types: clinical overextensions, critical omissions and factual hallucinations. In this comparative case, while Gemini-2.5-Pro aligned closely with human expert standards, distinct patterns of clinically critical errors were observed in the alternative models. Specifically, GPT-5 overextended the scope of the referral by including cardiology and hematology consultations, without explicitly specifying the invasive treatment. MedGemma also included a request for evaluation of prophylactic antibiotic use in the context of diabetes mellitus, as well as a fabricated clinical plan for root canal treatment that was completely absent from the original chart and not clearly supported by the source information.

These qualitative discrepancies highlight that LLM errors in dental-to-medical communication are rarely random; instead, they manifest as unsupported clinical overextensions or critical omissions of the planned dental procedure, thereby underscoring the potential patient-safety implications that mandate rigorous human-in-the-loop validation.

Importance of human evaluation to mitigate model-based bias

Many previous studies have employed GPT models for automatic evaluation; however, as reported in several works,23,24 GPT tends to exhibit a model-based bias, favouring outputs generated by its own model family. In this study, as shown in Table 2, Table 3, GPT-5 rated its own outputs more favourably than those of other models, which led to discrepancies in model rankings between human and GPT-5 evaluators. These findings suggest that evaluations conducted solely by GPT may have limitations in reliability and objectivity.

Furthermore, in alignment with the FDI World Dental Federation’s Communique on AI in Dentistry, addressing dataset constraints, managing algorithmic bias and maintaining definitive human responsibility and oversight remain crucial ethical prerequisites for clinical AI deployment. Incorporating structured expert criteria in this study directly serves to mitigate these algorithmic biases, upholding the governance principles of transparency and intelligibility advocated by global dental frameworks.25

To enhance evaluation objectivity, we established detailed evaluation criteria for each assessment item and conducted evaluations based on them. Moving forward, expert review and feedback will become increasingly important in designing and applying such evaluation guidelines. Because evaluation criteria and prompt design can substantially affect evaluation outcomes, careful prompt and protocol design is essential when using GPT-based evaluators. Therefore, GPT-based evaluation should be interpreted as a supplementary assessment rather than a replacement for human evaluation. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence from dental education showing that ChatGPT-assisted learning may provide supplementary benefits but does not necessarily outperform conventional active-learning approaches. In a prospective study comparing self-directed learning, manual tracing and ChatGPT-assisted learning for identifying anatomical landmarks on panoramic radiographs, manual tracing produced the most consistent improvement, whereas ChatGPT was beneficial only for selected landmarks and required structured guidance, prompt formulation and critical evaluation of AI-generated content.26

Furthermore, our current study design did not employ advanced mitigation strategies for LLM-based evaluation, such as multi-model ensembles or randomised output ordering, which could have further minimised the observed self-preference bias. Future research should adopt more robust, multi-LLM evaluation frameworks to enhance the objectivity and reliability of automated performance metrics.

The implementation of LLMs in clinical dental practice necessitates a robust ethical and regulatory framework. While LLMs can streamline administrative workflows, the ultimate legal responsibility for the generated content remains with the human practitioner, which underscores the necessity of a ‘human-in-the-loop’ approach. These considerations are further supported by recent evidence showing that dentists’ awareness of AI-related ethical issues, data security, anonymisation and legal regulations varies during the transition toward AI-driven clinical practice.27 Consequently, ensuring a foundational understanding of these regulatory frameworks and maintaining clinical oversight are prerequisites for the safe and ethical implementation of AI in multidisciplinary dental care.

A major limitation of the present study is the absence of clinician-written referral letters as a control group. Therefore, although our findings support a comparative evaluation of referral letters generated by different LLMs, they do not establish whether the best-performing LLM-generated letters reached the quality of referral letters written by clinicians. Future studies should include clinician-written referral letters as a reference standard or control group to more directly assess the clinical adequacy of LLM-generated referral letters relative to current human practice.

Conclusions

This study compared the performance of three LLM models to generate the referral letters utilising patient-reported data and clinical records. Our results, substantiated by 95% CI boundaries, showed that Gemini-2.5-Pro achieved the highest performance, while GPT-5 and MedGemma provided viable alternatives. However, given the inherent limitations of this study – including the low inter-rater agreement in the test dataset, the single-centre design, the limited number of evaluators, the absence of clinician-written referral letters as a control group, and the lack of prospective, real-world validation – the current findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Highlighting the paramount importance of human judgment in assessing clinical text quality, these models should be considered as clinician-supervised assistive tools rather than autonomous solutions.

Consequently, further prospective, multicentre studies incorporating rigorous human-in-the-loop protocols, as well as real-world workflow evaluations such as editing-time measurements or time-motion analyses, are imperative prior to routine implementation as clinical assistants. If validated in such settings, LLM-based assistant tools may have the potential to support dental-to-medical referral workflows in the future.
