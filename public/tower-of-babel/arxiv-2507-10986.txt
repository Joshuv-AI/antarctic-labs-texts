Abstract
Stellar flare forecasting, a critical research frontier in astronomy, offers profound insights into stellar activity. However, the field is constrained by both the sparsity of recorded flare events and the absence of domain-specific large-scale predictive models. To address these challenges, this study introduces StellarF (Stellar Flare Forecasting), a novel large model that leverages Low-Rank (LoRA) and Adapter techniques to parameter-efficient learning for stellar flare forecasting. At its core, StellarF integrates an flare statistical information module with a historical flare record module, enabling multi-scale pattern recognition from observational data. Extensive experiments on our self-constructed datasets (derived from Kepler and TESS light curves) demonstrate that StellarF achieves state-of-the-art performance compared to existing methods. The proposed prediction paradigm establishes a novel methodological framework for advancing astrophysical research and cross-disciplinary applications.

Introduction

Stellar flares, marked by the swift and powerful release of magnetic energy within a star’s atmospheric layer, represent a captivating astronomical phenomenon, as shown in Figure
1
. The high-energy radiation and particle streams emitted during these events not only profoundly impact the star’s evolutionary trajectory but also have far-reaching consequences for the atmospheric conditions, magnetic structures, and even the habitability of surrounding exoplanets
[
1
,
2
]
. In the broader context of astrophysics, understanding stellar flares is crucial for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of stellar magnetic activity, deciphering the intricate interactions within star-planet systems, and identifying potential habitable zones around exoplanets
[
3
,
4
]
. As the field of exoplanet detection and characterization experiences an unprecedented surge in interest, the ability to accurately forecast stellar flares has become a vital research domain, with profound implications for both fundamental science and the exploration of extraterrestrial life. Paradoxically, despite its significance, the study of stellar flare prediction remains underdeveloped, with a dearth of comprehensive research efforts in this domain.

Some observed images of stellar flares.

The pursuit of accurate stellar flare prediction is fraught with significant challenges. Firstly, observational limitations pose a substantial hurdle. Current observational instruments and time constraints result in sparse and incomplete light curve data, which are often marred by data gaps and high levels of noise
[
5
]
, as shown in Figure
2
. These issues severely impede the ability to fully capture the dynamic characteristics of stellar flares, making it difficult to discern patterns and predict future events. Secondly, the physical processes governing stellar flares are inherently complex. The wide-ranging diversity in stellar properties and evolutionary stages gives rise to highly variable and unpredictable flare activity patterns
[
6
]
. Traditional analytical methods, designed for more structured data, struggle to extract meaningful features from these complex and erratic datasets, leading to suboptimal prediction accuracy and reliability. Moreover, the absence of a standardized dataset dedicated to stellar flare prediction exacerbates the problem. Without a common benchmark, it becomes challenging to compare and validate results across different studies, effectively stalling progress in this nascent field.

To address these challenges, we introduce StellarF, a framework for stellar flare forecasting. First, we construct a large-scale task-specific dataset comprising about 3 million samples tailored to the target task. Second, extensive empirical experiments reveal that historical flare records and statistical information are closely linked to stellar flare prediction. To this end, we design two modules: the Historical Flare Information Module, which captures the temporal dynamics and periodic patterns of historical flare events through time-series feature collation, and the Flare Statistical Information Module, which characterizes macro-level static features of flare activity via unified encoding of physical attributes and numerical statistics. Lastly, we employ a large multi-modal model fine-tuned via LoRA
[
7
]
and Adapter
[
8
]
to derive features from the outputs of these modules, which substantially boosts prediction performance. The key contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

•

Model Advancement. StellarF fine-tunes multimodal large language models via LoRA and Adapter techniques, while integrating the “Flare Historical Record Module" with the “Flare Statistical Information Module". which enables StellarF to achieve superior performance compared to other models.

•

Dataset Innovation. We constructed the first large-scale labeled dataset for stellar flare prediction using Kepler and TESS light curve data, with a total sample size of nearly 3 million. It establishes a foundational data benchmark for future advancements in stellar flare prediction, facilitating the development and validation of new models and algorithms.

•

Performance Benchmarking. Extensive experimental evaluations demonstrate that StellarF outperforms state-of-the-art models, setting a new standard for stellar flare prediction.

2
Literature Review

2.1
Traditional Methods

In the early stages of stellar flare research, traditional approaches predominantly centered around physical models, statistical models, and empirical rule-based models. These methodologies laid a crucial foundation for subsequent studies, yet when confronted with the intricate system of stellar flare prediction, their inherent limitations became evident.

Statistical Models.
Time series analysis and regression analysis are commonly employed to explore the temporal dependencies of stellar luminosity and other physical quantities. For instance, the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model
[
9
]
excels in capturing linear trends (e.g., solar sunspot number analysis), while the Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH)
[
10
]
model analyzes solar flares intensity fluctuations by modeling dynamic noise. However, these models struggle to handle nonlinear dynamics in complex flare activities.

Physical Models.
Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)-based models aim to explain stellar flare mechanisms via physical principles. A key example is the MHD instability model
[
11
]
, which simulated magnetic reconnection to predict major flares in Solar Cycle 24. While these models provide mechanistic insights, their high computational cost and strict parameter requirements limit application to diverse stellar types.

Empirical Rule-based Models.
These models use historical stellar activity data and machine learning (e.g., decision trees, k-nearest neighbors (K-NN) algorithms) for prediction. Liu et al.
[
12
]
trained a random forest model using vector magnetic data from Solar Dynamics Observatory/Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (SDO/HMI) vector magnetic data, achieving moderate prediction accuracy for solar flares. However, limited by parameter accuracy and generalization, they struggle with flexible predictions in real-world scenarios.

Despite the contributions of traditional methods to the advancement of stellar flare prediction, they generally fail to effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships and heavily rely on substantial prior knowledge. With the exponential growth of data volume and the continuous enhancement of computational capabilities, data-driven approaches have emerged as a new research trend. For example, FLARE
[
13
]
integrates stellar properties and flare histories to improve light curve feature extraction and prediction accuracy, paving the way for new research directions.

2.2
Time Series Analysis Methods

Time series analysis methods play a pivotal role in numerous fields, especially in scenarios sensitive to time-dependent data scenarios, such as meteorological prediction and financial analysis. With the vigorous development of deep learning and big data technologies, this approach has achieved significant breakthroughs in handling complex patterns and long-term dependency relationships. Currently, mainstream time series analysis methods can be categorized into six types: MLPs, CNNs, RNNs, GNNs, Transformers, and PLMs.

Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs).
Dlinear
[
14
]
designed a simple linear model that surpasses complex Transformer models in Long-Term Time Series Forecasting (LTSF) tasks. FITS
[
15
]
innovatively processes time series via interpolation operations in the complex frequency domain, attaining state-of-the-art performance with a lightweight architecture of only
\sim
10k parameters.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
CNNs effectively capture local temporal patterns through convolution operations, particularly when handling nonlinear and complex data. SCINet
[
16
]
employs a recursive downsampling-convolution-interaction architecture, using multiple convolutional filters to derive valuable temporal features from downsampled subseries, significantly improving prediction accuracy.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).
RNNs efficiently capture temporal dependencies, but suffer from the vanishing gradient problem. LSTM addresses this by introducing memory units, yet struggles with more complex temporal dependency patterns. DeepAR
[
17
]
proposes a method to generate accurate probabilistic predictions, training on large sets of related time series to estimate future probability distributions and reducing manual intervention effectively.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).
GNNs
[
18
,
19
]
model time series variables as graph nodes and capture spatial dependencies between variables via graph convolutions. GCN
[
20
]
exhibits a linear scaling relationship with the number of graph edges, while learning hidden layer representations that encode local graph structures and node features, thereby markedly improving model performance.

Transformer Models (Transformers).
Based on self-attention mechanisms, Transformers excel in capturing long-term dependencies and improving training efficiency, becoming a critical research direction in time series prediction. PatchTST
[
21
]
enhances long-sequence modeling efficiency via patch partitioning and local-global attention mechanisms. iTransformer
[
22
]
addresses the insufficient variable interaction modeling in traditional Transformers for time series prediction by embedding each variable’s time series as independent tokens and using attention to capture multivariate correlations.

Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs).
With the development of pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT
[
23
]
, GPT series), increasing research applies them to time series analysis. Chronos
[
24
]
converts time series data into token sequences, leveraging language model architectures, pre-training on large-scale heterogeneous datasets, and data augmentation techniques to enhance generalization, demonstrating excellent zero-shot prediction performance.

Notwithstanding Transformer models’ remarkable achievements in general time series prediction, explorations in the astronomical domain—particularly for stellar flare prediction—remain relatively scarce. This paper integrates Transformer models with stellar flare prediction, building on Flare, we further enrich data processing and propose a novel StellarF framework that fuses a “Flare Record Module" with a “Flare Statistical Feature Module", exploring a multi-dimensional feature interaction mechanism suitable for stellar flare prediction. This provides new technical pathways and solutions for stellar flare prediction research.

Methodology

In this section, we elaborate on the proposed StellarF model, whose overall architecture is illustrated in Figure

. By curating the original dataset, we achieve a more balanced sample distribution. Meanwhile, we innovatively integrate flare statistical features and historical flare event records into the feature embedding process of light curves, constructing a multi-dimensional feature interaction mechanism that can simultaneously capture the temporal evolution patterns and statistical laws of stellar flares. Finally, we employ LoRA and Adapter techniques to fine-tune the large language model, enabling efficient extraction of deep semantic features from multi-modal data.

3.1
Problem Definition

Stellar flare prediction is a scientific problem that involves making a prediction of whether a flare will occur within a specfic future time window based on known observed light curve sequences. As the core observational data source, a stellar light curve systematically records the variation of a star’s brightness over time. For a single star, the light curve can be represented as a time series
L^{i}=\{l_{t}^{i}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, where
l_{t}^{i}
denotes the stellar photometric value measured for the i-th star at the time step
t
, and
T
is the total number of observation time steps. Meanwhile, based on the known observed light curve sequence, historical flare information can be extracted, which can be represented as a set of time points
H^{i}=\{h_{t}^{i}\}_{t=1}^{n^{i}}
, where
h_{t}^{i}
is the moment of the t-th flare eruption of the i-th star (marked by relative time within the observation period), and
n^{i}
is the total number of flares in the historical records. Flare statistical information characterizes the overall features of stellar flare activity from a macro perspective, expressed as a tuple
S^{i}=\{sid^{i},q^{i},n^{i},m^{i}\}
, where
sid^{i}
is the unique identifier of the i-th star,
q^{i}
represents the observation quarter, and
m^{i}
is the median flare flux. In summary, the input features for a single star can be integrated as
I^{i}=(L^{i},H^{i},S^{i})
, where
L^{i}
is the light curve time series,
H^{i}
is the historical flare information, and
S^{i}
is the flare statistical information. The core objective of stellar flare prediction is to construct a model
f(I^{i};\theta)
that, given input
I^{i}
and model parameters
\theta
, can predict the probability
p
of a flare occurring within the future time interval
[T+1,T+\Delta T]
(where
\Delta T
is the prediction range). The mathematical expression is as follows:

\mathrm{p}^{\mathrm{i}}=\mathrm{f}(\mathrm{I}^{\mathrm{i}};\theta)

3.2
Light Curve Embedding

During the collection of flare light curves, missing values often occur, which can have a non-negligible negative impact on model prediction. To tackle this issue, we adopt linear interpolation for filling in the missing values. The data comparison before and after interpolation is shown in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Linear Interpolation Effect on Light Curve Data. Original data (upper) with missing values vs. interpolated data (lower) preserving temporal trends.

Additionally, stellar flares are few-shot events, leading to extremely imbalanced distributions of positive and negative samples in the experiments. To address this issue, we perform patch partitioning on each light curve and tune three hyperparameters: patch_len (patch length), stride (step size), and pred_len (prediction length). By analyzing hyperparameter-sample ratio correlations (Figure
5
, pred_len most impatcful; Figure
6
for cross-check), we observed that larger pred_len values correlate with reduced imbalance. Considering the practical constraints of astronomical data analysis, we determined the optimal hyperparameters as follows: patch_len = 512, stride = 48, and pred_len = 480. After partitioning, the light curve can be expressed as
\hat{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, and
N=\left\lfloor\frac{K-P}{S}\right\rfloor
is the number of patches with length
P
, where
K
represents the total length of an original light curve and
S
is the stride.

3.3
Prompt Design

3.3.1
Motivation.

In the field of time-series prediction, numerous studies (such as Prompcast
[
25
]
, FLARE, LSTprompt
[
26
]
, etc.) have significantly improved prediction performance by integrating text prompts. Inspired by this, we further pondered, specifically, how to deeply excavate and utilize the associated information of stellar light curves to assist in enhancing flare prediction performance? Therefore, based on the research ideas of FLARE, we innovatively designed a flare statistical information module. By fusing multi-dimensional stellar attribute information, this module strengthens the model’s ability to capture flare occurrence patterns, achieving an improvement in prediction accuracy.

3.3.2
Historical Flare Information Module.

The historical flare information module focuses on the time-series characteristics of stellar flares. As shown in Figure
7
(a), this module systematically collates the distribution of time points of historical flare events, providing the model with critical “historical behavior" reference. These time-series features not only reflect the periodic patterns of flare activity but also reveal the potential dynamic changes in stellar magnetic activity.

3.3.3
Flare Statistical Information Module.

The flare statistical information module, conversely, characterizes the overall features of stellar flare activity from a macro perspective, as illustrated in Figure
7
(b). In the flare statistical information, any attribute value is expressed as an exact value. The unified encoding of semantic information of physical attributes and numerical features greatly assists the model in understanding the physical implications behind statistical laws.

Figure 7:
(a) Historical Flares Eruption Records of Example Stars. (b) Statistical Summaries (count, median flux) of Stellar Flares for Example Stars.

All text description vocabulary is embedded via a text encoder. Through the collaboration of dual modules, the model can simultaneously capture the temporal dynamics of stellar flares and statistical static features, thus constructing an input space with higher information completeness and laying a solid foundation for subsequent flare prediction tasks.

3.4
Pre-trained Large Language Model

Many studies have shown that training pre-trained language models (PLMs) from scratch often impairs model performance. However, by freezing most parameters and solely training a small subset of parameters, the powerful representation learning capabilities of PLMs can be effectively preserved. To improve parameter efficiency and avoid overfitting, we freeze most of the model parameters and only fine-tune the learnable
Q
,
K
and
V
layers. Additionally, we incorporate lightweight adaptation modules into the architecture: LoRA matrices are introduced in the multi-head attention layers to better capture cross-modal interactions, while Adapter modules are inserted after each Transformer feedforward network (FFN) layer to adaptively refine task-specific features with minimal computational overhead. Thus, the model can effectively learn from historical flare records
\tilde{H}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times P}
and flare statistical information
\tilde{S}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times P}
, where
T
is the number of tokens in the segmented text description. Subsequently, we use Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction of features, mapping them to the same dimensional space as the light curve embedding
\hat{H}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
and
\hat{S}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, to ensure compatibility of different modal data. Stellar statistical information serves as an important complement to light curves, offering marco-level insights into the statistical characteristics of flare activity. Meanwhile, flare historical records provide rich contextual information from a time-series perspective. Based on this, we enhance the light curve embedding by incorporating stellar statistical features, and further concatenate the historical flare records as separate input dimensions. Through this multi-modal feature fusion strategy, we finally construct an embedding representation
\hat{I}^{i}=(\hat{L}^{i},\hat{H}^{i},\hat{S}^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P\times 2}
, which effectively integrates spatial and temporal information.

4.1.3
Evaluation Metrics.

To evaluate the model’s generalization capability in the context of the original sample distribution, we employed a weighted evaluation metric framework, comprising Accuracy (Acc), Weighted Recall (W-Rec), Weighted F1-score (W-F1), Weighted Precision (W-Pre), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), and Area Under the PR Curve (AUPRC). All weighted metrics are computed by assigning class-specific weights proportional to the sample ratios, which better represents the model’s practical performance in real-world application scenarios. For the downstream task of stellar flare prediction, Accuracy and the Weighted F1-score are designated as the primary evaluation metric, complemented by the AUC curve for auxiliary assessment.

4.2
Performance Comparison

We benchmark StellarF against diverse baselines, performing at least three independent trials to derive average performance metrics, as detailed in Table
1
. Notably, StellarF exhibits exceptional performance in weighted metrics upon integrating Flare Historical Records (FHRs) and Flare Statistical Information (FSIn), outperforming all comparative methods by a significant margin. Our analysis yields three critical insights:

1.

When using raw data (light curves), it can be observed that models such as MICN and PatchTST also achieve promising results. This may stem from their architectures’ deep adaptation to time-series data. MICN extracts local features of light curves at different time scales in parallel through its multi-branch convolutional structure and multi-scale convolution kernels, avoiding information loss in the traditional feature extraction process. Furthermore, by further dividing long-sequence data into patches, PatchTST can deeply capture local statistical features and cross-scale long-range dependencies, thereby achieving excellent generalization and predictive capabilities.

2.

Our model, StellarF, achieves optimal performance. In contrast, we observe that models like moment and gpt4ts perform poorly, which may be due to their inherent architectural limitations in capturing complex patterns in stellar flare data. The moment model relies excessively on modeling statistical moment features. Although it can characterize the overall distribution, it fails to retain the fine dynamic information of flare eruptions, resulting in insufficient ability to distinguish flare signals from background noise. The fine-grained encoding mechanism of gpt4ts is susceptible to high-frequency noise interference. In contrast, StellarF, by integrating LoRA and Adapter, boosts the model’s ability to derive features from flare data through parameter-efficient lightweight parameter tuning, while suppressing noise interference, thus achieving optimal performance.

3.

Integrating historical flare records and statistical information improves the effectiveness of all baselines, yielding only marginal metric discrepancies. This finding underscores the critical role of FHRs and FSIn in stellar flare prediction.

4.3
Ablation Study

4.3.1
Effectiveness of Each Module.

To evaluate the effectiveness of each module in StellarF, we conduct an ablation study. The findings are displayed in Table
2
, with each module being removed one by one across the following scenarios: the original model configuration (StellarF), removal of the Flare Historical Records module (denoted as “StellarF w/o FHRs"), removal of the Flare Statistical Information module (denoted as “StellarF w/o FSIn"). “StellarF w/o Adapter" referring to excluding Adapter during fine-tuning, and the model fine-tuned without LoRA (“StellarF w/o LoRA").

Experimental results demonstrate that removing any of the four key modules in StellarF leads to performance decline in at least one evaluation metric for the model variants, while the full model maintains optimal performance across all metrics. Specifically, the absence of LoRA significantly impacts overall model performance, reflecting the core role of this module in parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Meanwhile, removing FHRs and FSIn significantly degrades key metrics, validating the effectiveness of the proposed stellar contextual information integration strategy in stellar forecasting. Notably, removing the Adapter induces a noticeable performance drop relative to the full model, yet it remains the best among ablated variants. Its retention, however, yields meaningful improvement in metrics such as Accuracy and Weighted F1, therby confirming the efficacy of this module.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present StellarF, an intelligent model for stellar flare prediction that deeply integrates flare statistical features with dynamic patterns of historical eruption sequences. Extensive experiments conducted on our self-constructed StellarDataKepler and StellarDataTESS datasets demonstrate that StellarF outperforms state-of-the-art models in prediction accuracy and exhibits remarkable generalization capability across diverse observational scenarios.

In future research work, we will further enhance the multimodal fusion capability of the model by integrating spectral data and astrophysical parameters, and strive to construct a hybrid intelligent model with physical constraints to improve prediction accuracy. In addition, we plan to deploy StellarF to real-time astronomical observation systems. This initiative will not only promote the in-depth integration of artificial intelligence technology and astrophysical research, but also provide strong support for breakthroughs in the field of dynamic analysis of stellar activities.
