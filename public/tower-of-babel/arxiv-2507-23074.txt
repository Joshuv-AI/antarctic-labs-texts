Harnessing Bayesian Statistics to Accelerate Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation
Qilin Li *, Atharva Vidwans *, Yazhen Wang, Micheline B. Soley †

Abstract
We establish a unified statistical framework that underscores the crucial role statistical inference plays in Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE), a task essential to fields ranging from chemistry to finance and machine learning. We use this framework to harness Bayesian statistics for improved measurement efficiency with rigorous interval estimates at all iterations of Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation. We demonstrate the resulting method, Bayesian Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation (BIQAE), accurately and efficiently estimates both quantum amplitudes and molecular ground-state energies to high accuracy, and show in analytic and numerical sample complexity analyses that BIQAE outperforms all other QAE approaches considered. Both rigorous mathematical proofs and numerical simulations conclusively indicate Bayesian statistics is the source of this advantage, a finding that invites further inquiry into the power of statistics to expedite the search for quantum utility.

Statistical Framework for QAE

2.1
Preliminaries

We define the QAE problem in terms of the action of a quantum oracle
\mathcal{A}
on an (
n+1
)-qubit zero state
\ket{0}_{n}\ket{0}
. The resulting output state may be expressed in terms of two normalized states
\ket{\psi_{0}}_{n}
and
\ket{\psi_{1}}_{n}
, which need not be orthogonal, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\ket{0}_{n}\ket{0}=\sqrt{1-a}\ket{\psi_{0}}_{n}\ket{0}+\sqrt{a}\ket{\psi_{1}}_{n}\ket{1},

where the oracle
\mathcal{A}
may be chosen freely without loss of generality with the states
\ket{\psi_{0}}_{n}
and
\ket{\psi_{1}}_{n}
defined accordingly. The objective of QAE is then to estimate the amplitude
a\in[0,1]
of the quantum state
\ket{\psi_{1}}_{n}\ket{1}
.

As a benchmark, Classical QAE (or the “standard-sampling” approach) approximates the quantum amplitude
a
as the sample mean of
n
measurement outcomes of the quantum circuit associated with oracle
\mathcal{A}
, where the ancilla states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
are mapped to the outcomes
0
and
1
, respectively. Where the
quantum sample complexity
is defined by the total number of accesses to the oracle, the quantum sample complexity required to achieve a target accuracy of
\varepsilon
with Classical QAE follows as on the order of
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})

[
1
]
. Restatement of the sample complexity in terms of asymptotic mean-square error (AMSE), as derived
in Appendix
A.1
, then yields a sample complexity required to achieve a mean squared error (MSE)
\varepsilon^{2}
of

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}a(1-a).

(1)

The relatively high measurement cost of Classical QAE is addressed via amplified estimation (or the “enhanced-sampling” approach), which yields quadratic improvement in the sample complexity
[
1
]
. Let the amplitude
a
be parametrized as

\displaystyle a=\sin^{2}{\theta},

where
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
, and let the Grover operator
\mathcal{Q}
be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}_{\psi_{0}},

where
\mathcal{S}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{n+1}-2\ket{0}_{n+1}\bra{0}_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\psi_{0}}\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{n+1}-2\ket{\psi_{0}}_{n}\bra{\psi_{0}}_{n}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}
. Repeated application of the Grover operator
\mathcal{Q}

k
times then results in the amplified state
[
1
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{k}\mathcal{A}\ket{0}_{n}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\cos{\left((2k+1)\theta\right)}\ket{\psi_{0}}_{n}\ket{0}

\displaystyle+\sin{\left((2k+1)\theta\right)}\ket{\psi_{1}}_{n}\ket{1}.

(2)

such that the
amplified target probability
is

\displaystyle p_{k}\coloneqq\sin^{2}{\left((2k+1)\theta\right)}.

(3)

Given that the probability
p_{k}
is amplified relative to the original probability
a
, where enhanced sampling estimates
p_{k}
with the same accuracy as
a
and subsequently rescales based on inference of
\theta
,
p_{k}
results in a higher-accuracy estimate of
a
than direct measurement of
a
itself (see Appendix
A.2
Example
1
).

Abstraction of the quantum amplitude
a
in amplified estimation from the amplified state therefore follows an analogous procedure to abstraction of the quantum amplitude
a
from the original state in Classical QAE – with an added identifiability challenge. For a fixed number of Grover operators
k
, the circuit
\mathcal{Q}^{k}\mathcal{A}
applied to the
(n+1)
-qubit zero state is measured
N_{k}
times. Let
X_{i,k}
represent the outcome of the
i
-th application. The sample mean is calculated as
\overline{X}_{k}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}X_{i,k}
. This mean approximates
p_{k}
, which for known
k
yields
\theta
, which is in turn used to calculate
a
according to the pipeline schematic in Fig.
1
.

For each value of
p_{k}
there exist
2k+1
valid solutions of Eq. (
3
)

\displaystyle(2k+1)\theta=\begin{cases}\arcsin{(\sqrt{p_{k}})}+l\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}&\text{if $l$ is even},\\
\arccos{(\sqrt{p_{k}})}+l\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}&\text{if $l$ is odd},\end{cases}

(4)

where

(k>0)

amplified angle

that corresponds to the true
l
value for a given

. This
quadrant index

may be readily interpreted geometrically as the quadrant index of the amplified angle

\displaystyle l(k)=\left\lfloor\frac{(2k+1)\theta}{(\pi/2)}\right\rfloor

(5)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor

2.2
Amplified Amplitude Estimation

The recently developed Amplified Amplitude Estimation (AAE) method
[
91
]
resolves the identifiability problem in amplified estimation via the small angle assumption and can be readily formulated in the statistical framework of AMSE (thus providing a conceptual stepping stone to IQAE and BIQAE), as follows:

To begin, we specify that the AAE approach
considers cases in which the amplitude
a
is sufficiently small that there exists only one solution to the identifiability challenge Eq. (
4
).

Assumption 1
.

For a given
k
,
a
is sufficiently small that
(2k+1)\theta
does not exceed
\frac{\pi}{2}
.

With Assumption
1
, the quadrant index
l(k)
in Eq. (
4
) must be zero, such that there exists only one valid solution.

According to the proof outlined in Appendix
C.1
, the quantum sample complexity of the approach can then be stated in the statistical framework of AMSE according to Theorem
1
:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds with a certain
k
. Then,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}\coloneqq\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{1}{2k+1}\arcsin{\sqrt{\bar{X}_{k}}}\right)}

(6)

is the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) for
a
. The corresponding asymptotic MSE is

\displaystyle\text{AMSE}_{\hat{a}_{0}}(a)=\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{2}}\frac{1}{N_{k}}a(1-a),

and to achieve a target accuracy
\varepsilon
, the quantum sample complexity is

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle}}=(2k+1)N_{k}=\frac{1}{2k+1}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}a(1-a).

(7)

Comparison of the quantum sample complexity of AAE Eq. (
7
) to that of Classical QAE Eq. (
1
) then confirms AAE decreases the quantum sample complexity by a factor
\frac{1}{2k+1}
, thereby demonstrating its advantage over Classical QAE and emphasizing that the greater the number of applied
\mathcal{Q}
operators, the greater the efficiency of the amplified estimator, provided Assumption
1
holds. Furthermore, in the case where
a=O(\varepsilon)
, the quantum sample complexity becomes
N_{\text{oracle}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)
, a conclusion that can be directly derived from Theorem
1
where the order of
k
is chosen to be
\Theta\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)
. The complexity
N_{\text{oracle}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)
exceeds Heisenberg-limited scaling in this context but does not refute the Heisenberg limit, as it relies on prior knowledge.

2.3
IQAE

We proceed by formulating IQAE in the same statistical framework of AMSE with the additional introduction of the normal approximation, which transparently demonstrates IQAE’s estimator for
a
achieves a quadratic advantage, up to a
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
factor, without the need for the small-angle assumption. Note the key insight exploited by IQAE here is that one can obtain the same AMSE and quantum sample complexity as AAE, provided that the quadrant index is known (see Appendix
A.4
for a formal statement), thereby replacing the need for the small-angle assumption.

To begin the statistical derivation of IQAE, we consider that, since
\theta\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
implies the initial quadrant index must be zero (
l(0)=0
) per the definition of the quadrant index Eq. (
5
), we may initiate the AAE process with zero Grover operators.

Progressively increasing the number
k
of Grover’s operators while carefully tracking the evolution of the quadrant index then provides the means to increase the efficiency of the estimator while preserving identifiability, as follows: Formally, let
0=k_{0}<k_{1}<k_{2}<\dots<k_{t}<\dots<k_{T-1}
be the sequence of
T

k
-values processed in pursuit of the desired accuracy. Beginning with
t=0
, for each
t
, the amplitude is amplified with
k_{t}
Grover operators and
N_{t}
measurements are performed on the corresponding quantum circuit, which constitutes a
k_{t}
-dependent amplification and measurement process we term
stage

t
. With the definition
K_{t}\coloneqq 2k_{t}+1
, which represents the number of accesses to the oracle
\mathcal{A}
in a single execution of the circuit with
k_{t}
applications of the Grover operator, we organize these stages into a sequence
\{(K_{t},N_{t}):t=0,1,\ldots,T-1\}
that we term the
schedule
that is designed to ensure the feasibility of tracking the quadrant index at all stages.

To develop a schedule that is both efficient and facilitates statistical analysis, we consider the distinct advantages of schedules previously employed in Maximum-Likelihood QAE (MLQAE)
[
7
]
and Iterative QAE (IQAE)
[
92
]
. MLQAE puts forward both linear and exponential
K
-schedules with preset values of
N_{t}
for all
t
. Prior numerical results indicate such an exponential schedule with fixed
N_{t}
values achieves a quadratic advantage at the expense of the lack of a theoretical guarantee of its feasibility. IQAE, in contrast, adaptively selects both
K_{t}
and
N_{t}
such that the quadrant index of each stage
l_{t}
is estimated based on measurements carried out in the preceding stage, which continuously refines a confidence interval at confidence level
\alpha
for
a
and guarantees that its radius eventually becomes smaller than a prescribed accuracy threshold
\varepsilon
. A rigorous theoretical analysis indicates IQAE achieves optimal complexity up to a multiplicative
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
factor; however, derivation of this complexity upper bound is relatively intricate due to IQAE’s fully adaptive algorithmic structure, which requires accounting for many possible algorithmic behaviors.

Based on these points, we develop two hybrid approaches that combine the analytical advantages of MLQAE’s exponential
K
-schedule with IQAE’s beneficial confidence intervals.

2.3.1
Base-3 Hybrid-Scheduled IQAE

We first introduce a hybrid
(K,N)
-schedule for IQAE to combine the benefits of exponential
K
-schedules and confidence intervals: a base-3 exponential
K
-schedule

\displaystyle K_{t}=3^{t},\quad t=0,1,\ldots,T-1

with an adaptive
N
-schedule in which the algorithm incrementally performs batches of
N_{\rm incre}
measurements (termed
iterations
) until the confidence interval for
\theta
is narrow enough that
l(k_{t+1})
is determined with a desired degree of high confidence
\alpha_{t}
. We set
\alpha_{t}=\alpha/T
such that, by the union bound, the choice of
\alpha_{t}
ensures that the final confidence interval for
a
achieves the overall confidence level
\alpha
. As derived in Appendix
C.2
, to obtain the desired confidence interval for
a
with a radius no more than
\varepsilon
, the proposed schedule requires the number of stages to be just

\displaystyle T=\left\lceil\log_{3}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right)\right\rceil.

(8)

To aid analysis, the resulting hybrid schedule admits both ternary-expansion and geometric interpretations: Combined with the formula for the quadrant index Eq. (
5
), imposition of the hybrid
(K,N)
-schedule ensures
l(k_{t})
represents the
t
-th digit in the ternary expansion of
\theta/(\pi/2)
. At stage
t
, the task of identifying the quadrant index is then equivalent to determining the
(t+1)
-th digit in this ternary expansion given its first
t
digits. Alternatively, from a geometric perspective, the task of identifying the quadrant index is equivalent to determining the
reference interval
to which the amplified angle
3^{t}\theta
belongs, where the
reference interval
at stage
t
is defined as one of the three equal-angle divisions of the quadrant containing
3^{t}\theta

\displaystyle l_{t}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}+j\cdot\frac{\pi}{6}+\left[0,\frac{\pi}{6}\right],\quad j=0,1,2,

where
l_{t}
is used as an abbreviation of
l(k_{t})
, the quadrant index at stage
t
.

To ascertain the quantum sample complexity, we consider the simplest case in which the confidence interval for the amplified angle is relatively broad at each stage relative to the reference intervals:

Assumption 2
.

Let
\varepsilon_{t}
be the radius of the confidence interval for
\theta
obtained at stage
t
. Assume that

\varepsilon_{t}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{1}{3^{t+1}}\frac{\pi}{2}

for some constant
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and for all
t=0,1,\ldots,T-2
.

The hybrid schedule and normal approximation then leads to the following upper bound for the quantum sample complexity (see detailed proof in Appendix
C.3
):

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumption
2
, the accumulated quantum sample complexity of the proposed base-3 hybrid schedule to obtain a confidence interval for
a
of radius
\varepsilon
with an overall confidence level of
\alpha

(\leq 0.1)
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{\pi}\left(\frac{3}{\gamma^{2}}+2\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\left\lceil\log_{3}\left(\frac{\pi}{4\varepsilon}\right)\right\rceil\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sqrt{a(1-a)}.

The proposed base-3 upper bound thereby achieves the same order quantum sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log{\log{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
as that of standard-scheduled IQAE
[
92
]
, and thus achieves the optimal order up to a double logarithmic factor. Note the use of the normal approximation in this derivation provides the conceptual and mathematical basis for the creation of Normal-BIQAE, introduced in Sec.
3.3.1
.

To observe the same
\varepsilon
-dependence of the upper bound of the sample complexity with hybrid-scheduled IQAE in extreme cases in which Assumption

In base 5, the reference intervals are

\displaystyle l_{t}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}+j\cdot\frac{\pi}{10}+\left[0,\frac{\pi}{10}\right],\quad j=0,1,\ldots,4,

3
BIQAE Method

3.1
BIQAE Framework

To harness and refine not only the interval estimate but also the
posterior distribution
of the amplitude,
we incorporate Bayesian inference into IQAE as follows:

For each stage
t
, we consider a prior distribution over
p_{k_{t}}
that models understanding of
p_{k_{t}}
thus far as

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}\sim\pi_{t}^{\text{prior}}(p_{k_{t}}),

(11)

where
\pi_{t}^{\text{prior}}(\cdot)
denotes a prior distribution whose form remains to be specified. We hold this prior distribution fixed from the conclusion of stage
t-1
throughout the remainder stage
t
.

In each iteration within stage
t
, we increment
N_{t}
by
N_{\rm incre}
to enhance the accuracy of the estimate. Let
N_{t}
denote the total number of measurements gathered at stage
t
. We map the measurement outcomes to 0 and 1. Then, the distribution of the sample average
\bar{X}_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}|p_{k_{t}}\sim\frac{1}{N_{t}}{\rm Bin}\left(N_{t},p_{k_{t}}\right).

(12)

We refer to this distribution of the sample average
\bar{X}_{t}
given
p_{k_{t}}
as the likelihood model and denote the density function by
\mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\bar{X}_{t}\middle|p_{k_{t}}\right)
.

A Bayesian update then integrates the
N_{t}
measurements collected at stage
t
with the prior distribution to compute a posterior distribution that reflects an updated understanding about
p_{k_{t}}
. This computation is carried out using Bayes’ rule as

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{\text{post}}\left(p_{k_{t}}\middle|\bar{X}_{t}\right)\propto\pi_{t}^{\text{prior}}(p_{k_{t}})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\bar{X}_{t}\middle|p_{k_{t}}\right),

(13)

where
\pi_{t}^{\text{post}}\left(p_{k_{t}}\middle|\bar{X}_{t}\right)
denotes the posterior distribution.

An interval estimate for the angle
\theta
is then obtained by first computing an interval estimate for
p_{k_{t}}
and then translating this interval to
\theta
. The interval estimate for
p_{k_{t}}
is derived from the posterior distribution of
p_{k_{t}}
by taking its upper and lower
\alpha_{t}/2
-quantiles

\displaystyle\left[p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\left[\Pi_{t}^{\text{post}}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\right),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad\left[\Pi_{t}^{\text{post}}\right]^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\right)\right],

(14)

where
\Pi_{t}^{\text{post}}
represents the cumulative density function of the posterior distribution. This approach, known as
credible intervals
in Bayesian statistics, replaces the confidence intervals in IQAE, as the latter is incompatible with the posterior distribution.

To obtain the
\theta
location information required to determine the next
k
, the resulting credible interval for
p_{k_{t}}
is then transformed by

\displaystyle\left[\theta_{t}^{l},\theta_{t}^{u}\right]=f_{t}\left([p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}]\right),

(15)

where
f_{t}(\cdot)
denotes the deterministic functional relationship between
\theta
and
p_{k_{t}}

\displaystyle f_{t}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{K_{t}}\left(\arcsin{(\sqrt{x})}+l_{t}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)&\text{if $l_{t}$ is even},\\
\frac{1}{K_{t}}\left(\arccos{(\sqrt{x})}+l_{t}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)&\text{if $l_{t}$ is odd}.\end{cases}

To track the evolution of the bases such that
l_{t}
is known, let
\left[\theta_{t-1}^{l},\theta_{t-1}^{u}\right]
denote the interval estimate for
\theta
from the previous stage. Then,
l_{t}
is readily computed as

\displaystyle l_{t}=\left\lfloor\frac{K_{t}\theta_{t-1}^{l}}{\pi/2}\right\rfloor.

Based on the interval estimate obtained from Eq. (
15
), a linear search is performed to find the largest feasible
k
-value as in standard-scheduled IQAE. Denote the
k
-value output as
k_{\rm next}
. If
k_{\rm next}=k_{t}
, the
k
-value remains identical, and the algorithm continues with a new iteration. If instead
k_{\rm next}>k_{t}
, the the number of
\mathcal{Q}
operators in the associated quantum circuit is increased to
k_{t+1}\coloneqq k_{\rm next}
to improve measurement efficiency. A new stage
t+1
then begins, which requires preparation of a new prior distribution for
p_{k_{t+1}}
before resumption of the iteration process.

To prepare the new prior upon identification of a greater feasible
k
-value,
the posterior distribution for
p_{k_{t}}
is transformed into a prior distribution of the target probability in the upcoming stage as

\displaystyle p_{k_{t+1}}=\sin^{2}\left(K_{t+1}\cdot f_{t}(p_{k_{t}})\right),

(16)

namely

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}\sim\pi_{t}^{\rm post}\xrightarrow{\sin^{2}\left(K_{t+1}\cdot f_{t}(\cdot)\right)}p_{k_{t+1}}\sim\pi_{t+1}^{\rm prior}.

(17)

In practice, this transformation is implemented approximately based on the chosen form of prior distribution.

3.2
BIQAE Workflow

We execute BIQAE according to the workflow diagram provided in Fig.
2(b)
, which differs from the workflow of IQAE in (i) the addition of a
BayesianUpdate
module, which updates the posterior distribution based on previous measurements and the prior distribution according to Bayes’ rule, (ii) the insertion of a
PreparePrior
module, which prepares the prior based on the posterior distribution of the preceding stage, and (iii) the substitution of IQAE’s
ComputeCI
module, which computes frequentist confidence intervals, with BIQAE’s
ComputeCRI
module, which computes Bayesian credible intervals in BIQAE. Note that whereas IQAE feeds measurements into the
ComputeCI
module to compute the confidence interval, BIQAE inputs new measurements into the
BayesianUpdate
module to refine the posterior distribution of the amplitude
a
(thereby integrating prior knowledge with new measurements) and subsequently passes the posterior distribution on to
ComputeCRI
to compute the credible interval. This flow of information ensures the algorithm learns from both the previous interval estimate and the shape of the distribution within this interval to further increase the measurement efficiency of the quantum amplitude estimate.

3.3
Implementation of BIQAE

We introduce two approaches to implement the aforementioned Bayesian framework: (i) BIQAE with a normal prior (Normal-BIQAE) for theoretical insights into quantification of the improvement brought about by injection of Bayesian inference into IQAE and (ii) BIQAE with a beta prior (Beta-BIQAE) for superior practical performance.

3.3.1
Normal-BIQAE

Prior.

To model the probability distributions in BIQAE according to a normal prior distribution, we adopt as the prior Eq. (
11
) a distribution of the form

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0,t},\Delta_{t}^{2})

(18)

for normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
, prior mean
\mu_{0,t}
, and prior variance
\Delta_{t}^{2}
at stage
t
.

BayesianUpdate.

To leverage the associated conjugate normal prior, we approximate the likelihood model in Eq. (
12
) in
BayesianUpdate
by the normal distribution

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}|p_{k_{t}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(p_{k_{t}},\frac{1}{N_{t}}p_{k_{t}}(1-p_{k_{t}})\right).

(19)

Combining Eqs. (
13
), (
18
), and (
19
), the posterior distribution follows as

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}|\bar{X}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\text{post},t},\sigma^{2}_{\text{post},t}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{post},t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\mu_{0,t}}{\Delta_{t}^{2}}+\frac{\bar{X}_{t}}{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}/N_{t}}}{\frac{1}{\Delta_{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}/N_{t}}},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\text{post},t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}/N_{t}}\right)^{-1},

(20)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\bar{X}_{t}(1-\bar{X}_{t})
is an asymptotic unbiased estimator for the variance.

ComputeCRI.

Since the resulting posterior distribution is normal, the credible interval for
p_{k_{t}}
Eq. (
3.1
) in the
ComputeCRI
module becomes

\displaystyle\left[p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}\right]=\mathrm{trunc}_{[0,1]}(\mu_{\text{post},t}\pm z_{\alpha_{t}/2}\cdot\sigma_{\text{post},t}),

(21)

where
z_{\alpha_{t}/2}
is the
(1-\alpha_{t}/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

PreparePrior.

An approximation is then required to prepare a normal prior from the normal posterior distribution Eq. (
20
) since, although the posterior distribution is normal and the posterior-to-prior transformation Eq. (
17
) is smooth, the exact prior distribution after transformation is no longer a normal distribution. Assuming
N_{t}
is sufficiently large that the normality in the central limit theorem applies, we thus approximate the prior distribution for
p_{k_{t+1}}
in the
PreparePrior
module by the normal distribution according to the delta method (see Appendix
A.6
), which yields

\displaystyle p_{k_{t+1}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{0,t+1},\Delta_{t+1}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{0,t+1}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\left(K_{t+1}\cdot f_{t}(\mu_{\text{post},t})\right),

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{K_{t+1}}{K_{t}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mu_{0,t+1}(1-\mu_{0,t+1})}{\mu_{\text{post},t}(1-\mu_{\text{post},t})}\cdot\sigma^{2}_{\text{post},t}.

Quantum Sample Complexity.

Where
N_{t}
is sufficiently large that the normality in central limit theorem applies, we derive for the resulting Normal-BIQAE implementation an upper bound on the quantum sample complexity at stage
t
required to obtain an interval estimate for
\theta
with radius
\varepsilon_{t}
of

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle},t}\leq\frac{z^{2}_{\alpha_{t}/2}}{4K_{t}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t-1}^{2}}\right),

(22)

where
\varepsilon_{t-1}
is the radius of the interval estimate for
\theta
obtained at stage
t-1
(see Appendix
A.7
). Compared to the upper bound of Normal-IQAE (see Appendix
C.3
)

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle},t}=\frac{z^{2}_{\alpha_{t}/2}}{4K_{t}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t}^{2}},

(23)

Normal-BIQAE therefore reduces the complexity by a factor of
\varepsilon_{t}^{2}/\varepsilon_{t-1}^{2}
.

3.3.2
Beta-BIQAE

Prior.

Given that Normal-BIQAE relies on the normal approximation, which deteriorates when
N_{t}
is small, and given that the beta distribution excels for small sample sizes, we introduce a beta implementation of BIQAE to handle a wider range of sample sizes. In Beta-BIQAE, we assume a prior Eq. (
11
) in the form of a beta distribution

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}\sim{\mathrm{Beta}}(a_{0,t},b_{0,t}),

(24)

where
a_{0,t},b_{0,t}
are the shape parameters.

BayesianUpdate.

A beta-distributed prior allows us to directly use the binomial distribution Eq. (
12
) to derive a beta-distributed posterior, which avoids the need for normal approximation. Using Eqs. (
12
), (
13
), and (
24
); the posterior distribution is

\displaystyle p_{k_{t}}|\bar{X}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim{\mathrm{Beta}}(a_{\text{post},t},b_{\text{post},t}),

\displaystyle a_{\text{post},t}

\displaystyle=a_{0,t}+N_{t}\bar{X}_{t},

\displaystyle b_{\text{post},t}

\displaystyle=b_{0,t}+N_{t}\left(1-\bar{X}_{t}\right).

(25)

ComputeCRI.

The interval estimate Eq. (
3.1
) in the
ComputeCRI
module then follows as

\displaystyle\left[p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},a_{\text{post},t},b_{\text{post},t}\right),\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},a_{\text{post},t},b_{\text{post},t}\right)\right]

to accommodate the beta-distributed posterior, where
\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}(\cdot,a,b)
is the inverse of the cumulative density function of the beta distribution with shape parameters
a
and
b
.

PreparePrior.

Analogously to Normal-BIQAE, given that the exact prior distribution of
p_{k_{t+1}}
after the transformation Eq. (
17
) is no longer a beta distribution, an appropriate beta distribution is required to approximate the true distribution. However, unlike Normal-BIQAE, Beta-BIQAE does not admit use of the delta method to enable the derivation of a closed-form approximation. We therefore employ a sampling procedure in
PreparePrior
to obtain an asymptotically optimal beta approximation of the exact prior distribution, where optimality is defined as minimization of the distance (Kullback–Leibler divergence) between the chosen approximation and the exact prior (see Appendix
A.8
for a rigorous establishment of this optimality result).

Specifically, given the posterior distribution derived in Eq. (
25
), we prepare the prior as follows:

1.

Draw a sufficiently large sample size
R
(for example,
R=1000
) of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) values
\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{R}\}
from the beta posterior in Eq. (
25
) to accurately approximate the underlying distribution.

2.

Map each value
y_{i}
to the parameter space for
p_{k_{t+1}}
through the transformation described in Eq. (
17
) to obtain

\tilde{y}_{i}\coloneqq\sin^{2}\left(K_{t+1}\cdot f_{t}(y_{i})\right)

where
\{\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{y}_{R}\}
is identical to a sample drawn from the exact prior distribution of
p_{k_{t+1}}
.

3.

Treat
\{\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{y}_{R}\}
as if they were drawn from a beta distribution and use the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) to infer its shape parameters.

4.

Use the maximum likelihood estimates as the shape parameters
(a_{0,t+1},b_{0,t+1})
of the beta distribution Eq. (
24
).

The chosen sampling procedure is performed only at transitions between stages, namely, a total of
T-1
times where
T=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
and requires less than a second of classical computing time per application, thereby circumventing the high classical cost associated with Monte Carlo techniques that are required where the form of the prior is not specified to allow conjugacy
[
54
]
.

3.3.3
BIQAE with a Noninformative Prior

We further recognize that, although not originally derived according to a Bayesian perspective, IQAE constitutes implementation of BIQAE with a noninformative prior, as derived in detail in Appendix
A.9
. Precisely, IQAE with Chernoff-Hoeffding confidence intervals (termed Normal-IQAE or IQAE-CH) corresponds to BIQAE with a noninformative normal prior and IQAE with Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals (termed Beta-IQAE or IQAE-CP) corresponds to BIQAE with a noninformative beta prior.

5
Discussion

The success of BIQAE for quantum amplitude estimation and molecular ground-state energy approximation shows conclusively the value of Bayesian inference as a way to reduce the cost of QAE and quantum computing algorithms generally.

Given the measurement cost reduction observed here for implementation of Bayesian inference in IQAE at the level of interval estimates, the next step for BIQAE would be to inject Bayesian inference at the level of hyperparameters or
(K,N)
-scheduling decisions. Bayesian optimization
[

103

104

105

N_{\text{incre}}

Likewise, the schedule employed here to facilitate direct comparison between BIQAE and IQAE literature results could be substituted with a Bayesian-informed schedule (such as that of BAE
[
54
]
) to make the most of limited measurement information.

In order to realize BIQAE’s gains, a key step would be to modify the algorithm for implementation on current and future quantum computers. The far-term method presented here could be readily modified for today’s near-term intermediate scale quantum computers to counter noisy gates and qubit decoherence. In particular, strategies may be imported from QAE methods recently successfully implemented on both superconducting and trapped-ion quantum computers

A.1
Quantum Sample Complexity of Classical QAE

As derived in Appendix
C.1
, the quantum sample complexity of Classical QAE is formally stated in a statistical framework as follows:

Theorem 3
.

Consider
n
instances of the quantum circuit associated with oracle
\mathcal{A}
. Let
X_{i}
denote the outcome of the
i^{\text{th}}
instance, where the state
|0\rangle
is mapped to the outcome
0
and
|1\rangle
is mapped to
1
. Then, the outcomes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli random variables with success probability
p=a
.

The sample mean
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
serves as the Uniformly Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator (UMVUE) for
a
. The mean squared error (MSE) of
\bar{X}
with respect to
a
is

\text{MSE}_{\bar{X}}(a)=\frac{1}{n}a(1-a).

To achieve a target accuracy
\varepsilon
, the required number of shots is

N_{\text{shots}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}a(1-a).

Since each shot requires one query to the oracle
\mathcal{A}
, the total number of accesses to the oracle is then

N_{\text{oracle}}=N_{\text{shots}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}a(1-a).

A.2
Mechanistic Foundations of Amplitude Estimation

To aid readers new to the field, we provide the following brief, simplified example of how amplitude amplification improves measurement efficiency:

Example 1
.

Suppose one seeks to estimate the head probability of a coin while in possession of (i) the ability to estimate the head probability of a biased coin with accuracy
0.02
and (ii) a technique that amplifies the head probability by a factor of
\times 4
. If the unknown head probability is
0.15
and one tosses the coin repeatedly, one will obtain an interval estimate of
[0.13,0.17]
without amplification or
[0.58,0.62]
with amplification.

At first glance, the interval lengths appear to be equal for both approaches. However, the interval in the amplified case must be rescaled by the inverse of the original factor (namely,
\times 1/4
) to reorient to the original head probability.
The interval after rescaling is
[0.145,0.155]
, which is four times narrower than the interval obtained without amplification. Thus, using the amplified approach improves accuracy in the sample case by a factor of four.

A.9
Equivalence of Interval Estimates

To demonstrate that IQAE can be derived as an implementation of BIQAE with a noninformative prior, we establish the similitude of the interval estimates in IQAE-CH and Normal-BIQAE and in IQAE-CP and Beta-BIQAE, as follows:

Consider Normal-BIQAE with a noninformative prior
\Delta_{t}^{2}\to\infty
. A Bayesian update according to Algorithm
1
yields a posterior distribution characterized by

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{post},t}\to\bar{X}_{t},\quad\sigma_{\text{post},t}\to\sqrt{\frac{\bar{X}_{t}(1-\bar{X}_{t})}{N_{t}}}

regardless of
\mu_{0,t}
. Approximation of the z-value as

\displaystyle z_{\alpha_{t}/2}\approx\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha_{t}}\right)}

then yields the credible interval

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{post},t}\pm z_{\alpha_{t}/2}\cdot\sigma_{\text{post},t}\approx\bar{X}_{t}\pm\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{X}_{t}(1-\bar{X}_{t})}{N_{t}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha_{t}}\right)},

(27)

which is directly comparable to the Chernoff-Hoeffding confidence interval for
p_{k_{t}}

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{2N_{t}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha_{t}}\right)},

(28)

where the truncation function
\mathrm{trunc}_{[0,1]}(\cdot)
is omitted for simplicity.
The credible interval of Normal-BIQAE Eq. (
27
) and the confidence interval of IQAE-CP Eq. (
28
) are thus structurally similar, possessing identical centers with a discrepancy only in first factor under the square root that enables the Chernoff-Hoeffding confidence interval to be slightly more conservative since
\frac{1}{4}
is always no less than
\bar{X}_{t}(1-\bar{X}_{t})
, such that construction of interval estimates with the two methods are highly similar; we thus do not distinguish linguistically between the Normal-IQAE method corresponding to the approach entailed by Eq. (
27
) and the IQAE-CH method corresponding to the approach entailed by Eq. (
28
) in this paper.

Likewise, the credible interval of Beta-BIQAE according to Algorithm
5

\left[p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}\right]=\left[\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},a_{\text{post},t},b_{\text{post},t}\right),\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},a_{\text{post},t},b_{\text{post},t}\right)\right]

(29)

is directly comparable to the Clopper-Pearson confidence interval used in IQAE-CP

\left[p_{k_{t}}^{l},p_{k_{t}}^{u}\right]=\left[\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},N_{t}\bar{X}_{t},1+N_{t}\left(1-\bar{X}_{t}\right)\right),\text{BetaCDF}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2},1+N_{t}\bar{X}_{t},N_{t}\left(1-\bar{X}_{t}\right)\right)\right]

(30)

for a noninformative prior
a_{0,t}=b_{0,t}=0.5
, in particular for relatively large sample sizes
N_{t}
(namely, for
Jefferys prior
with corresponding credible interval
Jefferys interval
). Given the absence of significant differences between practical applications of the two approaches, we do not distinguish linguistically between the IQAE-CP approach using Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals and the Beta-IQAE approach using Jeffreys intervals in this paper.

v
and standard deviation and where the
coverage rate
is defined as the proportion of repeated independent experiments in which the interval contains the true parameter value. As shown in Fig.
17
(left panel), for the critical value
v=1.96

\leq 87\%
. Likewise, as shown in Fig.
17
(right panel), for
v=10
, which corresponds to coverage of all but
1.523971\times 10^{-21}\%

v=10
case Beta-BIQAE yields the smallest intervals and frequently the highest coverage rates for all cases considered, which is indicative of an advantageous balance between a narrow interval radius and a high coverage rate (
i.e.
, accuracy and reliability). The observed performance difference between BIQAE and BAE is also consistent with BIQAE and BAE’s distinct methodological approaches. Namely, although BIQAE and BAE both employ Bayesian updates within an iterative QAE framework to extract information from measurement outcomes, (i) BIQAE adopts the

\displaystyle N_{\text{oracle},t}=\frac{z^{2}_{\alpha_{t}/2}}{4K_{t}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}+\left(\frac{\bar{X}_{t}\left(1-\bar{X}_{t}\right)}{\mu_{{\rm post},t}\left(1-\mu_{{\rm post},t}\right)}-1\right)\frac{z^{2}_{\alpha_{t}/2}}{4K_{t}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t}^{2}}-\frac{\bar{X}_{t}\left(1-\bar{X}_{t}\right)}{\Delta_{t}^{2}}K_{t}.

\displaystyle B\left(u\left(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}\right)+v\left(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=B\left(u\alpha_{1}+v\alpha_{2},u\beta_{1}+v\beta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}t^{u\alpha_{1}+v\alpha_{2}-1}(1-t)^{u\beta_{1}+v\beta_{2}-1}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}t^{u\left(\alpha_{1}-1\right)+v\left(\alpha_{2}-1\right)}(1-t)^{u\left(\beta_{1}-1\right)+v\left(\beta_{2}-1\right)}dt

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2},\quad\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}.
