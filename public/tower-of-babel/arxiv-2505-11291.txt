Theta classes: generalized topological recursion, integrability and 𝒲 \mathcal{W} -constraints
Vincent Bouchard, Nitin K. Chidambaram, Alessandro Giacchetto, Sergey Shadrin

Abstract
. We study the intersection theory of the Θ r , s \Theta^{r,s} -classes, where r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 and 1 ≤ s ≤ r − 1 1\leq s\leq r-1 , which are cohomological field theories obtained as the top degrees of Chiodo classes. We show that the recently introduced generalized topological recursion on the ( r , s ) (r,s) spectral curves computes the descendant integrals of the Θ r , s \Theta^{r,s} -classes. As a consequence, we deduce that the descendant potential of the Θ r , s \Theta^{r,s} -classes is a tau function of the r r -KdV hierarchy, generalizing the Brézin–Gross–Witten tau function (the special case r = 2 r=2 , s = 1 s=1 ). We also explicitly compute the 𝒲 \mathcal{W} -constraints satisfied by the descendant potential, obtained as differential representations of the 𝒲 ⁡ ( 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 r ) \mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r}) -algebra at self-dual level. This work extends previously known results on the Witten r r -spin class, the r r -spin Θ \Theta -classes (the case s = r − 1 s=r-1 ), and the Norbury Θ \Theta -classes (the special case r = 2 r=2 , s = 1 s=1 ).



1.
Introduction

Topological recursion
[
EO07
]
takes as input a spectral curve, which consists of a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, two meromorphic functions
x
and
y
on
\Sigma
, and a symmetric bi-differential
B
on
\Sigma^{2}
, subject to some particular conditions. Via an explicit recursive procedure, it outputs a collection of symmetric meromorphic differentials
\set{\omega_{g,n}}
called “correlators” and defined for all
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and
2g-2+n>0
. The standard assumption on
x
and
y
is that all zeros of
dx
are simple and
dy
does not vanish at these points
[
EO07
]
. However, this assumption can be relaxed: one can study spectral curves such that
dx
has higher order zeros. This is done in a variety of papers, first by Bouchard–Eynard
[
BE13
,
BHLMR14
]
, and then further developed in
[
BBCCN24
,
BKS24
,
BBCKS
]
. In this generalized context, the main building blocks of spectral curves are the
(r,s)
spectral curves
, which consist of

(1.1)

\Sigma=\mathbb{P}^{1},\qquad\qquad x=z^{r},\qquad\qquad y=z^{s-r},\qquad\qquad B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}},

with
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r+1]
with
r\equiv\pm 1\pmod{s}
. Those are building blocks for spectral curves since they control the local behaviour of general spectral curves near the zeros of
dx
.

One of the main areas of application of the theory of topological recursion is its connection to the intersection theory of the moduli spaces of curves
[
Eyn14
]
and, more specifically in some cases, to cohomological field theories
[
DOSS14
]
. In order to systematically develop this connection, one of the key questions is to identify the cohomology classes on the moduli space of curves whose intersection with
\psi
-classes (also known as descendant integrals) is controlled by the expansions of the correlators for the basic building blocks, the
(r,s)
spectral curves.

In the case when
s=r+1
, the correlators calculate descendant integrals for the
r
-spin Witten class, see
[
BE17
,
DNOPS19
,
CCGG24
]
. When
s=r-1
, the correlators calculate descendant integrals for the so-called
r
-spin
\Theta
-classes, which are defined as the top degrees of certain Chiodo classes
[
CGG
]
(in the special case
r=2,s=1
, it reproduces the Norbury
\Theta
-classes
[
Nor23
]
). However, for the other cases with
r\geq 3
,
s=2,\ldots,r-2
and
r\equiv\pm 1\pmod{s}
, the enumerative meaning of the correlators is an open question
[
BBCCN24
,
BKS24
,
CGG
]
.

There is an alternative approach to the definition of topological recursion that comes from the idea that the correlators should be compatible with the universal
x-y
swap formula
[
ABDKS24
,
ABDKS25
]
, as well as with a more general group of symplectic transformations
[
ABDKS24a
,
BDKS25
]
(see also
[
Hoc23
,
Hoc24
]
). This approach was further developed and related to the Bouchard–Eynard recursion in
[
ABDKS25a
]
, where it was dubbed
generalized topological recursion
. In this framework, the open question above is naturally replaced by a different one: what is the geometric meaning of the expansion of the correlators obtained by the generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curves? Note that this alternative question does not require any modularity constraint on
r
and
s
: generalized topological recursion applies to all
(r,s)
spectral curves with
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
.

Generalized topological recursion

The first goal of this paper is to answer this alternative question. We prove, in
theorem

2.8
and
corollary

2.10
, that the correlators produced by generalized topological recursion on all
(r,s)
spectral curves with
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
calculate descendant integrals of the so-called
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes. The
\Theta^{r,s}
-class (defined precisely in
definition

2.5
) is the top degree piece of the Chiodo class
C_{g,n}^{r,s}
, which in turn is defined via the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r,s-r}_{g,a}
of twisted spin curves with primary fields
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Theorem A

(Generalized topological recursion for
\Theta^{r,s}
)
.

The correlators
\omega_{g,n}
produced by generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve calculate the descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes. More precisely, for
g\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
such that
2g-2+n>0
, we have

(1.2)

\omega_{g,n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\left(-\frac{1}{r}\right)^{2g-2+n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\xi_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i}),

where
d\xi_{k,a}(z)=(rk+a)!^{(r)}\frac{dz}{z^{rk+a+1}}
.

This is a generalization of the
s=r-1
result of
[
CGG
]
, but our approach is quite different from
loc. cit.
In fact, it is interesting to note that for all
s\neq r-1
, the correlators produced by generalized topological recursion do not coincide with the correlators produced by the Bouchard–Eynard recursion. It thus remains a mystery what the geometric meaning of the latter is.

Integrability

Our second result concerns integrability of the descendant potential
Z^{r,s}
for the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes, see
equation

3.1
for the precise definition. A direct consequence of the generalized topological recursion framework of

]
is that the descendant potential is a KP tau function. In fact, we show in

is a tau function for the

-KdV hierarchy.

Theorem B

(Integrability)
.

The descendant potential
Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)
of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-class is a tau function of the
r
-KdV hierarchy corresponding to the unique
r
-KdV solution whose initial condition is given explicitly in
proposition

3.3

\Theta^{r,s}
-integrals.

This provides a generalized version of the Brézin–Gross–Witten tau function of the KdV hierarchy
[
BG80
,
GW80
]
, corresponding to the case
(r,s)=(2,1)
and whose geometric interpretation in terms of intersection numbers was conjectured by Norbury
[

]
and proved in
[

]
. The

case of the above theorem gives an enumerative interpretation to the tau functions studied in
[
YZ23
]
for certain choices of their constants

(see
proposition

5.12
). One can also view this statement as an analogue of the Witten
r
-spin conjecture (which corresponds to the case
s=r+1
)
[
FSZ10
]
.

Loop equations and
\mathcal{W}
-constraints

We then move on to the study of
\mathcal{W}
-constraints. In
[
BBCCN24
]
, it is shown that the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curves can be recast as a set of differential constraints satisfied by a partition function. These differential operators form a representation of the
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
-algebra at self-dual level. The precise correspondence goes through the formulation of abstract loop equations satisfied by the correlators, to which topological recursion is the unique solution.

As the correlators that generate descendant integrals for the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes satisfy the generalized topological recursion, but generally not the Bouchard–Eynard recursion, we cannot apply the results of
[
BBCCN24
]
directly to obtain
\mathcal{W}
-constraints for the descendant potential. Instead, in
section

4
we use the determinantal formulas that the generalized topological recursion correlators were proved to satisfy in
[
ABDKS25b
]
, and follow the strategy of
[
BEM18
]
to derive loop equations for them explicitly. These loop equations coincide with the usual ones when
s=r-1
, as expected from
[
CGG
]
. When
s=1
, we obtain a particular case of the “shifted loop equations” studied recently in
[
BBKN
]
. For other choices of
s
, we get a new system of loop equations.

We then recast those loop equations as differential constraints for the descendant potential. These differential operators from a representation (the so-called twist field representation) of the principal
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
-algebra at self-dual level. Thus, we obtain a set of
\mathcal{W}
-constraints for the descendant potential
Z^{r,s}
in
theorem

5.3
.

Theorem C

(
\mathcal{W}
-constraints)
.

Consider the modes
H^{i}_{k}
of
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
in the representation (
5.8
). Then, for any
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
, we have

(1.3)

H^{i}_{k}Z^{r,s}=\begin{cases}\hslash^{i}A_{i}\delta_{k,0}Z^{r,s}&i\in[r-s]\,,k\geq 0,\\
0&r-s+1\leq i\leq r\,,k\geq r-s-i+1,\end{cases}

where the constants
A_{i}
are defined in terms of the elementary symmetric polynomial
e_{i}
by

(1.4)

A_{i}\coloneqq e_{i}\left(\frac{2+s-r-1}{2(r-s)},\frac{4+s-r-1}{2(r-s)},\ldots,\frac{2(r-s)+s-r-1}{2(r-s)}\right),

and vanish unless
i
is even.

For
s=r-1
, the constraints match with the Airy structures studied in
[
BBCCN24
,
CGG
]
. For
s=1
, the constraints are a special case of the shifted Airy structures studied in
[
BBKN
]
and match the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints found in
[
YZ23
]
. In both of these cases, they uniquely fix the descendant potential. However, for other choices of
s
, we obtain new
\mathcal{W}
-constraints which do not uniquely fix the descendant potential. However, all is not lost; we define a reduced descendant potential, which plays the role of “initial conditions” for the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints. Once the reduced potential is given, the constraints uniquely fix the entire descendant potential.

Notation

For an integer
n\geq 1
, we use the notation
[n]=\set{1,2,\ldots,n}
, and the notation
z_{[n]}
for the set
\set{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}}
.

2.
Generalized topological recursion and determinantal formulas

In this section we give a geometric meaning for the correlators calculated by the
generalized topological recursion of
[
ABDKS25a
]
on the
(r,s)
spectral curve. We consider the nowhere semisimple cohomological field theory consisting of the classes
\set{\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}}_{g,n}
, which are defined as the top degrees of the Chiodo classes. We show that descendant integrals for the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes is calculated by the generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve. This yields an explicit determinantal formula for these intersection numbers.

2.1.
Chiodo classes, topological recursion and determinantal formulas

We first introduce the Chiodo classes and recall the main result of
[
Gia21
, Section 9.2.1]
and
[
ABDKS24
, Proposition 3.1]
, adjusted to our conventions and notation. Those results provide two different ways of computing intersection theory for the Chiodo classes, either through the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion or through a determinantal formula.

2.1.1.
Chiodo classes

Fix two integers
r,k
and an integral
n
-tuple
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying
r\geq 2
and the modular constraint
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\equiv k(2g-2+n)\pmod{r}
. Recall the definition of the moduli space
\smash{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,a}^{r,k}}
of twisted spin curves
[
Jar00
,
Chi08
]
: it parametrizes stable curves
(C,p_{1},\dots,p_{n},L)
of genus
g
with
n
marked points and a line bundle
L
on
C
satisfying
L^{\otimes r}\cong\omega_{\log}^{\otimes k}(-\sum_{i}a_{i}p_{i})
, where
\omega_{\log}\coloneqq\omega(\sum_{i}p_{i})
is the log-canonical bundle. It has a universal curve and a universal line bundle

(2.1)

\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,a}^{r,k},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C},

and it comes with the forgetful map
\epsilon\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,a}^{r,k}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
to the moduli space of curves which drops the choice of the line bundle. The Chern polynomial of the derived pushforward of the universal line bundle give a natural collection of classes on the moduli space of curves.

Definition 2.1
.

Define the
Chiodo class
as

(2.2)

C_{g,n}^{r,k+r}(a;\tau)\coloneqq\epsilon_{\ast}c(-\mathsf{R}^{\bullet}\pi_{\ast}\mathcal{L};\tau)\in R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})[\tau],

where
\mathsf{R}^{\bullet}\pi_{\ast}\mathcal{L}
is the derived pushforward of
\mathcal{L}
,

(2.3)

c(-\mathsf{E}^{\bullet};\tau)=\exp\left(\sum_{d\geq 1}(-\tau)^{d}(d-1)!\,\mathrm{ch}_{d}(\mathsf{E}^{\bullet})\right)

is the Chern polynomial of
-\mathsf{E}^{\bullet}
(where the minus sign should be interpreted as a minus sign in the Grothendieck ring, or alternatively a shift of the complex
\mathsf{E}^{\bullet}
in the derived category), and
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is the tautological ring of the moduli space of curves. For the total Chern class (that is, when
\tau=1
), we simply write
C_{g,n}^{r,k+r}(a)
.

The fact that the Chiodo class belongs to the tautological ring is a consequence of Chiodo’s formula
[
Chi08a
]
for the Chern characters of
\mathsf{R}^{\bullet}\pi_{\ast}\mathcal{L}
, which in turn generalizes Mumford’s and Bini’s computations
[
Mum83
,
Bin03
]
, for
r=2
and
k=-1
and for
r=2
and arbitrary
k
respectively, to arbitrary values of
r
. The analysis of the pushforward along the forgetful map
\epsilon
was carried out in
[
JPPZ17
]
. See
[
GLN23
]
for a list of known properties satisfied by the Chiodo class. Of importance for us is that the Chiodo classes form a semisimple cohomological field theory (in general, without flat unit).

From now on, we are going to fix
k=s-r
with
s\in[r-1]
, which motivates the shift above. For
s
in this range and
0\leq a_{i}\leq r-1
,
i=1,\dots,n
, a Riemann–Roch computation shows that
\mathsf{R}^{\bullet}\pi_{\ast}\mathcal{L}
is a vector bundle of rank

(2.4)

D^{r,s}_{g;a}\coloneqq g-1+\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+|a|}{r}.

Thus
C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a;\tau)=\sum_{d=0}^{D^{r,s}_{g;a}}[C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)]_{d}\,\tau^{d}
, where by
[\alpha]_{d}
we denote the
d
-th homogeneous component of a class
\alpha\in R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
.

2.1.2.
Chiodo classes and topological recursion

Since the Chiodo classes form a semisimple cohomological field theory, it follows from
[
Eyn14
,
DOSS14
]
that descendant integrals of the Chiodo classes are calculated by the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion. The Eynard–Orantin topological recursion
[
EO07
]
takes as input a spectral curve, which consists of a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, two meromorphic functions
x
and
y
on
\Sigma
, and a Bergman kernel
B
on
\Sigma^{2}
. It outputs a collection of symmetric
n
-differentials
W_{g,n}(z_{[n]})
on
\Sigma^{n}
(called
genus-
g
,
n
-point correlators
) for
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
2g-2+n>0
.

The relation between intersection theory of the Chiodo classes and topological recursion is stated in the following proposition, which was proved in
[
Gia21
, Section 9.2.1]
. It generalizes to arbitrary values of
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
the computations of
[
LPSZ17
]
, performed for
t=1
, which in turn generalize the computations of
[
SSZ15
]
performed in the case
t=1
and
s=r+1
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
SSZ15
,
LPSZ17
,
Gia21
]
)
.

Let
r\geq 2
,
s\in[r-1]
, and
0\neq t\in\mathbb{C}
. Consider the spectral curve
(\Sigma,x,y,B)
, where

(2.5)

\Sigma=\mathbb{P}^{1},\qquad x=z^{r}-t\log(z),\qquad y=z^{s-r}\qquad B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}.

For
2g-2+n>0
, the correlators
W_{g,n}(z_{[n]};t)
produced by the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion on this spectral curve take the form

(2.6)

W_{g,n}(z_{[n]};t)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(s-r)^{2g-2+n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\set{0,\ldots,r-1}\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)^{D_{g;a}^{r,s}}C_{g,n}^{r,s}\left(a;\tfrac{r}{t}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi^{k_{i}}d\theta_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i};t),

where the one-forms
d\theta_{k,a}
are given by

(2.7)

d\theta_{k,a}(z;t)\coloneqq r\,d\left(-\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{k+1}\frac{z^{r-a}}{r-a}

for
x=z^{r}-t\log(z)
.

Remark 2.3
.

The restrictions
s\in[r-1]
and
0\leq a_{i}\leq r-1
are not limiting. Indeed, shifting the parameter
s
by
r
results in a multiplication by
\kappa
-classes
[
Eyn14
]
, while shifting
a_{i}
by
r
results in a multiplication by
\psi
-classes (see
[
GLN23
, Theorem 4.1]
). Thus, knowing the descendant integrals for parameters
s
and
a_{i}
in the above ranges suffices to recover all descendant integrals for Chiodo classes for arbitrary values of the parameters (except when
s\equiv 0\pmod{r}
).

As usual, we define the unstable correlators to be

(2.8)

W_{0,1}\coloneqq y\,dx=z^{s-r}d(z^{r}-t\log(z)),\qquad\qquad W_{0,2}\coloneqq B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}.

It will then be useful to assemble the correlators into the generating series

(2.9)

W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0\end{subarray}}\hslash^{2g-2+n}W_{g,n}(z_{[n]};t),\qquad n\geq 1,

called the
n
-point correlator
. We get:

(2.10)

W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)-\delta_{n,1}z^{s-r}d(z^{r}-t\log z)-\delta_{n,2}\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}=\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\!\!\left(\frac{-\hslash}{s-r}\right)^{2g-2+n}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\set{0,\ldots,r-1}\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)^{D^{r,s}_{g;a}}\,C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a;\tfrac{r}{t})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\theta_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i};t).

2.1.3.
Chiodo classes, generalized topological recursion and determinantal formulas

It was found more recently in
[
ABDKS24
]
that the same differentials can be computed through a determinantal formula. The construction is a special case of the framework of generalized topological recursion developed in
[
ABDKS25a
]
.

Just like the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion, the generalized topological recursion takes as input a spectral curve. In this context, it consists of a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, two differentials
dx
and
dy
on
\Sigma
, a Bergman kernel
B
on
\Sigma^{2}
, and a set
\mathcal{P}\subset\Sigma
which is a subset of the so-called set of special points. The special points are those
o\in\Sigma
where the local behavior of
dx
and
dy
is given by

(2.11)

\displaystyle dx(z)=az^{p-1}\,dz\,\bigl(1+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(z)\bigr),\qquad dy(z)=bz^{q-1}\,dz\,\bigl(1+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(z)\bigr),\qquad a,b\neq 0,\ p,q\in\mathbb{Z},

where
z
is a local coordinate near
o
, with
p+q>0
and
p
and
q
not simultaneously equal to
1
.

For brevity, we bypass the definition of the generalized topological recursion here and simply state the resulting determinantal formula for the differentials in the case of interest. Let
r\geq 2
,
s\in[r-1]
, and
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Consider the spectral curve
(\Sigma,dx,dy,B)
, where

(2.12)

\Sigma=\mathbb{P}^{1},\;dx=d(z^{r}-t\log(z)),\;dy=d(z^{s-r}),\;B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}},\;\mathcal{P}=\Set{z=e^{\frac{2\pi a\mathrm{i}}{r}}\left(\tfrac{t}{r}\right)^{1/r}}_{a\in[r]}.

Here a choice of
r
-th root of
t/r
is fixed once and for all. The special points of the above spectral curve are at
z=e^{2\pi a\mathrm{i}/r}(t/r)^{1/r}
,
a\in[r]
. Thus, we chose
\mathcal{P}
to be the full set of special points. The generalized topological recursion then constructs differentials
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
through the following determinantal formula (see
[
ABDKS25a
, Theorem 3.6]
):

(2.13)

\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)\coloneqq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}\right)\sum_{\sigma\in C_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{dw^{+}_{i}dw^{-}_{\sigma(i)}}}{w^{+}_{i}-w^{-}_{\sigma(i)}},

where
\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}
and
w^{\pm}_{i}
are the operators and functions given by

(2.14)

\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}\coloneqq-\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}\left(-d_{i}\frac{1}{dx_{i}}\right)^{k}\,[u_{i}^{k}]\,e^{u_{i}(\mathcal{S}(u_{i}\hslash\partial_{y_{i}})x_{i}-x_{i})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad w^{\pm}_{i}\coloneqq e^{\pm\frac{1}{2}u_{i}\hslash\partial_{y_{i}}}z_{i},

for
\mathcal{S}(u)=u^{-1}(e^{u/2}-e^{-u/2})
,
x_{i}=z_{i}^{r}-t\log z_{i}
,
y_{i}=z_{i}^{s-r}
, and
C_{n}\subset S_{n}
being the subset of permutations consisting of
n
-cycles.

We remark that the
k=-1
term in the summation in the definition of
\mathsf{O}_{x}
in
equation

2.14
may appear unusual, but it is only used in the case
n=1
, where it serves to convert

(2.15)

\frac{\sqrt{dw^{+}_{1}dw^{-}_{1}}}{w^{+}_{1}-w^{-}_{1}}=\frac{dy_{1}}{u_{1}\hslash}\qquad\text{into}\qquad\left(d_{1}\frac{1}{dx_{1}}\right)^{-1}dy_{1}=y_{1}dx_{1}.

An alternative presentation would be to define
\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}
using the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
and then include an explicit term
\delta_{n,1}y_{1}dx_{1}
on the right-hand side of (
2.13
).

The main result is that, for
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, the
n
-point correlators
W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
constructed from the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion and the
n
-point correlators
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
constructed by generalized topological recursion coincide.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
ABDKS24
]
)
.

For
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,

(2.16)

W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)=\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t),

where
W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
is given by (
2.10
) and
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
is given by (
2.13
). In other words, the descendant integrals of the Chiodo classes are calculated by the determinantal formula (
2.13
).

Proof.

This follows from a key property of generalized topological recursion: when the set
\mathcal{P}
consists solely of simple zeros of
dx
, and
dy
does not vanish on
\mathcal{P}
, the differentials produced by the generalized topological recursion coincide with those of the original topological recursion of
[
EO07
]
; see
[
ABDKS25a
, Theorem 2.15]
. Since this condition holds for the spectral curve considered here (as
t\neq 0
), the proposition follows.
∎

2.2.
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes, generalized topological recursion and determinantal formulas

Proposition

2.4
says that intersection theory for the Chiodo classes (for
t\neq 0
) is calculated by the determinantal formula (
2.13
). In this section we study the limit as
t\to 0
. We show that the intersection theory of the top degrees of the Chiodo classes is also given by a determinantal formula and obtained via the generalized topological recursion.

2.2.1.
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes

The
t\to 0
limit of
proposition

2.2
motivates the definition of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes, which are the top degrees of the Chiodo classes.

Definition 2.5
.

Define the classes

(2.17)

\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)\coloneqq\frac{r^{\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+|a|}{r}}}{(s-r)^{2g-2+n}}\bigl[C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\bigr]_{D_{g;a}^{r,s}}\,,

which are the (appropriately normalized) top degrees of the Chiodo classes.

As outlined in
[
CGG
]
, the collection of classes
\set{\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)}_{g,n}
with
a\in[r-1]
defines a cohomological field theory of rank
r-1
. More precisely, consider the vector space
V\coloneqq\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r-1})
, along with the pairing

(2.18)

\eta(v_{a_{1}},v_{a_{2}})=\delta_{a_{1}+a_{2},r}.

Proposition 2.6
.

The collection of maps
\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}\colon V^{\otimes n}\to R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
given by the assignment

(2.19)

v_{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{a_{n}}\longmapsto\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})

and extended by linearity define a cohomological field theory of rank
r-1
without a flat unit on
(V,\eta)
. Moreover, this cohomological field theory satisfies the following modified unit axiom:

(2.20)

\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},s)=\psi_{n+1}\cdot p^{*}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}),

where
p\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is the forgetful map that forgets the last marked point.

Proof.

The proof of
[
CGG
, Proposition 2.6 and Theorem 2.7]
for the case
s=r-1
can be followed without any change for any
s\in[r-1]
.
∎

Note the exclusion of insertions (also know as primary fields) with
a_{i}=0
, which reduces the rank by one. Dropping these insertions is essential to obtain a cohomological field theory.

Remark 2.7
.

Assuming that the
a_{i}
are in the range
1\leq a_{i}\leq r-1
,
\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)
when
s\leq 0
is of degree bigger than
3g-3+n
and hence vanishes identically. On the other hand, when
s\geq r
,
\mathsf{R}^{\bullet}\pi_{*}\mathcal{L}
is generically a complex and not a vector bundle and thus the Chiodo class has terms in all cohomological degrees. This explains why we restrict ourselves to the range
s\in[r-1]
.

We also note that the Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold defined by the cohomological field theory above is nowhere semisimple (for instance,
v_{r-1}
is always a nilpotent element), and thus Teleman’s classification result
[
Tel12
]
does not apply. A workaround was proposed in
[
CGG
]
for the case
s=r-1
, using a deformation argument. This not only provides a method for computing the descendant integrals, but also produces relations in cohomology.

Here we propose a different method for computing the intersection numbers based on the
t\to 0
limit of
proposition

2.4
. Although this method does not address the class itself, but only the associated intersection numbers, it is much more direct.

2.2.2.
The
t\to 0
limit

First we recall the definition of the multi-factorial. For
m,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

(2.21)

m!^{(r)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}m(m-r)!^{(r)}&m>r,\\
m&1\leq m\leq r.\end{cases}

Theorem 2.8

(Determinantal formulas)
.

Let
W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
be given by (
2.10
) and
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
be given by (
2.13
). Then

(2.22)

\lim_{t\to 0}\;W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)=\lim_{t\to 0}\;\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t).

As a result, the descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes can be computed by the following determinantal formula:

(2.23)

\sum_{g\geq 0}\left(-\frac{\hslash}{r}\right)^{2g-2+n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\xi_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i})=\\
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}\right)\sum_{\sigma\in C_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{dw^{+}_{i}dw^{-}_{\sigma(i)}}}{w^{+}_{i}-w^{-}_{\sigma(i)}}-\delta_{n,1}z_{1}^{s-r}d(z_{1}^{r})-\delta_{n,2}\frac{dz_{1}dz_{1}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}},

where
\mathsf{O}_{x_{i}}
, and
w^{\pm}_{i}
are given by
equation

2.14
for the choices
x_{i}=z_{i}^{r}
and
y_{i}=z_{i}^{s-r}
, and the one-forms
d\xi_{k,a}(z)
are given by

(2.24)

d\xi_{k,a}(z)\coloneqq(rk+a)!^{(r)}\frac{dz}{z^{rk+a+1}}.

Proof.

First, both
equations

2.10
and
2.13
are rational functions in
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and for both formulas
t=0
is a removable singularity. Since the two formulas are equal by
proposition

2.4
, it implies that their limits for
t\to 0
are equal as well, which is (
2.22
).

To obtain the rest of the theorem, we evaluate the limits explicitly. First, we evaluate the limit of
W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
using (
2.10
). We have:

(2.25)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{(s-r)^{2g-2+n}}\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)^{D^{r,s}_{g;a}}\,C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a;\tfrac{r}{t})&=\frac{1}{(s-r)^{2g-2+n}}\bigl[C_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\bigr]_{D_{g;a}^{r,s}}\\
&=r^{-\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+|a|}{r}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a).\end{split}

Moreover, for the primary field
a=0
we have
d\theta_{k,0}(z;t)\to d\left(-\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{k+1}z^{r}
as
t\to 0
. But

(2.26)

\frac{d}{dx}z^{r}=\frac{rz^{r}}{rz^{r}-t}=1+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(t),

and hence
d\theta_{k,0}(z;t)\to 0
for all
k\geq 0
. Therefore, we omit the primary field
a_{i}=0
in the arguments of
\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)
. For
a\in[r-1]
we have

(2.27)

\begin{split}\lim_{t\to 0}\;d\theta_{k,a}(z;t)&=\lim_{t\to 0}\;r\,d\Big(-\frac{z}{rz^{r}-t}\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{k+1}\frac{z^{r-a}}{r-a}\\
&=r\,d\Big(-\frac{1}{rz^{r-1}}\frac{d}{dz}\Big)^{k+1}\frac{z^{r-a}}{r-a}\\
&=\frac{(rk+a)!^{(r)}}{r^{k}}\frac{dz}{z^{rk+a+1}}.\end{split}

Defining
d\xi_{k,a}(z)=(rk+a)!^{(r)}\frac{dz}{z^{rk+a+1}}
and putting all of this together, we get that

(2.28)

\lim_{t\to 0}\;W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)-\delta_{n,1}z^{s-r}d(z^{r})-\delta_{n,2}\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}=\\
\sum_{g\geq 0}(-\hslash)^{2g-2+n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}+\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+|a|}{r}}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\xi_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i}).

We finally notice that, for degree reasons, the integrals are necessarily zero unless

(2.29)

3g-3+n=D_{g,a}^{r,s}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}=g-1+\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+|a|}{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i},

and so we can replace the exponent of
r
by
2g-2+n
to get

(2.30)

\lim_{t\to 0}\;W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)-\delta_{n,1}z^{s-r}d(z^{r})-\delta_{n,2}\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}=\\
\sum_{g\geq 0}\left(-\frac{\hslash}{r}\right)^{2g-2+n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\xi_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i}).

As for the limit of
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
, it is straightforward, as the dependence on
t
in (
2.13
) is hidden in the definition of
\mathsf{O}_{x}
, and their limit for
t\to 0
is manifestly
\mathsf{O}_{x}
used in (
2.23
).
∎

2.2.3.
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes and generalized topological recursion

Theorem

2.8
gives a determinantal formula for descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes. It was obtained by taking the
t\to 0
limit of
Proposition

2.4
. For
t\neq 0
, the differentials
W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
from (
2.10
) were obtained via the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion, while the differentials
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
of (
2.13
) were obtained via the generalized topological recursion. It is natural to ask whether similar statements can be made for the limit
t\to 0
: are the differentials
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
and
\lim_{t\to 0}\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
computed by topological recursion?

This question is easy to answer from the generalized topological recursion side.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
. We define the
(r,s)
spectral curve
by the data

(2.31)

\Sigma=\mathbb{P}^{1},\quad dx=d(z^{r}),\quad dy=d(z^{s-r})\quad B=\frac{dz_{1}dz_{2}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}},\quad\mathcal{P}=\set{z=0}.

This is the
t\to 0
limit of the spectral curve considered previously.

Corollary 2.10

(Generalized topological recursion)
.

The
n
-point correlators
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)
produced by generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve calculate the descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes:

(2.32)

\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)=\delta_{n,1}z_{1}^{s-r}dz_{1}^{r}+\delta_{n,2}\frac{dz_{1}dz_{1}}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}+\\
+\sum_{g\geq 0}\left(-\frac{\hslash}{r}\right)^{2g-2+n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta_{g,n}^{r,s}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}\,d\xi_{k_{i},a_{i}}(z_{i}).

Proof.

Looking back at
proposition

2.4
, what the present proposition is essentially saying is that the limit
\lim_{t\to 0}\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
of the differentials constructed by the generalized topological recursion on the spectral curve with
dx=d(z^{r}-t\log(z))
and
dy=d(z^{s-r})
are equal to the differentials constructed by the generalized topological recursion on the limiting
t\to 0
spectral curve with
dx=d(z^{r})
and
dy=d(z^{s-r})
. But this follows from the following key property of generalized topological recursion.

If the input data of generalized topological recursion varies analytically with respect to a parameter
t
such that all points in
\mathcal{P}_{t}
belong to a domain
U
such that
dx
and
dy
are regular and non-vanishing along the boundary of
U
and there are no further special points in
U
, then the differentials of generalized topological recursion depend on
t
analytically, see
[
ABDKS25a
, Theorem 5.3]
. This implies that the
t\to 0
limit of the differentials produced by generalized topological recursion coincide with the differentials on the limiting spectral curve, which proves the proposition.
∎

What about the limit
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash;t)
? Are these correlators computed by topological recursion on the limiting spectral curve? It turns out that this question is quite subtle. First, in the
t\to 0
limit, the spectral curve becomes
x=z^{r}
,
y=z^{s-r}
. It has a single ramification point at
z=0
, which, for
r>2
, is not simple. Hence, the original Eynard–Orantin topological recursion does not apply. One can instead consider the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion
[
BE13
]
. Do the correlators
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{g,n}(z_{[n]};t)
then coincide with those produced by the Bouchard–Eynard recursion on the limiting spectral curve? This type of question was studied in detail in
[
BBCKS
]
. In this particular case, the answer is generally no: the two coincide only when
s=r-1
, which is precisely the case studied in
[
CGG
]
. In other words, while generalized topological recursion naturally “commutes with limits” (with a careful choice of
\mathcal{P}
), the Eynard–Orantin recursion—and its Bouchard–Eynard generalization—does not always do so.

Therefore, although generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve computes the descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes, the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion generally computes something else, except in the case
s=r-1
, where the two coincide. A geometric interpretation of the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion for other values of
s
remains unknown.

3.
Integrability

In the previous section, we showed that generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve produces differentials
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)
that compute the descendant integrals of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes. This leads to a clear integrability statement, which we explore in this section.

3.1.
r
-KdV integrability

A universal property of generalized topological recursion on genus zero spectral curves is that it always produces systems of differentials that are KP integrable, see
[
ABDKS
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
ABDKS25a
, Theorem 6.4]
.

In our case of interest, the resulting statement is that the partition function associated to the expansion of the differentials
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)
on the
(r,s)
spectral curve at any regular point is a KP tau function. In particular, the expansion of the differentials
\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)
at
z=\infty
in the local coordinate
z^{-1}
gives, up to a re-scaling of the times, the following tau function:

(3.1)

Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)\coloneqq\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,\,n\geq 1\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}(rk_{i}+a_{i})!^{(r)}x_{rk_{i}+a_{i}}\right).

Remark 3.1
.

While the appearance of the multi-factorials may look strange at first, they appear naturally if we allow the sum over
a_{i}
to be over all
a_{i}>0
and omit
\psi
-classes. Indeed we can rewrite
Z^{r,s}
as

(3.2)

Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)=\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,\,n\geq 1\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle a_{i}\rangle x_{rk_{i}+a_{i}}\right),

where
\langle a\rangle
denotes the remainder of the Euclidean division of
a
by
r
. This formula follows from the properties of the Chiodo class under the shift of
a
by
r
as proved in
[
GLN23
, Theorem 4.1(ii)]
.

We thus obtain the following integrability statement.

Theorem 3.2

(Integrability)
.

The descendant potential
Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)
of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes is a tau function of the
r
-KdV (also known as
r
-Gelfand–Dickey) hierarchy.

Proof.

This is an immediate observation combining the KP integrability of the partition function in (
3.1
) with its independence from the times
x_{rm}
, for
m\geq 1
. The latter follows from the absence of monomials
z^{rm}
in
d\xi_{k,a}(z)
in (
2.32
), since
a
only takes values in
[r-1]
, i.e.,
a=0
is excluded.
∎

3.2.
The initial conditions

Theorem

3.2
says that
Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)
is a
r
-KdV tau function, but it does not say
which
tau function. To specify the tau function we need to compute initial conditions for the normal coordinates of the
r
-KdV hierarchy. In other words, we want to compute

(3.3)

u_{\alpha}(x_{1};\hslash)\coloneqq\partial_{1}\partial_{\alpha}\log Z^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)\big|_{x_{i}=0\,\forall\,i\geq 2}

for all
\alpha\in[r-1]
. Here
\partial_{m}
is the shorthand notation for
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m}}
. The functions
\partial_{1}\partial_{\alpha}\log Z^{r,s}
are called the normal coordinates of the
r
-KdV hierarchy, so
u_{\alpha}
are the corresponding initial conditions. From the definition of
Z^{r,s}
, it follows that

(3.4)

u_{\alpha}(x_{1};\hslash)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,\,n\geq 2\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{(n-2)!}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(\alpha,1,\cdots,1)\,\alpha\,x_{1}^{n-2}.

We can express the initial conditions for the normal coordinates in terms of finitely many primary integrals (i.e. no
\psi
-classes) of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-class. The shape of these initial conditions looks different for
s=1
and
s>1
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The following initial conditions hold.

•

For any
r\geq 2
,
s=1
, and
\alpha\in[r-1]
:

(3.5)

u_{\alpha}(x_{1};\hslash)=\delta_{\alpha,2g-1}\left(\alpha^{2}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,1}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,1}(\alpha)\right)\left(\frac{\hslash}{1-\hslash x_{1}}\right)^{\alpha+1}.

•

For any
r\geq 3
,
s=2,\ldots,r-1
, and
\alpha\in[r-1]
:

(3.6)

u_{\alpha}(x_{1};\hslash)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,\,n\geq 2\\
2g-2+n>0\\
\alpha=s(2g-2+n)-n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{(n-2)!}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(\alpha,1,\cdots,1)\,\alpha\,x_{1}^{n-2},

which is a polynomial in
x_{1}
and
\hslash
.

Proof.

We want to understand when the integral in (
3.4
) is non-zero. For degree reasons, the integral is automatically zero unless

(3.7)

3g-3+n=g-1+\frac{(2g-2+n)(r-s)+\alpha+n-1}{r},

which simplifies to
\alpha=s(2g-2+n)-n+1
. For
s>1
, there are only finitely many
(g,n)
that satisfy the above equation. Thus, we see from
equation

3.4
that for
s>1
,
u_{\alpha}

is a polynomial in
x_{1}
and
\hslash
. This proves the second point of the thesis.

For
s=1
, the condition (
3.7
) simplifies further to
\alpha=2g-1
, which is independent of
n
. As a result, there are infinitely many values of
n
to take into account. Recall from
proposition

2.6
that the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes satisfy the modified unit axiom, that is

(3.8)

\Theta^{r,1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n},1)=\psi_{n+1}\cdot p^{*}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,n}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}),

where
p\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is the forgetful map that forgets the last marked point. Integrating over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}
, we get

(3.9)

\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n},1)=(2g-2+n)\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,n}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})

by applying the projection formula. As a consequence, we can compute
u_{\alpha}
for the case
s=1
as

(3.10)

\begin{split}u_{\alpha}(x_{1};\hslash)&=\delta_{\alpha,2g-1}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\hslash^{2g-1+n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,n+1}(\alpha,1,\cdots,1)\alpha x_{1}^{n-1}\\
&=\delta_{\alpha,2g-1}\left(\alpha\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,1}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,1}(\alpha)\right)\sum_{n\geq 1}\hslash^{\alpha+1}\frac{(2g-2+n)\cdots(2g-2+1)}{(n-1)!}(\hslash x_{1})^{n-1}\\
&=\delta_{\alpha,2g-1}\left(\alpha\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,1}}\Theta^{r,1}_{g,1}(\alpha)\right)\sum_{n\geq 1}\hslash^{\alpha+1}\frac{(\alpha+n-1)!}{(\alpha-1)!(n-1)!}(\hslash x_{1})^{n-1}\\

This proves the first point of the thesis.

4.1.2.
Another determinantal formula

A restatement of

Proposition 4.4
.

The
n

produced by generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)

(4.12)

\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)=\sum_{\sigma\in C_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}K(z_{i},z_{\sigma(i)};\hslash).

Proof.

In
[
ABDKS25b
, Section 3.2]

(4.13)

\tilde{K}(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\phi_{1-k}^{*}(z_{1};\hslash)\phi_{k}(z_{2};\hslash)\sqrt{dz_{1}dz_{2}}.

Specifically, the result reads

(4.14)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}(z;\hslash)

\displaystyle=\lim_{z_{0}\to z}\left(\tilde{K}(z,z_{0};\hslash)-\frac{\sqrt{dzdz_{0}}}{z-z_{0}}\right)+\frac{1}{\hslash}y(z)dx(z),

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(z_{[n]};\hslash)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in C_{n}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{K}(z_{i},z_{\sigma(i)};\hslash),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad n\geq 2.

The difference between
K
and

is simply in the exponential prefactor:

(4.15)

K(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)=e^{-\frac{1}{\hslash}\frac{r}{s}(z_{1}^{s}-z_{2}^{s})}\tilde{K}(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash).

Thus, we see that for

by regularization. In the formula for

We have the following alternative expression for the kernel as a

Proposition 4.5
.

For

K(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)=\frac{\sqrt{dx_{1}dx_{2}}}{x_{1}-x_{2}}\Biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{r-s}x_{1}\psi_{1-k}^{*}(z_{1};\hslash)\psi_{k}(z_{2};\hslash)+\sum_{k=r-s+1}^{r}\psi_{r+1-k}^{*}(z_{1};\hslash)\psi_{k}(z_{2};\hslash)\Biggr)

where

Proof.

We omit the dependence on
\hslash
for ease of notation. Multiplying the definition of the kernel by

\begin{split}(x_{1}-x_{2})K(z_{1},z_{2})&=\sum_{k\geq 1}\bigg[\left(\psi_{r+1-k}^{*}(z_{1})-\hslash\frac{2(1-k)+r-s-1}{2(r-s)}\psi_{r-s+1-k}^{*}(z_{1})\right)\psi_{k}(z_{2})\\

Finally, we use

4.1.3.
Matrix forms

In light of the differential system
4.6
, it is natural to consider the following matrix solution, called the wave matrix:

(4.19)

\Psi(z;\hslash)=\bigl(\Psi_{k,a}(z;\hslash)\bigr)_{k,a=1,\ldots,r}\coloneqq\bigl(\psi_{k}(\theta^{a}z;\hslash)\bigr)_{k,a=1,\ldots,r}

where
\theta=e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{r}}
is a primitive
r
-th root of unity. By design, the wave matrix satisfies
\hslash\frac{d}{dx}\Psi=\mathcal{D}\Psi
and its last column coincides with the vector of wave functions
\psi
considered in the previous section. Similarly, we define
\Psi^{*}
from the dual wave functions.

A simple computation implies that the inverse wave matrix satisfies
-\hslash\frac{d}{dx}\Psi^{-1}=\Psi^{-1}\mathcal{D}
, which looks like the same differential equation with the sign of
\hslash
swapped. This would imply a simple relation between the inverse wave matrix and its dual. However, this is not immediately clear, as the matrix
\mathcal{D}
does depend on
\hslash
. Nonetheless, the following relation still holds.

Lemma 4.7
.

The inverse wave matrix is given by

(4.20)

\Psi^{-1}_{a,k}(z;\hslash)=\begin{cases}x\psi^{*}_{1-k}(\theta^{a}z;\hslash)&1\leq k\leq r-s,\\[4.30554pt]
\psi^{*}_{r+1-k}(\theta^{a}z;\hslash)&r-s<k\leq r.\end{cases}

Proof.

Denote as
\tilde{\Psi}_{a,k}(z;\hslash)
the right-hand side of
equation

4.20
. A direct computation shows that the matrix
\tilde{\Psi}
satisfies the same differential equation as that satisfied by
\Psi^{-1}
. Hence, they coincide up to a normalization factor. This factor can be fixed by looking at the first coefficient in the
\hslash
-expansion of both matrices:

(4.21)

\Psi_{k,a}(z;\hslash)=\theta^{a(k-1-\frac{r-1}{2})}\frac{e^{\frac{1}{\hslash}\frac{r}{s}\theta^{as}z^{s}}}{\sqrt{rz^{r-1}}}z^{k-1}\bigl(1+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash)\bigr)

which implies that

(4.22)

\Psi^{-1}_{a,k}(z;\hslash)=\theta^{-a(k-1-\frac{r-1}{2})}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{\hslash}\frac{r}{s}\theta^{as}z^{s}}}{\sqrt{rz^{r-1}}}z^{r-k}\bigl(1+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash)\bigr).

One can check that the same asymptotics holds for
\tilde{\Psi}_{a,k}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

As we did for the wave function, it will prove useful to introduce the following matrix version of the kernel:

(4.23)

\mathcal{K}(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)=\left(\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)\right)_{a,b=1,\ldots,r}\coloneqq\left(K(\theta^{a}z_{1},\theta^{b}z_{2};\hslash)\right)_{a,b=1,\ldots,r}.

It can be compactly written in terms of the wave matrix as follows.

Lemma 4.8
.

The matrix kernel
\mathcal{K}
can be expressed as

(4.24)

\mathcal{K}(z_{1},z_{2};\hslash)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{\hslash}\Psi^{-1}(z_{1};\hslash)\mathcal{D}(x_{1};\hslash)\Psi(z_{1};\hslash)\,dx_{1},&\text{if }z_{1}=z_{2},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\Psi^{-1}(z_{1};\hslash)\Psi(z_{2};\hslash)}{x_{1}-x_{2}}\sqrt{dx_{1}dx_{2}},&\text{if }z_{1}\neq z_{2},\\
\end{cases}

where as usual
x_{i}=z_{i}^{r}
.

Proof.

The second equation follows from rewriting the result of
proposition

4.5
using the expression obtained in
equation

4.20
for
\Psi^{-1}(z;\hslash)
. As for the case when
z_{1}=z_{2}
, we use L’Hôpital’s rule to calculate the limit. When

(4.25)

\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(z,z;\hslash)=\lim_{z_{0}\to z}\frac{\left(\Psi^{-1}(z;\hslash)\Psi(z_{0};\hslash)\right)_{a,b}}{x-x_{0}}\sqrt{dxdx_{0}}=-\frac{1}{\hslash}\left(\Psi^{-1}(z;\hslash)\mathcal{D}(x;\hslash)\Psi(z;\hslash)\right)_{a,b}dx,

where we use the matrix differential system

(4.26)

\mathcal{K}_{a,a}(z,z;\hslash)=\frac{1}{\hslash}y(\theta^{a}z)dx+\\

Now, applying L’Hôpital’s rule produces an extra summand due to the exponential prefactor, which precisely cancels the

4.2.
Towards the loop equations

In this section, we use the determinantal formulas for the
n
-point correlators
\omega_{n}
derived in
proposition

4.4
to express a certain combination of these correlators as a characteristic polynomial. We essentially follow the approach of
[

]
, adapted to our (simpler) setting. This, in turn, allows us to extract conditions on the poles of such combinations in

, which are precisely the loop equations.

4.2.1.
Some definitions

First, let us introduce the following
(k+n)
-differentials:

(4.27)

\omega_{k;n}(z_{[k]};w_{[n]};\hslash)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{L}\vdash[k]\\
\bigsqcup_{L\in\bm{L}}M_{L}=[n]\end{subarray}}\prod_{L\in\bm{L}}\omega_{|L|+|M_{L}|}(z_{L},w_{M_{L}};\hslash).

They represent
k
-disconnected,
n
-connected correlators, or semi-connected correlators for short. For a graphical explanation of what these objects are, see
[
BBCKS
, Section 2.2]
.

Then, for
k\in\set{0,\ldots,n}
, we define the correlator
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)
as

(4.28)

\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subseteq x^{\leftarrow}(z)\\
|Z|=k\end{subarray}}\omega_{k;n}(Z;z_{[n]};\hslash),

where by convention
\mathcal{E}^{(0)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)=1
and

(4.29)

x^{\leftarrow}(z)\coloneqq x^{-1}(x(z))=\Set{z^{\prime}}{x(z^{\prime})=x(z)}

is the set of points of the spectral curve mapping to the same point as
z
. Notice that
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)
is a
k
-differential in
z
, and a differential in
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
.

The correlators
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
are the main character appearing in the abstract loop equations of the Bouchard–Eynard topological recursion, see
[
BE17
,
BBCKS
]
. As we are summing over all preimages of the point
x(z)
, the differential
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
is actually the pullback of a
k
-differential in
x(z)
from the base to the spectral curve. Furthermore, notice that
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
has the following genus expansion:

(4.30)

\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)=\sum_{g\geq 0}\hslash^{2g-k+n}\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{g,n}(z;z_{[n]}),

where the differentials
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{g,n}(z;z_{[n]})
do not depend on
\hslash
and coincide with the ones defined in
[
BBCKS
, Section 2.2]
.

4.2.2.
A characteristic polynomial equation

Our goal is to express the differentials
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
as the coefficients of a characteristic polynomial. In order to do so, we first need to realize the semi-connected correlators
\omega_{k;n}
as a determinant-like expression.

To this end, define
S_{k;n}\subseteq S_{k+n}
to be the subset of all permutations
\tau
in
S_{k+n}
on the elements
\set{1,2,\ldots,k+n}
that do not fix any non-empty subset of
\set{k+1,k+2,\ldots,k+n}
. In other words, when
\tau
is expressed as a product of disjoint cycles, each cycle that appears contains at least one element from the set
\set{1,2,\ldots,k}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
Z\subseteq x^{\leftarrow}(z)
be a subset of cardinality
|Z|=k
. Choose an arbitrary ordering of
Z
so that it can be written as the
k
-tuple
Z=(\theta^{a_{1}}z,\ldots,\theta^{a_{k}}z)
for some pairwise distinct integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in[r]
. Then, we have

(4.31)

\omega_{k;n}(Z;z_{[n]},\hslash)=\sum_{\tau\in S_{k;n}}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{k+n}K(\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{\tau(i)};\hslash),

where we have set

(4.32)

\hat{z}_{i}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\theta^{a_{i}}z&1\leq i\leq k,\\
z_{i-k}&k<i\leq k+n.\end{cases}

Proof.

First of all, note that the both sides of (
4.31
) do not depend on the choice of ordering of the elements in
Z
, since the first
k
elements are treated symmetrically in both
\omega_{k;n}
and
S_{k;n}
.

Now, recall the definition (
4.27
) of the semi-connected correlators, and apply the determinantal formula (
4.12
) to each factor
\omega_{|L|+|M_{L}|}(Z_{L},z_{M_{L}};\hslash)
. Each such factor is then a sum of products of elements of the form
\sgn(\tau_{L})K(\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{\tau_{L}(i)};\hslash)
, where
\tau_{L}
is a cycle of maximal length
|L|+|M_{L}|
on
L\sqcup M_{L}\subseteq\set{1,2,\ldots,k+n}
(and thus
\sgn(\tau_{L})=(-1)^{|L|+|M_{L}|-1}
). Thus, we find

(4.33)

\omega_{k;n}(Z;z_{[n]},\hslash)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{L}\vdash[k]\\
\bigsqcup_{L\in\bm{L}}M_{L}=[n]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\tau_{L}}\left(\prod_{L\in\bm{L}}\sgn(\tau_{L})\right)\left(\prod_{L\in\bm{L}}\prod_{i\in L\sqcup M_{L}}K(\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{\tau_{L}(i)};\hslash)\right).

Note that, by definition of
S_{k;n}
, each permutation
\tau\in S_{k;n}
can be uniquely expressed as a product of disjoint cycles
\prod_{L\in\bm{L}}\tau_{L}
, where the cycles are indexed by the non-empty subsets
L\subseteq\set{1,\dots,k}
that they involve. Moreover,
\sgn(\tau)=\prod_{L\in\bm{L}}\sgn(\tau_{L})
. In other words, the right-hand side of (
4.33
) is simply a reformulation of the right-hand side of (
4.31
), in which the sum over
\tau\in S_{k;n}
is reorganized as a sum over products of its disjoint cycles
\tau_{L}
.
∎

We can now realize the semi-connected correlators
\omega_{k;n}
as a principal minor of size
k
of an
r\times r
matrix, that is as a sum over the symmetric group
S_{k}
instead of
S_{k;n}\subseteq S_{n}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
Z\subseteq x^{\leftarrow}(z)
be a subset of cardinality
|Z|=k
, arbitrarily ordered as
Z=(\theta^{a_{1}}z,\ldots,\theta^{a_{k}}z)
. Then, we have

(4.34)

\omega_{k;n}(Z;z_{[n]};\hslash)=(-1)^{n}[\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{n}]\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Bigl(\mathcal{J}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash,\epsilon)\Bigr)_{a_{j},a_{\sigma(j)}},

where
\mathcal{J}_{n}

\mathcal{J}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash,\epsilon)\coloneqq\mathcal{K}(z,z;\hslash)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{\ell}\leq n}\epsilon_{i_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{i_{\ell}}\times\\
\times\mathcal{K}(z,z_{i_{1}};\hslash)E_{r}\mathcal{K}(z_{i_{1}},z_{i_{2}};\hslash)E_{r}\cdots E_{r}\mathcal{K}(z_{i_{\ell-1}},z_{i_{\ell}};\hslash)E_{r}\mathcal{K}(z_{i_{\ell}},z;\hslash)

and

elementary matrix whose only non-zero entry is

Proof.

We drop the
\hslash

\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{n}
:

(4.36)

(-1)^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\sgn(\sigma)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=[n]\\
I_{j}=(i^{(j)}_{1},\cdots,i^{(j)}_{|I_{j}|})\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{K}_{a_{j},r}(z,z_{i^{(j)}_{1}})\mathcal{K}_{r,r}(z_{i^{(j)}_{1}},z_{i^{(j)}_{2}})\cdots\mathcal{K}_{r,a_{\sigma(j)}}(z_{i^{(j)}_{|I_{j}|}},z).

In the innermost sum, each

for

is a possibly empty ordered set such that the disjoint union of all the

equals

. If a certain set

From a permutation

(viewed as a permutation in

) and the sets

appearing in

:

(4.37)

\tau=\sigma\circ\left(\sigma(1),k+i_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,k+i^{(1)}_{|I_{1}|}\right)\circ\cdots\circ\left(\sigma(k),k+i_{k}^{(k)},\ldots,k+i^{(k)}_{|I_{k}|}\right).

In fact, the above permutation clearly lives in

\sum_{\tau\in S_{k;n}\subseteq S_{k+n}}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{k+n}K(\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{\tau(i)}),

where we use the notation from
equation

4.32

∎

4.3.
Loop equations

We are finally ready to derive the loop equations, which is a statement about the behavior of the correlators
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
as
x\to 0
. Recall that the
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)
are pullbacks of
k
-differentials in
x=z^{r}
.

Theorem 4.12

(Loop equations)
.

As
x\to 0
, the differentials
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(k)}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)
behave as

(4.45)

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(k)}(z;z_{[n]};\hslash)=\begin{cases}\delta_{n,0}A_{k}\frac{dx^{k}}{x^{k}}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\frac{dx^{k}}{x^{k-1}})&1\leq k\leq r-s,\\[4.30554pt]
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\frac{dx^{k}}{x^{r-s}})&r-s<k\leq r,\end{cases}

where the constants
A_{k}
are given in terms of the elementary symmetric polynomial
e_{k}
by

(4.46)

A_{k}\coloneqq e_{k}\left(\frac{2+s-r-1}{2(r-s)},\frac{4+s-r-1}{2(r-s)},\ldots,\frac{2(r-s)+s-r-1}{2(r-s)}\right).

It is worth noting that the constants
A_{k}
vanish unless
k
is even.

Proof.

We begin with (
4.41
) for the differentials
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
. Write

(4.47)

\mathcal{P}dx\coloneqq ydx\,\mathrm{Id}_{r\times r}-\mathcal{A}_{n}=ydx\,\mathrm{Id}_{r\times r}+\frac{1}{\hslash}\,\mathcal{D}dx-\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}+\frac{1}{\hslash}\,\mathcal{D}dx\right).

The goal is to understand the terms of highest order in
1/x
, i.e. the most singular ones as
x\to 0
, contributing to the determinant of
\mathcal{P}dx
. Note that all the entries of
\mathcal{A}_{n}+\frac{1}{\hslash}\,\mathcal{D}dx
are regular as
x\to 0
. From the form of
\mathcal{D}
in
equation

4.7
, we see that there are singular terms appearing on the main diagonal and the
(s+1)
-th upper diagonal. Thus, all the singular contributions come from these elements of
\mathcal{D}
.

The key observation is to note that in the Laplace expansion for the determinant, the following two types of permutations contribute the highest powers of
1/x
to the determinant.

•

For any
p\in[s]
, consider the permutation

(4.48)

\sigma_{p}\coloneqq\left(p,(s+p),(2s+p),\cdots,\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor s+p\right).

Then, in the Laplace expansion of
\det\mathcal{P}dx
, we pick
\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor
of the
1/x
terms appearing on the
(s+1)
-th upper diagonal of
\mathcal{D}
. More precisely,
\sigma_{p}
contributes

(4.49)

\mathcal{P}_{\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor s+p,p}\prod_{b=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor}\mathcal{P}_{(b-1)s+p,bs+p}=\alpha\,x^{-\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}\bigl(x^{-\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor+1}\bigr)

where
\alpha
is a constant independent of
x
and
y
.

•

For any
p\in[s]
, consider the permutation
\tau_{p}
which acts as the identity on all the integers that appear in
\sigma_{p}
. Then the contribution from
\tau_{p}
is of the form

(4.50)

\prod_{b=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor}\mathcal{P}_{bs+p,bs+p}=\beta\,y\,x^{-\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}\bigl(x^{-\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor+1}\bigr),

where
\beta
is a constant independent of
x
and
y
, and the terms in
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(x^{-\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor+1})
may depend on
y
. In this case, in the Laplace expansion, we pick the
1/x
terms appearing in the main diagonal of
\mathcal{D}
for all the terms except the last. The last term is chosen to be
y
as
\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor s+p>r-s
, and thus, we cannot choose a term of the form
1/x
.

The most singular terms in the determinant are then produced by permutations that are products of the permutations
\sigma_{p}
and
\tau_{p}
for
p\in[s]
. In other words, we need to choose a permutation
\sigma_{p}
or
\tau_{p}
for every
p\in[s]
. Depending on the number of times we choose
\sigma_{p}
, say
0\leq t\leq s
times, the most singular term is

(4.51)

\gamma\,x^{-\sum_{p=1}^{s}\left\lfloor\frac{r-p}{s}\right\rfloor}y^{s-t}=\gamma\,x^{s-r}y^{s-t},

where
\gamma
is a constant independent of
x
and
y
. This proves the thesis for
r-s<k\leq r
in
equation

4.45
.

Consider now the case
1\leq k\leq r-s
. In order to get terms that contain
y^{s+t}
for
t\in[r-s]
, we need to consider a permutation
\sigma
in
S_{r}
that acts as the identity on at least
s+t
elements. Our previous argument shows that the most singular contributions that also contain the highest powers of
y
are obtained by choosing the permutation
\tau_{p}
for every
p\in[s]
. Thus the most singular terms we get are given by

(4.52)

y^{s}\prod_{i=1}^{r-s}\left(y+\frac{1}{x}\frac{2i+s-r-1}{2(r-s)}\right)=\sum_{t=0}^{r-s}y^{s+t}A_{r-s-t}\frac{1}{x^{r-s-t}},

where
A_{k}
is defined in
equation

4.46
. Notice that, as the above terms are independent of
\epsilon_{[n]}
, they only contribute to the correlator
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}_{n}
when
n=0
due to
equation

4.41
.

When
n>0
, we are forced to choose a term
(\mathcal{A}_{n}+\frac{1}{\hslash}\,\mathcal{D}dx)_{c,c}
for some
1\leq c\leq r-s
on the diagonal instead of a term from
\mathcal{D}_{c,c}
that contributes
1/x
. Thus the most singular contribution with the power
y^{s+t}
is

(4.53)

\delta\,y^{s+t}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^{r-s-t-1}

for some
t\in[r-s]
, and some constant
\delta
independent of
x
and
y
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
(r,s)
be coprime. Then the correlator
\omega_{g,1}
of the generalized topological recursion on the
(r,s)
spectral curve admits the following formula:

(4.60)

\omega_{g,1}(z_{0})=\Res_{z=0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subseteq x^{\leftarrow}(z)\setminus\{z\}\\

where we adopt the convention that

for all

Proof.

We can obtain a stronger version of the loop equations of

4.12

(4.62)

5.1.
\mathcal{W}
-constraints for the descendant potential

In this section, we recast the loop equations derived in the previous section as a set of
\mathcal{W}
-constraints for the descendant potential.

5.1.1.
Twist-field representations

We are interested in the principal
\mathcal{W}
-algebra of
\mathfrak{gl}_{r}
at the shifted level
k+r=1
(known as the self-dual level), which we denote by
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
for notational simplicity. The algebra
\mathcal{W}(\mathfrak{gl}_{r})
is strongly and freely generated, as a vertex algebra, by
r
fields denoted
W^{i}(z)
for
i\in[r]
. The field
W^{i}(z)
has conformal weight
i
.

We use the convention

(5.1)

W^{i}(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}W^{i}_{k}\,z^{-i-k}

for the mode expansion of the generating fields
W^{i}(z)
, for any
i\in[r]
. The modes
W^{i}_{k}
together with the commutator
[\,,\,]
form a non-linear Lie algebra, and we denote by
\mathcal{U}_{r}
its universal enveloping algebra. The modes
W^{i}_{k}
form a PBW basis for
\mathcal{U}_{r}
.

There is a natural exhaustive ascending filtration on
\mathcal{U}_{r}
, given by conformal weight. We denote by
F_{n}\mathcal{U}_{r}
the subspace of elements of conformal weight
\leq n
, and introduce a parameter
\hslash
using the Rees construction:

(5.2)

\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\hslash}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\hslash^{n}F_{n}\mathcal{U}_{r}.

This endows
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\hslash}
with a graded algebra structure in powers of
\hslash
. We denote by
W^{\hslash,i}_{k}\coloneqq\hslash^{i}W^{i}_{k}
the homogenization of the modes in
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\hslash}
.
See, for instance,
[
Bou
, Section 2.6.4]
or
[
BBKN
, Section 2.1.4]
for more details.

To obtain differential constraints, we aim to construct a representation of
\mathcal{U}_{r}^{\hslash}
in terms of differential operators. We begin by considering the Weyl algebra in the variables
\bm{x}=\set{x_{i}}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
, that is, the algebra of differential operators in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},
with polynomial coefficients. Since the number of variables is infinite, we take a suitable completion of the Weyl algebra, denoted
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}

5.2.1.
Airy structures

Let us first recall the definition of Airy structures. These were introduced in
[
KS18
]
as an algebraic reformulation (and generalization) of the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion
[
EO07
]
. We provide only a brief overview here and refer the reader to the lecture notes
[

]
and the papers
[
KS18
,
ABCO24
,
BBCCN24
,
BCJ24
,
BBKN
]
for further details.

Let
A
be a finite or countably infinite index set, and let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
denote the Weyl algebra in the variables
\set{x_{a}}_{a\in A}
. Let
\mathcal{D}^{\hslash}_{A}
be the Rees–Weyl algebra associated with the Bernstein filtration, as in the previous section. Airy structures (or Airy ideals) are particular left ideals in
\mathcal{D}^{\hslash}_{A}
.

Definition 5.4
.

A left ideal
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\hslash}_{A}
is called an
Airy structure
(or
Airy ideal
) if there exists a bounded
4
4

4

See
[
BCJ24
, Definition 2.15]
or
[
Bou
, Definition 2.6]
for the definition of a set of bounded operators.

generating set
\set{H_{a}}_{a\in A}
for
\mathcal{J}
such that:

(1)

The operators
H_{a}
take the form

(5.16)

H_{a}=\hslash\partial_{a}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash^{2}).

(2)

The left ideal
\mathcal{J}
satisfies
[\mathcal{J},\mathcal{J}]\subseteq\hslash^{2}\mathcal{J}
.

The principal motivation for studying Airy structures is the following foundational theorem from
[

]
.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
KS18
]
)
.

Let

2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}F_{g,n}\right),

where each

is a homogeneous polynomial of degree

.

5.2.2.
The
(r,s)
Airy structures

The notion of Airy structures is relevant because, if we can show that the constraints from

An interesting class of Airy structures to compare with was constructed in

(

)

Let

Remark 5.7

(Admissibility)

Note that these Airy structures are defined only when the admissibility condition

]

5.2.3.
The shifted
(r,s)

A slightly larger class of Airy structures can be obtained as representations of

The idea is simple: we start with one of the

by terms of the form

for some constants
5
5

5

[

Definition 5.8

(

We then obtain the following new class of shifted

(

5.3.3.
The remaining cases

We now consider the remaining cases, namely

5.3
do not coincide with either the
(r,s)

or the shifted
(r,s)

5.9

r\geq 4
and

, at least one of the shifts—specifically

—in the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints is non-zero, and hence they cannot coincide with the unshifted
(r,s)
Airy structures of
theorem

5.6
. Therefore, they could only potentially match the shifted
(r,s)
Airy structures of
theorem

5.9

, the only possibility compatible with the definition of
s

5.8
is

In fact, as we shall now see, for these values of
(r,s)
the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints of
theorem

5.3
do not define an Airy structure at all. To verify this, we examine the
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(1)
and
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash)
terms in the operators
H^{i}_{k}
from
theorem

5.3
to determine whether condition (
1
) in the
definition

5.4
of Airy structures is satisfied. Let us first formalize the relevant index set from
theorem

5.3
.

Definition 5.13
.

Let
I_{r}\coloneqq\Set{(i,k)}{i\in[r],\ k\in\mathbb{Z}}
. We define the subset
I_{r,s}\subset I_{r}
by the conditions:

•

for
i\in[r-s]
, we require
k\geq 0
;

•

for
r-s+1\leq i\leq r
, we require
k\geq r-s-i+1
.

With this notation, the operators in
theorem

5.3
are the
H^{i}_{k}
for
(i,k)\in I_{r,s}
.

The
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(1)
term in the
\hslash
-expansion of the operator
H^{i}_{k}
from (
5.8
) has the form (originating from the dilaton shift in
J_{-s}
):

(5.42)

H^{i}_{k}=\mu^{i}_{r,s}\,\delta_{k,-\frac{is}{r}}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash),

where
\mu^{i}_{r,s}
is a non-zero but irrelevant constant. However, it is easy to see that
\left(i,-\frac{is}{r}\right)\notin I_{r,s}
for all
i\in[r]
, so none of the operators in
theorem

5.3
contain
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(1)
terms.

The
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash)
term of
H^{i}_{k}
also arises from the dilaton shift and takes the form

(5.43)

H^{i}_{k}=\nu^{i}_{r,s}\,\hslash J_{(i-1)s+rk}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash^{2}),

where again
\nu^{i}_{r,s}
is an irrelevant non-zero constant. In order to satisfy condition (
1
) of
definition

5.4
, we would need all modes
J_{m}
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
to appear exactly once as the
\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash)
term of some
H^{i}_{k}
. To analyze whether this condition is met, we introduce the following map.

Definition 5.14
.

Let
I_{r}\coloneqq\Set{(i,k)}{i\in[r],\ k\in\mathbb{Z}}
as above. We define the map

(5.44)

\Pi_{r,s}\colon I_{r}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad(i,k)\longmapsto(i-1)s+rk.

Condition (
1
) in
definition

5.4
is then equivalent to the requirement that the restriction of
\Pi_{r,s}
to
I_{r,s}\subset I_{r}
is a bijection onto
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
K_{r,s}\coloneqq\Set{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}{m\neq a(r-s)+bs\text{ for any $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$}}
. Note that
K_{r,s}
is finite if and only if
r
and
s
are coprime, and
K_{r,s}=\emptyset
if and only if
s=1
or
s=r-1
. Then, for all
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
,

(5.45)

\Pi_{r,s}(I_{r,s})=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus K_{r,s},

and
\Pi_{r,s}\big|_{I_{r,s}}
is injective if and only if
r
and
s
are coprime.

Proof.

First, for the initial remarks. Let
g=\gcd(r,s)
. Clearly, all positive integers that are not multiple of
g
are in
K_{r,s}
. Therefore
K_{r,s}
is infinite if
g\geq 2
. As for the coprime case
g=1
, it is a classical result (the Frobenius coin-exchange problem) that the largest positive integer that cannot be written as
m=a(r-s)+bs
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is
s(r-s)-r
. Consequently,
K_{r,s}
is finite. Moreover,
K_{r,s}
is empty when
s=1
or
s=r-1
, while if
2\leq s\leq r-2
, then
1\in K_{r,s}
and hence it is non-empty.

Now to the main part of the lemma. Write
I_{r,s}=I^{1}_{r,s}\cup I^{2}_{r,s}
, where

(5.46)

\displaystyle I^{1}_{r,s}

\displaystyle=\Set{(i,k)}{i\in[r-s],\ k\geq 0},

\displaystyle I^{2}_{r,s}

\displaystyle=\Set{(i,k)}{r-s+1\leq i\leq r,\ k\geq r-s-i+1}.

Simple index manipulations show that:

(5.47)

\displaystyle\Pi_{r,s}(I^{1}_{r,s})

\displaystyle=\Set{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}{m=k(r-s)+(i-1+k)s,\ i\in[r-s],\ k\geq 0},

\displaystyle\Pi_{r,s}(I^{2}_{r,s})

\displaystyle=\Set{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}{m=(i+k)(r-s)+ks,\ i\in[s],\ k\geq 0}.

This gives us a description of
\Pi_{r,s}(I_{r,s})=\Pi_{r,s}(I^{1}_{r,s})\cup\Pi_{r,s}(I^{2}_{r,s})
.

Now consider the set
J_{r,s}=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus K_{r,s}
of positive integers that can be written as
a(r-s)+bs
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Clearly,
\Pi_{r,s}(I_{r,s})\subseteq J_{r,s}
. Let us prove the other inclusion. Set
g=\gcd(r,s)
as above, let
m\in J_{r,s}
, and pick a pair
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
m=a(r-s)+bs
. Any other pair
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
m=a^{\prime}(r-s)+b^{\prime}s
will satisfy

(5.48)

a^{\prime}=a-\frac{ks}{g},\qquad b^{\prime}=b+\frac{k(r-s)}{g}\qquad\text{for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In particular,
b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}=b-a+\frac{kr}{g}
. This means that for any
m\in J_{r,s}
, we can find a unique pair
A,B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
m=A(r-s)+Bs
and
-\frac{s}{g}\leq B-A\leq\frac{r-s}{g}-1
. When
B-A\geq 0
,
m\in\Pi_{r,s}(I^{1}_{r,s})
, while when
B-A<0
,
m\in\Pi_{r,s}(I^{2}_{r,s})
. This shows that
J_{r,s}\subseteq\Pi_{r,s}(I_{r,s})
, and we conclude that
J_{r,s}=\Pi_{r,s}(I_{r,s})
.

Since the representation of
m\in J_{r,s}
in terms of
A
and
B
is unique, this also shows that the restriction
\Pi_{r,s}\big|_{I_{r,s}}
is
g
-to-one. In particular, it is injective if and only if
g=1
, that is, if and only if
r
and
s
are coprime.
∎

As a result of
lemma

5.15
, we can immediately conclude that:

Corollary 5.16
.

For
r\geq 4
and
2\leq s\leq r-2
, the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints satisfied by the descendant potential of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes from
theorem

5.3
do not form an Airy structure.

However, all is not lost. The
\mathcal{W}
-constraints still fix a large part of the descendant potential.

Definition 5.17
.

Let
Z^{r,s}
be the descendant potential of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes. We define the
reduced descendant potential

\hat{Z}^{r,s}
to be:

(5.49)

\hat{Z}^{r,s}(\bm{x};\hslash)\coloneqq\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,\,n\geq 1\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 0\\
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in[r-1]\\
rk_{i}+a_{i}\in K_{r,s}\end{subarray}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\Theta^{r,s}_{g,n}(a)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}x_{rk_{i}+a_{i}}\right).

In other words, we single out all polynomials in the variables
x_{m}
with
m\in K_{r,s}
.

Proposition 5.18
.

For any
r\geq 2
and
s\in[r-1]
, given the reduced descendant potential
\hat{Z}^{r,s}
of the
\Theta^{r,s}
-classes, the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints of
theorem

5.3
uniquely fix the descendant potential
Z^{r,s}
.

Proof.

Write the descendant potential as

(5.50)

Z^{r,s}=\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,n\geq 1\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hslash^{2g-2+n}}{n!}\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}F_{g,n}[m]\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{m_{i}}\right).

The operators in
theorem

5.3
take the form

(5.51)

C_{\ell}\,\hslash\partial_{\ell}+\mathord{\mathrm{O}}(\hslash^{2})

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus K_{r,s}
, where the
C_{\ell}
are irrelevant non-zero constants. As a result, acting on the descendant potential
Z^{r,s}
, we get a recursion of the form

(5.52)

F_{g,n}[\ell,m_{1},\ldots,m_{n-1}]=G_{g,n}[\ell,m_{1},\ldots,m_{n-1}],

where
G_{g,n}[\ell,m_{1},\ldots,m_{n-1}]
is a combination of
F_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
with
2g^{\prime}-2+n^{\prime}<2g-2+n
. We get such an equation for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus K_{r,s}
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. By symmetry, this means that the constraints uniquely fix all
F_{g,n}[m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}]
with at least one entry in
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\setminus K_{r,s}
recursively in terms of
F_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}[m_{1},\ldots,m_{n^{\prime}}]
with
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
2g^{\prime}-2+n^{\prime}<2g-2+n
.

What remains unfixed by the constraints is the reduced descendant potential, i.e. the
F_{g,n}[m]
with
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in K_{r,s}
. In other words, given the reduced descendant potential, the
\mathcal{W}
-constraints uniquely fix recursively the full descendant potential.
∎
