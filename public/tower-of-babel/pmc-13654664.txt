Human–artificial intelligence collaboration in emergency medical services: from diagnostic algorithms to shared decision support
Authors: Hang A Park
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly expanding across health care and emergency medical services (EMS), with applications in emergency dispatch, electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation, triage, risk prediction, and destination selection [1,2]. Recent studies have reported diagnostic and predictive performance comparable to, and in some cases better than, conventional methods [3]. AI-assisted ECG interpretation has reduced inappropriate catheterization laboratory activations, and retrieval-augmented large language models have shown potential for improving the consistency of prehospital triage [4–6]. Natural language processing has also been used in emergency dispatch calls to support earlier recognition of time-critical conditions, including out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [2]. These studies indicate that AI can support several clinically relevant tasks in prehospital emergency care.

As evidence of algorithmic performance accumulates, AI research in EMS is moving from the question of whether AI can predict accurately to the question of how it should be integrated into clinical decision-making. Newer systems are extending beyond disease recognition to support triage prioritization, destination hospital selection, and resource allocation [3,6]. This shift moves AI from pattern recognition toward clinical decision support. Once AI begins to inform clinical decisions rather than simply provide diagnostic information, trust, accountability, workflow integration, and human oversight become central implementation concerns [7]. A clear framework for human-AI collaboration is therefore needed to guide the responsible use of AI-enabled decision support in prehospital care.

FROM PATTERN RECOGNITION TO DECISION SUPPORT

The development of AI in EMS reflects a broader movement from algorithm-centered performance testing to clinical integration. Early applications focused mainly on pattern recognition and functioned as diagnostic support tools, helping EMS providers identify time-sensitive conditions. In this first generation of AI in EMS, the central question was, “What is happening?” Representative examples included AI-assisted ECG interpretation for detecting acute coronary syndrome and machine learning algorithms for prehospital stroke recognition [5,8]. These systems showed potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce unnecessary resource use, and facilitate earlier identification of patients requiring specialized care. In this role, AI helped identify clinically important disease patterns from the limited information available in prehospital settings.

However, identifying disease is only one part of prehospital care. After assessing a patient’s condition, EMS providers must determine transport priority, choose an appropriate destination hospital, allocate limited resources, and prioritize interventions during transport. These decisions require providers to combine incomplete clinical information with operational constraints and scene-specific context. For this reason, AI in EMS has begun to move from diagnostic support toward broader clinical decision support. Recent studies have shown that AI systems can predict injury severity, mortality risk, the need for life-saving interventions, and appropriate trauma triage pathways [7]. When incorporated into clinical decision support systems (CDSS), these predictive models can assist EMS providers with on-scene decisions. Bakidou et al. [7] illustrated this transition by developing an AI-based CDSS for trauma assessment that emphasizes explainability and workflow integration.

This development changes the practical role of AI in EMS. Rather than only identifying disease, newer systems can synthesize clinical information to support triage prioritization, destination selection, and other time-critical decisions. AI in EMS is therefore moving from the question “What is happening?” toward the question “What should we do next?” As AI becomes more directly involved in prehospital clinical decision-making, the central challenge is no longer algorithm performance alone. It is also determining how EMS clinicians and AI systems can work together safely and effectively in real-world practice.

WHY EMS NEEDS HUMAN-AI COLLABORATION

This shift toward decision support is especially relevant in EMS because prehospital care differs substantially from hospital-based practice. EMS providers often make clinical decisions under uncertainty, time pressure, limited diagnostic information, and restricted access to specialist consultation [9,10]. At the same time, they must assess patients, initiate treatment, communicate with patients and families, coordinate with receiving hospitals, and prepare for transport. These overlapping responsibilities make prehospital decision-making both operationally complex and cognitively demanding.

The prehospital environment adds another layer of complexity. Patient conditions may deteriorate rapidly, and limited space, noise, adverse weather, and scene safety concerns can interfere with assessment and treatment. Delayed recognition of a critical condition or selection of an inappropriate intervention may adversely affect patient outcomes. EMS providers must therefore make timely decisions with incomplete information and few opportunities for consultation [9].

These features make EMS a strong setting for human-AI collaboration [7]. AI should not replace clinical judgment, but it can support decision-making by supplying additional information, identifying clinically relevant patterns, and helping providers prioritize care under uncertainty [2]. For example, AI-assisted ECG interpretation may detect subtle abnormalities that could otherwise be missed [5], whereas AI-based trauma triage models can identify high-risk patients, recommend destination hospitals, and support triage prioritization [11]. These systems do not make clinical decisions independently. Instead, they produce recommendations that EMS providers can weigh against contextual information, clinical expertise, and patient-specific factors before making final decisions. In this model, AI contributes to decision support while EMS providers retain the central clinical role in prehospital care [1,7].

CHALLENGES OF HUMAN-AI TEAMING

Despite the promise of AI-based decision support, most AI applications in emergency medicine have been evaluated retrospectively, and their performance in real-world clinical settings remains uncertain [12]. Their performance with unstructured, real-time data from prehospital environments is also unclear. For advanced algorithms to function effectively in practice, they must be supported by systems that can continuously acquire high-quality, real-time data. Prehospital decision-making also depends on contextual information that structured datasets may not fully capture. Scene conditions, patient preferences, EMS provider experience, and situational awareness all influence clinical decisions [7,9], but whether these factors can be adequately incorporated into AI algorithms remains uncertain. AI recommendations should therefore support, rather than substitute for, clinical judgment, and meaningful human oversight remains essential.

Human-AI collaboration also creates human factor–related challenges. Excessive reliance on AI recommendations could erode EMS providers’ expertise and reduce the role of clinical intuition developed through field experience [2]. Automation complacency may lead clinicians to accept AI recommendations uncritically and overlook alternative diagnoses or management strategies. Frequent alerts may also contribute to alert fatigue, making providers less responsive to clinically important notifications [9]. These concerns show that implementation depends not only on algorithm performance, but also on how EMS providers understand, evaluate, and act on AI recommendations during real-world care.

Human-AI collaboration also raises legal and ethical concerns. Responsibility for errors involving AI-assisted decisions remains unresolved, and accountability may extend to software developers, EMS providers, and health care organizations [13]. Real-time transmission of audio, video, and patient health information also raises concerns about privacy, cybersecurity, and data confidentiality. These issues require legal and ethical frameworks as well as calibrated trust between clinicians and AI systems.

Trust in AI-based CDSS has consistently been identified as a key determinant of adoption and sustained use [1]. Trust is shaped by the transparency, reliability, and explainability of AI recommendations, as well as perceived risk, usability, workflow integration, and alignment with clinical judgment [14]. Training, hands-on evaluation, and confidence in system developers’ credibility are also important for building trust in AI-based CDSS. Implementation should therefore prioritize transparent AI development, comprehensive training, and real-world evaluation before widespread deployment. Trust should be treated not as a prerequisite for AI adoption, but as a product of transparent, reliable, and human-centered design.

A FRAMEWORK FOR HUMAN-AI COLLABORATION IN EMS

To translate trust into safe and effective clinical practice, the responsibilities of AI systems and EMS providers must be clearly defined. As AI becomes more involved in prehospital decision-making, boundaries are needed to ensure that technology supports rather than weakens clinical practice. Without clear boundaries, reliance on AI recommendations may create uncertainty about accountability, transparency, and ethical responsibility. A framework for how AI systems and EMS providers should interact is therefore necessary for responsible integration of AI into prehospital emergency care and for safe collaboration [9].

Within this framework, AI should generate clinically relevant options and recommendations rather than determine the course of care. EMS providers should evaluate these recommendations together with contextual information, patient preferences, and professional judgment before making final clinical decisions. They also remain responsible for communicating with patients and receiving hospitals, integrating information that AI cannot access, and weighing ethical and operational factors that influence patient management. Effective human-AI collaboration in EMS should therefore follow three principles: (1) AI should provide information and recommendations but not make clinical decisions; (2) EMS providers should retain final clinical authority and responsibility; and (3) AI-generated recommendations and human expertise should operate as complementary elements of shared decision-making (Table 1) [1,7,9]. Clearly defined responsibilities, transparent communication, and appropriate accountability are necessary if AI is to augment, rather than replace, clinical expertise in prehospital emergency care.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

To implement this framework, the next stage of AI research in emergency medicine must move beyond algorithm performance studies and examine how AI can be integrated safely and effectively into clinical practice. Sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve remain essential for validating AI systems, but these metrics cannot determine whether AI improves clinical decision-making in real-world settings. Future research should therefore examine system reliability, workflow integration, implementation feasibility, and the effects of AI-assisted decisions on patient outcomes and health system performance.

Prospective studies of human-AI interaction in clinical practice will be particularly important. In addition to measuring predictive accuracy, future research should examine how EMS providers interpret AI recommendations, when they accept or override them, and how these interactions affect clinical decisions, workflow, and patient outcomes. Successful integration of AI into emergency medicine will ultimately depend not only on increasingly sophisticated algorithms, but also on collaborative systems in which AI provides decision support and human expertise directs patient care.

CONCLUSIONS

AI in EMS is moving beyond pattern recognition and toward support for complex clinical decision-making. As AI is used more extensively in triage, destination selection, and patient management, successful integration will depend not only on algorithm performance but also on how well AI systems and EMS providers work together. Trustworthy, transparent, and human-centered systems with clearly defined roles for AI and clinicians will be necessary for realizing the potential of AI-enabled prehospital care.
