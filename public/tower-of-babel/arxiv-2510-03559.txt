Zeya Chen, Jianing Wen, Ruth Schmidt, Yaxing Yao, Toby Jia-Jun Li, Tianshi Li

Abstract
. UX professionals routinely conduct design reviews, yet privacy concerns are often overlooked—not only due to limited tools, but more critically because of low intrinsic motivation. Limited privacy knowledge, weak empathy for unexpectedly affected users, and low confidence in identifying harms make it difficult to address risks. We present PrivacyMotiv , an LLM-powered system that supports privacy-oriented design diagnosis by generating speculative personas with UX user journeys centered on individuals vulnerable to privacy risks. Drawing on narrative strategies, the system constructs relatable and attention-drawing scenarios that show how ordinary design choices may cause unintended harms, expanding the scope of privacy reflection in UX. In a within-subjects study with professional UX practitioners (N=16), we compared participants’ self-proposed methods with PrivacyMotiv across two privacy review tasks. Results show significant improvements in empathy, intrinsic motivation, and perceived usefulness. This work contributes a promising privacy review approach which addresses the motivational barriers in privacy-aware UX.



1.
Introduction

UX design choices can unintentionally cause severe privacy harms. Features created to enhance usability or social connection often produce risks far beyond their intended scope. For example, Spotify’s “Friend Activity” feature represents genuine user demand yet inadvertently enabled location tracking and emotional surveillance, transforming a social enhancement into a privacy risk
(
Spotify Community 2015
;
Perez 2022
;
Spotify 2023
;
Firm_Alfalfa_6017 2024
;
u/heylookitsme 2021
)
.
User testimonials illustrate the unintended consequences: “
After we broke up… I see her friend activity on Spotify. She only listens to music in the car, so if she is on Spotify, she is going somewhere
”
(
Firm_Alfalfa_6017 2024
)
.
Such incidents demonstrate that well-intentioned designs can cause privacy harms, and certain users are inherently more vulnerable—such as those experiencing stalking and harassment—and that these risks intensify in particular contexts, including breakups, abusive relationships, or other power-imbalanced situations.
Attending to these users who are subject to higher vulnerabilities in context is crucial to ensure that well-intentioned features do not become instruments of surveillance and harm
(
Sannon and Forte 2022
)
.

Therefore, it is an important responsibility of designers to remain mindful of these issues and to proactively detect and address them from the early stages of design, in line with the “proactive not reactive” Privacy by Design principle
(
Cavoukian et al. 2009
)
.
However, UX practitioners rarely engage in privacy-aware UX
(
Zhang-Kennedy et al. 2024
)
.
Prior research has identified multiple reasons, including limited privacy knowledge
(
Wong and Mulligan 2019
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
, competing priorities
(
Zhang-Kennedy et al. 2024
)
, low confidence in diagnosing harms
(
Li et al. 2018
)
, and lack of empathy for affected users
(
Marsden and Haag 2016
;
Zhu et al. 2019
;
Choi et al. 2025
)
. Importantly, these barriers are not simply about missing tools—they reflect a deeper problem: a low
intrinsic motivation
to prioritize privacy during design work
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
Existing methods such as compliance checklists
(
Matte et al. 2020
;
Nouwens et al. 2020
;
Graßl et al. 2021
;
Gunawan et al. 2021
;
Gunawan et al. 2022
)
, static taxonomies
(
Mathur et al. 2021
;
Bösch et al. 2016
;
Forbrukerrådet 2018
;
ForbrukerrÅdet 2018
)
, and post-hoc evaluations
(
Marsden and Haag 2016
;
Turner and Turner 2011
;
Hertzum and Jacobsen 2003
)
have proven insufficient, as they rarely connect abstract principles to the lived experiences of real users
(
Mildner et al. 2023
;
Nie et al. 2024
)
.
Addressing these gaps requires methods that motivate designers to reflect on privacy by evoking empathy toward vulnerable users and showing how seemingly routine features can produce harmful consequences.

To address this challenge, we propose using
speculative persona journeys
as scaffolds for privacy review in UX design to foster empathy for vulnerable users and enhance intrinsic motivation toward privacy-aware UX design.
Our speculative persona journey concept is informed by the classic UX design tool, the user journey map, which typically takes the form of a visual representation of user actions over time as they experience products or services, helping designers create intuitive and engaging experiences aligned with user expectations
(
Stickdorn and Schneider 2011
;
Mei et al. 2025
;
Kaplan 2023
)
.
We adapt this widely used UX tool to our context by developing a more lightweight version. Instead of illustrating a full and continuous user journey, we focus on a snapshot of the user experience where they are exposed to a privacy harm, described in a “story” format—a short vignette that provides the necessary background and highlights the harmful situation.
This design choice aligns with the low-frequency yet high-impact nature of privacy incidents.
The use of persona enables perspective-taking and fosters empathy building, which is a key driving factor for enhancing motivation
(
Oh and Roh 2022
)
.

We develop a proof-of-concept system,
PrivacyMotiv
, which leverages insights from the rich literature on the privacy tensions, responses, and costs experienced by various vulnerable populations
(
Sannon and Forte 2022
;
Vashistha et al. 2018
;
Gautam 2020
;
Quyoum et al. 2025
;
Arunasalam et al. 2024
;
Matthews et al. 2017
;
Zezulak et al. 2023
;
Knight et al. 2023
;
Aly et al. 2024
;
Das 2024
)
, as well as the social commonsense of LLMs
(
Mittelstädt et al. 2024
)
, to first distill the insights from literature into speculative personas, and then generate UX persona journey stories informed by these personas situated within the user flow designs under review.
Unlike conventional personas, PrivacyMotiv deliberately constructs privacy-vulnerable personas and speculative persona journey stories illustrating the potential harms, drawing on behavioral theories of narrative and framing
(
Kahneman and Tversky 1984
;
Tversky and Kahneman 1981
)
. By situating these relatable and attention-drawing stories within the familiar practice of design review, PrivacyMotiv reframes privacy review as a structured task that both surfaces hidden risks and fosters empathic reflection and motivation, supporting privacy-oriented design diagnosis.
With PrivacyMotiv, we conducted an initial investigation of our proposed design method, guided by the following research questions:
How does the use of LLM-generated speculative persona journeys affect (1) UX practitioners’ intrinsic motivation to proactively engage in privacy-aware design, (2) their empathy toward users experiencing unintended and unexpected privacy incidents, and (3) their ability to identify concrete design problems and propose actionable redesign suggestions?

We conducted a series of within-subjects studies with professional UX practitioners (
N=16
) to evaluate PrivacyMotiv in privacy review tasks.
Specifically, participants were placed in a hypothetical scenario where they conducted privacy reviews for a new app feature. We first asked them to describe their usual design practices and then selected the approach they said they would use in their real work. We refer to this as the
self-proposed method
and use it as the baseline for comparison with PrivacyMotiv.
Our study used a counterbalanced design with two fictional app features, inspired by real-world designs that have led to unintended privacy harms, to mitigate order and carryover effects.
Our results showed that PrivacyMotiv significantly improved empathy, intrinsic motivation, and perceived usefulness, compared to the self-proposed method, highlighting gaps in the current status quo of practices and demonstrating that the speculative persona journey method is a promising approach to bridging them.
PrivacyMotiv also improved privacy review outcomes: it enabled a higher volume of findings (42% more identified problems and 56% more proposed solutions than the self-proposed approach), revealed more specific issues that led to concrete improvement proposals, and achieved broader coverage across the Privacy by Design principles.

The professional designers confirmed that current professional design review practices largely overlook privacy altogether, underscoring the value of integrating structured privacy reviews into everyday UX work.
They exhibited optimistic attitudes about adopting a tool like PrivacyMotiv, envisioning it as a useful addition to the regular UX workflow to augment their professional judgement.
The lightweight format also makes it suitable for integration into current design review practices, which are usually frequent, short, and informal.
Finally, we observed a secondary educational effect: PrivacyMotiv broadened participants’ conception of privacy from a relatively narrow and rigid view (e.g., “
it’s basically about what permission you’re giving
”) to a more contextual perspective that considers concrete harms and the lived consequences for users and bystanders.

This paper contributes to the usable privacy and UX design research by:

•

Proposing a novel privacy review approach that transforms UX user flows into speculative persona journeys, enabling better problem identification and design suggestions by enhancing empathy and motivation.

•

Developing an LLM-powered system that functions as a privacy review tool and generates a library of privacy-vulnerable personas to help transfer the insights from privacy research on vulnerable populations into practices.

•

Conducting within-subjects studies with professional UX practitioners (
N=16
) to evaluate the efficacy of the proposed method, which yielded promising results demonstrating significant improvements in motivation and empathy compared to the self-proposed methods, and informing guidelines for integration into the design process as well as directions for future improvements.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Privacy Design Support for Practitioners

There has been research on software developers’ privacy understanding
(
Li 2023
;
Prybylo et al. 2024
)
and tools
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2021
)
while less on UX designers.
Research has found that practitioners’ ignorance is caused and supported by limitations of awareness and understanding, along with insufficient tools to effectively implement
Privacy by Design (PbD)

(
Danezis et al. 2015
;
Alhazmi and Arachchilage 2021
)
.
PbD’s seven foundational principles
(
Cavoukian et al. 2009
)
have been influential in shaping both policy discussions and conceptual models for building privacy-preserving systems.
However, they are often abstract and difficult to operationalize in early-stage design processes. Existing methods such as Hoepman’s privacy eight design strategies
(
Hoepman 2014
)
and engineering frameworks like LINDDUN
(
Wuyts et al. 2020
)
provide structured approaches to privacy threat modeling and mitigation but are largely aimed at system architects and security engineers, rather than interaction designers or product teams.

This gap in tool support for designers leaves PbD under-implemented in practice and contributes to a continued misalignment between user expectations and actual system behaviors. Our work seeks to address this gap by providing designers with a set of interactive, context-aware tools that support the integration of privacy concerns directly into the design process, thereby making the principles of PbD more actionable and effective in real-world applications.

2.2.
The Empathy Deficit in Design Practices

Empathic design is a cornerstone of user-centered practice, with methodologies like Design Thinking encouraging designers to "step into the user’s shoes" through techniques like personas and journey mapping
(
Kaplan 2023
;
Buchenau and Suri 2000
)
. Despite the widespread adoption of these methods, a significant gap persists between theory and practice. The "imagined users" central to empathic design often oversimplify complex realities and fail to represent perspectives beyond a designer’s immediate context, particularly regarding privacy harms that affect non-typical user group
(
Marsden and Haag 2016
;
Turner and Turner 2011
)
. Consequently, empathy is often confined to the initial discovery phase of design, with little application to the auditing and reviewing stages where privacy risks become most apparent.

This empathy deficit is not an individual failing but a systemic challenge. Fortunately, research also suggests that empathy is malleable and can be cultivated to promote prosocial behavior
(
Taylor et al. 2019
)
. Building on frameworks that position empathy as the cornerstone of proactive privacy support
(
Srinivasan and San Miguel González 2022
)
, our work uses an empathy-driven approach to address the motivational barriers in UX design. Rather than focusing on blame or compliance, our approach uses speculative narratives
(
Wong 2021
)
to help designers “experience potential privacy violations from users’ perspectives before they occur
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
.” By generating contextually rich personas and scenarios, we aim to bridge the empathy deficit and motivate designers to recognize and mitigate potential privacy harms proactively.

2.3.
Privacy Research on Vulnerable and Marginalized Populations

The third wave of security and privacy, inclusive security and privacy, aims to design mechanisms that accommodate diverse characteristics, abilities, needs, and values, ensuring underserved populations can protect their privacy and security
(
Wang 2017
)
.
This shift has led to a growing body of research in the HCI and usable security and privacy communities that investigates the unique challenges faced by marginalized and vulnerable populations. These studies span a wide range of user groups, including individuals in resource-constrained regions
(
Vashistha et al. 2018
)
, sex-trafficking survivors
(
Gautam 2020
)
, minoritised ethnic communities
(
Quyoum et al. 2025
)
, refugees facing online toxic content
(
Arunasalam et al. 2024
)
, survivors of intimate partner abuse
(
Matthews et al. 2017
)
, people with disabilities
(
Zezulak et al. 2023
)
, older adults
(
Knight et al. 2023
;
Aly et al. 2024
;
Das 2024
)
, and more.
A recent comprehensive review
(
Sannon and Forte 2022
)
surveyed privacy research from 2010 to 2020 across HCI, communication, and privacy-focused venues, introducing the “Privacy Responses and Costs” framework, highlighting the tensions in protecting privacy in marginalized contexts, emphasizing the need for more transparent and inclusive methodological practices.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate a substantial and growing body of literature addressing the security and privacy needs of diverse vulnerable and marginalized user groups. However, this body of work often remains disconnected from mainstream system design and deployment. There remains a critical need to transfer the knowledge and guidelines from this research into real-world systems and practices. This gap at the intersection of usable security & privacy and inclusive design motivates our work.

2.4.
LLM-powered Tools for UX Design Review

There is a growing body of literature exploring the use of LLMs for UX design.

Ahmed and Imran 2025
has shown that off-the-shelf LLM conversational agents, such as ChatGPT and Gemini, have been used throughout the design life cycle.
Research has also explored customizing design tools powered by LLMs.
For example,
Yang et al. 2025
proposed GenieWizard, an LLM-powered IDE plugin that can analyze multimodal app skeleton and identify missing app features.

Feng et al. 2025
proposed Canvil, a Figma widget that operationalize designerly adaptation for exploring LLMs as a design material.

Duan et al. 2023
built an LLM-based heuristic evaluation plugin for
Figma, which designers can use to evaluate their UI mockups.
However, relatively little research has addressed the challenge of using LLMs to tackle harmful or unintended behaviors in design.
A closely related work is the Farsight system
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, an in-situ interactive tool that helps people identify potential harms in the AI applications they are prototyping.
Their study demonstrated a positive effect on users’ awareness of AI harms, while also acknowledging the challenges of fostering sustained motivation.
In our work, we build on this spirit by exploring the use of LLMs to diagnose harmful UX design choices, specifically in the context of privacy review.
A key contribution is our empathy-driven approach, which introduces the speculative persona journey story method and shows significant improvements in designers’ empathy and intrinsic motivation to adopt privacy-aware UX design.
Our method further expands this line of research and has the potential to be applied beyond privacy-related issues.

3.
PrivacyMotiv Design and Implementation

The disconnect between abstract privacy principles and the lived experiences of vulnerable populations, who often face real-world consequences of privacy violations is a critical gap in privacy design.
PrivacyMotiv
is a speculative UX review assistant tool created to bridge this gap by fostering critical awareness among designers of how digital systems can inadvertently violate the privacy of vulnerable individuals. Hence, the first design goal of PrivacyMotiv is:

D1:

Center on the diverse privacy needs of vulnerable populations.

The design of PrivacyMotiv is directly informed by the need to address the core motivational barriers preventing practitioners from engaging in privacy-aware design. Our approach targets the three key components of intrinsic motivation as outlined in Self-Determination Theory
(
Ryan and Deci 2000
)
: connectedness, competence, and autonomy, by translating them into concrete design goals for our system.

To foster a sense of
connectedness
with users who are often overlooked, our second design goal is to:

D2:

Foster empathy and increase users’ motivation to engage with privacy through contextualized and relatable user interactions.

To build designers’
competence
in identifying and understanding privacy violations and to empower designers and enhance their sense of
autonomy
, giving them the specific evidence needed to advocate for change, our third goal is to:

D3:

Make the relationship between design choices and harms visible and traceable for UX practitioners.

PrivacyMotiv System Overview

The target users of PrivacyMotiv are UX design practitioners.
PrivacyMotiv analyzes UX design user flows with vulnerability-centered personas and generates speculative persona journey stories that reveal potential harms to vulnerable users (Figure
1
).
The system operates in three stages. First, we generate vulnerability-centered personas (Section
3.2
). These personas capture established vulnerability contexts based on domain-specific literature and can be reused across different application explorations.
Second, we construct contextual scenarios and user journey narratives (Section
3.3
), which are generated iteratively by pairing a persona with specific combinations of user flows corresponding to application features.
These stories embody the dynamic layer of the system: they change depending on which flows are paired and reveal the situated harms experienced by particular vulnerable users.
Finally, we implement visual storyboards with annotated flows (Section
3.4
), which translate user flows into low-fidelity (lo-fi) diagrams to communicate insights in a format more closely aligned with designers’ review practices.

3.1.1.
Example Usage Scenario

Alice, a professional UX designer, is tasked with conducting a privacy review of the early-stage design for a new social feature to be launched in the app’s next version.
In prior workflows, such reviews typically happened internally within the UX team, or in cross-functional meetings with product managers, researchers, and engineers. These sessions often focused on usability, aesthetics, and feature completeness, with privacy considerations only occasionally mentioned. Without a structured approach to considering privacy from the user’s perspective, Alice found it difficult to predict how real users might feel exposed or harmed by a design, especially for users whose privacy needs differ significantly from her own. While some privacy frameworks and guidelines were available, they were often too vague, legalistic, or difficult to apply in the early stages of prototyping. As someone without prior experience in privacy, she decided to try out
PrivacyMotiv
to help with this task.

Using
PrivacyMotiv
, Alice begins by selecting from a set of personas representing a range of user vulnerabilities and privacy-sensitive concerns. She selects Maya, a 30-year-old gender non-conforming outreach coordinator whose high online visibility makes her vulnerable to targeting and online abuse. Maya carefully manages her digital presence to balance safety and advocacy. The system presents a narrative harm scenario grounded in the early-stage design of the same social feature Alice is reviewing. In the scenario, Maya posts a protective comment during a livestream, which is then paraphrased and repeated aloud by the streamer, leading to her handle being exposed on-screen. The archived video, along with persistent comments and metadata, later circulates in hostile online spaces, resulting in unwanted attention and harassment.

Alice then explores several lo-fi user flows of the design under review on the right side of the same screen, consisting of a sequence of interface screens and system responses.
The selected flows are corresponding to the narrative harm scenario constructed by PrivacyMotiv, which demonstrates how the app feature might function in practice, grounding the story in specific design elements.

PrivacyMotiv
supports Alice trace how certain interactions, UI components, or system behaviors may have contributed to the harms illustrated in the scenario by annotating them on the lo-fi user flows.
For example, annotations highlight where accidental disclosures may have occurred, such as the app’s prominent display of granular location and timestamp on a publicly accessible incident page, without offering a privacy mode for sensitive contexts.
By connecting the narrative to its underlying design flow,
PrivacyMotiv
enables Alice to reason about privacy risks at the level of specific design decisions, surfacing concerns she might have overlooked in a traditional design review and fostering greater empathy for the affected users.

Vulnerable populations face distinct and often disproportionate privacy harms that are frequently overlooked in mainstream design and policy processes
(
McDonald and Forte 2022
;
Sannon and Forte 2022
)
. These risks challenge normative assumptions about what privacy is and who it is for, underscoring the need for approaches that foreground the diverse needs of at-risk users from the outset of design
(
McDonald et al. 2020
)
. The vulnerability-centered personas are a core component of
PrivacyMotiv
to promote empathetic understanding of privacy vulnerabilities (
D2
) across diverse identities typically excluded in mainstream UX design (
D1
).

While persona based methods have long been used in HCI and UX research to communicate user needs and contexts, emerging work increasingly explores the use of LLMs to assist or fully automate persona generation. LLMs demonstrate good social commonsense and match or even outperform humans in selecting socially appropriate responses
(
Mittelstädt et al. 2024
)
. Studies show that AI-generated personas can be perceived as more informative and consistent than human-crafted ones, though they may also lean toward stereotypical representations, revealing both the promise and the need for greater emphasis on fidelity and representation when using LLMs for persona creation
(
Lazik et al. 2025
)
. In parallel, other studies highlight that the personas generated through human-AI collaboration can be more representative and empathy-evoking, thus helping user researchers understand target users’ behaviors and empathize with them
(
Shin et al. 2024
)
. This presents an opportunity to scale the creation of detailed, plausible personas.

In our work, we define a
persona type
as a representative user category that captures key dimensions of vulnerability shaped by identity, life circumstances, systemic marginalization, and related factors. Each persona type represents a user group, such as person with physical disability, gender non-conforming individual, or elderly individual with cognitive decline.
Drawing on the
Privacy Responses and Costs
framework by
Sannon and Forte 2022
, we define each persona as a structured profile comprising three key components:
basic demographic information
,
contextual vulnerability details and privacy concerns
, and
privacy responses and consequences
.
Each instantiated persona reflects its type by detailing attributes such as the individual’s name and age, technology comfort level, privacy awareness level, and the types of personal information they seek to protect. Furthermore, each persona details the specific privacy tensions experiences, their behavioral responses, and associated costs or consequences. This structured representation ensures that each persona reflects plausible lived experiences while surfacing critical design-relevant insights into how privacy harms manifest in the lives of vulnerable users.

Given both the opportunities and the limitations of LLM-based approaches, we developed an iterative human-AI collaboration approach for persona construction.
We began by reviewing and synthesizing prior research that defines and categorizes vulnerable populations
(
Limantė and Tereškinas 2022
;
Rukmana 2014
;
Numans et al. 2021
)
, from which we developed a custom vulnerability taxonomy (Table
4
).
We then compiled lists of ‘responses to privacy threats and their costs and consequences’ and ‘high-level privacy-related tensions and trade-offs’ for vulnerable populations synthesized from prior research
(
Sannon and Forte 2022
)
.
Using these two resources, we prompted LLMs to ideate a list of persona types aligned with the taxonomy and to generate initial structured profiles that included detailed demographic information, along with the relevant privacy tensions, responses, and costs drawn from the pre-compiled list.
Next, for each persona type, we conducted a focused literature search for studies addressing the specific vulnerabilities, further expanding the associated privacy tensions, responses, and costs. This process was facilitated by LLMs for information extraction and reformatting from the papers, followed by human verification (see Figure
2
,
Output1
).

3.3.
Speculative Persona Journey Story Generation: Contextualizing Privacy Tensions and Harms

3.3.1.
User Flows as Input

Figure 3.
Three formats of user flows (conceptually equivalent).
(A)
A traditional text-based task flow drawn in Figma.
(B)
A JSON-formatted user flow manually converted from the text-based version to improve model interpretability.
(C)
A traditional low-fidelity wireframe flow, offering a more visually intuitive representation for designers.

To ground the speculative user journey in the app design, we use user flows, a common artifact for representing interaction logic. In our framework, we distinguish between features, functions, and user flows. A feature is a higher-level capability in the app (e.g., WeMusic’s
Friend Activity
). Each feature consists of multiple functions, which are specific user tasks supported within that feature (e.g., viewing friend activity, adding friends via Facebook connection, starting a private session, or removing friends). Each function can be decomposed into multiple user flows, where a user flow represents a possible linear path a user can execute to accomplish the function.

User flows vary in fidelity and modality, ranging from diagrams (e.g., in Figma, Figure
3
A) to lo-fi (Figure
3
C) or hi-fi prototypes in design practices, but they share core elements. We define a user flow format that captures these elements and enables integration into multiple stages of the design process. The JSON representation (Figure
3
B) defines a user flow with a unique
flow_id
and an ordered list of
steps
. Each step includes fields such as
step
number,
action
(what the user does),
interface
(what is shown on screen), and optionally
system_action
(system response). The flow concludes with an
endpoint
flag and a
true_reasoning
or
false_reasoning
description of the outcome.

3.3.2.
Harm-Informed User Journey Story and Design Diagnosis Generation

We leverage state-of-the-art reasoning models (e.g., GPT-5) by specifying an analysis procedure to the LLM. Given a persona and a designated sequence of app functions, each comprising multiple user flows, the model generates a user journey grounded in the design, together with a design diagnosis that identifies privacy-violating elements.

The process begins with the overarching goal of identifying situations that may cause privacy harms, guided by a privacy harm taxonomy
(
Citron and Solove 2022
)
.
To achieve this goal, it first enriches the persona with a short identity sentence that included their gender, occupation or role, and how these attributes connected to their vulnerability group.
The identity sentence also contextualize their tech comfort level by explaining how their daily experiences shaped their familiarity with technology (e.g., Eva is a 16-year-old girl and high-school student who is facing online bullying from classmates. She has high tech comfort because she moderates Discord servers and edits videos for school clubs, making her confident with app settings but still vulnerable to social risks). Next, for each function in the sequence, the LLM is prompted to select a single user flow that best aligns with the unfolding story context. This structured combination of persona and function-level interactions serves as the foundation for generating the scenario-based user journey narrative, where the LLM simulates how that particular individual might realistically interact with the system. Each resulting story reflected the persona’s original privacy concerns and responses while demonstrating how specific interface elements and design decisions within the app could lead to privacy harms (
D3
).

The output is a structured narrative annotated with specific fields (Figure
2
,
Output2
): the user’s motivations, the sensitive information potentially leaked, the specific steps within each flow that cause the leakage, the UI design problems at problematic steps, and the resulting harms and consequences. This format emphasizes not just what was leaked, but how the harms unfold over time through settings like interface defaults, hidden system actions, and gaps between user expectations and system behavior. By focusing on the process rather than just the outcome, the system encourages people to move from abstract, policy-level discussions to more human-centered reflections on privacy.
We present a walk-through of one example generated journey story in
Appendix A
.

3.4.
UX of PrivacyMotiv: Making Design Consequences Visible and Relatable

3.4.1.
Personal Selection and Analysis Result Presentation

Upon launching the tool, users are allowed to choose from a set of speculative personas. This respects their agency and preferences, while also encouraging greater engagement and empathy by letting them explore populations that feel most relevant or compelling (
D2
).
The persona preview displayed on this page includes details about their specific vulnerabilities such as refugees, older adults, and gender non-conforming individuals, as well as the types of personal information they care about most, including health data, location, financial status, and more.

PrivacyMotiv displays the analysis results based on the selected persona types on the next page.
We foreground the detailed persona because designers typically begin their process by clarifying who the user is. This step establishes the human context necessary for empathetic engagement and helps frame the subsequent analysis.

A core mechanism of our system is the visual presentation of privacy harms through annotated user flow storyboards. Each user flow is rendered as a low-fidelity UI sequence with step-by-step annotations, enabling designers to trace specific interaction points where privacy violations occur (
D3
). These annotations are generated as part of the output of the user journey story (see
Section 3.3.2
), including specific interface elements, default settings, and interaction patterns that contribute to harm. They directly signal to designers how privacy can be gradually violated during seemingly normal usage.

Annotations highlight problematic design decisions through color-coded callouts that identify specific interface flaws and their privacy implications. This visual mapping enables designers to see precisely how interface choices lead to privacy consequences. The system provides zoom functionality to allow designers to examine interface details closely, and supports pan/drag navigation for comprehensive flow exploration. Each annotation panel uses color coding to distinguish between user actions (blue) and design flaws (orange).

For example, in Eva’s case (see Figure
5
), the flow titled
“Enjoying private listening via Private Session”
reveals that the interface provides no visible timer, warning, or countdown to indicate session expiration. After six hours, Private Session ends automatically without notifying Eva. The specific steps in this flow illustrate three key design flaws: (1) the hidden session timer, (2) default resumption of sharing without user consent (sharing turns back ON), and (3) the lack of per-follower privacy controls, enforcing an all-or-nothing sharing model. The system makes these harms are clearly linked to specific steps and interface choices within the specific flow, emphasizing that privacy violations may arise from default settings and overlooked design, not just malicious intent.

3.5.
Implementation

3.5.1.
App Implementation

PrivacyMotiv is implemented as a web app built with React (TypeScript) using Vite for bundling and development. We designed the app to be lightweight and accessible through standard web browsers without requiring installation. Its core functionalities include persona generation and selection, persona-specific exploration of privacy-invasive technology use journeys, and design diagnosis embedded in lo-fi user flows. All functionality is implemented on the frontend;
As a proof-of-concept system, all user flows used as system input for design analysis are currently pre-generated and hard-coded for the user studies.
We envision that with more engineering work, the app can be easily extended to support a dynamic upload of user flows following our domain-specific format to allow for on-demand analysis of any early-stage UX design.

3.5.2.
Persona Generation

We used
gpt-o4-mini
to generate initial personas (Appendix
F.1
), with structured outputs as described in Section
3.2
.
To ensure contextual relevance and empirical grounding, one author then conducted targeted literature reviews for each persona to refine and supplement the initial LLM-generated personas.
We created 20 personas in total in the current system, reflecting diverse demographic and risk contexts.

3.5.3.
User Journey Story and Harm Analysis Generation

Based on each generated persona, we prompt
gpt-5
to produce structured user journey narratives as described in Section
3.3.2
. These narratives are generated using a standardized prompt template (Appendix
F.2
) that integrates persona information and user flow details.
In the current system prototype, these narratives are pre-generated and embedded for consistency during user studies. Future versions of the system are intended to support real-time harm analysis on dynamically uploaded user flows.

4.
User Study: Evaluating PrivacyMotiv’s Impacts through Privacy UX Review

To evaluate the effectiveness of
PrivacyMotiv
in enhancing UI/UX designers’ intrinsic motivation for privacy-aware design through empathy building, we conducted a within-subjects study with UI/UX designers. Our study investigates the following main research question:

How does the use of LLM-generated speculative persona journeys affect UI/UX design practitioners’ intrinsic motivation to proactively engage in privacy-aware design by fostering empathy toward users affected by unintended and unexpected privacy incidents?

We explore this question from the following four aspects, each represented by an evaluation question (EQ):

•

EQ1 (Empathy):
How does
PrivacyMotiv
influence designers’ empathy toward vulnerable users affected by privacy incidents?

•

EQ2 (Motivation):
What is the impact of
PrivacyMotiv
on designers’ intrinsic motivation to proactively engage in privacy-aware design?

•

EQ3 (Review Outcomes):
Compared to their self-proposed review methods, how does
PrivacyMotiv
affect designers’ review results of identified privacy-invasive design problems and alternative design suggestions?

•

EQ4 (Usage Patterns):
How do designers use and perceive about
PrivacyMotiv
, and what are their intentions to adopt it in real-life professional practices?

4.2.
Study Design

We conducted a within-subjects study to evaluate our LLM-powered tool,
PrivacyMotiv
, against designers’ self-proposed methods. A within-subjects design was chosen to control for individual differences in designers’ baseline empathy, privacy awareness, and professional experience, which could otherwise confound the results
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

Each participant were asked to complete two privacy review tasks: one for each condition on a different application scenario assigned for each task. We define a
privacy review
as a similar activity of UX design review but focus on identifying potentially privacy-invasive design problems and proposing alternative, privacy-preserving suggestions.

To mitigate order and learning effects, we counterbalanced the order of both the conditions and the application scenarios across four participant groups:

•

Group 1:
Self-Proposed Approach (App A)
\rightarrow

PrivacyMotiv
(App B)

•

Group 2:

PrivacyMotiv
(App A)
\rightarrow
Self-Proposed Approach (App B)

•

Group 3:
Self-Proposed Approach (App B)
\rightarrow

PrivacyMotiv
(App A)

•

Group 4:

PrivacyMotiv
(App B)
\rightarrow
Self-Proposed Approach (App A)

4.2.1.
Experimental Conditions

Baseline: Self-Proposed Approach

As structured "privacy reviews" are not yet standard practice in many UX design workflows (as supported by our findings in Section
5.2.2
), we established a realistic baseline condition. Participants were asked to conduct the privacy review using any approach that felt natural to their professional experience. This included applying design principles, considering user needs, or drawing from general work experience (specific approaches are detailed in Section
5.2.3
). This flexible approach allows us to capture the diversity of privacy-related reasoning currently found in industry practice.

Intervention: PrivacyMotiv

In the intervention condition, participants used our LLM-powered tool,
PrivacyMotiv
, to conduct their review. The tool provided materials designed to foster perspective-taking and empathy, including speculative user personas, journey narratives illustrating potential feature misuse, and analyses of potential privacy harms. These materials were generated with an LLM pipeline (see Section
3.3
for the detailed generation process) and were intentionally not manually edited. This allowed us to authentically test the LLM’s generation capabilities within the study’s context.

4.2.2.
Fictional Applications and Materials

We developed two fictional applications for the privacy review tasks, a practice consistent with prior research
(
Li et al. 2021
)
. This approach allowed us to control complexity, ensure both apps contained similar types of privacy concerns, and ground the tasks in concrete, relatable examples of how social transparency features can raise unexpected privacy issues. Furthermore, using fictional apps helps mitigate potential biases stemming not only from participants’ prior attitudes and experiences with real applications, but also from the LLM’s pre-existing knowledge of them. Each application was presented as a set of early-stage design materials, including a design brief and lo-fi wireframes in Figma.

The fictional applications were designed for
ecological validity
, grounded in popular real-world applications to be relatable and reflect genuine user needs; this ensures the designs represent well-intentioned features valued by users, not malicious ones. For
privacy relevance
, they were inspired by features with documented histories of causing at least ten unintended privacy incidents. To mirror industry practice where UX designers typically work on adding single features to established products rather than building entire apps from scratch, the task scope was limited to a
single feature
. To ensure
comparable complexity
, each feature included four functions. Finally, the apps were from different
domains
to reduce carryover effects.

Based on these criteria, we chose two real-world reference points - Spotify’s
Friend Activity
and Citizen’s
OnAir Livestream
to inspire the design of two fictional apps used in our study:
App (A): WeMusic—Friend Activity
, and
App (B): NeighborNet—LIVE+
. These applications reflect well-intentioned designs with strong user value but also carry inherent privacy risks. Participants interacted with each app’s key social features, designed to surface tensions between utility and privacy. Detailed descriptions of both apps are provided in Appendix
B
. Ultimately,
WeMusic
and
NeighborNet
served as realistic and comparable cases for our study, representing common industry scenarios where well-intentioned features inadvertently create unintended privacy implications.

4.3.
Procedure

All study sessions were conducted remotely via Zoom and lasted approximately 90 minutes. Participants used their own devices, simulating a realistic remote work environment. The fictional application materials were provided in a shared Figma file, and
PrivacyMotiv
was accessed through a web browser. To minimize fatigue, a short break was provided between the two main tasks. All sessions were audio and video recorded with participant consent for later analysis.

The study followed a four-step procedure for each participant:

(1)

Onboarding and Interview (20 minutes):
Prior to the study, participants were asked to complete a pre-session survey to collect basic demographic and professional information. The live session then began with a semi-structured interview to understand their professional backgrounds, familiarity with user flows, and experiences with design reviews in practice.

(2)

Privacy Review Tasks (25 minutes
\times
2):
Participants were sequentially assigned to one of the four counterbalanced groups. They performed two 25-minute privacy review tasks: one for each fictional application and under each condition.

Scenario Setting.

For each task, we framed a realistic scenario by asking participants to imagine they were a UX designer working on a new, high-priority feature (Friend Activity or LIVE+) for an established product (WeMusic or NeighborNet). This framing mirrors a common industry practice where designers are tasked with designing features based on user research or business requirements, with the well-intentioned belief that they are serving user needs.

Task Execution.

During the task, participants followed a think-aloud protocol to verbalize their reasoning and findings. They were required to document their findings, at least one privacy issue and one corresponding design suggestion, as free-form comments in a provided questionnaire. In the
self-proposed approach
condition, participants could use any methods or external tools they chose and were asked to describe their process. In the
PrivacyMotiv
condition, they were instructed to explore at least three different persona journeys to ensure sufficient engagement with the system.

(3)

Inter-Task Questionnaires (5 minutes
\times
2):
After each of the two review tasks, participants completed a brief questionnaire measuring their empathy toward users, intrinsic motivation of privacy review, and the perceived usefulness of the approach they had just used.

(4)

Post-Study Debrief Interview (20 minutes):
The session concluded with a final semi-structured interview. We asked participants to compare their experiences and preferences between the two conditions, discuss any differences in how they considered users, their attitude and understanding of privacy, and share their general thoughts on incorporating privacy considerations into design practice.

This study protocol was approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). Participants received $75 as compensation for the 90-minute session, a suggest rate consistent with compensation practices in prior CHI studies involving professional designers
(
Palani et al. 2022
;
Solyst et al. 2025
)
.

4.4.
Data Collection and Analysis

We used a mixed-methods approach, collecting quantitative data from inter-task questionnaires and qualitative data from participants’ written review outcomes and think-aloud protocols.

Inter-Task Questionnaires (EQ1, EQ2, EQ4).

After each of the two review tasks, participants completed a questionnaire containing scales adapted from established instruments, all using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 4 = Neutral, 7 = Strongly Agree).

To measure
empathy (EQ1)
, we used a 6-item scale adapted from two frameworks to capture three key dimensions. From the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI)
(
Davis 1983
)
, we selected items to measure two core, other-oriented dimensions:
cognitive empathy
, operationalized through the Perspective-Taking subscale (imagining the user’s standpoint), and
affective empathy
, operationalized through the Empathic Concern subscale (feeling concern for user harm). To this, we added
Accountability
from HCI empathy probes
(
Luusua et al. 2015
)
to measure how empathy translates into a desire for design action.

For
intrinsic motivation (EQ2)
, we adapted a 7-item scale from the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI)
(
Ryan et al. 1983
)
. We chose four subscales to assess key aspects of this experience:
Interest/Enjoyment
as the core measure of intrinsic motivation,
Perceived Competence
to assess self-efficacy in the privacy review task,
Value
to capture the activity’s perceived meaningfulness, and
Perceived Importance
to gauge participants attitude towards privacy review.

To measure
perceived usefulness (EQ4)
, we developed a 5-item task-level scale. Drawing on the System Usability Scale (SUS)
(
Brooke et al. 1996
)
and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
(
Davis 1989
)
, we created a shorter measure tailored to the privacy review context. The scale was designed to capture participants’ immediate judgments of each approach’s perceived utility and their intention to adopt similar practices in professional design work.

Review Outcomes (EQ3).

To analyze the written review outcomes, we developed a rigorous qualitative coding scheme. For
specificity
, we adapted Garrett’s five planes
(
Garrett 2022
)
, a model classifying user experience across five levels of abstraction:
Strategy
(L1, high-level concept or feature decision),
Scope
(L2, functions or options that support features),
Structure
(L3, user flows and timing),
Skeleton
(L4, page layout and placement), and
Visual
(L5, specific UI components and text). We applied a “highest-level” decision rule, coding each item at its most concrete plane. For example, “Place a red color
highlighted toggle on the Share screen” is coded as L5, not L4. For
thematic content
, we adapted the seven principles of Privacy by Design (PbD)
(
Cavoukian et al. 2009
)
: Proactive not Reactive; Privacy as the Default; Privacy Embedded into Design; Full Functionality; End-to-End Security; Visibility & Transparency; and Respect for User Privacy. Following a “best-fit” assignment rule, each issue was coded with the single principle that best captured its core. The complete codebooks are in the appendix (Tables
5
and
6
).

To ensure reliability, two authors first independently coded 25% of the data. This process yielded substantial inter-rater reliability for both schemes: Cohen’s Kappa was
\kappa=0.76
for the specificity levels and
\kappa=0.71
for the PbD themes. Following this, one author coded the remaining data. The full coded dataset was then reviewed by two other authors, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion among all three to ensure the final accuracy of the results. Finally, we analyzed the coded data quantitatively by comparing the frequencies and distribution of codes between the two conditions.

Think-Aloud Protocols (EQ1–4).

Finally, to supplement all findings, two researchers conducted a qualitative thematic analysis of the think-aloud recordings.
Findings were discussed iteratively with the entire team.
No inter-rater reliability was calculated because the goal was to identify new themes rather than seeking agreement and validating a taxonomy.
This analysis provided in-depth context and explanatory insights into participants’ survey responses and behaviors across all four evaluation questions.

Themes of Outcomes

Our thematic analysis, structured by the PbD framework
(

First, the tool helped practitioners focus more deeply on the user-centric experience, particularly how design choices manifest directly in the interface design. This aligns with our earlier finding that PrivacyMotiv prompts more concrete, visual-level feedback. The largest increases in problem identification occurred in
Visibility & Transparency
and
Respect for User Privacy
. By following the journeys of vulnerable personas, the under-represented user that typical design processes might overlook, practitioners were confronted with confusing interfaces and a lack of user agency. This led them to identify tangible issues.

Second, PrivacyMotiv surfaced themes entirely overlooked by the self-proposed approach. It was solely responsible for identifying problems related to
Privacy as the Default
(7 instances) and
Full Functionality
(2 instances). For example, multiple participants (eg. Proactive and Preventative
problems (11 problems), it failed to generate corresponding solutions (only 2 suggestions). “When onboarding, maybe there can be a way to tell the user that if you are a minor, this needs to be used under the supervision of your parents.”
This highlights how the tool not only broadened the thematic scope but also helped bridge the gap between identifying abstract risks and proposing tangible, preventative controls.

Beyond counts and themes, we observed qualitative shifts in participants’ reasoning. In the Self-Proposed condition, several participants expressed
uncertainty
about whether an issue was truly privacy-related. P9, for example, noted that a confusing UI was a problem but hesitated before concluding it was a privacy issue because it could lead to unintentional sharing. This suggests that without a guiding framework, practitioners may struggle to delineate the boundaries of privacy.

In contrast, PrivacyMotiv appeared to foster more confident and articulate reasoning. Participants often used the persona’s story to explain
why
a design flaw constituted a privacy harm. The tool also did not stifle
originality
. While it provided a scaffold, some of the most novel suggestions extended beyond the prompts provided. For instance, expanding beyond PrivacyMotiv’s design diagnosis, P14 proposed an anti-stalking feature that would
“notify users of frequent profile viewers,”
P8 suggested a speculative feature to
“automatically blur faces in live streams.”
and P9 suggested a
“voice-changing feature”
for anonymous broadcasting. These instances highlight that a structured tool like PrivacyMotiv can serve as a springboard, rather than a replacement, for practitioner creativity.

5.2.
Qualitative Results

5.2.1.
State of design review in UX industry

We begin by summarizing the status quo of design review as described by the participants.
Overall, the current review ecology is frequent, fast, and experience-based; yet largely informal and oriented toward delivery risks rather than privacy; this sets the stage for examining how privacy is (and is not) reviewed in practice.

Design Review as a Routine and Highly Valued Practice

13 out of 16 participants reported weekly or bi-weekly cadences, often with multiple touchpoints in a single week. Names vary by company (e.g., “design review,” “crit/critique,” “playback”), but the purpose converges on rapid, multi-perspective feedback:
“sometimes there are two or three design reviews in a week—it’s how we move work forward”
(P10). Reviews span stages (discovery through pre-launch) and involve UX designers, researchers, and PMs by default, with develop team or market team joining occasionally as needed. Regardless of label, frequent review is a core practice and a natural insertion point for methodological scaffolds.

Informal Formats of Design Review

12 out of 16 participants described casual screen-share walk-throughs with live Q&A or Figma comments (e.g.,
“peers just come and offer different perspectives,”
P4); 4 participants cited structured methods (e.g., Nielsen’s heuristic checklists, IBM’s “video+feedback” playbacks), typically appearing later when higher-fidelity prototypes exist. This finding demonstrates a gap for lightweight, structured review methods for early and mid stages of a design process, not only at handoff.

Short Time per Person and Project in Frequent, Multi-Party Reviews

Across review session duration mentions, the median was about 10–30 minutes per project or per person. Participants noted
“ten-minute silent reviews”
(P4) or being allotted
“fifteen minutes for my part”
(P10). Feedback quality was often attributed to reviewer seniority (e.g., P5 finds PMs triaging quickly and provide more valuable feedbacks than peer designers), while early-mid career designers needed more scaffolding. This finding supports the appropriateness of our study’s 20-minute individual review task. It also shows that identifying issues under tight timeboxes favors tacit expertise; checklists and prompts could level the field for less-experienced reviewers.

Design Review not Focused on Privacy

Internal designer review sessions concentrate on visual consistency and design-system adherence, interaction clarity, accessibility, usability, and even microcopy tone, like P9 states
“we always start from the [UI component] system—spacing, tokens, components—before anything else”
. Cross-functional reviews foreground feasibility and sequencing (sprint fit, tech-debt), performance/reliability, analytics impact (conversion, retention), and launch risk (e.g.,
“engineering asks, ‘can this ship in the next sprint, and what’s the risk?”
(P1);
“in playback we talk numbers—why retention or conversion is low”
(P7)). Leadership/external touchpoints probe strategic fit, value proposition, and brand alignment. This finding summarizes the common focus of review norms, and also reveals that privacy is rarely the lead lens and tends to surface only when prompted by compliance or specific risk concerns.

5.2.2.
Privacy Review for UX

Prior privacy review experience.

When asked whether they had ever conducted a design review
focused on privacy
, all sixteen answered
no
. Nevertheless, 11 of 16 participants described encountering privacy-related gates in their broader workflows, most often in cross-functional forums (e.g., stakeholder/engineering playbacks, and monthly org update meeting) rather than in their typical design review sessions.
For example, P9 recounted discovering privacy issues only
after
harms occurred via user complaints to the marketing team; after feedback surfaced in an organization-wide meeting, the design team was prompted to retrofit the design. Another common pattern was reliance on PM or senior co-workers’ guidance, either because PMs had deeper industrial knowledge or because they relayed leadership/content requirements; as P10 put it,
“my project manager can see a lot (potential privacy-invasive issues) … because they have connections with the legal team and have worked [at this company and on similar projects] for a long time.”
A few participants from large companies (e.g., P11, P7) noted the presence of independent legal and ethics departments, but these stakeholders remained peripheral to routine design review; occasional privacy/security awareness workshops were optional and oriented toward general literacy rather than project-specific UX decisions. Overall, participants had engaged with privacy concerns only superficially and typically
reactively
and through others’ lines of responsibility, rather than proactively identifying privacy risks within design review.

Attitudes toward privacy review

Before using PrivacyMotiv, a majority (11/16) endorsed privacy review in principle, yet many treated responsibility as diffuse or located elsewhere in the organization.
Five out of 16 framed it as a PM, legal, or security remit or
“not front of mind”
for UX design reviews, e.g.,
“[Privacy review is not the responsibility of designers because] I need to go through policies and requirements first”
(P11),
“I think about ethical reviews more; I don’t think about privacy reviews as much”
(P13). After trying PrivacyMotiv, most participants (13/16) reported intent to integrate privacy into routine reviews, but emphasized conditional adoption: they seek lightweight scaffolds that fit fast cadences—concise prompts/checklists anchored to existing design artifacts (such as lo-fi wireframes) and typical timeboxes (10-30 minutes). As one put it,
“I’ll start thinking more about these design choices around privacy”
(P15); others noted expertise gaps that PrivacyMotiv suggestions could offset:
“with my own hat, I cannot think about any [vulnerable] use cases … I will probably work with my teammates; we can … brainstorm”
(P16). In summary, participants’ attitudes towards privacy review shifted from delegated/diffuse ownership to personally owned and actionable; participants converged on an ideal privacy review as a brief, timeboxed (
\leq 30
min), established theory/principle-guided, and work as an add-on to existing design review practices. PrivacyMotiv, seeded with persona-based scenarios and concrete harm cues, and aligned with current review rhythms, offers a pragmatic starting point for privacy review in UX.

Understanding of privacy

Before using PrivacyMotiv, participants tended to describe privacy narrowly in compliance- or attribute-centric terms such as data handling, permission prompts, and a few salient attributes (especially location), e.g.,
“it’s basically about what permission you’re giving to the app”
(P7);
“privacy in my mind is … users’ location”
(P1). Several equated privacy with
data
per se (
“previously, I think privacy is only about the data-sharing function, like sharing what data to which company,”
P16). After trying PrivacyMotiv, understandings broadened along three recurring themes: (a) 12 of 16 participants articulated concrete harms and lived consequences when describing their identified privacy-invasive design; (b) 10 of 16 participants reasonated their detection with specifc context and audiences, including who sees what, when, and through which linkages (e.g., follower syncs, cross-surface exposure, re-sharing, persistence); and (c) 9 of 16 participants emphasized vulnerable cases and bystander perspectives when answering how their identified problems are related to privacy. Several explicitly contrasted pre- and post-use understandings; for example, P16 explained that after using PrivacyMotiv they could recognize how
“bad design will be harmful
and that
“privacy is no longer [just] a tech issue.”
In summary, participants shifted from rules and attributes toward scenarios, actors, and longitudinal effects; PrivacyMotiv’s persona-based narratives made
who/what/when
concrete and catalyzed a more user-centered, situational understanding of privacy.

5.2.3.
Privacy Review Approaches and Adoption Intention

This section details the results from our analysis of 16 participants’ approaches to privacy reviews, their interactions with the PrivacyMotiv tool, and their perspectives on adopting such a tool in their professional workflows.

Self-Proposed Approaches

When tasked with conducting a privacy review without a dedicated tool, the 16 participants’ primary methods fell into four categories:

•

Bystander’s Perspective (6 participants):
The most common approach, where participants acted as detached professional evaluators. They methodically scanned design files, applying their knowledge of UX heuristics and design principles to identify flaws in user control and interaction patterns.

•

“Imagine Myself as the User” (5 participants):
These participants relied on personal empathy, simulating a user’s perspective to, as P7 put it, “gut check what it feels like” and identify potential privacy discomforts based on their own experiences.

•

External AI Assistant (4 participants):
These participants used conversational AI like ChatGPT or Claude.ai. P1 used ChatGPT-4o by providing screenshots of all four user flows alongside a detailed prompt; P14 used ChatGPT’s voice function to describe the app and ask for general guidance; P6 and P12 used Claude.ai by screenshotting selected single interface or user flow they felt were problematic to get targeted feedback.

•

Collaborative Walkthrough (1 participant):
One participant preferred an interactive approach, asking the moderator to present the design and engaging in a real-time discussion to uncover issues.

These self-proposed approaches offered several
advantages
that align with existing team practices. Participants noted their methods felt efficient for identifying obvious flaws and fit well within their regular, lightweight design review process. P15, for instance, described a real-world process where he would ask the designer questions on the fly during a live walkthrough, a method that is both fast and collaborative. This ad-hoc nature provided a sense of ownership and responsibility; as P15 reflected on his self-proposed approach for our study’s review task, “
the pressure was a lot more… I felt more responsible that I need to find every bit of thing that can be a privacy issue
”.

However, these approaches also revealed significant
drawbacks and blind spots
. The most critical issue was a lack of context for identifying harms beyond their personal experience, particularly for vulnerable or non-typical users. P12 acknowledged this limitation, stating, “
I might not have considered some users
” without specific personas to guide her. This led to a feeling of being unsystematic and unprepared, which P16 described the privacy review task as “
solving a math problem, but without any clue
”. Participants using external AI also faced challenges; while the AI provided a starting point, they found it difficult and time-consuming to provide sufficient context. P1 noted the friction in his process: “
I need to take a screenshot… then copy paste into my prompt… it’s a lot of work
”. This suggests that while practitioners are resourceful, their default methods for privacy review are often ad-hoc and lack the structure needed to confidently identify a wide range of potential harms.

PrivacyMotiv Usage

In contrast to self-proposed approaches, participants’ interactions with PrivacyMotiv followed more structured patterns as they learned to navigate the tool to find the most salient information. We observed three primary behavioral patterns:

•

Narrative-First Approach:
The majority of participants (11 of 16) prioritized understanding the context of the privacy harm before examining the UI. They spent most time on the Persona Info, Story Description, and Harm & Consequence sections to build empathy before examining the UI. As P7 noted, the story “
is very helpful for a designer… to feel the users
”.

•

UI/Flow-First Approach:
Other participants (5 of 16), particularly those with a stronger design focus, started by analyzing the Persona Usage Flow and the Design Diagnostics. This approach was more about deconstructing the mechanics of the failure, with P4 explaining, “
[Privacy] harms & consequences come from the flows
”.

•

Triage Approach:
Many participants from both groups also adopted an efficient triage strategy, using Persona Type labels (e.g., “Domestic Violence Survivor”) to jump directly to the Harm & Consequence section to quickly grasp an issue’s severity.

Strengths & Advantages

Participants appreciated several key aspects of PrivacyMotiv, highlighting its ability to build empathy, provide structured guidance, and efficiently surface overlooked privacy dimensions. The most praised feature was the rich, narrative context provided by the personas and stories, which participants found highly
educational and empathy-building
. As P7 noted, the story “
is very helpful for a designer… to feel, have empathy
”. This perspective shift was potent; P15 remarked that the tool heightened his “
privacy spidey sense,
” while P16 noted it transformed his view of a “
bad design
” into a “
harmful
” one. This structured approach was also seen as highly
fast and efficient
, saving the “time-consuming” work of creating scenarios from scratch. Furthermore, participants found the structure of the
User Flow with Integrated Diagnostics
to be particularly
designer-friendly
. The ability to see a critique directly mapped to a specific UI element in a flow was described as clear and intuitive. P1 found it “
very easy for me to identify where… the problem happened in the UI flow
”, while P11 compared the diagnostics to “
tickets that’s been filed
”, framing the tool’s output in the context of a familiar design and development workflow.

Improvement Suggestions

While the tool was valued, participants also identified several areas they hoped to improve, including the realism of content, tool usability, and the AI’s underlying process. Many participants felt the personas represented
extreme edge cases
, leading them to question their realism. P1 described some stories as “a little bit extreme”. Interestingly, this initial skepticism was often resolved after reading the detailed story, which convinced participants that such vulnerable users, while non-typical, could be real and were likely being overlooked by their company’s focus on mainstream personas. Another common critique was the
high content density
, with several participants found the Persona and Story sections overly text-heavy, Some also noted a degree of
redundancy
between Story & UsageFlow or Privacy Tensions & Harms sections, feeling they could infer the harms directly from the story description without needing a separate section. A few participants expressed a deeper, methodological
skepticism regarding AI
. P3 worried that over-reliance on such a tool could erode designers’ critical thinking skills. Finally, over half the participants (9 of 16) expressed a strong desire for
persona customization
, recommending the ability to input their own company’s typical personas to better integrate privacy reviews into their standard, business-focused design workflows.

Implementation & Adoption Recommendations

In general, participants were optimistic about adopting a tool like
PrivacyMotiv
, envisioning it as a way to
augment, not replace
, their professional judgment. They emphasized using it to expand their thinking while still needing to “verify information on my own”. The most common suggestion was to use the tool during the early stages of design, after creating lo-fi wireframes but before committing to high-fidelity work, in order to “speed up this process”.

Practically, participants proposed embedding the tool’s output into existing practices to keep usage low-friction. They described several concrete integration points: (1) using the report as a
pre-critique artifact
attached to design reviews; (2) serving as a
lightweight gate or checklist
in sprint rituals; (3) linking reports in tickets and PRDs to formally document privacy trade-offs; and (4) integrating the tool directly into design software via a
Figma plugin or template
.

Beyond these workflow integrations, participants highlighted the tool’s collaborative potential. A key pathway involved (5)
pairing the tool with dedicated privacy and legal experts
for escalations. As P11 noted, this could “
make our collaboration with the company’s privacy experts more efficient
”. Finally, participants saw the tool’s narrative output as a powerful asset for (6)
supporting dialogue with stakeholders
, arguing that the concrete stories could make abstract harms tangible and help convince product managers and leadership to prioritize privacy issues they might otherwise be overlooked.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
Speculative Persona Journeys: An Empathy-Driven Approach to Unintended Privacy Harms

Our results validate that
designers face challenges in identifying and addressing problematic designs that can cause unintended privacy harms, even when they are explicitly tasked with improving privacy and approach the task with a sense of responsibility and good intentions (
Section 5.2.2
).
This finding extends prior literature that examines responsible, ethical, and privacy-respectful practices among design practitioners, which has primarily focused on cases where manipulative design patterns are intentionally employed and violate users’ values
(
Gray et al. 2018
)
.
It highlights more nuanced challenges in fostering the adoption of ethical design practices in real-world contexts.

Lacking motivation is regarded as a major barrier to the adoption of responsible design practices
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Rakova et al. 2021
;
Madaio et al. 2020
)
.
Our results show significant improvement in empathy and motivation (
Section 5.1.1
), demonstrating the potential of our proposed speculative persona journey method to scaffold privacy review and address this gap.
We emphasize that the empathy-driven approach is the core factor enabling this success outcome.
The design of
PrivacyMotiv
is directly informed by the contextual and edge-case nature of privacy incidents, and we see potential to extend this approach to broader challenges in responsible design.
Specifically, in comparison with
Wang et al. 2022
, which also proposes an LLM-powered tool for informing practitioners of potential AI harms, our method takes a step further by constructing a set of personas, user journey stories, and harm analyses situated within detailed design choices.
This approach moves beyond
raising awareness
to
provoking both cognitive and affective empathy
with prospective users who are especially vulnerable to privacy harms, thereby addressing the motivational gaps observed in prior work.

6.2.
From Vague to Concrete: Empowering UX Designers to Improve and Negotiate Privacy

We found
PrivacyMotiv
helped designers identify more privacy problems and generate more solutions.
Moreover, these identified problems and solutions were more specific and provided more comprehensive coverage of the Privacy by Design (PbD) principles (
Section 5.1.2
).
Underlying these improvements in review outcomes, we also observed a more nuanced understanding of privacy, encompassing both about the theoretical concept and its practical applications in the design review process (
Section 5.2.2
).
Our findings both echo and address the narrow understanding of privacy
(
Li et al. 2018
)
and a skewed application of privacy design principles
(
Tahaei et al. 2022
)
among software developers.

We specifically emphasize that achieving privacy-aware design is a challenging task, not only because of individual barriers such as limited awareness or a lack of suitable techniques, but also because designers often experience a lack of agency as their design solutions are constrained by high-level strategic decisions over which they have no control.
For example, a typical type of L1 Strategy level comment we observed, which was a major category in the self-proposed condition, concerned viewing the entire feature as inherently privacy-invasive (e.g., P15 remarked, “The concept of ‘friend activity’ itself encourages surveillance.”).
Because these proposals are skewed toward high-level problem identifications and solution concepts, designers may feel powerless, perceiving the decision to implement such features as already fixed and leaving little room for improvement.
In contrast, with the help of
PrivacyMotiv
, designers identified additional alternatives at more specific levels, such as user flows, layouts, and the choice of icons or wording.
This both generates design ideas that directly address the issues and provides concrete proposals for communicating with other stakeholders, all while staying aligned with general privacy design principles.

6.3.
Integrating Proactive Privacy Review in UX Workflows

In
Section 5.2.1
and
Section 5.2.2
, we found that design reviews are a common practice, while privacy is not a primary focus in this process, echoing prior research
(
Zhang-Kennedy et al. 2024
)
.
When designing
PrivacyMotiv
, we made intentional design decisions to support a natural integration of the method and the tool into regular design workflows.
Our study results provide encouraging evidence supporting this approach.
First, our participants, representing a range of roles in the UX design industry, expressed strong interest in our tool, requesting for access to the personas after the study, and consistently highlight the value of integrating
PrivacyMotiv
-like tools into the early stages of UX workflows, envisioning it as a way to augment, not replace, their professional judgement (
Section 5.2.3
).
Second, our participants confirmed that the key design choices of our system, such as using user flows as the design representation, showcasing harm analysis, and integrating annotated design diagnosis on top of the lo-fi prototypes, are well-aligned with their common practices.

We outline a few directions to further enhance and extend the current proof-of-concept
PrivacyMotiv
system to become a regular component in the UX workflows.
The first theme concerns designers’ need to customize the personas, specifically, to conduct similar analyses based on their own company’s typical personas so that the results better align with their business interests.
We consider this a meaningful direction for improving our persona journey story generation pipeline, shifting focus beyond population-specific vulnerabilities to a more situational understanding of vulnerabilities.
In fact, this perspective aligns with recent proposals from the HCI and CSCW research community on addressing diversity and inclusion, which advocate a socio-ecological approach that encourages researchers to “look beyond static and stigmatizing definitions of vulnerability — instead, focus on the situations”
(
Tang et al. 2025
)
.
Such an approach also helps reduce the risk of perpetuating othering and stereotypes.

Another theme concerns the need for scalable methods to generate accurate user flows in textual format from the actual artifacts designers create, which contain the same content but are less convenient for LLMs to analyze.
We have observed the importance of this at two stages.
First, when developing the
PrivacyMotiv
system, we noticed that specifying all possible user interactions in accurate and neutral form being critical to general unbiased stories and avoid hallucinations.
This is a tricky task because underspecification in the user flows may lead to inaccurate projections of behaviors that do not align with the design (i.e., hallucinations), while overspecification, such as providing examples of user data, may bias the AI to focus only on a narrow set of scenarios.
Second, we observed that four participants actively sought help from off-the-shelf conversational AIs such as ChatGPT and Claude.ai in the self-proposed condition but failed to receive useful suggestions. One major reason is that they entered the system design either through screenshots or through verbal descriptions.
Future research should further investigate and evaluate this problem to ensure a smooth integration into UX practitioners’ workflows.

6.4.
Toward Translating Research Recommendations into Design Practice

Despite a wealth of privacy research promoting consideration of vulnerable users, adoption of these ideas in real-world UX design remains limited. Although our methods show promising results, our approach still has limitations that highlight fundamental challenges requiring further attention.
As we observed in the persona selection phase of
PrivacyMotiv
, some participants articulated the selection rationales as focused on those who seemed to resemble their envisioned profiles of “typical users,” and chose not to select certain personas because they did not align with those expectations.
We consider this as an interesting cognitive barrier to the effectiveness of our proposed intervention that requires additional research and mitigating methods.
Furthermore, some participants expressed skepticism regarding the AI-generated stories.
We view this as a healthy form of awareness, since AI systems can indeed be prone to hallucinations and stereotypical behaviors.
We see the main value of
PrivacyMotiv
as providing a starting point for behavior change, evidenced by improved empathy and motivation, rather than serving as an end in itself.
Future research should investigate what comes next: supporting designers in thinking critically and conducting dedicated research to refine and apply privacy-improving ideas in practice, thereby sustaining the momentum.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we present a novel approach to structured privacy design review, leveraging LLMs to generate speculative persona journeys that enhance UX designers’ empathy and intrinsic motivation to proactively identify and address design issues that could cause unexpected privacy harms.
We designed, implemented, and evaluated a proof-of-concept system, PrivacyMotiv, which embodies this idea, with 16 UX professional practitioners completing two privacy review tasks in a within-subjects study.
Our results showed that using PrivacyMotiv significantly improved designers’ empathy toward vulnerable users, motivation to conduct privacy-aware UX design, and perceived usefulness of the method compared to their self-proposed approaches.
Furthermore, using PrivacyMotiv also improved the design review outcomes in terms of the specificity and coverage of the identified problems and proposed solutions.
Our results establish the importance of providing UX designers with structured support for privacy and demonstrate a promising direction for addressing this challenge through an empathy-driven approach.

Appendix A
Example Generated User Journey Story

We present an example to demonstrate how the process-oriented narrative structure is critical for achieving our design goal of uncovering hidden consequences.
Eva as a 16-year-old high school student facing online bullying represents a persona type designed to reveal the privacy risks experienced by teens navigating social technologies under conditions of peer harassment
(
Balas et al. 2023
;
Rizza and Pereira 2013
;
Obaidat et al. 2023
)
. Within
PrivacyMotiv
, this narrative serves as the foundational story that justifies Eva’s inclusion as a vulnerable group and seeds the specific privacy traits and concerns that will be surfaced through the interactive persona experience. Her story begins with a legitimate need to protect herself through a privacy feature. She activates the platform’s “Private Session” mode to mask her listening activity, assuming this will keep her behavior hidden from peers. However, because the session expires silently after six hours (a design choice surfaced in step 3 of the flow
“Enjoying private listening via Private Session”
), her activity becomes visible again without her realizing it. A former bully, still present in her friend list, sees her late night listening and school related playlists, triggering ridicule and emotional distress. The harms she faces include: (1) reputational harm from peers mocking her late-night listening and school-related playlist, (2) psychological distress from the breach of what she believed was a private space, (3) autonomy loss resulting from the app overriding her privacy expectations, and (4) relationship strain due to reactivated bullying dynamics among classmates. Ultimately, Eva responds by withdrawing from the platform, losing access to the social connection and music discovery features she once relied on.

This example illustrates how
PrivacyMotiv
uses structured narratives to enable designers to empathize with privacy harms as consequential experiences shaped by app behaviors and individual vulnerabilities (
D2
). The system’s approach of mapping design level decisions (e.g., silent expiry, persistent sync, poor feedback) to specific harm outcomes allows designers to see how protective features can become sources of harm through the lens of vulnerable users (
D3
). These structured scenario narratives make privacy harms legible as lived consequences of design interactions over time.
