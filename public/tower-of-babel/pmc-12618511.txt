Heavy metal accumulation risk and source analysis in soils of antimony mining areas
Authors: Jianyang He, Huimin Zhang, Ke Yang, Yingmei Li, Li Bao, Liu Gao, Naiming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Co-contamination of heavy metals from antimony (Sb) mining poses significant threats to soil quality and human health. Although many studies have examined Sb sources, few have provided quantitative source apportionment combined with population-specific risk assessment. This study examined a representative Sb mining area in southwestern China through an integrated approach. The methodology combined Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF), Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS), the geo-accumulation index (Igeo), the enrichment factor (EF), the modified Nemerow pollution index (INI), and health risk assessment models. Results indicated a mean Sb concentration of 125.61 mg·kg−1, nearly 50 times the regional background value (2.50 mg·kg−1), with pronounced spatial variability (CV = 246.97%). PMF analysis revealed three major sources: regional mixed sources (36.8%), natural geological sources (30.1%), and industrial point sources (33.1%). MCS results suggested high ecological risks for Cd and Sb, with associated probabilities of 94.43% and 83.45%, respectively. The probability of non-carcinogenic risk (HI > 1) in children reached 85.61%. The total carcinogenic risk (TCR) exceeded acceptable thresholds for all individuals, with children showing higher susceptibility. Natural geological sources accounted for 57.9% of carcinogenic risk and 62.3% of non-carcinogenic risk in children. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted pollution control and remediation strategies to mitigate both ecological and human health risks in Sb mining areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23789-z.

Introduction

Soil heavy metal contamination, characterized by its invisibility, persistence, and bioaccumulation, poses a serious threat to global ecological health and sustainable development1,2. Mining areas, often involving extensive land occupation and continuous heavy metal release during extraction, have suffered severe and persistent soil co-contamination3,4. Prolonged mining leads to continuous heavy metal emissions, causing accumulation in surface soils, alteration of soil physicochemical properties, and transfer into biological cycles through agricultural activities5. A survey by China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment reported that up to 33.4% of soil sampling points near mining areas exceeded heavy metal thresholds6. Due to their toxicity and persistence, these metals migrate and accumulate within the soil–crop system, degrade cultivated land quality, and pose health risks through the food chain, becoming a major bottleneck to sustainable development in mining regions7,8. Specifically, antimony (Sb) and co-occurring heavy metals, including arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and chromium (Cr), can induce severe health effects, encompassing both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic outcomes, as well as developmental disorders. These elements are recognized globally as environmental pollutants under strict regulatory control and remain a critical concern in environmental science and public health9. The spatiotemporal distribution of soil heavy metal pollution in mining areas is influenced by mining intensity, topography, geological background, and hydrological conditions. However, limited quantitative data on exposure pathways introduce uncertainties in source apportionment and risk assessment10. Therefore, an integrated assessment system, combining multidimensional indicators (e.g., spatial distribution, ecotoxicity, health risk) with probabilistic models (e.g., Monte Carlo simulation), is essential for designing targeted remediation strategies in Sb mining areas.

Antimony (Sb), a toxic metalloid, is widely released through mining and smelting, leading to significant contamination of surrounding soils and crops11. Although Sb naturally occurs in rocks, anthropogenic activities—particularly ore mining, smelting, and industrial emissions—substantially increase its concentration and mobility12. In China, the Xikuangshan mining area in Hunan Province—the world’s largest Sb deposit—exhibits soil Sb levels hundreds to thousands of times above background values, frequently co-occurring with As, Cd, and Pb. This has led to heavy metal accumulation in crops and contamination of drinking water, emphasizing the environmental and health risks associated with long-term Sb mining13. In contrast to Hunan, the study region in Yunnan exhibits unique geological and hydrogeological characteristics. Sb mineralization in this region occurs in Devonian siliceous rocks and is structurally controlled by folds and faults. Moreover, the area belongs to the Guangnan karst region, where well-developed carbonate aquifers and subterranean rivers facilitate metal migration and redistribution. This karst environment, coupled with prolonged multi-metal accumulation, amplifies the spatial heterogeneity of soil contamination and elevates the ecological vulnerability of the region14,15. Variations in soil pH further influence Sb speciation and mobility, resulting in contamination patterns distinct from those in non-karst areas such as Hunan. Despite prior studies on single-metal pollution, systematic investigations of multi-metal co-contamination, spatial heterogeneity, and risk assessment under karst conditions remain limited. Most existing studies rely on site-specific surveys or extrapolate risk thresholds from single-metal standards, introducing uncertainties in ecological and health assessments16. In recent years, quantitative source apportionment methods, such as Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF), have been increasingly applied. However, their results are highly sensitive to the selection of local factors and the underlying geological background, limiting their applicability and validation in specific regions—particularly in karst environments like the present study area17,18.

Risk assessment in antimony (Sb) mining areas faces considerable technical challenges, limiting its application in targeted management. Traditional models—such as the geo-accumulation index (Igeo), enrichment factor (EF), and potential ecological risk index (RI)—often overlook inter-individual variability in exposure parameters, which may result in biased health risk estimates19. Furthermore, most studies treat ecological and human health risks separately, lacking an integrated framework that simultaneously incorporates source contribution, quantitative risk assessment, and identification of priority pollutants20. example, Li et al. applied Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS)—a probabilistic risk assessment method that quantifies uncertainty and variability through random sampling and statistical computation—to evaluate health risks of potentially toxic elements in agricultural soils, generating separate probabilistic estimates for adults and children21. However, their study did not identify the specific pollution sources responsible for these risks, nor did it establish a quantitative link between source apportionment and health risk. Similarly, Al-Rubaye et al.22 demonstrated, using MCS, the significant health risks posed by potentially toxic elements in street dust, but did not clarify their source-specific contributions. Although integrating geochemical indices with receptor models, such as PMF, can improve source identification by providing statistical estimates of source contributions23, the results remain strongly dependent on model assumptions and parameter settings, and are highly sensitive to factors such as the number of output factors, potentially causing significant errors in source contribution estimates and model fitting24. In complex multi-source contamination scenarios typical of Sb mining areas, the pathways through which different sources contribute to health risks remain poorly understood25. Notably, recent international studies have proposed integrated “source apportionment–health risk” frameworks, enabling precise quantification of health risks attributable to specific pollution sources. For instance, Čakmak et al. conducted a pioneering case study in the Zajača Sb mining area, Serbia, by coupling the PMF receptor model with MCS. Their study not only identified five major sources of As, Pb, and Sb in soils (e.g., historical smelting, current battery recycling, and geological background), but also quantitatively attributed the probabilistic contributions of each source to non-carcinogenic (HI) and carcinogenic (TCR) risks in both children and adults, by integrating PMF-derived source contribution rates with health risk models26. This approach effectively bridges the gap between “identifying pollution sources” and “quantifying source-specific health risk,” offering a novel methodological paradigm for precision environmental management. However, in Chinese Sb mining areas, systematic studies coupling probabilistic health risk assessment with source apportionment, while simultaneously addressing both ecological and human health dimensions, remain limited. Considering the abundant Sb reserves and the long mining history in the study area, addressing this knowledge gap is especially critical.

Therefore, to address these knowledge gaps and reinforce the scientific foundation for soil pollution control, this study investigates the geochemical characteristics and environmental behavior of heavy metals in the soils of a representative karst Sb mining area. The novelty and key contributions of this study are highlighted as follows: (1) It develops an integrated framework that couples PMF for quantitative source apportionment with MCS for probabilistic health risk assessment, enabling quantitative allocation of health risks to specific pollution sources; (2) It specifically addresses the unique geochemical conditions of karst environments, examining the interactions of Sb with co-occurring metals and the effects of soil pH on their mobility and associated risks, often overlooked in non-karst settings; (3) It provides a holistic assessment by simultaneously integrating source identification, ecological risk, and human health risk within a single, comprehensive evaluation framework. The objectives of this study are: (i) elucidate the geochemical characteristics, spatial distribution, and migration behavior of Sb and associated metals; (ii) identify and quantify contributions from natural and anthropogenic pollution sources; (iii) assess ecological and human health risks, accounting for variability in exposure; and (iv) identify priority pollutants to inform targeted and cost-effective remediation strategies. By integrating source apportionment with risk assessment, this study aims to provide a scientific foundation for pollution prevention, risk-based soil management, and coordinated remediation in karst Sb-contaminated areas.

Materials and methods

Overview of the study area

This study was conducted in a typical Sb mining area in southwestern China, located within the Guangnan karst region of Yunnan. The area contains Sb reserves accounting for approximately 12% of the national total. It extends from 105.347° to 105.386° E and 23.972° to 24.013° N, covering 822.89 hectares, with elevations ranging from 535 to 1337 m above sea level. The climate is humid subtropical monsoon, with a mean annual temperature of 23.5 °C and annual precipitation of 840–1200 mm, over 70% of which occurs between May and October, indicating distinct wet and dry seasons. Geomorphologically, the terrain is high in the southwest and low in the northeast, with NW–SE trending ridges and valleys, deeply incised gorges, and well-developed gullies.

The bedrock is predominantly carbonate rocks with localized silicification and baritization, and ore bodies are mainly composed of stibnite, pyrite, cervantite, senarmontite, and kermesite, with an annual Sb production capacity of nearly 10,000 tons.

Geological setting of the study area

The study area is underlain by sedimentary rocks of Devonian and Quaternary age, forming a hydrogeologically complex system with aquifers and aquitards. Loose rock pore aquifers are present in alluvial–proluvial and residual slope deposits, mainly consisting of sandy clay, sandy gravel, and clay with shale fragments. Bedrock fissure aquifers occur in sandstones, siltstones, and siliceous rocks, while carbonate karst aquifers are developed in limestones and dolomitic limestones, showing variable water-bearing capacity. Aquitards, mainly shales interbedded with limestone, restrict groundwater flow.

Hydrologically, the area belongs to the Xiyangjiang River basin, with surface water supplied by rivers and seasonal gullies. Groundwater occurs as loose rock pore water, bedrock fissure water, and karst water, generally of HCO₃-Ca or HCO₃-Ca·Mg type with low mineralization.

Sample collection, preparation, and analysis

Soil sample collection

Sampling sites were arranged in a checkerboard pattern considering topography, soil distribution, and the extent of the mining area. At each site, five subsamples were collected from the top 0–20 cm layer, thoroughly mixed, and quartered to obtain a representative composite sample, following the Chinese national standard HJ/T 166–2004 (Soil Environmental Quality—Sampling Technical Requirements). A total of 77 surface soil samples were collected (Fig. 1). All samples were labeled, sealed, and transported to the Yunnan Soil Fertility and Pollution Remediation Engineering Research Center for further analysis.

Sample preparation and analysis

Sample pretreatment

Soil samples were air-dried at room temperature in a well-ventilated environment, gently disaggregated, and sieved through 1.00 mm (for pH measurement) and 0.149 mm (for heavy metals) meshes, following HJ/T 166–2004.

Soil pH determination

Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil-to-water suspension according to ISO 10390:2005. Briefly, 10.00 g of soil (< 1.00 mm) was mixed with 25 mL deionized water, shaken for 5 min, equilibrated for 30 min, and measured using a calibrated pH meter (OHAUS a-AB41pH-B).

Total heavy metal determination

The total concentrations of Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, Cu, Sb, and As in soil samples were determined following aqua regia digestion in accordance with ISO 11466:1995 and USEPA Method 3050B, with modifications for soil matrices. Briefly, 0.2000 g of air-dried soil (< 0.149 mm) was accurately weighed into a pre-cleaned 50 mL glass digestion tube. Then, 10 mL of freshly prepared aqua regia (HNO₃: HCl = 3:1, v/v) and 10 mL of deionized water were added. The mixture was refluxed at 95 °C for 2 h with intermittent shaking to ensure complete acid–sample interaction. Aqua regia digestion dissolves most heavy metals in soils, except for those tightly bound in silicate mineral lattices27. After cooling, the solution was allowed to settle, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, diluted to 50 mL with deionized water, homogenized, and stored at 4 °C until analysis. Procedural blanks were processed in parallel.

Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, and Cu were quantified using flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (FAAS, AA-6880, Shimadzu). Calibration standards were prepared from a 1000 mg L−1 multi-element stock solution diluted with 1% HNO₃. Method detection limits were 0.019, 0.250, 0.023, 2.015, and 2.323 µg·g−1, respectively, with calibration curves showing R² > 0.999.

Sb and As were measured by atomic fluorescence spectrometry (AFS-230E, Haiguang) according to HJ 694–2014, adapted for soil matrices. Calibration standards were prepared by transferring 0, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00, 3.00, and 5.00 mL of working solution into 50 mL volumetric flasks, adding 5 mL HCl (ρ = 1.19 g·mL−1) and 10 mL thiourea–ascorbic acid solution, standing for 30 min (or in a 30 °C water bath if ambient temperature < 15 °C), then diluting to volume. Soil sample solutions were treated identically to ensure matrix consistency. Calibration curves showed R² > 0.999, recoveries ranged 85–105%, and detection limits were 0.015 µg·g−1 (Sb) and 0.040 µg·g−1 (As).

Quality control included the certified reference material GBW07451 (GSS-22), procedural blanks, and duplicate samples. Each sample and reference material was digested and analyzed in triplicate, and mean values were reported. All reagents were analytical grade, and ultrapure water was used throughout. Containers were soaked in 20% HNO₃ for at least 24 h, rinsed thoroughly with ultrapure water, and dried prior to use28.

Quality assurance and quality control analysis (QA/QC)

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) were rigorously implemented throughout the study to ensure the reliability and representativeness of the data. Soil sampling followed the Chinese national standard HJ/T 166–2004 “Soil Environmental Quality—Sampling Technical Requirements,” with a five-point sampling method combined with quartering to obtain composite samples, which were sealed in polyethylene bags. During sample preparation, soils were air-dried in a shaded, ventilated environment and sieved, while all glassware was pretreated by soaking in 20% HNO₃ for at least 24 h, rinsed with ultrapure water, and dried prior to use. Each sample, along with quality control samples, was analyzed in triplicate, and procedural blanks were included every 20 samples to monitor potential contamination. The average variation between duplicate samples ranged from 1.5% to 7.8%, which is considered acceptable according to Kwayis et al.29, demonstrating that the analytical procedures yielded precise and reliable results.

Enrichment factors

To evaluate the degree of heavy metal contamination, the enrichment factor (EF) was calculated following Mokhtarzadeh et al.35 using the formula:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}EF=\frac{{\left(\frac{{C}_{\dot{i}}}{{C}_{ref}}\right)}_{{S}_{ample}}}{{\left(\frac{{C}_{i}}{{C}_{ref}}\right)}_{{B}_{ackground}}}\end{array}$$\end{document} where Ci is the concentration of the target element, and Cref is the concentration of the reference element. Copper (Cu) was selected as the reference element due to its low coefficient of variation (CV) in the samples35. The background concentrations used for calculating the enrichment factors of Sb, As, Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, and Cu were 2.44, 17.4, 0.218, 65.2, 40, 89.7, and 46.3 mg·kg−1, respectively36。.

The classification of EF is as follows: EF < 1 indicates no enrichment; EF < 3 indicates slight enrichment; EF = 3–5 indicates moderate enrichment; EF = 5–10 indicates moderately severe enrichment; EF = 10–25 indicates severe enrichment; EF = 25–50 indicates very severe enrichment; and EF > 50 indicates extremely severe enrichment.

Accumulation of heavy metals in mining area soils

Seven heavy metals—Sb, As, Cd, Cr, Pb, Zn, and Cu—were analyzed in the soils of the study area, and the results are presented in Table 3. Soil pH ranged from 3.90 to 8.15, with an average of 6.97, indicating a generally weakly acidic to neutral condition. Compared with the background values (BG) of soils in Yunnan Province, the average concentrations of Sb, As, Cd, Cr, and Zn were elevated, whereas Pb (14.88 mg·kg−1 < 40 mg·kg−1) and Cu (41.47 mg·kg−1 < 46.3 mg·kg−1) remained below their background levels. The average concentration of Sb reached 125.61 mg·kg−1—over 50 times its background value (2.44 mg·kg−1). As (40.13 mg·kg−1) and Cd (0.75 mg·kg−1) also significantly exceeded their respective background values (Table 3).

The coefficients of variation (CV) and standard deviations (SD) revealed differing levels of spatial variability among the elements. Sb and Cd showed high variability, with CVs of 246.97% and 107.22%, respectively, suggesting considerable anthropogenic influence (Fig. 2). According to the screening values (SV) for soils with pH 6.5–7.5 outlined in the Risk Control Standard for Soil Contamination of Agricultural Land (GB 15618–2018), the average concentrations of As and Cd exceeded their respective thresholds, indicating potential environmental risks. In contrast, the concentrations of Cr, Pb, Zn, and Cu remained below their respective screening values. Since no official screening limit has been established for Sb in China, its contamination risk cannot be directly assessed under the current standard.

Discussion

Spatial heterogeneity of heavy metal contamination in mining soils

Wilding (1985) reported that a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 35% indicates high variability42, reflecting significant spatial heterogeneity in soil heavy metal concentrations43. In this study, Sb showed exceptionally high spatial variability (CV = 246.97%), far exceeding values reported for non-karstic antimony mining areas, including the Hunan tin mine (87.38–125.44%)9,18, agricultural soils (41.30%)44, and urban systems (64.44%)45. This striking discrepancy requires a mechanistic explanation. We primarily attribute this to the hydrogeological characteristics of karst terrains. Carbonate bedrock, well-developed fissure–conduit networks, and concentrated monsoon rainfall likely facilitate rapid vertical infiltration of Sb³⁺ and As³⁺, creating localized contamination hotspots (Fig. 2a). This heterogeneity is further amplified by complex geological evolution, repeated anthropogenic disturbances, and distinctive migration pathways46, collectively increasing uncertainties in ecological and health risk assessments. By contrast, in alluvial plains like the Hunan tin mine, heavy metals are mainly redistributed by river runoff, resulting in more spatially clustered patterns9. While areas near smelting sites often show only moderate variability47, pollutant dispersion in karst landscapes remains highly unpredictable. This also explains the elevated mean Sb concentration observed in this study (125.61 mg·kg−1; approximately 50 times the background level).

Regionally, Sb concentrations in the study area are lower than those in large Sb mines (Table S5), such as the Hunan tin mine (1409.34 mg·kg−1)17,18, likely due to smaller-scale mining and a shorter pollution history. However, they are markedly higher than in non-Sb regions, including the Southwest Ba mining area (2.75 mg·kg−1)48 and northwest Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (1.08 mg·kg−1)49, indicating the direct influence of local Sb mining rather than other mining or agricultural activities. For As, the mean concentration (40.13 mg·kg−1) exceeds the background levels reported in Saudi Arabia (3.78 mg·kg−1)49 and northwest Iran (7.04 mg·kg−1)50, where no local As or Sb mining occurs, yet remains lower than in abandoned As mines (119.51 mg·kg−1)16 and the Hunan tin mine (139.17 mg·kg−1)17. This suggests that As enrichment in the study area mainly results from Sb-associated co-release rather than intensive As mining.

Beyond spatial factors, the acidic soil conditions typical of the karst system likely enhance Sb bioavailability, thereby increasing ecological and health risks even at comparable total concentrations. Co-occurring elements, such as As and Cd, exhibited synergistic enrichment. As averaged 2.3 times the background level, exceeding values reported for typical polymetallic mining areas. This pattern aligns mechanistically with metallogenic geochemistry: stibnite (Sb₂S₃) commonly co-occurs with arsenopyrite (FeAsS) and, under oxidizing conditions, releases soluble Sb³⁺ and As³⁺, promoting multi-interface and multi-pathway contamination51. Elevated concentrations were also influenced by cumulative agrochemical inputs, including fertilizers and pesticides52. Although no national soil standard currently exists for Sb, its substantial enrichment warrants regulatory attention and supports incorporating Sb into future soil quality standards and risk-based management.

Soil heavy metal source contributions

Enrichment factor (EF) analysis revealed substantial differences in heavy metal enrichment across the study area (Fig. S2b), consistent with Shahab et al.53, who reported that EF variations reflect diverse heavy metal sources and heterogeneous pollution intensities. Wide fluctuations in EF values among elements (e.g., Sb CV = 237.79%; As CV = 161.73%) indicate pronounced spatial heterogeneity in anthropogenic impacts, likely reflecting the distribution of localized pollution sources. Sb showed the highest enrichment, with an average EF of 68.39, and over 90% of samples exhibited moderate to extremely high enrichment, markedly higher than other elements. This intense enrichment strongly suggests that Sb primarily originates from anthropogenic sources54. In comparison, Cd and As exhibited low-to-moderate enrichment. Cd showed moderate enrichment, likely associated with agricultural practices and industrial emissions55. Their enrichment levels were notably higher than those of Cr and Zn, indicating stronger anthropogenic contributions to Cd. Conversely, Cr, Zn, and Cu exhibited relatively low enrichment. For Zn, over 88% of samples fell into the minimal or no enrichment category. These findings align with previous studies, reflecting the dominance of natural background inputs56.

This preliminary EF-based interpretation was further confirmed through PMF source apportionment. PMF results identified Source 1 as dominated by Cd (78%) and Sb (47%), with hotspots located near the antimony processing plant, major traffic routes, and nearby villages (Fig. 3c). Cd enrichment mainly originated from traffic emissions (exhaust, tire, and brake wear), agricultural inputs, and smelting-related coal combustion, with spatial patterns closely matching the intensity and frequency of human activities57–59. Sb was primarily attributed to traffic-related emissions in mining areas, strongly influenced by high local population density, with release pathways including gasoline combustion and brake wear59. Consequently, Source 1 was defined as a “regional composite source,” driven by diverse, low-intensity, long-term human activities, resulting in diffuse and complex pollution. Source 2 was characterized by Cr, Zn, and Cu (Fig. 3c), with concentrations near geochemical background values, low coefficients of variation, and levels below agricultural land risk screening thresholds (Table 3). These elements mainly originate from parent rock weathering and pedogenic processes. Cr is ubiquitous in parent materials, whereas Zn is locally enriched in specific lithological units, reflecting clear lithological controls16,60,61. Therefore, Source 2 was classified as a natural source. Source 3 was dominated by Sb, As, Pb, and Cd, primarily distributed within the mining area and adjacent downwind regions. In the study area, Sb ores consist mainly of stibnite, frequently co-occurring with arsenopyrite and galena. Solid waste leaching, wastewater discharge, and atmospheric deposition during mining and smelting represent the primary pathways for these elements to enter the soil51,62,63. Inadequate management during ore extraction and processing can result in significant heavy metal release into the surrounding environment. Consequently, Source 3 was defined as a typical industrial point source.

In summary, EF analysis revealed distinct enrichment patterns and potential sources of heavy metals in the study area, while PMF modeling further quantified source contributions and spatial distributions. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate that anthropogenic Sb inputs and mining activities are the primary drivers, whereas natural geological backgrounds strongly control Cr, Zn, and Cu distributions. This integrated framework provides robust support for identifying pollution sources and developing targeted risk management strategies.

Ecological and human health risks

Ecological risk index (RI) analysis identified Sb and Cd as the primary contributors to ecological risk, accounting for 100% and 96.44% of the total RI, respectively. Monte Carlo simulations indicated that 7.3% of samples fell into the high-risk category (RI > 600), primarily located near mining and smelting facilities (Fig. S3), highlighting the significant ecological impact of industrial activities64,65. Although Sb is not included in current national soil quality standards, its substantial enrichment and high probability-based ecological risk (83.45% rated as “very high”) suggest considerable potential for ecotoxicological effects through food chain transfer62.

Igeo results indicated that all elements, except Cd (classified as unpolluted), ranged from moderate to extremely severe pollution (Fig. S2a). The INI values (2.8–13.2) further confirmed that the study area is under extreme pollution pressure. Notably, this contamination level far exceeded values reported in agricultural soils (PN = 0.04–1.18)66 and urban systems (PLI = 3.6)67, highlighting the amplifying effect of Sb mining on multi-metal synergistic pollution.

Cd is frequently reported as a cross-environmental driver of ecological risk, contributing, for example, 58.8% of risks in agricultural soils due to fertilizer inputs68. However, the prominence of Sb-related risk is characteristic of mining regions. Unlike Pb/Zn in urban areas, primarily derived from vehicle emissions67 Unlike Pb/Zn in urban areas, primarily derived from vehicle emissions69, Sb in this study exhibited exceptionally high probabilistic risks despite the absence of regulatory thresholds, emphasizing the environment-dependent prioritization of pollutants64,65. Furthermore, probabilistic methods demonstrated superior capacity to resolve spatial uncertainties compared to deterministic approaches. For example, while deterministic ecological risk index assessments categorized 92.7% of samples as “considerable risk” (300–600), Monte Carlo simulations identified high-risk hotspots (RI > 600) specifically near smelting facilities (Fig. S3). This refined resolution surpasses the diagnostic capability of deterministic methods used in agricultural or urban studies and is crucial for targeted remediation in heterogeneous mining landscapes.

Exposure risk assessments indicated that children are significantly more vulnerable to health hazards. Specifically, across different exposure pathways, children exhibited substantially higher hazard index (HI) values via oral ingestion and dermal contact compared with adults, while total carcinogenic risk (TCR) via oral ingestion reached 93% in adults and 100% in children, consistent with previous studies22. Regarding overall health risks, hazard quotients (HQs) for Cr, As, and Sb exceeded acceptable thresholds in children by 83.70%, 39.41%, and 30.11%, respectively, whereas in adults only As and Sb exceeded the thresholds. This disparity is attributable to children’s higher intake per unit body weight, frequent hand-to-mouth behaviors, and immature detoxification systems70,71, consistent with findings by Huang et al.16 in a decommissioned arsenic mining area in Yunnan. Carcinogenic risk assessments revealed that Pb and Cr were the principal carcinogens in children (risk levels > 1E-4), followed by As (Fig. S6). Although no cancer slope factor is available for Sb, its elevated concentrations in the mining zone raise concerns9.

Source-oriented risk assessment

Identifying pollution sources and their priority target elements is essential for effective soil heavy metal management in mining areas. Previous studies in Chinese Sb mining districts have mainly relied on deterministic models or single-source analyses9,28, often overlooking interactions among multiple sources, ecological risks, and population-specific vulnerabilities. To address this gap, the present study employed a source-contribution and risk-oriented framework.

Source apportionment results (Fig. 8a) indicated that the regional composite source (Pollution Source 1) contributed 47.4% of ecological risk, with Cd as the dominant driver72. Although the industrial point source (Pollution Source 3) contributed less (35.2%), the Sb, As, and other high-risk elements it released were highly biotoxic.

Health risk source analysis revealed that natural sources dominated both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks in children, significantly exceeding contributions from anthropogenic sources (Factors 1 and 3) (Fig. 8b). This phenomenon is relatively uncommon in studies of antimony mining areas. While international studies have mainly attributed health risks to human activities26, our study found that natural sources contributed significantly more than anthropogenic sources. This is likely attributable to the study area’s karst landscape, where carbonate bedrock, under specific geographic conditions, accelerates the weathering of primary minerals (e.g., arsenopyrite FeAsS and stibnite Sb₂S₃) and vertical migration of heavy metals, directly contaminating shallow groundwater73.

Pollution from natural sources exhibited greater persistence and broader spatial effects, whereas anthropogenic sources (e.g., industrial emissions) were more localized. Consequently, cumulative risk from natural sources at the population scale was substantial, likely due to the persistence of pollutants that are difficult to isolate or remediate74. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms require further investigation, highlighting the importance of incorporating geological context into risk assessments. In summary, this study underscores the need for differentiated risk management strategies tailored to specific pollution sources, ensuring coordinated protection of both ecological integrity and public health.

Conclusion and prospects

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of soil heavy metal pollution and associated health risks in a karst antimony mining area in southwestern China. The main pollutants identified were Sb, Cd, As, Cr, and Zn, with their spatial distribution patterns and source characteristics analyzed. Results showed significant spatial heterogeneity in Sb contamination, with concentrations at certain sampling sites exceeding regional background values. Fracture seepage and acidic soil conditions may increase the mobility of Sb–As complexes from natural sources, thereby elevating ecological risks and potential health hazards for children. This study presents an assessment framework integrating PMF source analysis with Monte Carlo simulations for the studied domestic antimony mining area, quantitatively characterizing the spatial distribution and uncertainty of multi-source pollution risks. Mining and smelting activities were the primary contributors to children’s health risks, while natural geological sources were the main drivers of regional ecological risks. Simulation results suggested a high likelihood of ecological risks from Sb and Cd, with both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks being higher in children than in adults. The study recommends incorporating Sb screening values into soil pollution risk control standards, addressing the synergistic risks of Sb–As from natural sources, establishing a groundwater heavy metal monitoring system, and implementing appropriate remediation measures, such as phytostabilization and geological passivation, based on regional geological conditions. This study offers methodological guidance and empirical evidence for soil pollution zoning and risk management in karst mining areas of southwestern China.

Future perspectives and remediation strategies

(1) Study Limitations: This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, detailed measurements of key soil physicochemical properties (e.g., organic matter, carbonate, EC, CEC) were not available, which may affect the interpretation of contaminant behavior. Second, sampling around the mining area was relatively coarse and did not differentiate land-use types (agricultural, forest, or construction land), potentially limiting the spatial representativeness of the results. Third, health risk assessments are subject to uncertainties due to the limited data and sampling coverage, and therefore the estimated risks should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies addressing these limitations will help improve the reliability and applicability of the findings.

(2) Emerging Contaminants and Research Directions: To further advance this work, future research should focus on (i) detailed characterization of soil properties to better explain contaminant behavior, (ii) refined sampling strategies accounting for land-use heterogeneity, (iii) enhanced health risk assessments based on expanded datasets, and (iv) assessment of emerging contaminants, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and radionuclides, in soil, water, and air systems. These research directions will provide a more comprehensive understanding of environmental hazards in contaminated areas.

(3) Remediation Strategies for Sb-Contaminated Soils: Targeted remediation strategies should be developed for Sb-contaminated soils in the study area and other regions of China. These strategies include phytoremediation using hyperaccumulator plants, soil stabilization with biochar or activated carbon, microbial bioremediation, and integrated approaches for Sb–As co-contamination, emphasizing field-scale application and long-term effectiveness.
