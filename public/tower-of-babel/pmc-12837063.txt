Comparative effects of high intensity interval and functional training on performance outcomes in adolescent female volleyball players
Authors: Ömer Faruk Bilici, Tuba Kizilet Topateş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVolleyball requires repeated explosive actions, agility, and technical precision, demanding contributions from both aerobic and anaerobic energy systems. Time-efficient training methods such as resistance-based high intensity interval training (HIIT) and high intensity functional training (HIFT) have been proposed to enhance multidimensional performance in young athletes. However, direct comparisons of their effects in adolescent female volleyball players are limited.MethodsThirty-two licensed female volleyball players (aged 15–18 years) were randomly assigned to a resistance-based HIIT group (n = 10), a HIFT group (n = 11), or a control group (n = 11). The training interventions lasted 12 weeks with two supervised sessions per week, in addition to regular volleyball practice. The HIIT program consisted of progressive resistance-based high intensity intervals performed at 85–95% HRmax, while the HIFT program comprised multimodal circuit-style functional exercises performed at comparable intensities (~ 85–95% HRmax). Performance assessments were conducted pre- and post-intervention and included the countermovement jump (CMJ), standing long jump (SLJ), medicine ball throw (MBT), pro-agility test, 20 m sprint, repeated sprint fatigue index (RSI), volleyball skill test, Yo-Yo IR1 distance, VO₂max, blood lactate concentration, and maximal heart rate (MaxHR). Data were analyzed using a 3 × 2 mixed-model ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests, and effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d and partial eta squared (ηp²).ResultsSignificant Group × Time interactions were observed for CMJ (F(2,29) = 9.14, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.39), SLJ (F(2,29) = 12.08, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.45), VO₂max (F(2,29) = 11.53, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.44), MaxHR (F(2,29) = 3.56, p = 0.041, ηp²=0.20), and volleyball skill test (F(2,29) = 7.44, p = 0.002, ηp²=0.34). HIFT produced the greatest improvements in explosive power (CMJ Δ=+5.15 cm; SLJ Δ=+12.82 cm), RSI (Δ=–1.56), and volleyball skill performance (Δ=+13.18 points), with large effect sizes (d = 1.3–3.2). HIIT showed relatively greater improvements in aerobic capacity (VO₂max Δ=+0.90 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) and endurance (d = 0.7–2.0). No significant between-group differences were observed for lactate.ConclusionsBoth resistance-based HIIT and HIFT proved effective in improving several physical and volleyball-specific performance components in adolescent female players. HIIT produced slightly greater gains in aerobic capacity, while HIFT showed larger numerical improvements in explosive strength, agility, and technical skill performance; however, these between-intervention differences were not statistically significant. Overall, the findings suggest that each modality offers complementary benefits, and integrating both HIIT and HIFT may provide a balanced conditioning approach for the holistic development of youth volleyball athletes.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07181031. Registered on September 04, 2025.

Introduction

Volleyball is a high-intensity intermittent sport requiring repeated explosive actions such as jumping, sprinting, blocking, and rapid directional changes, performed alongside complex technical tasks including serving, setting, and spiking [1–3]. These demands require athletes to integrate neuromuscular power, agility, and technical precision under fatigue, relying on both aerobic and anaerobic energy systems [4–6]. While aerobic capacity supports recovery between rallies and overall match endurance, anaerobic power is crucial for explosive actions and rapid transitions.

The increasing density of competition schedules and limited recovery opportunities, especially among adolescent athletes, highlight the need for efficient and sport-specific conditioning approaches [7, 8]. Traditional endurance-based training offers limited transfer to volleyball because it does not replicate the sport’s intermittent and multidirectional nature [9, 10]. Consequently, modern training methods have shifted toward high-intensity modalities that stimulate both metabolic and neuromuscular adaptations relevant to volleyball performance [11, 12].

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is widely implemented and consists of repeated bouts of near-maximal efforts interspersed with structured recovery. HIIT has been shown to enhance aerobic and anaerobic performance, repeated-sprint ability, and metabolic efficiency across youth and adult athletes [13–15]. However, HIIT often relies on repetitive locomotor drills, which may not fully capture the multidimensional physical demands of volleyball.

High-intensity functional training (HIFT) has emerged as an alternative that integrates functional, plyometric, resistance, and agility-based movements within circuit-style sessions performed at high intensity with limited rest [16–18]. Unlike HIIT, HIFT emphasizes multi-planar, task-oriented, and sport-specific motor patterns that simultaneously challenge strength, power, coordination, and metabolic capacity [19]. This multimodal structure may offer enhanced transfer to volleyball, where athletes must repeatedly execute explosive and technically demanding movements under physiological stress. Differences in recovery structure also distinguish the two modalities: while HIIT typically employs predetermined rest intervals, HIFT features shorter or variable rest periods, imposing greater neuromuscular and metabolic stress [16].

Despite increasing interest, direct comparisons of HIIT and HIFT in competitive youth athletes are scarce, particularly among adolescent female volleyball players-a population experiencing rapid growth, neuromuscular maturation, and heightened training responsiveness [19–22]. Filling this gap is essential for informing evidence-based programming during a critical developmental period.

Therefore, the present study compared the effects of HIIT and HIFT on anthropometric, physiological, and volleyball-specific performance outcomes in adolescent female volleyball players. We hypothesized that while both training modalities would elicit improvements relative to a control group, the multimodal and sport-specific characteristics of HIFT would result in superior gains in explosive strength, agility, and volleyball skill performance.

Methods

This study employed a randomized controlled pre-post test design with two intervention groups and one control group. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and the ethical principles for research with human [23]. The protocol was approved by the Marmara University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol no: 09.2021.06; Date: 08 January 2021). The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07181031) on September 04, 2025.

During testing and training, the regulations and safety precautions related to the COVID-19 pandemic, as mandated by the Turkish Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Youth and Sports, were strictly followed. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians before data collection. The study also received official institutional permission for the use of laboratory facilities and the sports hall. This randomized controlled trial was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. A CONSORT flow diagram is provided in Fig. 1, and the completed CONSORT checklist is available upon request.

Randomization was conducted after baseline testing using a simple random allocation method to assign participants to one of three groups: high intensity interval training (HIIT), high intensity functional training (HIFT), or control. Ethical and methodological standards for youth exercise research were considered throughout. Participant flow is shown in Fig. 1, summarizing the randomization of 32 players into HIIT, HIFT, and control groups, and the overall structure of the 12-week interventions and testing process.

Study setting

All tests and measurements were conducted in the Research Laboratory of the Faculty of Sport Sciences, Marmara University. The training interventions were implemented in the indoor sports hall located in the Pınarhisar district of Kırklareli province. Prior to data collection and training implementation, official authorization for the use of these facilities was obtained from the relevant institution.

Participants

The study population consisted of licensed female volleyball players aged 15–18 years competing in the regional league. Inclusion criteria were: ≥2 years of active playing experience, no chronic or acute medical conditions, no smoking, alcohol, or drug use, and no regular medication or ergogenic supplementation. All participants were medically cleared and held valid competition licenses. Exclusion criteria were; failure to meet age or experience requirements, refusal to provide informed consent, or lack of parental/guardian approval. Participants were randomly assigned to the HIIT, HIFT, or control group using a computer-generated random sequence created by an independent researcher who had no role in baseline testing or intervention delivery. Allocation was concealed until all baseline assessments were completed to minimize the risk of selection bias. A total of 36 players volunteered to participate in the study. Parental informed consent and athlete assent were obtained prior to enrollment, in accordance with ethical requirements for research involving minors. During the intervention period, 4 participants were excluded due to non-compliance or health-related reasons, resulting in a final sample of 32 athletes included in the analysis. The sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of age, competitive level, and training background, which enhances the internal validity of the study [24, 25].

Power analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted prior to participant recruitment using GPower software (v3.1.9.2, Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The analysis was based on a one-way ANOVA model with three groups (α = 0.05, f = 0.55), which indicated that a minimum total sample size of 30 participants would achieve an estimated power of 0.72. The selected effect size reflected Cohen’s conventional thresholds for medium-to-large effects [26], and GPower is widely used in exercise science research to ensure adequate statistical sensitivity in randomized designs [27]. Although the preliminary power estimation suggested that enrolling 36 participants would provide more robust statistical power to detect moderate effects, logistical constraints and the limited availability of elite adolescent female volleyball athletes resulted in a final analyzed sample of 32. Consequently, the achieved statistical power (0.72) fell below the conventional benchmark of 0.80, which reduces the probability of detecting true between-group differences and warrants a cautious interpretation of the quantitative findings.

Training protocol and implementation

All groups continued their routine volleyball training (3–4 sessions per week). In addition, the HIIT and HIFT groups performed two supervised conditioning sessions per week for 12 weeks, while the control group completed the same number of extra training sessions consisting of regular volleyball practice. Training load progression was carefully adjusted to ensure progressive overload and adequate recovery, and overall training volume was thereby balanced across groups.

High intensity interval training (HIIT) protocol

Participants in the HIIT group engaged in a 12-week resistance-based high intensity interval training program, designed to target both aerobic and anaerobic energy systems. Training sessions were conducted twice per week in addition to the athletes’ regular volleyball practice schedule. Each session commenced with a standardized 10-minute warm-up involving low-intensity jogging and dynamic stretching, and concluded with a 5-minute cool-down of light jogging and static stretching to promote recovery. The main training component consisted of short, repeated high intensity bouts (30 s) of multi-modal exercises-including sprint drills, plyometric movements, functional bodyweight resistance exercises, multi-joint strength movements, agility drills, and aerobic conditioning-performed at 85–95% of maximal heart rate (HRmax) [13, 28]. Each bout was followed by 30 s of active recovery (e.g., walking or light movement).

Heart rate responses were continuously monitored using Polar Team² telemetry systems to ensure that athletes remained within the prescribed target zones. Individual training loads were adjusted when necessary to maintain relative intensity and ensure comparable internal load across participants. High intensity functional training (HIFT) protocol

Participants completed a 12-week high-intensity functional training (HIFT) program designed to improve aerobic capacity, anaerobic power, muscular strength, and agility. The structure and progression of the intervention were informed by prior work showing that functional circuit-style training can effectively enhance strength, endurance, and overall physical performance in active populations. Accordingly, the program incorporated multi-modal, task-oriented exercises organized in a circuit format to maximize neuromuscular stimulation and metabolic demand [29]. Training sessions were performed twice weekly in addition to regular volleyball practices. Each training session followed a standardized structure, beginning with a 10-minute warm-up of light jogging and dynamic mobility exercises, followed by a main training phase consisting of circuit-style functional workouts that combined sprint drills, plyometric exercises, bodyweight resistance movements, multi-joint strength exercises, agility drills, and aerobic activities; each exercise was performed for 30 s at maximal effort with no fixed rest between exercises (≤ 10 s transition allowed), and participants completed 4–6 sets per session with 1–2 min of rest between sets, while emphasizing maximal movement speed and explosive execution of all repetitions, and concluding with a 5-minute cool-down of low-intensity jogging and static stretching.

Heart rate was continuously monitored using Polar Team² telemetry systems to ensure participants trained within the prescribed intensity zones. Exercise selection was updated every 2–3 weeks to maintain progressive overload and maximize neuromuscular adaptations. Control group

The control group continued their regular volleyball training program throughout the 12-week study period. Athletes completed the same total weekly training duration as the intervention groups by participating in standard team practices that included technical–tactical drills, small-sided games, and routine physical preparation activities prescribed by the team coach. No additional conditioning sessions beyond their usual volleyball practice were introduced. This ensured that weekly training volume was balanced across all three groups, allowing differences in outcomes to be attributed to the HIIT and HIFT protocols rather than total training time.

Determination of training intensity

Training intensity was prescribed individually based on each participant’s maximum heart rate (HRmax), determined by the formula HRmax = 220 – age. Target training zones were expressed as percentages of HRmax, in line with previous studies recommending heart rate–based prescription for high intensity training [13, 28]. Athletes’ HR responses were continuously monitored with telemetry (Polar Team 2, Polar Electro, Finland) to ensure adherence to the designated intensity ranges. To ensure comparability across groups, the total weekly training load was standardized. Although the HIIT and HIFT groups completed two additional supervised sessions per week, the control group maintained an equivalent overall training duration by participating in extended volleyball practice sessions that matched the time commitment of the intervention groups. As a result, all three groups accumulated a similar weekly training volume, allowing observed differences to be attributed more directly to the specific characteristics of the HIIT and HIFT protocols rather than to discrepancies in total workload.

Safety and harms

All testing and training procedures were conducted under the supervision of qualified sport scientists and licensed coaches to ensure participant safety. Prior to each laboratory assessment and training session, athletes were screened for acute illness or injury, and participation was postponed if any contraindications were present. Standardized warm-up and cool-down routines were included in all exercise sessions to minimize the risk of musculoskeletal injury.

Throughout the 12-week intervention, no serious adverse events or medical complications were reported. Minor transient symptoms, such as delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and exercise-induced fatigue, were observed in some participants but were managed with routine recovery strategies and did not interfere with study completion. The dropout cases were unrelated to acute injury and were primarily due to non-compliance or personal reasons. Overall, the intervention protocols were well tolerated, and the risk of harm was considered minimal.

Limitations

The external validity of the present study is limited by several factors. The sample consisted of a relatively small and homogeneous group of adolescent female volleyball players from a single region, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, including male athletes, adults, or players from different competitive levels or training backgrounds. Detailed contextual information-such as competition ranking, season phase (pre-season, in-season, off-season), or prior exposure to HIIT/HIFT-style conditioning-was not available, further limiting the applicability of the results. Moreover, the study was conducted during a period affected by COVID-19-related restrictions, which may have influenced training consistency, recovery, and daily routines. These factors collectively constrain the external validity of the study and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Test procedures and tests

All performance tests were conducted in an indoor sports hall under standardized environmental conditions (temperature 20–22 °C; relative humidity 40–50%). To minimize acute physiological fluctuations, athletes were instructed to refrain from strenuous activity, caffeine intake, and ergogenic supplements for at least 24 h prior to testing. Each assessment session was scheduled at the same time of day (± 1 h) to reduce potential circadian influences on performance. Although blinding of participants and coaches was not feasible due to the practical nature of the training interventions, outcome assessors were not informed of group assignments during testing. Standardized verbal encouragement procedures were used to limit assessor-related bias, and participants were instructed not to disclose their group allocation to evaluators.

Before testing, participants completed a 10-minute standardized warm-up consisting of light jogging, dynamic stretching, and mobility drills. This was followed by specific submaximal trials for each test to ensure familiarization. All tests were supervised by experienced sport scientists, and strong verbal encouragement was provided to maximize performance. The sequence of tests was organized to minimize fatigue effects, and the overall testing sequence is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Sufficient rest periods (3–5 min between jumps, throws, and sprint trials; 10 min before endurance tests) were provided to avoid residual fatigue. All equipment (e.g., timing gates, metabolic analyzer, lactate analyzer) was calibrated before each session. The same evaluators administered the tests throughout the study to ensure inter-rater reliability.

Anthropometry and body composition

Standing height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer to the nearest 0.1 cm, with participants barefoot and in the Frankfurt plane. Body mass and composition (fat %, lean mass, BMI) were assessed using a multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Tanita MC-780MA, Tokyo, Japan), a device with established accuracy and reproducibility for field use [30].

Countermovement jump (CMJ)

The countermovement jump (CMJ) is a widely used assessment of lower-limb explosive power. It involves a rapid eccentric–concentric action, where the participant performs a preparatory downward movement followed by a maximal vertical jump. This movement utilizes the stretch-shortening cycle, making CMJ a valid indicator of neuromuscular function and training adaptations [31].

Measurement systems such as force plates or infrared timing devices (e.g., Newtest Powertimer 300-series) provide reliable indices of jump height and flight time. CMJ performance is particularly relevant for volleyball, where explosive vertical jumping is essential for spiking and blocking [32].

Standing long jump (SLJ)

The standing long jump (SLJ) test evaluates horizontal explosive power of the lower limbs. Participants start with feet parallel behind a line, swing their arms, and jump forward maximally, with performance measured as the distance from the line to the heels.

SLJ is a simple, field-based test requiring minimal equipment, yet it is reliable and valid as an indicator of muscular fitness in youth and athletes [33]. It has been widely included in fitness test batteries and shows associations with sprinting and general lower-body strength [34].

3-kg medicine ball throw (MBT)

The medicine ball throw (MBT) is a practical field test for evaluating upper-limb explosive strength and power. In this test, participants sit on the floor with their back against a wall (to minimize trunk and leg contribution) and throw a medicine ball (commonly 2–3 kg) forward with maximal effort using a two-handed chest pass technique. Performance is measured as the horizontal distance from the starting point to where the ball lands.

The MBT is a reliable and valid test in both youth and adult populations and is sensitive to training-induced changes in upper-body power [35]. It has been applied in various sports contexts, including volleyball, basketball, and handball, where upper-body explosive actions such as serving, spiking, and throwing are essential [36].

Pro-Agility test (5–10–5 shuttle Run)

The Pro-Agility Test, also known as the 5–10–5 shuttle run, is a standardized field-based assessment of agility and change of direction (COD) performance. The test requires the athlete to sprint 5 yards to one side, then 10 yards in the opposite direction, and finally 5 yards back to the starting point. Time is typically measured with electronic timing gates (e.g., Newtest Powertimer), which improve measurement accuracy and reliability compared to manual timing.

This test is widely used in team sports such as volleyball, basketball, soccer, and American football because it replicates the frequent accelerations, decelerations, and rapid COD movements performed during gameplay [37]. Performance in the Pro-Agility Test has been shown to correlate with sprinting, strength, and plyometric ability, making it a useful indicator of multidimensional athleticism [38].

20-m sprint test

The 20-m sprint test is one of the most commonly used field assessments of linear sprint speed in youth and elite athletes. It evaluates acceleration capacity and short-distance maximal velocity, which are critical for team sports such as volleyball, soccer, and basketball [39]. Athletes start from a stationary position behind a line, and the time to cover 20 m is recorded, often using photocell timing gates (e.g., Newtest Powertimer) for accuracy [40].

Short sprint distances (10–30 m) provide reliable and valid measures of explosive speed, and the 20-m distance is particularly relevant because it reflects the acceleration phase rather than maximal sprint velocity. In volleyball, this test is valuable for monitoring improvements in quick court coverage and defensive reactions, as athletes rarely sprint longer than 20 m during play.

10 × 20 m sprint test with fatigue index

The 10 × 20 m repeated sprint test is a widely used field protocol to assess repeated sprint ability (RSA), which reflects an athlete’s capacity to perform maximal sprints interspersed with short recovery periods. Athletes complete ten consecutive 20-m sprints with a standardized rest interval (commonly 20–30 s). Sprint times are recorded using electronic timing gates, and both best sprint time and mean sprint time are analyzed [41].

To evaluate fatigue across repeated efforts, the Fatigue Index (FI) is calculated. A common formula is: FI (%) = ((Worst sprint time – Best sprint time) / Best sprint time) × 100.

This provides an estimate of performance decrement, with higher values indicating reduced sprint endurance and greater fatigue [13]. The 10 × 20 m test is particularly relevant for intermittent sports such as volleyball, where players must repeatedly accelerate, decelerate, and recover rapidly during rallies and transitions.

Volleyball skill test (Serve, Pass, Set, Block, Spike)

The volleyball skill test developed by Alnedral, Garri, and Yendrizal [42] provides a comprehensive evaluation of fundamental technical skills-overhand serve, underarm pass, forearm pass, block, and smash (spike)-for advanced-level volleyball students. This test was structured using a research and development design, incorporating need analysis, development, trial, and validation phases [42].

Each skill is performed five times and scored based on accuracy: points are assigned according to target zones (A, B, C, D), while out-of-bounds attempts receive zero points. The cumulative score across attempts represents the overall technical performance. Importantly, the test demonstrated high validity (r ≈ 0.72-0.79) and very high reliability (r ≈ 0.90-0.97) across all five components, qualifying it as a valid and reliable tool for assessing technical skill proficiency in advanced students [42].

Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test level 1 (YYIR1)

The Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test Level 1 (YYIR1) is a standardized field test designed to measure intermittent endurance capacity in team sport athletes. It consists of repeated 20-m shuttle runs at progressively increasing speeds, interspersed with 10 s of active recovery.

YYIR1 is considered valid and reliable for assessing the ability to repeatedly perform high intensity exercise with limited recovery. Test-retest studies report a coefficient of variation of approximately 4–5%, confirming its reproducibility [43]. It is widely applied in youth and elite sport contexts because it reflects the physiological demands of intermittent sports such as volleyball, soccer, and basketball [44].

Maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max)

Maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) represents the gold standard for assessing aerobic fitness and cardiorespiratory capacity. In this study, VO₂max was estimated during the YYIR1 test using a portable breath-by-breath metabolic analyzer (Cortex MetaMax 3B, Leipzig, Germany). The system was calibrated with reference gases before each session to ensure accuracy. Portable analyzers such as the MetaMax 3B have been shown to provide valid and reliable measurements when compared to laboratory-based systems [45]. While YYIR1-derived VO₂max values are strongly correlated with treadmill-based measures, some studies note a slight underestimation in female athletes, which should be considered in interpretation [46].

Blood lactate

Capillary blood samples were taken from the fingertip before warm-up and 2 min post-YYIR1, and analyzed using a portable handheld device (Lactate Scout, EKF Diagnostics, Germany). The analyzer requires only 0.5 µL of blood and provides rapid results within approximately 10 s. Portable devices such as the Lactate Scout are considered practical and reliable tools for field testing, though their accuracy may be slightly lower compared to benchtop laboratory analyzers [47]. Validation studies confirm their usefulness for monitoring metabolic stress in athletes [48].

Heart rate

Heart rate (HR) was continuously monitored during the YYIR1 test using Polar Team chest-strap telemetry systems (Polar Electro, Finland). Such devices have been shown to provide valid and reliable HR data both at rest and during exercise, making them widely applicable in team sport settings. Data were automatically stored and exported for later analysis [49].

Rating of perceived exertion (RPE)

Immediately after completing the YYIR1, athletes reported their subjective level of effort using the Borg 6–20 scale, where 6 corresponds to “no exertion at all” and 20 indicates “maximal exertion.” This psychophysiological tool integrates both central (cardiorespiratory strain) and peripheral (muscular fatigue, lactate accumulation) sensations into a single numerical rating.

The RPE scale has been widely validated across endurance and intermittent sports, showing strong correlations with objective physiological markers such as heart rate and post-exercise blood lactate [50]. Because of its simplicity, non-invasiveness, and immediate applicability, RPE is frequently used in field studies to complement physiological measurements and to monitor internal training load [51].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were calculated for all variables at pre- and post-test, and change scores (Δ = Post − Pre) were computed to quantify within-group alterations. A mixed-design ANOVA (3 groups × 2 time points) was used to evaluate training effects for each dependent variable. Prior to analysis, normality assumptions were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, homogeneity of variances was assessed with Levene’s test, and sphericity was examined using Mauchly’s test; Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied where sphericity was violated. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (ηp²), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for pre–post change scores to increase interpretive precision. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustments for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) were applied, and this methodological choice is acknowledged as a limitation. Parametric analyses were selected because the data met all required assumptions and mixed-model ANOVA offers greater statistical power for repeated-measures designs.

Results

The results of the present study are presented in relation to the effects of 12 weeks of resistance-based high intensity interval training (HIIT) and high intensity functional training (HIFT) on physical, physiological, and sport-specific performance outcomes in adolescent female volleyball players. Descriptive statistics for each group are reported first, followed by mixed-design ANOVA analyses examining time, group, and interaction effects. Significant improvements were observed across multiple domains, with differential adaptations between the HIIT and HIFT interventions. Post hoc comparisons are reported to highlight between-group differences where applicable, providing a comprehensive evaluation of training-induced changes relative to the control group (Tables 3 and 4).

The mixed-design ANOVA demonstrated significant main effects of time for countermovement jump (CMJ), standing long jump, pro-agility, 20 m sprint, repeated sprint fatigue index, Yo-Yo distance, maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max), and volleyball skill performance (all p < 0.05), indicating consistent improvements from pre- to post-test across participants. The magnitude of these changes was also reflected in the corresponding 95% confidence intervals presented in Table 4. For example, the HIFT group showed substantial improvements in CMJ (Δ = +5.15 cm, 95% CI: 4.14 to 6.16) and SLJ (Δ = +12.82 cm, 95% CI: 9.77 to 15.87), while the HIIT group demonstrated moderate but meaningful gains (CMJ Δ = +4.58 cm, 95% CI: 3.42 to 5.74; SLJ Δ = +8.00 cm, 95% CI: 3.05 to 12.95). In contrast, the control group exhibited only small changes, with wide confidence intervals that crossed zero.

Significant Group × Time interactions were found for CMJ (F(2,29) = 9.14, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.39), standing long jump (F(2,29) = 12.08, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.45), VO₂max (F(2,29) = 11.53, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.44), maximal heart rate (F(2,29) = 3.56, p = 0.041, ηp²=0.20), and volleyball skill test scores (F(2,29) = 7.44, p = 0.002, ηp²=0.34). These interaction effects were supported by the group-specific confidence interval patterns. For VO₂max, for instance, HIFT produced the largest improvement (Δ = +1.45 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 95% CI: 0.84 to 2.06), followed by HIIT (Δ = +0.90, 95% CI: 0.65 to 1.15), whereas the control group’s interval (Δ = +0.18, 95% CI: − 0.11 to 0.47) included zero. Skill performance exhibited a similar profile, with HIFT again showing the greatest enhancement (Δ = +13.18 points, 95% CI: 10.41 to 15.95).

Post-hoc comparisons indicated that both training groups (HIIT and HIFT) outperformed the control group, while the HIFT protocol consistently produced the largest improvements in jump performance, aerobic capacity, and volleyball-specific skills. For variables such as medicine ball throw, sprint fatigue index, blood lactate concentration, and Yo-Yo Borg ratings, no significant interaction effects were observed (p > 0.05), which corresponded with overlapping confidence intervals across the three groups—suggesting comparable response patterns rather than differential training effects.

For agility and sprint-related measures, a consistent pattern emerged. The pro-agility test demonstrated a significant main effect of time (F(1,29) = 36.34, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.56), reflecting overall improvements across all groups; this was evidenced by Δ values with confidence intervals that remained below zero (e.g., HIFT Δ = − 0.27 s, 95% CI: − 0.40 to − 0.14; HIIT Δ = − 0.14 s, 95% CI: − 0.27 to − 0.01). Similarly, significant time effects were also observed for the 20 m sprint (F(1,29) = 7.14, p = 0.012, ηp²=0.20) and repeated sprint fatigue index (F(1,29) = 18.88, p < 0.001, ηp²=0.39), with the HIFT group showing the largest numerical improvements (e.g., RSI Δ = − 1.56%, 95% CI: − 2.32 to − 0.80). However, the Group × Time interactions did not reach statistical significance (pro-agility: F(2,29) = 2.64, p = 0.089; 20 m sprint: F(2,29) = 1.02, p = 0.375; fatigue index: F(2,29) = 2.03, p = 0.149), indicating that the magnitude of change did not significantly differ between protocols. These results suggest that agility and sprint performance improved as a function of general training exposure rather than intervention-specific adaptations.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to compare the effects of resistance-based high intensity interval training (HIIT) and high intensity functional training (HIFT) on performance outcomes and volleyball-specific skills in adolescent athletes. Both training modalities produced meaningful improvements compared with the control group; however, the nature and magnitude of adaptation differed between them. Overall, HIFT tended to elicit larger gains in explosive strength, agility, and volleyball-specific skill performance, whereas resistance-based HIIT produced more pronounced improvements in aerobic capacity. These findings contribute to the growing literature on high-intensity conditioning in youth sports by providing experimental data on how distinct training structures may influence multidimensional performance characteristics. Nevertheless, the modest sample size and resulting statistical power place limits on the strength of comparative conclusions, and the observed trends should be interpreted as preliminary.

Although descriptive patterns suggested greater lower-body power and skill improvements following HIFT and slightly larger aerobic enhancements following HIIT, these differences did not reach statistical significance in post-hoc comparisons between the two interventions. Thus, while both modalities demonstrated effectiveness relative to the control group, the study cannot conclusively determine superiority for either HIIT or HIFT. The limited sample size, absence of multiplicity correction, and wide confidence intervals further underscore the need for caution when interpreting these results. Future research employing larger, adequately powered samples is warranted to clarify whether the performance trends observed here reflect true intervention-specific effects or sampling variability.

Aerobic capacity and endurance

The mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant Group × Time interaction for VO₂max, accompanied by a strong main effect of time for Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test performance. Post hoc comparisons showed that both HIIT and HIFT groups improved significantly relative to the control group, with HIIT demonstrating slightly larger numerical gains in VO₂max; however, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously, as no statistically significant differences were confirmed between the two intervention groups. These observations are broadly consistent with meta-analytic findings indicating that repeated high intensity bouts can stimulate cardiorespiratory adaptations, including increases in maximal oxygen uptake, enhanced cardiac efficiency, and improved aerobic endurance [52, 53]. Such adaptations are relevant in volleyball, where the ability to sustain high intensity actions and recover rapidly between rallies contributes to overall match performance.

HIFT also produced meaningful improvements in aerobic outcomes. This may reflect the metabolically demanding structure of functional sessions, which combine multi-joint, compound, and multidirectional movements performed at high intensities. Although primarily designed to target neuromuscular qualities, such protocols can place considerable stress on the cardiovascular system, thereby contributing to aerobic adaptations [54]. Recent evidence supports the notion that high intensity functional training can enhance endurance capacity alongside strength and power development [19, 55].

The broader literature similarly indicates that high intensity interval-based approaches are effective for improving repeated sprint ability and Yo-Yo test performance in youth athletes [56, 57]. Such gains enhance an athlete’s capacity to tolerate high tempo play, accelerate recovery between explosive actions, and maintain technical execution under conditions of fatigue. Complementary findings from school-based youth interventions further show that HIIT is feasible and beneficial in adolescents, leading to improvements in cardiometabolic profiles and overall aerobic health [58, 59].

Taken together, the present findings indicate that both HIIT and HIFT can positively affect aerobic performance in adolescent volleyball players, although the specific patterns of adaptation varied across measures. While HIIT produced slightly larger numerical improvements in VO₂max, HIFT also contributed to aerobic gains through the high physiological demand inherent in functional movement sequences. From an applied perspective, integrating elements of both methods may provide a balanced conditioning approach, offering complementary endurance and multidimensional fitness benefits for youth volleyball athletes. Nevertheless, given the modest sample size and exploratory scope of the study, these interpretations should be considered preliminary and warrant further confirmation in larger trials.

Explosive strength

The present study revealed significant Group × Time interactions for both countermovement jump (CMJ) and standing long jump performance, with the largest numerical improvements observed in the HIFT group, followed by HIIT and the control group. These findings suggest that functional, multi-planar exercises may provide a favorable stimulus for lower-body explosive adaptations in adolescent athletes. Previous research has similarly reported that multi-joint, high intensity movements can enhance stretch–shortening cycle efficiency, tendon stiffness, and neuromuscular coordination, contributing to increases in both vertical and horizontal jump performance [60–63]. Given the central role of repeated jumping, rapid accelerations, and explosive direction changes in volleyball, such adaptations are particularly relevant for athletic performance.

In contrast, improvements in upper-body explosive strength, as assessed by the medicine ball throw, were evident across all groups; however, no significant Group × Time interaction was detected. Although the HIFT group exhibited slightly larger numerical gains, the absence of a statistically significant interaction indicates that both training protocols contributed similarly to upper-body performance. This outcome may reflect the multifactorial nature of overhead throwing tasks, which depend on trunk stability, inter-limb coordination, and overall motor control. Nevertheless, recent studies suggest that functional training incorporating integrated upper- and lower-body movements may support transfer to volleyball-specific overhead actions [64, 65], although this pattern was not statistically confirmed in the present study.

Taken together, the more pronounced improvements in lower-body explosive performance observed in the HIFT group may be attributable to the program’s multi-planar and task-oriented structure, which imposes greater neuromuscular and mechanical demands on the lower extremities. This interpretation aligns with recent evidence demonstrating that the structure and timing of training sessions can influence power production in adolescent volleyball players [66]. While both HIIT and HIFT led to comparable improvements in upper-body performance, the movement-rich nature of HIFT may offer practical advantages when the goal is to enhance lower-limb explosive capacity. From an applied standpoint, incorporating functional, sport-relevant exercises into youth training programs may support the development of explosive performance qualities in volleyball athletes [66]. However, given the modest sample size and exploratory nature of the study, these interpretations should be treated cautiously and verified in larger trials.

Agility and Sprint-Related performance

The results demonstrated a consistent pattern across agility- and sprint-related parameters. Significant main effects of time were observed for the pro-agility test, 20 m sprint, and repeated sprint fatigue index, indicating that all groups improved from pre- to post-test. Although the HIFT group exhibited the largest numerical improvements, the Group × Time interactions were not statistically significant (pro-agility: p = 0.089; 20 m sprint: p = 0.375; fatigue index: p = 0.149). This suggests that enhancements in agility and sprint performance represented general training adaptations rather than differential effects attributable to either intervention.

The comparatively larger numerical changes observed in the HIFT group may relate to the multi-planar and functional nature of its exercises, which can enhance intermuscular coordination and change-of-direction ability—qualities that are integral to volleyball performance. Nonetheless, because both HIIT and control groups also demonstrated improvements, factors such as regular volleyball practice, natural growth, or familiarization with testing procedures may have contributed to the observed gains. The lack of significant interaction effects underscores the need for cautious interpretation and indicates that the study was not sufficiently powered to detect potentially small protocol-specific differences.

These outcomes align partially with previous research reporting improvements in agility and change-of-direction ability following high intensity or multi-directional training interventions [67–70]. Given the importance of rapid directional changes, repeated accelerations, and agility in volleyball, future studies employing larger samples or longer intervention periods may help clarify whether functional or interval-based methods provide distinct advantages in enhancing these capabilities. Moreover, integrating sport-specific drills with conditioning programs may provide additional insight into how physical improvements transfer to on-court performance.

Sport-specific skills

The mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant Group × Time interaction for volleyball-specific skill performance, indicating that both HIIT and HIFT interventions produced greater improvements than the control condition. Although the HIFT group showed the largest numerical gains, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two training interventions. Therefore, any apparent trend favoring HIFT should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in light of the modest sample size and the exploratory nature of the study.

The observed improvements are consistent with previous research demonstrating that training programs incorporating multidirectional movements, coordination tasks, and sport-related motor patterns can enhance technical performance in young volleyball players. Costa et al. [71], for example, highlighted the importance of reliable and sport-specific testing procedures when evaluating skill proficiency. Additionally, principles of motor learning suggest that varied and contextually rich practice environments can facilitate skill acquisition and transfer, which may help explain the general improvements observed across both intervention groups. Evidence from perceptual–motor training research—such as studies involving visual-motor or reactive-agility tasks—further supports the idea that combining physical and cognitive demands contributes to more adaptable skill performance in youth athletes [72]. Taken together, these findings emphasize that volleyball skill development is multifactorial, emerging from the interplay between physical conditioning, perceptual processing, and motor learning rather than from a single training modality alone.

Physiological adaptations

The results indicated no significant Group × Time interactions for blood lactate levels, suggesting that short-term high intensity interventions may not substantially influence lactate tolerance in adolescent athletes. This aligns with previous research indicating that adaptations related to buffering capacity, mitochondrial enzyme activity, and lactate clearance generally require longer training exposure or more targeted metabolic conditioning [53, 73]. Accordingly, although both HIIT and HIFT provide substantial neuromuscular and cardiovascular demands, the duration and structure of the present interventions may not have been sufficient to meaningfully alter lactate responses.

A small but statistically significant Group × Time interaction was observed for maximal heart rate (MaxHR) (p = 0.041); however, the magnitude of change was modest and should be interpreted cautiously. MaxHR is typically regarded as a relatively stable physiological parameter influenced primarily by age and genetics [55]. Thus, the observed variation may reflect normal testing variability or subtle training-related fluctuations rather than meaningful physiological change. Measures such as submaximal heart rate responses or recovery heart rate may offer more sensitive indicators of cardiovascular adaptation in youth populations.

Overall, these findings suggest that while short-term HIIT and HIFT interventions can improve neuromuscular, aerobic, and volleyball-specific outcomes, their influence on markers of metabolic tolerance and maximal cardiac function appears limited within the timeframe of this study. Future research employing longer intervention periods, incorporating biochemical markers of oxidative metabolism, and evaluating heart rate variability may provide a more comprehensive understanding of physiological adaptations to different high-intensity training modalities.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study found that both resistance-based high intensity interval training (HIIT) and high intensity functional training (HIFT) were associated with improvements in several physical performance measures and volleyball-specific skills in adolescent athletes when compared with the control group. While descriptive patterns differed across outcomes, the statistical analyses did not reveal significant differences between the two intervention modalities. Improvements in explosive strength, aerobic capacity, agility, sprint performance, and skill execution were observed over time, reflecting general training-related adaptations rather than effects attributable to one specific protocol. Lactate responses showed no meaningful changes, and only a modest interaction effect was identified for maximal heart rate.

Taken together, these findings indicate that both HIIT and HIFT can contribute positively to the physical and sport-specific development of young volleyball players. However, due to the limited sample size, absence of multiplicity correction, and the exploratory nature of the study, the observed patterns should be interpreted with caution. From a practical standpoint, incorporating structured high-intensity conditioning-whether interval-based or functional in format-may offer coaches useful options for enhancing multiple components of performance. Future research with larger, adequately powered samples is needed to determine whether distinct training characteristics lead to differential adaptations between HIIT and HIFT.
