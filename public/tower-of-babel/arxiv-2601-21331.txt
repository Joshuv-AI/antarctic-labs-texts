Convex Loss Functions for Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Neural Networks
Author, corresponding author: Filippo Portera

Abstract
We propose a new convex loss for Support Vector Machines, both for the binary classification and for the regression models. Therefore, we show the mathematical derivation of the dual problems and we experiment with them on several small datasets. The minimal dimension of those datasets is due to the difficult scalability of the SVM method to bigger instances. This preliminary study should prove that using pattern correlations inside the loss function could enhance the generalisation performances. Our method consistently achieved comparable or superior performance, with improvements of up to 2.0 % 2.0\% in F1 scores for classification tasks and 1.0 % 1.0\% reduction in Mean Squared Error (MSE) for regression tasks across various datasets, compared to standard losses. Coherently, results show that generalisation measures are never worse than the standard losses and several times they are better. In our opinion, it should be considered a careful study of this loss, coupled with shallow and deep neural networks. In fact, we present some novel results obtained with those architectures.

1
Introduction

The effort here exposed was measuring the generalisation performances of several Machine Learning algorithms through a novel loss function.
We apply a modification of the generalised quadratic loss described initially in
[
4
]
. The convex loss developed here, theoretically, will perform equally or better than the standard loss, since it is a generalisation.
We wrote the mathematical steps to obtain two dual objective functions that can be computed using kernel functions for SVM (both for binary classification and for regression tasks).
The proposed loss was then applied to some Shallow and Deep Learning tasks, where it was more challenging to measure given its computational cost.
A set of different Radial Basis Functions was evaluated.
The significance of this work consists in the results obtained with this new loss and several settings and datasets.

(1)

Where
i\in[1,..,l],j\in[1,..,l]
, and
\gamma_{S}
is another hyper-parameter, we would like to solve the following binary classification problem:

\vec{w}^{*},b^{*}=\text{argmin}_{\vec{w},b}\frac{1}{2}||\vec{w}||^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{i}}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}

(2)

y_{i}(\vec{w}^{\prime}\vec{x_{i}}+b)\geq 1-\xi_{i},\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(3)

\xi_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(4)

Here
\vec{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

are the SVM parameters that have to be learnt in order to predict new examples.

In this setting, for sufficiently big

We add that any set

3.1
Support Vector Classification (SVC)

We derived the relative dual problem through the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions.
The Lagrangian of (
2
) is:

\vec{\alpha}^{*},\vec{\eta}^{*},\vec{w}^{*},b^{*}=\text{argmax}_{\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}}\text{argmin}_{\vec{w},b}L=

(5)

\frac{1}{2}||\vec{w}||^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{i}}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}[y_{i}(\vec{w}^{\prime}\vec{x_{i}}+b)-1+\xi_{i}]-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\eta_{i}\xi_{i}

(6)

With:

\xi_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(7)

\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(8)

And:

\eta_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(9)

The possible saddle points
(\vec{\alpha}^{*},\vec{\eta}^{*},\vec{w}^{*},b^{*})
are the optimal solutions.

The KKT optimality condition on the variable
\xi_{i}
is:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_{i}}=C\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_{i}}(\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{k}}S_{j,k}+\sqrt{\xi_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{k}}S_{j,i})-\alpha_{i}-\eta_{i}=0

(10)

Thus:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_{i}}=C\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_{i}}(2\sqrt{\xi_{i}}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}+\xi_{i}S_{i,i})-\alpha_{i}-\eta_{i}=0

(11)

C(2\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\xi_{i}}}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}+S_{i,i})=\alpha_{i}+\eta_{i}

(12)

C(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi_{i}}}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j})=\alpha_{i}+\eta_{i}-CS_{i,i}

(13)

C\frac{\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}}{\sqrt{\xi_{i}}}=\alpha_{i}+\eta_{i}-CS_{i,i}

(14)

C\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}=\sqrt{\xi_{i}}(\alpha_{i}+\eta_{i}-CS_{i,i})

(15)

The KKT optimality condition on
\vec{w}
is:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec{w}}=\vec{w}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\vec{x_{i}}=0\Rightarrow\vec{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\vec{x_{i}}

(16)

The KKT optimality condition on
b
is:

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}=0

(17)

Here
K(\vec{x_{i}},\vec{x_{j}})=e^{-\gamma_{K}||\vec{x}_{i}-\vec{x}_{j}||^{2}}
is the dot product in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space parameterised by
\gamma_{K}
,
and the simplified Lagrangian is:

\vec{\alpha}^{*},\vec{\eta}^{*}=\text{argmax}_{\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\alpha_{j}y_{j}K(\vec{x_{i}},\vec{x_{j}})+

(18)

+C\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{i}}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\alpha_{i}+\eta_{i})

(19)

Subject to:

\xi_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(20)

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}=0

(21)

0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq C\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}}{\sqrt{\xi_{i}}}+S_{i,i}\right),\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(22)

\eta_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(23)

Since the part of the objective function containing the
\vec{\xi}
vanishes, this becomes:

\vec{\alpha}^{*},\vec{\eta}^{*}=\text{argmax}_{\vec{\alpha},\vec{\eta}}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\alpha_{j}y_{j}K(\vec{x_{i}},\vec{x_{j}})

(24)

Subject to:

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}=0

(25)

0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq C\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{l}\sqrt{\xi_{j}}S_{i,j}}{\sqrt{\xi_{i}}}+S_{i,i}\right),\hskip 19.91684pti\in[1,..,l]

(26)

With:

\vec{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\vec{x_{i}}

(27)

and the estimator function can be expressed as:

f(\vec{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}y_{i}K(\vec{x_{i}},\vec{x})+b

(28)

Where
b
can be determined with
SVM b
.

This dual problem can be solved with a Sequential Minimal Optimization-like algorithm, considering the new constraints on
\vec{\alpha}
.
At present, we have written the SVM optimiser only for binary classification tasks.

Method

We decided to test several
S
matrices that could take into account pattern similarities (all of them are parameterised by the
\gamma_{S}
hyper-parameter):

G_{i,j}=e^{-\gamma_{S}||\vec{x}_{i}-\vec{x}_{j}||^{2}}

(53)

Q_{i,j}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{S}||\vec{x}_{i}-\vec{x}_{j}||^{2}}

(54)

M_{i,j}=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\gamma_{S}||\vec{x}_{i}-\vec{x}_{j}||^{2}}}

(55)

We carried out a 5-fold cross validation procedure for models validation, and we selected the hyper-parameters that perform best in terms of F1 or MSE scores (depending on the problem focus) on each validation set. The hyper-parameters are
C
,
\gamma_{K}
, and
\gamma_{S}
. We adopted a grid search over those hyper-parameters:
C\in\{1E5,\dots,1E-2\}
,
\gamma_{K}\in\{1E1,\dots,1E-6\}
, and
\gamma_{S}\in\{1E2,\dots,1E-4\}
with steps of unit powers of
1E1
.

We used a PC equipped with 32 GB of RAM, 11th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-11700 @ 2.50GHz with
16
cores (hyper-threading on) and a GPU (used only with Graphs, Masks, and CIFAR-10 datasets).

6.1
Analysis

We discuss some time and space complexity measures about the proposed algorithms.

On time complexity measures of non Neural Networks algorithms employed in this work, it is difficult to determine a big
O(N)

(where
N
is the number of input samples) function since the non-linear nature of the tasks and different stopping criteria.
We observed that, for SVMs, surely it depends on
N
, but also on
C
, the trade-off constant, and on the other hyper-parameters
\gamma_{K}
and
\gamma_{S}
.
We note that with SVMs, for certain hyper-parameter configurations, the training could not converge at all.

About the Neural Network experiments, the time complexity is mainly driven by the number of epochs
E
, the number of examples
N
, the number of features
D
of each example, the number of network weights
W
,
the batch-size
B
, and the type of loss adopted.

The algorithm that uses a standard loss has 4 distinct functions that are called for each epoch and batch:

1.

Batch model computation:
O(WB)

2.

Criterion computation:
O(B)

3.

Backpropagation:
O(WB)

4.

Optimization(weights update):
O(WB)
.

Therefore, the complexity of the standard loss Neural Network algorithms is
O(E\frac{N}{B}WB)=O(ENW)
.

The algorithm that uses
S(\gamma_{S})
loss has still 4 distinct functions that are called for each epoch and batch:

1.

Batch model computation:
O(WB)

2.

Criterion computation:
\bm{O(B^{2}D)}

3.

Backpropagation:
O(WB)

4.

Optimization(weights update):
O(WB)
.

So, comparing each of the 4 functions described above, the second function is smaller than the other 3 functions since in our model the number of network weights
is fixed to
W=26369
,
B\in\{16,32,64\}
, and
D=1024
.
Since the search of the best
\gamma_{S}
value takes
G
steps, the whole model selection algorithm has a time complexity of

O(GE\frac{N}{B}WB)=O(GENW)
.

About space complexity, the whole
S(\gamma_{S})
matrix has to be computed once for small datasets and shallow networks (
N\times N
float numbers).
This is feasible as long as a
S(\gamma_{S})=(N\times N)
tensor fits into memory, in combination with the network weights.
Nevertheless, for bigger problems, we resorted to use
S(\gamma_{S})=(B\times B)
tensors allocated once per loss computation.

Shallow Neural Networks Regression

We tested some regression problems with Neural Networks.
A small an shallow neural network has been evaluated with
3
fully connected linear hidden layers with (
11
,
50
,
15
) nodes per layer and a Rectified Linear Unit activation function applied after the first, second, and third layer.
We used an MSE loss, and we compared it with the loss that exploits the pattern correlation matrix.
In practice, at each loss computation, given
l
as the batch size, the matrix
S(\gamma_{S})
was computed (as in
1
) and the loss became:

\text{loss}=\frac{|\vec{t}-\vec{o}|^{\prime}S(\gamma_{S})|\vec{t}-\vec{o}|}{l}

(56)

Where
\vec{t}
is the target vector and
\vec{o}
is the network output vector. Here, there is no need to calculate the element-wise square root of the
prediction error vector because we are comparing our loss with the MSE loss.
We took the whole dataset as a batch, since it is small.
We varied the
\gamma_{S}
between
1E4
to
1E-3
, with steps of
1
power of
10
.
The other fixed parameters are: epochs =
2000
, lr =
0.001
and the chosen optimiser is PyTorch’s Adam.

The algorithms are called Neural Network (NN) for the standard MSE loss, Neural Network (NN)_S for the new loss with
S=I
, and Neural Network (NN)_A where A
\in\{G,M,Q\}

S(\gamma_{S})
.

A synthetic dataset containing face images with and without anti-COVID masks is evaluated.
The dataset is made of

The number of test images is

In this case we studied the impact of considering two different loss functions, the Cross-Entropy loss and the following:

\text{loss}=\frac{\sqrt{\vec{vp}}^{\prime}S(\gamma_{S})\sqrt{\vec{vp}}}{p}+\frac{\sqrt{\vec{vn}}^{\prime}S(\gamma_{S})\sqrt{\vec{vn}}}{n}

(57)

Where
S(\gamma_{S})
is the
(B\times B)
matrix that considers pattern correlations that is computed (as in
1
),
B
is the batch-size,
\vec{vp}_{i}=CE_{i}
if
y_{i}=1
, otherwise
\vec{vp}_{i}=0
,
and
\vec{vn}_{i}=CE_{i}
if
y_{i}=-1
, otherwise
\vec{vn}_{i}=0
.
We add that
\vec{CE}
is the Cross-Entropy loss vector, and
p
is equal to the number of positive samples in the current batch, and

n
is the number of negative samples in the current batch. If the denominator of one of the two fractions is
0
, we only consider the non-infinite fraction.

The purpose of this modification is dividing the space of the
2
target samples distribution, and considering pattern correlation separately.

Masks
.

7
Discussion

Summarising the results, it is experimentally showed that, as expected, the generalisation performances are equal or better to the standard loss case.
This behaviour is theoretically established since the use of the standard loss is always considered by our method.
We add that this approach suffers from a scalability problem since the loss function for big tasks (such as
[
7
]
) is expensive to compute,
both in time and space dimensions.

8
Conclusions

We introduced a novel loss function for Machine Learning problems.
Results with this loss confirm the theory: the generalised loss presents performances that were not worse than the standard case, although we tested it mostly on small problems. On bigger problems (Masks and CIFAR-10), the generalisation ability of the new loss is proportional to the sub size of the used
S
matrix.
It seems that, in those cases, when we selected portions of the
S
matrices there is little evidence of improvement.
The reasons for this behaviour could be the local construction of
S
matrices, while global information should be taken into account.
A strategy to build more efficient and more effective
S
matrices for Deep Neural Networks should be investigated.

Furthermore, Anisotropic Kernels developed in
[
3
]
could be explored in order to refine the quality of the similarity matrices
S
, and other vector norms can be considered to build
S
.

As shown by the experiments, it should be also considered the fact concerning the unique sample distribution of each dataset, so every generalisation scheme has to be tested to obtain better performances. Otherwise an algorithm that determines a priori which is the best scheme should be devised.

At last, the example trial on masks binary classification introduced target information for the separation of the correlation space.
In our opinion this should be carefully evaluated for other tasks and datasets.
