Clinical and pathological characteristics, immune microenvironment, and survival analysis of molecular subtypes of surgically resected small cell lung cancer
Authors: Juncai Tian, Jiaxin He, Huohuan Tian, Xuewen Hou, Dan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) remains a lethal malignancy with limited therapeutic progress, largely due to its heterogeneity and the lack of biomarkers for patient stratification. This study aims to validate the prevalent molecular subtyping system in a surgically resected cohort and elucidate the subtype-specific tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and its clinical implications.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical and pathological characteristics of 80 patients with surgically resected SCLC. The expression levels of Achaete-scute family BHLH transcription factor 1 (ASCL1), neuronal differentiation 1 (NEUROD1), POU class 2 homeobox 3 (POU2F3), as well as the immune markers CD8, forkhead box P3 (FOXP3), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), were assessed in 80 pathological tissue samples using immunohistochemistry (IHC). Statistical analyses, including the chi-square test, t-test, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression, were performed to evaluate the associations between SCLC molecular subtype expression levels and patients’ clinicopathological features, immune marker profiles, and prognostic outcomes.ResultsAll four molecular subtypes were successfully identified and exhibited distinct distribution patterns: ASCL1-dominant SCLC (SCLC-A, 53.8%), NEUROD1-dominant SCLC (SCLC-N, 5.0%), POU2F3-dominant SCLC (SCLC-P, 3.8%), and SCLC-triple-negative (37.5%). These subtypes were not associated with conventional clinicopathological characteristics. Each subtype displayed unique TIME features. SCLC-A correlated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment enriched in FOXP3 + regulatory T cells (Tregs) (r = 0.458, P < 0.001). SCLC-triple-negative, despite showing higher PD-L1 expression, exhibited an immune-infiltrated phenotype and was positively correlated with CD8 + T cells (r = 0.451, P < 0.001). In contrast, SCLC-N and SCLC-P presented an “immune-desert” phenotype. Furthermore, elevated levels of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and PD-L1 were identified as independent prognostic risk factors for shorter overall survival (OS), and these molecular subtypes were significantly associated with OS (log-rank P = 0.043).ConclusionsThis study based on IHC detection, validated the proposed molecular subtyping classification of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) through the expression patterns of transcriptional regulators. The findings demonstrated that SCLC-A and SCLC-triple-negative were respectively associated with distinct tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) features and poorer prognostic outcomes. Furthermore, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) were identified as independent prognostic factors in surgically resected SCLC patients. These conclusions may contribute to treatment decision-making for SCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16618-7.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), Molecular subtypes, Tumor immune microenvironment, Immunohistochemistry (IHC), Prognostic factors

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignant immune-infiltrated phenotypes worldwide. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), the most aggressive histopathological subtype of lung cancer, accounts for 13–15% of new lung cancers [1]. SCLC is characterized by highly malignant, genetically unstable, with a tendency to metastasize at an early stage, a strong growth and invasive ability, and can cause a carcinoid syndrome [2]. Platinum-based chemotherapy has been the standard first-line treatment for extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC), with radiotherapy used for palliative or consolidative indications. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) remains the standard treatment for most patients with limited-stage SCLC (LS-SCLC), whereas surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy is reserved for carefully selected patients with very early-stage disease (e.g., T1–2N0) after appropriate mediastinal staging. The addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to platinum-etoposide chemotherapy has established a new standard first-line regimen for extensive-stage SCLC.

In recent years, to enable more effective treatment strategies, the molecular classification of SCLC has been redefined based on the expression levels of key transcription factors. This system categorizes SCLC into four subtypes: Achaete-scute family BHLH transcription factor 1 (ASCL1) -dominant SCLC (SCLC-A), neuronal differentiation 1 (NEUROD1) -dominant SCLC (SCLC-N), POU class 2 homeobox 3 (POU2F3) -dominant SCLC (SCLC-P), and inflamed SCLC (SCLC-triple-negative) [3–5]. Most previously studied SCLC samples were predominantly of the ASCL1-driven subtype [2].

The new categorization of SCLC will help to study different molecular models of SCLC for precision therapy and improve overall survival. This molecular classification holds significant clinical implications, as it enables the identification of subtype-specific molecular features and clinically relevant biomarkers, thereby offering new strategies for targeted therapy in SCLC. Defining the distinct gene expression profiles of SCLC patients will form a critical foundation for selecting the most effective treatments. Given that immunotherapy, biomarker-directed therapy, and chemotherapy act on different targets and mechanisms, combination or synergistic therapeutic approaches may enhance treatment efficacy [6].

Despite the success of targeted therapies and immunotherapies in NSCLC, SCLC remains highly lethal due to its significant heterogeneity and the lack of well-defined molecular biomarkers for patient stratification [7, 8]. Our research seeks to systematically elucidate the subtype-specific molecular features and tumor microenvironment characteristics of SCLC, identify clinically actionable biomarkers, and lay the necessary foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies, including rational combination therapies, to improve the overall survival of SCLC patients.

Methods

Patients and tissue samples

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 80 adult patients with surgically resected SCLC at our hospital between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2017. All patients underwent curative-intent surgery (e.g., lobectomy or segmentectomy) as initial treatment. Postoperative adjuvant therapy decisions followed then-current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines. Adjuvant chemotherapy with etoposide plus platinum (EP) was administered to 60% of patients (n = 48); the remaining 40% (n = 32) did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy due to patient refusal, advanced age, comorbidities, or physician discretion. No patient received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or other tumor-directed therapy prior to surgery. Patients were excluded if they had other primary malignancies, received prior tumor-directed therapy, or were diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer or other unspecified lung cancer types. For patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy, treatment completion and cycle number were recorded. Postoperative radiologic surveillance was performed per institutional protocol to monitor for disease recurrence. Patients were followed up until May 31, 2023, for survival outcomes. Follow-up data were obtained by reviewing inpatient and outpatient electronic medical records, supplemented by telephone interviews when necessary. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause, and surviving patients were censored at the last follow-up.

IHC staining and scoring criteria

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens were prepared, and consecutive 4 μm-thick tissue sections were cut from the blocks for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. All sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using a fully automated staining machine (LEICA ST5020). Antigen retrieval was performed under high-pressure heating at 100 °C for 20 min using a fully automated immunohistochemistry pretreatment system (Dako PT Link). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used and their working dilutions were as follows: ASCL1 (Abcam, ab213151, 1:200), NEUROD1 (Abcam, ab213725, 1:150), POU2F3 (CST, #36135, 1:50). The EnVision™ FLEX+ detection system (Dako, K8002) was used for secondary antibody incubation and signal amplification, and antibody diluent (Dako, S3022) was employed for antibody dilution. Finally, visualization was achieved using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin. The stained sections were independently examined and evaluated by two pathologists using an OLYMPUS BX3 microscope. Selected sections were digitally scanned and archived using a YUNA PRECICE610 high-throughput digital pathology scanner.

Two pathologists and one clinical physician jointly evaluated the immunohistochemically stained sections, and the results were averaged. In cases where two markers showed comparable IHC scores (e.g., co-expression of ASCL1 and NEUROD1), the subtype was assigned based on the marker with the highest score. If scores were equivalent, both pathologists reviewed the case jointly with a third pathologist to reach consensus. Tumors with ambiguous or weak staining for all three markers were re-evaluated on additional sections if available; persistent triple-negativity was required for SCLC-triple-negative classification. Whole-slide images were acquired under consistent conditions using an OLYMPUS BX3 microscope at magnifications of 50×, 100×, 200×, and 400×. Five representative high-power fields (400×) were randomly selected for assessment. The percentage of positive cells was calculated as the mean ratio of positively stained cells to total cells across the five fields. The IHC score was calculated as the product of staining intensity (0–3) and the percentage of positive cells (0-100%). Subtypes were assigned based on the highest IHC score among ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3; tumors negative for all three markers were classified as SCLC-triple-negative. Optimal cut off values for high versus low expression were determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Pathological diagnosis adhered to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Thoracic Tumors and the 2023 CSCO guidelines for small cell lung cancer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (V.26.0; IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism (V.8.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) for data visualization and statistical analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were converted into categorical variables based on clinical relevance for analysis. Differences in clinicopathological characteristics among different molecular subtypes of SCLC (SCLC-A, -N, -P, -triple-negative) were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare immunohistochemistry scores between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Correlations between SCLC molecular subtypes and clinicopathological features or immune markers were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: 0.00-0.19, very weak; 0.20–0.39, weak; 0.40–0.59, moderate; 0.60–0.79, strong; 0.80-1.00, very strong. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons between groups were performed with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify independent prognostic factors. For comparisons of the positive expression rates (ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, FOXP3, CD8, and PDL1) between the adjuvant chemotherapy group and the non-chemotherapy group, independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and sample characteristics

Eighty patients with resected SCLC were analyzed. The median age was 64 years (range, 42–79 years), and 73 (91.3%) were male. Ten (12.5%) patients were never-smokers. According to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer 8th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system, 51 (63.8%) patients had stage I–II disease and 29 (36.3%) had stage III disease. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 48 (60.0%) patients.

Subtype distribution did not differ significantly by gender, age, smoking history, tumor location, size, TNM stage or adjuvant chemotherapy (all P > 0.05, Table 1). Descriptively, SCLC-A harbored the highest proportion of stage I–II disease (72%), whereas SCLC-triple-negative was more often located in the upper/middle lobe (77%) and less frequently in stage I–II (50%). All SCLC-P tumors were ≤ 3 cm and arose in smokers; SCLC-N was too rare (n = 4) for meaningful comparison. Detailed subtype characteristics are given in Table 1.

Multivariate analysis demonstrated that CEA (HR 1.083, 95% CI: 1.015–1.156, P = 0.016) and PD-L1 (HR 1.449, 95% CI: 1.013–2.070, P = 0.040) were independent risk factors for poor prognosis (Table 3).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in OS among the four molecular subtypes (log-rank test, P = 0.043). SCLC-A and SCLC-triple-negative subtypes demonstrated poorer prognosis, while SCLC-N and SCLC-P subtypes showed superior survival outcomes. To assess whether postoperative treatment heterogeneity contributed to these observed survival differences, we compared the adjuvant chemotherapy group and the non-chemotherapy group. The positive expression of ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, FOXP3, CD8, and PDL1 showed no statistically significant difference between the adjuvant chemotherapy group and the non-chemotherapy group (all p > 0.05), revealed no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05) (see Supplementary Materials).

Discussion

This study provides further evidence supporting the classification of SCLC into four molecular subtypes of SCLC (A, N, P, Triple-negative) through immunohistochemical profiling and delineates their distinct tumor immune microenvironment landscapes, providing a rationale for precision immunotherapy.

Based on ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3 expression, we defined four SCLC subtypes. SCLC-A, the most common, exhibits high ASCL1 and supports a neuroendocrine-driven classification [9]. SCLC-N represents an alternative neuroendocrine pathway via NEUROD1 [10], while SCLC-P, defined by POU2F3, suggests an origin in tuft-cell-like populations with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) potential [11]. In contrast, SCLC-triple-negative shows low expression of these factors but high immune infiltration, indicating potential immunotherapy sensitivity [12]. The SCLC-triple-negative subtype accounted for 37% of the cohort, a proportion notably higher than that reported in previous studies. This relatively high prevalence may partly reflect selection bias inherent to our retrospective study design. Future prospective studies utilizing multi-center cohorts are warranted to confirm the true prevalence of SCLC-triple-negative.

Consistent with previous reports [9, 13, 14], our study found no significant association between SCLC molecular subtypes (A, N, P, Triple-negative) and baseline clinical characteristics, including gender, age, smoking history, or TNM stage. By analyzing a broader spectrum of clinical variables, we confirmed this consistent pattern, reinforcing that the molecular classification is independent of conventional clinicopathological parameters. Instead, the biological distinctions among subtypes likely stem from their intrinsic molecular driver programs, as delineated by Rudin et al. for ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, and YAP1 [3]. Discrepancies with some studies [15, 16] may stem from our cohort’s predominance of limited-stage disease and variations in immunohistochemical standards, underscoring the need for prospective multicenter validation.

SCLC subtypes demonstrated distinct tumor immune microenvironments (TIME). The SCLC-A subtype displayed an immunosuppressive milieu, correlating with FOXP3 + regulatory T cells (Treg), which suggests potential resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and supports combining checkpoint inhibitors with Treg-targeting agents [17–19]. Conversely, SCLC-triple-negative showed high PD-L1 expression and CD8 + T cell infiltration alongside T-cell exhaustion [3]. Thus, despite an inflamed phenotype, functional immunity is compromised. This discordance not only questions PD-L1’s utility as a standalone biomarker [20] but also reveals coexisting resistance mechanisms, necessitating combination immunotherapy to overcome T-cell exhaustion [21, 22]. Furthermore, SCLC-N and SCLC-P exhibited an “immune-desert” phenotype with minimal T-cell infiltration [18], underscoring the futility of monotherapy immune checkpoint blockade and prioritizing the development of subtype-specific targeted therapies [23].

Beyond molecular subtypes, both PD-L1 and CEA were identified as potential independent prognostic factors in our cohort. The prognostic value of PD-L1 is intrinsically linked to its role within the tumor immune microenvironment, consistent with its adverse prognostic association reported in resectable SCLC [24]. The interpretation of PD-L1 and CEA is highly context-dependent, influenced by tumor biology and detection methods for the former, and postoperative dynamics for the latter [25, 26]. This substantial context-dependency likely underlies the inconsistent prognostic reports across studies.

Significant overall survival differences among SCLC molecular subtypes likely reflect their distinct pathogenic drivers. The poor prognosis of SCLC-A may be attributed to its high proliferative capacity, often linked to myelocytomatosis oncogene cellular homolog (MYC) family amplification (e.g., v-myc avian myelocytomatosis viral oncogene lung carcinoma derived homolog (MYCL)) [27], and ASCL1-mediated BCL-2 upregulation conferring apoptosis resistance [23]. Although the SCLC-triple-negative subtype is characterized by high CD8 + T cell infiltration and is theoretically expected to be sensitive to ICIs [12], in practice, the presence of immunosuppressive components such as regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages represents a well-established mechanism of resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [28]. Consequently, even in SCLC-triple-negative tumors with substantial immune infiltration, a coexisting immunosuppressive microenvironment may still lead to limited clinical responses to ICIs [29]. SCLC-N and SCLC-P may exhibit better outcomes due to lower proliferative potential from MYC suppression by NEUROD1 [5] and reduced tumor mutational burden mediated by POU2F3 [30], respectively, potentially enhancing platinum sensitivity. The survival association we identified contrasts with some reports [2], a discrepancy potentially arising from cohort and treatment heterogeneity [31] and the lack of standardized subtyping methodologies, which can lead to patient misclassification [9]. Our findings suggest the importance of developing standardized protocols and defined clinical contexts to establish the prognostic value of molecular subtyping. Our finding that SCLC-triple-negative subtype was associated with poorer prognosis appears to contrast with the study by Dora et al. [32], who reported that CD45 + and CD8 + immune cell infiltration conferred significantly improved overall survival in surgically resected SCLC. This discrepancy likely reflects that SCLC-triple-negative defined by triple-negative does not equate to true CD8 + inflamed phenotype, as directly demonstrated in their study. Future studies incorporating CD8/CD68 markers are needed to clarify this.

The absence of neoadjuvant therapy in our cohort is a strength, as prior studies have demonstrated that chemotherapy can induce subtype switching and alter SCLC heterogeneity [33], ensuring that our IHC-defined subtypes reflect intrinsic tumor biology rather than therapy-induced lineage plasticity. This study also has several limitations. Its retrospective nature introduces potential selection bias, and the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, approximately 40% of patients in this cohort did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy, which may introduce substantial treatment heterogeneity and confound survival analyses. The lack of detailed treatment response data precludes an analysis of how specific therapies interact with molecular subtypes. The poorer prognosis of SCLC-A and SCLC-triple-negative in our cohort likely reflects the pre-immunotherapy treatment era, where patients were treated with conventional chemotherapy and surgery without modern immunotherapy regimens. Beyond treatment context, the biological characterization of SCLC-triple-negative in our study remains incomplete. The absence of CD4 immunohistochemistry, which could serve as a positive surrogate marker for SCLC-triple-negative identification, represents an additional limitation of this retrospective study due to limited tissue availability. Future larger prospective studies incorporating multi-omics validation, including transcriptomic profiling and CD4 or YAP1 immunohistochemistry, are warranted to confirm these findings. Additionally, whether the survival associations identified in this cohort can be reproduced in independent, multi-center datasets with standardized treatment protocols warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, our immunohistochemistry-based study validates the proposed molecular classification of SCLC using the expression patterns of key transcriptional regulators. We found that SCLC-A and SCLC-triple-negative subtypes were associated with distinct tumor immune microenvironments and poorer survival outcomes. In addition, CEA and PD-L1 were identified as independent prognostic factors for surgically resected SCLC.
