Beyond the left ventricle: modern echocardiographic assessment of right ventricular function in tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Martijn GH Vrijkorte, Nina C Wunderlich, Alison Duncan, Bushra S Rana, Leo Timmers, Robert van der Pol, Martin J Swaans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe right ventricle (RV) and tricuspid valve (TV) have historically been neglected in cardiovascular research and clinical practice. However, growing evidence has established RV dysfunction and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) as independent predictors of mortality and morbidity across a range of cardiac pathologies. The emergence of transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions (TTVI) has further emphasized the need for accurate and reproducible assessment of RV function in the presence of TR.ObjectiveTo provide a comprehensive review of current echocardiographic approaches of RV assessment in the context of TR. This review highlights their strengths, limitations, and clinical relevance. We focus particularly on advanced imaging modalities and their role in patient selection and outcome prediction following TTVI.MethodsWe review contemporary literature on RV anatomy and pathophysiology and critically evaluate echocardiographic modalities, including two-dimensional (2D) indices, three-dimensional (3D) volumetry, strain imaging, and noninvasive RV-pulmonary artery (PA) coupling surrogates, for their role in diagnosing RV dysfunction, guiding risk stratification, and predicting clinical outcomes in patients considered for TTVI.ResultsConventional echocardiographic measures of RV function such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and fractional area change (FAC) are limited by geometric assumptions, regional motion bias, and load dependence. Emerging echocardiographic techniques, including 3D imaging, free wall longitudinal strain (FWLS), effective RV ejection fraction (eRVEF), and RV-PA coupling indices, demonstrate improved accuracy and prognostic utility. In patients with TR, progressive RV remodeling, altered contraction patterns, and altered ventricular interdependence contribute to clinical decompensation. While TTVI provides hemodynamic and symptomatic benefit, it can also reveal latent RV dysfunction, underscoring the importance of thorough pre- and postprocedural evaluation.ConclusionAccurate assessment of RV structure and function is critical for optimal management of TR, particularly in candidates for TTVI. A multiparametric echocardiographic strategy that integrates advanced imaging techniques with functional indices provides a more complete characterization of RV performance, supports procedural planning, and improves risk stratification. Introduction

For decades, the right ventricle (RV) and tricuspid valve (TV) were considered hemodynamically subordinate to the left ventricle (LV) and received limited attention in cardiovascular research and clinical practice. Early experimental and clinical observations reinforced the perception of the RV as a passive, low-pressure chamber with limited physiological significance. In animal studies, disabling the RV produced no significant rise in venous pressure, and in congenital heart disease, surgical approaches that bypassed the RV, such as the Fontan operation, were initially associated with favorable early outcomes [1–3].

Consequently, clinical assessment and therapeutic innovations historically focused on the anatomy and physiology of the LV, with most diagnostic tools and imaging protocols developed for LV evaluation. Furthermore, the RV exhibits unique structural and functional characteristics, including a thinner myocardial wall, a crescent-shaped geometry, and complex contraction patterns, which complicates the direct application of LV-focused approaches to right-sided heart disease [4].

Recent advances have however fundamentally reshaped the understanding of RV physiology, underscoring its central role in maintaining circulatory stability. A growing body of evidence has established RV function as a key determinant of outcomes in various cardiovascular conditions, including pulmonary hypertension (PH) [5], heart failure (HF) [6], and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) [7, 8]. In these contexts, RV dysfunction consistently emerges as an independent predictor of adverse prognosis. Simultaneously, the TV, previously regarded as a passive structure, has been recognized as an active contributor to right-sided cardiac pathology. TR is now understood not merely as a consequence of RV disease but also as a driver of maladaptive RV remodeling, establishing a bidirectional interplay that amplifies disease progression [9].

Central to this is the self-reinforcing “RV-TR vicious cycle”, in which chronic volume overload from TR drives progressive RV and annular dilation, impaired contractility, and further worsening of regurgitation [10]. The ensuing hemodynamic derangements culminate in systemic venous congestion, clinically manifesting as jugular venous distension, ascites, peripheral edema, and end-organ failure, including cardiohepatic and cardiorenal syndromes. These systemic sequelae underscore the pivotal role of the RV in preserving global cardiovascular homeostasis and highlight its prognostic relevance in risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making [11]. This evolving understanding has also fueled the rapid development of transcatheter tricuspid valve Interventions (TTVI), shifting the clinical focus toward earlier detection and treatment of TR.

In this context, accurate evaluation of RV size and function is critical for monitoring disease progression and guiding therapeutic decision-making. This review highlights the role of echocardiography in the assessment of the RV in the setting of TR and TTVI. Particular emphasis is placed on the technical challenges posed by the retrosternal location of the RV, its complex geometry, and its pronounced load dependence. We further examine both established and emerging echocardiographic modalities, evaluating their value in quantifying RV performance and informing clinical management. Although cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and invasive hemodynamic assessment remain reference standards, echocardiography continues to serve as the first-line, widely accessible imaging tool. By synthesizing current evidence, this review aims to promote standardization in RV assessment and support evidence-based strategies for the management of right-sided cardiac disease.

Anatomy and physiology of the right ventricle

The RV is a thin-walled (3–5 mm), crescent-shaped chamber that partially encircles the thicker, more spherical LV [12]. Embryologically, the RV arises from precursor cells in the anterior heart field of the pharyngeal mesoderm, distinguishing it morphologically and functionally from the LV, which derives from the first heart field [13]. Anatomically, the RV comprises three distinct regions: the inlet (including the TV apparatus), the trabeculated apical myocardium, and the smooth-walled infundibulum or outflow tract [14]. Its unique geometry, coupled with its integration into the low-pressure pulmonary circulation, makes it particularly sensitive to changes in both preload and afterload [15].

RV mechanics are strongly influenced by myocardial fiber orientation. The majority of systolic shortening (~ 80%) is generated by subendocardial longitudinal fibers extending from the tricuspid annulus (TA) to the apex [14, 16]. In addition, approximately 25% of the RV wall is composed of outer circumferential fibers, which are continuous with the LV myocardium and contribute mainly to radial contraction. The contraction of the RV outflow tract (infundibulum) accounts for approximately 15% of the total stroke volume (SV). This contraction typically occurs 20–50 ms later than that of the RV apex, producing a peristaltic motion that facilitates smooth, laminar ejection of blood into the pulmonary circulation [17–19]. This contraction sequence is uniquely adapted to the low-resistance pulmonary circuit and contrasts with the helical contraction pattern observed in the LV, reflecting the distinct hemodynamic demands imposed on the two ventricles.

Although the RV comprises only ~ 30% of the myocardial mass of the LV, it is capable of generating an equivalent SV while operating with approximately half the energy expenditure per unit of force produced [20, 21]. This high efficiency reflects both its compliant geometry, which reduces wall stress in accordance with Laplace’s law, and the contribution of systolic ventricular interdependence, whereby LV contraction, via septal motion and shared myocardial fibers, augments up to 30% of RV stroke output [22]. Equally important, however, is diastolic interdependence, where RV dilatation within the fixed pericardial space can restrict LV filling and thereby reduce overall cardiac output (CO) [4].

These structural properties render the RV particularly vulnerable to dysfunction through three principal mechanisms: pressure overload (e.g. pulmonary hypertension), volume overload (e.g. TR), and intrinsic myocardial disease (e.g. arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy), which often coexist [23]. In chronic overload states such as TR, the RV initially activates a series of compensatory mechanisms including eccentric hypertrophy to reduce wall stress, heterometric adaptation via the Frank-Starling mechanism, and increased contractility through the Anrep effect, an intrinsic augmentation of myocardial contractility in response to acute increases in afterload [24]. Over time, however, persistent hemodynamic stress exhausts these adaptive processes, leading to progressive RV dysfunction and failure. To describe this continuum, Santas et al. proposed a pragmatic staging system (grade 1–4) for RV dysfunction (RVD), integrating the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE)/pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) ratio with functional TR severity. This stepwise framework progresses from early, subtle impairment to advanced RV-pulmonary artery (PA) uncoupling with severe TR. This classification demonstrated a strong and graded association with mortality in HFpEF patients, with patients in stage 4 representing the highest-risk subgroup [25].

Given its pivotal role in preserving CO and hemodynamic stability, accurate assessment of RV function is of paramount importance. Yet, the inherently complex geometry of the RV, its strong dependence on ventricular interdependence, and its marked sensitivity to loading conditions make reliable evaluation particularly challenging, especially in dynamic clinical settings such as atrial fibrillation (AF) and valvular heart disease. To address these limitations, a multiparametric imaging strategy is recommended, integrating advanced techniques such as three-dimensional echocardiography (3DE). These approaches enhance precision of RV assessment, enable earlier detection of dysfunction, and are increasingly critical as novel therapeutic options for right heart disease continue to emerge.

Echocardiographic assessment of right ventricular function: beyond conventional metrics

Conventional two-dimensional (2D) transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the primary imaging modality for evaluating RV function owing to its widespread availability, bedside applicability, and ease of use. However, the diagnostic accuracy is often constrained by technical limitations in evaluating global RV function, including suboptimal acoustic windows, foreshortened imaging planes, and inadequate delineation of endocardial borders. These challenges are particularly pronounced in patients with arrhythmias or marked RV dilation. To address these limitations, current guidelines emphasize a multiparametric strategy that integrates several complementary indices of RV function. The TVARC recommends the following parameters and cut-off values to grade RV dysfunction severity: TAPSE (mild 14–17 mm, moderate 10–13 mm, severe < 10 mm), tissue Doppler S′ velocity (TDI S′: mild 9–11 cm/s, moderate 6–8 cm/s, severe < 6 cm/s), RV global longitudinal strain (GLS: mild 18–21%, moderate 14–17%, severe < 14%), RV free wall strain (FWS: mild 20–23%, moderate 15–19%, severe < 15%), fractional area change (FAC: mild 34–37%, moderate 30–33%, severe < 30%), and 3D RV ejection fraction (RVEF: mild 45–50%, moderate 35–45%, severe < 35%) [10, 26, 27]. Given the intrinsic load dependence of these indices, their reliability diminishes in dynamic states such as severe TR. Consequently, advanced imaging modalities are increasingly advocated to complement conventional echocardiography and improve diagnostic accuracy (Table 1).

Although each parameter carries independent prognostic value, their sensitivity to loading conditions may result in discordant findings [27, 28]. This significantly complicates both the interpretation of findings and the accurate staging of RV dysfunction. For instance, TAPSE and FAC primarily assess the longitudinal and radial components of RV contraction, respectively, but neither fully capture global RV performance. Consequently, discrepancies may occur, such as a reduced 3D RVEF with a preserved FAC (Fig. 1) [29, 30]. These inconsistencies underscore the limitations of relying on isolated measures and highlights the importance of comprehensive, integrated assessment. In line with this, combined reductions in TAPSE and FAC have been shown to be more strongly associated with adverse outcomes than isolated impairment of TAPSE with preserved FAC [11]

In a large multicenter cohort, Tolvaj et al. confirmed these limitations by showing that conventional 2D parameters (TAPSE, FAC, free wall longitudinal strain (FWLS)) demonstrated only modest concordance with 3D RVEF, with misclassification rates reaching up to 49% for FAC. Crucially, patients reclassified by 3D RVEF as having RV dysfunction despite normal conventional indices had a more than fourfold increased risk of death, underscoring the superior prognostic accuracy of 3D RVEF with a cutoff value of < 45%. Among conventional measures, FWLS exhibited the strongest agreement with RVEF (AUC 0.877), yet outcomes were poorest when ≥ 2 parameters were impaired. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for a multiparametric strategy when 3D imaging is unavailable and highlight the importance of pathology-specific standardization, given the variability in reclassification across subgroups [31]

Diastolic evaluation of the right ventricle

Assessment of RV diastolic function remains relatively underexplored compared with systolic evaluation. Current approaches primarily rely on Doppler-derived indices such as the tricuspid inflow E/A ratio, E/e’ ratio, and hepatic venous flow patterns. However, these measures are highly preload-dependent and are confounded by arrhythmias, limiting their reliability in dynamic clinical conditions [27, 28, 32]. Speckle-tracking–derived strain analysis has emerged as a promising alternative, providing insights into myocardial relaxation that appear less influenced by loading conditions. However, these parameters have no yet been validated against invasive hemodynamic measurements, limiting their clinical applicability.

Growing evidence underscores the pivotal contribution of right atrial (RA) size and function in regulating RV filling, particularly in older populations where loss of the atrial contribution (“atrial kick”) becomes increasingly relevant. RA strain imaging, especially assessment of reservoir and conduit function, has demonstrated value as an early marker of RV diastolic dysfunction and carries independent prognostic significance [30, 33]. In atrial functional TR secondary to AF, the loss of coordinated RA contraction further compromises hemodynamics, underscoring the importance of integrating RA mechanics into the evaluation of RV diastolic function. In these contexts, RA and RV stiffness, as well as filling pressures, have been identified as predictors of adverse outcomes [34].

Despite these advances, the complex interplay among RA function, RV stiffness, filling pressures, and diastolic performance remains incompletely understood. Future work should prioritize the standardization of strain-based metrics, the establishment of normative reference values across diverse populations, and validation against invasively measured hemodynamic parameters in different disease states, in order to optimize their application in both clinical practice and research.

Advancing beyond geometric assumptions

Conventional 2D echocardiographic metrics rely on simplified geometric models that fail to capture the RV’s complex crescentic anatomy (Figure 2). As a result, these conventional indices show only modest agreement with 3DE-derived RVEF, often resulting in substantial reclassification of RV systolic function [31]. The RV-focused apical view, introduced in 2010, improved accuracy by better aligning with RV geometry [35]. However, even this optimized view excludes important anatomical regions, most notably the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), which accounts for approximately 25% of total RV volume [36]. Furthermore, the true RV apex is frequently foreshortened or excluded in 2D imaging planes, making it unidentifiable with conventional echocardiography and thereby limiting accurate functional and volumetric assessment (see figure 2) [37].

Limitations of 2D Echocardiography in Right Ventricular Evaluation: The complex 3D geometry of the right ventricle (green surface) - including the tricuspid valve (TV) and inflow tract on the left, and the pulmonary valve (PV) and outflow tract on the right - is not fully visualized using conventional 2D echocardiographic planes such as the apical four-chamber (A4C) and parasternal short-axis (PSAX) views. These standard views fail to capture the full spatial orientation and structure of the right ventricle (RV).

Speckle-tracking echocardiography, in both 2D or 3D applications, provides direct quantification of myocardial deformation [30]. Although this technique offers important advantages, its accuracy still depends on adequate acoustic windows and clear wall visualization; thus, geometric constraints are not entirely eliminated [30]. Among deformation parameters, FWLS and RV GLS provide relatively load-independent measures that more directly reflects intrinsic myocardial dysfunction. These indices demonstrate good feasibility and strong prognostic value for the early detection of RV impairment [38–40]. Nonetheless, the lack of standardized acquisition protocols and the persistence of vendor-specific variability continue to limit widespread clinical implementation. Similarly, the RV index of myocardial performance (RIMP), a Doppler-derived parameter that integrates systolic and diastolic time intervals, provides a relatively load-independent global functional assessment. However, RIMP does not account for the RV’s multidirectional contraction mechanics and therefore cannot fully characterize the complexity of RV function [41].

dP/Dt Evaluation

RV dP/dt, derived from the continuous-wave Doppler TR signal, provides a simple, non-invasive surrogate of RV contractility in patients with measurable, high-quality TR envelopes. Typically calculated from the time for TR velocity to increase from 1 to 2 m/s, a value >400 mmHg/s correlates with preserved CMR-derived RVEF, with improved accuracy when indexed to peak TR velocity (dP/dt/Vmax) to mitigate preload dependence. In pulmonary hypertension, a dP/dt<410 mmHg/s independently predicted mortality [42]. To address load dependence, Kanzaki et al. proposed the dP/dt/Pressure(max) index, which declines progressively with worsening NYHA functional class, outperforming dP/dt(max). When obtainable, dP/dt, particularly dP/dt/Pressure(max), offers incremental prognostic and functional information beyond conventional RV indices [43].

Cardiac magnetic resonance

CMR remains the reference standard for RV volumetric and functional assessment, providing high reproducibility, true 3D visualization without geometric assumptions, and the ability to characterize myocardial tissue. It is particularly valuable for detecting subtle RV dysfunction, quantifying regurgitant volumes, and assessing myocardial fibrosis. Nonetheless, its clinical application in severe TR is constrained by practical and patient-related factors. Limitations include restricted availability, cost, long acquisition times, and contraindications such as non-MRI-conditional cardiac devices, advanced renal dysfunction, or severe claustrophobia. Additionally, arrhythmias and respiratory motion may degrade image quality. Importantly, CMR is unable to directly quantify effective RVEF, which remains a critical limitation in advanced TR.

3D Echocardiography: volumetric accuracy and emerging insights

3DE has revolutionized the evaluation of the RV by enabling direct, assumption-free volumetric quantification. Novel software solutions, such as the Ventripoint system, allow reconstruction of a 3D model of the RV from conventional 2D views and anatomical landmarks via knowledge-based reconstruction, providing volumetric and functional metrics comparable to CMR but with greater speed, accessibility, and reduced patient burden (Figure 3). When optimized acquisition protocols are applied, feasibility of 3D imaging in routine clinical practice reaches 85-90% [44–46]. Measurements of RV volumes and RVEF obtained by 3DE demonstrate strong agreement with CMR, the reference standard [47–49], although accuracy is influenced by image quality, rhythm stability, and operator experience.

Normative reference ranges defined by Muraru et al. classify RVEF as normal (>45%), mildly reduced (40-45%), moderately reduced (30-40%), and severely reduced (<30%) [50]. Importantly, sex-, age-, and ethnicity-related differences are evident. Men typically exhibit larger indexed RV volumes (≤87 mL/m² compared to ≤74 mL/m² in women), whereas women demonstrate higher RV FAC and RVEF values [27, 44, 45, 51]. Similarly, 3DE-derived TA measurements reveal that men have significantly larger TA areas than women at end-diastole (10.4 ± 2.2 cm² vs. 9.1 ± 1.7 cm²), with differences persisting even after indexing for body surface area (BSA). These sex-specific differences have important implications for the timing and selection of treatment strategies. Notably, conventional 2D echocardiography systematically underestimates TA size and fails to capture its complex elliptical shape, underscoring the superiority of 3DE, also for TV assessment [52].

Sex-Specific prognostic differences

Beyond anatomical variation, sex also influences the prognostic significance of RV and TV parameters. Tomaselli et al. identified sex-specific cutoffs for secondary TR (STR), demonstrating that women had comparable risks of death or heart failure hospitalization (HFH) at lower EROAs (0.36 vs. 0.43 cm²) and regurgitant volumes (31 vs. 35 mL) than men, despite having smaller RV volumes (end-diastolic volume (EDV) 81 vs. 96 mL/m²; end-systolic volume (ESV) 37 vs. 49 mL/m²) and annular dimensions. Interestingly, women exhibited adverse outcomes at a higher RVEF threshold (49% vs. 41%). Prognostic clinical determinants also diverged: in women, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, chronic kidney disease, RV volumes, and tricuspid tenting volume independently predicted outcomes, whereas in men, RA volume and RVEF were more predictive [53]. These findings reinforce the need for sex-specific interpretation of RV parameters for accurate risk stratification.

The World Alliance of Societies of Echocardiography (WASE) study refined the understanding of RV mechanics by quantifying longitudinal, radial, and anteroposterior contributions to global RVEF with 3DE. In >1,000 healthy subjects, normal reference ranges were established, revealing consistent demographic patterns: women had higher RVEF and strain values, aging was marked by reduced longitudinal shortening partly offset by increased radial contraction in women, and Black subjects, particularly men, exhibited lower RVEF with a distinctive shift toward radial rather than longitudinal motion. These data highlight that RV performance extends beyond longitudinal shortening alone, with compensatory adaptations across sex, age, and race. Clinically, the adequacy of TAPSE and similar indices as surrogates of global function is limited, supporting instead a multidimensional 3DE approach. Although current feasibility is constrained by stitching artifacts, wall dropout, and reduced accuracy in dilated ventricles, continued technical advances are expected to expand its clinical adoption [54].

Myocardial work: a paradigm shift in load-adjusted assessment

Emerging indices such as RV myocardial work (MW) integrate myocardial deformation with non-invasively pressure-strain relationships, providing a load-adjusted assessment of ventricular performance and a closer approximation to RV contractility than strain alone. RV global myocardial work efficiency (GWE) and wasted work (GWW) demonstrated strong correlations with prognostic hemodynamic indices in PH and TR, outperforming the discriminatory capacity of conventional parameters such as TAPSE and TAPSE/PASP ratio [55].

Fabian et al. introduced a 3DE-derived pressure-strain-volume loop model that incorporates instantaneous RV volumetric data, enabling calculation of a volume-adjusted global myocardial work index (GMWIV). In STR, GMWIV emerged as an independent predictor of mortality, unlike the standard global myocardial work index (GMWI, area under the pressure-strain loop), suggesting its potential to detect subclinical RV dysfunction in volume-overload states [56].

Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) have helped mitigate residual load dependency in RV functional assessment. Szijártó et al. providing a multilayer model capable of reconstructing RV pressure curves from peak echocardiographic pressures, thereby enabling afterload-adjusted pressure-strain loop analysis [57]. In parallel, Nonaka et al. synthesized current evidence, showing that RV MW exhibits closer agreement with invasive load-independent indices, such as stroke work index, than conventional strain parameters or TAPSE. Collectively, these findings support RV MW as a reliable biomarker of RV dysfunction across a spectrum of complex loading conditions, including significant TR [58].

RV-PA coupling: assessment and clinical implications

RV-PA coupling describes the efficiency with which the RV transfers energy to the pulmonary circulation and represents a fundamental determinant of right heart performance. The reference standard for its assessment is right heart catheterization with pressure-volume loop analysis, which enables quantification of end-systolic elastance (Ees, an index of RV contractility) and effective arterial elastance (Ea, an index of afterload). The ratio of Ees to Ea provides an integrated measure of RV-PA interaction, with values <1.0 indicative of RV-PA uncoupling. Such uncoupling reflects impaired ventricular-vascular interaction, is associated with hemodynamic deterioration, and has been consistently linked to adverse clinical outcomes [59].

Non-invasive echocardiographic assessment of RV-PA Coupling

Given the invasive nature of pressure-volume loop analysis, several echocardiographic surrogates of RV-PA coupling have been proposed. Among the most studied are the TAPSE/PASP ratio and the 3D SV/ESV ratio. Both indices have demonstrated superior prognostic value compared with conventional measures of RV function, such as RVEF [60, 61]. However, important limitations exist. TAPSE primarily reflects longitudinal shortening and may not capture global RV contractility, while PASP estimation can be inaccurate in the setting of severe TR, where low-velocity regurgitant jets tend to underestimate PASP (PASP=4(Vmax)2+RA pressure). In such cases, alternative Doppler-based surrogates, including hepatic vein flow patterns and liver stiffness assessment, provide better qualitative insights into RA pressure [62]. Additional echocardiographic parameters, such as shortened RVOT acceleration time (≤105 ms) and PA dilation (>25 mm at end-diastole), are also associated with elevated PA pressures and increased RV afterload, however, their reliability is reduced in AF due to beat-to-beat variability [26]. It is also important to note that RV-PA coupling involves a diastolic component, reflecting both RV diastolic compliance and PA diastolic pressure. This aspect of ventricular-vascular interaction remains poorly understood, as current Doppler-derived indices lack validation against invasive pressure-volume loop analysis.

Stress-based assessment of RV-PA Coupling

RV-PA coupling can also be evaluated under dynamic hemodynamic conditions. Exercise echocardiography, using the ratio of TAPSE to PASPnon-invasive, provides a practical and non-invasive approach for assessing RV contractile reserve. This method allows the detection of subclinical RV dysfunction that may not be apparent at rest, particularly in patients with reduced afterload, such as those with severe TR [63]. Integration of stress-based RV-PA coupling into clinical assessment may refine patient selection for TTVI and inform therapeutic strategies. This approach is particularly valuable in patients with TR and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), as it unmasks latent RV dysfunction by replicating the hemodynamic stress of increased preload.

Novel indices of RV-PA Coupling

A recently proposed RV-PA coupling index that integrates RVFWLS, 3D-RVEDV, and invasively measured PASP (RVFWLS/[3D-RVEDV∗PASPinvasive]) has demonstrated superior prognostic accuracy compared with conventional non-invasive RV-PA coupling parameters, particularly in predicting 1-year mortality following TTVI (C-index: 0.68 vs 0.57) [64]. In a large cohort of TR patients, Ancone et al. further reported that RVFWLS/PASP was independently associated with both all-cause mortality and RV heart failure. Importantly, an RVFWLS/PASP threshold of >0.26%/mmHg identified patients with improved survival and outperformed both TAPSE/PASP and RVFWLS alone for prognostic stratification [65].

Comprehensive assessment of RV function requires a multimodal echocardiographic approach. 2D imaging provides a rapid initial overview, whereas 3D volumetric analysis and myocardial strain imaging offer superior diagnostic accuracy. Dynamic assessment of RV-PA coupling further contextualizes RV performance within the pulmonary circulation. In addition, emerging techniques, including pressure-strain loop analysis, stress echocardiography, vendor-neutral standardization, and machine learning-based automation, hold promise for improving early detection, prognostic stratification, and personalized management of RV dysfunction [66].

Tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction: pathophysiology and clinical implications

Severe TR exerts a profound and dynamic impact on RV structure and function through complex pathophysiologic interactions that complicate both diagnostic evaluation and prognostic assessment. The RV, physiologically adapted to the low-resistance pulmonary circulation, is highly vulnerable to both chronic volume and pressure overload. In advanced TR, persistent volume overload, frequently compounded by concomitant PH, and RV pressure overload, initiates a maladaptive remodeling cascade that progressively impairs RV performance. In the early stages, the RV compensates through eccentric hypertrophy and the recruitment of contractile reserve, maintain forward stroke volume via both homeometric (Anrep effect) and heterometric (Frank–Starling) mechanisms. As disease advances, these compensatory adaptations become exhausted: longitudinal shortening and systolic function decline, while circumferential and radial contraction transiently increase as a compensatory mechanism. Ultimately, this cascade culminates in progressive RV dysfunction, accompanied by RV dilation and worsening of TR. These changes drive escalating systemic venous congestion with symptoms that become increasingly refractory to diuretic therapy [67–69].

Persistent volume and pressure overload in severe TR drive maladaptive structural and functional changes, including progressive RV dilation, RA “ventriculization,” elevation of RA and PA pressures, and ultimately RV–PA uncoupling. Clinically, these alterations manifest as systemic venous congestion and overt signs of right heart failure (RHF), such as jugular venous distension, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema, and biochemical derangements including elevated NT-proBNP and liver transaminases [67]. Soler et al. showed that while NT-proBNP predicted mortality in acute heart failure without severe TR, its prognostic value was lost in severe TR, where CA125 remained an independent and more robust predictor, particularly in the setting of RV dysfunction and systemic congestion [70].

To provide a standardized approach, Dietz et al. proposed a four-stage classification of RHF (Stages 1-4) based on the severity of RV dysfunction and clinical presentation. Higher stages were strongly associated with increased five-year mortality, offering a pragmatic tool to support prognostic assessment and to inform the timing of intervention [8]. Complementing this, Lahm et al. introduced a conceptual framework for staging RV failure (Stages A-D), analogous to left-sided heart failure models. This framework delineates disease progression from compensated remodeling to advanced decompensation: Stage A identifies individuals at risk; Stage B reflects asymptomatic structural or functional abnormalities; Stage C involves symptomatic RV failure; and Stage D corresponds to refractory, end-stage disease. Importantly, this model emphasizes the dynamic and potentially reversible nature of early RV disease, reinforcing the rationale for timely recognition and intervention [71].

Beyond hemodynamic and mechanical stressors, maladaptive RV remodeling is increasingly recognized to involve complex molecular and cellular mechanisms, including myocardial fibrosis, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming. These pathobiological processes may contribute to persistent or progressive RV dysfunction even after TTVI, emphasizing the need for more refined strategies of risk stratification, patient selection, and longitudinal monitoring [71].

Diastolic Function of RV Remodeling in TR

Diastolic dysfunction is often an underappreciated aspect of RV function, yet it represents a clinically meaningful component of RV remodeling in TR. RV diastolic dysfunction, characterized by decreased compliance and relaxation leading to elevated filling pressures, is an independent contributor to RHF. Diastolic functional indices, such as RA strain have demonstrated significant prognostic relevance in TR, supporting the integration of diastolic assessment into routine RV evaluation [33, 72].

TR Phenotypes and RV Dysfunction

According to the TVARC consensus, STR can be broadly classified into two phenotypes, atrial (A-STR) and ventricular (V-STR), which differ in pathophysiology, remodeling patterns, and prognostic implications [10]. The atrial phenotype (A-STR) arises primarily from RA dilation and TA enlargement, commonly in the setting of AF or HFpEF. In contrast, the ventricular phenotype (V-STR) is characterized by leaflet tethering, leaflet restriction, and RV dilation and dysfunction, typically in association with pulmonary hypertension and left-sided valvular or myocardial disease. Among these, V-STR carries a less favorable prognosis, as it perpetuates a cycle of worsening regurgitation, maladaptive RV remodeling, and progressive RV dysfunction [73].

Patients with A-STR generally exhibit more favorable structural and functional characteristics, including smaller leaflet tenting height, preserved longitudinal RV function (higher TAPSE and RVFWLS), and more favorable chamber geometry. Clinically, these features translate into a lower rate of HFH and all-cause mortality compared with V-STR. Distinct remodeling patterns further differentiate the two phenotypes: A-STR is generally associated with conical RV geometry, whereas V-STR is characterized by spherical remodeling with midventricular dilatation, a configuration that exacerbates RV inefficiency and loss of contractile reserve [74]. This distortion of the native geometry increases wall stress in accordance with Laplace’s law, further reduces contractile performance, and induces interventricular septal flattening. The resultant septal shift produces the characteristic D-shaped LV, impairs LV diastolic filling, reduces SV, and ultimately compromises CO [75].

From a prognostic perspective, the determinants of adverse outcomes vary according to phenotype: in A-STR, TR severity is the dominant determinant, whereas in V-STR, both the severity of TR and the extent of RV dysfunction independently predict clinical outcomes [76, 77]. Quantitative assessment of TR offers further refinement of risk stratification: Tomaselli et al. demonstrated that an effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) >0.47 cm² (0.56 cm² in severe TR) was independently associated with a two-fold increase in death or HFH. Among patients categorized as “low-risk” based on EROA (≤ 0.47 cm²), outcomes were instead predominantly determined by RV remodeling and dysfunction, as characterized using 3D imaging. Advanced 3DE measures of RV size, systolic function, and RV–PA coupling were able to identify patients within the lower EROA group who experienced event rates comparable to those seen in patients with higher EROA. This argues for a comprehensive 3D evaluation of RV structure and function, integrated with quantitative TR assessment, to achieve more accurate risk stratification [78].

Assessment Challenges and Advanced imaging

RV assessment in TR is difficult, as conventional indices such as TAPSE and FAC are load-dependent and tend to overestimate function by masking intrinsic dysfunction through regurgitant afterload reduction [79].

Speckel-tracking echocardiography-derived RVFWLS provides a more sensitive marker of RV function, with strong correlation to clinical outcomes [38, 39]. Another advanced and clinically valuable imaging parameter in the setting of TR is the 3DE-derived effective RV ejection fraction (eRVEF). It is calculated as the ratio of effective SV (i.e. total SV minus TR regurgitant volume, determined by PISA) to EDV. An eRVEF < 20% has been shown to independently predict mortality [80]. Importantly, while eRVEF decreases in parallel with TR severity, conventional RVEF does not significantly differ across TR grades [81].

Contractile Patterns and Clinical Implications

RV contraction patterns provide important insights into disease severity, reversibility, and therapeutic decision-making. A compensated pattern, characterized by preserved RVEF, increased circumferential strain despite impaired longitudinal function, has been associated with more favorable outcomes [67, 82]. In contrast, a decompensated pattern with reductions in both longitudinal and circumferential strain, likely reflects irreversible remodeling. This maladaptive stage is linked to adverse outcomes, with one-year mortality approaching 50%, the highest PASP, and markedly elevated NT-proBNP levels [67, 79]. At this advanced stage, TTVI may no longer provide meaningful clinical benefit

Given the complex loading conditions in TR, conventional indices often underestimate true RV performance. Novel echocardiographic measures, including RV myocardial work (derived from pressure-strain loops) and indices of RV–PA coupling (e.g., TAPSE/PASP and SV/ESV ratios), have demonstrated stronger associations with clinical outcomes in this population.

RV dysfunction and TR are also highly prevalent in patients with HFpEF, affecting approximately one-third of cases when defined by a FAC of <35%. Importantly, RV dysfunction in HFpEF represents an independent and powerful predictor of mortality, irrespective of LV function or PA pressures [83]. Recent registry data have also demonstrated sex-specific differences: women with HFpEF exhibit a higher prevalence of moderate-to-severe TR compared with men, in whom TR is more commonly observed in the setting of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Severe TR was particularly predictive of mortality in women with HFpEF and in men with HFrEF, while it was linked to higher HFH rates in both sexes [84]. In severe TR, impaired RVFWLS (> −21.5%) identifies early RV dysfunction and confers a six-fold increased risk of HFH or death, outperforming conventional echocardiographic parameters [85]. Furthermore, in patients with moderate or greater TR, the pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi), calculated as the ratio of PA pulse pressure to RA pressure, serves as a robust non-invasive marker of RV dysfunction. A PAPi <4 is independently associated with increased mortality, while values <2 identify a particularly high-risk subgroup, even in the absence of PH [86].

Hemodynamic assessment adds a critical layer of complexity in evaluating patients with TR. Importantly, patients with invasively confirmed PH, but falsely low echocardiographic estimates have been shown to experience the worst outcomes after intervention (T-TEER). This finding highlights two key points: first, echocardiography alone has limited accuracy for defining PA pressures in severe TR; and second, reliance on non-invasive echocardiographic estimates may lead to inappropriate patient selection. Integrating invasive right heart catheterization with echocardiography provides complementary information, and improves diagnostic precision. For clinicians, a multimodal approach to hemodynamic evaluation should be viewed as essential, not optional, in the work-up of patients considered for T-TEER [87].

Advanced echocardiographic techniques, particularly speckle-tracking and 3D imaging, play a pivotal role in the early detection of subclinical RV dysfunction in patients with TR. By providing metrics that are less load-dependent and more reflective of intrinsic myocardial contractility, and by incorporating dynamic RV-PA coupling indices, these techniques enable a more accurate evaluation of true RV function in TR. This also allows for the early identification of maladaptive contraction patterns, supports more accurate patient selection, and optimizes the timing of intervention, ultimately improving long-term outcomes in this high-risk population

Periprocedural echocardiographic assessment of RV function in TTVI

RV dysfunction, as formalized in the TRI-SCORE risk model, is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality and HFH following tricuspid valve surgery [88]. However, this model relies on conventional measures of RV function, such as TAPSE. More recently, Ancona et al. demonstrated that RVFWLS provides incremental prognostic value and significantly improves the predictive accuracy of the TRI-SCORE in estimating peri-operative mortality among patients undergoing isolated tricuspid valve surgery [89]. These findings underscore the importance of advanced RV phenotyping to improve risk stratification and optimize treatment planning.

The limitation of conventional echocardiographic indices are also evident in the setting of TTVI. Fortmeier et al. showed that extracardiac and hemodynamic markers, including mPAP, NT-proBNP, RA area, and eGFR, were stronger predictors of two-year mortality than traditional RV indices [90]. By contrast, advanced measure, particularly 3D parameters, correlate more strongly with RV reverse remodeling (RVRR) and clinical outcomes after TTVI [81, 91, 92].

A more nuanced view of RV function has been provided by Schlotter et al., who demonstrated a U-shaped, non-linear relationship between baseline RV performance and outcomes after TTVI. In their multicentre, propensity-matched analysis, the reduction in mortality after TTVI was not uniform across all levels of RV function. Patients with mid-range dysfunction (TAPSE 13–17 mm) derived the clearest advantage, achieving 1-year survival comparable to those with preserved function. In contrast, patients with markedly reduced function (TAPSE <13 mm) or preserved function (TAPSE >17 mm) did not experience a significant reduction in mortality. These findings suggest that patients with incipient RV dysfunction represent the subgroup most likely to benefit from TTVI, a population that might be overlooked when using conventional binary thresholds of RV function [93].

In contrast, survival analyses from Vogelhuber et al. demonstrated a clear divergence based on baseline RV function. Patients with preserved RV function (RVFAC ≥35%, TAPSE ≥17 mm) experienced significantly better long-term survival, despite showing transient reductions in functional indices after transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER). Conversely, those with impaired RV function at baseline (RVFAC <35% and TAPSE <17 mm) faced markedly higher 2-year mortality (27.0% vs. 56.3%), even though their RV parameters did not deteriorate following TEER. This pattern suggests a "ceiling effect": once advanced RV dysfunction is established, the prognostic benefit of intervention is substantially attenuated, particularly when longitudinal and circumferential impairment coexist. Accordingly, timely identification of RV impairment is critical, as therapeutic efficacy appears to diminish once maladaptive remodeling has progressed beyond a reversible threshold [11, 67, 94].

In light of these observations, advanced 3DE should be regarded as a key component of preprocedural planning of TTVI. Weckbach et al. demonstrated that achieving a reduction in TR by at least two grades to ≤1+ was associated with significant RVRR and improved survival [95]. Moreover, 3DE provides clinically relevant thresholds, with a 3D-derived RVEF <45% consistently associated with adverse outcomes [96]. Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of advanced imaging modalities in guiding therapeutic strategies for patients undergoing TTVI.

Hemodynamic Adaptation Following TTVI

TTVI induces significant hemodynamic and mechanical changes that complicate the conventional evaluation of RV systolic function. The acute post-procedural changes in loading conditions, characterized by reduced RV preload and increased afterload, often result in transient declines in TAPSE, FAC, and RVFWLS. Importantly, these reductions do not necessarily signify intrinsic RV contractile impairment, but instead reflect normalization of loading conditions and have not been associated with worse outcome [11, 97]. Tanaka et al. demonstrated the prognostic importance of RV function in patients undergoing T-TEER. Patients with baseline RV dysfunction who showed improvement in RV FAC after T-TEER (“RV responders”) experienced superior clinical outcomes. In contrast, low baseline RV FAC was a strong predictor of adverse prognosis. Responders were characterized by greater TR reduction and smaller baseline RV dimensions, underscoring the prognostic significance of achieving complete TR resolution [98].

Beyond these acute functional changes, accumulating evidence points to structural remodeling of the RV, referred to as RVRR, as a pivotal determinant of outcomes after TTVI.

An increasing body of evidence from advanced imaging studies (3DE and CMR) consistently demonstrates RVRR, a process characterized by reduction in RV chamber size, normalization of geometry, and partial functional recovery following TTVI (Table 2)[81, 96, 99]. In the early post-procedural phase, RV EDV typically decreases, while ESV remains stable, leading to a transient reduction in RVEF. This phase may also unmask pre-existing RV dysfunction previously obscured by chronic low afterload [81, 92].

Longitudinal data from Stolz et al. also suggested this biphasic remodeling pattern: normalization of RV EDV occurs first, followed by a delayed reduction in RV ESV. These structural changes are accompanied by improvements in eRVEF and RVOT SV, both associated with improved long-term survival [85, 92]. Achieving a reduction in TR of ≥70% appears to amplify these benefits, whereas residual TR ≥3+ impairs early remodeling and diminishes the survival advantage [95]. CMR studies further indicate that recovery of eRVEF is driven by structural remodeling rather than by increases in pulmonary flow. However, the relative prognostic importance of volumetric versus functional recovery remains to be clarified and represents a key target for future studies aimed at optimizing patient selection and therapeutic strategies [81].

RV-PA Coupling and prognostic implications

Although echocardiographically derived PASP has inherent limitations in accuracy, its widespread feasibility renders the TAPSE/PASP ratio a practical surrogate for assessing RV-PA coupling in TTVI patients. This parameter has emerged as a strong, independent predictor of survival. A baseline TAPSE/PASP ratio of >0.41, indicative of preserved RV contractile function, correlates with significantly lower one-year all-cause mortality. Interestingly, a decline in RV-PA coupling after TTVI, particularly in patients with higher baseline ratios, was linked to improved survival, suggesting the concept of sufficient afterload reserve in these patients. This decline was more pronounced in patients who achieved greater TR reduction, reflected by a corresponding decrease in the TAPSE/PASP ratio [60]. This phenomenon likely reflects an abrupt increase in RV afterload under more normalized loading conditions. Notably, such hemodynamic changes are consistently correlated with improved clinical outcomes [60, 99]. Nonetheless, it should be emphasized that TAPSE/PASP is a simplified surrogate and may not fully account for the complex ventricular-arterial interactions.

In a recent study, RV-PA coupling quantified by the ratio of RV free wall strain (RVFWS) to RV systolic pressure (RVSP), emerged as a powerful prognostic marker. A threshold of <0.43 %/mmHg identified patients with moderate or severe TR who had a two- to threefold higher mortality risk. When incorporated into the TRIO score (TRIO-RV), this index reclassified nearly 40% of patients from low- or intermediate- to high-risk, refining risk stratification and supporting earlier, targeted intervention [100].

RV-LV Interdependence and diastolic mechanics

TTVI exerts important effects on RV-LV interdependence through both mechanical and hemodynamic pathways. By reducing RV volume overload, TTVI alleviates pericardial restraint and increases LV transmural pressure, thereby facilitating more effective LV filling without raising filling pressures. Advanced imaging modalities, including 3DE and CT, have confirmed restoration of septal motion with resolution of the characteristic D-shaped LV, accompanied by increases in LV EDV and SV [30, 75]. These geometric and functional improvements reflect enhanced transmural pressure gradients and a rightward shift of the LV end-diastolic pressure-volume relationship, translating into more favorable diastolic properties. Importantly, attenuation of leftward septal bowing during early diastole improves early LV filling and breaks the cycle of adverse ventricular interaction. In patients with coexisting HFpEF, these changes are particularly meaningful, with up to one-third achieving normalization of LV filling pressures, a marker linked to improved prognosis [75, 79].

Despite these benefits, increased RV forward flow after TTVI may transiently elevate NT-proBNP levels or unmask subclinical LV dysfunction, particularly in A-STR patients with prevalent HFpEF.

An invasive exercise study by Baratto et al. highlight the pathophysiological role of severe secondary STR in HFpEF. Their study demonstrated that HFpEF patients with severe STR exhibit markedly reduced forward SV and CO compared with matched HFpEF controls. Although these patients had higher left-sided filling pressures at rest, they failed to achieve an exercise-induced rise in LV transmural pressure, consistent with absent preload reserve and relative LV underfilling. This reflects the constraining effect of elevated RA pressures and right-sided congestion on LV diastolic mechanics. These findings suggest that severe STR, when superimposed on HFpEF, undermines the typical HFpEF pathophysiology by promoting RA hypertension, pulmonary vascular derecruitment, and LV underfilling [101].

Another important marker of RV-LV interdependence is the LV eccentricity index, which reflects ventricular interaction and carries prognostic relevance after TTVI. Tanaka et al. reported that a baseline index ≥1.2 predicts increased mortality and HFH, whereas post-procedural improvement is associated with more favorable outcomes [102].

Of note, the acute increase in forward SV following TTVI may transiently exacerbate mitral regurgitation, and whether a stable hemodynamic equilibrium is subsequently achieved remains uncertain. While these findings underscore the systemic impact of TTVI on biventricular mechanics, the long-term consequences of altered RV-LV interdependence, including the interplay between TR reduction, LV dysfunction, and MR progression, require further investigation to refine therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Accurate assessment of RV size and function in severe TR and TTVI remains challenging due to the complex geometry and load dependence of the RV. While TTE is the first-line imaging modality, conventional 2D parameters lack the sensitivity to detect early or regional dysfunction, risking underestimation of RV disease severity. Advanced imaging techniques, particularly 3D echocardiography analysis, offer promising improvements in accuracy and reproducibility, showing strong agreement with CMR, the reference standard for volumetric RV assessment [103, 104]. However, clinical implementation remains limited by challenges including suboptimal image quality (with only 50% of studies yielding analyzable data), limited visualization of RV segments, and low operator confidence, with fewer than 20% of clinicians routinely using 3D or strain imaging despite widespread availability (in 90% of echo labs) [54, 105, 106].

Moreover, the lack of standardized definitions of RV dysfunction across different clinical contexts, such as in the context of severe TR, combined with the absence of sex- and age-specific reference values, hampers consistent clinical decision-making and individualized risk stratification [71]. Prognostic thresholds for subclinical dysfunction and the hemodynamic consequences of residual TR post-TTVI also remain poorly defined. A particularly important unresolved issue is the identification of a “point of no return” in RV remodeling, beyond which the potential benefits of TTVI may be markedly attenuated. This will be critical for optimizing patient selection and procedural timing. In addition, clarifying the relative prognostic impact of TR severity versus intrinsic RV dysfunction, both pre- and post-TTVI, could further refine therapeutic strategies and improve outcome prediction.

Moving forward, key priorities include the standardization of imaging protocols, validating emerging analytic tools, such as AI-driven analysis, across diverse populations, and the development of multiparametric, outcome-driven algorithms that capture the dynamic interplay among RV function, TR severity, and biventricular remodeling. Addressing these gaps through translational research and structured clinician education will be essential to integrate advanced RV assessment into routine workflows and improve long-term outcomes in this high-risk patient population.
