ReDDiT: Rehashing Noise for Discrete Visual Generation
Tianren Ma 1 Xiaosong Zhang 1 Boyu Yang 2 Junlan Feng 2 Qixiang Ye 1

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models are gaining traction in the visual generative area for their efficiency and compatibility. However, the pioneered attempts still fall behind the continuous counterparts, which we attribute to the noise (absorbing state) design and sampling heuristics. In this study, we propose the rehashing noise framework for discrete diffusion transformer (termed ReDDiT), to extend absorbing states and improve expressive capacity of discrete diffusion models. ReDDiT enriches the potential paths that latent variables traverse during training with randomized multi-index corruption. The derived rehash sampler, which reverses the randomized absorbing paths, guarantees the diversity and low discrepancy of the generation process. These reformulations lead to more consistent and competitive generation quality, mitigating the need for heavily tuned randomness. Experiments show that ReDDiT significantly outperforms the baseline (reducing gFID from 6.18 to 1.61) and is on par with the continuous counterparts with higher efficiency.

Despite the popularity of DiTs, the community shows a growing interest in discrete diffusion models in recent years

Early endeavors

[

Recent studies
[

improved the generation quality, demonstrating great potential of visual generation in discrete domain.

Despite the progress, discrete diffusion methods remain lagging behind their continuous counterparts.
State-of-the-art approaches,

[

which restricts the model’s expressive capacity and makes the prediction performance sensitive to adaptions even with extensive training, Fig.

.

To address these limitations, we propose two key hypotheses.
First, while previous methods learn to recover plausible tokens from a monotonous
[mask]
canvas, this noise design may not be well-suited for discrete visual generation.
In continuous diffusion, Gaussian noise is used to progressively degrade the input to learn a smooth distribution shift
[
15
,
22
]
.
Discrete masking mimics this paradigm by collapsing all masked tokens into a single absorbing state, which however, lacks the variability of Gaussian noise, in terms of both vocabulary richness and latent diversity.
Consequently, the discrete process offers a far coarser signal, which limits its ability to capture and represent diverse data distributions
[
33
,
1
]
.
Moreover, while continuous diffusion models introduce stochasticity at every inference step through noise injection, discrete unmasking is inherently binary: tokens are either masked or deterministically decoded. This rigid mechanism further constrains the flexibility of sample refinement during generation.

Second, the predict-remask sampler of MVTMs introduces a form of handcrafted randomness, which is often implemented using Gumbel-max, to approximate sampling diversity.
However, this sampler may compromise the probabilistic fidelity of the generation, and the need to carefully balance the token numbers decoded per step to mitigate accumulation errors leads to redundant sampling passes.
As a result, the Gumbel-max has evolved to a heavily tuned time variant trick, leading to unstable performance, particularly when scaled to large-vocabulary codebooks.
The above factors, rather than quantization alone, attribute to the performance gap between discrete and continous models.

In this study, we propose a discrete diffusion model with an elaborate rehashing noise design, Fig.
1
(lower).
Our approach, termed
ReDDiT
, addresses the limitations of the uni-mask design by redefining absorbing states towards larger representational capacity, through enriching the potential paths that latent variables can traverse during diffusion.
Specifically, we expand the masks to multiple indices along with the codebook and randomize them during data corruption.
A rehash sampler is also derived with principled discrete diffusion theories to reverse the diffusion path for generation, guaranteeing the diversity and low discrepancy of the sampling process.
We demonstrate that this rehashed noise helps learning a superior and flatter lower error bound while eliminating reliance to hyper-parameterized randomness during sampling.

We further revisit the commonly used discrete diffusion objective (
e.g.
, the masked cross-entropy loss) and update it with empirical modifications.
By adopting an improved ELBO
[
31
,
36
]
with representation alignment (RepA)
[
46
]
loss, we achieve better training efficiency and generation quality.
Moreover, ReDDiT aligns with recent advances in discrete flow matching
[
11
,
34
]
, enabling token refreshment during sampling without training post-correction models
[
19
]
.

Diffusion Models.

Diffusion models

.

Recent studies unlocked the principled discrete diffusion model (DDM)

Unlike MVTMs, the principled DDM and DFM mostly derive a time-weighted cross-entropy loss to supervise the training procedure and apply a gradual unmasking method based on probabilities.

3.1
Preliminary: Discrete Diffusion Model

DDM defines a forward process over discrete variables by gradually corrupting the image tokens into absorbing states (masks) through a continuous-time Markov process.
Assume that the data consists of tokens from a finite vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
,
x\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
represents a sequence of tokens (
e.g.
, an image tokenized into indices) with length
L
. We denote the clean data as
x_{t=0}
(
x_{0}
for short), and noise it gradually as
t\rightarrow 1
.
DDM defines an absorbing token
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{V}
, such that once a token is noised to
\mathbf{m}
it remains unchanged.
At the terminal time
t=1
,
x_{t}
fully transits to
\mathbf{m}^{L}
, which means
x_{1}^{i=1\sim L}=\mathbf{m}
.

Let
\alpha_{t}
be the noise scheduler (a monotonically decreasing survival function that satisfies
\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=0
).
For
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, denote
\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}=\alpha_{t}/\alpha_{s}
, the forward corruption process is governed by a continuous-time transition kernel
q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{s}^{i})
on the
i
-th element, as

q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{s}^{i})=\begin{cases}1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow},&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{m},x_{s}^{i}\neq\mathbf{m},\\
\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow},&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}=x_{s}^{i},x_{s}^{i}\neq\mathbf{m},\\
1,&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}=x_{s}^{i},x_{s}^{i}=\mathbf{m},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

Given the transition kernel, the corrupted data distribution at time
t
is determined
[
31
]
as

x_{t}\sim\alpha_{t}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{m}^{L}

(2)

The generative model learns the reverse process
p_{\theta}(x_{s}\mid x_{t})
, which denoises sample
x_{t}
at arbitrary time
t\in(0,1]
to a less noised state
x_{s}
at time
s<t
.
Denote
\delta(x_{t}^{i},\textbf{m})
as the indicator function that only computes on masked tokens, and
\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}
, the learning objective is derived
[
36
,
31
]
as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDM}}=-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},\ x_{t}}\int_{t=0}^{t=1}[\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\delta(x_{t}^{i},\textbf{m})\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})]\mathrm{d}t\ .

(3)

For a linear scheduler, Eq.
3
is simplified via variable substitution
[
36
,
23
]
to an equivalent form:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDM-linear}}=-\mathbb{E}_{t,\ x_{0},\ x_{t}}[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\delta(x_{t}^{i},\textbf{m})\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})]\ .

(4)

For conditional generation, class information
c
(
e.g.
, labels or text prompts) is introduced to the denoising model as additional input.
Following classifier-free guidance
[
14
]
, the model is trained with a random drop of labels, and the prediction is interpolated at inference:

\hat{p}_{\theta}(x_{t},c)=\ p_{\theta}(x_{t},\varnothing)+w\cdot(\,p_{\theta}(x_{t},c)-\ p_{\theta}(x_{t},\varnothing)),

(5)

where
w\geq 0
controls the guidance strength.

3.2
Discrete Diffusion with Rehashing Noise

The ordinal structure inherent in discrete data provides a valuable inductive bias for designing transition kernels in diffusion dynamics. Prior studies
[
1
,
3
]
show that assigning higher transition probabilities to neighboring pixel values—forming a
discrete Gaussian-like noise
—outperforms the single absorbing state approach on pixel-level datasets like CIFAR-10.
However, when using visual tokenizers, the structure of discretized latents is learned rather than pre-defined, making such ordinal assumptions inapplicable. This insight motivates us to extend the conventional mask token into a set of indices, and reverse the diffusion path with noise rehashing.
Such a procedure allows the model to optimize its embedding space during training, enhancing its ability to model flexible and data-driven noise structures. We visualize the learned distributions in Fig.
2
(right).

Reformulation.

Given
d
categories, let
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be its one-hot vector where the
i
-th value is
1
.
We denote
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid i=1,\ldots,d\}
as the basis of a categorical distribution (known as
d
-simplex),
and a basis for absorbing states with capacity
m
:
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbf{m}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid j=1,\ldots,m\}
.
The sum of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{M}
can be denoted as

\mathcal{V}_{(d,m)}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{v}_{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+m}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{v}_{(i,j)}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{e}_{i}\oplus\mathbf{0}_{m},&\text{for }i=1,\ldots,d,\ j=0\\
\mathbf{0}_{d}\oplus\mathbf{m}_{j},&\text{for }j=1,\ldots,m,\ i=0\end{cases}\right\}.

(6)

We further denote the subspace
\mathcal{E}_{d},\ \mathcal{M}_{m}\in\mathcal{V}_{(d,m)}
which contain either valid or mask tokens, as

\mathcal{E}_{d}=\left\{\mathbf{v}_{(i,0)}\in\mathcal{V}_{(d,m)}\,\middle|\,i=1,\ldots,d\right\},\ \mathcal{M}_{m}=\left\{\mathbf{v}_{(0,j)}\in\mathcal{V}_{(d,m)}\,\middle|\,j=1,\ldots,m\right\}.

(7)

To exploit visits across all the possible paths, we rewrite the transition kernel defined by Eq.
1
into

q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{s}^{i})=\begin{cases}1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow},&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\ x_{s}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow},&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}=x_{s}^{i},\ x_{s}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
1/m,&\text{if }x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\ x_{s}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

We introduce the essential transition matrix to model the process. Let
\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}
,
\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}
and
\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}
be matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{(d+m)\times(d+m)}
, defined as

\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{d}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{1}_{d\times m}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

(9)

where
\mathbf{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix, and
\mathbf{1}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a vector of ones.
The transition matrix
Q_{t|s}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+m)\times(d+m)}
corresponding to Eq.
8
is

Q_{t|s}=\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}+(1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}+\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}

(10)

which can be demonstrated intuitively:

Q_{t|s}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}&0&\cdots&0&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}\\
0&\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}&\cdots&0&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}}{m}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\frac{1}{m}&\frac{1}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1}{m}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\frac{1}{m}&\frac{1}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1}{m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\frac{1}{m}&\frac{1}{m}&\cdots&\frac{1}{m}\end{bmatrix}

\quad\quad\quad\ \begin{array}[]{c}\underbrace{\hskip 80.00012pt}_{\text{\small$\times d$}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \underbrace{\hskip 100.00015pt}_{\text{\small$\times m$}}\end{array}

The corrupted data distribution is a direct derivative of Eq.
10
by setting
s=0
:

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{0}Q_{t|0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}x_{0}\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}+(1-\alpha_{t})x_{0}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}+x_{0}\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{t})x_{0}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}

\displaystyle\sim\alpha_{t}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{t})\,\text{U}(\mathcal{M}_{m}^{L})

(11)

where
\text{U}(\mathcal{M}_{m}^{L})
is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{M}_{m}^{L}
.

Rehash Sampling.

To generate a sequence of length
L
, the reverse process starts with
x_{1}\sim\text{U}(\mathcal{M}_{m}^{L})
. Let
\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{b}
denote the Hadamard product between two vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, the reverse process is inferred from D3PM deduction
[
1
]
as:

\displaystyle q(x_{s}\mid x_{t})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{Q_{t|s}x_{t}\odot Q_{s|0}^{\top}x_{0}}{x_{t}^{\top}Q_{t|0}^{\top}x_{0}}\ \ \ \ \text{(D3PM)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{[\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}x_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}x_{t}+\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}x_{t}]\odot[\alpha_{s}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{s})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}]}{x_{t}^{\top}[\alpha_{t}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}+\pi_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}]}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{[\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}\mathbf{I}_{(d,m)}x_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}x_{t}+\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}x_{t}]\odot[\alpha_{s}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{s})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}]}{\alpha_{t}x_{t}^{\top}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{t})x_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}}

(12)

We consider the separate cases:
x_{t}^{i}=x_{0}^{i}
and
x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}
.

Case 1.

For
x_{t}^{i}=x_{0}^{i}
, Eq.
3.2
is simplified as

\displaystyle q(x_{s}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i}=x_{0}^{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow}x_{0}^{i}\odot\alpha_{s}x_{0}^{i}}{\alpha_{t}x_{0}^{i\ \top}x_{0}^{i}}

\displaystyle=1

(13)

Case 2.

For
x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle q(x_{s}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{[(1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}x_{t}^{i}+\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}x_{t}^{i}]\odot[\alpha_{s}x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{s})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}]}{(1-\alpha_{t})x_{t}^{i\ \top}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{[(1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\leftarrow})\alpha_{s}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}x_{t}^{i}\odot x_{0}+\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}(1-\alpha_{s})x_{t}^{i}\odot\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}]}{(1-\alpha_{t})x_{t}^{i\ \top}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}x_{t}^{i}\odot x_{0}+(1-\alpha_{s})\bm{\pi}_{(d,m)}x_{t}^{i}\odot\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}}{(1-\alpha_{t})x_{t}^{i\ \top}\mathbf{M}_{(d,m)}^{\top}x_{0}}

(14)

Denote
\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow}=\frac{1-\alpha_{s}}{1-\alpha_{t}}
to describe the reverse transition rate, and we have

q(x_{s}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\mid x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m})=\dfrac{1-\alpha_{s}}{m(1-\alpha_{t})}=\dfrac{\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow}}{m}

(15)

q(x_{s}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m}\mid x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m})=\dfrac{\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}=1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow}

(16)

Combining case 1 with case 2, we have

q(x_{s}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x_{s}^{i}=x_{t}^{i},\ x_{t}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow}/m,&\text{if }x_{s}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\ x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
1-\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow},&\text{if }x_{s}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m},\ x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

(17)

Following MDLM’s deduction, assume that the denoising network can reconstruct
x_{0}
perfectly, we use

to approximate this reverse process for complex sequences, and get

p_{\theta}^{i}(x_{s}^{i}|x_{t})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x_{s}^{i}=x_{t}^{i},\ x_{t}^{i}\notin\mathcal{M}_{m},\\
\alpha_{t|s}^{\rightarrow}/m,&\text{if }x_{s}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\ x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{m},\\

\end{cases}

(18)

Experiment

4.1
Implementation

Datasets.

The experiments are conducted on ImageNet-1K
[
6
]
, which consists of 1000 categories, 1281167 images at resolution
256\times 256
for training.
The generation quality is evaluated using Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)
[
13
]
and the Inception Score (IS)
[
32
]
.
FID measures the distance between the distributions of generated and real images in the feature space of a pre-trained Inception network, where lower values indicate higher generation quality.
IS evaluates both the confidence and diversity of generated images by analyzing the predicted label distribution. We follow a common practice and compute generation FID (gFID
\downarrow
) and IS
\uparrow
on 50k generated samples.

Pre-processing.

Following the setting in LlamaGen
[
39
]
, we apply the ten-crop augmentation on images, and use pre-trained tokenizers to convert them to discrete tokens.
We pick IBQ-f16
[
35
]
tokenizer as default for its scalable and promising performance in generation tasks, which uses a
16\times 16
downsampling ratio and converts a
256\times 256
image into 256 discrete tokens. The tokenizer has a codebook with 16384 entries.
The LlamaGen-f16 (used in Tab.
3
) and LlamaGen-f8 tokenizer
[
39
]
(used in Tab.
2
) are also used for comparison with recent discrete generation methods. All tokenizers are used out-of-the-box without any tuning or modification.

Training with Representation Alignment.

Recent study
[
46
]
has shown that the alignment of intermediate representations between diffusion transformers and vision encoders accelerates training convergence of diffusion models.
Accordingly, the alignment is designed as a regularization term with
\lambda=0.5
.
We extract diffusion transformer’s
n
-th layer intermediate feature
\textbf{h}^{[n]}(x_{t})
and align it with the original image’s dinov2-b
[
25
]
encoded features
f_{\text{enc}}(x_{0}^{\text{ori}})
.
The intermediate features are projected by a small trainable MLP
h_{\varphi}
. The
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
computes the mean of element-wise cosine similarity between embeddings, as

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDM-linear}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{RepA}},\ \ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RepA}}=-\mathbb{E}_{x,\ t}[\ \text{sim}(f_{\text{enc}}(x_{0}^{\text{ori}}),\ h_{\varphi}(\textbf{h}^{[n]}(x_{t})))\ ]\ .

(20)

This alignment was proposed for continuous diffusion models, and we firstly validate that it’s also suitable for training discrete models in Tab.
1
(a).

Model Training.

The proposed model is based on DiT
[
28
]
architecture, with reference to its discrete prediction version
[
31
]
.
2D-RoPE
[
38
]
and min-SNR
[
47
]
are applied for training efficiency.
The model is optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a cosine decay.
Training is conducted for 500k iterations on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs with a global batch size 1024. Class-conditional training is enabled using class embeddings and a drop-rate of 0.1 for generation with classifier-free guidance.

5
Conclusion

We proposed ReDDiT, a discrete visual generative model built upon a principled discrete diffusion architecture with novel noise designs and efficient sampling strategies.
Our key contribution lies in the integration of rehashing noise and samplers, which together ensure both diversity and low discrepancy throughout the generative process.
By introducing rehashing noise, ReDDiT enriches the potential paths that latent variables can traverse during training, enhancing representational capacity.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that discrete generative models can achieve performance on par with their continuous counterparts while offering significantly improved efficiency.
This work paves a promising way for discrete generative modeling and offers fresh insights toward unifying visual and language generation—a path we leave for future exploration.
