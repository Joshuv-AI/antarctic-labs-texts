Methane cycling microorganisms drive seasonal variation of methane emission in mangrove ecosystems
Authors: Cui-Jing Zhang, Junjie Hou, Jinjie Zhou, Dayu Zou, Hanyun Zhang, Qiaoli Ma, Meng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal mangroves are one of the significant hotspots of natural methane (CH4) emissions, yet the seasonal dynamics of these emissions and the underlying microbial drivers remain poorly understood. A clearer understanding of these processes is critical for predicting and mitigating methane emissions from these crucial ecosystems. In this study, we conducted a seasonal investigation (from March 2021 to January 2022) in mangrove sediments of the Futian Natural Reserve. We measured in situ methane fluxes and analyzed the microbial community structure via 16S rRNA gene sequencing, metagenomics and metatranscriptomics. Our results revealed significant seasonal variations in methane emissions, with the highest rates occurring in summer. Based on relative abundance of 16S rRNA gene amplicons and methyl-coenzyme M reductase (mcrA) and particulate methane monooxygenase (pmoA) gene sequences obtained from metagenomes, we identified three dominant methanogenic lineages (hydrogenotrophic Methanomicrobiales, acetoclastic Methanosaeta and H2-dependent methylotrophic Methanomassiliicoccales), two anaerobic methanotrophic archaea (ANME-1 and ANME-2b) and one group of aerobic methanotrophic bacteria (Methylococcaceae). Metatranscriptomic data further illuminated that the transcripts of methanogenic mcrA genes were significantly higher in summer and autumn, while the transcriptional activity of anaerobic (ANME-mcrA) and aerobic (pmoA) methanotrophs were most pronounced in autumn. Correlation analyses established a significantly negative relationship between methane emissions and salinity levels. This study highlights that salinity is a key environmental factor mediating methane emissions in mangroves, likely through suppressing methanogenic activity. Our findings thus reveal that seasonal microbial interactions regulate mangrove methane flux, providing critical insights for modeling global methane budgets and guiding climate-smart mangrove management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00801-7.Keywords: Methane, Microbial community, Methanogens, Methane-oxidizing microorganisms, Mangroves, Season

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is a strong greenhouse gas with a global warming potential significantly greater than that of carbon dioxide (CO2) over a century timescale. The global methane budget is profoundly influenced by microbial activity, wherein methanogens and methane-oxidizing microorganisms (methanotrophs) are microbial producers and consumers of methane [1]. In natural habitats, methanogens are responsible for approximately 70% of global methane emissions, and around 30% of the methane produced is consumed by methanotrophs [2]. Methanogenesis can be categorized into five distinct types based on the substrates employed: H2/CO2, acetate, methylated compounds, methoxylated compounds, and long-chain alkanes [3–6]. All the pathways require the final step of methane synthesis catalyzed by the methyl-coenzyme M reductase (MCR) [7]. According to the latest classification of GTDB (Genome Taxonomy Database), methanogens mostly belong to Halobacteriota and Methanobacteriota [8]. In addition, there are potential methanogens in the Bathyarchaeia [9], Korarchaeia [10], Methanomethylicales [11], and Nezhaarchaeales [12]. Methane oxidation is a process facilitated by two distinct groups of microorganisms: anaerobic methane-oxidizing archaea (ANME) and aerobic methane-oxidizing bacteria (MOB) [13]. ANME, which also harbor mcr genes, can further classified into three main clusters: ANME-1, 2, and 3 [14, 15]. MOB are dispersed across two bacterial phyla: Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia [16].

Coastal mangrove ecosystems, renowned as crucial “blue carbon” sinks due to their high capacity for sequestering atmospheric CO2 and storing organic carbon in sediments [17], also represent significant natural sources of methane emissions[18]. Since 2006, atmospheric CH4 concentrations have been rising rapidly, with wetlands identified as a major natural contributor to this increase [19]. Methane emission partially offsets climate mitigation benefits of mangroves, making a comprehensive understanding of its dynamics essential for accurately assessing the net carbon budget of these vital ecosystems [20, 21]. The rates of methane emission from mangroves exhibit substantial spatial and temporal variations, typically ranging from 0.2 μmol to 1.1 mmol CH4 m−2 h−1 [22–25]. For instance, mangroves impacted by aquaculture and sewage have exhibited elevated methane emissions, with rates often exceeding 0.75 mmol CH4 m−2 h−1 [26, 27]. Furthermore, methane emissions from freshwater-influenced mangroves (ranging from 0 to 43 μmol CH4 m−2 h−1) have been found to be significantly higher than those from hypersaline mangroves (ranging from 0 to 4.4 μmol CH4 m−2 h−1) [28]. While previous studies have documented the presence and diversity of methane-cycling microorganisms in mangroves [29–31], the majority have relied on single time point sampling. Consequently, the seasonal dynamics of methane fluxes and the underlying microbial drivers, particularly the in situ activity of key functional groups-remain poorly resolved. Understanding the temporal patterns of both the microbial community and its transcriptional activity is crucial to elucidating the drivers behind the observed variations in methane emissions.

Recent advances in molecular ecology, specifically the integration of metagenomics and metatranscriptomics, provide a powerful approach to address this knowledge gap. While metagenomics reveals the genetic potential of a community by identifying which functional genes are present [32], metatranscriptomics directly probes gene expression, indicating which metabolic pathways are actively being used in the environment at the time of sampling [33, 34]. The combined application of these techniques offers an unprecedented opportunity to link the structure of methane-cycling microbial communities with their actual activity [35], thereby providing mechanistic insights into the microbial processes governing methane production and consumption.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of methane emissions and microbial diversity, with a specific focus on methanogens and methane-oxidizing microorganisms, in Futian Nature Reserve from March 2021 to January 2022. This study aims to: (i) characterize the spatiotemporal patterns of methane emissions across different habitats and seasons; (ii) elucidate the abundance of methanogenic and methane-oxidizing communities via metagenomics and, crucially, the in situ transcriptional activity via metatranscriptomic analyses; and (iii) identify the key environmental and microbial drivers governing methane emission dynamics. The systematic investigation combining high-resolution field measurements and molecular ecological analyses provided a more mechanistic understanding of methane cycling in mangroves, which is vital for predicting their role in the global methane cycle and informing future management strategies.

Materials and methods

Sample site and measurements of CH4 and CO2

The field experiment was conducted in the Futian National Nature Reserve of Shenzhen, which is situated at the mouth of the Shenzhen River and distinguished as the only national nature reserve embedded within an urban area. As previously described [36], three mangrove sites (MG) and three mudflat sites (MF) were selected across different locations to represent our research sites. Sediment samples were carefully collected near the roots of Kandelia candel plants at the MG sites. We conducted monthly in situ collections of CH4 and CO2 emissions using the static chamber method at six designated sites from March 2021 to January 2022, ensuring a comprehensive temporal coverage. The static chambers’ tops were made of PVC material and equipped with a fan for consistent air circulation. The covers featured two holes; one was used to connect the fan’s external power supply, while the other was sealed with a rubber plug for gas collection. During the sampling process, we first activated the fan to ensure proper air mixing within the chamber. After a five-minute interval, once the air inside the chamber was thoroughly stirred, we commenced gas sample collection. Initially, we extracted the chamber’s air using a 20 ml syringe and then injected the gas sample into a vacuum tube and transported back to the laboratory. Gas samples were collected for three times, with a 15-min interval between each collection. A total of 18 gas samples were obtained from the six sample sites monthly. Over the 11-month period, a total of 198 gas samples were systematically collected from static chambers. The concentrations of CH4 and CO2 were simultaneously quantified using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890A, Agilent Technologies Co. Ltd., USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). High purity nitrogen (N2) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 30 mL min−1. Standard curve for quantification was generated using certified standard gas mixtures (CH4: 10 ppm; CO2: 500 ppm) (Dalian Special Gases Co. Ltd., China). We also collected surface (0–10 cm) sediment samples from March 2021 to January 2022, resulting a total of 66 samples (11 months × 3 MG repetitions + 11 months × 3 MF repetitions). The sediment samples were collected using stainless-steel corers and stored at − 40 °C for physiochemical and molecular analyses.

Physicochemical parameter analysis

We measured physicochemical parameters of sediment samples, including temperature, salinity, pH, ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), total carbon (TC), total organic carbon (TOC), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP) and total sulfur (TS) (Table S1). Temperature was measured in situ using a thermometer at the sampling site. Salinity and pH were determined using fresh sediments with an automatic compensation salinity refractometer (ATAGO Co., Japan) and a pH meter (Mettler-Toledo Instruments Co., Switzerland), respectively. All other parameters were examined using air-dried sediment samples. The contents of NH4+ and NO3− were determined by ultraviolet spectrophotometry following potassium chloride extraction (detection limit: 0.1 mg/kg). The contents of TC were directly determined by an element analyzer (detection limit: 0.1 g/kg). The contents of TOC were determined by potassium dichromate oxidation and external heating method (detection limit: 0.1 g/kg). The contents of TN were determined by Kjeldahl method with sulfuric acid-catalyst digestion (detection limit: 0.01 g/kg). The contents of TP were determined by NaOH alkali-molybdenum-antimony resistance spectrophotometry (detection limit: 0.001 g/kg). The contents of TS were determined by ICP-AES/Barium sulfate turbidimetric method after digestion with nitric acid, hydrogen peroxide and hydrofluoric acid (or via alkali melting) (detection limit: 0.001 g/kg). The physical and chemical properties of samples in each month were shown in Table S1.

DNA extraction and quantitative PCR

DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of sediment samples using a DNeasy PowerSoil kit, following the protocols provided by the manufacturer (Qiagen, Germany). Subsequently, the concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000c UV–Vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). The extracted DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C for further experiments. Microbial 16S rRNA and mcrA genes were amplified using primers 515F/806R (annealing temperature 58 °C) [37] and mlas-mod-F/mcrA-rev-R (annealing temperature 55 °C) [38], respectively. The PCR reaction mixture (25 μL) comprised 2.5 μL of 10 × Ex Taq buffer, 1 μL of dNTP Mixture (2.5 μmol/L), 0.25 μL of Ex Taq (5 U/μL), 1 μL each of the primers (10 μmol /L), 1 μL of template DNA (20 ng/μL), and the remaining volume was supplemented with ddH2O. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, primer Tm annealed for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target DNA bands were excised and purified using a DNA recovery kit, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The extracted plasmid from the positive clone was used as a template. Serial dilutions of the template ranging from 10−1 to 10−8 were employed to generate a standard curve for qPCR analysis. Negative controls (no template DNA) were included in all PCR amplifications to check for potential contamination.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and data processing

The amplified products of 16S rRNA genes were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform (San Diego, CA, USA) at Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd.. Across the 66 samples, a total of 8,402,244 paired-end reads were generated, with a minimum of 56,384 reads per sample. Paired-end reads were merged using the join_paired_ends.py script from the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) [39]. The obtained single-end sequence were processed using the QIIME 2 pipeline (version 2022.2) [40]. Briefly, sequences were denoised, dereplicated, and clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the DADA2 plugin. Taxonomy was assigned to ASV representatives using the classify-sklearn method against the SILVA v138 reference database [41]. All samples were rarefied to a uniform depth of 56,384 sequences per sample, a depth determined by the minimum number of sequences per sample. Alpha diversity metrics, including the Shannon–Wiener index, were calculated using the diversity module in QIIME 2.

Metagenome sequencing, assembly, binning and annotation

To characterize the metabolic potential of methanogens and methane-oxidizing microorganisms across four seasons, we collected samples from depths of 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, 20–30 cm, 30–40 cm, and 40–50 cm at MG and MF sites in January, May, August, and November 2021. A total of 40 metagenomic libraries (4 seasons × 5 depths × 2 sites) were prepared, and sequencing was performed using an Illumina Hiseq 2000 platform at Magigene (Guangzhou, China). Each sample yielded approximately 60 gigabases (Gbp) of raw sequence data. Metagenomic datasets were processed with MetaWRAP [42]. In brief, the raw reads were quality controlled using the read_qc module and co-assembled for the five layers using MEGAHIT module with default parameters. Scaffolds longer than 1000 bp from the eight metagenomic co-assemblies were retained, and coding sequences were predicted using Prodigal with the “-p meta” parameter [43]. The assembled fragments were sorted into metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) via the binning module, which integrates MetaBAT2 [44], CONCOT and MaxBin2 [45] algorithms with default parameters. The quality of these MAGs was subsequently assessed using CheckM [46]. Only MAGs with > 50% completeness and < 10% contamination were retained for downstream analysis. Taxonomic classification of the MAGs was performed against the Genome Taxonomy Database with GTDB-Tk [47]. After dereplication with dRep [48] with an ANI cut-off of 97%, a total of 36 methanogens and 10 methane-oxidizing microorganisms MAGs were obtained. The MAGs were translated by Prodigal using the “-p meta” parameters [43]. For each predicted coding sequence (CDS), the protein function was annotated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) server [49] and eggNOG-mapper [50].

Identification of mcrA and pmoA genes

To identify mcrA genes associated with methanogenesis and anaerobic methane oxidation by methanogen and ANME, we used hidden Markov model (HMM) profiles from the PFAM database targeting conserved domains of methyl-CoM reductase alpha subunit (PF02249 for MCR_alpha and PF02745 for MCR_alpha_N). To identify pmoA genes (the alpha subunit of particulate methane monooxygenase) involved in aerobic methane oxidation by bacteria, a custom HMM profile was constructed using curated PmoA protein sequences. Genes screening was performed using hmmsearch in HMMER (v.3.1b2) [51] with a default E-value cutoff of 1e−5, followed by dereplication of nucleic acid sequences at 100% identity threshold using CD-hit [52]. The amino acid sequences of McrA and PmoA homologs were independently aligned using MUSCLE [53]. Phylogenetic trees for McrA and PmoA sequences were constructed using FastTree 2 [54]. Resulting trees were visualized in iTOL [55]. A total of 372 McrA and 20 PmoA sequences were retained, which were subsequently used as references for metatranscriptomic reads mapping.

Metatranscriptomic sequencing and processing

The 40 samples (representing 4 seasons × 5 depths for MG and 4 seasons × 5 depths for MF) described above were also collected for metatranscriptomic sequencing. Immediately after collection, samples were preserved in LifeGuard™ Soil Preservation Solution (Qiagen) to prevent RNA degradation. RNA extraction, quality assessment, genomic DNA removal, ribosome RNA depletion, cDNA synthesis and Illumina Hiseq 2000 library preparation were performed by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd (Tianjin, China) with their in-house pipelines (https://en.novogene.com). Each sample yielded approximately 30 Gbp of raw sequencing data. Raw RNA-seq reads were quality-controlled by removing low-quality sequences using Trimmomatic [56] with the default parameters and were filtered using SortMeRNA to remove tRNA and rRNA sequences [57]. To estimate metabolic activity of methanogens and methane-oxidizing microorganisms, cDNA reads were mapped to the above reference McrA and PmoA sequences using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA) [58]. SAM files generated from alignment were converted to BAM format via SAMtools [59], followed by read coverage analysis using BEDTools [60]. Transcript abundance was qualified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) to normalize gene expression levels across samples.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, 16S rRNA and mcrA gene copy numbers were log10-transformed to satisfy normality assumptions. Sample were classified into eight groups based on collection site (MF [mudflat] and MG [mangrove]) and seasonal categories (Spring [March–May], Summer [June–August], Autumn [September–November], and Winter [December–January]). Two-way ANOVA and one-way ANOVA with Duncan’s post hoc test (α = 0.05) was implemented via the agricolae R package to assess significant differences in: CH4/CO2 emission fluxes, microbial abundance/diversity metrics, relative abundances of dominant taxonomic orders/families. Prokaryotic community structure variation was analyzed through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) at genus level across groups using the vegan R package. Relationships between the relative abundances of methane-related microorganisms and environmental factors were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

Results

Seasonal dynamics of CH4 and CO2 emission fluxes

Figure 1 and Table S1 illustrated the distinct spatiotemporal patterns of CH4 and CO2 emission fluxes from in situ sediments in mangrove (MG) and mudflat (MF) sediments. Mean CH4 emissions were significantly lower in mangrove sediments (6.063 mmol m−2 h−1) compared to mudflat sediments (18.835 mmol m−2 h−1) (Duncan’s test, P < 0.05). Seasonal variation analysis revealed that CH4 emission fluxes were significantly higher in summer than in other seasons (Duncan’s test, P < 0.05). To be specific, the peak flux in mangrove sediments occurred in September (96.084 mmol m−2 h−1), while the peak in mudflat sediments was observed in July (141.917 mmol m−2 h−1). In contrast, there was no significant seasonal variation of CO2 emission fluxes. The average CO2 emission fluxes in the sediments of mangrove and mudflat were 5.161 mmol m−2 h−1 and 8.961 mmol m−2 h−1, respectively.

Seasonal variation of microbial abundance, diversity and community composition

Quantitative PCR analysis revealed significant spatial heterogeneity in microbial abundance (Fig. 2a and Table S2). Mangrove sediments exhibited significantly higher 16S rRNA gene copy numbers (log10 average = 10.35) than mudflat sediments (log10 average = 10.32) (Duncan’s test, P < 0.05). Alpha diversity, assessed via the Shannon index, displayed significantly seasonal dynamics (Duncan’s test, P < 0.05), peaking in autumn (7.02) and reaching minima in spring (6.78) (Fig. 2b). Microbial community composition analysis (Fig. 2c and Table S3) identified bacteria as the dominant prokaryotic group (97.8% of total sequences) and archaea as a minor fraction (2.2% of the total sequences). Taxonomic classification revealed 13 most abundant phyla, with Proteobacteria (33.9%), Chloroflexi (19.3%), and Firmicutes (16.2%) collectively constituting 69.4% of the average relative abundance. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) demonstrated that both season and site significantly influenced the prokaryotic community composition (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05), with PC1 and PC2 accounting for about 32.7% of the total variability (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the relative abundances of different phyla across groups were plotted (Fig. S1). The relative abundance of Epsilonbacteraeota was significantly higher in mangrove sediments than in mudflat sediments (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05). Seasonal shifts were also evident at the phylum level. For instance, Chloroflexi (21.2% in summer-autumn and 17.6% in spring-winter), Planctomycetes (5.6% in summer-autumn and 4.3% in spring-winter), and Verrucomicrobia (0.68% in summer-autumn and 0.51% in spring-winter) were more abundant in summer-autumn, whereas Epsilonbacteraeota (2.7% in summer-autumn and 4.1% in spring-winter) and Firmicutes (13.6% in summer-autumn and 19.0% in spring-winter) attained maximal abundance in spring-winter (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05). Archaeal communities were predominated by Crenarchaeota and Euryarchaeota, with Crenarchaeota demonstrating a significantly higher relative abundance in autumn (0.95% of total prokaryotic community) compared to other seasons (0.4% of total prokaryotic community) (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05).

Phylogeny of methanogens and methane-oxidizing microorganisms

We identified methane-cycling microorganisms using a multi-method approach that combined 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to determine taxonomic composition, metagenomic sequencing to reconstruct genomes and identify key functional genes, and metatranscriptomic sequencing to quantify the activity of these functional genes. Analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequences detected diverse methanogens (including four hydrogenotrophic orders: Methanobacteriales, Methanocellales, Methanococcales and Methanomicrobiales, two H2-dependent methylotrophic orders: Methanofastidiosales and Methanomassiliicoccales, one mixotrophic order: Methanosarcinales), anaerobic methanotrophic archaea (ANME-1, ANME-2 and ANME-3), and aerobic methanotrophic bacteria (Beijerinckiaceae, Methylococcaceae, Methyloligellaceae and Methylomonaceae) (Table S4). Metagenomic analysis yielded 349 non-redundant mcrA contigs (Table S5) and 20 non-redundant pmoA contigs (Table S6). Phylogenetic analysis of McrA (methyl-coenzyme M reductase alpha subunit) confirmed the presence of key methanogen lineages and ANME groups (Fig. S2). PmoA protein sequences were affiliated with Methylococcaceae and Methylocystaceae (Fig. S3). Furthermore, we reconstructed 36 medium- to high-quality methanogen MAGs and 10 methane oxidizing microorganism MAGs from 40 metagenomic datasets after dereplication. Genome size, GC content, completeness, and contamination were summarized in Table S7. The completeness ranged from 50.1 to 98.0%, and the contamination degree ranged from 0 to 9.7%, as assessed using CheckM. The methanogen MAGs represented the dominant lineages identified above, while the MAGs of methane-oxidizing microorganisms corresponded to five lineages: ANME-1, ANME-2, Methylomirabilia, Methyloceanibacter and Methylococcales. This consolidated phylogenetic framework confirmed the key functional guilds involved in methane cycling.

Seasonal dynamics of methanogenic and methane-oxidizing communities

The abundance and activity of methane-cycling microorganisms showed clear seasonal patterns. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that the mcrA gene copy numbers were significantly higher in mangrove sediments (log10 average = 10.16) than in the sediments of mudflat (log10 average = 10.13) (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05, Fig. 3a and Table S2). According to 16S rRNA gene sequences, the relative abundances of methanogens showed no significant difference across four seasons and two habitats (Fig. 3b and Table S4). Metatranscriptomic analysis demonstrated that mcrA gene expression was the highest in summer (total FPKM values = 285.2), followed by autumn (total FPKM values = 156.3), spring (total FPKM values = 15.8) and winter (total FPKM values = 9.2) (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05; Fig. 4a). The most active methanogen was hydrogenotrophic Methanomicrobiales, followed by acetoclastic Methanosaeta and H2-dependent methylotrophic Methanomassiliicoccales among the nine methanogenic taxa (Fig. 4b). Their transcriptional activities were significantly higher in summer and autumn (two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05).

Discussion

Methane emission dynamics in Futian mangrove ecosystems

By integrating seasonal flux measurements with microbial community structure and in situ activity data, we reveal the Futian mangrove as a significant methane emission hotspot, with an average rate (12 mmol m−2 h−1) at the upper extreme for coastal wetlands (− 0.004 – 3.922 mmol m−2 h−1) [61]. While consistent with other studies in observing peak emissions in summer [22, 62], our integrated approach reveals that these high fluxes are not merely a function of temperature but arise from a synergy of anthropogenic pressure from rapid urbanization and industrialization [63]. Nutrient enrichment from urban wastewater and runoff stimulates primary production, thereby increasing the availability of labile organic matter for methanogenesis [27]. Concurrently, freshwater inputs during the summer monsoon reduce salinity (< 8‰) (Table S1), releasing physiological constraints on methanogens [64, 65]. The significantly higher emissions from mudflats (average value: 19 mmol m−2 h−1) compared to vegetated sediments (average value: 6 mmol m−2 h−1) further highlight the role of habitat heterogeneity, possibly due to differences in organic matter quality, rhizosphere effects, or sediment texture [66].

The regression analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between methane emission and salinity (R2 = 0.12, P < 0.01) (Fig. S4a), possibly driven by two interconnected mechanisms: the direct inhibitory effect of osmotic stress on methanogens, and the increased sulfate availability that favors sulfate-reducing bacteria in substrate competition and promotes sulfate-dependent anaerobic methane oxidation [67]. In addition, a weak but statistically significant positive correlation was also observed between ammonium and methane emissions (R2 = 0.05, P = 0.01) (Fig. S4b), suggesting a dual role for nitrogen as a general microbial nutrient and a potential source of methylated amines [68]. This CH4-NH4⁺ coupling is amplified in Futian by anthropogenic nitrogen loading [22, 63]. In contrast to temperate ecosystems, temperature had no significant influence, indicating that in these subtropical mangroves, salinity and substrate availability override thermal effects. Consequently, the high emissions in Futian are best explained by a synergy of seasonal hydrology, anthropogenic nutrient enrichment, and organic matter inputs.

Microbial interplay regulates the methane flux

The methane fluxes are fundamentally mediated by complex microbial activity, involving methanogenesis, aerobic methane oxidation, and anaerobic methane oxidation, a dynamic previously poorly resolved in mangroves. Our metatranscriptomic data uniquely capture how this balance shifts seasonally. The summer emission peak coincides with high transcriptional activity of methanogens (Fig. 4a), a period when low salinity minimizes physiological constraints on methanogens and reduces competition with sulfate-reducing bacteria [67]. Furthermore, additional sulfate would also support sulfate-dependent anaerobic methane oxidation, leading to lower methane emissions. During this time, methanogenic activity appears to outpace methane oxidation. In autumn, despite continued significant methanogenic activity, we observed a pronounce peak in the transcription of genes associated with both anaerobic (ANME-mcr) and aerobic (pmo) methanotrophs (Fig. 4c). This elevated methanotroph abundance correlates with a significantly enhanced CH4 oxidation capacity, indicating that the microbial ‘methane filter’ becomes highly active. The autumn peak in methane oxidation likely mitigates the potential flux resulting from the legacy of summer methanogenesis, partially explaining the lower measured fluxes in autumn compared to summer. This temporal decoupling of peak production (summer) and peak consumption (autumn) is a key mechanistic insight from our study, highlighting that net emissions are shaped by the relative timing and magnitude of these opposing microbial processes.

Notably, the persistent transcriptional activity of methanogens alongside ANME during autumn provides compelling evidence for cryptic methane cycling—the simultaneous production and consumption of methane within the sediment [69–71]. While this process has been identified in salt marshes [72, 73], our transcriptomic data offer a unique lens into its occurrence in mangroves. The ability of ANME to rapidly consume methane produced in situ suggests an efficient internal recycling mechanism that modulates net emissions.

Dominant microbial guilds, their functional niches, and future perspectives

Beyond quantifying activity, our analysis reveals the specific functional groups driving methane cycling. Metatranscriptomic profiling identified three most active methanogens: hydrogenotrophic Methanomicrobiales, followed by acetoclastic Methanosaeta and H2-dependent methylotrophic Methanomassiliicoccales (Fig. 4b, Table S8 and Fig. S5). Within Methanomicrobiales, Methanoculleus emerged as the most transcriptionally active genus in mangroves (Table S5), consistent with its documented prevalence in marine and coastal sediments [74, 75]. Methanosaeta, traditionally classified as an obligate acetoclastic methanogens, was found to be able to reduce CO2 to produce methane using electrons obtained by direct interspecific electron transfer [76, 77]. The transcription activity of methanol:coenzyme M methyltransferase (mtaA) of Methanomassiliicoccales, which are related to the methanol pathway, exhibited a significant increase in autumn, as salinity decrease (Fig. S5). This pattern was supported by the recognized predominance of methylotrophic pathway in coastal sediments [31, 78, 79]. Methanomassiliicoccales occupied a specific niche through utilization of non-competitive C1 substrates (e.g., methanol from plant root exudates and leaf litter), effectively bypassing substrate competition with sulfate-reducing bacteria in marine habitats [80, 81]. A previous study reported that Methanomassiliicoccales and Methanoculleus showed positive correlations, likely mediated by H2 availability and complementary substrate utilization strategies [82].

On the consumption side, metatranscriptomic analysis resolved three dominant methane-oxidizing microorganisms: ANME-2b (HR1), ANME-1 (Methanophagales), and aerobic bacterial family Methylococcaceae (Fig. S6), indicating that both anaerobic and aerobic pathways are crucial for methane removal from mangroves [83]. ANME-1 and ANME-2b predominantly engage in syntrophic partnerships with sulfate-reducing bacteria through direct interspecies electron transfer [84]. Notably, ANME-2b harbored genetic markers for nitrogen fixation (nifH genes) (Table S8), supporting earlier reports of its multifunctional metabolic capacity in anoxic sediments [85]. Among aerobic methanotrophs, Methylococcaceae were the most active, consistent with their known preference for nutrient-rich niches and their status as the most transcriptionally active methanotroph family in tidal marsh ecosystems [86–88]. The synchronized activity of these diverse methanotrophs creates a robust microbial filter that modulates the ultimate release of methane to the atmosphere.

The identification of Futian as a seasonal hotspot raises the question of how climate change might alter methane emission patterns. Our mechanistic framework allows for some predictive insights. Increased rainfall intensity could prolong low-salinity periods, potentially extending the window of high methanogenic activity. In contrast, sea-level rise may increase salinity through saltwater intrusion, suppressing methanogenesis, but could also submerge larger areas of organic-rich soils, creating new anoxic habitats—a complex outcome requiring further study. A critical future question is whether methanotrophic communities can keep pace with prolonged increases in methane production, or if their capacity would be saturated, leading to higher net emissions.

Conclusions

In summary, our study moves beyond correlative observations to provide a mechanistic understanding of methane cycling in urban mangroves. The principal novel contribution lies in the integration of meta-omics, which directly linked seasonal methane flux patterns to the in situ transcriptional activity of key microbial guilds. This approach revealed the temporal decoupling between peak methanogenic activity in summer and peak methanotrophic activity in autumn and provided evidence for cryptic methane cycling. These findings open avenues for targeted strategies, such as modulating salinity regimes or leveraging the non-competitive substrate niche of methylotrophic methanogens like Methanomassiliicoccales. Enhancing the activity of naturally dominant methanotrophs (e.g., ANME, Methylococcaceae) is also a promising bio-mitigation strategy. We acknowledge that our findings are based on a single site, and the generalizability of the proposed model requires validation across a wider range of mangrove habitats. Future research should incorporate stable isotope probing with multi-omics to directly quantify pathway-specific fluxes, ultimately enabling robust predictions of mangrove greenhouse gas emissions under climate change.
