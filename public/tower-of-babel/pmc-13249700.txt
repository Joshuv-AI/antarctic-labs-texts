Underrecognized depressive and anxiety symptoms in inflammatory arthritis: implications for systematic screening using PHQ-9 and GAD-7
Authors: Monika Kolasińska, Mateusz Wilk, Dagna Polak, Marcin Siwek, Anna Julia Krupa, Mariusz Korkosz, Zofia Guła
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression and anxiety are common comorbidities in patients with inflammatory arthritis (IA), including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Despite their clinical relevance and impact on disease outcomes, these mental health conditions remain frequently underrecognized in routine rheumatology practice. To evaluate the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms in patients with IA in a real-world clinical setting and to compare the utility of different patient-reported questionnaires for their identification. Cross-sectional, real-world observational study. Patients with RA, axSpA, and PsA were evaluated. Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, along with specific items of the Multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (MDHAQ). A total of 255 patients were included (RA n = 105, PsA n = 60, axSpA n = 90), with mean ages 59, 47, and 42 years, respectively. Across all groups, the prevalence of depression symptoms identified using PHQ-9 were more frequently observed than reflected in prior routine clinical records .In RA, depressive symptoms prevalence increased from 8.6%(n = 9) to 24.8% (n = 26); in PsA, from 5% (n = 3) to 18.3%(n = 11); and in axSpA, from 14.4% (n = 13) to 21.1%(n = 19). A similar pattern was observed for anxiety symptoms. When assessed using GAD-7 the prevalence increased from 4.9% (n = 5) to 20% (n = 21) in RA, from 5.1% (n = 3) to 16.7% (n = 10) in PsA, and from 7.8% (n = 7) to 12.2% (n = 11) in axSpA when evaluated. No significant differences in median PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores were observed between the groups. MDHAQ mental health items showed strong correlations with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 composite scores. Depression and anxiety symptoms are underrecognized yet highly prevalent in patients with inflammatory arthritis. They can be effectively identified using dedicated screening questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. Background

Inflammatory arthritis (IA), encompassing rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), is frequently associated with a significant burden of mental health comorbidities, particularly depression and anxiety [1–5]. These disorders significantly influence disease activity scores, treatment effectiveness, and quality of life [6–10]. Depression and anxiety in individuals with IA present a complex clinical challenge due to overlapping symptoms such as pain, low mood, sleep disturbances, and impaired daily functioning [9, 11–15]. These symptoms may make the diagnosis and management of IA complicated and are associated with poorer clinical outcomes—including more hospital admissions and overall mortality [7, 8, 10, 15, 16] —and may further contribute to difficult-to-treat disease by amplifying perceived pain, functional limitations, and low-grade inflammation [7, 17–19].

The reported prevalence of depression in IA varies substantially across studies, largely depending on the case definition (diagnostic criteria vs. screening thresholds) and the instrument used. Studies focusing on clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder (MDD) in IA report current prevalence rates ranging from 8% to 22.5%, while lifetime prevalence has been reported to reach up to 47% [20]. Meta-analyses have reported an MDD prevalence of 16.8% in RA [21], and another reported a pooled prevalence of 32.1% [2]. In PsA, MDD prevalence ranged from 5 to 51% [1, 22–24]. Similarly, in axSpA, reported MDD prevalence ranges widely, from 11% to 64% [16, 25]. Anxiety is also a common psychiatric comorbidity in patients with IA. In RA, the reported prevalence of anxiety ranges from 18.6% to 30% [26, 27]. In PsA, between 4% and 61% of patients [1, 22, 24] report anxiety, and among axSpA patients, approximately 38% [3] report anxiety.

Recognizing the significant impact of depression and anxiety on individuals with IA, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) strongly recommends routine mental health assessment and monitoring in IA [28, 29]. This underscores the importance of early identification and intervention to improve treatment outcomes and overall well-being. However, accurately assessing depression and anxiety in IA remains challenging. Gold-standard psychiatric interviews are time-consuming and require training and experience, which is why we need to seek convenient, helpful tools for everyday practice.

The current study addresses the need for effective mental health screening in IA in a real-world outpatient setting. Prompted by previous findings indicating low recognition of these comorbidities in the Polish population [5, 30], we aimed to further investigate the prevalence of these conditions using standardized instruments aligned with DSM-5 criteria, including the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and to examine their correlation with the corresponding mental health domains of the Multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (MDHAQ), which may provide a feasible approach for screening these disorders in routine rheumatology care [41], [42].

Methods

This cross-sectional observational study included adult patients with inflammatory arthritis (IA). Data were collected between March 2024 and October 2024 at the rheumatology outpatient clinic as part of the Polish-Norwegian “POLNOR-RHEUMA” project [30]. Participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of RA, PsA, or axSpA. Diagnoses were established according to the respective classification criteria: 2010 ACR/EULAR for RA [31], CASPAR for PsA [32], and ASAS for axSpA [33, 34].

Data collection

Data were collected during routine outpatient visits as part of standard clinical care using the GoTreatIT® Rheuma software [35]. The collected variables included demographic data (age, sex, BMI, smoking, physical activity, and educational level), laboratory measures (erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR, mm/h] and C-reactive protein [CRP, mg/L]), clinical assessments (joint counts evaluated by a physician, including the 28 swollen joint count [28SJC] and 28 tender joint count [28TJC]), global pain assessment (visual analogue scale [VAS], 0–100), back pain at night (VAS, 0–100), fatigue (VAS, 0–100), and disease activity scores (DAS28-CRP for RA and PsA, BASDAI for axSpA, DAPSA for PsA). Disease duration was calculated by subtracting the current date from the date of final disease diagnosis according to relevant classification criteria.

Mental health was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depressive symptoms [36] and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 questionnaire (GAD-7) for anxiety symptoms [37]. The PHQ-9 is a 9-item questionnaire with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. A cutoff score of 10 was used to identify patients at risk for moderate to severe depressive symptoms. The GAD-7 is a 7-item questionnaire with a total score of 0 to 21. A cutoff score of 10 was used to identify patients at risk for generalized moderate to severe anxiety disorder. Other research groups have previously validated both tools and used them as the screening tools for mental disorders [37, 38]. The Polish translations of both tools were also previously validated [39, 40].

Health status was evaluated using the Multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire (MDHAQ), which offers a comprehensive assessment of several aspects of patient health status. The version of the questionnaire used in this study included assessment of physical function (MDHAQ Fn), psychological distress (MDHAQ Ps), pain, joint pain, back pain, back pain at night, morning stiffness, patient global, patient satisfaction, change in condition, physical exercise, fatigue, Routine Assessment of Patient Index Data 3 (RAPID3), Minimum Clinically Important Improvement (MCII), Patient Acceptable Symptom State 1 and 2 (PASS1 and PASS2). The physical function component comprises 10 questions about the ability to perform typical daily activities over the last week. The psychological distress component includes questions related to depression, anxiety, coping with stress, and sleep over the last week.

The MDHAQ examines physical function (MDHAQ Fn) (10 items, 0–3 scale per item) by measuring daily living activities over the last week, with a total score from 0 to 10, and psychological status (MDHAQ Ps) (3 items, 0–3.3 scale per item) by assessing sleep, depression, and anxiety over the last week, with a total score from 0 to 9.9. In particular, MDHAQ includes two items related to anxiety and depression, each rated on a 0–3 scale.

A predefined query was used to extract demographic and clinical data from the PolNorRHEUMA registry database. Data extraction was conducted on November 8, 2024, including relevant information from the latest visit, related to questionnaire collection. The extracted data were curated and organized using Microsoft Excel prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Only participants with complete questionnaire data were included in the analysis. Continuous variables are presented as median (25th, 75th percentiles), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Data distribution was assessed using histogram inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the data were not normally distributed, nonparametric statistical methods were used. Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. For correlation analysis, Spearman’s rho coefficient was calculated, with correlation strength classified as weak (< 0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), or strong (> 0.5). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All p-values were two-tailed. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28. Regarding missing data, the completeness of the dataset was high, with data density close to 100% for the key variables included in the main analyses. The small amount of missing data did not affect the principal outcome measures; therefore, no imputation procedureswere applied.

Ethics approval and patient involvement

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University Medical College (protocol N 1072.6120.247.2020) and complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patients were not involved in the study’s design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination.

Diagnosis of mental disorder as a comorbidity based on clinical data on enrollment

Before enrollment in the study, depressive disorders were diagnosed as a comorbidity in 9 (8.6%) participants in the RA group, in 3 (5%) in the PsA group, and in 13 (14.4%) in the axSpA group. Anxiety disorders were previously diagnosed in 5 (4.9%) of patients with RA, 3 (5.1%) of patients with PsA, and 7 (7.8%) in the axSpA group.

Strong positive correlations (r > 0,5) were observed between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores and MDHAQ psychological status, MDHAQ Anxiety, and MDHAQ Depression across all diseases (except a moderate correlation of MDHAQ Anxiety and Depression with GAD-7 in PsA). The MDHAQ sleep item was moderately but significantly correlated with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. The MDHAQ functional score (FN) correlated moderately but significantly with depression in all IA and with anxiety in PsA and axSpA, whereas no significant association with anxiety was observed in RA.

Discussion

As previously reported [5, 30], we observed a low rate of clinically recognized depression and anxiety among patients with RA, PsA, and axSpA in routine clinical practice. This contrasted with the substantial prevalence of clinically relevant symptoms of these disorders identified by validated screening instruments, the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, with no significant differences among the three patient groups. So far, there is a paucity of studies directly comparing the prevalence of depression and anxiety across different forms of IA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first single-center study to simultaneously compare patients with RA, PsA, and axSpA with respect to the frequency of mental health disorders, the diagnostic utility of different questionnaires, and the correlations and potential interchangeability between these instruments.

There are several possible reasons for underdiagnosing mental health disorders in rheumatologic practice. First, depression and anxiety often go unnoticed and untreated by clinicians who are not specialized in mental health [43]. Particularly, rheumatologists may lack sufficient training in recognizing and managing these conditions, and time constraints can further hinder effective care [44]. Second, patients themselves may downplay their symptoms, fear stigma, or believe that mental health struggles are a sign of weakness [45]. This highlights the need for practical, relatively simple tools to help identify mood disorders in this population.

In our study, we found a strong correlation between PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores and psychological domains on the MDHAQ, indicating that emotional distress is an integral component of disease burden. Sleep disturbances and functional limitations were also significantly associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, although these relationships were weaker, suggesting that psychological factors may have a more direct impact on mental health outcomes than physical disability alone [53–55]. In contrast to disorder-specific instruments such as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, which are designed to facilitate identification and severity assessment of depressive and anxiety disorders according to established diagnostic constructs, the MDHAQ is not a dedicated psychiatric screening tool and does not permit formal diagnostic classification. Nevertheless, in our analysis, MDHAQ scores demonstrated significant correlations with measures of depressive and anxiety symptoms. These findings suggest that, although not diagnostic, the MDHAQ, as a relatively short and practical instrument, may facilitate communication between patients and clinicians, serving as an initial step in identifying patients who require further psychological assessment and appropriate specialist referral [46, 47].

Importantly, although median disease activity indicated remission or low disease activity across all groups, depressive and anxiety symptoms remained substantial, suggesting that psychological distress may persist even in the context of well-controlled inflammatory disease. Previous research has demonstrated a bidirectional association between the severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms and inflammatory joint disease activity, as well as an ambiguous effect of treatment type for IA [48–52]. Psychological distress may exacerbate perceived disease burden and inflammation, while higher disease activity may, in turn, worsen mental health outcomes. Our findings reinforce the notion that psychological symptoms may persist despite adequate control of inflammatory disease, underscoring the need for their independent recognition and targeted management as a distinct component of comprehensive IA care, beyond traditional disease-modifying therapies.

Limitations

Our research has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, our study is based on patients from a single tertiary center with low disease activity, inclusion of only complete questionnaire data and recruitment from routine outpatient visits which makes it difficult to clearly assess the prevalence of psychiatric disorders across the entire population of patients with IA. Second, the majority of participants in our study were receiving biological and targeted synthetic DMARDs, which limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader IA population, particularly those with higher disease activity or those managed with conventional synthetic DMARDs or NSAIDs. Our study population consists of patients with long-standing disease and extensive exposure to advanced therapies. In such individuals, cumulative disease burden, structural damage, and long-term functional limitations may substantially influence psychological outcomes, independently of current disease activity levels. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes us from drawing definitive conclusions about causality. It is also important to acknowledge that the absence of formal psychiatric assessment limits our ability to determine whether the findings reflect clinically diagnosed disorders or differences in symptom reporting or recognition. The lack of matching or multivariable adjustment restricts the interpretability of between‑group comparisons, particularly the finding of no significant differences.

Conclusions

Depression and anxiety are relatively common but still underrecognized in patients with inflammatory arthritis. Symptoms measured by the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 were strongly associated with patient-reported MDHAQ, particularly reflecting psychological domains.

These findings support the routine integration of psychological screening into standard rheumatology care to facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate interventions.
