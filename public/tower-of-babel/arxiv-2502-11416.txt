Generalized quantum two level model and its application in astrophysics
Orkash Amat, Nurimangul Nurmamat, Yong-Feng Huang, Cheng-Ming Li, Jin-Jun Geng, Chen-Ran Hu, Ze-Cheng Zou, Xiao-Fei Dong, Chen Deng, Fan Xu, Xiao-li Zhang, Chen Du

Abstract
Complicated time-dependent curved spacetime and electric field are involved in many astrophysical situations, including the early universe, Hawking radiation, the Schwinger effect, and gravitational pair production. In this Letter, a generalized quantum two-level model (GQTLM) is developed, which is applicable to arbitrary time-dependent curved spacetime and electric field. The model is found to be consistent with quantum kinetic theory, and is characterized by its simplicity and versatility. The momentum distribution of particles and the effects of gravitational distortions can be correctly described. Quantum properties concerning vortex structures, such as the intrinsic orbital angular momentum of particles and antiparticles can also be conveniently calculated. The model is expected to significantly advance the quantum exploration of the universe. It could refine the prediction of primordial gravitational waves and relevant non-Gaussian signals, extend the calculation of Hawking radiation to general black hole configurations, help to distinguish neutron stars from strange quark stars, and elucidate the gravitational pair production mechanism.

III
Generalized quantum two-level model

A common feature of
these four situations is the particle-antiparticle pair creation
from the vacuum, which is the core process underlying each
scenario. This common feature leads to a unified perspective for
understanding the phenomena. Here we develop the GQTLM to present
a unified description for the scalar vacuum pair production
process. The quantum properties of scalar particles and fermions
are nearly identical despite their difference in the distribution
function
[
53
]
. This allows us to focus on the
essential physics without introducing additional complexity. So,
we can simply focus on the scalar vacuum pair production process.

IV
Quantum kinetic theory

The quantum Vlasov equation
(QVE) and the Wigner function are collectively called QKT. Note
that the GQTLM is also consistent with QKT, as detailed below. The
QVE of scalar vacuum pair production for the early universe,
Hawking radiation, Schwinger effect and gravitational pair
production in a curved spacetime and electric field can be
obtained as [see the Appendix
E
]

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)~\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}},

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{G}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)~(1+2\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}})-2\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{H}_{a,~\mathbf{k}},

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}~\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}.

(11)

where

\displaystyle W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}Q_{a,~\bf{k}}(t).

(12)

Omitting the effects of curved spacetime and electric field, the
initial condition can be expressed as

\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=\mathcal{H}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=0.

(13)

The scalar particle momentum distribution is

\displaystyle f_{a,~\bf k}(t)=\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t).

(14)

The number density of created particles can be calculated by
integrating the momentum distribution function

n_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t).

(15)

Furthermore, when only consider the effect of the electric field,

The advantage of QVE is that it can explain chirping, tunneling,

The equivalence between our GQTLM and QVE means that our model is

Origin of intrinsic orbital angular momentum

Our GQTLM
can not only describe the momentum distribution of particles but
also provide extensive quantum information for the particle
creation process under the influence of spacetime curvature and
electric field. For instance, the topological charge or the
intrinsic orbital angular momentum quantum number

created particles and antiparticles can be determined

l

\displaystyle l=\frac{\phi_{\rm Berry}}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{A}}\cdot d\bf{k},

(16)

In the momentum space (
\bf{k}

connection of the complex function (
c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{\text{Re}\left(c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{*(2)}(t)\left[-i\nabla_{\bm{k}}\right]c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\right)}{\lvert c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\rvert^{2}}=\nabla_{\bm{k}}\left(\arg\left[c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\right]\right),

(17)

where the term “arg” represents the argument of the complex
function
c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)
, i.e.,
\arg\left[c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\right]={\rm arctan}\left(\Im\left[c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\right]/\Re\left[c_{a,~\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\right]\right)
. It indicates that the intrinsic orbital angular momentum
of particles can be interpreted through the GQTLM. In short, the
GQTLM is an informative tool. It encompasses the momentum
distribution, intrinsic orbital angular momentum, and other

electric field.

VI
Outlook

While the cosmic inflation theory excels in
explaining the anisotropy and spectral index of the cosmic
microwave background (CMB), some uncertainties remain in its
specific predictions
[
106
]
. For example, the
amplitude of primordial gravitational waves is yet to be
determined, and the predicted non-Gaussian signals still could not
be detected
[
107
]
. Addressing these issues is
crucial for refining the inflation theory and validating its
specific predictions (Fig.
1
a). Our GQTLM model provides
strong support for the inflation as well as a fresh perspective
for understanding the process. It lays a theoretical foundation
for uncovering the essence of the inflation and points the way for
future in-depth research.

Hawking radiation is interpreted by adopting quantum field theory
in curved spacetime, but its microscopic physical mechanism
remains unclear
[
21
]
. For example, the exact
process of particle generation and the underlying dynamics are not
fully understood. Additionally, experimental or observational test
of the Hawking radiation mechanism is still a challenge. For
higher-dimensional, charged or rotating black holes, the
computation and analysis of Hawking radiation are more complex.
Extending the theory to encompass more general black hole models
is an important area of ongoing research
[
108
]
. Our
GQTLM could help address these issues and may have the potential
to extend the study of Hawking radiation to broader black hole
configurations (Fig.
1
b). It provides a fresh perspective
on uncovering the mysteries of black hole physics and opens up new
possibilities for related researches.

Distinguishing neutron stars from strange quark stars remains a
longstanding challenge in astrophysics from both theoretical and
observational perspectives
[
109
]
. Attempts have been made based on their
difference in the mass-radius relationship, cooling
speed
[
110
,
111
]
, limiting rotation
period
[
112
]
, high energy
bursts
[
113
]
, and gravitational wave radiation

[
111
]
. However, none of these methods is effective
enough. Developing more effective methods is still a challenging
task. Note that the electron-positron pair winds emanating from
these two kinds of objects may be very different
(Fig.
1
c), which can be calculated by using our GQTLM.
The electric field generated by the hot electrosphere of a strange
quark star is much stronger than that of a neutron star. As a
result, the electron-positron pair winds of strange quark stars
are more intense than that of neutron stars. This characteristics
could help us distinguish the two types of compact stars.

Theoretically, strong gravitational waves can polarize and
interact with quantum fields in a vacuum, leading to the
generation of particle-antiparticle pairs
[
114
]
.
However, the detailed dynamics of this process, the response of
the quantum fields, and the coherence conditions are not yet fully
understood ( Fig.
1
d). Our GQTLM could offer profound
theoretical insights into these challenging problems. It can help
uncover the fundamental mechanisms behind the issue.

The GQTLM may also be applied in other fields, such as quantum
Hall effect. It can explain particle vortex phenomena, which
further enhances its theoretical relevance
[
115
]
.

Despite its advantages, the GQTLM has several limitations that
warrant further theoretical exploration. First, it does not account for the effects of the magnetic field and spatiotemporal dependence of the gravitational metric tensor, represented as
g_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}(t,x)
.
Second, some effects predicted by the model are extremely
difficult to test through astronomical observations, posing a
clear challenge for experimental verification.

VII
Conclusions

In this study, a generalized quantum two
level model is developed to describe the pair creation process
involving complicated arbitrary time-dependent curved spacetime
and electric field. It can be applied to various astrophysical
situations such as the early universe, Hawking radiation, the
Schwinger effect, and gravitational pair production. The model is
shown to be consistent with QKT, highlighting its effectiveness in
describing these phenomena. The GQTLM exhibits significant
advantages in the following aspects. First, it accurately
characterizes the momentum distribution of particles created in
arbitrary time-dependent curved spacetime and electric fields.
Second, it effectively incorporates the impact of gravitational
field on particle dynamics, such as the gravitational distortion
effect. Third, the GQTLM model conveniently describes the
particles’ quantum properties involving vortex structures, such as
the intrinsic orbital angular momentum by engaging the Berry
phase.

Appendix A
Generalized quantum two-level model in context of the early universe

The exact generalized quantum two-level model (GQTLM) can be used
to study the scalar vacuum pair production in the early universe.
To deal with the complicated time-dependent curved spacetime and
electric field, we start from the Lagrangian density in the
Einstein representation
[
104
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}=\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{M_{p}^{2}}{2}R_{1}+\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm inflaton}+\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm QED}\right],

(18)

where
{\mu,\nu}=\{0,1,2,3\}
,
g_{\mu\nu}
is an arbitrary
time-dependent matric,
g={\rm det}(g_{\mu\nu})
,
M_{p}=(8\pi G)^{-1/2}=2.43\times 10^{18}\,{\rm GeV}
is the reduced Planck
mass, and
R_{1}
is the scalar curvature. The inflationary term
can be expressed by engaging a single scalar inflation field

\phi
with a potential of
V(\phi)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm inflaton}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-V(\phi).

(19)

The Lagrangian density of the scalar quantum electrodynamics (QED) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm QED}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}+\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm ch},

(20)

where the electromagnetic and charged scalar fields can be further
expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(A_{\mu},\phi)=-\frac{1}{4}I(\phi)F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\rm scalar}_{\rm ch}

\displaystyle=g^{\mu\nu}(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\chi)^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\chi)-(m^{2}-\xi_{1}R_{1})\chi^{\dagger}\chi.

(22)

Here
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
denotes
the field strength of the external field,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}

describes the interaction of the electromagnetic field with the
inflation that leads to the generation of electromagnetic field,

I(\phi)
is the kinetic coupling function between the inflation
field and the
U(1)
gauge field,
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-iqA_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative acting on
the scalar field
\chi
with a charge
q
and mass
m
. The
coupling constant
\xi_{1}
quantifies the nonminimal coupling of
the scalar field
\chi
with gravity
[
104
]
.

The dynamics of the scalar field
\chi
can be obtained by using
the Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\left[\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\chi\right]+(m^{2}-\xi_{1}R_{1})\chi=0.

(23)

The solution of the scalar field
\chi
for

A^{\mu}=(0,\mathbf{A}(t))
and
{\rm div\,}\mathbf{A}=0
can be
written as

\displaystyle\chi(t,\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}(-g)^{1/4}}\left[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}+\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\Phi^{*}_{-\mathbf{k}}(t)e^{-i\mathbf{k\cdot\mathbf{x}}}\right],

(24)

where the creation (
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
,
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
) and annihilation (
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}}
) operators satisfy the standard bosonic commutation relations, i.e.,

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}},\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}]=[\hat{b}_{\mathbf{k}},\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}]=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}).

(25)

Adopting the transformation of

\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int^{t}P(\tau)d\tau}
, we can obtain a
Schrödinger like equation by substituting Eq. (
24
)
into Eq. (
23
),

\displaystyle\ddot{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)+\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(t)\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=0,

(26)

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\dot{P}(t)-\frac{1}{4}P^{2}(t)},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\dot{g}^{00}}{g^{00}}+\frac{g^{0j}}{g^{00}}(-ik_{j})+\frac{g^{0j}}{g^{00}}(-ieA_{j})+\frac{g^{i0}}{g^{00}}(-ik_{i})+\frac{g^{i0}}{g^{00}}(-ieA_{i})\right],

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{3\dot{g}^{2}-4\ddot{g}}{16g^{2}}-\frac{\dot{g}}{4g}\cdot\frac{\dot{g}^{00}}{g^{00}}+\frac{\dot{g}^{0j}}{g^{00}}(-ik_{j}-ieA_{j})\right]+\frac{\dot{g}^{0j}}{g^{00}}(-ie\partial_{0}A_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{g^{i0}}{g^{00}}\left(\frac{\dot{g}}{4g}ik_{i}+ieA_{i}\cdot\frac{\dot{g}}{4g}\right)+\frac{g^{0j}}{g^{00}}\left(\frac{\dot{g}}{2g}(-ik_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+g^{ij}\left(-k_{j}k_{i}-eA_{j}k_{i}-eA_{i}k_{j}-e^{2}A_{i}A_{j}\right)+\left(m^{2}-\xi_{1}R_{1}\right).

(29)

We now apply the Bogoliubov transformation by defining
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)
and
\beta^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)
as

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=\frac{\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{-i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}+\frac{\beta^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)},

(30)

then we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{-i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}+\frac{\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)},

(31)

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\left(\frac{\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{-i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}-\frac{\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}}e^{i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\right),

(32)

where
\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)=\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int^{t}P(\tau)d\tau}
and

\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)=\beta^{{}^{\prime}}_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int^{t}P(\tau)d\tau}
. It is easy to further get

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\Omega}_{\bf{k}}(t)}{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{2i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)},

(33)

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\Omega}_{\bf{k}}(t)}{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{-2i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}.

(34)

The Bogoliubov transformation changes the time-independent bases
of creation
a_{\bf k}
and annihilation
b_{-{\bf k}}^{\dagger}

operators to the time-dependent bases of creation
\tilde{a}_{\bf k}(t)
and annihilation
\tilde{b}_{-{\bf k}}^{\dagger}(t)

operators, through a linear transformation of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{a}_{\bf k}(t)\cr\tilde{b}_{-{\bf k}}^{\dagger}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{\bf k}&\beta_{\bf k}^{*}\cr\beta_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{\bf k}\cr b^{\dagger}_{-{\bf k}}\end{pmatrix},

(35)

where the bosonic commutation relations are preserved by
|\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)|^{2}-|\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)|^{2}=1
.

Now we introduce two useful new coefficients as

\displaystyle c^{(1)}_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{-i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)},

(36)

\displaystyle c^{(2)}_{\bf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)e^{i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}.

(37)

After taking the derivatives of time on the above equations, we
can obtain

i\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t)\\
c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{\bf{k}}(t)&-iQ_{\bf{k}}(t)\cr-iQ_{\bf{k}}(t)&-\Omega_{\bf{k}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t)\\
c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(38)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\bf{k}}(t)=-\frac{\dot{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}.

(39)

The initial conditions are
c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t_{0})=1
and

c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t_{0})=0
. The scalar particle momentum
distribution can be calculated by using the coefficient

c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)
as

\displaystyle f_{\bf k}(t)=2|c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)|^{2}.

(40)

Appendix B
GQTLM in context of the Hawking radiation

Hawking radiation is a quantum field phenomenon arising from
quantum fluctuations near the event horizon of a black hole, where
intense gravitational field leads to the creation of
particle-antiparticle pairs
[
34
]
. Additionally,
the strong electric field around a charged black hole may also
induce pair production, converting vacuum fluctuations into real
particle-antiparticle pairs. To explore the combination of these
two ingredients, we need the GQTLS that takes into account both
the Hawking radiation and the Schwinger effect. For this purpose,
the scalar QED Lagrangian density in arbitrary time-dependent
curved spacetime and electric field is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+g^{\mu\nu}(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\chi)^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\chi)-(m^{2}-\xi_{2}R_{2})\chi^{\dagger}\chi\right],

(41)

where
g^{\mu\nu}
is an arbitrary time-dependent matric.

Similar to the above section, the exact GQTLM in an arbitrary
time-dependent curved spacetime and electric field can be
characterized by

i\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t)\\
c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{\bf{k}}(t)&-iQ_{\bf{k}}(t)\cr-iQ_{\bf{k}}(t)&-\Omega_{\bf{k}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t)\\
c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(42)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\bf{k}}(t)=-\frac{\dot{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}{2\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}.

(43)

The initial condition is
c_{\bf{k}}^{(1)}(t_{0})=1
and

c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t_{0})=0
. The scalar particle momentum
distribution can be calculated by using the coefficient

c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)
as

\displaystyle f_{\bf k}(t)=2|c_{\bf{k}}^{(2)}(t)|^{2},

(44)

with

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\dot{P}(t)-\frac{1}{4}P^{2}(t)}.

(45)

If only gravity is present but without any external electric
field, then Eq. (
42
) reduces to the exact GQTLM of the
traditional Hawking radiation. If only an external electric field
is present but without any gravity, then Eq. (
42
)
reduces to the GQTLM of the Schwinger effect
[
53
]
.
If both the gravity and the external electric field can not be
ignored, then Eq. (
42
) corresponds to the GQTLM that
considers the coupling of the Hawking radiation and the Schwinger
effect. Especially, note that the difference between Eq.
(
42
) and Eq. (
38
) is that the former does not
include the inflationary effect and the interaction of the
electromagnetic field with the inflation.

Appendix C
GQTLM in context of the Schwinger effect

For compact astrophysical objects, such as neutron stars or
strange quark stars, the scalar QED Lagrangian density under
time-dependent curved spacetime and electric field is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-g}\left(g^{\mu\nu}(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\chi)^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\chi)-(m^{2}-\xi_{3}R_{3})\chi^{\dagger}\chi-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\right).

(46)

Similarly, the exact GQTLM can be established by using this
Lagrangian. For simplicity, we do not repeat the process here.

Appendix D
GQTLM in context of the gravitational pair production

When a massive compact astrophysical object merge with a black
hole, the varying curvature of spacetime caused by the strong
gravity of the two objects, along with the strong electric field
of the charging black hole, may lead to the quantum vacuum decay.
Particle-antiparticle pairs would be produced during the process.
Again, GQTLM involving time-dependent curved spacetime and
electric field is needed. In this case, the scalar QED Lagrangian
density can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-g}\left(g^{\mu\nu}(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\chi)^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\chi)-(m^{2}-\xi_{4}R_{4})\chi^{\dagger}\chi-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\right).

(47)

Following a similar derivation, the exact GQTLM can be established
in context of the gravitational pair production.

Appendix E
Quantum kinetic theory for curved spacetime with electric field

The quantum Vlasov equation (QVE) and the Wigner function are
collectively called Quantum kinetic theory (QKT). QVE can also be
used to deal with scalar vacuum pair production for the early
universe, Hawking radiation, Schwinger effect and gravitational
pair production. This can be achieved by using two coefficients,

\alpha_{\bf{k}}(t)
and
\beta_{\bf{k}}(t)
, because there are
only three real degrees of freedom in the scalar vacuum pair
production. As a result, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|\beta_{a,~\mathbf{k}}\big|^{2},

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\Re e\left(\alpha_{a,~\mathbf{k}}~\beta^{*}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}e^{-2i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\right),

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Im m\left(\alpha_{a,~\mathbf{k}}~\beta^{*}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}e^{-2i\int^{t}d\tau\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(\tau)}\right),

(50)

where the index
a=\{1,2,3,4\}
represents the four situations
of the early universe, Hawking radiation, the Schwinger effect,
and gravitational pair production, respectively. Taking the time
derivative of the above set of equations, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)~\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}},

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{G}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)~(1+2\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}})-2\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}~\mathcal{H}_{a,~\mathbf{k}},

(52)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Omega_{a,~\mathbf{k}}~\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}},

(53)

where

\displaystyle W_{a,~\bf{k}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}Q_{a,~\bf{k}}(t).

(54)

The initial condition is given by

\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=\mathcal{G}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=\mathcal{H}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t_{0})=0.

(55)

The scalar particle momentum distribution is expressed as

\displaystyle f_{a,~\bf k}(t)=\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t).

(56)

The particle number density can be calculated by integrating the
momentum distribution function
\mathcal{F}_{a,~\mathbf{k}}(t)

over the momentum
\mathbf{k}
,

n_{a}(t)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{a,~\bf k}(t).

(57)

It can be seen that our exact GQTLM is equivalent to the QVE in
cases of the early universe, Hawking radiation, the Schwinger
effect, and gravitational pair production. When the spacetime
curvature is weak so that it could be neglected, our Eqs.
(
51
)-(
53
) will become consistent with the
results presented in Ref.
[
116
]
, which is a natural
expectation.
