Clinical characteristics and booster vaccine effectiveness of the Omicron variant
Authors: Lijun Sun, Biye Zhou, Yijia Guo, Yunfei Huang, Zihui Tang, Zhaojun Wu, Yuwei Li, Liping Zhao, Yi Shan, Xiaolong Xu, Hui Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough many people received the COVID-19 vaccine, shortly after the Chinese government announced that the three-year COVID-19 restrictions were being eased, the first large number of Omicron infections appeared in Beijing. We describe epidemiological characteristics, clinical severity, and time-to-event distribution of patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 in Beijing Omicron outbreak, comparing those who received the booster vaccine with cases of full-course/partial-course vaccines.MethodsWe collected epidemiological, clinical, laboratory, and clinical management data from the hospital information system (HIS) for 1495 cases during Dec 2022-Jan 2023. We also collected illness onset time, diagnosis time, hospital admission time, and start and end times of each treatment. In addition, we collected the time of vaccination, inoculation times, and type of COVID-19 vaccination thorough the vaccination system. We described the epidemiological characteristics across vaccine inoculation doses, and estimated the risk of death, mechanical ventilation, and admission to the intensive care unit for patients admitted to hospital. We used the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate the survival rate and plot the survival curve, and the Cox proportional hazards model to assess the effect of covariates on survival time.ResultsOf the 1495 cases, 58.1% were male. The median ages in the non-vaccinated and vaccinated groups were 80 and 47 years. Elderly with underlying medical conditions and lower BMI less willing to vaccinate(p < 0.05). Both the inactivated vaccine and adenovirus vaccine could reduce clinical severity and prolong survival time, and the protective effect of booster vaccination was the best. The clinical severity increased linearly from the booster vaccinated group to the full-course vaccinated group and non-vaccinated groups, and the death risk of COVID-19 cases without vaccination was the highest.ConclusionBooster vaccines of COVID-19 can provide greater protection against severe illness and death, and continuous monitoring and regular assessment are needed to minimize the risk of a recurrence of the pandemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10261-y.

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2, which emerged in 2019, poses a huge threat to human beings. In the early stages, the emergence of numerous cases, the lack of awareness of the infectious disease, and the absence of effective pharmaceutical interventions meant that many patients were unable to receive timely and effective treatment, and there were many deaths. As one of the most effective methods of preventing and controlling respiratory infectious diseases, vaccines have been developed by countries around the world since the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. China has followed five technical routes to research and develop vaccines simultaneously and successfully launched them with conditions. As of November 11, 2022, 1.34 billion Chinese had been vaccinated with the COVID-19 vaccine, of which 1.27 billion had completed the full course of vaccination.

However, vaccine efficacy against COVID-19 infections declined by 20–30% over six months [1–4]. In addition, several new variants of SARS-CoV-2, such as Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta and Omicron, exhibit strong immune evasion to the neutralizing antibodies induced by primary vaccination [5–9]. Recent studies have shown that booster vaccinations could provide effective protection against severe illness and hospitalization caused by Delta, and Omicron [2], and increase the durability of vaccine-induced protection. Therefore, many countries have promoted booster vaccines. Israel began to offer a second booster to the elderly and immunocompromised individuals in January 2022 [10, 11]. The United States offered second RNA boosters to at- risk people in March 2022 [2, 12]. At the end of 2021, China began to offer the third dose of the inactivated vaccine and the second dose of the adenovirus vaccine, and the vaccination rate was 38.41%.

On December 7, 2022, the Chinese government announced that the three-year COVID-19 pandemic restrictions were being eased. Subsequently, the first large number of Omicron infections suddenly appeared in Beijing. Although most cases had mild or non-existent symptoms, there were still many severe cases that led to hospitalization and treatment. Many studies have shown that the COVID-19 vaccine is very effective in preventing severe cases and deaths, especially booster vaccinations, which were essential for obtaining long-lasting immunity against Omicron [13]. Although many studies focus on the booster vaccination and full-course vaccination of COVID-19 vaccines, the relationship between the vaccination type and dose of COVID-19 and the onset time and clinical severity has not been comprehensively examined. In this study, we summarize and assess the survival time, clinical severity, and key epidemiological parameters of the time-to-event distributions of COVID-19 cases vaccinated with different types and doses in Beijing Omicron outbreak. We hope these results can be used for the formulation of COVID-19 vaccination policies in the post pandemic era.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a retrospective study including 1,495 cases with COVID-19 infection in hospitals between December 2022 and January 2023. All cases were diagnosed based on the diagnosis and treatment protocol of the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (ninth edition). All were confirmed to have Omicron variant SARS-CoV-2 by RT-PCR.

Case definitions

Booster vaccination was defined as receiving the third dose inactivated vaccine (SARS CoV 2 vaccine, Vero cell) from Sinovac and China-Biotics or the second dose recombinant COVID-19 vaccine (adenovirus type 5 vector) from ConSino according to the national immunization plan. Non-booster vaccination was defined as receiving one or two doses of the inactivated vaccine or one dose of the adenovirus vaccine. Unvaccinated was defined as not receiving any dose of vaccine.

Data collection

We collected epidemiological, clinical, laboratory, and clinical management data for the 1495 cases from the hospital information system (HIS) using standardized forms. We also collected the illness onset time, diagnosis time, hospital admission time, and start and end times of each treatment. In addition, we collected the time of vaccination, inoculation times, and type of COVID-19 vaccination thorough the vaccination system. We divided the total cases into two groups based on whether they had been vaccinated: the first group was unvaccinated, and the second group was patients who had been vaccinated. The latter was divided into two: the booster vaccinated group and the non-booster vaccinated.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical methods were adopted to analyze the continuous and categorical variables. We used the Gaussian density estimation method, which is a nonparametric technique, to create a smoothing approximation of time-to-event distributions. The time-to-event distributions we assessed were presumed to be the time from vaccination to illness onset. Clinical severity measurements were estimated using the number of deaths, mechanical ventilation, and high-flow oxygen therapy divided by the total number of hospitalized COVID-19 cases with clear outcomes, and estimated 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the risks by binomial tests. We used the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate the survival rate and plot the survival curve. Before conducting a log-rank test between different survival curves, we constructed a test for the proportional hazards (PH) assumption on the groups by creating a time-dependent covariate in a PH model. The effect of covariates on survival time was assessed using the Cox proportional hazards model. We used Cox regression to estimate vaccine efficacy, with correction factors including gender and age. We built a forest plot to show the hazard ratios (HRs) of different risk factors and their 95% confidence interval (CI) using Excel.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Demographic characteristics and comorbidities

A total of 1495 COVID-19 cases were analyzed in this study. Of these, 754 (50.4%) were vaccinated, and the overall vaccinated-to-non-vaccinated ratio was 1.02:1. More than half of the participants were male (58.1%; 41.9% were female). The median ages in the non-vaccinated and vaccinated groups were 80 and 47 years, respectively, and the 35–60 age group had the highest proportion of vaccinations, followed by the < 35 group (31.8%) and the 60–85 group (21.9%). Compared to cases with BMI between 18.5 and 24.9, more obese cases (BMI: 25–29.9) were vaccinated and the proportion was 25.1%. Of the 754 vaccinated cases, 31.0% had one or more underlying medical conditions, which was significantly higher than in the non-vaccinated group.

According to the results of the logistic regression analysis, the elderly, people with underlying medical condition, and people with lower BMIs were less willing to be vaccinated (p < 0.05, Table 1). The median times from the first dose vaccination to illness onset were longer in the booster vaccinated group than in the non-booster vaccinated group, with median times of 601 days (IQR: 564–631) and 382 days (IQR: 249–575), respectively. Cases in the inactivated vaccine group had a longer time (602 days) from first dose vaccination to illness onset than those in the adenovirus vaccine group (579 days). However, the time interval from booster adenovirus vaccination to illness onset was actually shorter than for one dose of adenovirus vaccine (579 days vs. 585 days; Fig. 1).

Clinical severity and risk factors

The clinical severity of COVID-19 cases admitted to hospital was closely related to age and vaccination (Fig. 2). A linear increase in fatality risk, mechanical ventilation, and high-flow oxygen therapy was recorded from the booster vaccinated group to the non-booster vaccinated group to the non-vaccinated group, regardless of age (Fig. 2A). In all vaccination groups, the fatality risk, mechanical ventilation, and high-flow oxygen therapy gradually increased with age (Fig. 2B-D).

The effect of covariates on survival time from hospital admission to death was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model, and the potential risk factors include older age (HR: 1.56, 95% CI: 1.28–1.89) and severe illness (1.71, 95% CI: 1.27–2,31, Table 2). Cases older than 60 years in the non-vaccination group had a higher risk of death, and the HRs increased from 2.7 (95% CI: 1.2–5.8) for those with booster vaccinations, to 2.8 (1.1–7.5) for those without booster vaccinations, to 4.6 (2.3–8.9) for those without vaccination (p < 0.05). In addition, cases with underlying medical conditions consistently had a higher risk of death (Table 3).

Antiviral treatment effectiveness

More than 50% of COVID-19 hospitalized cases had not received antiviral treatment, which can reduce the risk of death. Paxlovid treatment can lower the risk of death; the HR was 0.47 (0.32–0.71, Table 2). compared to unvaccinated cases without treated with Paxlovid or Azvudine, Lower risk of death in unvaccinated cases treated with Paxlovid or Azvudine and the ORs were 0.6 (0.4–0.9) and 0.4 (0.2–0.9), respectively (Table 3).

COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness

The effect of covariates on survival time from hospital admission to death was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model, and the potential protective prognostic factors included receiving vaccination; the HR was 0.61 (0.44–0.85, Table 2). It was found that the death risk of COVID-19 cases without vaccination was higher than for those who were vaccinated (p < 0.05). The death risk for patients with the inactivated vaccination was lower, and the adjusted HR was 0.6 (95% CI: 0.4–0.8, p = 0.001). The death risk was lowest for cases who had been booster vaccinated with the inactivated vaccine, and the difference was statistically significant (adjusted HR = 0.5, 95% CI: 0.3–0.7, p < 0.001). Moreover, although the death risk for COVID-19 cases with one or two doses of the inactivated vaccine was lower than for those in the unvaccinated group (adjusted HR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.5–1.3, and adjusted HR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.4–1.9, respectively), the difference was not statistically significant. For the adenovirus vaccine, the death risk for booster vaccination and full-course vaccination was not significantly different (adjusted HR: 0.2, 95% CI: 0.03–1.3, p = 0.09, Table 4).

Discussion

In December 2022, a large-scale outbreak of COVID-19 infection occurred in Beijing, which is the first large-scale outbreak in Beijing. Given that there was no previous large-scale outbreak in Beijing, no comprehensive clinical severity study of massive COVID-19 infection in Beijing has been conducted. In addition, a large number of people in Beijing have already received booster vaccines, but there have been no studies on the efficacy of booster vaccine. Our study presents the clinical severity of COVID-19 cases with different vaccine types and injections, and we found that vaccination can reduce clinical severity and prolong survival time. In addition, the protective effect of full-course vaccination was better than that of partial-course vaccination. However, there was no difference between the protective effects of inactivated vaccines and adenovirus vaccines.

In this study, compared to no vaccine, both inactivated and adenovirus vaccines can reduce the risk of death, with improved effects after booster vaccination. Specifically, 76.3% of deaths were unvaccinated cases, while 40.5% and 11.5% of surviving cases had received booster vaccination and full-course vaccination. Previous studies have also shown that the effectiveness of full-course and booster mRVA vaccinations against the Omicron variant is 55.9% and 80.8%, respectively [14–16]. Although studies have shown that the Omicron variant can escape neutralizing antibodies and present a serious threat to COVID-19 vaccinesn [14, 17, 18] this study indicates that a booster vaccine of COVID-19 can help reduce the incidence of severe cases and deaths. Recently one study also presented that boosters would probably offer even greater levels of protection against severe and fatal disease [19]. Given the superior effectiveness of the booster vaccine against the Omicron variant, our findings support maximizing coverage with the booster vaccine on the national scale, especially for the elderly and people with underlying medical conditions.

Many studies have shown that decline in vaccine efficacy or effectiveness against severe COVID-19 disease over time since vaccination; however, the vaccine effectiveness against severe disease remained high for up to six months after vaccination for booster vaccines [20]. Our study also found that the booster vaccine significantly prolonged the duration from vaccination to illness onset compared with full-course vaccination and only one dose. In addition, the risk for death and severity showed an increasing trend from the booster vaccine group to the full-course vaccine group to the non-vaccine group. This demonstrates that although the effectiveness of vaccines decreases over time, the booster vaccine has a good protective effect. Possible reasons for the decrease in vaccine efficacy are related to the amount of most vaccine-derived antibodies decling, including neutralized antibodies [21, 22]. Compared with people who had only received the first vaccine dose, the efficacy of the vaccine increased after a booster dose, which supports evidence for possible waning immunity 23.24. Therefore, to minimize the occurrence of severe illnesses and deaths, booster vaccines should be given at regular intervals.

Booster vaccinations are especially important for vulnerable and high-risk groups [2, 25]. Israel began to offer a second booster to elderly individuals in January 2022. (10–11) The United States began to offer second mRNA boosters to the elderly and people with underlying medical conditions in March 2022 [12]. Many studies have shown that the booster vaccine reduces COVID-19 related hospitalizations and deaths in people over 60 years old [26]. Subgrouping by age in this study revealed that the risk of death and serious illness increased significantly with age, and booster vaccination provides better protection than non-vaccination or full-course/non full-course vaccination. Therefore, increasing booster vaccination of the elderly is crucial for the prevention and control of COVID-19, especially for the prevention of severity. However, our study found that elderly people were reluctant to vaccinate, although they were the priority population for vaccination. Adverse effects were an important reason why they were unwilling to receive vaccines. Although severe adverse events have been reported, including thrombosis, thrombocytopenia, myocarditis, and pericarditis, etc [27, 28], the most common adverse effects are mild. This information needs to be publicized through multiple channels and avenues.

In the large-scale outbreak of Omicron variant infection of SARS-2-CoV in Beijing, Paxlovid and Azvudine were the two main drugs used for clinical treatment, and the results showed that Paxlovid treatment can lower the risk of death. Paxlovid is an emergency treatment that was authorized by the FDA for the treatment of COVID-19 on December 22, 2021 [29]. Research has shown that Paxlovid can reduce the risk of hospitalization or death by 30%, [29, 30] especially in the era of Omicron [31]. Although elderly patients are the most affected by and vulnerable to COVID-19, the use of Paxlovid in elderly patients may promote recovery and reduce the viral load without adverse events [32].

Although vaccines can reduce severe illness and death and are widely utilized against SARS-CoV‐2 [34–37], the efficacy of vaccines continues to decline. For example, the efficacy of the COVID-19 vaccine in reducing hospitalizations caused by the Omicron variant was lower than it was against the Delta variant [33, 37]. Moreover, a significant proportion of the population remains unvaccinated [33]. Therefore, specialty antivirals and vaccines are essential. This study found that both Paxlovid and Azvudine could reduce the risk of death for unvaccinated adults with COVID-19. Some studies also found that early use of antiviral drugs is effective for reducing the duration of both the fever [33], and virus shedding [38].

This study has several limitations. First, only cases with Omicron variant infection were enrolled in this study, and no comparisons were made with other strains of SARS-CoV-2. Second, no blood was collected to determine the serum antibody titer levels to quantitatively assess the cases’ antibody levels at the time of illness onset. Third, our study analyzed only hospitalized cases with SARS-CoV-2 infection, and excluded clinically mild cases. Therefore, the full effectiveness spectrum of COVID-19 vaccines was not assessed. Lastly, the study period is during the concentrated outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic, and the duration from vaccination to illness onset could be affected by the vaccination in population and the current epidemic, resulting in selection bias of sample, external factors interference, etc.

In conclusion, we have described the epidemiology, clinical severity, and efficacy of vaccines in hospitalized cases with the Omicron variant of COVID-19 in Beijing city. Both the inactivated vaccine and adenovirus vaccine could reduce clinical severity and prolong survival time, and the protective effects of booster vaccination were the best. Considering the continued spread and rapid mutation of SARS-CoV-2, continuous monitoring and regular assessment of epidemiological characteristics and clinical severity are necessary for pandemic risk assessment. Furthermore, regular booster vaccinations and the development of new vaccines to respond to SARS-CoV-2 mutations.
