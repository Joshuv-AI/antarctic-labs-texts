Lnc-TIM3 promotes acute lymphoblastic leukemia progression through miR-5094-mediated regulation of TIM3
Authors: Jing Sun, Jiou Zhao, Wenfeng Fu, Huimin Li, Meiyun Kang, Yongjun Fang, Yaping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common hematologic malignancy in children and is associated with dysregulated long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), some of which may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, the expression pattern and biological role of Lnc-TIM3 in pediatric ALL remain poorly understood. This study aimed to characterize Lnc-TIM3 expression in pediatric ALL and investigate its functional role and underlying regulatory mechanism, with a particular focus on the Lnc-TIM3/miR-5094/TIM3 axis.MethodsThe expression levels of lncRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) were measured using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Cell proliferation, DNA synthesis, migration, and invasion capabilities were assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays, soft-agar colony assays, flow cytometry, and in vitro transduction experiments, respectively. In vivo validation was conducted using a zebrafish model. The competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory mechanism was examined using dual-luciferase reporter assays, whereas protein expression was assessed by Western blotting.ResultsLnc-TIM3 was highly expressed in primary bone marrow samples from patients with ALL and in ALL cell lines. Lnc-TIM3 knockdown reduced TIM3 expression and significantly inhibited ALL progression in both in vitro and in vivo models. Furthermore, inhibition of miR-5094 partially reversed the phenotypic effects of Lnc-TIM3 knockdown. Changes in GAL9 and downstream signaling components suggested, but did not establish, the involvement of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling.ConclusionsLnc-TIM3 promotes the progression of ALL by binding to miR-5094 and upregulating TIM3 expression.Keywords: Lnc-TIM3, TIM3, miR-5094, GAL9, competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Lnc-TIM3 was upregulated in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) samples and cell lines. Its knockdown suppressed cell proliferation, clonogenicity, migration, and invasion, promoted apoptosis, and reduced leukemia growth and dissemination in a zebrafish model.

• Lnc-TIM3 regulated TIM3 expression by competitively binding miR-5094, and miR-5094 inhibition partially reversed the molecular and cellular effects of Lnc-TIM3 knockdown.

What is known and what is new?

• Dysregulated long non-coding RNAs contribute to ALL biology, but the expression and function of Lnc-TIM3 in pediatric ALL have remained unclear.

• This study identifies the Lnc-TIM3/miR-5094/TIM3 axis as a regulatory pathway associated with ALL progression. Changes in GAL9 and downstream signaling components suggest possible involvement of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling but do not establish a functional autocrine loop.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The Lnc-TIM3/miR-5094/TIM3 axis warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic target and exploratory biomarker in pediatric ALL. Independent clinical validation and direct functional testing of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling are required before clinical application; no immediate change in clinical practice is supported by the present findings.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) accounts for up to 26% of childhood malignancies worldwide (1). Contemporary treatment strategies have substantially improved outcomes in childhood ALL, with 5-year event-free survival (EFS) rates approaching 90%, compared with approximately 10% six decades ago (2-4). However, outcomes in the St. Jude Total Therapy Study 16 showed no significant improvement compared with those in Study 15 (5), and approximately 20% of pediatric patients still experience relapse or death (6). Therefore, the pathogenesis of ALL should be further explored to facilitate the development of new therapies.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are typically defined as RNA molecules longer than 200 nucleotides that lack open reading frames. They constitute an abundant and widely distributed class of non-coding RNAs in eukaryotes (7-9). LncRNAs actively regulate tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence (10-12). Previous findings have demonstrated that aberrant lncRNA expression contributes to ALL progression (13-16), making lncRNAs potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for pediatric ALL.

T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (TIM3), as a recognized immune checkpoint, specifically marks a functionally impaired subset within leukemia tumor-infiltrating CD8+ PD1+ T cells (17). Ji et al. first reported that Lnc-TIM3 (ENST00000443947.1, AC011288.2, located in chromosome 7) can specifically bind to TIM3 to promote T cell exhaustion in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and disease progression (18). We have previously detected that Lnc-TIM3 is significantly up-regulated in pediatric ALL bone marrow samples, but its functions and pathological mechanisms remain uncharacterized. Most lncRNAs regulate gene expression by sponging microRNAs (miRNAs) (19). Bioinformatic prediction identified miRNAs that could potentially bind both Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3. Subsequent verification using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) identified miR-5094 for further investigation. Here, we aimed to establish a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network of Lnc-TIM3 to illustrate how Lnc-TIM3 cooperates with miR-5094 to promote ALL progression. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1285/rc).

Methods

Clinical specimens

This single-center observational study included 178 children with newly diagnosed ALL who were treated at Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China) between 2018 and 2021. Bone marrow samples were collected at diagnosis before initiation of antileukemic treatment. Control bone marrow samples were obtained from 100 children without hematologic malignancies. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 202302002-1), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

The 178 patients constituted the full ALL cohort. Pretreatment Lnc-TIM3 expression in this cohort was used to evaluate associations with clinical characteristics and to perform the exploratory survival analysis. A nested subset of 100 of these 178 patients, for whom sufficient pretreatment bone marrow material was available for parallel measurement of Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3, was compared with the 100 independent controls and was used for the case–control expression, correlation, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. Within this 100-patient subset, 22 patients also had bone marrow samples collected after documented remission; their diagnostic and remission samples were analyzed as matched pairs. Thus, the 100-patient and 22-patient sample sets were nested analytical subsets of the 178-patient cohort rather than independent cohorts, whereas the 100 control samples constituted a separate comparison group. Each patient contributed once to the cross-sectional analyses, and the two samples from each of the 22 paired patients were used only for the within-patient diagnosis-remission comparison.

Cell lines and cell culture

Jurkat (T-cell), Nalm-6 (B-cell), and 293T cells were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and maintained in our laboratory. The cells were cultured at 37 ℃ in an incubator containing 5% CO2 using RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from bone marrow and cell samples strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions for TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription of lncRNA and messenger RNA (mRNA) was performed using HiScript QRT SuperMix (Vazyme, China), whereas miRNA reverse transcription was performed using the miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). For miR-5094, Bulge-LoopTM miRNA RT-qPCR primer sets containing reverse-transcription primers and qPCR primer pairs were designed and synthesized by General Biol (China). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Vazyme). GAPDH served as the reference gene for lncRNA and mRNA, whereas U6 was used as the reference for miRNA. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All experiments were performed independently three times. The gene-specific primers are listed in Table S1.

Cell transfection, lentivirus packaging and infection

The pcDNA3.1 vector containing full-length Lnc-TIM3 cDNA, the empty pcDNA3.1 vector, shRNAs targeting Lnc-TIM3, and the corresponding negative-control shRNA were designed and synthesized by GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). miR-5094 mimics and the corresponding negative-control mimics, as well as miR-5094 inhibitors and the corresponding negative-control inhibitors, were provided by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Oligonucleotides were transfected into ALL cells using LipofectamineTM 3000 reagent (Invitrogen). Stable transfectants were selected using puromycin (Invitrogen) at a concentration of 4 µg/mL.

Cell proliferation

Stably transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5×103 cells per well and cultured for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. At each time point, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Kobayashi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader.

Soft agar colony formation

The assay was performed in 6-well plates using a two-layer agar system. Equal volumes of 1.2% melted Noble agar and twofold-concentrated complete culture medium were mixed, and 1.5 mL of the mixture was added to the bottom of each well. For the top agar layer, 0.5 mL of 0.7% agar was mixed with an equal volume of cell suspension at a concentration of 104 cells/mL. Complete RPMI 1640 medium (500 µL) was then added to maintain the moisture of the top agar layer. After 2–4 weeks of incubation, colonies were stained with nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (10 mg/mL; BioFrox, Guangzhou, China) for visualization and counting.

Flow cytometry

A total of 1×106 stably transfected cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 µM EdU for 24 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). DNA synthesis was quantified using the Cell-LightTM EdU Apollo®567 Cell Tracking Kit (RiboBio) and flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). For cell-cycle analysis, 1×106 logarithmic-phase cells were harvested and stained using the Cell Cycle Staining Kit (Lianke Bio, Zhejiang, China). The proportion of cells in each phase of the cell cycle was determined using the standard cell-cycle analysis model.

Transwell cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8-µm pores (NEST, China). For migration assays, the membranes were coated with 0.1 mg/mL fibronectin. For invasion assays, the membranes were precoated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) diluted 1:8 and allowed to solidify at room temperature for 6 h. The lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and 5×105 cells suspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 36 h, cells that had not migrated or invaded were removed from the upper surface of the membrane. The migrated or invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min at room temperature.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Using the 3' untranslated region (UTR) sequences of Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3 as templates, wild-type and mutant fragments containing the potential miR-5094-binding sites were designed and synthesized. These fragments were subsequently cloned into the pmiRRB reporter vector (GeneChem Co., Ltd.). The resulting constructs were designated Lnc-TIM3-WT-luc, TIM3-WT-luc, Lnc-TIM3-MT-luc, and TIM3-MT-luc; 293T cells were seeded at 1×105 cells per well in 24-well plates. After 24 h, the cells were co-transfected with 50 ng of the psiCHECKTM-2 reporter vector and 50 nM miR-5094 mimic or the corresponding negative control using LipofectamineTM 3000 reagent (Invitrogen). Forty-eight hours after transfection, luciferase activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, USA).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) supplemented with PMSF protease inhibitor, sonicated for 30 s, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bollinger, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% defatted whey protein for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with specific primary antibodies. Primary antibodies against AKT2, TIM3, ERK, NF-κB, p-AKT2, and p-ERK (CST; 1:1,000), and GAPDH (CST; 1:2,000) were diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Progenics, China). After overnight incubation, the membranes were washed thoroughly and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibodies (Jackson Immunological Research). Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bollinger). Band intensities of total AKT2, total ERK, and NF-κB were quantified by background-corrected densitometry and normalized to GAPDH.

Subcellular fractionation

The PARISTM Kit (Invitrogen; AM1921) was used to separate the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of ALL cells, and RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The levels of Lnc-TIM3, GAPDH, and U6 were subsequently measured by RT-qPCR, and relative abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Xenograft assays in vivo

The animal experiments were conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All zebrafish experiments were performed under ethical approval (No. HJSW-26071601) granted by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, in compliance with the national and institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Adult zebrafish were maintained under standard laboratory conditions, and embryos were obtained through spontaneous mating. At 2 days post-fertilization (2 dpf), the embryos were anesthetized with tricaine (Thermo Fisher). Tumor cells labeled with CM-DiI dye (Invitrogen) were microinjected into the perivitelline space at approximately 200 cells per embryo. The injected embryos were subsequently transferred to 24-well plates for culture. Their condition was monitored and documented every 3 days. Tumor growth was imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus), and xenograft fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. Tumor growth was quantified using the geometric mean fluorescence-intensity ratio, and metastatic potential was assessed by measuring the migration distance of the tumor cells.

At the end of the experimental observation period (or when predefined humane endpoints were reached, such as significant weight loss, lethargy, or tumor burden exceeding a maximum permitted size), zebrafish embryos and adult fish were euthanized by immersion in a solution of 300 mg/L tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) buffered with sodium bicarbonate to a neutral pH. The fish were kept in the solution for at least 10 minutes after cessation of opercular movement to ensure death. Death was confirmed by absence of heartbeat and loss of response to tactile stimuli.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Experimental units were individual patients for clinical analyses, zebrafish embryos for xenograft analyses, and independent cell-culture experiments for in vitro assays. All cell-based experiments included three independent biological replicates. Technical replicates were averaged within each experiment and were not treated as independent observations.

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two independent groups were compared using a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test when normality and equal-variance assumptions were met, Welch’s t-test for normally distributed data with unequal variances, or the Mann-Whitney U test when normality was unsupported. Paired diagnostic and remission samples were analyzed using a paired t-test when within-pair differences were approximately normally distributed and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test otherwise.

Three or more groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s test for comparisons with a common control or Tukey’s test for all pairwise comparisons. When ordinary ANOVA assumptions were unmet, Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 test was used for unequal variances, and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction for non-normal data. CCK-8 time-course data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with treatment and time as factors, followed by Šídák’s test for prespecified between-group comparisons at each time point. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed the association between Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3 expression. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. ROC curves were summarized by the area under the curve with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Lnc-TIM3 is upregulated in ALL and is associated with selected clinical characteristics

The expression levels of Lnc-TIM3 were detected in bone marrow samples from 100 pediatric patients with ALL and 100 controls. Results showed significantly elevated expression of both Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3 in ALL patients (Figure 1A), a finding validated in cell lines (Figure 1B). Correlation analysis indicates a highly significant linear relationship among their levels of expression (Figure 1C). ROC analysis showed that Lnc-TIM3 discriminated ALL samples from control samples within the present cohort (Figure 1D). Because the ROC analysis was conducted in the same cohort and was not evaluated in an independent validation set, this result was considered exploratory and did not establish clinical diagnostic performance.

Within the cohort, 22 patients had matched bone marrow samples obtained at diagnosis and after remission. Lnc-TIM3 expression was measured at both time points (Figure 1F) and was significantly lower after remission than at diagnosis. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups using the median Lnc-TIM3 expression level. Survival did not differ significantly between the two groups (Figure 1G). Lnc-TIM3 gene promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ALL cells in vitro

To investigate the functional significance of Lnc-TIM3 in ALL, Jurkat and Nalm-6 cells were independently transfected with two distinct shRNAs targeting Lnc-TIM3. RT-qPCR confirmed that both shRNAs markedly reduced Lnc-TIM3 expression in both cell lines compared with the negative-control group (NC). We then conducted a comprehensive functional analysis using CCK-8, soft-agar colony-formation, flow-cytometry, and Transwell migration and invasion assays (Figure 2A).

CCK-8 assays showed that Lnc-TIM3 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation, as reflected by the growth curves (Figure 2B). Soft-agar colony-forming capacity was markedly reduced following Lnc-TIM3 knockdown compared with the NC group (Figure 2C). Flow cytometry analysis showed a notable decrease in the proportion of EdU-positive cells (Figure 2D) and reduced proportions of cells in the S and G2 phases (Figure 2E), indicating impaired DNA synthesis.

Moreover, Transwell assays showed that Lnc-TIM3 knockdown significantly reduced the numbers of migrating and invading cells compared with the NC group (Figure 2F,2G). These in vitro findings indicated that Lnc-TIM3 enhanced the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ALL cells.

Lnc-TIM3 gene promotes the development of ALL in vivo

To clarify the role of Lnc-TIM3 in regulating ALL growth and metastasis in vivo, Jurkat or Nalm-6 cells stably expressing shNC or either of two distinct shRNAs targeting Lnc-TIM3 were microinjected into the perivitelline space of zebrafish embryos at approximately 200 cells per embryo. At 3 days post-injection, tumor growth was assessed using the geometric mean fluorescence-intensity ratio, and metastatic potential was assessed by measuring the migration distance of tumor cells (Figure 3A). Lnc-TIM3 knockdown significantly reduced the geometric mean fluorescence-intensity ratio in both Jurkat and Nalm-6 xenografts (Figure 3B) and markedly decreased the metastatic distance (Figure 3C). These findings indicated that Lnc-TIM3 knockdown inhibited ALL development in vivo.

Lnc-TIM3 gene sponges miR-5094 gene to regulate the expression of TIM3 gene

To investigate the molecular mechanism of Lnc-TIM3, we first examined its coding potential using LGC (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/lgc). The coding-potential score was below 0, indicating that Lnc-TIM3 was a non-coding RNA.

As predicted by lncLocator (https://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/lncLocator2), Lnc-TIM3 was distributed in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. To determine its subcellular localization, Jurkat and Nalm-6 cells were fractionated, RNA was extracted, and Lnc-TIM3 localization was analyzed by RT-qPCR. The results were consistent with the lncLocator predictions (Figure 4A,4B). LncRNAs can act as ceRNAs by sponging miRNAs in many diseases, particularly cancer. A Venn diagram was used to illustrate the overlap between miRNAs predicted to interact with Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3. Seven miRNAs were predicted to bind both Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3 (Figure 4C). RT-qPCR analysis of these miRNAs in ALL cells showed that miR-5094 exhibited the most pronounced downregulation in Jurkat and Nalm-6 cells (Figure 4D).

To validate the interaction between miR-5094 and Lnc-TIM3/TIM3, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted. The data revealed that that co-transfection of Lnc-TIM3-wild type (Lnc-TIM3-WT), TIM3-WT and miR-5094 mimics, but this activity displayed no difference after co-transfection of mutant-type Lnc-TIM3 (Lnc-TIM3-MT), TIM3-MT and miR-5094 mimics (Figure 4E).

To determine whether Lnc-TIM3 and miR-5094 affected TIM3 expression, TIM3 mRNA and protein levels were measured in cells with Lnc-TIM3 knockdown. Lnc-TIM3 knockdown reduced TIM3 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4F). Similarly, in the presence of miR-5094, TIM3 mRNA and protein levels decreased significantly (Figure 4G). These results indicated that both Lnc-TIM3 and miR-5094 regulated TIM3 expression.

To explore how Lnc-TIM3 exerted its oncogenic role by regulating TIM3 expression, miR-5094 mimics or the corresponding negative control were transfected into shLnc-TIM3-expressing cell lines for rescue assays. TIM3 expression at the mRNA and protein levels was first examined in the presence of miR-5094. Lnc-TIM3 downregulation markedly reduced TIM3 abundance at both the mRNA and protein levels. Conversely, miR-5094 overexpression restored the functional defects caused by Lnc-TIM3 deficiency in ALL cells (Figure 5A,5B). Inhibition of miR-5094 significantly alleviated the effects of Lnc-TIM3 knockdown on cell proliferation (Figure 5C), colony formation (Figure 5D), the proportion of EdU-positive cells (Figure 5E), the proportion of cells in the S and G2 phases (Figure 5F), migration, and invasion (Figure 5G) in both Jurkat and Nalm-6 cells. Collectively, Lnc-TIM3 regulated TIM3 expression in ALL cells by binding miR-5094.

miR-5094 gene attenuates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ALL cells

Previous results showed that miR-5094 expression was markedly downregulated in both ALL bone marrow samples and cell lines. To investigate the function of miR-5094 in ALL, cells were treated with a miR-5094 inhibitor or the corresponding negative control, and cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were evaluated. Following treatment, cell growth (Figure 6A), the number of soft-agar colonies (Figure 6B), the proportion of EdU-positive cells (Figure 6C), the proportion of cells in the S and G2 phases (Figure 6D), and the numbers of migrating and invading cells (Figure 6E) were reduced. In the zebrafish xenograft model, tumor growth and metastasis were markedly inhibited by the miR-5094 inhibitor (Figure 6F).

GAL9 expression and changes in AKT2, ERK, and NF-κB after Lnc-TIM3 knockdown

We had proven that Lnc-TIM3 promoted ALL progression by the miR-5094/TIM3 axis, but the downstream in ALL is still unknown. Galectin 9 (GAL9) has been found as a ligand for TIM3 (20,21). Akashi et al. have identified that TIM3+ acute myeloid leukemia (AML) stem cells secrete its ligand GAL9 in an autocrine manner and promotes AML progression, resulting in NF-kB activation (20). According to the analysis of the Wiki Pathways, the combination of TIM3 and GAL9 up-regulates the expression of AKT and ERK, inducing the Phosphorylation of AKT and ERK, and then activates the expression of NF-kB to play a biological function (22). Inspired by these findings, The mRNA levels of GAL9 were detected in ALL and 293T cell strains. GAL9 was up regulated in ALL cell lines, as well as the bone marrow samples from ALL patients (Figure 7A), which implies a high expression of GAL9 in ALL and a possible autocrine loop of TIM3/GAL9 in ALL cells. From the TARGET database, we also found AKT2, ERK and NF-kB expression levels were up-regulated in ALL (Figure 7B), suggesting that the TIM3/GAL9 autocrine stimulatory loop was activated in ALL. Western blotting showed that Lnc-TIM3 knockdown was accompanied by lower GAPDH-normalized abundance of total AKT2, total ERK, and NF-κB in Jurkat and Nalm-6 cells (Figure 7C). These observations indicate an association between Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3/GAL9-related signaling components. However, because TIM3 or GAL9 was not functionally interrupted, the present data do not establish a TIM3/GAL9 autocrine loop in ALL.

Discussion

Accumulating evidence indicates that lncRNAs have critical roles in the initiation and progression of cancers, including cervical cancer (23), breast cancer (24), and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (25,26). A number of lncRNA-based diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets have been identified in cancer (27-29). Numerous lncRNAs are dysregulated in ALL, suggesting their involvement in this disease (30-33), although their molecular mechanisms remain elusive. This study demonstrated for the first time that Lnc-TIM3 was highly expressed in both bone marrow samples from patients with ALL and ALL cell lines. Lnc-TIM3 downregulation effectively inhibited ALL progression in vitro and in vivo, supporting its oncogenic role in this disease.

TIM3, codified from hepatitis A virus cell receptor 2 (HAVCR2), is located on chromosome 5 at the q33.2 locus (34), is a cell surface molecule found on both immune cells and some cancer cells (35-39). Several studies have revealed that TIM3 is associated with disease progression in ALL (40-44). In our study, we confirmed that TIM3 was overexpressed in bone marrow samples and cell lines, which aligns with previous findings. A positive relationship was observed between the expression of Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3 in ALL samples, suggesting that Lnc-TIM3 exerts its oncogenic role by regulating TIM3 expression. Furthermore, the TIM3/GAL9 autocrine circuit serves a critical function in tumorigenesis. Research indicates that the interaction between TIM3 on tumor cells and its ligand GAL9 can enhance tumor cell survival, promote immunosuppression, and facilitate cancer progression, thereby contributing to a more aggressive tumor phenotype. This underscores the critical involvement of the TIM3/GAL9 axis in the oncogenic process within the context of ALL.

As an interaction network of RNAs, the ceRNA has been widely used in the study of lncRNAs (19,45). LncRNAs can sequester miRNAs through the ceRNA mechanism, thereby modulating the target gene regulation associated with cancer initiation, growth, apoptosis, and drug resistance (46,47). miRNAs can bind to the 3' UTR of target RNAs to degrade target genes (48). We found that miR-5094 serves as a bridge connecting Lnc-TIM3 and TIM3. miR-5094 has been reported to suppress the proliferation of HeLa cells, Jurkat cells and CD4+ T cells (49). Our study further ascertains this effect of miR-5094 as an antioncogene. miR-5094 inhibitor could partially reverse the expression of TIM3 down-regulated by Lnc-TIM3 knockdown. The downregulation of total proteins AKT2, ERK, and NF-kappaB may result from the combined effect of several factors. First, miR-5094 may directly target the mRNA of these proteins, inhibiting their transcription levels. Additionally, these proteins may be tagged for degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thereby reducing their overall quantity. Moreover, intracellular negative feedback mechanisms could also suppress signaling pathways, further affecting protein expression. Furthermore, if cells undergo apoptosis or cell cycle arrest, this would further decrease the expression levels of these key signaling proteins. The rescue experiments supported a regulatory relationship among Lnc-TIM3, miR-5094, and TIM3. Inhibition of miR-5094 partially restored TIM3 expression and partially reversed the phenotypic effects induced by Lnc-TIM3 knockdown. These findings support the Lnc-TIM3/miR-5094/TIM3 axis in ALL. The TIM3/GAL9 axis has been reported to support self-renewal and leukemic progression in AML stem cells (50). In the present study, GAL9 was highly expressed in ALL samples and cell lines, and Lnc-TIM3 knockdown was accompanied by reductions in AKT2, ERK, and NF-κB protein abundance. These observations are consistent with possible involvement of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling in ALL. However, they do not establish an autocrine loop because TIM3 and GAL9 were not functionally interrupted. The role of this pathway in ALL therefore requires direct validation in future studies.

TIM3 inhibitors are commercially available, and their current use in cancer treatment warrants further investigation. Several TIM3 inhibitors, including blocking antibodies and small-molecule agents, have been developed and are currently undergoing clinical trials for various cancers, including solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. These inhibitors are intended to disrupt TIM3-mediated immunosuppressive signaling in tumor cells and restore antitumor immunity.

In ALL, the therapeutic potential of TIM3 inhibitors is particularly relevant given the observed overexpression of TIM3 in leukemic cells. Combining TIM3 inhibition with other immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors or CAR-T-cell therapies, may improve therapeutic efficacy relative to existing regimens. These combinations may improve outcomes in patients with ALL by promoting a more robust immune response against leukemic cells.

This study has several limitations. The clinical analyses were conducted in a single-center cohort, and the exploratory ROC findings were not assessed in an independent validation set. The survival analysis did not show a statistically significant difference between the Lnc-TIM3 expression groups and therefore does not support a prognostic conclusion. In addition, TIM3 or GAL9 was not functionally interrupted, so the observed downstream protein changes cannot establish a TIM3/GAL9 autocrine loop. Independent clinical validation and direct functional testing will be required to determine the diagnostic relevance of Lnc-TIM3 and the contribution of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling in ALL.

Additionally, we found that Lnc-TIM3 is expressed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, suggesting it may exert its effects through multiple pathways, including regulation of gene expression and cellular signaling. Understanding whether Lnc-TIM3 employs additional pathways to act on ALL requires in-depth analysis. Such research would not only clarify its role in leukemia progression but also establish the potential of Lnc-TIM3 as an efficient diagnostic and therapeutic target in ALL. This exploration of newly available TIM3 inhibitors and their application in clinical settings holds promise for improving outcomes for patients, particularly those suffering from drug-resistant forms of ALL.

Although the sample size was reasonable, it remained limited. In addition, the single-center collection of samples may have introduced bias and limited the generalizability of the results. The uneven age distribution of the cohort may also have affected the representativeness of the gene-expression profiles, particularly in pediatric ALL. Future studies should include larger samples and integrate multicenter clinical data to improve diversity and representativeness. Such an approach would enable a more comprehensive evaluation of the role of the TIM3/GAL9 autocrine circuit in ALL and help validate the present findings. These efforts may permit more accurate evaluation of the molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets associated with ALL.

Conclusions

Lnc-TIM3 was overexpressed in ALL and promoted leukemic cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft growth. Mechanistically, Lnc-TIM3 regulated TIM3 expression through miR-5094. These findings support the Lnc-TIM3/miR-5094/TIM3 axis as a potential regulatory pathway in ALL, whereas the contribution of TIM3/GAL9-related signaling remains to be established.
