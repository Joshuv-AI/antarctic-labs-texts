Introduction

Antibiotics are widely used in human medicine, livestock production, and aquaculture, and their continuous discharge into aquatic environments has become a growing environmental concern [1], [2]. Following administration or improper disposal, antibiotic residues can enter wastewater systems and receiving waters through incomplete metabolism and excretion, municipal wastewater effluents, agricultural runoff, and discharges from pharmaceutical manufacturing. As a result, antibiotics are frequently detected in wastewater treatment plants, pharmaceutical effluents, and river systems worldwide [3], [4], [5]. These residues are of concern because they can induce ecotoxicological effects and exert selective pressure on microbial communities, thereby promoting the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance [6], [7], [8]. Moreover, many antibiotics cannot be efficiently removed by conventional wastewater treatment processes due to their structural stability, biological activity, and limited biodegradability [9], [10]. Tetracycline (TC), one of the most widely used broad-spectrum antibiotics, is frequently selected as a model refractory contaminant owing to its extensive use and molecular recalcitrance. Its fused tetracyclic scaffold structure contributes to molecular recalcitrance and limited biodegradability [10], [11], while its multiple ionizable and chelating functional groups facilitate complexation and sorption onto sludge, minerals, and sediments, thereby promoting its retention in environmental matrices [11], [12]. The resulting prolonged environmental residence can extend the exposure of aquatic organisms and microbial communities to biologically active TC. Consequently, developing efficient technologies for TC degradation is important for improving the treatment of refractory antibiotic-containing wastewater.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have attracted considerable attention for treating refractory antibiotic-containing wastewater because they generate highly reactive oxidative species capable of degrading structurally stable and biologically active contaminants [13]. Among these technologies, ultraviolet (UV)-assisted oxidant activation has been extensively investigated for TC degradation, with UV/H2O2 and UV/PDS representing two radical-based oxidation pathways [13], [14]. Owing to differences in activation mechanisms, reactive-species generation, pH dependence, and oxidant utilization efficiency between H2O2 and PDS, these UV-assisted systems can exhibit markedly different degradation kinetics and overall treatment performance [14], [15]. However, the practical performance of UV-based AOPs is often limited by high oxidant demand, radical scavenging, inefficient light utilization, and mass-transfer limitations. Therefore, integrating UV-based oxidation with suitable process-intensification strategies is desirable to improve overall treatment efficiency [16], [17], [18], [19].

Hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) has emerged as a promising process-intensification strategy for wastewater treatment [20], [21]. During HC, the formation, growth, and violent collapse of cavitation bubbles can generate localized high-temperature and high-pressure microenvironments, accompanied by intense turbulence, microjet formation, and rapid interfacial renewal [21], [22]. These effects can enhance mass transfer, improve oxidant dispersion, and promote the generation of reactive oxidative species [23]. Acoustic cavitation (AC), generated by an externally applied ultrasonic field, can likewise produce intense physicochemical effects through bubble oscillation and collapse [24], [25]. In contrast to this acoustically driven mode, HC is generated by hydrodynamic pressure changes as liquid passes through flow constrictions, such as Venturi tubes or orifice plates, making it readily compatible with flow-through operation, continuous processing, and scale-up [20], [21]. Consequently, HC has increasingly been integrated with advanced oxidation systems for the degradation of refractory organic pollutants. Previous studies have demonstrated that HC can significantly enhance the degradation of antibiotics and other persistent contaminants, particularly when coupled with oxidants or UV irradiation, owing to improved radical generation and mass transfer characteristics [23], [26]. However, cavitation behavior is strongly influenced by reactor geometry, local boundary conditions, and cavitation regime [27], [28], [29]. Establishing the relationship between cavitation dynamics and pollutant degradation is thus important for the rational design and optimization of HC-assisted oxidation systems.

Although various HC-assisted oxidation processes involving H2O2, PDS, or UV irradiation have been investigated, direct comparisons of H2O2– and PDS-assisted HC/UV systems under identical hydrodynamic operating conditions remain limited [23], [26]. Because cavitation behavior and treatment performance are strongly influenced by HC operating conditions [20], [30], comparisons across independent studies may reflect not only oxidant-dependent chemistry but also differences in the hydrodynamic environment. It therefore remains unclear how H2O2 and PDS differ in their response to HC/UV coupling when evaluated within the same reactor platform under consistent HC operating conditions. To address this gap, the present study employed a single Venturi-based HC/UV platform to compare TC degradation in H2O2– and PDS-assisted systems under the same HC operating conditions. Cavitation behavior at different inlet pressures was first visualized to establish the hydrodynamic basis for the subsequent comparison. The subsequent investigations evaluated individual, binary, and ternary processes, oxidant dosage, pH response, reactive-species involvement, transformation products, predicted toxicity, applicability, and energy performance. Overall, this study clarifies the distinct responses of H2O2 and PDS to HC/UV coupling and provides a basis for oxidant selection and the rational design of HC-assisted advanced oxidation processes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Tetracycline (TC; molecular weight: 444.45 g mol−1, molecular formula: C22H24N2O8) was selected as the target pollutant in this study. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and sodium peroxydisulfate (PDS) were employed as the oxidants in the HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS systems, respectively. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were used to adjust the initial pH of the reaction solution. tert-Butanol (TBA), p-benzoquinone (BQ), and methanol (MeOH) were employed as radical scavengers to probe the possible involvement of different reactive species. HPLC-grade methanol and acetonitrile, together with oxalic acid dihydrate, were used for sample preparation and chromatographic analysis. Rhodamine B (RhB), malachite green (MG), and doxycycline hydrochloride (DCHCl) were selected as representative probe pollutants to evaluate the applicability of the developed systems. Detailed information regarding the purity, grade, and supplier of each of the major chemicals is provided in Table S1. Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were used as received without further purification. Ultrapure water was used throughout the experiments.

Experimental setup and HC/UV system

A self-designed Venturi-based HC/UV system was employed in this study, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1 (a). The experimental setup primarily consisted of a jacketed circulation tank, a centrifugal pump, a recirculation loop, a Venturi, two pressure gages, a flowmeter, UV lamps, and sampling ports. The working volume of the system was 15 L, and the bulk-solution temperature was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C by circulating cooling water through the tank jacket. For experiments involving HC, the reaction solution was continuously recirculated through the Venturi, while the inlet pressure was controlled using regulating valves and monitored by the pressure gages installed upstream and downstream of the Venturi. Four UV lamps, each with a nominal power of 8 W and an emission wavelength of 254 nm, were immersed in the circulation tank and were switched on when UV irradiation was required. For control experiments without HC (i.e., UV, H2O2, PDS, UV/H2O2, and UV/PDS), the Venturi was removed from the recirculation loop and replaced with a straight-pipe section with the same internal diameter as the main pipeline. The centrifugal pump remained in operation, and the recirculation flow rate was maintained at the same value as that used in the corresponding HC experiments, thereby providing the same recirculation flow rate. Further details regarding the configuration and operating principle of the HC/UV system were reported in our previous study [26].

V1–V3 represent flow-control valves. All dimensions are in mm. Reprinted from Wang et al. [26], Copyright (2025), with permission from Elsevier.

Cavitation behavior inside the transparent poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) Venturi was visualized using a high-speed camera (Phantom VEO 1310, Vision Research) to examine cavity evolution and flow characteristics at different inlet pressures. The camera was positioned perpendicular to the Venturi section, and an external light source was employed to enhance image contrast and cavity visibility. Images were captured after the flow conditions had stabilized at each inlet pressure. The recorded images were subsequently used to qualitatively evaluate cavitation inception, cavity growth, and cloud development near the throat and within the downstream divergent section of the Venturi.

Degradation experiments

Batch recirculation degradation experiments were conducted with TC solutions at an initial concentration of 30 mg L−1. Unless otherwise specified, each experiment was performed at 25 ± 2 °C for 60 min using the Venturi-based HC/UV system. Before each run, a 5 mL aliquot was collected as the initial sample, and the solution pH was adjusted to the desired value using H2SO4 or NaOH. After oxidant addition, the HC system and UV lamps were started immediately to initiate the reaction. At predetermined time intervals, 5 mL samples were withdrawn, immediately quenched with methanol to terminate further oxidation, and filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE syringe membrane filters prior to analysis. All experiments were conducted at least in duplicate, and the error bars represent the mean deviations of the values obtained from replicated measurements.

To compare the degradation performance of different oxidation routes, individual, binary, and ternary processes were evaluated, including HC alone, UV alone, oxidant alone, and their coupled combinations. For the H2O2-assisted route, the effects of oxidant dosage and initial pH on TC degradation performance were further investigated. For the PDS-assisted system, the influence of PDS dosage and initial pH was evaluated under otherwise identical operating conditions. The applicability of the optimized processes was subsequently assessed using RhB, MG, and DCHCl as representative organic probe pollutants.

Reactive-species quenching experiments were performed under the selected HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS conditions to elucidate the roles of the dominant oxidative species. The scavengers were added before initiating the treatment process, and the TC degradation efficiencies obtained in the presence of each scavenger were compared with those of the corresponding control experiments conducted without scavenger addition. TBA and BQ were employed as commonly used scavengers to probe the possible involvement of •OH and O2•-, respectively [14], [23]. In the PDS-assisted system, MeOH was introduced to assess the contribution of sulfate-radical-related oxidation pathways. Representative second-order rate constants for the reactions of these scavengers with the relevant reactive species are summarized in Table S5.

The potential toxicity of TC and its transformation products was estimated using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T., version 5.1.2; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency) [31]. The proposed molecular structures of the transformation products were converted into Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) notations for computational toxicity prediction and evaluated using the consensus method in T.E.S.T. Fathead minnow 96 h LC50, Daphnia magna 48 h LC50, developmental toxicity, and mutagenicity were selected as representative toxicity endpoints. The obtained results were used solely for a preliminary in silico toxicity assessment and for the comparative evaluation of toxicity evolution during TC transformation.

Analytical methods

The pH of the reaction solution was measured using a pH meter (PHS-25, INESA, China). The residual TC concentration was determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; LC-2050C, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a ShimNex UP C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile, methanol, and oxalic acid solution (0.01 mol L−1) at a volumetric ratio of 11: 67: 22. The flow rate was 1.0 mL min−1, the column temperature for TC was set at 25 °C, the detection wavelength was 254 nm, and the injection volume was maintained at 10 μL. For the applicability experiments, the concentrations of RhB, MG, and DCHCl were determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometry (DR6000, HACH, USA) using external calibration curves, with analytical wavelengths of 554 nm for RhB, 265 nm for MG, and 275 nm for DCHCl. TC transformation intermediates were tentatively identified using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled with a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer. Detailed UHPLC–HRMS/MS operating conditions, including the ionization mode, scan range, mobile-phase composition, and MS/MS parameters, are provided in the Supplementary Information (Table S2).

Results and discussion

Cavitation behavior in the Venturi reactor

To establish the hydrodynamic basis for TC degradation, cavitation behavior in the Venturi reactor was first visualized at varying inlet pressures. A detailed description of the visualization setup has been reported in our previous study, while similar high-speed imaging and x–t diagram methods have been widely employed to characterize sheet/cloud cavitation behavior and cavity shedding dynamics in Venturi systems [27], [34]. Representative time-resolved images of cavitation cloud evolution are shown in Fig. 2. As the inlet pressure increased from 0.20 to 0.35 MPa, the visible cavitation region progressively extended downstream from the Venturi throat, indicating more extensive cavitation development and enhanced vapor cavity growth. This trend agrees well with previous observations in Venturi flows. Liu et al. reported that the average cavity length increased with decreasing cavitation number [35], while Wang et al. similarly observed an extension of the cavitation zone as the exit-to-inlet pressure ratio decreased using high-speed photography [36]. In the present reactor, the cavitation number decreased from 0.39 to 0.27 as the inlet pressure increased from 0.20 to 0.35 MPa, accompanied by a progressive downstream extension of the visible cavity. Although the Venturi geometries and operating conditions differ among these studies, the consistent trends suggest that the observed cavity growth follows the characteristic pressure-dependent development of Venturi cavitation. During cavity-cloud formation, cavitation nuclei activated in the low-pressure region grow and are convected downstream, and their subsequent interaction and coalescence may contribute to the formation of larger, more contiguous vapor structures that contribute to cavity development [36], [37]. Although individual bubble-coalescence events could not be directly resolved at the spatial scale of the present visualization, the progressive downstream extension and development of the cavity cloud with increasing inlet pressure are consistent with this reported mechanism.

Time-resolved visualization of cavitation evolution in the Venturi at different inlet pressures: (a) 0.20 MPa (σ = 0.39), (b) 0.22 MPa (σ = 0.34), (c) 0.30 MPa (σ = 0.30), and (d) 0.35 MPa (σ = 0.27). The images show the progressive transition from compact attached cavitation to more developed cloud cavitation and intensified cloud shedding with increasing inlet pressure.

At relatively low inlet pressures of 0.20–0.22 MPa, the cavity cloud remained comparatively compact, and its periodic evolution was relatively stable. When the inlet pressure increased to 0.30–0.35 MPa, the cavity length increased noticeably and local cloud shedding became more pronounced, indicating a transition toward a more unsteady cavitation regime. Comparable unsteady behavior has been reported by Xu et al., who observed periodic sheet/cloud cavitation shedding in an axisymmetric Venturi and attributed the associated shedding and collapse dynamics to the combined influence of re-entrant flow and shock waves [34]. Gawandalkar and Poelma further demonstrated that interactions between pressure waves and the vapor cavity play an important role in cavity destabilization and shedding in axisymmetric Venturi flows, particularly under developed cavitation conditions [37]. These findings are qualitatively consistent with the more pronounced cloud shedding observed here at σ ≈ 0.27–0.30. The development of larger vapor structures through bubble growth and coalescence may further influence the subsequent shedding and collapse behavior of the cavity cloud [36], [37]. At 0.35 MPa, the more developed and dynamically active cavity cloud may therefore favor stronger collapse-related physicochemical effects [21], consistent with the higher HC-alone TC removal observed in Fig. 3. Overall, the cavitation behavior evolved from relatively compact attached cavitation to more developed cloud cavitation with increasing inlet pressure [38]. This pressure-dependent evolution provides the hydrodynamic basis for understanding the subsequent TC degradation behavior.

Effect of inlet pressure on TC degradation and cavitation performance

Based on the cavitation visualization results, the influence of inlet pressure on TC degradation was subsequently investigated using the HC-alone system. As shown in Fig. 3 (b), TC removal by HC alone remained relatively limited under all tested operating pressures, indicating that HC without supplementary oxidants or UV irradiation provided only modest oxidative capacity for TC degradation. A relatively rapid decrease in TC concentration was observed during the initial reaction stage, especially within the first few minutes, followed by a progressively slower removal rate. This behavior can be partly explained by the apparent pseudo-first-order kinetics used to describe TC degradation (Eq. (3)), for which the instantaneous degradation rate is proportional to the TC concentration. Thus, the higher initial TC concentration is associated with a greater absolute degradation rate at the beginning of the reaction, whereas the rate gradually decreases as TC is consumed. In addition, because HC alone provides only limited oxidative capacity, the decreasing concentration of parent TC reduces its probability of reaction with cavitation-generated reactive species, while transformation products formed during degradation may additionally compete for these species. These effects may further contribute to the slower parent-TC removal observed at later reaction times [39], [40].

Although cavity-length analysis was conducted over the visualized pressure range of 0.20–0.35 MPa, an additional HC-alone degradation experiment at 0.40 MPa was included to examine whether further increasing the inlet pressure could continue to improve TC removal. As shown in Fig. 3 (a), increasing the inlet pressure led to a progressive increase in cavity length, accompanied by a decrease in the cavitation number, consistent with progressive cavity development within the Venturi reactor. When the inlet pressure increased from 0.20 to 0.35 MPa, the more developed cavity cloud and intensified shedding indicated substantial changes in the cavitation dynamics. Under otherwise unchanged reactor geometry and solution conditions, increasing the inlet pressure modifies the pressure gradient and cavitation regime within the Venturi, which may affect both the intensity and occurrence of productive bubble-collapse events [41], [42], [43]. During productive collapse, rapid compression of the vapor/gas phase can generate higher transient local temperatures and pressures, promoting the thermal dissociation of water vapor and the formation of cavitation-derived reactive species, particularly •OH [40], [41]. Previous numerical and experimental studies have similarly shown that, within an appropriate operating range, variations in upstream pressure can substantially affect •OH production and the corresponding degradation performance of HC systems [39], [42]. Thus, the enhanced oxidative capacity observed within the investigated pressure range may be attributed to pressure-induced changes in cavitation dynamics and productive bubble-collapse behavior rather than to inlet pressure alone. However, when the inlet pressure was further increased to 0.40 MPa in the degradation experiments, TC removal decreased slightly. This result suggests that a further increase in pressure did not necessarily improve the effective cavitation contribution to TC degradation. Similar optimum-pressure behavior has been reported in HC systems, where excessive increases in inlet pressure may alter cavity population, bubble interactions, and collapse dynamics, thereby reducing the effectiveness of individual cavity-collapse events and limiting reactive-species generation [44], [45]. Therefore, a higher inlet pressure does not necessarily correspond to a higher degradation efficiency. This observation highlights that effective HC performance depends on achieving an optimal balance between cavitation intensity and productive bubble collapse rather than simply increasing the operating pressure [21], [44].

The kinetic analysis was consistent with the observed degradation profiles. The apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant increased from 0.0010 min−1 at 0.20 MPa to 0.0013 min−1 at 0.35 MPa, before decreasing to 0.0010 min−1 at 0.40 MPa (Table S3). The HC-alone apparent rate constants obtained in this study are of the same order of magnitude as values reported in previous HC studies of TC. In particular, the value obtained at 0.35 MPa (0.0013 min−1) is close to the 0.0014 min−1 reported by Wang et al. for HC-alone degradation of 30 mg L−1 TC in a Venturi-based system [46]. In comparison, a recent study using a self-excited oscillating cavitation jet reported a higher first-order rate constant of 0.0058 min−1 for HC-alone TC degradation at an initial concentration of 10 mg L−1 and an inlet pressure of 0.5 MPa [47]. These differences should be interpreted cautiously because apparent rate constants in HC systems can vary with reactor geometry, pollutant concentration, treated volume, operating pressure, and other experimental conditions. Therefore, the present values should be regarded as condition-dependent apparent kinetic descriptors rather than intrinsic kinetic constants. This trend further supports the existence of an optimal cavitation regime for TC degradation within the investigated pressure range. The corresponding TC removal efficiency after 60 min increased from 16.48% to 19.53% and subsequently decreased to 17.82% at the higher pressure (i.e., 0.4 MPa). Based on the combined degradation and kinetic results, 0.35 MPa was selected as the optimal inlet pressure for the subsequent experiments.

Baseline degradation performance and synergistic effects of HC/UV-based oxidation systems

To establish the baseline degradation characteristics of the Venturi-based HC/UV platform, the performances of individual, binary, and ternary processes were compared under identical hydrodynamic operating conditions. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the individual HC, UV, and H2O2 processes exhibited relatively limited TC degradation, with removal efficiencies of 19.53%, 13.47%, and 5.60%, respectively. The measurable TC removal observed under UV irradiation alone can be primarily attributed to direct photolysis of TC. At the 254-nm wavelength used in the present study, TC can absorb incident photons, leading to electronically excited states and subsequent photochemical transformation [14], [48]. In addition to this direct pathway, previous studies have suggested that UV irradiation of aqueous systems can also promote the formation of reactive species through photon-induced processes, while dissolved oxygen may participate in secondary reactions leading to species such as O2•- and HO2•[49], [50]. These photoinduced reactive pathways may therefore provide an additional contribution to pollutant degradation under UV-only conditions.

TC degradation kinetics and comparative performance of individual and coupled processes involving HC, UV, H2O2, and PDS: (a) individual processes (HC, UV, H2O2, and PDS) together with the common HC/UV binary process; (b) H2O2-assisted coupled processes (HC/H2O2, UV/H2O2, and HC/UV/H2O2); (c) PDS-assisted coupled processes (HC/PDS, UV/PDS, and HC/UV/PDS); (d) TC degradation efficiencies at 60 min; and (e) corresponding apparent pseudo-first-order rate constants (kobs). Reaction conditions: initial TC concentration = 30 mg L−1, inlet pressure = 0.35 MPa, temperature = 25 ± 2 °C, UV power (PUV) = 8 W × 4 lamps, H2O2 concentration = 1 mM, PDS concentration = 1 mM.

The binary HC/UV and UV/H2O2 systems exhibited enhanced degradation efficiencies of 34.27% and 37.60%, respectively, whereas the HC/H2O2 process remained comparatively ineffective (Fig. 4 (b)). Notably, although UV alone showed lower TC removal than HC alone, coupling UV with H2O2 resulted in substantially greater enhancement than coupling HC with H2O2, indicating that UV irradiation was more effective than HC alone in activating H2O2 under the tested conditions. This difference can be attributed to their distinct H2O2 activation mechanisms. Under 254-nm irradiation, H2O2 undergoes direct photolysis to generate •OH, thereby providing an effective photochemical pathway for continuous reactive-species generation [51]. In contrast, radical generation during HC originates primarily from localized and transient bubble-collapse events and is strongly dependent on the cavitation regime, reactor configuration, and operating conditions [52]. Therefore, under the present Venturi operating conditions, HC alone likely provided insufficient chemical activation of H2O2 to achieve an enhancement comparable to that of UV/H2O2.

When HC, UV, and H2O2 were combined into a ternary system, the TC degradation efficiency increased substantially to 72.65%, accompanied by an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.0197 min−1. These results indicate that H2O2 activation was most effectively enhanced when HC and UV irradiation were simultaneously applied. In the HC/UV/H2O2 system, UV irradiation promoted the photolysis of H2O2 to generate reactive species, while HC enhanced local turbulence, interfacial renewal, and mass transfer, thereby improving oxidant dispersion and H2O2 utilization [26], [46].

A similar comparison was conducted for the PDS-assisted systems. As shown in Fig. 4 (c), PDS alone showed negligible degradation capability, achieving only 2.13% TC removal after 60 min. In contrast, substantial enhancement was observed when PDS was coupled with either HC or UV, demonstrating the necessity of external activation for effective PDS utilization [14], [23]. The HC/PDS and UV/PDS systems achieved TC degradation efficiencies of 53.62% and 79.20%, respectively, indicating that both HC and UV irradiation could effectively activate PDS under the present conditions. Further coupling of HC and UV with PDS increased TC removal to 88.51%, with an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.0281 min−1, the highest among the investigated processes (Fig. 4 (d, e)). Compared with the H2O2-assisted route, the PDS-assisted route exhibited a more pronounced response during the binary-coupling stage, suggesting that PDS was activated more effectively by either HC or UV under the investigated operating conditions. This enhanced responsiveness may be attributed to the efficient generation of reactive sulfate-based oxidizing species following PDS activation, enabling substantial degradation even without simultaneous HC and UV coupling [53]. This behavior is consistent with previous UV/PDS and HC-assisted persulfate oxidation systems, where effective PDS activation promoted the generation of sulfate-radical-related oxidizing species, resulting in enhanced degradation of refractory organic pollutants [14], [23], [53]. The findings further suggest that the activation efficiency of PDS under the present conditions was sufficiently high to produce a substantial oxidation response even in the binary systems.

To further evaluate whether the observed enhancement originated from genuine process synergy rather than a simple additive contribution of the individual processes, the SI values of the coupled systems were calculated based on the apparent kinetic rate constants (Fig. 4 (e) and Table S4). This analysis provides a quantitative assessment of the extent to which process integration improves degradation performance beyond the sum of the individual treatment effects [54]. In the H2O2-assisted route, the HC/UV and UV/H2O2 systems exhibited positive synergistic effects, with SI values of 1.55 and 2.91, respectively. In contrast, the HC/H2O2 system produced an SI value below 1, indicating the absence of meaningful synergy and suggesting that HC alone was insufficient to activate H2O2 effectively under the tested conditions. This result further highlights the dominant role of UV irradiation in promoting H2O2 activation and hydroxyl radical generation.

The ternary HC/UV/H2O2 system exhibited the strongest synergistic effect in the H2O2-assisted route, with an SI of 4.89, suggesting that HC further intensifies UV-assisted H2O2 activation and improves the overall efficiency of radical generation and oxidant utilization. In the PDS-assisted route, both HC/PDS and UV/PDS displayed pronounced positive synergy, with SI values of 4.16 and 11.68, respectively. These results indicate that PDS was more responsive to binary process coupling than H2O2 under the investigated conditions, particularly in the UV/PDS system, where highly efficient oxidant activation contributed substantially to the observed enhancement in TC degradation [23].

Although the HC/UV/PDS system achieved the highest TC degradation efficiency and apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant among all investigated processes, its SI value was lower than that of the UV/PDS system. This observation highlights that SI and apparent rate constant (kobs) characterize different aspects of process performance. The kobs reflects the apparent degradation rate, whereas SI quantifies the extent to which a coupled system exceeds the combined contributions of its individual components [54], [55]. Consequently, a process may exhibit the highest degradation efficiency while not necessarily showing the highest degree of synergistic enhancement. Thus, the UV/PDS system already enabled efficient PDS activation and substantial TC degradation. The subsequent addition of HC further accelerated TC degradation and increased the apparent rate constant, but the incremental improvement was insufficient to produce a proportional increase in the relative synergistic effect. These results demonstrate the oxidant-dependent nature of HC/UV coupling. The H2O2-assisted route benefited most from simultaneous HC and UV coupling, whereas the PDS-assisted route showed pronounced enhancement under binary coupling with either HC or UV [14], [56]. This difference highlights the distinct activation characteristics of the two oxidants and suggests that the optimal process configuration depends strongly on the selected oxidant and its activation pathway.

Oxidant-dependent process optimization and degradation kinetics

After identifying the ternary HC/UV/oxidant configurations as the most effective routes for TC degradation, the effects of oxidant dosage and initial pH were further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 5 (a) and (b), increasing the H2O2 dosage from 0.5 to 2.0 mM progressively enhanced TC degradation in the HC/UV/H2O2 system. The apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant increased from 0.0140 min−1 at 0.5 mM to 0.0383 min−1 at 2.0 mM, indicating that higher H2O2 availability improved the overall oxidation efficiency under simultaneous HC and UV activation. The greater availability of H2O2 likely favored reactive-species formation under UV irradiation and cavitation-assisted conditions, thereby accelerating TC degradation. Similar enhancement trends have been reported in UV/H2O2 treatment systems for TC-type antibiotics, where increasing oxidant dosage promoted radical-mediated oxidation and contaminant degradation [57]. The investigated H2O2 dosage range was selected based on preliminary experiments and previous reports on UV/H2O2-based oxidation systems [16], [51], [57]. Within this range, no inhibitory effect was observed, and increasing H2O2 dosage continuously enhanced TC degradation. Previous UV/H2O2 studies nevertheless indicate that the optimal H2O2 dosage is system-dependent and that excessive oxidant input can become unfavorable because of radical scavenging and other competing reactions [16], [51]. Therefore, 2.0 mM H2O2 was selected as the operating dosage for subsequent experiments because it gave the highest degradation efficiency and apparent rate constant within the tested range.

For the HC/UV/PDS system, increasing the PDS concentration similarly enhanced TC degradation (Fig. 5 (c) and (d)). The apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant increased from 0.0144 min−1 at 0.5 mM to 0.0420 min−1 at 2.0 mM, suggesting that greater oxidant availability facilitated PDS activation and sulfate-radical-related oxidation [23], [53]. Although 2.0 mM PDS yielded the highest apparent rate constant within the investigated range, the improvement observed from 1.0 to 2.0 mM was relatively modest compared with the additional oxidant input. Unlike the H2O2-assisted route, which showed continued kinetic improvement up to 2.0 mM within the investigated range, the PDS-assisted route exhibited diminishing kinetic returns above 1.0 mM, likely due to reduced oxidant utilization efficiency, radical self-quenching, competing side reactions, or constraints in PDS activation [58], [59]. A similar system-dependent dosage response has been reported for an HC/UV/PS process treating Congo Red, in which 30 mg L−1 PS was identified as the optimum within the investigated range of 0–50 mg L−1 [60]. Although the absolute dosages cannot be directly compared because of differences in target pollutant and operating conditions, both studies indicate that increasing persulfate input does not necessarily result in proportional performance gains. Therefore, 1.0 mM PDS was selected as a balanced operating dosage for the subsequent mechanistic investigations, applicability assessments, and energy-performance analyses [61], [62]. This selected dosage represents a condition-specific compromise between degradation efficiency and reagent consumption rather than a universal optimal PDS concentration.

The influence of initial pH is presented in Fig. 6. In the HC/UV/H2O2 system, acidic conditions clearly favored TC degradation, with the highest apparent rate constant obtained at pH 3 (k = 0.0607 min−1). The rate constants decreased at pH 4, unadjusted pH, pH 9, and pH 10, indicating that the H2O2-assisted route was highly sensitive to pH variation. This pronounced pH dependence can be rationalized by the important role of •OH-mediated oxidation in the H2O2-assisted process, because the effective contribution of •OH to pollutant degradation can vary substantially with solution pH. TC reacts rapidly with •OH, with a reported second-order rate constant of 6.3 × 109 M−1 s−1 [63], which is consistent with the pronounced inhibition by TBA observed in the present HC/UV/H2O2 system (Section 3.5). Previous photochemical studies on TC have also shown that its degradation kinetics are strongly affected by solution pH and that the addition of H2O2 can substantially accelerate TC degradation [64]. This enhancement is consistent with the additional formation of •OH through UV-induced H2O2 activation, while the high reactivity of TC toward •OH provides a kinetic basis for the resulting degradation enhancement [63], [64]. Experimental and kinetic studies of UV/H2O2 processes have further shown that, under alkaline conditions, the effective contribution of •OH may decrease because pH can alter radical reactivity and promote competing reactions that consume reactive radicals, thereby reducing the fraction available for pollutant oxidation [65]. Acid-favored degradation has also been reported experimentally in a Venturi-based HC system, where the addition of H2O2 further enhanced pollutant removal [66]. Together with the present scavenging results, these findings support the interpretation that the pronounced pH response of the HC/UV/H2O2 system arises from the combined influence of pH on TC reactivity and on the generation, consumption, and effective utilization of •OH in the coupled process.

Effect of initial pH on TC degradation in the HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS systems: (a) TC degradation profiles in the HC/UV/H2O2 system, (b) corresponding pseudo-first-order kinetic fits, (c) TC degradation profiles in the HC/UV/PDS system, and (d) corresponding pseudo-first-order kinetic fits. Reaction conditions: initial TC concentration = 30 mg L−1, inlet pressure (Pin) = 0.35 MPa, H2O2 concentration = 2 mM, PDS concentration = 1 mM, temperature = 25 ± 2 °C, UV power (PUV) = 8 W × 4 lamps, and solution volume (V) = 15 L. The unadjusted initial pH values were 5.74 ± 0.07 and 5.90 ± 0.16 for the HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS systems, respectively.

In contrast, the HC/UV/PDS system maintained relatively high degradation efficiency over a broader pH range (Fig. 6 (c) and (d)), indicating greater resistance to pH fluctuations. Although pH 3 yielded the highest apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant (k = 0.0536 min−1), the HC/UV/PDS system also maintained substantial activity at pH 11 (k = 0.0464 min−1), demonstrating better pH adaptability than the HC/UV/H2O2 system. By contrast, Xu et al. reported that TC degradation in a UVC/PS system increased with increasing solution pH [14]. This difference suggests that the optimal pH may vary among persulfate-based systems with different activation configurations and operating conditions. The relatively broad pH tolerance observed in the present HC/UV/PDS system may be associated with the coexistence and pH-dependent redistribution of SO4•- and •OH, whose relative contributions have been shown to shift with solution pH in persulfate-based systems [14], [67]. Consistent with the broader pH tolerance observed here, a direct comparison of UV/H2O2 and UV/persulfate processes under 254-nm irradiation reported that the persulfate-based route remained effective over a wider initial pH range than the H2O2-based route [68]. This broader pH tolerance may provide a practical advantage for treating real wastewaters with fluctuating pH characteristics, where extensive pH adjustment is often undesirable.

Overall, the two oxidants exhibited distinct optimization behaviors on the HC/UV platform. The H2O2-assisted system showed strong dependence on acidic conditions and continuous improvement with increasing H2O2 dosage within the investigated range. In contrast, the PDS-assisted system maintained high degradation performance across a broader pH range, whereas the kinetic enhancement became less pronounced when the PDS concentration exceeded 1.0 mM. Based on the combined evaluation of degradation efficiency, kinetic performance, and oxidant utilization, 2.0 mM H2O2 at pH 3 and 1.0 mM PDS at pH 3 were selected for the subsequent mechanistic investigations.

Reactive species and oxidant-dependent degradation mechanisms

To gain further insight into the oxidant-dependent degradation behavior, reactive-species quenching experiments were conducted under the selected HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS operating conditions. The selected scavengers were used to probe the possible involvement of different reactive species, and TC degradation in the presence of each scavenger was compared with the corresponding control. As shown in Fig. 7, the two systems exhibited distinct inhibition patterns in the presence of the selected scavengers, suggesting differences in the scavenger-sensitive oxidation pathways between the two oxidant-assisted systems [59], [69]. Scavenger addition slowed TC degradation throughout the reaction, which is consistent with the involvement of reactive species throughout TC oxidation. These results provide clear evidence of oxidant-dependent degradation behavior on the HC/UV platform and help explain the differences in degradation performance observed between the H2O2– and PDS-assisted systems.

Effects of radical scavengers on TC degradation under the selected operating conditions: (a) TC degradation profiles in the HC/UV/H2O2 system; (b) TC degradation profiles in the HC/UV/PDS system; (c) TC degradation efficiencies after 60 min; and (d) apparent inhibition efficiencies calculated as [(DEcontrol − DEscavenger)/DEcontrol] × 100%.

In the HC/UV/H2O2 system, TBA and BQ produced apparent inhibition efficiencies of 41.89% and 60.43%, respectively (Fig. 7 (d)). The inhibition caused by TBA is consistent with an important role of •OH-related oxidation in TC degradation. However, because BQ reacts rapidly with both •OH and O2•- (Table S5), the stronger inhibition observed with BQ relative to TBA cannot be attributed exclusively to O2•-. Instead, it may reflect a broader BQ-sensitive response that includes a possible component involving O2•-. These results are consistent with the simultaneous coupling of UV-assisted H2O2 activation and hydrodynamic cavitation. UV irradiation can promote H2O2 photolysis to generate •OH, whereas cavitation bubble collapse creates localized high-temperature and high-pressure microenvironments and enhances micromixing, interfacial renewal, and mass transfer [51], [70]. Therefore, the improved degradation performance of the HC/UV/H2O2 system can be attributed not only to reactive-species formation but also to cavitation-enhanced oxidant dispersion, mass transfer, and overall reaction efficiency [26], [51], [70]. These combined effects explain the strong synergistic behavior observed in the ternary HC/UV/H2O2 system.

A different inhibition pattern was observed in the HC/UV/PDS system, where TBA, BQ, and MeOH produced apparent inhibition efficiencies of 53.12%, 69.94%, and 56.68%, respectively (Fig. 7 (d)). The marked inhibition by TBA is consistent with an important role of •OH-related oxidation in the PDS-assisted system. MeOH reacts rapidly with both •OH and SO4•-, whereas TBA is considerably more reactive toward •OH than toward SO4•- (Table S5). The additional 3.56 percentage points of inhibition observed with MeOH relative to TBA are consistent with an additional sulfate-radical-related response. BQ caused 16.82 percentage points of additional inhibition relative to TBA. Because BQ also reacts rapidly with •OH, this increment is not specific to O2•- but is consistent with an additional BQ-sensitive pathway that may involve O2•-. These responses support the involvement of oxidation pathways related to both •OH and SO4•-, together with a possible contribution from pathways involving O2•- in the HC/UV/PDS system. In this system, both HC and UV irradiation may contribute to PDS activation, leading to sulfate-radical-related oxidation and subsequent transformation among reactive species [14], [23], [56]. Depending on the reaction conditions, SO4•− and •OH may coexist and undergo interconversion, forming a more complex reactive-species network than that in the H2O2-assisted route [14], [71]. This reactive-species flexibility is consistent with the broader pH adaptability observed for the HC/UV/PDS system compared with the HC/UV/H2O2 route. The strong enhancement observed in the HC/PDS and UV/PDS binary systems further supports the greater responsiveness of PDS to binary process coupling under the present operating conditions. Compared with H2O2, which benefited most from simultaneous HC and UV coupling, the PDS-assisted route showed pronounced enhancement with either HC or UV alone, as reflected by the high SI values of HC/PDS and UV/PDS. Therefore, TC degradation in the HC/UV/PDS system likely involved multiple coupled processes, including PDS activation, reactive-species generation and transformation, and cavitation-enhanced mass transfer, which together contributed to efficient oxidant utilization and faster degradation kinetics [23], [56].

The apparent inhibition efficiencies obtained from the quenching experiments provide a semiquantitative comparison of the scavenger-sensitive pathways rather than an absolute partitioning among individual reactive species. Their interpretation depends on the reaction kinetics and effective selectivity of each scavenger at the concentration used [72], [73]. Therefore, the residual TC degradation observed in the presence of the tested scavengers may be associated with direct UV photolysis, direct oxidant oxidation, cavitation-induced physical effects, non-radical oxidation pathways, residual radical reactions, or the incomplete selectivity of the scavengers [69], [74]. These factors indicate that TC degradation in the HC/UV/oxidant systems resulted from the combined action of several complementary oxidation pathways rather than a single isolated mechanism.

Transformation pathways and predicted toxicity of TC transformation products

To further elucidate the transformation behavior of TC on the HC/UV platform, transformation products were tentatively assigned based on UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis and comparison with previously reported TC oxidation products. The proposed molecular formulas and tentative structures of the detected products are summarized in Table S6. As shown in Fig. 8, the initial transformation of TC was mainly associated with oxygenation reactions. The appearance of P1 (m/z = 460) and P2 (m/z = 476) suggests sequential oxygen addition to the parent TC structure. P1 was tentatively assigned to a mono-oxygenated TC derivative [75], whereas P2 corresponded to a further oxygenated product formed through additional oxidative attack [76]. P3 (m/z = 458), which contains fewer hydrogen atoms than P1, was consistent with subsequent oxidative dehydrogenation and/or dehydration of oxygenated intermediates [77]. These assignments are consistent with previously reported TC oxidation studies in which intermediates with m/z values of 460 and 476 were identified as oxygenated transformation products [76], [78], [79], suggesting that oxygenation is a common early-stage transformation route during TC oxidation.

Representative tentative transformation pathway of TC on the HC/UV platform.

Further transformation involved side-chain modification, partial disruption of the TC framework, and the formation of lower-molecular-weight products. P4 (m/z = 432) and P6 (m/z = 416) were tentatively assigned to side-chain-modified or partially cleaved products, suggesting that oxygenated TC derivatives could undergo dealkylation, decarbonylation, and cleavage of oxygen-containing functional groups [77], [80]. P5 (m/z = 430) was tentatively assigned as a demethylated and/or dealkylated TC-derived intermediate, indicating progressive removal of alkyl substituents from the parent structure during oxidation [23]. In addition, P7 (m/z = 309), P8 (m/z = 213), and P9 (m/z = 174) were assigned as downstream products with substantially lower m/z values than TC, indicating further structural fragmentation [81]. Overall, TC degradation in the HC/UV-based oxidation systems mainly proceeded through oxygenation, oxidative dehydrogenation, side-chain modification, dealkylation, partial skeleton cleavage, and subsequent fragmentation. These transformation pathways are consistent with degradation mechanisms previously reported for TC in various advanced oxidation processes, indicating that the HC/UV platform promotes similar fundamental oxidation reactions despite differences in oxidant activation routes [76], [77], [82]. Although the H2O2– and PDS-assisted systems differ in their reactive-species compositions, several major TC transformation reactions are expected to be common to both routes, including oxygenation, dehydrogenation, dealkylation, side-chain modification, and structural fragmentation. Fig. 8 therefore provides a representative mechanistic framework for these major transformation processes on the HC/UV platform. Differences in the participation of •OH and SO4•- may nevertheless influence the formation and relative distribution of individual intermediates, even though the major transformation motifs remain broadly comparable between the two oxidant-assisted routes [53], [83]. Together with the kinetic and reactive-species results, these transformation pathways provide a molecular-level interpretation of TC degradation under the two oxidant-assisted conditions.

To further evaluate the potential toxicity of the tentatively assigned transformation products, the toxicities of TC and these products were estimated using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (T.E.S.T.) [31], [84]. Four representative endpoints were selected, including Fathead minnow 96 h LC50, Daphnia magna 48 h LC50, developmental toxicity, and mutagenicity (Table S7). As shown in Fig. 9, most transformation products showed higher predicted LC50 values than TC for Fathead minnow and Daphnia magna, indicating lower estimated acute aquatic toxicity. However, these results do not indicate that decreasing molecular size or structural fragmentation necessarily leads to toxicity attenuation. In particular, P7-P9 retain aromatic and/or cyclic moieties despite their lower m/z values, and transformation products retaining such structural features may still exhibit appreciable toxicity [76], [83]. Developmental toxicity and mutagenicity did not follow the same trend as the acute toxicity endpoints. Several transformation products were still predicted to retain developmental toxicity or mutagenic potential, indicating that the predicted toxicity of the transformation products was endpoint-dependent and that reduced acute toxicity did not necessarily imply complete risk reduction [85], [86]. The T.E.S.T. results are therefore interpreted as a preliminary structure-based assessment of the potential toxicity of individual tentatively assigned products rather than as evidence of complete detoxification of the treated solution.

Predicted toxicity profiles of TC and its transformation products obtained using the T.E.S.T. model: (a) 96 h LC50 for Fathead minnow, (b) 48 h LC50 for Daphnia magna, (c) developmental toxicity, and (d) mutagenicity. The results provide a preliminary in silico assessment of the potential toxicity of the tentatively assigned transformation products.

Applicability and energy performance of the HC/UV oxidation systems

To preliminarily assess the applicability of the selected HC/UV oxidation systems beyond TC degradation, RhB, MG, and DCHCl were selected as representative probe pollutants at an initial concentration of 30 mg L−1. As shown in Fig. S1, the HC/UV/H2O2 system achieved nearly complete apparent removal of RhB and DCHCl within 60 min and removed 91.2% of MG. Under the optimal HC/UV/PDS conditions, the apparent removal efficiencies of RhB, MG, and DCHCl reached 92.05%, 82.27%, and 97.17%, respectively. These results indicate that both HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS could achieve substantial apparent removal of structurally different organic pollutants, including dyes and antibiotics, under the tested conditions. The differences in removal efficiencies among the tested pollutants may be related to their molecular structures, chromophoric groups, electron-rich moieties, and reactivities toward radical-mediated oxidation [26], [87]. Therefore, the selected HC/UV/H2O2 and HC/UV/PDS systems show preliminary potential as flexible oxidation strategies for treating different organic contaminants.

As shown in Table 1, the optimized HC/UV/H2O2 system exhibited a CY of 4.09 × 10−5 mg J−1 and an EEO of 126.52 kWh m−3 order−1, whereas the corresponding values for the HC/UV/PDS system were 4.04 × 10−5 mg J−1 and 143.28 kWh m−3 order−1, respectively. The similar CY values indicate that the two systems achieved comparable TC removal per unit energy input. However, the slightly lower EEO of the HC/UV/H2O2 system suggests more favorable energy performance under the selected operating conditions. In the baseline comparison, PDS showed a stronger response to binary coupling, particularly in the HC/PDS and UV/PDS systems. However, after oxidant dosage and pH selection, the HC/UV/H2O2 system achieved a slightly lower EEO than the HC/UV/PDS system. This result indicates that strong synergistic effects do not necessarily translate directly into superior energy efficiency, because energy performance depends on the combined effects of reaction kinetics, oxidant activation, oxidant dosage, pH condition, radical transformation, and competing side reactions [17], [88]. Therefore, oxidant selection should consider not only removal efficiency but also pH adaptability, reagent consumption, energy demand, and the target treatment scenario.

The comparison demonstrates that the developed systems achieve competitive degradation performance while providing additional insight into the oxidant-dependent effects on synergy, degradation pathways, toxicity evolution, and energy efficiency. Compared with several previously reported HC-based hybrid processes, the Venturi-based HC/UV/oxidant systems achieved high TC removal in a relatively larger working volume while maintaining lower EEO values than some reported systems. Overall, the results suggest that coupling UV-assisted oxidant activation with a recirculating HC platform can improve TC degradation performance. From an application perspective, the H2O2-assisted route may be preferable when energy efficiency is prioritized under acidic conditions, whereas the PDS-assisted route may be advantageous when broader pH adaptability or sulfate-radical-related oxidation is desired [20]. From a broader engineering perspective, the choice between H2O2 and PDS should be guided by wastewater characteristics, operating constraints, and the desired balance among treatment efficiency, process flexibility, and energy performance [94].

Conclusion

This study directly compared H2O2 and PDS-assisted oxidation on the same Venturi-based HC/UV platform for TC degradation and demonstrated that the two oxidants exhibit distinctly different responses to hydrodynamic and photochemical coupling. HC alone achieved the highest TC degradation at an inlet pressure of 0.35 MPa, indicating that effective cavitation performance depends on achieving an optimal balance between cavity development and productive bubble collapse rather than simply maximizing cavity length. In the oxidant-assisted systems, the H2O2-assisted route benefited most from simultaneous HC and UV coupling, whereas the PDS-assisted route exhibited pronounced enhancement in both HC/PDS and UV/PDS binary systems. Following optimization of oxidant dosage and pH, the HC/UV/H2O2 system exhibited a slightly lower EEO, indicating a modest advantage in energy efficiency, while the HC/UV/PDS system maintained high degradation performance over a broader pH range. These findings highlight a practical trade-off between energy efficiency and operational flexibility, suggesting that treatment objectives and wastewater characteristics should guide oxidant selection.

Reactive-species quenching experiments and UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis provided complementary evidence that TC degradation proceeded via multiple oxidation pathways, including oxygenation, oxidative dehydrogenation, side-chain modification, dealkylation, partial skeleton cleavage, and fragmentation. The detected transformation products suggest progressive structural breakdown of the TC molecule rather than simple functional-group modification.

T.E.S.T. predictions indicated that most transformation products exhibited lower acute aquatic toxicity than the parent TC molecule. However, several intermediates remained positive for developmental toxicity or mutagenicity, demonstrating that toxicity evolution was endpoint-dependent and that sufficient oxidation is required to minimize the risks associated with residual transformation products. Applicability tests using RhB, MG, and DCHCl demonstrated that the optimized HC/UV systems achieved substantial removal of structurally diverse organic pollutants, indicating their broad applicability beyond TC degradation. Overall, the results provide mechanistic and practical insights into oxidant-dependent behavior in cavitation-assisted UV oxidation systems and establish a basis for selecting suitable oxidants for the treatment of refractory antibiotic-containing wastewater, balancing treatment efficiency, process flexibility, and energy consumption.
