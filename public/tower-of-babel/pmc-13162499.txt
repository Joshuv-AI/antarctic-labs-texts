Clinical applications of the amniotic membrane in pediatric ocular pathology: a scoping review
Authors: Laura Valentina Botia Suárez, Laura Vanesa Henao Ramírez, José Enrique Graterol Porras, María Paola Lubo López, Lev Bladimir Ramirez Martinez, Maria Alejandra Charria Mejia, Luis Gustavo Celis Regalado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe amniotic membrane (AM), which is derived from the placenta and is composed of columnar epithelium, a basement membrane, and an avascular stroma, is commonly preserved through cryopreservation. Its biological properties—combining safety, versatility, and efficacy—have positioned it as an innovative therapeutic option in pediatric ophthalmology for multiple ocular pathologies.ObjectiveTo explore the available evidence on the clinical applications of the AM in pediatric ocular pathologies. Methodology: A scoping review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, using databases such as PubMed, LILACS, Scopus, SciELO, and EBSCO, without date restrictions. Sixteen studies published between 2002 and 2025 were included, encompassing retrospective studies, cohort studies, case reports, and narrative reviews. The evidence was categorized into general benefits, benign neoplasms, surface defects, severe hypersensitivity, cryptophthalmos, ocular trauma, and glaucoma.ResultsThe reviewed studies demonstrated that AM, owing to its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antiangiogenic properties, is effective as a biological bandage in various pediatric ocular conditions. Its application promotes re-epithelialization, modulates inflammation, and improves healing. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to establish standardized protocols and validate their long-term effectiveness in this age group.ConclusionsThe use of the AM has shown promising results as a safe and effective intervention in pediatric ophthalmology, highlighting its therapeutic value in challenging, multifactorial clinical scenarios.Keywords: Amnion, Transplant, Pediatrics, Ophthalmology

Introduction

The AM has been studied in medicine since the early 20th century, particularly for treating skin burns and preventing tissue adhesions in head, neck, abdominal, genitourinary, and laryngeal surgeries [1]. In 1910, Davis performed the first amniotic membrane transplant (AMT) as a skin substitute for open wounds. Later, in 1940, De Roth introduced its ophthalmological use as a biological dressing to treat conjunctival defects [2]. However, its application has been limited due to preservation difficulties and the risk of infection transmission. In the 1990s, the introduction of cryopreservation techniques led to the resurgence of AMT, establishing it as a therapeutic tool for managing various ophthalmic pathologies [2–4].

The AM is the innermost layer of the placenta and, together with the chorion, forms the amniotic sac that protects the fetus by providing an appropriate developmental environment. It is a thin, semitransparent, resilient, avascular tissue composed of a single layer of columnar epithelial cells, a thick basement membrane, and an avascular fetal connective tissue stroma with sparse fibroblasts [2, 3, 5, 6]. It is obtained from the placentas of elective cesarean deliveries, ensuring the absence of maternal or fetal infections through strict selection criteria and informed consent [3, 7], thereby reducing inter- and intradonor variability [8].

Owing to its biological characteristics, AM possesses multiple properties that make it valuable in ophthalmology. It serves dual functions as a biological dressing and a substrate for cell adhesion and proliferation. Its application helps reduce inflammation and pain by stimulating the apoptosis of inflammatory cells. Additionally, its low immunogenicity significantly reduces the risk of immune rejection in the receptors. Its antiangiogenic effect is attributed to the release of vascular growth inhibitors, and it also exhibits antimicrobial properties, protecting the ocular surface from external infections [2, 4, 5]. Furthermore, cells derived from the AM have shown pluripotent potential because they express stem cell markers such as OCT-4 and HNF-3β, suggesting a broad differentiation capacity [6].

There are three clinical approaches for human AMT in ocular surface disorders: grafting, patching, and filling [2]. These techniques have been extensively used for treating persistent corneal epithelial defects, corneal ulcers, bullous keratopathy, chemical and thermal burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and other ocular surface pathologies [5].

The scientific interest in AMT has grown steadily, driven by advances in preservation and storage techniques that have improved its availability and facilitated its application in pediatric ophthalmology and other medical specialties. Although its efficacy and safety have been documented, further research is necessary to strengthen the clinical evidence supporting its use [9]. However, widespread adoption remains limited due to the lack of awareness among some professionals regarding preparation, preservation, and clinical application techniques [7].

In parallel, other promising alternatives are being evaluated, such as AM extract, which, owing to its rich composition of growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular matrix components, facilitates tissue repair and regeneration [8, 10]. Despite its increasing use, clinical evidence in pediatric populations remains limited, highlighting the need for additional studies to establish accurate indications, efficacy, and long-term safety.

In this context, the present work aims to explore the available evidence regarding the clinical applications of the AM in pediatric ocular pathologies through a scoping review.

Methodology

This scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension. A detailed search strategy was conducted through the following steps: (1) Identification of the research question: what is the current evidence on the use of the AM in the treatment of ocular pathologies in pediatric patients? (2) Identification of relevant studies, (3) Study selection, (4) Data extraction, and (5) Synthesis and reporting of results.

Eligibility criteria

Clinical studies published in English or Spanish, regardless of publication date, evaluating the use of the AM in treating ocular pathologies in pediatric populations (under 18 years old) were included. Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, observational studies (prospective or retrospective), case reports, and narrative reviews, provided that they contained relevant clinical data on the intervention.

Studies conducted in animal models, articles without full-text availability, and studies that did not specify the pediatric age of participants or did not clearly identify outcomes related to AM use were excluded.

As this scoping review aimed to map the breadth of available evidence in a relatively underexplored field, no formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias was performed. The study limitations were instead considered narratively during data synthesis.

Search strategy

Databases such as PubMed, LILACS, Scopus, Scielo, and Ebsco were searched to identify studies published in English or Spanish through February 2025, with no date restrictions. Boolean operators and DeCS/MeSH terms were used.

Data extraction and synthesis

The included studies were organized according to key variables, including title, authors, country, year of publication, study type, population characteristics, and main findings.

Most of the analyzed studies were retrospective or case reports involving patients under 18 years old with various ocular pathologies. The intervention evaluated was AMT.

Data analysis was conducted qualitatively and quantitatively, depending on the available information. A narrative synthesis was used to integrate and describe trends in AM use in pediatric ocular pathologies, highlighting the strengths and limitations of the available evidence.

General benefits

The use of the AM in the pediatric population has been documented in various ocular pathologies, including glaucoma, dermoid cysts [3], orbital implant exposure, xerophthalmia [5], burns, and symblepharon, as well as other corneal or conjunctival lesions such as corneal ulcers or perforations [3, 5]. Ahmad et al. (2017) and Abudi et al. (2015) reported beneficial effects associated with its use, notably including pain reduction, enhanced re-epithelialization, antimicrobial properties [3, 5], reduced inflammation and vascularization, and overall improved healing [3]. No associated complications were reported [3, 5].

Benign neoplasms

Villalón et al. (2018) [11], Cho et al. (2018) [12], Pirouzian et al. (2010) [13], and Lang et al. (2014) [14] described the use of the human AM in pediatric patients with benign ocular neoplasms, specifically epibulbar or limbal dermoid cysts. These studies highlighted its utility both as a standalone treatment and in combination with other materials. Despite technical variations, such as the use of 0.02% mitomycin C (Villalón et al., 2018) [11], tissue adhesive and conformer ring assistance (Cho et al., 2018) [12], fibrin for sutureless fixation (Pirouzian et al., 2010) [13], or Vicryl 7.0 sutures (Lang et al., 2014) [14], all patients achieved favorable outcomes. These included good graft adherence, inflammation reduction, rapid corneal re-epithelialization, minimal postoperative scarring, and no significant complications during follow-up, except for pseudopterygium in some cases reported by Lang et al. (2014) [14].

Ocular surface defects

With respect to surface defects, Savino et al. (2010) [15] and Ramaesh et al. (2007) [16] demonstrated that the AM is an effective tool for ocular surface healing [15, 16]. AM serves as a “transplanted basement membrane,” facilitating epithelial cell migration and strengthening epithelial adhesion. It represents a viable substrate in conditions such as congenital corneal anesthesia [16] and significantly reduces the number of inflammatory cells [15, 16]. Furthermore, when combined with corneal patching, it is effective in treating herpetic corneal perforation, preventing graft rejection and reducing the severity of complications [15].

Severe hypersensitivity

Gervasio et al. (2020) [17] and Baş et al. (2019) [18] reported cases of conjunctival injury secondary to Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), respectively, treated with AMT. Whether performed with sutures [17] or not, directly at the bedside in pediatric intensive care units [18], AMT yields favorable outcomes. In both studies, the AM adhered well to the injured tissue, promoting stable re-epithelialization, effective inflammation modulation, and proportional scar formation during postoperative evolution [17, 18].

Cryptophthalmos

Stewart et al. (2002) [19] reported the case of an infant with partial cryptophthalmos in the left eye who underwent multiple reconstructive procedures. Initially, buccal mucosa and hard palate grafts were used, but failed to maintain the depth of the fornix due to poor tissue vascularization. Ultimately, an AM graft was employed, resulting in successful reconstruction without the recurrence of symblepharon during a one-year follow-up [19].

Ocular trauma

Tejeda García et al. (2015) [9], Ortiz-Morales et al. (2024) [20], and Kate et al. (2023) [21] described the clinical use of the AM in pediatric patients with ocular trauma, highlighting its efficacy in cases of both chemical burns and closed corneal injuries. Despite variations in etiology and injury severity, all three studies reported significant visual recovery following graft application. AM has been shown to be beneficial in pediatric ocular reconstruction because it promotes reepithelialization, minimizes scarring, and prevents complications such as amblyopia [9, 20, 21].

Glaucoma

Both the clinical trial conducted by Mahdy et al. (2010) [22] and the case report by Atakan et al. (2021) [23] investigated the benefits of the AM in ophthalmic surgical procedures for glaucoma. A clinical trial confirmed that combining lyophilized AM with mitomycin C (MMC) significantly improved trabeculectomy success rates and reduced overall postoperative complications in patients at high risk of surgical failure. This case report describes a novel double-layer AM technique used to resolve conjunctival erosion over an Ahmed valve tube, maintaining stable intraocular pressure without recurrence after one year. Both studies highlight the versatility and efficacy of AM in enhancing surgical outcomes and preventing postoperative complications in glaucoma management, even under challenging clinical conditions [22, 23].

Discussion

AM was first used in 1910 as a graft for skin burn treatment. Given favorable clinical outcomes, its application has expanded to other medical fields such as urology, dermatology, neurosurgery, and head and neck surgery. The first documented ophthalmic use occurred in 1940 for the management of ocular burns, marking the beginning of its role in ocular surface reconstruction [24]. In recent decades, improvements in processing, preservation, and biosafety have renewed clinical interest in AMT [4, 5, 25]. These developments have broadened its use in various ocular pathologies because of its potential as a biological dressing, along with its anti-inflammatory, immunological barrier, antimicrobial, and antiangiogenic properties [4, 5, 14, 17, 18, 24, 25].

Within this context, AM has emerged as a promising therapeutic tool in ophthalmology. The findings synthesized in this review suggest that the clinical value of AM in pediatric ophthalmology does not rely on disease-specific effects, but rather on shared biological mechanisms that are applicable across diverse conditions. Regardless of etiology—whether inflammatory, traumatic, congenital, or postoperative—AM consistently supports epithelial regeneration, modulates inflammation, and promotes organized wound healing [24–29]. These effects are especially relevant in pediatric patients, where excessive inflammation, scarring, or delayed epithelialization can have long-term visual consequences.

Although data on the use of AMT in pediatric ocular disease remain limited, the available evidence consistently demonstrates meaningful clinical benefits, both as a primary treatment and as an adjunct to surgical procedures. A key observation across the reviewed studies is that favorable outcomes appear largely independent of the specific surgical technique employed, including the fixation method (sutureless, fibrin glue, or sutured) and AM preparation type (natural, lyophilized, or cryopreserved). AMT was associated with comparable therapeutic benefits, underscoring its versatility and adaptability in pediatric ocular surgery.

In addition to its mechanical role, AM appears to function as a biological scaffold and regulatory interface, rather than as a passive graft. From a pediatric standpoint, its capacity to reduce inflammation and pain while accelerating epithelial recovery is clinically significant. Children are particularly susceptible to secondary complications such as amblyopia, symblepharon formation, and visual axis opacification. The evidence reviewed suggests that early stabilization of the ocular surface with AM may mitigate these risks by shortening healing time and limiting cicatricial changes during critical periods of visual development. Despite its low immunogenicity, as an allogeneic tissue, AM carries a potential risk of immune rejection due to graft antigen exposure [25]. Although complications such as immune reactions, infection transmission, or graft-related adverse events have rarely been reported, strict donor screening and standardized processing protocols are essential [26]. The low incidence and mild nature of reported complications, however, support the overall safety profile of AMT in pediatric patients [4, 5, 30].

While this review focuses on the use of AMT in pediatric populations, the current evidence base remains limited. Most available studies consist of case reports or small case series, with substantial heterogeneity in indications, techniques, and outcome measures. This variability limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness or optimal treatment protocols. Additionally, long-term outcomes—particularly those related to visual development and recurrence rates—are insufficiently reported.

Therefore, AMT represents a valuable adjunctive and, in some cases, primary therapeutic option for pediatric ocular disease. It has shown promising results, with a low incidence of adverse effects and a minimal risk of complications. However, the currently available evidence remains limited, particularly in the pediatric population, underscoring the need for prospective studies and controlled clinical trials to better define its efficacy and establish standardized clinical use protocols [26].

Taken together, the available evidence supports that AMT appears to be a safe intervention in pediatric ophthalmology, with a low incidence of adverse events when appropriate donor screening and processing protocols are followed. Its biological effects are consistently observed across a wide range of indications. In addition, clinical improvement has been reported regardless of AM preparation type or fixation technique, suggesting that its therapeutic benefit is primarily driven by intrinsic biological properties rather than procedural variables. Nevertheless, important uncertainties remain. The heterogeneity of included studies precludes definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness between techniques, optimal timing of intervention, and standardized indications.

Limitations

Despite the findings of this scoping review, several important limitations must be acknowledged. Most of the included studies were case reports or case series with small sample sizes and limited or inconsistently reported follow-up periods, which hindered comprehensive evaluation of the durability of therapeutic effects, increasing the risk of overestimating treatment benefits and underdetecting late-onset complications. In addition, the absence of control or comparative groups in the majority of studies limits causal inference and precludes robust assessment of comparative effectiveness against alternative therapeutic approaches, thereby increasing susceptibility to selection and reporting bias.

Significant methodological heterogeneity was also observed with respect to clinical indications, surgical techniques employed, types or preparations of AM used, fixation strategies, and the use of concomitant therapies. This variability precludes meaningful cross-study comparisons and limits the generalizability of findings.

Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for future research employing prospective designs, standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up periods, more robust methodologies, representative sample sizes, and homogeneous inclusion criteria to validate the efficacy, safety, and clinical applicability of AMT in pediatric ophthalmology.

Conclusion

The use of the AM in various pediatric ocular pathologies has shown promising results as a safe and effective intervention. The analyzed cases showed favorable outcomes, including strong graft adherence, significant inflammation reduction, rapid and stable corneal re-epithelialization, minimal postoperative scarring and pain reduction, with few reported complications.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the consideration of AMT as both a primary and adjunctive treatment in pediatric patients. However, given the heterogeneity and limited methodological quality of the available evidence, its use should be guided by careful patient selection and cautious clinical judgment. Furthermore, prospective and controlled clinical studies are needed to establish standardized indications and treatment protocols, as well as longer follow-up periods to adequately detect and manage potential adverse effects or complications.
