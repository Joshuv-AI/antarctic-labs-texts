Abstract
. Group awareness —the ability to perceive the activities of collaborators in a shared space—is a vital mechanism to support effective coordination and joint data analysis in collaborative visualization. We introduce collaborative attention-aware visualizations (CAAVs) that track, record, and revisualize the collective attention of multiple users over time. We implement this concept in HeedVision , a standards-compliant WebXR system that runs on modern AR/VR headsets. Through a user study where pairs of analysts performed visual search tasks in HeedVision, we demonstrate how attention revisualization enhances collaborative performance in immersive analytics. Our findings reveal that CAAVs substantially improve spatial coordination, search efficiency, and task load distribution among collaborators. This work extends attention awareness from individual to multi-user settings and provides empirical evidence for its benefits in collaborative immersive analytics. 1 1 1 All supplementary materials used in this research, including data can be found at osf.io/frsp8 .

1.
Introduction

Attention is a scarce commodity.
Human cognition uses attention as a selection mechanism that determines which sensory inputs are prioritized for processing at any given moment
(
Treisman and Gelade 1980
)
.
When applied to visual representations, attention helps the analyst select among multiple charts to address the current analysis goal
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
.
It is particularly important in collaborative visualization
(
Isenberg et al. 2011
)
settings, where retaining awareness of what each collaborator is doing—so-called
group awareness

(
Gutwin and Greenberg 1998b
;
Gutwin and Greenberg 1998a
)
—is vital for effective analysis coordination.
However, such awareness of the team’s attention over time is difficult to track and interpret in immersive 3D.

In this paper, we address this gap by extending the concept of attention-aware visualizations (AAVs)
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
to multi-analyst settings in what we call
collaborative attention-aware visualizations
(CAAVs).
Like the original AAV concept, CAAVs measure and record user attention over time, but extended to multiple users.
As a result, the
revisualization
of attention data—whether embedded in or separate from the target visualizations—must either distinguish or aggregate each individual user’s attention based on task.

To validate our collaborative attention-aware visualization concept, we propose
HeedVision
, an immersive analytics environment that tracks and revisualizes the attention of multiple collaborators.
Although the original AAV concept incorporated immersive 3D, it tracked only the attention of a single user.
Our HeedVision implementation uses a distributed voxel representation of the immersive analytics environment, tracking and revisualizing attention based on the head movement of each collaborator.
The system is built entirely using open web technologies and runs on consumer-level AR/VR headsets (HMDs) supporting WebXR, such as the Meta Quest 3.
Using the implementation, we conducted an informal user study involving multiple collaborators working together to perform a joint search task using different immersive 3D visualizations.
Our findings show that group awareness through HeedVision yielded much improved team performance for the common search task.

We claim the following contributions:
(1) the generalization of attention-aware visualization to collaborative, multi-user immersive settings;
(2) the HeedVision implementation of a collaborative attention-aware visualization environment in immersive 3D; and
(3) empirical results from a study assessing the impact of collaborative attention tracking on group awareness in immersive analytics. All supplementary materials used in this research, including data can be found at
osf.io/frsp8
.

2.
Background

Our work lies at the intersection of attention and collaborative visualization.
Below we review prior art in these domains.

2.1.
Attention in Visualization

Visual attention plays a fundamental role in how users perceive and interact with visualizations.
When viewing complex visualizations, users cannot process all marks simultaneously, leading to selective attention that prioritizes certain marks over others.

Pre-attentive processing
enables viewers to identify certain visual features almost without conscious effort.
Healey et al.
(
Healey et al. 1999
)
demonstrated how visualization designers can leverage pre-attentive processing by using visual features like color, size, and orientation to highlight important data.
These principles help create visualizations where patterns “pop out” to viewers before conscious scanning begins.

The concept of
visual saliency
extends these ideas by modeling which elements naturally draw attention.
Jänicke and Chen
(
Jänicke and Chen 2010
)
proposed quality metrics for visualizations based on such saliency models.
Matzen et al.
(
Matzen et al. 2018
)
further developed these approaches with their data visualization saliency model, which predicts viewer attention patterns specific to data representations.
This helps designers determine whether visualizations direct attention appropriately.

Eye tracking provides empirical insight into how viewers control their attention across charts.
Burch et al.
(
Burch et al. 2011
)
used eye tracking for different tree diagram layouts, revealing significant differences in how viewers scan traditional, orthogonal, and radial layouts.
Kim et al.
(
Kim et al. 2012
)
studied whether eye tracking data aligned with self-reported decision-making strategies, finding notable discrepancies between where users claimed to look versus their actual gaze patterns.

Analysis methods for eye tracking data have become increasingly sophisticated.
Blascheck et al.
(
Blascheck et al. 2016
)
combined eye movement data with interaction logs and think-aloud protocols to triangulate user behavior.
Kurzhals et al.
(
Kurzhals et al. 2016
)
reviewed eye tracking for visual analytics, highlighting the need for specialized metrics and visualizations that connect gaze data to semantic elements in interfaces.
Burch’s book on the topic
(
Burch 2021
)
synthesizes approaches for both collecting and analyzing eye tracking data in visualization.

Key Insight

Visualizing attention patterns reveals insights about user behavior that would otherwise remain invisible.

2.2.
Gaze Interaction

Gaze can also serve as an active input mechanism.
The history of gaze-based interaction in computing dates back to Bolt’s seminal “Gaze-orchestrated dynamic windows”
(
Bolt 1981
)
, which demonstrated how a user’s gaze could control which of multiple video streams received audio focus.
This early work established the potential for using eye movement as an implicit control mechanism.

Multimodal approaches that combine gaze with other input modalities have proven particularly effective.
Stellmach et al.
(
Stellmach et al. 2011
)
explored gaze-supported multimodal interactions for exploring large image collections, showing how gaze can be combined with keyboard controls to create fluid navigation experiences.
These multimodal approaches mitigate the “Midas touch” problem
(
Jacob 1990
)
, where everything a user looks at triggers an action, by requiring explicit confirmation through a secondary input.
Nevertheless, Midas touch remains a concern even for our HeedVision approach.

Gaze offers unique advantages for target acquisition.
Stellmach and Dachselt
(
Stellmach and Dachselt 2012
)
investigated “look & touch” techniques that use gaze for coarse targeting while using touch input for precise selection and manipulation.
Their work demonstrated improvements in both speed and accuracy compared to traditional input methods, particularly for distant or small targets in large visual spaces.

Several works have expanded the gaze interaction design space.
Turner et al.
(
Turner et al. 2013
)
developed “Eye Pull, Eye Push” for moving objects between large displays and personal devices using gaze and touch.
Pfeuffer et al.
(
Pfeuffer et al. 2014
)
introduced Gaze-Touch, combining gaze with multi-touch for interaction on the same surface.
They later extended these concepts to tablets
(
Pfeuffer and Gellersen 2016
)
, as well as for pinch gestures in virtual reality
(
Pfeuffer et al. 2017
)
.
Gaze control is central to the new generation of XR devices, such as the Apple Vision Pro and Android XR.

Key Insight

Gaze data can serve dual roles: as an implicit indicator of attention and as an explicit input mechanism.

2.3.
Collaborative Visualization

Collaborative visualization enables multiple analysts to jointly make sense of data.
This method builds on fundamental concepts from computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW), but with distinct challenges specific to visualization tasks
(
Isenberg et al. 2011
)
.
While overcoming these challenges can be difficult, the benefits of collaborative visualizations are many.
Mark et al.
(
Mark and Kobsa 2005
)
found significant improvements in analytical results when multiple analysts worked together vs. individually.
Balakrishnan et al.
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2008
)
similarly observed performance gains when analysts used shared visual representations.

Establishing
common ground
—"
mutual knowledge, mutual beliefs, and mutual assumptions
"
(
Clark and Brennan 1991
)
—is essential for effective collaboration.
This requires
group awareness
: an understanding of others’ activities within the shared workspace
(
Gutwin and Greenberg 1998b
;
Gutwin and Greenberg 1998a
)
.
Group awareness is particularly challenging in distributed settings where physical cues are absent.
Various techniques address this challenge, such as radar overviews
(
Gutwin et al. 1996
)
, collaborators’ arms
(
Tang et al. 2006
)
, and their fingertips
(
Kim et al. 2010
)
.

Group awareness in collaborative visualization takes specialized forms.
Social navigation techniques
(
Dourish and Chalmers 1994
)
record and visualize the presence and activities of multiple users.
Concrete implementations include Scented Widgets
(
Willett et al. 2007
)
, which embed visual representations of prior use directly within interface components, and collaborative brushing approaches
(
Isenberg and Fisher 2009
;
Hajizadeh et al. 2013
)
that reveal others’ selections across documents or datasets.
More recently, Saffo et al.
(
Saffo et al. 2023
)
addressed the challenge of cross-platform collaborative visualization between VR and desktop environments with their “Eyes and Shoes” technique.
This approach provides asymmetric awareness cues where desktop users can see a VR user’s viewpoint (“Eyes”) and temporarily adopt their perspective (“Shoes”), significantly improving coordination between collaborators using different platforms.

One of the benefits of awareness is a better understanding of the
collective data coverage
: ensuring the group examines all relevant data without unnecessary duplication.
Sarvghad and Tory demonstrated that visualizing dimension coverage increases exploration breadth without sacrificing depth for individual users
(
Sarvghad et al. 2017
)
and reduces work duplication in asynchronous collaboration
(
Sarvghad and Tory 2015
)
.
Badam et al.
(
Badam et al. 2017
)
extended this concept with their “team-first” approach, which visualizes group activity to help teams balance individual exploration with collective sensemaking to improve data coverage.

Key Insight

Making collective attention visible improves coordination and data coverage while reducing redundancy.

2.4.
Attention-Aware Visualization

Attention-aware visualizations (AAVs) represent a specialized class of systems that track, analyze, and respond to user attention patterns.
Srinivasan et al.
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
formally defined this concept and developed a framework categorizing how visualization systems can use attention data.
They identified three key components: attention capture mechanisms (how systems track user attention), attention models (how systems interpret attention data), and adaptation mechanisms (how systems respond to attention patterns).
Their work demonstrates how attention awareness can enhance user experience by reducing cognitive load, guiding users to relevant content, and enabling retrospective analysis of attention patterns.

Exploration Awareness
(
Shrinivasan and van Wijk 2009
)
is an early implementations of AAV principles that tracks and visualizes user exploration paths through information spaces.
This approach helps users maintain orientation during complex analytical tasks and provides a record of their analytical process.
More specialized applications include adaptive annotations that respond to viewing patterns
(
Kim et al. 2012
)
and systems that modify visual complexity based on measured attention capacity.

Key Insight

Systems that track and respond to user attention can adapt visualizations to better support analytical goals, but must be reimagined for collaborative multi-user settings.

For immersive environments, attention awareness presents unique opportunities and challenges.
Lu et al.
(
Lu et al. 2014
)
investigated subtle visual cues for directing attention in augmented reality, finding that appropriate cue designs can guide visual search without disrupting the primary task.
This balance between directing attention and avoiding distraction is particularly critical in immersive settings where users may already experience an increased cognitive load.

Current attention-aware approaches have notable limitations, particularly for collaborative scenarios.
Most systems track and respond to a single user’s attention, neglecting the complex dynamics of multiple users working simultaneously.
The challenges multiply in immersive analytics settings
(
Billinghurst et al. 2018
)
, where collaborators must navigate 3D space with different viewpoints and focus areas.

Key Insight

Immersive analytics present both unique opportunities and challenges for collaborative attention-aware systems, requiring specialized approaches for navigation and viewpoint disparities.

3.
Collaborative Attention-Aware Visualization

Building on the concept of attention-aware visualizations (AAVs)
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
, we define
Collaborative Attention-Aware Visualizations
(CAAVs) as visualizations that track, record, and revisualize the attention of multiple users engaged in collaborative analysis tasks.
Here we propose our design framework for collaborative attention-aware visualizations that addresses each of these research questions.

3.1.
Measuring Collective Attention

If attention in isolation represents a person’s selective concentration on specific elements while ignoring others, then
collective attention
extends this concept across multiple analysts.
The involvement of multiple agents yield increased complexity and concepts:

Joint Attention
, when multiple users focus on one element;

Distributed Attention
, when analysts distribute their focus across different elements;

Sequential Attention
, when analysts build on each other’s focus areas over time; and

Attention Coverage
, as the collective span of a team’s attention across the visualization.

Since attention is a cognitive process that cannot be easily measured, we must use observable behaviors as proxies.
In collaborative settings, these proxies must work for multiple users: tapping or clicking; pointer movement; head direction as an indication of gaze; and eye movement to indicate precise focus.

Each proxy offers different tradeoffs between precision, implementation complexity, and intrusiveness.
For example, gaze provides the most direct measurement of visual attention but requires specialized hardware, while selection actions are easily captured but represent only deliberate interactions.
Similarly, measuring attention across multiple users in practice introduces several challenges, including distinguishing between users, aligning interaction events to a common timeline, managing scalability as team size increases, and accommodating users on different devices or platforms.

3.2.
Recording Collective Attention

Just like AAVs, CAAVs can take two fundamentally different approaches to recording attention:
data-agnostic
(no knowledge of the underlying visualization) and
data-aware
(specific knowledge of the geometric extents of the underlying visualization).

Data-agnostic.

This approach tracks attention as coordinates or regions without knowledge of the underlying visualization elements.
In other words, here the approach does not know what the visualization represent, only that the user’s attention is moving over it.
Data-agnostic approaches are more generalizable but limit the system’s ability to interpret attention patterns.

Data-aware.

This method associates attention with specific visualization marks, data points, or semantic components.
To achieve this, the approach has specific knowledge of exactly what parts of the visualization that the user is focusing their attention on.
Because of this knowledge, data-aware approaches enable richer analysis but require deeper integration with the visualization system.

Representing collective attention.

For each
attention target
that can receive attention, we must maintain separate attention accumulators for each user.
These accumulators start at zero (they have initially received no attention by any user), increase as a user directs attention to the target, and can be combined to represent team attention using various aggregation methods:
sum
of collective attention (the total team attention);
maximum
(highest individual attention);
difference
(balance of team member attention); and
count
(number of analysts who attended to the target).

Collective attention decay.

Human memory decays naturally over time.
To model this, recorded attention values should gradually decrease to reflect fading relevance.
Decay is particularly important in collaborative settings where attention patterns evolve as team members respond to each other’s discoveries and insights.

3.3.
Revisualizing Collective Attention

As discussed by Srinivasan et al.
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
, attention does not have to be displayed and could be used solely for analysis or evaluation, without exposure to viewers.
This paper focuses on how the visualization might actively incorporate to this data—for example, to help coordination, promote exploration, or support reflection.
We refer to this process as
revisualizing
the attention.

Presentation.

The revisualization of collaborative attention can be achieved through two fundamental presentation styles:
(a)

embedding
the attention directly into the visualization; or
(b)

separating
the attention from the visualization in a view or overlay.

In collaborative contexts, the choice between approaches affects how easily team members can develop shared understanding of team attention.
Furthermore, the presentation choice can also be left to the individual collaborator.
Finally, the actual implementation for the presentation depends on whether the approach is data-aware or data-agnostic, and whether it is displayed in 2D or 3D.

View Integration.

We also distinguish between whether the attention is integrated into the

world space
or the

camera space
of the display.
This distinction is mostly relevant for immersive 3D, whereas it is of less importance for a 2D space (except for multiscale visual spaces supporting panning and zooming).

Triggering.

Just like for AAVs
(
Srinivasan et al. 2025
)
, the triggering mechanisms for revisualization may significantly impact user experience.
We distinguish between

always-on
, which continuously displays attention (may lead to Midas touch or reinforcing loops);

explicit
, where users deliberately activate revisualization; and

implicit
, where the system automatically shows attention based on context.

In collaborative settings, different triggering mechanisms might apply to personal versus shared attention displays.
Furthermore, the ability to trigger might depend on user role within the team.

Collaborative revisualization.

Several patterns emerge as particularly useful for collaborative settings:

attention heatmaps
that show density of aggregate attention across visualization elements;

coverage maps
that highlight areas that have received little attention; and

focus convergence
that emphasize where multiple users are focusing.

4.
System: HeedVision

We present
HeedVision
, a collaborative attention-aware visualization system for immersive 3D analytics, implemented with a focus on a subset of our proposed design space where attention is

embedded
in the

world space
and triggered

explicitly
.
Built on web-based spatial computing frameworks, our system captures, records, and revisualizes collective attention in real time to support group awareness during exploration and analysis.

4.1.
Attention Model

The core architecture of HeedVision features a distributed attention model that captures, records, and visualizes collective user attention across multiple visualizations within a shared workspace.
Unlike single-user AAV systems, our model maintains separate attention maps for each collaborator on each visualization.

The attention model is implemented as a 3D voxel grid overlaid on each visualization, with individual voxels storing attention values for specific regions.
This approach supports both individual and aggregated team-level attention maps, enabling users to review personal focus areas and identify regions of collective interest.

The system updates these attention values in real-time as collaborators interact with visualizations.
The attention model follows principles similar to those of human short-term memory: attention accumulates when a user focuses on a specific region and decays over time as attention shifts elsewhere.

Only voxels that intersect with the actual geometry of a visualization register and accumulate attention data.
This creates a
data-aware
approach that directly links attention metrics to the visualization’s underlying geometric structure.
This same infrastructure can support
data-agnostic
approaches through alternative attention capture mechanisms (see Section
4.3
).

4.2.
Voxelization

For our standard
data-aware
implementation, at initialization, HeedVision converts the geometry of a visualization into a discrete grid of invisible voxels with a configurable resolution. These voxels serve as the fundamental units for capturing attention, becoming visible only when attention revisualization is triggered.

The voxel-based approach enables fine-grained spatial tracking as each voxel independently accumulates attention data. This granularity creates several advantages for collaborative contexts: it establishes a consistent spatial reference framework across users, simplifies the detection of joint attention when multiple users focus on identical regions, and optimizes computational efficiency when calculating attention coverage metrics.

To optimize this voxelization process, we employ Bounding Volume Hierarchies (BVHs) to accelerate voxelization by efficiently identifying mesh regions intersecting with voxel boundaries. For dynamic visualizations and interactive 3D models, the system must periodically refresh the voxelization. While this ensures accurate attention mapping as visual elements change position or form, the refresh process can become computationally intensive depending on selected voxel resolution and update frequency requirements.

4.3.
Attention Capture

Given true gaze information, we can use the direction of the user’s gaze to capture attention.
Using headsets without eye-tracking capabilities (such as the Meta Quest 3), we use head orientation as a proxy for user attention.
Regardless, we use the following approach:

(1)

For each gaze update, we cast a ray from the user’s view position in the direction of the user’s gaze.

(2)

We determine the nearest voxel intersection with this ray.

(3)

At the intersection, we create a spherical
region of influence
.

(4)

All voxels within this region receive incremental attention values, with the center receiving the highest increment.

For a
data-agnostic
approach, the algorithm would accumulate attention in
every
voxel that the gaze ray passes through.

4.4.
Triggering Mechanisms

HeedVision implements an

explicit
triggering mechanism where, participants press a controller trigger button while pointing at the visualization to reveal the collaborative attention patterns with their partner.
This approach prevents Midas touch
(
Jacob 1990
)
while giving users control over when to consult the attention visualization.

4.5.
Revisualizing Collective Attention

HeedVision implements an
Opacity
-driven approach for revisualizing collective attention in immersive 3D environments.
In this approach, voxels act as visual indicators of sustained attention: each voxel dynamically adjusts its opacity based on accumulated attention data, becoming more opaque as attention increases and remaining fully transparent when unattended or below a user-controlled threshold.
This creates a real-time coverage map that reveals where user attention has been concentrated over time.

Color
is used as an additional information channel to distinguish between different collaborators.
Each collaborator is assigned a unique color, allowing users to distinguish between individual attention footprints.
When multiple users attend to the same voxel, a blended or shared color is applied, making overlapping or joint attention areas visually salient.

This attention-based voxel rendering is directly embedded in the 3D world space, allowing users to explore and interpret attention patterns from any viewpoint while preserving spatial context.

4.6.
Implementation

HeedVision is implemented entirely using open web technologies to ensure broad compatibility and accessibility.
For the core rendering and immersive capabilities, we use Three.js for 3D rendering and WebXR for immersive device support.
The system leverages the Spatialstrates/Dashspace
(
Borowski et al. 2025
)
, Webstrates
(
Klokmose et al. 2015
)
, and Varv
(
Borowski et al. 2022
)
frameworks to create a tool compatible with consumer-level AR/VR headsets.

5.
User Study

To validate our proposed collaborative attention-aware visualization approach and the HeedVision implementation, we conducted a qualitative evaluation.
Our goal was to understand how visualizing multiple users’ attention patterns impacts group awareness, coordination, and coverage in immersive settings.

5.1.
Participants

We recruited 8 participants (6 male, 2 female) who formed 4 collaborative pairs.
Participants were drawn from graduate-level Computer Science, Visualization and HCI courses, ensuring sufficient background knowledge in data visualization.
Ages ranged from 24 to 37 (mean 28.6), and all had normal or corrected-to-normal vision with no self-reported color vision deficiency.
The participants varied in their experience with immersive analytics, co-located collaboration, and visual analytics (from novice to experienced).

5.2.
Apparatus

All participants used our implementation running on Meta Quest 3 headsets in a shared physical and virtual environment.
The system tracked head position and orientation to determine attention for each participant.
Participants were co-located in the same laboratory space (6×3×3m), allowing for verbal communication.

Each participant’s headset ran the HeedVision software on the Quest Browser.
The world-embedded attention display could be explicitly triggered by participants using a controller button.

5.3.
Experimental Conditions

We employed a 2×2 within-subjects design with two factors:

•

Visualization Type.
The 3D chart type used.

–

Terrain:

A 3D terrain visualization of Mt. Bruno’s elevation with targets positioned along the surface.

–

Scatter:

A discrete 3D scatterplot where targets were positioned around 100 randomly-sampled data points.

•

Attention Visualization.
Attention display method.

–

No-CAAV (control):

No attention display.

–

CAAV:

World-embedded attention display.

This produced four experimental conditions:
Scatter
\times
CAAV
,
Scatter
\times
No-CAAV
,
Terrain
\times
CAAV
, and
Terrain
\times
No-CAAV
.
Conditions were counterbalanced across participant groups using a Latin square design to mitigate learning effects.

5.4.
Task

All participant pairs engaged in a collaborative visual search task (a treasure hunt; see Figure
2
).
During each 10-minute session, they searched for white target objects hidden within a 3D visualization.
These targets were initially invisible and only became visible—appearing to glow white—when a participant looked at them from the correct angle and within a 10 cm proximity.

In the CAAV condition, attention-based coloring was tailored to support two-user collaboration.
Voxels changed color to indicate where participants had looked: blue for areas explored by the local user, orange for areas explored by the partner, and pink for areas explored by both.
The number of targets, defined as a five percent of total voxels, varied by visualization type but was kept consistent across conditions within each type.

5.5.
Procedure

Participants provided informed consent at the beginning of their session.
They filled out a demographic form and received a brief introduction to the system and the treasure hunt task.
The order of CAAV and No-CAAV conditions was counterbalanced across pairs to control for learning effects.

Participants were then given a training session to familiarize themselves with the XR environment, controls, and—for those in the CAAV condition—how to trigger and interpret the attention visualization.
After training, participants proceeded to the trials.

Each pair completed the treasure hunt for all visualizations. After each visualization, participants completed a brief questionnaire about their experience and coordination strategies.

Throughout the study, we recorded several metrics: number of targets found; spatial coverage; attention overlap; attention visualization triggering; and verbal communication patterns.

After completing tasks, participants were administered a System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire and participated in a semi-structured interview about their experience with the system.

6.
Results

We conducted a mixed-methods analysis of Collaborative Attention-Aware Visualizations (CAAVs) to evaluate their impact on team collaboration during visual search.
Our findings reveal how attention visualization affects team performance, spatial coordination, interface usage, cognitive workload, and communication strategies.

6.1.
Task Performance

CAAV substantially boosted performance (Figure
3(a)
).
For scatter, teams with CAAV found 81.7% of targets (vs. 71.4% without) and covered 78.9% of the space (vs. 72.0%).
For terrain, CAAV teams found 29.1% of targets (vs. 17.9%) and covered 27.7% (vs. 18.2%).
Coverage efficiency exceeded 1.03 with CAAV but dropped below 1.0 without, indicating more effective search behavior.

CAAV also enhanced team synergy (Figure
3(b)
).
Target gain was higher with CAAV in both tasks (1.23 and 1.48 vs. 1.18 and 1.17), as was coverage gain—especially in the terrain task (1.55 vs. 1.19).
Notably, in this scenario, target and coverage gains aligned closely (1.48 vs. 1.55), suggesting that attention awareness helped teams translate broader exploration into successful discoveries, particularly under complex conditions requiring tighter coordination.

6.2.
Spatial Coverage and Coordination

To assess spatial coordination, we examined coverage efficiency and redundancy, two complementary metrics.
While coverage efficiency (previously defined) captures how effectively teams converted exploration into discoveries, redundancy reflects how well they avoided overlapping efforts.
We computed redundancy using a normalized Shannon entropy measure, which quantifies the distribution of observations across voxels: lower entropy implies more overlap (i.e., higher redundancy), while higher entropy indicates a more diverse, non-redundant exploration pattern.

As shown in Figure
3(a)
, teams using CAAV achieved slightly higher coverage efficiency, consistently exceeding 1.03 across tasks, compared to values just below 0.99 in the Control condition.
This suggests that attention-awareness cues helped participants more effectively focus on unexplored areas that yielded new targets.

6.3.
Attention Visualization Usage

To understand how participants utilized the CAAV interface, we logged toggle frequency, visibility duration, and toggle patterns throughout the collaborative sessions.
These measures revealed how participants managed the potential benefits of attention awareness against any visual disruption it might cause.
The majority of participants left the attention-awareness visualization active for most of the task duration.
Figure
5
visualizes the toggle states across participant groups, showing that most participants kept the visualization on (blue segments) for the majority of their sessions.
On average, users in the CAAV condition had the visualization turned on for approximately 87% of the task time, with several participants (P3, P5, P8) never toggling it off at all.

Toggle behavior varied considerably between individuals.
While some rarely disabled the visualization, others like P6 toggled it frequently, explaining:

“it was nice to be able to tell what the other person had seen… but having it turned off made the interface less overwhelming”
[P6, G3].
Even among frequent togglers, the “off” periods (orange segments in Figure

“had to toggle visualization on and off”

it helped them

6.5.
Qualitative Feedback

Participants strongly favored the attention-awareness visualization in their feedback.
They described the visualization as providing a sense of teamwork and coordination that was difficult to achieve otherwise.
One participant noted that seeing their partner’s focus was
“like seeing my partner’s footsteps or breadcrumbs in real time”
[P7, G4], which helped them decide where to go next without asking.
The color encoding of attention was frequently mentioned as helpful:

“it was orange where they looked and got pink if I looked at it as well”
[P5, G3], making it easy to distinguish explored regions.
P8 explained that the shared visualization
“helped a lot in reducing redundant search for us.
Also kind of support the awareness of my partner’s attention”
[P8, G4].
Similarly, P5 noted that the
“color encoding of what the other person saw (orange) was helpful to get an idea of how much space we covered as a team”
[P5, G3].

In contrast, without the visualization, participants struggled with coordination:

“I did not see, where my partner looked already”
[P2, G1] and
“it was hard to know for sure.
We talked about it and hope to have being talking about the same areas”
[P5, G3].
P6 elaborated that without the visualization,
“it was hard to tell if I was looking at something new, or just the same as my partner”
[P6, G3].
Furthermore, participants offered constructive feedback for improvement.
Several mentioned challenges with visual clutter:
“as we had to fight the visual noise created in order to understand which targets has been found by both of us”

Others suggested improvements to help distinguish which areas had been seen by both partners versus just one:
“there should be clear which targets are seen by both people, and which are only by me”
[P1, G1].

7.
Discussion

Our findings reveal that Collaborative Attention-Aware Visualizations (CAAVs) substantially improve spatial coordination, search efficiency, and task load distribution in immersive analytics environments. These improvements align with our goal of enhancing group awareness and coordination in shared analysis.
Here we discuss their implications, potential for generalization to other contexts, and limitations that point to future research directions.

7.1.
Explaining the Results

The effectiveness of CAAVs in our study can be attributed to several factors.
First, CAAVs directly address the critical need for group awareness in collaborative visualization settings.
By making attention patterns visible, collaborators gained immediate insight into which areas had been explored, allowing them to make more informed decisions about where to focus their efforts.
This aligns with previous work by Gutwin and Greenberg
(
Gutwin and Greenberg 1998a
)
that emphasizes the importance of workspace awareness for effective collaboration.

A particularly interesting finding was how CAAVs supported emergent collaboration without explicit coordination.
When participants could see the attention history of their partners, they naturally distributed their efforts across the visualization space with minimal verbal communication.
This pattern resembles “stigmergy”
(
Grassé 1959
)
in biological systems, where individuals respond to environmental cues left by others rather than communicating directly.
In our case, the attention visualization served as these cues, creating an implicit coordination mechanism that reduced redundant exploration.

The attention heatmaps essentially function as “read wear”
(
Hill et al. 1992
)
for collaborative visualization—a form of social navigation visualization that shows how others have traversed a virtual space.
This builds on the concept of social navigation described by Dourish and Chalmers
(
Dourish and Chalmers 1994
)
, where users’ movements through an information space leave traces.
In traditional interactive systems, these traces are often invisible; CAAVs make them explicit and actionable.

Participants reported lower cognitive load when using CAAVs, likely because they no longer needed to maintain a mental model of their partner’s activities or verbally coordinate their explorations.
This cognitive offloading enabled them to focus more on the analytical task itself rather than on collaboration mechanics, resulting in more efficient searches and discoveries.

7.2.
Generalizing the Results

While our study focused on synchronous, co-located collaboration, we believe the benefits of CAAVs could extend to other collaborative settings with appropriate adaptations.
For distributed collaboration, where collaborators lack shared physical context, attention visualization could prove even more valuable by providing awareness that would otherwise be completely absent.
However, this would require rethinking how attention information is communicated across distance and how temporal attention decay is handled.

For asynchronous collaboration, the attention decay mechanism would need significant redesign.
Rather than modeling short-term memory, it might instead represent historical exploration patterns with temporal landmarks that indicate when exploration occurred.
This approach could support handoffs between collaborators working at different times, potentially addressing challenges identified in prior work on asynchronous collaborative visualization
(
Sarvghad and Tory 2015
)
.

Beyond our specific search task, attention visualization could enhance various collaborative scenarios including comparative analysis tasks, evidence tracking among collaborators, teaching novices through expert attention patterns, and bridging awareness gaps in cross-platform collaboration between immersive and desktop environments.

7.3.
Safe and Responsible Innovation Statement

To safeguard privacy in our HeedVision prototype, only essential gaze-derived cues were shared, with users retaining full control over how and when these cues appeared.
Rather than accessing raw gaze data, the system derives anonymized attention from the object of focus, preventing others from precisely identifying individual behavior.
The logging was anonymized and separate from the core system.
We promote inclusivity by offering non-immersive access to attention-aware collaboration via Spatialstrates, the underlying system.
Our goal is responsible innovation: enhancing collaboration while upholding privacy, autonomy, and user comfort.

7.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Our work has several limitations that suggest directions for future research.
First, performance concerns in our implementation need addressing.
The voxel-based approach, while conceptually straightforward, becomes computationally expensive at high resolutions or with many users.
Future iterations could explore adaptive resolution techniques or leverage GPU-accelerated approaches for more efficient attention tracking and visualization.

Our implementation used head direction as a proxy for visual attention due to limitations of consumer-level HMDs such as the Meta Quest 3.
While this approximation proved effective for the spatial search task in our study, more fine-grained eye tracking would offer greater precision for tasks requiring detailed attention to smaller visualization elements.
We hypothesize that our general findings would transfer to eye-tracking implementations, potentially with even stronger effects, but this remains to be confirmed.

The single task type (a collaborative treasure hunt) limits the generalizability of our findings.
Different analytical tasks—such as pattern recognition, anomaly detection, or relationship identification—might benefit from different attention visualization approaches.
For example, complex data analysis might require attention visualization that distinguishes between different levels of analytical engagement, from cursory scanning to in-depth examination.

Finally, our study involved pairs of collaborators, but real-world visualization scenarios often involve larger teams with diverse roles.
Scaling CAAVs to larger groups while maintaining visualization clarity presents both technical and design challenges that future research should address.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced Collaborative Attention-Aware Visualizations (CAAVs), extending the concept of attention awareness from individual to multi-user immersive analytics.
Our HeedVision implementation demonstrated how tracking and visualizing collective attention patterns through a distributed voxel representation can improve group awareness, coordination, and task efficiency in collaborative 3D exploration.
Results from our user study confirm that making attention visible to collaborators reduces redundant exploration, enables emergent coordination strategies, and decreases cognitive load during joint analytical tasks.
While our current implementation focuses on head orientation as an attention proxy in synchronous, co-located settings, the CAAV framework provides a foundation for future work across various collaborative configurations, visualization types, and attention-tracking modalities.
As immersive analytics continues to evolve toward multi-user environments, attention-aware approaches offer promising paths for enhancing collaborative sensemaking and analytical workflows in these complex spatial workspaces.
