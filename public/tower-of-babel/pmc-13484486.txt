Energy and carbon estimation in construction and maintenance of pavements using conventional and alternative materials: a comparative analysis
Authors: B. Pradeep, N. Lavanya, M. R. Archana, S. M. Basutkar, V. Anjaneyappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pavement construction and maintenance significantly contribute to environmental impacts and infrastructure expenditure. This study evaluates embodied energy (EE), embodied carbon (EC), and construction–maintenance costs for five flexible pavement alternatives and four rigid pavement alternatives designed for traffic levels ranging from 30 to 150 million standard axles (MSA). Flexible pavements were designed according to IRC:37-2018 with varying base and sub-base configurations, while rigid pavements were designed based on IRC:58-2015. The rigid pavement alternatives incorporated design features such as tied shoulders, dowel bars, widened lanes, and Pavement Quality Concrete (PQC) placed over Dry Lean Concrete (DLC). A constant California Bearing Ratio (CBR) was assumed for flexible pavement design, whereas rigid pavement slab thickness was considered independent of CBR. Embodied energy and embodied carbon were estimated for both construction and scheduled maintenance phases over the design period. In addition to conventional materials, alternative scenarios incorporating up to 50% ground-granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) as cement replacement and up to 30% recycled aggregates were evaluated. Energy and carbon intensity values were obtained from established life-cycle inventory sources, while equipment productivity and cost estimates were derived from standard construction references. The results indicate that EE, EC, and costs increase with traffic loading, whereas the use of alternative materials can reduce EE and EC by approximately 20–30% and overall costs by 5–15%, supporting more sustainable pavement design practices.

Introduction

Sustainable pavement construction has gained increasing global attention due to the environmental challenges associated with conventional road building practices, particularly the high energy consumption and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions arising from material production, construction, and maintenance activities. In recent years, life-cycle thinking has been widely applied in pavement engineering to better understand the cumulative energy demand, carbon emissions, and cost implications of different pavement design and material choices. Within this context, indicators such as embodied energy, embodied carbon, and construction - maintenance cost have emerged as practical metrics for evaluating the environmental and economic performance of pavement systems while ensuring compliance with structural and functional performance requirements1,2. The use of recycled materials, warm mix asphalt (WMA) technologies, and industrial by-products such as fly ash, steel slag, and reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) has shown considerable potential for reducing energy use, carbon emissions, and material costs in pavement construction3–5.

Numerous studies have investigated the environmental performance of asphalt pavement technologies by quantifying energy consumption and carbon emissions associated with different materials and construction techniques. Mao et al.6 analysed typical regeneration methods for asphalt pavements and reported notable variations in energy use and carbon emissions across recycling techniques. Vandewalle et al.7 assessed multi-recycled RAP mixtures and demonstrated lower environmental impacts compared to mixtures relying solely on virgin materials. The use of industrial by-products has also been examined, with Xie et al.8 evaluating basic oxygen furnace slag in asphalt mixtures and reporting significant reductions in energy demand and emissions. Similarly, Georgiou and Loizos ,4 showed that warm mix asphalt incorporating steel slag and high RAP content improved the environmental performance of wearing courses.

With respect to alternative binders and stabilizers, Joshi and Patel3 investigated the application of Class C fly ash layers in flexible pavements and highlighted potential reductions in both environmental impacts and construction costs. Comprehensive review studies by Suwarto et al.1 and Alaloul et al.9 synthesized previous research on energy, carbon, and cost assessment approaches for asphalt pavements, emphasizing the importance of consistent assumptions, transparent data sources, and harmonized evaluation procedures. In addition, Rohani and Das2 proposed a probabilistic framework to support sustainable pavement selection based on energy, carbon, and economic indicators, while Zargari and Sadiq10 applied life-cycle thinking to identify strategies for reducing GHG emissions in road infrastructure projects.

Several case studies further demonstrate the relevance of energy- and carbon-focused assessments in pavement engineering. Krishna and Kumar11 evaluated maintenance strategies for bituminous concrete pavements by quantifying agency costs and carbon footprints, while Espinoza et al.12 and Alzard et al.13 estimated carbon emissions for road construction projects in Costa Rica and the United Arab Emirates, respectively, highlighting the strong influence of local materials, construction practices, and supply chains on environmental performance. Liu et al.14 linked pavement structural design choices with carbon emissions by integrating mechanistic–empirical design concepts into carbon footprint estimation. Furthermore, studies on recycled aggregates by Mantalovas et al.15 and Vega et al.16 confirm that the use of recycled materials in pavement construction can substantially reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions through cross-sectoral material recovery.

Previous studies have shown that embodied energy, embodied carbon, and cost indicators are effective tools for evaluating the sustainability of pavement systems by capturing both environmental and economic impacts of construction and maintenance activities. However, the results of such assessments are strongly influenced by regional factors such as material availability, pavement design practices, traffic conditions, and maintenance strategies. Therefore, sustainability evaluations must incorporate local design standards, representative material properties, and realistic maintenance approaches to provide meaningful and practical guidance for pavement planning.

Several studies have explored methods to improve the sustainability of flexible and rigid pavements. In flexible pavements, the use of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), warm mix asphalt, industrial by-products, and optimized maintenance practices has been reported to reduce embodied energy, carbon emissions, and life-cycle costs. For rigid pavements, longer service life, supplementary cementitious materials, and recycled aggregates have demonstrated potential for lowering environmental impacts and overall costs. Despite these developments, most studies assess flexible and rigid pavements separately, making direct comparison difficult because of variations in system boundaries, functional units, analysis periods, and data sources17–23.

A comprehensive comparison of flexible and rigid pavement alternatives within a unified analytical framework remains limited in the literature. Few studies simultaneously evaluate different material replacement levels while considering local traffic demand in terms of million standard axles (MSA) and subgrade strength represented by California Bearing Ratio (CBR). In addition, the integration of nationally adopted pavement design guidelines with region-specific construction cost data and realistic maintenance scenarios has received limited attention. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing reliable assessments of the environmental and economic performance of pavement systems and for supporting sustainable pavement decision-making.

Research significance

To address these gaps, the article explores sustainability indicators of design alternatives for flexible and rigid pavement under varying traffic levels ranging from 30 to 150 MSA. Embodied energy, embodied carbon, and construction - maintenance cost associated with construction and scheduled maintenance activities is quantified to evaluate the sustainability indicators. Conventional materials (such as ordinary Portland cement and virgin aggregates) and alternative materials (including fly ash, GGBS, and recycled aggregates) are considered in the current study. By systematically examining design configurations, material substitution levels, and maintenance strategies, the study aims to identify pavement solutions that achieve an effective balance between structural performance, cost efficiency, and reduced environmental impact.

Flexible pavements, designed in accordance with Indian Road Congress (IRC) 37:2018 guidelines24. Five flexible pavement alternatives were analysed, including granular base systems, cement-treated bases, and configurations incorporating reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP). To isolate the influence of traffic loading on pavement thickness, a constant California Bearing Ratio (CBR) value was adopted.

Rigid pavements, governed by IRC 58:201525, are commonly used for roads subjected to heavy axle loads and urban traffic conditions. Four rigid pavement design alternatives were evaluated, including pavements with tied concrete shoulders and dowel bars, widened outer lanes, and pavement quality concrete (PQC) slabs bonded to dry lean concrete (DLC) layers. These alternatives provide a comparative analysis of influence of design configuration and material selection on embodied energy, embodied carbon, and cost outcomes.

By comparing flexible and rigid pavement design alternatives under identical traffic conditions and consistent functional unit like lane width, construction and maintenance. This study provides a structured basis for energy, carbon, and cost based decision-making in sustainable pavement planning.

Methodology

The methodology adopted in this study begins with pavement design, considering traffic levels ranging from 30 to 150 million standard axles (MSA) and subgrade California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values between 8% and 12%. Based on these inputs, the layer composition and thickness of the pavement structures are determined. Both flexible and rigid pavements are designed in this study. For flexible pavements, five alternative design configurations are developed in accordance with IRC 37:201824 guidelines, while four alternative rigid pavement configurations are prepared following IRC 58:201525 provisions. For rigid pavement layers, the material mix design is adopted based on IRC 44:201726,27 guidelines for cement concrete and IRC 49:201928 for Dry Lean Concrete (DLC). These codes are also referred to for incorporating alternative material scenarios, wherein cement replacement of up to 50% using supplementary cementitious materials and aggregate replacement of up to 30% using recycled materials are considered, as permitted by the respective IRC provisions. After finalizing the pavement designs, the quantities of materials required for each pavement layer are estimated. Embodied energy (EE) and embodied carbon (EC) associated with construction materials are then quantified for both conventional materials and alternative material scenarios. The analysis covers material production, transportation, and construction processes. Subsequently, maintenance strategies for flexible and rigid pavements are incorporated based on recommended practices, and the associated EE, EC, and costs are estimated over the defined analysis period. A cumulative assessment of embodied energy and embodied carbon is performed for construction and scheduled maintenance stages, providing a measure of the total environmental burden associated with each pavement alternative. To enable a uniform basis for comparison, the total embodied energy, embodied carbon, and cost values obtained over the design life periods 15 years for flexible pavements and 25 years for rigid pavements are normalized to equivalent annual (per year) values. Finally, a life-cycle-based cost analysis is conducted using regional Schedule of Rates (SoR) data to estimate construction and maintenance costs. The combined evaluation of embodied energy, embodied carbon, and cost enables a comparative assessment of pavement design alternatives and supports energy-, carbon-, and cost-informed decision-making for sustainable pavement planning.

Embodied energy and embodied carbon estimation

Embodied energy (EE) and embodied carbon (EC) values were calculated using material emission factors obtained from the ICE v2.0 database. These factors were multiplied with the corresponding quantities of materials to estimate the total EE and EC associated with material usage. All materials were assumed to be transported over a lead distance of 1 km. Vehicle productivity and fuel consumption rates for construction activities were adopted from the CAT Handbook (2022). EE and EC associated with construction processes—including excavation, laying, and compaction—were also calculated. The analysis was carried out layer-wise for subgrade, Granular Sub-Base (GSB), Dry Lean Concrete (DLC), Pavement Quality Concrete (PQC), Wet Mix Macadam (WMM), and asphalt layers. Similarly, EE and EC were calculated for maintenance activities over a design period of 15 years for flexible pavements and 25 years for rigid pavements. The same procedure was applied for alternative material scenarios, where Ground Granulated Blast Furnace Slag (GGBS) replaced Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) at levels ranging from 10% to 50%, and recycled aggregates replaced virgin aggregates at levels ranging from 10% to 30%. To ensure a consistent basis for comparison, the total EE and EC values over the respective design life periods were converted into equivalent annual (per year) values, and all computed EE and EC results presented in this study represent annualised values for both conventional and alternative material cases29.

Construction: maintenance cost analysis

Construction and maintenance costs were estimated using the Public Works Department (PWD) Karnataka Schedule of Rates (SoR) for the year 2023–2024. Unit costs per cubic metre were calculated for each pavement layer based on material quantities and corresponding SoR rates. Cost estimation was carried out for both conventional material scenarios and alternative material scenarios, in which Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) was partially replaced with Ground Granulated Blast Furnace Slag (GGBS) at replacement levels ranging from 10% to 50%, and natural aggregates were replaced with recycled aggregates at levels between 10% and 30%.

For alternative material scenarios, material unit costs were modified according to the replacement percentages, while construction execution costs and labour costs were assumed to remain constant across all scenarios. Maintenance costs were similarly estimated based on the adopted maintenance strategies and SoR unit rates. The cumulative construction and maintenance costs over the defined analysis period were then used to compare the cost performance of flexible and rigid pavement alternatives. To ensure uniform comparison, the total costs over the design life periods (15 years for flexible pavements and 25 years for rigid pavements) were converted into equivalent annual (per year) values, and all cost results presented in this study represent annualised values for both conventional and alternative material scenarios.

Results and analysis

Effect of varying traffic with pavement thickness

The design thickness for both flexible and rigid pavement layers was determined based on traffic categories ranging from 30 to 150 MSA. For flexible pavements, the CBR value was kept constant across all traffic categories. As the design traffic increased, the total thickness of flexible pavement layers like Granular sub-base(GSB), Wet Mix Macadam(WMM), Dence Bituminous Macadam, and Bituminous Concrete (BC) also increased to accommodate higher axle loads. Among the five flexible pavement options, designs incorporating treated bases and recycled materials showed marginally higher structural performance and required comparatively less increase in thickness. In flexible pavements, embodied energy (EE) and embodied carbon (EC) increase slightly with higher traffic levels, with values rising by around 10–12% for EE and 8–10% for EC when traffic changes from 30 MSA to 150 MSA for the same material replacement percentage. This indicates that while heavier traffic demands result in slightly greater environmental impacts due to thicker or stronger pavement layers, the influence of traffic is relatively small compared to the effect of material replacement.

When considering material replacement from 0% to 50%, EE and EC decreases by approximately 18-20% and 16-18% for pavements with Granular Base and Granular Subbase, 20% and 18-20% for Pavements with Cementitious Base and Cementitious Subbase with aggregate interlayer for crack relief, 20% and 19-21% for pavements with Cementitious base and subbase with SAMI at the interface of base and the bituminous layer, 17-20% and 18-20% for pavements with Foamed bitumen/bitumen emulsion treated RAP, and 20% and 19-22% for Pavements with Cementitious base and granular subbase with crack relief layer of aggregate layer respectively

For Rigid pavement across all four configurations, EE and EC increase slightly with rising traffic. For EE, the increase is roughly 6–8%, while EC increases by about 5–7%. This is due to the thicker slab or additional reinforcement needed at higher traffic loads.

When considering material replacement from 0% to 50%, both EE and EC reduce significantly. EE decreases by approximately 18% for pavements with dowel bars, 20%for pavement without dowel bars, 19% for pavements with widened outer lanes, and 18% for PQC bonded with DLC. EC shows slightly higher percentage reductions of 23% for pavements with dowel bars, 24% for without dowel bars, 23% for pavement with widened outer lanes and 23% for PQC bonded with DLC.These comparative results clearly demonstrate that material substitution strategies can effectively reduce the embodied environmental footprint of both flexible and rigid pavement systems under varying traffic conditions.

For flexible pavements, cost shows a gradual increase with rising traffic levels, ranging from about 8–10% when traffic grows from 30 MSA to 150 MSA for the same material replacement percentage. This is primarily due to the requirement for thicker or more robust pavement structures at higher traffic loads. When shifting from 0% to 50% material replacement, costs decrease by roughly 5–6% for pavements with a Granular Base and Granular Subbase, 3–4% for pavements with a Cementitious Base and Cementitious Subbase with an aggregate interlayer for crack relief, 8–10% for pavements with a Cementitious Base and Subbase with SAMI at the interface of the base and bituminous layer, 6–7% for pavements with Foamed Bitumen/Bitumen Emulsion treated RAP, and 6–7% for pavements with a Cementitious Base and Granular Subbase with a crack relief aggregate layer.

For rigid pavements, cost increases moderately with higher traffic levels, rising by about 6–8% when traffic changes from 30 MSA to 150 MSA for the same material replacement percentage. This increase stems from the need for thicker slabs or additional reinforcement to handle greater traffic loads. When material replacement increases from 0% to 50%, costs decrease by around 5–6% for all design options.These cost comparisons affirm that using alternative materials can deliver not only environmental but also substantial economic benefits in long-term pavement planning.

Discussion of results

The results indicate that material substitution has a greater influence on embodied energy (EE), embodied carbon (EC), and annualised cost than the increase in traffic intensity. Although increasing traffic from 30 MSA to 150 MSA caused moderate increases in EE, EC, and cost for both pavement types, the incorporation of alternative materials resulted in substantially larger environmental and economic benefits. This confirms that sustainable material selection is an effective strategy for reducing pavement-related environmental impacts.

For flexible pavements, increasing the proportion of alternative materials from 0% to 50% resulted in reductions of approximately 17–20% in EE and 16–22% in EC. These trends are consistent with the findings of Georgiou and Loizos4, who reported significant environmental benefits from the use of warm mix asphalt with recycled materials and steel slag. Similarly, Xie et al.,8 observed reductions in life-cycle energy consumption and emissions through the incorporation of industrial by-products in asphalt pavements. The magnitude of reductions obtained in the present study therefore agrees well with previously reported improvements associated with recycled and alternative pavement materials. The reductions observed in flexible pavements are primarily attributed to the lower energy demand and carbon emissions associated with recycled aggregates, supplementary materials, and reduced use of virgin bituminous materials. The present results further show that traffic intensity has a comparatively smaller effect on EE and EC than material replacement, indicating that sustainable material strategies can offset a significant portion of the environmental burden associated with higher traffic levels.

For rigid pavements, increasing the replacement level of conventional materials with supplementary cementitious materials and recycled aggregates produced EE reductions of about 18–20% and EC reductions of approximately 23–24%. The comparatively higher reduction in EC is mainly associated with partial replacement of cement, which is one of the major contributors to carbon emissions in rigid pavement construction. Similar trends were reported by Vega et al.16, who demonstrated that recycled concrete aggregates can substantially reduce the carbon footprint of pavement construction. The EC reductions obtained in the present study are therefore within the range generally reported in previous sustainability studies on recycled and cement-reduced pavement systems.

From an economic perspective, flexible pavements showed cost savings ranging from 3% to 10%, while rigid pavements demonstrated savings of approximately 5–6% with increasing material replacement. These findings are consistent with the conclusions of Rohani and Das2, who reported that sustainable pavement alternatives can simultaneously improve environmental and economic performance when evaluated using integrated life-cycle assessment and life-cycle cost approaches. The cost savings observed in the present study are mainly associated with reduced consumption of virgin materials and lower transportation and maintenance requirements.

Overall, the results indicate that while increasing traffic inevitably raises embodied energy, embodied carbon, and costs for both pavement types, the magnitude of this increase is relatively modest compared to the substantial reductions achievable through alternative material use. Flexible pavements demonstrate slightly higher sensitivity to traffic in terms of EE, EC, and cost, whereas rigid pavements exhibit greater reductions in embodied carbon through material substitution. These findings highlight that strategic selection of sustainable materials is a more effective approach for reducing environmental burdens and costs than traffic-driven design modifications alone.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study evaluated the environmental and economic performance of five flexible pavement alternatives and four rigid pavement alternatives through the quantification of embodied energy (EE), embodied carbon (EC), and cumulative construction and maintenance costs. The assessment considered traffic levels ranging from 30 to 150 million standard axles (MSA) and examined both conventional material scenarios and alternative material scenarios, including partial replacement of cement with ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) and natural aggregates with recycled aggregates. The results demonstrate that pavement material selection and structural configuration play a dominant role in determining environmental and cost performance. For both flexible and rigid pavements, increasing traffic intensity leads to higher EE, EC, and cumulative costs due to increased material quantities and layer thicknesses; however, the magnitude of this increase is relatively modest when compared to the reductions achievable through alternative material use. Across all pavement types, the incorporation of alternative materials resulted in substantial reductions in embodied energy and embodied carbon, typically in the range of 20% to 30%, along with cost savings of approximately 5% to 10% depending on the design configuration and traffic category. Among flexible pavement options, designs incorporating reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) and recycled aggregates (Options III and V) exhibited superior environmental and economic performance, while for rigid pavements, the PQC bonded to DLC configuration (Option IV) emerged as the most efficient option in terms of reduced embodied energy, embodied carbon, and overall cost. Overall, the findings indicate that strategic use of alternative materials offers a more effective pathway for reducing environmental burdens and costs than traffic-related design modifications alone. Therefore, the adoption of GGBS and recycled aggregates should be encouraged in national specifications and public infrastructure projects, flexible pavement designs incorporating RAP and rigid pavement configurations with bonded DLC should be prioritised where applicable, and future research should incorporate pavement performance modelling (such as fatigue, rutting, and roughness progression) along with vehicle operating costs (VOC) to enable more comprehensive long-term sustainability assessments. This study highlights the importance of integrating embodied energy, embodied carbon, and cost considerations at the early stages of pavement planning and design to support environmentally responsible and economically efficient transportation infrastructure.
