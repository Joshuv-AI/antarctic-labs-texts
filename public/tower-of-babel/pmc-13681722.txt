Spinal intradural arachnoid cyst in an infant with motor developmental delay showing intraoperative MEP improvement: illustrative case
Authors: Ayako Toda, Kenichiro Nagai, Yoshitomo Suzuki, Yuka Matsumoto, Hiroto Sasaki, Toshiaki Hayashi, Masazumi Fujii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSpinal intradural arachnoid cysts (SIACs) are relatively rare, but they can cause neurological symptoms. Spinal intradural arachnoid cysts (SIACs) are relatively uncommon space-occupying lesions arising between the spinal cord and the dura mater. They predominantly occur in middle-aged populations and are rare in children, especially infants. These cysts are usually asymptomatic but may produce neurological symptoms, such as pain, numbness, or muscle weakness, by compressing the spinal cord or nerve roots. If symptomatic, early surgical intervention is required.1,4

On the other hand, in young children, assessing subjective symptoms and performing accurate neurological examinations can be challenging. Especially in infants and toddlers with immature neurological development, delays in motor development may sometimes be a variation within normal growth stages.5, 6

We report the case of a 1-year-old child diagnosed with an SIAC via MRI following lower limb motor developmental delay. We suspected the condition was symptomatic and performed surgical decompression of the cyst. Intraoperative electrophysiological testing revealed waveform changes consistent with postoperative improvement in neurological findings, providing physiological evidence that the SIAC was indeed responsible for the patient’s neurological deficits.

Illustrative Case

Preoperative Findings

The patient was a 14-month-old girl with no significant medical history. At her 8-month infant health checkup, she was unable to sit or roll over, leading to suspicion of delayed motor development in the lower limbs. Follow-up and rehabilitation were initiated at the pediatrics department of the previous hospital. Although her motor development showed slight progress, she continued to fall behind her ­age-appropriate developmental milestones; therefore, brain and spine MRI studies were performed. MRI revealed an SIAC at the upper thoracic level compressing the spinal cord, and she was subsequently referred to our department (Fig. 1).

Preoperative sagittal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted MR images of the spine, demonstrating a dorsal thoracic arachnoid cyst compressing and deforming the spinal cord.

On initial examination at our department, no lower limb deformities or limitations in the range of motion were observed, and deep tendon reflexes were normal. While spontaneous movements were preserved, the patient had difficulty pulling herself up to a standing position and could not lift her hips from a prone position, suggesting mild lower limb muscle weakness. Scoliosis and paraspinal muscle wasting were not observed. No findings suggestive of bladder or bowel dysfunction were identified, such as frequent urination (absence of dry periods), a history of urinary tract infection, cloudy urine, or severe constipation. Neither bladder ultrasonography nor urodynamic studies were performed. Superficial reflexes were not specifically documented.

The Enjoji Scale for Infant Development revealed that while upper limb motor function and language development were age-appropriate, lower limb neurological findings showed a 4-month delay (Fig. 2). The Enjoji Scale is a simplified screening tool for infant development that is widely used in Japan. It evaluates developmental stages across three domains and six subitems: motor (locomotion and fine motor), social (basic habits and interpersonal relations), and comprehension/language (speech and language understanding). It is particularly useful for tracking developmental progress by comparing serial changes in developmental charts.7,8

Pre- and postoperative (1 and 5 months) results of the Enjoji Scale for Infant Development. Psychomotor development is evaluated across three domains and six items by marking achieved tasks. The preoperative discrepancy in locomotion narrowed at 1 month postoperatively and recovered to the chronological age level by 5 months.

MRI demonstrated an SIAC on the dorsal side of the spinal cord at the C7–T4 level, with the spinal cord exhibiting flattening due to compression. No obvious intracranial lesions were identified. We considered the SIAC to be clinically symptomatic and, after obtaining informed consent from the family, planned surgical cyst fenestration.

Surgery

Two months after the initial visit, the operation was performed in the prone position under intraoperative motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring. Somatosensory evoked potential (SEP) monitoring was also attempted; however, no reliable waveforms were obtained. Skin incisions were made at the rostral and caudal ends of the cyst. Laminectomy and cyst fenestration were performed through each incision site. Adequate communication with the normal subarachnoid space was established, resulting in the resolution of the arachnoid cyst.

Intraoperative Neurophysiological Monitoring

Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was performed using the MEE-1200 series system (Nihon Kohden). Corkscrew electrodes were placed at C3, C4, and Cz according to the international 10–20 system. The stimulating cathode was positioned at Cz, with anodes at C3 and C4. Transcranial MEPs were elicited using a stimulus intensity of 110 mA, a pulse width of 0.5 msec, and trains of 5–10 pulses. MEPs were recorded from the bilateral abductor hallucis (AH) muscles and the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle. From the induction of anesthesia until cyst fenestration, MEP amplitudes in the lower extremities were markedly lower than those in the right APB. Therefore, the recording sensitivity was adjusted between 1 mV/div and 50 μV/div as appropriate to facilitate monitoring (Fig. 3). Immediately after cyst fenestration and relief of spinal cord compression, the amplitudes of the lower extremity MEPs began to increase and remained elevated until the end of surgery (Fig. 3). Specifically, before fenestration, the mean MEP amplitudes were 0.078 mV in the left lower extremity and 0.136 mV in the right lower extremity, corresponding to 2% and 4%, respectively, of the upper extremity control amplitude (3.44 mV). After fenestration, the amplitudes increased to 0.26 mV on the left and 0.52 mV on the right, representing 3.4-fold and 3.8-fold increases, respectively, compared with the prefenestration values. When the maximum changes of amplitude were calculated by comparing the preoperative minimum with the postoperative maximum, increases of 16-fold (0.03 to 0.48 mV) on the left and 9.5-fold (0.10 to 0.95 mV) on the right were observed. Postoperative Course

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day (POD) 8. Lower extremity weakness began to improve 2 weeks after surgery. At the 5-month postoperative follow-up, motor developmental assessment demonstrated age-appropriate lower extremity motor function (Fig. 2). MRI performed 6 months postoperatively revealed complete resolution of the cyst and improvement in spinal cord morphology (Fig. 5).

Sagittal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted MR images obtained at 6 months postoperatively. The cyst has disappeared, and the morphology of the previously compressed spinal cord has improved.

Discussion

SIACs can cause a wide range of symptoms, including back pain, leg pain, lower limb muscle weakness, and bladder/rectal disturbances, due to compression of the spinal cord or nerve roots. Symptomatic cases warrant surgical intervention, such as cyst fenestration or resection. While shunt procedures are performed in some cases, postoperative outcomes are reported to be comparable across different surgical techniques.1,3,9 However, several studies have suggested that achieving complete neurological recovery may be challenging when the duration of symptoms is prolonged.1,2

Although SIACs in adults are well documented, reports in the pediatric population remain limited.1,3,4,9,10 Our literature review identified only 5 reported cases in patients aged 0–2 years. Of these 5 cases, 3 patients (aged 1.5–2.5 years) presented with lower limb and back pain, one of whom also exhibited gait instability. The remaining 2 cases involved preverbal infants. One was a 4-month-old girl with a ventral SIAC at the medulla oblongata who presented with respiratory abnormalities, dysphagia, and neck/limb weakness.10 The other was a 12-month-old boy with a lower thoracic SIAC who exhibited motor developmental delay and unilateral lower limb weakness.4 Notably, all reported cases underwent fenestration, resulting in symptomatic improvement or restoration of normal developmental milestones. Combined with the findings from our case, these data suggest that early surgical intervention should be considered for symptomatic lesions, even in infants and young children.

Observations

In the present case, a thoracic SIAC was identified during an investigation into the cause of lower limb motor developmental delay and was deemed a symptomatic lesion. Conversely, after the developmental delay was first noted, the patient continued to show gradual progress, with no observed deterioration in acquired motor functions. A subset of infants exhibit lower limb motor developmental delay but naturally catch up by around age 2 years, a condition known as “shuffling baby” syndrome; this was considered in the differential diagnosis for our patient.

Because the patient was very young and a precise neurological examination was difficult, MEP monitoring provided objective data that supported our clinical impression. Intraoperative MEP changes also provided physiological evidence that the cyst was functionally significant. The extremely low MEP amplitude rose immediately and was maintained after spinal decompression by cyst fenestration, and thus paralleled the postoperative clinical course.

While there have been previous reports of intraoperative MEP and SEP monitoring during the resection or fenestration of spinal arachnoid cysts, detailed data remain scarce.2,4 On the other hand, similar associations between intraoperative MEP improvement and postoperative neurological recovery have been reported in other spinal compressive disorders,11 and suggested that intraoperative MEP improvement may have prognostic value, which may be consistent with the findings in our case.

Notably, despite the cervicothoracic spinal cord compression, the patient exhibited neither hyperreflexia nor lower extremity spasticity. This atypical neurological presentation was one of the reasons why we initially questioned whether the cyst was truly symptomatic. One possible explanation is that, because the patient was an infant, the immaturity of the nervous system may have resulted in less apparent upper motor neuron signs.12,13 In addition, spasticity is thought to arise from a disturbance in the balance between supraspinal inhibitory and excitatory inputs controlling the stretch reflex,14 and recent studies have suggested that injury to the reticulospinal tract, which contributes to supraspinal inhibitory control, is involved in the development of spasticity.15 In the present case, the spinal cord was compressed from the dorsal side, and the descending reticulospinal tract located in the lateral aspect of the spinal cord may therefore have been relatively spared, potentially contributing to the absence of overt spasticity.

This is a single-case report, and we do not suggest that MEP amplitudes are sufficient to distinguish between symptomatic and asymptomatic lesions. Further cases are needed to determine whether such MEP findings can be consistently used as supportive evidence of symptomatology in patients with an SIAC.

Finally, several practical considerations regarding MEP monitoring should be noted. In this case, by increasing the sensitivity, we were able to monitor changes in amplitude before and after fenestration. Furthermore, when performing MEP monitoring, we routinely monitor the unaffected side (left/right) or the upper/lower limbs to exclude false-negative or false-positive results. This protocol allowed us to recognize the potential difference between the upper and lower limbs and identify the decline in lower limb motor function.

Lessons

We presented a case of symptomatic spinal arachnoid cyst in a young child, initially identified through a delay in lower limb motor development. Intraoperative MEP changes correlated well with the favorable clinical course, providing physiological evidence that supports our clinical judgment.
