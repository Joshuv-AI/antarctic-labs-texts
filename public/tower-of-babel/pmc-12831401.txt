Aptamer-engineered NIR-II Ru-doped carbon quantum dot nanozyme for photoacoustic imaging-guided synergistic photothermal and catalytic cancer therapy
Authors: Yumin Hao, Zihan Wang, Yihong Chen, Chenxing Li, Huiping Wang, Chuan Dong, Shaomin Shuang, Ding Ding, Xiaojuan Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carbon quantum dot (CQD)-based nanozymes have attracted growing interest for advanced biomedical applications owing to their superior catalytic activity, photostability, and biocompatibility. However, the rational design of a multifunctional nanoplatform that integrates active targeting, multimodal imaging, and synergistic therapy through a green and sustainable approach remains highly challenging. Herein, we report a facile and environmentally benign solvothermal strategy for the synthesis of ruthenium-doped CQD nanozymes (Ru-CQDs) functionalized with AS1411 aptamers and polyethylene glycol (PEG), denoted as Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. This eco-friendly synthesis avoids toxic reagents and harsh conditions while yielding highly uniform and biocompatible nanozymes. The obtained Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibit strong NIR-II absorption, high photothermal conversion efficiency, and pronounced peroxidase-mimicking catalytic activity. The AS1411 aptamer confers active tumor-targeting capability, while PEGylation enhances aqueous dispersibility and physiological stability. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG enable precise photoacoustic tumor imaging and potent tumor ablation under NIR-II laser irradiation via synergistic photothermal and catalytic reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Introduction

Carbon quantum dot (CQD)-based nanozymes have emerged as promising candidates for biomedical applications due to their excellent catalytic activity, photostability, and biocompatibility [1–3]. These unique properties enable their integration into multifunctional theranostic platforms for cancer diagnosis and treatment [4, 5]. However, rational design of CQD nanozymes that simultaneously achieve active tumor targeting, multimodal imaging, and synergistic therapeutic efficacy remains a significant challenge—particularly when green and sustainable synthetic strategies are desired to minimize toxicity and environmental burden.

Aptamers, antibodies, and small molecules have all been explored as targeting ligands [6–9], among which aptamers have garnered increasing interest due to their small size, low immunogenicity, ease of modification, and ability to penetrate solid tumors inaccessible to conventional antibodies [8, 10, 11]. In vitro selection (SELEX) and solid-phase synthesis allow for scalable, reproducible production and site-specific conjugation of aptamers, facilitating their integration into multifunctional therapeutic systems [6, 7, 11, 12]. Notably, aptamer-nanozyme hybrids have shown impressive antitumor efficacy in preclinical models [13, 14]. Ouyang et al. introduced the concept of “nucleoapzymes”, where aptamers are conjugated to catalytic nucleic acids or molecular catalysts to form artificial enzyme-like constructs [15]. These conjugates enable modular positioning of catalytic domains at the 5’, 3’, or internal regions of aptamers via flexible linkers [16], and have demonstrated catalytic transformations including the oxidation of dopamine and N-hydroxy-L-arginine, ATP hydrolysis, and C-H bond oxygenation [17–19]. While significant advances have been made in developing homogeneous catalyst-aptamer hybrids, the concept of integrating aptamers with nanozymes—termed “aptananozymes”—has garnered increasing attention [15, 16, 20, 21]. These aptamer-functionalized nanozymes benefit from both active targeting and enhanced catalytic performance. The integration of aptamers to nanozymes improves substrate affinity, specificity, and catalytic efficiency by promoting substrate enrichment at the active site (molarity effect), and enables advanced features such as switchable, cascade, or cooperative catalysis [21, 22]. For instance, aptamers can act as molecular switches to trigger catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA), thereby amplifying signals through nanozyme-catalyzed reactions [23, 24]. CQDs modified with Cu2+ and functionalized with dopamine or tyrosinamide aptamers have been reported to catalyze selective oxidative transformations, demonstrating promising applications in both biomedical and analytical fields [16, 20]. These aptananozymes represent a promising strategy for precision oncology by combining targeted recognition with catalytic tumor therapy.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) is emerging as an attractive cancer treatment strategy due to its spatial controllability, minimal invasiveness, and limited side effects [25]. Compared to the first near-infrared window (NIR-I, 700–900 nm), the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) offers superior tissue penetration, reduced scattering, a higher signal-to-background ratio, and a higher maximum permissible exposure (MPE), making it highly favorable for deep-tissue imaging and therapy [26]. Numerous NIR-II-absorbing photothermal agents—including metal chalcogenides, 2D materials, and semiconducting polymers—have been developed for photoacoustic imaging (PAI) and PTT [27]. For instance, Pd@Au nanoframe hydrogels exhibit remarkable photothermal therapeutic efficacy for wound healing under NIR laser irradiation [28]. PAI is a hybrid optical-ultrasound imaging technique that provides high-resolution images of deep tissues based on optical absorption [29, 30]. However, endogenous absorbers such as lipids can generate background signals that limit PAI specificity [31]. Thus, developing exogenous PAI agents with strong NIR-II absorption is essential to improve in vivo imaging quality [32, 33]. Importantly, many PAI agents also possess photothermal conversion capability, making them dual-functional for both imaging and therapy [34]. The combination of PAI and PTT has gained traction as a powerful approach for cancer diagnosis and treatment [35]. In particular, CQDs exhibit a suite of desirable properties—small size, π-conjugated structure, tunable fluorescence, and high surface area—that render them excellent candidates for use as nanocarriers, imaging probes, and photothermal agents [36].

In this study, we developed Ru-doped CQD nanozymes (Ru-CQDs) via a one-pot hydrothermal method using DNA and RuCl3 as precursors. To confer tumor-targeting capability, the AS1411 aptamer and polyethylene glycol (PEG) were conjugated to the surface of Ru-CQDs, yielding three functional nanostructures: Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. The resulting Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG nanozyme exhibits excellent peroxidase-like catalytic activity and robust photothermal performance under 1064 nm NIR-II laser irradiation. In addition, the material provides high-contrast photoacoustic signals, enabling real-time tumor imaging and image-guided PTT. In vivo experiments demonstrated significant tumor ablation efficacy in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1). Overall, the integration of catalytic, photothermal, and targeting functionalities into a biocompatible nanoplatform highlights the promise of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG as a next-generation agent for precision cancer theranostics.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Ru-CQDs

DNA (0.05 µmol, sequence: 5’-ATC TAA CTG CTG CGC CGC CGG GAA AAT ACT GTA CGG TTA GA-3’) and RuCl3 (0.05 g) were dissolved in 10 mL of ultrapure water. The solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 6 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the product was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 10 min. The precipitate was washed three times with ultrapure water to remove residual impurities and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain Ru-CQDs.

Synthesis of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG

NH2-AS1411 (6 OD) was first activated with EDC (80 mg) and NHS (50 mg) for 1 h. Ru-CQDs (10 mg) were then added to the activated aptamer solution and stirred for 24 h, followed by addition of DSPE-mPEG2000 (20 mg) and further reaction for 1 h. The mixture was dialyzed against ultrapure water (MWCO: 8000–10000 Da) for 24 h, and the final product, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, was obtained via freeze-drying.

Intracellular ROS imaging

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in 4T1 cells treated with Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, or Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was evaluated using a DCFH-DA assay. 4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates at and incubated with nanomaterials (200 µg•mL− 1) under pH 6.5 or 7.4 for 24 h. For dose-dependent studies, cells were treated with Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (0, 50, 150, 200 µg•mL− 1, pH 6.5) for 24 h. After treatment, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM, Beyotime, China) in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min, washed thrice with DPBS, and imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Ex/Em = 488/525 nm).

Cell apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis induced by photothermal treatment was quantified via flow cytometry. 4T1 cells (3 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h, then treated with Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, or Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (200 µg•mL− 1) for another 24 h. Cells were subsequently irradiated with a 1064 nm NIR laser (1.0 W•cm− 1) for 5 min, stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Quantitative evaluation of photothermal ablation

The photothermal ablation efficacy was quantified via a CCK-8 assay. 4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then treated with Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, or Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (200 µg•mL− 1, 24 h). For dose-dependent studies, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (0, 25, 50, 100, or 200 µg•mL− 1) was used. Cells were then irradiated with a 1064 nm laser for 5 min at various power densities, incubated for an additional 24 h, and analyzed using CCK-8. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and relative viability was calculated against untreated controls.

Mouse models

Female nude mice (6–8 weeks) were subcutaneously injected with 4T1 cells (1 × 106 cells in 100 µL PBS) into the right flank. Experiments were initiated when tumor volumes reached ~ 100 mm3. Mice were randomized into control and treatment groups for subsequent in vivo assays.

Ethics

All animal experiments were conducted under protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. RJ2023-096 A).

Tissue distributions

For biodistribution analysis, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (tumor size: 100–200 mm3) were intravenously injected with Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (Ru = 0.86 mg•mL− 1). At predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h), major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys), tumors, and blood were harvested, digested in Lefort aqua regia, and analyzed for Ru content using ICP-OES.

In vivo photoacoustic (PA) imaging

To assess in vivo imaging, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, or Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (3.0 mg•mL−1), following literature-reported protocols [37, 38]. PA images were captured at different time points using a PA imaging system (λ = 808 nm, frequency = 37 MHz, PA gain = 37.0 dB).

In vivo antitumor effect

Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into 12 groups (n = 6): (1) PBS, (2) AS1411, (3) Ru-CQDs, (4) Ru-CQDs-AS1411, (5) Ru-CQDs@PEG, (6) Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, (7–12) the same treatments plus NIR laser irradiation (1064 nm, 0.5 W•cm− 2, 10 min). One-hour post-injection, laser-treated groups were irradiated at the tumor sites. Tumor temperatures were monitored using an infrared (IR) thermal camera. Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05 (*) and highly significant at p < 0.01 (**).

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG

The synthetic route of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG is schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a. Ru-CQDs were synthesized via a one-pot hydrothermal method using DNA and RuCl3 as precursors. In this synthesis, DNA serves a dual function: (i) its nucleobases, rich in functional groups, partially remain on the surface of the resulting carbon quantum dots (CQDs), providing abundant active sites for further modification; and (ii) the coordination interaction between DNA bases and Ru3+ ions promotes the formation of stable multidentate complexes, effectively preventing Ru3+ aggregation and enabling uniform atomic-level Ru doping within the carbon framework. Optimization studies revealed that 0.05 g of RuCl3 (Fig. S1a, b) and a reaction duration of 6 h (Fig. S1c, d) yielded Ru-CQDs with the highest catalytic activity and absorbance intensity. HRTEM imaging (Fig. 2b) demonstrated that all synthesized nanostructures were uniformly dispersed. The average diameters of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG were 1.71 ± 0.1 nm, 2.11 ± 0.1 nm, 2.99 ± 0.2 nm, and 4.57 ± 0.5 nm, respectively, with corresponding size distributions of 1.24–2.32 nm, 1.49–2.75 nm, 2.36–3.91 nm, and 3.31–6.49 nm. The stepwise increase in particle size confirms the successful layer-by-layer surface functionalization.

To elucidate the surface composition and chemical states, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analyses were performed. The XPS survey spectra (Fig. 2c) revealed characteristic peaks at 529.0, 485.0/462.0, 397.0, 282.0, 198.0, and 119.0 eV, corresponding to O 1s, Ru 3p, N 1s, C 1s/Ru 3d, Cl 2p, and P 2p, respectively [39–42]. In the high-resolution C 1s/Ru 3d spectra (Fig. 2d), three main peaks at 287.5, 285.0, and 281.2 eV were assigned to C = O, C-H, and Run+ species, respectively [41, 42]. A distinct signal at 283.6 eV, attributed to C-Ru bonding [43], appeared in Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. Peaks corresponding to metallic Ru0 were detected at 280.6 eV (Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs@PEG) and 279.6 eV (Ru-CQDs-AS1411) [41]. The N 1s spectra (Fig. 2e) exhibited characteristic peaks at 398.5 eV (pyridinic N) [44], 399.2 eV (pyrrolic N, in Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG) [44], and 400.5 eV (graphitic N, in Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG) [45]. O 1s spectra (Fig. S2a) showed two main peaks at 531.2 and 529.6 eV, assigned to C-O and O-N bonds [45, 46], while an additional signal at 530.9 eV (C-OH) appeared exclusively in Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG [45]. The Ru 3p spectra (Fig. 2f) displayed three consistent peaks at 484.5, 465.4, and 462.3 eV, corresponding to Ru0 (Ru 3p3/2), Ru0 (Ru 3p1/2), and Run+ species, respectively [41, 47]. A peak at 487.9 eV, also assigned to Run+, was present in Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, and Ru-CQDs@PEG [41]. The P 2p spectra (Fig. S2b) revealed P-C bonds (132.7 eV) in all samples, along with P = O signals at 133.3 eV and 132.2 eV [41, 45, 47]. The Cl 2p spectra (Fig. S2c) contained peaks at 196.6 eV (Cl-Ru) in all samples [47]. Cl-C bonds (198.1 eV) were detected in Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG [42], while Cl-N peaks appeared at 197.1 eV (Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs@PEG) and 200.3 eV (Ru-CQDs-AS1411) [39, 42]. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2g) further confirmed the coexistence of inorganic and organic functionalities. Characteristic absorption bands were observed at 841 cm− 1 (Cl-Ru), 960 cm− 1 (C = C), 1120 cm− 1 (C = O), 1563–1651 cm− 1 (Ru-CO), 2365 cm− 1 (C ≡ N), 2897 cm− 1 (N-H/C-H stretching), and 3424 cm− 1 (COOH/O-H stretching) [39, 41, 42, 47, 48].

UV-vis absorption spectra (Fig. 2h) revealed a new absorption peak at ~ 254 nm in Ru-CQDs-AS1411 and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, characteristic of the AS1411 aptamer. This band, absent in Ru-CQDs, confirms successful aptamer conjugation. A peak at ~ 237.5 nm, corresponding to PEG, appeared only in Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, validating PEG grafting [49]. Mass spectrometry further supported the aptamer attachment: a distinct peak at 8452 Da (AS1411) was detected, while Ru-CQDs-AS1411 and AS1411-Ru-CQDs@PEG displayed additional peaks at 8452 Da and higher, confirming covalent linkage (Fig. 2i) [50]. Zeta potential analysis (Fig. S3) provided complementary evidence of functionalization. The zeta potentials of Ru-CQDs, AS1411, and PEG were + 8.73 mV, −14.8 mV, and − 10.8 mV, respectively. After conjugation, the potentials shifted to −9.4 mV (Ru-CQDs-AS1411), −7.3 mV (Ru-CQDs@PEG), and −11.0 mV (Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG), indicating consumption of surface carboxyl groups and introduction of negatively charged moieties [49]. Collectively, the TEM, XPS, FTIR, UV-vis, MS, and zeta potential results unambiguously verify the successful stepwise construction of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. This rational surface engineering strategy imparts multifunctionality—combining catalytic activity, photothermal responsiveness, and tumor-targeting capability—laying the groundwork for the subsequent therapeutic investigations.

Peroxidase-like activity and photothermal property of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG

TEM and XPS analyses confirmed the successful incorporation of Ru into both Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. The redox flexibility of Ru in multiple valence states suggests promising potential for biocatalysis applications. Accordingly, the peroxidase-like activity of Ru-CQD and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was systematically investigated. Peroxidase-mimicking nanozymes catalyze the oxidation of TMB by H2O2, generating oxidized TMB (oxTMB), which displays a characteristic blue color with an absorption peak at 652 nm. To assess this activity, we employed the TMB-H2O2 system as a model reaction. As depicted in Figs. S4a and S4b, the reaction systems containing H2O2, TMB, and either Ru-CQDs or Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibited a prominent absorption peak at 652 nm, confirming their peroxidase-like catalytic capability. Given that enzyme-like activity can be influenced by nanozyme concentration and pH, we evaluated the effects of varying concentrations of Ru-CQDs (0–7.5 µg•mL− 1) and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (15–35 µg•mL− 1), as well as different pH conditions (pH 3.0–7.4). As displayed in Figs. 3a and S4c, the catalytic activity of both Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was found to be pH-dependent, with the highest activity observed at pH 6.5. Furthermore, as shown in Figs. 3b and S4d, increasing the nanozyme concentration resulted in enhanced peroxidase-like activity in a dose-dependent manner. These results collectively demonstrate that both Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibit strong peroxidase-mimicking behavior. This catalytic functionality lays a solid foundation for their subsequent application in bio-catalysis and therapeutic platforms.

To further evaluate the peroxidase-like activity of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, steady-state kinetic assays were conducted using TMB and H2O2 as substrates under optimal conditions. As displayed in Figs. 3c, 3d, and S4e, f, the catalytic reactions of both substrates followed classic Michaelis-Menten kinetics. The corresponding Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) and maximum initial velocities (Vmax) were derived from the intercepts and slopes of the Lineweaver-Burk double reciprocal plots (Figs. 3c, 3d, S4g, and S4h). The Km values for Ru-CQDs were 0.24 µM (TMB) and 2.04 µM (H2O2), while the Vmax values were 0.047 × 10− 8 M•s− 1 and 0.052 × 10− 8 M•s− 1, respectively. In contrast, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibited lower Km values of 0.03 µM (TMB) and 1.18 µM (H2O2), and Vmax values of 0.008 × 10− 8 M•s− 1 and 0.009 × 10− 8 M•s− 1, respectively. These results suggest that Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG possess a higher substrate-binding affinity (lower Km), albeit with slightly reduced catalytic efficiency (lower Vmax), possibly due to steric hindrance introduced by surface modifications. When compared with previously reported peroxidase-mimicking nanozymes [51–56], both Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibit superior affinity toward TMB, underscoring their potential as efficient artificial enzymes (Table S1).

To elucidate the underlying catalytic mechanism, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during the catalytic process using specific scavengers. Tryptophan (Try), benzoquinone (BQ), and isopropanol (IPA) were employed to quench 1O2, •O2−, and •OH, respectively. As shown in Figs. 3e and S4i, the catalytic activity of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was markedly suppressed by Try, while the addition of BQ and IPA exhibited negligible effects. This result indicates that 1O2 is the predominant ROS involved in the catalytic oxidation process, whereas •O2− and •OH are not significantly involved. To further validate 1O2 generation, EPR spectroscopy was performed using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as the spin-trapping agent. As depicted in Figs. 3f and S4j, the Ru-CQDs/Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG + H2O2 + TEMP system exhibited a distinct three-line EPR spectrum with an intensity ratio of 1:1:1, characteristic of the TEMP-1O2 adduct, confirming the generation of 1O2 during the nanozyme-catalyzed reaction. In summary, kinetic analysis and ROS identification collectively demonstrate that Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG could act as efficient peroxidase mimics, with 1O2 as the key reactive intermediate. These findings offer critical insights into their catalytic mechanism and provide a theoretical basis for their further therapeutic applications.

Given the broad optical absorption of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG in both the visible and near-infrared (NIR) regions (Fig. 3g), their photothermal performance was evaluated under NIR-Ⅱ laser irradiation (1064 nm, 0.5 W•cm− 2). Suspensions of various concentrations were exposed to the laser for 600 s. As shown in Fig. S5a, upon irradiation with 808 nm and 1064 nm lasers for 10 min, the temperature of Ru-CQDs/Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG solutions increased by 40.0 ℃ and 64.0 ℃ for Ru-CQDs, and by 34.2 ℃ and 42.5 ℃ for Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, respectively. These results indicate significantly stronger absorption at 1064 nm, and thus, the 1064 nm laser was selected for subsequent studies. The temperature increase showed a clear concentration-dependent behavior. For Ru-CQDs, the final temperatures reached 33.0 ℃, 37.3 ℃, 47.6 ℃, and 63.8 ℃ at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 200 µg•mL− 1, respectively, after 10 min of irradiation (Fig. S5b). Similarly, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG suspensions reached final temperatures of 33.0 °C, 36.2 °C, 45.1 °C, and 47.4 °C under the same conditions (Fig. 3h), indicating effective and tunable photothermal response. To further investigate the photothermal conversion efficient, suspensions of 100 µg•mL− 1 were irradiated with 1064 nm lasers at varying power densities. As displayed in Figs. S5c and 3i, both Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibited pronounced laser power-dependent temperature elevation, with final temperatures increasing from 34.7 °C to 75.0 °C and 29.0 °C to 71.0 °C as laser power increased from 0.25 W•cm− 2 to 1.50 W•cm− 2, respectively. These results demonstrate the excellent capability of both materials to efficiently convert NIR laser energy into heat. The photothermal stability was also evaluated by subjecting the materials to four successive on-off laser irradiation cycles. As illustrated in Figs. S5d and 3j, no significant changes in the heating-cooling curves were observed, confirming the good photothermal stability and repeatability of the Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. To visually confirm the photothermal effects, thermal imaging of concentrated aqueous suspensions was conducted. As shown in Figs. S5e and 3k, a clear color transition over time was observed in the thermal images of Ru-CQDs/Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG and PBS solutions, corresponding to temperature increases. The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was calculated to be approximately 3.15% for Ru-CQDs (Figs. S6a and S6b) and 1.25% for Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (Fig. 3l). Although these η values are lower than those of some previously reported photothermal agents (e.g. noble metal nanoparticles with PCE >20%) [57], they are sufficient to achieve mild photothermal therapy (≤ 45 °C), effectively suppressing tumor growth while minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues [58]. It is well established that undoped CQDs suffer from limited absorption and low η within the biologically transparent NIR window [59]. To overcome this intrinsic limitation, Ru doping was strategically employed to enhance the intrinsic photothermal performance of CQDs. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG possess stable and tunable photothermal conversion properties, showing significant promise for application in photothermal therapy.

In vitro ROS generation and photothermal anticancer effects of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG

The cellular uptake behavior of Ru-CQDs and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG in 4T1 cells was first evaluated using ICP-OES. 4T1 cells were incubated with both nanomaterials for 0, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h, followed by quantification of intracellular Ru content. As shown in Fig. S7a, the Ru content in cells treated with Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was consistently higher than that in the Ru-CQDs group, suggesting enhanced cellular internalization due to AS1411 aptamer modification. Additionally, both 4T1 tumor cells and normal LO2 cells were incubated with Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG under the same time intervals. As presented in Fig. S7b, significantly higher intracellular Ru content was observed in 4T1 cells than in LO2 cells. These findings confirm that AS1411 aptamer effectively improves both the tumor-targeting capability and cellular uptake of Ru-CQDs, enabling selective accumulation in tumor cells.

Prior to further biological applications, the cytocompatibility of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was evaluated in LO2 cells using a standard CCK-8 assay. As depicted in Fig. S8a, except for the unmodified Ru-CQDs, all other materials showed neglectable cytotoxicity, with cell viabilities exceeding 70% after 24 h of incubation. Particularly, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG treated cells exhibited excellent biocompatibility. Moreover, Fig. S8b indicates that even at a high concentration of 200 µg•mL− 1, LO2 cells maintained over 81% viability after exposure to Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG for 24 h. These results confirm the biocompatibility of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, supporting its potential for biological and therapeutic applications.

Due to the high cytotoxicity observed with unmodified Ru-CQDs, the ROS generation capacity of Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was further investigated in 4T1 cells via fluorescence imaging. Cells were incubated with DPBS (as control), free AS1411, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG for 24 h. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the ROS-sensitive probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), which fluoresces green upon oxidation. As shown in Fig. S9, under physiological pH (7.4), negligible green fluorescence was observed in all groups, indicating minimal ROS generation. However, under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG induced significant green fluorescence (Fig. 4a), suggesting enhanced ROS production in the tumor-mimicking environment. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 4b, the intensity of green fluorescence increased with higher concentrations of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, confirming a dose-dependent ROS generation effect. These results highlight the acidic pH-responsive ROS-generating capability of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, which is beneficial for selective tumor therapy.

Given that the pH of the tumor microenvironment is approximately 6.5 while that of normal tissues is around 7.4 [60], we evaluated the photothermal therapeutic efficiency of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG under different pH conditions using 4T1 cells as the model. The photothermal cytotoxicity of DPBS, AS1411, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was quantitatively assessed via CCK-8 assays, with and without 1064 nm laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. S10, under physiological pH (7.4) and in the absence of laser irradiation, all tested formulations exhibited negligible cytotoxicity, with 4T1 cell viabilities remaining above 90%, confirming their good biocompatibility under neutral conditions. Upon exposure to 1064 nm laser irradiation, however, cell viabilities dropped to approximately 60% in the Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups, indicating effective photothermal-induced cytotoxicity. Notably, as illustrated in Fig. 4c, under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), even without laser treatment, 4T1 cell viability decreased to approximately 60%, likely due to enhanced intracellular ROS production and acid-triggered effects. When combined with 1064 nm laser irradiation, the viability further declined to ~ 30% for the Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups, confirming significantly enhanced photothermal therapeutic efficacy in the acidic tumor-like environment. Furthermore, Fig. 4d demonstrates a concentration-dependent photothermal therapeutic (PTT) effect for Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG at pH 6.5 under 1064 nm laser exposure. In particular, at a concentration of 200 µg•mL− 1, the cell viability dropped dramatically to below 10.0%, highlighting the strong photothermal ablation capability of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG. These results suggest that increased cellular internalization of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG leads to enhanced PTT efficacy. Overall, these findings strongly support the potential of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG as an effective photothermal conversion agent for tumor-specific PTT applications in cancer therapy.

The in vitro photothermal therapeutic efficacy of the Ru-CQDs-based formulations was further evaluated using a live/dead cell staining assay on 4T1 tumor cells. The cells were incubated with AS1411, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, followed by 1064 nm laser irradiation and co-staining with calcein-AM (live cells, green fluorescence) and propidium iodide (PI, dead cells, red fluorescence). As depicted in Figs. S11a and S11b, under physiological pH (7.4) without laser irradiation, the 4T1 cells exhibited bright green fluorescence across all treatment groups, indicating good cell viability and minimal cytotoxicity. However, upon exposure to 1064 nm laser irradiation, faint red fluorescence appeared in the Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups, indicating laser-induced photothermal damage to the tumor cells. At pH 6.5, a similar pattern was observed (Figs. S11c and 4e). Cells treated with the same formulations but without laser irradiation showed predominantly green fluorescence, confirming their viable state. In contrast, cells exposed to laser irradiation exhibited significantly enhanced red fluorescence, reflecting increased cell death induced by photothermal effects under acidic tumor-mimicking conditions.

To further validate the concentration-dependent photothermal effect, 4T1 cells were incubated with various concentrations (0, 50, 150, and 200 µg•mL− 1) of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG at pH 6.5, follow by 1064 nm laser treatment and calcein-AM/PI staining. As depicted in Figs. S11d and 4f, in the absence of laser irradiation, cells maintained strong green fluorescence even at the highest concentration, suggesting that Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG alone exhibited negligible cytotoxicity. Laser irradiation alone (without nanomaterial treatment) also did not cause observable cell death, as evidenced by the persistent green fluorescence. However, when Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was combined with laser irradiation, the cells displayed intense red fluorescence, indicating a significant degree of cell death. At higher concentrations, more than half of the cells in the imaging field were stained red, confirming the potent photothermal ablation efficacy of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG under tumor-relevant conditions. These findings further demonstrate the excellent photothermal performance and concentration-dependent cytotoxicity of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, highlighting its strong potential as a targeted PTT agent for effective cancer treatment.

To further quantify the in vitro photothermal therapeutic efficacy of the Ru-CQDs formulations, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining followed by flow cytometry analysis was performed on 4T1 cells after treatment. As shown in Figs. S12a and S12b, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4) and in the absence of 1064 nm laser irradiation, more than 80% of the cells in the PBS, AS1411, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups were located in the lower-left quadrant (Annexin V−/PI−), indicating high viability and negligible cytotoxicity of all formulations. However, after laser irradiation, the proportions of apoptotic cells markedly increased, with apoptosis rates of 81.0%, 57.8%, and 46.0% for the Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups respectively, confirming their laser-induced photothermal ablation ability against tumor cells. At acidic pH (6.5), similar trends were observed (Figs. 4g and S12c). In the absence of laser irradiation, more than 70% of cells in all groups remained viable. Upon 1064 nm laser exposure, apoptosis rates increased significantly to 66.36%, 63.27%, and 77.86% for the Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups, respectively. These results underscore the photothermal efficacy of the Ru-CQDs formulations, with Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG showing the most pronounced effect, likely due to its tumor-targeting and synergistic photothermal properties. The comparable performance of Ru-CQDs-AS1411 and Ru-CQDs@PEG further suggests that ruthenium doping plays a crucial role in enhancing the photothermal conversion efficiency of CQDs.

Furthermore, the concentration-dependent photothermal cytotoxicity of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG was investigated using flow cytometry at pH 6.5. As shown in Figs. 4h and S12d, in the absence of laser irradiation, over 70% of the cells remained viable across all concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 µg•mL− 1), indicating minimal inherent toxicity of the nanomaterial. Upon 1064 nm laser exposure, apoptosis rates increased with concentration, reaching 5.05%, 18.22%, 33.81%, 69.70%, and 85.63%, respectively. These results clearly demonstrate a strong dose-dependent photothermal ablation effect. Collectively, the flow cytometry data confirm that Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG induces irreversible damage to tumor cells under NIR irradiation and possesses outstanding photothermal therapeutic potential for cancer treatment applications.

In vivo photoacoustic imaging (PAI) and biodistribution

Encouraged by the strong NIR-II absorption and excellent in vitro photothermal performance, we further evaluated the photoacoustic imaging (PAI) capability of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG as imaging agents. As shown in Fig. S13a, all four formulations exhibited strong PA signals, indicating their excellent photoacoustic properties in vitro. Subsequently, in vivo PAI was conducted in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice to assess tumor-targeted imaging performance over time. As shown in Figs. 5a and 5b, following intravenous injection of the nanomaterials, the PA signal intensity at the tumor site gradually increased and peaked at 1 h post-injection for all groups. In contrast, the control group displayed negligible PA signal throughout the imaging period (Fig. S13b). These results clearly demonstrate the effective tumor accumulation and PAI contrast capability of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG in vivo, highlighting their potential for PAI-guided antitumor therapy.

To further assess the in vivo biodistribution of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, the Ru content in major organs and tumors was quantitatively determined using ICP-OES (Fig. S14a). Similar to many other nanomaterials used in biomedical applications, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG demonstrated hepatic uptake, with significant accumulation in the liver observed at 48 h post-injection [26, 61]. Importantly, the highest accumulation of Ru at the tumor site was recorded at 1 h post-injection (Fig. S14b), consistent with the PAI results. Although discrepancies between the ex vivo PA imaging and ICP-OES measurements are expected due to the different analytical principles, the overall conclusions from both methods are consistent. These results collectively confirm the excellent tumor-targeting ability and favorable biodistribution profile of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG, supporting its promising potential as a multifunctional nanoplatform for PAI-guided photothermal cancer therapy.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG under NIR irradiation

The Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG platform represents a significant advancement in nanozyme-based theranostics by integrating multiple essential functionalities typically dispersed across different systems. While Mn SACs [62] and Zr-Fc MOF [63] exhibit strong photothermal-catalytic synergy, they lack integrated imaging capability for treatment guidance. Similarly, platforms such as DNA-Ag@Pd NCs [64] and AuPt@CeO2 [65] enable PAI but rely mainly on passive accumulation due to poor tumor-targeting efficiency. In contrast, our Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG system uniquely consolidates synergistic therapy, self-guided NIR-II PAI, and aptamer-mediated targeting into a single multifunctional agent, thus overcoming these major limitations and advancing precision cancer theranostics.

Encouraged by the excellent photothermal therapeutic effects in vitro, we further evaluated the in vivo antitumor efficacy of various synthesized CQD formulations using 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 5c. Due to their strong photothermal conversion abilities, thermal imaging was employed to monitor temperature changes at the tumor sites during NIR-II laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5d, all treated groups exhibited progressive temperature elevation at the tumor site over time. Specifically, as presented in Fig. 5e, the temperature in the tumor region of the saline control group increased by only 5.5 °C after irradiation. In contrast, the Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups showed temperature increases of 11.8 °C, 15.1 °C, 14.4 °C, and 17.9 °C, respectively. The highest temperature elevation was observed in the Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG group, which is attributed to its enhanced accumulation at the tumor site, likely due to both PEG-mediated enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects and AS1411-mediated active targeting. To further quantify therapeutic efficacy (Figs. 5f and S15a), tumor growth was monitored for 12 consecutive days, and tumors were excised for each group at the study endpoint. Tumor growth curves (Figs. 5g and S15b) revealed that in the saline and AS1411 groups (without photothermal effect), tumor volumes increased by 11.85- and 9.42-fold, respectively, compared to day 1, indicating negligible anticancer activity. In contrast, tumor growth was significantly suppressed in groups treated with Ru-CQDs (6.24-fold), Ru-CQDs-AS1411 (4.90-fold), Ru-CQDs@PEG (3.43-fold), and especially Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG (1.69-fold) under 1064 nm laser irradiation. These results highlight the superior tumor inhibition efficacy of the Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG nanoplatform. Histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to further evaluate tumor tissue damage post-treatment. As displayed in Figs. 5i and S15c, the tumor sections from Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG groups after laser irradiation exhibited notable tissue damage and apoptosis, with the most pronounced tumor cell destruction observed in the Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG group. Importantly, no significant body weight loss or abnormalities were observed in any group during the 12-day treatment period (Figs. 5h and S15d), suggesting minimal systemic toxicity of the formulations.

The long-term in vivo biosafety of Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG is critical for their future biomedical applications. Therefore, comprehensive toxicity assessments were conducted in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, including hematological, biochemical, and histopathological analyses. For hematological and serum biochemical evaluation, multiple parameters were monitored to assess the physiological status and potential toxicity toward major organs. These markers included red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean platelet volume (MPV), platelet distribution width (PDW), hematocrit (HCT), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), lymphocytes (Lymph), monocytes (Mon), granulocytes (Gran), and total white blood cell count (WBC). As depicted in Fig. S16, no significant deviations were observed in any of these parameters among the experimental groups compared to the untreated control group (day 0). All measured values remained within normal physiological reference ranges throughout the study period, indicating no apparent hematological or biochemical toxicity. To further evaluate potential histological toxicity, the major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested at the end of the treatment and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. As presented in Figs. 5i and S15c, no obvious signs of inflammation, tissue damage, or pathological lesions were detected in any of the organ sections across all treatment groups. Take together, these results demonstrate that Ru-CQDs, Ru-CQDs-AS1411, Ru-CQDs@PEG, and Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG possess favorable biocompatibility and negligible in vivo toxicity, supporting their potential as safe and effective nanotheranostic agents for imaging-guided cancer therapy.

Conclusions

In summary, a multifunctional nanoplatform based on AS1411 and PEG-modified ruthenium-doped carbon quantum dots (Ru-CQDs-AS411@PEG) via a facile and environmentally benign solvothermal method has been successfully developed. The obtained Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG exhibit robust peroxidase-like catalytic activity and strong optical absorption spanning from the UV to the NIR-II region. Owing to the tumor-targeting capability of the AS1411 aptamer and the enhanced biocompatibility imparted by PEGylation, the nanozyme demonstrates high-contrast photoacoustic (PA) imaging both in vitro and in vivo, enabling precise tumor localization. Furthermore, Ru-CQDs-AS1411@PEG achieve efficient photothermal conversion, realizing synergistic PA-guided photothermal therapy (PTT) and catalytic ROS-mediated tumor ablation. Collectively, this green-engineered nanozyme platform integrates targeting, imaging, and therapeutic functionalities, offering a promising strategy for precise, noninvasive, and sustainable cancer theranostics.
