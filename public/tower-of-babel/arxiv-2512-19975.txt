An Explicit dg–Lie Model Governing Jordan Algebra Deformations
Vincent E. Coll, Jr

Abstract
Over a field of characteristic 0 0 we place the deformation theory of Jordan algebras on a concrete, computation–friendly differential graded Lie algebra. On the symmetric cochains C n ​ ( J ) = Sym n ​ ( J ∨ ) ⊗ J C^{n}(J)=\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(J^{\vee})\otimes J we define an unshuffle–averaged insertion whose commutator is a graded Lie bracket. For the Jordan product μ ∈ C 2 ​ ( J ) \mu\in C^{2}(J) the differential d μ = [ μ , ⋅ ] d_{\mu}=[\mu,\cdot] governs deformations. We prove that [ μ , μ ] = 0 [\mu,\mu]=0 is equivalent to the Jordan identity (via polarization), hence d μ 2 = 0 d_{\mu}^{2}=0 , and record the standard Maurer–Cartan/gauge recursion together with low–degree coboundary formulas. A small two–dimensional unital example is worked out explicitly. The note is deliberately lean and self–contained.

1
Introduction

Let
(J,\circ)
be a Jordan algebra over a field
k
of characteristic
0
.

A
Jordan algebra
over
k
is a
k
–vector space
J
equipped with a bilinear product

\circ:J\times J\to J
that is commutative,
x\circ y=y\circ x
, and satisfies the

Jordan identity

(x\circ x)\circ(y\circ x)=x\circ(x\circ(y\circ x))\quad\text{for all }x,y\in J.

If there exists an element
e\in J
such that
e\circ x=x
for all
x\in J
, we call
J

unital
and refer to
e
as the unit.

The deformation theory of Jordan algebras was developed classically by McCrimmon through
linearization of the Jordan identity and explicit construction of cochain complexes and
coboundary operators; see, for example,
[
4
, §I.3]
and related papers.
In this approach, cocycles and obstructions are described by polynomial identities written
degree by degree, and deformation theory is formulated without reference to an underlying
bracket or Maurer–Cartan formalism.

A central principle of modern deformation theory is that formal deformations of an algebraic
structure are governed by a differential graded Lie algebra (dg–Lie algebra): Maurer–Cartan
elements encode deformations, cohomology controls infinitesimal deformations and obstructions,
and gauge equivalence corresponds to equivalence of deformations. For associative algebras this
role is played by the Gerstenhaber complex, and for Lie algebras by the
Nijenhuis–Richardson bracket.

Aim.

The purpose of this note is to place Jordan algebra deformations into this dg–Lie framework.
Working on the symmetric cochains

C^{n}(J)=\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(J^{\vee})\otimes J,

we define an explicit unshuffle–averaged insertion whose graded commutator is a Lie bracket.
For a Jordan product
\mu\in C^{2}(J)
, the operator
d_{\mu}=[\mu,\cdot]
defines a differential.
We show that the Maurer–Cartan condition
[\mu,\mu]=0
is equivalent to the Jordan identity,
via polarization in characteristic
0
. Consequently, the resulting dg–Lie algebra governs
formal deformations of
(J,\circ)
in the standard sense.

From this perspective, the classical Jordan coboundary operator of McCrimmon arises naturally
as the inner derivation
d_{\mu}=[\mu,\cdot]
, while higher–order deformation equations and gauge
equivalence are encoded uniformly by the Maurer–Cartan formalism. The presentation here is
deliberately concrete and self–contained, with all formulas written explicitly for direct
computation.

Relation to classical Jordan cohomology.

Jordan algebra cohomology and deformation theory were developed classically by McCrimmon via
linearized polynomial identities and explicit coboundary operators written degree by degree.
The present note does not rederive that theory from first principles; rather, it places Jordan

elements correspond exactly to Jordan products. In particular, the classical Jordan differential
appears here as an inner derivation

gauge framework not present in the original formulations.

The governing dg–Lie complex

Fix
\operatorname{char}k=0
. Write
\mu(x,y)=x\circ y
for the symmetric product on

Cochains and degrees

C^{n}(J):=\mathrm{Sym}^{n}(J^{\vee})\otimes J\qquad(n\geq 0),\qquad|f|:=n-1\ \text{ for }f\in C^{n}(J).

Insertion and bracket

For
f\in C^{m}(J)
,
g\in C^{n}(J)
define the unshuffle–averaged insertion

(f\!\circ g)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m+n-1}):=\frac{1}{(m-1)!\,n!}\!\!\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sh}(m-1,n)}f\big(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(m-1)},\,g(x_{\sigma(m)},\dots,x_{\sigma(m+n-1)})\big),

(3.1)

where
\mathrm{Sh}(p,q)\subset S_{p+q}
denotes
(p,q)
–unshuffles. Set the graded commutator

[f,g]:=f\!\circ g-(-1)^{|f||g|}\,g\!\circ f.

Lemma 3.1

(Insertion preserves symmetry)
.

If
f\in C^{m}(J)
and
g\in C^{n}(J)
are totally symmetric, then
(f\circ g)\in C^{m+n-1}(J)
is totally symmetric. In particular,
C^{\bullet}(J)
is closed under
\circ
and under
[\ ,\ ]
.

Proof.

For any
\pi\in S_{m+n-1}
, relabel the inputs by
\pi
. The set
\mathrm{Sh}(m-1,n)
is stable under the induced relabeling, and symmetry of
f
and
g
allows reordering their arguments without changing values; the averaging factor is unchanged. Thus
(f\circ g)\circ\pi=(f\circ g)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Low–degree Jacobi check for the given normalization)
.

For
m=n=\ell=2
, set

(f\!\circ g)(x,y,z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(f(g(x,y),z)+f(g(x,z),y)\big).

Then the graded commutator
[f,g]=f\circ g-(-1)^{|f||g|}g\circ f
satisfies

(-1)^{|f||h|}[f,[g,h]]+(-1)^{|g||f|}[g,[h,f]]+(-1)^{|h||g|}[h,[f,g]]=0.

Proof.
Expand each double commutator using the displayed formula; terms cancel in pairs after permuting the two inner arguments and accounting for the Koszul signs. (Full expansion is routine and omitted.)

Differential and MC identity

Let
\mu\in C^{2}(J)

[\mu,\mu]=2(\mu\!\circ\!\mu),\qquad(\mu\!\circ\!\mu)(x,y,z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\big(\mu(\mu(x,y),z)-\mu(x,\mu(y,z))\big).

(3.2)

Theorem 3.4

(MC
\Longleftrightarrow
Jordan)
.

if and only if

Proof.

By (

[\mu,\mu]=0

\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\big(\mu(\mu(x,y),z)-\mu(x,\mu(y,z))\big)=0.

4
Maurer–Cartan recursion and gauge

Let
\mu_{t}=\mu+t\varphi_{1}+t^{2}\varphi_{2}+\cdots
with
\varphi_{i}\in C^{2}(J)
. The Maurer–Cartan equation
[\mu_{t},\mu_{t}]=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle t^{1}:

\displaystyle\quad d_{\mu}\varphi_{1}=0,

\displaystyle t^{2}:

\displaystyle\quad d_{\mu}\varphi_{2}=-\tfrac{1}{2}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{1}],

\displaystyle t^{n}:

\displaystyle\quad d_{\mu}\varphi_{n}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i+j=n}[\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}]\qquad(n\geq 3).

Gauge by
T_{t}=\exp(tf_{1}+t^{2}f_{2}+\cdots)
with
f_{i}\in C^{1}(J)
acts by
\mu_{t}^{\prime}=T_{t}\!\cdot\!\mu_{t}
; in particular
\varphi_{1}^{\prime}\sim\varphi_{1}+d_{\mu}f_{1}
.

5
Low–degree coboundaries

For
f\in C^{1}(J)=\text{End}(J)
and symmetric
\varphi\in C^{2}(J)
,

\displaystyle(d_{\mu}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x\circ y)-f(x)\circ y-x\circ f(y),

\displaystyle(d_{\mu}\varphi)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\big(\mu(\varphi(x,y),z)-\varphi(\mu(x,y),z)\big).

These are the formulas used in first and second order deformation calculations.

Let

Remark 6.1

(Rigidity and second cohomology)
.

As in other deformation theories, vanishing of the second cohomology

H^{2}(C^{\bullet}(J),d_{\mu})
implies formal rigidity of the Jordan algebra
J
.
For Jordan algebras over
\mathbb{C}
, the converse question is more subtle:
while rigidity with
H^{2}\neq 0
occurs for Lie algebras and for Jordan

super
algebras, the existence of an ordinary (non-super) Jordan algebra
that is rigid despite having nonvanishing second cohomology appears to remain
open. We do not pursue rigidity questions further here.

Appendix A: Polarization of the Jordan identity

Let

Cubic
\Rightarrow

Assume the Jordan identity

Polarize by replacing

Appendix B: Detailed graded–Lie proofs

Throughout we use the conventions of §
3

|f|=n-1

B.1. Right pre–Lie identity and Jacobi

Recall the
(p,q)
–unshuffle set
\mathrm{Sh}(p,q)\subset S_{p+q}
and write

\underline{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m+n+\ell-2})
.

Lemma B.1

(Right pre–Lie)
.

For homogeneous
f,g,h
,

(f\!\circ g)\!\circ h-f\!\circ(g\!\circ h)=(-1)^{|g||h|}\Big((f\!\circ h)\!\circ g-f\!\circ(h\!\circ g)\Big).

Proof.

Expand both sides using (
3.1
). Terms
f(\dots,g(\dots),h(\dots))
appear with the same scalar

\frac{1}{(m-1)!\,n!}\cdot\frac{1}{(m+n-2)!\,\ell!}
.
Both sides therefore carry the identical scalar factor

\frac{1}{(m-1)!\,n!\,(m+n-2)!\,\ell!}.

There is a canonical bijection between pairs of unshuffles

(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathrm{Sh}(m{-}1,n)\times\mathrm{Sh}(m{+}n{-}2,\ell)\;\longleftrightarrow\;(\gamma,\delta)\in\mathrm{Sh}(n{-}1,\ell)\times\mathrm{Sh}(m{-}1,n{+}\ell{-}1)

obtained by first marking which
m{-}1
inputs go directly to
f
,
then which
n
feed the
g
–slot (the rest go to
h
), versus first
marking which
\ell
feed
h
, then which
n{-}1
feed
g
.
Swapping
g
and
h
introduces the Koszul sign

(-1)^{|g||h|}
(since
|g|=n-1
,
|h|=\ell-1
). Hence the identity.
∎

Proposition B.2

(Jacobi for the commutator)
.

The commutator
[f,g]=f\circ g-(-1)^{|f||g|}g\circ f
satisfies the graded Jacobi identity

(-1)^{|f||h|}[f,[g,h]]+(-1)^{|g||f|}[g,[h,f]]+(-1)^{|h||g|}[h,[f,g]]=0.

Proof.

This is standard: the graded Jacobi is equivalent to the right pre–Lie identity
(Loday–Vallette, Prop. 6.6.4). Alternatively, expand the three double commutators
and group terms into pairs that cancel using Lemma
B.1
.
∎

B.2. The identity
(d_{\mu})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{[\mu,\mu]}

Set
d_{\mu}:=\mathrm{ad}_{\mu}=[\mu,\cdot]
. Since
|\mu|=1
(odd), Jacobi yields:

Lemma B.3
.

In any graded Lie algebra and for homogeneous
x
,

\mathrm{ad}_{x}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{ad}_{[x,x]}.

In particular, with
x=\mu
,

(d_{\mu})^{2}f=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[[\mu,\mu],f]\quad\text{for all }f.

Proof.

Apply graded Jacobi to
x,x,f
:

[x,[x,f]]=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[[x,x],f],

because the two occurrences
[x,[x,f]]
coincide (as
|x|
is odd) and the third term

[f,[x,x]]
equals
-[[x,x],f]
by graded antisymmetry. Set
x=\mu
.
∎

Corollary B.4
.

If
[\mu,\mu]=0
then
d_{\mu}^{2}=0
. Conversely, if

then

[[\mu,\mu],f]=0

By the explicit formula (

B.3. Low–degree sanity check:
C^{1}\!\to C^{2}\!\to C^{3}

For
f\in C^{1}(J)=\text{End}(J)
and symmetric
\varphi\in C^{2}(J)
we have

\displaystyle(d_{\mu}f)(x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x\circ y)-f(x)\circ y-x\circ f(y),

\displaystyle(d_{\mu}\varphi)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\big(\mu(\varphi(x,y),z)-\varphi(\mu(x,y),z)\big).

A direct expansion shows

(d_{\mu}^{2}f)(x,y,z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[[\mu,\mu],f](x,y,z),

since the twelve terms split into two groups: those with a
