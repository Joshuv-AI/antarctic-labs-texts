Protocol for adoptive neutrophil transfer to study bone marrow and peripheral blood hematopoietic stem cell kinetics in mice
Authors: Carlotta Boscaro, Gian Paolo Fadini, Mattia Albiero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe homeostatic clearance of neutrophils regulates the kinetics of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the bone marrow. We present a protocol for studying the kinetics of HSPCs in mice using adoptive neutrophil transfer. We describe steps for isolating neutrophils from the bone marrow and performing an intravenous injection. We detail procedures for bone marrow flushing, immunomagnetic selection, neutrophil labeling, and successful tail-vein injection.For complete information on the generation and use of this protocol, please refer to Rodella et al.1Subject areas: Flow Cytometry, Immunology, Stem Cells

Before you begin

The maintenance of a steady pool of neutrophils relies on tightly coordinated recycling mechanisms within the bone marrow (BM), their major site of disposal.2 The homeostatic clearance of aged neutrophils from the circulation plays a critical role in sensing hematopoietic demand and regulates the kinetics of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs). Indeed, the rhythmic clearance of aged neutrophils triggers signaling pathways within the BM that modulate the hematopoietic niche and influence circulating levels of HSPCs,3 which in turn contribute to immune surveillance and hematopoietic equilibrium.4,5 Recently, we and others have uncovered the key role of Oncostatin-M (OSM) as an important regulator of the kinetics of HSPCs in response to G-CSF. OSM is mainly expressed by myeloid cells and particularly by mature neutrophils,6,7 and Osm−/− mice show higher levels of HSPCs in peripheral blood, consistent with its role in regulating the retention of HSPCs in the BM. Therefore, we aimed to study the role of neutrophil-derived OSM in the kinetics of HSPCs under homeostatic conditions, exploiting the versatility of adoptive neutrophil transfer.1 Here, we present a detailed and complete protocol to perform adoptive neutrophil transfer to investigate the mechanisms regulating both the kinetics of HSPCs and the BM microenvironment by evaluating both the soluble factors within the BM and the trafficking of functionally active HSPCs and immune cells in the peripheral blood.

Accordingly, specific BM-derived cytokines involved in the mobilization of HSPCs and hematopoiesis can be assessed, while the number and functional potential of HSPCs in peripheral blood can be measured by flow cytometric analysis and colony-forming unit (CFU) assays, respectively. These experimental tools can help uncover the fine-tuned trafficking of HSPCs in homeostatic and pathological conditions. The section below describes the materials necessary to implement this protocol. The required equipment is listed in the key resources table.

Innovation

This protocol provides a comprehensive and streamlined workflow that integrates all critical phases of adoptive neutrophil transfer—starting from bone marrow isolation and high-purity immunomagnetic selection to fluorescent labeling and tail vein injection—into a single, cohesive procedure.

In contrast to conventional transgenic models that require extensive breeding resources and long experimental timelines, our approach enables rapid functional interrogation of neutrophil-derived signals in vivo. By condensing these complex steps into a standardized, ‘all-in-one’ workflow, we significantly reduce animal usage while preserving scientific robustness and reproducibility. This technique allows for the simultaneous assessment of bone marrow microenvironment modulation and HSPC kinetics, offering a versatile tool that can be easily adapted to study neutrophil roles in various tissues and disease models.

Animal model

All experimental procedures are performed on three to five-month-old (twelve to twenty-weeks-old) female and male mice on a C57BL/6J background. Mice are housed with a maximum of 5 animals per cage with access to food and water ad-libitum and with environmental enrichments. The animals are kept at 23° C in constant 12-h dark/light cycle where the lights turn on at 07:00 a.m. (Zt0). C57BL/6J (Wt) and C57BL/6-Tg(UBC-GFP) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and established as a colony since 2001 and 2017, respectively. All personnel performing mice manipulation and procedures is required to wear proper personal protective garments, in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Step-by-step method details

Bone marrow isolation from mouse femurs and tibiae

Timing: 45 min to 1 h

1.Bones collection. These steps can be performed outside a biological safety cabinet.a.Humanely sacrifice the mouse according to the institutional guidelines.b.Place the mouse in supine position on a sterile dissection surface.c.Spray both hind limbs with 70% ethanol to wet the fur and reduce hair contamination.d.Make a skin incision with fine tip scissors around the ankle (Figure 1A).e.Using surgical tweezers with interlocking teeth remove the skin exposing the underlying skeletal muscle (Figure 1B).f.Remove the quadriceps muscle using fine scissors and forceps.g.Locate the hip joint and using fine scissors disarticulate the femoral head from the acetabulum.CRITICAL: Avoid breaking femurs and tibiae with the scissor to preserve the sterility of the bone marrow.h.Cut the ligaments and the muscles of the knee to separate the femurs from the tibiae.i.Cut the ligaments of the ankle to remove the foot.j.Clean the tibiae from all the muscles by removing the gastrocnemius and anterior tibialis muscles using fine scissors and forceps. If still present, cut and discard the fibula (Figure 1C).Immediately transfer the bones to a sterile tube and keep on ice until ready to proceed to next step.

2.Bone marrow flushing.a.In a biosafety cabinet under sterile conditions, fill the wells of a 6-well plate with sterile 70% ethanol and ice-cold sterile PBS. Use the lid of the 6-well plate as a sterile working surface.b.Rapidly rinse the bones with sterile 70% ethanol followed by a thorough wash with sterile ice-cold PBS.c.Clean each bone with a sterile gauze and remove any residual connective tissue and muscle using sterile forceps and scissors (Figure 1D).d.Cut both epiphyses from the femur and tibia to expose the marrow cavity (Figure 1E).e.Insert 1mL syringe with a 26G needle filled with ice-cold sterile PBS into the marrow cavity.f.Flush the marrow over a 70 μm cell strainer into a sterile 50 mL conical tube by gently pushing the buffer through the bone cavity. Repeat the operation from both ends of the bone to ensure full marrow recovery. troubleshooting 1Note: Avoid excessive pressure when flushing the cavity of the bone, to avoid losing or spraying the bone marrow outside the cell strainer.CRITICAL: Using the syringe, gently scrape the inner walls of the bone to dislodge the bone marrow mechanically and improve the yield of cells. The bones should appear clear white throughout their length (Figure 1F). Residual bone marrow will appear as red pulp scattered along the length of the bone (Figure 1G). Ensure to achieve a complete marrow recovery by repeating Steps 2e-2f.g.Gently sift the BM against the mesh of the strainer with the tip of a sterile plastic Pasteur pipette to dissociate any clumps (Figure 1H).h.Wash the strainer with ice-cold PBS to collect the cells.Note: The mesh of the strainer should appear clear without red clumps. Fibrous fragments may remain (Figure 1I).i.Centrifuge the cell suspension at 400 x g for 5 min. Remove the supernatant and resuspend the pellet in 2mL of ice-cold PBS. Keep the cells on ice until further use.

Neutrophil isolation from the bone marrow

Timing: 1–2 h

This section describes the steps for the isolation of neutrophils from a cell suspension of mouse bone marrow using a commercially available kit which purifies neutrophils through a negative immunomagnetic selection (Cat. 130-097-658 Miltenyi Biotec, see the key resources table). In this protocol non-neutrophils are magnetically labelled with a cocktail of monoclonal antibodies conjugated to magnetic nanoparticles, that are captured using a magnet, while unlabeled neutrophils can be collected. Neutrophils can be isolated also using density gradient centrifugation and FACS sorting, according to the experimental needs.

CRITICAL: all the steps should be performed under sterile conditions, using pre-cooled solutions and keeping cells on ice during all the steps. This will prevent capping of antibodies on the cell surface and non-specific cell labeling and neutrophil activation.

Expected outcomes

We established a reproducible protocol to investigate the functional role of neutrophils in the regulation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) mobilization following adoptive transfer. This approach was specifically designed to provide a reliable experimental framework for dissecting the complex interplay between innate immune cells and stem cell trafficking. Our goal was to contribute a novel methodological tool for the study HSPC dynamics in murine models, with potential applications in the investigation of various physiological and pathological contexts, including inflammation.

In this study, adoptive transfer of neutrophils was found to significantly influence HSPC kinetics, suggesting a regulatory role for these cells in the retention of stem and progenitor cells from the bone marrow niche. This finding underscores the importance of neutrophil-mediated signals in orchestrating the delicate balance between stem cell quiescence, activation, and peripheral mobilization.

Assessment of the purity of isolated neutrophils from bone marrow by flow cytometry

The assessment of purity of isolated bone marrow–derived neutrophils by flow cytometry is expected to confirm a highly enriched neutrophil population with minimal contamination from other hematopoietic lineages. Following isolation, cells are typically evaluated by staining for neutrophil-associated markers (Anti-Ly-6G-FITC and CD11b-PeCy7 in mice) while simultaneously excluding non-myeloid or immature populations using a lineage panel. A successful isolation yields a highly pure population of neutrophils (≥95% of Ly-6G+CD11b+ cells, Figure 2D), with very low or negligible representation of lymphoid, monocyte, and progenitor markers. If the separation results in low purity neutrophils (Figure 2C, central plot), cells can be loaded onto a second column to increase purity. Additionally, the absence of significant doublets, debris, or dead cells within the gated population supports both the quality of the isolation and the reliability of downstream functional assays.

Cytokine profiling in bone marrow extracellular fluid

Following adoptive neutrophil transfer, we expect to observe a significant alteration in the cytokine milieu within the bone marrow extracellular fluid. Increased presence of homeostatic cytokines such as CXCL12 may reflect a return to a bone marrow microenvironment less conducive to hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) egress.

Tracking of neutrophils

Genetically- or biochemically labelled neutrophils can be detected and quantified within the bone marrow or in the peripheral blood using flow-cytometry to confirm the transfer or to describe their kinetic in vivo. Labelled cells should stain positive for neutrophil markers such as Ly6-G (Figure 4A) and should be found within the morphological gate of granulocytes (Figure 4B).

Flow cytometric analysis of circulating LSK cells

Flow cytometric quantification of circulating Lineage− Sca-1+ c-Kit+ (LSK) cells in peripheral blood is expected to show a statistically significant reduction in circulating HSPCs in mice receiving adoptive neutrophil transfer compared to control animals (Figure 6). This reduction is indicative of the suppressive effect exerted by the adoptively transferred neutrophils on stem cell egress from the bone marrow niche.1 A concurrent increase in LSK cell retention within the bone marrow compartment may also be observed upon comparative analysis.

Limitations

The adoptive transfer of neutrophils has limitations. One major challenge is the short lifespan and limited functional stability of neutrophils ex vivo, which complicates their isolation, storage, and manipulation prior to reinfusion. In mice, variability in isolation techniques can produce variable yields and purities depending on the strain, age, and inflammatory status of the donor animals, affecting downstream outcomes. Moreover, neutrophils are highly sensitive to environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and oxygen levels, all of which can alter their activation state and skew experimental results. Mechanical stress during centrifugation or pipetting can also inadvertently activate neutrophils, leading to premature degranulation or NET (neutrophil extracellular trap) formation. In certain pathological conditions, such as chronic inflammation, the local immune microenvironment can further modulate neutrophil behaviour post-transfer, leading to unpredictable or even deleterious effects. Taken together, these technical and environmental factors highlight the need for rigorous quality control measures in murine systems to ensure reproducible and physiologically relevant results.

Neutrophil purity and yield are low.

Problem 3

Premature activation or degranulation of isolated neutrophils.

Potential solution

•Strictly maintain all reagents and cell suspensions on ice (4°C) throughout the entire isolation and labeling procedure to prevent non-specific activation.

Strictly maintain all reagents and cell suspensions on ice (4°C) throughout the entire isolation and labeling procedure to prevent non-specific activation.

Problem 4

Resistance at the injection site and/or loss of vein visibility.

Potential solution

Change the syringe with a new one with a sharp needle, then check the position of the mouse and warm it again under the lamp to allow vasodilation.

Potential solution
