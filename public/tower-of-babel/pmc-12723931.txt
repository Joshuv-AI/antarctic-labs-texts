AI-generated biochemistry test item parameters in MST test conditions
Authors: Murat Polat, Engin Karadag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated whether ChatGPT 4o could accurately estimate the difficulty of medical assessment items by comparing its predictions with empirically-derived parameters from multistage testing simulations.MethodsUsing a hybrid simulation-validation design, the researchers had ChatGPT 4o generate 80 multiple-choice biochemistry questions with difficulty estimates (b-parameters), which were then administered via simulated multistage testing to 5,000 virtual examinees.ResultsThe analysis revealed moderate agreement between AI-generated and simulation-derived difficulty parameters (r = 0.612, 95% CI [0.472, 0.725]), though ChatGPT systematically overestimated item difficulty with a mean bias of 0.240 (SD = 0.503). While the mean absolute error was relatively modest at 0.447, with 91% of items showing errors below 1.0 logits, the AI’s estimates were particularly inaccurate for very easy items, where 83% exhibited absolute errors exceeding 0.5 logits compared to only 29% for medium difficulty items. These findings suggest that while ChatGPT 4o demonstrates promise as a tool for preliminary item generation in medical education assessment, it requires empirical calibration and expert oversight before operational implementation, as the systematic bias indicates the AI lacks access to real-world performance feedback.ConclusionsThe study’s conclusions are tempered by important limitations, including its reliance on simulation-based validation rather than actual student performance data and its single-institution sample, underscoring the need for rigorous psychometric validation when integrating artificial intelligence into medical education assessment.

Introduction

The landscape of educational assessment has undergone significant transformation with the advent of artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies. Traditional psychometric approaches, while foundational to educational measurement, face increasing challenges in meeting the demands of modern educational environments that require adaptive, efficient, and personalized assessment solutions. The need for an evaluation system that employs smart technologies is inevitable for measurement processes to keep pace with the same development speed as education and training processes. The measurement and evaluation method widely applied today is the traditional method in which tests are produced by course instructors [1]. In a system in which smart technologies are integrated into education and training processes at a level that can be characterized as a paradigm shift, conducting measurement operations through traditional methods poses an obstacle to obtaining healthy results. For this reason, it is necessary to ensure that measurement and evaluation processes are carried out by employing methods that allow precise measurements in line with the requirements of the age. This situation has precisely determined the starting point of the study, and the research design has been shaped in this direction as a study that employs artificial intelligence together with MST (Multistage Testing), a test presentation method that allows precise measurements [2]. The integration of AI technologies into educational assessment represents not merely a technological upgrade but a fundamental paradigm shift that promises to revolutionize how we measure and evaluate student learning outcomes [3]. Medical education, in particular, demands rigorous and precise assessment methods due to the high-stakes nature of healthcare practice. The competency-based medical education (CBME) framework requires continuous and accurate evaluation of student progress across multiple domains of knowledge, skills, and attitudes [4]. Traditional assessment methods, while reliable, often lack the flexibility and adaptability needed to provide real-time feedback and personalized learning pathways that modern medical education demands [5].

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT has opened new possibilities for educational assessment. These AI systems demonstrate remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, suggesting potential applications in test item development, scoring, and psychometric analysis [6]. However, the integration of AI into high-stakes educational assessment requires careful validation to ensure that these technologies maintain the psychometric properties essential for reliable and valid measurement. Multistage Testing (MST) represents a sophisticated approach to adaptive assessment that combines the efficiency of computerized adaptive testing with the practical advantages of test booklet administration [7]. MST allows for precise ability estimation while maintaining content balance and providing opportunities for item review and revision during the test session. The integration of AI-generated content with MST methodology presents an opportunity to leverage the strengths of both approaches while addressing their individual limitations [8]. This research addresses a critical gap in the literature by empirically examining the feasibility of using AI-generated item parameters in conjunction with established psychometric methodologies. The study specifically focuses on the comparison of difficulty parameters (b-parameters) estimated by ChatGPT with those derived through traditional MST implementation. The findings have significant implications for the future of educational assessment in medical education and beyond.

Literature review

Artificial intelligence in educational assessment

The application of artificial intelligence in educational assessment has evolved from simple automated scoring systems to sophisticated platforms capable of generating test items, providing adaptive feedback, and conducting complex psychometric analyses. Early applications focused primarily on automated essay scoring (AES) systems that could evaluate written responses with reliability comparable to human raters [9]. These systems demonstrated the potential for AI to handle subjective assessment tasks previously thought to require human judgment. Early samples of AI systems in educational assessment started with language assessment. The Educational Testing Service’s (ETS) e-rater system exemplifies early AES technology, analysing essay features such as grammar, usage, mechanics, style, organization, and development to assign holistic scores. The system achieved correlation coefficients of 0.87–0.94 with human raters across various writing tasks, demonstrating comparable reliability to inter-rater agreement between human scorers [10]. Similarly, Pearson’s Intelligent Essay Assessor (IEA) used latent semantic analysis to evaluate essay content by comparing student responses to a corpus of pre-scored essays, achieving correlations of 0.85–0.92 with human scores across multiple subjects. Moreover, The Graduate Management Admission Test (GMAT) Analytical Writing Assessment represents one of the most successful implementations of AES in high-stakes testing. Since 1999, the system has processed millions of essays, with each response scored by both the automated system and a human rater. When scores differ by more than one point, a second human rater provides the final score, ensuring quality control while maintaining efficiency [11].

Recent developments in natural language processing and machine learning have expanded the scope of AI applications in assessment. Generative AI models, particularly large language models like GPT-3 and GPT-4, have shown remarkable capabilities in creating educational content, including test items that appear to maintain appropriate difficulty levels and content validity [12]. However, the psychometric properties of AI-generated items require systematic investigation to determine their suitability for high-stakes assessment contexts. Computer Adaptive Testing (CAT) systems like those used in the Graduate Record Examination (GRE) and Test of English as a Foreign Language (TOEFL) represent sophisticated AI applications. These systems use item response theory algorithms to select questions in real-time based on student performance, adjusting difficulty to match ability levels [13]. For instance, the GRE’s adaptive algorithm can provide score precision equivalent to traditional paper tests while reducing testing time by approximately 50%.

Research by Wang et al. [14] demonstrated that AI-generated multiple-choice items could achieve comparable reliability and validity to human-authored items when properly calibrated. Their study used GPT-3 to generate biology test items, with human experts rating AI-generated items as having 85% content validity compared to 90% for human-authored items. The AI system successfully created items across Bloom’s taxonomy levels, though it showed particular strength in knowledge and comprehension questions. Gierl and Lai [15] showed that automatic item generation (AIG) techniques could produce large numbers of test items with predictable psychometric properties. Recent applications of large language models in item generation show promising results across disciplines. For example, researchers at Stanford University used GPT-4 to generate reading comprehension questions for elementary students, producing items that achieved 78% agreement with human-expert ratings on appropriateness and 82% agreement on difficulty level estimation. In science assessment, AI systems have successfully generated scenario-based items requiring scientific reasoning [16]. A chemistry item generation system created problems involving stoichiometry calculations embedded in real-world contexts, such as pharmaceutical manufacturing or environmental chemistry scenarios, demonstrating the AI’s ability to integrate content knowledge with authentic applications.

These studies provide foundational evidence for the potential of AI in test development while highlighting the need for empirical validation of item parameters. Current research focuses on establishing quality assurance protocols for AI-generated content, including bias detection algorithms, content validity frameworks, and automated psychometric analysis procedures [17]. The University of Georgia’s AI Item Review System exemplifies emerging quality control mechanisms, using natural language processing to flag potentially problematic content such as cultural bias, inappropriate difficulty progression, or construct-irrelevant variance before human expert review. This hybrid approach maintains the efficiency of AI generation while ensuring the quality standards required for educational assessment.

Unlike educational assessment systems that are calibrated against actual student performance data, ChatGPT lacks real-world feedback on how difficult users actually find different concepts [17]. It can’t learn from patterns of where people struggle versus where they succeed. Moreover, the model conflates conceptual complexity with learning difficulty. It may judge difficulty based on the number of steps, abstract concepts involved, or prerequisite knowledge required, without considering that humans often grasp complex ideas intuitively. In addition, there’s likely an implicit bias toward overestimating difficulty as a form of intellectual humility - it’s safer to assume something is hard and provide more explanation than to assume it’s easy and leave someone confused.

Multistage testing methodology

MST is a multi-stage test presentation method developed as an alternative to CAT (Computerized Adaptive Testing) test presentation method. Multi-stage tests are most generally defined in the literature as a modern way of designing, combining, and managing independent, automatically adaptable units for various potential purposes [7]. MST incorporates an adaptive structure that is presented through item clusters called modules rather than on an item-by-item basis, as in the CAT method, in terms of test generation and administration technique [18–20]. The relationship between candidates’ ability levels and the difficulty profile of items or item sets (modules) is defined by Item Response Theory (IRT) models [21], which will be explained in the following part, form the basis of MST idea.

Multistage Testing represents an evolution in adaptive testing methodology that addresses some of the limitations of traditional computerized adaptive testing (CAT) while maintaining many of its advantages. MST organizes test items into modules or stages, with routing decisions made between stages based on examinees’ performance [2]. This approach provides several advantages over linear testing, including improved measurement precision, reduced test length, and enhanced content control. Moreover, the psychometric foundations of MST are well-established, with extensive research demonstrating its effectiveness across various educational domains. Yan et al. [7] conducted comprehensive studies showing that MST can achieve measurement precision comparable to CAT while providing greater flexibility in test administration. The ability to review and revise responses within stages makes MST particularly suitable for high-stakes assessments where examinees benefit from the opportunity to reconsider their answers. Meanwhile, the implementation of MST requires careful consideration of module construction, routing rules, and scoring procedures. The optimal design depends on factors such as the target population, test length constraints, and desired measurement precision [22]. Research has shown that well-designed MST can reduce standard errors of measurement by 20–30% compared to linear tests of equivalent length while maintaining content validity and face validity.

The main studies related to the theory began to be applied by Lord in the 1950s, and especially after the 1970 s, it found application as an alternative to Classical Test Theory (CTT) scoring methods in test development studies [24]. Item Response Theory (IRT) provides the mathematical framework underlying modern educational measurement, including both traditional and adaptive testing approaches [25]. The difficulty parameter (b-parameter) in IRT models represents the ability level at which an examinee has a 50% probability of answering an item correctly [21]. Accurate estimation of item parameters is crucial for effective test construction and score interpretation.

Traditional parameter estimation methods rely on empirical data collected from large samples of examinees. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and Bayesian approaches are commonly used to derive item parameters from response patterns [26]. These methods require substantial sample sizes and multiple administrations to achieve stable parameter estimates, making them resource-intensive and time-consuming.

Item Response Theory (IRT) is designed to evaluate how well a question discriminates among test takers and to what extent it discriminates across different ability ranges, using the ability level (theta) of candidates taking a test as reference [27]. For example, it is believed that a test consisting of relatively difficult items is the most useful item type for discriminating among candidates and selecting the best candidate, but it is not very useful for less able test takers because the item content is complex and the student’s attention is lost after a while. We can say the opposite for an easy test; it is useless for finding and selecting the top tier in terms of ability because the items are not discriminative.

The test information function shown in Fig. 3 quantitatively determines items at different difficulty levels and can show which items are most useful for student selection according to the ability curve, because this application can convert measurement standard error into ability estimation prediction. Therefore, if a test contains only difficult items and no items that can be considered moderate or easy, the test measures the best students and cannot measure lower students because it does not contain items directed at them. The test information function evaluates the calculus derivative of the candidate’s response function. When an item provides more slope regarding ability, it provides more information about the candidate’s abilities [25]. Additionally, we should remind again that this calculation is computed according to the theta scale, not according to the number-correct scale. Finally, the possibility of AI-assisted parameter estimation represents a potential solution to these challenges. If AI systems can provide accurate initial estimates of item parameters, the traditional calibration process could be streamlined while maintaining psychometric quality. However, this approach requires careful validation to ensure that AI-generated estimates align with empirically derived parameters.

Medical education assessment: challenges and AI integration

Medical education assessment faces unique challenges due to the complexity of medical knowledge, the high-stakes nature of medical practice, and the need for both knowledge assessment and clinical skill evaluation [28]. The stakes are particularly high given that assessment failures can have direct implications for patient safety and healthcare quality. For instance, the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) serves as a gateway to medical practice, with failure rates of approximately 3–5% for first-time US medical school graduates on Step 1, but rising to 20–30% for international medical graduates, highlighting the critical role of assessment in ensuring competency standards.

The move toward competency-based medical education has emphasized the importance of continuous assessment and feedback rather than relying solely on summative examinations (Holmboe et al., 2017). This paradigm shift is exemplified by initiatives such as the Association of American Medical Colleges’ (AAMC) Core Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs), which define 13 essential tasks that graduating medical students should be able to perform independently. These EPAs require assessment methods that go beyond traditional testing to evaluate real-world performance [29].

Traditional medical education assessments, on the other hand, often rely on multiple-choice questions (MCQs) that test factual knowledge and clinical reasoning. While these assessments have demonstrated reliability and validity, they may not capture the full range of competencies required for medical practice [30]. Research by Wass et al. [31] analysing over 10,000 medical student assessments found that traditional MCQ-based examinations showed correlation coefficients of only 0.42–0.58 with actual clinical performance measures, indicating substantial gaps between tested knowledge and practical application. The National Board of Medical Examiners reported that while USMLE Step 1 scores correlate moderately with residency performance (r = 0.31–0.45), they fail to predict important outcomes such as professionalism, communication skills, and patient care quality. The 2019 decision by the USMLE to transition Step 1 from a scored to pass/fail examination reflected growing concerns about the limitations of high-stakes standardized testing. Studies showed that Step 1 scores had become disproportionately important in residency selection, despite weak correlations with clinical competency.

The integration of AI technologies offers opportunities to develop more sophisticated assessment approaches that can evaluate complex reasoning processes and provide personalized feedback. Current AI applications in medical education assessment include several innovative approaches such as natural language processing in clinical documentation, virtual patient simulations and adaptive clinical reasoning assessment.

Furthermore, biochemistry education, specifically, requires students to master both fundamental scientific principles and their clinical applications. Assessment in this domain must evaluate not only factual knowledge but also the ability to apply biochemical concepts to clinical scenarios [32]. Traditional biochemistry assessments often focus on memorization of metabolic pathways, enzyme structures, and chemical reactions without sufficient emphasis on clinical relevance. A multi-institutional study by the American Society for Biochemistry and Molecular Biology found that 68% of biochemistry examination questions across medical schools tested factual recall rather than application or analysis skills [33]. Students frequently struggle to connect basic science knowledge to clinical practice, with only 34% of medical students able to correctly explain the biochemical basis of common diseases like diabetes mellitus in clinical context assessments.

Considering the example of diabetes mellitus, traditional approaches might ask students to draw the insulin signalling pathway or list the enzymes involved in glucose metabolism; however, clinically relevant assessment would require students to explain why metformin works through AMPK activation, how glucagon-like peptide-1 agonist affect incretin pathways, or why patients with certain genetic polymorphisms respond differently to sulfonylureas. These complex reasoning tasks require assessment methods that can evaluate integrated understanding rather than isolated facts. Thus, the development of AI-generated biochemistry questions offers an opportunity to create large item banks while maintaining content validity and appropriate difficulty levels.

Methods

This research employs a hybrid simulation-validation design to evaluate AI-generated item parameter estimates in a multistage testing context. The study generated 5,000 virtual examinee responses using DexterMST software, simulating MST routing based on AI-estimated parameters to create controlled conditions for parameter comparison. These AI-generated estimates were then validated against simulation-derived parameters by assessing systematic bias and error distributions to evaluate AI estimation accuracy. The research is classified as a proof-of-concept pilot study utilizing comparative psychometric analysis within a criterion-related validity framework, where AI estimates serve as the predictor and simulation parameters serve as the criterion. The simulation-based approach was necessitated by the infeasibility of conducting direct empirical testing with 5,000 real medical students. This methodological choice aligns with established psychometric research practices, as simulation-based validation represents a recognized preliminary step that allows for controlled examination of parameter recovery before undertaking expensive and resource-intensive field trials [21, 34]. The research was conducted in three distinct phases: (1) AI-generated item development and parameter estimation, (2) empirical testing using MST methodology, and (3) comparative analysis of parameter estimates.

AI parameter estimation

Following item generation, the 5,000-examinee sample was generated through a systematic simulation procedure designed to create realistic response patterns under controlled conditions. Virtual examinees were assigned ability levels (θ) drawn from a standard normal distribution with a mean of 0, standard deviation of 1, and a range spanning − 3.0 to + 3.0, covering 99.7% of the theoretical distribution and verified for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Item responses were simulated using the 80 ChatGPT-estimated difficulty parameters (b-parameters ranging from 1.0 to 2.4) with discrimination fixed at a = 1.0 to maintain Rasch model assumptions, and no guessing parameter was included given the implausibility of random guessing among medical students. Binary responses (0/1) were generated via Bernoulli trials based on the probability function P(θ) = exp (θ - b)/[1 + exp (θ - b)], with a fixed random seed of 12,345 ensuring reproducibility. The multistage testing routing algorithm directed all examinees through a medium-difficulty module containing 15 items in Stage 1, then routed them to Stage 2 modules based on ability estimates: examinees with θ < −0.43 SD received the easy module, those with − 0.43 ≤ θ ≤ +0.43 received the medium module, and those with θ >+0.43 received the difficult module, with similar routing rules applied between Stages 2 and 3. Finally, item parameters were re-estimated using DexterMST conditional maximum likelihood estimation procedure with a convergence criterion of 0.001, a maximum of 100 iterations, and typical convergence occurring within 15–25 iterations. In the second phase of the study, the item parameters obtained from ChatGPT were loaded into the DexterMST package [36] in the R environment (R Core Team, 2024), which can be used to generate MST test simulation environments. The MST design consisted of three stages with routing decisions made after stages 1 and 2. The first stage contained 15 items of medium difficulty administered to all examinees. Based on performance in stage 1, examinees were routed to either an easy, medium, or difficult second stage module, each containing 12 items. Similarly, routing after stage 2 determined the difficulty level of the final stage module (8 items). While generating this data, it attempts to achieve the highest possible compliance with the given item parameters. Data for 5000 individuals were generated using the DexterMST package. While generating this data, the lowest and highest values of the b parameters reported by ChatGPT were used as the extreme boundaries of the theta distribution required by DexterMST,

The routing rules were established based on preliminary analysis of item difficulties and were designed to provide optimal measurement precision across the ability range. Cut scores for routing decisions were set at the 33rd and 67th percentiles of the expected ability distribution. Subsequently, the ChatGPT 4o model was asked to estimate what the b parameters might be if these questions were to be administered to 1st year medical faculty students. The obtained data were tabulated.

Data analysis

In the data analysis phase, the b parameters obtained from ChatGPT 4 were compared with the b parameters obtained from DexterMST. For this purpose, the difference between the b parameter estimated by ChatGPT and the b parameter obtained from DexterMST was calculated for each question. Thus, the distribution of differences between the estimated and obtained values of b parameters for all questions was obtained.

Discussion

Artificial intelligence and GENAI represent a significant development in the field of online education and assessment following the COVID pandemic, with the potential to disrupt traditional approaches in medical education, enrich educational content, and transform assessment and evaluation methods, providing new ideas to both faculty members and medical students. As AI and GENAI are advancing rapidly, it is impossible to remain silent or passive observers to this development. Many recent studies [37–47] emphasize the importance of adapting to changing technologies in education and incorporating them into assessment practices while maintaining academic integrity and the quality of educational content during this period. In this context, developing ChatGPT’s capability to generate questions that activate different cognitive levels and integrating good examples into assessment presents exciting opportunities and challenges for both educators and learners in medicine [48].

Studies in the literature acknowledge that artificial intelligence tools like ChatGPT provide automation for all stakeholders (learners, educators, decision-makers, etc.) at different stages of educational processes [6], while emphasizing that they cannot immediately replace educational and assessment expertise [49]. Moreover, Bang and colleagues [50] note that ChatGPT can sometimes produce inconsistent or meaningless information, and that the reasons for this may be related to model limitations, poor quality of training data, and incorrectly prompted specific user queries. Qadir [51] emphasizes that the context and manner of asking questions affects the answers, therefore caution should be exercised when using ChatGPT. Similarly, Lo [45] in his literature review noted that ChatGPT is a valuable tool that facilitates processes for teachers and students, but should be used carefully. The aspects of all these studies that align with current research findings are that AI tools like ChatGPT would be beneficial when used under expert control, with careful review of the content produced and its quality.

Looking at the field of medical education, a study by Kung and colleagues [52] investigated ChatGPT’s performance on the United States Medical Licensing Examination. According to the evaluation results of artificial intelligence’s performance on this exam, ChatGPT was able to provide answers at or near the passing threshold without any domain-specific fine-tuning. Based on these results, the researchers argue that large language models could be a powerful tool to assist medical education and ultimately clinical decision-making processes.

Another researcher, Jukiewicz [53], compared grades given by ChatGPT with those given by medical instructors in his study. The results show a strong positive correlation (agreement) between ChatGPT and instructors. Latif and Zhai [54], in their research, concluded that ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) achieved significantly higher scoring accuracy than BERT (Google’s similar application) across all labels [54]. In conclusion, when all these research results are considered, it has been observed that ChatGPT can be quite successful both in question generation and in generating answers to questions, and shows significant agreement in terms of being close to the quality of questions or answers produced by humans in real situations (not simulations), and can be efficient at an acceptable level [55].

The issue of integrating ChatGPT into the assessment phase of education in universities is frequently brought up by most researchers in the literature, examining its possible advantages and disadvantages. The findings obtained from studies align with the findings of this research. For example, Sharples [56] in his study points out that it would be beneficial for distance education universities to include ChatGPT in assessment practices to encourage learning and help faculty members stay current with technology. In another study, Zhai [57] claimed that teachers can create learning assessment elements using ChatGPT, save time and effort, and potentially improve the quality of questions by adhering to a standard framework. Thanks to the capabilities offered by ChatGPT, teachers can develop open-ended question prompts that align with the learning objectives and success criteria of their courses [58]. Particularly in medical education, considering the fact that most academics spend a great deal of time creating monthly tests and exams, which means they will have less time for their clinical work, there appears to be an opportunity for educators to reduce the pressure of exam preparation and assessment by getting help from ChatGPT [59].

The findings show that the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in education is part of an ongoing development process, and ChatGPT is an extension of this [60, 61]. Therefore, adopting ChatGPT as a writing assistant in educational processes has both opportunities and challenges. The need here is to understand ChatGPT’s role as an assistant and facilitator for both students and instructors, as “chatbots” are relatively useful tools for facilitating and supporting the academic process.

This research, which examines how a process works where the same methods are employed together in the evaluation of Artificial Intelligence and the questions it generates in measurement and assessment for the Biochemistry course at the beginning of medical education, has reached important conclusions. The generation of questions by ChatGPT and the MST method, the simulative analysis of the possible difficulty levels of these questions, and the results obtained reveal the original aspect of the study. Conclusion

This study provides important empirical evidence for the feasibility of using ChatGPT for item parameter estimation in question preparation and results evaluation for the 1 st year Medical Faculty Biochemistry course. The results demonstrate that AI-assisted parameter estimation can achieve reasonable accuracy for practical applications while offering significant efficiency advantages over traditional calibration approaches. The strong correlation between AI and empirical estimates, combined with acceptable error margins, supports the potential for controlled implementation of AI-assisted assessment development. However, the findings, as observed in different studies, emphasize the continued importance of empirical validation, particularly for high-stakes assessments and specialized content areas.

The integration of AI technologies into educational assessment represents a paradigm shift that offers both opportunities and challenges. This study contributes to the growing evidence base supporting thoughtful integration of AI tools while maintaining the psychometric rigor essential for valid and reliable measurement. Future research should focus on expanding validation across different content areas, populations, and assessment contexts. Additionally, investigation of methods for improving AI parameter estimation accuracy through training and feedback mechanisms could further enhance the practical utility of these approaches. The finding that error margins between ChatGPT-estimated and MST-calculated b-parameters were “not substantially high” suggests promising potential for AI integration in test development. However, it must also be kept in mind that this indicates suitability for “controlled use” rather than replacement of traditional methods.

To conclude, this research contributes to ongoing discussions about technology-enhanced assessment in several ways. First, AI-assisted item generation could significantly reduce the time and resources required for test development, particularly valuable in medical education where content expertise is expensive and time-consuming to access. Next, the approach could potentially enable more frequent, adaptive assessments that better track student progress over time using less resources and preparation time. Finally, the comparative methodology provides a framework for validating AI-generated content against established psychometric standards which might be critical for quality assurance.

Limitations & suggestions

A number of limitations and considerations have emerged from this work. First, the research relied on simulated rather than authentic student responses, meaning that while the 5,000 virtual examinees provided controlled conditions for parameter comparison, they may not fully capture the complexity of real test-taking behaviours such as strategic omissions, test anxiety effects, or fatigue-related response patterns that actual medical students might exhibit during high-stakes examinations. Second, the item pool was derived from single-institution content specifications, limiting the external validity of the findings; for instance, the biochemistry curriculum emphasis and clinical integration approach at one medical school may differ substantially from those at institutions with problem-based learning frameworks or different accreditation standards. Third, the study focused exclusively on a single academic domain—biochemistry—which restricts the ability to generalize findings across other medical sciences such as pharmacology, pathology, or clinical medicine, where item characteristics, difficulty distributions, and cognitive demands may vary considerably. Next, the cross-sectional rather than longitudinal design means the study provides only a snapshot of AI estimation accuracy at one point in time, precluding examination of how AI-generated parameters might perform across successive test administrations, how parameter stability evolves as AI models are updated, or how estimation accuracy might change as examinees progress through different stages of medical training from preclinical to clinical years. Finally, the study focuses solely on difficulty parameters (b-parameters), but comprehensive item analysis typically requires examination of discrimination parameters and guessing parameters as well. Thus, further research on AI-generated assessment methods is crucial.

For future implications, the findings reveal that the future of educational assessment may involve hybrid approaches that leverage AI capabilities while maintaining human oversight and empirical validation. Such approaches could democratize access to sophisticated assessment technologies while preserving the scientific foundations of educational measurement. What is more, for medical education specifically, this research suggests that AI could potentially assist in creating more frequent, low-stakes assessments that help identify learning gaps earlier in the educational process. Given the high-stakes nature of medical training, having more precise measurement tools could improve both student outcomes and patient safety by ensuring more accurate assessment of competencies.

In sum, the study represents a thoughtful first step toward understanding how AI might enhance rather than replace traditional assessment methods in medical education, maintaining the rigor required for professional training while potentially improving efficiency and precision. As for the final word for future studies: working with large question pools containing higher numbers of items and designing those item pools for a variety of courses would give better insights for the use of AI in item generation. The last but not the least, developing software programs that can perform analyses suitable for MST data structure can be recommended, since the accuracy of achievement tests is crucial in medical training programs.
