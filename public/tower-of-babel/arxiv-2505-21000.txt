Simulating generalised fluids via interacting wave packets evolution
Andrew Urilyon, Leonardo Biagetti, Jitendra Kethepalli, Jacopo De Nardis

Abstract
One–dimensional integrable and quasi- integrable systems display, on macroscopic scales, a universal form of transport known as Generalized Hydrodynamics (GHD). In its standard Euler-scale formulation, GHD mirrors the equations of a two-dimensional compressible fluid but ignores fluctuations and becomes numerically unwieldy as soon as integrability-breaking perturbations are introduced. We show that GHD can be efficiently simulated as a gas of semiclassical wave packets —a natural generalisation of hard-rod particles—whose trajectories are efficiently mapped onto those of point particles. This representation (i) provides a transparent route to incorporate integrability-breaking terms, and (ii) automatically embeds the exact fluctuating-hydrodynamics extension of GHD. The resulting framework enables fast, large-scale simulations of quasi-integrable systems even in the presence of complicated integrability-breaking perturbations. It also manifest the pivotal role of two-point correlations in systems confined by external potentials: we demonstrate that situations where local one-point observables appear thermalised can nevertheless sustain long-lived, far-from-equilibrium long-range correlations for arbitrarily long times, signaling that, differently from what previously stated, true thermalisation is not reached at diffusive time-scales.

II
Generalised Hydrodynamics and effective viscosities

GHD is known to be the correct hydrodynamic theory in the context of 1D quantum integrable systems, where it describes the non-equilibrium evolution of density fields of conserved charges, provided that the inhomogeneity is sufficiently smooth.
There is a particularly natural way to understand GHD by beginning with the continuity equation for the aforementioned local density fields and local current field

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{i}(x,t)+\partial_{x}j_{i}(x,t)=0.

(1)

These local density fields are defined within fluid cells, assumed to have a hydrodynamic length scale
\ell
, that have relaxed such that around their space-time positions, scaled by this hydrodynamic length, these cells are relaxed.
Within each of these fluid cells, a probability distribution is approximately given by a slowly varying generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE)

\displaystyle\rho\sim\exp\!\left[-\ell\int\!dx\,\beta^{i}(x,t)\,q_{i}(x)\right],

(2)

where from now on we shall use the repeated-index convention.
From this fluid-cell assumption a hydrodynamic expansion can be made, which, provided the generalized temperature is sufficiently smooth, determines the current as a functional of the averages of all the conserved charge densities
\langle q_{k}(x,t)\rangle
,

\displaystyle\langle j_{i}(x,t)\rangle=j_{i}[\vec{q}](x,t)+\frac{\delta j_{i}}{\delta\beta^{j}}\bigg|_{[\vec{q}]}\partial_{x}\langle q_{j}(x,t)\rangle+\dots\,.

(3)

At zeroth order,
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{0})
, this yields Euler GHD, and at
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1})
a Navier–Stokes-type correction associated with diffusive GHD can be found
De Nardis et al. 2018
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018
.
Importantly, Euler GHD in the presence of external trapping potentials lacks any mechanism for thermalisation (which is expected as the underlying integrability of the system is broken) and requires the presence of the Navier–Stokes correction before any thermalisation can be observed
Bastianello et al. 2020
;
Durnin et al. 2021b
;
Biagetti et al. 2024
.

Although diffusive GHD successfully captures certain integrability-breaking relaxation dynamics, the Navier–Stokes correction determined from the hydrodynamic expansion implies that no long-range correlations can develop between the fluid cells.
This is valid at very short times, within the linear-response regime; however, at intermediate times long-range correlations are known to develop
Doyon et al. 2023a
;
Doyon et al. 2023b
.
These transient long-range correlations can be derived using the cumulant expansion, as described in Ref.
Hübner et al. 2025
;
Hübner et al. 2025
. There, it was shown that typically integrable systems fail to locally equilibrate, invalidating the applicability of the hydrodynamic states (
2
).
Therefore the correct equilibrium states are those that retain non-trivial information about two-point functions,

\rho_{\text{LR}}=Z^{-1}\,\exp\!\Bigl[-\ell\!\int\!dx\,\beta_{(1)}^{i}(x)\,q_{i}(x)\\
-\ell\!\int\!dx\,dx^{\prime}\,\beta_{(2)}^{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\,E_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\Bigr],

(4)

where
E_{ij}(x,x^{\prime})\sim\ell^{-1}
are the regularised (namely, with the divergent part in
x=x^{\prime}
subtracted out) two-point correlations of the hydrodynamic charges; namely, the dynamical correlation functions are composed of a local
\delta
-peaked component and a regular long-range part,

\displaystyle S_{ij}(x,y,t)=\delta(x-y)C_{ij}(x,t)+E_{ij}(x,y,t).

(5)

Using the local-equilibrium states (
4
) one can expand the current to include
1/\ell
corrections and derive the hydrodynamic equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{i}+A^{k}_{i}\partial_{x}q_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2\ell}\frac{\delta^{2}j_{i}}{\delta q_{r}\delta q_{k}}\bigg|_{\vec{q}}E^{\text{sym}}_{rk}(x)\right)

(6)

\displaystyle\overset{t\ll 1}{\approx}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell}\,\partial_{x}\!\left(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\;k}\,\partial_{x}q_{k}\right),

with the point-splitting defined as
E^{\text{sym}}_{ij}(x)\equiv(E_{ij}(x^{+},x^{-})+E_{ij}(x^{-},x^{+}))/2
.
At intermediate times the regular correlation must be evolved simultaneously, according to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{ij}+\partial_{x}(A_{i}^{\;k}E_{kj})+\partial_{y}(E_{ik}A_{j}^{\;k})

\displaystyle=-\delta(x-y)\,\langle j^{-}_{h},q_{i},q_{j}\rangle C^{hk}\partial_{x}q_{k}.

(7)

Evolution at the diffusive scale from an initial locally equilibrated GGE state thus requires the long-range correlation functions to be evolved simultaneously, making the dynamics substantially more complicated than those of Euler GHD.

It is often convenient to recast the hydrodynamic description in terms of quasiparticles instead of local charges.
A charge density
q_{i}(x)
can be written in terms of the quasiparticle density
\rho(\theta,x)
as

q_{i}(x)=\int d\theta\,h_{i}(\theta)\,\rho(\theta,x),

(8)

where the form factors
\{h_{i}(\theta)\}
form a complete basis, so that Eq. (
8
) is invertible.
Quasiparticles may be regarded as travelling wave packets (see the following sections) whose group velocity is a continuous function of the rapidity
\theta
,
v^{\text{bare}}(\theta)
.
Their dynamics involve only elastic two-body scattering events, characterised by the phase shift
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
.
The bare velocity
v^{\text{bare}}(\theta)
, together with the bare momentum
k(\theta)
and the scattering phase
\varphi(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, fully specifies the microscopic model at hand.

In the quasiparticle representation, the naive Navier–Stokes equation reads

\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}+\partial_{x}(v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{x}\bigl[\mathfrak{D}\,\partial_{x}\rho\bigr]_{\theta},

(9)

where the effective velocity and the diffusion constant are given in terms of the local quasiparticle density,

\displaystyle v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{\text{bare}}(\theta)+\int d\mu\,\mathfrak{a}_{\theta\mu}\,\rho_{\mu}\,(v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}-v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}),

(10)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\alpha\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{-t}_{\alpha\theta}\,\frac{\delta_{\theta\mu}\bigl[\int d\lambda\,W_{\lambda\theta}\bigr]-W_{\theta\mu}}{\rho_{T,\theta}\rho_{T,\mu}}\,R^{t}_{\mu\gamma},

(11)

\displaystyle W_{\theta\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}\,f_{\theta}\,\bigl[\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\theta\mu}\bigr]^{2}\bigl|v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}-v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}\bigr|,

(12)

with
R_{\alpha\theta}\equiv\delta_{\alpha\theta}-\varphi_{\alpha\theta}n_{\theta}
and the rescaled scattering shift
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta\mu}=2\pi\varphi_{\theta\mu}/k^{\prime}_{\theta}
.
Here
\rho_{T,\theta}=k^{\prime}(\theta)/(2\pi)+\int d\mu\,\varphi_{\theta\mu}\rho_{\mu}
is the total density,
g^{\text{dr}}_{\theta}=g_{\theta}+\int d\mu\,\varphi_{\theta\mu}n_{\mu}g^{\text{dr}}_{\mu}
denotes dressing,
n_{\theta}=\rho_{\theta}/\rho_{T,\theta}
is the occupation function, and
f_{\theta}=\{1,\,1-n_{\theta}\}
is the statistical factor (classical or Fermi-Dirac, respectively).
As specified above, the Navier–Stokes equation is only an approximation, valid at short times, whereas the correct diffusive dynamics are described by the two coupled equations (
6
) and (
II
), which, in quasiparticles, read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}+\partial_{x}(v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{x}\!\bigl(H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}\,E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\bigr),

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(A_{\theta}^{\;\alpha}E_{\alpha\theta^{\prime}})+\partial_{x^{\prime}}(A_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}E_{\theta\alpha})

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}\,\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha},

with repeated indices integrated.
The tensor operators are

\displaystyle A_{\theta}^{\;\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{-t}_{\theta\mu}\,v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha},

(15)

\displaystyle M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{-t}_{\theta\mu}R^{-t}_{\theta^{\prime}\gamma}\,\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\mu\gamma}\,\tfrac{\rho_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}}{\rho_{T,\mu}}\,(v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}-v^{\text{eff}}_{\gamma})\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha}\bigr]_{(\theta,\theta^{\prime})},

(16)

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\,(v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}-v^{\text{eff}}_{\gamma})\,\tfrac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\gamma}}\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha}R^{t}_{\gamma\beta}\bigr]_{(\alpha,\beta)},

(17)

where
[\bullet]_{(\theta,\theta^{\prime})}
denotes the sum over permutations of the indices
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
.
The inclusion of two-point correlations is crucial for accurately capturing hydrodynamic evolution up to diffusive time scales.
As demonstrated in Ref.
Hübner et al. 2025
;
Hübner et al. 2025
,
the Navier–Stokes equation applies only in the linear-response regime
—i.e. for hydrodynamic evolution of initial states weakly perturbed from equilibrium—
or at very short times
, when the long-range part
E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})
can be neglected.
However,
in generic systems that support ballistic modes—provided the mode velocity depends non-linearly on the local state—hydrodynamic evolution generates long-range correlations that do not decay in time
.
As argued below, the same remains true in the presence of weak integrability breaking, such as that induced by a trapping potential, making the inclusion of long-range correlations necessary in those cases as well.

III
Trap-induced integrability breaking and thermalisation

As shown in Refs.
Bastianello et al. 2020
;
Doyon and Yoshimura 2017
;
Durnin et al. 2021b
;
Biagetti et al. 2024
, starting from a generic integrable Hamiltonian
H_{0}
one may introduce a spatially inhomogeneous perturbation of the form

H=H_{0}+\int dx\,V(x)\,q_{i_{0}}(x),

(18)

where
q_{i_{0}}(x)
is an arbitrary conserved charge density and
V(x)
is a smooth external potential.

Under this perturbation quasiparticles not only drift in real space but also acquire a flow in rapidity (momentum) space.
Their current can be written as a two-dimensional vector in this space
Doyon and Yoshimura 2017
,

\mathbf{J}=\bigl(v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta},\,a^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta}\bigr),

(19)

where
a^{\rm eff}
is defined analogously as the effective velocity, bare velocity replaced by the
bare acceleration

a^{\text{bare}}=-h_{i_{0}}\,\partial_{x}V(x)
.
Because transport now proceeds in both real and rapidity space, the Euler–Navier–Stokes equation generalizes to

\partial_{t}\rho+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{J}=\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{D}}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\rho\bigr),\qquad\boldsymbol{\nabla}\equiv(\partial_{x},\partial_{\theta}),

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{D}}
denotes the rapidity-resolved diffusion tensor.

Equation (
20
) possesses the thermal local-density-approximation Gibbs ensemble as a fixed point of evolution, namely the thermal state modulated at each point by the external potential,

\rho_{\text{th}}(x)=Z^{-1}\,\exp\!\bigl[-\beta\,(H_{0}+V(x)\,q_{i_{0}}(x)-\mu N)\bigr].

(21)

Although a formal proof is still missing, it is widely believed to be the unique steady state, apart from exceptional choices of
V(x)
.
Accordingly, integrable systems subjected to external potentials are expected to thermalise on diffusive time scales—a prediction borne out numerically except for a few notable deviations in trapped hard-rod gases
Cao et al. 2018
;
Bagchi et al. 2023
.

However, recent developments have shown that the Navier–Stokes equation (
20
) is not correct on pre-thermalisation time scales
Biagetti et al. 2024
.
We therefore have to formulate the problem including two-point correlation functions.
Below we derive coupled evolution equations for the density and its connected two-point function.
They coincide with those in the previous section, but include the acceleration terms induced by the external force field

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}

\displaystyle+\partial_{x}(v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta})+\partial_{\theta}(a^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}\rho_{\theta})=

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{x}\!\bigl(H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{\theta}\!\bigl(H_{\theta}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha\beta}E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\partial_{x}(A_{\theta}^{\;\alpha}E_{\alpha\theta^{\prime}})+\partial_{x^{\prime}}(A_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}E_{\theta\alpha})+

(23)

\displaystyle+\partial_{\theta}(A_{\theta}^{\mathfrak{f},\gamma}E_{\gamma\theta^{\prime}})+\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}(A_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{f},\gamma}E_{\theta\gamma})=

\displaystyle=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\bigl(M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}+M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\bigr)\,.

The matrix definitions are

\displaystyle A_{\theta}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{-t}_{\theta\mu}\,a^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha},

(24)

\displaystyle M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{-t}_{\theta\mu}R^{-t}_{\theta^{\prime}\gamma}\,\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\mu\gamma}\,\tfrac{\rho_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}}{\rho_{T,\mu}}\,(a^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}-a^{\text{eff}}_{\gamma})\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha}\bigr]_{(\theta,\theta^{\prime})},

(25)

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\,(a^{\text{eff}}_{\mu}-a^{\text{eff}}_{\gamma})\,\tfrac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\gamma}}\,R^{t}_{\mu\alpha}R^{t}_{\gamma\beta}\bigr]_{(\alpha,\beta)}.

(26)

In Appendix
A
we explicitly derive Eq. (
22
) and (
23
) and show that, for uncorrelated initial states
E(t=0)=0
, in the small time limit
t\to 0^{+}
they reproduce Eq. (
20
).

Two remarks are necessary.
First, a faithful simulation of the system requires evolving the fully inhomogeneous correlation function, which is numerically challenging.
Second, one should ask about the fate of thermalisation in this system of coupled equations.
We cannot provide a definitive answer here and leave it as an open question.
However, since there is no term that dissipates correlations, the system cannot thermalise at order
O(\ell^{2})
, contrary to what the Navier–Stokes equation would suggest.
In fact, full thermalisation of the two-point functions appears only at dispersive order in the hydrodynamic expansion, i.e. on time scales
\sim O(\ell^{3})
.
More precisely, the relaxation of the two-point function involves three-point functions
Biagetti et al. 2025
and can be captured by a diffusive contribution proportional to

\partial_{t}E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}\propto H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta\theta^{\prime}}(x^{+},x^{-},x^{\prime})+\dots,

(27)

where
E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta\theta^{\prime}}
is the three-point function, symmetrised with respect to the first two spatial variables.
In this hierarchical structure, the three-point function
E^{\text{sym}}_{\alpha\beta\theta^{\prime}}
is expected to evolve through an equation coupled to the first two layers of the hierarchy.
The resulting cascade of correlations is clearly too complex to numerically simulate, the following section provides a numerical method that circumvents the problem by simulating the effective wave-packet gas, which provides the correct discretisation and contains by definition the correct correlations’ dynamics.

IV
Wave packet gas description of GHD

As we have seen in the previous section, a complete solution of the Generalized Hydrodynamics (GHD) including diffusive effects that is capable of describing a system’s thermalisation would require solving two coupled equations, one of which describes two-point functions. From a numerical standpoint, the two-point function is quite a complex object. Therefore, especially to go beyond the realm of strictly integrable systems and to be able to efficiently incorporate integrability-breaking perturbations, it is desirable to introduce a particle model whose averages over realizations coincide with those of integrable models in the hydrodynamic limit.

Following recent works,
Doyon et al. 2024
;
Doyon et al. 2022
;
Bonnemain et al. 2025
;
Aggarwal 2025
;
Doyon and Hübner 2023
we introduce the following gas of wave packets (WPG) for this purpose. First, we observe that for a simple model such as the repulsive hard rods, the wave packets coincide with the rods themselves. For a generic interacting integrable model, the WPG is simply described by a gas of interacting solitonic-like particles that scatter via the model-dependent scattering shift. While such an effective gas of particles was already introduced in
Doyon et al. 2018b
as the “flea gas,” it is crucial to impose the correct dynamics on the particles in order to capture hydrodynamic fluctuations at all orders. This method generalizes a similar approaches already employed for evolving the repuslive hard rod gas to more general settings and models.

IV.1
The classical hard rods gas

To elucidate our method, we consider a classical hard rod gas, consisting of either attractive or repulsive particles. In the repulsive case this is the most familiar form of a hard rod gas
Lebowitz et al. 1968
;
Percus 1969
;
Boldrighini et al. 1983
;
Spohn 1991
;
Boldrighini and Sukhov 1997
;
Pozsgay et al. 2021
, whose extended particles have a finite length
a>0
that are unable to overlap and instead bounce off of one another exchanging momenta whenever their edges come into contact. In the attractive case the particles all have regions around their center such that all neighbors within a distance
|a|
away are pulled into the center, as a consequence when a collision occurs these attractive particles will stick together for a period of time based on the relative speed of the colliding particles. The position of the
i
-th particle,
x_{i}
, is transformed to a corresponding bare particle position,
X_{i}
, by use of the transformation

\displaystyle X_{i}=x_{i}\mp\frac{a}{2}\sum_{k}{\rm sign}(x_{i}-x_{k}).

(28)

with the sign corresponding to repulsive (
-
) and attractive (
+
) case, respectively, and where the bare particles coordinates are given by free ballistic propagation

X_{i}(t)=X_{i}(0)+\theta_{i}\ t.

(29)

For the repulsive case this transformation is easily inverted, since the bare particle positions
\{X_{i}\}
and WPG
\{x_{i}\}
coordinates are identically ordered, which implies
{\rm sign}(x_{i}-x_{k})={\rm sign}(X_{i}-X_{k})
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle x_{i}=X_{i}+\frac{a}{2}\sum_{k}{\rm sign}(X_{i}-X_{k})\quad({\rm repulsive}\,\,{\rm HR})\,.

(30)

For attractive hard rods, a naive inversion will result in cases with bare coordinates
X_{i}<X_{k}
and interacting coordinates
x_{i}>x_{k}
differently ordered whenever particles are too close together. Additional care is thus needed here to keep the mapping consistent. As a resolution, whenever wave packets sit too close to one another they merge into a single position in an example of clustering. This behavior is also observed for more general WPGs, where the trajectories stick together before separating at a later time.

While the dynamics given by (
28
) is deterministic, one then needs to sample over an ensemble of initial states in order to describe a thermodynamic state. However the advantage of working with (
28
) is crucial: one does not need to know or use the invariant or thermal distribution of interacting rods, but one can simply initialise the bare particles variables
X_{i},\theta_{i}
, whose statistics is known to be simple Poissoinian, and then perform the mapping to interacting coordinates. We shall show below the algorithm to efficiently sample generic classical hard rods particles.

To make use of the transformation for attractive hard rods the transformation must be inverted, a gradient transform can be implemented; however, for the attractive hard rods a series of inequalities can also be used to determine the interacting positions from the bare coordinates. The key to this method is the introduction of a naive transformation, where
{\rm sign}(x_{i}-x_{j})\to{\rm sign}(X_{i}-X_{j})
, recalled to be the correct inversion for repulsive hard rods, although generally incorrect in the attractive case. In addition to this naive transformation, the ordering of the bare positions
X_{j}<X_{k}
implies
x_{j}\leq x_{k}
when
x_{j}=x_{k}
these particles are said to be within a cluster. With this in mind it is possible to identify the positions of clusters by finding where this weak ordering fails after a naive transformation.

Concretely, the bare coordinates
X_{I_{1}}<\dots<X_{I_{m}}
belonging to a single cluster are ordered as
I_{1}<\dots<I_{m}
. Surprisingly, but usefully, the average position of clustered particles in both the true and naive transformations are identical. As a consequence the naive transformation implies the following condition on the first,
x^{\rm naive}_{I_{1}}
, and last particle positions,
x^{\rm naive}_{I_{m}}
, within a cluster of
m
-particles relative to the true cluster position
x_{I}

\displaystyle x^{\rm naive}_{I_{m}}<x_{I}<x^{\rm naive}_{I_{1}}.

(31)

This condition can be used to group the particles into clusters by hand, which effectively inverts the transformation for the attractive hard rod gas. With this approach numerical solutions could be reproduced more quickly and stably for the attractive hard rod gas.

IV.2
Generic integrable models and quantum statistics

Generic integrable systems can be viewed as deformations of the hard-rod gas.
In particular, they are characterised by effective quasi particles whose large-scale generalised hydrodynamics coincide with that of hard rods under the substitution

a\;\longrightarrow\;\mathfrak{a}_{ij}=\frac{2\pi\,\varphi(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})}{k^{\prime}(\theta_{i})}.

(32)

A relevant example is provided by the Lieb-Liniger model, which describe interacting (quantum) bosonic atoms
Lieb and Liniger 1963
;
Cazalilla 2004
;
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Bouchoule and Dubail 2022
with Hamiltonian given by

H=\sum_{j}\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2m}+2c\sum_{i<j}\delta(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(33)

and whose associated scattering shift is
\mathfrak{a}_{ij}=2c/((\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})^{2}+c^{2})
.

The hard-rod mapping of Eq. (
28
) then generalises to generic models, relating interacting and bare particle coordinates through

\boxed{X_{i}\;=\;x_{i}\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathfrak{a}_{ik}\,\mathrm{sign}(x_{i}-x_{k}),\quad\quad\dot{X}_{i}=v^{\rm bare}(\theta_{i}),}

(34)

where the bare velocity parametrization
v^{\rm bare}(\theta)
can account for different types of models (in generic Galilean invariant models one has typically
v^{\rm bare}(\theta)=\theta
).
A crucial difference compared to the hard rods mapping is that it cannot be inverted explicitly. Therefore, in order to invert the transformation a convex surface defined by the phase space position of each particle,
(\vec{X},\vec{\theta})
, is introduced such that the interacting positions are those positions that maximize the surface

\displaystyle S_{\vec{X},\vec{\theta}}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{(x_{i}-X_{i})^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\mathfrak{a}_{ij}\,|x_{i}-x_{j}|,

\displaystyle\vec{x}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm arg\,min}\,S_{\vec{X}(t),\vec{\theta}}(\vec{x}).

(35)

Numerically, a gradient descent method is capable of carrying out this minimization, thus identifying a set of interacting coordinates from a set of bare coordinates. By inverting this formula an initial fluctuating state of bare particles is mapped to an initial fluctuating state of a general quantum integrable system. Averages over these initial states reproduce the correct expectation values and correlation functions.

It should be mentioned that there has been a previous implementation of particle GHD realization in the past. In Ref.
Doyon et al. 2018b
the authors introduced the so-called flea gas algorithm, where hard rods-like particles scatter when they find themselves at distance
\mathfrak{a}_{ij}
. However such an algorithm does not properly treat multi-scattering processes and, even if it gives the correct average effective velocities
Mestyán and Alba 2020
;
Doyon et al. 2018b
;
Caux et al. 2019
, it fails to give the correct diffusive terms as both initial fluctuations and correct dynamics are not properly described.

The WPG dynamics of Eq. (
34
) obviously reproduces the correct effective velocity, defined as
t^{-1}\langle x_{i}\rangle=v^{\rm eff}(\theta_{i})
at large times, and since the WPG contains accurate initial fluctuations it also reproduces additional hydrodynamic effects such as the diffusive spreading of quasiparticles, namely the quantity
\delta x_{i}=t^{-1}\langle(x_{i}-\langle x_{i}\rangle)^{2}\rangle
at large times, see Fig.
2
. The latter is known to be related to the diffusion constant given by
Nardis et al. 2019
;
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018

\delta x_{\theta}=\int d\alpha\rho_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}|v_{\theta}^{\rm eff}-v^{\rm eff}_{\alpha}|\left(\frac{\varphi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\rm dr}}{\rho_{T,\theta}}\right)^{2},

(36)

which clearly depends, through the factor
f
, on the statistics of the initial fluctuations, which can be chosen to be either quantum or classical. Below we sketch the particle sampling procedure for both classical or Fermi-Dirac statistics.

Classical Procedure for Sampling

1.

Identify a target density for a particle species,
\rho^{(s)}
, along with a fixed length
L
for the interacting model, such as a hard rod gas (HR). From this given target density, the bare particle density
\rho^{\rm bare}
is determined as

\displaystyle\rho^{(s)}_{\rm bare}=\frac{\rho^{(s)}}{2\pi\rho^{(s)}_{T}}=\frac{n^{(s)}}{2\pi}

(37)

with
\rho^{(s)}_{T}
being the total density of states.

2.

Initialize a bare coordinate system for the particle species index
s
, initially independent of the other particle species.

3.

For each coordinate system, labeled by a particle species
s
, draw a distance
r_{i}
from an exponential distribution weighted by the integrated bare density,

\displaystyle P^{(s)}(r)\sim e^{-r\int d\theta\,\rho^{(s)}_{\rm bare}(\theta)},

(38)

a procedure known as a Poisson point process. A bare particle is then placed a distance
r_{i}
away from the previously placed particle, or the origin.

4.

Assign a momentum to the newly placed bare particle by sampling the distribution of momenta for the initial state.

5.

Transform from bare coordinates to interacting ones by inverting Eq. (
34
). Break the loop if the transformed position of the new particle is outside the system size
L
; if not, return to step 2.

6.

Once all particle species have been placed combine all the independent coordinates onto a single amalgamated bare particle coordinate system, which will consist of many species. Transform this amalgamated set of bare coordinates to interacting coordinates and truncate to the imposed system size
L
.

Quantum Procedure for Sampling

1.

Introduce a trapping potential so that outside of a fixed region there will be no particles.

2.

Here, the initial input will be the occupation function,
n(\theta,X)
, determined from TBA and determines the bare particle density as

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm bare}(\theta,X)=\frac{n(\theta,X)}{2\pi}.

(39)

Chop the bare phase space into a set of bins subject to the quantization condition
d\theta dX=2\pi
.

3.

Sample a random number
p_{ij}
, whenever
p_{ij}<n(\theta_{i},X_{j})
a particle is inserted into the corresponding phase space bin.

4.

Transform from bare coordinates to interacting ones by inverting Eq. (
28
). Re-bin the particles in the resulting interacting phase space according to the same discretization
d\theta dX=d\theta dx=2\pi
.

V
Generalised fluids (I): integrability breaking via external potentials

When the dynamics is given entirely by integrable Hamiltonian, the corresponding bare particles positions
X_{i}(t)
evolve freely. However, in the presence of an external potential as given by Eq. (
18
), these trajectories are now modified. The logic is simple: consider the interacting Hamiltonian and map it to bare particles using the bare-to-interacting mapping of Eq. (
34
). While this mapping makes the interacting Hamiltonian
H_{0}
trivial, it gives a new effective potential for the bare particles

H_{0}+\int\differential{x}V(x)q_{i_{0}}(x)\to\sum_{i}\Big[\frac{(v^{\rm bare}(\theta_{i}))^{2}}{2}+V(X_{i}(x_{\cdot}))\Big].

(40)

As the potential is computed at position
X_{i}(x_{\cdot})
we are required to know the position of the interacting particles
x_{i}
. The result is a system of interacting particles with an interaction mediated by the external potential.

As an example, let us consider the case of a repulsive hard rods gas in the presence of a quadratic external potential
V(x)=\omega^{2}x^{2}/2
. In this case, the Hamiltonian evolution can be directly mapped to an interacting Hamiltonian for the bare particles as

H_{\rm HR(a)}[X,\theta]=\sum_{i}\Big[\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\omega^{2}X_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{a\omega^{2}}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\Big]\,.

(41)

The latter corresponds to the Hamiltonian of a self-gravitating gas
Hohl and Campbell 1968
;
Rybicki 1971
;
Wright et al. 1982
;
Reidl and Miller 1988
;
Joyce and Worrakitpoonpon 2010
;
Teles et al. 2011
;
Roule et al. 2022
confined in a harmonic trap, whose equation of motions are written as

\dot{X}_{i}=\theta_{i}\quad,\quad\dot{\theta}_{i}=-\omega^{2}y_{i}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2a}\sum_{i\neq j}{\rm sgn}(X_{i}-X_{j})\,.

(42)

In an analogous way, in generic interacting models the equations of motion can be written via the mapping (
34
) but with the bare particles now satisfying the following equations of motions, whose trajectories are shown in Fig.
1
:

\boxed{\dot{X}_{i}=v^{\rm bare}(\theta_{i}),\quad\quad\dot{\theta}_{i}=-(\partial_{x}V(x))\bigg|_{x=x_{i}}.}

(43)

In particular, Eq. (
43
) shows the main complication of this approach: bare particles have a trivial kinetic spreading but they feel a complicated, state dependent, non-local force that is a functional of the interacting particles coordinates.
Hence, it requires transforming between the bare and interacting coordinate systems for each time step and represents the main computational bottleneck of the numerical algorithm. This phenomenon resembles the intrinsic many-body complexity of interacting integrable systems.

It is important now to stress that, although the single realization of microscopic time evolution is fully deterministic, the average over the initial state ensemble introduces the statistical complexity through the initial state fluctuations, that are transported through the dynamics according to Eq. (
23
).
Hence, this method is not only capable of reproducing the Euler evolution of the gas, but in principle any higher order correction to the hydrodynamics.
On the other hand, since the
n-
th hydrodynamic corrections produces effects suppressed as
1/\ell^{n-1}
, the expected number of initial states over which perform the average is expected to grow as
\sim\ell^{2(n-1)}
in order to be able to observe the effect of
n-
th term in the hydrodynamic expansion.
Thus, to show that this method reproduces the correct diffusive order hydrodynamics, we focus on two toy models of WPG: attractive and repulsive hard rods, for which the mapping can be more easily inverted.
More precisely, in Fig.
3
, we compare diffusive hydrodynamics obtained from WPG simulations with
Navier–Stokes (
20
) for the evolution in the periodic potential defined in Eq. (
44
). As expected
Biagetti et al. 2024
,
for both attractive and repulsive HR the WPG simulations match almost perfectly with the diffusive equation at small times
t/\ell<1
, i.e. when Eq. (
20
) is expected to be predictive. This must be considered as a non trivial benchmark that confirms the validity of the numerical method.

V.1
Simulating trapped integrable systems: discussion on the results

We shall now use the methods introduced in the previous section to study the dynamics of integrable systems in the presence of external traps.
We consider here, for convenience, the case of attractive and repulsive hard rods
, with scattering shift
\mathfrak{a}_{ij}=\pm 1
and with classical statics. The attractive hard-rod model can be considered a toy model for the Lieb–Liniger gas at high temperature. We start by considering particles in a cosine potential

V(x)=V_{0}\cos\!\left(2\pi x/\ell\right).

(44)

This potential was also considered in previous works
Bastianello et al. 2020
;
Biagetti et al. 2024
, where it was concluded that the system thermalises on, diffusive, time scales proportional to
(\ell/V_{0})^{2}
. Moreover, it was remarked in Ref.
Biagetti et al. 2024
that diffusive hydrodynamics with Navier–Stokes (
20
) can capture only the initial Euler-like dynamics and the final thermalisation dynamics, while there is an intermediate time window in which the dynamics are strongly discontinuous (such region was denoted as turbulent phase) and where hydrodynamics and exact simulation do not fully agree. Such an effect is enhanced by larger values of
\ell
which prolong such turbulent phase in time. Here, see Fig.
3
, we show that for both the repulsive and attractive regimes the distribution of rapidities
\rho(\theta,x)
converges to the expected thermal distribution (which is also the fixed point of the Navier-Stokes GHD equation Eq. (
20
), see
Biagetti et al. 2024
), namely a Gaussian (thermal) function,

\rho(\theta,x)\mathrel{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{t\sim\ell^{2}}}e^{-\beta\bigl(\theta^{2}+V(x)-\mu\bigr)},

(45)

up to very small deviations. The analysis of the two-point correlations instead reveals a different picture. We here focus on two different types of connected correlations, the density-density
\langle N(x)N(y)\rangle^{c}
and energy-energy
\langle e(x)e(y)\rangle^{c}
, namely

\begin{split}E_{0,0}&=\int d\theta d\theta^{\prime}E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(x,y)=\langle N(x)N(y)\rangle^{c}_{{\rm reg}},\\
E_{2,2}&=\int d\theta d\theta^{\prime}\theta^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}E_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(x,y)=\langle e(x)e(y)\rangle^{c}_{\rm reg},\end{split}

(46)

where for regularised correlations
\langle{\cdot}\rangle_{\rm reg}
we mean that they can be obtained by binning the microscopic system with some mesoscopic space interval, (in this paper we take quite a large one to improve statistics,
dx=3\ell\sim 30
), and we exclude the singular contribution at
x=y
.
We observe that as the system evolves these correlations develop a finite long-range part (namely, particles correlate over multiple units of
\ell
) and,
even when the one-point function appears thermal, such correlations remain finite, signaling that the state is not thermal but is instead a long-range state of the type (
4
)
. Said otherwise, true thermalisation would imply a correlation length of the order of the thermal correlation length, which is
O(1)
, whereas here we observe correlation lengths of order
O(10\,\ell)
, which therefore signals that the system has not properly reached thermal equilibrium even at times of order
\ell^{2}
. As specified in the previous section, one should therefore expect an even larger time scale of order
\ell^{3}
in order, at least in principle, to see two-point functions vanish. Notice that such an effect is clearly due to inter-particle interactions, as in the limit of vanishing interaction one would have only independent trajectories inside the potential.

We then move to the case of harmonic and quartic traps, namely with
V(x)=\omega^{2}x^{2}/2
and
V(x)=\omega^{4}x^{4}/4
. To ensure extensive energy, the trap frequency is scaled as
\omega=\omega_{0}/\ell
and
\ell=N,
which corresponds to weak-integrability breaking. We first study the repulsive
(\mathfrak{a}=-1)
and attractive hard rod
(\mathfrak{a}=+1)
gases prepared in an initial Bragg state, as shown in Figs.
4
, and released into the respective traps. For the harmonic trap, the one-point function struggles to thermalise, a phenomenon that was well discussed in previous works
Cao et al. 2018
;
Bagchi et al. 2023
. Here we show that, while the distribution of rapidities fails to approach a Gaussian, even the two-point correlations remain finite and quite large at times of order
\ell^{2}
. Meanwhile, in the quartic case, there are some indications that at times larger than
\ell^{2}
both the one-point function and the correlations will thermalise. This seems consistent with the results of Ref.
Bagchi et al. 2023
for the quartic potential, where due to strong integrability breaking (
\omega\sim\omega_{0}
) the one-point function approaches a Gaussian at
t\sim O(N)
. Our work extends these findings by probing the two-point correlations–unexplored in previous studies–which decay very slowly.

Figure 5:
Same as in Fig.
4
with the only difference in the initial state preparation: we initialize the system in a thermal state in the interval
[-\ell,\ell]
with temperature

and

, i.e. homogeneous in space and with Gaussian rapidity distribution.
Then, at time
t=0

are removed and we evolve the two chopped clouds.

To amplify and elucidate the role of these correlations, we implement a second protocol (Fig.

): starting with a Gaussian rapidity distribution and uniform spatial density, we remove the particles from the center of the system, creating a Newton’s cradle setup, but which can be more efficient realizable experimentally. Strikingly, both harmonic and quartic traps now exhibit a non-Gaussian rapidity distribution and long-range two-point correlations that fail to vanish even at time-scales that are one order of magnitude more than diffusive ones.

Comparisons of the pure trap to the chop protocol make it clear that the two-point correlations are clearly modified between the two settings and that are strong enough to be detectalbe in current state-of-the-art experimental platforms. Particularly notable is the very large energy-energy correlator, which clearly supports the idea that there are preferred initial states for investigating these long-range correlators. Therefore, testing different initial states could be used to identify promising experimental protocols that could in turn be realized in cold atom experiments to investigate the emergence of these long-range correlations.

The approach introduced in the previous sections allows us to tackle more complex integrability-breaking potentials.
In this section, we consider integrable systems perturbed by two-body interactions, a scenario motivated by recent cold-atom experiments.
For example, in
Tang et al. 2018
, a gas of ultracold bosonic dysprosium atoms is tightly confined in one-dimensional tubes formed by a two-dimensional optical lattice.
These atoms exhibit dipole–dipole interactions (DDI) roughly 100 times stronger than those of rubidium.
Whereas ultracold bosons usually interact locally and are well described by the integrable Lieb–Liniger model, the DDI introduces a long-range, two-body, integrability-breaking interaction that drives late-time thermalization of the gas, namely the system is described by the Hamiltonian

H=H_{0}+\sum_{i}V(x_{i})+\sum_{i<j}U(x_{i}-x_{j})

(47)

where
H_{0}
is the integrable Lieb-Liniger one.
As shown in
Biagetti et al. 2025
, the hydrodynamic evolution of such a quasi-integrable gas is governed by a hierarchy of nonlinear integro-differential equations, analogous to the BBGKY hierarchy.
Under the assumptions of weak interactions and a large hydrodynamic scale
\ell
, this hierarchy can be closed, but its numerical solution remains computationally prohibitive.
We now illustrate how the numerical method developed here can efficiently simulate the hydrodynamics of these systems.

Consider the long-range potential

U(x-x^{\prime})\;=\;\frac{U_{0}}{\ell}\biggl[1+\bigl(\tfrac{|x-x^{\prime}|}{\ell}\bigr)^{3}\biggr]^{-1},

(48)

written in interacting coordinates and evolved via the bare-particle mapping.
Our wave-packet gas (WPG) algorithm applies directly, requiring only the coordinate transformations between bare and interacting frames.
In the presence of an external trap
V(x)
, the equations of motion become

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=v^{\rm bare}(\theta_{i}),

(49)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\bigl.\partial_{x}V(x)\bigr|_{x=x_{i}}\;-\;\sum_{k\neq i}\bigl.\partial_{x}U(x)\bigr|_{x=x_{i}-x_{k}}.

(50)

In bare coordinates, the two-body integrability-breaking potential acquires dependence on the interacting-particle positions, yielding a
1/\ell
expansion of many-body interactions in which the
n
-body term scales as
1/\ell^{n-2}
.

As an illustrative example, Fig.
6
shows the dynamics of an attractive WPG with
\mathfrak{a}_{ij}=c
, interacting via the potential (
48
).
This toy model approximates the high-temperature limit of a DDI-interacting Lieb–Liniger gas.
Starting from a Bragg-pulse initial state, we track the density and connected correlations.
The kurtosis of the rapidity distribution relaxes rapidly on a time scale set by the integrability-breaking strength.
After this fast relaxation, the density reaches a quasi-steady plateau and then drifts slowly toward the thermal distribution.
Simultaneously, intermediate-time correlations build up and relax to a correlation length of order
\ell
.
Since the true thermal state exhibits correlations of the same range, Fig.
6
confirms that the system indeed thermalizes.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown that the semiclassical wave-packet formulation of GHD
Doyon et al. 2024
;
Doyon et al. 2022
;
Bonnemain et al. 2025
;
Aggarwal 2025
;
Doyon and Hübner 2023
provides a transparent and efficient algorithm for numerically evolving both classical and quantum integrable systems, including all orders of fluctuating hydrodynamics.
Importantly, our method can incorporate arbitrary integrability-breaking terms.
On one hand, it overcomes the challenge of solving the full hydrodynamic hierarchy for a quasi-integrable model, yielding all-order hydrodynamics efficiently; on the other, it embeds generic perturbations—many-body interactions, particle loss, or impurity scattering—simply by adding the corresponding forces to the bare-particle dynamics.

Motivated by recent cold-atom experiments, we applied the WPG algorithm to two paradigmatic scenarios: an external periodic potential (Eq. (
44
)) and a long-range dipolar two-body interaction.
In the periodic trap, at times
O((\ell/V_{0})^{2})
the density relaxes to a nearly Gaussian form, consistent with previous findings
Biagetti et al. 2024
, however, correlations reveal persistent far from thermal long-range terms that are built-up at intermediate time-scales and that fail to dissipate at large times.
According to Eq. (
27
), two-point functions are expected to relax on the much longer scale
\ell^{3}
. On the other hand,
for polynomial traps, in agreement with Refs.
Cao et al. 2018
;
Bagchi et al. 2023
, the rapidity distribution remains non-Gaussian and long-range correlations persist for even longer times, indicating that true thermalization is still yet to be reached. The final reason for such a failure of dissipation of correlations is left for future works, even if the bare-to-interacting mapping shown here seems to point to the fact that the integrable anyway the weakly mixing nature or trajectories of the associated bare particles may play an important role.
Moreover, varying initial states strongly affect the generation of long-range correlations, suggesting that tailored initial conditions could enable experimental protocols to probe correlation dynamics in cold-atom setups.

Our findings raise new questions about the role of long-range many-body correlations in the relaxation of quasi-integrable systems, particularly regarding the absence of thermalization in harmonic confinement.
They also open exciting directions for studying nearly integrable systems with diverse integrability-breaking mechanisms, potentially modeling realistic features of cold-atom experiments.

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}v^{\rm eff}_{\theta}}{\delta\rho_{\alpha}\delta\rho_{\beta}}

A.2
Limit of small times and comparison with Navier-Stokes eq. (
20
)

In this section we derive the diffusive equation for density of quasiparticles emerging at initial time and we show that this is equivalent with the result of
Durnin et al. 2021b
.
More precisely, we consider uncorrelated local equilibrium initial states, i.e. with
E_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime};t=0)=0
.
Firsly, we solve Eq. (
65
) in normal modes
E^{(n)}
, reading

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(R^{-t}E^{(n)}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}R^{-1}\big)+\Big[\partial_{x}(R^{-t}v^{\rm eff}_{\theta}E^{(n)}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}R^{-1})+

\displaystyle+\partial_{\theta}(R^{-t}a^{\rm eff}_{\theta}E^{(n)}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}R^{-1})+(x,\theta\leftrightarrow x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\Big]=

\displaystyle=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\bigl(M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\;\alpha}\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}+M_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\bigr)\,,

(69)

where in this symbolic notation the rotation matrices are acting from left and right on the inner object.
The latter equation can be solved in the small time limit through the following ansatz

E^{(n)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime},t\to 0^{+})=\frac{1}{2}K^{(I)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\text{sgn}(x-x^{\prime})+\\
+\frac{1}{2}K^{(II)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\text{sgn}(x-x^{\prime}+\Delta v^{\rm eff}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}t)\,.

(70)

where
K^{(I)}
and
K^{(II)}
are two time evolving functions.
This solution represent the small ballistic evolution of initial fluctuation through the characteristics, that for
t\to 0^{+}
are not perturbed by the force (the acceleration is suppressed as
t^{2}
).

In particular, the initial time condition imposes
K^{(II)}=-K^{(I)}
. For small times, this ansatz exactly solves the r.h.s of (
A.2
). In particular, we find

\displaystyle K^{(I)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta v^{\rm eff}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}}\Big[\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}\tfrac{\rho_{\theta}^{\prime}f_{\theta}^{\prime}}{\rho_{T,\theta^{\prime}}}\Delta v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}R^{t}_{\theta\alpha}\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}+

(71)

\displaystyle+\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}\tfrac{\rho_{\theta}^{\prime}f_{\theta}^{\prime}}{\rho_{T,\theta^{\prime}}}\Delta a^{\text{eff}}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}R^{t}_{\theta\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}+(\theta\leftrightarrow\theta^{\prime})\Big]\,.

Hence, we can now write the initial time regularized correlation as

R^{t}E^{\rm sym}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}(x^{+},x^{-};t\to 0^{+})R=\\
=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{sgn}(\Delta v^{\rm eff}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}})}{\Delta v^{\rm eff}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}}\Big[\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}\frac{\rho_{\theta^{\prime}}f_{\theta^{\prime}}}{\rho_{T,\theta^{\prime}}}\big(\Delta v^{\text{eff}}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}R^{t}_{\theta\alpha}\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}+\\

(72)

Plugging this solution into Eq. (
51
) we find four additive diffusive terms involving derivatives in space and rapidity. Firstly we consider the two terms coming from the multiplication with tensor
H

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{\alpha\beta}E^{\rm sym}_{\alpha,\beta}(t\to 0^{+})=

(73)

\displaystyle=R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\gamma}}|\Delta v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu,\gamma}|\big(\rho_{\mu}f_{\mu}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\mu}}R^{t}_{\gamma\alpha}-\mu\leftrightarrow\gamma\Big)\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\gamma}}\frac{\Delta a^{\text{eff}}_{\mu,\gamma}}{\text{sgn}(\Delta v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu,\gamma})}\big(\rho_{\mu}f_{\mu}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\mu}}R^{t}_{\gamma\alpha}-\mu\leftrightarrow\gamma\Big)\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}

where we used the definition of inverse rotation matrix

and where the factor

Similarly we now consider the two terms coming from the multiplication of

with the tensor
H^{\mathfrak{f}}

\displaystyle H_{\theta}^{\mathfrak{f},\alpha\beta}E^{\rm sym}_{\alpha,\beta}(t\to 0^{+})=

(74)

\displaystyle=R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\gamma}}\frac{\Delta a^{\text{eff}}_{\mu,\gamma}}{\text{sgn}(\Delta v^{\text{eff}}_{\mu,\gamma})}\big(\rho_{\mu}f_{\mu}\frac{\varphi^{\text{dr}}_{\gamma\mu}}{\rho_{T,\mu}}R^{t}_{\gamma\alpha}-\mu\leftrightarrow\gamma\Big)\partial_{x}\rho_{\alpha}

A.4

61
) and (
62
)

In this section we derive Eq. (
61
) and (
62
). In particular, Eq. (
61

\rho(x)
and

On the other hand, the argument to show (

\varphi
. In this section we specialize to Galilean invariant systems where the scattering shift depends only on the difference of rapidities
\varphi_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}=\varphi(\theta-\theta^{\prime})
and
\rho_{T,\theta}=1^{\rm dr}_{\theta}
.
We now derive the some useful relation for the rapidity derivatives. Firstly we compute the derivative of
the filling function
n_{\theta}

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}n_{\theta}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\theta}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\theta}}-\frac{n_{\theta}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\theta}}\varphi^{\rm dr}_{\theta,\gamma}1^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}n_{\gamma}\,,

\displaystyle(\delta_{\theta\gamma}+n_{\theta}\varphi^{\rm dr}_{\theta\gamma})1^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}n_{\gamma}=\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\theta}\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}n_{\theta}=\frac{R^{t}_{\theta\gamma}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\theta}}\partial_{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma}\,,

where in the first line we used
R^{-1}_{\theta\gamma}=\delta_{\theta,\gamma}+\varphi^{\rm dr}_{\theta\gamma}n_{\gamma}
and that
1^{\rm dr}_{\theta}=R^{-1}_{\theta\gamma}1_{\gamma}
.
We now can compute the derivative of a generic dressed function
f^{\rm dr}_{\theta}
, where
f_{\theta}
is a state independent function of rapidity

\partial_{\theta}f^{\rm dr}_{\theta}=R^{-1}_{\theta\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}+\varphi^{\rm dr}_{\theta\gamma}\frac{f^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}}R^{t}_{\gamma\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\,,

(84)

in which we mainly used the property
\partial_{\theta}\varphi_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}=-\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}\varphi_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}
and integration by parts. In particular, this crucially permits to compute the derivatives of the dressing function

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}R^{-t}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{-t}_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}+R^{-t}_{\theta\gamma}\frac{T^{\rm dr}_{\gamma\theta^{\prime}}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}}R^{t}_{\gamma\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}R^{-1}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}+T^{\rm dr}_{\theta,\gamma}\frac{R^{t}_{\gamma,\alpha}}{1^{\rm dr}_{\gamma}}\partial_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}R^{-1}_{\gamma,\theta^{\prime}}\,.

(86)

defined in Eq. (

\displaystyle(\partial_{\theta}+\partial_{\theta^{\prime}})B^{\mathfrak{f}}=R^{-t}_{\theta,\gamma}f_{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}(a^{\rm eff}_{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma})R^{-t}_{\gamma,\theta^{\prime}}+

(87)
