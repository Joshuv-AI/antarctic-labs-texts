Submucosal Pressure Ejection Endoscopic Dissection (SPEED): A New Technique in Increasing the Safety and Efficacy of ESD (With Video)
Authors: Tao Chen, Zhu Yun Leng, Ai Ping Xu, Jing Jing Lian, Hai Bin Zhang, Li Shen, Li Zhang, Mei Dong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe universal adoption of endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is limited by its technical demands, slow learning curve, long procedure time, and high risks of bleeding, muscular layer injury, and perforation. We developed the submucosal pressure ejection endoscopic dissection (SPEED) technique that combines pressure injection, incision, dissection, and coagulation and avoids frequent device exchange during ESD. We aimed to introduce this novel SPEED strategy and evaluate its therapeutic advantages for superficial gastrointestinal neoplasms.MethodsAltogether 985 patients who underwent ESD for early gastrointestinal tumors between January 2020 and April 2024 were included and divided into the SPEED group and the conventional ESD group. Clinical outcomes, including en bloc and R0 resection rates, resection time, intraprocedural application of hemostatic forceps, procedure‐related adverse events, and local recurrence, were evaluated.ResultsSPEED achieved en bloc and R0 resection rates of 99.85% and 99.40%, respectively. Compared with conventional ESD, SPEED was associated with a shorter median resection time (33 min vs. 57 min), less frequent intraprocedural application of hemostatic forceps (2.71% vs. 19.25%), a lower intraprocedural perforation rate (0.15% vs. 2.48%), and a lower post‐procedure hemorrhage rate (1.51% vs. 5.90%).ConclusionsWith the additional high‐pressure injection, the SPEED technique greatly improves ESD efficiency and ensures a clearer visual field during ESD, thereby reducing risk of complications. It efficiently improves the speed and safety of dissection during ESD.

Introduction

Although endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has great advantages in the resection of superficial gastrointestinal (GI) lesions, it remains significantly challenging for many endoscopists. Currently, universal adoption of ESD has been limited by its technical demands, slow learning curve, prolonged procedure time, and high risks of bleeding, muscular layer injury, and perforation. For instance, when assessing the potential barriers of the adoption of ESD in Canada, procedure time was at the forefront of the discussion by the surveyed endoscopists [1]. Moreover, to prevent bleeding and achieve an optimal operative visual field, intraoperative submucosal injection is commonly performed to lift mucosal tissues, and coagulation forceps are frequently adopted. However, in clinical practice, the operative visual field is often unsatisfactory, leading to incomplete resection and procedure‐related adverse events, such as hemorrhage and perforation.

To reduce the difficulty of ESD and solve the aforementioned problems, we developed a submucosal pressure ejection endoscopic dissection (SPEED) system that combines high‐pressure injection, incision, dissection, and coagulation. The SPEED system provides a novel strategy that reduces the need for frequent device exchange to facilitate an easier and smoother procedure. Particularly, for mucosal lesions with severe submucosal adhesions caused by inflammation, infiltration, or fibrosis, and even recurrent lesions, the SPEED system establishes good submucosal cushions by continuous injection of high‐pressure liquid water at different standard atmospheric pressures (ATM) to make the dissection layers clear, reduce the risk of perforation, and improve the success rate and safety of the procedure. Therefore, we hypothesized that SPEED could improve the efficiency of the procedure, shorten the resection time, and reduce the risks of procedure‐related adverse events. In this study, we introduced this novel SPEED system, aiming to evaluate its therapeutic advantages for superficial GI neoplasms, particularly in terms of procedural efficiency and adverse outcomes.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

In this retrospective cohort study, 985 consecutive patients who underwent SPEED or conventional ESD at Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China) between January 2020 and April 2024 were enrolled. Only those with histopathologically confirmed premalignant lesions or early carcinomas of the esophagus, stomach, or colorectum through pretreatment biopsies, with follow‐up data for at least one year after ESD were included for analysis. Exclusion criteria were: (a) patients with lesions < 2 cm or > 8 cm in diameter; and (b) those with incomplete follow‐up data for analysis. All the included patients underwent systematic evaluation of demographics, procedural outcomes (en bloc/R0 resection rate), and clinicopathological determinants of treatment efficacy. As both groups were comparable regarding baseline characteristics of both the patients and the lesions (Table 1), propensity score matching analysis was not conducted.

SPEED Procedure

All the SPEED procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists who had completed more than 300 standard ESD procedures. The primary steps of the SPEED procedure followed the protocols of ESD outlined in previous studies [2, 3]. The SPEED technique served distinct purposes depending on the target regions, as described below (Figure 1). For each procedure, marking dots were initially made approximately 3–5 mm away from the lesion. A resection knife with injection capability or a 23‐gauge needle was then used for submucosal injection of a premixed solution containing 5% saline (100 mL) and 0.4% indigo carmine (1 mL), with or without epinephrine (1 mg), followed by an incision along the marking dots. The submucosal tissues beneath the lesion were dissected. For different lesions, the processes of SPEED technique are demonstrated in Figure 2, Figures [Link], [Link], [Link], and Video S1. During each procedure, therapeutic endoscopes, including GIF‐Q260J, GIF‐H290T and PCF‐Q260JI (all from Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and resection knives with injection capabilities including the ORISE ProKnife series (M00519371; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA), and the micro‐knife series (MK‐T‐2‐195 or MK‐T‐4‐195; Micro‐Tech [Nanjing] Co. Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China), were used.

The varied infusion pressures could be simply generated by the electronic sensor‐based injection model of the Erbe water‐jet system (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) or manual injection mode, which enables rapid replenishment of the water cushion, facilitates tissue separation, and minimizes the risk of perforation. The cooling effect of the water flow helps reduce eschar formation and smoke production during cutting, thereby enhancing surgical precision. Furthermore, it supports vessel pretreatment and timely hemostasis, significantly reducing the risk of bleeding.

Outcomes, Definitions, and Follow‐Up Evaluation

En bloc resection was defined as a complete removal of the lesion in a single piece, without fragmentation. Complete resection referred to the confirmation by pathologists that both the lateral and basal margins of the tissue specimen were negative for tumor invasion. Resection time was defined as the time duration from the initial submucosal injection to removal of the lesion. Intra‐procedure application of hemostatic forceps was defined as any bleeding that required hemostasis using electrocoagulation forceps during the resection. Intraprocedural perforation was defined as an endoscopically visible full‐thickness defect with exposure of the extraluminal space, peritoneal cavity, or adjacent structures. While post‐procedure perforation was defined as free intraperitoneal, mediastinal, or retroperitoneal air attributable to the ESD procedure on plain radiography or computed tomography (CT) scan after completion of the procedure. Minor bleeding was treated with immediate coagulation and was not regarded as a complication [3]. Hemorrhage was identified by a decrease in hemoglobin greater than 20 g/L [4]. When there was hematemesis or melena, or fresh blood was continuously drained or aspirated from the nasogastric tube, post‐procedure hemorrhage was clinically identified. Post‐procedure infection was defined as the presence of pneumonia, pleural effusion, abdominal infection, or bacteremia, as detected by postoperative CT scan and laboratory test results, which required antimicrobial therapy [5, 6].

Endoscopic surveillance intervals were individualized according to lesion location, final histopathological diagnosis, resection status, and relevant guideline recommendations. Patients underwent endoscopic examination at least annually during the follow‐up period, which ranged from 1 to 5 years. Local recurrence referred to the detection of tumor cells at or near the original resection site during the follow‐up endoscopic examination. A comprehensive telephone interview was conducted 30 days after surgery or at least once within the first post‐procedure year to collect information on symptom relief, postoperative adverse events, and any examinations or interventions received at other healthcare facilities.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.13 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). Normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. As the continuous variables were not normally distributed, they were presented as median values with ranges and were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, and were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

For the analyses of binary outcomes, including intraprocedural use of hemostatic forceps and post‐procedure hemorrhage, univariable logistic regression analyses were initially performed. Variables with a p value < 0.05 in the univariable analyses were subsequently entered into the corresponding multivariable logistic regression models. Age and lesion size were dichotomized according to their median values. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. In addition, the factors associated with resection time were evaluated using multivariable linear regression, with unstandardized coefficients, standardized coefficients, and 95% CIs reported. The assumptions of the linear regression model were assessed by inspecting residual plots and Q–Q plots. All the statistical analyses were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the Patients and Lesions

Altogether 985 patients with a median age of 65 years (range 21–90 years) were enrolled for analysis, including 616 (62.54%) males and 369 (37.46%) females. Most lesions were located at the stomach (n = 434, 44.06%) and colorectum (n = 432, 43.86%), but less common in the esophagus (n = 119, 12.08%). The median tumor size was 3.5 cm (range 2.0–8.0 cm). Regarding the macroscopic type, most of the lesions were flat lesions, accounting for 80.00% (n = 788) of all cases. High‐grade dysplasia (HGD) was the most common histopathological diagnosis (n = 362, 36.75%), followed by adenocarcinoma (n = 265, 26.90%), low‐grade dysplasia (LGD) (n = 229, 23.25%), squamous carcinoma (n = 67, 6.8%), and others (n = 62, 6.29%) (Table S1). The SPEED and conventional ESD groups comprised 663 and 322 patients, respectively. The baseline clinicopathological characteristics did not differ between the two groups (all p > 0.05; Table 1).

Overall Clinical Outcomes and Complications

The overall ESD outcomes in our cohort demonstrated a high overall success rate (Table S2). En bloc resection and complete resection were achieved in 99.49% (980 of 985) and 99.19% (977 of 985) of all cases. Postoperative complications mainly included hemorrhage in 2.94% (29 of 985), perforation in 0.30% (3 of 985), and infection in 0.41% (4 of 985) of all patients. The median resection time was 38 min. During the follow‐up period, local recurrence was observed in 3 (0.30%).

Assessment of SPEED on Clinicopathological Outcomes

Compared to conventional ESD, SPEED achieved a higher en bloc resection rate (99.85% vs. 98.76%, p = 0.042) and a comparable complete resection rate (99.40% vs. 98.76%, p = 0.450). Notably, the SPEED system significantly shortened the resection time (33 min vs. 57 min, p < 0.001) and reduced the need for hemostatic forceps utility during the procedure (2.71% vs. 19.25%, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, postoperative outcomes of SPEED likewise improved, as it was associated with a lower rate of post‐procedure hemorrhage (1.51% vs. 5.90%, p < 0.001). While post‐procedure perforation and infection, as well as local recurrence rate, were comparable between the two groups (all p > 0.05; Table 2).

Discussion

ESD has become the gold standard for endoscopic removal of GI mucosal lesions, which has largely revolutionized the management of early GI neoplasia by enabling en bloc resection and precise histopathological assessment. However, its universal adoption has been limited especially for lesions located at anatomically complex regions such as the dentate line and gastric cardia, where inadequate submucosal lifting and dense vasculature are associated with increased risks of complications and prolonged procedure time [7, 8, 9]. Previous studies have reported concerning complication rates including procedure‐related bleeding (1.5%–11.9%) [10, 11] and perforation (0.22%–20.4%) [12, 13], highlighting the need for technical improvements. Although various strategies like the pocket‐creation method [14], saline immersion [15], and clip and rubber band triangular traction devices [16] have been developed to enhance ESD safety and efficiency, their implementation may require additional devices, specific expertise, or procedural modifications, and their availability varies among centers. Therefore, more accessible and efficient technical solutions are urgently needed.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of the SPEED technique by using high‐pressure liquid water injection during ESD to simplify the procedure and reduce the difficulties. This high‐pressure injection could be easily generated by an electronic sensor‐based injection model of the water‐jet system or a manual injection model (Video S2). When SPEED is performed using an injection‐capable knife, submucosal injection, dissection, and coagulation can be completed with the same device. Therefore, additional injection needles and hemostatic forceps are generally not required, except when injection or rescue hemostasis is clinically necessary. This reducing the need for accessory exchange and contributing to procedural efficiency. In addition, the special hemostatic function and effect of the SPEED technique, including satisfactory submucosal elevation, clear visualization of tissue layers, and effective hemostasis, ensure a good visual field during the operation, reduce the risks of complications such as massive hemorrhage and perforation, and greatly enhance the safety of the operation.

Our integrated SPEED system represents a significant technological advancement by combining submucosal high‐pressure injection, dissection, vessel precoagulation, and hemostasis into a single device, thereby resolving key limitations of conventional ESD. Rather than merely streamlining the workflow, SPEED changes the procedural strategy from a series of separate injection, dissection, and hemostatic steps to an integrated pressure‐guided approach. During the procedure, fluid injection is used dynamically to maintain or re‐establish the submucosal cushion, delineate the dissection plane, improve vessel visualization, and facilitate coagulation. Compared with conventional ESD, the superior hemostatic control achieved by SPEED is evidenced by significantly reduced intraprocedural hemostatic forceps application, which might be attributable to its dual mechanical–thermal mechanism combining pressurized water occlusion with immediate coagulation that enables precise bleeding point management without device exchange. Meanwhile, the advantage of SPEED in improving resection efficiency is likely through the following three synergistic mechanisms. First, continuous submucosal injection maintains optimal dissection planes through real‐time low‐volume irrigation, eliminating disruptive tool exchanges. Second, vessel‐specific precoagulation using tiered water‐jet pressures enables targeted vessel blanching before transection, which reduces or prevents interruptions for additional hemostasis. And third, continuous water irrigation mitigates thermal injury by reducing eschar formation and surgical smoke that typically obscure visualization [17, 18].

In addition, the clinical benefits of the SPEED system may extend to postoperative outcomes, which reduce post‐procedure hemorrhage risk through two key mechanisms, that is, precise vascular sealing via graded water pressure, which exposes and immediately coagulates bleeding points while decreasing eschar‐related rebleeding risks, and minimizes tissue damage through continuous cooling that avoids deep vascular thermal injury. While other techniques, such as underwater ESD or traction‐assisted methods attempt to simplify dissection, they carry inherent limitations including risks of tumor cell dissemination and obscure visibility during bleeding (underwater ESD), increased cost, or procedural complexity (traction devices), whereas SPEED integrates seamlessly into standard ESD workflows without requiring additional devices or extensive training. Finally, multivariable analysis showed that lesion size, lesion location, histology, and application of the SPEED technique were independently associated with resection time. This may facilitate the scheduling of ESD procedures with reasonable time within busy clinical workflows.

The limitations of this study included its single‐center, retrospective nature and the absence of randomized group allocation, which might have introduced potential selection bias. Moreover, the limited sample size might have restricted the generalizability of our findings. Larger‐scale, multicenter, randomized controlled trials are warranted to further confirm our findings.

In conclusion, the SPEED technique combines high‐pressure injection, incision, dissection, and coagulation. Particularly, it can establish submucosal cushions and make the dissection layers easier to identify by continuously injecting different levels of high‐pressure liquid water, which efficiently improves the efficiency and safety of the dissection and resection during ESD. Based on our experiences and the reduced use of hemostatic forceps in the current study, SPEED technique may decrease the need for additional instruments and device exchange; however, this potential advantage requires confirmation in prospective controlled studies. Besides, whether the reduced use of additional instruments translates into lower procedural costs should be evaluated in future cost‐effectiveness studies.
