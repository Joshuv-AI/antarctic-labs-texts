Real-world data on patients with HR+, HER2- early breast cancer prescribed with abemaciclib adjuvant therapy for 2 years in Japan
Authors: Yukinori Ozaki, Moemi Urano, Nobuyuki Sekine, Mayumi lida, Satoshi Osaga, Genta Toshima, Ryo Takemura, Norikazu Masuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate patient characteristics and treatment patterns and completion rates over a 2-year follow-up period among Japanese patients with early breast cancer (EBC) who received adjuvant therapy with abemaciclib in real-world clinical practice.MethodsThis retrospective observational study used a Japanese administrative claims database to examine patients with hormone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative EBC who received abemaciclib adjuvant therapy from December 2021–November 2024. Results were summarized descriptively.ResultsOf the 2042 eligible patients, 439 (median age [min-max] of 55 [26–84] years, with 99.3% being females) had a ≥2-year follow-up (median duration of follow-up: 890 days). Among these, 70.6% completed 2 years of abemaciclib+ endocrine therapy [ET], 21.9% discontinued abemaciclib but continued ET, and 7.5% discontinued both within 2 years. A dose of 300 mg/day was initiated in 92.3% of patients and dose reduction occurred in 58.3% of patients in 2-year follow-up group. Anti-diarrheal agents and probiotics were frequently prescribed initially, with rates declining over time.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that adjuvant abemaciclib+ET is being used at the labeled dosage in most Japanese patients. Furthermore, 2-year completion rates in patients with 2-year follow-up were comparable to the clinical trial data from monarchE (69%).

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent cancer among women in Japan, representing 22% of newly diagnosed cancer cases and 10% of cancer-related deaths among women in 2024 [1]. The majority of breast cancers are diagnosed at an early stage, and of these, approximately 70% are hormone receptor-positive (HR+) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) [2]. Endocrine therapy (ET) is a common treatment for HR+ and HER2- early BC (EBC), and patients generally have a favorable prognosis; however, recurrence remains a possibility. The 5-year probability of recurrence or death in these patients is 17.2% [3]. Additionally, more than 30% of individuals with high-risk clinical or pathological features may experience distant recurrence in 10 years [4,5]. Moreover, resistance to ET is one of the possible reasons for both early and late recurrence, influenced by changes in estrogen receptor signaling, compensatory pathways, and the tumor microenvironment [6]. Therefore, strategies to minimize recurrence and resistance are critical. The role of dual inhibition approaches, such as ET with cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors 4 and 6 (CDK 4/6) inhibitors, has demonstrated promising efficacy in reducing recurrence [6,7].

CDK 4/6 inhibitors are a relatively new class of drugs in the adjuvant setting that have significantly changed ET [8]. Furthermore, findings from the Phase III, monarchE trial, which assessed adjuvant therapy with abemaciclib (a CDK4/6 inhibitor) plus ET versus ET alone in patients with HR+, HER2- EBC at high risk of recurrence, showed that addition of abemaciclib to ET (abemaciclib+ET) significantly improved invasive disease-free survival (IDFS) compared to ET alone (hazard ratio [HR] 0.75; 95% CI, 0.60–0.93; p = 0.01), with 2-year IDFS rates of 92.2% vs. 88.7% [9]. In the monarchE trial, high risk was defined as having four or more positive axillary lymph nodes, or one to three positive axillary nodes with either a tumor size of ≥5 cm or histological grade 3 or centrally assessed Ki-67 ≥ 20% [9]. Furthermore, a prespecified overall survival (OS) analysis from the monarchE trial demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful OS improvement in abemaciclib+ET arm (HR: 0.84, 95%CI, 0.72–0.98, p = 0.03) with a 7-year OS rate of 86.8% compared to 85.0% with ET alone, showing an absolute difference of 1.8% [10]. Adjuvant abemaciclib+ET continued to reduce the risk of invasive and distant disease recurrence with longer follow-up: IDFS HR was 0.73 (95% CI, 0.66–0.82), distant relapse-free survival (DRFS) HR was 0.75 (95% CI, 0.66–0.84), with absolute improvements at 7 years of 6.5% for IDFS and 5.1% for DRFS [10]. Of note, approximately 24% of the patients in the monarchE trial were Asians. In this group, abemaciclib+ET resulted in improved IDFS compared with ET alone (HR: 0.57; 95% CI: [0.43–0.77]), which is comparable to the benefit observed in the overall ITT population (HR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.58–0.76) [11]. Other CDK 4/6 inhibitors have been tested in the adjuvant setting. While no IDFS benefit was observed in either the PALLAS or PENELOPE-B trials, which evaluated palbociclib in the adjuvant treatment of HR+, HER2- EBC [12,13], the NATALEE trial evaluated adjuvant ribociclib in the treatment of HR+, HER2- EBC and showed a statistically significant improvement in IDFS at 3 years compared to ET alone (HR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.62–0.91; p = 0.003) [14].

Based on the results of the monarchE trial, abemaciclib (150 mg twice daily) was approved in Japan in December 2021 for adjuvant treatment of high-risk HR+, HER2- EBC, in combination with ET. During the time of this study, abemaciclib was the first and only drug approved in Japan for this indication, and is recommended by the Japanese Breast Cancer Society guidelines [11,15,16].

Despite these developments, real-world data on long-term utilization and treatment patterns of adjuvant abemaciclib in EBC remain limited, particularly in Japan. Given the scarcity of data, we have previously characterized its early post-marketing use in Japan [17]. Further, real-world data from Japan are particularly important given potential differences between trial and clinical populations, including age distribution, comorbidity burden, body composition, and patterns of perioperative chemotherapy use [17]. Furthermore, treatment decisions regarding tolerability management and dose adjustment are guided by the abemaciclib label in Japan [18], which incorporates local risk-benefit considerations that may differ from international recommendations.

In this study, we aim to retrospectively investigate patient characteristics, treatment patterns (starting dose, dose modification patterns, and use of concomitant medications), and 2-year treatment completion rates among Japanese patients with EBC who received adjuvant abemaciclib therapy in real-world clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study used data from the Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd. (MDV) database, a de-identified database of inpatient and outpatient administrative data (including Claims and Diagnosis Procedure Combination [DPC] data) provided by acute care hospitals in Japan [19]. The DPC is a case-mix classification system linked to a flat-fee payment system. As of December 2025, the MDV database contained data on approximately 55.02 million patients across 550 acute-phase hospitals, representing approximately 28% of acute hospitals in Japan with a potential over-representation of larger, designated cancer hospitals [19].

Patients who received abemaciclib as adjuvant therapy were identified during the patient identification period (December 2021 to November 2024) based on their first prescription of abemaciclib (index date) (Figure 1). Patients were followed from the index date until the last hospital visit date or the end of data availability, whichever occurred later (follow-up period). Study design.

This study included patients ≥18 years of age at the index date, with a confirmed diagnosis of Stage I-III BC, HR+, HER2- EBC (cancer staging defined based on Union for International Cancer Control TNM staging 8th edition). Eligible patients received abemaciclib and ET (as a surrogate for HR+) and did not receive anti-HER2 therapy (as a surrogate for HER2-) during the index period. All included patients had their first breast resection within 12 months before the index date and were prescribed ET within 3 months before or after the index date. Patients already on ET alone and added abemaciclib after its approval in Japan were also included if ET was started within 3 months before the start of abemaciclib. Patients were excluded if they received abemaciclib monotherapy (without ET 3 months before or after the index date), had a prescription for another CDK 4/6 inhibitor or fulvestrant on or before the index date, or were diagnosed with Stage IV BC or metastases, or recurrence (identified using ICD-10 diagnosis codes recorded in the claims data) before or within 3 months after the index date.

The ICD-10 Diagnosis Codes, Procedure Codes, and Drug Codes used in this study were provided in the supplementary appendix.

Primary objective, study variables, and measures

The primary objective of this study was to describe patient characteristics, treatment patterns (starting dose, dose modification patterns, and use of concomitant medications), and 2-year treatment completion rates among Japanese patients with EBC who received adjuvant abemaciclib therapy in real-world clinical practice.

Patient demographics (age at the index date, sex, and body mass index [BMI]), hospital information (including the number of beds and hospital type [i.e., whether the hospital was designated as a cancer hospital or not]), and baseline characteristics (cancer stage [defined using the Tumor Node Metastasis classification], and smoking history) during the baseline period were summarized. The most common comorbid conditions, concomitant medications, and Activity of Daily Living (ADL) status [20] were also reported in the baseline period. Prescription of luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) agonists (as a surrogate for menopause status) on or after the index date was described.

The preoperative period was defined as the time up to 12 months before the first breast resection surgery, since many patients with high-risk EBC are advised to undergo up to 6 months of neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to surgery. The postoperative period was defined as the interval from the first breast resection surgery to the day before the index date.

The proportion of patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and ET during the preoperative period and adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation therapy during the postoperative period was assessed.

All of the aforementioned variables were evaluated in the following groups: 1) the overall patient population; 2) patients who had a ≥2-year follow-up (defined as patients whose follow-up duration from the index date to the end of data availability or last recorded activity was ≥700 days, allowing for a 30-day window from the 2-year [730-day] threshold) regardless of the length of the treatment with abemaciclib; and 3) patients who completed 2 years of abemaciclib plus ET.

The 2-year treatment completion rates in patients with EBC who received adjuvant abemaciclib therapy were assessed in the following groups: i) patients who completed 2 years of abemaciclib and continued ET over 2 years, ii) patients who discontinued abemaciclib and continued ET over 2 years, and iii) patients who discontinued both therapies within 2 years.

Two-year treatment completion was defined as continuous abemaciclib exposure for at least 730 days from the index date, allowing a 30-day grace period to account for typical prescription refill intervals in clinical practice [21]. The exposure period was defined as the time from the index date to the estimated end of the last prescription supply (i.e., the last prescription date plus the number of days supplied on that prescription). If the medication was interrupted during treatment, resuming the prescription was considered a continuation of the treatment.

Common adjuvant ETs initiated with abemaciclib among patients aged <55 years and ≥55 years were described (using these age groups as the cutoffs for pre- and post-menopausal status).

The starting dose of abemaciclib (daily dose of abemaciclib on the index date) and the proportion of patients who had dose modifications (≥50 mg decrease in daily abemaciclib starting dose) were evaluated. Furthermore, we analyzed the 2-year completion rates, the starting dose, and the dose reduction of abemaciclib in a subgroup of patients categorized by age groups (<65, ≥65 years vs ≥75 years), BMI subgroups based on the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity (JASSO) underweight categories (<18.5 vs ≥18.5 kg/m2) [22], and history of perioperative chemotherapy (received neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy vs did not receive neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy). These variables were selected for subgroup analyses because they were considered likely to influence treatment completion rate and starting dose. Age, perioperative treatment status, and BMI were used in our previous study [17]. As the treatment duration of abemaciclib for EBC is 2 years, a 2-year follow-up period was used for the analyses.

The proportion of patients who received concomitant therapy, including painkillers, bone resorption drugs, anti-diarrheal agents, probiotics, antiemetic agents, liver protection drugs, along with abemaciclib adjuvant therapy, was described.

Concomitant therapy was defined as a prescription of drugs from the index date until the earliest of these events: 1 month after the last dose of abemaciclib, the end of the study period, the occurrence of metastatic BC, or death.

The proportion of patients who received anti-diarrheal agents and probiotics after the index date was reported for the following periods: 1 month, 1–3 months, 3–6 months, 6–12 months, and over 12 months post-index date.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for all variables, with numbers, mean (standard deviation), and median (interquartile range [IQR]); range [minimum-maximum]) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. In addition, the following subgroup analyses were performed. First, a subgroup analysis was conducted in patients with and without abemaciclib dose reduction to evaluate the 2-year abemaciclib treatment completion rate. Furthermore, pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed based on age, perioperative chemotherapy status, and BMI. For each subgroup, the 2-year abemaciclib treatment completion rate, the starting dose of abemaciclib, and the occurrence of dose reduction were assessed. Missing data for BMI, smoking status, and ADL were not imputed. Patients with missing values were included in the overall analysis but excluded from subgroup analyses requiring these variables. The proportion of patients with unknown smoking status was reported as a separate category. All analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 and R Version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient selection

Of the 638,083 patients with confirmed diagnosis of BC, 10,450 patients received a prescription for abemaciclib between December 2021 and November 2024. Of these, 2042 eligible patients with Stage I–III EBC who underwent their first BC resection surgery within 1 year prior to their index date were included. Among these, 1,603 patients had not yet reached the 2-year follow-up milestone at the time of data cutoff, primarily due to the relatively recent initiation of treatment following abemaciclib’s approval in Japan in December 2021. A total of 439 patients had a ≥2-year follow-up from the index date (Figure 2). The median duration (IQR) of follow-up from the index date to the last observed date of prescription for the overall patients, the 2-year follow-up group and 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group was 467 days (14–1128 days), 890 days (700–1128 days), and 881 days (795–970 days), respectively.

Most patients were female (>99.0% in all three groups; <1% patients were male in three groups), with a median age (range) of 56 (26–89 years), 55 (26–84 years), and 53 years (27–82 years), in the overall patients, 2-year follow-up group, and 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion groups, respectively. LHRH was prescribed to 16.6% of overall patients, 20.7% in the 2-year follow-up group, and 22.9% in the 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group. The median age (range) was 59 years (50–68 years), and LHRH was prescribed to 15.5% (20/129) among patients other than the 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group.

More than 90% of patients were diagnosed with Stage II or III BC in each group (Table 1).

Prior to index date, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was received by 44.1%, 42.8%, and 41.6% of patients in each group, while adjuvant chemotherapy was received by 45.6%, 46.0%, and 49.4% of patients, respectively. Neither neoadjuvant nor adjuvant chemotherapy was received by 12.8%, 14.4%, and 11.6% of patients, respectively. Among patients other than the 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group, 20.9% (27/129) did not receive either of them. Additionally, postoperative radiotherapy was received by 72.6%, 72.9%, and 74.2% of patients in each group (Table 1).

Among overall patients, in both neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy settings, epirubicin/cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel was the most frequently used regimen (Supplementary Table S1).

Common adjuvant ETs initiated with abemaciclib among overall patients aged <55 years and ≥55 years

Among overall patients, tamoxifen was the most frequently prescribed adjuvant ET for those <55 years old (68.1%; 649/953). In contrast, letrozole was most commonly prescribed to patients aged ≥55 years (55.5%; 604/1089) (Figure 3). Among overall patients aged <55 years, LHRH was co-prescribed with tamoxifen in 41.6% (270/649), with letrozole in 21.3% (36/169), and with anastrozole in 20.8% (27/130). Discussion

This retrospective study provides valuable real-world insights into the patient characteristics and treatment patterns in Japanese patients with HR+, HER2- EBC. This study is the first to report the 2-year completion rate of abemaciclib+ET in this patient population. However, given the descriptive nature of this study and the lack of clinical outcome measures such as recurrence, IDFS, or OS, we cannot draw causal inferences about treatment efficacy, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly.

Among the 439 patients with 2-year follow-up, adjuvant abemaciclib was initiated at the recommended dose of 300 mg/day in the majority of patients, which is consistent with previous research [17]. Over 70% completed the 2 years of abemaciclib+ET, closely aligning with the 69% reported in the monarchE study, indicating substantial tolerability among Japanese patients in clinical practice [11]. 22% discontinued abemaciclib but continued ET, and <10% discontinued both treatments within 2 years. Dose reductions were common in real-world practice, occurring in 58.3% in the 2-year follow-up group and 60.6% in the 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group, with most dose modifications implemented early after treatment initiation.

In this study, similar completion rates of abemaciclib+ET were observed among the subgroup of patients who initiated abemaciclib at 300 mg/day. While elderly patients (≥75 years, 45.2%; ≥65 years, 57.9%), those without prior neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy (57.1%), and those with a BMI <18.5 (66.7%) exhibited lower 2-year completion rates for abemaciclib+ET, nevertheless, the majority in subgroups were able to complete the prescribed therapy. Given the 46.6% discontinuation rate (over 2-years) observed in the ≥65 years subgroup in monarchE study, these results may indicate comparable outcomes [23]. The numerically higher 2-year completion rate among patients who underwent dose reduction (73.4%) than those who did not (66.7%) is a clinically relevant finding. This pattern may suggest that dose reductions represent proactive toxicity management that facilitates long-term treatment adherence, rather than indicating treatment intolerance.

The patient and clinical characteristics in this study are similar to other real-world studies in Japan [17], though several differences from the monarchE trial population were noted (refer to limitations and Supplementary Table S9). Notably, <1% of male patients were included in this study, consistent with the known rarity of male breast cancer in clinical practice [24]. Subgroup analysis was not performed due to the small number. Male patients were underrepresented in clinical trials, including monarchE [9], which enrolled predominantly female patients. Nevertheless, recent real-world evidence supports the use of CDK4/6 inhibitors in male patients with HR+/HER2- BC [25–27].

In terms of patient characteristics, the 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group was slightly younger in age, had a slightly higher proportion of LHRH use, and a higher rate of adjuvant chemotherapy (prior to index date) than those in the 2-year follow-up group. The 2-year abemaciclib+ET completion group showed slightly higher rates of starting at 300 mg/day and of subsequent dose reduction than the 2-year follow-up group, indicating that completion is feasible with appropriate dose reductions.

The median time to dose reduction in our study was 42 days, which aligns closely with findings from the monarchE study [28]. In monarchE, dose reductions occurred most frequently during the second month following treatment initiation; this pattern was also observed in our current research. In our study, dose reductions were frequently observed, particularly among elderly patients (≥65 years, >67%). The higher rate of dose reduction in our study (58.3% in the 2-year follow-up group) compared to monarchE (43.7%) may reflect differences in patient populations [23], as our real-world cohort may include patients who would not meet strict trial eligibility criteria, including those with more comorbidities or frailer health status. Additionally, Japanese clinical practice may favor more proactive dose adjustments to maintain treatment adherence. However, whether higher dose reduction rates affect long-term treatment effectiveness cannot be assessed from this study, as it was not designed to assess efficacy outcomes. Existing evidence from the monarchE trial provides important context for interpreting the dose-reduction rates observed in this real-world study. Exploratory analyses reported by Goetz et al. demonstrated that the efficacy of adjuvant abemaciclib was not compromised by protocol-mandated dose reductions [23]. Across patient subgroups defined by relative dose intensity, 4-year IDFS rates were generally consistent, and time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models showed comparable treatment effects between patients who remained on the full dose (150 mg twice daily) and those who required dose reductions to 100 mg or 50 mg twice daily for both IDFS and DRFS [23].

Diarrhea was the common adverse event reported in patients who received abemaciclib. In the monarchE trial, diarrhea was generally low grade (grade 1/2: 76%); Grade 2/3 events were highest in the first month (20.5%), most were short-lived (≤7 days) and did not recur [28]. Given the early onset and transient nature of abemaciclib-related diarrhea, the frequent prescriptions of anti-diarrheal agents and probiotics observed in this study, particularly during the initial treatment period, are consistent with expectations. The subsequent decline in prescriptions after the first month suggests that gastrointestinal symptoms may be most prominent early in treatment and manageable with appropriate supportive care. However, since claims data do not capture adverse event grades, supportive medication use serves only as an indirect proxy for adverse events and may not reflect the true severity of symptoms.

The study has several strengths. The study provides comprehensive data on 2-year completion rates (for more than 400 patients who had over 2 years of follow-up), dose reductions by age, BMI, and peri-operative chemotherapy status, offering detailed insights into real-world practice among Japanese patients with HR+, HER2- EBC. The description of supportive medication use enhances the understanding of toxicity management. The MDV database contains precise prescription data, enabling identification of treatment timing, dose amount, and patterns of abemaciclib and supportive therapy use. The large, nationwide database ensures a diverse and representative patient population, enhancing the generalizability of the findings to real-world clinical practice in Japan. It also provides valuable insights into the use of concomitant medications, informing best practices for managing adverse events in daily practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a fundamental limitation of claims-based studies is the inability to determine the specific reasons for treatment discontinuation or dose reduction, which significantly constrains the interpretation of completion rates. Second, among the 2,042 eligible patients, only 439 (21.5%) had a 2-year or longer follow-up, mainly due to administrative censoring related to database enrollment timing. Specifically, patients with an index date within two years prior to the data cutoff (November 2024) were not eligible for the analysis of treatment completion rates. In addition, early discontinuation or transfer to other institutions may have resulted in loss to follow-up. The generalizability of these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further, the completion rates were assessed in only 439 patients, which may introduce selection bias. Third, ET prescription was used as a surrogate for HR positivity, and absence of anti-HER2 therapy as a surrogate for HER2 negativity, which may introduce misclassification bias as receptor status was not directly available. These proxy definitions could lead to some misclassification, and their performance cannot be formally validated. However, a previous validation study using an analogous claims-based algorithm reported a positive predictive value of approximately 90% for HR+/HER2− identification, suggesting that misclassification is likely to be limited in magnitude [29]. Further, LHRH agonist prescription was used as a surrogate for premenopausal status, which may lead to misclassification of some patients. Future studies with more precise data on menopausal status are needed. Fourth, the MDV database covers approximately 28% of acute-phase hospitals in Japan, with potential over-representation of larger designated cancer hospitals may introduce selection bias, limiting generalizability to smaller institutions or community-based oncology practices. Fifth, this study was designed primarily as a descriptive analysis, and inferential statistical analyses, such as multivariable regression to identify independent predictors of treatment completion or dose reduction, were not performed. This reflects the predefined study objectives rather than an analytic limitation. Nevertheless, the absence of inferential analyses limits the ability to assess factors independently associated with treatment outcomes. Identification of predictive factors remains an important topic for future research using inferential designs. Sixth, the present real-world cohort differs from the monarchE trial population in several important respects, which should be considered when interpreting comparisons of completion and dose-reduction rates between the two studies. Key differences in study design, eligibility, and baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S9. In particular, the MDV database does not capture variables required to fully apply monarchE high-risk criteria, specifically the Ki-67 index and histological grade, and does not distinguish pathological from clinical T/N staging. The proportion of patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy was lower in our cohort (46.0% in the 2-year follow-up group) than in monarchE (61.8%) [11], and the proportion who received neither neoadjuvant nor adjuvant chemotherapy was higher (14.4% vs. 4.5%) [9]. Nevertheless, several baseline features align between the two populations: the majority of our patients had Stage II–III disease (92.1%), node-positive disease (73.6%), and had received perioperative chemotherapy (88.8%), suggesting substantial overlap with the monarchE-eligible population [11], though exact eligibility cannot be confirmed. Direct comparisons of outcomes between the two studies should therefore be interpreted with caution. Seventh, the MDV database does not capture the clinical intent behind each prescription, such as whether a medication was prescribed prophylactically in anticipation of an adverse event or reactively in response to one that had already occurred. Additionally, the MDV database only tracks patients within a single hospital (and is unable to capture treatment outside a single hospital) and lacks data distinguishing pathological versus clinical staging, as well as reasons for treatment discontinuation or dose reduction and recurrence. Consequently, reasons for treatment discontinuation, including disease recurrence, adverse events, or patient/physician decision, cannot be determined. This limitation significantly constrains the interpretation of completion rates.

Conclusions

This descriptive study provides comprehensive real-world evidence on the utilization of adjuvant abemaciclib+ET among Japanese patients with HR+, HER2− EBC. The majority of patients initiated abemaciclib at the recommended dosage of 300 mg/day, consistent with the approved product labeling. Among patients with ≥2 years of follow-up, the 2-year completion rate (70.6%) was comparable to that observed in the monarchE clinical trial (69%), despite this real-world cohort likely encompassing a more heterogeneous population with regard to baseline recurrence risk. Proactive management of adverse events such as diarrhea, evidenced by the use of supportive medications and dose reductions, likely contributes to the favorable treatment completion rates observed. These data could aid in selecting treatment strategies, including for elderly patients and those with comorbidities, and help optimize treatment patterns for patients with high-risk EBC. However, the descriptive nature of the study and the lack of clinical outcomes data restrict causal inferences on treatment efficacy. Future studies with longer follow-up and more detailed clinical data are warranted to further validate these findings and optimize patient outcomes.
