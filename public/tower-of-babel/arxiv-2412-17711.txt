Ab initio theory of the non-resonant Raman effect in crystals at finite temperature in comparison to experiment: The examples of GaN and BaZrS 3
Florian Knoop, Nimrod Benshalom, Matan Menahem, Paul Gartner, Tommaso Salzillo, Omer Yaffe, Olle Hellman

Abstract
We present an ab initio theory of the non-resonant Raman scattering process in crystals at finite temperature in direct comparison with experiments. The theory naturally incorporates the scattering geometry and polarization dependence of the Raman process and the small but finite wave vectors of the phonons for correctly describing the scattering with longitudinal optical (LO) modes in optically anisotropic solids. We implement the theory for first-order Raman scattering and showcase the approach for wurtzite Gallium Nitride and the complex chalcogenide perovskite BaZrS 3 in comparison to experiment. We subsequently discuss several common estimates for second-order Raman scattering in complex materials, and highlight similarities and differences to established theoretical approaches and simulation protocols both from phonon theory and molecular dynamics.

Compared to other forms of vibrational spectroscopy, such as neutron scattering, Raman spectroscopy is an accessible table-top technique available in many laboratories.
It is therefore an invaluable tool for the characterization of materials and molecules in a wide range of fields, and numerous variants such as stimulated or tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy are in active use

.
While used primarily as a tool to characterize structural properties, recent years have led to a range of applications in the study of atomic vibrations in catalysts

The comparison between experiment and atomistic simulations is particularly fruitful in elucidating the nuclear dynamics, as microscopic simulations allow us to directly assess and characterize the nuclear vibrations leading to scattering at a particular frequency.
Since the Raman effect originates in modulations of the electronic susceptibility, atomistic modeling requires an explicit treatment of electronic response, which can be obtained
ab initio
in the framework of
density functional theory (DFT)
and
density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)

[
18
,
19

On the other hand, the nuclear vibrations need to be described by solving the equations of motion for the nuclei. The most common approach for materials is to use the harmonic approximation to obtain the phonon eigenfrequencies and subsequently compute the modulation of the electronic susceptibility by the eigenmode displacements

,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25

At finite temperatures, anharmonic effects can lead to significant linewidth broadening (reduced phonon lifetime), phase transitions that change the Raman spectrum completely, and significant low-frequency scattering background from higher order phonon interactions

,

,

,

,

,

,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.

While these approaches have been successfully employed in the past, particularly in the context of second-order Raman scattering, they typically rely on a simplified description of the Raman scattering process developed for cubic materials

,
32

{{\bf{q}}}

This can lead to ambiguities when connecting the Raman spectrum to the vibrational dynamics, e. g., via response functions, and hinders theoretical understanding of anisotropic systems where the vibrational dynamics depend explicitly on the probing wave vector

We demonstrate the approach for two anisotropic materials, wurtzite

For both materials, we achieve excellent agreement regarding mode assignment and polarization dependence, as well as the temperature dependence of spectral broadening and frequency shifts, besides typical systematic errors like underestimation of phonon frequencies

I
Raman theory

I.1
Direction-dependent spectral Raman tensor

Following the development of classical scattering theory in Ref.
[
50
,
51
]
,
we describe the probing laser as an incident monochromatic electric field with amplitude
{{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}
and frequency
{\omega_{\rm i}}
propagating in direction
{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}

as

\displaystyle{{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}({{\bf{r}}},t)={{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}\cdot{{\bf{r}}}-{\omega_{\rm i}}t)}~,

(1)

with wave vector

\displaystyle{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}=n({\omega_{\rm i}})\frac{{\omega_{\rm i}}}{c}\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}~,

(2)

where
n({\omega_{\rm i}})
is the refractive index of the medium at frequency
{\omega_{\rm i}}
,
c
is the speed of light, and
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}
denotes the unit vector in the propagation direction.

Neglecting surfaces and boundaries, we label the unit cells of a crystal with
L
and the Bravais lattice vectors pointing to them as
{{\bf{R}}}_{L}
. The induced dipole in a unit cell
L
at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf{p}}_{L}(t)=\chi({{\bf{R}}}_{L},t){{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}({{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}-{\omega_{\rm i}}t)}~,

(3)

with
\chi({{\bf{R}}}_{L},t)
the electronic susceptibility in cell
L
modulated in time through the atomic motion – this is the origin of the Raman effect.

We note that this expression assumes a
local
and
instantaneous
response and that the electronic susceptibility is independent of the incoming laser, i.e., for excitation frequencies well below the electronic band gap.

The susceptibility in a unit cell can be obtained from atomic contributions via averaging since typical Raman studies employ lasers in the optical range thus the wavelength of the field is
\lambda\simeq\mathcal{O}(100\,{\rm nm})
, much larger than the interatomic spacing.
\chi({{\bf{R}}}_{L})
can, therefore, be obtained in the sense of a dipole approximation from averaging over a spatial extent
\ll 100
nm, i.e., one or more unit cells, as long as the result does not depend on the details of the averaging scheme. We comment on this point further in Sec.
II.3
.

The frequency contribution of the oscillating dipole at scattering frequency
{\omega_{\rm s}}
is obtained via Fourier transformation,

\displaystyle{\bf{p}}_{L}({\omega_{\rm s}})=\chi_{L}({\omega_{\rm s}}-{\omega_{\rm i}}){{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}}~.

(4)

The accelerated dipole induces the scattered field at an observer point
{{\bf{r}}}
far away
[
51
]
,

\displaystyle{\bf{E}}_{{\rm s},L}({{\bf{r}}},{\omega_{\rm s}})=\frac{k_{\rm s}^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{\rm s}r}}{r}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}}\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}\times\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}\times{\bf{p}}_{L}({\omega_{\rm s}})~,

(5)

where we have approximated
\lvert{{\bf{r}}}-{{\bf{R}}}_{L}\rvert\approx r-\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}
to separate out the phase shift at
{{\bf{R}}}_{L}
,
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
is the observer direction, and
{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}
is the scattering wave vector defined analogously to Eq. (
2
).

The total scattered field is obtained by summing over the individual contributions,

\displaystyle{{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm s}({{\bf{r}}},{\omega_{\rm s}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{L}{\bf{E}}_{{\rm s},L}({{\bf{r}}},{\omega_{\rm s}})

(6)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{s}^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{s}r}}{r}\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}\times\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}\times\chi({{\omega_{\rm s}}-{\omega_{\rm i}}},{{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}}){{{\bf{E}}}}_{\rm i}~,

(7)

with the
total crystal susceptibility

\displaystyle\chi({{\omega_{\rm s}}-{\omega_{\rm i}}},{{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}})\equiv\sum_{L}\chi_{L}({{\omega_{\rm s}}-{\omega_{\rm i}}})\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}[{{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}}]\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}}~.

(8)

We simplify the notation by introducing the
Raman shift

\displaystyle{\Omega}={\omega_{\rm i}}-{\omega_{\rm s}}~,

(9)

defined as
positive
when the scattered frequency is
smaller
than the incident frequency,
and the scattering wave vector

\displaystyle{{\bf{q}}}\equiv{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}~.

(10)

In this notation,

\chi({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\equiv\chi({\omega_{\rm i}}-{\omega_{\rm s}},{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s})~,

(11)

so that

\chi({\omega_{\rm s}}-{\omega_{\rm i}},{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i})=\chi(-{\Omega},-{{\bf{q}}})=\chi^{\ast}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})~.

(12)

The differential scattering cross section
\sigma
for Raman is proportional to the ratio of the intensity of the scattered field in direction
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
and polarization
{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm s}\perp\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
to the incident field intensity in polarization
{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm i}

[
51
]
.
It, therefore, depends on the scattering geometry with scattering vector
{{\bf{q}}}
and the polarizations so that

\displaystyle\sigma_{{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm i}{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm s}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}}{;T})

\displaystyle\propto{\omega_{\rm s}}^{4}{e}_{\rm s}^{\alpha}{e}_{\rm s}^{\beta}I^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime},\beta\beta^{\prime}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}}{;T}){e}_{\rm i}^{\alpha^{\prime}}{e}_{\rm i}^{\beta^{\prime}}

(13)

where superscript index denotes the vector component in the Cartesian coordinate
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Non-essential prefactors have been omitted for clarity, and the
spectral Raman tensor
is introduced,

\displaystyle I^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime},\beta\beta^{\prime}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}}{;T})=\left\langle\chi^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\chi^{\ast,\beta\beta^{\prime}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\right\rangle_{T}~.

(14)

This tensor is typically proportional to the square of the susceptibility fluctuations
[
52
, p. 27]
and is temperature-dependent through the thermodynamic averaging
\langle\cdot\rangle_{T}
.

Using the susceptibility in Eq. (
8
),
we get

\displaystyle I^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime},\beta\beta^{\prime}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}}{;T})

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{LK}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{{\bf{q}}}\cdot({{\bf{R}}}_{L}-{{\bf{R}}}_{K})}\left\langle\chi_{L}({\Omega})\chi_{K}^{\ast}({\Omega})\right\rangle_{T}

(16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{LK}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{{\bf{q}}}\cdot({{\bf{R}}}_{L}-{{\bf{R}}}_{K})}\int{\rm d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\Omega}t}\left\langle\chi_{L}(t)\chi_{K}(0)\right\rangle_{T}~,

(17)

via the Wiener-Khinchin theorem
[
27
]
, where we note that
\chi_{L}(t)
is real in the non-resonant setting. This relates the spectral Raman tensor given by the spectral density of the crystal susceptibility to the Fourier transform of the real-time and real-space susceptibilities.

The important difference to the form used by some of us in
[
32
]
is that the scattering geometry is still explicitly included via
{{\bf{q}}}={{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}
. Therefore, the phase relation between different unit cells in Eq. (
8
) is preserved. Neglecting this phase relation by assuming
{{\bf{q}}}={{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}-{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}\approx 0
within the scattering region in Eq (
8
) leads to the form

\displaystyle I^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime},\beta\beta^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})=\int{\rm d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\Omega}t}\left\langle\chi^{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)\chi^{\beta\beta^{\prime}}(0)\right\rangle_{T}~,

(18)

in which the crystal is treated as one big molecule. This form is typically used in literature on second-order Raman effect in cubic crystals
[
37
,
53
,
32
]
, or molecular dynamics studies
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
, but it is not strictly valid in the usual experimental scenario when the penetration depth of the light spans several wavelengths in a single crystal.
Neglecting the photon momentum, therefore, does not allow to account for direction-dependent LO/TO splitting in optically anisotropic solids for small but finite scattering vectors
\lvert{{\bf{q}}}|\approx 2k_{\rm i}
in backscattering geometry, or the much smaller but still finite scattering vectors in small-angle forward scattering probing the Polariton region
[
59
]
.

In the case of birefringence, the discussion above needs to be adapted by accounting for the polarization dependence of the incident and scattered wave vectors
{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}
and
{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}
, which will lead to a superposition of fields. This effect is particularly important in thin films where the size of the scattering region is constrained by the film thickness
[
60
,
61
]
.

I.2
Polarization oriented (PO) Raman

As discussed in the previous section, the general Raman scattering cross section defined in Eq. (
13
) depends on the directions and polarizations of the incident and scattered fields,
{{{\bf{k}}}_{\rm i}({{\bf{e}}}_{\rm i}{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm s}){{\bf{k}}}_{\rm s}}
, in Porto notation
[
3
]
.

By denoting the angle with respect to the scattering plane spanned by
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}
and
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
, or some arbitrary reference in the case of backward or forward scattering (
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}\parallel\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
) as
{\theta}
, the polarizations are unambiguously defined by
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}
,
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
, and
{\theta}
.
We then define the total
unpolarized cross section
as

\displaystyle\sigma({\Omega})=\int\frac{{\rm d}{\theta}}{2\pi}\left({\sigma^{\parallel}_{\theta}}({\Omega})+{\sigma^{\perp}_{\theta}}({\Omega})\right)~,

(19)

where
{\sigma^{\parallel}_{\theta}}
denotes the
parallel
configuration

\displaystyle{\sigma^{\parallel}_{\theta}}({\Omega})

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm i},{\theta}}{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm s},{\theta}}}({\Omega})~,

(20)

and
{\sigma^{\perp}_{\theta}}({\omega_{\rm s}})
denotes the
perpendicular
configuration with

\displaystyle{\sigma^{\perp}_{\theta}}({\Omega})

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm i},{\theta}}{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm s},{\theta}+\pi/2}}({\Omega})~,

(21)

where
{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm i/s},{\theta}}
are the polarization directions for a given angle
{\theta}
.
In the special cases of backward or forward scattering with
\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm i}\parallel\hat{{{\bf{k}}}}_{\rm s}
, we simply have
{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm s},{\theta}}\parallel{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm i},{\theta}}
for parallel scattering, and
{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm s},{\theta}+\pi/2}\perp{{\bf{e}}}_{{\rm i},{\theta}}
for perpendicular scattering, respectively.

It is important to point out that the
unpolarized
cross section defined in Eq. (
19
) is not equivalent to the
isotropic
average defined in Ref.
[
21
,
22
,
27
,
25
]
. In the present spectral framework, the isotropic average reads

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm isotropic}({\Omega})=45\alpha^{\prime 2}({\Omega})+7\beta^{\prime 2}({\Omega})~,

(22)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}9\alpha^{\prime 2}&=\sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy}+\sigma_{zz}\\
&\quad+2\left(\sqrt{\sigma_{xx}\sigma_{yy}}+\sqrt{\sigma_{xx}\sigma_{zz}}+\sqrt{\sigma_{yy}\sigma_{zz}}\right)~,\end{split}

(23)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta^{\prime 2}&=\sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy}+\sigma_{zz}\\
&\quad-\left(\sqrt{\sigma_{xx}\sigma_{yy}}+\sqrt{\sigma_{xx}\sigma_{zz}}+\sqrt{\sigma_{yy}\sigma_{zz}}\right)\\
&\quad+3\left(\sigma_{xy}+\sigma_{xz}+\sigma_{yz}\right)~,\end{split}

(24)

where
\sigma_{xx},\sigma_{yy},\ldots
are the projections of the cross section defined in Eq. (
13
) on the Cartesian axes
\set{x,y,z}
, and all quantities depend on the frequency
{\Omega}
, omitted for readability in Eq. (
23
) and (
24
).
This isotropic average is valid for gas and liquid phases with random orientations of the microscopic scattering centers with respect to the laboratory frame. In a single crystal, however, the accessible components of the cross section are only those perpendicular to the scattering directions, and therefore only a subset of the components entering the isotropic cross section defined in Eq. (
22
) will be measured in the unpolarized cross section defined in Eq. (
19
) for a fixed scattering geometry.

A mixed case are polycrystalline solids: In this case, a full average over polarization directions and scattering direction needs to be performed while taking into account the explicitly
{{\bf{q}}}
dependence of LO frequencies in the case of polar materials, see discussion in Sec.
II.2
and Ref.
[
38
]
.

I.3
First-order Raman

To compute the first-order spectral Raman tensor via phonon theory, we expand the susceptibility to first order in the nuclear displacements
u_{iL}
,

\displaystyle\chi_{L}(t)=\sum_{i\gamma}\frac{\partial\chi_{L}(t)}{\partial u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)}u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)=\sum_{i\gamma}\chi_{i}^{\gamma}u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)~,

(25)

where we omit the second-rank Cartesian indices
{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}},{\beta\beta^{\prime}}
on the tensor
\chi
to simplify the notation.
Then Eq. (
17
) becomes

\displaystyle\langle\chi({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\chi^{\ast}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\rangle_{T}=

(26)

\displaystyle\sum_{iL\gamma,jK\delta}\chi_{i}^{\gamma}\chi_{j}^{\delta}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{{\bf{q}}}\cdot({{\bf{R}}}_{L}-{{\bf{R}}}_{K})}\int{\rm d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\Omega}t}\langle u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)u_{jK}^{\delta}(0)\rangle_{T}~,

(27)

where

\langle u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)u_{jK}^{\delta}(0)\rangle

is the autocorrelation function for nuclear displacements, which is closely linked to the phonon propagator
[
50
]
.
The displacements can be expanded using phonon amplitudes
{A_{{{\bf{q}}}s}}
via
[
32
,
49
]

\displaystyle u_{iL}^{\gamma}(t)=\sum_{{{\bf{q}}s}}\text{v}^{\gamma}_{i{{\bf{q}}}s}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{{\bf{q}}}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}}{A_{{{\bf{q}}}s}}(t)

(28)

with lattice-periodic eigenvectors
{\bf{v}}_{i{{\bf{q}}}s}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{{\bf{q}}}\cdot{{\bf{R}}}_{L}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle\chi({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\chi^{\ast}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}})\rangle_{T}

(29)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\chi_{{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}s}\chi_{{{\bf{q}}}s^{\prime}}\int{\rm d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\Omega}t}\langle A_{{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}s}(t){A_{{{\bf{q}}}s^{\prime}}}(0)\rangle_{T}~,

(30)

with the first-order change of the susceptibility in mode space,

\displaystyle\chi_{{\bf{q}}s}=\sum_{i\gamma}\chi_{i}^{\gamma}\text{v}^{\gamma}_{i{{\bf{q}}}s}=\frac{{\rm d}\chi_{L}}{{\rm d}{A_{{{\bf{q}}}s}}}\equiv R_{{\bf{q}}s}~,

(31)

where
{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}\equiv-{{\bf{q}}}
, and where we identify
\chi_{{\bf{q}}s}
with the second-rank Raman tensor commonly denoted as
R_{{{\bf{q}}}s}
which determines the mode activity
[
52
]
.
The scattering wave vector
{{\bf{q}}}
defined in Eq. (
10
) is selected from the expansion in Eq. (
28
) via the closure relation of the lattice sums.

Treating the nuclei as quantum particles, we can use that
[
50
,
32
]

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}t\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\Omega}t}\left\langle A_{{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}s}(t){A_{{{\bf{q}}}s^{\prime}}}(0)\right\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle=(n({\Omega}{;T})+1){J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega})~,

(32)

\displaystyle\equiv{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega}{;T})

(33)

where
{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega})
is the
phonon spectral function
,
n({\Omega}{;T})
is the Bose function at temperature
T
, and
{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega}{;T})
is the Bose-weighted phonon spectral function. This result is equivalent to Eq. (10) in
[
32
]
for finite scattering
{{\bf{q}}}
.

A positive frequency
{\Omega}
in the phonon spectral function, Eq.(
32
), is associated with phonon creation
[
62
]
.
However, from the light probe’s perspective, phonon creation (
{\Omega}>0
) means energy loss, i.e., photon annihilation with
{\omega_{\rm s}}<{\omega_{\rm i}}
.
By defining the Raman shift as
{\Omega}={\omega_{\rm i}}-{\omega_{\rm s}}
in Eq. (
9
)
we have, therefore, associated photon loss at frequency
{\Omega}
with phonon creation, i.e., Stokes scattering.

For negative
\Omega
with
{\omega_{\rm s}}>{\omega_{\rm i}}
, i.e., phonon absorption, we can use that the spectral function is odd in frequency
[
63
]
,
{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}(-{\Omega})=-{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega})
, as well as
n(-\omega)=-n(\omega)-1

[
50
]
, so that

\displaystyle{J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}(-{\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\hbar{\Omega}}{k_{\rm B}T}\right){J_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}}({\Omega}{;T})~.

(34)

This explains the ratio of scattering amplitudes between Stokes scattering (
{\Omega}>0
) and Anti-Stokes scattering (
{\Omega}<0
).
This result follows purely from a quantum mechanical treatment of the nuclear dynamics, while a classical treatment of the light scattering process is sufficient
[
30
]
.

When the spectral function is diagonal in mode space,
\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
, Eq. (
13
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sigma_{{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm i}{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm s}}({\Omega},{{\bf{q}}}{;T})\propto({\omega_{\rm i}}-{\Omega})^{4}\sum_{s}\lvert{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm i}\cdot R_{{\bf{q}}s}{{\bf{e}}}_{\rm s}\rvert^{2}{J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}}({\Omega}{;T})~,

(35)

which is the form typically used in the literature but with non-empirical inclusion of
{{\bf{q}}}
-dependence and an anharmonic spectral function
[
38
]
.
In general, the spectral function does not need to be diagonal in mode space, as anharmonicity can lead to more complicated mode coupling. This can be particularly important in organic molecular crystals
[
64
]
. In conventional inorganic crystals, this effect is often negligible.

I.4
Second order Raman: Simple estimates

Following earlier work in Ref.
[
32
,
26
]
, the second-order spectrum can be obtained by an expansion of the susceptibility to second-order in the nuclear displacements
[
37
]
, leading to the convolution

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}({\Omega}{;T})\propto

\displaystyle\sum_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}s^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})~,

(36)

where
J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}({\Omega}{;T})

is the Bose-weighted spectral function as defined earlier, for which
J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}({\Omega}{;T})=J_{{\bar{{\bf{q}}}}s}({\Omega}{;T})
.
In the harmonic approximation, we have

\displaystyle J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}({\Omega})

\displaystyle=-\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})+\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})~,\quad\text{and}

(37)

\displaystyle J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=n_{{{\bf{q}}}s}\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})+(n_{{\bf{q}}s}+1)\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}),

(38)

and therefore
[
32
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})=&[n({\Omega})+1](1+n_{{\bf{q}}s}+n_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\left[\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})-\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\right]\\
&~~+[n({\Omega})+1](n_{{\bf{q}}s}-n_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\left[\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})-\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\right]~.\end{split}

(39)

Several simplified expressions for second-order Raman scattering can be inferred from here.

I.4.1
2PDOS

By setting
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}=1
in Eq. (
36
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\rm 2PDOS}{}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})

(40)

\displaystyle=g_{2}^{(1)}({\Omega}{;T})+g_{2}^{(2)}({\Omega}{;T})

(41)

with the Bose-weighted
joint density of states (JDOS)
functions
[
52
,
65
,
66
]

\displaystyle g_{2}^{(1)}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=[n({\Omega})+1]\sum_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}(1+n_{{\bf{q}}s}+n_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\left[\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})-\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\right]

(42)

\displaystyle g_{2}^{(2)}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=[n({\Omega})+1]\sum_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}(n_{{\bf{q}}s}-n_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\left[\delta({\Omega}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})-\delta({\Omega}+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s}-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}})\right]~,

(43)

which describe crystal-momentum-conserving
({{\bf{q}}}+{{\bf{q}}}^{\prime}=0)
phonon recombination and decay. These two functions together give the Bose-weighted
two-phonon density of states (2PDOS)
,

\displaystyle g_{2}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=g_{2}^{(1)}({\Omega}{;T})+g_{2}^{(2)}({\Omega}{;T})~,

(44)

which is sometimes used to estimate second-order Raman scattering
[
52
,
67
,
68
]
.

I.4.2
2\omega
-DOS

An even simpler estimate can be obtained by setting
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
36
), which yields the Bose-weighted
overtone density of states (
2\omega
-DOS)

[
69
,
70
,
71
,
52
,
72
,
19
]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{{2\omega{\rm-DOS}}}({\Omega}{;T})

(45)

\displaystyle\quad\propto\sum_{{{\bf{q}}}s}J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})

(46)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{{{\bf{q}}s}}n_{{\bf{q}}s}^{2}\delta({\Omega}+2\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})+(n_{{\bf{q}}s}+1)^{2}\delta({\Omega}-2\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})

(47)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv g_{2\omega}({\Omega}{;T})~.

(48)

Estimates based on the
2\omega
-DOS
have been used for a range of systems inlcuding Si
[
73
,
74
]
, diamond
[
75
]
, GaSb
[
76
]
, SiC
[
77
]
, Ga
2
O
3

[
78
]
, and CuI
[
68
]
.

I.4.3
DOS convolution

As a third estimate, we relax the requirement of crystal-momentum conservation and estimate
[
26
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{{\rm cDOS}}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{{{\bf{q}}},ss^{\prime}}J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bf{q}}s^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})

(49)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{{{\bf{q}}}{{\bf{q}}}^{\prime},ss^{\prime}}J_{{\bf{q}}s}({\Omega}{;T})\ast J_{{\bf{q}}^{\prime}s^{\prime}}({\Omega}{;T})

(50)

\displaystyle=g({\Omega}{;T})\ast g({\Omega}{;T})~,

(51)

where
g({\Omega}{;T})
is the ordinary Bose-weighted one-phonon
density of states (DOS)
,

\displaystyle g({\Omega}{;T})=\sum_{{{\bf{q}}s}}n_{{{\bf{q}}}s}\delta(\omega+\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})+(n_{{\bf{q}}s}+1)\delta(\omega-\omega_{{\bf{q}}s})~.

(52)

We denote the
DOS
autoconvolution in Eq. (
51
) as cDOS.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{{\rm 2PDOS}}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=g_{2}({\Omega}{;T})

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{{2\omega{\rm-DOS}}}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=g_{2\omega}({\Omega}{;T})

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}^{{\rm cDOS}}({\Omega}{;T})

\displaystyle=g({\Omega}{;T})\ast g({\Omega}{;T})~.

(55)

We discuss the relevance of these estimates for complex materials further below.

II
Methods

II.1
Stochastic Temperature Dependent Effective Potential (sTDEP)

While the basic theory for lattice dynamics in the framework of
TDEP
has been presented elsewhere
[
34
,
79
,
32
,
49
]
, we briefly discuss essential aspects for completeness.

We use the
stochastic Temperature Dependent Effective Potential (sTDEP)
scheme to compute phonons described by the force constants
\{\Phi_{ij}^{{\alpha\beta}}(T)\}
at a temperature
T

[
80
]
. This scheme is formally equivalent to the QSCAILD method described in Ref.
[
81
]
and relies on minimizing the mean-square error of the harmonic model forces,

\displaystyle S[\Phi,T]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i\alpha}\left\langle(f_{i}^{\alpha}-f_{\Phi;i}^{\alpha})^{2}\right\rangle_{\Phi,T}~,

(56)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Phi,T}
denotes a thermal average with respect to the force constants
\Phi
at temperature
T
,
ij,\alpha\beta
denote atomic and Cartesian indices. The harmonic force
f_{\Phi;i}^{\alpha}
is given by
f_{\Phi;i}^{\alpha}=-\sum_{j\beta}\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}u_{j}^{\beta}
, where
{\bf{u}}_{j}
are the atomic displacements from the thermal average positions, which are obtained by minimizing the average forces
\langle f_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\Phi,T}
, respectively.

As shown and discussed in Ref.
[
81
,
82
]
, the least-squares solution to Eq. (
56
) is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(T)=-\left\langle u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\right\rangle_{\Phi,T}^{-1}\left\langle f_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\right\rangle_{\Phi,T}^{\phantom{-1}}\equiv\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial u_{i}^{\alpha}\partial u_{j}^{\beta}}\right\rangle_{\Phi,T}~,

(57)

i.e.
, the configuration average of the second derivative of the potential energy
V
with respect to the effective harmonic distribution of atomic positions at target temperature
T
. In
sTDEP
, the solution is obtained self-consistently by starting from a trial set of force constants
[
80
]
, and solving Eq. (
57
) until convergence. This solution is equivalent to minimizing the Gibbs-Bogoliubov free energy and yields the best trial free energy of harmonic form similar to the
stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation (SSCHA)
method
[
35
,
82
,
83
]
. Further practical details are given in
[
32
,
39
,
80
,
84
]
.

After second-order force constants are converged, third-order force constants are fitted to minimize the residual force
[
85
]
. From these, phonon lifetimes are computed as described in
[
85
]
, and the phonon spectral functions are computed as described in
[
86
,
87
,
88
,
32
]
. While beyond the scope of the current work, we note that including fourth-order force constants is possible as well
[
89
,
90
,
91
]
.

II.2
Lattice dynamics for polar materials

To describe long-range electrostatic interactions in polar materials, it is common practice to use the
ansatz
popularized by Gonze and coworkers for the interaction of atomic dipoles
{\bf{p}}_{i}=Z_{i}{\bf{u}}_{i}
induced by nuclear displacements
{\bf{u}}_{i}
, where
Z_{i}
is the Born effective charge tensor of atom
i

[
92
,
93
]
:
In an anisotropic dielectric, the dipole-dipole force constants can be written as
[
93
]

\displaystyle\left.\Phi^{\mathrm{dd}}\right|_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\delta}Z_{i}^{\alpha\gamma}Z_{j}^{\beta\delta}\widetilde{\Phi}_{ij}^{\gamma\delta}~,

(58)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{det}\epsilon)^{-1/2}\left[\frac{\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right)^{\alpha\beta}}{\lvert\Delta_{ij}\rvert_{\epsilon}^{3}}-3\frac{\Delta_{ij}^{\alpha}\Delta_{ij}^{\beta}}{\lvert\Delta_{ij}\rvert_{\epsilon}^{5}}\right]~,

(59)

where
\epsilon=1+4\pi\chi
is the ion-clamped dielectric tensor of the medium which can be used to define a metric space in which
\Delta_{ij}^{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}(\epsilon^{-1})^{\alpha\beta}r_{ij}^{\beta}
is the displacement vector between atom
i
and
j
, and

\lvert\Delta_{ij}\rvert_{\epsilon}=\sqrt{{\bf\Delta}_{ij}\cdot{\bf r}_{ij}}

[
94
]
.
Fourier transforming this expression into reciprocal space yields the well-known non-analytical dynamical matrix in the
{{\bf{q}}}\to 0
limit
[
95
,
92
]
,

\displaystyle\left.C^{\rm NA}\right|^{{\alpha\beta}}_{ij}(\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0)=\frac{4\pi}{V_{\rm uc}}\sum_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\frac{\left(q^{\alpha^{\prime}}Z_{i}^{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}\right)\left(q^{\beta^{\prime}}Z_{j}^{\beta^{\prime}\beta}\right)}{\mathbf{q}\cdot\epsilon\mathbf{q}}~,

(60)

where
V_{\rm uc}
is the unit cell volume. This mechanism is responsible for LO/TO splitting in polar materials, where an extended
longitudinal optical (LO)
mode generates a macroscopic electric field, and this extra work results in a higher frequency compared to the
transverse optical (TO)
counterpart
[
96
,
95
]
.

As comprehensively discussed by Zhou and coworkers in Ref.
[
97
]
, the force constants defined in Eq. (
59
) do not automatically fulfill basic physical properties like hermiticity and the acoustic sum rule in low-symmetry materials
[
98
]
. Several workarounds have been presented in the literature, such as imposing hermiticity on the full force constants via modifying the short-range force constants
[
97
]
, or by brute-force symmetrization
[
99
]
. In the present work, we implement an approach similar to the one suggested in
[
97
]
by modifying the short-range force constants to cancel sum-rule violations of the long-range model. In line with our previous work
[
79
,
39
]
, we impose space-group symmetry and hermiticity, as well as global translational, rotational, and Huang invariances. This way, we can use the long-range model by Gonze and coworkers for crystals of arbitrary symmetry in the way discussed in Ref.
[
32
]
while satisfying all of the aforementioned physical constraints.

We note in passing that the non-analytical dynamical matrix in Eq. (
60
) is valid for small but finite
{{\bf{q}}}
with
qc/\omega\gg 1
for which the electrostatic approximation holds
[
100
]
. This is typically satisfied in Raman backscattering where
\lvert{{\bf{q}}}|\approx 2k_{\rm i}
. However, for small-angle forward scattering, the resulting
\lvert{{\bf{q}}}|
can become so small that retardation effects become noticeable, leading to a strongly dispersive modification of Eq. (
60
) that restores the analytic behavior of the dynamical matrix. The resulting coupled photon-phonon modes are the so-called polaritons
[
101
,
102
]
.

II.3
Evaluation of susceptibility derivatives

To evaluate the susceptibility derivatives entering the Raman scattering cross section via Eq. (
31
), we first compute the real-space derivatives
\chi_{i}^{\gamma}
via central differences by displacing each atom in the unit cell by
\pm 0.01
Å and computing the susceptibility in the cell via
DFPT
. To obtain the susceptibility derivatives in mode space,
\chi_{{\bf{q}}s}
, the transformation is performed subsequently using the phonon eigenvectors
{{\bf{v}}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{{i{{\bf{q}}}s}}
. This means that for any input
{{\bf{q}}}
, the same set of susceptibility calculations can be reused, while the spectral function
{J_{{{\bf{q}}}s}}({\Omega})
needs to be recomputed. This reduces the number of
DFPT
calculations to
6N
, where
N
is the number of atoms in the unit cell.
We have verified for
GaN
that the resulting activities are insensitive to the size of the simulation cell, i. e., larger supercells yield the same Raman activities
[
103
]
. This justifies the averaging scheme leading to the definition of a susceptibility per unit cell entering Eq. (
3
).

In the present scheme, two effects are neglected: i) temperature renormalization of the mode activity defined in Eq. (
31
) is not accounted for
[
31
,
32
]
, and ii) the electro-optic effect is neglected, where the mode activity of an
LO
mode is changed by the coupling of its macroscopic field to the second-order nonlinear optical coeffcient
[
104
,
24
]
.

II.4
Experimental details

All measurements were performed in back-scattering geometry on a home-built Raman system based on a 1 m long Horiba FHR-1000 dispersive spectrometer with an 1800 gr/mm holographic grating and a Synapse Plus CCD detector (Horiba Inc.). Spectral resolution was
<

{}^{\text{-}1}

We used Ondax notch filters to allow access to the low-frequency region (

Undoped polished

Polarization
GaN

We used a 2.54 eV (488 nm) solid-state (Coherent Sapphire SF 488-100 CDRH) laser in ambient conditions with 60 mW excitation power.
To control the polarization of the incident and scattered light (5
∘

]
for details).

Temperature dependent
GaN

A 2.33 eV (532 nm) ultra narrow-band frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser was used (Coherent Prometheus 100) with 22 mW excitation power.
The temperature was set and controlled by a liquid-N
2
-cooled cryostat (ST-500, by Janis Inc.) and a temperature controller (Lakeshore, Model 335), allowing 30 mins of thermalization at the target temperature between measurements.

BaZrS
3

We used a 1.58 eV (785 nm) pump-diode laser (Toptica Inc., USA), which is below the band gap of
BaZrS

(1.9 eV) with 24 mW excitation power.
The sample was mounted into a liquid-He-cooled cryostat, cooled to 10 K at a rate of 1 K/min, and was allowed to thermalize for a few hours prior to measurement.

, which is not reflected in the experimental spectrum. However, around 600 cm
-1
a peak in the overtone
2\omega
-DOS
spectrum coincides with an increased response in the experimental spectrum, potentially hinting that this response stems from overtones of the Sulphur-dominated phonon modes around 300 cm
-1
which are barely active in first-order Raman scattering.

Given that the
2PDOS
gives a better estimate for the experimental background spectrum in
BaZrS
3
, we calculate another, closely related estimate, from the convolution of the Bose-weighted
DOS
, denoted as cDOS in Eq. (
55
). The comparison between 2PDOS and cDOS in
BaZrS
3
is shown in Fig.
8
. Both expressions yield a very similar spectrum for
BaZrS
3
, which means that crystal-momentum-conservation is of little importance for the computation of its 2PDOS. This finding can likely be generalized to other complex materials with many phonon branches and reinforces the use of cDOS to estimate second-order Raman scattering used in earlier work, c. f., Ref.
[
26
]
.
Motivated by the similarity of 2PDOS and cDOS  we use the computationally simpler cDOS in Fig.
6
(b) to correct the computed first-order spectrum with the background estimation based on the cDOS  which leads to overall excellent agreement of the spectra besides the second-order feature around 380 cm
-1
.

IV
Conclusion

We have presented an
ab initio
theory of the Raman scattering process in crystals at finite temperature and applied it to two exemplary materials,
GaN
and
BaZrS
3
, in comparison to the experiment. We have shown how to include the scattering geometry dependence, focusing on polarization dependence and the small but finite phonon momentum
{{\bf{q}}}
, which influences the phonon spectral function in optically anisotropic solids with LO/TO splitting. Furthermore, we have shown that perturbative phonon theory based on
DFT
simulations can provide an accurate way to describe the spectral properties of mildly anharmonic materials up to a complexity of 20 atoms in the unit cell in the case of
BaZrS
3
, establishing the presented method as a unified framework to treat Raman scattering including anharmonic effects in simple to complex materials on the same theoretical footing and in a numerically efficient way. This will be particularly interesting for materials with strong LO/TO splitting or appreciable nuclear quantum effects, for which
MD
-based methods require an additional effort
[
11
,
129
,
130
]
.

While accessing second-order Raman for complex materials within a phonon framework remains a big challenge
[
32
]
, we have discussed several common estimates for the broad spectral signatures of second-order Raman response in these systems and shown that
2PDOS
and a plain convolution of the Bose-weighted
DOS
(cDOS) are very similar in
BaZrS
3
, allowing to rapidly estimate the second-order background via the cDOS in line with previous work
[
26
]
.

For complex materials, higher-order Raman scattering can likely be more easily accessed with approaches that rely on
MD
, accelerated through machine-learned surrogate models for the interatomic forces and dielectric properties
[
131
,
132
,
129
,
58
,
54
,
57
,
55
,
56
]
, in particular when coupled with mode mapping for microscopic analysis
[
11
,
56
]
.

MD
-based approaches are also preferred for strongly anharmonic systems that feature effects like defect formation
[
133
,
134
]
, superionic diffusion
[
13
]
, or dynamic disorder
[
29
]
, which are not well described with a phonon picture assuming static reference positions. For anharmonic systems for which a renormalized phonon picture is adequate, the presented approach, as well as related approaches like the
time-dependent self-consistent harmonic approximation (TDSCHA)
, offer benefits in terms of computational effort and high precision
[
83
,
31
,
33
]
.

While briefly mentioned, non-backscattering geometries and birefringence have not been further discussed in this work. We leave this discussion to future work.

Another direction not discussed in this work is the inclusion of electronic excitations when using laser energies above the band gap of the material of interest, leading to effects like resonant scattering
[
135
,
136
]
. While computational approaches that take into account resonance effects exist
[
30
,
137
,
138
,
139
]
, they typically rely on the harmonic approximation and an otherwise unmodified scattering theory. Reconciling anharmonicity with electronic excitations is an interesting future direction.
