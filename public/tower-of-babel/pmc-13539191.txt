Comments on "Palliative resection versus palliative stenting for intestinal obstruction in patients with metastatic left-sided colonic cancer: a propensity score–matched analysis"
Authors: Rıza Deryol
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Lau et al. [1], which compared palliative resection and palliative stenting for intestinal obstruction in patients with metastatic left-sided colonic cancer using propensity score–matched analysis. The study addresses a clinically important dilemma in colorectal surgery: how to balance the short-term safety of endoscopic decompression against the potential durability of operative palliation and long-term disease control.

We commend the authors for evaluating not only survival but also clinically meaningful secondary outcomes, including morbidity, length of hospital stay, stoma formation, and readmission [1]. These outcomes were appropriately reported in the original study and provide important context for interpreting the short-term trade-offs between palliative resection and stenting. In patients with unresectable metastatic disease, such endpoints are particularly important because local symptom control must be considered alongside quality of life and the ability to proceed with systemic therapy [2, 3].

However, the clinical interpretation of these findings could be strengthened by clarifying which patient- and disease-related factors informed treatment selection in routine practice. The clinical value of this study would be further enhanced by a more explicit discussion of treatment selection criteria beyond the variables included in propensity score matching and beyond the limitations already acknowledged by the authors. In routine practice, the choice between palliative resection and stenting is often shaped by expected durability of decompression, peritoneal tumor burden, technical feasibility of stent placement, perforation risk, physiologic reserve, functional status, metastatic disease burden, and the urgency of initiating systemic treatment [2, 4]. Even after propensity score matching, residual confounding may persist if these clinically relevant factors are not fully captured or quantified. Clarifying how these elements were weighed would help readers apply the findings more appropriately in bedside decision-making.

A second point concerns oncologic intent in the palliative setting. Although palliative resection may provide more durable local control in selected patients, stenting may reduce procedural burden and support earlier recovery, which can be critical for patients with limited physiologic reserve or aggressive metastatic disease [2–4]. In this context, time to chemotherapy initiation, tolerance of systemic treatment, and avoidance of treatment interruption are clinically important outcomes that may influence the perceived benefit of each palliative strategy. Therefore, the most informative interpretation may not be which modality is universally superior, but which subgroup of patients is most likely to benefit from each strategy when local symptom control is considered together with systemic treatment planning.

Overall, this study is a timely and clinically useful contribution. We congratulate the authors and suggest that a brief expanded discussion of patient selection, residual confounding after propensity score matching, and integration with systemic treatment planning would further improve the study’s practical applicability.
