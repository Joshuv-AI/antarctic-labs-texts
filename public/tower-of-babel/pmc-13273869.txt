Delayed-onset portopulmonary hypertension following liver transplantation with near hemodynamic normalization on triple therapy
Authors: Maie Abdullah, Stephen Roche, John Mwangi, Liam Arnold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH) is a distinct subtype of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) occurring in the setting of portal hypertension. Although liver transplantation (LT) may relieve portal pressure, pulmonary vascular remodeling can persist or, rarely, develop de novo after transplantation, posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Delayed-onset PoPH after LT remains poorly characterized, with limited data to guide recognition and management.We describe a case of a 67-year-old male who developed severe PoPH three years after orthotopic LT for end-stage liver disease secondary to alcohol use and metabolic dysfunction– associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). He presented with hypoxic respiratory failure, dyspnea, and volume overload. Echocardiography and right-heart catheterization (RHC) confirmed severe precapillary pulmonary hypertension. Combination therapy with intravenous epoprostenol, macitentan, and sildenafil resulted in marked hemodynamic and clinical improvement. He was subsequently transitioned from intravenous epoprostenol to oral selexipag and has maintained near-normal pulmonary pressures for two years. This case highlights delayed post-LT PoPH and demonstrates that even late-presenting disease may achieve substantial hemodynamic improvement with aggressive, guideline-directed PAH therapy.

Introduction

Portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH) represents the intersection of hepatic and pulmonary vascular disease [[1], [2], [3]]. It occurs in 2–8 % of liver-transplant candidates and accounts for up to 10 % of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) cases referred to tertiary centers [4,5]. Historically, PoPH carried a poor prognosis, but outcomes have improved with modern vasodilator therapy [2,4,6,7].

The pathophysiology of PoPH, while not fully elucidated, is thought to involve portosystemic shunting that allows vasoactive mediators – including endothelin-1, serotonin, and thromboxane A2 – to reach the pulmonary circulation, causing vasoconstriction, inflammation, and progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling [2,3,8,9]. Additionally, genetic susceptibility (BMPR2, CAV1 variants) may predispose patients to PoPH [8,9]. Although normalization of portal pressure after LT often ameliorates these abnormalities, established remodeling may persist or, rarely, re-emerge [4,7,10].

We report a rare case of delayed post-transplant PoPH presenting with acute respiratory failure treated with combination pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male with end-stage liver disease due to alcohol use and MASLD cirrhosis, complicated by ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hepatic encephalopathy, presented with hepatorenal syndrome and a MELD score of 43. He underwent orthotopic liver transplantation. Pre-transplant evaluation included transthoracic echocardiography demonstrating an extracardiac right-to-left shunt with otherwise normal cardiac function and a right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) of 22 mmHg. Arterial blood gas analysis did not demonstrate clinically significant shunting.

His postoperative course was complicated by hypoxia-related cardiac arrests, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, shock, prolonged mechanical ventilation requiring tracheostomy, femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring surgical intervention, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, and upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Despite these complications, he was eventually discharged home in stable condition following rehabilitation. He remained clinically stable without cardiopulmonary limitations for three years following transplantation.

Three years post-LT, he presented with a two-week history of progressive dyspnea, hypoxia (oxygen saturation 82% on room air), and peripheral edema. He required non-invasive positive pressure ventilation on admission. After exclusion of alternative etiologies of pulmonary hypertension, a diagnosis of portopulmonary hypertension was established.

He was admitted to the intensive care unit and initiated on intravenous epoprostenol and sildenafil, resulting in improvement in respiratory status. He was discharged on epoprostenol infusion and sildenafil, with macitentan added during outpatient follow-up. 3 Epoprostenol was gradually titrated to a maximum dose of 23 ng/kg/min; however, he developed significant side effects including jaw pain, headache, abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea. Repeat RHC demonstrated marked improvement in pulmonary hemodynamics.

He was subsequently transitioned from intravenous epoprostenol to oral selexipag during a planned ICU admission over one week.

He remains clinically stable on selexipag 1600 mcg twice daily, sildenafil 20 mg three times daily, and macitentan 10 mg daily. At two-year follow-up, his six-minute walk distance was 345 m (above the lower limit of normal of 286 m). Repeat echocardiography demonstrated normalization of right ventricular size and function, with normal RVSP.

Repeat RHC confirmed sustained near-normalization of pulmonary pressures (Fig. 2). He is currently able to perform moderate physical activity, including playing golf, without supplemental oxygen.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of portopulmonary hypertension in the post-transplant setting. Notably, our patient developed severe PoPH three years after liver transplantation despite a normal pre-transplant cardiopulmonary evaluation.

Only a limited number of cases describing delayed-onset PoPH after LT have been reported, and the natural history of this condition remains poorly defined 10–12. This case adds to the growing body of literature suggesting that pulmonary vascular disease may persist, recur, or newly emerge following transplantation.

PoPH is a severe manifestation of liver disease associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, with reported five-year survival rates as low as 40% in registry studies [5]. Even among patients diagnosed with PoPH prior to liver transplantation, long-term pulmonary vasodilator therapy is often still required following transplant. In one series with a median follow-up of 7.8 years, four of six patients continued oral PAH therapy years after transplantation [11].

In contrast, delayed-onset PoPH diagnosed for the first time after liver transplantation remains exceedingly rare and has been described primarily in isolated case reports. In a United Kingdom registry cohort of 110 patients with PoPH, only one patient was diagnosed with PoPH after transplantation [7]. This case therefore contributes to the limited literature suggesting that pulmonary vascular disease may persist, recur, or newly emerge despite successful liver transplantation.

Reported cases of delayed-onset PoPH after liver transplantation can be broadly categorized into two groups: cases associated with graft dysfunction and cases occurring despite preserved graft function. Prior reports associated with graft dysfunction include patients who developed recurrent PSC with graft cirrhosis, hepatic artery stenosis, graft rejection, or liver abscesses preceding development of pulmonary hypertension [12]. In such cases, recurrent portal hypertension and liver dysfunction likely contributed to redevelopment of pulmonary vascular disease [13]. In another reported case, a 46-year-old man developed new-onset PoPH several months after LT in the setting of hepatic artery stenosis, liver abscesses, and eventual graft rejection. After re-transplantation, his hemodynamics improved and he was successfully weaned from epoprostenol [13]. These observations support the concept that recurrent portal hypertension and graft dysfunction may contribute to redevelopment of pulmonary vascular disease after transplantation.

In contrast, our patient maintained preserved graft function, suggesting a different pathophysiologic mechanism. At least one similar case has been reported in a patient who developed PoPH four years after transplantation despite preserved graft function and subsequently improved with combination pulmonary vasodilator therapy [10].

The mechanisms underlying delayed-onset PoPH remain incompletely understood. One potential explanation is persistence of endothelial dysfunction despite normalization of portal pressures following transplantation. Prolonged pre-transplant exposure to circulating vasoactive mediators—including endothelin-1, serotonin, estrogen, and thromboxane A2—may promote irreversible or slowly progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling [2,14]. The persistence or progression of pulmonary vascular disease despite normalization of portal pressures suggests that endothelial injury and vascular remodeling may continue beyond the hepatic disease state itself. In addition, calcineurin inhibitor–based immunosuppression may contribute to endothelial injury through reduced nitric oxide bioavailability and upregulation of endothelin pathways [8,9,15], potentially allowing pulmonary vascular disease to emerge or progress even after successful transplantation.

An additional intriguing feature in this case was the presence of an extracardiac right-to-left shunt identified on pre-transplant echocardiography. Although not clinically significant at the time, this finding may have reflected early hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) or broader pulmonary vascular dysregulation. It is possible that vasodilatory physiology related to HPS may have counterbalanced or masked evolving pulmonary vasoconstrictive disease prior to development of clinically overt PoPH.

Importantly, this case also demonstrates substantial hemodynamic improvement approaching near-normalization following aggressive combination therapy targeting the prostacyclin, endothelin, and nitric oxide pathways. While encouraging, this observation represents a single-patient experience and may not be generalizable across all cases of delayed-onset PoPH. Several factors may have contributed to the favorable response observed in this patient, including early recognition, prompt initiation of combination therapy, and potential patient-specific differences in treatment responsiveness.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PoPH in patients with a history of portal hypertension who present with unexplained dyspnea, even years after liver transplantation. Early evaluation with echocardiography followed by confirmatory right heart catheterization remains essential. In the present case, the patient underwent extensive evaluation for alternative causes of pulmonary hypertension, including CT pulmonary angiography, echocardiography to assess for left-sided heart disease, and autoimmune serologies. Given the patient's prolonged history of portal hypertension and absence of alternative identifiable etiologies, delayed-onset PoPH was considered the most likely diagnosis.

This case further suggests that pulmonary vascular remodeling in PoPH may persist or evolve despite successful transplantation and normalization of portal pressures, underscoring the need for continued long-term cardiopulmonary surveillance in selected post-transplant patients.

Conclusion

This case has several important clinical implications. First, it reinforces that PoPH should remain a diagnostic consideration in patients with a history of portal hypertension who develop unexplained dyspnea after liver transplantation, regardless of the time elapsed since transplant. Second, it highlights the importance of early hemodynamic evaluation and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy, as timely treatment may allow for substantial improvement of pulmonary pressures. Finally, it underscores that post-transplant PoPH does not uniformly portend irreversible disease and that durable clinical and hemodynamic recovery is possible with contemporary medical management.
