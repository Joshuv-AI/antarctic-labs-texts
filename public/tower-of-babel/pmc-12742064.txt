Knowledge, attitude and practices towards out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation among patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy in a Chinese hospital: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kunkun Liu, Fufen Zhang, Xiaofang Chen, Wenting Wang, Ruibing Xie, Tao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitude and practices (KAP) of patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy regarding out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThe study took place at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University in Haikou from January to June 2024.ParticipantsA total of 638 participants were included, with 339 (53.13%) being male. Among them, 232 (36.36%) had previously received out-of-operating-room anaesthesia during gastrointestinal endoscopy.InterventionsParticipants completed a questionnaire designed to evaluate their KAP related to anaesthesia and sedation.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes measured were knowledge scores (possible range: 0–10), attitude scores (possible range: 8–40) and practice scores (possible range: 6–30), which were derived from responses to the KAP questionnaire. Secondary outcomes included factors associated with proactive practices.ResultsMean scores for KAP were 4 (1, 7), 31 (30, 35) and 24 (20, 27), respectively. Multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that knowledge score (OR=1.210, p<0.001), attitude score (OR=1.273, p<0.001), female gender (OR=1.899, p=0.008) and agreeing to family presence (OR=1.772, p=0.014) were associated with proactive practices. Structural equation modelling showed knowledge directly influenced both attitude (β=0.761) and practices (β=0.733), while attitude directly influenced practices (β=0.791).ConclusionsPatients displayed inadequate knowledge but positive attitudes and proactive practices regarding out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation. Educational interventions by healthcare providers are recommended to improve patient knowledge, enhancing attitudes and practices.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, GASTROENTEROLOGY, Endoscopy

Introduction

The out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation have become increasingly common in gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures, addressing the need for enhanced comfort and patient satisfaction beyond the traditional operating room environment.1 These strategies play a critical role in alleviating discomfort and anxiety associated with endoscopic procedures, thereby enhancing both procedural quality and patient tolerance. However, an important clinical question persists: What is the extent of patients’ knowledge and feelings regarding out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation? Gaining insight into these dimensions is essential for improving patient care and ensuring that medical practices are aligned with patient expectations.2 3 Over the past decade, the demand for gastrointestinal endoscopy has steadily increased, underscoring its importance as a diagnostic and therapeutic tool.4 5 Notably, more than half of these procedures are performed under sedation, with anaesthesia services playing a pivotal role in ensuring patient comfort and procedural efficacy.6 7 The primary goal of sedation in gastrointestinal endoscopy is to reduce patient discomfort and anxiety, enhance the quality of the examination and minimise recall of the procedure, thus fostering a more tolerable experience for patients.8

The knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) model is a well-established framework in healthcare research, widely used to assess individuals’ understanding, attitude and behaviours towards medical interventions.9 The KAP questionnaire serves as a valuable tool for evaluating the KAP of target populations, as well as their acceptance and demand for healthcare interventions.10 Within this framework, knowledge refers to patients’ awareness and understanding of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia; attitude captures their concerns, preferences or confidence regarding the procedure; and practices reflect their actions, such as adherence to pre-procedural instructions, anxiety management, communication with healthcare providers and post-procedure self-monitoring, but not directly patient satisfaction. The KAP model postulates that knowledge positively influences attitude, which in turn guides individual practices and behaviours.11

With the increasing reliance on out-of-operating-room anaesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy, it is essential to evaluate how well patients understand these practices and how their attitude and behaviours affect their overall experience. Despite the growing importance of this topic, no studies have yet examined patients’ KAP regarding out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation in the context of gastrointestinal endoscopy in China. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further research. Therefore, this study aims to assess the KAP of patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy concerning out-of-operating-room anaesthesia, with the goal of identifying areas for improvement in patient education and procedural practices.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January and June 2024 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University, Haikou. Inclusion criteria: (1) patients receiving sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy; (2) the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification I–III; (3) aged between 18 and 85 years; (4) able to understand and complete the questionnaire, with assistance from medical staff if needed and (5) provided informed consent. All participants were scheduled patients undergoing anaesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy, and none had a history of sedation for this procedure. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with mental illness, consciousness disorders or communication difficulties; (2) patients with substance use disorder (SUD) and (3) long-term use (>3 months) of antidepressants, anti-anxiety medications or other psychotropic drugs.

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

A self-designed questionnaire was developed based on the previous literature.12 13 It was revised based on feedback from experts in the field of anaesthesiology and public health to enhance its clarity and relevance. A pilot study with 33 participants was conducted to assess the instrument’s reliability and validity. The questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.8685. Specifically, the reliability coefficients for each section were 0.8391 for knowledge, 0.8640 for attitude and 0.7289 for practices. The final version, administered in Chinese, comprised four sections: demographic characteristics (19 items), knowledge dimension (10 items), attitude dimension (8 items) and practices dimension (6 items).

In the knowledge dimension, correct responses were assigned 1 point, while incorrect or uncertain answers received 0 points, resulting in a total score range of 0–10. The attitude and practices dimensions were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 8 to 40 and 6 to 30, respectively. These total scores served as the primary outcome variables in the analysis. For the attitude dimension, most questions (1–5, 7–8) were scored from 5 (strongly positive) to 1 (strongly negative), while question 6 was reverse-scored from 1 to 5. In the practices dimension, responses were similarly scored from 5 to 1, with the score for question 1 averaged into the total score. A score above 70% of the maximum in each section was considered to indicate adequate knowledge, positive attitude or proactive practices.14 15

The electronic questionnaire was distributed using the Sojump platform (https://www.wjx.cn/) via a QR code shared through WeChat. Participants accessed and completed the questionnaire by scanning the QR code. A researcher was available to assist participants and verify the completeness, consistency and reasonableness of responses. Incomplete or logically inconsistent responses were deemed invalid.

Participants were recruited from the gastroenterology department of the hospital and were approached consecutively before undergoing endoscopy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York). The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s α. Descriptive analyses were conducted on demographic data and KAP scores. For continuous variables, normality tests were conducted. If the data followed a normal distribution, the mean and SD were reported; for non-normally distributed data, the median, 25th percentile (Q1) and 75th percentile (Q3) were used. Categorical variables, including demographic characteristics and responses to each question, were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %).

(1) For two-group comparisons, independent-sample t-tests were used for normally distributed data, while Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests were employed for non-normally distributed data. (2) For comparisons involving three or more groups, analysis of variance was applied to normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for non-normally distributed data.

Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to analyse the relationships between KAP scores. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify the factors associated with adequate knowledge, positive attitude and proactive practices. Variables with a p value <0.25 in univariate analysis, as recommended by Hosmer and Lemeshow, were included in the multivariate model. ORs and 95% CIs were reported. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was used to examine the relationships among the dimensions of KAP, with specific hypotheses: (1) knowledge has a direct impact on attitude; (2) knowledge directly influences practices and (3) attitude directly affects practices. A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 728 patients scheduled for gastrointestinal endoscopy were recruited. A total of 638 valid questionnaires were collected with a valid response rate of 87.64% (638/728). Among the participants, 339 (53.13%) were male, with a mean age of 51.05±14.40 years. Most participants (354, 55.49%) resided in rural areas, and 360 (56.43%) had an education level of middle school or below. A total of 342 (53.61%) participants had a body mass index (BMI) in the range of 18.5–23.9 kg/m². Additionally, 219 (34.33%) underwent gastrointestinal endoscopy for unexplained abdominal pain, and 232 (36.36%) had prior experience with out-of-operating-room anaesthesia during a gastrointestinal endoscopy.

The median scores for KAP were 4 (1, 7) (range: 0–10), 31 (30, 35) (range: 8–40) and 24 (20, 27) (range: 6–30), respectively. Knowledge scores varied significantly across demographic characteristics, including gender (p=0.009), education (p<0.001), household income per capita (p<0.001), medical insurance (p=0.015), smoking status (p<0.001), drinking status (p=0.040), history of gastrointestinal endoscopy (p=0.010), receipt of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia (p=0.040), awareness of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia (p<0.001) and reduction of preoperative anxiety by family member accompaniment (p=0.003). Attitude scores varied by residence (p=0.027), education (p=0.001), household income per capita (p<0.001), awareness of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia (p<0.001) and living alone (p=0.044). Practice scores differed by residence (p=0.023), education (p=0.032), household income per capita (p=0.019), smoking status (p=0.002), receipt of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia (p=0.025), awareness of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia (p=0.003) and reduction of preoperative anxiety by family member accompaniment (p<0.001) (table 1).

Distribution of KAP dimensions

In the knowledge dimension, the three items with the lowest accuracy were “Patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy should fast and avoid drinking for at least 2 hours before the procedure” (K4, 5.33% accuracy), “Patients with acute coronary syndrome, uncontrolled severe hypertension, severe arrhythmias, severe heart failure, acute respiratory infections or during an asthma attack may undergo sedation and anaesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy” (K7, 22.57% accuracy), and “Are you familiar with the preparation steps and process of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia?” (K8, 35.27% accuracy). In the attitude dimension, while most participants expressed positive views, 26.80% strongly agreed and 36.99% agreed with feeling anxious about post-procedure recovery (A6). Regarding practices, 23.67% rarely and 12.23% never experienced pre-procedural anxiety (P1). However, 18.50% rarely and 13.95% never received an anaesthesia evaluation before the procedure (P2), and 8.31% rarely and 6.11% never had a detailed pre-procedural discussion about sedation (P4) (table 2).

Multivariable analysis

Using 70% of the maximum score in each KAP section as the cut-off values, 519 participants (81.35%) had insufficient knowledge, 69 (10.82%) had negative attitude and 201 (31.50%) demonstrated suboptimal practices. These binary classifications were used as dependent variables in the logistic regression models shown in table 4.14 15 Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that a household monthly income of 2000–5000 renminbi (RMB) (OR=2.485, 95% CI 1.232 to 5.013, p=0.011), household monthly income of >5000 RMB (OR=3.270, 95% CI 1.410 to 7.584, p=0.006), BMI≥24.0 (OR=0.275, 95% CI 0.111 to 0.682, p=0.005) and experience of gastrointestinal endoscopy (OR=3.322, 95% CI 1.638 to 6.736, p<0.001) were independently associated with adequate knowledge. Knowledge score (OR=1.354, 95% CI 1.212 to 1.511, p<0.001), household monthly income of >5000 RMB (OR=2.46, 95% CI 1.006 to 6.041, p=0.048) and score of family atmosphere (OR=1.220, 95% CI 1.032 to 1.442, p=0.020) were independently associated with positive attitudes. Knowledge score (OR=1.234, 95% CI 1.145 to 1.329, p<0.001), attitude score (OR=1.263, 95% CI 1.179 to 1.352, p<0.001) and agreeing to family presence (OR=1.743, 95% CI 1.103 to 2.755, p=0.017) were independently associated with proactive practices (table 4).

The model demonstrated that knowledge had direct effects on attitude (β=0.761, p<0.001) and practices (β=0.733, p<0.001), while attitude had a direct effect on practices (β=0.791, p<0.001) (table 6 and figure 1).

Discussion

Patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy exhibited inadequate knowledge but generally positive attitude and proactive practices regarding out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation. Targeted educational interventions are necessary to improve patients’ understanding of anaesthesia and sedation, which could enhance their preparedness and reduce anxiety prior to procedures.

The results of this study demonstrate that while patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy exhibited inadequate knowledge about out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation, they generally displayed positive attitude and proactive practices. These findings align with previous research, which has also identified a knowledge gap among patients concerning anaesthesia procedures.16 17 It is notable that the demographic characteristics of patients in the Haikou region of Hainan reveal a predominantly low education level among participants, which significantly contributes to the observed knowledge deficiencies. This highlights the need for targeted educational interventions tailored to populations with lower educational attainment. It is plausible that this lack of knowledge contributes to ongoing suboptimal outcomes in patient compliance and satisfaction during gastrointestinal procedures. Moreover, despite the knowledge deficit, patients’ positive attitude and practices suggest a potential willingness to engage with healthcare providers and adhere to instructions, provided that they are given appropriate information and support. When analysing the relationships between household income, education and KAP, significant differences were observed. The univariate and multivariate analyses in this study revealed that individuals with higher monthly household income and higher levels of education exhibited better KAP. These findings suggest that socioeconomic status and education level play a critical role in shaping patient understanding and behaviours. For instance, individuals with college-level education or above demonstrated substantially higher knowledge scores, which translated into more positive attitudes and proactive practices. The merging of income and education categories in the revised analysis further underscores these trends, showing a strong link between better socioeconomic and educational backgrounds and improved KAP outcomes.

In terms of the relationships between KAP, the correlation analyses and SEM results jointly reveal significant positive associations between KAP, which are consistent with prior studies on patient education in medical settings.18 19 Specifically, higher knowledge scores were strongly linked to more positive attitude and more proactive practices, highlighting the crucial role that patient knowledge plays in shaping their attitude and behaviours. This finding underscores the importance of improving patient education as a means to enhance their overall experience and compliance with medical procedures. Furthermore, the SEM results confirmed that attitude also had a direct effect on practices, emphasising that fostering positive attitude through targeted interventions could directly enhance patient practices.

When analysing the significant differences across demographic variables, both univariate and multivariate analyses indicated that household income was a critical factor influencing KAP. For example, participants with a monthly household income of >5000 showed significantly higher knowledge scores, which also translated into more positive attitude and proactive practices. Similarly, those with prior experience of out-of-operating-room anaesthesia had better knowledge and practices scores, which was further supported by multivariate analysis. These findings suggest that socioeconomic factors and previous medical experiences play a substantial role in shaping patient KAP, likely due to better access to healthcare resources and information.20 Furthermore, participants with a normal BMI demonstrated better knowledge scores, which is consistent with conventional evidence that healthier individuals tend to have greater health awareness and more proactive health behaviours. In addition, higher satisfaction with family atmosphere was linked to more positive attitudes, suggesting that supportive family environments may enhance patients’ perceptions and willingness to engage in recommended practices.

Regarding the distribution of the knowledge dimension, the results highlight several areas where participants demonstrated inadequate understanding, particularly in topics such as fasting requirements before endoscopy and the conditions contraindicating sedation and anaesthesia. This lack of awareness has been observed in similar studies and could be attributed to insufficient patient education.21 22 To address these gaps, specific educational programmes could be implemented, focusing on pre-procedural preparation and the risks associated with anaesthesia. These interventions should include clear, accessible information, possibly delivered through multimedia platforms or in consultation settings, ensuring patients fully understand the critical aspects of their procedure. Additionally, healthcare providers should routinely assess patients’ understanding before procedures, with follow-up educational materials provided post-procedure to reinforce important information.23 24

In terms of attitude dimension results, most patients exhibited positive attitude, particularly in their belief that sedation and anaesthesia could alleviate procedural anxiety. However, a notable proportion expressed anxiety about the post-procedure process, a finding consistent with other studies on patient anxiety related to gastrointestinal procedures.25 26 This suggests that although patients generally trust the sedation process, they remain uncertain about post-procedure recovery. Addressing this concern could involve providing comprehensive post-procedural counselling and detailed information on recovery expectations. Implementing structured follow-up support, such as phone consultations or digital health monitoring, could help alleviate patient anxiety and improve overall satisfaction with the procedure.27 28

For the practices dimension, while most patients reported following instructions and engaging with healthcare providers, there were deficiencies in some areas. For instance, only 53.61% of patients received thorough discussions about sedation and anaesthesia from healthcare providers, suggesting a communication gap. This mirrors findings from similar research, where inadequate communication was linked to lower patient satisfaction and compliance.29 30 To improve this, healthcare professionals should be trained to offer more comprehensive and patient-friendly discussions about the procedure. Additionally, protocols ensuring that all patients undergo a detailed pre-procedure consultation, regardless of their prior experience, could help mitigate these gaps. For lower-income or less-educated patients, these efforts should be particularly targeted, as multivariate analysis showed these groups are more likely to have lower KAP scores, reinforcing the need for tailored communication strategies.31 32

However, this study has several limitations. First, the use of self-reported questionnaires may introduce response bias, as participants might overestimate or underestimate their KAP. Second, the study was conducted at a single hospital, limiting the generalisability of the findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Larger multicentre studies involving diverse patient populations across different regions are needed to validate and generalise the findings. Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causality between KAP, which would require longitudinal studies for more robust conclusions. Additionally, although the questionnaire was developed through expert consultation and pilot testing, the possibility of ambiguous or misinterpreted items cannot be entirely ruled out. Moreover, the study did not explore local cultural or traditional beliefs that may shape patients’ attitudes towards anaesthesia and sedation, which could have provided additional contextual understanding.

Conclusions

Patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy in the Haikou region of Hainan predominantly exhibited lower education levels, contributing to significant knowledge deficits. Despite this, they generally showed a positive attitude and proactive practices concerning out-of-operating-room anaesthesia and sedation. Simultaneously, integrating into clinical practices optimises the preoperative visit for gastroscopy and enteroscopy anaesthesia sedation. Before the visit starts, patients fill out questionnaires to understand their basics and identify key concerns. Then, medical staff can have more precise and in-depth exchanges with patients, enabling targeted educational interventions, enhancing patient understanding, alleviating fear and anxiety, creating a better medical experience and promoting service quality improvement.
