A higher serum lipase cut-off improves the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis in patients with non-epigastric abdominal pain
Authors: Sarah Jahangir, Arti Anand, Mohamed Elmallahy, Hally Chaffin, Prasad Rajalingamgari, Mahmoud Morsy, Megan Summers, Shubham Trivedi, Yu-Hui Chang, Christine Snozek, Vijay P Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute pancreatitis (AP) may present with non-epigastric, i.e., atypical pain. Current guidelines recommend the same diagnostic lipase cut-off irrespective of pain location, which increases cross-sectional imaging requirements. 863 emergency department patients with serum lipases >3-times upper-limit of normal (ULN) were accrued over 6 years. Alternate lipase cut-offs for atypical pain were studied in training (n = 539) and validation cohorts (n = 324) using imaging-proven AP as the reference. At >3-times ULN lipase cut-off, 15% (i.e., 129/863) patients had atypical pain. These were imaged more often (89% vs. 75%) but had lower AP diagnoses (34% vs. 62%) than others. A >7.5-times ULN cut-off for atypical pain reduced imaging by 40%, increased imaging-proven AP diagnosis (56–77% in both cohorts), increased specificity by 2-fold, while retaining sensitivity of ≥95%. Therefore, a >7.5-fold ULN improves AP diagnosis in atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain. This may reduce costs, expedite AP management when cross-sectional imaging is not feasible, and improve outcomes.Subject areas: Endocrinology, Public health

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) typically presents with acute epigastric abdominal pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting. There were 5.8 million emergency department visits in the United States (US) for abdominal pain in 2018, with AP diagnosed in 288,220 patients.1 The incidence of AP has increased since then.1 In 2007, AP cost $2.2 billion/year,2 at $9870/case.2 More recent estimates are $22817/case.1 Early management of AP with intravenous (IV) fluids such as normal saline or Ringer's Lactate improves AP outcomes. These are inexpensive and widely available, including in rural settings. Thus, an early, easy, and accurate AP diagnosis may hasten management, reduce severity, improve outcomes, shorten hospitalization, and reduce disease burden even when diagnostic imaging is unavailable or unfeasible.

The Revised Atlanta Criteria,3 the 2024 guidelines of the American College of Gastroenterology,4 and the most recent International Association of Pancreatology Revised Guidelines on Acute Pancreatitis 20255 diagnose AP based on 2/3 criteria using the same lipase cut-off irrespective of the location of pain. The Revised Atlanta Criteria3 which were the reference when this article was written describe the 3 criteria as [A] typical persistent epigastric pain often radiating to the back (yellow in Figure 1A), [B] Serum amylase and/or lipase ≥ 3-times the upper limit of normal (ULN), [C] Characteristic findings of AP on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) or other abdominal imaging. Therefore, in patients with serum lipase >3-fold ULN, a typical, i.e., epigastric pain location does not require abdominal imaging to diagnose AP; however, an atypical pain location (i.e., non-epigastric) requires imaging to diagnose AP. This increases the requirement of abdominal imaging in patients with atypical-pain, which can sometimes be impractical, delay AP diagnosis, management, and may thus lead to worse outcomes.

AP mostly causes typical epigastric pain. However, atypical non-epigastric pain locations (red in Figure 1A) are also possible. A previous study6 showed ⁓10% among 320 patients with lipase >3-times ULN had atypical pain. These patients trended toward a lower rate of AP diagnosis compared to those presenting with typical pain.6 The current ≥ 3x ULN cut-offs of amylase or lipase for diagnosing AP are based on patients with epigastric pain, or AP verified on surgery or imaging.7,8,9,10 The studies showed ≥ 3x ULN cut-off provided a specificity of ≥93% and sensitivity ≥55% for AP. However, they focused on typical epigastric pain without discussing atypical pain. Moreover, the current guidelines retain the >3x ULN (in our institution, >180 U/L lipase with our ULN being 60 U/L) cut-off irrespective of pain type.

Imaging for abdominal pain in US emergency departments has increased from 3.9% in 1997 to 37.8% in 2016.11 The American College of Radiology recommends “appropriateness criteria” for imaging in AP. Appropriateness includes suspected AP with atypical signs and symptoms, an equivocal amylase and lipase increase, or when other diagnoses (e.g., bowel perforation or bowel ischemia) are possible.12 The risks of CT imaging, e.g., of radiation in women of childbearing age, of IV contrast, and large variations in access to imaging worldwide, reinforce the need to evaluate a simple and rapid diagnostic test for atypical pain that is low risk, economical, and reliable enough to initiate treatment for AP along with clinical criteria.

The current study thus determined the ideal lipase cut-offs for diagnosing AP in patients with atypical pain using positive abdominal imaging as a reference. Amylase was not included due to its lower specificity for pancreatic origin. In the past, several seminal studies that have shaped clinical practice had a small number of patients from a single institution.13,14,15 The need for clinical awareness on how to approach lipase elevation in atypical pain thus requires this study to be analyzed as a single center index study that may help improve AP diagnosis using a previous widely used, low cost, consistent, rapid turnaround diagnostic blood test. Studies that reproduce, improvise, refute, or support the generalizability of the concept can come in the future if the current work is scientifically valid and clinically relevant.

Results

At ≥3 upper limit of normal lipase, more atypical, i.e., patients with non-epigastric abdominal pain are imaged, with a low diagnostic yield

While pain in AP is mostly typical (yellow, Figure 1A), it can also be atypical pain, i.e., in one or more non-epigastric locations (red area, Figure 1A), which the current guidelines do not discuss.3,4 To study relevant lipase cut-offs, a training cohort of 539 emergency room patients with lipase levels ≥3-fold ULN, i.e., ≥180 U/L (peach, Figure 1B), was studied separately from the 324 validation cohort patients (blue, Figure 1B). Sixty-three (12%) training cohort patients had atypical pain. These were compared with the “other” patients (typical pain or painless) with a lipase ≥3-fold ULN (Figure 1C).

Although the demographics and body mass index (BMI) were like “others,” patients with atypical pain presented later (median 2 days vs. 1 day). Fewer had AP on abdominal imaging (29%, n = 16/55) vs. “others” (64%, n = 219/344) (Figure 1C), despite more patients with atypical pain undergoing abdominal imaging (87%, n = 55/63) compared to the other group (72%, n = 344/476).

Noting this trend, we went on to identify unique characteristics in patients with atypical pain diagnosed with AP, and further to validate or refute these characteristics in a validation cohort accrued later (blue shade, Figure 1B).

Atypical, i.e., patients with non-epigastric pain with acute pancreatitis have a serum lipase ≥7.5-fold upper limit of normal

CECT was the most common imaging (47/55) performed, consistent with the revised Atlanta criteria.3 The remaining 8 patients with imaging underwent non-contrast CT (n = 5), abdominal ultrasound (n = 1), and MRI (n = 2). The 16/55 patients with atypical pain who were diagnosed with AP on imaging had lipase levels significantly higher (Mean ± SD: 1546 ± 1021 U/L) vs. imaging negative patients (611 ± 660 U/L). Their lipase levels are shown in Figure 2C (red dots). The 2 of 8 patients with atypical pain clinically diagnosed with AP (without imaging) had similarly high lipase levels (green dots, Figure 2C; mean ± SD: 1952 ± 1413 U/L) as those clinically negative for AP (714 ± 816 U/L). BMI of patients with positive imaging was higher (mean ± SD: 30 ± 7 kg/m2) versus those without AP (24 ± 5 kg/m2), but both groups had a similar pain duration (Figure 2B). Based on the training data, we fitted two logistic regression models. Model 1 included lipase as a continuous independent variable, and model 2 included lipase and BMI as independent variables (both were treated as continuous variables). The results showed that BMI was not a confounder (p = 0.08), and the beta coefficient for lipase did not change after BMI was added to the model (0.001 in Model 1 and 0.001 in Model 2). Furthermore, adding BMI did not improve the diagnostic performance, with the AUC changing from 0.88 to 0.86 (data not shown). Therefore, neither BMI nor pain duration adds further diagnostic value.

The receiver operator curve (ROC) generated with the lipase levels and imaging-based diagnosis of AP in patients with atypical pain revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.88 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.80–0.97 (red line, Figure 2D). Inclusion of 8 patients without imaging did not alter the ROC (green line, Figure 2E). Here, increasing the lipase cut-off till 450 U/L retained a 100% sensitivity while improving specificity (Figures S2A and S2B). A lipase cut-off of >450 U/L (7.5-fold ULN) yielded a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI 81–100%), and specificity of 74% (95% CI 59–85%) with a likelihood ratio of 3.9 (Figure 2F). This was significantly greater than the ≤28% (95% CI; 0.13–44%) specificity noted at lower lipase cut-offs up to 300 U/L (i.e., 5-fold ULN). When the Youden’s index was used to identify the threshold value, a value of 556 U/L yielded a Sensitivity of 93.8% (95% CI: 71.7–98.9), Specificity of 82.1% (95% CI: 67.3–91.0%), and a likelihood ratio of 5.2. Since the 95% confidence intervals of the >450 U/L cut-off overlapped with that of the Youden’s index, there was no difference between the two cut-offs. We thus chose the less stringent >450 U/L value, which was closer to the conventional > 3-fold cut off and provided 100% sensitivity. As shown in Figures 2E and 2G, inclusion of the 8 patients with atypical pain who did not undergo imaging did not significantly affect the AUC of the ROC curve, sensitivity, or specificity (note overlapping 95% confidence intervals) compared to those who were diagnosed solely based on imaging (Figures 2D and 2F).

Since the comparisons so far were with the “other” group, we next studied if the diagnostic lipase cut-off in typical pain differs from the painless group. On combining both these cohorts in the “other” group, as shown in Figure S3A, patients with painless lipase elevation were older, had a lower serum lipase with a lower proportion imaged, and a lower diagnosis of AP. However, changing the lipase cut-off in either group did not improve AP diagnosis (Figures S3B and S3C). We thus evaluated the diagnostic utility of the 7.5-foldULN cut-off for atypical pain in the validation cohort while retaining comparisons to “others” when needed.

Atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain patterns, are similar in patients with acute pancreatitis and non-acute pancreatitis

We first compared the demographics in patients with atypical pain of the two cohorts before testing the lipase cut-off. The validation cohort had 324 patients with a serum lipase ≥3-fold ULN. Among these, 66 patients (20%) presented with atypical abdominal pain. Comparison of patients with atypical pain of both cohorts revealed similar demographics and lipase levels (Figure 3A). Figures 3B–3D show the location of atypical pain in the training (red) and validation cohorts (blue). Pain in a single quadrant is shown as dots, and that extending beyond one quadrant is shown as lines. Diffuse abdominal pain, the most common presentation, is shown in gray. There was no characteristic pain pattern unique among patients with AP (Figure 3B) or non-AP (Figures 3C and 3D). Hence, a diagnosis of AP cannot be suspected based on the location of atypical pain.

A lipase cut-off ≥7.5-times upper limit of normal in atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain improves diagnostic yield and could reduce abdominal imaging

Sixty out of 66 (91%) patients with atypical pain in the validation cohort underwent abdominal imaging, including 7 with non-contrast CT and 1 with abdominal ultrasound (Figure 4A). Among these, the 23 of 60 (38%) diagnosed with AP also had significantly higher lipase (Mean ± SD: 1603 ± 1113 U/L) than patients with non-AP (571 ± 487 U/L; p < 0.0001, Figure 4B and blue dots in Figure 4C). Patients clinically diagnosed with AP (green dots, Figure 3C) also had higher lipase levels (mean ± SD: 1026 ± 536 U/L) vs. clinically negative patients (353 ± 50 U/L).

The imaging-based ROC curve of the validation cohort had an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.72–0.94; Figure 4D). A diagnostic lipase of ≥450 U/L (≥7.5-fold ULN) yielded a sensitivity of 91% (CI: 73–98%) and specificity of 56% (CI: 40–70%) for AP (Figure 4F). The validation cohort ROC after including a clinical diagnosis of AP was similar (AUC 0.84, 95% CI 0.75–0.94) (Figure 4E), with an overall likelihood ratio of 2.2 (Figure 4G). The AUC from the training set was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.80–0.97) and is 0.83 (95% CI: 0.72–0.94) from the validation data. Based on Delong’s test, there was no difference in the AUCs between training and validation data (p = 0.70).

Overall, 115/129 patients with atypical pain were imaged (Figure 5A). Thirty-nine had AP on imaging, and 5 were clinically diagnosed without imaging. Interestingly, 14/76 patients with negative imaging were clinically diagnosed with AP (dark green, Figure 5A) as detailed in Figure S4A. Figure 5B combines patients with atypical pain from both cohorts and compares these to the “others” group. Both groups were similar, except for minor differences in BMI, serum lipase, and miscellaneous etiologies detailed in Figure S1B. As seen in Figures S5A and S5B, the specificity of AP diagnosis increased gradually up to 450 U/L lipase while keeping sensitivity ≥95% in patients with atypical pain irrespective of whether the diagnosis was purely imaging based or imaging along with clinical judgment. A lipase ≥450 U/L (>7.5-fold ULN) also increased the diagnostic yield of abdominal imaging for AP from 34% to 56%, making it equivalent to the “others” group (62%) (gray shaded area in the middle of Figure 5B). Similarly, the higher cut-off in atypical pain increased AP diagnosis to 60% when combining imaging and clinical criteria and from 45% to 77% when AP was diagnosed irrespective of imaging (gray shade in the middle of Figure 5B). The greater proportion of patients with atypical pain imaged at a lipase >180 U/L, i.e., 115/129 (89%) vs. the “others” group (75%, n = 552/734; p < 0.001, Figures 5C and 5D) was also reduced to 51% (66/129; p < 0.001) at a lipase cut-off of ≥450 U/L. To determine whether the predicted probabilities at the >7.5-fold ULN were well calibrated, we combined training and validation data and fit a logistic regression model to generate a calibration plot. In the model, lipase was treated as a binary variable using 450 U/L as the cutoff value. The mean predicted and observed proportion of acute pancreatitis for each quintile is shown in Figure S6A, and the calibration plot in Figure S6B. As can be seen, the observed probabilities aligned well with the predicted probabilities when the probability was <0.5. Due to the small sample size, the probabilities >0.5 may not seem to align well between the observed and predicted probabilities. More data or larger studies are needed in order to generate a calibration plot with better accuracy or better reflect the true performance.

The overall diagnostic accuracy of serum lipase in atypical pain based on imaging (reference method) is summarized in Figure 6A. A ≥450 U/L cut-off (≥7.5-fold ULN) had a sensitivity of 95% and increased the specificity to 64% (95% CI 53–74%) from ≤26% (95% CI 0.5–37%) at a cut-off 180-300 U/L, which were 97–100% sensitive. This also improved the likelihood ratio from 1.0–1.3 to 2.7. The ROC for this is shown to the right (AUC 0.85; 95% CI 0.78–0.92). These patterns were similar on combining the gold standard with a solely clinical diagnosis, even when the diagnosis was irrespective of imaging (Figures 6B and 6C). Therefore, increasing the diagnostic cut-off to ≥7.5-fold ULN improved the specificity of AP diagnosis by > 2-fold, and would reduce imaging requirements for AP diagnosis by 40% while retaining a sensitivity of ⁓95%.

>70% of patients with atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain, a non-acute pancreatitis diagnosis has a serum lipase <7.5-fold upper limit of normal

Lastly, we went on to study the non-AP diagnoses of patients with atypical pain with serum lipase >3-fold ULN. These excluded the 44 patients positive for AP on imaging and the 14 with AP diagnosed clinically despite negative imaging (Figure S4A). Please note, 12/14 among these had a lipase ≥450 U/L and would have qualified as AP. Of the remaining 71 patients with non-AP (peach box, Figure 5A), 52 had a lipase <450 U/L ULN. These included non-specific abdominal pain (n = 10), functional GI disorders such as cyclical vomiting syndrome, gastroenteritis, or diverticulitis (n = 8), and inflammatory bowel disease (n = 5). (Figure S4B). The diagnoses of 19/71 with lipase >450 U/L, i.e., >7.5-fold ULN, included small bowel obstruction (4), acute kidney injury (4), hepatobiliary pathology including autoimmune cholangiopathy, cholecystitis, periampullary mass and malignant biliary obstruction (5), liver transplant (2), and one each of COVID-19, ovarian abscess, testicular pain, and dehydration. Thus, patients with lipase elevation >7.5-fold ULN may have commonly available blood, urine tests, or plain abdominal X-rays to support non-AP causes mentioned above. A diagnosis of AP or other hepatobiliary pathology can be looked at for the remaining patients with dedicated imaging if these simpler modalities are equivocal. Therefore, serum lipase <7.5-fold ULN in patients with pain atypical for AP strongly suggests a non-AP cause.

Discussion

This study shows the current diagnostic lipase cut-off for AP, i.e., ≥3-fold ULN, increases the proportion of patients with imaged atypical (i.e., non-epigastric) pain without improving diagnostic yield. Out of the 863 patients presenting with lipase >3-fold ULN, 15% (n = 129) had atypical pain. Among these, 89% were imaged compared to 75% in “others,” but had a lower diagnostic yield for AP at 34%, compared to 62% in “others.” Altering the lipase cut-off in the typical pain or painless groups comprising “others” provided no benefit (Figure S3). However, in patients with atypical pain, increasing the diagnostic lipase cut-off to ≥7.5-fold ULN (>450 U/L) increased AP diagnosis to 56% when supported by imaging and to 77% when irrespective of imaging. These numbers retained a sensitivity of 95% for diagnosing AP and are similar to “others.” Moreover, the higher cut-off for AP diagnosis reduced imaging requirements by 40%. Therefore, increasing the current diagnostic lipase cut-off for atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain from ≥3-fold ULN to ≥7.5-fold ULN may expedite AP management with a rapid and accurate diagnosis, especially when imaging resources are limited. We can estimate the economic and health impact of applying the ≥7.5-fold cut-off based on previous studies. The average cost of an abdominal CT scan for diagnosing AP was estimated to be $4510 in 2019.16 The most recent update estimates 4.8 million ER visits for abdominal pain per year in the USA, with 240,000 cases diagnosed with AP.17 Assuming 1 in 4 patients (i.e., 1.2 million) with abdominal pain had lipase testing, 1 in 3 of whom (400,000) were ≥3-fold ULN, and 15% of these had atypical pain, would bring the total atypical-pain cases requiring imaging to 60,000 cases, among whom 1 in 3 (i.e., 20,000 cases, i.e.,⁓ 8% of AP diagnosis) would have imaging proven AP based on the table in Figure 5B. Raising the lipase cut-off to ≥7.5-fold ULN would therefore reduce imaging requirements in patients with atypical-pain to ⁓half (i.e., 66/129, based on Figure 5B), thus reducing 30,000 CT scans/year, with an estimated saving of $135 million/year. While these savings seem modest compared to the annual cost of AP ($3 billion/year17), the additional benefit of expediting fluid resuscitation in scenarios where imaging is impractical or unavailable cannot be underestimated.

Being a single center study with >800 patients and 2 cohorts accrued over 6 years, and using the same lipase assay has its strengths since the consistent normal ranges and lipase cut-offs18 provide a solid foundation for analysis and conclusions over the entire study duration. The newly proposed cut-off, i.e., ≥450 U/L (ULN 60 U/L) translates to ≥7.5-fold ULN, and this ULN based cut-off can be tested at other institutions that may have different normal ranges for serum pancreatic lipase. Other strengths include the high rate of pancreatic imaging (667/863, i.e., 77% overall) at our institution to diagnose AP. Imaging provides a dependable reference to compare lipase cut-offs. This is especially notable among the 89% (115/129) imaged in patients with atypical pain, among whom 99 (77%) had CECTs in agreement with the revised Atlanta criteria for AP.3 Moreover, similar AUCs of ROCs ranging from 0.83 to 0.88 (Figures 2D, 2E, 4D, and 4E) for imaging positive AP and a pure clinical diagnosis further add strength to the conclusions.

Technically, our study was prospective since it was in real time and included all comers with >3-fold lipase elevation with predefined exclusions, i.e., H/O pancreatic transplant or cyst-gastrostomy, and therefore had no selection bias. Additionally, the diagnosis of pancreatitis was not made by us by looking back at data, and therefore, there is no selection bias. Pancreatitis was diagnosed by physicians after lipase testing in patients with ER, and therefore, retrospective selection bias of first choosing patients with pancreatitis and then looking at their lipase is not relevant to our studies. Similarly, the weaknesses of tools to accrue retrospective data (e.g., diagnostic codes) are not relevant. Therefore, while the study was technically prospective, in compliance with the STROBE guideline,19 we have not used the terms prospective or retrospective, but clearly described how the study was conducted.

There are numerous causes for lipase elevation other than pancreatitis that may contribute to a higher baseline in patients with atypical abdominal pain, the commonest being liver and renal failure, shock, and malignancy.20,21 Lipase elevation is also noted after burns, trauma, hemorrhage, and critical illnesses,22,23,24,25,26 and previous studies have proposed a higher cut-off of 532 IU/L to diagnose pancreatitis in critically ill patients.21 Moreover, the pancreas is exquisitely sensitive to ischemia during hemorrhage,27 endotoxin infusion,28 and sepsis.29,30 These factors may result in higher cut-off requirements in patients with atypical pain.

While our study is small, it introduces a concept that is largely supported by the findings. A single center study that introduces a concept is not a weakness but rather avoids the issues of heterogeneity (e.g., type of lipase assay, proportion imaged, and so forth) that result from multi-institutional studies. Small, single-center studies have previously led to changes in clinical management. E.g. the first trial of anti-TNF agents in Crohn’s disease from the Academic Medical Center, Amsterdam was a single arm study with 10 patients.13 The seminal study showing the benefits of neostigmine for Ogilvie’s syndrome from the University of Washington had only 10–11 patients in each arm.15 The first reports on H. pylori in gastric biopsy samples was on 135 gastric biopsy samples by J. Robbin Warren, and the detailed morphological description of their length and spirals by Barry Marshal did not specify the number of samples examined or replicates.14 These seminal studies have been reexamined over the last 3-4 decades in numerous ways and have excelled. Similarly, the current work needs scientific and clinical scrutiny at this stage. Invoking the need for larger multi-institutional studies to reproduce, improvise, refute, or support the generalizability of the concept would be premature at this stage. These may follow if the study passes relevant scrutiny.

Future larger studies or studies at other institutions are needed to validate the >7.5-fold ULN cut-off, especially since this cut-off questions the universal application of the >3-fold ULN guidelines that are currently followed irrespective of pain type. This study has a size comparable to previous studies on AP.31,32,33 A previous study proposing a higher lipase cut-off (532 U/L) to diagnose AP in critically ill patients had 417 patients.21 Our study also provides an approach for larger, multi-institutional studies in the future. The critical points for future studies include: (A) Using a fold-ULN cut-off may be more helpful than numerical lipase (U/L) values across institutions to compare diagnostic accuracy. (B) A reliable independent reference, such as CECT, to support AP diagnosis is essential to validate the cut off. (C) The lipase assay used should be specific for pancreatic lipase, since less specific assays used in the past measured other lipases such as lipoprotein lipase.34

In summary, we note that in patients with atypical, i.e., non-epigastric pain, a higher lipase cut-off, i.e., >7.5-fold ULN, improves the specificity for AP diagnosis vs. the currently recommended >3-fold cut-off. The higher cut-off retains a sensitivity of 95% and reduces the requirement for dedicated pancreas imaging, which is needed more often when making an AP diagnosis at the current cut-off. Furthermore, increasing the lipase cut-off to > 7.5-fold ULN in atypical pain may hasten AP diagnosis, improve its management and outcomes especially when such imaging is unavailable or contraindicated. Whether our findings in patients with atypical pain are reproduced by other investigators or institutions in the future remains to be seen.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of our study is that our comparison group for atypical pain was the “other” group, consisting of 734 patients, with >75% having typical pain. The “no-pain” group that formed the remaining was combined with “typical” as “others.” However, we note that both these groups are best diagnosed with the currently accepted lipase cut-off of >3-fold ULN (Figure S3). Thus, the differences between atypical pain and “others” are valid and negate the need to separate the 3 separate groups when comparing all parameters. Another limitation of our study is that we only used the lipase assay, since it is the sole assay consistently used for AP diagnosis, in our institution, and <5% patients had a concurrent amylase assay done. Whether the >7.5-fold cut-off is relevant to serum amylase levels in those with atypical pain remains unknown.

Experimental model and study participant details

Accrual

Each patient presenting to the Mayo Clinic Arizona emergency department with a lipase level >3-fold ULN were flagged automatically when the result was released and prospectively followed. This resulted in 539 patients being in the training cohort from March 2016 till March 2021. The validation cohort (n = 324) was from April 2021 to December 2022. Please note, the STROBE guidelines recommend that authors refrain from calling a study prospective or retrospective.19 Parameters followed included demographics, lipase, pain location, abdominal imaging findings, dismissal diagnosis and AP etiology. The allocation to the non-epigastric or atypical pain group was based on the location of pain documented in the clinical records.

Lipase testing was performed using the Roche colorimetric assay. Data was entered in a secure excel sheet. The Institutional Review Board of Mayo Foundation approved the study. Exclusions included pancreatic transplant or pancreatic surgery like cyst-gastrostomy. A total of 863 participants were thus included in this study. The mean age of the study population was 56 ± 18 years. The sample size comprised 47% females (n = 409) and 53% males (n = 454), with no significant sex-based differences in location of pain, imaging or diagnosis of AP observed between cohorts, suggesting that sex was unlikely to have influenced the study outcomes. The majority of participants identified as White (83%), followed by African American (4%), Hispanic (4%), Asian (3%), and Native American (2%), while race was not documented for 4% of participants. AP etiology and demographics of both cohorts combined is detailed in Figures S1A and S1B.

Method details

Characterization of abdominal pain

This was from the emergency room provider’s description of pain and characterized as: 1) typical pain, i.e., epigastric pain with/without radiation to the back (yellow Figure 1A). This definition of typical pain was based on Revised Atlanta Criteria3 which defines abdominal pain consistent with acute pancreatitis as acute onset of a persistent, severe, epigastric pain often radiating to the back. 2) Atypical pain, i.e., Non-epigastric location of pain, i.e., pain is located in one or more non-epigastric quadrant (shown in red in Figure 1A). 3) Lipase elevation without pain, as previously studied,6 was typically done for delirium, stupor, or unexplained debility and comprised <25% of the current patients. For a purpose of retaining focus on atypical pain, the painless and typical pain groups were combined into “others”. As we shall see later, this was appropriate since there was no benefit in altering cut-offs for either the typical pain group or the painless group.

Diagnosis of AP

Patients were “diagnosed with AP on Imaging” when the radiologist diagnosed AP on cross sectional imaging. The timing of imaging compared to the timing of release of lipase results varied among different patients. We did not question the radiologist whether they first looked at the lipase level before making a radiologic diagnosis of pancreatitis, and so we are unaware of the extent of blinding of the radiologist. A “Clinical diagnosis of AP” was defined as when the clinician diagnosed AP without imaging or despite negative imaging. “Clinically negative” AP patients were those without abdominal imaging and not diagnosed with AP by the clinician.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous data are reported as means ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range where specified. Categorical data are represented by numbers with the percentage in parentheses. Continuous variables were compared using unpaired t test or Mann-Whitney test depending on normality of distribution. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Graphing and statistical calculations were done using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed for all atypical pain patients for each diagnostic category. The ROC curve was used to determine the optimal threshold lipase values at which AP was diagnosed while retaining a sensitivity above a desired cut off (e.g.,>95%) level.
