Heart Failure Pharmacotherapy and Long-Term Outcomes After Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation: A Nationwide Real-World Cohort Study
Authors: Feng-Cheng Chang, Yu-Ting Cheng, Chun-Yu Chen, Chia-Pin Lin, Ying-Chang Tung, Fu-Chih Hsiao, Chia-Ling Wu, An-Hsun Chou, Yu-Tung Huang, Shao-Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe examined prescription trends and associations between post-discharge heart failure (HF) guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) and long-term outcomes in patients with cardiogenic extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), a population with limited evidence on long-term management.MethodsThis nationwide retrospective observational cohort used Taiwan’s real-world National Health Insurance Research Database. Adults undergoing cardiogenic ECMO during 2011–2023 who survived at least 6 months after discharge were included. Qualifying exposure required at least 28 cumulative prescription days during the first 6 months after discharge. Follow-up began at the 6-month landmark and continued through December 31, 2024. Associations were estimated using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting; propensity score matching served as a sensitivity analysis.ResultsAmong 4,428 patients, 3,164 received qualifying HF pharmacotherapy and 1,264 did not. Use shifted from angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and digoxin toward angiotensin receptor blockers, beta-blockers, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and ivabradine. HF medication use was associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.51–0.65), the composite of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.65–0.92), and cardiovascular death (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.53–0.79). Renin-angiotensin system inhibitors and beta-blockers were each associated with lower mortality (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.61–0.78). Sensitivity analyses were consistent.ConclusionAmong 6-month survivors of cardiogenic ECMO, a high-risk and clinically unique population often excluded from HF trials, post-discharge HF pharmacotherapy was associated with lower long-term mortality. These findings support structured cardiovascular reassessment and individualized HF medication review after ECMO.

Introduction

With high morbidity and mortality rates, heart failure (HF) remains a major concern and significantly contributes to healthcare costs.1 The overall prevalence of HF is approximately 1–2% in developed countries and the prevalence is higher in older age groups. Numerous clinical trials have revealed the potential of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) in mitigating HF symptoms and improving disease prognosis.2 Since the issuance of the 2016 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the treatment of HF, the triad of pharmacotherapy consisting of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) has been used to reduce the risks of HF-related rehospitalization and death in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).3 The angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI; sacubitril–valsartan) and ivabradine are beneficial for specific patient populations.4,5 Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2 inhibitors) have been shown to confer prognostic benefits.6,7 Notably, the 2022 American Heart Association and 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for patients with HFrEF added SGLT2 inhibitors to the aforementioned pharmacotherapy triad.8,9 Despite advances in medical treatment, the 5-year mortality rate remains high (75%) in patients with HF, regardless of ejection fraction.10

Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is widely used in patients with hemodynamic compromise. The most common indications for venoarterial ECMO include cardiogenic shock with various etiologies, such as postcardiotomy complications, acute myocardial infarction, acute myocarditis and cardiac arrest.11 Patients receiving venoarterial ECMO for these conditions are at risk of acute HF. Although clinical outcomes after ECMO support have improved over the past decade, the in-hospital mortality rate remains high at 60%.12–14 Patients who survive after receiving ECMO support often have poor cardiac function, which increases the likelihood of subsequent HF treatment and the risks of HF-related rehospitalization and cardiovascular (CV) death.13,15

Despite these risks, ECMO survivors constitute a clinically distinct post-discharge population with heterogeneous underlying indications and variable cardiac recovery. Because ventricular recovery, hemodynamic stability, and renal function may evolve substantially after ECMO, the timing and selection of HF pharmacotherapy during post-discharge recovery remain clinically challenging. Yet these patients are frequently underrepresented or excluded from pivotal HF trials, leaving limited evidence to inform long-term HF medication use. We therefore conducted a nationwide cohort study of patients who survived at least 6 months after hospital discharge following ECMO for cardiogenic causes to (1) describe temporal trends in post-discharge HF medication use and (2) evaluate the association between such use and long-term mortality and cardiovascular outcomes. We further evaluated individual medication classes, heterogeneity by ECMO indication, and mortality according to the number of qualifying medication classes.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), which contains comprehensive claims for more than 99% of Taiwan’s approximately 24 million residents. The National Health Insurance program reimburses outpatient visits, hospitalizations, procedures, and prescription medications, permitting longitudinal ascertainment of ECMO treatment, HF pharmacotherapy, rehospitalization, and advanced HF interventions. Mortality and cause-specific death were ascertained through linkage to the Taiwan Death Registry. Prior studies have validated NHIRD-based identification of ECMO use and long-term outcomes.13,16 This retrospective study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the NHIRD research committee and the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (registration number: 202300947B0), with informed consent waived.

Study Cohort and Landmark Design

We identified 23,553 ECMO hospitalizations between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2023. We excluded records with missing demographic information (n = 529), patients younger than 18 years (n = 978), and those who received ECMO for noncardiogenic indications, such as respiratory failure or trauma (n = 4,244). Among the remaining 17,802 patients, we further excluded those who died during the index hospitalization (n = 12,068) or within 6 months after discharge (n = 721), as well as those with an ECMO duration of 0 or more than 90 days (n = 585). The final cohort comprised 4,428 patients who survived for at least 6 months after hospital discharge. The first 6 months after hospital discharge constituted the exposure-assessment window. The landmark date, defined as 6 months after discharge, was the time origin for all outcome analyses. This ensures that exposure status is defined before outcome follow-up begins and that only events occurring after the landmark contribute to the outcome analyses. Based on medication exposure during this window, 3,164 patients were classified into the medical group and 1,264 into the control group (Figure 1). Medication Exposure, Covariates, and Outcomes

Medication exposure was assessed using NHI-reimbursed prescriptions during the first 6 months after hospital discharge. A qualifying exposure was defined as at least 28 cumulative prescription days during this window to avoid classifying brief or transitional prescribing as medication exposure. The five medication categories used to define the medical group were (1) Renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNI); (2) beta-blockers; (3) MRAs; (4) SGLT2 inhibitors; and (5) ivabradine. Patients meeting the exposure criterion for at least one category were classified into the medical group, whereas those meeting none of these criteria were classified into the control group. ACEIs, ARBs, and ARNI were counted as a single medication category in the medication-count analysis. Digoxin and diuretics were evaluated only in the class-specific analyses and were not used to define the primary exposure groups. Pre-ECMO HF medication use was assessed during the 6 months preceding the index hospitalization.

Baseline covariates included age, sex, urbanization level, calendar year and hospital level of the index ECMO hospitalization, ECMO duration, and ECMO indication. Comorbidities recorded during the 12 months preceding the index admission were identified using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes. ECMO discontinuation status was classified as successful weaning, left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation, or heart transplantation using NHI procedure codes.17 The primary outcome was all-cause mortality after the 6-month landmark. Secondary outcomes were CV death, a composite of CV death or HF hospitalization, and all-cause readmission. Patients were followed from the landmark until the occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, or December 31, 2024, whichever came first.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analysis estimated the average treatment effect using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including age, sex, urbanization level, index year, the 11 prespecified comorbidities, hospital level, pre-ECMO HF medication use, ECMO duration, and ECMO indication. The Charlson Comorbidity Index was reported descriptively but was not included in the propensity score model. Stabilized weights were calculated using each patient’s propensity score and the overall probability of belonging to the medical or control group. For the medical group, the stabilized average treatment effect weight was defined as the marginal probability of medication use divided by the propensity score; for the control group, it was defined as the marginal probability of nonuse divided by one minus the propensity score. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (ASMDs), with values below 0.10 considered negligible.

All-cause mortality, CV death, and the composite outcome were compared using time-to-event curves, Log rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models. All-cause readmission was analyzed using a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model with death treated as a competing event. Effect estimates are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) or subdistribution HRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Absolute incidence rates were calculated per 100 person-years from the landmark. Individual medication classes were modeled as separate binary exposures in the IPTW cohort; the reference group for each model comprised patients without qualifying use of that class. These weighted models did not simultaneously adjust for other medication classes or additional covariates after IPTW. Temporal prescription trends from 2011 through 2023 were assessed using Joinpoint regression.

Additional analyses included stratification by ECMO indication (acute myocardial infarction, postcardiotomy, myocarditis, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, pulmonary embolism, and advanced HF), with interaction terms used to test heterogeneity. We also repeated IPTW after excluding patients who received an LVAD or heart transplantation. A separate analysis categorized the unweighted cohort by the number of qualifying HF medication categories (0, 1, 2, or at least 3) and used multivariable Cox regression adjusted for the covariates included in the propensity score model.

As a sensitivity analysis, propensity scores were estimated using the same covariates as in the primary IPTW analysis. Patients were then matched 1:1 using an 8-to-1 digit greedy matching algorithm implemented with the SAS OneToManyMTCH macro, without a prespecified caliper. A two-sided P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and the Joinpoint Regression Program version 4.5.0.1 (Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute).

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Prescription Trends

Of 23,553 ECMO hospitalizations identified during 2011–2023, 4,428 patients met the eligibility criteria and survived to the 6-month post-discharge landmark (Figure 1). The medical group included 3,164 patients (71.5%), and the control group included 1,264 (28.5%). The most frequent ECMO indications were acute myocardial infarction (n = 1,615; 36.5%), postcardiotomy (n = 1,304; 29.4%), and myocarditis (n = 371; 8.4%). Overall, 94.4% of patients were successfully weaned from ECMO, 1.6% received an LVAD, and 4.1% underwent heart transplantation.

Before weighting, the medical group was older, included a higher proportion of men, and had higher prevalences of hypertension and diabetes mellitus than the control group. Acute myocardial infarction was more frequent in the medical group, whereas postcardiotomy, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and pulmonary embolism were more frequent in the control group. Median follow-up from the 6-month landmark was 3.58 years (interquartile range [IQR], 1.66–6.38) in the medical group and 3.95 years (IQR, 1.38–7.53) in the control group. Long-Term Outcomes and Medication Class–Specific Associations with Mortality

In the IPTW cohort, post-discharge HF medication use was associated with lower all-cause mortality than nonuse (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.51–0.65; P < 0.001), with incidence rates of 5.49 and 9.74 per 100 person-years in the medical and control groups, respectively (Figure 3A). The medical group also had lower risks of the composite of CV death or HF hospitalization (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.65–0.92; P = 0.003; incidence rates, 3.25 vs 4.30 per 100 person-years) and CV death (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.53–0.79; P < 0.001; incidence rates, 2.18 vs 3.41 per 100 person-years) (Figure 3B and C). All-cause readmission did not differ significantly between groups (subdistribution HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.95–1.19; P = 0.305; incidence rates, 11.26 vs 12.11 per 100 person-years) (Figure 3D). Sensitivity Analyses and Medication-Count Associations with All-Cause Mortality

After 1:1 propensity score matching, 1,117 patients were included in each group, with adequate covariate balance (Supplemental Table 1). The medical group remained associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.52–0.73; P < 0.001), with incidence rates of 4.99 versus 8.18 per 100 person-years (Supplemental Figure 1A). CV death was also lower (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.58–1.00; P = 0.046), whereas the composite of CV death or HF hospitalization (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.71–1.13; P = 0.362) and all-cause readmission (subdistribution HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.87–1.16; P = 0.970) did not differ significantly between groups (Supplemental Figure 1B–D). Class-specific analyses similarly showed lower all-cause mortality with ACEI/ARB/ARNI and beta-blocker use (Supplemental Table 2).

After excluding patients who received an LVAD or heart transplantation and repeating IPTW, the medical group remained associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.47–0.60; P < 0.001), with incidence rates of 5.42 versus 10.42 per 100 person-years (Supplemental Figure 2A). Lower risks of the composite outcome and CV death were also observed, whereas all-cause readmission did not differ significantly between groups (Supplemental Figure 2B–D). Class-specific estimates were directionally similar to those of the primary analysis, with the most consistent associations observed for ACEI/ARB/ARNI and beta-blocker use (Supplemental Table 3). Overall, both sensitivity analyses yielded findings directionally consistent with the primary IPTW analysis, particularly for all-cause mortality and these two medication categories.

In the medication-count analysis, use of 1, 2, and ≥3 HF medication classes was associated with lower all-cause mortality compared with no qualifying medication use (adjusted HR, 0.63; 0.54; and 0.53, respectively; P for trend < 0.001; Supplemental Table 4). Mortality risk estimates were similar between patients receiving 2 and ≥3 medication classes (HR, 0.98; P = 0.815).

Discussion

In this nationwide 6-month landmark cohort of survivors of cardiogenic ECMO, post-discharge HF medication use was associated with lower long-term all-cause mortality, CV death, and the composite of CV death or HF hospitalization, but not all-cause readmission. The most consistent class-specific associations were observed for ACEI/ARB/ARNI and beta-blocker use. The mortality association varied by ECMO indication and was strongest after acute myocardial infarction, with a lower mortality estimate also observed after postcardiotomy. Findings for all-cause mortality were consistent after propensity score matching and after excluding patients who received an LVAD or heart transplantation. During the study period, prescription patterns shifted away from ACEIs and digoxin toward ARBs, beta-blockers, SGLT2 inhibitors, and ivabradine.

These findings extend evidence from conventional HF trials to a distinct post-ECMO population but should not be interpreted as reproducing trial-based treatment effects. Evidence-based HF therapies reduce mortality and HF hospitalization in appropriately selected patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction.3,4,6–9,18,19 ECMO survivors, however, comprise a heterogeneous population in whom ventricular dysfunction may result from acute myocardial infarction, transient postcardiotomy stunning, recovering myocarditis, cardiac arrest, pulmonary vascular obstruction, or advanced chronic HF. Some patients recover ventricular function, whereas others remain dependent on advanced HF therapies.11,17 Consequently, the observed associations between medication use and survival cannot be assumed to represent the pharmacologic effects demonstrated in trial-eligible HF populations.

Prescription patterns shifted away from ACEIs and digoxin toward ARBs, beta-blockers, SGLT2 inhibitors, and ivabradine. Increasing ARB use may partly reflect ACEI intolerance, including the greater susceptibility to ACEI-related cough reported in Asian populations.20 MRA use increased numerically from 8.8% in 2011 to 34.4% in 2023, although the temporal trend did not reach statistical significance, and its uptake remained lower than that of ARBs and beta-blockers. This relative gap may partly reflect concerns regarding renal dysfunction and hyperkalemia.21–25 SGLT2 inhibitor use increased rapidly after 2016 and reached 27.8% in 2023.19,26 Although SGLT2 inhibitor use was associated with a numerically lower mortality risk, this association did not reach statistical significance. This may partly reflect the limited number of exposed patients and duration of follow-up, as uptake was concentrated late in the study period and reimbursement for HF indications began near the end of the study period. The mortality estimates for MRAs and SGLT2 inhibitors were directionally consistent across the primary and sensitivity analyses. Ivabradine use increased after 2015 but plateaued at approximately 10–12% in later years, likely reflecting its restricted use in selected patients with HFrEF and persistent sinus tachycardia despite beta-blocker therapy.27,28 No significant mortality association was observed, although this estimate was derived from a small, selectively treated subgroup and may not be generalizable to the broader ECMO survivor population.

The medication-count analysis showed lower mortality estimates among patients receiving one or more HF medication classes than among those receiving none. However, estimates were similar for 2 and ≥3 classes, suggesting that use of ≥3 classes was not associated with an additional survival advantage over use of 2 classes. Although the overall pattern is compatible with broader implementation of evidence-based therapy, it may also reflect differences in treatment eligibility, hemodynamic stability, renal function, cardiac recovery, adherence, and access to longitudinal care. Heterogeneity across ECMO indications provides important context for the overall findings. Mortality associations were most apparent after acute myocardial infarction and postcardiotomy, conditions in which residual ventricular dysfunction may prompt ongoing HF treatment.29 In contrast, the wide CIs for myocarditis, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, pulmonary embolism, and advanced HF indicate limited precision and should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. Patients receiving an LVAD or heart transplantation follow distinct treatment pathways and have different prognoses; the persistence of the main findings after excluding these patients reduces concern that advanced therapies entirely drove the overall association.

These findings identify the post-ECMO transition as an important opportunity to reassess and optimize HF pharmacotherapy. Survivors of cardiogenic ECMO should undergo structured cardiovascular follow-up with repeated evaluation of ventricular recovery, hemodynamic status, renal function, congestion, rhythm, contraindications, and medication tolerance rather than receive a uniform treatment regimen. The consistent associations observed for ACEI/ARB/ARNI and beta-blockers support consideration of these therapies during individualized medication review in eligible and hemodynamically stable patients. Integrating medication review and titration into post-ECMO care pathways may improve the delivery of evidence-based HF care in this high-risk, understudied population, while prospective studies are needed to determine whether such strategies improve clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, to avoid immortal-time bias, we used a 6-month landmark design in which medication exposure was defined during the first 6 months after discharge, and outcome follow-up began only among patients who survived to the landmark. This approach ensures correct temporal alignment but limits inference to patients who survived 6 months and does not capture early post-discharge outcomes. Second, residual confounding may persist despite IPTW and propensity score matching, as these methods adjust only for measured variables. The NHIRD lacks echocardiographic and other detailed clinical data on ventricular function and recovery, renal and hemodynamic status, treatment tolerance, and follow-up intensity. Consequently, we could not fully characterize HF phenotypes or determine whether differences in ventricular function, renal or hemodynamic status, treatment tolerance, or follow-up intensity influenced treatment selection and outcomes. Third, SGLT2 inhibitor use was limited and occurred mainly in the later study period, resulting in a small sample size and limited follow-up, which reduced statistical precision and precluded firm conclusions regarding long-term mortality effects.

Conclusion

In this nationwide 6-month landmark cohort, post-discharge HF pharmacotherapy was associated with lower long-term mortality among survivors of cardiogenic ECMO, with the most consistent class-specific associations observed for ACEI/ARB/ARNI and beta-blocker use. These findings highlight the potential value of structured cardiovascular reassessment after ECMO, with HF pharmacotherapy individualized according to patient eligibility, cardiac function and recovery, hemodynamic and renal status, and contraindications. By providing long-term real-world evidence in this high-risk population—often underrepresented in clinical trials—this study supports integrating individualized HF medication review and optimization into post-ECMO care pathways and establishes a foundation for prospective studies to define optimal pharmacotherapy.
