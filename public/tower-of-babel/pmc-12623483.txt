Explainable convolutional neural network architectures for high-performance taxonomic classification of gasteroid macrofungi
Authors: Eda Kumru, Fatih Ekinci, Abdullah Aydoğan, Mehmet Serdar Güzel, Mustafa Sevindik, Ilgaz Akata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gasteroid fungi represent a morphologically diverse and taxonomically challenging group due to their convergent evolution and closed fruiting bodies. This study presents a novel deep learning-based framework for the classification of six macrofungi species: Battarrea phalloides, Crucibulum laeve, Cyathus olla, C. striatus, Tulostoma brumale, and T. fimbriatum. A total of 1200 high-resolution images were processed using eleven convolutional neural networks (CNNs), each pre-trained on ImageNet and fine-tuned for this classification task. DenseNet121 emerged as the best-performing model with 96.11% accuracy, 96.09% F1-score, and an AUC of 99.89%. ResNeXt and RepVGG followed closely with accuracies of 95.00% and 93.89%, respectively. Operational metrics showed that ShuffleNetV2 achieved the fastest inference time (0.80 s), while RepVGG exhibited the highest energy efficiency (16.5%). Explainable AI techniques, including Grad-CAM and Guided Backpropagation, were applied to enhance model interpretability, revealing biologically meaningful image regions. These results demonstrate that deep learning architectures can be effectively applied to fungal taxonomy with high accuracy and transparency. Furthermore, the proposed methodology is scalable and adaptable to other biological domains such as fungal spore classification, plant pollen analysis, and rare species identification in ecological monitoring. This study offers a robust, interpretable, and computationally efficient solution for automating biodiversity assessments using image-based AI techniques.

Introduction

Gasteroid fungi are a polyphyletic group within Basidiomycota, characterized by diverse shapes, enclosed fruiting bodies, and convergent features, which complicate their taxonomy. Identification of gasteroid fungi relies on both macroscopic and microscopic features, including fructification body shape, peridial layers, and gleba color, as well as spore size and ornamentation1,2. Molecular methods are essential for accurate classification and resolving phylogenetic relationships, as they overcome common morphological convergence3–9.

This evolutionary shift, believed to be an adaptation to arid or fragmented environments, has led to the loss of active spore discharge mechanisms, replaced by passive dispersal through decay, rupture, or animal activity. Genera such as Cyathus Haller, Crucibulum Tul. and C. Tul., Battarrea, and Tulostoma Pers. within the order Agaricales Underw. exemplify this transformation1–9.

These genera encompass ecologically diverse and morphologically distinctive species, underscoring the evolutionary and ecological breadth of gasteroid fungi. For instance, Crucibulum laeve (Huds.) Kambly and Cyathus olla (Batsch) Pers., commonly known as bird’s nest fungi, are often found on woody debris and grasslands. At the same time, Cyathus striatus Willd. is notable for its hairy exterior and striate inner surface, thriving in temperate forest litter2. Among stalked puffballs, Battarrea phalloides (Dicks.) Pers. is of global conservation concern due to habitat loss, typically appearing in sandy, arid soils8. Tulostoma brumale Pers. is adapted to winter fruiting in dune systems, whereas T. fimbriatum Fr. is widely distributed in Europe, identifiable by its fringed pore and scaly stipe9. Though the morphological and ecological characteristics cited above are mainly taxonomically informative, these species are quite variable.

Despite their taxonomic and ecological importance, gasteroid fungi pose challenges for traditional classification due to morphological convergence and limited public awareness. To address this gap, the present study proposes a novel deep learning-based approach for accurately classifying six ecologically significant species: Battarrea phalloides, Crucibulum laeve, Cyathus olla, C. striatus, Tulostoma brumale, and T. fimbriatum. The goal is to develop a high-performance identification model that enhances fungal identification through photographs, while supporting conservation monitoring and public education in fungal biodiversity.

To achieve this, fungal image data were processed using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for feature extraction and subsequently classified with a Kohonen self-organising map (SOM). This hybrid method is the first known attempt to integrate SOMs traditionally used for unsupervised clustering with CNN architectures in classifying gasteroid fungi10. Additionally, the study introduces Kolmogorov Arnold Network (KAN) layers into this framework and evaluates the performance of advanced architectures such as MaxViT-S, marking a pioneering step in the application of deep learning in fungal taxonomy10,11. The structure of this paper is as follows: Section “Materials and methods” outlines the dataset and experimental setup; Section “Results” presents the model evaluation and results; and Section “Discussion” discusses the limitations and future directions in AI-assisted fungal classification.

Materials and methods

A total of 1200 images representing six different mushroom species Battarrea phalloides, Crucibulum laeve, Cyathus olla, C. striatus, Tulostoma brumale, and T. fimbriatum (Fig. 1) were used in this study. The images were sourced from the authors’ own dataset and the Global Core Biodata Resource (www.gbif.org)12, with the specific proportions of data from each source detailed in Table 1. All images were acquired at a fixed resolution of 300 dpi and in JPEG format (Table 1).

The complete dataset was partitioned as follows: 65% for training, 15% for validation, 15% for testing, and 5% reserved for fine-tuning. This structured split ensured that the model development process, hyperparameter tuning, and final evaluation were conducted on independent subsets. This dataset can be accessed at https://tinyurl.com/ye5utx2h and https://tinyurl.com/yxj5xh85.

To enhance robustness, generalization, and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation techniques were applied only to the training subset. Each image was subjected to several transformations13. These included random rotations to simulate different viewing angles and horizontal flipping to mimic mirror-image scenarios14. Brightness and contrast adjustments were used to address lighting variability. Gaussian blur was applied to introduce slight distortions15. Finally, random cropping combined with resizing helped enforce scale invariance. These augmentation procedures effectively tripled the number of training images, thereby enriching the dataset without compromising its integrity16.

Eleven different deep learning (DL) models were employed for classification, including DenseNet121, EfficientNetB0, EfficientNetB4, GhostNet, RegNetX-400MF, RegNetX-800MF, RepVGG, ResNeXt, ShuffleNetV2, SqueezeNet, and Xception. Each model was initialized with pre-trained weights on the ImageNet dataset. In this study, PyTorch17 was employed as the primary deep learning framework. Model architectures were loaded using both the official TorchVision library18 and the timm (PyTorch Image Models) package19.Through TorchVision, models such as DenseNet121, EfficientNetB0, EfficientNetB4, RegNetX-400MF, RegNetX-800MF, ResNeXt, ShuffleNetV2, and SqueezeNet were utilized, whereas GhostNet, RepVGG, and Xception were integrated via the timm library. For data preprocessing, TorchVision’s transforms and datasets modules were applied. Mathematical operations were supported by NumPy20 while additional evaluation metrics, such as log loss, were computed using scikit-learn21. System performance monitoring was conducted with psutil22, and standard Python libraries such as os, time, and json were employed for file management and execution control23.

DenseNet121 introduces a dense connectivity pattern where each layer is directly connected to every subsequent layer, promoting feature reuse throughout the network24. DenseNet121 minimises the vanishing gradient problem and model redundancy by using dense blocks and transition layers that downsample feature maps and manage complexity. This design ensures efficient information flow and strong feature propagation, making it effective for diverse deep learning tasks25. EfficientNetB0 uses compound scaling to balance depth, width, and resolution, achieving high accuracy with low computational cost, ideal for image classification24,25.

EfficientNetB0 employs a compound scaling method that uniformly adjusts network depth, width, and input resolution using fixed scaling coefficients26. It uses mobile inverted bottleneck blocks and squeeze-and-excitation modules for better feature recalibration, with balanced scaling optimising all dimensions to enhance model efficiency27. EfficientNetB0 effectively allocates computational resources, offering a scalable and streamlined architecture for deep learning applications26,27.

EfficientNetB4 builds upon the compound scaling strategy by proportionally increasing network depth, width, and input resolution compared to its counterparts28. Its architecture utilizes mobile inverted bottleneck convolution blocks along with squeeze-and-excitation modules, which serve to refine and recalibrate feature representations29. EfficientNetB4’s systematic scaling balances expansion across all dimensions, optimising parameters and efficiency while maintaining a streamlined structure for deep learning28,29.

GhostNet is characterized by its innovative ghost module design, which generates intrinsic feature maps through a series of inexpensive linear operations30. This architecture effectively reduces computational overhead while maintaining the integrity of feature representation31. GhostNet emphasises generating redundant yet informative features to enhance resource efficiency. Its modular structure also supports scalability and streamlined construction, making it adaptable to a wide range of deep learning applications30,31.

RegNetX-400MF is structured around a simple and scalable design paradigm that employs stage-wise blocks with a consistent parameterization scheme. The network uses sequential bottleneck blocks to transform features and support gradient flow, with a design optimised for 400 million FLOPs to balance complexity and performance. The model’s straightforward design enhances interpretability and provides a clear framework for analyzing resource allocation across layers32.

RegNetX-800MF is designed with a scalable architecture that employs stage-wise bottleneck blocks to facilitate effective feature transformation and gradient propagation33. The model is carefully structured to maintain a balance between network capacity and computational cost within an 800 million FLOPs budget. Organised into sequential stages, it refines feature representations through systematically designed layers. This modular and straightforward design enhances resource efficiency while supporting clear performance analysis at each stage32,33.

RepVGG employs a VGG-inspired architecture that utilizes structural re-parameterization to transition from a multi-branch training configuration to a streamlined, single-branch model during inference34,35. RepVGG balances complexity and efficiency by using a simple structure of convolutional layers with batch normalization and activations, benefiting from higher capacity during training and efficient computation in deployment through re-parameterization35,36.

ResNeXt builds upon the principles of residual learning by incorporating a split-transform-merge strategy that aggregates parallel transformations37. ResNeXt introduces cardinality by employing parallel group convolutions to capture diverse features, enhancing representational capacity without simply increasing depth or width. This modular and efficient design supports balanced feature extraction and residual mapping, providing a scalable and effective framework for deep learning models37,38.

ShuffleNet-V2 is engineered with a focus on optimizing computational efficiency through innovative channel split and shuffle operations39. ShuffleNet-V2 uses pointwise group convolutions and channel shuffle to boost inter-channel communication while reducing memory cost. Its design ensures balanced resource use and efficient feature transformation, offering a scalable and computationally efficient framework39,40.

SqueezeNet is characterized by its compact architecture, primarily achieved through the innovative use of fire modules41. Each fire module uses a 1 × 1 squeeze layer to reduce dimensionality, followed by 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 expand layers to restore spatial details. This design lowers parameter count while preserving feature quality, enabling efficient computation and memory use ideal for resource-constrained environments41,42.

Xception is built upon the concept of depthwise separable convolutions, which decouple spatial and cross-channel correlations to enhance computational efficiency43. Xception replaces traditional inception modules with a streamlined design that supports deeper networks without increasing parameters. Residual connections aid gradient flow and feature extraction, while factorized convolutions balance efficiency and representational power43,44.

The model was trained on the custom dataset with a batch size of 64 and an initial learning rate of 0.0001 for 10 epochs, using CrossEntropyLoss and the Adam optimizer. After this initial training, a fine-tuning phase was performed on a reserved subset to improve performance. During fine-tuning, the batch size remained 64, but the learning rate was reduced to 0.00005 for more controlled updates. This phase also lasted 10 epochs with the same loss function and optimizer. Using consistent hyperparameters across all models ensured fair comparison, stable convergence, and efficient multi-class classification.

To assess the performance of each CNN model, 12 distinct metrics were employed to capture various aspects of classification outcomes, including correctness, reliability, and resource usage10,11. These metrics comprise accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, specificity, log loss, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), confusion matrix, ROC & AUC, inference time, memory usage, and energy efficiency41. Together, they offer a comprehensive view of how each model handles both predictive and operational considerations. In the equations, TP (true positive) represents the number of positive instances correctly classified as positive (Eqs. 1, 2, 3 and 7), whereas TN (true negative) denotes the number of negative instances correctly classified as negative (Eqs. 1, 2, 5 and 7). Conversely, FP (false positive) is the number of negative instances incorrectly classified as positive (Eqs. 1, 2, 5 and 7), and FN (false negative) is the number of positive instances incorrectly classified as negative (Eqs. 1, 3 and 7). The term yij refers to an indicator variable for sample i, which takes a value of 1 if that sample belongs to class j and 0 otherwise; N denotes the total number of samples in the dataset, and M indicates the total number of classes considered in the classification task (Eq. 6) 11,45.

Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions (1), while precision quantifies the fraction of true positive predictions among all positive predictions (2), and recall (sensitivity) evaluates the proportion of actual positives correctly identified (3). The F1-score, being the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balances these two aspects (4), whereas specificity indicates the proportion of true negatives accurately classified (5). Log loss (cross-entropy loss) assesses the quality of predicted probabilities by penalizing overconfident errors (6). Logloss is a metric used to evaluate the accuracy of a model’s predictions in classification problems. This metric measures the alignment between the probabilities predicted by the model and the actual class labels. For each instance, the logarithmic difference between the predicted probability and the actual label is calculated. When higher probabilities are assigned to the correct classes, the logloss value decreases, while it increases when higher probabilities are assigned to incorrect classes. Thus, logloss serves as a metric that measures the model’s accuracy and reliability. And the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) offers a balanced measure that takes into account true and false predictions across both classes (7). Additionally, the confusion matrix offers a detailed view of true and false classifications, while the ROC curve and AUC (Area Under the Curve) highlight the balance between sensitivity and specificity across thresholds. Operational metrics like inference time, memory usage, and energy efficiency assess the model’s speed, resource needs, and power consumption during deployment11,45.

In order to thoroughly evaluate classification performance, a set of metrics (1–7) was calculated for each model examined. Given the inherently opaque nature of deep neural networks often referred to as ‘black boxes’ three Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques were employed to enhance interpretability: Grad-CAM, thresholded Grad-CAM, and Guided Backpropagation. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) generates coarse heatmaps corresponding to the target class by propagating class-specific gradients into the final convolutional layer46. To further refine these heatmaps, a threshold of 100 was applied to the pixel intensity values, thereby generating binary masks that isolate the most salient areas above this threshold level. Guided Backpropagation improves standard backpropagation by passing only positive gradients, producing high-resolution saliency maps47. Together, these techniques highlight both coarse and fine-grained image features influencing model predictions, thus clarifying model behaviour and strengthening trust in their use for macrofungi classification46,47.

This study aims to assess both the predictive performance and the practical applicability of the models in macrofungi classification. Subsequently, test results are compared using accuracy and F1-score graphs to evaluate the effectiveness and balance of predictions. ROC curves are also plotted to highlight each model’s discrimination capacity based on test data. Inference time is compared to assess computational efficiency during deployment, while memory usage and energy efficiency metrics are analyzed to gauge operational demands. In addition, Grad-CAM and Guided Backpropagation visualizations are employed to elucidate the models’ decision-making processes by highlighting the salient features contributing to their predictions. Finally, a detailed table summarizing all test metrics is provided to offer a holistic view of performance, thereby enabling a robust and multi-faceted evaluation of the models’ capabilities.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of deep learning models applied to the classification of gasteroid macrofungi species. A total of 11 different CNN architectures were evaluated, with DenseNet121 achieving the highest performance, reaching 96.11% accuracy and an F1-score of 96.09%. ResNeXt followed with 95.00% accuracy, and RepVGG with 93.89%. In contrast, RegNetX-400MF recorded the lowest accuracy at 79.44%. According to ROC-AUC values, DenseNet121 (99.89%) and ResNeXt (99.90%) demonstrated near-perfect class separation. While models like ShuffleNetV2 offered rapid inference times (~ 0.80 s) suitable for real-time deployment, models such as Xception, despite their high accuracy, presented higher latency (~ 10.07 s), which may limit their operational use. Memory usage ranged between 55% and 57%, while energy efficiency peaked at 16.5% for RepVGG. These findings highlight the diverse advantages and limitations of different architectures in terms of classification accuracy, computational efficiency, and interpretability.

This approach is not limited to gasteroid fungi but can be extended to other biological structures with similar microscopic or macroscopic characteristics48. Examples include lichens, pollen grains, leaf tissues, or bacterial colony morphologies, all of which could be classified effectively using similar architectures49. Moreover, datasets derived from light microscopy or scanning electron microscopy (SEM) could also be analyzed with these models50. Expanding the study with different datasets and imaging systems would enhance the generalizability of the models and their applicability in biological research51. Such applications may also contribute directly to biodiversity monitoring, ecosystem modeling, and conservation biology.

The comparative assessment of the applied algorithms clearly demonstrates how architectural differences affect classification performance. For instance, DenseNet121, with its densely connected layers, facilitates information flow and excels especially in data-limited contexts52. The EfficientNet series achieves high accuracy with efficient resource utilization through compound scaling strategies53. RepVGG, with its re-parameterizable design, offers balanced performance across training and inference phases54. Conversely, lightweight models such as ShuffleNetV2, while offering speed and low memory usage, showed only moderate classification performance55. These differences emphasize that in model selection, operational factors such as inference time, power consumption, and hardware compatibility should be considered alongside accuracy.

Deep learning-based classification approaches like those used in this study are not confined to biological contexts they can be readily transferred to medicine and engineering56. For instance, CNN-based models can be employed in cancer histopathology to automatically classify tumor subtypes57. Likewise, SEM images of implant materials, polymer surfaces, or nanostructured biomaterials can be effectively analyzed using similar networks. In engineering applications, such models can aid in analyzing fracture surfaces of composite materials or detecting micro-defects in metal surfaces58. The comprehensive algorithm comparison and interpretability analyses presented in this study offer a solid foundation for interdisciplinary applications of artificial intelligence, spanning biology, medicine, and materials science.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that deep learning models can effectively classify six gasteroid macrofungi species with high precision and reliability. Among the evaluated architectures, DenseNet121 achieved the highest classification accuracy at 96.11%, with an F1-score of 96.09%, AUC of 99.89%, and a specificity of 99.22%, indicating superior discriminative power and low false positive rates. ResNeXt followed closely with 95.00% accuracy and 99.90% AUC, while RepVGG recorded 93.89% accuracy and the highest energy efficiency at 16.5%, demonstrating a favorable trade-off between performance and operational cost. In contrast, RegNetX-400MF showed the lowest performance with 79.44% accuracy and higher log loss, indicating limitations in learning complex features. Additionally, ShuffleNetV2, despite its moderate accuracy (87.22%), was the fastest model (0.80 s inference time), highlighting its real-time application potential. These findings emphasize that model selection must be aligned with the target use-case, balancing predictive strength, computational efficiency, and interpretability.

Given the model’s proven ability to learn from high-resolution macrofungi images, this approach could be extended to a variety of biological classification tasks using microscopic imagery.
