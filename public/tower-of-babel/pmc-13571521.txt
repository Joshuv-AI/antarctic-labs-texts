A dataset of genuine and synthetic Portuguese speech covering European, Brazilian, African, and regional accents - An open resource for speech deepfake detection and trustworthy AI research
Authors: André Miguel Viegas Oliveira, Rafael Geraldo dos Santos, Helder Daniel, José Valente de Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPortuguese is spoken by approximately 300 million people, making it one of the most widely spoken languages in the world, with speakers distributed across every continent. Despite its global reach, Portuguese remains significantly underrepresented in current synthetic speech detection technologies. This dataset aims to address this critical gap in the existing detection solutions. This dataset may be used in tasks related to biometric security, speaker verification, anti-spoofing systems, and deepfake protection, supporting the development and evaluation of models for Portuguese language speech data.The Synthetic Speech Detection - Portuguese (SSD_PT) dataset consists of 242,156 audio samples in the Portuguese language, containing short speech segments ranging from 1 to 7 s each. The dataset is organized into two balanced classes: genuine speech samples (bonafide) and synthetic speech samples (spoof).For the Brazilian Portuguese portion of the dataset, the genuine speech samples were collected from publicly available repositories, namely VoxForge, Mozilla Common Voice, and OpenSLR. As no publicly available repositories with comparable coverage were found for the remaining Portuguese varieties, the genuine speech samples for the remaining portions of the dataset were collected from television interviews, radio programs, and podcasts representing different regions of the Lusophone world, including mainland Portugal, the Azores, Madeira, Angola, and Mozambique.The synthetic speech samples were generated from genuine speech material. For Brazilian Portuguese, a text-to-speech model based on Coqui TTS (XTTS-v2) was used. For other Portuguese variants, voice conversion techniques were applied using the RVC (Retrieval-based Voice Conversion) framework, requiring the creation of speaker-specific models trained on multiple audio segments to capture individual vocal characteristics.All audio files were standardized to a 16 kHz sampling rate and converted to mono format. The dataset is split into training, development, and evaluation subsets, accompanied by protocol files that associate each sample with its corresponding label.Keywords: ASVspoof challenge, Audio deepfakes, Voice conversion, Synthetic speech detection, Portuguese speech

Background

Although several datasets have been developed for voice deepfake detection in English [1], resources available for Portuguese remain limited in terms of both availability and linguistic diversity. This dataset aims to fill this gap, supporting the development and evaluation of synthetic speech detection systems for the Portuguese language, extending the work presented in [2].

This dataset was developed to provide a corpus suitable for training and testing machine learning models designed to distinguish between bonafide human speech and spoofed speech. The data were collected and organized following a binary classification framework widely adopted in automatic speaker verification research and in the ASVspoof challenge [1].

File structure

As shown in Fig. 1, the dataset is organized in a hierarchical directory structure. The wav_T, wav_D, and wav_E_eval directories contain only audio files in WAV format, corresponding to the training, development and evaluation subsets, respectively. The SSD_PT_protocols directory stores three TSV protocol files, each associated with one of these dataset partitions. Specifically, SSD_PT.train.tsv, SSD_PT.dev.track_1.tsv, and SSD_PT.eval.track_1.tsv contain the metadata, including the audio filename, attack type and ground-truthlabel, for the training (wav_T), development (wav_D) and evaluation (wav_E_eval) subsets, respectively.

Benchmark protocol

To ensure reproducibility and enable a fair comparison between future synthetic speech detection methods, the SSD_PT dataset provides predefined training, development, and evaluation splits, as previously described. Models should be trained exclusively on the training set, while the development set is used to monitor the training process and select the hyperparameters. The final performance should be reported solely based on the evaluation set.

The recommended evaluation metrics are Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, and Equal Error Rate (EER). True Positives (TP) denote spoof samples correctly classified as spoof; True Negatives (TN) denote bonafide samples correctly classified as bonafide; False Positives (FP) denote bonafide samples incorrectly classified as spoof; and False Negatives (FN) denote spoof samples incorrectly classified as bonafide. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Bonafide audio collection

The bonafide speech recordings were collected from publicly available sources representing different Portuguese-speaking regions. For the European Portuguese, Angolan Portuguese, and Mozambican Portuguese accents, recordings were obtained from television broadcasts, radio programmes, interviews, and podcasts. Specifically, audio data were collected from RTP Açores [4] for the Azores accent, RTP Madeira [5] for the Madeira accent, RUA FM [6] for the Algarve accent, RUM – Campus Verbal [7] for the Northern Portugal accent, Voz da Planície [8] for the Alentejo accent, Rádio Moçambique [9] for the Mozambican accent, and DW Notícias [10] together with RTP Angola [11] for the Angolan accent.

Audio extraction from these sources was performed using the yt-dlp framework [12], an open-source command-line tool widely used for downloading audio and video content from online platforms.

After audio collection, given that the recordings were lengthy, voice activity detection (VAD) was applied to segment the audio into shorter excerpts, removing prolonged silences and noise. Subsequently, these segments were randomly selected, ensuring speaker and content diversity while avoiding continuous reproduction of the original recordings.

For Brazilian Portuguese, bonafide speech recordings were obtained from publicly available speech repositories, namely VoxForge [13], Mozilla Common Voice [14] and OpenSLR [15]. These repositories provide speech recordings produced by native speakers.

Finally, all audio samples were converted from various formats into uncompressed WAV files, followed by resampling to 16 kHz and conversion to mono channel. The resulting recordings were subsequently incorporated into the dataset as bonafide samples.

The source recordings were obtained exclusively from publicly accessible broadcasts and repositories whose terms permit research use or whose use is authorised under applicable copyright exceptions for scientific research.

Spoof audio generation

Synthetic speech samples were generated from the bonafide recordings using speech synthesis and voice conversion frameworks. Different approaches were adopted.

Brazilian Portuguese

For Brazilian Portuguese, spoof samples were generated using CoquiTTS (XTTS-v2) [16]. XTTS-v2 is a multilingual text-to-speech framework capable of generating synthetic speech while preserving speaker-specific vocal characteristics from a reference audio sample.

No additional model training was performed. Synthetic audio was directly generated using the pre-trained model and the provided speaker reference.

European Portuguese, Angolan Portuguese, and Mozambican Portuguese

Since XTTS-v2 does not currently provide native support for European Portuguese, synthetic speech for the remaining accents was generated using RVC (Retrieval-based Voice Conversion) [17].

For each accent, between 10 and 12 speakers were selected. For each speaker, approximately one to two hours of speech recordings were collected and used to train an independent RVC model. During the training process, each model learned the vocal characteristics of the corresponding speaker from the available data.

After the training phase, genuine speech recordings were used as input to the trained models. The RVC framework generated new synthetic audio samples, preserving the original linguistic content of the input recordings while converting the vocal identity to the voice of the target speaker represented by the trained model. On average, between approximately 1200 and 1700 synthetic audio samples were generated per model.

All audio files were converted to a 16 kHz sampling rate and mono format. The resulting synthetic recordings were subsequently incorporated into the dataset as spoof samples.

The full audio processing pipeline was implemented using custom Python scripts developed for this study. These scripts cover audio extraction, VAD-based segmentation, and preprocessing steps applied to both bonafide and spoof data. The complete source code is publicly available from a GitHub repository, archived on Zenodo to ensure reproducibility [18].

The audio generative models were trained exclusively for the purpose of generating synthetic speech samples used in this dataset. The trained voice-conversion models are not distributed as part of the released dataset. Only the resulting synthetic audio samples are made publicly available, together with the scripts used to construct the dataset, thereby ensuring reproducibility while preventing redistribution of the speaker-specific voice-conversion models.

Limitations

A limitation of the dataset is that it relies exclusively on two spoof audio generative methods, RVC and CoquiTTS (XTTS-v2), which may restrict the diversity and generalizability of spoofing scenarios represented. Future extensions could incorporate additional speech synthesis and voice conversion architectures, such as FreeVC [19] and YourTTS [20], to broaden the range of synthetic speech generation techniques and spoofing scenarios covered by the dataset.

Ethics Statement

The dataset is composed of audio segments of up to 7 s of duration collected from publicly available audio and audiovisual sources. The audio segments were randomly clipped from long recordings, stripped of all identifying metadata, thoroughly shuffled across sources, assigned unrelated filenames, before their inclusion in the dataset. Those audios collected from VoxForge, Mozilla Common Voice, and OpenSLR do not require any usage consent. All the others, including accessible radio broadcasts, and online media comply with the applicable copyright exceptions for scientific research and educational purposes under the legal frameworks of Portugal (Decree-Law No. 47/2023, amending Decree-Law No. 63/85), Angola (Law No. 15/14), and Mozambique (Law No. 9/2022).

Moreover, besides the above mentioned audio clips, no other person identifiable or otherwise data were collected.

Because all source recordings were obtained from publicly available repositories or lawfully accessible public broadcasts and were used exclusively for scientific research under the applicable legal frameworks, explicit individual consent from the speakers was not required under the applicable legal framework governing the use of publicly available recordings for scientific research.

The trained generative models are not distributed, are not reusable for speaker impersonation, and were employed exclusively as intermediate research tools to generate synthetic speech samples for machine-learning research. The released dataset contains only short speech excerpts and synthetic outputs. It is not intended nor suitable for recreating the vocal identity of any individual speaker.
