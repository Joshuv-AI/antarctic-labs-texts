Joint robust estimation
Xiang Li, Jun S. Liu, Qiang Sun, Lihu Xu

Abstract
. We introduce a joint robust estimation method for three parametric statistical models involving heavy-tailed data: mean estimation, linear regression, and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -penalized linear regression, where both the trend parameters and the error variance are unknown. Our approach is based on solving two coupled Catoni-type equations—one for estimating the target trend parameters and the other for estimating the error variance. Notably, this joint estimation strategy cannot be derived by minimizing a single loss function involving both the trend and the variance parameters. The proposed method offers four key advantages: (i) The length of the resulting ( 1 − ϵ ) (1-\epsilon) -confidence interval scales as log ⁡ ϵ − 1 \sqrt{\log\epsilon^{-1}} , matching the order achieved by classical estimators for subgaussian data; (ii) It is tuning-free, eliminating the need for separate variance estimation; (iii) It allows flexible selection of the Catoni-type functions tailored to the data characteristics; (iv) It delivers superior performance for high-variance data, thanks to the explicit inclusion of the variance term in the denominators of both equations. We rigorously establish the consistency and asymptotic efficiency of the proposed joint robust estimators using new analytical techniques. Specifically, the coupled equations are inherently complex, rendering the theoretical analysis of their solutions challenging. To address this challenge, we employ the Poincaré–Miranda Theorem to show that the solutions lie within certain geometric regions, such as cylinders or cones, centered around the true parameter values. This methodology is of independent interest and can be extended to solve other statistical problems.

1.
Introduction

A growing body of empirical evidence indicates that many datasets deviate significantly from Gaussian or sub-Gaussian behavior and instead exhibit pronounced heavy tails; for example, see
[
14
]
for financial data,
[
10
]
for complex network data, and
[
42
]
for gene expression data. Unlike sub-Gaussian distributions, heavy-tailed data often contain a substantial number of outliers, causing many classical methods to perform poorly. This has motivated sustained interest in robust methodologies that retain essentially sub-Gaussian-type deviation guarantees even when the data themselves are non-sub-Gaussian (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
37
]
).

Robust methods provide a foundational approach for handling outliers, making them versatile across diverse data structures, models, and application domains. Their applicability is not limited to independent observations; they have also been successfully adapted to dependent settings, including various time series contexts (
[
9
,
7
,
19
,
41
,
44
]
). Likewise, robustness principles extend beyond simple models and have proven effective in high-dimensional regression (
[
12
,
27
]
) as well as in matrix and tensor estimation (
[
1
,
17
,
25
,
30
]
).

Consequently, robust estimation has been widely adopted in numerous fields such as machine learning (
[
3
,
18
,
26
,
32
]
) and finance (
[
16
,
34
]
), among others. For a comprehensive treatment of the theoretical foundations and methodological developments in robust statistics, the reader is referred to the survey and monographic expositions in
[

,
25
]
.

1.1.
Literature review

The concept of robust estimation was first introduced by Huber in the 1960s. In
[
20
,
21
]
, Huber proposed a new loss function
\ell_{\tau}
to replace the classical square loss:

\displaystyle\ell_{\tau}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}x^{2},&|x|\leq\tau,\\
\tau|x|-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2},&|x|>\tau,\end{cases}

where
\tau>0
is the robustification parameter to be tuned. This function is known as Huber’s loss function, and the corresponding estimation procedure is called Huber’s estimation.

The main idea of Huber’s estimation is to reduce the influence of outliers whose magnitudes are beyond
\tau
. The choice of
\tau
is critical for balancing bias (or and robustness. A large
\tau
retains information from extreme data points, resulting in smaller bias but weaker robustness, whereas a small
\tau
suppresses such information, enhancing robustness at the cost of increased bias. Asymptotic properties of the Huber estimator have been well established (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
29
,
46
]
and references therein). Regarding the selection of
\tau
, Huber later suggested setting
\tau=1.345\sigma
based on the
95\%
asymptotic efficiency criterion in
[
22
]
, where
\sigma
is the standard deviation. However, Sun, Zhou, and Fan
[
37
]
showed through a theoretical analysis that, in linear regression models, the optimal non-asymptotic performance requires choosing
\tau
adaptively based on sample size, dimension, and moments. Further research on Huber-type robust estimation methods can be found in
[
33
,
43
,
44
,
47
]
and the references therein.

In his seminal work
[
6
]
, Catoni introduced a novel non-asymptotic deviation analysis for robust mean estimation under heavy-tailed data, providing key insights into the balance of robustness and bias. More precisely, when the data have finite variance, Catoni proposed a class of non-decreasing influence functions
\psi_{1}
satisfying

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\log\left(1-x+\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\psi_{1}(x)\leq\log\left(1+x+\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right).

and proposed his robust mean estimator as the following:
\widehat{\theta}
is defined as the solution of the following equation

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha(X_{i}-\theta)\right)=0,

where
\alpha\propto\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}
is a robustification parameter that balances bias and robustness, analogous to
\tau
in the Huber loss. According to
[
6
, Proposition 2.4]
, the
(1-\epsilon)
-confidence interval of this estimator has length on the order of
O\big(\sigma\sqrt{{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}/{n}}\big)
, matching that of the classical sample mean under sub-Gaussian data.

In contrast to Huber’s estimator, Catoni’s method (
1.2
) allows flexibility in the choice of
\psi_{1}
. On one hand, the narrowest choice,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}-\operatorname{sign}(x)\log\left(1-|x|+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right),&|x|\leq 1,\\
\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(2),&|x|>1,\end{cases}

exhibits behavior similar to Huber’s estimator. On the other hand, the widest choice,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}\log\left(1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right),&x\geq 0,\\
-\log\left(1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right),&x<0,\end{cases}

preserves information from large-magnitude data at a logarithmic scale. This flexibility enables practitioners to select
\psi_{1}
between these extremes according to the problem at hand.

Catoni’s approach has been widely applied in diverse models and scenarios involving heavy-tailed data (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
13
,
30
,
24
,
11
,
37
,
41
,
43
]
). For distributions with only a finite
\beta
-th moment, where
\beta\in(1,2)
, Chen et al.
[
8
]
extended Catoni’s idea by replacing (
1.1
) with

(1.3)

\displaystyle-\log\left(1-x+\frac{|x|^{\beta}}{\beta}\right)\leq\psi_{2}(x)\leq\log\left(1+x+\frac{|x|^{\beta}}{\beta}\right),

thereby extending the applicability of Catoni-type estimators to distributions with finite
\beta
-th moments.

In practice, both Huber’s and Catoni’s estimators require the selection of robustification parameters that depend on the variance
\sigma^{2}
and the sample size. For details on adaptive methods, see
[
37
,
45
]
. Since the variance
\sigma^{2}
is usually unknown, it must be estimated from the data. Catoni
[
6
]
employed the Lepski method, while Sun et al.
[
37
]
used the sample variance combined with cross-validation. However, for heavy-tailed or high-dimensional problems, neither method is efficient or reliable.

In the high-dimensional setting, Sun and Zhang
[
36
]
introduced a novel tuning-free approach by proposing a new loss function for Lasso estimation that simultaneously estimates the trend and the variance parameters. This work inspired the development of the square-root Lasso
[
2
]
. For further research along this line, see
[
5
,
23
,
31
]
and references therein.

In the heavy-tailed setting, Sun
[
35
]
proposed a tuning-free method based on a new pseudo-Huber loss, which enables simultaneous estimation of the mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Specifically, given
n
observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, Sun considered the minimization problem

(1.4)

(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{v})={\rm argmin}_{\mu,v}L(\mu,v)\ \ \ {\rm where}\ \ L(\mu,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\psi_{\rm ps}\left(\frac{\alpha(X_{i}-\mu)}{v}\right)v+\lambda v\right],

where
\psi_{\rm ps}(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}-1
is the pseudo-Huber loss function, and
\alpha>0
,
\lambda>0
are adjustment factors. The pair
(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{v})
is then used to estimate
\mu
and
\sigma
simultaneously, obtained by solving

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}L(\mu,v)=0,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial v}L(\mu,v)=0.

Another tuning-free method was proposed by Wang et al.
[
45
]
, who introduced a new Huber-type estimator for the robustification parameter
\tau
, under the assumption that the mean
\mu
is known. Since
\tau\propto\sigma
up to constants depending on
n
and
d
, estimating
\tau
is equivalent to estimating
\sigma^{2}
. Their estimator achieves performance comparable to the sample variance in the sub-Gaussian setting. Building on this, and the adaptive Huber estimator for the mean from
[
37
]
, they developed a splitting algorithm that sequentially estimates
\tau
and
\mu
, and further extended the method to regression problems. However, they did not establish theoretical guarantees for the convergence of their algorithms.

1.2.
Our contributions and methods

In this paper, we introduce a joint robust estimation method for three parametric statistical models involving heavy-tailed data: mean estimation, linear regression, and
\ell_{2}
-penalized linear regression, where both the targeted trend parameters and the variance parameter are unknown. Our approach is based on solving two coupled Catoni-type equations—one for estimating the target parameters and the other for estimating the variance parameter. Our main contributions are summarized as the following.

(i) Our joint estimators differ substantially from existing approaches
[
35
,
36
]
. Their methods rely on minimizing a loss function
L(\bm{\theta},v)
, where
\bm{\theta}
estimates the target parameter and
v^{2}
approximates the variance. Since
L
is differentiable, the first-order condition

(1.5)

\nabla L(\bm{\theta},v)=0

always admits solutions, with
\nabla
being taken with respect to both
\theta
and
v
. Note that
\nabla L(\bm{\theta},v)
serves as a generalized score function, although it is often difficult to construct such a loss function for simultaneously estimating two distinct types of parameters. This difficulty arises because the parameters must be coupled in a specific form. For instance, in our setting (
2.2
) below,
\theta
and
v
appear jointly as
(X-\theta)/v
, leaving little flexibility in designing
L(\theta,v)
. By contrast, our method is based on solving two coupled Catoni-type equations (see (
2.2
), (
2.7
), (
2.11
)), which cannot be written in the form (
1.5
). Thus, extablishing the existence of the solutions to these equations is our first challenge.

(ii) The length of the resulting
(1-\epsilon)
-confidence interval with
\epsilon\in(0,1)
scales as
\sqrt{\log\epsilon^{-1}}
, matching the order achieved by classical estimators for subgaussian data.

(iii) Our joint approach estimates the target parameters and the variance simultaneously, and thus achieves the tuning-free property specified in
[
36
,
35
]
. In contrast, although
[
35
]
requires only a weaker second-moment condition, it does not provide variance estimation at any predictable rate, whereas our method estimates the variance at a rate governed by the availability of higher moments, thereby exhibiting a form of adaptivity in variance estimation. Furthermore, our framework offers flexibility in the choice of Catoni-type functions. In Section
3.6
, we also conducted experiments to examine how different choices of Catoni-type function affect the estimation performance and offered our preliminary recommendations of function selection.
Developing a systematic and data-driven strategy for selecting Catoni-type functions remains an interesting direction for future research.

(iv) The explicit presence of the variance in the denominators of both equations (see (
2.2
), (
2.7
), (
2.11
)) enhances the robustness of our estimators against heavy-tailed distributions. In particular, as the variance of the data increases, our joint method demonstrates substantially stronger performance than existing robust techniques. This intuition is confirmed by the simulation studies in Sections
3.1
–
3.3
.

(v) We rigorously establish the consistency of the three joint robust estimators introduced in Section
2
using analytical techniques. As illustrated in (
2.2
), (
2.7
), and (
2.11
), the coupled equations exhibit substantial complexity, rendering the theoretical analysis of their solutions challenging. To address these challenges, we employ the Poincaré–Miranda Theorem to show that the solutions lie within certain geometric regions, such as cylinders or cones, centered around the true parameter values. Crucially, we verify that the conditions for the theorem hold with high probability. This is a delicate procedure: deriving auxiliary algebraic equations, developing a refined comparison framework, and bounding suprema along the boundaries of the geometric regions. The resulting methodology is of independent interest, as it provides a feasible and general framework for developing non-asymptotic theory for joint or coupled estimation of multiple parameters, and can be extended to other statistical problems.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section
2
introduces our joint robust estimators for three settings: mean estimation, linear regression, and high-dimensional linear regression with an
\ell_{2}
penalty, and presents the corresponding main results (Theorems
2.1
–
2.4
). Section
3.1
provides simulation studies and real-data analyses. We demonstrate that our joint robust estimators consistently outperform existing methods such as Catoni’s estimator and adaptive Huber estimators, with the performance gap widening as the heaviness of the tails increases. Two real-data examples are considered: the Boston housing dataset, which explores the relationship between environmental factors and average regional housing prices, and the NCI-60 dataset, where we investigate the relationship between RNA and protein expression in cancer cell lines (see Section 3.4 for details).

The detailed proofs of the main results in Section
2
are deferred to the appendices. Appendix A collects two auxiliary lemmas: an algebraic comparison lemma used to characterize the regimes of solutions to the proposed estimators, and a maximal inequality for bounding exponential moments. Appendices
B
–
D
contain the proofs of Theorems
2.1
–
2.4
, together with outlines of their proof strategies.

2.
Joint robust estimations

In this section, we introduce three joint robust estimation methods: joint robust mean–variance estimation, joint robust regression, and joint robust ridge regression, presented in Sections
2.1
–
2.3
, respectively.

2.1.
Joint robust mean and variance estimation

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from an unknown distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathds{R}
satisfying

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mu,\quad\mathbb{E}|X_{i}-\mu|^{2}=\sigma^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}|X_{i}-\mu|^{2\beta}=m_{2\beta}<\infty,

for some
\beta\in(1,2]
. Our goal is to estimate
\mu
and
\sigma
simultaneously.

For a given confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
, we consider the following coupled equations for
(\theta,v)
:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&f_{1}(\theta,v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)}{v}\right)=0,\\
&f_{2}(\theta,v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{2}\left(\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{v^{2}}-1\right)\right)=0\end{cases},

where

(2.3)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(n^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}},\quad\alpha_{2}:=\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}},

and
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are continuous non-decreasing functions satisfying (
1.1
) and (
1.3
), each with a root at
0
.
If we take
\psi_{1}(x)=x
and
\psi_{2}(x)=x
, then the corresponding equations in (
2.2
) become the well-known moment equations and immediately yield the classical sample mean and variance estimators. Conditions in (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) for the two functions can help reduce the effects of the data far from the true mean, while
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
determine a scale for such reduction.

We show that (
2.2
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\theta},\widehat{v})
that consistently estimates
(\mu,\sigma)
. It is evident that there is no loss function
F(\theta,v)
such that

\nabla F(\theta,v)=[f_{1}(\theta,v),f_{2}(\theta,v)]’,

that is,
[f_{1}(\theta,v),f_{2}(\theta,v)]’
is not the gradient of any function. Thus, (
2.2
) cannot be solved by minimizing a loss function. Instead, we develop a delicate comparison argument to establish that (
2.2
) admits a solution converging to the true value
(\mu,\sigma)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the joint estimation (
2.2
) with any confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,{1}/{4})
. There exists a constant
C=C(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta)
such that, for all
n\geq C[\log(\epsilon^{-1})+1]
, (
2.2
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\theta},\widehat{v})
satisfying, with probability at least
1-4\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\big|\widehat{\theta}-\mu\big|\leq 6\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(n^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}},

and

\displaystyle\left(1+c_{0}\left(\tfrac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\tfrac{\beta-1}{\beta}}\right)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\sigma\leq\widehat{v}\leq\left(1-c_{0}\left(\tfrac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\tfrac{\beta-1}{\beta}}\right)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\sigma,

where
c_{0}=c_{0}(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta)
does not depend on
n
or
\epsilon
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is provided in Appendix
B
, with its proof strategy outlined at the beginning.

The theorem above shows that our approach can estimate both the mean and variance with only logarithmic dependence on
1/\epsilon
, a property unattainable by non-robust methods such as the sample mean when only a
2\beta
-moment exists. Furthermore, our result demonstrates that the variance is estimated at a rate dictated by the availability of higher moments, thereby exhibiting a form of adaptivity in variance estimation too. In contrast, although
[
35
]
requires only a weaker second-moment condition, it does not provide variance estimation at any predictable rate.

2.2.
Joint robust regression

Consider i.i.d. data samples
(y_{1},\bm{x}_{1}),\dots,(y_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\in\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}^{d}
following the linear model

(2.4)

\displaystyle y_{i}=\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{*}\rangle+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
are i.i.d. noise terms satisfying

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|\bm{x}_{i}]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}|\bm{x}_{i}]=\sigma^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{2\beta}=\mathbb{E}\left[|\varepsilon_{i}|^{2\beta}|\bm{x}_{i}\right]<\infty,\quad\text{for some}\quad\beta\in(1,2],

and
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
denotes the true regression coefficient. Let

(2.6)

L=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}<\infty.

Our goal is to estimate
\bm{\theta}^{*}
and
\sigma
simultaneously.

For a given confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{6})
, consider the following coupled equations for
\bm{\theta}
and
v
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\tilde{f}_{1}(\bm{\theta},v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}\frac{(y_{i}-\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle)}{v}\right)=\bm{0},\\
&\tilde{f}_{2}(\bm{\theta},v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{2}\left(\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{(y_{i}-\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle)^{2}}{v^{2}}-1\right)\right)=0,\end{cases}

where

(2.8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{\log(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}},\alpha_{2}:=\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}},

and
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
are non-decreasing continuous functions satisfying (
1.1
) and (
1.3
), each with a root at
0
.
Similar to the intuitive interpretation in Section
2.1
, if we take
\psi_{1}(x)=x
and
\psi_{2}(x)=x
, then the equation (
2.7
) immediately yields the classical linear regression. The conditions (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) and the choices of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
reduce the effect of the data far from the true values and thus produce robustness.

We will show that (
2.7
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}},\widehat{v})
that consistently estimates
(\bm{\theta}^{*},\sigma)
. Note that there exists no loss function
\tilde{F}(\bm{\theta},v)
such that
\nabla\tilde{F}(\bm{\theta},v)=[\tilde{f}_{1}(\bm{\theta},v),\tilde{f}_{2}(\bm{\theta},v)]’
, so the coupled equations cannot be solved via standard loss minimization. Instead, we employ a delicate comparison argument, which can be of independent interest.

Assumption 1
.

The empirical Gram matrix

(2.9)

\mathbf{S}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{T}

is nonsingular. Moreover, there exist constants
c_{l},c_{u}>0
such that

(2.10)

c_{l}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\leq c_{u},

where
\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbf{S}_{n}\right)
and
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{S}_{n}\right)
denote the minimal and maximal eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Although Assumption
1
is stated in a deterministic form, a high-probability or other random-design variant (e.g.,
[
38
]
) does not affect our main results. Since our focus is on heavy-tailed noise, this assumption avoids detailed discussions on spectral properties of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
under random designs. For further reference, see
[
39
,
40
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider (
2.7
) with confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,{1}/{6})
, and suppose Assumption
1
holds. There exists a constant
C=C(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{l},c_{u},L)
such that, for

n\geq Cd\left[\log(\epsilon^{-1})+1\right],

with probability at least
1-6\epsilon
, (
2.7
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}},\widehat{v})
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d\log\left(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}},

and

\displaystyle\left(1+C_{2}\left(\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma\leq\widehat{v}\leq\left(1-C_{2}\left(\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma,

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{l},c_{u},L)
,
C_{2}=C_{2}(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{l},c_{u},L)
, and

\displaystyle\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}=\sqrt{\frac{d\log\left(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}}+\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is provided in Appendix
C
, with the proof strategy outlined at the beginning.

2.3.
Joint robust ridge regression

Recall the linear regression in Section
2.2
. We now consider this problem in the context of penalized regression models, which are widely used in high-dimensional settings to improve estimation accuracy and interpretability. Our proposed method can be naturally extended to such penalized frameworks.

Specifically, we focus on the case with an
\ell_{2}
-penalty (ridge regression). For a given confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{6})
,
consider the following coupled equation about
({\bm{\theta}},{v})
:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\tilde{f}_{1}^{ridge}(\bm{\theta},v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\psi_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}\frac{\left(y_{i}-\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle\right)}{v}\right)-\lambda\bm{\theta}=\bm{0},\\
&\tilde{f}_{2}^{ridge}(\bm{\theta},v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{2}\left(\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{\left(y_{i}-\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle\right)^{2}}{v^{2}}-1\right)\right)=0,\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
are defined as in (
2.8
),
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\alpha_{1}
and
\lambda_{0}
is the penalty coefficient which will be determined later. Our assumptions remain the same as in Section
2.2
. We can intuitively interpret the equation (
2.11
) in a way similar to that in Section
2.2
, here the term
\lambda\bm{\theta}
is obtained from differentiating the penalty
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}_{2}
.

We shall show that (
2.11
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}},\widehat{v})
which is a consistent estimator for
(\bm{\theta}^{*},\sigma)
. Similar to the previous two cases, we cannot solve the equation (
2.11
) by minimizing a loss function and need to use a very delicate comparison argument. To more clearly illustrate the impact of the minimum eigenvalue of the Gram matrix on the estimator, we explicitly present the dependence on
c_{l}
(defined in (
2.10
)) in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Recall (
2.11
) with a confidence level
\epsilon\in(0,{1}/{6})
. Let Assumption
1
hold. There exists a constant
C=C(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{u},L)
such that, as long as

\displaystyle n\geq Cd(c_{l}+\lambda_{0})^{-1}[\log(\epsilon^{-1})+1],

with probability at least
1-6\epsilon
, (
2.11
) admits a solution
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}},\widehat{v})
which satisfies the following error bounds:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}^{*}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}L}{\lambda_{0}+c_{l}/\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{0}+c_{l}/\sigma}\|\bm{\theta^{*}}\|_{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle\left(1+C_{2}\left(\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{\theta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma\leq\widehat{v}\leq\left(1-C_{2}\left(\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{\theta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma,

with
C_{1}=C_{1}(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{u},L),C_{2}=C_{2}(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{u},L)
and

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=C_{1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}L}{\lambda_{0}+c_{l}/\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda_{0}+c_{l}/\sigma}\|\bm{\theta^{*}}\|_{2}\right).

In particular, if we take
\lambda_{0}=O\left(L\sqrt{\frac{d\log(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}^{*}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq O\left(L\sqrt{\frac{d\log(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}\right).

The proof of Theorem
2.4
will be given in Appendix
D
, with its strategy being clearly sketched out at the beginning.

3.
Simulations and real data analysis

In this section, we present three numerical experiments and two real-data applications to examine the performance of our joint estimators. The numerical experiments cover mean estimation, linear regression, and high-dimensional sparse linear regression. Performance is assessed using two criteria: (i) the quantiles of the estimation errors at levels between 0.9 and 1, and (ii) the dependence of the 99% quantile of the estimation errors on parameters characterizing heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., degrees of freedom, shape parameters). The first real-data application involves the Boston housing dataset, which examines the relationship between environmental factors and average housing prices across regions. The second uses the NCI-60 Cancer Cell Lines dataset to study the relationship between gene and protein expression levels. In both cases, we compare our joint robust estimators with ridge regression and adaptive Huber estimators. We also briefly discuss how to choose Catoni type functions in practice in mean estimation problems.

Throughout the numerical experiments, we set
\epsilon=0.01

•

\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
;

•

standard student’s

•

Pareto distribution;

•

Fréchet distribution.

In this experiment, we evalute the error stability of three type of Catoni-type robust estimators:

For the first two estimtors, we use the widest influence function satisfying (

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\log\left(1+x+x^{2}/2\right),&x\geq 0\\

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}}.

For our joint estimator (3), following Theorem

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(n^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}},\quad\alpha_{2}=\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{1/\beta}.

3.6.
A brief discussion on the selection of Catoni type functions

In the simulations and real data analysis in Sections
3.1
-
3.5
, we chose special
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
and did not give reasons for such choices. The theory about selecting Catoni functions is an important and delicate topic, we leave it for the future research and demonstrate how to practically choose such functions through the example of mean estimation.

We conduct numerical experiments to compare joint Catoni-type estimators under three choices of the
\psi_{1}
function across two pairs of data-generating distributions (symmetric vs. asymmetric variants). Specifically, we consider:

(1)

The widest function satisfying Catoni’s conditions (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{wide}(x):=\begin{cases}\log\left(1+x+x^{2}/2\right),&x\geq 0\\
-\log\left(1-x+x^{2}/2\right),&x<0\end{cases}

(2)

The narrowest function satisfying Catoni’s conditions:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{narrow}(x)=\begin{cases}-\operatorname{sign}(x)\log\left(1-|x|+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right),&|x|\leq 1,\\
\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(2),&|x|>1,\end{cases}

(3)

An intermediate profile constructed by mixing the widest and narrowest function:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{mid}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{1}^{wide}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{1}^{narrow}(x).

For all three joint Catoni methods,
\psi_{2}
is set to the widest choice

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}\log\left(1+x+|x|^{\beta}/\beta\right),&x\geq 0\\
-\log\left(1-x+|x|^{\beta}/\beta\right),&x<0\end{cases}

and the parameter settings are the same as in Section
3.1
:
\beta=(2.1-0.01)/2
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(n^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}}\text{ and }\alpha_{2}=\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{1/\beta}.

Under this design, we evaluate the estimation error of the three joint Catoni methods when samples follow the following distribution pairs:

(i)

Student t distribution vs. one-sided (half) t distribution with degree of freedom
=2.1
;

(ii)

symmetric double Pareto distribution vs. (one-sided) Pareto distribution with shape parameter
=2.1
.

For each distribution pair, we perform
1,000
independent trials with
n=500
observations per trial. The quantiles of three Catoni joint estimation method at quantile levels between
0.9
and
1
are reported in Figure
9
. From the figure, the three methods exhibit different relative performance under symmetric versus asymmetric settings. When the sample distribution is symmetric, the narrowest Catoni variant consistently performs best, while the widest Catoni variant yields the largest errors. In contrast, when the distribution is asymmetric, this ordering reverses: the widest Catoni becomes the best among the three. The middle Catoni performs in between the narrowest and the widest variants across all cases.

We interpret these patterns as follows. The narrowest Catoni attenuates outliers most aggressively, which most effectively reduces variance under symmetry. However, when the distribution is asymmetric, discarding the magnitude information of outliers introduces substantial bias, thereby worsening overall error. Based on this understanding, we suggest choosing the Catoni function according to the degree of symmetry in the data (e.g., the gap between the mean and the median). If nothing is known about the target distribution’s symmetry, the middle Catoni may be a robust default that provides a balanced trade-off.

Moreover, in linear regression models the noise is typically assumed to be symmetric. However, we argue that symmetry of the noise alone is insufficient to determine which choice is preferable; one must also account for the effect of
\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}\rangle
. This point needs further investigation.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
X
be a random variable with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then for any
x,y\in\mathds{R}
, and
0<a_{-}\leq a_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{v\in[a_{-},a_{+}]}\left\{\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)}{v}\right\}\right]\leq\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(\mu-y)}{a_{+}}+\left(\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{a_{-}a_{+}}\right)\left(\sigma+|\mu-y|\right).

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, it is clear that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{v\in[a_{-},a_{+}]}\left\{\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)}{v}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)}{a_{-}}1_{\{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)\geq 0\}}+\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)}{a_{+}}1_{\{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)<0\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(\mu-y)}{a_{+}}+\left(\frac{1}{a_{-}}-\frac{1}{a_{+}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)1_{\{\operatorname{sign}(x)(X-y)\geq 0\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(\mu-y)}{a_{+}}+\left(\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{a_{-}a_{+}}\right)\left(\mathbb{E}|X-\mu|+|\mu-y|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x)(\mu-y)}{a_{+}}+\left(\frac{a_{+}-a_{-}}{a_{-}a_{+}}\right)\left(\sigma+|\mu-y|\right).

So the proof is complete.
∎

By changing the variable with
t=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{v^{2}}-1
, we can obtain

(C.45)

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{v}_{+}(v)

\displaystyle\leq a|t|^{\beta}+bt+c:=\bar{B}^{v}_{+}(t),

for some positive

\displaystyle a=O\left(\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}\right),\quad b=1+O(\alpha_{1}),

and

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}+\frac{(1+\frac{1}{\theta_{0}})c_{u}\theta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{2})\sigma^{2}}+\frac{2^{5\beta-4}\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}}{\beta}\left(\frac{m_{2\beta}}{\sigma^{2\beta}}+1+\frac{c_{u}L^{2\beta-2}}{\theta}_{0}^{2\beta}{(1-\alpha_{2})\sigma^{2\beta}}\right)+\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}).

Recall that

\alpha_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(dn^{\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-1}\right)}{n}},\alpha_{2}=\left(\frac{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}.

It is clear that we can find some positive constant
C=C(m_{2\beta},\sigma,\beta,c_{l},c_{u},L)
such that

\displaystyle b-a(2c)^{\beta-1}>\frac{1}{2},

provided
n\geq Cd[\log(\epsilon^{-1})+1]
.
In this case, Lemma (
A.1
)(i) implies that the equation
\bar{B}^{v}_{+}(t)=0
has two roots. Denote the largest one by
t_{+}
and
v_{+}:=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{t_{+}+1}}
. Lemma (
A.1
)(i) also tells us that

\displaystyle t_{+}\geq-\frac{c}{b-a(2c})^{\beta-1}\geq-2c\geq-c_{0}\left(\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}\right),

whenever
n\geq Cd[\log(\epsilon^{-1})+1]
for some (possibly larger) positive constant

, which implies

\displaystyle v_{+}\leq\left(1-c_{0}\left(\sqrt{d}\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{\beta-1}\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma=V_{0}.

By (
C.45
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{v}_{+}(v_{+})\leq\bar{B}_{+}^{v}(t_{+})=0,

which is exactly (
C.35
). The (
C.36

∎
