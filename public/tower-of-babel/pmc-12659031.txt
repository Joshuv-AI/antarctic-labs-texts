The impact of respiratory diseases on quality of life since the pandemic: a dual perspective based on objective consumption upgrading and subjective well-being
Authors: Yiyan Chen, Lijun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated the global burden of respiratory diseases (RD), intensifying syndemic threats and the “immunity debt” effect. However, evidence gaps persist regarding RD impacts on Quality of Life (QoL) and socioeconomic disparities in the post-pandemic context.MethodsCross-sectional data from the 2022 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) covered Chinese residents aged 15–89. Surveys were conducted from May to December 2022. QoL was assessed via Consumption Upgrading Index (CUI) and Subjective Well-Being (SWB). Regression models, Propensity Score Matching (PSM), and mediation/moderation analyses examined RD-QoL relationships.Results(1) RD prevalence reached 39.94% in 2022 (a 1.29-percentage-point increase from 2020). (2) RD showed significant negative correlations with QoL, reducing both CUI and SWB. (3) Subgroup analyses revealed greater CUI reductions among males, rural residents, the unemployed, and Eastern China residents; more pronounced SWB declines occurred among females and Eastern residents. (4) Pandemic incidence exacerbated RD’s negative impact on CUI (particularly for females). (5) Hospitalization (health mediator) and reduced non-medical spending (economic mediator) were core pathways.ConclusionsPost-pandemic RD burden creates “dual deprivation” of QoL, undermining economic resilience and mental health. Policies addressing sex/urban-rural disparities and strengthening integrated health-economic safeguards are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25300-9.Keywords: Pandemic, Respiratory diseases, Quality of life, Consumption upgrading, Subjective well-being

Introduction

Quality of Life (QoL) is a cornerstone of public health, serving as a key indicator of individual health outcomes and a critical measure for evaluating public health initiatives and societal development [1, 2]. The World Health Organization defines QoL as “individuals’ perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live” [3], a concept that encompasses both subjective appraisal and objective living conditions. In line with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the actualization of a high quality of life extends beyond physical and psychological well-being to include the ability to pursue and attain an elevated standard of living [4]. This pursuit is materially reflected in “consumption upgrading”—the shift of household expenditure from basic subsistence towards developmental goods and services such as education, culture, and recreation, which are central to a fulfilling life.

Illness is a primary determinant that disrupts this pursuit [5]. Respiratory diseases (RD) represent a quintessential example, being one of the most prevalent non-communicable disease groups globally. They impose a massive and growing health burden. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2017 identified chronic respiratory diseases as the third leading cause of death and the fifth leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) worldwide [6]. A robust body of evidence confirms the profoundly detrimental impact of RDs on patients’ QoL, affecting both physiological and psychological dimensions [7–9]. Crucially, the economic burden of RDs—through direct medical costs and indirect costs such as productivity loss—forces a reallocation of household resources, often at the expense of developmental consumption. This process of “consumption downgrading” reshapes household consumption structures, making it a critical, yet often overlooked, objective indicator of diminished QoL [10].

The COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally altered the landscape of respiratory health, introducing a new layer of complexity and urgency. The virus itself causes significant respiratory sequelae, with many survivors experiencing persistent dyspnea, diminished pulmonary function, and impaired diffusion capacity, leading to substantial declines in Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQL) [11–13]. Furthermore, pandemic control measures and the concept of “immunity debt” have disrupted the circulation patterns of other respiratory pathogens, potentially increasing population susceptibility [14]. Critically, the pandemic has acted as a massive societal shock, exacerbating healthcare resource constraints [15], amplifying economic insecurity, and attaching stigma to respiratory symptoms [16]. This creates a novel syndemic context where pre-existing RD burdens interact with the direct and indirect effects of COVID-19, likely exacerbating their combined impact on QoL through both health and economic pathways.

However, current evidence remains inadequate to fully capture this evolving reality. Pre-pandemic research on RDs and QoL was predominantly clinically focused [17], while post-2020 studies often lack a comprehensive socioeconomic lens. Significant gaps persist in understanding: (1) the dual impact of RDs on both subjective well-being and objective economic living standards in the general population since the pandemic; (2) the underlying mechanisms, particularly the mediating roles of health shocks and constrained household consumption; and (3) how the severity of the pandemic itself moderates the RD-QoL relationship, a critical dimension of the pandemic’s exogenous shock.

To address these gaps, this study employs data from the 2022 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) to empirically analyze the impact of RD on QoL from a novel dual perspective. We integrate the subjective dimension (Subjective Well-Being) and the objective economic dimension (Consumption Upgrading Index) to provide a holistic assessment. Our analytical framework (Fig. 1) further investigates the mediating roles of hospitalization (health pathway) and non-medical consumption expenditure (economic pathway), and innovatively examines the moderating effect of regional COVID-19 incidence. By doing so, this research aims to elucidate the “dual deprivation” mechanism of RD on QoL in the post-pandemic era and provide empirical evidence for crafting integrated health and economic policies.

Analytical framework

Methods

Data

This study utilizes data from the 2022 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey conducted by Peking University. The CFPS employs a multi-stage probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling design, covering 25 provinces/municipalities/autonomous regions that represent about 95% of China’s population. Data collection for the 2022 wave occurred from May to December, primarily via telephone interviews due to the pandemic, with face-to-face interviews implemented only where permissible under local public health policies. The questionnaire included supplemental content related to the pandemic. Since respiratory disease-related questions were only fielded in the 2020 and 2022 waves, our descriptive analysis uses both years. For the empirical analysis, we employ the 2022 cross-sectional data. After excluding invalid responses and observations with missing key variables, our final analytical sample consists of 23,280 individuals.

Variables

Respiratory disease(RD)

The core independent variable is whether the respondent had suffered from a respiratory disease (RD) in the past year. This information was derived from the CFPS questionnaire item asking respondents to report the most severe illness they had experienced within the preceding 12 months. The ascertainment of RD was based on self-reported data: respondents provided a textual description of their illness, and trained interviewers subsequently classified this information into standardized disease categories. Individuals whose descriptions were classified as acute nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory tract infection, influenza, pneumonia, chronic pharyngitis, emphysema, other chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, asthma, or other respiratory conditions were defined as having experienced an RD and coded as 1; all others were coded as 0.

Given that the 2022 survey was conducted from May to December, the reference period for RD (the past 12 months) corresponds approximately to May 2021 through December 2022. This period fully overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic era in China. Consequently, all RDs captured in our analytical sample occurred during the pandemic, which aligns with our study’s objective of investigating the RD-QoL relationship within this specific context.

Quality of life(QoL)

This study operationalizes Quality of Life (QoL) through both subjective and objective lenses. For the subjective dimension, we use Subjective Well-Being (SWB) as a proxy. Conceptual convergence and overlapping core components between SWB and QoL support its use as a valid indicator of the evaluative aspect of life quality [18]. SWB is derived from the survey question “How happy are you?” on an 11-point scale (0–10). This single-item measure is a parsimonious and widely used instrument in large-scale surveys for capturing global life evaluation, demonstrating good reliability and validity [19].

For the objective dimension, we measure “consumption upgrading” as a key economic manifestation of an elevated living standard, a concept aligned with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. Moderators, mediators, and control variables

Moderator variable

Pandemic Incidence (PI). This study investigates the moderating role of the COVID-19 pandemic’s intensity. We constructed a provincial-level measure of Pandemic Incidence by aggregating the number of newly confirmed COVID-19 cases and asymptomatic infections for each province over the period from May 1, 2021, to April 30, 2022, sourced from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC). This time window was deliberately chosen to align with the 12-month recall period for respiratory diseases and the survey timing. The China CDC data, being national and province-level, was successfully merged with the nationally representative CFPS dataset based on the province of residence. This variable serves as a proxy for the contextual shock of the pandemic at the regional level, reflecting factors like healthcare system pressure and social disruption, rather than individual infection status.

Mediator variables

Within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, respiratory diseases have imposed substantial adverse impacts on both individual health and household consumption structures [22]. This study examines the mediating roles of health status and household consumption structure in the pathway through which respiratory diseases diminish QoL. Health status is operationalized as hospitalization experience (HE), defined as “whether hospitalized due to illness within the preceding 12 months” (Yes = 1, No = 0). Household consumption structure was gauged by the proportion of non-medical consumption expenditure (NMCE). The NMCE was calculated as total consumption expenditure minus out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure. This expenditure is directly derived from the CFPS survey question that asks for the household’s direct medical payments over the past 12 months, specifically excluding any reimbursed or expected-to-be-reimbursed amounts. A higher NMCE ratio signifies a greater level of developmental consumption; conversely, a lower ratio indicates a higher level of subsistence consumption. Furthermore, we controlled for basic medical insurance status (M_ins) in all models to account for systematic differences in healthcare coverage.

Control variables

This study selected demographic variables, individual characteristic variables, and household characteristic variables as controls, incorporating provincial dummy variables. Control variables at the individual level encompassed age, sex, hukou status (registered residence status), marital status, educational attainment, employment status, self-rated health, and health insurance coverage. Household-level control variables included total household income, household net assets, and household size. Statistical analysis

Basic analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 software. Descriptive statistics, including T-tests, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and Chi-square tests, were employed to examine data distributions. For regression analysis, relationships between variables were estimated using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) models. Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was implemented to address potential selection bias among respondents.

Propensity score matching

To address potential selection bias, we use Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) of respiratory diseases on QoL. This method matches individuals with respiratory diseases (the treatment group) to comparable individuals without them (the control group) based on their observed characteristics.

First, we estimate the propensity score (the probability of having a respiratory disease) using a logistic regression that includes all control variables. Then, we employ multiple matching algorithms (Kernel-Based, Nearest Neighbor, and 1:1 Matching) to ensure robustness. The ATT is calculated as the average difference in QoL outcomes between the matched treated and control groups. Finally, we conduct a sensitivity analysis using the Rosenbaum bounds to assess the potential influence of unobserved confounders.

Quality test and treatment effects of propensity score matching

To further bolster the robustness of the findings, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was employed to alleviate the influence of sample bias and individual heterogeneity. Four distinct matching methods were implemented: 1:1 Nearest Neighbor Matching (with replacement), 1:2 Nearest Neighbor Matching (employed to mitigate information loss from matching failures, given the relatively small treatment group size and numerous matching covariates), radius matching, and kernel matching.

Balance test results are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (see Supplementary Table 1, Additional file 1). After matching, pseudo R², mean bias, and median bias decreased, indicating improved balance between treatment and control groups. Post-matching standardized bias decreased for all variables (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2a), with most observations within common support and minimal sample loss (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Additionally, the kernel density curves reveal greater overlap between the treatment and control groups after matching, satisfying the common support assumption (Supplementary Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2c).

After accounting for sample selection bias using PSM, the results confirm a significant negative association between RD and QoL. Table 4 shows that the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) is significantly negative at the 5% level. Specifically, under the 1:1 nearest neighbor matching approach, residents with RD exhibited a 1% point decline in CUI and a 6.4% point reduction in SWB. Comparable results were obtained using alternative matching methods, reinforcing the robustness of the PSM methodology.

Cohort difference analysis

The RD-QoL correlation exhibits marked heterogeneity across population subgroups. Research indicates differential responses to RD between men and women [23]. Chronic respiratory disease prevalence and mortality are elevated in rural areas compared to urban settings, attributable to inadequate healthcare resources [24, 25]. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic precipitated widespread unemployment, heightening RD risk among the unemployed [26–28]. Although the pandemic initially emerged in Wuhan, China, in 2020, by 2022 multiple infection waves had occurred in several Eastern China provinces [29, 30]. Consequently, investigating regional disparities in the RD-QoL impact since the pandemic’s onset is imperative.

This study conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex, urban/rural residence, employment status, and geographic region, calculating the ATT for QoL within each group (see Table 5). The results reveal significant heterogeneity in the RD impact on QoL across different populations. From the perspective of consumption upgrading (CUI), RD exerted a significant negative effect on men, rural residents, the unemployed, and residents of Eastern China, exhibiting CUI reductions of 1.5%, 1.6%, 1.5%, and 2.7%, respectively. Regarding SWB, RD generated a significant negative impact solely for women and Eastern China residents, manifesting as substantial SWB reductions of 17.1% and 15.6%, respectively. Overall, residents of Eastern China experienced the most pronounced decline in QoL attributable to RD. The detrimental effect of RD manifested primarily through diminished consumption upgrading for men, rural residents, and the unemployed, whereas its impact on women was predominantly manifested through reduced subjective well-being.

Discussion

Escalating respiratory diseases burden since the pandemic era

Our analysis based on nationally representative data, confirms that respiratory diseases (RD) was the most prevalent health concern among Chinese residents in 2022, with a significant increase in prevalence compared to 2020. This trend resonates with global concerns about syndemic interactions and the “immunity debt” phenomenon [14, 33], wherein reduced population exposure to common pathogens during lockdown periods heightens susceptibility following the relaxation of restrictions.

Furthermore, the direct impact of COVID-19 infection itself on the respiratory disease burden is undeniable. Survivors of COVID-19 pneumonia frequently exhibit persistent respiratory symptoms, diminished pulmonary function (DLCO impairment), and declines in Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) [34], even months into recovery [12, 13, 35, 36]. Together, these factors suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly altered the landscape of respiratory health in China. This rising burden superimposed upon a healthcare system strained by the pandemic [15], presents a complex public health challenge for China in the post-pandemic era.

The dual pathway: RD’s detrimental impact on objective and subjective QoL

Our findings provide robust evidence of a “dual deprivation” mechanism, whereby RD significantly undermine both the objective economic foundation and the subjective psychological perception of quality of life. The objective impact primarily stems from the economic burden of disease. Medical expenditures for RD treatment and medication directly compete with discretionary household spending, precipitating “consumption downgrading” [10, 37]. This is particularly pronounced for non-essential, development-oriented expenditures captured by the CUI indicator (e.g., education, cultural activities). Our mediation analysis corroborates this pathway, revealing that RD elevates the likelihood of hospitalization (a primary cost driver) and reduces the share of non-medical expenditure, both of which significantly mediate the negative effect on CUI. Pandemic-induced economic shocks, triggering widespread unemployment and income instability [38, 39], likely exacerbated this financial fragility, diminishing households’ capacity to absorb RD-related costs without sacrificing consumption quality.

The subjective impact operates through physiological, psychological, and social channels. RD induces physical discomfort (dyspnea, cough, fatigue), functional limitations, and anxiety regarding disease progression, directly diminishing life satisfaction [8, 40]. Crucially, the COVID-19 pandemic superimposed a unique layer of psychological distress. Respiratory symptoms became stigmatized as potential indicators of COVID-19 infection, triggering fears of social exclusion and discrimination [16, 41]. This “dual health threat” – coping with the RD itself while simultaneously navigating the fear and stigma associated with any respiratory symptom during the pandemic – imposed substantial mental strain. Hospitalization emerged as a key mediator reducing SWB, representing not only a significant health event but also a source of isolation and anxiety, amplified amidst strained healthcare resources. These findings align with broader observations of deteriorated mental health and Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQL) among COVID-19 survivors and the general population due to pandemic stressors like isolation, fear, and economic hardship [42–45].

Heterogeneous impacts: unmasking vulnerability and the pandemic’s catalytic role

A significant contribution of our research lies in revealing marked disparities in the impact of RD across different population groups, underscoring pre-existing social and economic inequities.

Sex disparities

Our results demonstrate a distinct sex differential pattern. RD exerted a more pronounced negative impact on objective consumption upgrading (CUI) for men, while its effect on subjective well-being (SWB) was more significant for women. From a socio-cultural perspective, men typically function as the primary income earners in Chinese households. Consequently, RD afflicting a male breadwinner can precipitate more direct and severe income loss or medical debt, thereby directly constraining household consumption capacity – manifested in CUI decline. Conversely, women predominantly shoulder primary responsibility for household health management and caregiving. A diagnosis of RD, whether for themselves or a family member, imposes a substantial emotional toll and anxiety, significantly impairing their well-being (SWB). The pandemic further amplified female vulnerability; the moderating effect of pandemic incidence on the impact of RD on consumption upgrading was significant only for women. This likely reflects their overrepresentation in precarious employment within unstable, informal, or service sectors, which were disproportionately affected by lockdowns and economic downturns [46], alongside potentially intensified caregiving burdens during the health crisis [31, 32].

Urban-rural divide

Compared to urban residents, RD inflicted a significantly stronger negative effect on consumption upgrading (CUI) among rural residents. This starkly reflects persistent structural inequities in healthcare access and socioeconomic resources [47, 48]. Despite efforts to strengthen primary healthcare in rural China, challenges such as shortages of specialized equipment, healthcare personnel, and quality medications persist. Rural residents with RD often face higher treatment costs, and may ultimately incur expenses at costly urban tertiary hospitals, undeniably exacerbating their financial burden and time cost. Lower average incomes and less robust social safety nets in rural areas further diminish their capacity to shoulder these costs without drastic cuts to non-essential consumption.

Employment and regional vulnerability

The pronounced negative impact of RD on CUI among the unemployed underscores their extreme economic precarity. Without an income buffer, even moderate medical expenses can force severe consumption downgrading. Similarly, the significantly larger declines in CUI and SWB observed in Eastern China likely stemmed from experiencing more intense and frequent COVID-19 infection waves in 2022 [29, 30]. Higher population density, greater mobility, and potentially earlier exposure to new variants in this economically dynamic region may have contributed to higher infection rates, thereby amplifying the interplay between the RD burden and pandemic disruptions.

The pandemic as catalyst

Our findings robustly confirm that the COVID-19 pandemic served as a critical moderator, exacerbating the negative impact of RD on QoL, particularly concerning objective consumption upgrading (CUI). This amplification occurred through several intertwined mechanisms: (1) Economic Shocks: Pandemic-induced recessions and unemployment eroded household financial resilience, making it harder to manage RD-related costs without sacrificing consumption quality [38, 39]. (2) Healthcare Strain: Cyclical surges overwhelmed healthcare systems [15], potentially delaying non-COVID care, increasing out-of-pocket costs for RD management, and heightening anxiety about accessing timely treatment. (3) Stigma and Fear: Heightened awareness of respiratory symptoms fostered an environment where any cough or shortness of breath could evoke fears of a COVID-19 diagnosis and associated social isolation [16, 41], adding psychological distress to the physical burden of RD. (4) “Dual Burden” Effect: Having RD during the pandemic meant confronting the challenges of a chronic/acute condition amidst heightened health risks, resource constraints, and pervasive fear, exerting a uniquely synergistic negative effect on QoL. The critical mediating roles of hospitalization and reduced non-medical expenditure highlight how the pandemic intensified the traditional pathways through which RD erodes economic well-being.

Innovation and limitations

The contributions of this study are fourfold: (1) Quantification of the high and rising prevalence of RD in post-pandemic China using nationally representative data; (2) Adoption of a novel, pragmatic dual-perspective QoL assessment (CUI & SWB), effectively capturing core economic and psychological dimensions while avoiding the complexity of multi-domain instruments and providing enhanced clarity; (3) Provision of the first empirical demonstration of the moderating role of COVID-19 pandemic incidence on the RD-QoL relationship, particularly concerning its economic impact; and (4) Elucidation of critical heterogeneity in vulnerability, thereby informing targeted interventions.

Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, the CFPS commenced surveying disease experiences in 2020. Considering the potential lagged effects of diseases and the temporal relevance of the data, this study utilized cross-sectional data from 2022. While the application of Propensity Score Matching (PSM) mitigates the influence of selection bias to some extent, future research should incorporate longitudinal data to facilitate a more profound exploration of the causal relationship between respiratory diseases and QoL. Second, the PSM methodology inherently entails the loss of unmatched samples and a consequent reduction in sample size, potentially introducing residual bias. Third, constrained by data availability, the survey solely inquired whether respondents had experienced an RD within the preceding 12 months based on self-reported illness descriptions, which were subsequently classified by interviewers rather than by clinical diagnosis. This may introduce measurement error. We were unable to ascertain whether the reported RD was attributable to COVID-19 or other pathogens; this factor of etiological attribution will be incorporated into our future research agenda.

Conclusion

Utilizing data from the China Family Panel Studies, our research reveals that respiratory diseases (RD) constituted the most prevalent health concern in China during 2020–2022, exhibiting an upward trend and significantly impairing both the objective economic dimension (consumption upgrading) and the subjective psychological dimension (well-being) of Quality of Life (QoL). This deleterious impact is not uniformly distributed; it disproportionately burdens men (economically), women (psychologically), rural residents, the unemployed, and individuals in regions experiencing intense pandemic waves. The COVID-19 pandemic functioned as a potent amplifier, exacerbating the economic toll of RD, primarily mediated through heightened hospitalization risk and constrained household developmental expenditure. These findings underscore a “dual deprivation” mechanism of RD on QoL within the post-pandemic context and illuminate stark health inequities.

Addressing this challenge necessitates a paradigm shift in public health governance, moving from acute “disease treatment” towards a holistic “maintenance and enhancement of Quality of Life.” Policy interventions must be multifaceted: (1) Strengthening the “Prevention-Treatment-Safeguard” Continuum: Enhance vaccination programs, early diagnosis and management of chronic RD at the primary care level, and integrate mental health support into respiratory care pathways. (2) Financial Protection: Improve health insurance integration with targeted consumption subsidies or social safety nets to mitigate catastrophic expenditure risk and prevent RD from triggering a vicious cycle of impoverishment through consumption downgrading and human capital erosion. (3) Addressing Vulnerability: Develop sex-sensitive policies acknowledging the mental health burden on women and the RD-associated economic precarity among men. Intensify efforts to bridge the urban-rural health resource gap and bolster economic resilience in pandemic-prone regions. Confronting the epidemic of respiratory diseases and their socioeconomic repercussions demands proactive, integrated, and equitable strategies focused on safeguarding both physical health and holistic well-being in the post-pandemic world.
