Nonoperative Management of the Adult Spinal Deformity Patient: A Biopsychosocial Intervention Versus Care-as-usual - A Comparative Treatment-Based Cohort Study With One-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Evelien H. W. Hoevenaars, Michiel Beekhuizen, Ahmet Alanay, Yann Philippe Charles, Frank Kleinstueck, Ibrahim Obeid, John O’Dowd, Ferran Pellisé, Javier Pizones, Maarten Spruit, Marinus de Kleuver, Miranda L. van Hooff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study designComparative Cohort Study.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to compare a biopsychosocial intervention for chronic low back pain to unimodal, biomedically focused care-as-usual in symptomatic, conservatively managed adult spinal deformity (ASD) patients.MethodsA comparative cohort study of a biopsychosocial intervention cohort (n=95) and a care-as-usual cohort (n=95). Inclusion criteria of both cohorts: age ≥18, no surgical indication, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)>20, coronal Cobb angle ≥20, idiopathic (AdIS) or degenerative (de novo) scoliosis (DN), baseline and one-year follow-up ODI data. Primary outcome: functional status (ODIv2.1a). Secondary outcomes: pain intensity (NPRS) and quality of life (SF36-PCS and SF36-MCS). Clinical relevance: minimal important clinical change (MCIC; improvement in ODI≥10 points), patient acceptable symptom state (PASS; ODI≤22).ResultsAt one-year follow-up the patients in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort showed significantly greater improvement in functional status (ODI) compared to the care-as-usual cohort (z=-3.97, p=<.001). Clinical relevance in terms of functional status: 49% of the patients in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort reached MCIC and 34% reached a PASS, compared to 23% and 15% respectively in the care-as-usual cohort. All secondary outcomes followed a similar pattern: greater improvement in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort.ConclusionThis study shows clinically relevant and significantly greater improvement in function, pain, and quality of life in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort, compared to the unimodal, biomedically focused care-as-usual cohort at one-year follow-up. Targeting biopsychosocial factors offers a promising option in terms of improving outcome in nonoperative care for ASD patients with predominant back pain symptoms.

Introduction

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) is a malalignment of the spine in the sagittal, coronal and/or axial plane in a skeletally mature patient and a common symptom is back pain and limitations in function.1-4 ASD has a high prevalence in individuals aged older than 60 years, affecting up to 68% of that population. 1 The clinical and economic burden of ASD for society is growing5,6 and the global burden is huge compared to other chronic conditions. 7 The ASD spectrum includes primary degenerative scoliosis (de novo degenerative lumbar scoliosis [DN]), progressive adolescent idiopathic scoliosis in adulthood (AdIS) and secondary adult deformity (e.g. hyperkyphosis, iatrogenic sagittal deformity, post-traumatic spinal deformity, etc.).5,8 Despite the obvious necessity of effective ASD management, many questions remain and treatment planning is challenging. 9

ASD can be managed non-surgically or surgically. Non-surgical management includes invasive, non-invasive and no treatment, and is regarded as the first line treatment, despite an absence of strong supporting evidence. In general systematic reviews conclude that the overall evidence on non-surgical ASD treatment is lacking or of low quality and is derived from observational studies with a high risk of bias, is limited to extremely small case reports and expert opinion.5,10-12 A recent document on non-operative spine care created by the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) comprehensive care committee raises awareness to this lack in research. 9 Nonoperative treatment of ASD is a research topic on the SRS research agenda.

Although a plethora of nonoperative treatments exists for patients with back pain, 13 such as pain medication, physical therapy, injections, osteopathy/chiropractic, etc., many authors have emphasised the biopsychosocial influences on the development of chronic low back pain (CLBP) and persistence of symptoms 14 and a broad multidimensional approach is widely recognised. Evidence exists for an intensive combined physical and psychological pain management programme (biopsychosocial intervention) for patients with CLBP and clinically relevant improvements were shown in daily functioning, pain and quality of life. Positive one year results and reduction in healthcare utilization are maintained at a minimum of five year follow up.15,16 A recent study showed that this intervention might be beneficial, in terms of functioning, self-efficacy, pain and HR-QoL, for patients with ASD and with longstanding CLBP and for whom surgery is not an option. The clinically relevant improvements were maintained at one-year follow up, and appeared meaningful for patients. At one-year follow-up assessment a third of the ASD patients reached an acceptable symptom state with respect to functioning. 17 This is surprising given the assumption that the ASD cohort is in many respects a more complicated patient group to achieve a treatment effect in. Without a control group, the question remains whether these beneficial results in the ASD cohort can be attributed to the intervention or to other factors for example regression to the mean.

The purpose of our study is to evaluate whether the beneficial one-year follow-up (FU) results in the ASD-CLBP patient group, who were not eligible for spine surgery, can be attributed to the biopsychosocial intervention (RealHealth-NL [RH-NL]). This purpose will be evaluated by comparing two historical cohorts of nonoperative ASD patients; an intervention group (data derived from the RH-NL database; a stricter selection of ASD patients from the ASD cohort of the previous study 17 with a minimum coronal Cobb angle of 20o; ASD patients who participated in the intervention more recently than the 2022 study 17 have also been included in this study) and a control group (data derived from the European Spine Study Group [ESSG] database) both with a follow-up time of one-year.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A comparative treatment based cohort study was conducted according to STROBE guidelines, 18 comparing a biopsychosocial intervention ASD cohort and a care-as-usual ASD cohort. The STROBE reporting checklist is included in Supplementary File A. 19 The data from the biopsychosocial intervention cohort was extracted of historical data of ASD-CLBP patients who participated in the intervention in the period April 2014 – May 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institution’s internal investigational review board. Exemption for ethical approval was obtained by METC Oost-Nederland, as the Dutch Act on Medical Research involving Human Subjects does not apply to screening questionnaires that are part of routine clinical practice (registration number 2024-17535). All patients consented that their data may be used for scientific research in pseudo-anonymised form.

The data from the care-as-usual cohort was extracted of historical data of nonoperative ASD patients of the ESSG database in the period October 2010 – February 2017. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was secured from all participating centres, and informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients.

Participants

The participant flow chart is shown in Figure 1. The participants of the biopsychosocial intervention cohort consisted of ASD patients with CLBP who were referred by spine surgeons in a tertiary orthopaedic hospital, specialized in spinal care, and where continuous outcome monitoring is used (Nijmegen Decision Tool-CLBP). 20 Patients were eligible for referral if conservative treatment in primary care had failed and if there were no indications for surgery or invasive pain treatment.20,21 Main inclusion criteria were: CLBP for at least six months, no medical restrictions on activity, willingness to change behaviour and fluent in Dutch language. Exclusion criteria were: involvement in litigation and/or compensation claims, and primary psychiatric disorders. The final inclusion was based on an assessment by a multidisciplinary team, consisting of a psychologist and a physiotherapist or psychomotor therapist.Figure 1.Flowchart participant selection

Flowchart participant selection

The participants of the care-as-usual cohort consisted of nonoperative ASD patients of the ESSG database who did not participate in a biopsychosocial intervention before or during the one-year follow-up period. These patients were recruited during the outpatient clinic, which means they were either attending for a routine follow-up or actively seeking help because of complaints/wanting a second opinion.

The inclusion criteria of both cohorts: age ≥18, no surgical indication, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)>20, coronal Cobb angle ≥20, ASD diagnosis either idiopathic (AdIS) or degenerative (de novo) scoliosis (DN), baseline and one-year follow-up (1yFU) ODI data.

To increase comparability between the two cohorts, it was decided to equalise the AdIS:DN ratio in both groups. For this reason, 97 AdIS patients were randomly excluded from the ESSG cohort using the Excel RAND-function.

This led to a sample size of 95 patients per cohort, the maximum sample size that could be achieved given data availability and matching constraints.

Treatment/Intervention

The biopsychosocial intervention was a protocoled 100-hour interdisciplinary program, based on cognitive behavioural principles, NICE CLBP guidelines compliant 22 and combined acceptance and commitment therapy, graded exposure, graded activity, relaxation exercise, pain education and communication training. For a more detailed and TIDieR compliant 23 description of the intervention see Supplementary File B.

The interventions in the care-as-usual cohort consisted of pain medication (68%), brace (13%), injections (23%), osteopathy/chiropractic (8%), physical therapy/rehabilitation training (exercises to recover strength, mobility and balance) (56%), TENS (0%), alternative treatment (4%) and/or pain program (injections, medication or nerve block indicated by a specialized multidisciplinary team of doctors) (18%) or no treatment (9%).

To ensure that both cohorts are properly distinguished in terms of interventions, the Method of Assessing Treatment Delivery was applied in the cohort selection. This method was developed to verify whether a treatment can be differentiated from other treatments by ensuring that important treatment elements are addressed (maximal protocol adherence) while prescribed elements are not (minimal or no contamination). 24 In het care-as-usual cohort, biopsychosocial interventions were prohibited. In the biopsychosocial intervention cohort, biopsychosocial interventions were considered essential and unique; physical exercise was considered compatible but not essential. Although pain medication was not part of the biopsychosocial intervention, patients on pain medication at the start of treatment were not excluded from the cohort to be analysed, as the biopsychosocial treatment included helping patients to reduce their pain medication within the context of a healthy lifestyle.

Assessment took place at baseline (pretreatment) and one-year follow-up. In the biopsychosocial cohort the one-year follow-up took place one-year after the start of the two-week active treatment period. In the care-as-usual cohort, the one-year follow-up indicated that the intervention had taken place during the past year.

Pain Medication Use

Participants of the biopsychosocial intervention cohort provided data on type of pain medication use (paracetamol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAID], opioids, neuropathic pain medication and benzodiazepines) at baseline and one-year follow-up. Evaluation was described in terms of number of patients using pain medication at baseline and one-year follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data were tested for normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov). Counts (percentages) was used for descriptive statistics to describe categorical variables and median (interquartile range [IQR]) was used for all continuous variables, while none of these variables were normally distributed in both cohorts at the same time.

Per patient a difference score was calculated between baseline and one-year follow-up for functioning (ODI), pain (NPRS), and HR-QoL (SF-36) resulting in a median difference score per cohort. Differences between the cohorts were analysed using Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normal distributed variables. A p-value <.05 was considered significant. Clinical relevance was determined by a predefined MCIC, as identified in the above section Outcome measures. Differences in pain medication use between baseline and one-year follow-up were analysed using McNemar’s exact test. Correlations between ODI baseline score and other baseline characteristics (Cobb angle, pain and QoL) were calculated using Spearman’s rho. A score <±0.3 is considered ‘weak’ correlation and >±0.5 is considered as ‘strong’ correlation. No missing data was anticipated, as availability of baseline and follow-up data was an inclusion criterion. Although this increases the risk of selection bias, it will have less of an impact on the comparability of the cohorts, as the inclusion criterion applied to both cohorts. A complete case analysis was applied. All statistical analysis were performed with SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics 29).

Discussion

This study shows that a significantly larger and clinically relevant improvement in functional status, pain intensity and quality of life is achieved at one-year follow-up in patients who underwent a standardised intensive 100-hour residential biopsychosocial intervention compared to similar patients who were given care-as-usual. By comparing two intervention cohorts, the hypothesis is supported that the improvements in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort are attributable to the intervention and not to other factors such as placebo or regression to the mean. These findings are unique and in contrast to the current literature on nonoperative ASD treatment, which state that there is no evidence for nonoperative treatment.5,10-12

This study suggests that ASD patients who undergo a biopsychosocial intervention are able to learn and apply pain self-management principles that allows them to improve their functioning, without resorting to biomedical interventions such as pain medication. Targeting biopsychosocial factors therefore seems a promising treatment option in nonoperative care for ASD patients with predominant back pain symptoms.

We can speculate on the explanation for these positive results found in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort. It might be due to the different treatment focus. The common biomedical focus is on correcting the deformity (e.g. bracing, exercise) or aiming at direct pain relief (e.g. medication, injections). The overall evidence on these treatment modalities in nonoperative care is lacking5,10-12 and recently, injections for non-cancer chronic spine pain have even been strongly discouraged. 32 A focus on biopsychosocial factors in ASD treatment aligns with recent research that points towards a paradigm shift in the understanding of patient satisfaction following ASD treatment, in which the focus is shifting from objective radiographic measurements to a more holistic perspective, where functional status, pain and psychological well-being are seen as key predictors of patient satisfaction. 33

Focussing on the biopsychosocial model (rather than the biomedical model) has widely been accepted within the low back pain spine care community, but we have only found one study that targets biopsychosocial factors in ASD. Taniwaki et al studied a self-exercise program for ASD patients aimed at improving physical and mental health and found 42% of the patients reaching an MCIC in ODI (anchor based and distribution based of 4 point). 34

A relevant added benefit of the biopsychosocial intervention is that it reduces the use of pain medication in number of patients, which can potentially reduce pain medication induced adverse events and healthcare costs. It has been shown that persistent opioid use is associated with poor health outcomes, 35 increased healthcare costs 36 and opioid-induced hyperalgesia 37 and that long term use of NSAID’s is associated with serious gastrointestinal and vascular side-effects. 38 Still, pain medication use is common practice in conservative ASD treatment, as this was also prescribed in 68% of the patients in the care-as-usual cohort of this study with limited effectiveness as demonstrated in lack of clinical relevancy. Particularly in the case of chronic pain, it is important to carefully weigh the long-term costs and benefits when determining treatment policy, whereby pain medication does not appear to be the best option compared to a biopsychosocial intervention.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study are the comparison with a care-as-usual cohort as a control group, the large sample size of 95 ASD patients in both cohorts, a follow-up time of one year, an elaborate description of the patient characteristics and that the biopsychosocial intervention followed a standardized and predefined protocol. By reporting the intervention in accordance with TIDieR guidelines 23 (see Supplementary File B) this study is reproducible and its generalisability is enhanced.

There are also limitations to this study. A first limitation is that selection bias might have occurred in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort, were patients were routinely screened for participation in the intervention by therapists. In this screening, one of the selection criteria was ‘willingness to change behaviour’, resulting in motivated patients. Although this was not an explicit inclusion criterion in the care-as-usual cohort, we expect the occurrence of a similar selection bias, whereby patients only participated in the intervention if they were motivated to do so. Another selection criteria in the biopsychosocial intervention cohort was the exclusion of patients with a primary psychiatric disorder, which was not applied in the care-as-usual cohort and could potentially explain the found differences in treatment outcome between both cohorts. Differences in the number of patients with a primary psychiatric disorder between both cohorts, for which we lack well-documented or comparable information, might have led to differences in health literacy, ‘the cognitive and social skills which determine the motivation and ability of individuals to gain access to, understand and use information in ways which promote and maintain good health’. It is known that low health literacy is associated with poorer health outcomes. 39 An effort was made to minimize selection bias by applying inclusion criteria based on the information available, in terms of a Cobb angle≥200, a baseline ODI >20 points and a same ratio of ASD diagnoses, for both cohorts to enhance comparability.

A second limitation were the significant differences between both cohorts at baseline in terms of age, Cobb angle, pain and QoL scores, which could potentially explain the found differences in treatment outcome scores between both cohorts. However, these differences were not clinically relevant in terms of pain and physical QoL, as these were smaller than the predefined MCID. Furthermore to correct for any baseline differences, difference scores between baseline and one-year follow-up were used in the analyses comparing the two cohorts. Also, correlation analysis showed that there was no correlation between these characteristics and treatment outcome scores (ODI difference score), meaning that these baseline differences can be interpretated as not relevant. Although we found no correlation between treatment outcome (ODI difference score) and ASD diagnoses, we did decide to match both cohorts in terms of ASD diagnoses. This decision was supported by a study of Acaroğlu et al who advocate to stratify ASD by diagnosis. 40

A third limitation of the study was that patients who were using pain medication at the start of the biopsychosocial intervention were not excluded from the analysis of the biopsychosocial intervention cohort, resulting in overlap with an intervention in the care-as-usual cohort.

A fourth limitation is that the cohorts are not comparable in terms of nationality. Cultural differences could explain the differences in outcome scores as these were all patient reported outcome measures. Yagi et al described differences in satisfaction scores of ASD patients in Japan compared to the United States after a similarly effective surgical treatment. They argue that differences in lifestyle and cultural expectations may impact satisfaction scores. 41 All patients in our study were Europe citizens, where we expect cultural differences to be less pronounced than between Japan and the United States. Also a satisfaction score could be considered more susceptible to cultural influences in comparison to the primary outcome measure in our study which was functional status.

The limited description of radiological parameters could be seen as a fifth limitation of our study. However, the aim of this study was the evaluation of a biopsychosocial intervention and the intervention under study is not aimed at influencing these parameters. Therefore, a decision was made to omit these parameters. This decision is also supported by research showing that radiographic parameters explained the least variability in postoperative improvement at one-year follow-up 42 and that it is not a key indicator for patient satisfaction. 33

Conclusion

This study shows clinically relevant and significantly greater improvement in function, pain, and quality of life in patients with symptomatic ASD treated by a biopsychosocial intervention, compared to patients who received care-as-usual. It is supported that these improvements are attributable to the biopsychosocial intervention in which ASD patients are able to learn self-management skills to improve their functioning in the long term. This treatment offers a promising option, improving outcomes and reducing possible healthcare risks and costs by decreasing pain medication use, in this conservatively difficult to treat patient group.
