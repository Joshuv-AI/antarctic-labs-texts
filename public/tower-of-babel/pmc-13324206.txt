Cytogenomic profiling of Panax ginseng cultivars and in vitro root cultures through multi-color PLOP-FISH and flow cytometry reveals somaclonal variations
Authors: Eliazar A. Peniton Jr., Hong T. Nguyen, Nomar E. Waminal, Tae-Jin Yang, Hyun Hee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPanax ginseng Meyer (Korean ginseng) is valued as the “King of medicinal plants” for its broad therapeutic properties. Cytogenomic research that defines chromosome-level genome organization provides important information for breeding and cultivar authentication. However, despite the development of many ginseng cultivars, comparative chromosome-level cytogenomic data for identifying and distinguishing them remain limited. Recently, fluorescence in situ hybridization using pre-labeled oligonucleotide probes (PLOP-FISH) and flow cytometry (FC) have enabled efficient chromosome-level genome characterization.MethodsMulti-color PLOP-FISH using three universal tandem repeat probes (5S rDNA, 45S rDNA and Arabidopsis-type telomeric DNA) and four ginseng genome-specific repeats (Pg167TRa,Pg167TRb, PgDel1 and PgDel2) was performed to elucidate the detailed chromosomal composition of nine ginseng cultivars and four culture lines. Genome size variations were assessed by flow cytometry.ResultsAll nine cultivars and three in vitro root culture lines retained the standard chromosome number (2n = 48), whereas the transgenic hairy root line TYP1 showed aneuploidy (2n = 44), indicating loss of four chromosomes. Pg167TR signals revealed cultivar- and line-specific insertions, deletions, and transpositions, supporting Pg167TRa/b as informative cytogenetic markers. In TYP1, repeat-distribution patterns verified tetra-monosomy of chromosomes 8, 13, 16, and 17. Chromosome loss occurred more frequently in the PgDel2-rich subgenome than in the PgDel2-poor subgenome. Flow cytometry corroborated a significant reduction in nuclear DNA content in TYP1.ConclusionThis study provides comparative cytogenomic insights into P. ginseng cultivars and in vitro lines, highlighting Pg167TRa/b as effective markers and demonstrating that PLOP-FISH with FC can track chromosomal variation relevant to breeding, metabolite production, and genomic studies.

Introduction

Panax ginseng Meyer (Asian or Korean ginseng) is a highly valued medicinal plant with extensive therapeutic benefits [1,2]. This perennial species from the Araliaceae family has been a cornerstone of traditional oriental medicine for thousands of years. It can enhance cognitive function, improve immunity, reduce fatigue [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], and is widely used in the treatment of cancer [8], diabetes [9], neurodegenerative [10], and cardiovascular diseases [11]. The key bioactive components in ginseng, particularly ginsenosides, have been extensively studied for their pharmacological effects, which has increased global demand and made ginseng a valuable crop in countries such as Korea, the USA, China, Japan and Vietnam [12,13].

The therapeutic properties of ginseng have led to the development of several high-yield cultivars intended to meet global demand and improve agronomic traits over traditional landraces [[14], [15], [16]]. These breeding efforts ensure consistency and quality in ginseng products, which are vital for consumer trust and medicinal efficacy [17,18]. However, these advances may be compromised by genomic differentiation and instability, including somaclonal variation frequently observed among cultivars and tissue-culture-derived lines. Because in vitro culture can induce chromosomal instability that affects genes involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis [19], safeguarding the genetic integrity of cultivars is essential to preserve ginseng's therapeutic potential. Accordingly, comprehensive cytogenomic evaluation is critical for monitoring genomic stability, distinguishing cultivars, and supporting data-driven breeding and genome research.

Cytogenomic analysis, particularly fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), provides a physical, chromosome-level view of the genome and enables direct detection of large-scale structural variations such as aneuploidy, rearrangements, and chromatin loss [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. This information complements bioinformatics-based comparative genomics, which primarily relies on linear DNA sequences and assembly outputs [25], by validating whether sequence-inferred structural variants are supported at the chromosome level and by revealing instability that may be difficult to resolve in repeat-rich, polyploid genomes.

Recently, whole-genome sequencing has been completed for many plant species and pan-genome analyses based on multiple accessions have been actively conducted. These studies have revealed numerous large-scale chromosomal variations within species; however, cytogenetic investigations addressing such variations remain limited. Cytogenomic validation is thus essential to connect structural variation detected computationally with its physical chromosomal context. In ginseng, where genome differentiation and variation are frequently observed among cultivars and in vitro materials [[26], [27], [28], [29]], cytogenetic evidence provides an indispensable bridge linking molecular genome data to chromosomal-scale architecture. In this context, our study aims to provide cytogenetic evidence for such large-scale variations, thereby laying a foundation for future pan-genome and structural-genome research in P. ginseng.

Despite its importance, comprehensive comparative analyses of chromosome constitution and genome size (GS) across multiple P. ginseng cultivars and in vitro cultures remain limited. Such data can support cultivar discrimination, provide cytogenomic guidance for breeding, and improve genome-assembly strategies through validation of chromosomal anchoring. Moreover, stability assessment is directly relevant to maintaining genetic consistency for conservation and commercial applications and, ultimately, to sustaining therapeutic efficacy [10,16].

In this study, we performed comparative FISH karyotype analysis using seven pre-labeled oligonucleotide probes (PLOPs): three universal repeat probes for 5S rDNA, 45S rDNA, and the Arabidopsis-type telomeric repeat (TTTAGGG)n, and four ginseng-specific repeat probes - Pg167TRa and Pg167TRb, high-copy satellite DNAs previously used to refine the P. ginseng karyotype [30], and PgDel1 and PgDel2, which together account for a substantial portion of the genome [31]. We applied this multi-color PLOP-FISH framework together with genome-size estimation to nine commercially cultivated P. ginseng cultivars (Chungsun, Chunpoong, Geumpong, Gopoong, Sunhyang, Sunpoong, Sunun, Sunwon, and Yunpoong) and four Yunpoong-derived in vitro root culture lines. The four in vitro lines were selected to enable comparison within a shared genetic background: NYP1 and NYP2 are adventitious root lines representing the general instability risk associated with non-transgenic in vitro propagation, whereas TYP1 and TYP3 are Agrobacterium-mediated transgenic hairy-root lines used to assess potentially stronger instability associated with transformation. Moreover, these transgenic lines provide highly efficient, hormone-independent platform for continuous, large-scale production of valuable ginsenosides in bioreactors systems. Confirming their genetic and chromosomal stability is essential for selecting high-yield lines that remain reliable over long-term cultivation [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Together, these analyses characterize cytogenomic variation among cultivars and evaluate genome stability in vitro root cultures, providing insights that support ginseng breeding, biotechnology applications, and genome research.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Stratified seeds from Geumsan landrace of P. ginseng, a traditional and locally adapted variety of Korean ginseng, were purchased from the Geumsan Insam Dome Center (http://insamdome.com/). Nine cultivars were provided by the Ginseng Research Division, Department of Herbal Crop Research, National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science, Rural Development Administration, Korea (Table 1). Also, two adventitious root culture lines (NYP1 and NYP2) and two transgenic hairy root culture lines (TYP1 and TYP3), each maintained for approximately 3 years, were provided by Professor Yang's lab, Department of Plant Science, Seoul National University, Korea). These in vitro culture lines were transformed from the Yunpoong cultivar as detailed in the study of Binh, 2018 [32]. The stratified seeds were germinated in a 36 × 29 × 5 cm plastic container with a wet sorbent pad covered with aluminum foil. They were incubated in the dark at 12 °C for 2–3 d. Germinated seeds with 2–4 mm roots were transferred to an aerated container filled with 0.1 × Hoagland solution and acclimatized in a growth chamber at 25 °C for 24 h. The germinated seed roots were subjected to cell cycle synchronization and metaphase accumulation to increase the number of observable chromosomes because ginseng has a relatively low mitotic index [33]. The in vitro cultures provided by the Department of Plant Science, Seoul National University, Korea were propagated using Schenk & Hildebrandt liquid medium supplemented with 5% sucrose and 24.6 μM indole-3-butyric acid at 5.7–5.8 pH. They were maintained at 25 °C with 110 rpm shaking for 2 weeks, as described by Peniton et al. [33].

Chromosome spread preparation

Metaphase chromosome spreads were prepared using established protocols [20,21,33]. The cell cycle of P. ginseng root meristem cells was synchronized to accumulate metaphase chromosomes as previously described [33], yielding well-scattered, condensed chromosomes and expediting slide preparation. Protoplasts were obtained by enzymatic digestion with 1% pectolyase and 2% cellulase at 37 °C for 45 min (in vitro) or 90 min (seedlings). After treatment, protoplasts were fixed in aceto-ethanol (1:3, v/v), resuspended in 9:1 acetic acid:ethanol, and 20 μL was dropped onto pre-warmed slides and air-dried. Slides were fixed in 2% formaldehyde for 5 min and dehydrated in 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol (3 min each) before final air drying.

Multi-color fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

All seven repeat probes, namely, three universal repeat probes (5S rDNA, 45S rDNA, and the Arabidopsis-type telomeric repeat (TTTAGGG)n) and four ginseng-specific repeat probes (Pg167TRa, Pg167TRb, PgDel1 and PgDel2) were prepared for FISH [20,22]. The 5′ ends of each PLOP were modified with FAM (5S rDNA and Pg167TRa), Cy3 (45S rDNA and Pg167TRb), and Texas Red (Arabidopsis-type telomeric repeat). All PLOPs were synthesized by Bioneer Corporation, South Korea (https://www.bioneer.co.kr/). The PCR amplicons of PgDel1 and PgDel2 were labeled with biotin and digoxigenin (Roche, Germany), respectively, using nick translation labeling. The probes and primers with their respective sequences, lengths, and fluorochromes are summarized in Table S2. Approximately 2 μg of DNA templates from PgDel1 and PgDel2 amplicons were labeled with biotin and digoxigenin, respectively. Approximately 4 μL each of biotin and digoxigenin nick translation mix were added into the microtube, and the volume was increased to 20 μL with nuclease-free water. The labeling mixtures were incubated at 15.6 °C for 3 h. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 μL of 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid at 60 °C for 10 min [21].

A 40 μL FISH hybridization master mix (50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2 × saline-sodium citrate [SSC], and 50 ng μL−1 of each probe in nuclease-free water) was pipetted onto fixed slides with chromosome spreads and then denatured at 80 °C for 5 min. For probes labeled with biotin or digoxigenin haptens, the hybridization mix was first denatured at 90 °C for 10 min before being added to the prepared slides. The probes were hybridized overnight in a humid chamber at 37 °C. Afterward, the slides were sequentially washed in 2 × SSC at room temperature (RT) for 10 min, 0.1 × SSC at 42 °C for 25 min, and 2 × SSC at RT for 5 min. This was followed by dehydration in a series of increasing ethanol concentrations at 70%, 90%, and 100% at RT for 3 min each. After air drying, the slides were counterstained with 1:100 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (100 μg mL−1 stock) in Vectashield (Vector H1000, Vector labs, USA) [[21], [22], [23]].

An additional probe detection step was performed for the probes labeled with biotin (PgDel1) and digoxigenin (PgDel2). After hybridization and stringent washes with 2 × SSC, slides were treated with TNB [0.1 M Tris-HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 1% (w/v) blocking reagent] for 5 min at RT. Biotinylated PgDel1 was detected using streptavidin-Cy3 conjugate (Zymed, USA). Meanwhile, the digoxigenin-labeled PgDel2 probe was detected using an anti-digoxigenin fluorescein isothiocyanate conjugate (Sigma, USA). Both antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer at a ratio of 1:100 before being pipetted onto the slides and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The slides were then washed three times with TNT [0.1 M Tris-HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.2% (v/v) Tween-20] at 37 °C for 5 min each, dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations of 70%, 90%, and 100% at RT for 3 min each, and air-dried [24]. Chromosome counterstaining was performed as previously described.

Karyotyping

Chromosomes were imaged using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Co., Tokyo, Japan) with a DFC365 FS CCD camera (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Raw images were captured and enhanced using Adobe Photoshop CS6 (Adobe System Inc., California, USA). Chromosome lengths were measured using ImageJ ver. 1.51k (Wayne Rasband, Maryland, USA). Homologous chromosomes were paired and arranged based on length and FISH signals according to our previous study [20]. The distribution of PLOP-FISH signals was compared with that of the Geumsan landrace to identify and characterize genomic rearrangements and chromosomal variations. Ideograms were generated using Adobe Illustrator CS6 (Adobe System Inc., California, USA).

Estimation of DNA content

The total nuclear DNA content (2C-DNA value) of ginseng cultivars and in vitro root cultures was determined using flow cytometry, following the method described by Doležel et al. [34]. Fresh young leaves or proliferative roots of ginseng were chopped together with the internal reference standard (Aralia elata, 2C = 2.47 pg) in a glass Petri dish containing 500 μL of ice-cold LB01 isolation buffer (200 mM Tris, 4 mM MgCl2·6H2O, and 0.5% Triton X-100). The lysate was filtered sequentially through 50 μm and 30 μm nylon mesh filters (CellTrics Filters, Sysmex Asia Pacific, Singapore) to separate the nuclei from cellular debris. Subsequently, 50 μg/mL of propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, cat. no. P4170; Molecular Probes; cat. no. P3566) was added to the filtrate to stain the nuclei, and 10 μg/mL of RNAse (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, cat. no. R5000) was added to degrade RNA contaminants. The suspension was placed on ice for approximately 2 min before the flow cytometric analysis was performed.

Three measurements of DNA content were conducted for each sample with a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, California, USA). The standard and sample peaks were analyzed using CytExpert v2.3 software (Beckman Coulter Inc., Pasadena, CA, USA). The sample DNA content was computed as described by Doležel et al. [34]. Briefly, 5000–10,000 events, that is, nuclei count, were recorded to generate histograms. The 2C DNA content of ginseng cultivars and in vitro cultures was estimated by dividing the mean 2C peak of the experimental sample by the mean 2C peak of the internal reference and then multiplying by the 2C value of the reference. To calculate the GS in base pairs (bp), the 1C DNA content of the samples was multiplied by 0.978 × 109 bp. An unpaired t-test was performed to analyze the significance of the variation in DNA content between cultivars, with the significance level set at a P-value of 0.05.

Conversely, the TYP1 transgenic hairy root culture had a reduced chromosome count of 2n = 44 (Supplementary Fig. 1M), indicating the loss of four chromosomes. Morphologically, the transgenic hairy root lines (TYP1 and TYP3) exhibited the characteristic profuse lateral branching, rapid growth rate, and plagiotropic development typical of A. rhizogenes-transformed P. ginseng [32]. Notably, despite the reduced chromosome number subsequently identified in TYP1, no visually distinct morphological aberrations, growth penalties, or atypical physical traits were observed during in vitro subculturing when compared to the chromosomally stable TYP3 line or the source 'Yunpoong'.

Chromosomal rearrangements in ginseng cultivars

The P. ginseng landrace Geumsan and nine cultivars had identical loci numbers and distribution patterns for 5S rDNA, 45S rDNA, and Arabidopsis-type telomeric repeats. However, variations in the Pg167TR repeat distribution were found among the cultivars (Fig. 1A). Insertions, deletions, and transpositions were detected across 14 homologous chromosomes. Meanwhile, chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 19 showed no changes. A detailed ideogram illustrates these rearrangements (Fig. 1B). The Sunhyang and Sunwon cultivars had the most extensive rearrangements, with frequent Pg167TR insertions and deletions across seven chromosomes. Chromosome 18 in Sunun showed a unique transposition of Pg167TRa + b to a different locus, a rearrangement only found in this cultivar. Chromosomes 1, 10, and 23 were the most frequently rearranged. Chromosome 1 exhibited consistent insertions of Pg167TRa and Pg167TRb, except in Sunwon and Sunpoong. Chromosome 10 showed frequent insertions, particularly in Chungsun and Gopoong. Meanwhile, chromosome 23 had deletions of Pg167TRa + b in Sunwon and insertions in other cultivars. Additional rearrangements included deletions of Pg167TRb on chromosome 6 in six cultivars and on chromosome 16 in five cultivars and insertions of Pg167TRa on chromosome 24 in five cultivars. These variations in frequency and type highlight the dynamic distribution of Pg167TR repeats and their role in generating structural genomic diversity among ginseng cultivars.

Genomic instability in in vitro root cultures

Multi-color FISH analysis showed genomic rearrangements in the Yunpoong cultivar (2n = 48) and its derived in vitro root cultures - NYP1, NYP2, TYP1, and TYP3. The adventitious root cultures NYP1 and NYP2 had minor structural variations while maintaining a diploid chromosome complement (2n = 48). For example, NYP1 displayed insertions of Pg167TRa + b on chromosome 11 and Pg167TRb on chromosomes 9 and 18, and deletions of Pg167TRa on chromosome 1 and Pg167TRb on chromosomes 1 and 6 (Fig. 2). Similarly, NYP2 showed an insertion of Pg167TRa on chromosome 16 and deletion of Pg167TRb on chromosome 6. These changes suggest minor structural variability from in vitro culture conditions while maintaining genomic stability. Conversely, the TYP1 transgenic root culture had the most pronounced genomic instability, with aneuploidy (2n = 44) from the loss of four chromosomes (8, 13, 16, and 17) that were identified as monosomic (Table 2; Fig. 2). TYP1 displayed insertions of Pg167TRa on chromosomes 8, 11, and 18 and Pg167TRb on chromosomes 9, 14, and 18. These extensive rearrangements highlight the effects of transgenic manipulations and in vitro conditions on chromosomal integrity. The TYP3 culture retained the diploid chromosome number (2n = 48) but showed moderate structural changes, including deletions of Pg167TRa + b on chromosome 12; Pg167TRa on chromosome 23; and Pg167TRb on chromosomes 1, 6, 11, and 18. Pg167TRb was also inserted on chromosome 18. Chromosomal instability and rearrangements in TYP1 compared to Yunpoong

A comparative karyotype analysis of the Yunpoong cultivar (2n = 48) and TYP1 transgenic hairy root culture line (2n = 44) was used to identify significant chromosomal rearrangements and genomic instability in TYP1. The analysis allowed chromosome pairing using repeat signals for Pg167TRa and Pg167TRb, alongside rDNA, telomeric repeats, and PgDel1 and PgDel2 signals to assess repeat distribution and distinguish PgDel2-poor (chromosomes 1–12) and PgDel2-rich (chromosomes 13–24) subgenomes (Table 2; Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S2). The PgDel1 probe produced signals across all chromosomes except for the secondary constriction of chromosome 16. The 45S rDNA marked the nucleolar organizer region on chromosome 16. Meanwhile, the 5S rDNA identified chromosomes 2, 10, and 22. Arabidopsis-type telomeres were observed at the termini of all chromosomes, with intercalary loci on the long arm of chromosome 13 and weaker signals on chromosomes 15, 17, and 23 in Yunpoong. The comparison showed no nullisomy in TYP1 but identified four monosomic chromosomes (8, 13, 16, and 17), with three belonging to the PgDel2-rich subgenome. The monosomy of chromosome 16 was confirmed by only one 45S rDNA signal across all the chromosome spreads. Beyond chromosome loss, TYP1 exhibited rearrangements involving changes in the location and intensity of Pg167TR signals. Chromosome 8 gained a distinct Pg167TRa locus on the short arm, matching the intensity of the long arm locus in Yunpoong. Chromosome 9 showed hemizygous differences in Pg167TRa+b signal intensity, with one homologue having more significant loci. Chromosome 11 acquired a new Pg167TRa signal on its long arm, and chromosome 14 gained a Pg167TRb signal on its short arm, colocalized with Pg167TRa. Reduction in DNA content in TYP1

Flow cytometric measurements of DNA content were performed on ginseng cultivars and in vitro root cultures. Despite the reduced chromosome number in TYP1, its metaphase chromosomes showed no size changes indicative of chromosomal fusion. This confirmed the decrease in DNA content. TYP1 had a significantly reduced DNA content of 7.54 pg (1C = 3.69 Gbp), which was approximately 0.862 pg less than the average 8.40 pg observed in other cultivars and root cultures (Table 1; Fig. 4). Discussion

Advances in cytogenetic research in P. ginseng

The biological and breeding significance of cytogenomic study is closely tied to the intended application of TYP lines in large-scale propagation pipelines. Because maintaining chromosomal integrity is fundamental to ensuring consistent secondary metabolite profiles and robust growth, the severe instability observed in specific lines like TYP1 highlights a critical vulnerability in tissue culture-based breeding. Therefore, high-resolution cytogenomic screening must be integrated into the evaluation of transgenic P. ginseng lines to verify their clonal fidelity before they are advanced into commercial bioreactor production.

Over the past decade, cytogenetic research on P. ginseng has advanced considerably, leveraging molecular markers such as ribosomal DNA [31], leading to the establishment of additional repeat markers, including Pg167TRa and Pg167TRb, and construction of a comprehensive FISH karyogram for P. ginseng. This has contributed substantially to our understanding of its complex genomic composition [20,30,31]. Access to the nuclear genome sequence of the Chunpoong cultivar [35] has further supported these advancements, which have allowed insights into its repeat structures and chromosomal organization. However, challenges persist owing to the high repeat content in the P. ginseng genome and continued reliance on short-read sequencing technologies [36]. Ongoing efforts to resequence the genome using the TYP1 transgenic in vitro root culture aim to overcome these challenges by validating previous findings and achieving refined pseudo-chromosome assemblies. Our multi-color FISH and flow cytometry analyses have shown substantial chromosomal losses and genomic rearrangements in TYP1, offering critical insights for understanding P. ginseng genomic dynamics.

Chromosomal instability in TYP1: mechanisms and implications

Cytogenetic screening on ginseng cultivars, including both adventitious root cultures and transgenic lines were performed to generate comparative data on genetic variation and to determine the chromosomal localization of DNA marker probes. Among all the lines examined, the TYP1 transgenic line exhibited the most pronounced genomic instability. This indicates that the in vitro culture process affects each cell line differently, leading to varying degrees of chromosomal instability. The chromosome number variations in the TYP1 in vitro line of P. ginseng illustrate the complex and dynamic nature of plant genomes, especially under tissue culture conditions. Known as somaclonal variation, this phenomenon often results from the stress of artificial conditions such as phytohormones, including 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid, or the mediation of Agrobacterium spp., callus transformation, or prolonged in vitro culture conditions [19,36]. This can lead to genetic and chromosomal instability [37,38] as observed in several plant species like Oroxylum indicum [39], Coffea arabica [40], and Carica papaya [41]. These changes represent technical challenges and can result in the loss of crucial genes and regulatory elements, potentially altering the plant's ability to produce key compounds such as ginsenosides [42].

Moreover, the observation that chromosomal loss was restricted to the TYP1 line, despite sharing the same ‘Yunpoong’ cultivar background with the stable TYP3 line, suggests that this somaclonal variation is not strictly a genotype-dependent response. Rather, it likely represents a stochastic mutational event. We hypothesize that the combined physiological stresses of A. rhizogenes-mediated transformation and prolonged in vitro cultivation may have induced epigenetic instability or stochastic errors during mitosis, such as nondisjunction or the formation of lagging chromosomes [19,37,39,40]. Additionally, line-specific transformation effects (including the genomic context of Ri-DNA integration) could contribute to differential chromosomal stability between independently derived hairy root lines; however, this possibility requires further validation [32].

Interestingly, the profound chromosomal instability observed in TYP1 was not accompanied by any obvious phenotypic or morphological defects during standard in vitro cultivation. The aneuploid roots maintained a gross physical appearance indistinguishable from the stable TYP3 line. This uncoupling of gross morphology from cytogenomic integrity is of significant practical concern; it demonstrates that severe somaclonal variation can remain phenotypically 'hidden'. Consequently, relying solely on visual assessment for quality control is inadequate, reinforcing the necessity of molecular cytogenetic screening to ensure clonal fidelity [19,28,39].

While the phenotypic effects of monosomy in polyploids can be buffered by homologous chromosomes, this substantial genetic loss potentially leads to alterations in several gene expression that are possibly linked to traits related to growth, development, and stress responses. Similar issues have been observed in other crops, such as potatoes, where tissue culture-induced variations have affected traits such as disease resistance and growth patterns [43]. The loss of specific chromosomes in TYP1 also raises concerns about reduced genetic diversity, which could make the plant less adaptable to environmental changes [44,45]. Therefore, managing these risks through screening and selection in breeding programs and exploring alternative culture methods or genomic selection techniques is essential. Moreover, the TYP1 line is currently being used in ongoing efforts to resequence the P. ginseng genome, making its characterization particularly important. Analyzing its chromosomal instability through FISH provides essential validation and refinement data for the P. ginseng pseudo-chromosome assemblies, helping to identify, address, and correct sequence variations.

Pg167TR dynamics and PgDel-associated rearrangements in ginseng cultivars and root cultures

This study offers key biological insights into the cytogenomic structure of P. ginseng, showing both its structural diversity and its susceptibility to instability during in vitro culture. Satellite DNAs Pg167TRa and Pg167TRb proved to be effective markers for constructing a detailed karyogram and distinguishing the nine cultivars through detectable structural variations. The genomic instability observed in the TYP1 line underscores the risks of somaclonal variation. Multi-color PLOP-FISH provide practical cytogenomic tools and stability assessments that can guide breeding and genomic research.

The Pg167TR tandem repeats have revealed distinct patterns of insertions, deletions, and transpositions across cultivars, highlighting structural genomic diversity. When comparing the FISH karyogram of TYP1 to that of P. ginseng, the PgDel2-rich subgenome had a more significant loss of chromosomes than the PgDel2-poor subgenome. This suggests a predisposition to instability in these regions, which is a novel and important insight into the genomic architecture of P. ginseng under stress conditions, which has also been recorded in other allopolyploid species [46,47]. The increased susceptibility of the PgDel2-rich subgenome may probably derived from structural differences or epigenetic modifications that make these regions more vulnerable to tissue culture stresses [48].

Pg167TR probes, highly effective cytogenetic markers for identifying homologous chromosomes [20,30], showed increased hybridization intensity on specific TYP1 chromosomes compared to Yunpoong. This maybe suggest amplification of repeats caused by aneuploidy events or other genetic imbalances, potentially disrupting chromosomal organization and affecting the plant's ability to produce essential metabolites, such as ginsenosides [49], however this should be confirmed further through quantitative FISH analysis. The variation in Pg167TR distribution across specific chromosomes further suggests that TYP1 is predisposed to broad genetic rearrangements. This may have wider implications for gene expression and phenotypic traits in polyploid genomes, emphasizing the critical role of Pg167TR in our understanding of structural genomic dynamics.

rDNA loss, monosomy, and cytogenetic marker utility

The loss of a chromosome bearing the 45S rDNA loci was observed in TYP1, a phenomenon also reported in the synthetic allotetraploid Aegilops sharonensis × Aegilops umbellulata [49], mutant lines of Arabidopsis thaliana [50], and regenerants of Tulipa gesneriana [29]. In the synthetic allotetraploid Aegilops, rDNA loss was primarily associated with hypomethylation of the rDNA unit promoter [49]. Meanwhile, Arabidopsis resulted from disruptions in chromatin assembly factor 1 (CAF1) or the H3/H4 histone chaperone [50,51]. Although ribosomal DNA is crucial for genome maintenance and organization [44,45,47], it is also one of the most unstable genomic regions. Repeated and unequal crossing-overs can lead to the deletion of rDNA copies [28,[51], [52], [53]]. The loss of 45S rDNA copies significantly compromises plant stability.

With the loss of four chromosomes (8, 13, 16, and 17), monosomy in TYP1 highlights the complexities of maintaining chromosomal stability in polyploid species, including P. ginseng. Monosomy often arises from errors during chromosome segregation, which is linked to the fusion of aneuploid and euploid gametes or disruptions in spindle function [48,52]. However, their phenotypic effects in polyploid organisms are often mitigated by additional homologous chromosomes that preserve essential genetic functions [42,46,53]. In TYP1, the missing chromosomes might not significantly impact the plant's genetic functionality or ongoing resequencing efforts. This is because the genetic information may still be represented by their homologous counterparts, demonstrating the genetic resilience of polyploid species [54]. However, while the immediate effects of monosomy may be buffered, long-term consequences could include shifts in gene expression and phenotype, influencing traits related to growth, development, and stress responses [55].

The identification of Pg167TR as a highly abundant sequence in the P. ginseng genome has allowed refined cytogenetic mapping, providing signal patterns for each chromosome and supporting the development of a comprehensive karyogram [20,30,31]. In addition to its role in structural genome analysis, Pg167TR was identified as a crucial marker for the molecular differentiation of P. ginseng cultivars, generating specific insertion and deletion patterns at specific chromosomal loci. This is particularly important for breeding because it ensures accurate identification of cultivars. The utility of similar markers has been demonstrated in other crops, including durum wheat, oilseed rape, and hazelnut [56,57]. This highlights the broad applicability of cytogenetic markers in agricultural genomics. These markers enable precise cultivar identification and support the effective management of genetic resources and implementation of targeted breeding strategies, ultimately contributing to developing more resilient and productive crop varieties.

Reduced DNA content corroborated the chromosome loss in the TYP1 line

The reduction in DNA content in the tetra-monosomic TYP1 line of P. ginseng, which has a genetic content of 3.69 Gbp, 410 Mbp less than the mean GS of other ginseng samples, indicates substantial chromatin loss, unlike scenarios involving concerted chromosome fusion or chromosomal translocation, where the GS would remain comparable to other cultivars or in vitro cultures [58], the reduced GS in TYP1 can be attributed to the loss of four chromosomes. This emphasizes the impact of aneuploidy on GS and chromatin composition, potentially leading to gene expression and phenotype alterations. The estimated mean GS of 1C x‾ = 4.1 Gbp for P. ginseng in this study contrasts with that of Xu et al. [4], Hong et al. [59], and Kim et al. [36], who reported values of 3.12, 3.5, and 3.6 Gbp per haploid chromosome, respectively. These differences can be attributed to differences in methods because Hong et al. [59] used a flow-cytometry-based GS measurement with a conversion factor of 1 pg = 965.52 Mbp [60]. Conversely, Xu et al. [4] and Kim et al. [36] relied on k-mer analyses, which often project smaller GSs because of factors such as sequencing errors, repetitive elements, and data coverage bias. The variation in GS estimates may also have resulted from differences in the species used for the reference standard, staining methods, nuclei isolation buffers, sample handling, and the samples themselves [34]. However, our process, which included rigorous internal standardization, likely provided a more accurate reflection of GS dynamics in P. ginseng cultivars and in vitro root cultures.

The detection of chromosome loss in a single, superficially normal transgenic line (TYP1) highlights a practical risk for large-scale propagation, where genome differentiation and variation can arise during in vitro culture [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. This result suggests that random sampling or purely phenotypic screening may be insufficient to ensure the clonal fidelity needed to maintain genetic consistency, consumer trust, and medicinal efficacy in commercial applications [[16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, our findings support incorporating high-resolution cytogenomic screening, such as multi-color PLOP-FISH, during early stages of elite line selection to detect large-scale structural variation and reduce the risk of propagating genetically unstable lines in commercial bioreactor systems [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]].

In conclusion, this study provided critical insights into cytogenomic structure and chromosomal variations among cultivars and in vitro root cultures of P. ginseng, highlighting the dynamic nature of plant genomes and the importance of cytogenomic evaluations. Pg167TR tandem repeats have been proven to be highly effective cytogenetic markers for differentiated cultivars and identifying genomic rearrangements. The aneuploidy observed in TYP1 highlighted the genomic instability induced by in vitro tissue culture, underscoring the importance of cytogenomic evaluation in breeding and genomic research. These findings advance our understanding of genomic architecture in P. ginseng, promoting more informed approaches to cultivating and improving this valuable medicinal plant.
