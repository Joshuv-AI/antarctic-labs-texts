Prenatal lipopolysaccharide exposure programs the early-life lung development by modulating mesenchymal fibroblasts in a time- and dose-dependent manner
Authors: Ying Dong, Stefan Hadzic, Annika Leidner, Manuela Marega, Tara Procida-Kowalski, Marek Bartkuhn, Stefano Rivetti, Harald Ehrhardt, Saverio Bellusci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is one common and severe complication of preterm births. Prenatal infection/inflammation is the major cause driving preterm deliveries, but its contribution to the early-life lung development remains unclear.MethodsPregnant C57BL/6J mice were randomized at 15.5 or 18.5 days post coitum (dpc) to receive intraperitoneal injection of sterile saline, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) 50 μg/kg, or LPS 100 μg/kg. Animal experiments were performed compliantly to the ARRIVE guidelines. Lungs of newborn pups of mixed genders were harvested at the first postnatal day (P1) for histopathology to investigate prenatal LPS-induced structural changes. Expression of key pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators was assessed at both transcriptional and translational levels. Based on bulk RNA sequencing and exploratory transcriptomic analysis, signature gene and phenotypical expression of two major mesenchymal fibroblasts subsets, myofibroblasts (MYFs) and lipofibroblasts (LIFs), were analyzed with qPCR, immunofluorescence, and western blot. Human embryo-derived WI-38 fibroblast cell line was used as an in vitro model to investigate LPS-induced fibroblast inflammatory responses and MYFs-LIFs differentiation.ResultsNewborn mice exposed to prenatal LPS exhibited enlarged air spaces and thinner septal walls, with an enhanced interleukin 6 (IL6) response in lung tissues. The degree of lung structural changes is LPS dose- and timing-dependent. Exploratory transcriptomic analysis revealed the enrichment in myofibrogenic pathways in LPS-exposed lungs. Platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA)-expressing progenitor lung fibroblasts were generally suppressed by prenatal LPS exposure. The expression of MYFs signature markers (ELN and ACTA2) in relation to LIFs (PLIN2 and FGF10) demonstrated a highly heterogeneous and dynamic pattern, depending on LPS doses and timing. Upon LPS exposure, human WI-38 fibroblasts upregulated a panel of pro-inflammatory mediators via nuclear factor kappa B signaling and displayed diverse MYFs and LIFs differentiation depending on the dose and duration of LPS stimulation.ConclusionsPrenatal LPS exposure induces heterogeneous structural and molecular changes in the newborn mice lung, showing LPS dose- and time-dependent modulation of mesenchymal fibroblast differentiation. These findings may contribute to refine the risk stratification of preterm infants exposed to prenatal infection/inflammation. Mesenchymal fibroblast plasticity and MYFs-LIFs phenotypic shifts may inspire preventive and therapeutic strategies for BPD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04533-w.

Background

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is one common and severe complication of preterm infants, especially in extremely preterm infants (EPIs) born at 22–28 weeks of gestational age [1, 2]. The hallmarks of BPD are arrested alveolarization and vascular dysregulation, leading to long-term adverse respiratory outcomes [3, 4]. The pathogenesis of BPD has not been fully elucidated, largely due to its complex multifactorial origin spanning the pre-, peri-, and postnatal period [3, 4]. Until now, there is a paucity of evidence-based therapeutic approaches to prevent or treat BPD [3, 5], being diagnosed in up to 50% of EPIs [1, 2]. To drive the frontiers of perinatal-neonatal health care and further improve the pulmonary outcome of surviving EPIs, an in-depth pathomechanistic understanding of BPD is crucial.

Prenatal infection/inflammation particularly chorioamnionitis (CA) constitutes the major cause driving preterm births of EPIs [1, 2, 6]. There are heterogeneous results in clinical studies pointing towards an increased, decreased, or no risk of BPD associated with CA [7]. This discrepancy may be attributed to a wide spectrum of timing and severities of CA [6–8]. Due to the limited accessibility of intrauterine environment, preclinical animal studies are indispensable to elucidate pulmonary consequences of CA. Experimental mice present a rational model for studying early-life human lung development and diseases [9]. The lungs of most EPIs are still at the late canalicular/early saccular stage, sharing structural similarities with those of mice at delivery [10]. To induce CA in mice, maternal intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) represents one commonly used method in accordance with animal ethics and placental inflammation [8, 11], which still belongs to conventional CA definitions in clinical practice [12–14].

The lungs of both human and mice can be mainly divided into epithelial, endothelial, and mesenchymal compartments [10, 15–17]. Different to adult lungs, those of fetus and newborns harbor an abundance of resident mesenchymal cells (rMCs) including fibroblasts, which substantially contribute to the maturation, homeostasis, and injury/repair of the other two cellular compartments [15, 18]. Based on signature makers used for lineage tracing in lung organogenesis, two major functional subsets of mesenchymal fibroblasts can be grossly defined as myofibroblasts (MYFs) and lipofibroblasts (LIFs) [19–24]. MYFs can produce elastin (ELN) and collagen, exhibiting contractile properties due to alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; also ACTA2) [20, 24, 25]. LIFs are characterized by synthesis of surfactant lipids via perilipin 2 (PLIN2) and promoting alveolar niche activity through fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) [19, 21, 22]. How mesenchymal fibroblasts respond to prenatal infection/inflammation and their roles in lung homeostasis and disease during the early-life period have been scarcely investigated.

In this study, postnatal day 1 (P1) was particularly chosen, as P1 defines the end point of antenatal programing and represents the baseline for ensuing postnatal exposure [10]. Another rationale is that early postnatal patterns of respiratory diseases among EPIs, including lung structural and physiological manifestations, are closely associated with BPD development later in life [26–29]. The exact timing of CA remains usually unknown with varying latency duration (1–2 days to 25 days) before birth [30–32]. As such, it is clinically relevant to simulate prenatal infection/inflammation of both prolonged and acute processes in mice models. In current literature, time points used for i.p. application of LPS range between pseudoglandular and the late canalicular/early saccular stage of mice lung development [8, 11]. These two stages correspond to drastic changes in the pattern and abundance of mesenchymal fibroblasts, thus representing sensitive time windows for prenatal programming [16, 17]. In contrast to the clinical fact that CA is mostly subclinical in nature and results in live births of EPIs [13, 14, 31], many mice models of prenatal LPS exposure demonstrated high maternal and fetal mortality [8, 11, 33–35]. Therefore, relatively low-dosed LPS administration is desirable for mice models to better mimic clinical scenarios [8].

Taken together, this study aimed to tackle the ongoing controversy over the impact of prenatal LPS inflammation on the immature lung and would overcome the limitations of current mice studies by incorporating diverse onsets and doses of LPS exposure. Lung mesenchymal fibroblasts were targeted to understand cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying prenatal infection/inflammation-induced pulmonary alteration.

Methods

Experimental animals

Wild-type C57BL/6J mice purchased from Charles River Laboratory, Sulzfeld, Germany, were housed by the Animal Care and Veterinary Service of Justus-Liebig University (JLU), Giessen. Mice were fed ad libitum and kept in IVC type II long ventilated cage systems (Tecniplast, Buguggiate, Italy) with ambient temperature 22 °C ± 2 °C, relative humidity 55% ± 10%, and a circadian light–dark cycle alternatively every 12 h (6 a.m.–6 p.m.). Environmental enrichment was guaranteed with nesting papers, wooden toys, and mouse houses. Eight to 16-week old female mice were mated with males of the same background overnight and then checked next day between 6 and 8 a.m. for vaginal plug, the appearance of which indicates 0.5 days post coitum (dpc). Females were then transferred to new individual cages and weighted daily. A weight gain of 2 or more grams at 10.5–12.5 dpc as compared with that at 0.5 dpc confirms pregnancy.

In vivo experimental model

Animal experiments were performed compliantly with the ARRIVE guidelines (Additional file 1: The ARRIVE checklist) [36]. To induce prenatal LPS inflammation, pregnant mice received i.p. injection of LPS (serotype O111:B4, Cat. No. 14011, Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, Netherlands) dissolved in 100 µl sterile saline using a 26-G needle (BD PrecisionGlide Needle, Becton Dickinson, Heidelberg, Germany). Gentle handling (e.g., cupping) was applied to pregnant mice with daily health monitoring on standardized score sheets after i.p. injection. Two time points, namely 15.5 and 18.5 dpc, corresponding to the late pseudoglandular and early saccular stage of mice lung development respectively (Fig. 1), were selected for i.p. injection with reference to pre-existing literature [8, 11]. Pregnant mice were randomly assigned at 15.5 and 18.5 dpc to receive i.p. injection of sterile saline (sham control), LPS at 50 μg/kg (LPS 50), or 100 μg/kg (LPS 100) (Fig. 1). These doses of LPS were chosen with reference to literature and our preliminary experiments to result in generally unimpaired maternal status with viable litters. Preterm deliveries/stillbirths were excluded (n = 8). After delivery, pups were euthanized on P1 with a lethal dose (600 mg/kg) of i.p. pentobarbital (Narcoren, Boehringer Ingelheim, Germany). Power calculations using GPower 3.1.9.7 software indicated that a minimum of 3 pups per group were required to assess relevant changes in lung morphometry (ANOVA test: α value 0.05 and a test power of 0.8) [37]. A total number of 43 pups (n = 6–9 per group) with combined sexes were included in experiments, as previous data pointed towards a non-significant role of sex in early postnatal lung development [38, 39]. For lung harvests, the right ventricle of the heart was first perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.0) at 20 cm H2O as described previously [19, 40], with perfusion time being reduced to 5 min to avoid damage of P1 lung tissues. Afterwards, right lung lobes were excised for transcriptomic and protein analysis, and the left lobe was further processed for histology. Sample attrition occurred during tissue processing, and the final number of samples contributing to each individual experiment analysis was detailed later in results. Lung histology

Preparation of lung slices

The left lobe was perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) through a 26-G cannula in the trachea at 20 cm H2O for 5 min [40]. The pressure and duration of perfusion were determined based on our previous experiments to allow consistent inflation across samples and minimize variability due to collapse or excessive distension [40]. After being removed, the left lobe was fixed in 4% PFA for max. 24 h at 4 °C. Tissues were then serially dehydrated (30%, 50%, 70%, and 99.6% ethanol each 20 min) and embedded in paraffin with an embedding machine (EG 1150C, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Slices at a thickness of 3 μm from the midplane of the left lobe were chosen for further staining as in previous studies [19, 41].

Alveolar morphometry

Slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for assessment of alveolar morphometry, including air space, septal wall thickness, and the mean linear intercept (MLI). Total scans from each slice were gained using a Leica DM6000B microscope (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) equipped with Qwin V3 software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Bronchi and vessels > 50 μm in diameter were excluded prior to alveolar morphometric measurements [19, 37, 40]. Horizontal or vertical lines with a distance of 40 μm were put across each lung section. MLI is defined as the total length of the vertical (or horizontal) lines divided by the frequency of these lines crossing a septal wall [19, 37, 40]. The air space was determined as the percentage of non-parenchymatous and non-stained area in each lung section. The septal wall thickness (μm) was measured as the length of the line perpendicularly crossing a septum. Up to 9 fields of view per lung slice and 3 randomly selected slides per pup were used for analysis.

Elastic fiber quantification

Slices were stained for elastic fibers (purple) using Resorcinol-Fuchsin solution based on Weigert (Cat. No. X877.3, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) and nuclei counterstained with methyl green 1% (Cat. No. C. I. 42,590, Carl Roth). Two index parameters, namely lung parenchymal elastin content and elastic fiber-positive area relative to the total area of lung tissues, were analyzed in 5 fields of view per tissue section and 3 random tissue sections per animal using Duo Staining protocol of Qwin V3 software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). All images for calculation were taken at 40 × magnification under a bright field microscope (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Cranial and accessory lobes of the right lung were removed and frozen in liquid nitrogen. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit automatically via QIAcube®Connect (Cat. No. 74034, Qiagen, Hamburg, Germany). After quantification using a NanoDrop™ Spectrophotometer 2000/2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA), RNA was reverse transcribed (QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit, Cat. No. 205313, Qiagen) and cDNA was diluted to a concentration of 5 ng/μl. For real time (RT)-qPCR, SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. No. 11733–038, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used with Light Cycler 480 system (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Mouse primers were designed and blasted using NCBI software for specificity (Table 1). Samples were run in duplicates using hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (Hprt) as a reference gene for mice. Values of cycle threshold (CT) were obtained with dCT and ddCT being calculated as follows: dCT = CT (reference gene) − CT (gene of interest), ddCT = dCT (intervention) − dCT (control). RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

To preliminarily gain an overview of molecular changes of the lung after prenatal LPS exposure, one sample from each group with satisfying RNA quality control was subjected to sequencing transcriptomic analysis in the genomic facility of the Institute for Lung Health (ILH) at JLU. A total amount of 500 ng of RNA per sample was used to enrich for polyadenylated mRNA followed by cDNA sequencing library preparation utilizing the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. After quality control by capillary electrophoresis (4200 TapeStation, Agilent), cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform generating 50-bp paired-end reads. For demultiplexing and the subsequent FASTQ file generation, Illumina’s bcl2fastq (2.19.0.316) and RNA-seq v3.7 bioinformatics pipeline (NEXTFLOW version 23.04.03) were used. Trimmed mean of M-values was used to normalize the gene expression. The genome of Mus musculus and gene annotation were used as downloaded from Illumina’s iGenome repository. The resulting tables with raw read counts were used to generate heatmap. Log2 of the fold change was used for comparison between groups. A pathway enrichment analysis was carried out via Metascape online platform (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1). RNA-seq data of mice lung samples are deposited at EBI’s ArrayExpress under the accession number E-MTAB-16258.

Protein extraction and western blot

The remaining right lung lobes were frozen and stored at − 80 °C for protein extraction. Lungs were homogenized with 1 × Halt™ protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat. No. 78429, Thermo Fischer Scientific). The protein concentration was measured using BCA Protein assay (Cat. No. 23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Loading buffer constituting 1 × RIPA (Cat. No. 9806, Cell Signaling Technology), 2 × Laemmli buffer (Cat. No. 1610737, Bio-Rad, USA), and β-mercaptoethanol (Cat. No. M6250, Sigma Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) was added to protein samples, denatured for 10 min at 95 °C, and cooled on ice. An absolute amount of 25 µg protein was loaded per well on 10% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gels (Cat. No. 4561034, Bio-Rad) and run at 100 V for 1 h. For indicators of molecular weight, precision plus protein dual color standards (Cat. No. 610374, Bio-Rad) was used. Samples were then transferred to a 0.2-µm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Cat. No. 1704156, Bio-Rad) by semi-dry electro blotting (25 V, 2.5 A) for 15 min using Transblot Turbo System (Cat. No. 1704150, Bio-Rad). The membrane was then blocked with 6% skimmed milk (Cat. No. 68514–61-4, Carl Roth) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature (RT) on a shaker for 1 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibody (Table 2) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1 × TBST three times for 10 min each, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody at RT for 1 h, followed by 3 times washing with 1 × TBST buffer for 10 min each. The protein bands were detected by ECL western blotting reagents (Cat. No. RPN2235, Amersham, Germany), and chemiluminescent signals were quantified by Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad). Immunofluorescence staining

Lung sections were deparaffinized and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Cat. No. 001–000–162, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cambridgeshire, UK) with 0.4% Triton X-100 (Cat. No. X100, Sigma Aldrich) in PBS at RT for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies (Table 2) at 4 °C overnight. After washing slides for 3 times in PBS for 15 min each, secondary antibodies (Table 2) were incubated at RT for 1 h and then washed 3 times before being mounted with nucleic counterstains 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Cat. No. 62248, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For FGF10 staining, autofluorescence reduction using Maxblock reagents (Cat. No. MB-M, MaxVision Biosciences Inc., Washington, USA) and heat-mediated antigen retrieval for 30 min in Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, Cat. No. ZUC029-500, ZytoVision GmbH, Bremerhaven, Germany) were performed prior to primary antibody incubation. Images were acquired via Leica DM5500 B fluorescence microscope or STELLARIS 5 confocal scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), with the same setting (e.g., brightness and exposure) being applied to all slides derived from animals of different groups.

IL6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Protein extracts was further assessed using the ELISA method to determine the IL6 synthesis. ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s guidance (Mouse DuoSet IL6 Kit, Cat. No. DY406, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA). Briefly, ELISA plate was incubated with IL6 capture antibody (1:120) overnight at RT, followed by adding 100 µl sample per well and incubating at RT for 2 h. The plate was then washed and 100 µl of detection antibody (1:60) was added to each well for incubation at RT for 2 h. Finally, 100 µl of streptavidin-HRP (1:40) was added to the plate and incubated for 20 min at RT. After incubation with 100 µl of substrate for streptavidin-HRP, the optical density was measured at 450 nm with wavelength correction at 540 nm by using Infinite® 200 PRO (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

In vitro model of human-derived lung fibroblasts stimulated with LPS

Cell culture

The WI-38 non-cancerous human lung fibroblast cell line derived from normal embryonic tissues provides a reliable model to investigate MYFs and LIFs differentiation [23]. The WI-38 cell line (#CCL-75) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Cat. No. 21885–025, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat. No. 10270–106, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cat. No. 15140–22, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells between passage 10 and 20 were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates (Cat. No. 83.3920.300, Sarstedt, Nuembrecht, Germany), followed by overnight incubation to achieve > 90% confluence [23]. Due to limited data of WI-38 cells being stimulated with LPS, preliminary experiments were performed with various concentrations of LPS (20, 40, 80, and 160 µg/ml) and co-incubation periods (8 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Cells incubated in DMEM medium without LPS served as the vehicle control.

Transcriptomic analysis and immunofluorescence

In accordance with in vivo experiments, one sample from each in vitro cell exposure group was subjected to bulk RNA sequencing and exploratory transcriptomic analysis. RNA-seq data of cell samples were deposited at EBI’s ArrayExpress under the accession number E-MTAB-16236. In the following, qPCR was carried out for signature marker expression (primers are listed in Table 1). After treatment, cells were collected in lysis buffer (Cat. No. 79216, Qiagen), and RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. No. 74104, Qiagen, Hamburg, Germany). The following procedures were the same as aforementioned. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the reference gene. For immunofluorescence, WI-38 cells were fixed in 4% of PFA in RT for 15 min and then blocked for 1 h in 5% of BSA after treatment with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at RT. Then, primary and secondary antibodies were added sequentially in 5% BSA to incubate overnight and for 2 h respectively, with dilutions being demonstrated in Table 2. Images were acquired using the EVOS M7000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 6 Software was used for statistical analyses and graph assembly. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. As two independent variables (LPS doses and exposure time) were incorporated in this study, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test was performed with p values being modified accordingly. Differences in means were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. In addition, linear regression models were used to analyze separately the time- and dose-related effect of gene expressions as in previous literature [42], with CT values as the response variable (continuous) and LPS time or dose as the binary predictor variable (i.e., 15.5 dpc vs. 18.5 dpc and LPS 50 vs. LPS 100). The number of biological samples (depicted as n) is shown in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Neonatal and maternal outcome

After i.p. injection of LPS, pregnant mice showed generally no sign of stress or pain as assessed by a predefined scoring sheet integrated in the ethical approval (G76/2021, 1066_GP). The majority of pups were live born at term between 19.5 and 20.5 dpc, demonstrating no significant differences in birth weights across different exposure groups (mean birth weight 1.2 g).

Discrete lung architectural alteration after varying prenatal LPS exposure

Compared to the saline controls, dimensional alveolar morphometry revealed generally increased airspace percentages and decreased septal wall thickness in prenatal LPS-exposed newborn mice at P1 (Fig. 2A, B). This pattern of lung structural changes was observed for LPS exposure at both time points of 15.5 and 18.5 dpc (Fig. 2B). An increasing LPS dose was associated with reduced septal wall thickness, but this trend did not apply for air spaces (Fig. 2B). At 15.5 dpc, prenatal LPS exposure was associated with a non-significant increasing trend of MLI, whereas at 18.5 dpc LPS 50 significantly enhanced MLI but LPS 100 induced the opposite effect (Fig. 2B). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first preclinical mice study to explore in detail the impact of various clinically relevant scenarios of prenatal LPS exposure on the developing lung. The rationale of particularly distinguishing pre- and postnatal LPS exposure in this study as compared to previous ones lies in the distinct ontogeny in pulmonary responsiveness of LPS [39, 45, 46]. As such, results of mice models on postnatal infection contributing to lung pathology cannot be extrapolated to the context of prenatal infection/inflammation. Here, we found unexpectedly diverse structural and molecular changes in the newborn lung, with prenatal LPS dose- and time-dependent modulation of fibroblast subpopulations MYFs and LIFs.

Due to advanced perinatal-neonatal health care in the post-surfactant era, there is a paradigm shift in the pathological manifestation of BPD from generalized airway and vascular damage (“old BPD”) to larger and simplified alveoli with aberrant vascularization (“new BPD”) [3]. In our study, enlarged air spaces in LPS-exposed newborn mice lungs are in line with new BPD pathological hallmarks [3]. Septal wall thickness in some pre-existing preclinical BPD models were found to be increased [33, 35, 47–49], as opposed to thinner septa observed in this study. Possible explanations may include a different end point at P1, and more importantly, prenatal LPS-induced general inhibition of PDGFRA-expressing progenitor fibroblasts, which are considered to be major components of septa at early postnatal time [20, 25]. We also demonstrated a highly divergent pattern of MLI in response to different doses and timing of LPS, which underlines the plasticity of developing lungs upon prenatal programming factors.

Despite diverse routes and agents used in mice models to mimic CA, none of them accurately recapitulates all features of this complex entity [8, 11]. CA encompasses a heterogeneous setting of conditions with discordance and overlap in polymicrobial, inflammatory, pathological, and clinical manifestations [12–14, 30]. Latest recommendations propose the use of term Triple I to extensively cover infection/inflammation in the amniotic fluid, placenta, fetal membranes, and umbilical cord vessels [12]. An i.p. administration of LPS represents a systemic pathway to elicit placental inflammation, whereas local injection in the uterine or amniotic sacs preferentially mimic intra-amniotic inflammation as a large part of CA cases show no systemic signs (e.g., fever) [50]. Importantly, i.p. doses of LPS were deescalated in this study to avoid excessive maternal systemic reactions and consequent preterm delivery/stillbirth, based on our study aim to investigate pulmonary outcomes of surviving offspring exposed to prenatal infection/inflammation. As supplement to the in vivo mice model, an in vitro model of human WI-38 fibroblast cell line enables LPS to directly signal lung fibroblasts, mimicking the absence of maternal influence. IL6 has been demonstrated to be a potent cytokine implicated in the development of BPD [51]. Our in vivo results corroborated pulmonary IL6 responses of newborn mice upon maternal LPS systemic administration [34, 35]. The lack of TLR-NFκB significance in mice transcriptomic profiling, in turn, indicated that LPS did not cross placenta via i.p. route [52]. In WI-38 cells challenged with LPS, the induction of inflammatory mediators and activation of TLR-NFκB signaling suggests that fibroblasts are not only stromal cells but also direct contributors to shape the inflammatory milieu of lung. This is supported by recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, which shed light on chemokine-expressing inflammatory fibroblast clusters with immunomodulatory potential [53].

Compared to epithelial and endothelial cells, rMCs are more plastic by continuously sensing environmental cues and adapting their expression profile especially in the early-life period [16–18]. Recent omics data have defined a plethora of rMCs subpopulations based on lineage histories, function, spatial, and temporal specifications [15–18, 25, 43]. However, the extent of overlap among them remains largely unclear with a lack of consensus on standardized nomenclature [15, 18, 25]. Specifically for mesenchymal fibroblasts, numerous subpopulations (e.g., alveolar, matrix and adventitial fibroblast, proliferating fibroblast, mesenchymal alveolar niche cell) have been annotated [15–18, 25], which complicates the interpretation of their roles in lung development and disease. In this exploratory study, we stick to gross functional classification of LIFs and MYFs in accordance with canonical lineage markers validated by previous investigations including our own scRNA-seq data [19–24]. Despite their differences in ontogeny and function, there is evidence indicating transdifferentiation between MYFs and LIFs in the particular context of injury and regeneration. In lung fibrosis and hyperoxia-induced BPD mice models, attenuation of lipogenic identity with a shift towards myofibrogenic profile has been noticed [19, 54, 55]. Indeed, two-way phenotypic switch between MYFs and LIFs during lung fibrosis formation and resolution was demonstrated in both in vivo mice models [44, 56, 57] and in vitro human WI-38 fibroblast cells [23]. Furthermore, intermediate states in LIF to MYF transdifferentiation and vice versa have been specified [23, 57]. Of note, current understanding of MYFs-LIFs shifts is largely limited to postnatal hyperoxia-induced pulmonary injury and lung fibrosis in young and adult mice [19, 23, 44, 55–57]. By targeting prenatal LPS exposure as a different context than before, our results reinforced the plasticity of mesenchymal fibroblasts by showing heterogeneous expression patterns of MYFs and LIFs hallmark markers in response to various timing and doses of prenatal LPS.

Based on their ontogenetic significance, MYFs and LIFs signature markers may have clinical implications for predicting and treating BPD. PDGFRA-expressing progenitor rMCs emerge early at 9.5 dpc and give rise to MYFs as well as LIFs later [20, 25]. Similar as our data, decreased PDGFRA expression was reflected in rodent hyperoxia BPD models [58, 59] and has been found to be associated with increased risk of BPD in human infants [60]. Traced by PLIN2 expression, LIFs emerge in mice lungs during the late pseudoglandular stage (15.5–16.5 dpc) [21], and the potent pro-regenerative factor FGF10 is predominantly expressed by LIFs [21, 22]. Decreased PLIN2 and FGF10 expressions are detected by mice and human data to be associated with various lung diseases including BPD [19, 40, 56, 61]. The administration of recombinant FGF10 was identified to trigger de novo alveologenesis in mice with hyperoxia-induced BPD [19]. In our study, PLIN2 and FGF10 were upregulated by certain LPS exposure circumstances in parallel to an elevated pulmonary IL6 response, with NFκB activation being also indicated by the in vitro model (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Of note, previous studies have suggested a dual role of NFκB-mediated inflammation to attenuate or exacerbate lung injury, depending on the developmental stage at which inflammation occurred and its intensity [52, 62, 63]. In normal mice lung organogenesis, ACTA2-positive fibroblast subsets were found to rise prior to birth and wane shortly after P7 [24, 64]. Accordingly, sustained expression of ACTA2 in mesenchymal cells from tracheal aspirates of preterm infants was found to be a useful prognostic marker for severe BPD [65]. ELN and COL1A1 are validated by scRNA-seq data to be hallmark genes of MYFs [19, 23, 25, 54, 58], with interspecies particularities. Contrary to human lung fibroblasts which already express collagens in the embryonic period [23] (Fig. 9), mice lungs begin to abundantly deposit collagens the alveolar stage [66, 67]. These characteristics rationalize our choice of MYFs-specific markers separately for human and mice-derived samples.

In our study, differential regulation between the transcriptional and translational level was noticed for MYFs and LIFs signature markers. Possible explanations may include LPS effect being dampened by time-lapse before birth, the intrinsic temporal difference between mRNA and protein synthesis, as well as post-transcriptional regulation and protein turnover. Newly identified mechanisms like the abrogation of FGF10 nuclear translocation [68] may also be implicated in the difference between mRNA and protein expression of MYFs and LIFs markers.

There are limitations in our study. First, the method used to establish an in vivo mice model partly replicates the true pathophysiology of CA, which may confine the extrapolation of preclinical results in clinical practice. However, the doses and timing of LPS have been determined to best simulate clinically relevant scenarios. Second, longitudinal analyses beyond P1 were not performed to investigate the accumulative and persisting disease evolution of BPD. Given our focus on diverse scenarios of LPS exposure prior to birth, additional postnatal conditioning (e.g., chronological follow-up and multiple hits) were excluded to avoid excessive complexity in experimental design and data interpretation. For future investigations, pre-selected prenatal LPS scenario in combination with postnatal hit factors is desirable in in vivo models to approximate the multifactorial origin of BPD and its long-term sequelae [69]. Third, mechanistic investigations are largely restricted to phenotypic and genotypic changes of two grossly defined lung mesenchymal fibroblast subpopulations. This pilot study is set to gain a preview of mesenchymal fibroblasts’ roles in the particular context of prenatal LPS-induced lung injury as seldom explored before. Our transcriptomic profiling is based on limited samples and thus of an exploratory nature. In the following, lineage tracing tools combined with scRNA-seq are intended to dissect the heterogeneity and dynamics of distinctly specified fibroblast subpopulations upon prenatal LPS stimulation. Last but not the least, interspecies differences between mice and humans may limit the translational extrapolation of preclinical data in clinical settings, as noted by divergent regulation trends of MYF- and LIF-specific markers upon LPS exposure between in vivo and in vitro experiments. Despite having a highly relevant human origin, WI-38 embryonic fibroblast cell line represents a monoculture model lacking the in vivo microenvironment and interaction with immune cells to comprehensively recapitulate inflammatory injury.

Conclusions

Our results yielded significant but less drastic morphological changes in newborn mice lungs exposed to LPS prenatally, as compared to pre-existing studies [33–35, 66]. This probably better reflect the de facto effect due to our more clinically relevant settings of prenatal LPS exposure. This study indicates that prenatal LPS exposure programs the early-life lung development by modulating mesenchymal fibroblasts in a time- and dose-dependent manner. There seems to be an intricate balance between MYFs and LIFs phenotype and gene expression profiles during normal lung development, whereas disturbed MYFs-LIFs differentiation may contribute to lung diseases. Results of this study may help to refine the risk categorization of patients exposed to various prenatal infection/inflammation scenarios, with the aim to target subgroups at risk of severe BPD. Based on detailed dissection of distinct fibroblast subpopulations with temporal, spatial, and functional specifications, strategies to modulate MYFs-LIFs differentiation may hold promise for BPD prevention and treatment.
