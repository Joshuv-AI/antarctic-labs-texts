Who’s Driving? Game Theoretic Path Risk of AGI Development
Robin Young

Abstract
Who controls the development of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) might matter less than how we handle the fight for control itself. We formalize this ”steering wheel problem” as humanity’s greatest near-term existential risk may stem not from misaligned AGI, but from the dynamics of competing to develop it. Just as a car crash can occur from passengers fighting over the wheel before reaching any destination, catastrophic outcomes could arise from development competition long before AGI exists. While technical alignment research focuses on ensuring safe arrival, we show how coordination failures during development could drive us off the cliff first. We present a game theoretic framework modeling AGI development dynamics and prove conditions for sustainable cooperative equilibria. Drawing from nuclear control while accounting for AGI’s unique characteristics, we propose concrete mechanisms including pre-registration, shared technical infrastructure, and automated deterrence to stabilize cooperation. Our key insight is that AGI creates network effects in safety: shared investments become more valuable as participation grows, enabling mechanism designs where cooperation dominates defection. This work bridges formal methodology and policy frameworks, providing foundations for practical governance of AGI competition risks.



1
Introduction

The development of Artificial General Intelligence represents one of humanity’s biggest opportunities and most consequential challenges. While technical alignment - ensuring AGI systems pursue human-compatible objectives - has dominated safety discussions, we argue that a more immediate existential risk could preempt alignment concerns entirely: catastrophic competition between developers on the pathway to AGI. Just as nuclear weapons research triggered arms races and near-catastrophic conflicts long before any bombs were deployed, AGI competition could precipitate disaster before any system is actually built. Nation-states and corporations racing to achieve AGI first may not only sacrifice safety precautions or deploy untested systems, they could engage in preemptive strikes or trigger military conflicts based merely on the perception of rival progress. We formalize this as the
steering wheel problem
: humanity risks disaster not from the destination (AGI itself), but from the struggle to control the journey.

Current AGI risk paradigms focus overwhelmingly on technical alignment challenges. While critical, this neglects the structural dynamics that could render alignment irrelevant. Historical precedents from nuclear arms races to antibiotic resistance demonstrate how rational actors in competitive systems often converge on collectively suboptimal outcomes
(
Schelling 1960
)
. Early signs of analogous dynamics appear in modern AI development: proprietary model secrecy, compute arms races, and geopolitical preemptive sanctions over semiconductor access. Without intervention, AGI development may become a prisoner’s dilemma where defection (reckless acceleration) dominates cooperation (measured, safe progress).

While prior work has rigorously analyzed technical alignment challenges in AGI
(
Bostrom 2014
;
Yudkowsky 2006
)
, three gaps persist in addressing competitive dynamics. First,
Armstrong et al. 2016
formalize AGI races but stop short of proposing concrete mechanisms to stabilize cooperation. Second,
Dafoe 2018
survey governance paradigms but lack equilibrium analyses showing when cooperation could emerge. Third,
Critch and Krueger 2020
advocate for ethical norms without grounding them in incentive-compatible structures. Recent work on compute governance
(
Sastry et al. 2024
)
and model staging
(
Shevlane et al. 2023
)
provides empirical grounding for our mechanisms.

Our framework bridges these gaps by (1) proving AGI-specific conditions for cooperative equilibria (Theorem
1
), (2) coupling this theory with mechanisms like cryptographic pre-registration (§
3
), and (3) demonstrating how network effects in safety investments invert traditional arms race dynamics. Unlike nuclear or biological analogs, our approach exploits AGI’s dependence on shared knowledge growth (§
2
) and inspectable software infrastructure, providing a path to deterrence even if technical alignment remains unsolved.

2.1
Players and Actions

Let
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
denote the set of AGI developers (nation-states and private labs). Each player
i\in\mathcal{N}
is characterized by:

•

Compute resources
:
c_{i}\sim\text{LogNormal}(\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2})

•

Technical expertise
:
e_{i}\in[0,1]
, where
e_{i}=1
represents cutting-edge capability

•

Risk tolerance
:
\rho_{i}\in[0,1]
, modulating defection incentives

•

\phi
: Network multiplier coefficient (small positive value, e.g.
\phi=0.1
)

At each discrete timestep
t
, players simultaneously choose:

•

Development strategy
:
a_{i}(t)\in\{\text{Cooperate},\text{Defect}\}

•

Resource commitment
:
r_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
, fraction of
c_{i}
allocated to AGI R&D

•

Information sharing
:
s_{i}(t)\in\{0,1\}
, where
s_{i}=1
denotes full transparency

2.2
State Dynamics

The system evolves via:

Technical capability:

\displaystyle T_{i}(t+1)=T_{i}(t)+\alpha r_{i}(t)c_{i}e_{i}\cdot(1+\gamma s_{i}(t))

(1)

Shared knowledge:

\displaystyle K(t+1)=K(t)+\beta\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}s_{i}(t)T_{i}(t)

(2)

Verification status:

\displaystyle V_{i}(t+1)=\mathbb{I}_{\text{audit}}(i)\cdot\text{Bernoulli}(p_{\text{audit}})

(3)

Security commitments:

\displaystyle S(t+1)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}V_{i}(t+1)T_{i}(t+1)

(4)

where
\mathbb{I}_{\text{audit}}(i)=1
if player
i
is randomly selected for a verification audit in timestep
t
. The term
\text{Bernoulli}(p_{\text{audit}})
indicates that if player
i
is audited, verification succeeds with probability
p_{\text{audit}}
(and fails with probability
1-p_{\text{audit}}
).

2.3
Payoff Structure

Player
i
’s utility at time
t
:

U_{i}(t)=\underbrace{\lambda T_{i}(t)+\mu K(t)+\phi s_{i}(t)K(t)}_{\text{Economic}}+\underbrace{\sigma S(t)-\xi\sum_{j\neq i}T_{j}(t)}_{\text{Security}}-\underbrace{\left(\eta r_{i}(t)^{2}+\theta(1-V_{i}(t))\right)}_{\text{Costs}}

(5)

Total discounted utility:
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}U_{i}(t)\right]
, where
\delta\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.

2.4
Security Commitments

The shared security infrastructure
S(t)

\displaystyle S(t+1)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}V_{i}(t)T_{i}(t)

(6)

Where:

•

V_{i}(t)\in\{0,1\}

•

T_{i}(t)
represents player

The security term

Shared threat intelligence between verified players

•

Collective sanctions against defectors

Reduced preemptive strike risks through transparency

2.5
Key Assumptions

Assumption 1

(Bounded Rationality)
.

Players use trigger strategies conditioned on public history
h^{t}=\{K(0),\dots,K(t),S(0),\dots,S(t)\}

Assumption 2

(Imperfect Monitoring)
.

Verification audits detect defection (
a_{i}=\text{Defect}

Assumption 3

(Dynamic Entry)

\lambda=0.05
, initializing with

2.6
Equilibrium Analysis

Theorem 1

(Cooperative Equilibrium Existence)
.

Under conditions:

1.

Network effects dominate:
\beta>\gamma+\frac{\xi}{\mu}

2.

Verification is affordable:
\theta\leq\theta_{\text{max}}=\frac{\mu\beta}{\delta}

3.

Punishments are credible:
\xi\geq\xi_{\text{min}}=\frac{\lambda\alpha}{\delta}

there exists a subgame perfect equilibrium where
\forall i,t
,
a_{i}(t)=\text{Cooperate},s_{i}(t)=1
.

Proof Sketch.

Applying
Fudenberg and Maskin 1986
folk theorem for infinitely repeated games with perfect public monitoring, cooperation is sustainable if:

\delta\geq\frac{\pi^{\text{Defect}}-\pi^{\text{Cooperate}}}{\pi^{\text{Defect}}-\pi^{\text{Punishment}}}

(7)

Where:

•

\pi^{\text{Cooperate}}=\mu\beta
(knowledge growth benefits)

•

\pi^{\text{Defect}}=\lambda\alpha
(private capability gains)

•

\pi^{\text{Punishment}}=\xi
(security losses)

Condition 3 ensures punishment credibility:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\delta(\pi^{\text{Cooperate}}-\pi^{\text{Punishment}})}_{\text{Future loss}}\geq\underbrace{\pi^{\text{Defect}}-\pi^{\text{Cooperate}}}_{\text{Immediate gain}}

(8)

∎

Theorem 2

(Defection Prevention)
.

For mechanisms
\mathcal{M}=(\text{pre-registration},\text{staged deployment})
, the defection probability satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(\text{Defect}_{i})\leq\epsilon(p_{\text{audit-freq}},\xi,\tau)=\frac{1}{1+p_{\text{audit-freq}}\xi\tau}

(9)

where
p_{\text{audit-freq}}
represents the frequency of verification audits, influenced by mechanisms like pre-registration, and
\tau
represents the planned duration until the next major agreed-upon capability milestone in AGI development.

Intuition.

Pre-registration mechanisms are designed to increase the frequency of verification audits (
p_{\text{audit-freq}}\uparrow
, meaning audits are conducted more often across the consortium). Staged deployment raises synchronization (
\tau\uparrow
), and sanctions boost penalty severity (
\xi\uparrow
). Their combined effect, represented by the product
p_{\text{audit-freq}}\xi\tau
, suppresses defection incentives, leading to the defection probability decreasing as
\epsilon(p_{\text{audit-freq}},\xi,\tau)
becomes smaller.
∎

Intuitive Explanation of Supermodularity

While a formal mathematical proof of supermodularity in this dynamic game is complex, the intuition behind why we expect payoffs to exhibit supermodularity in information sharing is quite straightforward and stems from the network effects inherent in knowledge creation.

Supermodularity, in this context, means that the incentive for player
i
to increase their information sharing (
s_{i}=1
) is strengthened when other players, such as player
j
, also increase their information sharing (
s_{j}=1
). This positive feedback loop arises because of the shared knowledge pool
K(t)
.

When player
i
shares information, it contributes to the growth of
K(t+1)
. Crucially, when player
j
also shares information, they further contribute to
K(t+1)
, making the shared knowledge pool even larger. Because all players benefit from a larger
K(t)
through the economic payoff term
\mu K(t)+\phi s_{i}(t)K(t)
, the combined effect of both
i
and
j
sharing is greater than the sum of their individual effects if they were to share in isolation.

In essence, by contributing to a larger, richer shared knowledge base, each player enhances the returns for all participants, including themselves. This positive externality and reinforcing loop is the core of the ”network effects in safety” we emphasize. Cooperation in information sharing becomes more attractive as more actors participate, creating a virtuous cycle that can help sustain a cooperative equilibrium.

2.7
Discussion

The existential risk posed by AGI development lies not merely in the technical challenge of alignment but in the structural incentives that govern how actors compete to build it. Imagine a high-stakes game where players, nation-states and private labs, must balance short-term tactical advantages against collective survival. Each lab wields unique resources: compute clusters rivaling small nations, proprietary datasets, or algorithmic/architectural secret recipes guarded like state secrets. At every turn, they face a dilemma: share progress to lift the collective ceiling of knowledge or hoard advances to seize a lead.

This tension is formalized through three interlocking systems. First, technical capability evolves dynamically where investing resources accelerates progress, but secrecy breeds inefficiency, as isolated efforts miss the compounding gains of open collaboration. A lab that defects (prioritizing speed over transparency) might spike its capability temporarily, but at the cost of eroding the shared knowledge pool that sustains long-term progress. Second, verification mechanisms act as the immune system of cooperation: randomized audits, inspired by nuclear inspection regimes, detect defection with an 80% reliability, injecting accountability into an otherwise opaque race. Third, security commitments quantify trust, rewarding verified collaborators with preferential access to collective resources while isolating defectors.

The payoff structure mirrors real-world tradeoffs. Economic gains flow from both private breakthroughs and shared knowledge, but shadowing this is the existential dread of rivals pulling ahead, a fear quantified as a security penalty proportional to others’ progress. Costs escalate nonlinearly, modeling the reality that scaling compute yields diminishing returns (10,000 GPUs aren’t 10x better than 1,000). Players thus walk a tightrope: maximize private gains without triggering a collapse of cooperation that would leave all worse off.

Cooperation stabilizes only under precise conditions. Network effects where each participant’s contributions amplify the value of others’ must outweigh the allure of short-term defection. Verification must be both affordable and credible; audits resembling theatrical compliance checks (like early AI ethics boards) would fail, while overly intrusive ones would stall progress. Most crucially, punishments must be immediate and severe enough to render defection irrational, akin to financial markets’ circuit breakers that halt trading during crashes.

The proposed mechanisms discussed below should rewire these incentives. Pre-registration forces upfront commitment to safety protocols, much as clinical trials require public binding plans before experimentation. Staged deployment synchronizes progress, ensuring no actor can unilaterally “win” the race, much like international space missions demand consensus before launch. Together, they transform AGI development from a zero-sum sprint into a relay race where passing the baton (shared knowledge) becomes the optimal strategy.

3
Mechanism Design

The existential risks inherent in AGI development demand more than technical safeguards; they require concrete, verifiable mechanisms that transform competitive instincts into sustainable cooperation. Drawing lessons from humanity’s most successful governance experiments while accounting for AGI’s unique challenges, we propose a framework centered on three interlocking mechanisms that align individual incentives with collective survival.

3.1
Imaginary Consortium

To illustrate how our theoretical framework might translate into practice, we present one possible instantiation of an AGI Development Consortium. While this structure is necessarily speculative and alternative implementations might prove superior, exploring a concrete example helps illuminate both the potential and challenges of our proposed mechanisms.

The consortium could establish objective technical thresholds for participation, creating natural membership tiers based on demonstrable capabilities. Inner circle membership might require both significant compute resources (e.g., 20% of current leading labs’ capacity) and demonstrated technical achievement (such as reaching 80% of SOTA performance on standard benchmarks). This two-factor requirement would help ensure members can meaningfully contribute while preventing resource-rich but technically limited actors from gaining undue influence.

Recent developments illustrate why such graduated access could be valuable. The emergence of labs like Deepseek demonstrates how new players can rapidly achieve significant capabilities, suggesting the importance of clear paths to legitimate participation. Under this structure, core membership would grant real-time access to the shared weight pool and full voting rights on consortium decisions, while creating corresponding obligations for regular contribution and transparency.

The consortium might establish three membership tiers:

Core members meeting both compute and technical thresholds would gain full access to shared weights and checkpoints, voting rights on major decisions, and seats on technical oversight committees. This inner circle would likely include major AI labs and national programs with demonstrated capabilities. In exchange, they would commit to complete weight sharing, pre-registration compliance, and compute contribution to shared infrastructure.

Associate members meeting one but not both thresholds would receive delayed access to shared resources and limited voting rights, with a clear path to core membership through capability demonstration. This tier could include emerging labs showing technical promise but still building compute capacity, or well-resourced organizations developing technical expertise.

Observer status would enable monitoring and partial participation while working toward thresholds, creating transparency without compromising the consortium’s technical standards. This could include academic institutions, safety research organizations, and emerging players.

This tiered structure, while just one possible implementation, illustrates how theoretical mechanisms might be instantiated. The specific thresholds, voting structures, and access rights would need careful calibration based on empirical evidence and stakeholder input. Alternative arrangements emphasizing different criteria or organizational principles might ultimately prove more effective.

Critical questions remain about optimal threshold levels, voting mechanisms, and enforcement protocols. How should technical capability be measured? What compute commitments create meaningful skin in the game without being prohibitively exclusive? How should voting rights be weighted between different member categories? These implementation challenges highlight the need for further research and experimentation.

The key insight is that any practical instantiation must balance inclusivity with meaningful standards, create clear incentives for participation and compliance, and establish objective criteria for membership privileges. While this particular structure may not be optimal, it demonstrates how theoretical principles of verification, graduated access, and automated enforcement could be translated into concrete institutional frameworks.

3.2
Mechanisms

Pre-registration
acts as the cornerstone, establishing a foundation of verifiable development transparency. Before initiating any major training run, developers must publicly commit to detailed technical specifications—architecture blueprints, parameter ranges, and compute allocation plans—through cryptographic commitments that prevent retrospective manipulation. This creates an immutable development covenant that enables verification without exposing proprietary innovations. The mechanism leverages zero-knowledge proofs to verify compliance without accessing trade secrets: a lab could prove its model stayed within pre-registered parameter bounds without revealing exact architectural details, akin to how nuclear inspectors verify enrichment levels without accessing classified designs.

Weight and checkpoint sharing
forms the core of the cooperative framework, creating powerful network effects that make collaboration increasingly attractive as more actors participate. This mechanism requires consortium members to regularly share model weights and training checkpoints in a ”closed-open source” fashion, establishing a growing pool of technical capability that benefits all participants. The system operates on graduated access: inner circle members who meet technical and transparency requirements gain real-time access to the cutting edge of AGI development, while contributing their own advances to the shared pool. This creates a natural incentive structure where the benefits of cooperation such as access to the combined insights and capabilities of all participants increasingly outweigh any potential advantages of solitary development.

Automated deterrence
completes the framework by making defection both immediately detectable and swiftly punishable. The system monitors technical metrics including compute usage patterns, checkpoint consistency, and training trajectories to verify compliance with pre-registered plans. Violations trigger automatic consequences: access to the shared weight pool is immediately revoked, compute resources are restricted, and consortium membership is suspended. These sanctions activate within 72 hours of verified non-compliance, eliminating the political wrangling that has historically weakened international governance frameworks. The system includes graduated responses, from warnings for minor deviations to full exclusion for major breaches, with clear paths to redemption through verified compliance.

3.3
Mechanisms Discussion

This mechanism design transforms AGI development from a competitive race into a cooperative endeavor where shared progress dominates unilateral attempts. By making weight and checkpoint sharing the central cooperation mechanism, we create concrete, verifiable protocols that align individual incentives with collective safety. The system acknowledges but mitigates inevitable challenges in the form of technical verification complexity, proprietary concerns, and coordination overhead through careful mechanism design that makes cooperation the dominant strategy for all participants who seek to remain at the cutting edge of AGI development.

The framework provides several theoretical guarantees. First, cooperation becomes verifiable through concrete technical metrics rather than subjective assessments. Second, network effects in the shared weight pool grow stronger over time, increasing the cost of defection. Third, automated enforcement ensures swift and predictable consequences for non-compliance, creating credible deterrence. Together, these mechanisms aim to transform the prisoner’s dilemma of AGI development into a coordination game where mutual cooperation emerges as the rational equilibrium strategy.

This is not utopian institutionalism but hard-nosed mechanism design, a recognition that cooperation emerges not from goodwill but from carefully engineered incentive structures. Just as TCP/IP’s packet acknowledgment system transformed the anarchic early internet into a reliable communication network, these protocols aim to channel AGI’s disruptive potential into survivable innovation pathways.

4
Limitations and Future Work

The framework proposed here is not a final roadmap but a first sketch of how humanity might collectively steer toward AGI without crashing the car. Like all early navigation systems, it has blind spots and unresolved challenges that demand scrutiny.

4.1
Limitations

Assumptions of Rationality
pose the most pressing constraint. Our model presumes actors will consistently prioritize long-term survival over short-term gains, a stance contradicted by historical arms races and modern AI’s move fast and break things ethos. In the car metaphor, this assumes passengers will never prefer a reckless shortcut over the safe route, even if it risks a crash.

Verification Gaps
further complicate the picture. Cryptographic pre-registration and probabilistic audits, while innovative, cannot eliminate all forms of obfuscation. A lab could train a compact AGI seed model below detection thresholds, then rapidly scale it post-pause akin to hiding a nitro booster in the trunk until the last straightaway.

Geopolitical Fragmentation
threatens to split the car into rival vehicles if political will is not capable of aligning cooperative intents. Our consortium model assumes a single governance structure, but reality may yield competing blocs (e.g., U.S.-aligned vs. China-aligned AGI ecosystems). This echoes Cold War space races, where duplicated efforts wasted resources but avoided direct conflict, a suboptimal but plausible equilibrium.

Dynamic Entry
of new actors (startups, rogue states) remains underdeveloped. Current mechanisms focus on known players, much like air traffic control struggles with rogue drones. Without provisions for rapid onboarding and enforcement, a single defector could hijack the steering wheel.

4.2
Future Directions

Three pathways could address these gaps while advancing the metaphor:

Behavioral Steering Wheels
would integrate prospect theory into game dynamics, modeling how real actors, not hyper-rational agents, respond to existential risks. Experiments could test whether framing milestones as losses to avoid (crash scenarios) versus gains to achieve (safe AGI) alters cooperation rates.

Anomaly Detection Systems
would operationalize the car’s dashboard warnings through ML monitoring of compute markets, energy grids, and hardware supply chains. Federated learning on encrypted infrastructure logs could spot hidden training runs, much like anti-lock brakes preempt skids.

Crash Test Simulations
using agent-based models would stress-test the framework under adversarial conditions:

•

Sudden capability jumps (analogous to black ice)

•

Coalitional defection (e.g., multiple labs bypassing milestones)

•

External shocks (cyberattacks, pandemics)

The road to safe AGI remains uncharted, but our choice is clear: drive together with imperfect maps or crash separately in the dark.

5
Conclusion

Humanity’s journey toward AGI is not a solo race but a shared voyage in a single vehicle, one where passengers fighting over the steering wheel risk careening into the abyss long before reaching their destination. The existential stakes of this metaphor cannot be overstated: our species’ survival at the present moment depends less on who controls the wheel than on whether we collectively avoid crashing the car. This paper has argued that the greatest near-term danger lies not in AGI’s hypothetical misalignment, but in the all-too-human impulse to yank the wheel through reckless competition.

The mechanisms proposed here: pre-registration, staged deployment, and automated deterrence, are institutional seatbelts for this precarious ride. By synchronizing development timelines, they ensure no actor can suddenly swerve the car into uncharted risks. By enforcing transparency, they replace the fog of suspicion with a shared dashboard of progress. By automating sanctions, they make reckless acceleration as irrational as slamming the gas pedal toward a cliff. These are not constraints on innovation but guardrails for a survivable journey.

Critics may argue that such cooperation is politically naive, pointing to the collapse of climate treaties or the chaos of AI governance today. But history shows that even bitter rivals can coordinate when stakes are existential: U.S. Soviet arms control during the Cuban Missile Crisis, Allied-Axis scientific collaboration post-WWII, and the global eradication of smallpox all demonstrate humanity’s capacity to steer collectively under threat. What distinguishes AGI is the asymmetry of urgency: once the car veers into a crash trajectory, no individual can correct course.

This framework rejects both techno-utopianism and fatalism. It acknowledges that AGI’s steering wheel cannot be relinquished. Someone must drive, but the wheel be designed for shared control through aligned incentives and goals. Labs and states need not dissolve their ambitions, only channel them through protocols that align self-interest with collective survival. The alternative is a scramble for unilateral advantage that has the passengers wrestling over the wheel at highway speeds, ensuring catastrophe regardless of who ”wins.”

The path forward is clear but narrow. Near-term actions for establishing compute registries, piloting milestone pauses, and formalizing deterrence protocols must precede AGI’s emergence, just as seatbelts must be buckled before the car moves. Delay risks locking in competition dynamics that no mechanism can later unwind.

In the end, this is not a call for altruism but for rational self-preservation. When all passengers share the same fragile vehicle, cooperation ceases to be idealism and it becomes the only sane strategy. The steering wheel is in our hands; the choice is whether to fight over it or steer together.
