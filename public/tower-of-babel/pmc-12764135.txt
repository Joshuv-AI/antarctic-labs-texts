The association between dietary intakes of nitrate with nitrite from animal and plant food sources and the incidence of metabolic syndrome: a prospective study
Authors: Milad Davarpanah, Zahra Bahadoran, Mitra Hasheminia, Fatemeh Norouzi Rostami, Maryam Javadi, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIn the current study the potential effect of dietary nitrate (NO3)/nitrite (NO2) on the incidence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) was investigated.MethodsTotal of 1911 men and women (mean age of 37.5 ± 12.8 and 58.9% women), participated in the third (2006–2008) examinations of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), were included and followed up to 2018–2022. A semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire was used at baseline to estimate dietary intakes of NO3 and NO2. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazard models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of incidence of MetS across quartile categories of NO3 and NO2 dietary intakes.ResultsMean (SD) daily intake of NO3 and NO2 were 436 (246) mg/d and 9.5 (4.7) mg/d, which were mostly derived from plant sources (95.2% and 56.1% respectively). The highest compared to the lowest quartile of total and plant-based dietary NO3 was accompanied with a significant decreased risk of MetS (HR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.54–0.98, HR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.56-1.00); however, no significant association was observed between animal-based NO3 intakes and incidence of MetS. Morover, no significant association between total, plant-based and animal-based dietary intakes of NO2 and the incidence of MetS was found.ConclusionHigher total and plant-based NO3 intakes may reduce risk of MetS in adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01263-w.Keywords: Dietary nitrate, Dietary nitrite, Metabolic syndrome, Plant-based nitrate, Nitric oxide, Cohort study, Glucose homeostasis

Introduction

MetS is a common multifactorial condition characterized by high triglyceride (TG) levels, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, hypertension (HTN), impaired fasting glucose (IFG), and abdominal obesity (AO). These factors collectively increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and diabetes, as well as the likelihood of developing MetS itself [1–3]. It has been reported that 30.1% of Iranian adults are affected by MetS [4].

The potential toxicity of inorganic NO3 and NO2 has gained more attention in recent years [5, 6]. These compounds are believed to have beneficial effects due to their ability to convert to nitric oxide (NO) [7, 8]. Endogenous NO oxidation and exogenous dietary sources represent the main origins of NO3 and NO2 in humans, with dietary intake responsible for approximately half of circulating steady-state NO metabolites [9]. For NO3 and NO2, the acceptable daily intake (ADI) has been determined as 3.7 and 0.06 mg/kg body weight, respectively [10]. Both reduced and elevated levels of NO production can significantly affect the development of chronic diseases such as IFG, HTN and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [11, 12].

Certain metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance (IR) and IFG [13], may result from the formation of nitrosamines, which are produced through the endogenous conversion of NO2 [14–16]. Evidences also suggest a connection between the components of MetS and the dietary intake of NO3 and NO2 as a higher consumption of NO2 potentially can increase the risk of developing IFG. Some other studies have also reported an association between the components of MetS and levels of nitric oxide metabolites (NOx) [17, 18].

However, considering that dietary patterns, food consumption habits, and lifestyle factors vary across countries and sources of dietary NO3 and NO2 in Iran (e.g., local vegetables, traditional and industrial meat products) differ from those in other countries and rising prevalence of metabolic syndrome and lifestyle-related disorders among Iranian adults [4], investigating the effects of dietary NO3 and NO2 intake on the incidence of MetS in Iranian adults is particularly important. The current evidences linking NO3 and NO2 to MetS have remained limited and sometimes inconsistent. This prospective study was designed to investigate the relationship between dietary intake of NO3 and NO2 and the incidence of MetS in the Iranian population over a 12-year follow-up period. This study will contribute to understanding the role of these compounds in the development of MetS and to provide insights for dietary recommendations to reduce the risk of chronic metabolic diseases.

Subjects and methods

Study population

The current study was conducted within the framework of TLGS. In brief, TLGS is a community-based prospective research that was carried out in district 13 of Tehran, the Iranian capital city. The TLGS’s initial phase started in March 1999, and data is still being collected every three years [19]. The characteristics of the entire population in the third phase of TLGS were comparable to those of participants who completed the validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [20]. Individuals with prevalent MetS (n = 3399), with incomplete biochemical and anthropometric data, and with missing nutritional data were excluded from the 9645 individuals who were > 21 years old at baseline assessment (2006–2008) for the purposes of this study. Final analyses were conducted on 1911 adults who followed up for a median of 9.5 (IQR = 6.6–11.5 y) years from the baseline examination. The flowchart of study participants is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study population

Written informed consents were obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the ethics research council of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: 43010420), in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 1983.

Demographic, lifestyle, anthropometric, and biochemical measurements

Standard questionnaires were used by trained interviewers to gather information. Detailed measurements of variables in TLGS have been reported elsewhere [21].

Participants wore little clothes and no shoes while their weight was measured to the nearest 100 g using a digital scale. A tape meter was used to measure height to the nearest 0.5 cm, when the subject was standing [22]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by square of the height (m²). A tape meter with an accuracy of 0.5 cm was used to measure the waist circumference (WC). Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured twice on the right arm using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer following a 15-minute sitting rest period. Blood pressure was calculated by mean of two measurements. The Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ), which tracked a variety of activities throughout the previous year, including work, leisure and home chores, was used to assess the degree of physical activity [23]. The amount of physical activity was measured in metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes per week (MET-min/week) and low physical activity was defined as < 600 METs min/wk [23, 24].

All participants had fasting blood samples taken after a 12- to 14-hour overnight fast in order to measure their fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and lipid levels. On the day of blood collection, laboratory tests were carried out utilizing automated Selectra 2 analyzers (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, the Netherlands) and commercially available kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran). Only samples that satisfied the acceptable standards for internal quality control were examined. Both inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation of all assays were < 5%. Detailed measurements of biochemical variables in TLGS have been reported [25].

Dietary assessment

To evaluate average food intakes during the last year, a validated 168-item FFQ was used [26]. Participants were asked to indicate how frequently they consumed each food item over the course of the previous year on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis by trained dietitians who had at least five years of experience with the TLGS survey. Grams were then used to represent the portion sizes of the items consumed as stated in household measurements [27]. The validity of the FFQ was evaluated by comparing the energy and nutrient intakes it reported with the average values from twelve 24-hour dietary recalls. Its reliability was checked by comparing results from two FFQs. Pearson and intra-class correlation coefficients showed good agreement between the FFQs and the 24-hour recalls, as well as between the two FFQs [26].

Since the Iranian Food Composition Table is lacking and provides incomplete information on the nutritional content of raw foods and drinks, the US Department of Agriculture Food Composition Table was used to assess the energy and nutritional content of meals and beverages (apart from NO3 and NO2).

NO3 and NO2 dietary composition data were obtained from a recent assessment of commonly eaten foods among Iranians [28]. In conclusion, established spectrophotometric techniques were used to evaluate the NO3 and NO2 concentrations of 87 food items, including grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, dairy products, meats, and processed meats. In addition to the dietary NO3 and NO2 from all meals, separate calculations were performed for NO3 and NO2 obtained from plants and animals.

Validity of NO3 and NO2estimation by FFQ

A biomarker research was conducted on a subsample (n = 251) of the TLGS cohort group to assess the correlation between urine NO3/NO2 levels and estimated food intakes of NO3/NO2. After adjusting for intra-to-inter-individual variance and other confounders like age, body mass index, sex and serum creatinine levels, there was a significant correlation between their food intakes and urine NO3/NO2 values (r = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.49, 0.67, and r = 0.29, 95% CI = 0.17, 0.41) [29].

Definition of terms

MetS was defined based on the criteria outlined in the Joint Interim Statement (JI) [30] as the presence of at least three of the following criteria: elevated fasting serum glucose (FSG) levels (≥ 5.6 mmol/L) or the use of medications to lower glucose levels, elevated serum TG levels (≥ 1.7 mmol/L) or the use of lipid-lowering drugs, reduced levels of HDL-C (< 1.03 mmol/L for men and < 1.29 mmol/L for women), elevated blood pressure (BP) (≥ 130/85 mmHg) or the use of antihypertensive medications, and larger than average waist circumference (WC) (≥ 95 cm) based on country- and population-specific cutoff criteria for Iranian adults of both sexes of the National Committee on Obesity in Iran. The risk score of future development of T2D was calculated for every individual based on the American Diabetes Association (ADA) risk prediction model [31]. The ADA-T2D risk score is composed of the following variables: age (age < 40 y, 40–49 y, 50–59 y, and ≥ 60 y, is scored as 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively), sex (men = 1, women = 0), history of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) (yes = 1, no = 0), FHD (yes = 1, no = 0), history of hypertension (HTN, yes = 1, no = 0), PA (yes = 0, no = 1), and body mass index status (BMI < 25, 25–30, 30–35, and ≥ 35 kg/m2, is scored as 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively) [31]. We scored PA as 0 for MET < 600 min/week and 1 for MET ≥ 600 min/week [32]. The total score was calculated between 0 and 10, and the cut point 5 or higher shows the high risk for T2D.

Statistical analysis

The residuals methods were used to adjust dietary intakes of NO3/NO2 as well as other nutrients for total energy consumption. The mean (SD) values and the frequency (%) of baseline characteristics of individuals with and without MetS, were compared using the independent t-test or chi square test.

A univariate analysis was used to identify confounding factors, including dietary total fat, carbohydrate, protein, and fiber intakes, as well as ADA score. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of MetS incidence across quartile categories of NO3/NO2 intakes. Model 1 was adjusted for ADA diabetes risk score (composed of age, sex, family history of diabetes, history of gestational diabetes, HTN, physical activity, BMI). Model 2 was additionally adjusted for energy intake (kcal/d), total fat (g/d) and fiber intake (g/d).

The interval between the first time the event was observed and the most recent follow-up visit prior to the diagnosis was the incidence date of MetS. Lastly, the follow-up time was calculated by subtracting the computed mid-time date from the day the subject entered the study. The interval between the initial and last observations was the survival time for the precipitants who were censored and lost to follow-up. IBM SPSS for Windows version 20 was used for all analyses, and a two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered as significant.

Result

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of participants based on the event. The participants’ mean age was 37.5 (12.8) years and 58.9% of them was women. At baseline, those with MetS had higher FSG (88 vs. 84 mg/dl), higher rate of HTN (6.2 vs. 3.7%), higher BMI (27.8 vs. 24.7 kg/m2), and higher SBP (110 vs. 105 mmHg) than those without Mets and they were also older (42.1 vs. 34.5 years) than those without MetS. Subjects with and without events did not differ significantly in their rates of smoking and low physical activity and participants with MetS consumed lower meat and processed meats (31.5 vs. 35.9 gr) and total fats (79.3 vs. 84.2 mg) than those without events. Other dietary characteristics did not differ significantly between two groups.

Based on diagram of the study, after excluding cases with missing anthropometric, biochemical, and nutritional data, the final study sample included 1911 individuals. Over the follow-up period, a total of 753 (39.4%) cases of MetS were identified among the participants. Supplementary Table 1 shows baseline characteristics and dietary intakes across quartiles of total NO3 intake. Particpants in the highest quartile had significant higher intake of energy, total fat, carbohydrate, protein and fiber intake. The prevalence of low-PAL also was significantly lower in this group.

The overall dietary intake of NO3 from total, plant and animal food sources did not differ between individuals with and without MetS. Daily mean (SD) dietary intakes of NO3 in MetS and non-MetS subjects were 470 (189) and 465 (191) mg/d respectively, which were mostly derived from plants (95.5%). Such an imbalance between dietary intakes of plant-based and animal-based NO3 is due to high consumption of green leafy vegetables, cucumber, lettuce, and tomato and also grains including white rice and traditional breads. In Iran, the use of nitrogen-based fertilizers is also common and can lead to high levels of NO3 in agricultural products. The average daily intake of dietary NO2 in MetS and non-MetS was 9.5 (3.7) and 9.7 (3.7) mg/d, respectively; animal-based NO2 accounted for 41.7% of this intake. No significant difference in dietary intakes of total and plant-based NO2 between two groups was found but animal–based intakes of NO2 significantly was different between MetS and non-MetS participants.

The relationships between NO3 and NO2 intakes and risk of the event are shown in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Results from Cox regression models showed a significant relationship between the incidence of MetS and dietary intakes of both total and plant-based NO3 as the incidence of MetS was lower in the fourth quartile of dietary NO3 compared to the second quartile (HR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.54–0.98, HR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.56–1.00 total and plant-based sources, respectively); however no significance association was observed between dietary intakes of animal-based NO3 with the incidence of MetS. The results also showed that dietary intakes of total, plant-based, and animal-based NO2 do not significantly correlate with the incidence of MetS after adjustment for all potential confounders.

In contrast, animal-derived nitrate showed no association, possibly due to differences in accompanying nutrients. The lack of association for nitrite further indicates that nitrate, rather than nitrite, drives the observed metabolic benefits.

The present findings suggest that higher consumption of NO3, particularly from plant-based sources, may play a protective role against the development of MetS. This protective effect is likely due to the role of dietary NO3 as a substrate for NO synthesis, which contributes to improve endothelial function, insulin sensitivity, and blood pressure regulation. The absence of significant associations for NO2 intake also indicates that dietary NO3, rather than NO2, might be the main contributor to the observed metabolic benefits.

Discussion

In the current cohort study, the association between dietary intakes of NO3 and NO2 and the incidence of MetS in Iranian population was investigated. A decreased incidence of MetS was found in the participants, whose intakes of total and plant-based NO3 was higher; however no significance association was observed between intakes of animal-based NO3 and all sources intakes of NO2 with the incidence of MetS. Mean dietary intake of NO3 and NO2 of participants were nearly double (1.8 and 2.2 times, respectively) of adequate daily intakes (ADI), that determined by the Scientific Committee on foods (3.7 and 0.06 mg/per kg body weight) and confirmed by Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) [33]. Given that the Iranian diet is predominantly high in carbohydrates (accounting for ~ 60–70% of daily energy intake), grain-based foods, including bread have significant contribution in total NO3 intake. Considering the high daily consumption of traditional Iranian bread, which is made from wheat cultivated with substantial nitrogen fertilizers, the total NO3 intake in the Iranian diet is likely higher than in Western or Mediterranean diets, although the source differs. In Mediterranean and vegetarian diets, the majority of dietary NO3 is derived from fresh leafy vegetables, whereas in Iran, a considerable portion comes from bread and other grains. This difference in NO3 sources may influence metabolic outcomes and should be taken into account when interpreting studies on dietary NO3 intakes and MetS [9, 34].

The potential health risk of excessive intake of NO2 can be methemoglobinemia, cancers and metabolic disorders [5, 35]. The lack of a significant association between dietary intake of NO2 and incidence of MetS, despite previous reports of a protective effect against HTN as a factor of MetS, may be explained by differences in study design, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions. Unlike short-term or cross-sectional studies, our 12-year cohort of 1911 adults allowed for long-term assessment. In addition, more than 95% of nitrate intake in our population came from plant-based foods, which also provide antioxidants and polyphenols that may neutralize the harmful effects of high intake levels of NO3 and NO2.

In a study of 851 children and adolescents, aged 4–19 years, a higher prevalence of MetS (13.2% vs. 6.1%) was observed in the highest compared to the lowest quartile of serum NOx concentrations (33.0 vs. < 19.0 mmol/L) [36]. The results of another investigation showed that there is no association between intakes of nitrate and risk of HTN but higher dietary intakes of NO2 can be a protective factor against the development of HTN as a component of MetS [34]. Some animal studies had also done, as after eighteen months of the LND (low- NO3/NO2 diet), factors of body weight, HTN and IR significantly was increased but following a sodium nitrate therapy, these anomalies which were strongly linked to endothelial NO synthase (NOS) down-regulation, adiponectin deficiency and gut microbiota dysbiosis, were reversed [37]. Another animal study looked into how dietary NO3 affected the lipid profile and glucose tolerance. In rats, nitrate treatment increased SBP back to normal levels and significantly improved glucose tolerance (AUC: 20264 ± 659 vs. 17923 ± 523; p < 0.05 for the diabetes (D) and diabetes + nitrate (DN) groups, respectively) [38]. Improvement of glucose tolerance and reduction of body weight, amount of visceral fat, plasma triglycerides and levels of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) in the mice after NO3 supplementation compared to control mice were also reported [39]. In a rat model of T2D, Khalifi et al. found that NO3 supplementation can improve glucose tolerance, lipid profile as well as bringing the high SBP back to normal [38]. In mice fed a high-fat and high-fructose diet, dietary intervention of NO3 improved IR and lowered serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL-c and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol [40].

Some clinical trials also were conducted on features of MetS and NO3 supplementation. A 24-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial after a long-term showed no significant effect of NO3 supplementation on blood HbA1c, FSG, insulin levels and insulin sensitivity index [41]. Another clinical trial study with the use of a semi-randomized crossover design reported that nitrate-rich beetroot juice as a source of NO3 can lower SBP (from 118 ± 2 to 113 ± 2 mm Hg; P < 0.001) 150 min after ingestion [42].

It has been reported that dietary inorganic NO3 and NO2 can serve as a substrate or backup system for non-enzymatic endogenous production of NO, which is why inorganic NO3 and NO2 are highlighted as having positive therapeutic effects on metabolism that is due to their potential ability to convert to NO [43]. In mammalian physiology and pathology, NO is one of the most important signaling molecules [44, 45]. NO is produced endogenously by a family of NO synthases (neuronal [nNOS], inducible [iNOS], and endothelial [eNOS]) from a precursor L-arginine [46]. NO can also (as a crucial signaling molecule) can simulate the absorption of glucose by human primary skeletal muscle cells [47] and disturbed NO metabolism might have a significant role in the pathogenesis of IR and IFG [48] and IR seems also to be strongly associated with dyslipidemia as a feature of MetS [49]. NO regulates insulin secretion [49] and raises blood flow-dependent glucose delivery, trans-endothelial insulin transfer, membrane translocation, and glucose transporter 4 expression, all of which improve skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity and glucose absorption [50]. These effects of NO are mediated by activation of insulin signaling pathways, including Akt and p85 phosphorylation, reducing IL-1β and reducing fat accumulation in liver that can improve insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake [51, 52]. According to some recent researches, NO2 may be a stable endocrine carrier and a transducer of NO-like bioactivity in the circulation; the acute effects of dietary NO3 and NO2 might include systemic vasodilatation via the NO- cyclic guanosine-5’-monophosphate (cGMP) pathway [53, 54] as NO acts as a vasodilatory agent for lowering blood pressure [55]. Based on a recent study, when mice are given a high-fat diet, dietary nitrate does not stop the development of the metabolic syndrome that might be due to decreases in the levels of significant short-chain fatty acids. The high-fat diet caused a much higher rate of weight gain that was unaffected by dietary NO3. In addition, mice fed a high-fat diet had poor glucose tolerance, which was unaffected by dietary NO3 [56]. Cyclic guanosine-5’-monophosphate (cGMP) might promote conversion of white to beige adipocytes which can work as an anti-obesity agent and improve insulin sensitivity [57]. NO3 can also improve dyslipidemia [39] and can also target cGMP in adipose tissue and improve browning process through NO3-NO2-NO pathway [56, 58]. These results support the theory that NO is the missing component in obesity-induced insulin resistance [59]. eNOS and iNOS have been also found in adipose tissue which shows that NO is produced in mentioned tissue [60]. Overall, the effects of dietary NO3 and NO2 on components of MetS were suggested to be regulation of glucose homeostasis, lowering of BP, improvement of endothelial function and dyslipidemia [39, 61, 62].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our investigation revealed that higher dietary intakes of total and plant-based NO3 is associated with the lower incidence of MetS and no significant association between all source of NO2 and the risk of MetS was observed. In summary, the findings of the present study indicate potential benefits of adding more nitrate-rich plant foods, such as vegetables, grains and legumes, in the diet for supporting metabolic health. While these results are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution, they provide useful insights that could guide future dietary recommendations and public health strategies. Further research is needed to confirm these associations and better understand the underlying mechanisms.

The strengths and limitations of this study should also be taken into consideration. The most notable strengths include its prospective design, large sample size, extended follow-up period, and separate evaluation of plant and animal-sourced NO₃ and NO₂. A validated FFQ for a comprehensive assessment of dietary intake was used. Frequently consumed foods among our population was investigated for estimation of NO3 and NO2 intake of diet [28]. As limitations, estimation and analysis of drinking water was not done due to the lack of information of water intake and low contribution of drinking water in overall NO3/NO2 exposure (6.7 and 26.6% for NO3 and NO2, respectively). Some misclassification may have occurred due to potential changes in an individual’s diet, variations in the NO₃ and NO₂ content of food items, and fluctuations in other MetS risk factors during the study follow-up. This could have led to biased hazard ratio estimates. Further prospective cohort studies are needed to validate our findings and provide additional valuable evidence. Future studies should also focus on whether dietary approaches targeting production of NO may cause MetS progression.
