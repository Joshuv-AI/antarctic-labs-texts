Redifferentiation-enabled TSHRCART cells overcome antigen loss in aggressive thyroid cancers
Authors: Claudia Manriquez-Roman, Elizabeth L Siegler, Justyna J Gleba, Truc N Huynh, Grace E DeFranco, Aylin Alasonyalilar Demirer, Matthew L Pawlush, Michael Redig, Skyeler M Klinge, Long K Mai, James L Miller, Erin E Miller, Brooke L Kimball, Erin E Tapper, R Leo Sakemura, Carli M Stewart, Ismail Can, Olivia L Sirpilla, Jennifer M Feigin, Kun Yun, Omar L Gutierrez-Ruiz, Hong Xia, Mehrdad Hefazi, Kendall J Schick, Ekene J Ogbodo, Gloria Olivier, Yushi Qiu, Robert C Smallridge, Abba Zubair, Han W Tun, John A Copland, Saad S Kenderian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntigen loss remains a major barrier to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell efficacy in solid tumors. In aggressive thyroid cancers, dedifferentiation is accompanied by coordinated loss of lineage-restricted surface antigens, limiting immune recognition.MethodsThyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) expression was assessed on multiple histotypes of thyroid cancer via immunohistochemistry. TSHR-directed chimeric antigen receptor T (CART) cell therapy was assessed against differentiated thyroid cancer subtypes which highly expressed TSHR and dedifferentiated thyroid cancer subtypes which downregulated TSHR in vitro and in xenograft and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mouse models. MAPK inhibitors were assessed in combination with TSHR-CART cell therapy in dedifferentiated thyroid cancers which downregulated TSHR in vitro and in PDX mouse models.ResultsUsing TSHR as a clinically relevant antigen target, we demonstrated that pharmacologic tumor redifferentiation can restore target expression and sensitize tumors to CART cell therapy. TSHR-CART cells mediate durable antigen-specific cytotoxicity in TSHRhigh differentiated thyroid cancer models but are limited in TSHRlow dedifferentiated tumors. In patient-derived anaplastic thyroid cancer xenografts, MAPK inhibition restores TSHR expression and converts tumors from CAR-resistant to CAR-responsive. Concurrent redifferentiation therapy and CART cell treatment yields superior tumor control and survival versus monotherapy, without impairing CART cell function.ConclusionsThese findings establish tumor redifferentiation as a generalizable strategy to overcome antigen loss and enhance CART cell therapy in thyroid cancer and potentially other solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02744-0.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, CART cell therapy, Redifferentiation therapy, Thyroid cancer

Introduction

Tumor heterogeneity and loss of lineage identity are hallmarks of advanced and treatment‑refractory solid tumors [1, 2]. As cancers evolve under therapeutic pressure, transcriptional reprogramming and dedifferentiation frequently lead to coordinated suppression of lineage‑restricted genes, including surface antigens that are otherwise attractive therapeutic targets. This phenotypic plasticity represents a major barrier to antigen-specific immunotherapy and has emerged as a central mechanism of resistance across multiple tumor types [3–6].

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has demonstrated curative potential in a subset of patients with hematologic malignancies, yet its efficacy in solid tumors remains limited [7–12]. Among the key obstacles are inadequate and heterogeneous target antigen expression, particularly in solid tumors [11, 13, 14]. Antigen loss or low antigen density not only diminishes CART cell activation but also enables immune escape, constraining both the depth and durability of responses [15–17]. Strategies that can restore or stabilize tumor antigen expression therefore represent an unmet need for extending the reach of cellular immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Tumor redifferentiation—the pharmacologic reactivation of lineage‑specific transcriptional programs—offers a mechanistically distinct approach to this challenge. Rather than engineering increasingly complex CAR designs to overcome antigen scarcity, redifferentiation aims to reprogram the tumor itself, reinstating expression of therapeutically actionable antigens. Clinically, this concept has been most clearly demonstrated in thyroid cancer, where inhibition of oncogenic MAPK signaling restores expression of genes required for iodine uptake, enabling renewed sensitivity to radioactive iodine therapy [18–21]. Whether similar redifferentiation strategies can be leveraged to overcome antigen loss in cellular immunotherapy remains largely unexplored.

Thyroid‑stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) is a lineage‑restricted surface antigen whose expression is tightly linked to thyroid differentiation status. TSHR is abundantly expressed in differentiated thyroid epithelium and remains expressed in differentiated metastatic thyroid cancer subtypes that have become radioactive iodineinsensitive [22–24]. However, TSHR is markedly downregulated in poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (PDTC) and anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC), coinciding with activation of MAPK signaling pathways [23, 24]. Importantly, pharmacologic inhibition of MAPK signaling with MEK and BRAF inhibitors—agents already approved for advanced thyroid cancer—has been shown to restore TSHR expression in patients and preclinical models, providing a clinically actionable redifferentiation platform. [18–21]

In this study, we test the hypothesis that pharmacologic redifferentiation can be used to restore tumor antigen expression and sensitize solid tumors to CART cell therapy. Using TSHR‑directed CART (TSHR-CART) cells, we demonstrate that antigen density is a critical determinant of CAR efficacy and that dedifferentiation‑associated antigen loss underlies resistance in aggressive disease. We show that MAPK pathway inhibition re‑establishes TSHR expression in dedifferentiated and anaplastic thyroid cancer models. Importantly, redifferentiation therapy does not impair CART cell function and synergizes with CART cell treatment to produce durable tumor control in patient‑derived xenograft models.

This work introduces a mechanism‑guided, translatable framework for addressing one of the most fundamental barriers to effective cellular immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

FTC-133, XTC.UC1, and THJ-529T cell lines were graciously provided by Dr. Copland’s lab at Mayo Clinic, Jacksonville, Florida. The FTC-133 cell line is derived from a differentiated follicular thyroid carcinoma with known mutations that include FLCN, MSH6, NF1, PTEN, TERT, and TP53 (https://www.cellosaurus.org/CVCL_1219). The THJ-529T cell line was derived from a PDTC tumor containing BRAF and TERT mutations (https://www.cellosaurus.org/CVCL_9916). [25] XTC.UC1 is the only characterized HCC cell line and the only known cell line to continue to express endogenous TSHR in vitro. [26] All cell lines were DNA short tandem repeat (STR) validated prior to use. For indicated experiments, THJ-529T and FTC-133 cell lines were transduced with a firefly luciferase (Luc+) vector containing a hygromycin resistance gene (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) as well as a TSHR vector and a puromycin resistance gene (Genecopoeia Inc., Rockville, MD, USA). These cells were then cultured in selection media containing either hygromycin (1 µg/mL) or puromycin (1 µg/mL) to obtain a pure population as previously described [27]. FTC-133 and XTC.UC1 cell lines were cultured in D10 medium made with DMEM (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), 10% FBS (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 1% PSG (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). For specific in vitro assays, FTC-133 cell lines were transduced with different MOIs of a TSHR vector containing puromycin resistance gene (GeneCopoeia Inc., Rockville, MD, USA), and TSHR absolute counts were determined by flow cytometry using absolute counting beads (CountBright™, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Mycoplasma testing was performed and confirmed to be negative monthly.

CAR construct and CART cell production

The use of recombinant DNA in the laboratory was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC), IBC number HIP00001516.06. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from de-identified normal donor blood apheresis cones [28] obtained under a Mayo Clinic IRB approved protocol, using SepMate tubes (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) and Lymphoprep™ solution (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada). T cells were separated with negative selection magnetic beads using EasySep™ Human T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada). Second-generation 4-1BB-costimulated TSHR CAR constructs were synthesized de novo (IDT, Coralville, USA) and cloned into a third-generation lentivirus under the control of the EF-1α promoter. The TSHR-targeted single chain variable fragment was derived from an autoantibody from a patient with Graves’ disease, clone K1-70 [29]. TSHR-CART cells were then generated through the lentiviral transduction of normal donor T cells as previously described [30, 31]. Briefly, lentiviral particles were generated through the transient transfection of plasmid into 293T virus-producing cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) in the presence of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G), and packaging plasmids pCMVR8.74 (Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA). The titers and multiplicity of infection (MOI) were analyzed and calculated by flow cytometry as previously described [27].T cells isolated from normal donors were stimulated using anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Life Technologies, Oslo, Norway) at a 3:1 beads-to-cell ratio and then transduced 24 h after stimulation with lentivirus particles at an MOI of 3. T cells were cultured in T cell medium containing X-VIVO™ 15 media (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), 10% human AB serum (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), and 1% PSG (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Magnetic bead removal and the evaluation of CAR expression on T cells by flow cytometry were performed on day 6 by staining with a goat anti-human IgG H + L secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). CART cells were harvested and cryopreserved on day 8 for future experiments. Prior to cryopreservation, CART cells were stained and evaluated to assess T-cell subset and phenotype expression patterns using the following antibodies: CD45 APC (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), CD3 BV605 (BioLegend), CD4 PE-Cy7 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), CD8 BV421 (BD Biosciences), CCR7 PE (BioLegend) and CD45RA APC-Cy7 (BioLegend). CART cells were thawed and rested in T cell medium overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2 prior to experimental use.

Having validated TSHR as a potential antigen to target with CART cell therapy, we designed and generated TSHR-CART cells using a single chain variable fragment isolated from the TSHR autoantibody clone K1-70 and cloned into a lentiviral CAR construct containing 4-1BB and CD3ζ (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Healthy donor T cells transduced with CAR-encoded lentiviral particles (Supplemental Fig. S1B) demonstrated high CAR expression as assessed by flow cytometry (Supplemental Fig. S1C). TSHR-CART cells displayed comparable CD4:CD8 ratio and phenotype compared to control untransduced T cells (UTD) (Supplemental Fig. S1D-E).

Multi-parametric flow cytometry

Samples were prepared for flow cytometry as previously described [27, 30–33]. In all analyses, the population of interest was gated based on forward vs side scatter characteristics, then by singlet gating, and then by live cell gating using LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Flow cytometry was performed on a three-laser CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Chaska, MN, USA). All analyses were performed using FlowJo v10.7.1 software (Ashland, OR, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded tissue. Thyroid tissue microarray (TMA) was made from archival samples under Mayo Clinic IRB approval with sample size for each tumor at 1.5 mm. Paraffin blocks were cut into 5 µm thick sections. Slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated with decreasing concentrations of ethanol, and finally with water. Additionally, a normal human tissue microarray was purchased (Bio SB, Santa Barbara, CA, USA), which included 23 different normal human tissue types. Antigen retrieval was performed using Antigen Retrieval Solution (pH 6 or pH 9) (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) for at least 15 min in an autoclave, and cooling for 30 min at room temperature. Slides were washed with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 10 min to block the activity of endogenous peroxidases. Primary anti-human TSHR antibody was used for 60 min at room temperature at 1:500 (abcam, ab218108, Cambridge, UK). The infiltration of human T lymphocytes into tumors was assessed using anti-human CD3 at 1:500 (abcam, ab52959, Cambridge, UK). The primary antibody was detected using the Envision Labeled Kit (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After washing, slides were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Slides were dehydrated through ethanol and xylene and cover‐slipped using a xylene‐based mounting medium (Fisher Scientific). Samples were examined under bright-field illumination at 20 × objective, and digital images were obtained using Aperio AT2 (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Results were processed using Aperio eSlide Manager and H-Score values were estimated using the Aperio ImageScope Software (both Aperio Technologies, Vista, CA, USA). The staining of the TMA punches was scored using an algorithm in the ImageScope Software (Aperio Technologies) created by a histologist based upon membrane staining signal intensity (0, 1 +, 2 +, or 3 + which is determined for each cell in a fixed field. H score was then calculated based upon signal intensity and percentage of cells using the following formula: [1 x (%cells 1 +) + 2 x (% cells 2 +) + 3 x (% cells 3 +)]. Cases were excluded from the study if a section could not be assigned a score due to insufficient quantity of tumor tissue present. Normal tissues were used as a positive and negative control.

For cell line preparation for IHC, cells were washed three times with PBS (Corning, NY, USA), scraped from the bottom of the plate using a cell lifter (Fisherbrand, Waltham, MA, USA), transferred to 50 mL polypropylene tubes and centrifuged at room temperature for 2 min at 500 × g. The supernatant was aspirated, and 10% neutral buffered formalin (Fisher Scientific) was added for 30 min at room temperature. HistoGel (Thermo Scientific) was heated in the microwave for 3 s at maximal power and converted to a liquid state. Cells were transferred to the HistoScreen Tissue Cassettes (Thermo Scientific) and covered with liquid Histogel. Samples were placed at room temperature and allowed to solidify. HistoGel blocks were transferred into tissue embedding cassettes (Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, USA), dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were prepared as described in the immunohistochemistry section. Primary anti-human TSHR antibody was used for 60 min at room temperature at 1:1500 (abcam, ab218108, Cambridge, UK). Envision labeled polymer (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) was used for 30 min as a secondary antibody. Slides were stained with diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) chromogen (Dako) for 5 min at room temperature and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Control staining with hematoxylin and eosin (Sigma-Aldrich) was also performed. Aperio AT2 scanner (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) was used for the digital image, at 20 × objective. Images were analyzed using the Aperio ImageScope software (Aperio Technologies).

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

THJ-529T cells (2 × 104 cells/chamber) were plated in a 4-well chamber slide (Nunc, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), incubated overnight. After 24 h, media was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS (Corning, NY, USA). Then, 2% paraformaldehyde was added for 20 min at room temperature. Again, cells were washed three times with PBS. Next, ice-cold 100% methanol (Fisher Scientific) was added for 7 min at −20 °C. Methanol was aspirated, and samples were allowed to air dry for 10 min. Next, serum-free-blocking diluent (Dako) was used for 30 min at room temperature. Primary anti-human TSHR antibody was used for 60 min at room temperature at 1:1500 (abcam, ab218108, Cambridge, UK). Slides were subsequently prepared as described in the immunohistochemistry section.

In vitro T cell function assays

Cytotoxicity assays were performed as previously described [27, 30–33]. Wildtype (TSHR−) Luc+ FTC-133, TSHR+ Luc+ FTC-133, wildtype (TSHR−) Luc+ THJ-529T, or TSHR+ Luc+ THJ-529T cells were used as target cells. UTD or TSHR-CART cells were co-cultured with target cells at various effector: target (E:T) ratios in T cell medium. Cytotoxicity was calculated by bioluminescence imaging on the Promega GloMax Explorer (Promega Corporation, Fitchburg, WI, USA) as a measure of residual live cells at indicated time points. Samples were treated with 1µL D-luciferin (30 µg/mL) per 100µL sample volume (Gold Biotechnology, St. Louis, MO, USA) prior to imaging. In vitro cytotoxicity assays were conducted exclusively using immortalized cell lines. Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor specimens were not used for these assays, as they cannot be reliably maintained or expanded in conventional in vitro culture systems and are therefore utilized only for in vivo studies.

Proliferation assays were performed utilizing two approaches as previously described [27, 30–33]. Briefly, for the first approach, TSHR-CART cells were stained with CFSE (Invitrogen) cell dye on day 0, then washed and counted prior to being plated at a 1:1 E:T ratio with target cell lines. Each assay included T cells with media only as a negative control and T cells with PMA and ionomycin as a positive control. On day 5, percent of CFSE CART cells was determined via flow cytometry after staining with APC-H7 anti-human CD3 (BD Biosciences) and Zombie R718™ (BioLegend) dye. Alternatively, the same assay was repeated but without the addition of CFSE and with LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Aqua as the viability dye.

For in vitro experiments testing the cytotoxic and proliferation effects of TSHR-CART cells exposed to different levels of TSHR on target cells, TSHR-CART cells were co-cultured with FTC-133 tumor cells expressing different levels of TSHR and cytotoxicity and proliferation were measured as described above.

For cytokine assays, media was collected on day 3 of co-culture of TSHR-CART cells with target cells, and supernatants were isolated and purified via centrifugation as previously described [30]. Supernatants were prepared following manufacturer’s protocol for the MILLIPLEX® Human Cytokine/Chemokine/Growth Factor Panel A 38 Plex Premixed Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore Sigma, Ontario, Canada). Cytokine data was collected using a Luminex LX200 (Millipore Sigma).

For degranulation assays [27, 30], UTD or TSHR-CART cells were incubated with various target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:5, and antibodies against CD107a FITC (BD Biosciences), CD28 (BD Biosciences), and CD49d (BD Biosciences), as well as monensin (BioLegend), were added at the start of the incubation. After 4 h, cells were harvested and stained with LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Aqua. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized (FIX & PERM Cell Fixation & Cell Permeabilization Kit, Life Technologies, Oslo, Norway) and stained for CD3 APC (, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and intracellular cytokines including IL-2 PE CF594 (BD Horizon), GM-SCF BV421 (BD Horizon) and MIP-1β Pe-Cy7 (BD Pharmingen).

For in vitro assays performed to assess the impact of MAPK inhibitors on TSHR-CART cells, TSHR-CART cells were cultured with target tumor cells or media alone, along with either vehicle control (DMSO), trametinib (10 nM) alone, or trametinib plus dabrafenib (10 nM). Effector functions of TSHR-CART cells (proliferation, cytotoxicity, degranulation and cytokine profile) were assessed as described above. Alternatively, TSHR-CART cells were cocultured in an in vitro model for CART cell exhaustion previously described by our laboratory [34]. Here, TSHR-CART cells were cultured at a 1:1 ratio with irradiated target cells along with either vehicle control (DMSO), trametinib (10 nM) alone, or trametinib plus dabrafenib (10 nM). Drug-containing media and irradiated target cells were added to the TSHR-CART cell cocultures every other day for 7 days. Then, chronically stimulated TSHR-CART cells were isolated using CD4 (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA, USA) and CD8 (Miltenyi Biotec) microbeads and were subsequently plated with fresh, non-irradiated target cells to assess cytotoxic and proliferative capacities as described above.

In vivo models

Male and female 6–8-week-old NSG mice were purchased (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and maintained in an animal barrier space that is approved by the institutional Biosafety Committee for BSL2 + level experiments at the Mayo Clinic animal facility (IBC #HIP00001516.06). Mice were treated on an IACUC-approved protocol (A00001767). Supplemental Table 1 lists the cell lines and PDX models used in this manuscript, their histotype, and mutations.

XTC.UC1 model

The purpose of this model was to test initial TSHR-CART activity in a thyroid cancer model which retains endogenous TSHR expression in vivo. XTC.UC1 is a Hurtle cell carcinoma cell line which retains TSHR expression when passaged in mice but not in vitro. 1 × 107 XTC.UC1 cells were suspended in a 50% Matrigel basement medium and subcutaneously injected into the rear flank of NSG mice. Serial caliper measurements were performed to confirm tumor engraftment. Mice were then randomized based on tumor burden to receive 1× 107 UTD or TSHR-CART. Tumor growth was monitored by serial caliper measurements (width x height x length × 0.523 = mm3). Mice were euthanized once IACUC-approved endpoint criteria were met, and remaining tumors were collected for IHC as indicated in each specific experiment.

TSHR+ THJ-529T model

The purpose of this model was to assess a TSHR-CART dose escalation. TSHR+ THJ-529T is a poorly differentiated thyroid cancer cell line made to overexpress TSHR. 2.5 × 106 TSHR+ THJ-529T cells were suspended in a 50% Matrigel basement medium and subcutaneously injected into the rear flank of NSG mice. Serial caliper measurements were performed to confirm tumor engraftment. Mice were then randomized based on tumor burden to receive 5× 106 UTD, 5 × 106 TSHR-CART, or 1 × 107 TSHR-CART. Tumor growth was monitored by serial caliper measurements (width x height x length × 0.523 = mm3). Mice were euthanized once IACUC-approved endpoint criteria were met, and remaining tumors were collected for IHC as indicated in each specific experiment.

THJ-560 model

The purpose of this model was to assess TSHR upregulation or TSHR-CART activity after MAPKi administration. This model reflects the strategy of TSHR-CART and MAPK inhibitor combination to be used in patients with ATC. THJ-560 is an ATC PDX with downregulated TSHR expression. All NSG mice were surgically engrafted with 5mm3 ATC PDX THJ-560 tumors in the rear flank. In TSHR upregulation experiments, mice were treated with the indicated doses of MEKi/BRAFi (trametinib, dabrafenib, or RO5126766). Upon euthanasia, tumors were harvested for IHC.

In the MAPK inhibitor and TSHR-CART combination experiments, upon tumor engraftment, mice were treated with a sub-therapeutic dose of 1 mg/kg trametinib and 1.5 mg/kg dabrafenib as a sensitizing regimen to induce TSHR expression on ATC tumor cells. On Day 7, additional mice were surgically engrafted with 5mm3 ATC PDX tumors in the same manner as the first arm but were treated with placebo rather than MAPK inhibitors. In the first MAPK inhibitor and TSHR-CART combination experiment, in which mice were treated sequentially or concurrently with MEKi/BRAFi and TSHR-CART cells, all mice were randomized based on tumor burden when tumors reached ~ 100mm3 and injected with 2 × 107 UTD or TSHR-CART; this higher TSHR-CART dose was a further dose escalation experiment. In the second MAPK inhibitor and TSHR-CART combination experiment, in which mice were treated concurrently with MEKi/BRAFi and TSHR-CART cells with various MEKi/BRAFi treatment stopping points, all mice were randomized based on tumor burden when tumors reached ~ 100mm3 and injected with 1 × 107 UTD or TSHR-CART. This lower dose of TSHR-CART was used since no further treatment benefit was observed with the higher 2 × 107 dose. For all mice in the MAPK inhibitor and TSHR-CART combination experiments, tumor growth was monitored by serial caliper measurements (width x height x length × 0.523 = mm3). Weekly tail vein bleeding after injection of CART cells was performed to assess T cell expansion. Mouse peripheral blood was lysed using BD FACS Lyse (BD Biosciences) and then analyzed with flow cytometry as previously described [27]. Mice were euthanized at IACUC-approved endpoints.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) were used to analyze raw experimental data. Statistical tests are described in figure legends.

Results

Dedifferentiation limits CART cell targeting by suppressing lineage antigen expression

First, we assessed TSHR expression in different thyroid cancer histologies. IHC analysis showed that, while TSHR expression was high in normal thyroid tissue as well as multiple thyroid cancer histotypes, anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) demonstrated attenuated TSHR expression (Fig. 1A, B). We further measured TSHR expression in a healthy tissue microarray and confirmed that TSHR expression was high in the thyroid and low or absent in other tissues including adult thymus and testis (Supplemental Fig. S2A-B), consistent with publicly available databases. Having validated TSHR as a rational choice for a CAR target, we then aimed to determine how TSHR-CART cells are able to exert their functions against different types of thyroid cancer with variable TSHR expression. We generated TSHR-CART cells with an anti-TSHR scFv derived from autoantibody clone K1-70 linked to a CD8α hinge and transmembrane domain, 4-1BB costimulatory domain, and CD3z signaling domain using established methods in our laboratory (Supplemental Fig. S1A-B) [27]. We then confirmed effective CAR transduction efficiency without significant differences in untransduced T cell (UTD) vs TSHR-CART phenotype (Supplemental Fig. S1C-D). Since thyroid cancer cell lines are known to downregulate TSHR in extended monolayer cell culture [36–39], we used both cell lines and PDX models to study TSHR-CART activity. In vitro, we utilized the follicular thyroid cancer cell line, FTC-133, and the BRAF-mutant, patient-derived PDTC cell line, THJ-529T, which were transduced to overexpress TSHR.

While TSHR-CART cells were not activated against wildtype (TSHR−) FTC-133 cells, TSHR-CART cells demonstrated profound antigen-specific effector functions against TSHR+ target cells in vitro, including cytotoxicity against target cells (Fig. 1C) as well as antigen-specific proliferation (Fig. 1D-E, Supplemental Fig. S3A), degranulation (Fig. 1F), and cytokine production (Fig. 1G-I, INF-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 shown individually, and Fig. 1J, all cytokines combined). Similar results were found for wildtype (TSHR−) and TSHR+ THJ-529T, a PDTC cell line (Supplemental Fig. S3B-H). Similarly, TSHR-CART cells led to a potent, dose-dependent antitumor activity in xenograft models engrafted with THJ-529T cells. Non-obese diabetic severe combined immunodeficiency IL2rγ−/− (NSG) mice were subcutaneously injected with 2.5 × 106 THJ-529T cells, and engraftment was confirmed by caliper measurement of tumor masses. When tumors reached ~ 100mm3, mice were randomized to treatment with 5 × 106 UTD, 5 × 106 TSHR-CART cells, or 1 × 107 TSHR-CART cells (Fig. 2A). Treatment with TSHR-CART cells led to significant and dose-dependent tumor control (Fig. 2B-E, Supplemental Fig. S4A-C), improved survival outcomes (Fig. 2F, Supplemental Fig. S4D), increased tumor-infiltrated human T cells (Fig. 2G), and increased human T cell proliferation in the peripheral blood (Supplemental Fig. S4E). Having shown that the 1 × 107 TSHR-CART dose yielded superior antitumor activity, we selected this dose level for future experiments. To study the activity of CART cell therapy in clinically relevant models for thyroid cancer, we utilized xenograft models of oncocytic thyroid cancer [previously known as Hurthle cell carcinoma (HCC)] as a model for TSHRhigh and ATC as a model for TSHRlow tumors. First, we measured TSHR expression via IHC on biopsies obtained from patients with normal thyroid tissue, primary HCC with minimal or extensive invasion, locally recurrent HCC, and metastatic HCC. Strong TSHR expression was maintained in recurrent and metastatic lesions as well as primary tumors (Supplemental Fig. S5A-B). We then assessed the in vivo activity of TSHR-CART cells against the HCC cell line, XTC.UC1. NSG mice were subcutaneously engrafted with 1 × 107 XTC.UC1, which retains high endogenous TSHR expression in vivo, and treated with 1 × 107 UTD or TSHR-CART cells when tumors reached ~ 100mm3. Mice in remission were rechallenged with 1 × 107 XTC.UC1 cells subcutaneously in the opposite flank (Fig. 3A). Serial caliper measurements revealed significant antitumor efficacy of TSHR-CART cells compared to UTD control; by Day 50 post-engraftment, all UTD-treated mice had reached humane endpoints, while all TSHR-CART-treated mice were in remission or had small tumors (Fig. 3B-C). Additionally, IHC analysis of harvested tumors demonstrated reduced TSHR expression following TSHR-CART treatment, consistent with effective elimination of TSHR+ tumor cells (Fig. 3D) and increased CD3+ T cell infiltration withing the tumors (Fig. 3E).These findings are consistent with effective tumor elimination by TSHR-CART cells, as loss of TSHR indicates depletion of tumor cells, while increased CD3 staining reflects accumulation of CAR-T cells at the tumor site. CART cell efficacy is governed by target antigen density

Having demonstrated that TSHR-CART cell therapy is highly potent against TSHR+ tumors but not as effective against ATC where TSHR is downregulated, we next aimed to assess the sensitivity of TSHR-CART cells to low expression levels of TSHR. We generated a series of TSHR+ FTC-133 cells with varied levels of TSHR expression. TSHR+ FTC-133 cells had TSHR levels ranging from ~ 40,000 to 300,000 TSHR per cell, compared to ~ 6,000 TSHR per cell for our ATC PDX model, THJ-560, and ~ 1,000 TSHR per cell for wildtype FTC-133 (Fig. 4A-B). TSHR-CART cells demonstrated potent cytotoxicity against all TSHR+ FTC-133 cell lines, with only the wildtype FTC-133 cells showing resistance to TSHR-CART cell killing (Fig. 4C). These findings indicated that TSHR upregulation may lead to robust antitumor activity of TSHR-CART cells.

Downregulated TSHR ATC is restored with MAPK inhibition

Our data indicate that ATC significantly downregulates TSHR expression compared to normal thyroid tissues or differentiated thyroid cancers (Fig. 1A, B), and TSHR downregulation limits TSHR-CART cell activity (Fig. 3G). We therefore tested MAPK inhibition as a strategy to restore TSHR downregulation. We first verified that the MEK inhibitor (MEKi), trametinib, restored TSHR expression in an ATC PDX proof-of-concept model. In this model, 25mm3 THJ-560 ATC PDX tissue was surgically implanted subcutaneously in NSG mice. When tumors reached approximately 100mm3, mice were randomized to daily treatment with either vehicle control or MAPK inhibitors at the indicated doses until the experimental endpoint (Fig. 4D). The administration of MEKi resulted in significant dose-dependent slowing of tumor growth (Fig. 4E) as well as TSHR re-expression which inversely correlated with pERK expression (Fig. 4F) after one week of daily trametinib treatment compared to placebo treatment. Because many ATC tumors are BRAF-mutant, [42–44] we performed a similar experiment in which mice received either vehicle control or the dual MEKi/BRAF inhibitor (BRAFi) RO5126766 at the indicated doses. Treatment with RO5126766 resulted in a significant dose-dependent slowing of tumor growth (Fig. 4G), and pERK expression inversely correlated with TSHR expression (Fig. 4H) after one week of daily RO5126766 treatment compared to placebo treatment. Similarly, treatment with combination of MEKi (trametinib) and BRAFi (dabrafenib) resulted in potent antitumor activity, increased TSHR expression, and decreased in pERK expression, in our ATC PDX model (Fig. 4I-K). While modest antitumor effects of MEKi and/or BRAFi were observed, the main purpose of these experiments was to assess TSHR upregulation upon MEKi and/or BRAFi treatment in PDX models of ATC. Collectively, these data support the therapeutic strategy of MEKi and BRAFi treatment to re-express TSHR and thereby reduce ATC resistance to TSHR-CART cell therapy.

We then tested if pretreatment with MEKi or MEKi/BRAFi enhanced TSHR-CART cell activity against dedifferentiated thyroid cancer cell lines in vitro. The PDTC cell line, THJ-529T, was cocultured with trametinib alone or trametinib plus dabrafenib (10 nM or 1 µM for trametinib and dabrafenib). After four days, THJ-529T cells were washed and assessed for TSHR expression by flow cytometry. THJ-529T cells demonstrated modest but consistently increased TSHR expression after treatment with MEKi or MEKi/BRAFi compared to vehicle-treated cells (Supplemental Fig. S6A). THJ-529T cells were then washed to remove residual MAPK inhibitor and plated with TSHR-CART cells for five additional days, at which point TSHR-CART cell proliferation was assessed via flow cytometry. TSHR-CART cells demonstrated more proliferation in response to THJ-529T cells which were pretreated with trametinib or trametinib plus dabrafenib compared to vehicle control-treated THJ-529 T cells (Supplemental Fig. S6B). Treatment with higher or more prolonged MAPK inhibitor doses to further increase TSHR expression resulted in tumor cell death to the point where post-coculture analyses could not be conducted; however, the modest TSHR upregulation observed with low doses of MAPK inhibitors resulted in increased TSHR-CART response.

MAPK inhibition does not negatively impact TSHR-CART cell activity

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate MEK and BRAF inhibition as a strategy to upregulate TSHR expression on thyroid cancer cells and increase the therapeutic index of TSHR-CART cell therapy. Given that T cells are shown to utilize MAPK signaling, we first tested for potential detrimental effects of MEKi or MEKi/BRAFi on CART cell activity. TSHR-CART proliferation (Fig. 5A), killing (Fig. 5B), degranulation (Supplemental Fig. S7A), and intracellular cytokine production (Supplemental Fig. 7SB) were not affected by the addition of trametinib alone or trametinib plus dabrafenib compared to media control. We next assessed the effects of prolonged MAPK inhibition on TSHR-CART function through an exhaustion assay developed with repeated CART activation [34]. Here, TSHR-CART cells were repeatedly stimulated with irradiated TSHR+ FTC-133 cells along with vehicle control, 10 nM trametinib, or 10 nM trametinib plus 10 nM dabrafenib. After one week of chronic stimulation, TSHR-CART cells were isolated and cocultured with fresh TSHR+ FTC-133 cells and vehicle control, 10 nM trametinib, or 10 nM trametinib plus dabrafenib for proliferation and cytotoxicity assays (Supplemental Fig. S7C). Here as well, the addition of MAPK inhibitors did not appear to negatively impact TSHR-CART cell proliferation (Fig. 5C, Supplemental Fig. S7D) or killing (Fig. 5D).

Combination of TSHR-CART cells plus MAPK inhibitors yields superior TSHR-CART cell activity

Having shown that the addition of MAPK inhibitors was not detrimental to CART function, we next studied the kinetics of TSHR expression after MEKi or MEKi/BRAFi to determine the longevity of TSHR re-expression after treatment with MAPK inhibitors. Mice were implanted with 25mm3 THJ-560 ATC PDX tumors. When tumors reached ~ 100mm3, mice were treated daily with either vehicle control or with 1.5 mg/kg dual MEKi/BRAFi (RO5126766) plus 1 mg/kg BRAFi (dabrafenib). TSHR expression was assessed by IHC and was found to be significantly elevated after 7 days of MAPK inhibitor treatment compared to vehicle control. However, TSHR expression was subsequently lost within two days after stopping MAPK inhibitor treatment (Supplemental Fig. S8A-B).

We next assessed how long MAPK inhibitors needed to be administered concurrently with TSHR-CART cell therapy for enhanced antitumor efficacy. Here, mice treated with UTD or TSHR-CART cells were given vehicle control, MEKi/BRAFi for two weeks after CART administration, MEKi/BRAFi for four weeks after CART administration, or continuous MEKi/BRAFi after CART administration until experimental endpoints were reached (Fig. 6D). Mice receiving the combination of TSHR-CART and MAPK inhibitors showed superior tumor control and survival, regardless of the duration of concurrent MAPK inhibitors (Fig. 6E-F, Supplemental Fig. S11A-F). This indicates that using MAPK inhibitors in combination with TSHR CART cells for 2 weeks is sufficient to induce TSHR upregulation and potent TSHR activity. Peripheral blood analysis at day 14 post-CART injection showed comparable human T cell expansion in mice treated with TSHR-CART cells plus vehicle control compared to mice treated with TSHR-CART cells plus MAPK inhibitors (Fig. 6G). In the residual tumor following treatment TSHR-CART combination with MEKi/BRAFi, there was increased vascular remodeling, and reduced tumor apoptosis (Supplemental Fig. S12A-D). TSHR expression (assessed via IHC staining of tumors) was low in the control group (UTD + vehicle) in this undifferentiated model and further reduced after TSHR-CART treatment (Fig. 6H-I). However, TSHR expression was markedly upregulated by MEKi/BRAFi (consistent with redifferentiation-driven antigen re-expression). Tumor cells were eradicated in combination group, and thus TSHR expression was not detected, demonstrating on-target elimination of the re-expressing population.

Discussion

A fundamental limitation of CART cell therapy in solid tumors is loss or insufficient expression of target antigens, particularly in dedifferentiated and treatment-refractory disease [45]. In this study, we demonstrate that tumor redifferentiation can be used as a therapeutic lever to restore antigen expression and enable CART cell–mediated tumor control. Using TSHR as a clinically relevant antigen in aggressive thyroid cancer, we show that pharmacologic inhibition of oncogenic MAPK signaling restores target expression and converts CAR-resistant tumors into CAR-responsive disease.

We show that TSHR-CART cell efficacy is governed by target antigen density and that dedifferentiation-associated antigen loss underlies resistance in aggressive disease. While TSHR-CART cells mediate potent and durable antitumor activity in differentiated and TSHRhigh models, efficacy is attenuated in PDTC and ATC, which are characterized by low TSHR expression. These findings reinforce antigen density, not merely antigen presence, as a critical determinant of CART cell functions.

Importantly, we identify pharmacologic redifferentiation as a mechanism-guided solution to this limitation. Increasing evidence indicates that TSHR downregulation and de-differentiation is mediated by MAPK signaling [18, 19, 44, 46, 47]. Inhibition of oncogenic MAPK signaling restores TSHR expression in dedifferentiated and ATC models in a pERK-dependent manner. This effect is observed in patient-derived xenograft models and is achieved using MEK and BRAF inhibitors that are already FDA-approved and routinely deployed in advanced thyroid cancer. Redifferentiation therapy alone yields modest tumor control, but its primary value lies in reprogramming the tumor to re-expose lineage antigens, thereby expanding the therapeutic window for CART cell engagement. In the clinic, dabrafenib in combination with trametinib are FDA-approved for the treatment of metastatic thyroid cancer [48]. Tumor redifferentiation using MAPK pathway inhibitors has been employed to restore NIS expression and re-sensitize radioactive iodine (RAI)-refractory differentiated thyroid cancers to radioiodine treatment [49]. TSHR-CART therapy offers a mechanistically orthogonal strategy; rather than attempting to restore a lost differentiation program, it exploits TSHR as a thyroid lineage-specific surface antigen expressed on > 80% of RAI-refractory lesions to achieve direct cytotoxic tumor elimination regardless of differentiation status [38]. Critically, the two approaches of TSHR-CART and are not mutually exclusive — MAPK inhibitors used for redifferentiation simultaneously upregulate TSHR expression on dedifferentiated tumor cells, providing a mechanistic rationale for potential combination therapy that may overcome the limitations of either modality alone.

Several reports have shown conflicting results regarding the potential impacts of MAPK inhibitors on T cell function [50–57]. A key finding of this work is that redifferentiation-based antigen restoration does not compromise CART cell function. MAPK inhibition did not impair CART cell proliferation, cytotoxicity, cytokine production, or persistence, even under conditions of chronic stimulation. In vivo, concurrent administration of MAPK inhibitors with TSHR-CART cells produced superior tumor control and survival compared with either modality alone, and relatively short courses of redifferentiation therapy were sufficient to achieve durable responses. These data support a treatment paradigm in which transient tumor reprogramming can be synchronized with cellular immunotherapy to maximize efficacy while minimizing toxicity.

Although this study focuses on thyroid cancer, the conceptual implications extend well beyond this disease context. Lineage plasticity and therapy-induced dedifferentiation are increasingly recognized across a broad spectrum of solid tumors, including melanoma, glioma, and epithelial cancers [58–60]. In many of these settings, oncogenic signaling pathways suppress differentiation programs and lineage-restricted antigens that could otherwise serve as effective CAR targets. Our findings suggest that pharmacologic reactivation of these programs may represent a generalizable strategy to expand the repertoire of actionable antigens for cellular immunotherapy.

This work introduces a complementary alternative to prevailing approaches aimed at overcoming antigen loss through increasingly complex CAR engineering strategies, such as dual-targeting, logic-gated receptors, or armored CAR designs [61]. Instead, we demonstrate that reprogramming the tumor itself, using clinically available agents, can restore target expression and re-enable sensitivity to existing CAR platforms. This tumor-intrinsic strategy may be particularly attractive in settings where antigen loss is reversible and driven by epigenetic or signaling-dependent mechanisms rather than irreversible genetic deletion.

From a translational perspective, TSHR represents a uniquely well-suited target for this redifferentiation-based approach. TSHR expression is largely restricted to thyroid tissue, and patients with aggressive thyroid cancers have undergone thyroidectomy, mitigating concerns regarding on-target off-tumor toxicity. Consistent with this, we did not observe treatment-related toxicities in preclinical models, and the scFv used in this CAR construct has demonstrated clinical tolerability in early-phase studies targeting TSHR in non-malignant disease [62, 63]. TSHR-CART cells have also been well tolerated in the few patients that have been treated with this approach to date [64, 65]. We have initiated IND-enabling studies to translate the combination of TSHR-CART cells with MAPK inhibitors into the clinic in a phase I clinical trial. Vigilant monitoring for extra-thyroidal effects will be essential as this strategy advances to the clinic and has been incorporated in our planned phase I clinical trial.

Limitations to our study include that redifferentiation-induced antigen restoration is transient and dependent on continued pathway inhibition, underscoring the importance of optimizing treatment scheduling and sequencing. In addition, not all tumors may retain sufficient plasticity to permit effective reprogramming, and identifying biomarkers that predict redifferentiation responsiveness will be critical for patient selection. Furthermore, it is important to note that our study includes only a single PDX model of ATC, which represents a limitation. This underscores the need for future studies to validate our findings in the planned Phase I clinical trial. Nonetheless, our work incorporates multiple xenograft models derived from diverse thyroid cancer cell lines, which capture key aspects of disease heterogeneity. Together, these models provide complementary evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of our TSHR-CART cells, while also highlighting opportunities for further investigation and validation by the field. We were surprised to observe increased CD31 expression and reduced CC3 expression following MEK/BRAF inhibition in our PDX model. One possible explanation is that treatment effectively eliminated the vast majority of tumor cells, leaving predominantly stromal and fibrotic tissue with relatively greater vascular density and lower levels of apoptosis. Additional studies are ongoing to further characterize the temporal kinetics of redifferentiation and TSHR upregulation following MEK/BRAF inhibition. Finally, while MAPK inhibition is a clinically validated redifferentiation strategy in thyroid cancer, analogous reprogramming approaches will need to be defined and validated for other tumor types.

In summary, this work establishes tumor redifferentiation as a clinically actionable strategy to overcome antigen loss and enable effective CART cell therapy in solid tumors. By pairing lineage-targeted CART cells with pharmacologic reprogramming agents, we provide a framework for restoring tumor antigenicity and converting resistant disease into CAR-responsive states. This target-restoration paradigm offers a broadly applicable and immediately translatable approach to addressing one of the most fundamental barriers to durable cellular immunotherapy.
