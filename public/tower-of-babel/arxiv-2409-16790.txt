Lectures on scattering theory in partial-wave amplitudes
J. A. Oller

Abstract
These lectures treat scattering theory from a non-perturbative point of view. The course begins with a review of formal aspects in scattering theory, discussing the in/out states and the S S matrix that connects them. Unitarity relations, phase space, and the Lippmann-Schwinger equation for the collision operator T T are discussed. The calculation of cross sections, the optical theorem and Boltzmann H H -theorem from unitarity of the S S matrix are also explained. Special emphasis is given in these lectures to expansions in partial-wave amplitudes of two-body scattering amplitudes, both for massive and massless particles, and to unitarity in partial-wave amplitudes. In this way, partial-wave expansions in terms of ℓ ​ S ​ J \ell SJ states, and using helicity states with definite total angular momentum J J are discussed. Crossing symmetry is also explained, and connected to crossed channels, analyticity and crossed-channel cuts in partial-wave amplitudes. Different non-perturbative techniques and general parameterizations are developed for partial-wave amplitudes that satisfy unitarity and are consistent with the analyticity properties that they must have. Then, the N / D N/D method and additional solutions generated by adding CDD poles are covered in detail. The change to different non-physical Riemann sheets and the search for resonant poles is discussed as well. A differentiation is made between dynamically generated resonances by the degrees of freedom explicitly accounted for versus pre-existing resonances derived from short-distance dynamics. We exemplify it with the cases of the σ / f 0 ​ ( 500 ) \sigma/f_{0}(500) and the ρ ⁡ ( 770 ) \rho(770) resonances in π ​ π \pi\pi scattering. The reader is also introduced to final-state interactions, Watson’s theorem, and general parameterizations to take them into account. It ends with an appendix dedicated to the Sugawara-Kanazawa theorem regarding the number of subtractions in dispersion relations.

Brief introduction

We cover some basic topics on scattering theory, emphasizing unitarity and analyticity of the
S
matrix while keeping an eye on phenomenology. These lectures could be useful to those interested in scattering processes that cannot be calculated by using only perturbative techniques in Quantum Mechanics or in Quantum Field Theory. The different concepts here are typically introduced from a nonperturbative point of view. The most formal part of the lectures concern the first two chapters, which are the longest ones. The first one is dedicated to formal topics in the theory of the
S
-matrix, while the second chapter develops in detail the expansion in partial-wave amplitudes of scattering amplitudes and related aspects, like unitarity in partial waves. The third chapter is an introduction to crossing symmetry and its applications. The other chapters are typically of narrower scope, typically more specific, and treat several interesting topics in partial-wave amplitudes: The
N/D
method, CDD poles, unphysical Riemann sheets and resonance poles there, their nature, and final-state interactions. There is an added appendix dedicated to the relatively unknown Sugawara-Kanazawa theorem, which is applicable to many situations of interest in phenomenology. There is a bunch of exercises along the manuscript typically asking the reader to complete some derivations or deduce new results.

These lectures are rather self-contained and could be used with only limited background knowledge on the subject. Books like

A scattering process for a finite-range interaction can be visualized, Fig.

n

t\to-\infty
, that interact and then become a final set of
n^{\prime}

t\to+\infty

|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\lambda\rangle
, where
{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the momentum,
\sigma
is the third component of spin or helicity (more details will be given below in Sec.
2.1
), and
\lambda
comprises the rest of necessary quantum numbers like its mass
m
, its spin
s
, and charges. These monoparticle states have the Lorentz invariant normalization

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=2w(p)(2\pi)^{3}\delta({\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{p}})\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}~,

(1.1)

with

A multiparticle state is made by the direct product of several monoparticle states,

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}\sigma_{2}\lambda_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n}\sigma_{n}\lambda_{b}\rangle

\displaystyle=|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1}\rangle\otimes|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}\sigma_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n}\sigma_{n}\lambda_{n}\rangle~.

(1.2)

\bullet

Scattering states, in and out states, and the Möller operators:

Formally speaking, let
H
be the Hamiltonian of the system split as
H=H_{0}+H^{\prime}
, where
H_{0}
is the free part (kinetic energy) and
H^{\prime}
is the interaction Hamiltonian. The spectrum of the theory comprises the discrete spectrum
\{\Psi_{n},E_{n}\}
,

\displaystyle\text{Discrete spectrum:~}H|\Psi_{n}\rangle=E_{n}|\Psi_{n}\rangle~,

(1.3)

and the continuum one that consists of the scattering states. For a given energy there are two types of scattering states: The in states,
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle
, and the out ones,
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{+}\rangle
, where the index
\alpha
is continuum:

\displaystyle\text{Continuum spectrum:~}H|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle=E_{\alpha}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle~,

(1.4)

There are two types of scattering states because the in states have an outgoing spherical wave component, while for the out ones the spherical wave component is incoming, as we show below in more detail, cf. Eqs. (
1.44
) and (
1.45
), and the discussions around them. The scattering states are in one-to-one correspondence with the free multiparticle states made out by plane waves (already introduced), and generically denoted by
|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle
. The relation between the in/out states with the plane-wave states is established by the Möller operators,
\Omega_{\pm}
, as

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\pm}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

(1.5)

The index
\alpha
is then the set of quantum numbers used to characterize the free multiparticle states, cf. Eq. (
1.2
). Being more specific, let us introduce the interaction or Dirac picture and obtain a formal expression for the Möller operators. Indicating by the subscripts
S
and
I
the Schrödinger the interaction pictures, respectively, we have

\displaystyle|\Psi_{I},t\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{0}t}|\Psi_{S},t\rangle=e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH(t-t_{0})}|\Psi_{S},t_{0}\rangle=e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH(t-t_{0})}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}|\Psi_{I},t_{0}\rangle~.

(1.6)

From here, it follows that the evolution operator in the interaction picture is

\displaystyle U_{I}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{0}t}e^{-iH(t-t_{0})}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}~.

(1.7)

Now, advocating the adiabatic approximation, with
H_{I}^{\prime}(t)\to e^{-\epsilon|t|}H^{\prime}_{I}(t)
and
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, we then have that

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{0}\to-\infty}U_{I}(0,t_{0})|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=\lim_{t_{0}\to-\infty}e^{iHt_{0}}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~,

(1.8)

\displaystyle H|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{0}\to-\infty}e^{iHt_{0}}He^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=\lim_{t_{0}\to-\infty}e^{iHt_{0}}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}(H_{0}+e^{-\epsilon|t_{0}|}H^{\prime}_{I}(t_{0}))|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=E_{\alpha}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle\,,

where we have used that
H_{0}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=E_{\alpha}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle
. By an analogous reasoning,

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{0}\to+\infty}U_{I}(0,t_{0})|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=\lim_{t_{0}\to+\infty}e^{iHt_{0}}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~,

(1.9)

\displaystyle H|\Psi_{\alpha}^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=E_{\alpha}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{+}\rangle\,.

\displaystyle\Omega(t_{0})=e^{iHt_{0}}e^{-iH_{0}t_{0}}~.

(1.10)

S
-matrix elements, and the
S
-matrix operator:

The
S
-matrix element
S_{\beta\alpha}
is

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\beta}^{+}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle~,

(1.12)

the probability amplitude between the considered in and out states. In terms of the Möller operators, Eq. (
1.5
), and the evolution operator in the interaction picture, Eqs. (
1.7
) and (
1.11
), it reads

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{\beta}|\Omega_{+}^{\dagger}\Omega_{-}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~,

(1.13)

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{l}t_{2}\to+\infty\\
t_{1}\to-\infty\end{array}}}\langle\Phi_{\beta}|e^{iH_{0}t_{2}}e^{-iHt_{2}}e^{iHt_{1}}e^{-iH_{0}t_{1}}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=\langle\Phi_{\beta}|U_{I}(+\infty,-\infty)|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

Therefore, the
S
-matrix operator acting on plane-wave states is

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\Omega_{+}^{\dagger}\Omega_{-}=U_{I}(+\infty,-\infty)\,,

(1.16)

and it is unitarity

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}S

\displaystyle=SS^{\dagger}=I\,.

(1.17)

\bullet

Expression in perturbation theory:

The evolution operator
U_{I}(t,t_{0})
, Eq. (
1.7
), satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}U_{I}(t,t_{0})=H^{\prime}_{I}(t)U_{I}(t,t_{0})

(1.18)

Its solution in the Neumann series is

\displaystyle U_{I}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle=I+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{2}\cdots\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{n}T\left[H^{\prime}_{I}(t_{1})H^{\prime}_{I}(t_{2})\cdots H_{I}^{\prime}(t_{n})\right]

(1.19)

\displaystyle=T\exp\left(-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}H^{\prime}_{I}(t^{\prime})\right)~,

where
T
indicates time ordering the operators
H_{I}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
in each term of the series expansion. Then, for the
S
-matrix element in Eq. (
1
) we can also write

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{\beta}|T\exp\left(-i\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt^{\prime}H^{\prime}_{I}(t^{\prime})\right)|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

(1.20)

Denoting by
{\cal H}^{\prime}(x)
the Hamiltonian density for the interacting part, with
x=(x^{0},{\boldsymbol{x}})
, we then have

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{\beta}|T\exp\left(-i\int d^{4}x{\cal H}^{\prime}_{I}(x)\right)|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

(1.21)

This expression is actually covariant because it is assumed that
[{\cal H}^{\prime}_{I}(x),{\cal H}^{\prime}_{I}(y)]=0
for
(x-y)^{2}<0

[
3
]
. This is also referred as local commutativity of the interacting Hamiltonian densities. Therefore, a change of frame does not modify the
T
product.

\bullet

T
-matrix operator, unitarity relations, and phase space:

The
T
-matrix operator is defined as

\displaystyle S=I+iT~.

(1.22)

Since
S
is an unitary operator this implies two unitarity relations for
T
:

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}S

\displaystyle=I~\rightarrow T-T^{\dagger}=iT^{\dagger}T~,

(1.23)

\displaystyle SS^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=I~\rightarrow T-T^{\dagger}=iTT^{\dagger}~.

(1.24)

Due to space-time translation symmetry the
S
- and
T
-matrix elements contain a global four Dirac delta function of conversation of energy and momentum. In the following, we factorize it out and keep in mind that the initial and final momenta must be the same. Namely,

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\to(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})S_{\beta\alpha}~,

(1.25)

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\to(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})T_{\beta\alpha}~.

In the unitarity relation of Eq. (
1.23
) we introduce in the right-hand side a decomposition of the identity by summing over the
m_{th}
intermediate states (or channels) containing
n_{m}
free particles. Then, it becomes

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha}-T^{\dagger}_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{m}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{m}}\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i})}\sum_{\sigma_{i},\lambda_{i}}\right)(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{T}-P_{m})\langle\Phi_{\beta}|T^{\dagger}|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}\rangle

(1.26)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}|T|\Phi_{i}\rangle~,

where
P_{T}=P_{\beta}=P_{i}
, with
P_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}
. Notice that in order to have a nonvanishing contribution from the right-hand side in the previous equation the total energy
E=P_{T}^{0}>s_{\rm th,1}
, where
s_{\rm th,1}
is the smallest threshold for the intermediate channels (we can think that they have been ordered with increasing thresholds).

The
3n_{m}-4
infinitesimal element of phase space is defined as

\displaystyle dQ_{m}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{m}}\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i})}\sum_{\sigma_{i},\lambda_{i}}\right)(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{T}-P_{m})~,

(1.27)

with four integrations done to remove the delta of conservation of total energy-momentum.

In the following, the sum in Eq. (

. In terms of phase space the unitarity relations in (

) reads

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha}-T^{\dagger}_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=i\sumint dQ_{m}\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}|T|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle^{*}\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}|T|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

(1.28)

\bullet

Hermitian unitarity, the Lippmann-Schwinger equation, and spherical waves in the scattering states:

The point of Hermitian unitarity is to show that
T^{\dagger}(E)=T(E^{*})
, with
E
the total energy. Before proceeding with its demonstration, when this fact is taken into Eq. (
1.28
) it implies that (we now explicitly show the argument
E
with a slight imaginary part
\pm i\epsilon
)

\displaystyle T_{\beta\alpha}(E+i\epsilon)-T_{\beta\alpha}(E-i\epsilon)

\displaystyle=i\sumint dQ_{m}\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}|T(E+i\epsilon)|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle^{*}

(1.29)

\displaystyle\times\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n_{m}}\sigma_{n_{m}}\lambda_{n_{m}}|T(E+i\epsilon)|\Phi_{i}\rangle~.

Then,
T_{\beta\alpha}(E)
has a right hand cut for
E>s_{\rm th,1}
, with its discontinuity given by the right-hand side of Eq. (
1.29
). This is a crucial point in
S
-matrix theory in which one takes the singularities in the
T
-matrix elements as due to intermediate physical states. There could be other so-called kinematical singularities for particles with spin, associated to the known properties of these particle states with spin under Lorentz transformations.

To proceed with its demonstration we first introduce the Lippmann-Schwinger equation for the scattering states
|\Psi^{\pm}_{\alpha}\rangle
. With
V\equiv H^{\prime}
, we rewrite Eq. (
1.4
) as

\displaystyle(H_{0}-E_{\alpha})|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle

\displaystyle=-V|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle~,

(1.30)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle-(H_{0}-E_{\alpha})^{-1}V|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle~.

(1.31)

This equation is not really meaningful because
H_{0}-E_{\alpha}
has no inverse for real positive
E_{\alpha}>s_{\rm th,1}
, being zero when acting over
|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle
. To make it meaningful we add a vanishing imaginary part
\pm i\epsilon
to
E_{\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle+(E_{\alpha}\mp i\epsilon-H_{0})^{-1}V|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle~.

(1.32)

To check this equation let us show that a wave packet of states
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}\rangle
behaves for
t\to\pm\infty
as a wave packet of free states, respectively
[
3
]
. Let
g(\alpha)
be a smooth function over some finite range
\Delta E
, and introduce a resolution of the identity in terms of plane waves
|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle
in between
(E_{\alpha}\mp i\epsilon-H_{0})
and
V
in Eq. (
1.32
). Then,

\displaystyle\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Psi^{\pm}_{\alpha}\rangle\!

\displaystyle=\!\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle+\int\!\!d\alpha\int\!\!d\beta g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}(E_{\alpha}\mp i\epsilon-E_{\beta})^{-1}\langle\Phi_{\beta}|V|\Psi^{\pm}_{\alpha}\rangle|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle\,.

(1.33)

The rapid oscillation of the exponential
e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}
for
t\to\pm\infty
in the second term on the right-hand side of the previous equations kills all contributions to this integral except for
E_{\alpha}
near
E_{\beta}
. This contribution can be evaluated by employing the Cauchy theorem for complex integration. For
t\to\rightarrow\pm\infty
the exponential factor
e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}
allows to close the integration contour in the complex
E_{\alpha}
plane with a semicircle at infinity running over its lower/upper half complex plane, respectively. For definiteness, consider
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{+}\rangle
with
t\to+\infty
. Then, the pole contribution at
E_{\alpha}=E_{\beta}+i\epsilon
lies outside the integration contour and the integral vanishes. Similarly, for
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle
we have the pole contribution at
E_{\alpha}=E_{\beta}-i\epsilon
, which again lies outside the integration contour and it gives no contribution.

The
T
-matrix operators can also be introduced by the relation

\displaystyle-V|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=-V\Omega_{-}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle=T|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle~.

(1.34)

To connect this definition with Eq. (
1.22
), let us consider what an in state looks at later times
t\to+\infty
. Again, we pick up only the contribution from
E_{\alpha}\approx E_{\beta}
in the double second integral term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
1.33
). But now, the pole at
E_{\alpha}=E_{\beta}-i\epsilon
for the
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle
case gives contribution because it lies within the integration contour closed by a semicircle at infinite along the lower half of the complex
E_{\alpha}
plane. The result of the integral is
-2\pi i
(the integration is closed in the clockwise sense) times the rest of the integrand (once excluded
(E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}-i\epsilon)^{-1}
):

\displaystyle\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Psi^{-}_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle-2\pi i\int d\alpha\int d\beta\delta(E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta})g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}\langle\Phi_{\beta}|V|\Psi^{-}_{\alpha}\rangle|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle+2\pi i\int d\alpha\int d\beta\delta(E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta})g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}\langle\Phi_{\beta}|T|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle~.

(1.35)

Now, according to Eq. (
1.12
), we can expand
|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle
in terms of out states
|\Psi_{\beta}^{+}\rangle
as (in Eq. (
1.12
) the total energy-momentum Dirac delta function was not factorized yet, and so we do along this discussion)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\beta S_{\beta\alpha}|\Psi_{\beta}^{+}\rangle~.

(1.36)

Upon multiplying by
g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}
and integrating,

\displaystyle\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\int d\beta g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}S_{\beta\alpha}|\Psi_{\beta}^{+}\rangle\,,

(1.37)

Next, we take into account that
S_{\beta\alpha}
is nonzero only for
E_{\alpha}=E_{\beta}
and make
t\to+\infty
, so that we can rewrite the term on the right-hand side as

\displaystyle\int d\beta e^{-iE_{\beta}t}|\Psi_{\beta}^{+}\rangle\int d\alpha g(\alpha)S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to+\infty]{}\int d\alpha\int d\beta g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\beta}t}S_{\beta\alpha}|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\int d\alpha g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}|\Psi_{\alpha}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to+\infty]{}\int d\alpha\int d\beta g(\alpha)e^{-iE_{\alpha}t}S_{\beta\alpha}|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle\,.

(1.38)

Comparing this result with the right-hand side of Eq. (
1.35
) we then conclude that

\displaystyle S_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\delta(\beta-\alpha)+2\pi i\delta(E_{\beta}-E_{\alpha})T_{\beta\alpha}~.

(1.39)

By homogeneity of space the matrix element
T_{\beta\alpha}
is also proportional to a linear momentum Dirac delta function
\delta(\bm{P}_{\beta}-\bm{P}_{\alpha})
. Once this is factorized out, we end with Eq. (
1.22
).

Cross section, and the optical theorem:

The cross section
\sigma_{\beta\alpha}
is defined as the number of transition events per unite time from the initial two-particle state
|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}\sigma_{1}\lambda_{1},{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}\sigma_{2}\lambda_{2}\rangle
to the final multiparticle state
|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle
. This rate is further normalized by the incident flux
\Phi
times the number of targets.

The number of transitions
\alpha\to\beta
is

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\beta}}\left(\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i})}\sum_{\sigma_{i},\lambda_{i}}\right)|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}\left[(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})\right]^{2}\,.

(1.46)

There is a four-Dirac delta function squared, which requires a proper interpretation. For that we take its Fourier representation

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}xe^{-ix(P_{\alpha}-P_{\beta})}\xrightarrow[P_{\beta}=P_{\alpha}]{}{\cal V}{\cal T}~,

(1.47)

with
{\cal V}
and
{\cal T}
the total volume and time, respectively, to be sent to infinity. The infinite factor
{\cal T}
cancel because we want the number of transitions per unite time.

Regarding the incident flux and number of targets we have to take into account the normalization of the monoparticle states, Eq. (
1.1
), for
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{p}}
:
\parallel|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\lambda\rangle\parallel^{2}={\cal V}2w(p)
. Thus, the number of targets is
2w(p_{2}){\cal V}
and the flux of projectiles
\Phi=(\text{relative velocity})\times(\text{density of projectiles})
is

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=v_{\rm rel}2w(p_{1})=\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}}{w(p_{1})}-\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}}{w(p_{2})}\right|2w(p_{1})~,

(1.48)

with
w(p_{i})=\sqrt{m_{i}^{2}+{\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}^{2}}
.
As a result, the differential cross section is

\displaystyle d\sigma_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\int\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\beta}}\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i})}\sum_{\sigma_{i},\lambda_{i}}\right)(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}}{v_{\rm rel}4w(p_{1})w(p_{2})}=dQ_{\beta}\frac{|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}}{4w(p_{1})w(p_{2})v_{\rm rel}}~.

(1.49)

In the center of mass (CM) frame,
{\boldsymbol{p}}={\boldsymbol{p}}_{1}=-{\boldsymbol{p}}_{2}
and the relative velocity is

\displaystyle v_{\rm rel}

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\boldsymbol{p}}|(w(p_{1})+w(p_{2}))}{w(p_{1})w(p_{2})}~.

(1.50)

In this frame Eq. (
1.49
) becomes

\displaystyle d\sigma_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\beta}}\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i})}(2\pi)^{4}\delta(P_{\beta}-P_{\alpha})|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}}{v_{\rm rel}4w(p_{1})w(p_{2})}=dQ_{\beta}\frac{|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}}{4(w(p_{1})+w(p_{2}))|{\boldsymbol{p}}|}~.

(1.51)

The total cross sections is the full integration of the differential cross section

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sumint dQ_{\beta}\frac{d\sigma_{\beta\alpha}}{dQ_{\beta}}

(1.52)

where the sum is over all the possible types of final states or outgoing channels.

Let us consider the two equivalent unitarity relations of Eqs. (

. Applying this result for

\displaystyle\sumint dQ_{\beta}|T_{\beta\alpha}|^{2}=\sumint dQ_{\beta}|T_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}\,.

Let us now define the transition rate from
|\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle
to a set of states
|\Phi_{\beta}\rangle

{\cal V}2w(p)

1.1
). Because of this, the transition rate (probability per unite time) for the initial state to end within a range
d\beta
of final states is

\displaystyle d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{dP(\alpha\to\beta)}{{\cal T}}=\frac{dQ_{\beta}|T(\alpha\to\beta)|^{2}{\cal V}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\alpha}}2w(p_{i}){\cal V}}\equiv\frac{{\cal V}dQ_{\beta}|T(\alpha\to\beta)|^{2}}{c_{\alpha}}\,,

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(2{\cal V})^{n_{\alpha}}w(p_{1})\cdots w(p_{n_{\alpha}})~,

(1.57)

with the factor
{\cal V}
due to the four-dimensional Dirac delta squared, cf. Eq. (

{\cal T}
since it is a rate of change. Notice that it is not necessary to introduce any factor

in Eq. (

) because this is accounted for by the definition of

, Eq. (
1.27

\displaystyle\sumint dQ_{\beta}c_{\alpha}\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{dQ_{\beta}}

of the space of multiparticle states

. We can actually write that

\displaystyle d\alpha=\int\frac{d^{4}P_{\alpha}}{(2\pi)^{4}}dQ_{\alpha}=\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\alpha}}\frac{d^{3}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2w(p_{i})}\sum_{\sigma_{i},\lambda_{i}}\right)\,.

(1.59)

The time derivative of the probability density

has two sources. On the one hand, it increases per unite time due to transitions from all the other states and, on the other hand, it decreases due to transitions to all other states. Therefore,

. Integrating over

in Eq. (

, one has

\displaystyle=\sumint dQ_{\alpha}\sumint d\beta P_{\beta}\frac{d\Gamma(\beta\to\alpha)}{dQ_{\alpha}}-\sumint d\alpha P_{\alpha}\sumint dQ_{\beta}\frac{d\Gamma(\alpha\to\beta)}{dQ_{\beta}}=0\,,

(1.61)

by simply exchanging of the integration variables in one of the two terms on the right-hand side of this equation.

given in Eq. (

because of Eq. (

is symmetric in
\alpha
and
\beta

The total entropy stops growing when the probability

becomes a function of conserved magnitudes, such as total energy and charge. In this the conservation laws require that

vanishes unless

\bullet

Two-body scattering, and Mandelstam variables:

In two-body scattering, two particles form the initial and final states:

\displaystyle A(p_{1})+B(p_{2})\to C(p_{3})+D(p_{4})\,,

(2.1)

In a two-body scattering process one has 4 momenta which implies 12 variables. Four of them can be fixed in terms of the others by the conservation of total four momentum, then we are left with 8. Additionally, a Lorentz transformation involves 6 parameters, so that from these remnant 8 variables we are left only with 2 Lorentz invariants.

The Mandelstam variables for the scattering in Eq. (
2.1
) are defined as

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}~,

(2.2)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2}~,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}~.

It is straightforward to show that

, and only two of the Mandelstam variables are independent.

In the CM frame

so that

\displaystyle s=(w(p_{1})+w(p_{2}))^{2}=E^{2}~.

(2.3)

In terms of the scattering angle

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=-2{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}(1+\cos\theta)=-4{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}\cos^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}~.

Rotational invariance

According to writing convenience we denote a rotation either by a vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\phi\hat{{\boldsymbol{n}}}
, where
\phi
is the rotation angle around the axis of rotation
\hat{{\boldsymbol{n}}}
, or in terms of the three Euler angles
(\phi,\theta,\gamma)
. For the latter,
\phi
and
\gamma
are the angles of rotations around the
z

\theta
is the angle of a rotation around the
y

independently of the vector space on which it acts. It is well known that rotations correspond to a unitary operator,
R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{\dagger}=R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}

Rotational invariance follows if the Hamiltonian is invariant under rotations

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\phi})VR(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}

\displaystyle=V\,,

(2.17)

as it should be the case for a closed system. Then, the
T
-matrix operator is also invariant under rotations. From the Lippmann-Schwinger equation (
1.41
),

\displaystyle RTR^{-1}

\displaystyle=-RVR^{-1}+RVR^{-1}\,R(E+i\epsilon-H_{0})^{-1}R^{-1}\,RTR^{-1}

(2.18)

\displaystyle=-V+V(E+i\epsilon-H_{0})RTR^{-1}~.

Therefore,

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\phi})TR(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}=T\,.

(2.19)

Time-reversal invariance, and PWAs are symmetric:

An important property of PWAs is that they are symmetric if time-reversal invariance is assumed to hold. Let us denote by
\theta
the time-reversal operator. It is well-known that it is an antiunitary operator. It is simple to give an understanding in Quantum Mechanics why
\theta
is antiunitary, because
\theta\bm{r}\theta^{-1}=\bm{r}
, and it is necessary that
\theta
is antiunitary because then
\theta{\boldsymbol{p}}\theta^{-1}=\theta\left(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}\right)\theta^{-1}=-{\boldsymbol{p}}
, as required.

We assume time-reversal invariance,
\theta V\theta^{-1}=V
. From the Lippmann-Schwinger equation (
1.43
) we then have that

\displaystyle\theta T\theta^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\theta V\theta^{-1}+\theta V\theta^{-1}\,\theta(E_{\alpha}+i\epsilon-H_{0})^{-1}\theta^{-1}\,\theta T\theta^{-1}

(2.81)

\displaystyle=-V+V(E_{\alpha}-i\epsilon-H_{0})^{-1}V\,\theta T\theta^{-1}\,,

where we have used that
\theta H_{0}\theta^{-1}=H_{0}
.
Therefore, we arrive to the result

\displaystyle\theta T\theta^{-1}=T^{\dagger}\,,

(2.82)

where we have also taken into account Eq. (
1.42
).

The action of
\theta
on a state
|jm\rangle
with definite angular momentum can be written as
\theta|jm\rangle=(-1)^{j-m}|j-m\rangle

[
11
]
. Recalling that if
|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle=\theta|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta^{\prime}\rangle=\theta|\beta\rangle
then
\langle\alpha^{\prime}|\beta^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle^{*}
, we then have for the PWAs

\displaystyle\langle p^{\prime}J\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}|T|pJ\mu,\ell S\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle=(\langle p^{\prime}J\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}|\theta)\theta T|pJ\mu,\ell S\rangle=((-1)^{J-\mu})^{2}\langle p^{\prime}J-\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}|\theta T\theta^{-1}|pJ-\mu,\ell S\rangle

(2.83)

\displaystyle=\langle p^{\prime}J-\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}|T^{\dagger}|pJ-\mu,\ell S\rangle=\langle p^{\prime}J-\mu,\ell S|T|pJ-\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}\rangle^{*}\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\langle p^{\prime}J\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}|T|pJ\mu,\ell S\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle pJ-\mu,\ell S|T|p^{\prime}J-\mu,\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime}\rangle

(2.84)

and, since the PWA is independent of
\mu
, we conclude that PWAs are symmetric under the exchange between the initial and final states.

\displaystyle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle\,,

(2.100)

\displaystyle R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}){\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}

\displaystyle={\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}.

Let us see how these monoparticle states transform under rotations:

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\phi})|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=R(\boldsymbol{\phi})R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})^{-1}RR({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle\,,

(2.101)

with
{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}=R(\boldsymbol{\phi}){\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}
. Using Eq. (
2.41
) we then have

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\phi})|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})R_{z}(\gamma)||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle=e^{-i\gamma\sigma}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle=e^{-i\gamma\sigma}|{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}\sigma\rangle\,.

(2.102)

This is a representation of the rotation group. Indicating by
\phi_{2}
and
\theta_{2}
the spherical angular variables for
{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime\prime}=R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}){\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}
, and by
\phi_{1}
,
\theta_{1}
those for
{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}=R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}){\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}
, for the compositions of two rotation matrices
R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})
we have

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{2},\theta_{2},0)R(-\phi_{2},-\theta_{2},0)R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle

(2.103)

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{2},\theta_{2},0)\underbrace{R(-\phi_{2},-\theta_{2},0)R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})R(\phi_{1},\theta_{1},0)}_{R_{z}(\gamma_{2})}\underbrace{R(-\phi_{1},-\theta_{1},0)R(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})R(\phi,\theta,0)}_{R_{z}(\gamma_{1})}||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}}\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{z}(\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{1})|{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime\prime}\sigma\rangle\,.

It is simple to see that
R_{z}(\gamma(-\boldsymbol{\phi}))
for
R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}
is
R_{z}(-\gamma(\boldsymbol{\phi}))
:

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle=R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}R(\boldsymbol{\phi})|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma\rangle

\displaystyle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})\underbrace{R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})}_{R_{z}(\gamma(-\boldsymbol{\phi}))}\underbrace{R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})^{-1}R(\boldsymbol{\phi})R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})}_{R_{z}(\gamma(\boldsymbol{\phi})))}||{\boldsymbol{p}}|\sigma\rangle\,,

(2.104)

as we wanted to show. Notice that in the derivation we have used that
R(\boldsymbol{\phi})^{-1}{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}={\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}
. Equation (
2.104
) is also a consequence of the composition law in Eq. (
2.103
).

\bullet

Two-particle states, common
R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})
, and normalization:

A two-graviton state in the CM frame is defined as the direct product

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=|{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma_{1}\rangle\otimes|-{\boldsymbol{p}}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.105)

Let us show that we can also write

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.106)

For that we have to analyze,

\displaystyle R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})|-|{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})R_{y}(\pi)||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{2}\rangle\,,

(2.107)

being
R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})=R(\pi+\phi,\pi-\theta,0)
. Now,

\displaystyle R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})R_{y}(\pi)

\displaystyle=R_{y}(-\pi+\theta)R_{z}(-\pi-\phi)R_{z}(\phi)R_{y}(\theta)R_{y}(\pi)=R_{y}(-\pi+\theta)R_{z}(-\pi)R_{y}(\theta)R_{y}(\pi)

\displaystyle=R_{y}(-\pi+\theta)\underbrace{R_{z}(-\pi)R_{y}(\pi+\theta)R_{z}(\pi)}_{R_{y}(-\pi-\theta)}R_{z}(-\pi).

(2.108)

We notice that
R_{z}(-\pi)R_{y}(\pi+\theta)R_{z}(\pi)
is a rotation of the same angle
\pi+\theta
but around the axis of rotation
-\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
, which is the same as a rotation around
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
in the opposite sense. Thus,

\displaystyle R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})^{-1}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})R_{y}(\pi)

\displaystyle=R_{y}(-2\pi)R_{z}(-\pi)\,.

(2.109)

Taking this result to Eq. (
2.107
)

\displaystyle R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})|-|{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})R_{y}(-2\pi)R_{z}(-\pi)||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{2}\rangle=e^{i\sigma_{2}\pi}e^{i\sigma_{2}2\pi}R(-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{2}\rangle=e^{i\sigma_{2}\pi}e^{i\sigma_{2}2\pi}|-{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.110)

For a graviton
\sigma_{2}=\pm 2
and the exponential factor
e^{i\sigma_{2}\pi}e^{i\sigma_{2}2\pi}=+1
. Then, it follows Eq. (
2.106
).

The normalization of these two particle states is given by Eq. (
2.8
) but taking into account that the particles are massless. Then,
\sqrt{s}=2|{\boldsymbol{p}}|
and this equation simplifies as

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=32\pi^{2}\delta({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}\,,

(2.111)

where the total momentum delta function has been removed.

\bullet

Partial-wave states, normalization, and Bose-Einstein symmetry:

We now proceed with the decomposition of these plane-wave states into states with total angular momentum well defined, that we call the partial-wave states. For that we notice that a state
||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle
has angular momentum
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}
along the
z
axis, which can take the values
\pm 4
or 0. Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{J\geq|\sigma|}C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}|J\sigma,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.112)

Now, acting with
R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})
,

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})||{\boldsymbol{p}}|{\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle=\sum_{J}C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}R({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})|J\sigma,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle=\sum_{J}\sum_{M=-J}^{J}C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.113)

The coefficients
C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}
could depend on
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
, but since these remain invariant under rotations the coefficient
C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}
is just a constant for every multiplet
J
(each of them with its invariant values of
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
). Then, the coefficients
C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}
can be fixed by the normalization of the partial-wave states.

Before reaching this point, let us invert Eq. (
2.113
). For that, we are going to use the orthogonality properties of the rotation matrices

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)^{*}=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{2J+1}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\,.

(2.114)

We multiply and integrate Eq. (
2.113
) as (we only keep the subscript
J
in
C_{J\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,M}C_{J}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

(2.115)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)\,.

In order to make use of Eq. (
2.114
) we multiply the integrand on the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.115
) by
1=e^{i\sigma\varphi}e^{-i\sigma\varphi}
, so that the integral is the same as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)^{*}D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{2J+1}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}\,.

(2.116)

Employing this result in Eq. (
2.115
) (removing unnecessary primes to show the final result)

\displaystyle|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2J+1}{4\pi C_{J}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.117)

To simplify the writing we denote by
\int d{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta
.
Regarding the normalization of these states

\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}M^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle=\frac{(2J+1)(2J^{\prime}+1)}{(4\pi)^{2}C_{J}C_{J^{\prime}}}\int d{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}\int d{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(\phi^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},0)D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}\underbrace{\langle{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle}_{32\pi^{2}\delta({\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}})\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}}

\displaystyle=32\pi^{2}\frac{(2J+1)(2J^{\prime}+1)}{(4\pi)^{2}C_{J}C_{J^{\prime}}}\frac{\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi D^{(J^{\prime})}_{M^{\prime}\sigma}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,\varphi)^{*}

(2.118)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi(2J+1)}{C_{J}^{2}}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}\,.

Here, we have applied the same trick as in Eq. (
2.116
) when the integration over
\varphi
was also included. Notice that the fact of having
\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}
implies also that
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
, and this is why we have taken advantage of it to put them equal in the subscripts of the rotation matrices to introduce
1=e^{i\sigma\varphi}e^{-i\sigma\varphi}
. Finally, we require the normalization

\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}M^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}\,,

(2.119)

which fixes

\displaystyle C_{J}=2\pi\sqrt{(2J+1)}\,.

(2.120)

Taking this to Eq. (
2.117
), the partial-wave states with definite angular momentum are finally

\displaystyle|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2J+1}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,,

(2.121)

and from Eq. (
2.113
) it follows also that

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,M}2\pi\sqrt{2J+1}D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\,.

(2.122)

Let us now take into account that two-gravitons state must be symmetric under the exchange of their constituents:

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle+|-{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\rangle\right)\,.

(2.123)

Using for each state on the right-hand side the Eq. (
2.122
)

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle_{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{J,M}2\pi\sqrt{2J+1}\left[D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle+D^{(J)}_{M-\sigma}(\pi+\phi,\pi-\theta,0)|JM,\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\rangle\right]\,.

(2.124)

Now, recalling that

\displaystyle D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)=e^{-iM\phi}d^{J}_{M\sigma}(\theta)\,,

(2.125)

with
d^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\theta)
the Wigner small
d
matrices, we have

\displaystyle D^{(J)}_{M-\sigma}(\pi+\phi,\pi-\theta,0)

\displaystyle=e^{-iM\pi}e^{-iM\phi}d^{J}_{M-\sigma}(\pi-\theta)\,.

(2.126)

Next, we use the property

\displaystyle d^{J}_{M-\sigma}(\pi-\theta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{J+M}d^{J}_{M\sigma}(\theta)\,.

(2.127)

Therefore, we have that Eq. (
2.126
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle D^{(J)}_{M-\sigma}(\pi+\phi,\pi-\theta,0)

\displaystyle=e^{-iM\phi}d^{J}_{M\sigma}(\theta)(-1)^{2M}(-1)^{J}=D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)(-1)^{J}\,.

(2.128)

We use this result in Eq. (
2.124

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{J,M}\sqrt{2J+1}D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle+(-1)^{J}|JM,\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\rangle\right)\,.

(2.129)

We introduce the notation,

\displaystyle|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle_{S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle+(-1)^{J}|JM,\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}\rangle\right)\,.

(2.130)

In the case that
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
it follows that only
J=
even is allowed, otherwise
|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle_{S}=0

The final expression for Eq. (
2.129
) is:

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{J,M}\sqrt{2J+1}D^{(J)}_{M\sigma}(\phi,\theta,0)|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle_{S}\,.

(2.131)

Its inversion is analogous to Eq. (
2.121
):

\displaystyle|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2J+1}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\,D_{M\sigma}^{(J)}(\phi,\theta,0)^{*}|{\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}\,.

(2.132)

Regarding the normalization of the symmetrized states, we have from Eqs. (
2.119
) and (
2.130
)

\displaystyle{{}_{S}\langle}J^{\prime}M^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{2}}+(-1)^{J}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\sigma_{2}}\delta_{\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\sigma_{1}}\right)\,,

(2.133)

\displaystyle{{}_{S}\langle}JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}{\rangle}_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1+(-1)^{J}\delta_{\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}}\right)\,.

(2.134)

Thus,

\displaystyle{{}_{S}\langle}JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}|JM,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\pi 2^{|\sigma|/4}}\,,

(2.135)

with
\sigma=\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}
, as defined above.

Crossing symmetry, physical regions, and analyticity:

Taking the set up of perturbative Quantum Field Theory, a generic quantum filed
\phi_{i}(x)
contains both
annihilation
a_{i}({\boldsymbol{p}})
and creation
b_{i}({\boldsymbol{p}})^{\dagger}
operators for particles and antiparticles, respectively
[
3
]
. The former term is multiplied by the space-time exponential factor

\exp(-ipx)
and the latter by
\exp(ipx)
:

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}2w_{i}(p)}\left(a({\boldsymbol{p}})e^{-ipx}+b({\boldsymbol{p}})^{\dagger}e^{ipx}\right)\,.

(3.1)

The simplest case to get the basic idea of crossing involves only zero-spin fields.
Because of Eq. (
3.1
), the same vertices in a given scattering process
can be associated with an initial
particle of momentum
p
or with a final antiparticle with momentum
-p
, and viceversa (when considering
\phi_{i}^{\dagger}
).
As a result, the scattering amplitude for

\displaystyle a_{1}(p_{1})+a_{2}(p_{2})+\ldots\to b_{1}(p^{\prime}_{1})+b_{2}(p^{\prime}_{2})+\ldots

(3.2)

governs any other process in which one or several particles
are crossed to their antiparticles from the initial/final to the final/initial channels and, simultaneously, their momenta change sign. For instance, for the previous reaction we could have many others
related by crossing, in particular

\displaystyle a_{1}(p_{1})+a_{2}(p_{2})+\ldots+\bar{b}(-p^{\prime}_{1})\to b_{2}(p^{\prime}_{2})+\ldots

(3.3)

where the bar corresponds to the antiparticle.

Let us particularize crossing to the two-body scattering
a+b\to c+d
. We can then
distinguish the following related processes

s
-channel process:

\displaystyle a(p_{1})+b(p_{2})

\displaystyle\to~~c(p_{3})+d(p_{4})~,

(3.4)

t
-channel process:

\displaystyle a(p_{1})+\bar{c}(-p_{3})

\displaystyle\to~~\bar{b}(-p_{2})+d(p_{4})~,

(3.5)

u
-channel process:

\displaystyle a(p_{1})+\bar{d}(-p_{4})

\displaystyle\to~~c(p_{3})+\bar{b}(-p_{2})~.

(3.6)

The
s
-channel is also called the direct one, while the
t
- and
u
-channels are also
denoted as crossed channels.
Apart from the scattering processes in Eqs. (
3.4
)-(
3.6
),
there are other three processes in which
a(p_{1})\to\bar{a}(-p_{1})
is exchanged instead of
b(p_{2})\to\bar{b}(-p_{2})

from the initial to the final state. These new scattering processes also stem from applying CPT invariance to those shown in these equations.

By changing signs in the momenta, the
s
,
t
and
u
variables for every channel can be related.
Let us denote by the subscript
t
and
u
the Mandelstam variables in the
t
- and
u
-channels, respectively. Therefore:

t
-channel:

\displaystyle s_{t}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2}=t~,

(3.7)

\displaystyle t_{t}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=s~,

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}=u~,

u
-channel:

\displaystyle s_{u}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}=u~,

(3.8)

\displaystyle t_{u}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2}=t~,

\displaystyle u_{u}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=s~.

Notice that the physical regions for the
s
-,
t
- and
u
-channels are disjoint. To simplify matters let us take that the four

, e.g. this is the case of
\pi\pi

s
,
t
and
u

2.4

\displaystyle s+t+u=4m^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\geq 4m^{2}~,~t\leq 0~,~u\leq 0\,.

(3.10)

Similarly, for the
t

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=s_{t}\geq 4m^{2}~,~s=t_{t}\leq 0~,~u=u_{t}\leq 0~\,,

(3.11)

and for the

-channel,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=s_{u}\geq 4m^{2}~,~s=u_{u}\leq 0~,~t=t_{u}\leq 0\,.

(3.12)

Analyticity connects the scattering amplitudes in the three disjoint physical regions for
the
s
-,
t
- and
u
-channels by the same analytical function
A(s,t,u)=A((p_{1}+p_{2})^{2},(p_{1}-p_{3})^{2},(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2})
of
s
and
t

[
u
is then given by Eq. (
3.9

The physical values for the three channels correspond to the boundary values of this analytic function.

C(s,t,u)=A(u,t,s)
.

A useful application of the previous relations is the determination of the three different isospin scattering amplitudes in
\pi\pi
scattering. For that one takes into account that
|\pi^{0}\rangle=|I=1,i_{3}=0\rangle
,
|\pi^{-}\rangle=|1,-1\rangle
and
|\pi^{+}\rangle=-|1,1\rangle
, cf. Eq. (
3.19
). Then, it results:

\displaystyle|\pi^{0}\pi^{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}|20\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}|00\rangle\,,

(3.14)

\displaystyle|\pi^{+}\pi^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}|20\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|10\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}|00\rangle\,,

and similarly for the other two pion states in the crossed channels.
We denote the isospin amplitudes by
T_{I}(s,t,u)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\pi^{0}\pi^{0}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}

\displaystyle A(s,t,u)=-\frac{1}{3}T_{2}(s,t,u)+\frac{1}{3}T_{0}(s,t,u)\,,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\pi^{0}\pi^{-}\to\pi^{0}\pi^{-}

\displaystyle A(t,s,u)=\frac{1}{2}T_{2}(s,t,u)-\frac{1}{2}T_{1}(s,t,u)\,,

\displaystyle\pi^{0}\pi^{+}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{0}

\displaystyle A(u,t,s)=\frac{1}{2}T_{2}(s,t,u)+\frac{1}{2}T_{1}(s,t,u)\,.

(3.16)

This is a linear system of algebraic equations from where one has

\displaystyle T_{0}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=3A(s,t,u)+A(t,s,u)+A(u,t,s)\,,

(3.17)

\displaystyle T_{1}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=A(u,t,s)-A(t,s,u)\,,

\displaystyle T_{2}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=A(u,t,s)+A(t,s,u)\,.

\bullet

Crossed channel cuts, and the Lippmann-Schwinger equation:

We already discussed in relation with Hermitian unitarity, cf. Eq. (
1.29
), that unitarity implies the existence of a cut in the scattering amplitude for
s\geq s_{\rm th,1}
. Following with the simplified kinematics of the equal mass case, this corresponds to
s>4m^{2}
. This applies also to the crossed channels.
In particular,
the unitarity cut in the
u
-channel with a constant value of
t

gives rise to a new cut in the complex
s
plane, apart from the
s
-channel unitarity cut. For the case referred, the cut runs for
u\geq 4m^{2}
so that
it correspond to the values

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=4m^{2}-t-u\leq-t~,

(3.18)

in addition to the unitary cut for
s\geq 4m^{2}
.
This is a simple example of a crossed-channel cut, also referred as an unphysical cut, because it implies
unphysical values of the Mandelstam variables in the
s
-channel. They arise
from a branch point singularity attached to a two(multi)-body
threshold.

The presence of the unitarity cuts either in the direct
s
-channel or in the crossed
t
- and
u
-channels is a reflection of the Lippmann-Schwinger equation (
1.41
), considered for each channel separately. Notice that it has a cut for
E_{\alpha}>\sqrt{s_{\rm th,1}}
in the
s
-channel but, similarly, this is also the case for
E_{\alpha}>\sqrt{t_{\rm th,1}}
and
E_{\alpha}>\sqrt{u_{\rm th,1}}
in the
t
- and
u
-channels, respectively. They just arise in each case from the energy flowing through the scattering process, irrespectively of the value of the other independent variable.

\bullet

Kinematical singularities, and Lorentz invariant amplitudes:

For particles with spin the analytical continuation of the scattering amplitude in the complex
s
and
t

planes is more delicate because of the so-called kinematical singularities, which origin is not
dynamical like for the unitarity cuts in the
s
- or crossed channels.
They arise from the algebraic dependence on the Mandelstam variables of the solutions to the relativistic
equations for the particles with spins
[
1
,
6
]
, e.g. they could introduce square roots, etc. Once these kinematical dependences are separated, the singularities in the different components of the scattering amplitudes are due to values of the Mandelstam variables for which intermediate particles are on shell.

A possible way to deal with the kinematics singularities is to isolate Lorentz invariant functions out of
the scattering amplitudes, which plays the role of the Lorentz invariant amplitudes for zero-spin meson-meson scattering.
Take for example
\pi^{a}(q)N(p,\sigma;\alpha)\to\pi^{a^{\prime}}(q^{\prime})N(p^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime};\alpha^{\prime})
,
where
a
and
a^{\prime}
denote the isospin Cartesian coordinates of pions.
The charged pions correspond to the combinations

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\frac{\pi^{a}\tau^{a}}{\sqrt{2}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{\pi^{0}}{\sqrt{2}}&\pi^{+}\\
\pi^{-}&-\frac{\pi^{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\end{matrix}\right)~.

(3.19)

Firstly, it is clear that there are two isospin amplitudes corresponding
to
I=1/2
and 3/2, because the pions and nucleons have isospin 1 and 1/2, respectively.
Any matrix in the isospin space of nucleons can be expressed as a superposition of
Pauli matrices
\tau^{a}
,
a=1,2,3
, and the 2
\times
2 identity matrix.
Let us consider two pions with isospin Cartesian indices
a
and
a^{\prime}
, then the tensors with good properties
under isospin rotations that can be built having
I=0
and 1 are
\delta_{aa^{\prime}}
and

[\tau_{a},\tau_{a^{\prime}}]
, respectively.
7
7

7

No tensor of rank 2 is allowed because its combination with isospin an 1/2 cannot
give rise again to an isospin 1/2.

In this way, we can write

\displaystyle T_{aa^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{a^{\prime}a}T^{+}+\frac{1}{2}[\tau_{a},\tau_{a^{\prime}}]T^{-}~.

(3.20)

The two amplitudes
T^{\pm}
are operators acting in the space of the Dirac
spinors and can expanded as a linear combination of the sixteen
matrices
I
,
\gamma^{\mu}
,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}
,
\gamma_{5}
and
\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}
with the Lorentz
indices contracted with four-momenta. The matrices
\gamma_{5}
and
\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}

violate parity and they do not appear. In addition to the identity and the gamma matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
, the matrices
\sigma^{\mu\nu}
do not contribute with any new structure because of the Dirac equations:

p\!\!\!/u({\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma)=mu({\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma)
and
\bar{u}({\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})p^{\prime}\!\!\!\!/=m\bar{u}({\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
. For instance, let us consider
\sigma^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}r_{\nu}
, where
r_{\nu}
is a vector built from particle momenta. Then, we anticommute
\gamma^{\mu}p_{\mu}
and
\gamma^{\nu}r_{\nu}
in the first term of
\sigma^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}r_{\nu}=(\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu})p_{\mu}r_{\nu}
until
\gamma^{\mu}p_{\mu}
always acts on
u({\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma)
, giving then
mu({\boldsymbol{p}},\sigma)
because of the Dirac equation. The rest of terms resulting from the anticommutation relation of the gamma matrices can be reabsorbed in the ones involving the identity matrix and the
\gamma^{\mu}
. The same would apply to
\sigma^{\mu\nu}p^{\prime}_{\mu}r_{\nu}
. On the other hand,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}q_{\mu}q^{\prime}_{\nu}
is the same as
\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\mu\nu}(q+q^{\prime})_{\mu}(q^{\prime}-q)_{\nu}
because
\sigma^{\mu\nu}
is antisymmetric. However, since
q^{\prime}_{\nu}-q_{\nu}=p_{\nu}-p^{\prime}_{\nu}
we again come back to the first case already discussed. All in all, we then arrive to
the standard form
[
14
]

\displaystyle T^{\pm}=\bar{u}(p^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\left[A^{\pm}(s,t,u)+\frac{1}{2}(q\!\!\!/+q^{\prime}\!\!\!\!\!/\,\,)B^{\pm}(s,t,u)\right]u(p,\sigma)~.

(3.21)

The combination
q^{\prime}-q
does not appear in the previous equation once the Dirac equations are taken into account because
q^{\prime}-q=p-p^{\prime}
.
The analytical properties of the Lorentz invariant functions
A^{\pm}
and
B^{\pm}
are determined in the same way as explained
for the scattering amplitude of zero-spin mesons. The other factors in Eq. (
3.21
) are, of course, important to
finally do actual calculations to establish relations between analyticity and experimental results.

\bullet

Cuts in the
s
plane for PWAs due to poles and cuts in crossed channels:

The crossed-channel poles, stemming from poles in the
t
- and
u
-channels,
produce crossed cuts in the complex
s
plane for PWAs.
For example, for
\pi^{-}n\to\pi^{-}n
scattering consider the
u
-channel pole due to the exchange of a proton,
u=m_{p}^{2}
.
For pion-nucleon scattering in the CM the
u
variable is given by
(
m_{p}
and
m_{\pi}
are the nucleon and pion masses, respectively)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=m_{p}^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2}-2\omega E-2{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}\cos\theta~,

(3.22)

where
E
and
\omega
are the nucleon and pion CM energies, respectively.
In the process of calculating a PWA, cf. Eq. (
2.77
), the scattering angle is integrated
\theta\in[0,\pi]
.
Thus, setting
u=m_{p}^{2}
in Eq. (
3.22
) and giving
\omega
,
E
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}
in terms of
s
,

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{s+m_{\pi}^{2}-m_{p}^{2}}{2\sqrt{s}}~,

(3.23)

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\frac{s+m_{p}^{2}-m_{\pi}^{2}}{2\sqrt{s}}~,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda(s,m_{p}^{2},m_{\pi}^{2})}{4s}~,

\displaystyle\lambda(s,m_{1}^{2},m_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=(s-(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2})(s-(m_{1}-m_{2})^{2})~,

with
\lambda(s,m_{1}^{2},m_{2}^{2})
the Källén triangle function,
the values of
s
that are solution of Eq. (
3.22
) as a function of
x=\cos\theta
are:

\displaystyle s_{1}(x)=\frac{m^{2}x+m_{\pi}^{2}(1+x)-\sqrt{m^{4}+2m_{\pi}^{4}(1+x)+2m^{2}m_{\pi}^{2}(-1+x+2x^{2})}}{1+x}~,

(3.24)

\displaystyle s_{2}(x)=\frac{m^{2}x+m_{\pi}^{2}(1+x)+\sqrt{m^{4}+2m_{\pi}^{4}(1+x)+2m^{2}m_{\pi}^{2}(-1+x+2x^{2})}}{1+x}~.

The first solution
s_{1}(x)
produces a cut along the negative real axis. We clarify that the radicand is larger than the square of
the terms in the numerator before the square root, with the difference between them being
(1-x^{2})(m^{2}-m_{\pi}^{2})^{2}
.
This also shows that the radicand is positive for
x\in[-1,1]
and arbitrary values for the masses. This cut extends from
-\infty
(for
x\to-1
) up to 0 (
x\to+1)
. This is a clear example of a left-hand cut (LHC).
For
s_{2}(x)
one has a finite cut which ranges along the positive real axis from
(m^{2}-m_{\pi}^{2})^{2}/m^{2}
up to
m^{2}+2m_{\pi}^{2}
.

Crossed-channel unitarity cuts give also rise to cuts in the complex
s
plane for PWAs.
For instance, let us take
\pi\pi
scattering. Then, the two-pion unitarity cuts along the
t
- and
u
-channels
happen for
t=-2(s/4-m_{\pi}^{2})(1-x)\geq 4m_{\pi}^{2}
and
u=-2(s/4-m_{\pi}^{2})(1+x)\geq 4m_{\pi}^{2}
. Solving
s
in terms of
x
we find that both cases give
rise to LHCs with
s\in]-\infty,0]
when
\cos\theta
moves along
[-1,1]
.

\bullet

Crossed cuts in PWAs for nonrelativistic scattering:

In the case of a nonrelativistic theory crossing does not apply. From the point of view of Quantum Field Theory, in the nonrelativistic case the quantum fields only involve annihilation operators (or creation ones
for the Hermitian conjugate field)
[
15
]
.
However, a LHC stems in this case due to the exchanged particles producing the potential. For instance, let us take a Yukawa potential

\displaystyle V(r)

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{e^{-rm_{\pi}}}{r}~.

(3.25)

Its Fourier transform is

\displaystyle V({\boldsymbol{q}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha\int d^{3}re^{-i{\boldsymbol{q}}\bm{r}}\frac{e^{-rm_{\pi}}}{r}=\frac{4\pi\alpha}{{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2}}~,

(3.26)

with
{\boldsymbol{q}}={\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{p}}
. Its partial-wave projection for particles
without spin, apply Eq. (
2.72
), is:

\displaystyle V_{J}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\,V({\boldsymbol{q}}^{2})\,P_{J}(\cos\theta)

(3.27)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\frac{P_{J}(\cos\theta)}{\cos\theta-(p^{2}+{p^{\prime}}^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2})/(2pp^{\prime})}~.

The LHC is produced when the denominator vanishes, and this can be isolated in the following way

\displaystyle V_{J}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\frac{P_{J}(\cos\theta)}{\cos\theta-\xi}=-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\cos\theta\frac{P_{J}(\cos\theta)-P_{J}(\xi)}{\cos\theta-\xi}

(3.28)

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}P_{J}(\xi)\int_{-1}^{+1}\frac{d\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\xi}~,

with

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}+{p^{\prime}}^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2}}{2pp^{\prime}}~.

(3.29)

There is no LHC from the term before the last one in Eq. (
3.28
) because when the denominator vanishes the numerator also does. The LHC then stems from the last term and the integration in

\cos\theta
can be done explicitly:

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}P_{J}(\xi)\int_{-1}^{+1}\frac{d\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\xi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}P_{J}(\xi)\left[\ln(1-\xi)-\ln(-1-\xi)\right]~.

(3.30)

Now, for real and positive
p
and
p^{\prime}
the difference of the two logarithms in the last term can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\alpha}{pp^{\prime}}P_{J}(\xi)\left[\ln((p+p^{\prime})^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2})-\ln((p-p^{\prime})^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2})\right]~.

(3.31)

This form is specially suitable for the analytical continuation to the complex
planes of
p
and
p^{\prime}
and so determine the position of the cuts, as fully exploited in
Ref.
[
16
]
. The cuts in the
p
variable for given
p^{\prime}

happen for the first logarithm when
(p+p^{\prime})^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2}<0
, and for the second one when
(p-p^{\prime})^{2}+m_{\pi}^{2}<0
. These cuts are vertical and are given by

\displaystyle p=(\pm)p^{\prime}\pm i\sqrt{m_{\pi}^{2}+x^{2}}~,~x\in\mathbb{R}~,

(3.32)

where the first
\pm
symbol is uncorrelated with the second one. Analogous reciprocal relations exist for the
cuts in the variable
p^{\prime}
for given
p
.

In the case of on-shell scattering,
p=p^{\prime}
, and particularizing Eq. (
3.32
), the
only meaningful case happens by selecting the minus sign in the
\pm
sign between the first brackets,

\displaystyle p=-p\pm i\sqrt{m_{\pi}^{2}+x^{2}}~.

(3.33)

Its solution is

\displaystyle p=\pm\frac{i}{2}\sqrt{m_{\pi}^{2}+x^{2}}~,

(3.34)

and for the variable
p^{2}
we have a cut for the values

\displaystyle p^{2}\leq-\frac{m_{\pi}^{2}}{4}~.

(3.35)

This is the LHC for nonrelativistic
NN
scattering
[
16
]
.

The
N/D
method and CDD poles

\bullet

Set up for the
N/D
method:

The crossed cuts for PWAs in the complex
s
plane are of two types. For
processes with equal masses, like
a+a\rightarrow a+a
, as
\pi\pi
scattering, there is only a
LHC for
s<s_{\rm L}
(for
\pi\pi
scattering
s_{\rm L}=0
since the lightest states exchanged in the crossed channels are two-pion states themselves). However, for scattering of the type

a+b\rightarrow a+b
with
m_{1}=m_{3}=m_{a}
and
m_{2}=m_{4}=m_{b}
, in addition to a LHC
there is a circular cut too in the complex
s
plane at
|s|=m_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}

[
1
]
,
where we have taken
m_{2}>m_{1}
. For simplicity
in the formalism, we refer to the LHC as if it comprises all the unphysical cuts. This is
enough for our introductory purposes here. It also worth mentioning that had we worked in the
complex
p^{2}
plane all the cuts for elastic scattering would be LHC ones for this variable. This is because the expression for
t
and
u
are linear in the modulus squared of the momentum, as in the equal mass case for the
s
variable.
Then, the analysis would be analogous to the one developed here in the complex
s
plane.

We first analyze the elastic case and then we generalize the results for coupled
partial-wave amplitudes.
Let us indicate simply by
T(s)
a PWA with total angular momentum
J
, together with other quantum numbers that would be needed to completely determine it. Above the threshold of the reaction
s_{\rm th}
along the unitarity cut, according to Eq. (
2.91
),

\displaystyle{\rm Im}T^{-1}(s)

\displaystyle=-\rho(s)=-\frac{|{\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}}|}{8\pi\sqrt{s}}~,~s\geq s_{\rm th}~.

(4.1)

Along the LHC for
s<s_{\rm L}
the discontinuity and Schwarz reflection theorem
8
8

8

This theorem states that an analytical function
f(z)
that is real along an open domain in the real axis then it satisfies
f(z^{*})=f(z)^{*}
. This is the case for the PWAs analyzed here with
s_{\rm L}<s_{\rm th}
.
leads to

T(s+i\epsilon)-T(s-i\epsilon)=2i{\rm Im}T(s)~,~s<s_{\rm L}\,.

(4.2)

The
N/D
method
[
17
]
allows to obtain
T(s)
fulfilling Eqs. (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) by expressing it as the quotient of two functions separating the LHC and unitarity cuts as

T(s)=\frac{N_{L}(s)}{D_{L}(s)}~,

(4.3)

such that
D(s)
only has unitarity cut for
s>s_{\rm th}
and

N(s)
has only LHC for
s<s_{\rm L}
. From Eqs. (
4.1
) and (
4.3
) these functions satisfy:

\displaystyle{\rm Im}D(s)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}T^{-1}(s)N(s)=-\rho(s)N(s)~,

\displaystyle s>s_{\rm th}

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}D(s)

\displaystyle=0~,

\displaystyle s<s_{\rm th}

\displaystyle{\rm Im}N(s)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}T(s)\;D(s)~,

\displaystyle s<s_{\rm L}

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}N(s)

\displaystyle=0~.

\displaystyle s>s_{\rm L}

Now, we write a dispersion relation (DR) for
D(s)
. We assume that
D(s)/s^{n}
vanishes in the complex
s
plane for
s\to\infty
. Therefore, we can a write an
n
-times subtracted DR. We notice that because along the unitarity cut
{\rm Im}D(s)=-\rho(s)N(s)
, Eq. (
4.4
), this asymptotic behavior also implies that
\lim_{s\to\infty}N(s)/s^{n}=0
(note that, cf. Eq. (
4.1
),
\rho(s)\to\text{constant}
for
s\to\infty
).
9
9

9

Because of the Kanasawa-Tomozawa theorem
[
18
,
7
]
, summarized in the Appendix
A
, for a function with only a cut without poles on it the same degree of divergence occurs in the whole complex plane for any direction of
s\to\infty
.
This also allows to write an
n
-times subtracted DR for
N(s)
. For that, we apply the Cauchy integration theorem to an integration contour in the complex
s
plane closed at infinity by a circle of infinite radius engulfing the unitarity cut along the real axis for
s>s_{\rm th}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\oint dz\frac{D(z)}{(z-s)(z-s_{0})^{n}}

\displaystyle=2\pi i\frac{D(s)}{(s-s_{0})^{n}}+2\pi i\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\left.\frac{d^{n-1}}{dz^{n-1}}\frac{D(z)}{z-s}\right|_{z=s_{0}}\,.

(4.6)

On the other hand, by dropping the contribution along the circle at infinity we are left only with the contribution to the integration along the unitarity cut,

\displaystyle\oint dz\frac{D(z)}{(z-s)(z-s_{0})^{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{\rm th}}^{+\infty}ds^{\prime}\frac{D(s^{\prime}+i\epsilon)-D(s^{\prime}-i\epsilon)}{(s^{\prime}-s)(s^{\prime}-s_{0})^{n}}=2i\int_{s_{\rm th}}^{+\infty}ds^{\prime}\frac{{\rm Im}D(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s)(s^{\prime}-s_{0})^{n}}\,.

(4.7)

Equating Eqs. (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), and taking into account Eq. (
4.4
), we then have the following dispersive expression for
D(s)
,

\displaystyle D(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(s-s_{0})^{n}}{\pi}\int_{s_{\rm th}}^{+\infty}ds^{\prime}\frac{\rho(s^{\prime})N(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s)(s^{\prime}-s_{0})^{n}}-\frac{(s-s_{0})^{n}}{(n-1)!}\left.\frac{d^{n-1}}{dz^{n-1}}\frac{D(z)}{z-s}\right|_{z=s_{0}}\,.

(4.8)

The last term is a polynomial of degree
n-1
,

\displaystyle-\frac{(s-s_{0})^{n}}{(n-1)!}\left.\frac{d^{n-1}}{dz^{n-1}}\frac{D(z)}{z-s}\right|_{z=s_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a_{m}s^{m}\,.

(4.9)

Taking this to Eq. (
4.8
),

D(s)=-\frac{(s-s_{0})^{n}}{\pi}\int^{+\infty}_{s_{\rm th}}ds^{\prime}\frac{\rho(s^{\prime}){N}(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s)(s^{\prime}-s_{0})^{n}}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a_{m}s^{m}\,.

(4.10)

We can proceed analogously for the function
N(s)
by closing the integration contour at infinity with a circle of infinite radius that engulfs the LHC for
s<s_{\rm L}
. Considering the discontinuity of
N(s)
along this cut, Eq. (
4.5
), and that
\lim_{s\to\infty}N(s)/s^{n}=0
, as discussed above, we then have

N(s)=\frac{(s-s_{0})^{n}}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{s_{\rm L}}ds^{\prime}\frac{{{\rm Im}T}(s^{\prime}){D}(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s)(s^{\prime}-s_{0})^{n}}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}b_{m}s^{m}~.

(4.11)

Equations (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) are a system of coupled linear integral equations for the functions

N(s)
and
D(s)
, whose input is
{{\rm Im}T}(s)
along the LHC (together with the coefficients
a_{m}
and
b_{m}
in the subtractive polynomials).

\bullet

CDD poles, and additional solutions:

However, Eqs. (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) are not the most general solutions
to Eqs. (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) because the inverse of a PWA along the unitarity cut is not defined when it crosses a zero. Therefore, Eq. (
4.4
) is actually ambiguous at such points. For other possible zeroes in the complex
s
plane one can always imagine that these are accounted for by zeroes in
N(s)
which would not pose any special problem for the DR of this function, only those along the unitarity cut have to be treated separately. These zeros are the Castillejo-Dalitz-Dyson (CDD) poles after Ref.
[
19
]
.

Let
\{s_{i}\}
be the set of zeroes along the unitarity cut at every of which
{T}(s_{i})=0
. We introduce a function
\lambda(s)
such that between
two consecutive zeroes
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
,

{{\rm Im}D}(s)=-\rho(s)N(s)=\frac{d\lambda(s)}{ds}~.

(4.12)

Therefore, for
s\in(s_{i},s_{i+1})

\lambda(s)=-\int_{s_{i}}^{s}\rho(s^{\prime}){N}(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}+\lambda(s_{i})~,

(4.13)

where
\lambda(s_{i})
is unknown. From this intermediate result we can then write along the
s
real axis that

\lambda(s)=-\int_{s_{\rm th}}^{s}\rho(s^{\prime}){N}(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}+\sum_{i}\lambda(s_{i})\theta(s-s_{i})~,

(4.14)

with
\theta(s)
the Heaviside function. Then, taking the derivative of
\lambda(s)
according to Eq. (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm Im}D(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\left[f(x+i\varepsilon)-f(x-i\varepsilon)\right]~,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\bar{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[f(x+i\varepsilon)+f(x-i\varepsilon)\right]~.

The Sugawara-Kanazawa theorem applies then to a function
f(z)
that can be found in many physical applications. This is e.g. the case for a meson-meson PWA with equal-mass mesons because

(A.4)

s\to+\infty-i\varepsilon
