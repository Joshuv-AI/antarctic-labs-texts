Opioid-related emergency department visits in Saudi Arabia: an interrupted time series analysis of visit patterns pre-COVID-19 and post-COVID-19
Authors: Raseel Alroba, Almaha Alfakhri, Maha Aldoughaim, Khalid Aldamiri, Nawaf Alrogi, Khaled Alboug, Ali Gado, Turki Althunian, Ohoud Almadani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe impact of emergency department (ED) visits related to opioid and psychotropic substances has become increasingly critical in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study evaluates whether the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with an increasing trend of opioid- and psychotropic-related ED visits in Saudi Arabia.MethodsAn interrupted time series analysis was conducted using data on ED visits from a centralised Saudi electronic health records database (1 February 2018–30 April 2022). Additionally, laboratory tests related to substance abuse/misuse were obtained from a centralised Saudi toxicology database (1 January 2019–31 May 2021). Adults (≥18 years) with ED visits related to opioid use disorders (OUDs) or psychotropic misuse were included. The immediate (pulse) and prolonged (slope) effects temporally associated with the March 2020 COVID-19 lockdown on substance abuse-related ED visits were evaluated using autoregressive integrated moving average models.Results3730 ED visits and 390 toxicology tests related to OUDs and 517 ED visits and 3513 toxicology tests related to psychotropic misuse were identified. The lockdown was temporarily associated with an immediate increase in OUD-related ED visits (pulse: 14.75 visits, 95% CI 9.65 to 19.86), followed by a monthly decrease (slope: −0.06 visits, 95% CI −0.08 to −0.03). It was also temporally associated with an immediate decrease in psychotropic-related ED tests (pulse: −185.7, 95% CI −309.50 to −61.99).ConclusionsThe COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with changes in both OUD-related ED visits and psychotropic ED tests. These findings underscore the need for policies to mitigate substance use risks during such interventions and to improve data quality for future research.Keywords: COVID-19, Drug Monitoring, Public Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Using an interrupted time series analysis design, this study has identified the first large-scale evidence in Saudi Arabia of a rapid rise in ED visits related to opioids immediately following the COVID-19 lockdown implemented in March 2020 and then a continued reduction thereafter and a rapid decline in positive results for confirmed psychotropic toxicology tests immediately after the lockdown.

Introduction

Over the last decade, a considerable increase in emergency department (ED) visits related to substance use disorders (SUDs) has been observed globally, accompanied by a corresponding increase in SUD-related fatalities.1,3 Globally, an estimated 167 000 SUD-related fatalities were reported, with opioids accounting for 67% of these deaths.2 4 Studies have also shown that the COVID-19 pandemic has contributed to the increased trend of ED visits related to SUDs.25,7 This COVID-19 related increased trend has been attributed to potential factors related to COVID-19 lockdowns, including the closure of outpatient addiction clinics, the suspension of harm reduction services and limited access to support groups.8 9 Despite the availability of this evidence, it remains unclear whether the COVID-19 lockdown had a comparable impact in Saudi Arabia.

Between 2018 and 2022, several studies reported a notable increase in ED visits and hospitalisations related to SUDs.23 5,8 10 11 For example, in the USA, the rate of SUD-related ED visits increased from 2.8% of all ED visits in 2018 to 3.4% of all ED visits in 2023.1 Similarly, ED visits for opioid use disorders (OUDs) increased from 9.47 per 1000 in the pre-COVID-19 period of February 2020 to 13.90 per 1000 during the COVID-19 period in April 2020.8 10 11 In Canada, the risk of fatalities due to OUDs increased by 91%, with hospitalisations related to SUDs rising by 9% from 2020 to 2022.12

In early 2020, Saudi Arabia took comprehensive actions to address the COVID-19 pandemic including lockdowns, travel restrictions and the suspension of daily activities.13 Based on local studies, these interventions—combined with the challenges posed by the infection itself—resulted in a moderate to severe psychological impact, with noticeable increases in depression and anxiety.14,17 However, there is a lack of sufficient evidence from large-scale studies within the Saudi population assessing the temporal association between COVID-19 and the risk of SUDs. As a result, we conducted this study to evaluate whether the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with changes in the patterns of ED visits related to OUDs in Saudi Arabia. We also examined potential temporal associations with ED visits related to the abuse/misuse of psychotropic substances.

Methods

Study design, data source and settings

In this study, an interrupted time series analysis was employed to evaluate patterns of ED visits and toxicology tests related to OUDs and psychotropic substances by using two different databases. First data source consisted of de-identified, individual-level longitudinal records from several major tertiary hospitals across Saudi Arabia, comprising approximately 3 812 408 unique patient records from the Saudi Real-World Evidence Network (SRWEN) of the Saudi Food and Drug Authority. The SRWEN database hosts comprehensive data on Saudi citizens and residents who are eligible for treatment in some major tertiary care public and private hospitals.18 This centralised database has been subjected to several quality assessment projects and has been used in other published studies related to real-world evidence in Saudi Arabia.18,21 The electronic health records in this centralised database include the following: patient demographics, diagnoses, procedures performed and prescriptions, all of which were recorded during ED visits, as well as inpatient or outpatient routine healthcare visits.18

Second data source was toxicological data related to SUDs targeted in this study was the Online Toxicology Analysis Request and Result (OTARR) platform, operated by the Saudi Ministry of Health.22 The OTARR platform serves as a centralised repository for drug and toxicology information, including clinical test results, and contains SUD-confirmation data from 75 hospitals that are not included in the SRWEN database. The inclusion of OTARR enhances the generalisability of the study findings.

SRWEN and OTARR database collect two different types of data; SRWEN collects data on patient patterns in ED visits recorded electronically in the patients’ medical record while OTARR provided the most comprehensive trend data on confirmed testing for toxicology across additional hospitals. Due to the difference in the structure and outcomes of each dataset, separate analyses were completed for each, and they were interpreted concurrently to determine if there are consistent temporal patterns occurring across both systems.

Study cohort

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients aged 18 years or older at the time of cohort entry, who had an ED visit related to OUDs (online supplemental table 1A) or the abuse/misuse of a psychotropic substance (online supplemental table 1B) between 1 February 2018 and 30 April 2022, were identified from the SRWEN database. Patients who had a documented use of medications indicated as an antidote to opioid overdose (ie, naloxone) or benzodiazepine overdose (ie, flumazenil) were also included.823,25 Subsequently, toxicological test data from the SRWEN database were used to determine the inclusion or exclusion of each ED visit based on the test results. Patients were finally included in the study only if the test was positive on the same day as the ED visit or were classified as suspected cases when no laboratory test result (positive or negative) was recorded on the same day (figure 1). However, we acknowledge that the suspected cases classification may introduce bias to our findings, as it lacks the laboratory confirmation. In the OTARR platform, patients were included if they had a positive toxicology test related to opioids or psychotropics between 1 January 2019 and 31 May 2021 (figure 1).

Study outcome

The primary outcomes of the study were the direct and prolonged rates of monthly ED visits and toxicological tests related to OUDs following the COVID-19 lockdown. The direct and prolonged rates of monthly ED visits and toxicological tests related to abuse/misuse of psychotropic substances following COVID-19 lockdown were assessed as secondary outcomes. Monthly data aggregation was used instead of weekly or bi-weekly because it allows for greater stability and interpretation of results than lower frequency aggregation that can create significant sparsity and volatility in the models and thereby increase the uncertainty in the estimated effects of interventions.

ED visits related to OUDs or to abuse/misuse of psychotropic substances were identified in the SRWEN database using codes from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (online supplemental table 2).26 27 Furthermore, we included visits during which an opioid or benzodiazepine antidote (ie, naloxone or flumazenil) prescription was dispensed.7 8 28 ED visits were finally confirmed using the results of toxicology tests related to opioids or psychotropic medications. In the OTARR platform, toxicological tests were identified based on the confirmed toxicology test type and result for clinical emergency.

Time series construction

The study spanned a 5-year period, from 1 February 2018 to 30 April 2022, in the SRWEN database and a 2-year period, from January 2019 to May 2021, in the OTARR platform. Both periods were divided into two distinct periods (pre-COVID-19 vs post-COVID-19 lockdown) to evaluate the study outcomes. The pre-COVID-19 versus post-COVID-19 assessment was based on March 2020, identified as the point of public intervention marking the onset of the COVID-19 lockdown in Saudi Arabia. To account for potential seasonal variations affecting ED visit patterns, each period included data from all four seasons.29

Statistical analysis

Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models were specified to estimate the direct (pulse) and the prolonged (ramp) changes temporally associated with the COVID-19 lockdown on the rates of monthly ED visits and confirmed tests related to OUDs and abuse/misuse of psychotropic substances. These rates were calculated per 10 000 ED visits/toxicology tests by dividing the number of monthly OUD- or SUD-related visits/toxicology tests by the total number of monthly ED visits/toxicology tests and then multiplying the resulting ratio by 10 000. The direct (pulse) effect captures the immediate change in ED visits/toxicology tests coinciding with the implementation of the COVID-19 lockdown, while the prolonged (ramp) effect reflects the month-to-month trends in ED visits/toxicology tests related to OUDs and abuse/misuse of psychotropic substances temporally associated with the lockdown period. The data for March 2020 were excluded from the analysis because it represents a transition period between the pre-intervention (pre-COVID-19 lockdown) baseline and full lockdown implementation. This exclusion avoids contamination of level-change estimates by lagged effects coinciding with the lockdown (ie, the during-implementation effect).

Diagnostics were conducted for the three model assumptions: stationarity, autocorrelation and seasonality.30 The stationarity assumption, requiring the mean, variance and autocovariance to remain constant over time, was evaluated using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test. This assumption was not met for the rates of ED visits and confirmatory tests related to OUDs, nor for the rate of ED laboratory-confirmed tests related to psychotropic substance abuse/misuse. As a result, first-order differencing was applied to address the identified non-stationarity prior to fitting ARIMA models. No autocorrelation was observed in the study data, as assessed through autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF). Seasonality was assessed visually and using ACF/PACF patterns. For monthly series, seasonal differencing used a seasonal period of 12 months (ie, lag-12 differencing) when indicated. The parameters of the final four ARIMA models, corresponding to the study’s primary and secondary outcomes, were determined using automated model selection algorithms. These algorithms selected the best-fitting models based on the lowest values of Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion.30 Furthermore, we performed multiple sensitivity analyses to examine temporal patterns against the primary analysis1: excluding the COVID-19 lockdown period,2 restricting the evaluation to the predominant gender subgroup and3 restricting the SRWEN analysis to laboratory-confirmed cases to better align with the OTARR platform (online supplemental figures 5–7). All statistical analyses were completed using R 4.4.2 and RStudio V.1.4.553, with an alpha significance level of 0.05. R packages used in this study were astsa, forecast, dplyr, zoo, tseries, aTSA and tabelone.

Results

Description of the study population

A total of 10 478 OUD- or SUD-related ED visits/toxicology tests were identified, of which 4356 were OUD- or SUD-related ED visits (3820 OUD-related and 536 SUD-related) and 6122 were toxicology test cases (796 opioid and 5326 psychotropic), within the period 2018 to 2022 in both datasets (online supplemental figure 1A–C). After excluding same-day duplicates and records lacking demographic information, we identified a total of 7977 OUD- or SUD-related ED visits/toxicology tests. In SRWEN, of the 4247 ED visits, 3730 (87.8%) were OUD-related, and 517 (12.2%) were psychotropic-related. While OTARR recorded, there were 3903 toxicology tests, of which 390 (10.0%) were OUD-related and 3513 (90.0%) were SUD-related (table 1). The mean age in the SRWEN dataset was 31.5 years, while the predominant age group in the OTARR platform was 19 to 40 years, accounting for 75.4% of the cases. In the SRWEN database, most cases were female (89.1%), while most cases were male in the OTARR platform (93.4%). Most cases in the SRWEN database were reported in the Saudi Central Region (n=3581 [84.3%]), while most cases in the OTARR platform were reported in the Western Region (n=1923 [49.3%]). The post COVID-19 lockdown period demonstrated a higher proportion of cases compared with the pre-COVID-19 lockdown period. In SRWEN, 58.0% of identified OUD/psychotropic-related ED visits occurred in the post-lockdown period, while in OTARR, 56.2% of positive toxicology tests occurred post-lockdown. Data from the OTARR platform provided detailed information on which SUDs were tested. Stimulants (especially amphetamine) were the most commonly reported substances of abuse in the OTARR platform, accounting for 1623 cases (41.6%). Information on substances of abuse was not reported in the SRWEN database. Complete patient characteristics are available in table 1.

Primary analysis: opioids-related emergency department visits/toxicology tests

In the SRWEN database, the ARIMA model showed that the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with an immediate increase (ie, a pulse effect) in the rate of ED visits related to OUDs by 14.75 ED visits per 10 000 (95% CI 9.65 to 19.86; p value: <0.001), which indicated an acute response to the lockdown (table 2). This sharp increase in the rate of ED visits was followed by a sustained decline in the rate (slope: −0.06; 95% CI −0.08 to −0.03; p value: <0.001). Figure 2 shows the predicted OUD-related ED visits under a counterfactual scenario where the COVID-19 lockdown did not occur compared with the observed data. Complete residuals diagnostics from regression are available in online supplemental figure 1. All sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary findings (ie, showed similar temporal patterns to the primary analysis), except when restricting to laboratory-confirmed cases, where the sustained decline in the rate was not statistically significant, supporting the robustness of our findings despite potential misclassification (online supplemental table 3).

Secondary analysis: psychotropic-related emergency department visits/toxicology tests

In the SRWEN database, the ARIMA model demonstrated no immediate increase in the rate of psychotropic-related ED visits (pulse: 1.7; 95% CI −0.4 to 3.9; p value: 0.12), as well as no general trend (slope: −0.01; 95% CI −0.04 to 0.02, p value: 0.6). Discussion

The study results showed that the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with a direct increase in the rate of OUD-related ED visits, which was subsequently followed by a steep decline. The lockdown was also temporally associated with a direct decrease in the rate of psychotropic-related confirmed tests.

Published studies have reported increased trends in ED visits and hospitalisations related to OUDs and psychotropic substance use following the COVID-19 lockdown, especially in the USA, Canada and the European Union.23 5,8 10 11 31 In these studies, documented ranges from 4.4 to 28.5 per 1000 encounters, with proportional increases in positive substance use screenings between 12.5% and 23.7%.23 5,8 10 11 31 Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with an immediate increase in OUD-related ED visits, followed by a gradual and sustained decrease. In contrast to the published studies, our study showed an immediate decrease in the rate of confirmed psychotropic substance use tests during the lockdown. These discrepancies in changes temporally associated with the lockdown on OUD-related ED visits and confirmed psychotropic substance use tests may be attributed to institutional and temporal differences between the two databases. Nevertheless, we cannot interpret the observed decrease in the rate of confirmed psychotropic substance use tests during the lockdown in OTARR as an actual decrease in psychotropic abuse/misuse, as it may reflect pandemic-related reductions in testing capacity, workflow disruptions or mobility restrictions rather than a true reduction in psychotropic abuse/misuse.32,34

The observed epidemiological discrepancies between SRWEN and OTARR likely reflect database structural and methodological differences rather than accurate epidemiological variation. While SRWEN captures de-identified, multi-institutional clinical ED diagnoses across both genders from a broad population, OTARR includes only toxicology-confirmed cases. Toxicology tests are more frequently conducted among males due to occupational, forensic and screening practices. Additionally, emerging evidence of increasing substance use and greater mental health help-seeking among females may also contribute to their higher representation in SRWEN.35 36

Our study represents the first large-scale regional investigation assessing the impact of the COVID-19 lockdown on SUDs. It also possesses methodological strengths stemming from the inclusion of two distinct regional databases to enhance generalisability, as well as from a robust analytical approach that employs ARIMA models with careful adjustment for various potential sources of bias. However, it should be noted that in this study we were not able to include several confounders because they are not available in the SRWEN or OTARR databases. These include socioeconomic status, psychiatric comorbidities, access to addiction services, changes in healthcare utilisation during lockdown and law enforcement activity during movement restrictions. To enhance future research and data accuracy, we recommend that these important confounders be captured more systematically in routine practice. Furthermore, the SRWEN database lacks information on the specific types of opioids and psychotropic substances used. It is therefore crucial for healthcare institutions in Saudi Arabia to consider systematically recording these variables to enable more comprehensive evaluations of public health interventions and policies. Given that the majority of suspected rather than confirmed cases in the SRWEN database, our analyses might be subject to potential risk of misclassification bias, which is likely to bias effect estimates towards the null. Future studies should incorporate additional social factors and more specific information about the types of substances involved. This expanded approach would offer a clearer understanding of the underlying inequalities in SUDs ED visits and support the development of more effective interventions both globally and within Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

Our study showed that the COVID-19 lockdown was temporally associated with changes in both OUD-related ED visits and confirmed psychotropic substances tests. These findings underscore the need for policies aimed at mitigating the risks of SUDs during such interventions and highlight the importance of improving data quality to enhance future research in this area.
