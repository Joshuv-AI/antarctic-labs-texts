Ward-Based High-Flow Nasal Cannula Led by the Medical Emergency Team: A Pragmatic Model for Resource Stewardship
Authors: Imran Khalid, Basel Ghurm Alshehri, Raafey Imran, Muhammad Ali Akhtar, Manahil Imran, Tabindeh Jabeen Khalid, Maryam Imran, Mohsin Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure is typically restricted to ICUs. We evaluated a ward-based, medical emergency team (MET)–supervised HFNC protocol (flow ≤ 40 L/min, Fio2 ≤ 0.40) with 2-/4-/8-hour nursing and respiratory therapist reassessments. Among 82 ward HFNC initiations (2021–2024), 38 (46%) required immediate ICU transfer (IMT) and 44 (54%) were Ward-Managed After MET (WMAM). Of WMAM patients, 18 transferred to ICU within 48 hours, and 26 remained on ward. WMAM patients accrued a median 1.46 ICU bed-days saved (interquartile range, 0.73–2.67); bootstrapped mean 1.63 (95% CI, 1.32–1.94), equivalent to 163 ICU days-saved per 100 initiations. Intubation (30% vs. 42%; p = 0.24) and 28-day mortality (32% vs. 39%; p = 0.47) were similar between WMAM and IMT; adjusted analyses were directionally consistent. Using an estimated $5,000 per ICU-day, cost avoidance was ≈$815,000 per 100 initiations. This MET-supervised model appears feasible, resource-sparing, and without apparent safety signal.

To the Editor:

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen is commonly used to support patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (AHRF). By delivering heated, humidified oxygen at high flows with precise Fio2 control, it improves oxygenation, reduces inspiratory effort, flushes dead space, and generates low-level positive airway pressure. These physiologic effects have translated into improved outcomes in ICU populations, including reduced intubation rates (1). HFNC is generally associated with improved comfort and less dyspnea compared with face masks, supporting its role as a valuable alternative to conventional oxygen systems.

Despite robust ICU evidence, adoption of HFNC on general wards has been limited. Conventional oxygen therapy is simple and low-resource, whereas HFNC requires specialized devices, titration, and closer monitoring. Owing to concerns that it may obscure deterioration and delay timely intubation, many institutions restrict HFNC use to monitored settings such as ICUs or step-down units (2). The possibility that nonresponders may appear transiently stable on HFNC has reinforced this caution, as delayed recognition of failure has been associated with worse outcomes (3).

During the COVID-19 pandemic, many centers broadened HFNC use on general wards. Multicenter reports confirmed feasibility but also flagged risks: higher mortality among nonresponders treated outside ICUs and dependence on specialist oversight (3–5). These pandemic-era models were crisis-driven and resource-intensive, often requiring physician rounds to maintain safety (6). Beyond COVID-19, evidence remains limited and largely confined to small, disease-specific cohorts (e.g., hematologic malignancy, rib fractures, interstitial lung disease near the end of life) (7). Collectively, these studies demonstrate feasibility but have not established a scalable, sustainable framework for diverse ward populations.

Meanwhile, pressures on ICU capacity are intensifying worldwide. With rising demand, limited staffed beds, and substantial per diem costs (conservatively estimated at $5000/d in the United States) (8), every ICU admission carries both direct financial and opportunity costs. Even modest reductions in avoidable admissions translate into meaningful system-level relief, particularly in hospitals where critical care occupancy routinely exceeds safe thresholds.

Extending HFNC safely beyond ICUs, therefore, requires reliable safeguards. The medical emergency team (MET) provides such a framework, bringing ICU expertise to deteriorating ward patients through structured rapid-response systems. Prior work shows that METs can safely deliver complex interventions like noninvasive ventilation on the wards in select patients (9). For HFNC, however, safety in many reports has depended on specialist or intensivist accompaniment rather than structured, protocolized follow-up. Embedding HFNC into standardized protocols, with predefined escalation thresholds and integrated monitoring tools such as the respiratory rate-oxygenation (ROX) index (10), offers a pragmatic strategy to balance safety with resource stewardship.

Building on this precedent, we evaluated outcomes of a ward-based HFNC strategy delivered under a standardized MET protocol. The primary aim was to quantify ICU bed-days saved, with secondary evaluation of safety outcomes including intubation and mortality.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a 500-bed tertiary academic hospital with a 31-bed ICU and active transplant programs. The MET, comprised of a medical resident, an ICU nurse, and a respiratory therapist, responded to over 1200 calls annually. All MET interventions and reassessments were documented in the electronic medical record Cerner (Cerner Corporation, Kansas City, Missouri). For this study, adherence to scheduled reassessments was verified through a review of EMR entries completed in real-time by nursing and respiratory therapy staff. Patient data for this study were collected under a waiver of consent as approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Center, Jeddah (Approval No. National Committee on Health Ethics/King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology Reg.# H-02-J-009/RAC-2024-122; protocol title: “Impact of Follow-up Policy by the Rapid Response Team on Patients Intensive Care Unit Transfer”; approval date: September 30, 2024). All study procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013.

Ward HFNC Protocol

The ward HFNC protocol, defined within the institutional ward-level respiratory care policy, mandated flow rates of less than or equal to 40 L/min and Fio2 less than or equal to 0.40. Brief exceedance during the initial MET evaluation was permitted; however, sustained requirements (> 30 min) for higher support mandated ICU transfer. These limits functioned as a standardized ward-level safety net and were consistently enforced throughout the study period. A ROX index less than 5 was the predefined escalation threshold for ICU care (10). For patients remaining on the ward, the MET nurse and respiratory therapist performed structured reassessments at 2, 4, and 8 hours after the MET encounter concluded. Subsequent monitoring was integrated into routine 4-hourly ward surveillance by the ward staff. The EMR automatically calculated the ROX index (oxygen saturation [Spo2]/Fio2 ÷ respiratory rate) from contemporaneous vital signs and Fio2 entries. Any predefined trigger (such as a ROX index < 5 or an EMR sepsis alert) or signs of clinical deterioration (including hemodynamic instability or abnormal laboratory values) prompted the MET nurse and respiratory therapist to notify the MET physician for immediate bedside evaluation and ICU consultation.

Patient Population

Between January 2021 and December 2024, we included adults (≥ 18 yr) with a MET activation for acute dyspnea or Spo2 less than 90% who required escalation beyond standard low-flow oxygen and were initiated on HFNC, consistent with AHRF. Per protocol, an arterial blood gas was obtained at or immediately after HFNC initiation. Patients with COVID-19 were excluded to evaluate the protocol for general hypoxemic respiratory failure distinct from the unique pathophysiology and resource pressures of the pandemic. Additional exclusions included hypercapnic respiratory failure, positive-pressure ventilation initiated during MET, immediate ICU transfer (IMT) without HFNC trial, or do-not-attempt-resuscitation (DNAR)/comfort-care status.

Patients were categorized as: 1) IMT, defined as ICU transfer during the MET call and 2) Ward-Managed After MET (WMAM), defined as continuation of HFNC on the ward beyond the MET. WMAM included patients with delayed ICU transfer (within 48 hr of MET conclusion) and those managed entirely on the ward (> 48 hr after MET conclusion).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ICU bed-days saved, defined as the total number of days of HFNC delivered on the ward before ICU transfer (for the delayed transfer group) or the total ward HFNC duration for those never transferred; IMT patients contributed zero by design. ICU bed-days saved were treated as a system-level capacity proxy (deferral of ICU occupancy), not a patient-centered endpoint.

Secondary outcomes were endotracheal intubation during the index hospitalization and 28-day mortality from the time of the MET encounter. Vital status at 28 days was determined via the EMR. Patients discharged alive before day 28 without a documented death in the EMR within that period were presumed alive.

Exploratory outcomes included hospital length of stay and illustrative cost avoidance, calculated using a conservative U.S. ICU per-diem estimate of $5000/d (not inflation-adjusted) (8).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms, then summarized as mean ± sd or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate; categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used the t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. For the primary outcome, ICU bed-days saved, we reported medians and bootstrapped means with 95% CIs (1000 replications). “Responder” proportions, defined as the proportion of patients avoiding greater than or equal to 24 and greater than or equal to 48 hours of ICU care, were estimated. Given potential confounding by indication, we performed brief, prespecified exploratory adjusted analyses for intubation and 28-day mortality. The adjusted models included age, gender, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II, Pao2/Fio2, sepsis, and baseline ROX index; adjusted risks were obtained from predictive margins. All analyses were conducted on a complete-case basis using Stata, Version 19.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Cohort and Baseline Features

Eighty-two patients met the inclusion criteria: 38 (46%) were designated as IMT and 44 (54%) as WMAM. Among WMAM patients, 26 (59%) remained on the ward throughout their HFNC course, and 18 (41%) required delayed ICU transfer. Gender distribution was comparable between groups (IMT 55% male vs. WMAM 52% male; p = 0.07). The most frequent causes of respiratory distress were pneumonia or pneumonitis (IMT 69% male vs. WMAM 64% male), followed by pulmonary edema/effusion and other etiologies, with similar proportions across groups (all p > 0.05). Baseline features were broadly similar between groups; however, the WMAM group presented with a higher initial ROX index (7.5 vs. 6) and a lower initial Fio2 (Table 1). A key finding was the greater early improvement observed in the WMAM group during the MET encounter. By the end of the MET call, the WMAM group demonstrated a more significant reduction in respiratory rate, decreasing from a mean of 32 to 24 breaths/min, whereas the IMT group’s respiratory rate decreased from 32 to 26.5 breaths/min. This clinical improvement was paralleled by a rise in the ROX index from 7.5 to 8.1 in the WMAM group, a larger increase than the rise from 6.0 to 6.8 seen in the IMT group. These early physiologic changes suggest that the protocol may have effectively identified patients more likely to respond to ward-based management.

Process and Safety Signals

Among WMAM patients, 35 of 44 (80%) completed all scheduled reassessments at 2, 4, and 8 hours. A ROX index less than 5 triggered escalation at one of these time points in 7 patients (16%). ROX data availability was high initially but decreased at later intervals: baseline and 2-hour ROX values were available for all patients, whereas 8-hour ROX was available for 50 of 82 total patients (61%); no imputation was performed. Most missing 8-hour ROX values occurred in patients who were escalated to the ICU, where ward-based HFNC was discontinued. Throughout the monitoring period, there were no ward cardiac arrests or airway emergencies; six repeat MET activations occurred, all of which resulted in ICU transfer.

Primary Outcome

WMAM patients accrued a median of 1.46 ICU bed-days saved (IQR, 0.73–2.67; Fig. 1), with a bootstrapped mean of 1.63 (95% CI, 1.32–1.94). The total ICU bed-days saved across the cohort were 70.3, representing a meaningful cumulative preservation of ICU capacity. A responder analysis showed that 64% (95% CI, 49–78%) of these patients avoided greater than or equal to 1 ICU day and 32% (95% CI, 17–46%) avoided greater than or equal to 2 ICU days. The results proved robust on sensitivity analysis: after excluding patients who died within 28 days, the bootstrapped mean was 1.57 (95% CI, 1.20–1.94); after excluding both deaths and transitions to DNAR status, the mean was 1.60 (95% CI, 1.15–2.05). Scaled to 100 ward initiations, this resource use reduction corresponded to 163 ICU bed-days preserved (95% CI, 132–194).

Secondary and Exploratory Outcomes

Intubation during the index hospitalization occurred in 42% of the IMT group (16/38) and 30% of the WMAM group (13/44). Twenty-eight-day mortality was 39% (15/38) vs. 32% (14/44), respectively; hospital length of stay was similar (median, 27 d [IQR, 12–46 d] vs. 21 d [IQR, 11–47 d]; p = 0.92).

Given the exploratory design and limited sample, the study was not powered to detect differences in intubation or mortality; adjusted analyses were prespecified as safety checks. Results were directionally consistent with the crude findings, and CIs included the possibility of no difference (adjusted intubation risk approximately 0.41 [IMT] vs. 0.30 [WMAM]; adjusted 28-d mortality risk approximately 0.45 vs. 0.30).

Among patients who ultimately reached the ICU, the post-MET ICU length of stay was similar between delayed transfers from the WMAM group and IMTs (median, 7.5 d [IQR, 7–13 d] vs. 6 d [IQR, 4–13 d]; p = 0.10), suggesting that ward “ICU bed-days saved” were not repaid by longer subsequent ICU stays. Within the WMAM group, patients managed entirely on the ward had a low intubation rate during the index hospitalization (2/26; 8%).

Using a benchmark cost of $5,000 per ICU day (8), the 163 ICU days preserved per 100 initiations corresponded to approximately $815,000 in illustrative avoided costs (not inflation-adjusted). At a range of $4,000–$6,000 per ICU day, avoided costs would range from approximately $652,000–$978,000 per 100 ward initiations.

DISCUSSION

This exploratory study suggests that a MET-supervised ward HFNC protocol can safely expand access to noninvasive respiratory support and reduce ICU bed occupancy when applied with clear limits and structured reassessment. In this cohort, the approach deferred or avoided ICU admission in more than half of patients with AHRF. This translated into meaningful capacity preservation and modeled cost avoidance, without a signal of increased intubation or mortality. Both crude and exploratory adjusted estimates were directionally consistent with these observations and are appropriately considered not definitive. While several larger prospective studies have examined ward-based HFNC use, our findings add a complementary, real-world perspective by evaluating a MET-supervised implementation model embedded within routine hospital practice.

A principal advantage of this approach is its operational efficiency and low-resource intensity. The protocol leveraged the existing MET structure, requiring only three scheduled reassessments (at 2, 4, and 8 hr) performed by the MET nurse and respiratory therapist, with on-demand MET physician involvement and no additional standing rounds. The use of explicit “40/40” safety-net ceilings (flow ≤ 40 L/min, Fio2 ≤ 0.40) combined with a simple ROX trigger for escalation standardized clinical decision-making. Trained MET personnel, clear escalation criteria, and reliable monitoring to promptly identify patients requiring ICU care were key to its successful implementation. This framework facilitated early identification of nonresponders while allowing improving patients to remain on the ward. Collectively, these features differentiate our model from prior reports that required daily specialist oversight during pandemic surges (3–6) and strongly support its scalability in routine operations.

Our findings align with ICU-based evidence demonstrating that HFNC improves oxygenation and reduces intubation risk (1). In contrast, experience with HFNC outside ICUs has been sporadic and disease-specific, often reliant on intensivist accompaniment (3–7). Despite the inevitable confounding by indication in this study, we observed no signal of harm associated with the ward-based protocol. By embedding HFNC within a rapid-response framework and utilizing predefined ceilings and triggers, we operationalized a reproducible ward model that effectively balances safety with resource stewardship.

Interestingly, the WMAM group presented with a higher initial ROX index and demonstrated a greater improvement in respiratory parameters following MET intervention, suggesting that these may be useful clinical indicators for identifying patients appropriate for a ward-based trial. Future prospective studies could refine patient selection by validating specific physiologic thresholds.

Importantly, post-MET ICU length of stay did not differ significantly between the delayed-ICU transfer and immediate-ICU transfer groups. This finding supports the interpretation that “ICU bed-days saved” represent a genuine initial deferral of ICU occupancy rather than a downstream prolongation of the total ICU stay. Furthermore, within the WMAM group, patients managed entirely on the ward had a very low intubation rate during the index hospitalization (2/26; 8%). This finding reinforces the premise that structured follow-up effectively filters nonresponders for timely ICU care.

From a systems perspective, ICU days are among the most constrained and costly resources. Avoiding 163 ICU days per 100 ward initiations, an illustrative estimate of ≈$815,000 using a conservative $5,000 per ICU-day (8), may meaningfully relieve capacity strain. Thus, even modest per-patient deferrals can translate into material institutional impact when scaled. These figures are based on a benchmark ICU-day cost rather than local institutional data and do not account for the costs of MET staffing, ward-level monitoring, or downstream complications; they should therefore be viewed as illustrative system-level estimates rather than formal cost-effectiveness data. In hospitals facing ICU capacity constraints, such a structured, MET-supervised ward HFNC pathway may serve as a practical surge-mitigation strategy, preserving ICU resources for patients with more severe illness while maintaining safety on the wards.

The findings of this single-center retrospective study, conducted over 4 years, should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. The analysis remains susceptible to selection bias and confounding by indication, as allocation to IMT vs. ward management was based on real-time clinical judgment rather than randomization; residual bias cannot be excluded despite adjusted analyses. The “40/40” ceilings were an intentional institutional safety-net design but may limit generalizability to centers using higher HFNC settings. The ROX less than 5 threshold was applied operationally to standardize escalation and was not revalidated here. Furthermore, “ICU bed-days saved” is a capacity proxy rather than a patient-centered endpoint, and the cost figures are illustrative estimates. The modest sample size also limits the precision of our estimates and increases the risk of a type II error for detecting true differences in secondary outcomes such as intubation and mortality. The study was also constrained by a relatively short follow-up interval and the absence of granular physiologic data, such as serial ROX index trends and detailed rationale for MET triage decisions, that could further clarify response trajectories.

Future work should focus on prospective, controlled evaluation of this ward-based HFNC model to better estimate causal effects on outcomes and costs. Incorporating standardized severity scores, physiologic response metrics, and time-to-escalation data could refine triage criteria and improve patient selection. Prospective studies with predefined safety endpoints are needed to confirm that this ward-based approach maintains patient safety across diverse settings. Formal cost-effectiveness analyses that account for MET staffing, equipment, and training requirements, as well as patient-centered outcomes such as comfort, functional recovery, and total hospital stay, are warranted. Finally, evaluating protocol variants with differing HFNC settings and reassessment intervals may help identify optimal thresholds for safety and effectiveness.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, a structured, MET-supervised ward HFNC model appears feasible, scalable, and resource-sparing for selected patients with AHRF. By leveraging the existing MET nurse-respiratory therapist team, it offers a pragmatic and low-intensity path to ICU capacity stewardship. Future work should confirm reproducibility across centers, refine patient selection criteria and ROX-based triggers, and assess formal cost-effectiveness and implementation outcomes, including staff and family perspectives.
