Bioefficacy of Trichoderma citrinoviride against some plant pathogenic fungi
Authors: Nesrain Abdulkareem Al-Mekhlafi, Amira Ali Al-Harethi, Nada Yahya Alzanam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrichoderma spp. are widely recognized as effective biocontrol agents used to control plant pests and reduce reliance on chemical pesticides. This study aimed to evaluate the in vitro antifungal activity of Trichoderma citrinoviride (AUMIC 13831) against five common plant pathogens: Alternaria sp., Aspergillus sp., Colletotrichum sp., Fusarium sp., and Rhizoctonia sp. Antagonistic potential was assessed using dual culture, volatile metabolite, non-volatile metabolite, and slide culture assays. T. citrinoviride exhibited significant antagonism across all methods tested. In the dual culture method, T. citrinoviride effectively inhibited the radial growth of all tested pathogens, showing inhibition percentages ranging from 50.88% ± 0.73 (against Rhizoctonia sp.) up to a maximum of 61.29% ± 1.86 (against Colletotrichum sp.). The non-volatile metabolites extracted from the culture filtrate proved to be the most potent fungistatic mechanism, demonstrating the highest overall efficiency, particularly against Fusarium sp. (80.95% ± 0.92) and Colletotrichum sp. (69.62% ± 0.72). The volatile metabolite assay exhibited moderate fungistatic effects, achieving a maximum growth inhibition of 36.36% ± 0.43(against Alternaria sp.). Furthermore, the mycoparasitic interaction between T. citrinoviride and the pathogenic fungi was confirmed through detailed microscopic observations. T. citrinoviride shows promising in vitro antifungal activity across multiple antagonistic mechanisms. Further studies in planta, toxicological/environmental assessments, and detailed metabolite identification using LC-MS are required to confirm its applicability as a commercial biocontrol agent. Introduction

Plants are an essential source of nutrition and form a major component of a healthy diet1. However, fungal diseases continue to pose a significant challenge to global agriculture and have remained a persistent threat for centuries2. Globally, it is estimated that plant diseases, with fungal pathogens being the primary culprits, account for the destruction of 10 to 20% of the world’s crops annually. Major agricultural diseases demand urgent and effective management strategies to safeguard crop yields.

Synthetic chemical fungicides have been widely used to manage plant diseases caused by pathogenic fungi3,4. Although these fungicides have contributed to reducing agricultural losses, their prolonged use presents several limitations. Pathogenic fungi may develop resistance, reducing fungicide efficacy over time. In addition, excessive use can negatively influence soil fertility, lead to the accumulation of harmful residues in crops, and cause a progressive decline in overall treatment performance5,6. Furthermore, chemical fungicides may affect beneficial non-target microorganisms and pose health and environmental risks7–9. Given these concerns, biological control has emerged as a promising and sustainable alternative strategy10,11.

Biological control utilizes beneficial microorganisms and their metabolites to suppress plant pathogens and decrease reliance on chemical inputs. Among the most extensively studied biocontrol agents are species of the genus Trichoderma. These fungi are widespread in diverse soil environments and are recognized as plant symbionts, saprotrophs, and mycoparasites12,13. Trichoderma is a biological fungus that exhibits significant potential and application value in the biological management of phytopathological diseases11. Trichoderma species employ multiple modes of action, including mycoparasitism, antibiosis, competition for nutrients and space, induction of plant systemic resistance, and secretion of hydrolytic enzymes14,15. Their rapid growth, abundant sporulation, strong environmental adaptability, and ability to colonize plant roots further enhance their effectiveness as biofungicides.

A key feature of Trichoderma species is their ability to produce diverse secondary metabolites, including both volatile and non-volatile compounds such as peptaibols, butenolides, pyridines, koninginins, and polyketides. Volatile organic compounds, including methyl jasmonate, can modulate plant physiological responses and influence rhizosphere microbial communities, thereby enhancing natural defense mechanisms against phytopathogens16–18. These metabolites play central roles in suppressing competing pathogens, promoting plant growth, and are increasingly utilized in the development of biofertilizers and biocontrol formulations19.

Among the taxonomically diverse genus Trichoderma, T. citrinoviride is recognized as a clinically and ecologically significant species, belonging to the longibrachiatum clade20. This species has been shown to be a highly effective biocontrol agent against various plant pathogens through multiple mechanisms21–23. However, the antagonistic potential of geographically specific isolates can vary significantly due to local adaptation and unique secondary metabolite profiles. Therefore, a comprehensive and multi-methodological in vitro assessment is essential to validate the utility of a specific strain.

We hypothesize that the local isolate, T. citrinoviride (AUMIC 13831), demonstrates a broad antagonistic potential against a range of major phytopathogenic microorganisms through the synergistic action of both volatile and non-volatile secondary metabolites, as well as direct mycoparasitic interaction.

The present study aimed to evaluate the antifungal activity of T. citrinoviride (AUMIC 13831) in vitro against several economically important plant pathogenic fungi—specifically Alternaria sp., Aspergillus sp., Colletotrichum sp., Fusarium sp., and Rhizoctonia sp. —using different methodologies. These methods included dual culture (measuring competition and direct antibiosis), volatile and non-volatile metabolite assays (quantifying fungistatic compound efficacy), and slide culture (microscopic confirmation of mycoparasitism), thus providing a comprehensive assessment of its biocontrol potential for future field applications.

Results

Antifungal activity of T. citrinoviride via dual culture method

Dual-culture assays demonstrated that T. citrinoviride was effective at inhibiting the mycelial growth of the tested plant pathogenic fungi with different growth inhibition percentages. The fungi most susceptible to T. citrinoviride were Colletotrichum sp. (61.29% ± 1.86), followed by Alternaria sp. (57.14% ± 0.01), Fusarium sp. (51.16% ± 0.05), Rhizoctonia sp. (50.88% ± 0.73) and in contrast, the lowest antagonistic activities of T. citrinoviride against Aspergillus sp. (28.57% ± 0.32). Slide culture method

Microscopic analyses revealed pronounced mycoparasitic activity of T. citrinoviride against all examined pathogenic fungi. Distinct and sequential stages of the mycoparasitic interaction were observed, encompassing hyphal adhesion, coiling around the host hyphae, penetration of the host cell wall, and subsequent lysis of the pathogenic fungal structures. In regions where hyphae overlapped, T. citrinoviride maintained a uniform and well-organized hyphal morphology, in contrast to the pathogenic fungi, which exhibited marked structural disorganization. The hyphae of the pathogens appeared disrupted, fragmented, and deformed, suggesting extensive cellular damage. Moreover, clear evidence of cell wall degradation and cytoplasmic disintegration was detected in the pathogenic hyphae, confirming the strong antagonistic potential and lytic capability of T. citrinoviride during its mycoparasitic interaction with the target fungi (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The dual culture assay conducted in the present study revealed that T. citrinoviride displayed the greatest inhibitory potential against several phytopathogenic fungi, with inhibition percentages ranging from 28.57% ± 0.32 to 61.29% ± 1.86, highlighting its broad-spectrum antifungal capability. These findings are consistent with prior reports on the high antagonism levels demonstrated by T. citrinoviride and other Trichoderma species against a range of pathogens, underscoring the genus’s robust biocontrol potential23–33.

The current investigation utilized Bell’s scale to qualitatively evaluate the direct antagonistic interactions. T. citrinoviride consistently demonstrated moderate to strong antagonistic capabilities, classifying it as Class 2 for the majority of pathogens (occupying approximately two-thirds of the culture plate), while exhibiting a more pronounced interaction class 1 against Alternaria sp. This ability to rapidly colonize and aggressively compete for resources is a fundamental characteristic of successful biocontrol agents, aligning with similar Class 1 and Class 2 interactions reported for highly effective strains like T. harzianum and T. viride34.

The study highlights the potent role of secondary metabolites. The non-volatile culture filtrates exhibited remarkable fungistatic activity, particularly against Fusarium sp. (80.95%) and Colletotrichum sp. (69.62%). These high inhibition rates contrast with the generally lower inhibition rates reported in comparable studies using culture filtrates of other Trichoderma species30,35–37.

The activity of volatile metabolites from T. citrinoviride presented promising but generally less potent results, achieving a maximum inhibition of 36.36% against Alternaria sp. These results are consistent with previous reports, in which volatile metabolites of Trichoderma species also exhibited relatively low inhibition percentages against different pathogenic fungi36,38. In contrast, Răut et al.39 demonstrated complete (100%) inhibition of R. solani by volatiles from Trichoderma T36, and total inhibition of R. solani and Pythium ultimum with just 50% culture filtrate concentration.

Synthesis of Findings and Mechanistic Link: Crucially, the higher inhibition rates observed in the non-volatile assays suggest that soluble metabolites—the products of antibiosis—play a more dominant role in this isolate’s overall antagonism compared to volatile compounds or direct competitive mechanisms. This indicates that the primary mode of action is the production of potent, stable antibiotic compounds.

The observed variation in susceptibility among the pathogens is primarily due to their intrinsic defense mechanisms, such as differences in cell wall structure, melanization, and the formation of resilient structures like sclerotia40–44.

Trichoderma species are well known for producing a wide variety of secondary metabolites with potent biological activities. These compounds, both volatile and non-volatile, exhibit antimicrobial, antifungal, and cytotoxic properties, and play key roles in pathogen suppression and plant growth regulation. Notably, many of these metabolites—such as peptaibols, terpenes, polyketides, and steroids—can cause mycolysis in target fungi, leading to cell wall lysis and abnormal hyphal growth. Comprehensive studies have catalogued hundreds of these bioactive compounds, underscoring their potential as sources of novel pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals45–49.

In the present study, microscopic analysis provided compelling evidence of mycoparasitism against all tested fungi. Various stages of direct interaction were observed, including hyphal adhesion, coiling, penetration, and subsequent lysis of the pathogenic hyphae. These observations are in strong agreement with previous reports, which have similarly documented Trichoderma species—such as T. virens and T. citrinoviride—exhibiting hyperparasitic interactions involving coiling, penetration, hyphal disintegration, and cytoplasmic degradation of pathogens like R. solani, Botrytis cinerea, and Pythium spp. Collectively, both the current and earlier studies highlight the multifaceted parasitic mechanisms of Trichoderma, emphasizing its effectiveness in directly attacking and suppressing a wide range of phytopathogenic50–54.

Several recent studies have emphasized the role of Trichoderma species as potent biological control agents against diverse phytopathogenic fungi. Although these studies collectively confirm the remarkable antagonistic potential of Trichoderma spp., most have been limited to specific host–pathogen systems or focused primarily on either metabolite characterization or greenhouse efficacy55–58.

The present study provides a novel and comprehensive in vitro evaluation of a locally sourced T. citrinoviride strain against a broad spectrum of pathogens, integrating three distinct antagonistic methodologies. Additionally, microscopic observations reveal direct mycoparasitic interactions, providing clear evidence of antagonistic mechanisms. However, its descriptive and comparative nature imposes certain limitations that delineate future research directions.

Firstly, the most significant limitation is the lack of mechanistic or biochemical analysis of the active compounds. Although strong antifungal activity is confirmed, the specific molecules responsible are not definitively identified. Therefore, a critical next step is the chemical characterization of the active secondary metabolites using advanced techniques like Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS).

Secondly, the biocontrol efficacy was only validated in vitro. While these results are promising, they cannot fully predict performance in complex environments. Future studies must focus on validating the efficacy and safety of this strain through rigorous greenhouse and field trials (in planta validation), coupled with toxicological and environmental assessments, to confirm its applicability as a commercial, sustainable agrochemical.

Materials and methods

Sterilization protocol

All culture media (PDA) and glassware (Petri dishes, flasks, glass slides) used throughout the study were sterilized following standard microbiological protocols via autoclaving at (autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min). Aseptic conditions were strictly maintained during all inoculation and transfer steps using a laminar flow hood.

Source and maintenance of T. citrinoviride

The strain of T. citrinoviride (AUMIC 13831) was obtained from the stock culture collection at the Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Biological Sciences, Sana’a University, Yemen. This strain was originally isolated, identified, and maintained by our research group59 from soil samples collected between 2013 and 2015.

The initial identification of T. citrinoviride was confirmed using the taxonomic keys of Rifai60, Bissett61, and Samuels et al.62 by the Assiut University, Mycological Center (AUMC), Egypt.

Following its initial purification and confirmation, the pure culture was preserved using established long-term techniques. The culture was maintained on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) slants under refrigeration at 4 °C for short-term storage. For long-term preservation, the culture was stored under a layer of sterile mineral oil (paraffin oil) on PDA slants to minimize metabolic activity and desiccation. These preserved slants were maintained at 4 °C in the laboratory’s cold storage facility.

For the present investigation, the culture was revived by transferring a small fragment of the preserved mycelium to fresh PDA plates, which were then incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for 5–7 days. To ensure optimal vitality and purity, the strain was sub-cultured onto fresh PDA plates three times prior to use in all subsequent experiments, and its morphological purity was routinely confirmed via microscopy.

Identification of phytopathogenic fungi

The pathogenic fungi (Alternaria sp., Aspergillus sp. and Fusarium sp.) were obtained from established stock cultures at the Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Biological Sciences, Sana’a University, Yemen. Colletotrichum sp. and Rhizoctonia sp. were obtained from the Plant Protection Center.

Prior to use, the identity of each pathogenic isolate was confirmed through routine morphological and cultural characterization using standard mycological keys.

The fungal isolates used in this study originated from various infected plant hosts exhibiting characteristic disease symptoms. Aspergillus sp. and Fusarium sp. were isolated from onion (Allium cepa) bulbs affected by black mould rot and Fusarium basal rot, respectively. Alternaria sp. was obtained from diseased tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) fruits showing early blight symptoms. Colletotrichum sp. was isolated from infected clover (Trifolium alexandrinum) leaves displaying anthracnose lesions, while Rhizoctonia sp. was recovered from Catha edulis roots exhibiting root rot. These fungal isolates were used as representative plant pathogens in the experimental assays.

Each fungal isolate was cultured on PDA at 28 ± 2 °C, and observations were made regarding colony color, texture, shape, pigment production, and growth rate. Microscopic examination was performed using lactophenol cotton blue-stained mounts prepared from actively growing colonies. Identification was based on the observation of reproductive and vegetative structures at 400× magnification. For Ascomycetes and Coelomycetes, diagnostic features included the arrangement of conidiophores, conidial shape and septation (such as dictyospores in Alternaria sp. and falcate macroconidia in Fusarium sp.), and the presence of acervuli in Colletotrichum sp. In Basidiomycetes, identification relied on hyphal characteristics, particularly the formation of sclerotia and right-angle branching with septa typical of Rhizoctonia sp. All isolates were maintained on PDA slants at 4 °C for short-term storage and were sub-cultured regularly to preserve their vitality before experimental assays.

Antifungal activity of T. citrinoviride via dual culture method

Plates containing PDA were inoculated with a 0.5 cm disc derived from a five-day-old culture of plant pathogenic fungi, positioned 10 mm from the periphery of the plate. Following a two-day incubation period, a 0.5 cm disc of T. citrinoviride (5 days) was subsequently placed on the opposite side of the pathogenic fungi disc63. In the control plate (pathogenic fungi without T. citrinoviride), a 0.5 cm disc of pathogenic fungi was placed at the edge of the plate, 10 mm from the periphery. The inoculated plates were incubated at 28 ± 1 °C for 5–7 days. The experiments were conducted in triplicate. After 7 days, the radial growth of the pathogen was measured in both the control and presence of antagonists (T. citrinoviride). Degree of antagonism between T. citrinoviride and plant pathogenic fungi

The purpose of the experiment is to determine whether T. citrinoviride is effective as a biocontrol agent by evaluating its competitive ability against plant pathogenic fungi. The plates were observed and growth antagonism ratings were made using the bell’s scale65 as follow:

Class 1: The antagonist completely overgrows the pathogen and occupies 100% of the medium surface.

Class 2: The antagonist overgrows at least three-quarters (75%) of the pathogen surface.

Class 3: The antagonist colonizes one-half (50%) of the pathogen’s growth.

Class 4: The pathogen and antagonist colonize approximately equal areas, or the pathogen slightly dominates the antagonist (< 50% overgrowth by the antagonist).

Class 5: The pathogen completely overgrows the antagonist and occupies the entire medium surface (100% pathogen dominance).

Effects of non-volatile metabolites

To determine the effects of non-volatile metabolites of T. citrinoviride on the mycelial growth of pathogenic fungi, three discs of mycelial agar plugs (5 mm in diameter) from the periphery of a young T. citrinoviride culture were inoculated into 100 ml of sterilized potato dextrose broth (PDB) contained in a 250 ml conical flask. Incubation was conducted at 28 ± 1 °C in a shaker incubator at 100 rpm for 14 days. The control conical flask was inoculated with three sterile PDA medium plugs (5 mm in diameter). The obtained filtrate was subsequently incorporated into molten PDA to achieve a final concentration of 10% v/v (was prepared by measure 10 mL of the filtrate into a sterile measuring vessel, then add 90 mL of PDA to bring the final volume to 100 mL). This medium was then distributed into Petri dishes at a volume of 20 ml per plate, followed by the inoculation of 5 mm mycelial plugs from the pathogens at the center of each plate, and incubated at 28 ± 1 °C for 7 days or until the fungal colony reached the periphery of the petri dish66. There were 3 replicates for each treatment. The inhibition percentage was calculated after 7 days as based on the formula mentioned in the dual culture method.

Effects of volatile metabolites

A mycelial disc (5 mm in diameter) derived from the margin of a 5-day-old culture of pathogenic fungi was positioned centrally on a PDA Petri plate. After a period of forty-eight hours, a disc measuring 5 mm in diameter from the 5-day-old culture of T. citrinoviride was placed at the center of a new PDA petri plate. Subsequently, the lids of these Petri dishes were removed within a sterile hood, and the dish harboring the plant pathogenic fungi was inverted on a Petri dish containing T. citrinoviride and sealed with parafilm. In control plate, the antagonistic fungus T. citrinoviride was replaced by a disc from PDA medium. The cultures were incubated at 28 ± 1 °C for 7 days. The percentage of inhibition was calculated after 7 days later67 based on the formula mentioned in the dual culture method.

Slide culture method

The mycoparasitic activity of T. citrinoviride against selected plant pathogenic fungi was assessed using the slide culture technique (microscopic assay). This method was chosen to directly observe hyphal interactions between the antagonist and the target pathogens.

To prepare the setup, PDA was poured into Petri dishes to a depth of approximately 3–4 mm and allowed to solidify. A sterile glass slide was gently pressed onto the surface of the solidified medium to form an impression. The agar was cut along the impression using a sterile blade, and the marked section was excised to create a hollow area. A sterile glass slide was then placed into the cavity, ensuring complete contact with the PDA surface. A 5-mm mycelial disc of each pathogenic fungus (5-day-old) was placed on one edge of the slide, while a similar disc of T. citrinoviride (5-day-old culture) was placed on the opposite edge (Fig. 3). The entire setup was incubated at 28 ± 1 °C.

Statistical analysis

The antagonistic activity of T. citrinoviride against plant pathogenic fungi was measured by the diameter of the inhibition zone (cm). The experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as the mean of triplicates ± standard deviation by IBM SPSS Statics, version 27. The homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Tukey test and one-way ANOVAs, differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Conclusion

The antagonistic strain T. citrinoviride (AUMIC 13831) exhibited strong antifungal activity against a range of plant pathogenic fungi, including Alternaria sp., Aspergillus sp., Colletotrichum sp., Fusarium sp., and Rhizoctonia sp., as demonstrated through various in vitro assays. The pronounced inhibition observed under non-volatile conditions suggests that its antagonistic activity is primarily mediated by soluble metabolites rather than volatile compounds or direct microbial interactions, as shown in the dual culture assay. Microscopic evidence of mycoparasitic behavior further supports the multifaceted antagonistic potential of T. citrinoviride. Overall, the results highlight the promising potential of this local isolate as a biocontrol agent for the sustainable management of plant diseases. Further studies are essential to confirm these findings and ensure safe application. These must include greenhouse validation (in planta efficacy), comprehensive toxicological and environmental assessments, and, critically, the definitive structural identification of the active secondary metabolites using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS).
