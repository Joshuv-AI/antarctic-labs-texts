The influence of caregiver’s malaria-related knowledge on the use of insecticide-treated net among children under-five: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Emmanuel Kumah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a critical public health challenge globally, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa where it significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality among children under-five. In Ghana, efforts to promote the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) as a preventive measure have been substantial but utilization rates remain suboptimal. This study examines the influence of caregivers' malaria-related knowledge on ITN use among children under the age of five years in a rural district of Ghana.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the Ahafo Ano South West District, Ghana, between June and October 2023. A total of 442 caregivers (mothers) of children under-five were selected using a two-stage sampling process. Data were collected through structured interviews and analysed using bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models to determine the association between caregivers’ malaria-related knowledge and ITN use among children.ResultsOut of 442 caregivers, 436 (98.6%) completed the survey. ITN use among children under-five the night before the survey was reported by 73.8% of caregivers. Overall, 36.8% of caregivers had good malaria-related knowledge, 41.2% had satisfactory knowledge, and 21.9% had poor knowledge. Multivariable analysis showed that caregivers with good malaria-related knowledge were 12 times more likely to use ITNs for their children (AOR = 12.06, 95% CI 2.30–53.20) compared to those with poor knowledge. Other significant predictors included education on ITN use, ITN ownership, child's age, and use of alternative malaria prevention methods.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical role of caregiver malaria-related knowledge in promoting ITN use among children under-five in rural Ghana. Caregivers with better knowledge were significantly more likely to use ITNs, emphasizing the need for targeted health education programs. Such interventions should enhance awareness of malaria risks and ITN benefits, empower caregivers to use ITNs effectively, and reduce barriers to ITN access. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers aiming to improve ITN utilization and reduce malaria morbidity and mortality in vulnerable populations.Keywords: Malaria, Insecticide-treated net, Caregiver knowledge, Children under-five, Public health, Ghana

Background

Malaria remains a significant global public health challenge, with approximately half of the world's population at risk of infection [1, 2]. Annually, nearly one million people succumb to the disease. In 2022, there were an estimated 249 million malaria cases and 608,000 malaria-related deaths reported across 85 countries worldwide [3]. The burden is particularly severe in the WHO African Region, which bears 94% of malaria cases (233 million) and 95% (580,000) of malaria-related deaths. Alarmingly, children under the age of 5 years constitute about 80% of all malaria deaths in this region [3].

Malaria poses a significant public health challenge in Ghana, particularly affecting children under-five, where it is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality. The 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) reveals a notable prevalence of 8.6% among children aged 6 to 59 months, with rural areas exhibiting a substantially higher prevalence of 12.8% compared to 4.3% in urban settings [4]. These observations underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions to combat malaria, considering its substantial contribution, accounting for over 38% of outpatient illnesses and approximately 31% of deaths in this vulnerable age group in Ghana [2].

The link between malaria and mortality among under-five children presents a significant threat to the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.2, which aims to reduce under-five mortality rates to internationally agreed levels, of at least 25 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2030 [5]. However, many countries, particularly those in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) such as Ghana, face challenges in meeting this objective. Therefore, tackling the issue of malaria among children under-five would greatly contribute to the global effort to combat under-five mortality [3].

One of the most effective tools for malaria prevention is the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) [6]. Consistent use of ITNs has been shown to reduce malaria transmission by up to 90% and mortality in children under-five by 44% [7]. Consequently, there has been a surge in both international and national funding and initiatives aimed at expanding the distribution of ITNs. Globally, 18 out of 31 countries (58%) successfully completed continuous ITN distribution campaigns, resulting in the distribution of 159 million ITNs, covering 72% of the targeted population in 2020 [6, 7].

Ghana has made concerted efforts to improve the affordability, ownership, and usage of ITNs since the establishment of the Roll Back Malaria (RBM) initiative in 1999. In 2002, the government of Ghana removed taxes on ITN imports and implemented a policy to freely distribute ITNs to pregnant women during their first antenatal visit to increase household utilization among children under-five [8]. In 2013, the National Malaria Control Programme (NACP), with funding from the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), held a school-based free ITN distribution campaign that reached 1.2 million primary school children [9]. Furthermore, through collaborative efforts with UNICEF, the Department for International Development (DFID), and other donors, the Ghana Health Service (GHS) facilitated the free distribution of nearly 12.5 million ITNs between December 2010 and October 2012, covering all regions of the country through a universal mass distribution campaign [10, 11]. These efforts have contributed to a progressive increase in ITN ownership in Ghana, with 74% of households owning at least one ITN as of 2019 [11].

However, utilization of ITNs in Ghana has not increased as anticipated. Across the country, the ratio of ITN use to access typically falls between 0.4 and 0.6, with lower figures observed in urban and peri-urban areas. According to the 2019 Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS), the ratio of ITN use to access stands at 0.47 in urban areas and 0.77 in rural areas [10]. The pattern of ITN use among children mirrors that of the general population. Between 2006 and 2019, ITN use among children under-five rose from 22 to 54% [10]. The 2022 Demographic and Health Survey reveals that 49% of under-five children slept under an ITN the night before the survey across all households, while among households with at least one ITN, this figure rose to 62% [4]. Thus, to achieve the National Malaria Control Programme’s goal of increasing the percentage of children under-five sleeping under ITNs to 85% [11], significant efforts lie ahead.

Several factors contributing to low ITN use among children under five in Ghana have been identified. These include the child's age, household size, wealth index, education level of the household head, place of residence (rural vs. urban), ownership and knowledge of ITN use, and the number of antenatal care visits [12–15]. Older children are less likely to sleep under ITNs, potentially due to limited household availability of bed nets or a belief they need less protection as they experience fewer malaria episodes [12]. Furthermore, households with more children under five, larger household sizes, and lower wealth quintiles are associated with lower ITN use [13, 14]. The education level of the household head is also significant, as higher educational attainment often correlates with better ITN use, as does rural residency where mosquito exposure is higher [13, 15].

Additionally, cultural factors play a role in ITN use; for instance, different sleeping patterns across ages and genders can influence ITN coverage [16]. In many households, older boys may sleep separately from younger children, and men and women may not always sleep together. These variations affect decisions about how many ITNs are purchased and used per household, as well as the prioritization of who sleeps under a bed net [16]. Such cultural nuances are often overlooked in global malaria control strategies, which may assume uniform sleeping arrangements and underestimate the number of nets required per household.

In a recent analysis, Yeboah et al. [14] examined data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey to explore how mothers' ITN use correlates with ITN use among children under-five. The authors observed that mothers who slept under ITN were more likely to have their children sleep under a mosquito bed net (RRR = 2.47, 95% CI 1.48, 4.12; p < 0.001), highlighting the influence of maternal behavior on children's protective practices.

The present study builds on these previous studies by providing evidence on the role of caregiver's malaria-related knowledge in influencing the use of ITNs among children under-five in a rural district in Ghana. Within the framework of the Health Belief Model [17], this study assumes that caregivers who have a higher level of malaria-related knowledge are more likely to perceive themselves and their children as being at risk of malaria, perceive malaria as a serious threat to their child's health, believe in the effectiveness of ITNs in preventing malaria, and perceive fewer barriers to using ITNs. This suggests that increasing caregivers' knowledge about malaria could lead to greater use of ITNs among children under-five. The study was guided by the following research question: Does caregiver’s malaria-related knowledge significantly influence the use of ITN among children under-five? The findings could inform policy and interventions aimed at improving ITN use among children in Ghana.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted to investigate the role of caregiver's malaria-related knowledge in influencing the use of ITNs among children under-five in the Ahafo Ano South West District in the Ashanti Region of Ghana between June and October, 2023. The Ahafo Ano South West District shares borders with four neighboring districts: to the north lies the Ahafo Ano South East District, to the south is the Atwima Mponua District, to the east is the Atwima Nwabiagya Municipal, and to the west is the Ahafo Ano North Municipal, all within the Ashanti Region [18]. Housing 107 communities, the district's population totals 65,770, accounting for 1.5% of the Ashanti Region’s populace. Males make up 50.9% of the population, while females constitute 49.1%. Children aged 0–17 years comprise 43.0%, working adults aged 18–59 years form 50.4%, the elderly (60 years and older) represent 6.6%, and the youth population aged 15–35 years amounts to 35.8%. The district has a total of 11 health facilities, with a doctor-to-population ratio of 1:33,773, a nurse-to-population ratio of 1:543, a midwife/WIFA (Women of Reproductive Age) ratio of 1:539, and health coverage extending to 1:9 communities [18]. The district was selected for this study due to its active involvement in ITN distribution campaigns. Malaria transmission persists year-round, with heightened occurrence during the rainy season, typically spanning April to November [18].

Study population, sample size and sampling procedure

The study focused on children under five years old. However, 442 caregivers (mothers) of these children, residing in 13 selected communities within the Ahafo Ano South West District, were invited to participate. The sample size of 442 was determined using the single population proportion formula [19], considering a 95% confidence level, a 49% rate of ITN use among children under five [4], a 5% margin of error, and a 20% nonresponse rate.

The respondent selection process comprised a two-stage sampling approach. Initially, a simple random sampling method was utilized to choose 13 out of the 107 communities within the district. To execute this, the names of the communities were written on uniformly sized pieces of paper. These papers were then folded, deposited into a bowl, and thoroughly mixed. A random selection procedure was performed by asking a passer-by to draw one paper from the bowl, noting down the chosen community's name. This process was repeated until 13 communities were selected. Subsequently, a consecutive sampling method was applied to select 34 caregivers of children under-five from each of the 13 chosen communities. Consecutive sampling involves selecting every eligible subject meeting the inclusion criteria until the desired sample size is met [20]. This method was preferred for its convenience in recruiting eligible participants successively until the requisite sample size was achieved. The 13 communities that participated in the study were: Okroase, Abompe, Kunso, Oseikrom, Mankranso, Barniekrom, Mpasaaso, Nyinanufu, Asuako, Domeabra, Wiawso, Mposo, and Awadua Nkwanta.

Variables and measures

The study’s primary outcome variable was the reported use of ITN for a child under-five the night before the study. This was a binary categorical outcome; where 0 represented non-use of ITN for a child the night before the study, and 1 for use of ITN for the child the night before the study.

The main predictor, malaria-related knowledge, encompassed a spectrum of dimensions shaped by five key factors: understanding the disease's cause, its transmission mechanisms, effective prevention methods, available treatments, and individual susceptibility. In assessing the caregivers’ malaria-related knowledge, 11 items aligned with these dimensions were employed, adapted from previous knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) studies [21–25]. Each item offered three response options: "Yes," "No," and "Do not know." For scoring, a correct response received a score of 1, while an incorrect or "Do not know" response was assigned a score of 0. Subsequently, the caregivers' knowledge scores were calculated by dividing the total number of correct responses by the total number of items and multiplying the result by 100. This method ensured that knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 100%, reflecting the percentage of accurately answered items. To interpret the scores, Bloom's cutoff points were utilized. Scores between 80 and 100% were classified as indicative of good knowledge, those between 60 and 79% as satisfactory, and scores equal to or below 59% as poor [26]. These categories were then coded numerically as follows: good knowledge = 3, satisfactory knowledge = 2, and poor knowledge = 1.

To address potential confounders, eight variables consistently associated with ITN use among children under-five were included in the analysis. These variables were: caregiver's number of children under five (categorized as 1, > 1), education on ITN and its use (Yes, No), use of alternative malaria prevention methods to ITN (Yes, No), caregiver's age (17–25, 26–35, 36 + years), child's age (< 12 months, 12–24 months, > 24 months), ITN ownership (Yes, No), caregiver's level of education (no formal education, primary, secondary, or higher), and caregiver's employment status (unemployed, employed).

Data collection

Data was collected from the caregivers using a structured interview guide, which included sections on background information, ITN ownership and use, knowledge about malaria, and knowledge about ITN use. A community-based data collection approach was employed for this study. To ensure construct validity, the survey instrument was pre-tested among 40 caregivers in Tepa, located in the Ahafo Ano North Municipal of the Ashanti Region, Ghana. This pre-test allowed for the rephrasing of ambiguous items in the instrument. Five field assistants were trained to assist with the data collection process.

Data analysis

Bivariate and multivariable analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between the outcome and explanatory variables. Chi-square tests assessed the significance and association between ITN use among children under-five and each explanatory variable. A multiple logistic regression model was then fitted to test the association between ITN use among children under-five and caregivers’ malaria-related knowledge, with the other explanatory variables introduced as controls. Variables significantly associated with ITN use among children under-five were identified based on the odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and p-values ≤ 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used as the first step in identifying the presence of multicollinearity among the explanatory variables. Tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values were then used for further analysis. All the test results indicated that multicollinearity was not a threat to the regression model. All analyses were done using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 20 (IBM© Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was sought from the Ghana Health Service Ethical Review Board (Protocol ID No. GHS-ERC 029/03/22) before conducting the study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. The study used unique identifiers for respondents to ensure the anonymity of participants. In addition, data collection was conducted solely to answer the research question in a manner that maximized the privacy and confidentiality of respondents.

Results

Out of the 442 administered questionnaires, 436 were returned, resulting in a response rate of 98.6%. The average age of the caregivers was 31.66 ± 9.74 years, while the average age of the children was 18.84 ± 12.83 months. The majority of the caregivers were employed (61.1%), had a primary level of education (41.6%), and had more than one child under five years old (52.6%). Regarding ITN ownership and use, 79.3% of the caregivers indicated that they owned an ITN, 73.8% reported that their child had used a bed net the night before the survey, 72.6% stated that they had received education on ITN use, and 46.2% mentioned that they had an alternative malaria prevention method to ITN, such as using mosquito repellents, maintaining environmental cleanliness, and installing window screens. Malaria-related knowledge

The results on caregivers’ malaria-related knowledge are presented in Table 2. Overall, the caregivers demonstrated adequate knowledge of malaria: 36.8% had good knowledge, 41.2% had satisfactory knowledge, and only 21.9% had poor knowledge. Among those with good knowledge of malaria, 73.6% reported that their child slept under an ITN the night before the survey, while 26.4% did not. Bivariate analysis of factors associated with ITN use among children under five

After controlling for six explanatory variables in the multivariable logistic regression model, caregiver malaria-related knowledge emerged as an independent predictor of ITN use among children under-five. Caregivers with good knowledge of malaria were found to be 12 times more likely (AOR = 12.06, 95% CI 2.30–53.20) to let their children sleep under an ITN compared to those with poor knowledge.

Other variables independently associated with ITN use among children under-five included: education on ITN use, the number of children the caregiver had, the child's age, ITN ownership, and the use of alternative malaria prevention methods. The odds of children sleeping under an ITN were higher among respondents who had received education on ITN use (AOR = 2.46, 95% CI 1.12–10.60), whose children were aged 12–23 months (AOR = 1.23, 95% CI 0.03–2.59) or younger, and those who reported owning an ITN (AOR = 1.46, 95% CI 0.49–4.33). Conversely, children were less likely to sleep under an ITN if the caregiver had more than one child (AOR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.38–2.01) or used an alternative malaria prevention method (AOR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.04–0.35). Discussion

This study aimed to understand the role of caregivers' malaria-related knowledge in influencing ITNs use among children under-five. To achieve this, a multivariable logistic regression approach was employed, incorporating eight additional explanatory variables as controls. The utilization of ITNs among children under five was found to be 73.8%. Caregivers' malaria-related knowledge emerged as an independent predictor of ITN use among children under-five. Additionally, five of the eight control variables independently predicted ITN use: education on ITN use, caregiver’s number of children, the child's age, ITN ownership, and the use of alternative malaria prevention methods.

The present finding of a 73.8% prevalence of ITN use among children under-five is higher than the findings reported by Aheto et al. [15] and Yeboah et al. [14] in Ghana. Their studies, based on data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey, reported ITN utilization rates of 57.4% and 61.88% respectively for children under-five. This discrepancy can be attributed to the rural–urban contexts of the study populations. Research indicates that children in rural areas are more likely to use ITNs compared to those in urban areas [27, 28]. Rural areas, with their bushes and streams conducive to mosquito breeding, create a higher perceived threat of mosquito bites, leading to increased bed net utilization [28]. The present study focused on rural caregivers, whereas the previous studies included both rural and urban caregivers in their overall prevalence calculations. For example, Aheto et al. [15] reported an overall ITN use prevalence of 57.4%, but noted higher utilization in rural areas (66.2%) compared to urban areas (43.5%).

The study's finding that caregiver malaria-related knowledge predicts ITN use among children under-five is consistent with the earlier assumption made, thus aligning with the HBM. According to the HBM, individuals are more likely to engage in health behaviours if they perceive themselves as susceptible to a health threat, view the threat as severe, recognize the benefits of the behaviour, feel capable of performing the behaviour, and encounter fewer barriers [17, 29]. In this context, caregivers with a better understanding of malaria likely perceived their children as more susceptible to and threatened by malaria, enhancing their motivation to use ITNs. They also might have perceived significant benefits in using ITNs, such as reducing malaria risk, and felt more capable of using ITNs effectively (as 72.6% reported they received education on ITN use). Additionally, these knowledgeable caregivers might have perceived fewer barriers, such as lack of access to ITNs, as 79.3% indicated they owned an ITN. Lastly, they might have received cues to action, like health education messages or reminders from healthcare providers, further encouraging ITN use.

One key finding of the present study that contradicts the existing literature is that a caregiver’s level of education does not independently predict ITN use among children under-five. Education is widely recognized as crucial in the adoption of ITNs for malaria prevention among young children. Previous research indicates that caregivers with higher education levels are more likely to utilize ITNs for their children under-five [12, 14]. In this study, the bivariate analysis did find a significant relationship between a caregiver’s education level and ITN use among children under-five. However, this significance did not hold in the multivariable logistic regression model. Mediation effect might have accounted for this outcome. For instance, educated caregivers may have better knowledge of malaria prevention, which in turn leads to higher ITN use. If this indirect pathway overshadows the direct effect of education, the multivariable analysis might not show a significant independent relationship. Differences in geographic location, cultural practices, healthcare infrastructure, or ITN distribution programmes could also explain the discrepancy in findings.

Strengths and limitations

This study significantly contributes to the existing literature by highlighting the pivotal role of caregivers' malaria-related knowledge in promoting ITN use among children under-five in rural Ghana. While previous research identified factors such as household size, wealth index, and education level influencing ITN utilization, this study uniquely underscores that caregivers with good malaria-related knowledge are substantially more likely to ensure their children's use of ITNs. By employing a comprehensive analytical approach and situating findings within the Health Belief Model, the research extends understanding of how targeted educational interventions can enhance ITN use, providing crucial evidence for policymakers and public health initiatives aimed at reducing malaria morbidity and mortality in vulnerable populations. However, limitations include the study's cross-sectional design, which prevents causal inferences, and its restriction to one rural district, potentially limiting generalizability to other regions. The reliance on self-reported data might also introduce reporting biases. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights for policymakers aiming to improve ITN use among vulnerable populations.

Implications for policy and practice

This study highlights the need for a theory-based approach to health promotion, specifically utilizing frameworks such as the Health Belief Model to guide social and behavioral change interventions. Enhancing caregiver knowledge about malaria is essential, but equally important is supporting their ability to make informed decisions about using ITNs. Therefore, health education programs should address not only the knowledge (content) but also empower caregivers by focusing on multiple dimensions of Social and Behaviour Change Communication (SBCC), including self-efficacy and overcoming perceived barriers.

To increase ITN utilization effectively, policymakers should design segmented health messages and utilize various modes of delivery to accommodate differences in literacy levels, languages spoken, and targeted behaviors. Programs should incorporate regular cues to action—such as health education messages and reminders from healthcare providers—that align with these tailored approaches. Such policies can strengthen health behaviors that protect children from malaria, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes in vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of caregiver malaria-related knowledge in promoting the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) among children under five in rural Ghana. Caregivers with good malaria-related knowledge were significantly more likely to use ITNs for their children, emphasizing the need for targeted health education programs. Such programs should enhance awareness of malaria risks and ITN benefits, empower caregivers to use ITNs effectively, and reduce barriers to ITN access. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers aiming to improve ITN utilization and reduce malaria morbidity and mortality among vulnerable populations.
