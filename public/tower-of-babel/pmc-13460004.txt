Strategies for Treating Acute Pain in Patients with Opioid Dependence, Opioid Use Disorder, or Long-Term Opioid Therapy: A Scoping Review
Authors: Megha Andrews, Sarah Levine, Joseph C Nowacki, Benjamin Saracco, Matthew Salzman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreating acute pain in individuals who use opioids is complex due to tolerance, physiological and psychological dependence, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and withdrawal risk. Evidence guiding management remains heterogenous and variably robust.ObjectiveTo map and synthesize available evidence on strategies for treating acute pain in individuals with opioid dependence, opioid use disorder, or opioid tolerance from long term therapy.DesignScoping review.Data SourcesMEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central, Web of Science, and Google Scholar were searched. Forward and backward citation tracking were conducted. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts and extracted relevant data from included full text sources.ResultsOf 4030 records screened, 55 works met inclusion criteria, including randomized trials, case reports, cohort studies, and reviews. Evidence spanned five patient categories: illicit opioid use, medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), chronic opioid therapy, mixed populations, and unspecified opioid exposure. Study design was heterogeneous, with a substantial proportion of lower-level evidence. Multimodal analgesia and continuation or adjustment of baseline opioid therapies were commonly associated with improved pain control. Non-opioid adjuncts, including ketamine, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and regional analgesia were frequently reported as beneficial. However, findings were inconsistent across studies, and several interventions demonstrated limited or context-dependent benefits.ConclusionAvailable evidence suggests that individualized, multimodal approaches and continuation of baseline medications for opioid use disorder may improve acute pain management in patients who use opioids. However, conclusions are limited by substantial heterogeneity, variable methodological quality, and reliance on small or non-comparative studies. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and highlight the need for higher-quality, patient-centered, comparative research.Keywords: opioid use disorder, opioid dependence, opioid tolerance, multimodal pain therapy

Introduction

Epidemiological Context

The United States is in the midst of a decades-long epidemic of opioid use. In 2023 and 2024, an average of 100,000 deaths in the United States were caused by drug overdose.1 Up to 80% of overdose deaths in the United States involve some form of opioid.2 National opioid overdose deaths have increased over the past 20 years, with 10 times the number of people dying of opioid overdose in 2022 as compared to 1999.3 In 2019, 10.1 million people aged 12 and older misused opioids.4 Some progress has been made in preventing opioid-related deaths, as September 2023 marked the beginning of a steady decline in overdose deaths.1

In addition to individuals with illicit opioid use or diagnosed opioid use disorder, a substantial population of patients receive opioid prescriptions for chronic pain and other medical conditions. These patients often require long-term opioid therapy to manage persistent pain but face complex risks including the development of tolerance, physical dependence, and, in some cases, progression to opioid use disorder. The intersection between medically prescribed opioid use and opioid misuse presents significant challenges for clinicians and underscores the need for careful monitoring and individualized pain management strategies. Recognizing this overlap is essential for understanding the full scope of the opioid epidemic and optimizing care for all patients affected by opioid use, whether prescribed or illicit.

In 2018, opioid-related hospitalizations occurred in the US at 286.1 per 100,000 people. Individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD) have higher rates of emergency department visits and hospitalizations than the general population, with healthcare utilization increasing substantially in recent decades.5,6 This places a significant burden on the health system, as OUD affects 2.1 million people in the US and over 16 million people worldwide.7

Over the past decade, fentanyl and other highly potent synthetic opioids have replaced heroin as the dominant opioid used illicitly.8,9 While more potent illicit opioids place the user at greater risk of overdose, other adverse effects of any long-term opioid use deserve acknowledgement. Opioids can negatively affect the gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, respiratory, cardiovascular, immune, endocrine, and central nervous systems.10

Certain groups of people are more likely to become dependent on opioids and develop OUD than others. Non-Hispanic whites, men, 18–25 year olds, those who make less than $20,000 per year, and are uninsured are at an increased risk for OUD.11 In addition, opioid overdose rates per 100,000 of the standard population are highest among black and indigenous populations, highlighting racial inequities in accessing healthcare and substance use disorder treatment services.12 Factors such as level of education, housing stability, and employment status have also been found to have a significant association with the frequency of opioid use.13 It is also known that people with pre-existing mental illness are more likely to initiate opioid use and develop OUD, as drug use may provide temporary relief from symptoms of mental illnesses.14

Acute Pain in Individuals with OUD

Acute pain is a physiological response to an adverse stimulus associated with surgery, trauma or acute illness.15 Patients with OUD are at high risk for transmissible and non-transmissible infections, as well as acute traumatic injuries, all of which can cause acute severe pain.16 People with OUD and opioid dependence face additional challenges with having their pain treated. One severe complication is opioid-induced hyperalgesia, in which patients taking long-term opioids have an increased sensitivity to pain and an extreme pain response out of proportion to opioid naive patients.17 Tolerance presents another challenge in treating acute pain in this patient population. Opioids are often used to treat severe acute pain, but the standard doses of opioids that would be used for an opioid naive patient will not work effectively in an opioid tolerant person.18 Furthermore, opioid dependent patients being treated for acute pain are at risk of withdrawal if opioid agonist therapy is not initiated promptly and at the appropriate dosage. Withdrawal can precipitate during long wait times or if the patient is refused opioids by a provider.19 Withdrawal symptoms cause additional suffering to the patient who is already experiencing acute pain. Lastly, individuals with OUD face stigma from healthcare workers, which can entail discrimination and suboptimal care in a healthcare setting.20–23 Many healthcare providers report working with opioid-dependent patients challenging and unsatisfactory.24

Rationale for This Scoping Review

Patients who use opioids represent a heterogeneous population that includes individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD), those receiving medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), individuals using opioids illicitly, and patients prescribed long-term opioid therapy for chronic pain. These groups differ in physiology, baseline opioid exposure, and clinical risk, yet are often treated similarly in acute care settings. A clear conceptual framework distinguishing these populations is essential to guide appropriate pain management strategies.

Existing literature highlights key challenges in this population, including opioid tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, withdrawal risk, and the impact of stigma on clinical care.25(insert madden reference here) Emerging evidence suggests that patient–provider dynamics and anticipated stigma may influence both pain reporting and prescribing behavior, contributing to suboptimal treatment.

Recent systematic reviews (eg, Buonora et al) have focused primarily on patients receiving MOUD and have applied more restrictive inclusion criteria. In contrast, this scoping review aims to provide a broader mapping of the literature across multiple opioid-use populations and clinical contexts, including patients in recovery and those on chronic opioid therapy. By incorporating diverse study designs, this review seeks to identify patterns in clinical practice, characterize gaps in evidence, and generate hypotheses for future research rather than establish definitive treatment recommendations.

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, Embase, and Scoping Review protocol registries including the Open Science Framework and Figshare was conducted in June 2022. Only one previous review studied the acute pain management in patients with opioid dependence.26 This 2020 review included patients taking medication for OUD, whereas this scoping review includes patients with opioid dependence regardless of treatment status. The previous study was also a rapid review which used a narrower search strategy, yielding less information sources. The search strategy for this scoping review is intentionally more broad to try and capture all relevant literature on treatment of acute pain in patients who are opioid dependent.

Methods

The scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.27 The protocol was published in the BMJ Open journal and the Open Science Framework was utilized for protocol development and research project and data management.28 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/BG6SJ). The study began in August 2022 and concluded in December 2024.

Stage 1: Defining the Research Question

Given the limited research on treating acute pain in patients with opioid dependence, a broad research question was defined to capture a wide range of data within the field. This scoping review addressed the question: what is currently the best standard of care for treating the acute pain of patients who are dependent on opioids?

Stage 2: Identifying Relevant Studies (Search Strategies)

Database search strategies were developed in collaboration with a trained medical librarian information specialist. No search limiters or filters were applied, such as language, country of publication, or study design. The information sources searched included MEDLINE via the PubMed interface, Embase, and Cochrane Central. Forward and backward citation searches were conducted on studies identified for final inclusion. Keyword searches in Google Scholar included the top 500 results, and conference proceedings were searched using the Web of Science: Conference Proceedings Citation Index as part of the grey literature search process.

The comprehensive search strategies for all databases can be found in Figure 1.

The scoping review was conducted according to Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and followed a comprehensive search strategy across MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Central, Google Scholar, Web of Science Conference Proceedings, and forward and backward citation searching. Records were deduplicated using Bond University’s Systematic Review Accelerator and screened independently by two reviewers using Rayyan and Covidence. Studies progressed through title/abstract screening and full-text review based on predefined eligibility criteria, with disagreements resolved by consensus or a third reviewer. The final included studies underwent data extraction and methodological appraisal using JBI critical appraisal tools.

Stage 3: Screening Studies for Inclusion

Articles obtained through the search strategy were exported to Rayyan and then Covidence software to facilitate the screening process. Covidence was used for all screening past the title abstract stage because this software best facilitated management of full-text uploads as well as the data extraction process. Duplicates were initially identified by using Deduplicator software that is part of Bond University’s Systematic Review Accelerator (SRA) software suite. Remaining potential duplicates were manually resolved by one team member, and the number of records was noted at each stage.

Phase 1 of screening involved reviewing relevance. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by two reviewers based on inclusion criteria, such as studies involving human subjects with opioid dependence receiving treatment for acute pain. Eligible papers moved to Phase 2. Both reviewers screened all articles for relevance. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with an additional reviewer.

Phase 2 comprised full-text reviews conducted independently by both reviewers. Disagreements in Phase 2 were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Inclusion criteria for full-text review were as follows:

Extracted patient characteristics included the type of acute pain (eg, breakthrough, traumatic, or postoperative pain) and treatment type (eg, medications used for acute pain management). Treatment outcomes, including measures of effectiveness such as patient satisfaction and pain scales, were also recorded. Critical appraisal of individual sources was conducted using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools. Although critical appraisal is not a standard requirement for scoping reviews, it was undertaken to enhance transparency and to better contextualize the strength of the available evidence.

Appraisal using JBI tools demonstrated variability in methodological quality across studies, reflecting differences inherent to study design. While most case reports scored highly within their respective appraisal frameworks (7/8 or 8/8), their findings were interpreted cautiously due to the absence of comparators and small sample sizes. Appraisal results were used to contextualize findings rather than to formally weigh the evidence.

Select Characteristics of Included Articles

Study Characteristics

An overview of the reviewed works can be found in Table 1.

The most common design in this review was case reports (36.4%) followed by randomized controlled trials (30.9%). Other study designs included cohort studies (14.5%), systematic reviews (3.6%), case control studies (3.6%), case series (3.6%), non-randomized experimental design (3.6%), cross-sectional studies (1.8%), and mixed study design (1.8%).

Most included works were published in the United States (50.9%) followed by Iran (16.4%), Australia (14.5%), and Canada (5.5%). Other sources of publication include New Zealand, Italy, Israel, Egypt, Switzerland, Denmark, and France.

Most included articles evaluated interventions to alleviate perioperative and postoperative pain (60%) Other types of acute pain that were studied in the included articles can be classified into acute pain experimental models, acute traumatic pain, acute non-traumatic pain, cancer-related pain, breakthrough pain, nociceptive pain, or multiple acute pain types.

There was a spectrum of opioid dependence that was studied in the included articles. This included illicit use (16.4%), buprenorphine or methadone use (27.3% and 14.5%, respectively), other long-term prescribed opioids for pain (20%). Other opioid dependence types that were included are buprenorphine plus illicit use, buprenorphine plus prescribed opioids for pain, methadone pulse illicit use, and naltrexone. Some articles included mixed opioid use types, while others did not specify opioid use type.

Qualitative Findings

The papers included in this review explored a broad spectrum of pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic strategies for managing acute pain in patients with opioid dependence.

Ketamine was addressed in several articles. Ketamine is a NMDA receptor antagonist that appears to be associated with reduced opioid-induced hyperalgesia and provides analgesia separate from the opioid pathway, which allows it to be an alternative or adjunct to traditional opioid therapies. It was shown to be efficacious in various settings, including perioperative and postoperative pain.

Pain Control Outcomes by Opioid Use History

Patients Illicitly Using Opioids

Nine manuscripts assessed pain management in patients with a history of illicit opioid use, including randomized controlled trials (n=6) and case reports (n=3). Among these, six articles (67%) reported effective pain management, while three did not (Table 1). Buprenorphine, ketamine, morphine, and carbamazepine were associated with effective control of acute pain. Meperidine, evaluated in two randomized trials, was consistently ineffective,35, and a combination of morphine and dexmedetomidine failed to improve outcomes in one trial.48 The heterogeneity of pain types (eg, postoperative vs acute non-traumatic pain) and variability in drug regimens limits the generalizability of findings. Overall, evidence suggests moderate effectiveness of select analgesics, though further high-quality, stratified trials are needed to inform best practices in this population.

Patients in Recovery from OUD (Prescribed MOUD)

22 articles evaluated pain management in individuals with diagnosed opioid use disorder receiving methadone or buprenorphine as medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD). Of these, 18 (82%) reported effective pain control. These reports consistently found that continuation of MOUD during the perioperative or acute pain period, either alone or with supplemental analgesia, was associated with improved pain control. Multimodal regimens—often including NSAIDs, acetaminophen, ketamine, tramadol, or hydromorphone—were frequently employed and effective.40,65,74 In contrast, morphine alone was ineffective in three sources,37,45,53 and hydromorphone showed reduced efficacy in experimental pain models.32 Despite these exceptions, overall effectiveness was high when MOUD was appropriately maintained or adjusted. These findings support MOUD continuation with individualized adjuncts as a preferred approach to managing acute pain in this population.

Patients Prescribed Opioids for Chronic Conditions

Fourteen articles included patients with chronic pain prescribed long-term opioids, all reporting effective pain control.36,39–72 Interventions in this population were typically multimodal, involving opioids such as morphine, fentanyl, ketamine, hydromorphone, and/or buprenorphine, combined with NSAIDs, acetaminophen, gabapentinoids, and other adjuncts. Notably, buprenorphine used specifically for chronic pain (not OUD) was effective in multiple case reports.54,57 The consistency of positive outcomes may reflect both stable opioid tolerance and individualized treatment planning. These findings suggest that tailored, multimodal analgesia is highly effective in this population, with no clear signal of ineffectiveness from any one drug or regimen.

Patients Using Opioids for Multiple Reasons

Sixteen reports examined mixed populations, including patients with overlapping indications such as OUD, chronic pain, or recent opioid substitution therapy.50,59–81, All articles (100%) reported effective pain management strategies. Frequently used approaches included MOUD continuation, multimodal regimens, and, in some cases, non-pharmacologic interventions such as virtual reality-based guided meditation.50 Pharmacologic interventions often featured ketamine, fentanyl, hydromorphone, and buprenorphine, typically delivered in conjunction with NSAIDs or acetaminophen. This group demonstrated uniformly positive outcomes, reinforcing the value of flexible, patient-centered pain management that preserves or adapts existing opioid treatments.

Unspecified Populations

Four randomized controlled trials included participants with unspecified opioid use histories.47,77,78,81 Among these, three works reported effective pain control using regimens such as ketamine, clonidine, dexmedetomidine, and lidocaine.47,77,78 One trial evaluating liposomal bupivacaine failed to demonstrate efficacy.81 Due to lack of patient stratification by opioid use history, these findings provide limited insight into effectiveness in opioid-tolerant populations. However, the results appear to be generally consistent with broader effectiveness patterns, with multimodal strategies again performing well.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to identify strategies for managing acute pain in patients with opioid dependence. The findings demonstrated a wide range of pharmacological and non-pharmacologic interventions that were aimed at addressing the unique challenges that this population faces, including opioid-induced hyperalgesia, tolerance, and withdrawal.

Primary Findings

This scoping review aimed to identify strategies for managing acute pain in patients with opioid dependence. The findings demonstrated a wide range of pharmacological and non-pharmacologic interventions tailored to address the unique challenges that this population faces, including opioid-induced hyperalgesia, tolerance, and withdrawal. Across 65 articles, pain management was generally effective in most opioid-using populations, although outcomes varied by drug class, treatment strategy, and population subgroup.

The results highlight the complexity of managing acute pain in individuals using opioids and emphasize the importance of individualized, multimodal treatment approaches. Sources involving patients with opioid use disorder (OUD) who were prescribed medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), such as buprenorphine or methadone, showed a high rate of success when MOUD was continued during acute pain episodes. Of 22 such reports, 82% reported effective pain control, particularly when MOUD was paired with supplemental analgesia. Conversely, morphine monotherapy was repeatedly ineffective in this population, especially in experimental models, suggesting that traditional opioid monotherapy may be inadequate in opioid-tolerant individuals.

In patients with a history of illicit opioid use, six of nine papers reported effective analgesia using various methods including opioids, MOUD, antipsychotics, antiepileptics, and acetaminophen. Ketamine and buprenorphine emerged as promising options across both controlled and case-based settings. However, meperidine was found to be consistently ineffective in two randomized controlled trials. In contrast, pain control among patients prescribed long-term opioids for chronic conditions was universally effective across 14 sources, likely reflecting the benefits of stable opioid tolerance, individualized care, and frequent use of multimodal regimens. Patients in mixed populations (eg, those with both chronic pain and OUD) similarly experienced high rates of effective analgesia, often through MOUD continuation, non-opioid adjuncts, and even non-pharmacologic strategies such as guided meditation.

Several sources highlighted the importance of ketamine, which was described as effective in 8 of 9 reports across multiple patient populations. Similarly, buprenorphine, whether used for OUD or chronic pain, was effective in the majority of works cited. The review also found regional anesthesia techniques, such as epidurals and peripheral nerve blocks, which minimized systemic opioid use while providing effective pain control, particularly important for patients at high risk of opioid-related complications.

Multimodal analgesia was consistently effective across populations and study types. The combination of opioids with non-opioid analgesics like acetaminophen, NSAIDs, gabapentinoids, and ketamine appeared to enhance analgesia, reduce total opioid requirements, and mitigate the risk of opioid-related adverse events. These findings reinforce guidelines that support multimodal therapy as a cornerstone of acute pain management in opioid-tolerant populations.

Though studied to a lesser extent, non-pharmacologic strategies were also employed and showed benefit in some cases. Cognitive behavioral therapy, psychology consults, and virtual reality-based interventions helped address the emotional and psychological dimensions of pain, which are often heightened in patients with OUD. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating holistic and non-opioid-based modalities into comprehensive pain management plans.

Despite the broad range of effective interventions, these findings also reveal significant variability in clinical practice. Differences in dosing, protocols, and medication selection across reports reflect a lack of consensus on optimal strategies, underscoring the need for more standardized approaches and stronger evidence to guide decision-making.

Interpretation and Strength of Evidence

While many included studies reported “effective” pain control, these findings must be interpreted cautiously. The evidence base is highly heterogeneous and includes a substantial proportion of case reports and small observational studies, which are inherently limited by lack of comparators, selection bias, and publication bias. Randomized controlled trials were fewer and often produced mixed or context-dependent results.

Importantly, studies of differing methodological rigor were presented within the same narrative framework, which may overemphasize perceived effectiveness. The frequent reporting of positive outcomes, particularly in small or uncontrolled studies, raises concern for overestimation of treatment benefit.

Rather than demonstrating consistent effectiveness of specific interventions, the literature more reliably supports broader principles, including the need for individualized care, the physiologic importance of maintaining baseline treatment for patients on MOUD, and the potential benefit of multimodal, opioid-sparing strategies.

These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of treatment efficacy.

Strengths and Weaknesses of the Study

The scoping review design allowed for inclusion of a diverse array of study designs, from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies to case reports and systematic reviews. This approach captured a wide range of real-world treatment strategies and patient characteristics, yielding a comprehensive overview of current practice.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The variability in study design and population characteristics limits direct comparisons across publications. While RCTs provided rigorous evidence, their findings were mixed, particularly when evaluating opioid monotherapy. In contrast, case reports and cohort studies more consistently reported effectiveness, potentially reflecting real-world flexibility and individualized care but limiting generalizability due to small sample sizes and publication bias. Additionally, few authors stratified outcomes by opioid use history, reducing clarity around which strategies are most effective for which subpopulations.

Future Directions

The results of this review underscore the need for individualized, multimodal pain management strategies that take into account the specific physiological and psychological challenges faced by people who use opioids. Expanding provider education and training is essential to reduce stigma, improve comfort with nontraditional strategies, and ensure evidence-based care.

Importantly, future research should focus on high-quality, randomized controlled trials that evaluate the comparative effectiveness of different analgesic strategies in well-characterized opioid-tolerant populations. More studies should also stratify outcomes by type of opioid exposure (eg, illicit use, MOUD, chronic opioid therapy) to clarify which treatments are most effective in each context.

Conclusion

This review highlights the wide array of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic strategies available to manage acute pain in patients with opioid dependence, OUD, or long-term opioid therapy. Although multimodal analgesia, MOUD continuation, and adjunctive therapies such as ketamine are frequently associated with improved outcomes, the evidence base is highly variable in quality and consistency.

Current findings are limited by considerable heterogeneity, reliance on lower-level study designs, and a lack of comparative trials. As such, no single strategy can be considered definitively superior. Instead, the currently available literature supports an individualized, patient-centered approach that accounts for opioid tolerance, withdrawal risk, and clinical context. Future research should prioritize well-designed studies that stratify patients by opioid-use characteristics to better inform evidence-based guidelines. Closing current knowledge gaps can empower clinicians to move toward more consistent and equitable pain care for this complex and growing patient population.
