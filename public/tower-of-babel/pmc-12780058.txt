The protective role of dietary nutrients in mitigating asthma risks induced by indoor air pollutants: a comparative toxicogenomics perspective
Authors: Zhongqing Sun, Yuefeng Yang, Dan Lin, Yan Liu, Ya Su, Bingling Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study integrated data from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) to investigate the molecular pathways by which selected dietary nutrients modulate the adverse effects of indoor air pollutants on asthma pathogenesis. Our analysis of 1199 curated chemicals-genes-phenotypes-disease interaction modules (CTD Tetramers) revealed about 60 key genes and 236 phenotypes involved in oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, proliferation, and immune regulation. Nutrients including gamma-tocopherol, vitamin A, resveratrol, theophylline, and zinc were found to influence these molecular targets. Notably, resveratrol significantly altered the expression of 51 genes (including both up- and down-regulation) and modulated 187 phenotypes, suggesting a pivotal role in attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine production and reducing oxidative damage. Further anatomical and systems-level analysis demonstrated that both pollutants and these nutritional factors affect critical components of the respiratory system, and the immune network. The modulation of key mediators such as IL6 and TNF supports the dual functions of these nutrients in managing acute inflammation and preventing chronic airway remodeling. Our findings provide molecular evidence that nutritional interventions hold promise as a complementary strategy for mitigating asthma induced by indoor air pollutants. Although our results support the protective effects of the examined nutrients, further in vitro and in vivo studies are necessary to detail the contributions of other dietary components such as flavonoids, vitamin C, and vitamin D. Overall, this study establishes a framework that links environmental exposures with targeted nutritional approaches, and it paves the way for future clinical trials and nutraceutical therapies in respiratory health. These results underscore the potential of diet-based interventions as a tool in global respiratory health improvement.Keywords: Asthma, Indoor air pollution, Nutritional intervention, Oxidative stress, Inflammation, Immune regulationSubject terms: Asthma, Mechanism of action, Environmental monitoring

Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease characterized by airflow limitation, airway hyperresponsiveness, and structural changes that often begin early in life and persist into adulthood1. With an estimated 300 million individuals affected globally—and projections suggesting an additional 100 million cases by 2025—the burden of asthma is immense, both in terms of premature mortality and socioeconomic costs2–4. For instance, costs per patient can exceed €27,500 in end-stage cases, and annual U.S. costs are estimated at $50.3 billion5,6. Although current medications alleviate symptoms, asthma remains incurable, and long-term drug therapies may carry risks of adverse effects. Thus, there is a compelling need to develop effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, the role of environmental factors has been increasingly recognized in both the onset and exacerbation of asthma. While outdoor air pollution has long been a focus of public health research, indoor air pollution now commands significant attention. Individuals spend more than 80% of their time indoors—with even higher proportions among children and the elderly7,8—rendering indoor air quality a critical determinant of overall exposure. Indoor pollutants originate from numerous sources, including dust, pet dander, mold, chemicals released by cleaning products, building materials, emissions generated during cooking or from cigarette smoke, and combustion byproducts such as nitrogen dioxide emitted by natural gas appliances9–12. Studies have documented elevated levels of mycotoxins and synthetic chemicals (e.g., herbicides, insecticides, additives) in the homes of children with asthma and allergic rhinitis, suggesting that these indoor metabolites may serve as robust environmental biomarkers of childhood asthma13. Moreover, exposure to particulate matter (PM), volatile organic compounds, pesticides, triclosan, and even polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzo(a)pyrene (BaP) has been linked to both the onset and exacerbation of asthma10–12,14–16. Mechanistically, inhaled pollutants may induce oxidative stress–mediated inflammation, largely through the activation of transcription factors like NF-κB, which upregulate inflammatory genes (e.g., IL6, BCL2, FOS, PTGS2, TNF). This process results in oxidative airway damage, inflammatory remodeling, and enhanced sensitization2,17,18. Longitudinal studies further reveal that critical windows of exposure—particularly during perinatal stages—compound the risk of developing asthma later in life19.

Alongside environmental risks, dietary factors have emerged as promising modulators of asthma risk and severity. Epidemiological evidence suggests that healthy dietary patterns rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and Mediterranean-style components can have both preventive and therapeutic effects on asthma20–24. It has been postulated that these benefits are mediated by dietary nutrients with immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties. Nutrients such as tocopherols, vitamin A, vitamin D, resveratrol, zinc, carotenoids, ascorbic acid, ubiquinone, flavonoids, and selenium have been shown to down-regulate pro-inflammatory gene expression including TNF-α, IL1B, and IL624–27. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which these nutrients interact with indoor air pollutants to modulate the risk and progression of asthma remain unclear.

In light of these observations, the present study aims to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms through which dietary nutrients may counteract asthma induced or exacerbated by indoor air pollutants. By exploring the modulation of gene expression relevant to inflammation and airway remodeling, this research seeks to provide novel insights and practical dietary recommendations for the prevention and management of asthma. In turn, these findings will contribute to a deeper understanding of asthma pathogenesis and potentially pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies to mitigate the global impact of this chronic disease.

Methods

CTD data version and web tools

We conducted our analysis using data mined from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org), a well-established public resource that curates scientifically robust data on chemicals, genes, phenotypes, anatomical sites, diseases, taxa, and exposures28,29. CTD’s content is manually curated by PhD-level scientists who use controlled vocabularies and ontologies to ensure transparency and traceability in the association of chemical exposures with human health outcomes. For the current study, we specifically focused on elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which dietary nutrients may mitigate the effects of indoor air pollution-induced asthma. Data for this analysis were accessed on August 1, 2024 (Revision 17440, data update on July 31, 2024); however, we note that CTD is updated monthly, and current counts or associations may evolve over time. In addition to data retrieval, we leveraged CTD’s public analytical and visualization tools, including the Set Analyzer (http://ctdbase.org/tools/) and CTD Tetramer tools (https://ctdbase.org/query.go?type=tetramer), to interrogate and visualize relevant datasets linking chemicals, interacting genes, induced phenotypes, and associated diseases28. CTD Tetramers are computationally derived chemical-gene-phenotype-disease modules. Their generation is contingent upon the concurrent existence of five specific, directly evidenced relationships in the database: chemical-gene (C-G), chemical-phenotype (C-P), chemical-disease (C-D), gene-disease (G-D), and imported gene-phenotype (G-P). It is critical to note that CTD is a dynamic resource updated monthly; consequently, the inventory of available CTD Tetramers is subject to change as new data is curated.

Indoor air pollutants data collection

The selection of the six key indoor air pollutants (formaldehyde, triclosan, pesticides, Benzo(a)pyrene (BaP), particulate matter (PM), and nitrogen dioxide) was based on the following rigorous, two-step process:

(i)Literature-Based Identification: We first identified candidate pollutants with strong prior evidence linking them to respiratory ailments and asthma development through mechanisms like oxidative stress and inflammation9–12,14–16.

Dietary nutrients data collection

We utilized the CTD Tetramers to explore potential protective interactions, which links chemicals, interacting genes, induced phenotypes, and diseases with direct experimental and observational evidence28. By querying the disease “asthma,” we extracted curated information on dietary nutrients that have been implicated in modulating the disease’s molecular pathways. Among the CTD Tetramers of asthma, only five dietary nutrients were found for further analysis: gamma-tocopherol, vitamin A, zinc, resveratrol, and theophylline. These nutrients have been associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and gene-regulatory properties which may counteract the effects of indoor air pollutants on asthma24,25,27.

Data visualization

The final step in our analytical pipeline involved comprehensive data visualization to enhance interpretability and facilitate network analysis of the curated associations. R software was employed for Sankey diagrams, while Cytoscape was used to construct detailed interaction networks, illustrating the relationships between indoor air pollutants, dietary nutrients, and asthma-associated gene expression profiles. This visualization strategy not only provided insights into the molecular interactions but also enabled the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

Results

Comprehensive molecular mechanisms of indoor air pollutant-induced asthma pathogenesis

Our analysis of 1251 CTD Tetramers encompassed six pollutants, 76 genes, and 226 phenotypes. Among these, particulate matter (PM) and benzo(a)pyrene (BaP) demonstrated the most extensive molecular interactions, suggesting that they may be the most potent inducers of asthma pathogenesis.

Genes in asthma associated with indoor air pollutants

Among the 76 genes, 11 genes (CAT, SOD1, TNF, IL6, PARP1, TNIP1, CCL5, PTEN, BCL2, TGFB1, and IL1B) were identified as critical through a multidimensional analysis that evaluated their universal targeting by these six pollutants, high connectivity in the protein-protein interaction network, and frequency of occurrence. Four genes (CAT, SOD1, TNF, and IL6) were targeted by these pollutants (Table 1; Fig. 1), positioning them as central players in the initiation and exacerbation of asthma. Subsequent protein-protein interaction network analysis of these 76 genes revealed a highly interconnected module of 31 genes. Within this network, five genes (PARP1, SOD1, TNIP1, CCL5, and PTEN) functioned as key hubs, each influencing over 5% of these genes in the network (Fig. 2), suggesting their pivotal regulatory roles in coordinating downstream pathogenic events. Furthermore, frequency analysis within the CTD Tetramers identified TNF, BCL2, IL6, TGFB1, and IL1B as the top five most frequently occurring genes, each appearing in over 5% of all gene occurrences. Their recurrent involvement across diverse phenotypes underscores their significance as critical mediators in asthma pathogenesis.

Key phenotypes linking indoor air pollutant-induced asthma

Notably, the phenotype “mitotic cell cycle” was the only one common to all six pollutants (Fig. 3), indicating a fundamental role for dysregulated cell division in pollutant-induced asthma. The prominence of these phenotypes points to enhanced proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune activation as the core mechanistic pathways through which indoor air pollutants drive asthma pathogenesis.

The integrated network of pollutants, genes and phenotypes in asthma

Our quantitative analysis of 1251 CTD Tetramers established PM and BaP as the most impactful pollutants, generating 601 and 364 tetramers and affecting the broadest ranges of genes (60 and 68) and phenotypes (160 and 100), with impacts declining across formaldehyde, triclosan, nitrogen dioxide, and pesticides.

Mechanistically, pollutants operated mainly through three specialized axes: PM, BaP, formaldehyde, triclosan and nitrogen dioxide primarily targeted cytokine/receptor genes (TNF, IL1B, IL6) to drive immune cell proliferation and inflammatory activation; PM, BaP and formaldehyde additionally modulated signal transduction genes (PTEN, PARP1, TGFB1) regulating cellular differentiation and tissue remodeling; while triclosan, nitrogen dioxide and pesticides engaged antioxidant genes (SOD1, HMOX1, CAT) mediating oxidative stress responses.

Further analysis of 573 core CTD Tetramers—constructed from six pollutants, eleven key genes, and ten key phenotypes—revealed that all pollutants converge on eight key genes (BCL2, CCL5, IL1B, IL6, PTEN, SOD1, TGFB1, TNF) through four fundamental phenotypes (“mitotic cell cycle”, “cell migration”, “gene expression” and “nitric oxide biosynthetic process”). This core network is complemented by three modules: cellular turnover, involving five pollutants and nine genes (BCL2, CAT, IL1B, IL6, PARP1, PTEN, SOD1, TGFB1, and TNF) through “cell population proliferation” and “apoptotic process”; stress response, mediated by four pollutants and ten genes (BCL2, CAT, CCL5, IL1B, IL6, PARP1, PTEN, SOD1, TGFB1, and TNF) via “xenobiotic stimulus” and “oxidative stress”; and inflammatory activation, driven by four pollutants and seven genes (CCL5, IL1B, IL6, PTEN, TGFB1, TNF, and TNIP1) through “inflammatory response” and “interleukin-6 production”.

Protective mechanisms of dietary nutrients against asthma induced by indoor air pollutants

A total of five dietary nutrients were identified from the asthma-related CTD Tetramers. These nutrients, together with 60 genes and 236 phenotypes distributed across 1199 tetramers, form a network which serves as the basis for our analysis of their protective mechanisms against pollutant-induced asthma.

Modulation of asthma-associated genes by dietary nutrients

Of the 60 genes identified, 59 were also targeted by indoor air pollutants, with the exception of the IFNL3 gene, establishing a direct molecular interface for nutrient-pollutant interplay. Further analysis revealed that resveratrol exhibited the highest degree of association, impacting 51 genes, followed by vitamin A (48 genes), zinc (36 genes), theophylline (14 genes), and gamma-tocopherol (6 genes).

When examining the eleven key genes previously identified as central to pollutant-induced asthma, resveratrol was found to regulate all eleven. Vitamin A and zinc each targeted ten genes (BCL2, CAT, CCL5, IL1B, IL6, PARP1, PTEN, SOD1, TGFB1, and TNF), theophylline targeted seven(BCL2, CAT, IL1B, IL6, PARP1, TGFB1, and TNF), and gamma-tocopherol targeted four (BCL2, CAT, SOD1, and TNF).

Notably, three genes (BCL2, CAT, and TNF) emerged as universal nutrient targets, being modulated by all five dietary components. Another seven genes (IL1B, IL6, PARP1, TGFB1, SOD1, CCL5, and PTEN) were also significant, each being modulated by three or four nutrients. Particularly noteworthy is that both gene CAT and gene TNF were identified as paramount molecular hubs, being significantly modulated by all five dietary nutrients and concurrently targeted by all six indoor air pollutants (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 1 and 4). This dual targeting positions them as critical nodes where nutritional interventions can most directly counteract pollutant-induced pathogenic signaling. These findings highlight the intricate interplay among genes, nutrients, and indoor air pollutants.

Modulation of asthma-associated phenotypes by dietary nutrients

Among the 236 curated phenotypes analyzed, resveratrol demonstrated the broadest regulatory scope, influencing 187 phenotypes, significantly surpassing the effects of vitamin A (78 phenotypes), zinc (53 phenotypes), theophylline (17 phenotypes), and gamma-tocopherol (9 phenotypes). Notably, 133 of these phenotypes were co-modulated by both indoor air pollutants and dietary nutrients, encompassing key biological processes including proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, migration, signaling, and metabolism. Within this shared group, resveratrol again exhibited the greatest influence (113 phenotypes), followed by vitamin A (52), zinc (41), theophylline (15), and gamma-tocopherol (8).

The phenotype “mitotic cell cycle” emerged as a particularly significant node, being impacted by all five dietary nutrients as well as all six indoor air pollutants, underscoring its central role in asthma pathogenesis and potential for nutritional intervention.

Evaluation of the ten key phenotypes associated with pollutant-induced asthma confirmed that dietary nutrients can also regulate these processes. Resveratrol modulated nine of these ten key phenotypes. Four core phenotypes (“cell population proliferation,” “apoptotic process,” “oxidative stress,” and “mitotic cell cycle”) were influenced by all five nutrients, identifying them as primary targets for nutritional strategies. The “inflammatory response” was specifically counteracted by resveratrol and zinc, while resveratrol uniquely affected “xenobiotic stimulus” and “interleukin-6 production.” Additionally, “cell migration” and “nitric oxide biosynthetic process” were modulated by both resveratrol and vitamin A, and vitamin A alone significantly impacted “gene expression.”

These findings collectively delineate the mechanisms through which dietary nutrients counteract asthma pathogenesis induced by indoor air pollutants. Resveratrol shows exceptional breadth in modulating critical biological processes, and the consistent targeting of specific phenotypes by multiple nutrients reveals convergent protective pathways.

Discussion

Asthma poses a substantial clinical burden worldwide, with current therapeutic strategies offering symptomatic relief but failing to modify disease progression or mitigate long-term sequelae. While the adverse effects of indoor air pollutants on asthma exacerbation are well-documented, growing epidemiological evidence points to the protective potential of dietary interventions—particularly diets rich in fruits and Mediterranean patterns—in reducing asthma incidence and severity21–23,30. Nevertheless, existing research has largely been confined to observational studies or investigations of isolated nutrients, lacking comprehensive analyses that integrate both environmental triggers and protective factors to elucidate their interacting molecular mechanisms. To address this fundamental gap, we systematically constructed the molecular networks underlying asthma pathogenesis and nutritional protection using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database, thereby providing a unified framework that reveals how dietary components counteract pollutant-induced asthma at the molecular level. To our knowledge, this is the first comparative toxicogenomics study to systematically contrast the gene-phenotype interaction networks of a diverse set of common environmental pollutants with a panel of dietary nutrients/phytochemicals in the context of asthma. Our primary novel finding is the identification of a core set of genes and phenotypes that are perturbed by pollutants and are conversely modulated by multiple nutrients, highlighting a potential shared mechanistic axis for intervention.

This study demonstrates that six indoor air pollutants contribute to asthma pathogenesis primarily through a network of 11 key genes—including gene TNF and IL6—and 10 critical phenotypes such as “inflammatory response”, “oxidative stress”, and “cell population proliferation”. These elements collectively drive asthma development via three interconnected pathways: disruption of redox homeostasis, dysregulation of inflammatory signaling, and disturbance of cell cycle regulation. Such disruptions impair fundamental cellular processes—including proliferation, apoptosis, and tissue remodeling—ultimately compromising airway structural integrity. Our proposed mechanism can be supported by many prior studies, which independently validates key elements of our model, including pollutant-induced ROS elevation, formaldehyde-mediated apoptosis through p38 MAPK activation, cigarette smoke-induced inflammatory signaling via CCR1/JAK/STAT/NF-κB pathway, the role of oxidative stress as a pathogenic driver, and the connection between specific pollutants and airway inflammation and remodeling31–38. Collectively, this proposed mechanism provides both mechanistic insights and translational opportunities for addressing complex environmental triggers of asthma.

In countering the effects of indoor air pollutants in asthma pathogenesis, our study finds five key dietary nutrients exert critical antagonistic effects by targeting all these three major pathogenic pathways, demonstrating their broad-spectrum protective potential against multiple mechanisms of asthma pathogenesis. Notably, resveratrol emerged as the most influential agent, followed by vitamin A and zinc.

Within the oxidative damage pathway, dietary nutrients counteract molecular damage induced by pollutants. When confronted with oxidative stress stemming from elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS)—a consequence of redox homeostasis disruption by BaP, PM, and formaldehyde—dietary nutrients exert protection through anti-oxidative mechanism. Five nutrients (resveratrol, gamma-tocopherol, vitamin A, zinc, and theophylline) can enhance the expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes including SOD1 and CAT, while directly scavenging free radicals to restore intracellular redox homeostasis and limit oxidative damage propagation39–49. Notably, zinc further reinforces this defense system by serving as an essential cofactor for CuZn-SOD, thereby stabilizing and potentiating the activity of this critical enzyme50. This pathway establishes that dietary nutrients employ a multi-level antioxidant system, operating through both enzymatic potentiation and structural stabilization, to comprehensively mitigate the oxidative burden imposed by indoor air pollutants. Within the inflammatory signaling pathway, dietary nutrients suppress inflammatory activation triggered by pollutant exposure. When pollutants induce the upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL1B, IL-6) and chemokines such as CCL5, dietary nutrients including resveratrol, vitamin A, and zinc counteract this response through complementary inhibitory actions. These nutrients target multiple nodes of the inflammatory cascade, primarily by suppressing IκB kinase activity and inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation, while also interfering with inflammasome activation and cytokine gene expression51–57. Such modulation not only reduces excessive inflammatory responses but also diminishes chemotactic signals that contribute to airway hyperresponsiveness and tissue damage58–61.This pathway shows that dietary nutrients utilize a multi-target anti-inflammatory machinery, operating through both transcriptional regulation and chemotactic signaling control, to effectively alleviate the inflammatory burden triggered by indoor air pollutants.

Within the apoptotic regulation pathway, dietary nutrients counterbalance pollutant-induced disruption of the apoptotic-proliferative equilibrium to maintain airway structural integrity. Resveratrol exerts protective effects through HIF-1α downregulation, miR-193a-mediated modulation of TGF-β signalling, and activation of the SIRT1/PTEN/p-Akt pathway, while also suppressing protein kinase G signaling and decreasing IAPs expression, thereby reducing apoptosis and cellular injury62–66. Zinc plays a protective role through BCL2 regulation67–69. Vitamin A demonstrates efficacy in mitigating hyperoxia-induced lung injury70. This pathway establishes that dietary nutrients engage a multi-mechanism regulatory network, operating through transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and signaling pathway modulation, to counteract pollutant-induced disruption of cellular turnover and tissue homeostasis. As no prior studies have comprehensively mapped the molecular interactions between pollutants and nutrients, our analysis—drawing exclusively on data from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database—may reflect certain biases toward well-characterized agents. This could potentially lead to underestimation of effects from less-studied pollutants or nutrients. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed validation using anatomically annotated data within the CTD’s Anatomy section. The data here mainly supported our molecular mechanisms.

Specifically, the pathophysiological roles of key pollutants were confirmed: nitrogen dioxide was shown to provoke inflammatory responses through genes such as GSTP1, TLR2, and TNF71, while PM disrupts cellular homeostasis and amplifies inflammation via a network encompassing IL1B, IL6, and TNF71,72. This mechanistic understanding was further extended to the phenotypic level, where a suite of pollutants—including BaP, formaldehyde, nitrogen dioxide, PM, and triclosan—was consistently associated with core adverse outcomes such as “cellular response to DNA damage”, “chemotaxis”, and “macrophage activation”73–77. Conversely, and in direct opposition, dietary agents exemplified a protective paradigm. Resveratrol attenuated airway inflammation78 by exerting multi-target modulation over pivotal signaling pathways like NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT. This protective activity was systemic; resveratrol, theophylline, and vitamin A collectively modulated fundamental processes including “apoptotic process”, “cell cycle”, “histamine secretion”, “leukocyte chemotaxis”, and diverse kinase signaling pathways79–84. Thus, the anatomical validation not only supports the proposed molecular mechanisms but also delineates a clear antagonism at the pathophenotypic level, where dietary agents counter the perturbations induced by environmental pollutants.

The network-based findings from this study provide a critical scientific foundation for developing targeted dietary and public health recommendations in the future. The translational roadmap can be outlined as follows: First, the nutrients screened in this study—such as resveratrol, vitamin A, and zinc—along with their key molecular targets (e.g., IL6, TNF), offer prioritized candidates and biomarkers for subsequent clinical intervention trials. Subsequent research should focus on validating whether supplementation or dietary fortification with these specific nutrients can effectively improve inflammatory markers and reduce asthma risk in vulnerable populations, such as children and the elderly. Ultimately, this empirical evidence may be integrated into public health guidelines, leading to concrete and actionable dietary recommendations—for instance, advising individuals at risk of indoor air pollution exposure to prioritize foods rich in these five nutrients. This would enable the translation of molecular network discoveries into precision public health prevention strategies.

Nowadays, theophylline remains the only one among them in current clinical use for asthma management, the therapeutic potential of the other four nutrients has not yet been evaluated in clinical settings. Nevertheless, supporting evidence from human observational studies indicates that their intake at dietary-relevant levels is associated with beneficial effects on asthma outcomes22–25,85. Although the curated CTD Tetramer evidence supports the protective roles of gamma-tocopherol, tretinoin, vitamin A, resveratrol, theophylline, and zinc, epidemiological and preliminary experimental studies suggest that additional nutrients, such as flavonoids, vitamin C, and vitamin D, may also confer protection against asthma22–26. Population-based analyses have implicated other compounds (including carotene, carotenoids, ascorbic acid, folic acid, choline, ubiquinone, selenium, and vitamin D) in modulating asthma pathophysiology. However, their specific molecular mechanisms remain less well defined than those five nutrients and require further investigation via rigorous in vitro and in vivo studies to observe their enflunce on genes and phenotypes. Meanwhile, it is important to recognize that diets present a complex mixture of nutrients. A key question for future research is whether these nutrients act synergistically to enhance or antagonistically to diminish their overall protective effects.

It is also important to acknowledge that indoor environments contain a complex mixture of contaminants, including biological agents such as bacteria, molds, and mites, which are critically implicated in asthma86. However, the primary focus of the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) is on chemical substances and their interactions. Consequently, the direct influence of biological agents is not as readily analyzable within this platform. To construct a clear and mechanistic framework for investigating the protective role of dietary nutrients, this study was deliberately designed to focus on a selected set of key chemical indoor air pollutants. This methodological choice does not imply that microbial influences are unimportant; rather, it defines the specific scope of our current analysis, which leverages the strengths of the CTD to elucidate defined molecular pathways. Future studies integrating data from microbiological and immunological databases are warranted to build a more comprehensive model of the indoor expose.

Despite the integrative nature of our analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on curated CTD Tetramers from the CTD may introduce selection bias and limit the inclusion of emerging or less-studied compounds. Second, while our findings highlight common pathways modulated by both pollutants and nutrients, direct causal relationships remain to be experimentally validated. Future research should integrate mechanistic studies with clinical and epidemiological data to establish dose-response relationships, validate molecular targets, and clarify the role of other potential dietary nutrients in asthma management. Comprehensive clinical trials and experimental studies will be essential to translate these findings into targeted nutritional interventions for asthma patients.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that indoor air pollutants and dietary nutrients exert opposing effects on asthma pathogenesis through complex molecular networks. Our integrative analysis using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database revealed that indoor pollutants such as BaP, PM, and formaldehyde disturb critical cytokine signaling and antioxidant mechanisms by modulating key genes (e.g., TNF, IL1B, IL6, SOD1, and CAT), thereby triggering inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress, and tissue remodeling in the respiratory system. In contrast, dietary nutrients (particularly resveratrol, vitamin A, and zinc) counteract these adverse effects by restoring redox balance, suppressing inflammatory pathways, and regulating apoptosis and cell proliferation. The overlapping molecular pathways identified in both pollutant-induced pathology and nutrient-mediated protection support the potential of nutritional interventions as complementary strategies to conventional asthma treatments. Moreover, the anatomical validation of these findings highlights that both pollutants and nutrients significantly influence respiratory, and immune functions. Despite inherent limitations related to database reliance and the need for further experimental validation, our results underscore the promise of targeted nutritional approaches in mitigating the detrimental effects of indoor air pollutants. Future research integrating mechanistic, clinical, and epidemiological data is essential to optimize dietary interventions and develop effective adjunctive therapies for asthma management, ultimately contributing to improved respiratory health on a global scale.
