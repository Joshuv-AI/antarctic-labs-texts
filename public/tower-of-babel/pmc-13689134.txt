Performance of AI chatbots in responding to patients’ frequently asked questions about laminate veneer restorations
Authors: Bike Altan, Şevki Çinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough AI (artificial intelligence) chatbot systems are increasingly used to obtain dental information, limited evidence exists regarding the reliability and readability of their responses in laminate veneer–related patient inquiries. The aim of the study is to assess the reliability, quality, and readability of the responses given by 3 different artificial intelligence chatbot systems to frequently asked questions about laminate veneer restorations by patients.MethodsTwenty-five frequently asked questions about laminate veneer restorations submitted to ChatGPT 5.2, Gemini 3, and DeepSeek-V3.2. AI chatbots to generate answers. The quality of these answers was assessed by two different experts using GQS (Global Quality Scale), reliability using modified DISCERN, and readability using FRES (Flesch Reading Ease Score) and FKGL (Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on data distribution, repeated-measures ANOVA or the Friedman test was used for comparisons among chatbots. Dunn’s test was applied for post hoc analyses. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were found among chatbot systems in terms of DISCERN, FRES, or FKGL scores (P > 0.05). Interobserver agreement for DISCERN was generally poor, with only Gemini demonstrating statistically significant agreement (ICC = 0.393; P = 0.023). Although a significant overall difference was observed in GQS scores (P = 0.015), no significant pairwise differences were detected. Readability analysis revealed that all chatbot responses exceeded the recommended 6th–8th grade reading level for patient education materials, with mean FKGL values corresponding to approximately 1–2 grade levels above the recommended upper limit. In addition, FRES values indicated that the generated content generally corresponded to a difficult reading level.ConclusionThe evaluated AI chatbot systems demonstrated largely comparable performance in responding to laminate veneer–related inquiries. While overall content quality was high, limitations in reliability and elevated readability levels indicate that AI-generated responses should be interpreted cautiously and used as supportive tools rather than substitutes for professional clinical guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09438-5.

Background

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), its applications have expanded across multiple domains of healthcare, particularly in the generation and dissemination of medical information [1, 2]. AI-based chatbots powered by large language models (LLMs) have gained widespread popularity due to their ability to provide instant and accessible responses to user inquiries [3, 4]. In dentistry, patients increasingly use these systems to obtain preliminary information regarding symptoms, treatment options, and clinical procedures [5, 6]. However, concerns remain regarding the accuracy, reliability, and readability of AI-generated health information [7, 8].

In recent years, several technology companies have introduced LLM-based chatbot systems. ChatGPT, developed by OpenAI, was first released in 2022. Gemini, introduced by Google in 2023, incorporates multimodal AI capabilities. DeepSeek, developed in China, emerged as an open-source large language model platform beginning in 2024. Although these systems share similar foundational architectures, differences in model design, training data, and optimization strategies may influence their performance in delivering patient-oriented clinical information [9–11].

Prosthodontics represents a field in which patients frequently seek detailed explanations about treatment procedures and expected outcomes [12]. Among contemporary restorative treatments, laminate veneer restorations have gained considerable popularity due to their minimally invasive nature and high esthetic predictability [13, 14]. Patients commonly seek information regarding indications, procedural steps, longevity, costs, and potential risks prior to initiating treatment [14]. The increasing visibility of esthetic dentistry through digital media platforms has further amplified public interest in veneer treatments, encouraging patients to access online resources before clinical consultation [13].

Previous studies have evaluated chatbot performance in prosthodontic areas such as implant-supported prostheses, fixed and removable prosthodontics, temporomandibular disorders, and bruxism [15–21]. However, despite the growing popularity of laminate veneer treatments and the increasing use of AI chatbots for obtaining dental information, limited evidence exists regarding the reliability, content quality, and readability of chatbot-generated responses specifically related to laminate veneer restorations. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively evaluated chatbot responses in this specific esthetic prosthodontic domain.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the reliability, content quality, and readability of responses generated by three AI chatbot systems (ChatGPT 5.2, Gemini 3, and DeepSeek-V3.2) to frequently asked questions about laminate veneer restorations. The null hypothesis was that no statistically significant differences would be observed among the evaluated chatbots in terms of readability and performance quality.

Methods

This study aimed to assess and compare the readability, quality, and reliability of responses generated by 3 chatbot systems to frequently asked questions related to laminate veneers. Frequently asked questions regarding laminate veneer restorations were identified through Google search results and the “People Also Ask” feature, similar to previously published chatbot-related dental studies [16], in order to better reflect real-world patient information-seeking behavior. Question collection was performed using an English-language browser environment under standardized search conditions using a single search query: “laminate veneer frequently asked questions.” A total of 76 initial questions were collected. After excluding duplicate, irrelevant, non-English, and advertisement-based content, two prosthodontists independently reviewed the remaining questions and selected the 25 most frequently encountered patient-oriented queries for analysis. The selected questions were then submitted to 3 chatbot systems: ChatGPT (GPT 5.2; OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA), Gemini (Gemini 3; Google, Mountain View, CA, USA) and DeepSeek (DeepSeek-V3.2; DeepSeek Inc., Hangzhou, China). The reported version labels reflected the version information displayed on the respective chatbot platforms at the time of data collection. These chatbot systems were selected because they represent widely used and publicly accessible large language model–based AI platforms currently utilized by individuals seeking online information. To minimize potential bias related to prior interaction memory, newly created accounts were used for each chatbot. All questions were submitted and responses were generated on the same day (February 1, 2026) to minimize temporal variability and ensure methodological standardization (Supplementary File 1).

Two prosthodontic specialists with 8 years of clinical experience in prosthetic dentistry independently evaluated the chatbot responses in terms of quality, reliability and readability using several evaluation tools: modified DISCERN, Global Quality Scoring (GQS), Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES), Flesch- Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL). Content quality was evaluated using the 5-point Global Quality Scale (GQS), which assesses the overall quality, coherence, and informational depth of the content. Scores range from 1 (very poor and incomplete) to 5 (excellent and comprehensive), with intermediate scores reflecting increasing levels of quality and completeness [22].

The modified DISCERN scale, adapted from the original DISCERN tool, was used to assess the reliability of the provided information. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater reliability and 5 representing the highest possible score [23].

Readability analysis was performed using the Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES) and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) metrics, two widely accepted quantitative readability indices for English-language texts. Both measures are derived from sentence length and word complexity, specifically calculated using the mean number of words per sentence and the mean number of syllables per word. The FRES generates a score reflecting reading difficulty, with higher values indicating easier comprehension and lower values corresponding to more complex text. In contrast, the FKGL estimates the minimum educational grade level required for adequate understanding of the material.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the same set of 25 questions was submitted to all three chatbot systems, the observations were treated as dependent (repeated) measures. Modified DISCERN and GQS scores were considered as ordinal rating data. Accordingly, Friedman tests were used for modified DISCERN, GQS, and FRES, whereas repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to FKGL, which satisfied the normality assumption. For Friedman tests, Kendall’s W effect sizes were obtained using the K Related Samples procedure (Legacy Dialogs). Post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test.

Interobserver agreement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC analysis was performed using a 2-way random-effects model with a consistency approach. This model assumes that both the evaluators and the evaluated samples are randomly selected and is considered appropriate for subjective assessment tools such as modified DISCERN and GQS. Comparative analyses among chatbot systems were subsequently performed using the ratings of Expert 1, who had greater clinical experience in prosthodontics.

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median (minimum–maximum) for non-normally distributed variables. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Interobserver agreement based on DISCERN scores was not statistically significant for ChatGPT (P = 0.073) or DeepSeek (P = 0.121), whereas Gemini demonstrated a statistically significant but poor level of agreement (ICC = 0.393; P = 0.023) (Table 2). While DeepSeek demonstrated numerically higher median DISCERN scores (median = 3) than ChatGPT and Gemini (median = 2 for both systems), this difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 3; P = 0.128). The observed effect size was small (Kendall’s W = 0.082), supporting the absence of substantial differences in DISCERN scores among the chatbot systems. The distribution of DISCERN scores, illustrated using box plots, is presented in Fig. 2.

Discussion

In this study, the performance of three widely used chatbot systems in responding to frequently asked questions about laminate veneer restorations was comprehensively evaluated. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the limited number of studies specifically focusing on laminate veneer–related patient inquiries while simultaneously assessing reliability, content quality, and readability using validated instruments. The findings revealed largely comparable performance among the evaluated chatbot systems. No statistically significant differences were observed among chatbot systems for DISCERN, FRES, and FKGL scores, whereas GQS analysis demonstrated a significant overall difference without significant pairwise comparisons. These results suggest that, within the scope of laminate veneer–related questions, AI chatbot performance appears largely comparable across platforms.

In terms of reliability, as assessed by the modified DISCERN scale, no statistically significant differences were observed among the chatbot systems, although DeepSeek demonstrated a numerically higher median DISCERN score than ChatGPT and Gemini. However, interobserver agreement was generally low, with only Gemini demonstrating a statistically significant yet poor level of agreement. This indicates that, although overall reliability levels appeared similar, the evaluation of reliability criteria may be inherently subjective and prone to variability between raters. The relatively lower interobserver agreement for DISCERN compared with GQS may be explained by the more structured and criterion-based nature of reliability assessment, which requires stricter judgment regarding referencing, balance, transparency, and acknowledgment of uncertainty. Additionally, variability between evaluators may have been influenced by differences in clinical background, professional experience, and expectations regarding the level of detail and evidentiary support that AI-generated responses should provide. Differing perceptions of what constitutes adequate citation, sufficient risk–benefit explanation, or appropriate acknowledgment of uncertainty may therefore have contributed to the observed interobserver variability. These findings may reflect the subjective application of DISCERN criteria to AI-generated responses rather than an inherent limitation of the modified DISCERN instrument itself. The non-significant ICC values observed for DeepSeek in both DISCERN and GQS assessments may reflect greater variability in evaluator interpretation rather than consistently inferior performance. Factors such as differences in response structure, variability in the depth of explanations, and differing evaluator perceptions regarding informational emphasis may also have contributed to this finding.

The modest DISCERN performance may also reflect intrinsic limitations of large language models in delivering structured, evidence-based information. While chatbot responses are typically coherent and scientifically plausible, they frequently lack explicit references, comprehensive risk–benefit discussions, and transparent source attribution—elements central to DISCERN-based reliability evaluation. As reported by Özyemişci et al. [26] AI-generated responses may provide informative content without consistently offering verifiable references or clearly addressing areas of uncertainty. In parallel with those findings, the present study indicates that insufficient referencing remains a critical limitation, potentially reducing the trustworthiness of chatbot-generated health information in evidence-driven clinical disciplines such as prosthodontics. Although DeepSeek demonstrated a numerically higher median DISCERN score than ChatGPT and Gemini, this difference did not reach statistical significance. The observed trend may suggest a potential difference in reliability performance; however, the limited number of evaluated questions may have restricted the ability to detect small-to-moderate differences between chatbot systems. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of equivalence among platforms.

Regarding content quality, as assessed by the Global Quality Scale (GQS), all chatbot systems demonstrated generally high scores, with median values corresponding to good-to-excellent levels. Although a statistically significant overall difference was detected, post hoc analyses did not reveal significant pairwise differences, indicating comparable quality performance across platforms. Although the Friedman test demonstrated a statistically significant overall difference among chatbot systems for GQS scores, post hoc pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni-corrected Dunn tests did not reveal statistically significant differences between individual chatbot pairs. This discrepancy may be related to the reduced statistical power associated with multiple-comparison corrections, as well as the possibility that the observed differences were modest and distributed across all groups rather than concentrated within specific pairwise comparisons. The higher mean rank observed for DeepSeek despite identical median GQS values suggests that the significant Friedman test result was driven by differences in score distributions across questions rather than differences in median values alone.

The elevated GQS scores suggest that chatbot-generated responses were coherent, well-structured, and generally informative. Nevertheless, moderate interobserver agreement indicates that quality assessment may still involve a degree of subjectivity, potentially influenced by expectations related to clinical depth, comprehensiveness, and contextual detail. Importantly, high presentation quality does not necessarily equate to strong evidentiary support, as well-organized responses may still lack transparent evidence foundations. In the present study, the mean GQS score reached the maximum value of 5 for all evaluated chatbots. This finding aligns with the study by Özcivelek et al. [27] on maxillofacial prostheses, which also reported high GQS values. Similarly, Esmailpour et al. [28] found mean GQS scores above 4 in their evaluation of chatbot responses related to dental prostheses. Although the prosthodontic topics differed among these studies, the consistently high GQS scores suggest that AI chatbots can generate responses with relatively high informational quality.

With respect to readability, no statistically significant differences were observed among chatbot systems in terms of FRES and FKGL values. This finding is consistent with Özyemişci et al. [26] who similarly reported no significant inter-model differences in readability indices. However, in contrast to Prasad et al. [29] who identified significant variability in FKGL scores across chatbot platforms in maxillofacial prosthodontic inquiries, the present laminate veneer–focused analysis demonstrated more homogeneous readability levels among systems. These discrepancies across studies may be explained by differences in topic complexity and terminology density. While maxillofacial prosthodontic questions often involve highly technical and multidisciplinary explanations, laminate veneer–related inquiries may require comparatively less specialized terminology, potentially resulting in reduced variability in language complexity across platforms. In the present study, the mean FRES exceeded 45 for all evaluated chatbots. According to conventional FRES interpretation, values above 45 still correspond to a difficult reading level and remain below the readability range generally recommended for patient education materials. Similar findings were reported in a previous study on maxillofacial prosthesis–related questions, which demonstrated mean FRES values above 40 [27]. In contrast, another study [28] reported higher readability scores, with mean FRES values exceeding 55. When FKGL was considered, the average reading grade level in both the present study and the previous maxillofacial prosthesis–related study [29] was above 9, whereas Esmailpour et al. [28] reported slightly lower grade levels, generally above 8.

Importantly, although inter-model differences were not statistically significant, the readability levels of all chatbot responses exceeded the recommended 6th–8th grade level for patient education materials [30]. This raises concerns regarding accessibility for individuals with limited health literacy. Even when responses are accurate and well-structured, linguistic complexity may limit their practical usefulness for laypersons. Therefore, improvements in language simplification, audience adaptation, and integration of transparent referencing mechanisms are necessary to enhance the clinical applicability of AI-generated patient information [31].

In contrast to prior investigations in prosthodontics [27, 28], which frequently identified significant inter-platform variability, the present study did not demonstrate statistically significant superiority among AI chatbot systems. Several studies [15, 28] conducted in maxillofacial prosthodontics, and implant-related domains have reported differences in accuracy, quality, reliability, and readability metrics across AI platforms. The absence of such differences in the current laminate veneer–focused analysis may be attributed to topic specificity and reduced linguistic complexity. Laminate veneer inquiries often emphasize esthetic expectations and patient-oriented explanations rather than highly technical or multidisciplinary clinical decision-making processes, likely reflecting the esthetic and minimally invasive nature of veneer treatments previously highlighted in the literature [12, 13]. This relative homogeneity in content structure may have minimized detectable variability across platforms. Additionally, the continuous optimization and iterative updating of LLMs—often trained on increasingly similar large-scale datasets—may contribute to performance convergence over time, thereby reducing observable inter-platform differences.

Overall, the present findings suggest that performance variability among chatbot systems may be more context-dependent than platform-specific. While LLMs demonstrate considerable potential in generating structured and informative responses, persistent limitations in transparency, evidentiary clarity, and readability indicate that AI-generated content should complement rather than replace professional clinical consultation. It should also be acknowledged that the analysis was restricted to English-language responses obtained at a single time point and generated by the freely available versions of the evaluated chatbots. As AI chatbot systems are continuously updated, their responses may vary over time due to evolving model architectures, training data, and platform-specific modifications. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the specific data collection period in which the analyses were performed. In addition, the number of evaluated questions was limited to 25, which may have restricted the ability to detect subtle differences among chatbot systems, particularly in reliability assessments. Therefore, non-significant findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of equivalence among platforms. Additionally, the generally poor interobserver agreement observed for DISCERN assessments should be acknowledged as a limitation of the present study when interpreting the reliability findings. In addition, the frequently asked questions were identified through Google search results and the “People Also Ask” feature, which may vary according to regional and algorithmic search dynamics. Considering the dynamic evolution of large language models and the potential variability between free and subscription-based versions, future research incorporating multilingual, longitudinal, and version-specific assessments may yield a more comprehensive evaluation of chatbot performance in prosthodontics.

Conclusions

AI chatbot systems demonstrated comparable performance in responding to frequently asked questions regarding laminate veneer restorations, with no statistically significant superiority among platforms in terms of reliability, readability, or overall content quality. Although DeepSeek demonstrated relatively higher numerical DISCERN scores and ChatGPT and DeepSeek showed comparatively more favorable readability values than Gemini, these differences did not reach statistical significance. While chatbot-generated responses were generally structured and informative, limitations related to insufficient referencing, evidentiary transparency, and reading levels above the recommended range for patient education materials indicate that AI-generated information should be used cautiously as a supplementary source rather than a substitute for professional clinical consultation.
