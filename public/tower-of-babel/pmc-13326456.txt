Longitudinal association between the weekend warrior physical activity pattern and cognitive function trajectories among middle-aged and older Chinese adults
Authors: Meihan Liu, Youqiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive decline is a major public health concern in aging societies, and physical activity (PA) is a key modifiable factor. Some individuals concentrate their PA into one or two days per week—the “weekend warrior” pattern—but its long-term relationship with cognitive changes remains unclear. This study aimed to examined the association between the weekend warrior pattern and cognitive trajectories among middle-aged and older adults in China.MethodsData were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2013–2018). A total of 1,530 adults aged ≥ 45 years who completed three survey waves were included. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct cognitive trajectories, and multinomial logistic regression with hierarchical adjustment assessed associations between the weekend warrior pattern and these trajectories.ResultsSix distinct cognitive trajectories were identified. Compared with the Cognitive Impairment–Persistent Low group, participants adopting the weekend warrior pattern were significantly more likely to belong to the Cognitive Normal–Stable group (Model5: OR = 2.685, 95% CI: 2.68–2.69, P < 0.001). Sensitivity and subgroup analyses yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the association.ConclusionThe weekend warrior PA pattern was significantly associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories among middle-aged and older adults. Concentrating PA within one or two days per week may offer long-term cognitive benefits and represents a practical strategy for promoting brain health in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01955-7.

Background

With the rapid progression of global population aging, cognitive decline has emerged as a major public health concern affecting the health and quality of life of middle-aged and older adults. The Global Burden of Disease Study reported that dementia ranks among the leading causes of death and disability in older populations, with approximately 55 million people worldwide currently living with dementia, and this number is projected to exceed 152 million by 2050 [1]. Cognitive decline is typically manifested in progressive deterioration of memory, attention, and executive function, and it substantially increases the risk of falls, disability, and comorbid chronic diseases [2–4]. At the individual level, cognitive impairment severely undermines daily self-care ability, reduces psychological well-being, and increases premature mortality [5, 6]. At the societal level, it imposes a heavy economic and caregiving burden, with the global cost of dementia reaching USD 1 trillion in 2019 and projected to double to USD 2 trillion by 2030 [7]. Therefore, cognitive decline has become an urgent global public health challenge.

Accumulating epidemiological and clinical evidence highlights the pivotal role of lifestyle factors in the onset and progression of cognitive decline, among which physical activity (PA) is recognized as one of the most modifiable protective determinants [8]. Previous studies reported that regular PA significantly reduces the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia, and contributes to preserving memory and executive function in older adults [9, 10]. Compared with genetic predisposition and other nonmodifiable risk factors, PA is a low-cost and scalable intervention widely recommended for promoting cognitive health in aging populations [11–13]. However, due to work and life constraints, some individuals may not engage in exercise regularly but instead concentrate moderate-to-vigorous PA into one or two sessions per week, a pattern referred to as the “weekend warrior”. Previous studies have suggested that weekend warriors may achieve comparable benefits to regular exercisers with respect to cardiometabolic health and all-cause mortality [14–16]. Nevertheless, their long-term effects on cognitive function trajectories remain insufficiently understood.

To address this gap, the present study utilized longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2013–2018) to characterize cognitive trajectories among middle-aged and older adults using group-based trajectory modeling. Furthermore, we examined the association between the weekend warrior PA pattern and distinct cognitive trajectories. By elucidating the longitudinal relationship between PA patterns and cognitive aging, this study provides novel insights into the dynamic interplay between lifestyle behaviors and cognitive health, offering scientific evidence for scalable and practical interventions aimed at mitigating the growing burden of cognitive decline.

Methods

Study population

The CHARLS is a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, designed to collect comprehensive information on demographics, socioeconomic status, health behaviors, medical history, and biomarkers. The baseline survey (2011) included 17,708 individuals from 10,257 households across 150 counties/districts in 28 provinces, with an 80.5% response rate. Participants were followed biennially via computer-assisted interviews, and blood samples were collected every other wave through 2018. Details of the study design have been published elsewhere [17] .All interviews were conducted by trained local healthcare workers under strict quality control. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB 00001052–11015), and all participants provided written informed consent [18].

For the present analysis, we included individuals who completed the 2013, 2015, and 2018 waves. Those identified as weekend warriors, reporting depressive symptoms, or aged < 45 years at baseline were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of 1,530 participants (Fig. 1).

Assessment of weekend warrior

PA in CHARLS was assessed through a self-reported questionnaire similar to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), but without sedentary behavior items and using categorical ranges for duration. The CHARLS PA data have been validated and widely used in prior studies [19, 20]. Participants reported whether they engaged in ≥ 10 min of vigorous or moderate activity in the past week. Vigorous activity (~ 8 METs) referred to tasks causing large increases in breathing and heart rate, while moderate activity (~ 4 METs) involved noticeable increases [19–21]. Frequency (days/week) and average duration were recorded, with sessions truncated at 4 h. Midpoints were assigned to categorical durations, and weekly moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) was calculated as minutes of moderate activity plus twice the minutes of vigorous activity. Participants accumulating ≥ 150 min/week within ≤ 2 days were classified as Weekend Warriors [22].

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function in CHARLS was evaluated using a standardized assessment consisting of two domains: episodic memory (0–10 points) and mental status (0–11 points) [23]. Episodic memory was measured through an immediate recall test of 10 words [24]. The mental status domain comprised three subtests: time orientation (5 points), calculation ability (5 points), and visuospatial ability (1 point) [25]. This cognitive assessment tool has been widely validated among Chinese middle-aged and older adults, demonstrating good reliability and construct validity. The total cognitive function score ranged from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. A score ≥ 11 was considered indicative of normal cognition [17].

Covariates

Covariates included age, gender, residence, education, work status, income, smoking, alcohol use, activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), and fall history, sleep duration, and self-reported chronic conditions (hypertension, depression, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cancer, chronic lung, liver, kidney and gastrointestinal diseases, coronary heart disease, stroke, mental illness, and arthritis) [26]. Depressive symptoms were assessed with the CESD-10, a 10-item 4-point Likert scale (0–30) covering mood, behavior, cognition, and physical symptoms. A score ≥ 10 indicates depressive symptoms and has been validated with good reliability in Chinese cohorts [27–29]. Fall history was assessed by asking participants whether they had experienced a fall during the previous two years or since the previous interview. Participants who reported at least one fall during this period were classified as having a fall history.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as means (standard deviations) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Repeated measures of cognitive function across survey waves were analyzed using group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) with quadratic time terms (d = 2) to accommodate potential non-linear change. The quadratic specification was prespecified given the limited number of waves (2013, 2015, 2018), providing a parsimonious yet flexible functional form without over-parameterization. Models with one to seven latent classes were estimated, and the optimal model was selected based on Bayesian information criterion (BIC), average posterior probability (AvePP > 0.70), odds of correct classification (OCC > 5), entropy, class size adequacy, and clinical interpretability. GBTM was used to characterize heterogeneity by identifying distinct latent subgroups of cognitive change and to relate the weekend warrior PA pattern to trajectory class membership, offering an interpretable complement to random-effects approaches that focus on continuous individual-specific variation.

Multinomial logistic regression was then used to assess the associations between physical activity trajectory classes and cognitive trajectories. Penalized maximum likelihood estimation (decay = 1e − 2, maxit = 500) was applied to reduce sparse data bias and stabilize parameter estimates. Five hierarchical models were sequentially and cumulatively adjusted as follows: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for functional status (ADL, IADL, and fall history); Model 3, additionally adjusted for sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education, income, work status, residence); Model 4, further adjusted for lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration); Model 5, additionally adjusted for self-reported chronic conditions (liver disease, heart disease, stroke, depression, kidney disease, gastrointestinal disease, mental illness, arthritis, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic lung disease, diabetes, and cancer).

Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The primary 95% CIs were computed using Wald-type standard errors based on the observed information matrix from the penalized multinomial regression model. Given the potential for sparse data and small reference groups to underestimate uncertainty, a bootstrap-based sensitivity analysis was further conducted to assess the robustness of the estimates. Sensitivity analyses excluded participants with missing covariates and tested alternative penalization strengths (decay = 1e − 3 and 1e − 4). Subgroup analyses were stratified by gender, age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), and residence (urban vs. rural). Missing data were imputed using mean or mode values. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1), with two-tailed P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Trajectory of cognitive function

Among 1,530 participants, model fit indices supported a six-class trajectory solution (BIC = 24,296.79), which demonstrated superior fit compared with models containing fewer classes and avoided the overfitting observed in the seven-class model (Table 1). Classification quality was acceptable, with AvePP ranging from 0.71 to 0.93, and all trajectory groups exceeding the 5% minimum size threshold, ensuring adequate statistical reliability. Overall, this six-class solution provided an optimal balance between parsimony and interpretability, delineating distinct and clinically meaningful cognitive trajectories.

Six distinct cognitive trajectories were identified (Fig. 2). The Cognitive Normal-Stable Group (Trajectory 6, n = 380) maintained consistently high cognitive scores with minimal fluctuation. The Cognitive Normal-Fluctuating Group (Trajectory 5, n = 470) remained within the normal range but exhibited mild score variability over time. The Cognitive Normal-Late Decline Group (Trajectory 3, n = 140) started with normal cognitive levels but showed a marked decline into impairment by the final wave. The Cognitive Impairment-Improvement Group (Trajectory 1, n = 153) demonstrated initial impairment but improved steadily to approach the normal range. The Cognitive Impairment-Rapid Decline Group (Trajectory 4, n = 311) exhibited baseline impairment with progressive deterioration. Finally, the Cognitive Impairment-Persistent Low Group (Trajectory 2, n = 76) remained below the impairment threshold throughout follow-up.

Baseline characteristics across six cognitive trajectory groups

Baseline characteristics across the six cognitive trajectory groups are summarized in Table 2. Overall, no significant differences were observed in age, sex, education, residence, income, work status, or most chronic conditions (all P > 0.05). In contrast, lifestyle factors——particularly alcohol consumption and depressive symptoms—showed significant heterogeneity among groups (both P < 0.001). The prevalence of the weekend warrior physical activity pattern differed modestly across trajectories (P = 0.204), with the highest proportion in the Cognitive Normal–Stable group (6.1%) and the lowest in the Cognitive Impairment–Persistent Low group (1.3%). This distribution suggests that even infrequent yet concentrated physical activity may cluster among cognitively stable individuals, implying a potential protective association against long-term cognitive decline.

These associations remained robust after sequential adjustment for functional status (Model 2), sociodemographic factors (Model 3), lifestyle-related factors (Model 4), and self-reported chronic conditions (Model 5) (Supplementary Tables S1–S5). In the fully adjusted model, the weekend warrior pattern was associated with higher odds of belonging to more favorable cognitive trajectories compared with the cognitive impairment–persistent low group. Bootstrap-based sensitivity analyses produced wider confidence intervals (Supplementary Tables S6) but showed directionally consistent point estimates, suggesting that the overall pattern of association was robust, although the precision of the estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with missing covariate data (Supplementary Table S7) and applying different penalization strengths (decay = 1e-3 and 1e-4; Supplementary Tables S8–S9) yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, confirming the robustness of the findings.

Subgroup analyses indicated stronger associations of weekend warrior status with favorable cognitive trajectories in male, in adults < 60 years, and among urban residents, with interaction tests significant in fully adjusted models (Supplementary Tables S10–S12).

Discussion

Using longitudinal data from the CHARLS (2013–2018), this study systematically characterized long-term trajectories of cognitive function among middle-aged and older adults and examined their associations with the weekend warrior PA pattern. Six distinct cognitive trajectories were identified, including the cognitive normal–stable group, cognitive normal–fluctuating group, cognitive normal–late decline group, cognitive impairment–improvement group, cognitive impairment–rapid decline group, and cognitive impairment–persistent low group. Our findings revealed that the weekend warrior pattern was significantly associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories. Compared with the cognitive impairment–persistent low group, individuals with a weekend warrior activity pattern were more likely to follow the cognitive normal–stable, cognitive normal–fluctuating, and cognitive impairment–improvement trajectories, and these associations remained robust after full adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates. Subgroup analyses further indicated that the protective effect was more pronounced among men, adults younger than 60 years, and urban residents. These results suggest that even when MVPA is concentrated within only one or two days per week, it may still exert long-term beneficial effects on cognitive maintenance.

Our findings are generally consistent with previous evidence supporting the health benefits of PA, even when it is performed in a concentrated manner [30]. Prior research has primarily focused on the association of the weekend warrior pattern with cardiometabolic outcomes and all-cause mortality, demonstrating that individuals engaging in one or two bouts of MVPA per week may achieve comparable protective effects to those exercising more regularly [14, 15, 31]. However, only a few cross-sectional studies have explored the relationship between the weekend warrior pattern and cognitive function, yielding limited and inconsistent evidence [32–35]. In prior cross-sectional studies, findings suggest that among healthy individuals, the weekend warrior pattern provides cognitive benefits comparable to those of regularly distributed activity, whereas among people with depression, regular activity appears to be more beneficial for cognitive function [33]. By employing a longitudinal trajectory approach, the present study extends this line of research and provides novel evidence that weekend warrior activity may be linked to more favorable long-term cognitive trajectories. This study carries important implications for public health and clinical practice. For individuals constrained by work or lifestyle demands, the weekend warrior pattern may still confer cognitive benefits, offering a practical option for middle-aged and older adults. In health management and policy, regular exercise should continue to be promoted, but the potential value of concentrated activity should also be recognized to enhance participation and maximize cognitive health benefits.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the association between the weekend warrior PA pattern and favorable cognitive outcomes. First, MVPA, even when performed intermittently, enhances cerebral blood flow and cardiovascular fitness, thereby improving the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the brain, which is essential for sustaining cognitive function [36–38]. Second, PA stimulates neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity through the upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and other neurotrophic signaling pathways, which play critical roles in memory consolidation and executive function [39–41]. Third, PA has been shown to reduce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are important contributors to cognitive decline in aging populations [9, 42]. Finally, regular engagement in PA, regardless of its distribution across the week, contributes to better metabolic health and reduced vascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, which are strongly linked to cognitive impairment [43]. Taken together, these pathways suggest that even concentrated bouts of exercise, characteristic of the weekend warrior pattern, may exert meaningful neuroprotective effects and slow the progression of cognitive decline.

This study has several limitations. Physical activity in CHARLS was self-reported and may be misclassified. The weekend warrior pattern reflects a concentrated MVPA distribution rather than an intervention, and should not be interpreted as recommending intense activity on one or two consecutive days, especially for older or frail adults; CHARLS also lacks detailed data on exercise related injuries or other short term adverse events to quantify potential risks. Although we adjusted for extensive sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health related covariates, including functional status (e.g., IADL), history of falls, and multiple chronic conditions, residual confounding, self-selection, and reverse causality cannot be fully excluded. Attrition across waves may introduce selection bias if dropout is related to both activity pattern and cognitive decline. Additionally, cognition was assessed with brief screening measures and we could not evaluate broader physiological outcomes or cost effectiveness. Future studies using device-based activity measures and methods addressing selection and time varying confounding are warranted. Finally, Attrition across waves may cause selection bias, as dropout in CHARLS (e.g., death, severe illness/disability, relocation, non-response) is more common among older and less healthy participants with poorer baseline cognition. Thus, the analytic sample may be relatively healthier, and some bias may remain if missingness is not random. Given the small reference class and sparse exposure distribution in some trajectory comparisons, Hessian-based Wald confidence intervals may underestimate statistical uncertainty. The bootstrap confidence intervals (Supplementary Tables S6) were substantially wider, indicating that the precision of the estimates should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the point estimates remained directionally consistent across approaches, and the overall pattern of association was unchanged.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study based on CHARLS data found that the weekend warrior PA pattern was associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories among middle-aged and older adults. These findings do not replace the WHO recommendation for regular activity across the week, but suggest that when people cannot exercise frequently, accumulating MVPA within one or two days may still support cognitive maintenance. Care is warranted when activity is highly concentrated, particularly for older or frail individuals.
