Enhanced CO2 conversion via synergistic effect of dielectric barrier discharge plasma and halogen-modified g-C3N4
Authors: Mudadla Umamaheswara Rao, Phyu Phyu Cho, Devthade Vidyasagar, Sneha Joseph, Shibani Patel, Guo-Ping Chang-Chien, Srinivaas Masimukku, Giridhar Madras, Challapalli Subrahmanyam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon dioxide (CO2) conversion is a key challenge in addressing global warming and the associated climate change. Non-thermal plasma activation of CO2 is a viable alternative to traditional thermal catalytic routes for converting CO2 into CO and O2. In this study, a dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma reactor operated under ambient conditions was developed and demonstrated for CO2 conversion to CO. The integration of halogen-modified g-C3N4 markedly enhanced CO2 conversion relative to the unpacked condition. The addition of a photocatalyst to the plasma reactor improved plasma discharge parameters, and the synergy present between the catalyst and plasma reactor promoted the conversion. The maximum conversion of CO2 attained was approximately 11% with Br/g-C3N4 at an applied voltage of 22 kV. Additionally, halogen doping enhanced CO selectivity and energy efficiency. Furthermore, the integration of the photocatalyst with DBD avoids coke formation, which is one of the limiting steps in high temperature CO2 activation.

Introduction

The concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2), one of the main greenhouse gases, has been increasing as a result of increasing energy needs and reliance on fossil fuels (IPCC, 2023).1 Mitigating CO2 emissions remains one of the most pressing environmental challenges of the twenty-first century. Several approaches have been explored to mitigate CO2, such as carbon capture and storage, CO2 conversion to value added chemicals, etc.2 However, CCS primarily focuses on temporary storage through biological, oceanic, and geological methods, which are not permanent solutions.3 These methods also present risks such as ground upheaval, seismic activity, and potential leakages, limiting their long-term viability.4 Therefore, innovative technologies are essential to effectively reduce carbon emissions in the earth's atmosphere.

Thermal catalysis is one such approach for CO2 conversion. However, it requires high operating temperatures, which may result in catalyst deactivation caused by coke deposition.5 Alternatively, non-thermal plasma (NTP) technology has emerged as a promising method for CO2 conversion, offering the advantage of operating under ambient conditions.6,7 Various types of non-thermal plasma reactors have been developed for CO2 conversion, such as corona discharge,8 gliding arc discharge,9 microwave discharge,10 glow discharge,11 and dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) reactors.12,13 Among these, DBD reactors are widely used due to their simple design and ability to generate a uniform discharge across the reaction zone at low temperatures.7,14,15

In a typical DBD reactor, the average electron temperature ranges between 1 and 10 eV.16,17 These energetic electrons facilitate key processes such as excitation, ionization, dissociation, and decomposition, all of which contribute to efficient CO2 conversion.18 Several research groups have investigated CO2 conversion using dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) reactors. Kaliyappan et al. including core shell SiO2@TiO2 spheres within a DBD plasma reactor enhanced both energy efficiency as well as CO2 conversion.19 Lu et al. demonstrated that plasma/g-C3N4 catalyst hybrid system converted CO2 twice as high as that of a plasma-only system.20 Zhu et al. utilized a self-cooled DBD reactor packed with Cu and Ni foam meshes, achieving maximum CO2 decomposition rates of 49.2% and 48.6%, respectively, at a gas flow rate of 20 sccm and a discharge power of 55 W.21 He et al. explored ZnO/g-C3N4 as a photocatalyst for highly efficient CO2 conversion.22 Ashford et al. used a bimetallic catalyst composed of Fe–Ce, which was supported on γ-Al2O3. They were able to attain 13.3% CO2 conversion rate and 7.4% energy efficiency by using a 10 Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst. Notably, incorporating bimetallic catalysts (5Fe5Ce/γ-Al2O3) enhanced energy efficiency to 13.6% and the CO2 conversion rate to 24.5%, this improvement may be attributed to the function that oxygen vacancies play in CO2 adsorption, activation and CO2 dissociation reactions.23 However, conventional photocatalysts such as TiO2,24 BaTiO3,25 LaNiO4,26 and g-C3N4 (ref. 27) exhibit limited visible light absorption, restricting their ability to fully utilize the plasma emission spectrum. Consequently, large-scale practical applications of CO2 conversion using hybrid plasma-photocatalysis systems are still not yet reported.

In this work, we have investigated the direct CO2 conversion with DBD plasma assisted with the g-C3N4 and halogen modified g-C3N4 photocatalyst. Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has attracted considerable attention as a metal-free catalytic material because of its excellent thermal stability, chemical robustness, tunable electronic properties, and abundant nitrogen-rich active sites. These features make it a promising for CO2 activation and plasma-assisted catalytic processes. Nevertheless, pristine g-C3N4 exhibits limitations, including relatively low electrical conductivity and limited active sites for efficient reactant adsorption and activation.28 To overcome these drawbacks, halogen modification has emerged as an effective electronic structure engineering strategy. The incorporation of halogen atoms can induce defects, redistribute charge density, alter the band structure, and enhance electron transfer characteristics.29 Such modifications are expected to improve plasma–catalyst interactions and facilitate CO2 adsorption and activation, thereby enhancing plasma-assisted CO2 conversion efficiency.

Experimental

Plasma reactor and gas analysis

Fig. 1 shows the experimental set-up. The dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma reactor schematic diagram is given in Fig. S1. The reactor is a quartz tube measuring 20 mm inner diameter and 23 mm outer diameter. An 11 mm stainless steel rod placed at the center was connected to high-voltage source. A stainless-steel mesh wrapped around the outer quartz tube acted as the ground electrode. With a discharge gap of 4.5 mm, the DBD reactor had a discharge length of 11 cm. The gas flow was 30 mL min−1 and the resulting residence time was 48 s.

Schematic experimental set-up.

For catalytic plasma treatment, 400 mg of the synthesized photocatalyst was placed in the discharge region. With the help of a mass flow controller (Dakota, USA) 30 mL min−1 of pure CO2 gas was introduced into the DBD reactor. The AC high-voltage power source (Jayanthi Transformers Pvt Ltd) that operated at steady frequency of 50 Hz and applied voltage (peak–peak) range of 16 kV to 22 kV and the applied voltage was measured using an Agilent high-voltage probe (34136 A), and the electrical signals were recorded using (Tektronix TDS2014B) four-channel digital oscilloscope. The discharge power was calculated using the Q-U Lissajous method. The gaseous products were analysed using gas chromatography (GC-2014, SHIMADZU), equipped with a thermal conductivity detector. A 3 m Porapak-Q column was employed to study the composition of the gas mixture emitted from the plasma reactor. Materials and methods

Polymeric carbon nitride (g-C3N4) and halogen-modified g-C3N4 were synthesized via pyrolysis following established literature methods.30,31 Initially, in an alumina crucible, 10 g of urea were put, with 1 g of melamine layered on top. The mixture was then heated at 550 °C for 2 h. The resulting product was thoroughly washed and dried at 100 °C, yielding g-C3N4. Halogen-modified g-C3N4 was synthesized in a single-step process by mixing 10 g of urea with 1 g of salt (KCl, KBr, or KI) and grinding the mixture for 15 minutes. To this, 1 g of melamine was added and grinding was continued in ball mill for 2 h. The mixture was then transferred to an alumina crucible, and calcined at 550 °C for 2 h in air environment. The obtained powder was washed with ethanol and dried overnight at 100 °C in an oven. The final products were labelled as X/g-C3N4, where X represents the incorporated halogen species (Cl, Br, or I).

Characterization details

XRD

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to the synthesized samples using a Rigaku Pro diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV, 200 mA) to identify the phases. Diffraction profiles were recorded over a 2θ range of 15°–80°.

XPS

The surface chemical composition of the photo catalysts was determined using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The AXIS Supra-Kratos analytical spectrophotometer, which has a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV), was used to carry out the analysis.

FTIR

To find the functional groups in the synthesized samples, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on a BRUKER ALPHA-E spectrometer with 4 cm−1 resolution.

UV-DRS

The diffuse reflectance UV-vis spectra in the 200–800 nm region were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-3660 UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer. BaSO4 was used as the standard.

TEM

To examine the morphology and microstructure of the synthesized sample, a JEOL F200 transmission electron microscope (TEM) was employed. At an acceleration voltage of 200 kV, elemental mapping using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was carried out.

N2 adsorption/desorption

In order to ascertain the physical characteristics of the freshly synthesized sample, nitrogen adsorption and desorption experiments were conducted using a Quantachrome NOVA 2200e automated gas sorption analyser in a controlled environment with a liquid nitrogen temperature of 77 K. Degassing the samples under vacuum at 200 °C for 3 h was done before analysis. Our catalysts' surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, and their pore volumes and sizes were assessed using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) desorption curve.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectra and photoelectrochemical measurements

The recombination behavior of the generated photocathode thin films was examined using photoluminescence spectra acquired at an excitation wavelength of 350 nm using a Fluoromax-4 Spectrofluorometer (Horiba Scientific). Chronoamperometric (I–t) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopic (EIS) were performed on an Autolab PGSTAT 302 N (Metrohm) equipped with a FRA32 module.

OES

To determining the gas-phase reactive species present in CO2 plasma, optical emission spectroscopy was carried out with the assistance of a Princeton Instruments Action SpectraPro SP-2300. This instrument was fitted with 600 g mm−1 gratings and a 500 nm blaze.

Results and discussion

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were employed to investigate the crystal structure of the g-C3N4 and X/g-C3N4 photocatalysts. The XRD patterns of all samples are similar, as illustrated in Fig. 2a, indicating that the graphitic-like carbon nitride phase was successfully prepared through a thermally induced polymerization reaction, with melamine serving as the precursor. Two characteristic XRD peaks at 2θ ≈ 13.2° and 27.6°, corresponding to the (100) and (002) planes of typical graphitic carbon nitride, respectively, are detected in all samples. The (100) peak reflects the in-plane repeating units of the heptazine heteroatom cycles, while the (002) peak arises from interlayer stacking of the conjugated aromatic layers. The intensity of the (002) peak gradually decreases after halogen doping (Fig. 2b), suggesting a slight reduction in stacking order/crystallinity. Notably, the (002) peak position shifts slightly toward higher 2θ values with halogen incorporation, from 27.6214° (g-C3N4) to 27.6413°, 27.6588°, and 27.6950° for Cl-, Br-, and I-doped g-C3N4, respectively. Using Bragg's law (nλ = 2d sin θ, λ = 1.5406 Å), the corresponding interlayer d-spacing was calculated to decrease progressively from 3.2275 Å (g-C3N4) to 3.2252 Å, 3.2232 Å, and 3.2191 Å for Cl-, Br-, and I-g-C3N4, respectively a contraction of ∼0.26% for I-g-C3N4 relative to pristine g-C3N4 and it is shown in Table S2. This trend correlates with the increasing atomic radius/polarizability of the halogen dopants (Cl < Br < I), and may be attributed to enhanced interlayer π–π stacking interactions or halogen substitution at terminal amino edge sites, which reduces steric hindrance and permits tighter interlayer packing, rather than direct lattice expansion via ionic-radius-driven doping.

The increased activity is mostly due to the electronic structure and surface features changing because of the halogen addition. Charge separation and charge transfer properties

Photoluminescence spectra

The steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra, obtained at an excitation wavelength of 375 nm, elucidate the dynamics of charge carriers which is shown in Fig. 6. Pristine g-C3N4 has a pronounced emission peak at 438 nm, resulting from the radiative recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs in the conjugated heptazine framework. Subsequent to halide alteration, the photoluminescence emission intensity significantly diminishes, and the emission maxima experiences a blue shift from 438 to 431 nm. The substantial reduction in PL intensity indicates that halide insertion effectively inhibits the radiative recombination of charge carriers by enhancing their spatial separation and encouraging charge migration. Concurrently, the blue shift indicates that halide inclusion alters the local electrical environment of the g-C3N4 framework, modifying the predominant emissive states. The electronic redistribution caused by substitution diminishes the influence of shallow defect-related or localized emissive states and promotes recombination via higher-energy intrinsic electronic transitions, leading to emission at a shorter wavelength. This trend signifies a more organized electronic structure with reduced radiative recombination sites, therefore enhancing charge transport efficiency.40

Steady-state photoluminescence spectra of g-C3N4 and halide modified X/g-C3N4 (X = Cl, Br, I).

Among the halide-modified samples, Br/g-C3N4 demonstrates the lowest photoluminescence emission intensity, signifying the most efficient inhibition of charge carrier recombination. This enhanced behavior can be ascribed to the excellent electrical interaction between bromine and the conjugated g-C3N4 framework.41 Due to its moderate electronegativity and strong polarizability, bromine efficiently alters the local electronic structure, improves charge delocalization, and promotes carrier transport without generating excessive defect states. Conversely, while iodine significantly reduces the band gap and enhances visible-light absorption, its greater atomic radius and pronounced structural disruption are likely to create localized electronic states that partially capture photogenerated carriers, consequently diminishing their effective involvement in surface redox reactions. As a result, Br/g-C3N4 attains the optimal equilibrium between visible-light absorption, effective charge separation, and fast interfacial charge transfer, culminating in the maximum photocatalytic activity among the examined samples.

Photoelectrochemical measurements

The photoelectrochemical performance of unmodified g-C3N4 and halide-modified g-C3N4 was assessed using chronoamperometry under intermittent light exposure at 1.23 V vs. RHE (Fig. 7a). All samples demonstrated rapid and consistent photocurrent responses during consecutive light ON/OFF cycles, signifying exceptional photo response and electrochemical stability. The pristine g-C3N4 exhibited a low photocurrent density of 0.37 µA cm−2, indicating swift recombination of photogenerated charge carriers. Following halide alteration, the photocurrent density markedly rose to 1.30, 4.60, and 9.50 µA cm−2 for I/g-C3N4, Cl/g-C3N4, and Br/g-C3N4, respectively, indicating improved charge separation and interfacial charge-transfer efficiency. Among the changed samples, Br/g-C3N4 demonstrated the highest photocurrent density, approximately 26 times more than pristine g-C3N4, signifying the most effective production, separation, and transport of photogenerated charge carriers. Despite I/g-C3N4 having the narrowest band gap, its inferior photocurrent relative to Br/g-C3N4 indicates that effective charge separation, rather than mere light absorption, dictates the photoelectrochemical performance. The results align excellently with the PL analysis, indicating that Br/g-C3N4 displayed the lowest emission intensity, hence proving reduced charge carrier recombination and enhanced charge-transfer properties. The interfacial charge-transfer characteristics of the prepared photocatalysts were further investigated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) (Fig. 7b). The Nyquist plots reveal that Br/g-C3N4 exhibits the smallest semicircle diameter, followed by Cl/g-C3N4, I/g-C3N4, and pristine g-C3N4, indicating the lowest charge-transfer resistance. This study demonstrates that a partial packing approach facilitates an efficient plasma–catalyst interaction, and which has the potential to improve CO2 conversion. The presence of packing materials affects plasma parameters. Under plasma-alone conditions, only filamentary discharges occur in the discharge zone. However, upon introducing the photocatalyst, the discharge transitions from filamentary to micro discharge and surface discharge. This transition improves the electron temperature, and electric field intensity which in turn enhances dielectric capacitance, peak-to-peak charge transfer and charge transfer per half cycle. Packing with Br/g-C3N4 further amplifies these effects as the applied voltage increases. The dielectric capacitance was measured at 0.35 µF under plasma-alone conditions, whereas the addition of Br/g-C3N4 increased it to 0.45 µF at an applied voltage of 22 kV, as shown in Fig. 8c. The Lissajous figure further demonstrated that charge transfer per half cycle and peak-to-peak charge transfer increased with packing conditions and higher applied voltages, as shown in Fig. 8d and e, for g-C3N4 and halogen modified g-C3N4.

Fig. 8f illustrates the conversion pathways of CO2 in a non-thermal plasma reactor. CO2 undergoes electron-induced cleavage, resulting in the formation of CO and O. Following that, the unstable oxygen atoms unite to form the end products, CO and O2. Moreover, CO may react with oxygen atoms, leading to the regeneration of CO2. Most of the energy in the DBD reactor is associated with the vibrational excitation of CO2. The formation of CO2+ cations, which generate CO through dissociative processes or interactions with electrons, was confirmed by optical emission spectroscopy (OES). Furthermore, oxygen atoms may rapidly recombine to form O2 and O3.

Evolution of CO2 conversion performance

Direct CO2 conversion was investigated with dielectric barrier discharge plasma reactor over photocatalyst. Halogen-modified g-C3N4 is an effective approach for modifying the electron structure and increasing the photocatalytic activity of graphitic carbon nitride.

CO2 conversion and CO selectivity

We investigated the impact of photocatalyst packing in a DBD plasma reactor on direct CO2 conversion, as illustrated in Fig. 9a. Photocatalysts generally enhance chemical stability, catalytic activity, charge carrier mobility, and visible light absorption.44 In this system, the DBD plasma simultaneously activates both CO2 molecules and the photocatalyst, thereby improving surface reactions, conversion efficiency, and product selectivity. The plasma generates high-energy electrons (1–10 eV), that react with CO2 molecules, initiating vibrational excitation and ultimately dissociation.

(a) Conversion of CO2versus applied voltage; (b) CO selectivity versus applied voltage; (c) energy efficiency versus maximum power and (d) carbon balance versus applied voltage.

In this study, we employed synthesized g-C3N4 and halogen-modified X/g-C3N4 (where X = Cl, Br, I). Among them, Br/g-C3N4 introduced intermediate energy levels within the bandgap of g-C3N4, thereby enhancing visible light absorption due to its narrower bandgap. The modified electronic structure facilitated improved charge separation and transfer efficiency.45 halogen modification introduces surface defects and alters the electronic structure of g-C3N4, which can improve charge separation and facilitate electron transfer during plasma-catalytic reactions.46 Furthermore, the presence of Br species increases the number of active sites for CO2 adsorption and activation, promoting the formation of key reaction intermediates. The synergistic interaction between Br-modified g-C3N4 and plasma-generated reactive species enhances CO2 dissociation and subsequently improves CO production. As a result, the Br/g-C3N4 photocatalyst demonstrated superior reaction performance compared to the other photocatalysts. The Br/g-C3N4-packed reactor achieved a higher CO2 conversion rate of approximately 11% and an energy efficiency of 1.2 mmol kJ−1. Conversion increased in correlation with the applied voltage.

The presence of g-C3N4 and halogen-modified g-C3N4 on the reactor surfaces enhanced CO2 adsorption, increasing the local gas residence time in the discharge zone. This effect contributed to higher CO2 conversion in catalyst-packed DBD conditions when compared to the unpacked state. Among the halogen-modified photocatalysts, Br/g-C3N4 exhibited the highest performance, which may be attributed to improved charge separation and migration, along with increased surface-active sites.47,48 These properties facilitated optimal bandgap modification, improved charge separation, and more efficient electron transfer compared to I-modified g-C3N4 and unmodified g-C3N4. Fig. 9b illustrates the CO selectivity as a function of applied voltage. CO selectivity decreased gradually from approximately 80% to 70% with increasing applied voltage. This decline was attributed to the formation of solid carbon due to side reactions (eqn (7) and (8)) occurring in the presence of the photocatalyst within the DBD reactor. The highest CO selectivity (∼90%) was observed in the DBD reactor without catalyst packing at lower applied voltage.

Optical emission spectroscopy

The optical emission spectroscopy (OES) of CO2 was recorded using a Princeton Instrument Action SpectraPro SP2300 spectrometer, equipped with 600 g mm−1 gratings and a 500 nm blaze. This analysis provided insight into the generation of reactive gas-phase products. The OES spectra for plasma alone and plasma with Br/g-C3N4 packing conditions are presented in Fig. 11b. To prevent air leakage between the dielectric and the outer electrode, the optical fiber was carefully positioned within the quartz tube, closer to the discharge zone. This placement ensured accurate recording of the OES data. The spectra revealed ionized CO2+ gas molecules at wavelengths of 338.5 nm, 357.3 nm, and 368 nm.55,56 An O2 emission peak was detected at 406 nm,57,58 while CO Angstrom bands were identified at 483 nm, 519 nm, and 553 nm.59,60 A comparative analysis of the spectra showed that plasma alone produced stronger emission peaks for CO2+, whereas the intensity of CO emission was relatively lower for the plasma catalytic conditions. The plasma reactor decorated integrated with Br/g-C3N4 exhibited enhanced CO formation, indicating an improved CO2 conversion.

Conclusions

The plasma-assisted conversion of undiluted CO2 into CO and O2 was carried out in a dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) reactor, both with and without packing materials. This study investigated the effects of packing materials, including g-C3N4 and halogen modified g-C3N4 (X/g-C3N4, where X = Cl, Br, and I) with DBD plasma on CO2 emission characteristics and conversion. When compared to plasma processes carried out without packing condition, the synergistic impact of integrating DBD plasma with g-C3N4 and X/g-C3N4 catalysts greatly increased CO2 conversion and energy efficiency. The physical characteristics of the plasma were significantly impacted by the presence of catalysts in the discharge gap, which increased the formation of reactive species, produced more energetic electrons, and strengthened the electric field. These plasma-related processes enhance the conversion of CO2. This work emphasizes the significance of energetic electrons produced in the plasma as the principal catalyst for activating photocatalysts in CO2 conversion. The combination of DBD plasma with photocatalysis exhibited a synergistic impact, facilitating effective CO2 conversion at lower temperatures.
