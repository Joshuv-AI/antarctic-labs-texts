The complexity of semidefinite programs for testing k k -block-positivity
Qian Chen, Benoît Collins

Abstract
We extend [ 3 ] by analyzing the complexity of the k k -block-positivity testing algorithm. In this paper, we investigate a symmetry reduction scheme based on rectangular shaped Young diagrams. Connecting the complexity to the dimensions of irreducible representations of U ⁡ ( d ) {\mathrm{U}}(d) , we derive an explicit formula for the complexity, which also clarifies why the semidefinite program hierarchy collapses in the k = d k=d case.



Introduction

A fundamental task in quantum information theory is to characterize linear maps. This involves determining not only whether a map is positive, but also its degree of positivity, that is, for which values of ‘
k
’ it is
k
-positive. The Choi operator of a
k
-positive map is
k
-block-positive, meaning that the operator yields nonnegative expectation value for all pure states with at most Schmidt rank
k
. It closely connects to the notions of bound entanglement and distillability of entanglement, e.g., the 2-copy distillability conjecture
[
5
]
. The set of
k
-block-positive operators is the dual of the set consisting of Schmidt number
k
states
[
10
,
9
,
7
,
6
]
.

Examining whether a operator is
k
-block-positive or not is a very challenging task.
We extend our work
[
3
]
by analyzing the complexity of the algorithm for testing
k
-block-positivity within the framework of semidefinite programs (SDPs). This algorithm converts the problem from testing
k
-block-positivity to testing
1
-block-positivity via
k
-purification, and then uses extendibility hierarchy to approximate its dual, the set of separable states.

Subsequently, we adopt a scheme that reduces some of the complexity from choosing Young diagrams in
[
3
]
.
The complexity associated with Young diagrams originates from the symmetry reduction under
{\mathrm{U}}(k)
-symmetry, which defines feasible states as positive semidefinite block matrices indexed by Young diagrams. As the extendibility hierarchy progresses to higher levels, the number of Young diagrams grows, thereby increasing the computational resources required for optimization, since the minimal value must be identified across all blocks. Thus, restricting the set of Young diagrams while maintaining the validity of the test will greatly simplify the algorithm.

Eventually, we study the complexity of this algorithm within the framework of semidefinite programs. Regarding the required SDP variables as computational resources, we derive a formula characterizing the size of this resource, which also provides a quantitative explanation of the phenomenon whereby the extendibility hierarchy should collapse in the
k=d
case.

We will show that establishing hierarchical SDPs on rectangular shape of Young diagrams is sufficient for testing
k
-block-positivity. Then we are led to the main result of this paper.

Theorem 1

(The SDP complexity of rectangular scheme)
.

Setting
N
by
N+k-1=kn
with integer
n
. The SDP complexity of the reduced SDP indexed by a rectangular Young diagram
(n^{k})
is

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{(n^{k})}=d\frac{k(d+n-1)}{k+n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{(d+n-r-1)!(k-r)!}{(k+n-r-1)!(d-r)!}.

(1)

That is to say, the SDP complexity is
O(n^{k(d-k)})
after symmetry reduction, compared with the unreduced one
O((kd)^{N+1})
.

1.2
Optimization problems for testing
k
-block positivity

This section provides a brief review of
k
-block-positivity and formulates two optimization problems for testing it. Recall that in a bipartite system
\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
, any bipartite pure state with Schmidt rank at most
k
can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ket{x_{i}}\otimes\ket{y_{i}}\,,\>\text{ where for all $i$, }\ket{x_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}},\>\ket{y_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}.

(2)

Denote by
\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
the set consisting of pure states with Schmidt rank at most
k
.
A hermitian operator
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}})
is said to be
k
-block-positive if
X
’s expectation value is nonnegative for all pure states in
\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
,

\displaystyle\langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle\geq 0\,,\>\forall\ket{\psi}\in\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B}).

(3)

A mixed state
\rho
is said to have Schmidt number
k
if it lies in the convex hull of
\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})

[
10
,
9
]
. Denote by
\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
the set of Schmidt number
k
states. In particular,
\mathrm{SN}_{1}(d_{A},d_{B})
is the set of separable states.
Clearly,
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}})
is
k
-block-positive iff
\tr(X\rho)\geq 0
for all
\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
. Throughout, we assume
d=d_{A}=d_{B}
, while occasionally retaining the subscripts
A
and
B
for notational clarity.

Definition 2

(Optimization: testing
k
-block-positivity)
.

The
k
-block-positivity of
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}})
can be tested by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{V}}=\min\tr{(X\rho)},

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\>\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B}),\>\text{ and }\tr\rho=1.

X
is
k
-block-positive iff
{\mathscr{V}}\geq 0
.

In the optimization problem,
\tr(X\rho)
serves as the
objective function
. The
feasible set
comprises all allowable states
\rho
, which are required to satisfy a set of constraints. This standard terminology is also used in the context of semidefinite programs.

It is clear that the minimal value of this linear objective function over the convex set
\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
is attained at an extreme point. Since the extreme points of
\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
are contained within the set of pure states of Schmidt rank at most
k
, the minimum can be found by optimizing over pure states in
\mathrm{SR}_{k}(d_{A},d_{B})
. Because of the inclusion
\mathrm{SN}_{1}(d,d)\subset\mathrm{SN}_{2}(d,d)\subset\cdots\subset\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d,d)\subset\cdots\subset\mathrm{SN}_{d-1}(d,d)\subset\mathrm{SN}_{d}(d,d)
, the optimal values of a minimization problem over these sets satisfy the following sequence of inequalities:

\displaystyle\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{d}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)}\leq\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{d-1}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)}\leq\cdots\leq\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{k}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)}

\displaystyle\leq\cdots\leq\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{2}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)}\leq\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{1}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)},

(5)

where
\min_{\rho\in\mathrm{SN}_{d}(d,d)}\tr{(X\rho)}
amounts to solving
X
’s minimal eigenvalue.

2.1
Reduced semidefinite programs indexed by Young diagrams

The optimization problem defined in Definition
5
is difficult to solve in principle,
but the hierarchical SDPs constructed from extendibility furnishes increasingly tight approximations, which are guaranteed to converge to the true solution as the levels of hierarchy tend to infinity.

Definition 7

(
k
-block-positivity testing SDP with
N
-Bose symmetric extension)
.

The
N
-level SDP of the Bosonic extendibility hierarchy is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{N}:=\min\tr{(X_{k,N}\rho_{k,N})},

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\>\rho_{k,N}\in\mathrm{Bos}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})_{+},\ \text{ and }\tr\rho_{k,N}=1,

where
{\cal H}=\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Here the
X_{k,N}
and
\rho_{k,N}
are defined to be

\displaystyle X_{k,N}

\displaystyle=k\Pi_{k}\otimes\mathds{1}_{k}^{\otimes(N-1)}\otimes X\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{B}}^{\otimes(N-1)},

(13)

\displaystyle\rho_{k,N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{0},\ldots,j_{N}=1}^{k}{\cal E}|i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{N}\rangle\!\langle j_{0}j_{1}\cdots j_{N}|{\cal E}^{\dagger}\otimes\rho_{i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{N},j_{0}j_{1}\cdots j_{N}},

(14)

where
\rho_{i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{N},j_{0}j_{1}\cdots j_{N}}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}d_{B}^{N}}(\mathbb{C})
.
The constraint
\rho_{k,N}\in\mathrm{Bos}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})_{+}
requires that
\rho_{k,N}\geq 0
and that
\rho_{k,N}
is invariant under all permutations of Bob’s systems, i.e.,
\rho_{k,N}=\Delta(\pi)\rho_{k,N}=\rho_{k,N}\Delta(\pi)
for all
\pi\in S_{N}
, the so-called Bosonic permutation symmetry, where the left and right actions of
\Delta(\pi)
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta(\pi)\rho_{k,N}&:=\sum_{i_{0},\ldots,j_{N}=1}^{k}({\cal E}\otimes\pi)|i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{N}\rangle\!\langle j_{0}j_{1}\cdots j_{N}|{\cal E}^{\dagger}\otimes(\mathds{1}_{d_{A}}\otimes\pi)\rho_{i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{N},j_{0}j_{1}\cdots j_{N}},\\

(15)

An alternative approach formulates the hierarchical SDPs via
N
-extendible states, i.e., states that arise as the marginal of a state that is symmetric under permutations of
N
subsystems. In our formulation, the trivial extension
\mathds{1}_{k}^{\otimes(N-1)}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{B}}^{\otimes(N-1)}
is used to construct such symmetric extensions.

It is clear that the reduced state of a Bose symmetric state is Bose symmetric as well. Consequently,
\mathscr{S}_{N}\leq\mathscr{S}_{N+1}
holds for every

, and eventually in the limit of infinite level

due to quantum de Finetti theorem
[
2
,
4
]

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathscr{S}_{N}\leq\cdots\leq\mathscr{S}_{\infty}={\mathscr{V}}_{k}\leq 0.

(16)

One can use the symmetries for reducing the difficulty of solving SDP.
Formally, suppose a group

\mathfrak{O}:G\to\mathrm{End}(V)

implies that we can restrict on
\mathfrak{O}^{*}(\mathscr{F})
where the

is the objective function and
\mathscr{F}
the set of feasible states. Therefore, by decomposing the representation

and

where

and

Every

Proposition 16
.

The complexity of the SDP in Definition

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{\lambda}:=\dim\mathrm{Hom}_{S_{N}}(\mathbb{Y}_{\lambda},\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}})^{\otimes N})=d\sum_{\mu\subset\lambda:\mu\searrow\lambda^{-}}\dim{\mathbb{U}}^{d}_{\mu}.

4.2
Complexity underlying rectangular shape of diagrams

Now we restrict ourselves to the rectangular shape scheme.
Proposition
16
indicates the characterization for the SDP complexity via the dimension of permutation-invariant Kraus operators. Having shown that the hierarchical SDPs for testing
k
-block-positivity can be established on rectangular shape Young diagrams, we will present the explicit formula for the SDP complexity.
Furthermore, this formula explains
why extendibility hierarchy would collapse in the
k=d
case.

Let us restate the main theorem in Introduction.

Theorem 18

(The SDP complexity based on rectangular scheme)
.

Setting
N
by
N+k-1=kn
with integer
n
. The SDP complexity of the reduced SDP Definition
8
is

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{(n^{k})}=d\frac{k(d+n-1)}{k+n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{(d+n-r-1)!(k-r)!}{(k+n-r-1)!(d-r)!}.

(63)

Proof.

As mentioned earlier,
(n^{k})
admits unique
\mu
with
\ell(\mu)\leq k
, that is
\mu=(n,(n-1)^{k-1})
.
The calculation is then straightforward by using formula

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{U}_{\mu}^{k}=\prod_{(i,j)\in\mu}\frac{k+j-i}{h_{\mu}(i,j)}.

(64)

to get the wanted dimension as follows,

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{U}^{d}_{(n,(n-1)^{k-1})}=\frac{k(d+n-1)}{k+n-1}\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{(d+n-r-1)!(k-r)!}{(k+n-r-1)!(d-r)!}.

(65)

Combing this expression with Proposition
16
, the proof is complete.
∎

Two corollaries can be directly read from Eq.(
63
) in Proposition
18
.

Corollary 19

(Hierarchy collapse)
.

The SDP complexity is independent on
n
if
k=d
.
In this situation, we say the SDP hierarchy of
d
-block-positivity testing collapses.

This corollary explains that the extendibility hierarchy collapses in the extreme situation
k=d
. Indeed, as already known, if
k=d
then
k
-block-positivity testing can be easily done by looking whether
X
’s minimal eigenvalue is negative or not. The extendibility hierarchy is thus not needed.

Corollary 20

(Relation of complexity for distinct
k
)
.

Let
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k\leq d
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\cal C}_{(n^{k})}}{{\cal C}_{(n^{k^{\prime}})}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{k}{d-k},\>&\text{ for }k^{\prime}=d-k,\\
&0,\>&\text{ for }k^{\prime}>d-k,\\
&\infty,\>&\text{ for }k^{\prime}<d-k.\end{aligned}\right.

(66)

Proof.

The expression

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal C}_{(n^{k})}}{{\cal C}_{(n^{k^{\prime}})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{k^{\prime}}\frac{k^{\prime}+n-1}{k+n-1}\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{k}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k-r+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})(n-1)^{d-k}}{\prod_{m=1}^{k^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k^{\prime}-m+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})(n-1)^{d-k^{\prime}}}

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)^{k(d-k)}}{(n-1)^{k^{\prime}(d-k^{\prime})}}\frac{k}{k^{\prime}}\frac{k^{\prime}+n-1}{k+n-1}\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{k}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k-r+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})}{\prod_{m=1}^{k^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k^{\prime}-m+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})}

(68)

\displaystyle=(n-1)^{(k-k^{\prime})(d-k-k^{\prime})}\frac{k}{k^{\prime}}\frac{k^{\prime}+n-1}{k+n-1}\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{k}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k-r+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})}{\prod_{m=1}^{k^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{d-r}+\frac{1}{n-1})\times\cdots\times(\frac{1}{k^{\prime}-m+1}+\frac{1}{n-1})}.

(69)

As

∎
