Consensus recommendations for the management of locally advanced rectal cancer
Authors: R-D Hofheinz, R Garcia-Carbonero, F Ciardiello, L Hamilton, P O’Donovan, R Perry, B D’Almeida, F Sclafani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) considerably impairs quality of life (QoL) and compromises prognosis. Immunotherapy induces complete responses in most patients with mismatch repair deficient/microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-h) LARC, enabling organ preservation; however, challenges include access and inconsistent guideline recommendations. This Delphi study aimed to understand the unmet needs of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC and seek recommendations for appropriate diagnosis and management.Materials and methodsEurope-based clinical experts participated in a double-blinded modified Delphi panel, with two online surveys and a virtual consensus meeting. Consensus was defined as ≥80% rating their agreement or disagreement on a 9-point Likert scale.ResultsOverall, 26 experts participated in the panel surveys, with 15 also participating in the final consensus meeting. Consensus was reached that dMMR/MSI-h LARC substantially affects QoL and a multidisciplinary team is needed for diagnosis, treatment planning, reflex mismatch repair/MSI testing, and staging. Panelists reached consensus that immunotherapy is an efficacious standard of care, offering high complete response rates, a favorable safety profile, and improved QoL. Consensus was also reached that unmet needs include long-term clinical data and biomarkers to predict treatment response and monitor for disease recurrence. Varied reimbursement policies across Europe underscore the need for updated guidelines and broader immunotherapy access.ConclusionsManagement recommendations include standardized use of immunotherapy as the preferred treatment approach due to its tolerability and efficacy. This informs the future treatment landscape direction and supports the need for long-term clinical data on immunotherapy use.Key words: consensus, locally advanced rectal cancer, management, microsatellite instability-high, mismatch repair deficient, recommendations

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second largest cause of cancer-related death and the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy in the world, with ∼1.9 million patients diagnosed each year.1,2 Rectal cancer comprises ∼40% of CRC cases, with just under 730 000 new cases of rectal cancers reported in 2022.2 Lynch syndrome, caused by germline mutations in mismatch repair (MMR) genes, is the most common heritable cause of CRC.3 Approximately 3%-5% of rectal cancers are mismatch repair deficient (dMMR), with ∼80% caused by sporadic mutations and 20% caused by a germline mutation in an MMR gene.4, 5, 6, 7 The incidence of rectal cancer is higher in men than in women and typically increases with age, although recent studies have highlighted increasing rates of early-onset CRC in young adults across multiple regions.1,4,8,9 In Europe, cases of rectal cancer reach 16-30 per 100 000 and the incidence ratio among men and women is 2 : 1, whereas in South Asia the incidence rate is ∼5 per 100 000.1,2,4 Locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) is generally classified as clinical stage II or III rectal cancer.10,11 LARC can have a considerable impact on patients’ lives, causing symptoms such as bleeding; abnormal bowel habits; abdominal, pelvic, and rectal pain; and weight loss,11,12 which can considerably impair quality of life (QoL) through persistent pain and discomfort, difficulties with sexual intimacy and body image, and increased anxiety and depression.12, 13, 14

The treatment of LARC, as advised by international guidelines, is dependent on multiple risk factors and treatment goals, leading to employment of multiple treatment options that vary on an individual basis.15 The following perioperative treatment options are mentioned: surgical management most commonly involves total mesorectal excision (TME), with abdominoperineal resection reserved for selected patients.15 Neoadjuvant treatment options include short-course radiotherapy, long-course chemoradiotherapy, systemic chemotherapy, and total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), with the treatment approach depending on tumor staging and localization, treatment intention, and response to preoperative treatment.15 The two primary TNT sequences include induction TNT, where chemotherapy is administered before chemoradiotherapy or short-course radiotherapy, or consolidation TNT, where chemotherapy is given after chemoradiotherapy or short-course radiotherapy.15 Notably, chemoradiation followed by consolidation chemotherapy has demonstrated improved organ preservation rates via greater TME-free survival than induction chemotherapy followed by chemoradiation and is thus generally a preferred option.16

TNT can result in a pathological complete response in ∼30% of patients and an estimated 21%-26% of patients experience recurrence, the majority of which occurs distantly.17,18 Furthermore, standard treatment can be associated with complications and toxic effects including bowel, urinary and sexual dysfunction, infertility, and impaired QoL.12,19,20 In patients undergoing surgery, resection of the rectum can be life altering and can warrant a permanent diverting colostomy or a low anastomosis in up to 56% of patients21; this can lead to subsequent patient issues with body image, sexual and intimacy difficulties, bowel function, and social life limitations.22,23

Over time, the treatment pathway for patients with LARC has become more complex, to balance increased survival rates while minimizing the effects on patient QoL. Nonoperative management leading to organ preservation is also a valid treatment option, with the 2025 European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) LARC clinical practice guidelines recommending the use of dostarlimab as immunotherapy for patients with dMMR/microsatellite instability-high (MSI-h) LARC, followed by either watch and wait for those that achieve a complete response or salvage resection should tumors persist.15 Immunotherapies have demonstrated considerable efficacy in the treatment of dMMR/MSI-h LARC. In a phase II study of patients with stage I-III dMMR LARC, 100% of patients treated with dostarlimab showed a complete clinical response with no disease recurrence, additional therapy, or serious adverse events.24 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as dostarlimab may spare patients with LARC from chemoradiotherapy and surgery, thereby avoiding treatment-related toxicity and associated declines in QoL. Additionally, ongoing studies are investigating optimal treatment algorithms for the watch-and-wait approach involving close, active patient surveillance.15

Despite the inclusion of dostarlimab in European guidelines, formal European Medicines Agency assessment is yet to be conducted; therefore, reimbursement plans are inconsistent across countries, preventing accessibility and standardized implementation. Better characterization and standardization of the watch-and-wait protocol, including response assessment and development of predictive molecular biomarkers, are needed. These aspects, alongside longer-term follow-up and additional research, are needed to validate this approach and support comprehensive disease-management recommendations.15 Therefore, the overall objective of this Delphi panel was to collate, clarify, and develop a consensus on the unmet needs of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC and reach a consensus on the appropriate diagnosis and treatment for these patients.

Materials and methods

A double-blinded modified Delphi study was used to elicit expert consensus on the unmet needs and appropriate diagnosis and treatment of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC. The modified Delphi study included two survey rounds and one virtual consensus meeting (Figure 1).

Steering committee

Four experts formed the steering committee (SC), contributing to material development and panel output review. To preserve objectivity and panel blinding, these experts did not participate in the Delphi rounds or the consensus meeting.

Study population

Following recommendation by the SC, experts were approached by a third-party agency and recruited based on prespecified screening criteria (see Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100419). The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all panelists, who were also provided background information on the study, the study rationale, the Delphi panel process, and their role and responsibilities throughout the Delphi panel rounds. Data collection and analysis

The first- and second-round surveys were programmed using an online platform and distributed to panelists through the third-party agency using e-mail links. Mandatory and optional questions were included to collect quantitative and/or qualitative feedback. Data were extracted and analyzed in Microsoft Excel using measures of central tendency (means, median, and mode) and levels of dispersion (standard deviation and interquartile range).

The first-round survey included broad, open-ended questions to gain qualitative insights and capture verbatim panelist responses, and several statements were ranked according to their importance to the panelist. A 9-point Likert scale was used, with 1 indicating strong disagreement and 9 indicating strong agreement (Figure 2). Consensus agreement was defined as ≥80% of panelists recording scoring 7-9, while consensus disagreement was defined as ≥80% scoring 1-3. The second-round survey included new questions reflecting topics raised by panelists in the first-round open-ended responses, whereas statements that reached consensus during the first round were excluded from the second-round survey. The second-round survey aimed to reach consensus for further statements and identify areas requiring clarification and exploration in the consensus meeting.

Likert scale used to assess consensus. Panelists were asked to rate their level of agreement using a 9-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating strong disagreement and 9 indicating strong agreement. Consensus agreement was defined as ≥80% of panelists recording a score between 7 and 9, while consensus disagreement was defined as ≥80% of panelists recording a score between 1 and 3.

After completion of the two survey rounds, a 3-h online consensus meeting was held. A smaller sample for the final meeting is desirable to allow full participation of all the attendees; however, notably, attendance was solely determined by availability as opposed to any selection process based upon previous survey answers. Panelists remained anonymous (without cameras on and using their unique identifiers) to avoid bias or hesitation to contribute. The meeting facilitated panelist discussion regarding statements or factors that did not reach consensus during the survey rounds. Panelists discussed the statements openly and were encouraged to alter statements where appropriate (e.g. syntax changes) to enable the panel to reach a consensus or to provide rationale as to why consensus could not be reached in agreement or disagreement.

The study explored the unmet needs of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC, considering aspects such as the appropriate diagnosis and treatment for these patients. Overall, 26 panelists were recruited based on prespecified screening criteria (see Supplementary Material, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100419). All panelists completed both questionnaires between August 2024 and March 2025. After completion of the two survey rounds, a consensus meeting was held in May 2025 with 15 panelists. Nonoperative management was defined as management of LARC without surgery, such as surveillance of disease (also known as watch and wait), and immunotherapies.

QoL of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC

Panelists identified multiple QoL parameters affected by LARC before treatment, including emotional wellbeing, family and social life, sexual function, self-esteem, work performance, physical activity, travel, daily activities, and ability to plan for the future. Psychological wellbeing, particularly relationships and sexual function, was reported as underdiscussed between patients and oncologists. The perceived importance of specific QoL domains was strongly influenced by patient demographics, especially age. Younger patients more frequently prioritized sexual function, fertility, and work performance, raising these topics early in consultations, whereas older patients tended to focus on physical functioning.

After treatment, the same demographic patterns persisted, with younger patients more likely to raise topics such as fertility and sexual function, while other concerns varied depending on tumor location, treatment type, and treatment duration. The heterogeneous nature of rectal cancer presentations and treatment pathways was highlighted as a key factor necessitating individualized care planning by the multidisciplinary team (MDT).

Although medical/gastrointestinal (GI) oncologists acknowledged the value of each QoL parameter, they reported that early consultations focused primarily on treatment options, side-effects, and prognosis. Additional specific QoL discussions were often addressed by other MDT members, such as surgeons or radiation therapists, who covered treatment-specific impacts in greater depth. This allows clinical priorities and patient-centered QoL considerations to be addressed in parallel, tailored to individual circumstances and life stage.

Diagnosis of dMMR/MSI-h LARC

The majority of panelists reported reflex MMR/MSI testing as a routine procedure for all CRC biopsies, whereas others noted instances where testing was omitted. Reasons for patients not being tested included referral from other institutions, gastroenterologists or pathologists overlooking the request, or delays due to reimbursement requirements for test authorization. Testing practices also differed depending on whether somatic or germline MMR/MSI testing was required. Somatic testing via immunohistochemistry was described as routine, whereas germline testing for Lynch syndrome required next-generation sequencing or polymerase chain reaction, genetic counseling, and patient consent, with the possibility of patient refusal. Consensus was reached that all patients with LARC should receive reflex MMR/MSI testing as part of their diagnostic pathway and that, in practice, this is usually carried out. For Lynch syndrome testing, panelists highlighted the need for patient consent and genetic counseling due to the hereditary implications.

In addition, consensus was achieved that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) should be used for LARC diagnosis, where available, with endoscopic ultrasound as an alternative.

Treatment of dMMR/MSI-h LARC

Standard of care and immunotherapy

Panelists discussed management approaches for stages II and III dMMR/MSI-h LARC, highlighting the heterogeneity of patient presentations and the need for individualized treatment. Factors such as tumor location, size, TNM (tumor–node–metastasis) stage, surgical eligibility, and patient consent were cited as determinants of treatment selection.

For stage II disease, many panelists favored organ-preserving strategies, noting high complete response rates with immunotherapy, particularly for low rectal tumors. Some reported universal use of immunotherapy in practice, while others cited barriers in countries where neoadjuvant immunotherapy is not reimbursed. Dostarlimab was frequently referenced for its near-100% response rates in recent clinical trials, although access varied across Europe.

Similarly, for stage III disease, consensus was reached that immunotherapy is the most suitable and internationally recognized standard of care (SoC). Panelists emphasized that dMMR/MSI-h LARC is less responsive to chemotherapy compared with immunotherapy and where immunotherapy was unavailable due to reimbursement restrictions, treatment such as TNT was utilized.

Contraindications to ICIs, such as autoimmune disorders, were acknowledged as a factor influencing treatment choice, alongside reimbursement-related administrative delays. Consensus was reached that immunotherapy-only should be offered wherever available, with combination approaches restricted to clinical trials.

Satisfaction with the current SoC was high, with panelists reaching consensus that immunotherapy is safer and better tolerated than other options for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC. It was also agreed that immunotherapy has fewer side-effects and decreases the chance of requiring surgery.

Overall, the panel confirmed that immunotherapy is the preferred and future-defining SoC for dMMR/MSI-h LARC, with variations in access representing the main barrier to standardized adoption.

Alternative treatment

Panelists agreed that the primary reasons patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC decline nonoperative management are fear of relapses, disease persistence, or recurrence, although experts agreed that long-term data suggest that relapses are rare. For patients who choose to avoid surgery, motivations include preserving the sphincter or rectum, avoiding a permanent colostomy, concerns about surgical side-effects, and maintaining overall QoL.

Surgical adverse effects are often described collectively as low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), encompassing, among others, bowel incontinence, GI disturbances, and sexual dysfunction. Panelists noted that although LARS is the most frequent complication, the severity and nature of individual symptoms vary between patients. Colostomy use is reported to be less common with the increasing use of immunotherapy, as most patients achieve a complete pathologically documented response and do not require surgery.

Unmet needs of patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC

The panelists reached consensus that patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC achieving complete response should be managed with nonoperative approaches, supporting organ preservation strategies such as immunotherapy. Panelists agreed that immunotherapy should be offered whenever available. Combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy, for example, was considered appropriate only in the context of clinical trials.

Panelists identified two main unmet needs for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC: long-term clinical data and novel biomarkers. Consensus was reached that the greatest unmet need is robust long-term clinical data for immunotherapy, as current evidence extends to just 3 years. Panelists emphasized the importance of 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival data, noting DFS is particularly valuable in nonmetastatic settings to assess recurrence risk and guide further treatment.

Additionally, consensus was reached on the need for biomarkers both to predict which patients will achieve complete response and to monitor for recurrence. Panelists distinguished between predictive biomarkers to guide treatment selection and surveillance biomarkers to detect tumor regrowth or recurrence. Development of these could refine patient selection for immunotherapy, reduce reliance on intensive radiological and endoscopic follow-up, and overall enhance the treatment of dMMR/MSI-h LARC.

Discussion

This modified Delphi study, supported by existing literature, demonstrates that MSI and/or MMR status should be assessed in all patients at diagnosis, highlighting a step toward biomarker-guided treatment. The findings indicate a future where immunotherapy plays a central role in dMMR/MSI-h LARC management. However, further research is required to understand the long-term QoL implications of treatment. Based on the findings from this study, QoL is considered of substantial importance to patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC, particularly regarding treatment planning. Panelists identified several areas where dMMR/MSI-h LARC affects QoL, including work performance, sexual function, emotional wellbeing, and the ability to plan for the future. Priorities differ by age, with younger patients placing greater importance on fertility and career-related issues, while older patients emphasize physical functioning. This highlights the need for individualized treatment plans that reflect what patients value and the importance of shared decision making between patients and physicians.

The diagnostic pathway was identified by panelists as another important consideration when treating individuals with dMMR/MSI-h LARC. Consensus was reached that all patients should undergo MMR/MSI testing as part of their diagnostic workup, with MRI recommended for staging and endoscopic ultrasound as an alternative. This is supported by the 2026 European Society of Gastrointestinal and Abdominal Radiology consensus recommendations, which emphasize the importance of the MRI-based anatomical landmark, the sigmoid take-off, in defining the upper boundary of the rectum and distinguishing rectal cancer from sigmoid cancer.25 Additionally, incorporating reflex MMR/MSI testing into the diagnostic pathway is important to guide treatment selection and identify patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC who may benefit from immunotherapy, which has shown higher response rates than traditional chemoradiotherapy-based approaches. This is supported by recent data in the phase II study of dostarlimab where responses proved durable with a recurrence-free survival rate of 96% at 2 years (95% confidence interval 86% to 99%).23

This study establishes consensus on the unmet needs for dMMR/MSI-h LARC and highlights the need for further research into predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy resistance mechanisms and disease recurrence. This would allow for the precise identification of patients most suited to receive immunotherapy treatment and enhance the treatment of dMMR/MSI-h LARC. Importantly, the study reached consensus regarding the use of immunotherapy as the SoC for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC and highlights a broader movement toward organ preservation and long-term improvements in QoL for patients. This aligns with updates to several CRC treatment guidelines. The 2025 ESMO LARC clinical practice guidelines recommend the use of dostarlimab for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC, followed by either watch and wait for those that achieve a complete response or salvage resection should tumors persist.15 Assessment of clinical complete response requires multimodal evaluation with rectal MRI, endoscopy, and digital rectal examination. Clearance kinetics of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) have emerged as a promising tumor-informed early marker of clinical response. Cercek et al.24 highlighted the dynamic response to programmed cell death protein 1 blockade whereby all patients with a complete clinical response cleared ctDNA by the end of treatment. In patients with incomplete responses or eventual recurrences, ctDNA was slow to clear and levels remained elevated throughout treatment in many cases.15 However, ctDNA is not yet validated as a stand-‍alone biomarker for response assessment or surveillance in rectal cancer, with studies reporting limitations in both specificity (OPRA trial26) and sensitivity (NO-CUT trial27). These findings highlight the need for further research to establish the role of ctDNA in monitoring treatment resistance, predicting recurrence, and guiding surveillance strategies after immunotherapy. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network 2026 update recommends either neoadjuvant or definitive immunotherapy for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC.28 Although guidelines may vary locally and nationally, the findings from this Delphi study support immunotherapy as a standardized approach for patients with dMMR/MSI-h LARC.

Furthermore, the panelists noted variation in reimbursement and access to immunotherapy across European health care systems as the primary barrier to standardized implementation. The agreement on immunotherapy as a SoC highlights the importance of global accessibility, which is critical to allow all patients to receive the safest, most efficacious treatment option and was highlighted as an unmet need in this modified Delphi study. Additionally, adequate surveillance infrastructure represents a prerequisite for the safe and equitable adoption of nonoperative management across European centers. Standardized implementation presupposes not only access and reimbursement but also multidisciplinary expertise, adequate institutional capacity, and sustained patient adherence.

This Delphi study was conducted in a robust and reliable manner, with 26 panelists completing the first- and second-round surveys and recruitment based upon previous market access archetypes noted within the literature. The recruitment of oncologists also aligned with existing literature, with medical oncologists and radiation oncologists identified as key members of the SoC MDT, providing rationale and precedent for the composition of the described Delphi panel.29 The panel size (N = 26) was sufficient to ensure representation from a range of oncologists across a broad selection of European countries and was large enough to draw robust conclusions and reach consensus on the diagnosis and management of dMMR/MSI-h LARC. Further, the utilization of a third-party agency to manage panelists allowed for the maintenance of study integrity and panelist anonymity and minimized bias.

We appreciate the limitations of our study. Although recruitment followed a prespecified screening framework, minor deviations occurred including the absence of a panelist from Czechia, recruitment of 26 rather than the planned 25 panelists, and the inclusion of 1 additional Spanish panelist. These deviations may have influenced the representativeness of the final consensus panel. In addition, the consensus meeting was conducted on the date with greatest panelist availability, with 15 panelists in attendance, which may also have affected representativeness. Although recruitment aimed to include a representative sample of stakeholders, of the 26 participants, 7 were clinical oncologists and 1 was a radiation oncologist, and the remaining panelists were medical/GI oncologists. This may have limited direct expert input on aspects of dMMR/MSI-h LARC management. Nevertheless, clinical and radiation oncologists are integral to the treatment pathway and are therefore well-positioned to provide informed perspectives. Furthermore, the screening criteria ensured stakeholder representation throughout all Delphi rounds.29,30 Surgeons were not included in the panel. Although they are not typically involved in selecting neoadjuvant therapy, they play an important role in discussing treatment options and counseling patients on surgery or nonoperative management. The study sponsor’s involvement in study design and manuscript review should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Conclusion

This Delphi study has established a series of robust consensus statements on recommendations for the management of dMMR/MSI-h LARC. The findings highlight panelist consensus on immunotherapy as the SoC and the preferred approach to treatment due to its tolerability, efficacy and safety. These findings not only inform the direction of the future treatment landscape but also support the need for long-term clinical data on the use of immunotherapy.
