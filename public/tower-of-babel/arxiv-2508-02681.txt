Accelerating Conjugate Gradient Solvers for Homogenization Problems with Unitary Neural Operators Publication type: Original article
Julius Herb, Felix Fritzen*, J. Herb, F. Fritzen

Abstract
Rapid and reliable solvers for parametric partial differential equations (PDEs) are needed in many scientific and engineering disciplines. For example, there is a growing demand for composites and architected materials with heterogeneous microstructures. Designing such materials and predicting their behavior in practical applications requires solving homogenization problems—typically governed by PDEs—for a wide range of material parameters and microstructures. While classical numerical solvers offer reliable and accurate solutions supported by a solid theoretical foundation, their high computational costs and slow convergence remain limiting factors. As a result, scientific machine learning is emerging as a promising alternative, aiming to rapidly approximate solutions using surrogate models. However, such approaches often lack guaranteed accuracy and physical consistency. This raises the question of whether it is possible to develop hybrid approaches that combine the advantages of both data-driven methods and classical solvers. To address this, we introduce UNO-CG, a hybrid solver that accelerates conjugate gradient (CG) solvers using specially designed machine-learned preconditioners, while ensuring convergence by construction. As a preconditioner, we propose Unitary Neural Operators (UNOs) as a modification of the established Fourier Neural Operators. Our method can be interpreted as a data-driven discovery of Green’s functions, which are then used much like expert knowledge to accelerate iterative solvers. We evaluate UNO-CG on various homogenization problems involving materials with heterogeneous microstructures and millions of degrees of freedom. Our results demonstrate that UNO-CG enables a substantial reduction in the number of CG iterations and is competitive with handcrafted preconditioners for homogenization problems that involve expert knowledge. Moreover, UNO-CG maintains strong performance across a variety of boundary conditions, where many specialized solvers are not applicable, highlighting its versatility and robustness, which is supported by our extensive numerical study.

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation by application

While it is assumed in many engineering applications that components are characterized by a homogeneous microstructure and can be described by explicit and closed-form material laws, this is not always the case in reality.
Real materials often exhibit a heterogeneous microstructure, which can significantly affect the material’s properties.
Pronounced examples of this are metal-matrix composites (MMCs
[
1
]
), which consist of a metallic matrix reinforced by fibers or particles of another, often ceramic, material. MMCs can show exceptional material properties, making them highly demanded materials for challenging applications, such as in aerospace engineering, the automotive industry, and the biomedical sector
[
2
]
.

However, determining the effective material behavior of components with heterogeneous microstructures remains challenging.
Multi-scale simulations are typically required to capture the nontrivial relation between macroscale behavior and microscale heterogeneities.
One established approach to address this challenge is the FE
2
(FE square) method
[
3
]
, where a finite element simulation on the macroscale is coupled with microscale finite element simulations at each Gauss point and iteration of the macroscopic analysis. Despite modern computational resources, the FE
2
approach remains prohibitively expensive from a computational perspective for many real-world applications.

Microscale simulations in this context represent homogenization problems
[
4
]
.
They are, e.g., crucial for the design of composite and architected materials
[
5
]
, which may also involve topology optimization
[
6
]
.
The microscale simulations are coupled to the macroscale simulation through the macroscopic loading acting as input, and the resulting effective stress and stiffness information is handed back to the macroscale simulation. This sought-after input-output relation depends heavily on the microstructure, a set of material parameters, and the macroscopic loadings, leading to a prohibitively high-dimensional input space.

From a mathematical point of view, these homogenization problems can be formulated as parametric partial differential equations (PDEs), with repeated solution queries involving different material parameters, microstructural geometry, and loading conditions. Over the past decades, numerous methods have been developed to accelerate the solution of such problems, which will be summarized in the following.

1.2
Direct solvers for parametric PDEs

The classical approach to solving parametric PDEs is to discretize them to an algebraic system of equations, for example, using the finite element method (FEM) or finite difference method.
The resulting linear or linearized system then consists of a stiffness matrix and a right-hand side vector that have to be assembled for a given parameter.
Usually, a solution to this algebraic problem is obtained by factorizing the stiffness matrix, e.g., using a LU or QR decomposition
[
7
]
, followed by forward and backward substitution.
These decompositions typically have a time complexity of
{\cal O}\left(n^{3}\right)
, where
n
is the number of degrees of freedom (DOF).
Often, the assembled stiffness matrix is sparse, and specialized algorithms for decompositions can be applied
[
8
]
, reducing the effort to
{\cal O}\left(n^{2}\right)
.
Still, the general drawback of this approach in a many-query scenario is that, for each new parameter, the stiffness matrix has to be reassembled and decomposed, which is computationally prohibitively expensive.

1.3
Numerical iterative solvers for parametric PDEs

Unlike direct solvers, iterative solvers approximate the solution iteratively with steadily improving accuracy, typically performing one or more matrix-vector products with the stiffness matrix in each iteration. Notably, direct access to the stiffness matrix is often not necessary, enabling the use of matrix-free methods and avoiding the computationally expensive assembly of the stiffness matrix.
It can be proven that many iterative methods converge to the exact solution, and error bounds can be derived, e.g., for the conjugate gradient method (
CG
[
9
]
) or the generalized minimal residual method (
\emph{GMRES}

[
10
]
).
If the problem at hand is poorly conditioned, e.g., induced by high contrast in the local material properties or fine discretizations, numerical iterative solvers can suffer from slow convergence.
Therefore, preconditioners are often applied to improve the convergence behavior. In order to guarantee convergence for the preconditioned solver, these preconditioners have to fulfill certain requirements. At the same time, the efficient computational evaluation of the preconditioner is of paramount importance for the overall performance of the solver.

For homogenization problems, special iterative solution methods exist that are based on alternative discretizations such as the generalized methods of cells
[
11
]
or the finite cell method
[
12
]
.
Particularly efficient are iterative solvers based on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), such as the Moulinec-Suquet scheme
[
13
]
and many other methods that build up on it, as summarized in
[
14
]
.
While many of these specialized solvers do not fit directly into the framework of established iterative solvers such as the
CG
method, some of these can act as preconditioners. For example, Fourier-Accelerated Nodal Solvers (FANS
[
15
]
) combine a FEM discretization with FFT-based approaches and are applicable as preconditioners for Krylov subspace methods.

1.4
Machine-learned surrogates for parametric PDEs

While classical solvers for parametric PDEs can reliably provide the solution with almost arbitrary accuracy and are equipped with a rich mathematical theory, including convergence guarantees and error bounds, they suffer from high computational cost.
Unfortunately, many application scenarios, such as multi-scale simulations or materials design, require the solution for many different parameters, i.e., they are used in a many-query context.
For example, homogenization problems have to be solved for a large number of different microstructures.
Often, such parametric variations lead to nontrivial changes of the solution, requiring unacceptable resources for the execution of simulations.

Due to these challenges, there has been an increase in research activities in the field of scientific machine learning (ML)
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Therein, the goal is to approximate the results of classical simulations by machine-learned surrogates, which often allow a significantly lower computational effort in the evaluation.
The aim of scientific ML is to uncover the hidden relation between input parameters and quantities of interest, e.g., emerging from the solution of parametric PDEs.
Ideally, the ML prediction is a good approximation of the exact solution while being much faster to evaluate than classical solvers once the training is completed.
Unfortunately, there are seldom guarantees that the predicted solutions are accurate or physically meaningful.
Especially for parameter extrapolation outside of the training data set, the accuracy can be poor, while there are typically no error estimates to indicate so, at the same time.
Furthermore, machine-learned surrogates are often black-box models with limited interpretability of the learned weights of the model.

One class of machine-learned surrogates for PDEs is based on physics-informed neural networks (PINNs
[
19
]
). These learn the solution of a PDE on a given discretization. Unfortunately, PINNs are often unable to generalize parametric PDEs for parameters outside the training data without the need for additional retraining. This property limits their applicability for parametric homogenization problems.
More interesting for this application are neural operators, which aim at approximating a so-called solution operator
[
20
]
for parametric PDEs and, thereby, learn a mapping between function spaces instead of relying on one fixed discretization.
Neural operators have been originally proposed in
[
21
,
22
]
and later formalized in the framework of parametric PDEs in
[
20
]
.
Especially, Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs
[
23
]
) turned out to be powerful.
Extensions of these have been explored in
[
24
,
25
]
.
As an alternative to neural operators, DeepONets are also used as machine-learned surrogate models for parametric PDEs, but are based on a different architecture involving several branch nets and trunk nets
[
26
]
.

1.5
Machine learning enhanced hybrid solvers for parametric PDEs

On the one hand, machine-learned surrogates discussed in
section

1.4
can rapidly provide approximate solutions for parametric PDEs, but without prior guarantees. On the other hand, iterative solvers addressed in
section

1.3
converge to the solution of discretized parametric PDEs with controllable accuracy, but often at the expense of many iterations.

In the present study, we focus on fusing iterative solvers with machine-learned surrogates to combine their undeniable advantages, namely the convergence guarantees of iterative schemes and the computational efficiency of machine learning. The resulting methods are referred to as
hybrid solvers
.

Although this concept is still relatively new and unexplored, there are already some approaches in this direction in the literature.
These can be categorized into direct and indirect preconditioning approaches that are, e.g., compared in
[
27
]
.
While in
direct preconditioning
a machine-learned model itself is applied as a preconditioner in each iteration, in
indirect preconditioning
only established algebraic preconditioners are constructed with the help of machine learning.
Often, algebraic multigrid (AMG) preconditioners are used for indirect preconditioning, as for example in
[
27
,
28
]
.
However, this usually involves a substantial additional computational overhead.

On the other hand, direct preconditioning is often more challenging to realize, as preconditioners for many iterative solvers have to fulfill certain properties to guarantee convergence.
For example, the
CG
method requires the preconditioner to be a symmetric and positive definite operator.
Instead, direct machine-learned preconditioners are proposed for relaxation methods in
[
29
]
, and for the Generalized Minimal Residual (
\emph{GMRES}
) method in
[
30
,
27
]
, which is a Krylov subspace method that is also applicable to matrices that are neither symmetric nor positive definite
[
10
]
.
On a different note, machine-learned preconditioners based on neural Green’s operators have been recently presented, giving rise to an interesting interpretation as machine-learned Green’s functions
[
31
]
.
However, this approach is also only applicable for the
\emph{GMRES}
method.

Despite the reported progress on hybrid solvers mainly for the
GMRES
method, the benefits of the oftentimes symmetric and positive definite systems found in most finite element discretizations cannot be exploited by the aforementioned hybrid solvers. Contrary to that, the
CG
method is far superior to the
\emph{GMRES}
method in terms of both performance and memory consumption: it benefits from the symmetry, avoids the storage of the Krylov subspace, and has fewer hyperparameters.
Moreover, the
CG
method is guaranteed to converge if the preconditioner is unconditionally symmetric and positive definite, a property that has not been hardwired into machine learning enhanced hybrid solvers to our knowledge.

The target of the present work is to extend previous efforts to enhance the performance of
CG
solvers by using machine-learned preconditioners inspired by neural operators in a direct preconditioning approach with convergence guarantees. A striking similarity of the resulting
UNO-CG
scheme with the established FFT-based solver
FANS
[
15
,
32
]
is uncovered, which draws a novel connection between very successful existing solvers and our new hybrid solver and gives rise to physical interpretation of the machine-learned preconditioner as machine-learned Green’s functions similar to
[
31
]
.

1.7
Problem setting

To formulate all covered methods in a common framework, we introduce a
linear parameter-dependent PDE (parametric PDE) that is described by a linear differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
, as established in numerical analysis and also in scientific machine learning
[
20
]
.
This operator may depend on parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
,
where the set of admissible parameters is denoted as
{\cal M}
.
The differential operator acts on the solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
of the parameter-dependent PDE with
c\in{\mathbb{N}}
components and maps it to a right-hand side
{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
that is given.
Both functions are defined on a bounded domain
\Omega\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
dimensions.
In the following, the function space containing all parameter-dependent solutions
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}
with parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
is referred to as
{\cal U}
.
Usually, these function spaces are chosen to be spaces of square-integrable functions
L^{2}(\bullet;\,\bullet)
or subspaces thereof. For example, a possible function space for the solution is typically given by the Sobolev space
{\cal U}=H^{1}(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})\subsetneq L^{2}(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})
of weakly differentiable functions.
Based on that, the parameter-dependent differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}:{\cal M}\to\mathfrak{L}\left({\cal U};{\cal U}{}^{\ast}\right)
is defined as a mapping from
{\cal M}
to the space of linear operators that themselves map the solution space
{\cal U}
to its dual space
{\cal U}{}^{\ast}
.
Then, the general formulation of a parametric PDE reads

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})={{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega\,.

(2)

It is complemented by a set of suitable boundary conditions (BC) such as, e.g., homogeneous Dirichlet BC, i.e.,
\left.{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, Neumann BC,
or periodic BC. Also, combinations of these are possible by partitioning the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Some parametric PDEs can be solved analytically using (in general matrix-valued) Green’s functions
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}:\Omega\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
that also depend on the parameters
\mu
.
Often,
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}
is also denoted as the PDE’s fundamental solution.
An admissible Green’s function fulfills the respective PDE’s boundary conditions and at the same time satisfies the equation

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\,{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\bullet)=\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\,,

\displaystyle\forall\;{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\Omega\,,

(3)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}
is the Dirac delta function (or distribution) that is centered at the position
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\Omega
.
This Green’s function can be interpreted similarly to an inversion of the differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
, and with its help, the solution of the parametric PDE in (
2
) can be obtained using the integral operator

{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}\,,

(4)

since for a linear differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
and by applying (
3
) it holds

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}={{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,.

(5)

In the special case where the differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
is translation invariant
[
33
]
, the Green’s function can be taken to be a convolution kernel, i.e., there exists a function
\overline{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{\mu}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
such that
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\overline{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})
, and thus (
4
) can be formulated as the convolution

{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\overline{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{\mu}\ast\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}\,.

(6)

However, the approach of directly solving parametric PDEs using a convolution with a Green’s function as in (
6
) is typically only straightforward for trivial cases, for instance, if the parameters
\mu
are constant over the domain, and if periodic BC are considered at the same time.

Many homogenization problems, as motivated in
section

1.1
, can be described in this framework of linear parametric PDEs if a linear material law is considered.
For many of the PDEs underlying homogenization problems, a Green’s function can be derived for spatially homogeneous parameters, i.e., a homogeneous material with constant material parameters.
However, the difficulty lies in the heterogeneity of the material parameters due to microstructures with different phases.
Solving these parametric PDEs is particularly challenging when there is a high contrast between the phases.
Thus, it is not possible to apply (
6
) directly for the presented homogenization problems, where the material parameters are heterogeneous, i.e., differ drastically in the different phases.
Nevertheless, we will see that the concept of Green’s functions will turn out to be useful in an indirect manner for solving homogenization problems multiple times throughout this work, using classical solvers and also using our proposed hybrid solver.

2
Machine-learned surrogates for parametric PDEs

2.1
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

A first approach to solving parametric PDEs using machine learning is given by Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs).
Originally proposed in
[
19
]
, PINNs aim at approximating the PDE’s solution for fixed parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
instead of approximating a solution operator that maps from the parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
to the solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\in{\cal U}
of the parameter-dependent PDE.
Thus, PINNs only have limited generalization capabilities for parametric PDEs, in general.
As soon as the parameters
\mu
, the right-hand side
{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}
, or the domain
\Omega
changes, PINNs usually have to be retrained, making their application unsuitable for parametric PDEs as introduced in
section

1.7
and, thus, for all homogenization problems covered in
section

5
.

2.2
Neural Operators

Recently, neural operators (NOs)
[
21
,
20
]
started to emerge as a new class of machine-learned surrogates that widely circumvent the limitations of Physics-Informed Neural Networks as they try to learn the mapping from the parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
to the corresponding solution of the parametric PDE
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}
directly instead of learning the solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}
for a single instance of the parametric PDEs with fixed parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
.
Originally, neural operators were proposed in
[
21
]
.
An extensive theoretical framework, including an overview of different realizations of neural operators and featuring a universal approximation theorem for nonlinear differential operators, is available in
[
20
]
.
Because of the generalization capabilities of neural operators that include extrapolation to previously unseen parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
during inference after the model is trained, neural operators can be considered a more powerful tool than PINNs in this regard.

In contrast to many classical approaches in scientific machine learning, neural operators do not just aim at learning a mapping between finite-dimensional vector spaces, for instance, a mapping from the discretized parameters on a grid to the discretized solution field on the same grid.
Instead, neural operators try to learn a mapping from the space of the parameters
{\cal M}
to the function space of the PDE’s solution
{\cal U}
. Thereby, trained neural operators can be potentially invariant to the discretization of the PDE
[
20
]
.
For a parametric PDE as defined in (
2
), a solution operator
\mathsf{G}
that maps the parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
to the corresponding solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\in{\cal U}
of the parametric PDE can be defined under the assumption that an inverse operator
\mathsf{L}^{-1}_{\mu}
of the differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
exists as

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}:{\cal M}\to{\cal U}\,,

\displaystyle\mu\mapsto\mathsf{L}^{-1}_{\mu}\,{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}={{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\,.

(7)

In fact, this is the operator that neural operators try to approximate for linear parametric PDEs
[
20
]
. By that, neural operators have the possibility to generalize to new parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
not part of the training data.

2.2.1
Architecture

First, neural operators apply a learnable lifting operator
\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{lift}}
from the parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
and potentially further arguments to a vector field
\underline{w}{}^{\{0\}}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{0}}
,
c_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consisting of artificial features.
This lifting operator could be realized using an ANN that is applied point-wise to the input field. The operator is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{lift}}:{\cal M}\times\dots\to L^{2}(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{0}})\,,

\displaystyle\underline{w}{}^{\{0\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{lift}}\left(\mu,\dots\right)\,.

(8)

Neural operators aim at approximating the operator
\mathsf{G}:{\cal M}\to{\cal U}
by learning matrix-valued integral kernels
\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}:\Omega\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{l}\times c_{l-1}}
in multiple neural operator (NO) layers that map from one feature field
\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
with
c_{l-1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
channels to another feature field
\underline{w}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
with
c_{l}\in{\mathbb{N}}
channels.
The superscript
\bullet{}^{\{l\}}
expresses the association with the
l
-th layer of the model, where
1\leq l\leq{L}
, and
{L}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
In addition, a linear operator in the form of the matrix
\underline{\underline{W}}{}^{\{l\}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{l}\times c_{l-1}}
based on learnable weights is applied as a bypass (or shortcut) in parallel to the integral operator in each layer.
Finally, each layer features a nonlinear activation function
\sigma{}^{\{l\}}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is applied point-wise at each spatial position
{\boldsymbol{x}}
to each feature channel.
Hence, the full mathematical description of the
l
-th neural operator layer reads

\underline{w}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sigma{}^{\{l\}}\left(\underline{\underline{W}}{}^{\{l\}}\,\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\int_{\Omega}\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\,{\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}\right)\,.

(9)

For
c_{l-1}=c_{l}=c
, i.e., if the number of input and output channels match the number of physical components, the integral kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}
is similar to the Green’s function that is used in the integral operator (
4
) to obtain the solution of linear PDEs.
However, the difference to an integral operator involving a classical Green’s function is that the integral operator in (
9

{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}
but on an arbitrary function that is the result of the previous NO layer.
Moreover, the number of channels
c_{l}
in each layer is usually chosen to be higher than the number of components of the PDE’s solution
c
.
Herein lies the approximation power of neural operators, similar to conventional ANNs that utilize hidden layers—with each having more artificial features than physical input or output features.

Finally, after
{L}
neural operator (NO) layers, a projection of the resulting vector field
\underline{w}{}^{\{{L}\}}
consisting of artificial features to an approximation of the solution is performed. This is described by the operator

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{proj}}:L^{2}(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{L}})\to{\cal U}\,,

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{proj}}\left(\underline{w}{}^{\{{L}\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega\,,

(10)

which—as the lifting operator
\mathsf{V}_{\mathrm{lift}}
—should be a point-wise operation in order to guarantee that the learned neural operator is invariant to the resolution used for the discretization of the parameters
\mu\in{\cal M}
and the solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\in{\cal U}
.

2.2.2
Fourier Neural Operators

In Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs), the integral kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}
is parameterized in the Fourier space.
To this end, it is assumed that a function
\overline{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\in L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{c_{l}\times c_{l-1}})
exists, such that
\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\overline{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})
.
Based on that, the application of the integral operator can be reformulated as the convolution operation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\underline{\underline{K}}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\,{\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\,{\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\left(\overline{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\ast\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}\right)({\boldsymbol{x}})\,.

(11)

By virtue of the convolution theorem, this can be computed efficiently using the FFT for
1\leq i\leq c_{l}
via

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\ast\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}\right)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{c_{l-1}}\overline{K}{}^{\{l\}}_{ij}\ast v{}^{\{l-1\}}_{j}={\cal F}^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{c_{l-1}}\widehat{K}{}^{\{l\}}_{ij}\odot{\cal F}\left(v{}^{\{l-1\}}_{j}\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{K}{}^{\{l\}}_{ij}={\cal F}\left(\overline{K}{}^{\{l\}}_{ij}\right)\,.

(12)

Overall, a single neural operator layer in FNOs (also called Fourier layer) is given by

\underline{w}{}^{\{l\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sigma{}^{\{l\}}\left(\underline{\underline{W}}{}^{\{l\}}\,\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})+{\cal F}^{-1}\left(\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\odot{\cal F}\left(\underline{w}{}^{\{l-1\}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\right)\right)\,,

(13)

where we use the operator
\odot
as shorthand notation for the matrix-valued Hadamard product with
\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}
from (
12
).

\displaystyle{\cal K}=\left\{\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}:\;1\leq i\leq{k_{\mathrm{max}}}\right\}\subsetneq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\underline{R}}}{}^{\{l\}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{k_{\mathrm{max}}}\times c_{l}\times c_{l-1}}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq l\leq{L}\,,

\displaystyle{k_{\mathrm{max}}}=\left|{\cal K}\right|\,.

(15)

In most applications, the solution

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\left(\mathrm{conj}\left(\underline{k}\right)\right)=\left(\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\left(\underline{k}\right)\right)^{\ast}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{conj}\left(\underline{k}\right)={\begin{bmatrix}k_{1}&\cdots&k_{d-1}&{-k_{d}}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}\left(\underline{k}\right)=\begin{cases}R{}^{\{l\}}_{i\bullet\bullet}&\text{if}\quad\exists i:\,\underline{k}=\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\cal K}\,,\\
\left(R{}^{\{l\}}_{i\bullet\bullet}\right)^{*}&\text{if}\quad\exists i:\,\underline{k}=\mathrm{conj}\left(\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\in{\cal K}_{\rm c}\,,\\
\underline{\underline{0}}&\text{else}\,,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\forall\underline{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\,.

(18)

Effectively, this means that the attributions of all frequency modes that are not in
{\cal K}
or

are set to zero.
For simplicity, a hyperparameter
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
is used to determine the desired number of modes in the sets
{\cal K}
and
{\cal K}_{\rm c}
in a convenient way.
For the mode selection, we introduce a notation of integer intervals as
{\left[a..b\right]}=\left[a,b\right]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In particular, the set
{\left[a..b\right]}
contains
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Then, the sets

and

can be, for example
†

\displaystyle{\cal K}={\left[{-M}..M\right]}^{d-1}\times{\left[0..M\right]}\,,

3
Iterative solvers for parametric PDEs

The machine-learned surrogates presented in
section

2
can hypothetically predict the solution of parametric PDEs with high performance.
However, these are difficult to interpret black-box models, and there are no guarantees for the accuracy of the predictions, which makes their use unacceptable in many scientific applications.
This is especially true for the homogenization problems introduced in
section

5
, as their use in multi-scale simulations requires highly accurate and physically consistent solutions of the underlying parametric PDEs.

In contrast, iterative solvers for PDEs follow a different paradigm.
In each iteration, the residual of the discretized PDE is evaluated to correct the approximated solution with different algorithms.
If implemented cleverly, for example, based on sparse linear algebra or even as matrix-free methods, a single iteration can be executed significantly faster than direct solvers and often even faster than machine-learned surrogates.
If only a few iterations are required to reach the desired accuracy, this can enable an efficient solution of parametric PDEs.
At the same time, convergence can often be proven, and there are a priori error bounds for the approximated solution in each iteration.
In this work, we only cover linear PDEs and iterative solvers for the corresponding linear algebraic systems.
However, many of them can be generalized to nonlinear problems, e.g., using a line search method.

3.1
Discretization

Here, we focus on the finite element method (FEM) often used in computational mechanics, while using other discretization methods, e.g., the finite difference method or the finite volume method, is also possible for parametric PDEs.
In the end, our presented framework of hybrid solvers with machine-learned preconditioners is independent of the discretization scheme used, and it can be applied to different discretizations.
We restrict ourselves to a regular grid for various reasons:

•

Parameters for parametric PDEs are often defined on regular grids. For example, microstructures in homogenization are often given by images that stem from CT scans or synthetic methods.

•

Fast solvers for homogenization problems and other parametric PDEs can take advantage of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and, hence, require a discretization based on regular grids.

•

Most approaches in scientific machine learning, including FNOs, operate only on regular grids.

•

A discretization with regular grids enables several technical performance optimizations since all grid cells are the same up to translation, e.g., the use of matrix-free implementations.

The considered
d
-dimensional regular grid consists of
{N_{\rm elem}}=N_{1}\cdots N_{d}
elements in form of rectangles (
d=2
) or bricks (
d=3
).
Thus, the grid contains in total
{N_{\rm nodes}}=(N_{1}+1)\cdots(N_{d}+1)
nodes.
The degrees of freedom (DOF) of the discretization are associated with a subset of these notes—called free nodes.
The number of free nodes will be denoted with
n
and depends on the boundary conditions.
When using periodic BC on all edges, there are
n_{\rm per}=N_{1}\cdots N_{d}
free nodes.
However, when using Dirichlet BC on all edges, only
n_{\rm Dir}=(N_{1}-1)\cdots(N_{d}-1)
free nodes remain.
Depending on the problem, one or multiple nodal DOF
c
are assigned per node.
Hence, the discretized problem includes
{N_{\rm dof}}=cn
DOF in total.

In the following, the parametric PDE from (

\displaystyle a({{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{w}};\mu)=l({\boldsymbol{w}};\mu)

\displaystyle\forall{\boldsymbol{w}}\in{\cal U}\,,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\cal M}\,,

(20)

with a parameter-dependent bilinear form
a(\bullet,\bullet;\mu)
and a parameter-dependent linear functional
l(\bullet;\mu)
.
In the FEM, the domain
\Omega
is divided into a finite number
{N_{\rm elem}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
of elements
\Omega^{e}
, where
1\leq e\leq{N_{\rm elem}}
. Based on the resulting mesh, an approximate solution
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\rm h}_{\mu}\in{\cal U}^{\rm h}
in a finite-dimensional function space
{\cal U}^{\rm h}\subsetneq{\cal U}
is determined, such that the variational form

\displaystyle a\left({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\rm h}_{\mu},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\rm h};\mu\right)=l\left({\boldsymbol{w}}^{\rm h};\mu\right)

\displaystyle\forall{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\rm h}\in{\cal U}^{\rm h}\,,

(21)

The discrete solution space
{\cal U}^{\rm h}
is spanned by the FE ansatz functions
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
, leading to the algebraic system (
1\leq i,j\leq cn
)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}\,{\underline{u}_{\mu}}={\underline{f}_{\mu}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu\in{\cal M}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}\right)_{ij}=a\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i};\mu\right)\,,

\displaystyle\left({\underline{f}_{\mu}}\right)_{i}=l(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i};\mu)\,.

(22)

Herein,
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}
denotes the FEM stiffness matrix and
{\underline{f}_{\mu}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
the right-hand-side vector, which both depend on the parameter
\mu
.
The vector
{\underline{u}_{\mu}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
contains the DOF of the solution
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}
in the discrete solution space
{\cal U}^{\rm h}
.
For elliptic problems, the bilinear form
a
is symmetric and coercive, implying symmetry and positive definiteness of
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}
.

3.2
Conjugate Gradient (
CG
) method

The conjugate gradient (
CG
) method is a well-known iterative solver to compute the solution of a linear system

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}\,\underline{u}=\underline{f}\,,

with

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}

\displaystyle\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{f}

\displaystyle\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{u}

\displaystyle\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

(23)

where
\underline{\underline{A}}
is a symmetric and positive definite matrix, often emerging from a FEM discretization.
The
CG
method belongs to the class of Krylov subspace methods, where approximate solutions
\underline{u}{}^{(m)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
are constructed iteratively based on certain optimality principles starting from an initial guess
\underline{u}{}^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
.
Each iteration
m=1,2,\dots
involves the computation of a search direction
\underline{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
and the corresponding optimal step width
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For that, the action of one matrix-vector product with the matrix
\underline{\underline{A}}
has to be evaluated per iteration at a computational effort in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
.

One of the problems with standard iterative solvers such as the
CG
method is that they suffer from slow convergence for poorly conditioned systems, e.g., induced by finely resolved discretizations, i.e., they require many iterations to converge.
In order to lower the number of required iterations, the
CG
method is usually used in combination with a problem-specific preconditioner.
Here, we consider the approach of left-preconditioning
[
35
]
using a preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}
.
This leads to the preconditioned linear system

\underline{\underline{P}}\,\underline{\underline{A}}\,\underline{u}=\underline{\underline{P}}\;\underline{f}\,.

(24)

Using a suitable preconditioner can lead to a significantly faster convergence.
In the case of the
CG
method, the preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}
has to be symmetric and positive definite. Then the
CG
solver applied to (
24
) converges to the solution of the original system.
Note that the trivial preconditioner
\underline{\underline{P}}=\underline{\underline{I}}
recovers the
CG
method without preconditioner (unpreconditioned
CG
).

A simple algebraic preconditioner is the Jacobi preconditioner defined as

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{P}}_{\emph{Jac}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\underline{\underline{P}}_{\emph{Jac}}\right)_{ij}=\begin{cases}1\,/\,A_{ij}&\text{if}\quad i=j\,,\\
0&\text{else}\,,\end{cases}

\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq cn\,.

(25)

In the context of the current work
\underline{\underline{A}}=\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}
,
\underline{u}={\underline{u}_{\mu}}
,
\underline{f}={\underline{f}_{\mu}}
holds.
This implies that the preconditioner
\underline{\underline{P}}
depends explicitly on the parameter-dependent stiffness matrix
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu}
, which has to be assembled and stored for each parameter realization
\mu\in{\cal M}
separately.
This is a downside as the stiffness matrix is often expensive to construct or not accessible at all.
The complete algorithmic description of the preconditioned conjugate gradient (
CG
) method is available in

.

3.3
Solvers tailored to homogenization problems

To overcome some of the challenges of solvers for parametric PDEs that arise when solving homogenization problems in a many-query context, different solution schemes specifically tailored to homogenization problems have been developed in the last decades.
Some of the most important methods are outlined in the following.

3.3.1
Lippmann-Schwinger equation

The foundation for many solvers tailored to homogenization problems is laid by the Lippmann-Schwinger decomposition that originates from computational quantum mechanics
[
38
]
.
There, it is convenient to utilize a decomposition of the Hamiltonian with an unknown eigenfunction into a suitable free Hamiltonian with a known eigenfunction that can be solved using a corresponding Green’s function, and into a remaining perturbation potential.
The Lippmann-Schwinger ansatz is also the basis for many solvers tailored to homogenization problems.
While these solvers are usually directly formulated for homogenization problems as introduced in
section

5
, we will formulate them in the general framework of parametric PDEs from
section

1.7
.
To this end, we decompose the differential operator of the linear parametric PDE (
2
) into a spatially homogeneous differential operator
\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
which is induced by a chosen set of reference parameters and a remaining differential operator for the perturbation
\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}=\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}+\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}={\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}-\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\,.

(28)

Plugging this ansatz into (
2
) yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}={{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}-\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}={{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}+\left(\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}-{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\right){{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\,.

(29)

Since
\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
is based on spatially homogeneous parameters, it is often possible to derive a corresponding Green’s function
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
such that
\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
.
Applying this to (
2
) leads to the fixed-point integral equation

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}}={\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\ast\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle\widecheck{{\boldsymbol{f}}}_{\mu}={{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}-\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}\,,

(30)

that is also known as the Lippmann-Schwinger equation. The problem
eq.

30
can be used to iterate for
{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}
using the fixed-point iteration

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m)}_{\mu}={\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\ast\left({{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}-\widecheck{\mathsf{L}}_{\mu}{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\mu}}^{(m-1)}\right)={\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m-1)}_{\mu}-{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\ast\left({\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}\,{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m-1)}_{\mu}-{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}\right)\,,

(31)

where
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the iteration number, and
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(0)}_{\mu}
an initial guess.

3.3.2
Collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet

In the pioneering work
[
39
]
, one of the first solvers for homogenization problems that employ the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is presented.
Nowadays, this is referred to as the
collocation method
of Moulinec-Suquet.
The main idea is to perform the convolution with an analytically derived Green’s function
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
as in (
30
) efficiently in the Fourier space, relying on the computational efficiency of the FFT.
While solvers for homogenization problems based on the FEM typically act on the primary variables such as displacements or temperature, the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet acts on secondary variables that depend on these, e.g., on strains and temperature gradients.
Reformulating the parametric PDE in these variables simplifies the solution process and gives rise to a Green’s function
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
for a spatially homogeneous reference material with parameters
\mu_{{\mathrm{r}}}
that can be easily represented in Fourier space.
Reformulating the convolution with the Green’s function
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
in (
30
) using the convolution theorem leads to

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m)}_{\mu}={\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m-1)}_{\mu}-{\cal F}^{-1}\left(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\odot{\cal F}\left(\mathsf{L}_{\mu}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{(m-1)}_{\mu}-{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}\right)\right)\,,

with

\displaystyle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{G}}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}={\cal F}\left({\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\right)\,.

(32)

Numerous extensions of the Moulinec-Suquet scheme (also:
basic scheme
) are available nowadays.
For instance, a generalization to nonlinear composites is presented in
[
13
]
.
In
[
40
]
, a significant acceleration of the basic scheme is achieved by replacing the fixed-point iteration by an iterative scheme that is similar to the preconditioned
CG
method in
Section

3.2
.
A comparison of different variants based on the basic scheme is available in
[
41
]
, and a variety of FFT-based solvers are summarized in
[
42
]
.

However, the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet and similar solution schemes that are purely FFT-based instead of employing a FEM discretization may suffer from various disadvantages.
For instance, checker-boarding or aliasing effects can be observed that originate from the formulation of the Green’s function in Fourier space.
Also, Gibb’s phenomenon introduces artifacts due to the mathematical nature of the Fourier series itself
[
15
,
43
]
, mainly triggered by jumps in the material parameters.
Moreover, the extensive mathematical theory behind the FEM does not apply.

3.3.3
Space Lippmann Schwinger (SLS) scheme

The Space Lippmann Schwinger scheme (SLS
[
44
]
) is another specialized solver for homogenization problems based on the Lippmann-Schwinger equation in (
31
) just like the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet.
However, it avoids the artifacts due to Gibb’s phenomenon by performing the Lippmann-Schwinger update rule (
31
) in each iteration based on a FEM discretization instead of directly in Fourier space.
Here, the convolution with
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
is computed in the real space with the help of truncated transformation tensors that are obtained using a FEM discretization
[
44
]
.
One main disadvantage of the Space Lippmann Schwinger scheme is that it is less efficient compared to the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet, as it does not rely on FFT-acceleration for the convolution operation.

3.3.4
Fourier-Accelerated Nodal Solvers (
FANS
)

In recent years, attempts have been made to merge very efficient FFT-based solvers like the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet from
section

3.3.2
into the established framework of the FEM, e.g., via the development of Fourier-Accelerated Nodal Solvers (
FANS
[
15
]
).
As the name suggests, the primary variables for
FANS
are the nodal values of a FEM discretization instead of secondary variables as in the collocation method of Moulinec-Suquet.
An alternative approach to
FANS
that also connects the FEM with Fourier-based solvers was simultaneously developed in
[
45
]
.
The goal of both approaches is to obtain solvers that reproduce the same results as a FEM discretization, i.e., avoiding the artifacts that purely FFT-based solvers usually introduce, but with an efficiency that is on par with classical FFT-based solvers.

FANS
also use a Lippman-Schwinger ansatz as in (
29
).
However, this is rewritten directly in the discretized setting as

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}\,{\underline{u}_{\mu}}={\underline{f}_{\mu}}+\left(\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}-\underline{\underline{A}}\right){\underline{u}_{\mu}}\,,

(33)

where
\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
is the FEM stiffness matrix arising from the discretization of the differential operator
\mathsf{L}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
corresponding to a reference material with spatially homogeneous parameters
\mu_{{\mathrm{r}}}
, and
\underline{\underline{A}}
is the FEM stiffness matrix for the parametric differential operator
\mathsf{L}_{\mu}
.
Under the assumption that
\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
is invertible, the ansatz in (
33
) is equivalent to the preconditioned linear system

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}\underline{\underline{A}}\,{\underline{u}_{\mu}}=\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}{\underline{f}_{\mu}}\,.

(34)

Comparing
eq.

34
and the preconditioned linear system
eq.

24
, the role of the preconditioner is taken by
\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}
, which is symmetric and positive definite for suitable reference parameters
\mu_{{\mathrm{r}}}
.
Hence, (
34
) suggests the use of iterative solvers such as the
CG
method, as long as
\mathrm{cond}_{2}(\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}\,\underline{\underline{A}})\ll\mathrm{cond}_{2}(\underline{\underline{A}})
.
Fortunately, this is the case if
\mu_{{\mathrm{r}}}
is chosen properly with respect to the set of admissible parameters
{\cal M}
for the parametric PDEs.
The idea of preconditioners based on a reference material such as
FANS
is discussed in detail in
[
46
]
, where it is also extended to other discretizations.
For spatially homogeneous reference parameters
\mu_{{\mathrm{r}}}
and if, e.g., the FEM on a regular grid is used as discretization approach, the matrix
\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
has a block circulant structure as discussed in
[
46
]
.
Due to the special structure of
\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}
, the preconditioner can be applied efficiently using a matrix-valued fundamental solution
\underline{\underline{\Phi}}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
that can be interpreted similarly to a Green’s function in the discretized setting.
For that, we introduce an underlying preconditioner operator
\mathsf{P}
, that maps a function
{\boldsymbol{r}}
associated with the residual vector in the discrete setting
\underline{r}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\right)
to a function
{\boldsymbol{s}}
associated with the preconditioned residual vector
\underline{s}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)
.
In
FANS
, the application of the preconditioning operator
{\boldsymbol{s}}=\mathsf{P}{\boldsymbol{r}}
is given by the convolution of the residual field
{\boldsymbol{r}}
with this fundamental solution

\displaystyle s_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{c}\Phi_{ij}\ast r_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{c}\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})r_{j}({\boldsymbol{y}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{y}}}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq c\,.

(35)

Again, the convolution is evaluated in the Fourier space by virtue of the convolution theorem as

\displaystyle s_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\cal F}^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{c}\widehat{\Phi}_{ij}\odot{\cal F}\left(r_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{ij}={\cal F}\left(\Phi_{ij}\right)\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq c\,,

(36)

In the discrete setting, the application of the
FANS
preconditioner, i.e.,
\underline{s}=\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}^{-1}\underline{r}
, can be computed via

\displaystyle\underline{s}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\underline{r}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\boxed{s_{i}}={\cal F}^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{c}\boxed{\widehat{\Phi}_{ij}}\odot{\cal F}\left(\boxed{r_{j}}\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq c\,.

(37)

at a time complexity of
{\cal O}\left(n\log n\right)
when relying on established FFT algorithms.
In this formulation, the
FANS
preconditioner maps the residual vector
\underline{r}
that contains the values of the discrete field
\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}}
to the preconditioned residual vector
\underline{s}
containing the values of
\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
, see also
fig.

5
.

The matrix-valued fundamental solution

\underline{\underline{A}}_{{\mathrm{r}}}
or directly in Fourier space based on the FEM gradient operators as explained in

4
Machine learning enhanced solvers for parametric PDEs

4.1
Machine-learned preconditioners

On the one hand, machine learning could be used to provide an initial guess for an iterative solver, such that it converges faster.
By that, a machine-learned surrogate model is used as a prior for the solver.
This is typically easy to realize as there are few requirements on the initial guess, but the reduction in computing time is often only moderate, as the solver still accounts for most of the computational effort, unless the solution is directly forecasted almost perfectly.
On the other hand, ML models can be integrated on a deeper level into iterative solvers to leverage their performance.
A common approach in numerical analysis that is backed by an extensive theoretical framework is the use of preconditioners, which are invoked in each iteration of the iterative solver.
It has been shown many times that by using algebraic-based preconditioners, e.g., based on an incomplete Cholesky factorization (IC
[
51
]
), or physical-based preconditioners such as
FANS

[
15
]
, the number of iterations required can be considerably reduced, almost independent of the quality of the initial guess.
This raises the question of whether machine-learned models can also take over the role as preconditioners, and to what extent convergence can be guaranteed.
The latter point is of crucial relevance, as the lack of error control is one major disadvantage over conventional machine-learned surrogates that forecast the solution directly.

Let
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}:L^{2}(\Omega,\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})\to L^{2}(\Omega,\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})
be the operator of a machine-learned surrogate such as a neural operator that is based on some vector of learnable weights
{\underline{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\rm w}}
, where
{N_{\rm w}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and that shall be used as a preconditioner.
We consider training data to calibrate the model in the form of finite sets of functions

\displaystyle{\cal R}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}:1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\right\}\subsetneq L^{2}(\Omega,\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})\,,

\displaystyle{\cal S}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}:1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\right\}\subsetneq{\cal U}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega,\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c})\,,

\displaystyle{N_{\rm s}}\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,

(38)

that satisfy the parametric PDE for some given arbitrary parameters
\mu{}^{(j)}
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}}{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

\displaystyle\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\,.

(39)

Hence, the data generation consists of solving the parametric PDE for many different parameters
\mu{}^{(j)}
and right-hand sides
{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}
.
Given training data
{\cal R}
and
{\cal S}
, the preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}
can be trained by minimizing a loss function of the form

\displaystyle\!\min_{\underline{\theta}}\quad{\cal L}\,,

where

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}l{}^{(j)}\,,

\displaystyle l{}^{(j)}=\ell\left(\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)},\,{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}\right)\,,

(40)

with a suitable sample-wise loss function
\ell:L^{2}\left(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c}\right)\times L^{2}\left(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}^{c}\right)\to{\mathbb{R}}
that measures the deviation of its arguments (prediction and reference fields).
By solving the optimization problem (
40
), we aim for a machine-learned preconditioner that is a good approximation of the inverse of the parametric differential operator
{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}
over the set of parameters
{\cal M}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\mathsf{L}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}}\approx\mathrm{id}\,,

\displaystyle\forall\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}\,.

(41)

Note that in this framework, the machine-learned preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{{\underline{\theta}}}
does not depend on the specific parameters
\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}
of the parametric PDE.
This is also the case for many classical preconditioners, including
FANS
(
section

3.3.4
).
Since the machine-learned preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}
is not aware of the present parameters and only has a limited design space, the objective in (
41
) obviously cannot be fulfilled exactly.
The ansatz of training a machine-learned preconditioner by solving the optimization problem in (
40
) based on the training data defined in (
38
) is visualized in
fig.

.

A remaining crucial aspect is how to guarantee the convergence for hybrid solvers that feature a machine-learned preconditioner.
For efficient Krylov subspace methods such as the
CG
solver, there are strict requirements on the preconditioner that are not easy to fulfill for machine-learned models.
A machine-learned surrogate, for example, could fulfill the objective in (
41
) very well, but as long as the convergence of the resulting hybrid solver can not be proven, all guarantees of the iterative numerical scheme are lost, representing a severe drawback.

4.2
Unitary Neural Operators as a preconditioner for
CG
(
UNO-CG
)

While most approaches for machine-learned preconditioners in the literature, as summarized in
section

1.5
, only deal with numerical iterative solvers having no special requirements on the preconditioner itself, such as (flexible)
\emph{GMRES}
, in the following, a method is presented to accelerate a conjugate gradient (
CG
) solver with a special machine-learned preconditioner.
To guarantee the convergence of a preconditioned
CG
solver, the preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}
must satisfy:

[A1]

Linearity
:
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\left(\alpha{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)}+{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)}\right)=\alpha\,\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)}+\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)},\;\forall\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)}

[A2]

Symmetry
:
{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)}\cdot\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)}={\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)}\;\forall{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(j)}

[A3]

Positive definiteness
:
{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}\,{\boldsymbol{r}}>0\;\forall{\boldsymbol{r}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}\,.

Furthermore, the condition of the problem should be improved (considerably) to lower the number of needed solver iterations. In the following, we carefully design a machine learning model meeting
[A1]
–
[A3]
, and, thus, with convergence guarantees.

4.2.1
Architecture

We propose an architecture inspired by a single Fourier layer from FNOs as introduced in
section

2.2.2
. Through specific modifications, the properties
[A1]
–
[A3]
are enforced.
First,
linearity

[A1]
is achieved by restricting the nonlinear activation to the identity mapping
\sigma{}^{\{l\}}{}=\mathrm{id}
.
Additionally, the forward and inverse Fourier transform
{\cal F},{\cal F}^{-1}
are replaced by a more general unitary transform
{\cal T}
and its inverse
{\cal T}^{-1}{}={\cal T}^{\ast}
, i.e., the inverse transform is equal to the adjoint transform.
This relaxation represents a significant generalization compared to classical Fourier layers: Unitary transforms comprise, e.g., the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), but also the discrete sine and cosine transform (DST, DCT). Most importantly, implementations of these algorithms with complexity
{\cal O}\left(n\,\log n\right)
exist, yielding comparable performance for all of them
[
34
]
.
Second, the number of output channels
c_{l}
is fixed to the number of nodal DOF
c
, which is one of several requirements to ensure symmetry
[A2]
.
Furthermore, the bypass is now applied in the transformed space, i.e., an
inner bypass
is used.
This results in the operator

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{s}}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle={\cal T}^{-1}\left(\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}\,{\cal T}\left({\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)+\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}\odot{\cal T}\left({\boldsymbol{r}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\right)\,,

(42)

with a bypass matrix
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
and a matrix-valued function
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
.
Both
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}
and
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}
must be learned from training data.
We refer to this modified Fourier layer as
UNO
preconditioner, whose architecture is shown in
fig.

8
.

4.2.2
Parametrization

Exactly like in FNOs as introduced in
section

2.2.2
, our proposed
UNO
preconditioner only acts on a finite number of (usually low-frequency) modes from a set
{\cal K}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
This is realized by parametrizing
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}
only on these discrete modes
\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\cal K}
via learnable weights.
While for FNOs,
\underline{\underline{W}}{}^{\{l\}}
is a general real-valued matrix and
\widehat{\underline{\underline{K}}}{}^{\{l\}}
is allowed to be complex-valued, in the
UNO
preconditioner we restrict
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}
and
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}
to be symmetric and real-valued to guarantee the symmetry
[A2]
.
In order to ensure
[A3]
, both
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}
and
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}
are required to be positive definite.
For that, we employ a local parametrization for symmetric and positive definite
c\times c
matrices
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{C}\to{Sym_{+}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}\right)}

that constructs these matrices from vectors containing
C=\frac{c(c+1)}{2}
values.
We propose an approach to assign weights
{\underline{\theta}}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
to the symmetric bypass matrix
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
and to the symmetric matrix-valued kernel
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}\left(\underline{k}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
for each mode
\underline{k}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\cal K}
with
1\leq i\leq{k_{\mathrm{max}}}
via

\displaystyle\widetilde{\underline{\underline{W}}}=\psi\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle 0\rangle}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\widetilde{K}}}(\underline{k})=\begin{cases}\psi\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)&\text{if}\quad\exists i:\underline{k}=\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\cal K}\,,\\
\psi\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)&\text{if}\quad\exists i:\underline{k}=\mathrm{conj}\left(\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\in{\cal K}_{\rm c}\,,\\
\underline{\underline{0}}&\text{else}\,,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\underline{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\,,

(43)

where a set of conjugate modes
{\cal K}_{\rm c}
similar to FNOs (see
section

2.2.2
) is considered.
The set
{\cal K}_{\rm c}
shall be chosen such that the output
{\boldsymbol{s}}
of the
UNO
preconditioner is always real-valued.
For the Fourier transform
{\cal T}={\cal F}
this is given by (
17
). For the sine transform
{\cal T}={\cal T}_{\mathsf{ST}}
, an empty set
{\cal K}_{\rm c}=\varnothing
is sufficient, for example.
Further, the weights
{\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
are collected in the vector

\displaystyle{\underline{\theta}}={\begin{bmatrix}{\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle 0\rangle}\right)}^{\sf T}&{\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle 1\rangle}\right)}^{\sf T}&\cdots&{\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle{k_{\mathrm{max}}}\rangle}\right)}^{\sf T}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\rm w}}}\,,

\displaystyle{N_{\rm w}}=C\left({k_{\mathrm{max}}}+1\right)\,.

(44)

For the local parametrization
\psi
, we utilize the Cholesky-like decomposition of a symmetric
c\times c
matrix based on a triangular matrix having only
C=\frac{c(c+1)}{2}
nonzero entries.
This approach is explained below for the most relevant special cases.

Special case of one nodal DOF (
c=1
):

In this case, the local parametrization
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
can simply be defined as
\psi\left(\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)=\left(\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)^{2}
for
0\leq i\leq{k_{\mathrm{max}}}
.

Special case of two nodal DOF (
c=2
):

Here, the local parametrization
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{Sym_{+}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}\right)}
is given by

\displaystyle\psi\left({\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)=\psi\left(\begin{bmatrix}\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}\\
\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{2}\\
\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}&0\\
\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}&\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{2}\end{bmatrix}{\begin{bmatrix}\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}&0\\
\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}&\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}\cdot\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}&\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}\cdot\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}\\
\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{1}\cdot\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}&\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{2}\cdot\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{2}+\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}\cdot\theta{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle 0\leq i\leq{k_{\mathrm{max}}}\,,

(45)

which is based on a Cholesky-like (note that the diagonal of the lower/upper triangular matrix is not enforced to be positive here, but it is in the Cholesky factorization) decomposition for each mode.

Special case of three nodal DOF (
c=3
):

Analogously, the local parametrization in the case of three nodal DOF is defined in
section

B.1
.

These variants of local parametrizations
\psi
and their derivatives with respect to
{\underline{\theta}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}
for
0\leq i\leq n
can be evaluated easily in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
, which makes them convenient to use in the preconditioner training.

4.2.3
Analysis on an algebraic level

While the
UNO
preconditioner is an operator that maps between function spaces just like general neural operators, in a hybrid solver, it is always evaluated on a discrete grid with a fixed resolution.
To facilitate further analysis, we introduce an algebraic notation to describe its action in the discrete setting.
For that, we assume that the discretization of the learned preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{\underline{\theta}}
is given by a matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}
that is determined by the learnable weights
{\underline{\theta}}
, and maps the residual vector
\underline{r}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
to the preconditioned residual vector
\underline{s}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
via
\underline{s}=\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}
.
On a discrete grid with
n
nodes and
c
nodal DOF, the unitary transform
{\cal T}
of the

UNO
layer can be represented by a unitary matrix
\underline{\underline{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{cn\times cn}
with
\underline{\underline{T}}^{-1}=\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{r}}=\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{r}=\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\,\hat{\underline{r}}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\underline{r}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{r}}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\hat{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{r}}}={\cal T}\left({\boldsymbol{r}}\right)\,.

(47)

Based on that, the mapping in (
42
) can be expressed on an algebraic level.
In fact, the linear operator that maps
\underline{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
to
\underline{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}
can be formulated explicitly as a matrix-vector product with the
UNO
preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}
via

\displaystyle\underline{s}=\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}=\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}\,,

(48)

with
\underline{\underline{Q}}
containing the values of the matrix-valued function
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c\times c}
from (
46
) on a discrete grid with
n
nodes.
The sparse matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
is a band matrix that is defined as

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{11}}\right)\right)&\cdots&\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{1c}}\right)\right)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{c1}}\right)\right)&\cdots&\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{cc}}\right)\right)\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}\,.

(49)

Recall that the vectorization operator
\mathrm{vec}\left(\bullet\right)
defined in
section

1.6
unwraps the values of
\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{ij}}
on a discrete grid into a vector according to a given mode ordering
\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, such as

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{\widetilde{\Phi}_{lm}}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\Phi}{}^{\langle 1\rangle}_{lm}&\cdots&\widetilde{\Phi}^{\langle n\rangle}_{lm}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}{}^{\langle i\rangle}_{lm}=\widetilde{\Phi}_{lm}\left(\underline{k}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq l,m\leq c\,.

(50)

A possible interpretation of the
UNO
preconditioner on an algebraic level is a Block-Jacobi-like preconditioner that consists of the sparse matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
that is, however, applied in a transformed space that is constructed by the given unitary transform
{\cal T}
in the continuous setting—or the unitary matrix
\underline{\underline{T}}
in the discrete setting.
Due to its symmetry, the unique nonzero entries of the band matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
can be collected in a vector
\underline{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Cn}
, which can also be separated into parts
\underline{v}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where each of them contains the diagonal entries of a unique block of the symmetric band matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
that is indexed with
1\leq i\leq C
, as in

\displaystyle\underline{v}={\begin{bmatrix}{\underline{v}}^{\sf T}_{1}&\cdots&{\underline{v}}^{\sf T}_{C}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Cn}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{v}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq C=\frac{c(c+1)}{2}\,.

(51)

To directly obtain the values
\underline{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Cn}
based on the learnable weights
{\underline{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\rm w}}}
, we introduce a global parametrization as

\displaystyle\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\rm w}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{Cn}\,,

\displaystyle{\underline{\theta}}\mapsto\Psi({\underline{\theta}})=\underline{v}\,,

(52)

that is implicitly defined based on the local parametrization
\psi
together with (
43
), (
46
), and (
49
) and can be evaluted in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
.

4.2.4
Convergence analysis of
UNO-CG

While
\underline{\underline{Q}}
is a sparse matrix with only
c^{2}n
nonzero entries, the preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}
itself is a dense matrix. However, it does not need to be assembled since the preconditioner is always applied in a matrix-free way.
Still, the algebraic form in (
48
) is helpful for further analysis and especially to prove convergence.
For that, the spectrum of the preconditioner is of interest.

Lemma 4.1
.

The spectrum (set of eigenvalues) of the
UNO
preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{eig}\left(\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\right)=\mathrm{eig}\left(\mathrm{blockdiag}\left(\underline{\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\right)=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{eig}\left(\underline{\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}\right)\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq n\,,

(53)

where
\mathrm{eig}\left(\bullet\right)
denotes the set of eigenvalues of
\bullet
, and
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}}{}^{\langle i\rangle}
is defined in (
50
) based on (
46
) and (
43
).

See
appendix

C
for a proof.
Following
lemma

4.1
, the preconditioner matrix
\underline{\underline{P}}_{{\underline{\theta}}}
is positive definite for suitable local parametrizations
\psi
as introduced in
section

4.2.2
.
Together with the symmetry of
\underline{\underline{P}}_{{\underline{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn\times cn}
, the

with the

preconditioner converges in exact arithmetic after at most
cn

following the established convergence theory in the literature
[

]
.

Using

as a preconditioner for the

Remark

4.3
Naive first-order training procedure for
UNO-CG

What remains to be explained is how the
UNO
preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{{\underline{\theta}}}
can be trained, i.e., how its learnable weights
{\underline{\theta}}
can be determined on the basis of training data.
For this purpose, a naive method similar to the standard techniques in scientific machine learning is presented first.
In a discrete setting, the training data from
section

4.1
that is given by the continuous fields
{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}
and
{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}
corresponds to vectors that contain their DOF according to a given discretization as in

\displaystyle\underline{s}{}^{(j)}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{r}{}^{(j)}=\mathrm{vec}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\,.

(56)

Then, the discrete operator
\underline{\underline{P}}_{{\underline{\theta}}}
of the machine-learned preconditioner
\mathsf{P}_{{\underline{\theta}}}
on a fixed regular grid with
n
nodes is trained by learning the relation between these discrete representations.
For that, we consider the given training data in the form of sets of vectors

\displaystyle{\cal S}^{\rm h}=\left\{\underline{s}{}^{(j)}:1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\right\}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal R}^{\rm h}=\left\{\underline{r}{}^{(j)}:1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\right\}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{cn}\,,

(57)

that satisfy the linear system of the discretized parametric PDE from (
22
) for given parameters, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}=\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\,,

\displaystyle\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\,,

(58)

where
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}
denotes the stiffness matrix of the discretized parametric PDE for different chosen parameters
\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}
.
As a specific realization of the loss function in (
40
), we use the MSE loss that is often used for regression problems in scientific machine learning on the DOF vectors, which leads to

\displaystyle\!\min_{\underline{\theta}}\quad{\cal L}\,,

where

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}l{}^{(j)}\,,

with

\displaystyle l{}^{(j)}=\ell\left(\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)},\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)=\left\lVert\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}-\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right\rVert^{2}\,.

(59)

In practice, the machine-learned preconditioner works best in a
CG
solver if the vectors
\underline{r}{}^{(j)}
,
\underline{s}{}^{(j)}
are close to the vectors that actually occur in the solver, see
section

3.2
.
Thus, a sensible approach is often to use

\displaystyle\underline{r}{}^{(j)}={\underline{f}{}^{(j)}_{\mu}}

and

\displaystyle\underline{s}{}^{(j)}=\underline{\underline{A}}^{-1}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}\,{\underline{f}{}^{(j)}_{\mu}}={\underline{u}{}^{(j)}_{\mu}}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\,,

(60)

as part of the training data sets
{\cal R}^{\rm h}
and
{\cal S}^{\rm h}
.
Here,
{\underline{f}_{\mu}}{}^{(j)}
is the actual right-hand side of the linear system to solve, that is,
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}\,\underline{u}{}^{(j)}={\underline{f}_{\mu}}{}^{(j)}
.
In order to gain a
UNO
preconditioner that works on an entire problem class of stiffness matrices
\underline{\underline{A}}_{\mu{}^{(j)}}
, many different parameters
\mu{}^{(j)}\in{\cal M}
for the parametric PDEs should be considered in the training data. For the naive approach of preconditioner training, we apply the standard training procedure for machine learning models based on gradient descent and its variants (SGD
[
53
]
, Adam
[
54
]
, etc.).
This requires derivatives of the evaluated loss with respect to the learnable weights as in

\displaystyle\underline{g}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\rm w}}}\,.

(61)

Here, backpropagation through the preconditioner, including the transformation
{\cal T}
is used to determine these derivatives in each epoch.
However, this is memory exhaustive and computationally expensive, having a time complexity of
{\cal O}\left({N_{\rm s}}n\log n\right)
.
In addition, these first-order optimization schemes suffer from poor convergence, especially when the global optimum has to be reached with high accuracy.

4.4
Efficient second-order training procedure for
UNO-CG

As outlined in
section

4.3
, the standard training procedure, consisting of backpropagation and first-order optimization methods such as gradient descent, is not efficient and suffers from poor convergence.
By exploiting the special advantageous structure of the
UNO
preconditioner on an algebraic level as analyzed in
section

4.2.3
, the training procedure can be drastically improved.
In fact, it is possible to derive analytical expressions for the gradients and the Hessian with respect to the learnable weights that can be evaluated in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
after preprocessing the training data.
This gives rise to a Newton-Raphson method for the preconditioner training that converges quadratically to the global optimum as long as the initial guess is good enough.
In contrast to the training procedure typically used for neural operators, the method derived in the following is efficient enough that it does not require the use of high-end GPUs to achieve short training times—even for large-scale high-dimensional problems.

We achieve this by interpreting the optimization problem from (
59
) as a nonlinear least squares problem.
To this end, we reformulate the sample-wise loss term by recalling the definition of
\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}
from (
48
) as

\displaystyle l{}^{(j)}=\left\lVert\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}-\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle={\left(\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}-\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\left(\underline{\underline{P}}_{\underline{\theta}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}-\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle={\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}-{\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}-{\left(\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{T}}{}^{\mathsf{H}}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}+\left\lVert\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle={\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{Q}}\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)-{\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)-{\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\left(\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}\right)+\left\lVert\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right\rVert^{2}.

Herein, the properties of the real symmetric matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
, and the unitary matrix
\underline{\underline{T}}
corresponding to the unitary transform
{\cal T}
are exploited.
In this result, the transformed samples of the training data

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}=\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{r}{}^{(j)}=\mathrm{vec}\left({\cal T}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{r}}{}^{(j)}}\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{s}}{}^{(j)}=\underline{\underline{T}}\,\underline{s}{}^{(j)}=\mathrm{vec}\left({\cal T}\left(\boxed{{\boldsymbol{s}}{}^{(j)}}\right)\right)\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq{N_{\rm s}}\,.

(62)

can be precomputed in a one-time effort after data generation and before training.
This is possible with a computational effort of
{\cal O}\left({N_{\rm s}}n\log n\right)
under the assumption that fast algorithms for the unitary transform
{\cal T}
exist, similar to the FFT.
These quantities are independent of the model’s weights and hyperparameters and can hence be reused in every epoch of the optimization scheme.
Furthermore, a scalar quantity
\delta
in the loss function
{\cal L}
can be precomputed via
\delta=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\delta{}^{(j)}
, where
\delta{}^{(j)}=\left\lVert\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\right\rVert^{2}
.
Based on that, the loss function can be simplified to
{\cal L}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}l{}^{(j)}
with the sample-wise loss term

\displaystyle l{}^{(j)}={\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}-{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\widehat{\underline{s}}{}^{(j)}-{\left(\widehat{\underline{s}}{}^{(j)}\right)}^{\sf H}\underline{\underline{Q}}\,\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}+\delta{}^{(j)}\,.

(63)

Since
\underline{\underline{Q}}
is a sparse band matrix with in total
c^{2}n
nonzero entries, the computational effort to evaluate the expression for the loss function in (
63
) is in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
.
Further, derivatives of the loss function with respect to the symmetric matrix
\underline{\underline{Q}}
are also easily available.
In the following, special cases are presented that account for a special structure of
\underline{\underline{Q}}
.
These cases build up on each other and are sufficient for all problems tackled in
section

5
, but can also be extended easily.

Special case of one nodal DOF (
c=1
)

The first case of having only one nodal DOF is, for instance, given for thermal homogenization problems.
If the parametric PDEs to solve is only scalar-valued, this reduces to determining the nonzero entries
\underline{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of
\underline{\underline{Q}}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{v}\right)
as introduced in
section

4.2.3
.
Following this, the loss
l{}^{(j)}
for the
j
-th sample in (
63
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle l{}^{(j)}=\left(\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)^{*}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)\cdot(\underline{v}\odot\underline{v})-2\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)^{*}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}{}^{(j)}\right)\cdot\underline{v}+\delta{}^{(j)}\,.

(64)

This result allows for to direct computation of the loss
{\cal L}
as well as its first-order and second-order derivatives with respect to the nonzero entries
\underline{v}
with a computational effort in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
via

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}l{}^{(j)}=\underline{\alpha}_{1}\cdot\left(\underline{v}\odot\underline{v}\right)-\underline{\beta}_{1}\cdot\underline{v}+\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\frac{\partial l{}^{(j)}}{\partial\underline{v}}=2\underline{\alpha}_{1}\odot\underline{v}-\underline{\beta}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,,\qquad\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}\partial\underline{v}}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\frac{\partial^{2}l{}^{(j)}}{\partial\underline{v}\partial\underline{v}}=2\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{1}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}\,,

(66)

by using the precomputed features
\underline{\alpha}_{1},\underline{\beta}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that are defined as

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}_{1}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)^{*}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{1}=\frac{2}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}{}^{(j)}\right)^{*}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}{}^{(j)}\right)\,.

(67)

Special case of two nodal DOF (
c=2
)

For mechanical homogenization problems in
d=2
dimensions,
c=2
nodal DOF are present, which requires a slightly more complicated procedure due to
C=c(c+1)/2=3
.
Therein, we introduce a split of the DOF vectors
\underline{r}{}^{(j)},\underline{s}{}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
into vectors containing the corresponding nodal DOF
\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{x}},\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{y}},\underline{s}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{x}},\underline{s}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
analogously to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{r}{}^{(j)}={\begin{bmatrix}\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{x}}\right)_{1}&\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{y}}\right)_{1}&\cdots&\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{x}}\right)_{n}&\left(\underline{r}{}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{y}}\right)_{n}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\,.\end{split}

(68)

In this case, the problem reduces to learning a symmetric band matrix that is parametrized via

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{v}_{1}\right)&\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{v}_{3}\right)\\
\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{v}_{3}\right)&\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{v}_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{v}={\begin{bmatrix}{\underline{v}}^{\sf T}_{1}\quad{\underline{v}}^{\sf T}_{2}\quad{\underline{v}}^{\sf T}_{3}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3n}\,.

(69)

Based on that, the loss
{\cal L}
over all samples as well as its first-order and second-order derivatives with respect to the nonzero entries
\underline{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3n}
of
\underline{\underline{Q}}
can again be computed in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
using

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\underline{\alpha}_{1}\cdot\left(\underline{v}_{1}\odot\underline{v}_{1}+\underline{v}_{3}\odot\underline{v}_{3}\right)+\underline{\alpha}_{2}\cdot\left(\underline{v}_{2}\odot\underline{v}_{2}+\underline{v}_{3}\odot\underline{v}_{3}\right)+\underline{\alpha}_{3}\cdot\left(\underline{v}_{1}\odot\underline{v}_{3}+\underline{v}_{2}\odot\underline{v}_{3}\right)-\underline{\beta}_{1}\cdot\underline{v}_{1}-\underline{\beta}_{2}\cdot\underline{v}_{2}-\underline{\beta}_{3}\cdot\underline{v}_{3}+\delta\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}}={\begin{bmatrix}2\underline{\alpha}_{1}\odot\underline{v}_{1}+\underline{\alpha}_{3}\odot\underline{v}_{3}-\underline{\beta}_{1}\\
2\underline{\alpha}_{2}\odot\underline{v}_{2}+\underline{\alpha}_{3}\odot\underline{v}_{3}-\underline{\beta}_{2}\\
2\underline{\alpha}_{1}\odot\underline{v}_{3}+2\underline{\alpha}_{2}\odot\underline{v}_{3}+\underline{\alpha}_{3}\odot\left(\underline{v}_{1}+\underline{v}_{2}\right)-\underline{\beta}_{3}\end{bmatrix}}^{\sf T}\,,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}\partial\underline{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}2\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{1}\right)&\underline{\underline{0}}&\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{3}\right)\\
\underline{\underline{0}}&2\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{2}\right)&\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{3}\right)\\
\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{3}\right)&\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{3}\right)&2\mathrm{diag}\left(\underline{\alpha}_{1}+\underline{\alpha}_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}\,,

(71)

with the precomputed features
\underline{\alpha}_{1},\underline{\alpha}_{2},\underline{\alpha}_{3},\underline{\beta}_{1},\underline{\beta}_{2},\underline{\beta}_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that are defined as

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}_{2}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}_{3}=\frac{1}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}+{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\mathrm{Re}\left({\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{2}=\frac{2}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\mathrm{Re}\left({\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{3}=\frac{2}{{N_{\rm s}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\mathrm{Re}\left({\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}+{\left(\widehat{\underline{r}}^{(j)}_{{\rm y}}\right)}^{\ast}\odot\widehat{\underline{s}}^{(j)}_{{\rm x}}\right)\,.

Special case of three nodal DOF (
c=3
)

For mechanical homogenization problems in
d=3
dimensions,
c=3
nodal DOF are involved.
A similar procedure for computing the loss and its derivatives in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
is available in

.

Newton-Raphson Method

In all these cases, the gradient
\underline{g}
and the Hessian
\underline{\underline{H}}={\underline{\underline{H}}}^{\sf T}
of the loss
{\cal L}
with respect to the learnable weights
{\underline{\theta}}
are available based on the parametrization
\Psi
via the chain rule in a straightforward way as in

\displaystyle\underline{g}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}}\frac{\partial\underline{v}}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}}\frac{\partial\Psi({\underline{\theta}})}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}}\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{H}}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal L}}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}\partial{\underline{\theta}}}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}\partial{\underline{\theta}}}\frac{\partial\underline{v}}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}}+\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial\underline{v}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi({\underline{\theta}})}{\partial{\underline{\theta}}\partial{\underline{\theta}}}\,.

(72)

The explicit gradient and Hessian evaluation in the proposed
UNO-CG
training enables a straightforward implementation of a Newton-Raphson scheme for the training, yielding second-order convergence and
significant
savings over the naive approach described in
section

4.3
.
Before training, the features
\underline{\alpha}{}_{m}
and
\underline{\beta}{}_{m}
for
1\leq m\leq C

{\cal O}\left({N_{\rm s}}n\log n\right)
.

UNO
preconditioner is performed with a time complexity of
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
as it is presented in
section

4.4
.
Note that the unitary transform
{\cal T}
, which represents the computationally expensive part of the
UNO

never
during the iterative training procedure.

{N_{\rm s}}cn

Cn\ll{N_{\rm s}}cn

5
Problem formulations

While the framework of
UNO-CG
is designed for general parametric PDEs as introduced in
section

1.7
, we apply it here to various homogenization problems, a common application scenario for which the
UNO-CG
hybrid solver can be benchmarked against existing, specialized solvers.
In computational homogenization, the overall goal is to determine the effective material behavior of a heterogeneous material based on a given microstructure using numerical simulations.
For that, the microstructure is assumed to be a periodic continuation of a representative volume element (RVE) with the domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The microscopic position in the RVE is denoted by
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega
.
We consider homogenization problems on both a two-dimensional and a three-dimensional RVE, i.e.,
d\in\{2,3\}
.
The two-dimensional problems are more convenient for visualization and analysis, whereas an application to three-dimensional problems shows the scalability towards real-world problems of our hybrid
UNO-CG
solver.
The domain of the RVE
\Omega=[-L_{0}/2,L_{0}/2]^{d}
(
L_{0}>0
) has boundaries

\displaystyle{\Gamma^{\pm}_{i}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\partial\Omega\;\Big|\;{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{(i)}=\pm\frac{L_{0}}{2}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq d\,,

\displaystyle{\Gamma^{\pm}}=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{d}{\Gamma^{\pm}_{i}}\,.

(73)

Each RVE is assumed to consist of two phases.
Hence, the domain
\Omega
is decomposed into subdomains (i.e., material phases)
\Omega_{0}
,
\Omega_{1}
with corresponding indicator functions
\chi_{0},\chi_{1}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
.
The assumption of two phases is only made for simplicity and does not represent a restriction of the presented methods.

Often, the microstructures for homogenization problems are given through images, i.e., each pixel or voxel of an image defines the assignment of the corresponding area or volume of the microstructure to one of the two phases.
Hence, it is convenient to use the underlying regular grid of these images as a discretization of the domain
\Omega
.
On the one hand, we consider data sets of two-dimensional microstructures with a resolution of
400\times 400
pixels that are available via
[
61
]
and have been used in
[
62
,
63
]
.
On the other hand, we will use a data set of three-dimensional microstructures with a resolution of
192\times 192\times 192
voxels (that is
\approx 7.1\cdot 10^{6}
voxels in total) that are available in
[
64
]
and lead to large-scale FEM problems.
Examples from these microstructure data sets are shown in
fig.

10
.
For all homogenization problems presented in the following, the discretization is performed using linear FEM on a regular grid as introduced in
section

3.1
.

5.1
Homogenization problems of linear heat conduction

As the first type of homogenization problems, we consider thermal problems to compute the effective thermal conductivity of microstructured materials.
Let
\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}})
be the temperature for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega
. The temperature gradient
{\boldsymbol{g}}
and the heat flux
{\boldsymbol{q}}
are related by Fourier’s law as in

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}})\otimes\nabla=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\partial\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\displaystyle\partial x_{i}}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{(i)},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{g}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

(74)

with a conductivity tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{Sym_{+}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right)}
.
The steady-state heat conduction problem then reads

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\nabla

\displaystyle=0\,.

(75)

The heat conductivity tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\left(\kappa_{0}\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\kappa_{1}\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right){\boldsymbol{I}}
is phasewise constant and assumed isotropic with conductivities
\kappa_{0}
,
\kappa_{1}
for each phase.
The assumption of isotropy is only made for simplicity and does not represent a restriction on the presented algorithms.
For homogenization problems, it is convenient to decompose the primary variable into parts that are either related to macroscopic or microscopic quantities.
For thermal homogenization problems, we decompose the temperature field into

\displaystyle\vartheta({\boldsymbol{x}})=\overline{\vartheta}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}+\widetilde{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega\,,

(76)

with the macroscopic temperature
\overline{\vartheta}
, the temperature field
\overline{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}
that is induced by a prescribed macroscopic temperature gradient
\overline{{\boldsymbol{g}}}
, and the temperature fluctuation field
\widetilde{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
The latter satisfies the zero-mean property, i.e.,
\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\vartheta}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}}=0
.
As boundary conditions for the fluctuation field
\widetilde{\vartheta}
, we consider the possibilities

•

periodic boundary conditions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left.\widetilde{\vartheta}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}}=\left.\widetilde{\vartheta}\right|_{\Gamma^{-}}\,
,

•

and Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left.\widetilde{\vartheta}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0\,
.

The differential operator of the linear heat conduction homogenization problem presented in the general framework of parametric PDEs from
section

1.7
and the right-hand side
{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\mu}}
depend on parameters
\mu=\left\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\right\}
that are given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}(\bullet)=\left(-\left(\mu_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{I}}\right)\left(\bullet\otimes\nabla\right)\right)\cdot\nabla\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\kappa_{0}\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\kappa_{1}\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}=\overline{{\boldsymbol{g}}}\,.

(77)

5.2
Homogenization problems of linear elasticity

In addition to thermal homogenization problems, we consider mechanical homogenization problems to determine the effective stiffness of microstructured materials.
Again, two-dimensional (
d=2
) and three-dimensional (
d=3
) microstructures are considered.
Given the displacement field
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the symmetric and positive definite fourth-order stiffness
{\mathbb{D}}\in{Sym_{+}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d\times d\times d}\right)}
, the strain
\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{Sym\left({\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}\right)}
and the stress
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in{Sym\left({\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}\right)}
are

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}(x)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{sym}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\otimes\nabla\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\otimes\nabla+\nabla\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{D}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,.

(78)

The quasi-static balance of linear momentum then reads

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}({\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\nabla

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{0}}\,.

(79)

Since
\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}({\boldsymbol{x}})
are symmetric second-order tensors, these can be expressed using vectors
\underline{\varepsilon},\underline{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d(d+1)/2}
based on the Mandel notation—see
appendix

A
.
For two-dimensional mechanical problems (
d=2
), the plane strain assumption is made.
We assume isotropic materials, for which the fourth-order stiffness tensor
{\mathbb{D}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathbb{D}}_{0}\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})+{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}_{i}=3\lambda^{\rm Lamé}_{i}\,{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{iso}}_{1}+2\mu^{\rm Lamé}_{i}\,{\mathbb{I}}^{\mathrm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d\times d\times d}\,,

\displaystyle i\in\{0,1\}\,,

(80)

with the Lamé coefficients
\lambda^{\rm Lamé}_{i},\mu^{\rm Lamé}_{i}>0
and the isotropic projectors as given in
appendix

A
. Again, the assumption of isotropic material behavior for each phase represents no general restriction of the presented algorithms.
As for thermal homogenization problems, we employ a decomposition for the field of the displacement field
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
into

{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}+\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

(81)

where the field
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}
is induced by a prescribed macroscopic strain
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the microscopic displacement fluctuation field satisfying
\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
.
The following boundary conditions are considered:

•

periodic boundary conditions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}}=\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{\Gamma^{-}}\,
,

•

Dirichlet boundary conditions or uniform kinematic boundary conditions (UKBC), i.e.,

\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}={\boldsymbol{0}}\,
,

•

and mixed boundary conditions as a combination of both, e.g., for some
1\leq j\leq d
,

\displaystyle\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{{\Gamma^{+}_{i}}}=\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{{\Gamma^{-}_{i}}}\,,

for

\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{j\}\,,

and

\displaystyle\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{{\Gamma^{+}_{j}}}=\left.\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right|_{{\Gamma^{-}_{j}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\,.

(82)

The different boundary conditions of homogenization problems are also discussed in, e.g.,
[
3
,
65
]
.
Mixed boundary conditions could be particularly useful for cold rolling applications
[
66
]
.
The linear elasticity homogenization problem with the Lamé coefficients
\lambda^{\rm Lamé}({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
\mu^{\rm Lamé}({\boldsymbol{x}})
can be described in the framework of parametric PDEs via

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}_{\mu}}(\bullet)={\rm div}\left(\left(3\mu_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{iso}}_{1}+2\mu_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\mathbb{I}}^{\mathrm{s}}\right){\mathrm{sym}\left(\bullet\otimes\nabla\right)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\mu=\left\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}\right\}\,,

(83)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\lambda^{\rm Lamé}_{0}\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\lambda^{\rm Lamé}_{1}\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}=\mu^{\rm Lamé}_{0}\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\mu^{\rm Lamé}_{1}\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\,

\displaystyle\mu_{3}=\overline{\underline{\varepsilon}}\,.

(84)

Résumé

7.1
Methodological summary

With
UNO-CG
, we propose a novel hybrid solver that accelerates conjugate gradient (
CG
) solvers using our machine-learned
UNO
preconditioner.
The solver is formulated in the general framework of parametric PDEs. In the present study, it is successfully applied to thermal and mechanical homogenization problems, but it can readily be applied to many other elliptic problems, e.g., to solve steady-state diffusion or permeability problems.
While many previous approaches for machine-learned preconditioners
[
30
,
29
]
are limited to use with
GMRES
or similar solvers requiring additional memory and compute time,
UNO-CG
can unleash the well-known benefits of the
CG
method commonly used for such problems.

UNOs
feature a general unitary transform instead of the Fourier transform used in Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs
[
23
]
). By limiting attention to a single linear Fourier layer with several constraints, and by replacing the Fourier transform by an arbitrary, more general unitary transform, a
Unitary Neural Operator
(
UNO
) is obtained that, by construction, denotes a linear, symmetric, and positive definite operator. The designated layout of UNO renders it admissible as a preconditioner for the
CG
scheme, and convergence of the gained hybrid
UNO-CG
solver can be guaranteed.
Interestingly, through a suitable choice of the unitary transform, it is easily possible to train
UNO-CG
for various, practically relevant boundary conditions beyond the common periodic BC, where classical solvers based on the FFT, such as
{\emph{FANS}}{}
, are not applicable straightforwardly.

Due to its architecture,
UNO-CG
can also be interpreted as a machine-learned extension of the existing Fourier-Accelerated Nodal Solvers (
FANS

[
15
,
32
]
) for homogenization problems.
This gives rise to a physical interpretation of this machine-learned preconditioner, e.g., in terms of fundamental solutions, see, e.g.,
fig.

19
. Hence, a direct comparison with existing and well-studied solvers based on explicit numerical Green’s functions is possible, whereas many approaches in the machine learning literature are based on pure black-box models.
In addition to the naive training methods for machine-learned preconditioners from the literature, usually based on computationally involved backpropagation (herein used for
UNO-CG (naive)
), we present a novel algorithm for highly memory-efficient and fast training of the
UNO
preconditioner that accounts for its special structure.
After a single preprocessing step of the training data with a computational cost in
{\cal O}\left({N_{\rm s}}n\log n\right)
, every optimization step in the training can be performed in
{\cal O}\left(n\right)
.

7.2
Result summary

In this work,
UNO-CG
is applied to linear thermal and mechanical homogenization problems, which are solved on two-dimensional and three-dimensional microstructures. This leads to linear systems of equations with up to 21 million DOF, for which direct solvers or standard iterative solvers take a prohibitively long time.
Even the use of simple algebraic preconditioners for
CG
, such as the Jacobi preconditioner, cannot sufficiently reduce the time to solution.
Further, the use of most algebraic preconditioners in a many-query scenario is hampered by the fact that they explicitly depend on the stiffness matrix and have to be reassembled for each new parameter.
By using the
UNO-CG
hybrid solver, it is possible to significantly reduce the number of iterations (measured by the preconditioner effectiveness) compared to unpreconditioned
CG
for all examined types of boundary conditions (periodic, Dirichlet, mixed). The number of iterations to convergence for periodic BC comes close to that of the analytically derived preconditioner
FANS
. Various analyses show that
UNO-CG
for periodic BC emulates FANS with high accuracy, although the machine-learned preconditioner is only trained on the basis of training data without physical insights such as, e.g., information on the local constitutive model.
A robustness study over the entire test dataset underlines the reliability of
UNO-CG
independent of the phase contrast
R
, which only mildly affects the iteration count.
The proposed novel training procedure for
UNO-CG
not only significantly reduces the cost of preconditioner training over
UNO-CG (naive)
, but the learned preconditioner also shows a much faster convergence behavior in the hybrid solver compared to naively trained
UNO-CG
.
Through this seminal improvement of the training,
UNO-CG
training can be performed for large-scale 3D problems without the need for excessive compute power and memory. For this reason, the use of
UNO-CG (naive)
for the 3D problems in the current study is infeasible. However, the numerical results indicate that the new training method is not only faster and less memory-demanding but also less prone to overfitting. It also leads to a much improved preconditioner effectiveness of
UNO-CG
vs.
UNO-CG (naive)
, see
table

4
.

7.3
Future perspective

The current study is confined to linear, parametric PDEs.
In future work, it would be interesting to investigate
UNO-CG
also for nonlinear parametric PDEs and especially homogenization problems with nonlinear material behavior.
For
FANS
, it has already been shown in
[
15
,
32
]
that a preconditioner designed for a linearized problem can also perform well for nonlinear problems involving plasticity or hyperelasticity at finite strains.
We think that
UNO-CG
might even outperform the existing solvers without needing to invest in the selection of hyperparameters (e.g., the reference material must be chosen suitably in
FANS
).
Regardless of this,
UNO-CG
could also provide acceleration for other boundary conditions where
FANS
is not applicable, also in the nonlinear regime.
Furthermore, the
UNO-CG
hybrid solver could be applied to multiphysical coupled homogenization problems such as electro-magneto-mechanical problems in a straightforward way.
Since our PyTorch-based implementation of
UNO-CG
is fully differentiable, it is well suited for solving inverse problems, which could also be explored in the future.
While we have restricted ourselves to regular grids in this work, it might be worthwhile to investigate whether an extension of
UNO-CG
could also operate on unstructured meshes similar to approaches in
[
67
,
68
]
.
Furthermore, the generalization of the preconditioner to depend on the parameters, i.e., on the microstructure, the material properties of the individual phases therein, and on the loading condition, is the subject of current investigations, but well beyond the scope of the current study.
