Eco-friendly fruit fly control through protein-gated mesoporous silica nanoparticles
Authors: Eva Garrido, Sandra Vacas, Maria Jose López Tendero, M. Dolores Marcos, Vicente Navarro-Llopis, Andrea Bernardos, Aitor Gavara, Ramón Martínez-Máñez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mediterranean fruit fly (Ceratitis capitata) is a top horticultural pest, affecting a variety of fruits and increasingly damaging extra-early citrus cultivars. Lambda-cyhalothrin, a commonly used pyrethroid insecticide, is losing effectiveness due to resistance in C. capitata, driven by repeated and sublethal treatments. In this work, we have developed an eco-friendly nanosystem based on mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with lambda-cyhalothrin and capped with hydrolysed corn protein that both attracts protein-feeding insects and seals the insecticide inside. This design protects the active ingredient from premature degradation and photolysis and ensures targeted release-triggered by protease enzymes in the fly’s saliva, thereby activating the insecticide only upon ingestion. Field tests on clementine leaves demonstrated sustained insecticidal activity for over four weeks, far longer than conventional pyrethroid sprays, while minimizing off-target exposure and sublethal dosing.

Introduction

Mediterranean fruit fly (Ceratitis capitata Wiedemann (Diptera: Tephritidae)) is considered one of the most destructive pests worldwide, infesting more than 300 different species.1) In Spain, the cultivation of extra-early citrus varieties has increased in the last decades, widening the damage window, causing higher fruit damage in early autumn, and allowing fruit fly populations to increase when temperatures are often high.2–4)

Nowadays, the fly population is mainly controlled with synthetic insecticides such as pyrethroids, most of them harmful to human health, diversity, and the environment.5–8) Among these pesticides for fruit fly control, the most significant in Mediterranean basin is λ-cyhalothrin.9) One of the main drawbacks of this pyrethroid is its poor persistence due to the rapid degradation by light. Thus, in the field and depending on the different climatic conditions, they hardly present a half-life longer than 6–7 days.10,11) Moreover, these pyrethroids generally damage the environment12) and the resistance of Spanish field populations of C. capitata to λ-cyhalothrin (6–14-fold) has been reported, achieving LC50 values (129–287 ppm) higher than the recommended concentration for field treatments (125 ppm).13) For all of this, new strategies to improve the use of pyrethroids, most of them aimed to reduce the required concentration, increasing their biocompatibility and effectiveness by affecting only target species and reducing C. capitata resistance by ensuring lethal doses should be investigated.

Among these strategies, the use of encapsulation protocols has been explored. Nowadays the pyrethroids encapsulation systems reported are usually based on organic polymers, such as nanostructured lipid carriers,11) or metal-organic frameworks, that release their cargo via degradation of the matrix or by release diffusion processes.14,15)

Based on this, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) represent a remarkable platform for a myriad of applications ranging from drug delivery,16–18) chemical communication,19–21) catalysis,22) sensing,23–25) imaging26,27) and agriculture.28,29) These nanostructures possess a highly ordered porous structure with pore sizes typically ranging from 2 to 50 nm, that can be precisely tuned to encapsulate various molecules. In addition, they have a high pore volume (ca. 1 cm3/g), a high specific surface area (ca. 1000 m2/g), and the external or internal surface can be functionalized with a wide variety of (bio)molecules.28) One of the distinguishing features of MSNs lies in their ability to act as hosts for guest molecules within their pores, thus functioning as molecular carriers. Besides, MSNs can be also equipped with molecular gates (also known as gatekeepers or nanovalves).30) This allows to design system showing “zero” delivery, yet allowing the release of the cargo from the nanopores in response to specific stimuli such as pH,31–33) temperature,34) light,35) or the presence of certain (bio)molecules.36) By incorporating molecular gates into MSNs, researchers have aimed to achieve precise control over the release kinetics of cargo molecules, enabling controlled delivery in various bio-medical and environmental applications.

Herein, we report the synthesis, characterization, and applications of an innovative eco-friendly nanodevice to control populations of C. capitata. The design is based on MSNs loaded with the pyrethroid λ-cyhalothrin and functionalized with a hydrolyzed corn protein (Nutrel) that acts as a molecular gate and as protein bait. The corn protein prevents the early release of pyrethroid, whereas insecticide delivery occurs when insect feeds on the protein due to the protease enzymes activity present in its saliva (Fig. 1). The pyrethroid-based nanosystem is tested under real field conditions on clementine leaves to study their insecticide activity at low concentrations and lasting effects. The new formulation37) is able to maintain the insecticide effect for more than 4 weeks compared with the free pyrethroid treatment, demonstrating an increment in the insecticide effect and a protection against photolysis processes of the pyrethroid.

Materials and methods

General techniques

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), elemental analysis, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), dynamic light scattering (DLS) and N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were employed to characterize the synthesized materials. PXRD measurements were taken on a D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (Bruker, Billerica, MA, U.S.A.). Thermogravimetric analyses were carried out on a TGA/SDTA 851e balance (Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, U.S.A.) in an oxidizing atmosphere (air, 80 mL min−1) with a heating rate program between 393-1273°C at 10°C min−1, followed by an isothermal heating step at 1273°C for 30 min. TEM images were taken with a 100 kV CM10 microscope (Philips, Amsterdam, NL). FTIR measurements were taken on a Tensor 27 (Bruker, Billerica, MA, U.S.A.). DLS experiments were performed using a ZetaSizer Nano ZS (Malvern). N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were recorded with a Tristar II Plus automated analyzer (Micromeritics, Norcross, GA, U.S.A.). The samples were degassed at 90°C or 120°C under vacuum overnight. Specific surface areas were calculated from the adsorption data within the low-pressure range using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) model. Pore size was determined following the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method.36,38)

Chromatographic method

The LC system was a high-pressure liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS); Agilent 1620 Infinity II HPLC (California, U.S.A.) with the autosampler operating at 25°C. Analyte separation was achieved using a Waters Atlantis™ T3 C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm, Wexford, Ireland) at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of CH3CN-H2O-formic acid (80 : 20 : 0.1, v/v/v) and was delivered using isocratic elution at a flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1 for a total run time of 8 min. After each injection, the autosampler needle was washed with 0.5 mL of CH3CN–H2O (50 : 50, v/v), and 5 µL of the sample was injected.

Mass spectrometry

An Agilent Ultivo triple quadrupole mass spectrometer was operated in positive electrospray ionization utilizing single ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The LC and the mass spectrometer were controlled by the Agilent MassHunter LCMS Acquisition software. The settings for the mass spectrometer were as follows: dwell time, 100 msec; fragmentor, 1 V; probe temperature, 300°C; nebulizer, 15 psi; and capillary voltage, 4000 V. Single Ion Monitoring (SIM) in positive ionization mode (ESI+) m/z transition was: 449.9 m/z for λ-cyhalothrin.

Solvents

All solvents were ACS reagent grade or better quality and were used without any further purification. Dichloromethane, chloroform, and acetonitrile were purchased from Scharlab S.L (Barcelona, Spain).

Chemical reagents

Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), n-cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTABr), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane, λ-Cyhalothrin and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Madrid, Spain). Nutrel (a commercial proteinaceous liquid, derived from corn and designed for use as an attractant and bait in insecticide sprays) was obtained from Agrichem (Madrid, Spain). 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) was purchased from Acros Organics (Geel, Belgium). Vivapur 30F was purchased from Rettenmaier Iberica (Barcelona, Spain). Nipagin (Methyl 4-hydroxybenzoate) and nipasol (Propyl 4-hydroxybenzoate) were obtained from Guinama (Valencia, Spain).

Synthesis of MCM-41 (Mobile Composition of Matter No. 41) mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs)39)

NaOH (2.00 mol L−1, 3.5 mL) was added to a solution of CTABr (1.00 g, 2.74 mmol) in deionized H2O (480 mL) at 40°C. The solution temperature was adjusted to 80°C, and then TEOS (5.00 mL, 2.57×10−2 mol), a liquid reagent, was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 2 hr to give a white precipitate. The solid was isolated by centrifugation, washed with deionized H2O, and then dried at 70°C for 12 hr to give the final MSNs. To remove the template phase, MCM-41 was calcined at 550°C for 5 hr in an oxidizing atmosphere.

Synthesis of MSNs-Cy

The pores of the MSNs were loaded with λ-cyhalothrin using an impregnation method. For this purpose, a saturated solution of λ-cyhalothrin (10 mg, 0.8 mmol/g MSNs) in 2 mL of CH2Cl2 was prepared and separated into two aliquots. 100 mg of MSNs were extended on a plate, and a first 1 mL of λ-cyhalothrin solution was added dropwise. The mixture was dried at 37°C, and then a second cycle of impregnation was carried out. The λ-cyhalothrin-loaded MSNs were obtained after drying the solid at 37°C. Then, the loaded nanoparticles were suspended in anhydrous CH3CN (5 mL) and an excess of (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (141 µL, 5 mmol/g solid) was added to the suspension. The solid was stirred at room temperature for 5.5 hr and then was collected by centrifugation, washed with CH3CN, and dried at 37°C to obtain the solid MSNs-Cy.

Synthesis of MSNs-Cy-Corn

A solution of hydrolyzed corn protein Nutrel (155 mg), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (16 mg), and N-hydroxysuccinimide (16 mg) in H2O (8 mL) was stirred for 30 min. After this time, solid MSN-Cy (30 mg) was added and left at room temperature overnight. Finally, the final solid was isolated by filtration and washed with H2O-miliQ at pH 7.5. A λ-cyhalothrin content of ca. 7% was calculated for solid MSNs-Cy-Corn using thermogravimetric analysis (see Fig. S3 in supporting information).

Release experiments from MSNs-Cy-Corn in the presence of proteases

For studying the protease-responsive release behavior of MSNs-Cy-Corn, the corresponding refrigerated solution of MSNs-Cy-Corn was washed with an aqueous solution at pH 7.5. In a further step, 500 µL of a MSNs-Cy-Corn solution (2 mg mL−1) were divided into two fractions. 1.250 mL of H2O at pH 7.5 was added to each aliquot up to obtain a final concentration of 0.5 mg mL−1. Then, 150 µL were taken from each aliquot, and this volume was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min to remove MSNs suspended. CH3Cl (300 µL) was added to the separated supernatant. The mixture was stirred in a vortex in order to extract of λ-cyhalothrin released from the aqueous phase. The organic phase was separated and stored in an HPLC vial. At the same time, 150 µL of H2O at pH 7.5 was added to the blank aliquot, and 5 mg of the protease was added to the other sample. Both samples were shaken over time. Aliquots were taken at scheduled times (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 24 hr) and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 5 min) following the same procedure previously described for the blank aliquot. All samples were measured using the HPLC/MS method.

MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation

The final formulation MSNs-Cy-Corn containing 25 mg λ-cyhalothrin/L was scaled-up to provide enough samples amount for laboratory and field efficacy tests. Samples were stabilized with 1% addition of microcrystalline cellulose (Vivapur 30F), showing homogeneous dispersion and no setting behavior. Finally, 0.2% Nipagin/Nipasol mixtures (1 : 1 mass proportion) were added for conservative purposes. The final formulation was an easily homogenizable suspension with 10-sec stirring.

Measurement of longevity of insecticidal activity in leaves

MSNs-Cy-Corn was tested at different ageing times to study how much time its activity remained in the field. The study was performed with the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1), a commercial λ-cyhalothrin pyrethroid (Karate Zeon, Syngenta, Switzerland) with Nutrel (2), and a control treatment with Nutrel (3). In the case of MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1), the product containing 25 mg λ-cyhalothrin/l was just mixed with H2O at 5% (w/w) because it already contains the protein bait. The Karate Zeon (Cy) was used at 0.12% (v/v) and mixed with 1.5% of Nutrel (Corn) as a protein bait (Cy-Corn) (2). The control contained H2O with the Nutrel protein bait at 1.5% (v/v) (Corn) (3). Each formulation (1, 2, and 3) was applied with a backpack sprayer at 2 atm on the south side of four 20-year-old clementine trees (Citrus reticulata), being treated 12 trees. Moreover, other 4 trees were included as a control. The 16 treated trees were identified with the date of treatment and treatment formulation, and in each tree, the leaves with drops of sprayed formulations were identified with a strip of tape. When leaves were dry, approximately 20 min after spraying, a sample of 20 leaves per treatment formulation (5 leaves per tree) were collected in plastic bags and taken to the laboratory to be tested. Likewise, 5 leaves per tree (20 per treatment formulation) were collected at 7, 14, 21, and 27 days after treatment and were taken to the laboratory to test the residual insecticide and the attractant activity at each ageing time. The collected leaves were introduced in plexiglass cages (10×10×10 cm) with 10 five-day-old C. capitata individuals, 5 females and 5 males. Each cage had a circular hole (6 cm diameter) in one of its sides covered with muslin fabric to allow ventilation. Flies were starved for 16 hr before the trials to ensure they were attracted to the bait sprayed on the leaves. A total of 20 cages were used for each ageing time, 5 cages per treatment formulation and 5 cages for the control. Four treated leaves were placed in each cage and kept in the cage for 3 hr. After this time the leaves were replaced by a rearing diet (yeast autolysate: sucrose; 1 : 4). Insecticide activity was measured by counting the number of dead flies at 1, 3, and 24 hr after the introduction of the treated leaves. Flies that did not move and did not respond to a brush touch were considered dead. The attraction of flies to the leaves was measured by counting the number of flies feeding on the leaves during the first hour of each test (summatory of the number of flies on the leaves each 5 min).

Mediterranean fruit flies were reared at our facilities in Universitat Politècnica de València, in a rearing chamber with a photoperiod of 16 : 8 (L : D) hr, 50–60% relative humidity, and temperature of 24–26°C. Adult flies were fed on a mixture of yeast autolysate and sucrose 1 : 4 (wt : wt). Larvae were reared on a mixture of wheat bran : sucrose : beer yeast : Nipagin : Nipasol : H2O: hydrochloric acid (20 : 5 : 1 : 0.5 : 0.5 : 10 : 0.1) by weight. This colony has been maintained in our laboratory since 1995. It is annually crossed with wild populations to minimize the loss of biological similarity to the wild population usually associated with laboratory colonization.

Field efficacy test

A one-month trial (from 20th of September to 17th of October 2018) was carried out in a 5,100 m2 commercial clementine orchard (Citrus reticulata cv. Clemenrubi) located in Silla (WGS 84: 39.340617, −0.406658) (Valencia, Spain). The orchard was divided into 9 plots of 24 trees (6 trees per row in 4 rows), three plots treated with each of the 3 formulations described in the previous laboratory testing: (1) MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation, (2) Cy-Corn, and (3) Corn. Each tree was sprayed in the south face fwith 200 mL of each mixture using the same conditions and backpack sprayer described in previous experiment. MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation treatment was carried out only once, on the 20th of September, 28 days before the harvest. Cy-Corn and Corn treatments were applied twice following treatment protocols,40) the first of them on the same day as the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation treatment and the second treatment 14 days later (4th of October) (Table 1). The 17th of October, two days before fruit harvest, a fruit sampling of at least 80 fruits per plot was performed, collecting all the clementines from the central tree of each plot with a total of at least 240 (3 plots×80 fruit/plot) fruits per treatment. The clementines were taken to the laboratory and kept for 7 days at 24°C and 50% RH to allow larval development. After this period, all mandarins were individually visually inspected to detect signs of fruit damage due to larval development.

Results and discussion

The nanodevice design, illustrated in Fig. 1, is based on mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) loaded with the pyrethroid λ-cyhalothrin and capped with hydrolyzed corn protein (MSNs-Cy-Corn in Fig. 1). Corn protein was chosen because C. capitata has a high affinity for this protein.41) Specifically, MSNs were synthesized following well-established procedures39) and the λ-cyhalothrin was loaded into the pores outlets. Subsequently, the external surface of MSNs was functionalized with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (MSN-Cy). In the following step, the hydrolyzed corn protein was covalently grafted to the amine groups on MSNs-Cy in the presence of EDC and NHS in an aqueous solution. The suspension was stirred at room temperature overnight. The final nanosystem (MSNs-Cy-Corn) was obtained by successive washes with H2O at pH 7.5 to remove unentrapped payload or unanchored corn protein (Scheme S1).

To validate the successful synthesis of the nanomaterials, we employed standard characterization techniques to analyze the MSNs scaffold and solids MSNs-Cy and MSNs-Cy-Corn. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) of the as-synthesized MSNs nanoparticles revealed characteristic low-angle reflections, indicative of a hexagonal array, with indexed peaks at (100), (110), (200), and (210) Bragg peaks (Fig. S1). Additionally, a displacement of the (100) peak was observed in the PXRD pattern of calcined nanoparticles, attributed to the condensation of silanol groups during the calcination process (Fig. S1). The PXRD pattern of solid MSNs-Cy and MSNs-Cy-Corn displayed decreased peak intensities and broadening of reflections, attributed to the filling of pore voids with λ-cyhalothrin and surface functionalization (Fig. S1). N2 adsorption–desorption studies revealed a type IV isotherm for the calcined MSNs, suggesting the presence of uniform and cylindrical mesopores (Fig. S2). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) model indicated a specific surface area of 1021 m2 g−1 for the calcined MSNs (Fig. S2). The lack of a hysteresis cycle within this range and the shape curve indicates the presence of uniform and cylindrical mesopores, with a pore diameter of 2.84 nm and a total pore volume of 0.83 cm3 g−1. These values were calculated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model on the adsorption branch of the isotherm. Likewise, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm of MSNs-Cy-Corn displayed characteristics typical of mesoporous systems with partially filled mesopores (curve b in Fig. S2). BET and BJH models demonstrated a significant decrease in specific surface area (367 m2 g−1) and a reduction in pore volume (0.58 cm3 g−1) for MSNs-Cy-Corn. From thermogravimetric and elemental analysis (Fig. S3), amounts of 72 and 320 mg of λ-cyhalothrin and protein respectively per gram of MSNs-Cy-Corn solid were calculated, corresponding to a 38% of organic matter content in MSNs-Cy-Corn. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 2a, b) confirmed the typical hexagonal porosity of the mesoporous matrix for all solids obtained. Furthermore, dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2c, d) were carried out to evaluate the different steps of nanodevice preparation. Hydrodynamic diameter values of 159±2 and 206±7 nm were found for the starting MSNs and MSNs-Cy-Corn solid, respectively. Additionally, the zeta potential was −25.2 for calcined MSNs and −35.7 mV for MSNs-Cy-Corn. The nanoparticles’ functionalization was also confirmed by FTIR analysis. FTIR spectrum of calcinated MSNs shows typical absorption bands at ca. 1070 cm−1 related to the bond stretching vibrations of Si–O–Si. Moreover, FTIR spectrum of solid MSNs-Cy-Corn shows absorption bands indicating the presence of the protein with characteristic bands at ca. 1635 and 3375 cm−1 (Fig. S4). The characterization techniques confirm the successful synthesis and functionalization of the nanomaterials.

After characterization of the nanoparticles, we carried out release cargo studies using MSNs-Cy-Corn (2 mg) in 2 mL of H2O at pH 7.5 both, in the absence and the presence of proteases from Streptomyces griseus (see experimental section for details) to confirm the relevant role of the hydrolyzed corn protein as molecular gate. The resulting payload delivery kinetics are depicted in Fig. 3. In the absence of enzymes, there is a low cargo delivery, whereas, in its presence, a significant release of λ-cyhalothrin from MSNs-Cy-Corn is observed. This delivery correlates with the protease-mediated hydrolysis of the corn protein from the nanoparticles surface, resulting in cargo delivery. The experiment demonstrates the performance of the hydrolyzed corn protein as gatekeeper.

Fig. 3. Delivery profiles of λ-cyhalothrin from MSNs-Cy-Corn in H2O at pH 7.5 in the absence (black line) and in the presence (green line) of protease from Streptomyces griseus (0.8 mg/mL).

Then, MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation was prepared by addition of 1% of microcrystalline cellulose and 0.2% Nipagin/Nipasol to MSNs-Cy-Corn suspensions. The final mixture showed a homogeneous dispersion and no setting behavior. The formulation was used for the measurement of the longevity of insecticidal activity in leaves. Fruit fly mortality was recorded at 1, 3, and 24 hr after their exposure to leaves treated at different ageing times (0, 7, 14, 21 and 27 days). The assay showed that the mortality was lower at 1 hr at which many flies remained affected but alive (data not shown). At 24 hr, on average, 17% of the flies recovered from the knockdown effect of the pyrethroid. This effect has been reported for susceptible and resistant C. capitata populations to λ-cyhalothrin, with 50% of the flies recovered from the knockdown from 4 to 24 hr after the treatment, depending on the susceptibility and the dose.42) Nevertheless, these knocked-down flies are susceptible to predation under field conditions, being eliminated before they can recover.43) For this reason, and to take into account the full knockdown effect, only the mortality of flies after 3 hr of exposure is depicted in Fig. 4, showing how insecticide activity persist over the time after spraying with MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1), Cy+Corn formulation (2) and the Corn control (3). Accordingly, flies’ mortality was over 80% when exposed to leaves recently (0 day) treated with both formulations containing λ-cyhalothrin. However, the mortality decreased when flies were exposed to leaves treated 1, 2, 3 or 4 weeks before conducting the test. This suggests the λ-cyhalothrin degradation over the time, which has been reported to be environmentally caused by light exposure, hydrolysis and microorganism activity.44) Flies’ mortality was similar in both λ-cyhalothrin formulations (1 and 2) when flies were exposed to leaves sprayed one week before (7 day) (Fig. 4). However, the activity of Cy-Corn formulation (2) was null two weeks after the treatment, whereas the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1) kept certain activity even three weeks days after the application. Significant differences in fly mortality can be observed between Cy-Corn treated leaves 2 and 3 weeks after spraying (Fig. 4). This indicates that the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1) remains active for at least two more weeks than the Cy-Corn (2). No significant differences were observed between the Cy-Corn and Corn control treatments from the second week after spraying treatments until the end of the trial. Results of bait sprays persistence obtained agree with studies conducted by Mangan et al. in which protein baits with insecticide remained effective less than 14 days.45) In our study persistence of Cy-Corn do not reach two weeks, which agrees with the directive of Spain Phytosanitary Service to repeat the treatments with baits plus insecticide every 10 days.

On the other hand, using the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation we are also reducing the repellent effect of the insecticide. Organophosphate and pyrethrins insecticides are known to be repellents to fruit flies46–48) and even a reduction of bait consumption has been reported in insecticide baits. However, in the case of MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation insecticide is protected, and only when flies secrete saliva onto dried bait and then suck up the liquefied sugars and proteins the insecticide is released. This represents a clear advantage for the insecticidal activity of the formulation since it increases the exposure to the toxicant.

On the other hand, no significant differences were observed in the attraction of MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation and the Corn control throughout the trial (Fig. 5). Thus, the phagostimulant effect of the bait necessary to cause fly mortality is maintained as long as the insecticide is active. Given that the Corn control bait was the same employed in Karate Zeon formulation (Cy-Corn) and taking into account the results depicted in Figs. 4 and 5, it can be concluded that the loss of efficacy of Cy-Corn treatments is not due to a reduction in the attraction of fruit flies to the bait, but because of a degradation of the insecticide in the Cy-Corn formulation. However, it can be observed that the insecticide activity is significant during at least 21 days in MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation. The difference in the insecticide activity, given that the attraction is similar with both formulations, is most likely due to the protection of λ-cyhalothrin by the nanodevice design.

Finally, field efficacy tests were carried out in a one-month trial out in a 5,100 m2 commercial clementine orchard in which the MSNs-Cy-Corn (1), Cy-Corn (2) formulations, and the Corn control (3) were tested. Results of field efficacy test show that fruit damage was significantly lower in the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation (1) and Cy-Corn (2) treated plots than in the control plot (Table 1). Although the average of damaged clementines was lower in the MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation treatment than in the Cy-Corn treatment, no significant differences were observed between treatments. However, it is important to highlight that only one treatment per month was needed with MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation to obtain similar results to those obtained with two standard treatments to control fruit flies.

Moreover, it is noteworthy that the quantity of λ-cyhalothrin used in each application of MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation treatment is 2.5 times lower than in Cy-Corn treatments (4 g λ-cyhalothrin/ha with MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation and 10 g λ-cyhalothrin/ha with Cy-Corn). As we need to duplicate the number of treatments with Karate Zeon as regards MSNs-Cy-Corn formulation, we are reducing 5 times the quantity of λ-cyhalothrin required. This not only means savings in treatment costs but also a reduction in the effect of this insecticide against non-target organisms and the environment. Should additionally be noted that the possibility of using this innovative eco-friendly nanodevice to control the population of C. capitata opens a wide range of new perspectives in the development of biocompatible delivery systems using mesoporous silica nanoparticles.

Conclusions

To sum up, an eco-friendly nanosystem has been developed for insecticide applications against C. capitata in the field. The nanodevice designed is based on the encapsulation of a pyrethrin, λ-cyhalothrin, inside the MSNs pores and capped with a specific protein (corn protein). This eco-friendly nanosystem is attractant and phagostimulant, which is a great advantage regard other insecticides and protein hydrolysates. The results suggest that the nanosystem remain active in field conditions for four weeks and the active ingredient is protected from light and degradation and result very specific for protein consuming species. Application of this eco-friendly nanosystem reduces fruit damage caused by C. capitata, protecting the fruit for a month with only one application.
