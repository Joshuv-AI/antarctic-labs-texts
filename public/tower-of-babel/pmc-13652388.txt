Pancreatic metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma: a rare clinical entity and therapeutic challenges
Authors: Maryem Ben Brahim, Ahmed Ben Mahmoud, Aida Khadhar, Amine Sebai, Youssef Chaker, Montasser Kacem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Pancreatic metastases from gastric adenocarcinoma are exceptionally rare and often present diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Their nonspecific clinical and radiological features make differentiation from primary pancreatic tumors particularly difficult, underscoring the need for a multidisciplinary approach.Case presentation:We report the case of a 54-year-old man who was initially treated in 2023 for gastric antral adenocarcinoma with independent cells. He underwent perioperative EOX chemotherapy followed by subtotal gastrectomy with Finsterer reconstruction and D2 lymphadenectomy (pT3N1). After 18 months, he developed a lesion of the pancreatobiliary junction with elevated CA 19-9 (192 U/mL). Biopsies were inconclusive, and a pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed in July 2025, preserving the gastrojejunostomy and creating a pancreaticojejunostomy on a Roux-en-Y loop. The postoperative course was complicated by a Grade B pancreatic fistula, bleeding requiring embolization of the gastroduodenal artery stump, and a superinfected hematoma requiring drainage, irrigation, and aspiration. On day 53, the patient was clinically stable with regression of the collections. Final pathology confirmed pancreatic and duodenal infiltration by independent-cell adenocarcinoma consistent with a gastric origin, with 8/12 positive lymph nodes and negative margins. Adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended after multidisciplinary discussion.Conclusion:This case illustrates the rarity of pancreatic metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma and highlights the importance of multidisciplinary discussion in the management of such uncommon presentations.

Introduction

Pancreatic metastases account for approximately 2% of all pancreatic malignancies, most frequently arising from renal, pulmonary, breast, or colorectal primaries. Metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma is exceedingly rare and has been described only in isolated case reports[1,2]. Distinguishing metastatic disease from primary pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma or local recurrence is essential but challenging in order to determine the appropriate treatment. This case emphasizes the importance of considering metastasis in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic lesions in patients with a history of gastric cancer. Case presentation

We present the case of a 54-year-old patient (ASA II, OMS 1) with a history of gastric adenocarcinoma diagnosed in 2023. He received perioperative EOX chemotherapy, followed by subtotal gastrectomy with Finsterer anastomosis and D2 lymphadenectomy. Pathologic examination revealed a T3 N1 gastric adenocarcinoma with independent cells. Eighteen months later, he presented with jaundice and pruritus, without abdominal pain or fever. Clinical examination did not reveal any other abnormalities. On physical examination, we did not find a palpable gallbladder or Troisier’s node. Laboratory tests showed abnormal liver function tests, with a total bilirubin of 263.6 μmol/L, conjugated bilirubin of 118.4 μmol/L, gamma-glutamyl transferaseof 128 U/L, alkaline phosphatase of 256 U/L, CA 19-9 of 193.2 U/mL, and carcinoembryonic antigen of 4.52 μg/L. We completed the workup with a computed tomography scan, which showed dilation of the intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts, with a common bile duct diameter of 18 mm, upstream of a 2 cm irregular stenosis of the distal common bile duct (Fig. 1). No secondary hepatic or peritoneal localizations were identified. The diagnosis of cholangiocarcinoma of the common bile duct was suspected. Given these findings, an magnetic resonance imaging was performed to further characterize the lesion, and it confirmed an enhancing stenosis of the distal bile duct measuring approximately 2 cm, without any suspicious hepatic lesions (Fig. 1). Endoscopic ultrasound was then performed; however, exploration of the pancreatobiliary region was technically challenging due to the patient’s previous Finsterer gastrojejunostomy. Despite these limitations, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) revealed an irregular thickening of the distal bile duct wall, highly suggestive of malignant stenosis. An EUS-guided biopsy was performed, but histological analysis was inconclusive, revealing a hyperplastic and adenomatous lesion with low-grade dysplasia of the ampulla of Vater. Given the clinical suspicion of a malignant tumor, we operated on the patient. At laparotomy, significant adhesions were found, making adhesiolysis challenging. No ascites, peritoneal carcinomatosis, or hepatic metastases were identified. Palpation revealed a 2 cm lesion of the pancreatobiliary junction without invasion of vascular structures. A pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed, preserving the pre-existing gastrojejunostomy. Reconstruction was achieved with a hepaticojejunostomy on the gastrojejunal limb and a pancreaticojejunostomy on a Roux-en-Y loop, with an internalized pancreatic drain. The postoperative course was marked by a grade B pancreatic fistula. Bleeding from the gastroduodenal artery stump (400 mL) occurred without hemodynamic instability, requiring embolization. In this case, arteriography did not reveal active bleeding or contrast blush; however, prophylactic embolization of the gastroduodenal artery stump was performed, as this site is the most common origin of post-pancreaticoduodenectomy hemorrhage. The patient also developed a superinfection of a postoperative hematoma, with positive bacteriological cultures for multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter, requiring appropriate antibiotic therapy. Initially, we used colistin, which was complicated by acute renal failure; therefore, we switched to a fourth-generation cephalosporin. Two percutaneous drainages were required to evacuate the hematoma. An irrigation–aspiration system was placed, initially yielding 200 mL of debris, which gradually cleared over the course of 1 month. Acute renal failure improved without the need for hemodialysis. At postoperative day 53, the patient was stable, with regression of intra-abdominal collections, normal renal function, and no major infectious signs. Final histologic examination confirmed pancreatic and duodenal infiltration by independent-cell adenocarcinoma consistent with gastric origin, with 8 positive lymph nodes out of 12 and negative margins (Figs. 2-3). Adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended after multidisciplinary discussion. At follow-up, no recurrence was identified after 6 months. Discussion

Pancreatic metastases are rare, accounting for approximately 2% of malignant pancreatic tumors, most often from renal cell carcinoma, lung, breast, or colon cancers[4]. Metastases from gastric adenocarcinoma are exceptional, described mainly in isolated case reports or very small series[1,2]. In our case, the lesion appeared 18 months after gastrectomy and was located at the pancreatobiliary junction. As differential diagnoses, a primary carcinoma of the pancreas and a metastasis of the gastric carcinoma were considered. Distinguishing metastasis of the gastric carcinoma from a primary pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma was difficult. Imaging alone cannot distinguish between them. Preoperative differentiation between primary pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and metastatic disease remains challenging based on imaging alone. EUS with fine-needle aspiration is considered the most accurate preoperative diagnostic tool; however, in our case, it was technically limited and non-diagnostic, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty[4]. From a histopathological perspective, poorly cohesive gastric adenocarcinoma (including independent-cell or signet-ring variants) is known for its aggressive course and atypical metastatic tropism, making pancreatic metastasis plausible. Immunohistochemical profiling can be useful to confirm the gastric origin of such lesions[5]. Management of a suspicious pancreatic lesion of uncertain origin remains particularly challenging and should be discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board[6]. The main therapeutic strategies in this case are either systemic chemotherapy, when surgical resection is not feasible, or surgical treatment, when the lesion is isolated and technically resectable[7–9]. The final decision depends on the patient’s overall condition, absence of extra-pancreatic disease, and the possibility of achieving a complete (R0) resection[10]. This multidisciplinary approach allows for a tailored strategy, balancing potential oncological benefit against operative risks. Management of such lesions requires multidisciplinary evaluation. Therapeutic options include systemic chemotherapy or surgical resection in selected patients with isolated and resectable disease[6–9]. Complete (R0) resection remains a key prognostic factor[10]. In our case, surgical resection was selected due to the absence of radiological extra-pancreatic disease and the resectable nature of the lesion. However, the presence of 8 positive lymph nodes among 12 raises an important issue regarding tumor biology and challenges the concept of truly “isolated” oligometastatic disease. This finding suggests a more systemic disease pattern rather than a strictly localized metastasis. If such nodal involvement had been known preoperatively, a systemic chemotherapy-first approach would have been strongly considered. Adjuvant chemotherapy was indicated after multidisciplinary discussion. Although no specific guidelines exist for pancreatic metastasis from gastric cancer, evidence from pancreatic and gastric cancers supports its use to improve survival outcomes in high-risk disease[11,12].

Conclusion

This case highlights the exceptional occurrence of pancreatic metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma. In patients with a history of gastric cancer presenting with a pancreatic mass, metastasis should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Therapeutic decisions should be individualized, based on resectability and general condition, and discussed within a multidisciplinary tumor board. The management of these situations remains non-consensual and depends on the experience of high-volume surgical centers.
