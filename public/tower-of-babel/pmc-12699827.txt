Natural variations in the OsCPK9 promoter confer chilling tolerance in rice by stabilizing OsSAPK8 through phosphorylation
Authors: Mingwang Liu, Yanping You, Kang Chong, Dongfeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe response and integration of cold signals are key processes when plants cope with cold stress. It remains a challenging problem to study the function of calcium (Ca2+) sensor in mediating the Ca2+ signaling during the early stage of cold signal transduction.ResultsHere, we focused on the Ca2+ sensor gene OsCPK9 to explore its function in chilling tolerance. Our results showed that OsCPK9 exhibits a significant cold-induced expression pattern, and contributes to chilling tolerance at seedling stages. The haplotype and population genetics analysis indicated that the promoter region of OsCPK9 harbors favorable allelic variations that were strongly selected during the domestication process of japonica rice. Furthermore, OsCPK9 interact with five members of the OsSAPK family whose C-terminal domain is the determining factor. Background

In the natural environment, low temperature is one of the primary environmental factors limiting plant growth and agricultural productivity. To cope with cold stress, plants have evolved sophisticated cold sensing and transducing mechanisms to activate effective responses [1]. Ca2+ is a ubiquitous second messenger in plants involved in multiple stresses, cold stress triggers the transient elevation of Ca2+ in the cytosol ([Ca2+]cyt), designated as Ca2+ signatures which exhibit stimulus-specific parameters in time, location, amplitude and frequency [2]. However, decoding the Ca2+ signal is challenging. The key question is how plants convert Ca2+ signals into downstream responses through different Ca2+ binding proteins during the early stage of cold signal transduction under cold stress.

Calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs/CPKs), as Ca2+ binding proteins with EF-hand domain, are one of the Ca2+ sensors that phosphorylate various substrates at serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) residues [2]. Generally, CPKs contain four characteristic domains, a highly variable N-terminal domain, a kinase catalytic domain, an autoinhibitory domain, and a C-terminal calmodulin-like domain with four EF hands [3, 4]. In plants, CPKs sense changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentration and translate them into phosphorylation events that initiate downstream signaling processes in stress tolerance [5]. Notably, a total of 31 OsCPK genes have been identified by genome-wide analysis in rice, and some OsCPK genes were found to be induced under stress conditions [6, 7]. To date, four genes, OsCPK7, OsCPK13, OsCPK17, and OsCPK24, have been implicated in cold stress responses in rice [8–12]. Among these genes, only substrates of OsCPK17 and OsCPK24 have been identified through phosphorylation modification analyses. Specifically, OsCPK24 phosphorylates and reduces the thioltransferase activity of OsGrx10; additionally, the putative target phosphopeptides and corresponding proteins of OsCPK17 have been identified by phosphor-proteomic analysis [8, 9]. Therefore, the phosphorylation modification of key proteins conducted by OsCPKs needed to be investigated during the early activation stage of Ca2+ signals under cold stress.

The sucrose nonfermenting1 (SNF1)-related protein kinases (SnRKs) are core serine/threonine kinases and essential for regulating growth, development, and stress responses in plants [3]. A total of ten SnRK2 genes, designated as stress-activated protein kinases (SAPKs), have been identified in rice [13–15]. Recent research has revealed that the OsSAPK family plays a crucial role in rice chilling stress. For example, as a homolog of OST1 in Arabidopsis thaliana, OsSAPK8 phosphorylates and activates the cyclic nucleotide-gated channel OsCNGC9 to trigger Ca2+ influx and positively regulates chilling tolerance [16]. In addition, OsSAPK6- and OsSAPK9-dependent phosphorylation enhances IPA1 and OsERF52 stability, respectively, which in turn leads to an up-regulation of the expression of OsDREBs/OsCBFs [17, 18]. However, the post-translational modifications, especially phosphorylation, of OsSAPKs mentioned in the above reports are still unclear under cold stress conditions. Considering the essential roles of phosphorylation modifications in OsSAPKs, it will be valuable to further investigate the mechanisms that fine-tune OsSAPKs stability and activity.

In this study, our goal is to identify and analyze the functions of OsCPKs that are involved in the early stage of cold signal transduction. We report that OsCPK9 functions in chilling tolerance at the seedling stage. The phylogenetic analysis, along with population genetic studies, demonstrated that OsCPK9 was retained by selection during japonica domestication, and further confirmed that OsCPK9 contained the excellent allelic variations in the promoter region. Based on the data of immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS), we identified and validated that OsCPK9 phosphorylates and stabilizes OsSAPK8 in a Ca2+-dependent manner to regulate the OsDREBs/OsCBFs pathway. Our findings provide novel insights into how OsCPK9 responds and regulates chilling stress by phosphorylating OsSAPK8. Additionally, we identify superior allelic variations with significant potential for molecular design breeding in rice.

Results

The expression profile of OsCPKs in response to chilling stress

CPKs function as the Ca2+ sensors in the Ca2+ signaling pathway [1], it is worth exploring the role of OsCPKs in the chilling tolerance of rice. According to the previous reports [6, 7], 30 of the 31 protein sequences of OsCPKs were obtained directly from the latest version of the Rice Annotation Project Database [19], and an unrooted tree of OsCPKs were constructed by using PhyloSuite [20]. The phylogenetic tree showed that 30 OsCPKs can be divided into four groups (Fig. 1A). To explore responses of OsCPKs to chilling stress, their expression profile was checked in transcriptome data from 15-day-old seedlings of Nipponbare (NIP) exposed to 4℃ for 3 h [21]. The transcriptional analysis showed that, among OsCPKs, OsCPK9 and OsCPK21 were the two genes that respond most strongly to chilling treatment (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the expression of seven representative genes belonging to four groups has been examined, and the results of quantitative real-time PCR were consistent with the previous transcriptome results (Fig. 1C). Previous studies have shown that OsCPK21 can affect the development of pollen, and the mutation of OsCPK21 causes pollen abortion in OsCPK21-RNAi transgenic plants [22]. Therefore, OsCPK9 was selected to test its role in chilling tolerance.

OsCPK9 positively regulates the chilling tolerance at the seedling stage

To confirm the role of OsCPK9 in regulating chilling tolerance, knockout mutants (KO) and overexpression lines (OE) of OsCPK9 were generated in japonica cultivar Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) background. The causal mutations of OsCPK9 were identified through PCR-based sequencing, and these mutations caused premature translational termination, thereby disrupting protein function (Fig. 2A and B, and Figure S1). Under chilling treatment at 4°C for 75 h and a recovery period of 8 weeks, the KO lines showed significantly lower survival rates compared to the wild-type (WT) ZH11 at the seedling stage (Fig. 2C). Conversely, two overexpression transgenic plants, OE1 and OE2, were identified in the ZH11 background and showed that they were more tolerant to chilling compared with ZH11 under chilling treatment at 4°C for 80 h and a recovery period of 4 weeks (Fig. 2D and E). Moreover, the electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde (MDA) content of KO and OE plants of OsCPK9 and WT were tested before and after cold treatment, and the results showed that the KO plants had higher levels of electrolyte leakage and MDA content, while the OE plants had lower levels of electrolyte leakage and MDA content (Figs. 2F), which was consistent with the phenotypic results of survival rate after cold treatment. Taken together, these results suggested that OsCPK9 positively modulates chilling tolerance at the seedling stage in rice.

OsCPK9 was strongly selected during domestication

To investigate the underlying factors at the genome level that contribute to chilling tolerance, we downloaded OsCPK9 genome sequence data of 447 rice accessions, including 176 indica, 128 temperate japonica, 58 tropical japonica, 19 aus, 12 O. glaberrima, 10 O. barthii, and 44 O. rufipogon accessions, from the Rice Annotation Project Database and Rice Super Pan-genome Information Resource Database [19, 23]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed by using the OsCPK9 promoter and coding region sequence obtained from above-mentioned 393 accessions of cultivars and 54 accessions of wild rice (Fig. 3A). Phylogenetic analysis of OsCPK9 revealed that alleles from indica and japonica grouped distinct clades, suggesting the divergence of OsCPK9 between the two subspecies during domestication in rice (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, nucleotide diversity analysis indicated that temperate japonica (π = 0.00054, θ = 0.00131) and tropical japonica (π = 0.00084, θ = 0.00190) exhibited significantly lower diversity compared with indica (π = 0.00228, θ = 0.00177). Notably, neutrality tests demonstrated that Tajima’s D values of this gene in the japonica cultivar group were negative and remarkable in statistical significance (temperate japonica, − 1.82844, p < 0.05; tropical japonica, − 1.90401, p < 0.05) (Table 1). In addition, the similar results were obtained from the nucleotide diversity and neutral test of OsCPK9 in O. glaberrima and O. barthii (Table 1). These observations suggested that OsCPK9 in japonica may have evolved under strong selection with environment temperature.

The natural variation site 12 (Var12) in the promoter of OsCPK9 is correlated to chilling tolerance

In order to further explore the key variation sites of OsCPK9 leading to differential chilling tolerance in two subspecies of indica and japonica, we conducted the haplotype analysis based on the OsCPK9 promoter and coding region sequence, respectively. Among the 393 analyzed accessions, haplotype analysis revealed 52 haplotypes based on 83 variation sites in the promoter region and 12 haplotypes based on 10 variation sites in the coding region, indicating the nucleotide diversity of the promoter region is higher than that of the coding region (Table S1 and S2). Considering that haplotypes with fewer accessions are not representative, these haplotypes containing less than 10 accessions (about 2.5% of the total) will be omitted from further analysis. For the 6 haplotypes (Hap1c, Hap2c, Hap7c, Hap8c, Hap9c, and Hap12c) of the coding region, no significant variation sites differentiating between indica and japonica were identified in the coding region (Figure S3 and Table S1). However, there were 9 haplotypes classified into two groups: the japonica group (Hap1, Hap2, Hap3, and Hap7) and the indica group (Hap31, Hap32, Hap33, Hap34, and Hap35) in the promoter region. Among these haplotypes, it is worth noting that 6 variation sites (Var12, Var22, Var28, Var30, Var38, and Var44) exhibited significant differences between indica and japonica haplotype groups in the promoter region (Fig. 3B and Table S2). Further predictive analysis of transcription factors binding sites revealed that among the 6 single nucleotide polymorphisms, only the variation at Var12 locus resulted in differences in transcription factor binding between indica and japonica. Specifically, at Var12, EIN3 type transcription factors were bound in japonica, while bZIP type transcription factors were bound in indica (Fig. 3B). Therefore, we speculated that Var12 is a key allelic variation site. Consequently, we classified the genotype of OsCPK9 into OsCPK9jap (with A at Var12) and OsCPK9ind (with G at Var12) (Fig. 3B and Table S2).

To elucidate the distribution of OsCPK9 genotypes between indica and japonica, the frequency of genotypes was calculated in 3609 accessions from RiceVarMap [24]. It was found that OsCPK9jap was present in 99.1% temperate japonica and 97.8% tropical japonica. In contrast, the frequency of OsCPK9jap was markedly lower in indica, ranging from 0.3% to 1.5% in three indica groups (Fig. 3C). Using the chilling tolerance phenotype of 132 rice accessions from published literature (Table S3) [25], and the genotypes of OsCPK9 in these accessions, the correlation between the genotypes and chilling tolerance was assessed, showing that 73 rice varieties with OsCPK9jap exhibited higher chilling tolerance compared with 59 varieties with OsCPK9ind across all three subspecies, including temperate japonica, tropical japonica, and indica (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that the genotypes of OsCPK9 may be associated with chilling tolerance.

Subsequently, the activities of the promoters of two genotypes were validated by using transient expression assay in N. benthamiana. At room temperature, the promoter activity of OsCPK9jap from NIP was significantly higher than that of the OsCPK9ind from 93- 11 (Fig. 3E and Figure S4). Furthermore, the difference in promoter activity between OsCPK9jap and OsCPK9ind was more obvious under chilling treatment conditions (Fig. 3F and Figure S4). The effects of the six variation sites in the promoter of OsCPK9jap genotype on promoter activity were also tested through the site-directed mutated promoter fragments method. Interestingly, under room temperature conditions, the fragments with OsCPK9ind-m1 (G changed to A at Var12) and OsCPK9ind-m6 (all six allelic variations changed from indica to japonica) mutations exhibited significantly higher relative activities than the OsCPK9ind genotype promoter (Fig. 3E and Figure S4). Similar to the promoter activities of OsCPK9jap and OsCPK9ind, the differences in promoter activity between OsCPK9ind-m1 and OsCPK9ind, as well as between OsCPK9ind-m6 and OsCPK9ind, were also more significant under chilling treatment conditions, showing increases of 58% and 76%, respectively (Fig. 3F and Figure S4). Furthermore, OsCPK9 expression was significantly upregulated in ZH11 (japonica) but weakly changed in Guangluai 4 (GLA4) (indica) under chilling treatment (Fig. 3G and Figure S4). These results indicated that Var12 was the main functional variation site responsible for the chilling induced differential expression between OsCPK9jap and OsCPK9ind.

OsCPK9 interacts with OsSAPKs whose C-terminal domain is the determining factor

OsCPK9 is a chilling tolerance gene with excellent allelic variation that positively regulates cold stress at the rice seedling stage. To uncover how it exerts this effect, we explored the subcellular localization and protein function of OsCPK9. According to UniPort annotation (https://www.uniprot.org/), it was inferred that the subcellular location of OsCPK9 is membrane-bound or membrane-associated, and this inference was confirmed by the assay of localization of OsCPK9-GFP fusion proteins in rice protoplasts (Figure S5).

To understand the function of OsCPK9, the IP-MS in OsCPK9 overexpression line with FLAG tag and WT ZH11 was performed and 64 candidate proteins were identified (Table S4). It is common knowledge that gene functional similarity can be used to classify a large number of genes based on biological functions, and the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) provides such a comprehensive set of functional annotation tools [26]. By using the gene functional classification tool with a low-level classification threshold, the 19 out of 64 candidate proteins were divided into 5 groups, and the fourth group includes three genes, namely OsCPK9, OsSAPK8, and OsSAPK10 (Table S5). This classification also suggested that OsCPK9 has similar functions to OsSAPK8 and OsSAPK10, which means there may be interactions between them. Moreover, since OsSAPKs serve as core components of the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway and this pathway plays a critical role in cold stress responses, we selected OsSAPK8 and OsSAPK10 as the candidate interacting proteins of OsCPK9 for further investigation (Table S4 and S5). In addition, the first three groups represent three regulation networks, including an ATP-dependent chaperone network, an RNA-surveillance and epigenetic apparatus, and a plasma-membrane pump system (Table S5). These three different networks have inherent connections in plants under environmental stress.

By the way, the other members of OsSAPK family were also tested for their interaction with OsCPK9 using the yeast two-hybrid assay. The results showed that OsCPK9 could interacts with five OsSAPKs, including OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, OsSAPK10, OsSAPK1, and OsSAPK2 (Fig. 4A). To explain the difference in interaction between OsCPK9 and different members of OsSAPK family, the domain and sequence alignment of OsSAPK proteins were analyzed. Sequence alignment analysis showed that the protein kinase domain is highly conserved among OsSAPK proteins, whereas the C-terminal domain exhibits low sequence similarity (Fig. 4B and Figure S6). Furthermore, based on OsSAPK protein evolutionary tree analysis, it was found that the five proteins interacting with OsCPK9 are more closely related and clustered together (Fig. 4A). Therefore, we speculated that the C-terminal domains of OsSAPKs determine their interactions with OsCPK9. To support this hypothesis, the interaction between C-terminal of OsSAPKs (OsSAPK-C) and OsCPK9 was tested, and the results showed that the interaction between OsCPK9 and OsSAPK-C was consistent with the interaction between OsCPK9 and the full-length of OsSAPKs (Fig. 4A). In summary, the above results indicated that the C-terminal domain is the determining factor for interaction of OsCPK9 and OsSAPKs.

According to the proven functionality of OsSAPK8 in regulating rice chilling tolerance [16], the interaction between OsSAPK8 and OsCPK9 was further validated by luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in Nicotiana benthamiana. When OsCPK9-nLUC and cLUC-OsSAPK8 were coexpressed in N. benthamiana leaves, the strong luminescence was observed (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, Co-IP assays were also performed through coexpression of OsCPK9-HA and MYC-OsSAPK8 in N. benthamiana. When OsCPK9-HA was immunoprecipitated from total protein extracts of the transfected leaves using anti-HA affinity beads, MYC-OsSAPK8 was detected in the immunocomplex by immunoblotting (Fig. 4D), which suggested that OsCPK9 interacted directly with OsSAPK8. In addition, in terms of spatial location, there is a possibility of interaction between OsCPK9 and OsSAPK8 located in the cytoplasm [27]. Take together, these results showed that OsCPK9 can interact with five members of the OsSAPK family, and the C-terminal domains of these OsSAPKs play important roles in their interactions with OsCPK9.

OsCPK9 phosphorylates OsSAPK8 in a Ca2+-dependent manner

As a typical Ca2+-dependent protein kinase, the putative OsCPK9 encodes 574 amino acids with a calculated molecular mass of approximately 64 kDa. To investigate the autophosphorylation activity with Ca2+, the purified His-OsCPK9 protein was subjected to phosphorylation assay in vitro. It was found that OsCPK9 had autophosphorylation activity, and the autophosphorylation sites was identified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Figure S8). Furthermore, the autophosphorylation activity was stimulated by low concentrations of Ca2+, but suppressed by high concentrations of Ca2+ (Fig. 5A), indicating that autophosphorylation activity of OsCPK9 was modulated by different Ca2+ concentrations.

To determine whether OsSAPK8 is a substrate of OsCPK9, the recombinant proteins His-OsCPK9 and His-OsSAPK8 were expressed and purified from E. coli for detecting the phosphorylation of His-OsSAPK8. The western blotting results showed that the abundance of phosphorylated His-OsSAPK8 (p-His-OsSAPK8) was increased when incubated with more His-OsCPK9 (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the result of LC-MS/MS showed that phospho-Ser178 and phospho-Ser187, in the protein kinase domain of OsSAPK8, were identified after in vitro phosphorylation (Fig. 5C and E, and Figure S9A). Meanwhile, the autophosphorylation sites, including Ser41, Ser83, Ser176, and Ser251, were mapped by LC - MS/MS analysis during OsSAPK8 self-incubation (Figure S9B).

Subsequently, the both His-tagged OsSAPK8S178A and OsSAPK8S187A mutant fusion proteins were purified for in vitro kinase assays. Compared with OsSAPK8, the OsSAPK8S178A and OsSAPK8S187A exhibited reduced phosphorylation level, suggesting that Ser178 and Ser187 in OsSAPK8 are the major residues phosphorylated by OsCPK9 (Fig. 5F). To further explore the effect of Ca2+ concentration on phosphorylation activity, we conducted phosphorylation assays with different Ca2+ concentrations to measure the phosphorylation level of OsSAPK8. The results showed that the abundance of phosphorylation of OsSAPK8 slightly increased as the Ca2+ concentration rose from 0.01 µM to 0.1 µM, reached a maximum under 0.1 µM Ca2+ conditions, and then decreased as the Ca2+ concentration increased further (Fig. 5G).

In addition, the kinase assays showed that OsSAPK9 was phosphorylated by OsCPK9, and the phosphorylation site was identified at Thr147, implying the phosphorylation modification sites of OsSAPK proteins by OsCPK9 may be non-conserved (Figure S11). Collectively, these results indicate that OsCPK9 is a Ca2+ dependent protein kinase, and capable of phosphorylating OsSAPK8 at Ser178 and Ser187, and OsSAPK9 at Thr147. These phosphorylation events are modulated by varying Ca2+ concentrations.

OsCPK9 stabilizes OsSAPK8 under chilling stress

Considering that OsCPK9 interacts with and phosphorylates OsSAPK8, we speculated that OsCPK9 may be involved in regulating the stabilization of OsSAPK8. To verify this possibility, we employed cell-free degradation assays to detect the degradation of MBP-OsSAPK8 incubated with total protein extracts from WT ZH11 or OsCPK9 KO1 mutant seedlings. Significantly, the results showed that when incubated with the total protein extract from WT seedlings, OsSAPK8 was more stable than when it was incubated with the OsCPK9 mutant extract (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, when MBP-OsSAPK8(S178A-S187A) mutated on both sites S178 and S187 was used to conduct the same assays, the experimental results showed the MBP-OsSAPK8(S178A-S187A) was degraded similarly when incubated with total protein extract from ZH11 and OsCPK9 KO1 seedlings, which indicates that S178 and S187 play an important role in inhibiting the degradation of OsSAPK8 or maintaining protein stability (Fig. 6A). However, when treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, OsSAPK8 degradation was greatly inhibited when incubated with the total protein extract from WT or OsCPK9 mutant seedlings under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, it was found that MYC-OsSAPK8 abundance was much higher when it was coexpressed with OsCPK9-HA than its expression alone in N. benthamiana leaves under chilling treatment (Fig. 6B). Additionally, no obvious change of the transcript levels of OsSAPK8 was observed between the WT ZH11 and OsCPK9 transgenic plants under chilling stress (Figure S13A). Altogether, these results indicate that OsCPK9 can stabilize or slow down the degradation of OsSAPK8 under chilling stress.

Previous reports have shown that OsSAPK6, OsSAPK8, and OsSAPK9, which are involved in the important pathway of chilling tolerance, indirectly regulate OsDREBs/OsCBFs in rice [16–18]. Specifically, OsSAPK6, OsSAPK9 and OsSAPK8 affect the expression of OsDREBs/OsCBFs through IPA1, OsERF52, and Ca2+ signaling, respectively. Therefore, the expression of OsDREBs and IPA1 were examined in WT and OsCPK9 mutant seedlings under chilling stress. Compared with the WT seedlings, the mutant line KO1 exhibited significantly reduced expression of OsDREB1A, OsDREB1B, and OsDREB1C (Figure S13B). Meanwhile, the expression of IPA1 was significantly lower in the KO1 mutant than in the WT ZH11 seedlings (Figure S13C). These results indicated that OsCPK9 is able to indirectly regulate the expression of OsDREBs/OsCBFs under chilling stress in rice.

Discussion

The OsCPK9-OsSAPK8 module is involved in sensing and activating Ca2+ signaling pathways under chilling stress

In this study, our findings showed that OsCPK9 interacts with five OsSAPKs (OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, OsSAPK10, OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2) using the yeast two-hybrid system, and further demonstrated that the region determining the interaction is located at the C-terminus of OsSAPKs, while protein sequence similarity analysis revealed sequence polymorphism at the C-terminus of OsSAPKs. Subsequently, we chose OsSAPK8 as a representative to analyze the interaction function, and the results showed that OsCPK9 can stabilizing OsSAPK8 through phosphorylation. However, it was found that the phosphorylation sites of OsSAPK8 and OsSAPK9 that were identified by mass spectrometry as being phosphorylated by OsCPK9 were different, suggesting that there may be different regulatory mechanisms. Although similar functionality was not validated for the other four OsSAPK members in this study, we can speculate that the mechanism of interaction is universal, while the regulatory mechanism may have specificity for the other four proteins.

It is widely recognized that cold stress triggers a rapid elevation in cytosolic Ca2+. Here, we demonstrate that chilling simultaneously induces the transcriptional up-regulation of OsCPK9, resulting in a progressive accumulation of the OsCPK9 protein. As a Ca2+-dependent protein kinase, OsCPK9 is ideally positioned to sense the rising Ca2+ signature. Consistent with in vitro assays, the combined increase in both Ca2+ concentration and OsCPK9 abundance leads to a pronounced enhancement of its kinase activity during the early stage of chilling stress. This activated OsCPK9 directly facilitates the phosphorylation on OsSAPK8 to slow down its degradation (Fig. 6A and B).

According to previous reports, OsSAPK8 phosphorylates and activates OsCNGC9 and positively regulates chilling tolerance, thereby mediating cold-induced Ca2+ influx into the cytosol and then activating the indirect downstream responses of OsDREBs [16]. Combining these findings, we can infer that there is a cold signal transduction pathway formed by Ca2+, OsCPK9, OsSAPK8, and OsCNGC9 in the early stages of chilling stress.

For different rice subspecies, natural variations within the OsCPK9 promoter region result in differences in the transcriptional expression patterns of OsCPK9 under chilling stress among different genotypes. When japonica rice varieties carrying OsCPK9jap face cold stress, the presence of six excellent allelic variations, including Var12, leads to a significant increase in OsCPK9 expression with increasing chilling stress time. In contrast, in indica rice varieties possessing the OsCPK9ind genotype, there is no major change in the expression of OsCPK9 under the same chilling stress conditions (Fig. 6C). Therefore, one of the reasons for the higher cold tolerance of japonica rice varieties compared to indica rice subspecies is due to the strong response of OsCPK9jap to cold stress. This allele enables rapid transcriptional induction and subsequent protein accumulation under low temperature. Once cytosolic Ca2+ reaches the threshold required for Ca2+-dependent activation, the accumulated OsCPK9 phosphorylates and stabilizes OsSAPK8, thereby activating the OsCNGC9 involved in Ca2+ signal pathway in the early stage of cold signal transduction.

The OsCPK9-OsSAPK8 module plays a crucial integration role during multiple stress responses

In the previous report, the transcription level of OsCPK9 was induced by abscisic acid (ABA), PEG6000, and NaCl treatments, and further revealed that OsCPK9 plays a positive role in drought stress tolerance and spikelet fertility [28]. More importantly, the OsCPK9 overexpressing lines exhibited an ABA-sensitive phenotype compared to the WT [28]. From the perspective of molecular mechanism, it was found that OsCPK9 affects the transcript levels of ABA- and stress-responsive genes under ABA treatment, including Rab21, Osbzip66, OsNAC45, OsRSUS, Osbzip23, OsLEA3, OsP5CS, OsNAC9, and Osbzip72 [28]. Indeed, OsSAPK8 acts as a positive regulator in abiotic stress responses including cold, drought, and salt stress, and also serves as an essential activator of S-type anion channel OsSLAC1 [29, 30]. In brief, these results showed that both OsCPK9 and OsSAPK8 are involved in various stress regulation responses. Interestingly, Li et al. has uncovered that CPK3/4/6/11/27 phosphorylate the SnRK2s on multiple phosphorylation sites to activate SnRK2s in controlling osmotic stress responses, which links Ca2+ signaling with the function of SnRK2s under osmotic stress in Arabidopsis thaliana [31]. Our results showed that there is a similar regulation mechanism in cold signal transduction in rice. Overall, it can be reasonably inferred that the OsCPK9-OsSAPK regulatory module may represent an important node in the integration of Ca2+ signaling pathway and ABA pathway during multiple stress responses.

The elite allelic variation of OsCPK9 is an excellent candidate site for precise editing to enhance rice’s cold tolerance

Based on the results of sequence alignment analysis, a few variation sites that lead to changes in the amino acids of the OsCPK9 protein’s sequence were processed in the rare accessions (Table S1). For instance, in Hap11c, the 1 bp deletion at encoding region 185 introduces a premature stop codon within OsCPK9, whereas in Hap4c, independent 1 bp deletions at positions 207 and 222 shift the reading frame, producing a protein whose downstream sequence is entirely discordant with the normal OsCPK9 translation product. Unsurprisingly, these sites would cause changes of OsCPK9 function in these accessions (Table S1 and Figure S3). These findings suggested potential functional differences that will be explored among different accessions in the future. Overall, the sequence conservation of the coding region is higher than that of the promoter region in OsCPK9 genome sequence.

Through population genetics analysis and experimental verification, we identified that Var12 is a crucial variant, which leads to changes of upstream regulatory transcription factors between OsCPK9jap and OsCPK9ind (Fig. 3C). Further analysis indicated that the divergence of the OsCPK9 genotypes according to the Var12 is associated with chilling tolerance (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the G to A change of Var12 in the promoter region can increase the expression of OsCPK9 (Fig. 3E and G). Notably, the importance of Var12 is closely related to the upstream transcriptional regulatory factor EIN3. According to PlantPAN prediction, the EIN3 transcription factors that can bind to the Var12 locus include Os08g0508700, Os07g0685700, Os03g0324300, and Os03g0324200. Among these four genes, the first three genes have obvious expression patterns under cold stress, indicating that they may be involved in the regulation of rice chilling tolerance. This provides clues for us to further analyze the function of Var12 excellent allele variations. In addition, in terms of enhancing promoter activity, five other variation sites in the promoter region also have a certain effect, but this effect is weaker than that of Var12 (Fig. 3E and F). Based on predictive analysis of transcription factor binding sites, the five other sites are not within any transcription factor binding motifs. Obviously, this approach of only considering transcription factors to regulate gene expression is not comprehensive, as there are many factors that affect gene expression, including but not limited to methylation, enhancers, suppressors, etc.

Significantly, the overexpression plants of OsCPK9 demonstrated excellent agronomic traits under drought stress, as well as showed improved pollen maturation and spikelet fertility under normal conditions [28]. Gene editing technology has great potential in future agricultural applications to address various stress and is highly desirable in crop improvement [32, 33]. Base editing can introduce the substitution of a single base to the target loci in the absence of double-strand breaks for targeted genome modification [34]. Therefore, in the future, we can edit the base from G to A in the promoter of OsCPK9 using a precise base-editing system in indica varieties possessing OsCPK9ind, thereby increasing the expression of OsCPK9 and enhancing chilling stress tolerance. Meanwhile, the OsCPK9 gene-editing lines provide an ideal platform to elucidate the mechanism of the upstream regulatory factor EIN3 involved in rice cold tolerance regulation.

Conclusions

The current study displays that OsCPK9, as a Ca2+ sensor, confers chilling tolerance at seedling stages in rice. Furthermore, we reveal that OsCPK9 phosphorylates and stabilizes OsSAPK8 by a Ca2+-dependent manner, thereby regulating the OsSAPK8 involved in downstream responses under chilling stress. Our findings offer new perspectives on the fact that the cold signal transduction pathway of the OsCPK9-OsSAPK8 module amplifies the cold signal from ambient temperature through protein phosphorylation modification, facilitating the activation of stronger downstream responses. Additionally, the analysis of evolutionary genetics supports that OsCPK9 is strongly selected during the domestication process of japonica rice, and this gene harbors a superior allelic variation that shows potential for the genetic improvement of cold tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this study, ZH11 (Oryza sativa L. subsp. japonica, Zhonghua 11), NIP (Oryza sativa L. subsp. japonica, Nipponbare), GLA4 (Oryza sativa L. subsp. indica, Guangluai 4), and 93-11 (Oryza sativa L. subsp. indica, 93-11), all varieties of rice seeds, were provided by China National Rice Research Institute (Hangzhou, China) and preserved in our laboratory after propagation. The mutants of OsCPK9 were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system, and the target site sequences were separately cloned into a CRISPR/Cas9 vector linearized with BsaI (Biogle Cat# BGK03), following the manufacturer’s instruction. To generate OsCPK9 overexpression lines, the full coding sequences of OsCPK9 was amplified from the cDNA of NIP, then cloned into pTFLGHAU linearized with BamHI and KpnI to form UBI::FLAG-OsCPK9 binary vector. The above two constructs were transferred into WT ZH11 through Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105-mediated transformation. The primers used were shown in Table S6.

Evaluation of cold tolerance

For the chilling treatment at the seedling stage, the treatments were performed as described in the previous reports with slight modifications [35]. The rice seeds were soaked in water at 28°C ~ 30°C for three days, and then the germinated seeds were placed in 96-well plates customized for cultivating rice seedlings with Kimura B nutrient solution in incubators and grown in a glasshouse during the seedling stage at 28/25°C (day/night) under a light-dark cycle of 10/14 h, with a light intensity of 120 µmol m− 2·s− 1, and a relative humidity of 70%. The duration of chilling treatment depends on experimental purpose: 75 h for OsCPK9 mutant and 80 h for OsCPK9 overexpression lines. Rice seedlings at the three-leaf stage were subjected to a cold-water bath at 4℃ ± 0.5℃, while the ambient temperature was maintained at a constant 20℃ under a 10 h-light/14 h-dark photoperiod (80 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and 70% relative humidity. The survival rate (percentage of living seedlings out of all tested plants) was determined after about 4–8 weeks’ recovery period. Each treatment was performed independently at least three times, each replicate contained ≥ 24 seedlings with consistent growth. The values represented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between treatment groups were assessed by using Student’s t test.

Phylogenetic and selection analysis of OsCPK9

OsCPK9 promoter and coding region sequence dataset (containing 176 indica, 128 temperate japonica, 58 tropical japonica, 19 aus, 12 O. glaberrima, 10 O. barthii, and 44 O. rufipogon) were downloaded from the Rice Annotation Project Database (IRGSP-1.0) (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/) and Rice Super Pan-genome Information Resource Database (http://ricesuperpir.com/) [19, 23]. This dataset was used for constructing phylogenetic trees and analyzing nucleotide diversity. Nucleotide diversity and neutrality tests analysis of OsCPK9 was performed using DnaSP (Version 6.12.03) [36]. The phylogenetic trees of OsCPK9’s genome and protein sequences were constructed by using PhyloSuite (Version 1.2.3) with the default parameters [20]. The resequencing data used for analyzing OsCPK9 from 3609 accessions were obtained from the RiceVarMap V2.0 Website (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) [24].

Gene functional classification

The detailed analysis process using the gene functional classification tool was performed as previously described [26]. The accessions of all identified interacting proteins were filled into the Upload Gene List box, and then select the identifier type and submit for analysis with the default settings.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from whole three leaf stage seedlings using a plant RNA kit (ZOMANBIO, Cat# ZP405K) following the manufacturer’s instruction. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (YEASEN, Cat# 10911). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, Cat# 11202ES08) following the instruction. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR® Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (Cat# QPK-201; Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) following the instructions in a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized by Ubiquitin as the internal reference gene. Analyses were performed using three biological replicates. The primers used were shown in Table S6.

Subcellular localization of OsCPK9

To determine the subcellular localization of OsCPK9, its full-length coding sequence was amplified and inserted into the XbaI/KpnI sites of pBI221-GFP using the pEASY-Uni Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit (TransGen, Cat# CU101). Pprotoplasts isolated from 10-day-old etiolated rice leaf sheaths were transfected via PEG-mediated transformation. The plasma-membrane marker PIP2–mCherry was co-transfected to validate membrane localization. After 16 h incubation at room temperature, GFP and mCherry fluorescence were imaged with a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope. All primers are listed in Table S6.

Protein expression in E. coli and purification

For the proteins used in the phosphorylation assay, OsCPK9, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK8(S178A), OsSAPK8(S187A) were cloned into the plasmid pET-28a as His fusion protein. Then they were expressed in E. coli strain BL21(DE3) with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) induced for 16 h at 16℃. The cells were collected at 5000 g for 10 min at 4℃. And then the cells were lysed and sonicated with the lysis buffer (25 mM Tri-HCl, 250 mM KCl, 25 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). His-tagged recombinant proteins were purified by Ni-NTA Agarose (QIAGEN, 30210) and eluted by the elution buffer (25 mM Tri-HCl, 250 mM KCl, 300 mM imidazole, pH 8.0).

For the proteins used in cell-free assay, OsSAPK8 and OsSAPK8(S178A-S187A) were cloned into the plasmid pMAL-c4x as fusion with maltose-binding protein (MBP). Then they were expressed in E. coli strain BL21(DE3) with 0.5 mM IPTG induced for 16 h at 16℃. The cells were collected at 5000 g for 10 min at 4℃. And then the cells were lysed and sonicated with the lysis buffer (50 mM Tri-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, pH 8.0). MBP-tagged recombinant proteins were purified by Amylose Resin (New England Biolabs, E8021) and eluted by 20 mM maltose in lysis buffer.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analysis was performed using the Matchmaker Gold Y2H System (Cat# 630489; Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA) as directed by the manufacturer. In brief, the full-length coding sequence of OsCPK9 was amplified from NIP and cloned into pGADT7 vector, and the full-length as well as C-terminal coding sequence of OsSAPK8, amplified from NIP, were cloned into pGBKT7 vector, respectively. The plasmid combination of pGADT7-OsCPK9 and pGBKT7 DNA-BD were used as negative control, respectively. The nucleotide sequences of all primers used in the present study are given in Table S6.

LCI assay

The LCI analysis was performed as previously described [37]. The coding sequence of OsCPK9 and OsSAPK8 were fused with the N or C terminus of luciferase, respectively. The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium and co-infiltrated into 3-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. Signals were observed through the low-light cooled charge-coupled device imaging apparatus (Tanon 5200) after 3-day of cultivation.

Coimmunoprecipitation assays

The Co-IP assays were performed as described previously with some modifications [25]. OsCPK9-HA and MYC-OsSAPK8 or MYC were co-transformed in the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. The Agrobacterium strains were cultured in YEB medium at 30℃ overnight. Subsequently, the Agrobacterium containing either MYC-OsSAPK8 or MYC was mixed with the Agrobacterium containing OsCPK9-HA. The combined bacterial cultures were harvested, resuspended in an infiltration buffer (containing 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES pH 5.6, and 150 µM acetosyringone), and then incubated for 3 h at 30℃. After incubation, the suspensions were infiltrated into the leaves of 3-week-old N. benthamiana plants. After three days of cultivation under weak light, Total proteins of N. benthamiana leaves were extracted with IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5%(v/v) NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1×Cocktail). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 30 min after vortex mixing and 50 µL supernatants were transferred into new 1.5 mL EP tubes, which were used as input samples. The rest of the supernatants were incubated with HA magnetic beads at 4℃ overnight. Then supernatants were removed, and beads were washed three times with IP buffer. Subsequently, the samples were separated using SDS-PAGE, and probed with anti-HA antibody (AE008, ABclonal Biotechnology) and anti-MYC antibody (M192-3, MBL International). Incubate the membrane with 1:5000 primary antibody diluted with 5% skimmed milk overnight at 4℃ and 1:5000 secondary antibodies diluted with 2% skimmed milk for 2 h at room temperature.

Mass spectrometry assays

For the identification of interacting proteins of OsSAPK9, total proteins were extracted with IP buffer from proUbi::Flag-OsCPK9-HA plants, following a 3 h treatment at 4℃, which were incubated with Flag magnetic beads at 4°C for 4 h. After 6 washes with IP buffer, the samples were stored in IP buffer. Subsequently, the proteins were digested with trypsin and analyzed using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for MS analysis. Then the peptide sequence was aligned against the Uniprot database. Analyses were performed using Proteome Discoverer™ (Version 2.4). The results are shown in Table S4.

For the analysis of phosphorylation sites of OsSAPK8 in vitro, 0.5 µg His-OsCPK9 proteins were incubated with 5 µg His-OsSAPK8 in the presence of 1 mM ATP in 50 µL of kinase reaction buffer [25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM b-glycerophosphate, 2 mM DTT, 0.1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM MgCl2] at 30°C for 30 min. Next, the reaction mixtures digested with trypsin and subjected to a Orbitrap Fusion Lumos high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for MS analysis. The method for analysis of phosphorylation sites of OsSAPK9 by OsCPK9 is the same as above.

Luciferase activity analysis system in N. benthamiana leaves

To test the impact of chilling treatment on the expression of OsCPK9jap and OsCPK9ind, luciferase (LUC) activity analysis system was constructed. The promoter regions (2000 bp upstream of ATG) of OsCPK9jap, OsCPK9ind, OsCPK9ind-m1, and OsCPK9ind-m6 were amplified or generated by site-directed mutagenesis and cloned into the modified pUN1301 vector with LUC as the reporter gene. In this vector, b-glucuronidase (GUS), driven by CaMV35S promoter, was employed as internal reference. The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium and co-infiltrated into 3-week-old N. benthamiana leaves respectively. After three days of cultivation under weak light, the leaves of N. benthamiana were exposed to 4°C for 3 h and collected immediately. the LUC and GUS activity in N. benthamiana leaves were measured by Promega GloMax. The LUC activity was normalized with GUS activity in each assay, respectively. All primers are listed in Table S6.

In vitro phosphorylation analysis

The in vitro phosphorylation assays were performed according to previously described methods with minor modifications [38]. 0.5 µg His-OsCPK9 proteins were incubated with 5 µg His-OsSAPK8 proteins in the presence of 1 mM ATP in 50 µL of kinase reaction buffer [25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM β-glycerophosphate, 2 mM DTT, 0.1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 µM CaCl2] at 30°C for 30 min, followed by adding 5×SDS loading buffer to stop the reaction. Proteins were boiled at 95°C for 5 min and then one half of protein products were separated by 8% SDS-PAGE for immunoblotting analysis using anti-His antibody was used as a loading control. The other half of proteins was separated by 8% SDS-PAGE for immunoblotting analysis using anti-Phospho-(Ser/Thr) antibody.

Cell-free protein degradation assay

Cell-free protein degradation assay was performed as described with some modifications [39]. Total proteins were extracted from 3-week-old seedlings of wild type ZH11 and OsCPK9 mutant KO1 with degradation buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP. Equal amounts of above total proteins were incubated with equal amounts of recombinant MBP-OsSAPK8 proteins at 25°C for the indicated time. MG132 (50 µM) was used to examine protein degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected with anti-MBP monoclonal antibody. Actin was used as loading controls.

Detection of OsSAPK8 stability in N. benthamiana leaves

The full-length coding sequences of OsCPK9 was inserted into pCsVMV-HA3 vector to generate pro35S::OsCPK9-HA. Simultaneously, the full-length coding sequences of OsSAPK8 was inserted into pCM1307-6MYC vector to generate pro35S::MYC-OsSAPK8. These constructs were then transferred into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. The bacterial cultures were grown in YEB medium at 30℃ overnight. Subsequently, the Agrobacterium containing either pro35S::OsCPK9-HA or pro35S::HA was combined with the Agrobacterium containing pro35S::MYC-OsSAPK8. The mixed bacterial cultures were harvested, resuspended in an infiltration buffer (containing 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES pH 5.6, and 150 µM acetosyringone), and incubated for 3 h at 30℃. After incubation, the suspensions were infiltrated into leaves of 3-week-old N. benthamiana plants. After three days of cultivation under weak light, total proteins were extracted from pro35S::OsCPK9-HA and pro35S::MYC-OsSAPK8, pro35S::HA and pro35S::MYC-OsSAPK8 in N. benthamiana plants after treated at 4°C for the indicated time, and protein was separated on 10% SDS-PAGE and detected with anti-MYC antibody. Actin was used as protein control.

Determination of MDA content

The MDA content determination method refers to the MDA content detection kit (BC6410, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Fresh leaf segments harvested from WT, mutant and over-expression lines before and after cold stress treatment were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a fine powder. A small amount of powder (recorded as W) was homogenised in 1 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was kept on ice until analysis. The reaction mixture was prepared on ice as follows: 400 µL reagent 1, 200 µL supernatant (sample tube) or 200 µL distilled water (blank), 200 µL reagent 2, 100 µL reagent 3 and 100 µL reagent 4. After vortexing, tubes were sealed and incubated in a boiling water-bath for 90 min, cooled in an ice bath, and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Absorbance of the supernatant was read at 532 nm and 600 nm against a water zero with a spectrophotometer pre-warmed for 30 min. ΔA was calculated as (A532sample – A532blank) – (A600sample – A600blank). MDA content was expressed as nmol g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW) using the formula: MDA = 32.258 × ΔA/W.

Electrolyte leakage assay

Electrolyte leakage was measured as described with some modifications [40]. Three seedlings were sampled after being subjected to a 3-day cold treatment at 4°C; untreated seedlings served as controls. Leaves were rinsed three times with distilled water, transferred to 15-mL centrifuge tubes containing 10 mL of distilled water, and the initial conductivity (S₀) was recorded with an electrical conductivity meter. Tubes were then shaken at 150 rpm overnight at room temperature, and the conductivity (S₁) was measured again. Subsequently, the samples were boiled for 1 h, cooled to room temperature, shaken for an additional 1 h at 150 rpm, and the final conductivity (S₂) was determined. Relative electrolyte leakage was calculated as: EL (%) = [(S₁ – S₀)/(S₂ – S₀)] × 100%.
