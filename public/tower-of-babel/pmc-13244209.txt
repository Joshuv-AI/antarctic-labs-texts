Treatment response trajectories for speech pathology intervention in chronic cough
Authors: Yuto Hamada, Jordan Jeffery, Peter G. Gibson, Anne E. Vertigan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpeech pathology intervention is an effective treatment for refractory and unexplained chronic cough (RUCC); however, data regarding treatment outcomes in real-world settings are limited. Similarly, the influence of cough phenotypes on treatment response variability is unknown. This study aimed to 1) identify trajectories of response to speech pathology intervention and 2) determine predictors of treatment response trajectories.MethodsThe medical records of 205 participants referred to speech pathology for assessment and management of RUCC were reviewed. Data included demographics, medical comorbidities, appointment dates, Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) scores and clinical outcome. Participants with three or more LCQ scores were included in the analysis. A growth mixture model was used to estimate participant treatment response trajectories and logistic regression models identified baseline predictors of trajectory membership.ResultsThree treatment response trajectory groups were identified: Severe Cough–Slow Response (21%), Severe Cough–Rapid Response (23%) and Moderate Cough–Slow Response (56%). Asthma diagnosis (p=0.034) and baseline LCQ score (p≤0.001) were predictors of trajectory group. Most patients (76%) achieved improvement exceeding the LCQ minimally clinically important difference. Notably, the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group demonstrated marked improvement in LCQ score with a shorter treatment duration (median 56 days).ConclusionsThis novel application of trajectory analysis to investigate treatment response to speech pathology intervention in RUCC supports the heterogeneous nature of RUCC. We identified a group with severe cough who demonstrated a faster and greater treatment response. Our findings demonstrate the effectiveness of speech pathology intervention and support further exploration of cough phenotyping.

Introduction

Refractory or unexplained chronic cough (RUCC) is a prevalent condition that negatively impacts quality of life and increases healthcare burden [1–4]. Speech pathology intervention is an effective treatment for RUCC [5–8]. Efficacy data are available in randomised trials [5, 9]; however, there are limited real-world data available to inform clinical practice. While the concept of cough phenotyping has gained recognition, the impact on treatment response variability requires further investigation [10, 11] and proposed chronic cough phenotypes show variation in the literature [12, 13].

In other disease domains, understanding treatment response heterogeneity represents an important aspect of clinical care linked to patient outcomes. For example, Hamada et al. [14] identified treatment response trajectories and their relationship to clinical remission following mepolizumab treatment for severe eosinophilic asthma. Assessing heterogeneity of treatment response using treatment response trajectory has not previously been reported for RUCC and may provide valuable clinical insight into management.

We hypothesised that distinct trajectories of response to speech pathology intervention exist among individuals with RUCC. The aims of this study were to 1) identify trajectories of response to speech pathology intervention for RUCC and 2) determine predictors of treatment response trajectories.

Method

Study design and setting

Adults with RUCC who received speech pathology treatment and attended at least three visits in the speech pathology clinic of a tertiary referral hospital (John Hunter Hospital, Newcastle, Australia) were eligible for inclusion. Data were prospectively recorded at appointments and collected through medial record review. Governance authorisation was provided by the Hunter New England Human Research Ethics and Governance Office. Details about clinic services, referral criteria, assessment and therapy procedures, routine outcome reporting, and discharge strategies are included in the supplementary material.

Participants

Participants were 205 individuals referred to speech pathology for management of RUCC between January 2019 and August 2024 (figure 1). Refractory chronic cough was defined as cough lasting >8 weeks despite optimal medical management of associated medical conditions. Unexplained chronic cough was defined as cough with no known causal factors.

Participant flowchart. LCQ: Leicester Cough Questionnaire; GORD: gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.

Inclusion criteria included prior medical management of cough by a respiratory physician or otolaryngologist involving optimally managed coexisting asthma, rhinitis/rhinosinusitis or gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD). Participants were required to be ≥18 years old with cough reported as their primary complaint. They needed to have attended at least three speech pathology sessions where LCQ [15] score was recorded.

Exclusion criteria included untreated asthma, GORD and/or rhinitis/rhinosinusitis, lower airways disease, neurological impairment and/or significant psychological factors that prevented engagement in therapy (figure 1).

Outcomes

Demographics, medical comorbidities including depression, appointment dates, LCQ scores at each visit (see supplementary material) and clinical outcome were extracted. The participant's outcome was defined as improved if their LCQ score improved by the minimally clinically important difference (MCID). We also noted whether the participant and/or the speech pathologist reported an improvement in symptoms.

Statistical analysis

We employed a growth mixture model using LCQ scores of all visits to estimate individual response trajectories to speech pathology intervention for RUCC. This model accommodates longitudinal data with missing data to estimate latent classes of response to the intervention while allowing individual variability within the same class [16]. In modelling with covariates of age and sex, we fitted linear and quadratic growth models with varying numbers of latent classes (ranging from one to four), using maximum likelihood estimation. The optimal number of latent classes was determined based on the Bayesian Information Criterion and the Akaike Information Criterion, class memberships assigned to each trajectory comprising at least 5% of the population, and clinical interpretability in terms of trajectory number and shape [16].

Locally estimated scatter plot smoothing was used to visualise the response trajectories over the follow-up period. Logistic regression models were used to identify baseline predictors of trajectory membership. Univariable regression analyses were first performed to identify variables associated with response trajectories. Variables with a p-value <0.10 in the univariable analysis were then included in the multivariable model to identify predictors of trajectory groups.

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were compared using the t-test or ANOVA, while those with a non-normal distribution were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher's test. The Bonferroni–Dunn test or Bonferroni correction were used for post-hoc analysis. Missing data were not imputed. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study consisted of 205 participants with RUCC. Most participants were female (72%) with a mean age of 61 years (table 1). Participants had a median cough duration of 3 years. 36% of participants were obese. Comorbid medical conditions were addressed prior to speech pathology referral and where necessary, participants remained on active treatment concurrently with speech pathology intervention. The mean±sd baseline LCQ score for the entire population was 12.0±3.5. The mean LCQ scores for trajectories 1 and 2 were 8.7 and 9.8, respectively, which were classified as severe [17].

Cough trajectories

Three treatment response trajectory groups were identified. Trajectory 1 (n=43 (21%)) included severe baseline LCQ scores with slow improvement over time (Severe Cough–Slow Response; mean LCQ score 8.7). Trajectory 2 (n=47 (23%)) also included severe scores at baseline but rapid improvement (Severe Cough–Rapid Response; mean LCQ score 9.8). Trajectory 3 (n=115 (56%)) included moderate scores at baseline and slow improvement (Moderate Cough–Slow Response; mean LCQ score 14.2) (figure 2 and supplementary table S1). The response was considered rapid if there was a substantial improvement in LCQ score between baseline and visit 2 and between visit 2 and visit 3. Conversely, the response was considered slow when improvement in LCQ score was less substantial.

a) Change in Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) scores over time and b) degree of change in LCQ scores over time, with the minimally clinically important difference of 1.3 shown by the dashed line, for the three trajectory groups: Severe Cough–Slow Response, Severe Cough–Rapid Response and Moderate Cough–Slow Response. Solid lines indicate the weighted mean. Confidence intervals are indicated by shading. Statistical differences were assessed visually based on whether the 95% confidence interval bands of each trajectory overlapped. The 95% confidence interval band of the Severe Cough–Rapid Response trajectory lies far above the other groups.

Baseline characteristics were compared between the three trajectory groups (table 2). There was no significant difference between trajectories according to sex, age, smoking status or comorbidities. Prevalence of asthma in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group was 42% compared to 21% and 22%, respectively, in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response and Moderate Cough–Slow Response groups. The results indicate that having comorbid asthma may impact the participant's trajectory (p=0.034), as does their baseline LCQ score (p≤0.001). There was a trend for a higher prevalence of comorbid inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO) (confirmed on laryngoscopy as previously described [18, 19]) in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group compared to the other trajectory groups; however, this did not reach the threshold for statistical significance (p=0.077). The baseline LCQ scores were similar between the Severe Cough–Slow Response and Severe Cough–Rapid Response groups, and less severe (although still abnormal) in the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group. The median (interquartile range (IQR)) time between appointments was 35 (21–56) days.

Predictors of treatment response

Multivariable regression analysis investigated predictors of the Severe Cough–Rapid Response and Moderate Cough–Slow Response trajectory groups, using the Severe Cough–Slow Response group (the least responsive) as the reference group (table 3). In comparison to the reference group, patients in the Moderate Cough–Slow Response and Severe Cough–Rapid Response groups had higher baseline LCQ scores, less asthma and less ILO. For every 1-point increase in baseline LCQ score, the odds of being in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group increased by 23%, although the effect size was small. Conversely, diagnosis of comorbid asthma reduced the odds of having a Severe Cough–Rapid Response trajectory by 63%. For non-smokers, the odds of having a Moderate Cough–Slow Response trajectory increased by 953%. Presence of comorbid ILO reduced the odds of having a Moderate Cough–Slow Response trajectory by 80%. There was no significant multicollinearity in the multivariable models based on the variance inflation factor.

Cox regression analysis (supplementary table S4) showed the chance of achieving an improvement in LCQ score of ≥1.3 was nearly double in patients with the Severe Cough–Rapid Response trajectory when controlling for asthma and ILO compared to the Severe Cough–Slow Response trajectory.

Impact of asthma and ILO on treatment trajectory

The impact of asthma and ILO on trajectories was explored. Overall, the trajectory for improvement was lower for patients with asthma but not significantly different for people with ILO (supplementary figures S1 and S2 and supplementary tables S2 and S3). The time taken to reach the MCID was longer in participants with asthma when compared to those without asthma (figure 3a). In contrast, the presence of ILO had no impact upon the median time to achieve the MCID (figure 3b). Patients without asthma had a 2.36 times higher likelihood of achieving the MCID than those with asthma after adjusting for rhinitis/rhinosinusitis and obesity (supplementary table S4).

Treatment outcomes

The median number of speech pathology appointments in each trajectory group was 4. Most participants (76%) improved by more than the MCID; however, the trajectory of improvement was more marked in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group (figure 2a). Furthermore, the degree of change was similar in the Severe Cough–Slow Response and Moderate Cough–Slow Response trajectories and higher in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response trajectory (figure 2b). Most participants (79%) self-reported symptom improvement, with speech pathologist documented improvement in 78% of participants. Post-treatment (final) LCQ scores were higher (i.e. less severe) in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response trajectory group with a shorter treatment time, followed by the Moderate Cough–Slow Response and then Severe Cough–Slow Response trajectories. There was a moderate correlation between baseline LCQ score and change in LCQ score as a percentage of baseline score.

The cumulative proportion of participants meeting the LCQ MCID is shown in figure 4a, with >75% of participants reaching the MCID by 119 days. This result differs between trajectory groups (figure 4b), with those in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group taking 91 days to reach the LCQ MCID, followed by the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group at 133 days and the Severe Cough–Slow Response group at 201 days. In the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group the median (IQR) change in LCQ score was 2.7 (1.1–4.3) and 71.3% of participants improved by more than the MCID. Furthermore, 83.5% of participants in this group reported improvement.

Kaplan–Meier curves for achieving Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) improvement of ≥1.3: a) all participants and b) participants classified by trajectory group.

The median (IQR) treatment duration for the entire cohort was 112 (77–196) days, comprising 163 (89–334) days in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group, 112 (76–196) days in the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group and 105 (65–132) days in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group. The Severe Cough–Slow Response group had a longer treatment duration (p<0.01). Interestingly, the median time between appointments 1 and 2, while longer for those in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group, was not significantly different between groups (p=0.079) (supplementary table S1). Participants in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group had the greatest pre- to post-treatment improvement in LCQ scores and the highest percentage who reached the MCID (table 4).

Discussion

This study identified three distinct response trajectories following speech pathology treatment of RUCC: 1) severe LCQ score at baseline with slow improvement over time (Severe Cough–Slow Response), 2) severe LCQ score at baseline with rapid improvement (Severe Cough–Rapid Response) and 3) moderate LCQ score at baseline with slow improvement (Moderate Cough–Slow Response). While most patients with RUCC responded to speech pathology intervention, there was a group who presented with RUCC and severe quality of life impairment who had a faster and greater response to speech pathology intervention in a shorter period (i.e. the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group). The remaining two trajectory groups had a slower albeit positive response to intervention. Most patients in the current study (n=155 (75.6%)) reached the MCID; however, the proportions differed between trajectory groups (table 4). The mean change in LCQ scores was consistent with other studies of speech pathology intervention for cough [1, 20–22]; however, the difference between pre- and post-treatment LCQ scores differed between groups (figure 2a).

The three distinct treatment trajectories identified in this study suggest that response trajectories are not uniform and support evidence for the heterogeneity of RUCC [23]. While trajectory analysis has been used in other diseases [14], this study is the first to use trajectory analysis in RUCC. This study is also the first to use trajectory analysis of cough symptoms as an outcome in RUCC. Recently, Zhang et al. [24] studied lung function trajectories in patients with cough in a prospective community-based cohort study and found that individuals with intermittent productive cough and chronic productive cough had worse lung function trajectories than those with chronic dry cough and allergy. One explanation for the different response trajectories identified in our study could be prioritisation or concurrent treatment of coexisting conditions such as muscle tension dysphonia and ILO. It is possible that different phenotypes of cough, including dry cough, productive cough and nocturnal cough, may be associated with different comorbidities and treatment response. Further research is recommended to explore this hypothesis. Our results demonstrate that speech pathology intervention is effective and there is heterogeneity in the treatment response trajectory. This supports the need to investigate phenotyping and tailoring of therapy.

Predictors of a rapid improvement trajectory in the current study were no asthma and better (higher) baseline LCQ score. Smoking and ILO were associated with the Moderate Cough–Slow Response trajectory group. Smoking and asthma have been associated with worse treatment outcomes in other studies, in those with cough [1, 10]. The presence of smoking and asthma is likely to increase the risk of a slower response trajectory. Smoking is also associated with worse asthma treatment outcomes [25]. Prior speech pathology treatment studies of RUCC exclude current smokers, hence the impact of behavioural interventions in people who smoke is unknown. Furthermore, behavioural cough suppression may not be appropriate for many current smokers and should only be undertaken in individuals judged to have a hypersensitive component to their cough.

The time taken to reach the LCQ MCID was longest for patients in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group. Furthermore, it took patients with asthma longer to achieve the MCID, regardless of trajectory group. Conversely, the median time to achieve the MCID was not significantly different between those with and without ILO regardless of trajectory group (table 4). This result may be due to the overlap in treatment approaches between cough and ILO, and while specific patient factors might impact the time taken to reach the MCID, these are not well understood. Laryngeal hypersensitivity and ILO are different entities albeit with some overlap. Laryngeal hypersensitivity can exist in RUCC occurring without ILO. In the current study, 67% of participants in the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group had coexisting ILO. Further, 85% of these participants had abnormal laryngeal hypersensitivity as measured by the Newcastle Laryngeal Hypersensitivity Questionnaire [26].

Time in each treatment trajectory was likely impacted by the availability of appointments. Whether cough is less responsive to speech pathology intervention in asthma or whether the speech pathology treatment requires modification for individuals with asthma is unknown. Baseline LCQ scores in our cohort were more severe than those reported in previous studies. Zhou et al. [27] hypothesised that asthmatic cough might be related to cough hypersensitivity rather than underlying asthma, and Satia et al. [28] proposed that there is some consistency between the sensory nerves in cough and those in asthma. Patients with asthma may have increased sensory response to low-level or non-tussive stimuli which makes cough suppression more challenging.

The difference in median time to cough improvement for the Moderate Cough–Slow Response group was greater than the difference between the Severe Cough–Slow Response and Severe Cough–Rapid Response groups. This result suggests that time to improvement alone may not clearly differentiate response categories.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective analysis using prospectively collected clinical data from a single site providing tertiary care and the results might not be generalisable to the larger population. Participants were seen by several speech pathologists who were all highly experienced in assessment and management of chronic cough. While procedures were standardised between clinicians, appointment scheduling was not rigid, and it is possible there were subtle differences in therapy.

Changes in comorbidities and medications were not systematically reported and adjusted; however, all patients had their medical treatment of cough optimised before referral to speech pathology. Some patients contracted COVID-19 and upper respiratory tract infections during the intervention which may have impacted the results. This study measured cough trajectory; however, trajectories of other symptoms such as ILO and dysphonia were not tracked. ILO and dysphonia scales such as the Dyspnoea Index [29] or Voice Handicap Index [30] might have been more relevant for these individuals.

Patients who attended two visits or fewer were excluded from this study. Their trajectory was similar to those in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group and only required two sessions. The need for three LCQ scores is a statistical requirement of trajectory analysis. The impact of this would be to underestimate the prevalence of the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group. As such, our data represent a conservative estimate of this important group. To address this issue, we have analysed the participants with only two LCQ scores (n=150). Of these patients, 85 (57%) had a significant improvement in LCQ score after two sessions. Of those who improved, the mean±sd improvement in LCQ score was 4.3±2.6.

While it is possible that the Hawthorn effect might overestimate the magnitude of change, it is likely to apply to each group equally and therefore does not minimise the significance of identifying the heterogeneity of treatment response. The timing of treatment response may have been influenced by availability of appointments. Most patients attend between four and six sessions, and these are often spaced due to appointment availability. 47% of patients in the Severe Cough–Slow Response group, 9% of patients in the Severe Cough–Rapid Response group and 30% of patients in the Moderate Cough–Moderate Response group were still in therapy at day 180. It is also possible that patients who remained in therapy for up to 180 days were those with less favourable responses to therapy, which may have introduced attrition bias.

Future implications

Many baseline characteristics were not significant in predicting response trajectory. This suggests the need to consider additional prognostic indicators for RUCC. The potential to identify rapid responders early would have implications for treatment planning. Further research is needed on the impact of asthma on treatment responsiveness and the association between the trajectory of LCQ score and other symptom trajectories. The impact of less tangible patient factors that might influence treatment response, e.g. interoception or externalised locus of control, is unknown and further research is needed.

Conclusion

This is the first study to examine treatment response trajectories in patients with RUCC receiving speech pathology intervention in a real-world clinical setting. The identification of three distinct improvement trajectories underscores the heterogeneous nature of RUCC and highlights the necessity for further investigation of phenotypes and endotypes in cough. We identified a group with severe cough who experience a rapid and better treatment response, in a shorter time frame. This group had severe cough without comorbid asthma. This is an important group to identify since baseline characteristics associated with a better treatment trajectory enable the prediction of positive responses to behavioural treatment. The slower trajectory for those with asthma suggests that additional support and modification is required in the behavioural treatment programme.
