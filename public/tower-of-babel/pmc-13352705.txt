Methodological estimation of pore size in hen eggshell membranes
Authors: Masahide Hagiri, Mayuko Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEggshell membranes (ESM) are proteinaceous biobarriers critical to avian embryonic development and microbial exclusion. Despite their importance, quantitative assessments of ESM pore dimensions remain limited. This study aimed to determine the effective pore size of chicken ESM and to validate methodological approaches for its estimation.ResultsTwo orthogonal strategies were employed to evaluate ESM pore architecture. First, pressure-driven pure-water permeation was performed within a low transmembrane pressure range (0.01–0.05 MPa). The resulting flux–pressure data were analyzed using the Guéout–Bjerrum–Manegold relation to approximate average pore dimensions. Second, size-selective filtration experiments were conducted using colloidal silica and polystyrene latex particles ranging from 14 to 204 nm. Rejection rates were plotted against particle size and interpreted through the Ferry–Renkin model to derive effective pore estimates. Comparison of the two methods demonstrated strong consistency, supporting their mutual applicability. While numerical outcomes suggested pore dimensions on the order of ~ 100–200 nm, the emphasis of this work lies in validating complementary approaches. Together, these techniques provide a robust framework for probing semipermeable biological barriers and open avenues for applying ESM, an abundant biowaste, in bioinspired membrane technologies and biosafety studies.

Introduction

Chicken eggs are among the most widely consumed avian eggs by humans and have been the focus of extensive scientific investigation. Each egg is enclosed by an oval, calcified shell with a pointed and a blunt end. Structurally, the shell comprises three distinct layers, arranged from outside to inside: the cuticle, the calcified shell, and the eggshell membranes (ESM) [1, 2]. The ESM form a semipermeable barrier 50–70 μm thick that adheres closely to the inner surface of the shell. They consist of two layers—the outer and inner shell membranes—which separate at the blunt end to create an air cell [1, 3]. The membranes are protein-rich, composed mainly of keratin, mucopolysaccharides, and related glycomolecules, and they exhibit a meshwork of intertwined fibers. Together with the shell and cuticle, the membranes are generally regarded as key barriers against ingress of water and microorganisms [4, 5].

Previous studies have addressed permeability of the eggshell to gases and vapors (oxygen and water vapor), and bacterial penetration has been evaluated using challenge tests [6–10]. However, aside from a few reports, there is limited quantitative information on the size of particles or molecules that can traverse the ESM and, by extension, on effective pore (void) size of the membranes [11–14].

Hen eggs also play a central role in influenza vaccine production. In current practice, influenza virus is inoculated into embryonated eggs, amplified, harvested, partially split with ether, and inactivated with formalin. This approach persists because eggs are readily available at scale and enable high-yield production [15, 16]. From the standpoint of both fundamental science and industrial biosafety, clarifying the effective void size of the ESM is therefore important.

In this report, we studied the pore-network architecture and selective transport properties of the ESM by treating it as a porous, semipermeable membrane. Specifically, we (i) measured pure-water permeation under pressure and estimated pore size from flux using the Guéout–Bjerrum–Manegold relation [17–19], and (ii) performed membrane filtration using colloidal silica and polystyrene latex as model solutes, estimating pore size from size-dependent rejection with the Ferry–Renkin equation [19, 20]. These two complementary approaches provide a practical route to elucidate porous features in biological membranes. By examining molecular and particulate transport as a fundamental membrane study, we anticipate expanded valorization pathways for ESM—currently generated in bulk as waste—in biomedical and engineering applications. Comparative analyses across avian species may also illuminate biological roles of ESM. More broadly, the present approach advances methodologies for evaluating biological “membranes” embedded in organismal structures.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Commercial, unfertilized White Leghorn eggs sold by Abukuma Keiran Inc. (Fukushima, Japan) were used. The eggs were classified as size “L” under the standards of Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (64–70 g per egg). The top of each egg was opened, the albumen and yolk were removed, and the shell–membrane interface was treated with 1.0 M HCl for ~ 15 min to separate the ESM. The ESM consists of an asymmetric bilayer composed of outer and inner membranes; we did not separate these layers. In all permeation and filtration experiments described below, the feed faced the outer membrane side. Circular specimens (13 mm diameter) were punched from the equatorial region of each egg mounted vertically. All thickness values used in the permeability analysis correspond to hydrated membranes. The membrane thickness was evaluated under hydrated conditions, and samples were consistently taken from the equatorial region of the egg to minimize spatial variation in membrane thickness.

Estimation of effective pore diameter by pure-water permeation

Prepared ESM discs were mounted in 13 mm polypropylene holders (Swinnex, Merck Millipore). A polyethersulfone reservoir containing 300 mL of distilled water was connected upstream, and nitrogen gas was introduced to provide the driving pressure. Permeate was collected, and the elapsed time t [s] and permeate mass wp [kg] were recorded with an electronic balance and data logger. Estimation of effective pore diameter by colloid rejection

To estimate effective pore size from rejection, we filtered colloidal dispersions through the ESM. Prepared ESM were mounted in the same holders and connected to polypropylene syringes fitted with compatible luer joints. Colloidal solutions of known concentration and particle size were loaded, the assembly was held vertically, and pressure was applied by loading a constant weight onto the syringe plunger through a flat plate. The wet plunger diameter was 16.0 mm, and the applied force was 2.0 kgf. Permeate was collected, and colloid concentration was determined using fluorescence spectrophotometer by measureing first-order scattering intensities against calibration curves. As colloidal silica, we used LUDOX HS-40 (Sigma-Aldrich; 14 nm), Snowtex O-40 (Nissan Chemical; 26 nm), Snowtex 30 L (47 nm), and Snowtex YL (59 nm). As polystyrene latex (Nissin EM), we used 95 nm (0.095 μm) and 204 nm (0.204 μm) particles. Prior to filtration, all colloidal suspensions were sonicated to ensure uniform dispersion and to minimize particle aggregation. The suspensions were used immediately after sonication.

Results and discussion

Estimation of effective pore diameter from pure-water permeation

Initially, the mass flux of water permeation was measured while gradually increasing the applied pressure from 0.050 to 0.30 MPa, followed by a similar measurement during stepwise pressure reduction from 0.30 MPa back to the initial level. The relationship between flux and applied pressure revealed a tendency toward saturation in the high-pressure range. Furthermore, the fluxes recorded during the pressurization and depressurization phases did not coincide, resulting in a hysteresis curve. These observations suggest that application of high pressure induces densification (compaction) of the ESM, thereby influencing the measurement outcomes. Consequently, it was determined that filtration coefficients KF​, which are typically derived from the linear increment of flux with respect to pressure, could not be reliably obtained under wide-range pressure scanning.

To address this issue, the pressure range was restricted to 0.01–0.05 MPa. Within this range, a linear relationship between pressure and flux was observed. When the pressure was cycled within this range, no noticeable hysteresis was observed, indicating that the membrane response was essentially reversible under these conditions. A representative plot for a single ESM sample is shown in Fig. 1. The dashed line in the figure represents the linear fitting function used to determine KF​.

From the slope of this linear plot, the filtration coefficient KF​ was calculated. Combining this value with the membrane thickness L and the volumetric porosity ε, the effective pore radius of the ESM was estimated for five specimens (A–E). The resulting average pore radius was 82.6 nm (165 nm, in diameter), with a standard deviation of 12.0 nm (24 nm, in diameter). The maximum and minimum values among the five samples were 97 nm and 67 nm, respectively.

Estimation of effective pore diameter from colloid rejection

The effective pore radius of the ESM was estimated based on the rejection rate of colloidal particles during filtration experiments. For all membrane samples, the particle rejection rate remained low for particle diameters up to 59 nm, whereas significantly higher rejection was observed for particles of 95 nm and above. The relationship between particle diameter and rejection rate exhibited a sigmoidal curve, which was analytically fitted using the Ferry–Renkin equation to determine the effective pore radius rFR​. This systematic size dependence and the sigmoidal profile are consistent with size-exclusion transport through membrane pores as described by the Ferry–Renkin model, suggesting that aggregation or adsorption effects did not dominate the observed rejection behavior. A representative plot for a single ESM sample is presented in Fig. 2. The solid sigmoidal curve shown in the figure represents the simulated result obtained when rFR was set to 70 nm.

The average effective pore radius determined from five individual specimens (A–E) was 78.0 nm (156 nm, in diameter), with a standard deviation of 8.4 nm (17 nm, in diameter). The maximum and minimum values observed were 90 nm and 70 nm, respectively.

Cross-validation of methods

Plotting rGBM (permeation-based) against rFR (rejection-based) showed a strong linear correlation (R = 0.954), indicating that the two orthogonal methods are consistent and mutually validating. The corresponding plot is shown in Fig. 3.

Cross-validation showed close agreement between pore sizes estimated from pure-water permeation and colloid rejection experiments, confirming the applicability of both approaches for evaluating ESM pore size.

With respect to the question of which method is superior, certain distinctions can be made. The colloid rejection test may be considered more accurate in that it directly assesses filtration of particles of defined size. However, repeated filtration of colloids of various diameters and the evaluation of corresponding rejection rates require extensive experimental effort. Importantly, the comparative analyses presented here demonstrate that reliable evaluation can also be achieved using pure-water permeation alone, underscoring the practicality of this simpler method.

The present measurements further indicate that the effective pore radius of the ESM is approximately 80 nm (equivalent to a diameter of ~ 160 nm). Such a pore dimension suggests that while gases and vapors can traverse the barrier, the membrane effectively excludes bacteria (0.2–2 μm) and fungi (> 2 μm). By contrast, viruses (0.02–0.3 μm) fall within a size range where some species may be physically retained, whereas others may not, a finding with important implications for understanding the defensive role of the eggshell in avian reproduction.

The methodological framework presented here is not limited to ESM. The combined use of pure-water permeability testing and colloid rejection analysis provides a practical approach for characterizing semipermeable biological membranes across diverse organisms, including amphibian egg envelopes, plant seed coats, and cuticles. Such approaches may also contribute to the design of bioinspired separation materials that mimic the selective permeability of natural membranes.

Limitations

The number of samples analyzed was relatively small, and all specimens were derived from commercially available unfertilized chicken eggs. Because biological materials inherently exhibit natural variability, the limited sample size constrains the robustness of the discussion. Although major differences are not expected between fertilized and unfertilized eggs or between wild and domesticated chickens, this assumption requires further verification.

In estimating pore size from colloidal rejection experiments, the particle size range used was limited and does not fully constrain the pore size distribution. Moreover, the permeability-based analysis assumes simplified cylindrical pore geometry, whereas the eggshell membrane consists of a fibrous and tortuous network that may compact under pressure. Therefore, the permeability-derived pore size should be interpreted as an effective hydraulic pore radius rather than a true geometric pore size.

The separation of the eggshell membrane from the shell required acid treatment, which may alter the native membrane structure to some extent. Despite these limitations, the proposed methodology provides a practical framework for estimating effective pore sizes in biological membranes and may facilitate future studies linking membrane structure and biological function.
