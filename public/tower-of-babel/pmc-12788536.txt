Cognitive Impairment in Post-COVID-19 Patients: A Prospective Neuropsychological Evaluation Study
Authors: Charan Sankar Sankaranarayanan, Koduvayur Roopa Lakshmi, Mannan Senthilnathan, A. G. Poorvika, Sasna S. Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Cognitive impairments, often termed “brain fog,” have emerged as prevalent sequelae among individuals recovering from COVID-19. Despite increasing recognition, systematic neuropsychological evaluation data remain limited.Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted between February 2023 and December 2024 in a tertiary care neurorehabilitation center. One hundred twenty post-COVID-19 patients (mean age 44.6 ± 11.3 years; 58.3% male) presenting with cognitive complaints at least 3 months after recovery were recruited. Patients underwent evaluation using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Trail Making Test A and B (TMT-A, TMT-B), and Digit Span Test. A control group of 60 age- and education-matched individuals without prior COVID-19 served as comparison. Statistical analysis included independent t-tests, chi-square tests, and multivariate logistic regression (P < 0.05).Results:Cognitive impairment (MoCA score <26) was observed in 66.7% of post-COVID-19 participants compared to 15.0% of controls (P < 0.001). Post-COVID patients showed significantly slower TMT-A (43.7 ± 12.9 vs 31.2 ± 9.6 sec; P < 0.001) and TMT-B times (95.8 ± 22.4 vs 76.5 ± 17.8 sec; P < 0.001). Working memory (Digit Span backward) was also impaired (mean score: 4.2 ± 0.9 vs 5.3 ± 0.8; P < 0.001). Severity of initial infection (hospitalized vs non-hospitalized) was associated with increased odds of impairment (OR: 2.87; 95% CI: 1.22–6.74; P = 0.015).Conclusion:Cognitive deficits are common in post-COVID-19 patients, particularly affecting attention, processing speed, and working memory. Structured cognitive screening and rehabilitation may be essential components of long-term COVID-19 care.

INTRODUCTION

The global COVID-19 pandemic has caused widespread health challenges extending far beyond the acute phase of infection. Among the emerging concerns is the manifestation of cognitive dysfunction in individuals who have recovered from SARS-CoV-2 infection, colloquially termed as “brain fog”.[1] While initial attention was centered on respiratory and systemic manifestations, a growing body of evidence indicates the virus may exert neurotropic effects leading to lasting neuropsychological consequences.[2]

Several mechanisms have been hypothesized, including direct viral invasion of the central nervous system, systemic inflammation, microvascular damage, and post-viral autoimmune responses.[3] Notably, cognitive symptoms such as impaired attention, memory lapses, reduced executive function, and processing delays have been reported across different age groups and severity spectrums of COVID-19.[4]

Cross-sectional and retrospective studies have provided preliminary insights into these cognitive impairments, but few have used standardized neuropsychological instruments in a prospective framework.[5] Moreover, variability in follow-up periods and lack of control groups have limited generalizability. Given the potential implications for workforce reintegration and quality of life, there is an urgent need to systematically assess the prevalence and profile of cognitive deficits post-infection.

The present study aimed to prospectively evaluate cognitive function using validated tools in individuals recovering from COVID-19 and to compare findings with a matched control group. The study also sought to explore clinical predictors of cognitive impairment, including initial disease severity and demographic factors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 120 post-COVID-19 patients were consecutively recruited from outpatient services. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age 18–65 years, (2) prior laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (RT-PCR), (3) at least 12 weeks post-recovery, and (4) self-reported cognitive complaints. Exclusion criteria comprised: history of neurological or psychiatric illness, pre-existing cognitive impairment, severe head trauma, or substance abuse.

Control group

Sixty age-, sex-, and education-matched healthy individuals without history of COVID-19 served as the control group.

Neuropsychological assessment tools

Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA): A 30-point screening tool evaluating multiple domains; scores <26 were considered indicative of cognitive impairment.

Procedures

Participants underwent assessments in a quiet environment by trained psychologists. Sociodemographic data, medical history, and COVID-19 illness severity (mild, moderate, severe) were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v26.0. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, proportions) were reported. Group comparisons were made using independent t-tests and chi-square tests. Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of cognitive impairment. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Among 120 post-COVID-19 patients, the mean age was 44.6 ± 11.3 years; 58.3% were male. The control group had a mean age of 43.1 ± 10.7 years; 56.7% male (P > 0.05 for both). Hospitalization was required in 38.3% of the post-COVID group.

Cognitive screening – MoCA scores

The mean MoCA score was significantly lower in the post-COVID group (24.1 ± 2.8) compared to controls (27.3 ± 1.9; P < 0.001). Cognitive impairment (MoCA <26) was observed in 80 patients (66.7%) vs 9 controls (15.0%) (P < 0.001).

Predictors of impairment

DISCUSSION

This prospective study reveals a high prevalence of cognitive impairment in individuals recovering from COVID-19, with significant deficits in memory, attention, and executive function. The findings align with prior studies indicating neurocognitive sequelae as part of the post-acute COVID-19 syndrome.[6]

In particular, the lower MoCA scores among post-COVID participants corroborate reports by Duindam HB et al. and Mattioli F et al., who also identified global cognitive deficits months after recovery.[78] The marked prolongation of TMT-A and B times in our cohort suggests impaired processing speed and cognitive flexibility, consistent with findings in Long COVID clinics.[9]

Impairments in the Digit Span backward test reinforce the vulnerability of working memory circuits, potentially linked to inflammatory or hypoxic injury to prefrontal regions.[10] Importantly, our results demonstrate that severity of the acute illness—especially hospitalization—significantly increases the risk of subsequent cognitive deficits, echoing outcomes from the PHOSP-COVID study.[11]

CONCLUSION

Cognitive impairment is prevalent among post-COVID-19 individuals, particularly affecting processing speed, attention, and memory. Hospitalization and older age are key risk factors. Routine cognitive screening and targeted interventions are warranted to mitigate long-term neuropsychological consequences of COVID-19.
