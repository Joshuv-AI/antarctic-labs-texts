A deep learning model for contact angle prediction in carbonate reservoirs under smart water assisted foam injection (SWAF)
Authors: Siavash Partonia, Seyyed Alireza Tabatabaei-Nezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the challenges in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) from carbonate reservoirs, this study investigates the application of machine learning models to predict contact angles under Smart Water Assisted Foam (SWAF) injection. SWAF, as an innovative and environmentally friendly approach, enhances sweep efficiency by altering reservoir rock wettability and controlling gas mobility. The primary objective is to develop accurate models for predicting contact angles based on input features such as ion concentrations (NaHCO₃, NaCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, KCl, Na₂SO₄), pressure, temperature, and contact time. The initial dataset comprised 1,615 samples from validated sources, which were expanded to 13,032 samples using data augmentation techniques including Gaussian noise, scaling transformations, and physically constrained Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). The models examined include Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), XGBoost, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Data were split into training, validation, and test sets in a 60:20:20 ratio, and hyperparameters were optimized using grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation. Results indicate that XGBoost achieved the best performance (RMSE = 0.09°; R² = 0.99997), followed by Random Forest (RMSE = 0.13°; R² = 0.99994), SVR (RMSE = 0.32°; R² = 0.99963), MLP (RMSE = 0.39°; R² = 0.99944), and CNN (RMSE = 1.48°; R² = 0.997). While CNN serves as a novel deep learning benchmark for exploring complex pattern recognition in tabular data, tree-based models like XGBoost outperform it for this tabular SWAF dataset, highlighting the value of ensemble methods. Feature importance analysis revealed the dominant influence of MgCl₂ (28%) and Na₂SO₄ (22%), whereas pressure had the minimal effect (~ 3%). Temperature exhibited an inverse relationship with contact angle. These models provide powerful tools for optimizing SWAF processes and reducing the need for costly experimental work. Limitations include the exclusion of crude oil properties and field-scale data. Future work is recommended to extend these models with dynamic and field-scale datasets to enhance practical applicability in the oil industry.Keywords: Enhanced oil recovery (EOR), Deep learning, Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), Smart water assisted foam (SWAF), Contact angle, Generalization capabilitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) encompasses advanced techniques to boost oil production from petroleum reservoirs after primary recovery declines. These methods often involve injecting water, gases (miscible or immiscible), chemicals, or thermal energy to stimulate reservoirs and improve oil displacement. For instance, miscible gas injection, such as carbon dioxide, reduces oil viscosity, enhancing flow in high-viscosity, low-API-gravity reservoirs by achieving gas-oil miscibility. Thermal methods, like steam injection, lower oil viscosity and raise reservoir temperature to facilitate extraction, particularly for heavy oils1.

In recent years, attention to new and innovative methods for enhanced oil recovery has intensified. One such method involves the use of smart water in combination with foam. Smart water possesses specific chemical properties that can alter the wettability of reservoir rocks. This wettability alteration leads to improved oil displacement and enhanced reservoir productivity. Foam injection, on the other hand, acts as a blocking agent to control gas mobility within the reservoir, thereby improving the efficiency of gas injection processes. Recent advances in machine learning, such as Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), have demonstrated exceptional accuracy in predicting material properties, offering a promising avenue for optimizing EOR techniques2,3. The combination of smart water and foam is particularly beneficial for carbonate reservoirs, which possess unique structural complexities and often present significant challenges for oil extraction. This combination assists in enhancing oil recovery by altering the wettability of carbonate rocks and improving the distribution of fluid phases within the reservoir3.

With growing concerns over climate change and depleting oil reserves, SWAF offers an environmentally friendly approach by improving recovery efficiency and reducing the need for new drilling. Given the concerns regarding climate change and the depletion of oil and gas reserves, the utilization of innovative and sustainable methods to enhance productivity and improve oil recovery has gained increasing importance. A bibliometric analysis reveals that applications of artificial intelligence and machine learning in the petroleum industry have grown exponentially in recent years, with over 62% of relevant publications appearing between 2018–20224. New methods that leverage the combination of smart water and foam have emerged as one of the potential and efficient options in this regard. These techniques not only contribute to improving the performance of existing reservoirs but also help to reduce the need for new drilling activities and support environmental protection efforts. In the context of EOR, the synergistic effect of SWAF has shown considerable potential in modifying rock wettability. The alteration mechanism is mainly driven by ion exchange between divalent cations (e.g., Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) and carbonate rock surfaces, which leads to expansion or compression of the electrical double layer. This interaction alters the surface free energy, facilitating a transition from oil-wet to more water-wet conditions. At the same time, foam acts as a mobility control agent by mitigating gas channeling and improving the sweep efficiency of the injected fluids. It should be noted, however, that wettability alteration is also affected by crude oil properties, including Total Acid Number (TAN), SARA fractions, and viscosity. Due to the unavailability of uniform crude oil property data across the dataset, these factors were excluded from the current model1,3.

Artificial intelligence (AI), as an innovative and transformative field within computer science, focuses on the development of systems and machines capable of performing complex tasks that were previously thought to be exclusive to human capabilities. This technology has been developed with the aim of simulating human abilities such as thinking, learning and reasoning within machines and systems5.

In a comprehensive study, a group method of data handling (GMDH) model was developed to predict interfacial tension (IFT) in normal alkane-nitrogen systems over a wide range of pressures (0.1–69 MPa) and temperatures (295–442 K), using 60% of a dataset for training and 40% for testing. The model achieved an average absolute relative error (AARE) of 3.81% in training and 3.91% in testing, outperforming established models like Linear Gradient Theory (LGT), Density Gradient Theory (DGT), and Parachor approaches combined with Volume Translated Predictive Peng Robinson Equation of State (VT-PPR EOS) in both accuracy and computational efficiency. Additionally, a vanishing interfacial tension (VIT)-based algorithm was proposed for minimum miscibility pressure (MMP) estimation, demonstrating high precision in paraffin-nitrogen systems6.

An experimental study using axisymmetric drop shape analysis (ADSA) measured equilibrium interfacial tensions (IFTs) between crude oil and CO₂ at temperatures of 30–80 °C and pressures up to 20 MPa, revealing a decreasing IFT trend with temperature at low pressures and an increasing trend at high pressures. Minimum miscibility pressures (MMP) and first-contact miscibility pressures (P_max) were determined via the vanishing interfacial tension (VIT) technique, showing linear increases with temperature (MMP from ~ 10 MPa at 30 °C to ~ 18 MPa at 80 °C). The study also highlighted the critical role of paraffin content in IFT behavior and noted that lower resin-to-asphaltene ratios increase asphaltene precipitation risks, while higher molecular weights of heavy components elevate MMP values7.

One of the most significant advancements in this domain is deep learning, a subset of machine learning, which operates based on multilayer artificial neural networks and possesses the ability to identify complex patterns within large datasets8,9. Deep learning, by employing complex and layered structures of neural networks, is capable of extracting key features from intricate data and providing highly accurate models10. This technique has had remarkable impacts particularly in the fields of image processing, speech recognition, and data analysis, leading to significant improvements in system performance and efficiency11.

Rigorous modeling of natural gas viscosity was performed using radial basis function neural network (RBFNN), least-squares support vector machine (LSSVM), and multilayer perceptron neural network (MLPNN) on over 3800 data points, with inputs including pseudo-reduced properties and molecular weight. The MLP-Levenberg-Marquardt (MLP-LM) model excelled with a root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.001, surpassing MLP-Bayesian regularization (BR) (RMSE = 0.002) and existing literature models in accuracy, while requiring fewer inputs (no density data). Sensitivity analysis identified molecular weight as the most influential variable, underscoring the models’ utility in upstream and downstream gas processes12. Advanced machine learning models, including multilayer perceptron (MLP) and radial basis function neural network (RBFNN), were applied to predict CO₂ solubility in brine based on NaCl molality, pressure, and temperature using 570 data points. Optimized by Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) for MLP and genetic algorithm (GA), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and artificial bee colony (ABC) for RBFNN, the RBFNN-ABC model achieved the highest accuracy with RMSE = 0.0289 and R²=0.9967, outperforming other models and literature correlations. The study confirmed model validity through outlier detection, identifying minimal doubtful data13.

One of the advanced applications of deep learning is the development of predictive models and data analysis tools, which can assist in uncovering hidden patterns and relationships within large datasets. These applications have proven highly effective across various fields, including data science, medicine, and market analysis. In particular, deep learning algorithms can process and analyze complex and unstructured data such as images and texts, thereby becoming indispensable tools in scientific and industrial research10. An artificial neural network (ANN) was designed to estimate formation permeability from geophysical well logs, using data from five wells in a heterogeneous reservoir. The three-layer feed-forward backpropagation network with 15 hidden neurons achieved accurate predictions, overcoming limitations of traditional porosity-permeability correlations (e.g., scattered plots with no credible linear fit). The ANN’s pattern recognition capabilities enabled superior permeability estimation compared to regression-based methods, demonstrating its effectiveness in heterogeneous formations14.

In the contemporary world, deep learning techniques are widely employed across diverse industries to enhance prediction accuracy, data analysis, and process automation. These advancements not only contribute to achieving more accurate and faster results but also lead to overall improvements in efficiency and productivity across scientific and industrial sectors10.

A pattern recognition-based AI approach using surrogate reservoir models (SRM) was developed for assisted history matching in a synthetic heterogeneous oilfield with 24 wells and 30 years of production history. The SRM, built from geological realizations and running in fractions of a second, accurately replicated full-field simulation results by tuning permeability, achieving high precision and speed. This method reduced computational time compared to traditional history matching, demonstrating SRM’s potential as an efficient tool for reservoir parameter adjustment15.

Seven advanced machine learning models, including cascade forward neural network (CFNN), generalized regression neural network (GRNN), wavelet neural network (WNN), and support vector regression (SVR) optimized by particle swarm optimization (PSO), farmland fertility algorithm (FFA), and grasshopper optimization algorithm (GOA), were applied to predict equivalent circulating density (ECD) in drilling mud using 2367 field data points from Iranian wells with water-based fluids. The GOA-SVR model outperformed others with average absolute percent relative errors (AAPRE) of 0.0823% (training), 0.0975% (testing), and 0.0869% (overall), while a group method of data handling (GMDH) correlation surpassed existing empirical models. Sensitivity analysis showed surface mud weight as the most influential variable, with leverage techniques identifying minimal outliers (~ 1-2.5%)16.

Recent studies on optimizing EOR processes through the integration of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and machine learning (ML) techniques highlight the potential of this approach for predicting interfacial properties between oil and brine, such as interfacial tension (IFT). In 2020, Alexsandro Kirch and colleagues applied MD simulations to represent oil–brine systems, followed by the implementation of four different ML algorithms, including a linear regression (LR) model, to predict IFT. The findings revealed that ML models significantly outperformed LR, providing higher predictive accuracy and demonstrating the effectiveness of combining MD with ML for interfacial property estimation.

In a 2020 study by Alexsandro Kirch et al., molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and four machine learning (ML) algorithms, including linear regression (LR), were used to predict interfacial tension (IFT). The results showed that ML models achieved higher accuracy than LR17.

In a 2020 study by Zhi Zhong et al., the cDC-GAN surrogate model, based on deep learning and zero-sum game theory, was applied to predict fluid distribution in reservoirs under water injection. The model accurately predicted water and oil compositions and production rates, demonstrating its effectiveness in accelerating reservoir simulations and optimizing water injection strategies18.

In a 2021 study by Rashida Salimova et al., it was shown that low salinity water (LSW) injection for enhancing oil recovery from carbonate reservoirs can be effectively evaluated using machine learning (ML) models. The study highlighted that ML models, particularly decision trees, can capture complex relationships among parameters such as salinity, ion concentration, and interfacial tension to assess LSW performance. Overall, integrating molecular dynamics (MD) with ML provides a powerful tool for more accurate prediction and optimization of oil recovery processes, supporting petroleum engineers in improving EOR and IOR strategies19.

In a study by Fiki Hidayat et al. (2021), the random forest regression algorithm was applied to assess the effectiveness of low salinity water injection (LSWI) in carbonate reservoirs. Using Computer Modelling Group Simulation Optimization Tool (CMOST) simulation software, they identified a few dominant parameters—particularly sulfate concentration and injection volume—as having significant influence on oil recovery, while other factors showed minimal impact. Their findings underline the importance of optimizing key ion concentrations and injection strategies in designing effective LSWI processes20.

In 2021, Hamidreza Afifi et al. investigated the use of SiO₂ nanoparticles to enhance the properties of smart water-based foam for EOR applications. Using a carbonate-patterned glass micromodel, they examined the effects of combining smart water with synthesized SiO₂ nanoparticles on foam stability and oil displacement efficiency. The results showed that the nanoparticle-enhanced foam was more stable than foams prepared with deionized water or smart water alone, with maximum stability achieved at 3000 ppm nanoparticle concentration. Under these conditions, foam half-life (t₁/₂) reached 3812 s and the R5 parameter rose to 99.2, compared to 2813 s and 91.5 for foam without nanoparticles. Foam injection experiments in a micromodel showed that silica nanoparticles enhanced foam stability by forming thicker layers, delaying foam collapse, and improving the oil recovery factor. The nanoparticles facilitated a stable emulsion between foam and oil droplets, reducing density and increasing sweep efficiency. The study concluded that smart water is an innovative EOR method that improves recovery by altering reservoir rock wettability through ion modification. Furthermore, combining smart water-based foam with SiO₂ nanoparticles can significantly enhance oil recovery and foam stability21.

In 2021, Anas Mohamed Hassan and colleagues conducted an experimental investigation on a hybrid method for improving oil recovery through SWAF injection in carbonate rocks. The approach involved three stages: (1) evaluating rock wettability to determine the optimal smart water composition, (2) chemical screening to identify stable foam systems in the presence and absence of crude oil, and (3) core flooding experiments to select the most effective formulations. The results showed that the combination of smart water (MgCl₂ at 3500 ppm) with the surfactant CTAB exhibited the greatest potential for enhancing wettability and foam stability. This formulation, tested with type A and type B crude oils, significantly altered wettability and increased oil recovery. Moreover, the generated foams remained stable even in the presence of crude oil. Overall, the study demonstrated that SWAF represents an innovative and effective technique for IOR/EOR, offering both environmental and economic advantages by improving wettability and foam stability22.

In 2022, Anas Mohamed Hassan et al. investigated foamability and foam stability within the SWAF technique for EOR, which integrates smart water and foam to enhance rock wettability and stabilize foam structures. A range of surfactants (CTAB, DTAB, AOS, Alpha Foamers) were examined with different crude oils, and the influence of CO₂ and N₂ gases on foam generation was assessed. The findings revealed that CTAB and AOS, particularly in MgCl₂ solutions, achieved the highest stability—approximately 80% in the presence of oil and 90% in its absence. Foam stability improved further with higher surfactant concentrations and when N₂ was used instead of CO₂. Moreover, MgCl₂ solutions with CTAB and AOS exhibited the lowest surface and interfacial tensions. Overall, the study demonstrated that SWAF is a novel, efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable method for EOR3.

In 2022, Anas Mohamed Hassan and colleagues presented a method for predicting the contact angle in carbonate rocks by combining SWAF with ANNs. The model provided an efficient alternative to conventional measurements, demonstrating high accuracy in analyzing existing data and supporting analytical processes in the oil and gas industry. It also facilitated the identification of optimal conditions for SWAF application to improve recovery efficiency. The study further highlighted model limitations and suggested directions for improvement23.

In 2022, Anas Mohammed Hassan and colleagues conducted a comprehensive study on the simultaneous injection of SWAF in carbonate reservoirs. This novel method was investigated as an EOR technology. The research, carried out through laboratory experiments and numerical simulations using CMG-STARS, demonstrated that SWAF can achieve up to 42% additional oil recovery (corresponding to a total of 92% of the original oil in place). Furthermore, surface complexation modeling (SCM) was employed to analyze the interactions among oil, brine, and rock. The findings revealed that, beyond its high recovery efficiency, this technology also offers economic and environmental advantages, thereby providing a solid basis for potential field pilot applications24.

In 2023, Anas Mohammad Hassan et al. conducted a comprehensive study on smart water-assisted foam (SWAF) injection in carbonate reservoirs using numerical modeling with CMG simulation software. A core-scale model was used for parameter sensitivity analysis via tornado diagrams, SOBOL, and Morris methods. Polynomial regression and radial basis function (RBF) neural networks were tested for surrogate modeling, with the RBF model outperforming polynomial regression. Sensitivity analysis identified liquid production rate, foam concentration, and Mg²⁺ concentration as key factors affecting oil recovery. Furthermore, particle swarm optimization (PSO) outperformed the DECE method, achieving a recovery factor of 97.89%. This study represents one of the first numerical modeling efforts of SWAF, providing a foundation for reservoir-scale design and pilot projects25.

Despite these advancements, prior research on SWAF in carbonate reservoirs has primarily focused on experimental evaluations of wettability alteration and foam stability, with limited integration of deep learning models for predictive purposes. While machine learning has been applied to related EOR parameters like IFT and solubility, there remains a gap in developing comprehensive deep learning frameworks specifically tailored for contact angle prediction under SWAF conditions, which is critical for optimizing wettability and recovery efficiency in heterogeneous carbonate systems. This study addresses this gap by proposing a deep learning model that accurately predicts contact angles in carbonate reservoirs during SWAF injection, leveraging key input parameters to enhance EOR decision-making and operational planning.

Modeling methodology

Data collection

In this study, a total of 1,615 datasets were compiled from peer-reviewed published sources for carbonate rocks from various countries in order to develop machine learning models — including convolutional neural networks (CNN), gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector regression (SVR), random forests (RandomForest), and multilayer perceptron (MLP) — to predict contact angle22,23,26–29. These data were extracted from articles focusing on contact-angle measurements under enhanced oil recovery (EOR) conditions and encompassed diverse scenarios, including smart-water injection alone (40%, 646 samples) and smart-water assisted foam (SWAF) injection (60%, 969 samples), thereby ensuring model generalizability across different EOR settings. The dataset was partitioned into three subsets: 60% for training (969 samples), 20% for validation (323 samples), and 20% for testing (323 samples). This split was chosen in accordance with common machine learning practice (e.g., Goodfellow et al., 2016) to balance pattern learning, hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation; the appropriateness of this partitioning is supported by the models’ outcomes (e.g., R² = 0.99997 for XGBoost). The dataset underwent quality control procedures, including cross-validation against original sources to verify data integrity and outlier removal using the interquartile range (IQR) method (excluding values outside 1.5×IQR), which led to the elimination of less than 2% of the data, thereby ensuring dataset reliability.

Input features used to predict contact angle comprised ionic and salt concentrations (in ppm) — specifically NaHCO₃, NaCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, KCl, and Na₂SO₄ — together with pressure (psia), temperature (°C), and time (hours). These features were selected based on surface-chemistry principles and prior EOR studies (e.g., Lashkarbolooki et al., 2014; Hassan et al., 2022), since they directly influence electrostatic interactions at the rock–fluid interface and play a key role in wettability alteration. Feature-importance analysis performed with XGBoost revealed that MgCl₂ (28%) and Na₂SO₄ (22%) exerted the largest influence on contact angle predictions, whereas pressure was of relatively low importance (~ 3%). Crude oil properties (e.g., TAN, SARA) and rock heterogeneity attributes (e.g., porosity) were excluded due to lack of uniformly available data, which may reduce model accuracy under heavy-oil conditions; future studies are recommended to incorporate these standardized features when available.

The variable “time” denotes the contact duration of the carbonate rock sample with the smart-water or SWAF solution, measured from initial contact until the contact-angle measurement. The temporal range was 1–48 h (mean 12.13 h), selected on the basis of experimental studies (e.g., Zhu et al., 2022) because it represents the time required to approach surface equilibrium in EOR experiments and aligns with field low-salinity injection timescales (from several hours to a few days). It should be noted that this “time” variable reflects static surface interaction rather than dynamic flow time. The associated data and their types are summarized in Table 1. Numerical and graphical data were extracted from the selected publications and the dataset was further expanded from 1,615 to 13,032 samples using data-augmentation techniques and generative adversarial networks (GANs); details are provided in the subsequent section. Augmentation procedures included the application of Gaussian noise (standard deviation = 0.01 × parameter value) and scaling transforms (factors between 0.95 and 1.05), and the GANs were constrained by physical priors (for example, the empirically supported inverse relationship between temperature and contact angle) to produce physically plausible synthetic samples. The augmented data distribution was validated against the original data using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test (p-value > 0.05).

Input data were extracted from validated experimental studies on carbonate rocks such as Indiana limestone, micritized wackestone, and dolomite, whose mineralogical compositions and petrophysical properties were confirmed by the cited references. These lithologies were selected because of their widespread use in EOR studies and their diversity in carbonate fabric, thereby enhancing the models’ generalizability.

In the expanded dataset, categorical group features—namely lithology, pore-type, and textural class—were incorporated via one-hot encoding to model their influence on wettability; this augmentation improved model performance (e.g., RMSE = 0.09° for XGBoost). All records contain both initial and final (post-reaction) contact-angle measurements; however, advancing and receding contact-angle data were generally unavailable. Consequently, the model employs the mean/equilibrium contact angle, a limitation explicitly acknowledged in the study. To address this constraint, future work should incorporate dynamic contact-angle measurements (advancing and receding) and field-scale datasets (e.g., CMG-STARS simulation outputs) to improve predictive accuracy under realistic reservoir conditions.

Model implementation process

Data enhancement and augmentation

This study employed three principal methods to augment and enhance data for the machine learning models: standard data augmentation, generative adversarial networks (GANs), and multi-task learning (MTL). These approaches were designed to expand the dataset from 1,615 to 13,032 samples, improve model accuracy (e.g., R² = 0.99997 for XGBoost), reduce overfitting, and increase generalizability in contact-angle prediction under SWAF conditions. Standard data augmentation for numerical features comprised adding Gaussian noise (standard deviation = 0.01 × parameter value, e.g., up to 50 ppm for MgCl₂) and applying scaling transforms (factors between 0.95 and 1.05) to emulate small experimental variations, while enforcing physical constraints (e.g., preventing negative concentrations). This procedure increased the dataset from 1,615 to approximately 5,000 samples. Image-based augmentations (such as rotations or added noise) were considered but not applied, since the study focused on tabular data (salt concentrations, temperature, pressure, time).

The GAN architecture consisted of two adversarial networks. The Generator comprised four Dense layers with 128, 256, 256, and 9 output neurons, using ReLU activations and Batch Normalization, and generated 9-dimensional synthetic feature vectors from Gaussian noise of dimension 100. The Discriminator comprised four Dense layers with 128, 256, 256, and 1 output neuron, using ReLU activations and Dropout (0.3) to distinguish real from synthetic samples. A physics-constrained GAN implementation was used, with constraints such as the inverse relationship between temperature and contact angle and the low influence of pressure, to prevent violations of physical laws. Training used a learning rate of 0.0002, batch_size = 64, and 1,000 epochs. Synthetic-data quality was validated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test (p-value > 0.05) and t-SNE visualizations of distributional similarity. The GAN step expanded the dataset from ~ 5,000 to 13,032 samples and—unlike naïve noise-based augmentation—produced synthetic data with distributions and correlations closely matching the real data, which improved model accuracy (e.g., RMSE = 0.09° for XGBoost). Multi-task learning (MTL) leveraged a shared model to learn multiple tasks simultaneously (see Fig. 1). In this study, MTL was applied to predict contact angle and to estimate feature importance (for example, MgCl₂ with 28% influence), thereby exploiting shared information across tasks (such as inter-salt correlations). The MTL architecture comprised shared layers with 128 neurons and task-specific output layers for each target, as depicted in Fig. 1.

Architecture of Multi-Task Learning.

The combination of the three methods—data augmentation, GANs, and MTL—enhanced model accuracy (e.g., R² = 0.99997 for XGBoost and R² = 0.997 for the CNN), mitigated overfitting (e.g., SD = 0.09° for XGBoost), and minimized the need for additional training data. However, limitations remain, such as the GAN’s reliance on pre-specified physics-based constraints and the lack of image-based data. For future work, we recommend incorporating dynamic measurements (e.g., advancing and receding contact angles) and crude-oil properties (e.g., TAN) into the GAN framework to increase data diversity. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

CNNs originally designed for image analysis, were employed in this study to model non-image data, specifically for predicting the contact angle from time-series signals. The schematic representation of the developed CNN architecture is shown in Fig. 2.

In this work, a one-dimensional CNN model (Eq. 1) was constructed and trained using data imported from comma-separated value (CSV) files. The dataset was divided into features (X) and target variables (Y), and further split into training (60%), testing (20%), and validation (20%) subsets to ensure robust model evaluation and to prevent overfitting.

The CNN architecture consists of a one-dimensional convolutional layer (Conv1D), a max-pooling layer (MaxPooling1D), a flattening layer (Eq. 2), and fully connected dense layers (Eq. 3). The max-pooling operation is expressed in Eq. 4. These layers are integrated with activation functions optimized through the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer and the mean squared error (MSE) loss function. The model was trained for 50 epochs using both the training and validation datasets.

To assess the performance of the CNN, several plots were generated, including the training and validation loss curves, as well as cross-plots comparing predicted and actual contact angles for the training, validation, and testing datasets.

To enhance the transparency and reproducibility of the model, the CNN architecture was structured with one-dimensional Conv1D layers (64 filters, kernel size = 3), followed by MaxPooling1D layers, ReLU activation, and dropout layers (dropout rate = 0.2) to prevent overfitting. The flattened output was connected to two dense layers (128 and 64 units), finalized with a single-node output layer for regression using linear activation. The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate = 0.001) and trained with the mean squared error (MSE) loss function over 50 epochs. Performance metrics included Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²).

To ensure robustness and prevent overfitting to specific data splits, 5-fold cross-validation was employed. Feature normalization was performed using standard scaling (zero mean and unit variance), and categorical variables such as lithology and texture were encoded via one-hot encoding. The pre- and post-augmentation data distributions were verified using boxplots and density plots to maintain statistical consistency (Fig. 3-A).

Conclusion

In enhanced oil recovery (EOR) from carbonate reservoirs, contact angle prediction is essential for assessing wettability alteration and optimizing sweep efficiency under Smart Water Assisted Foam (SWAF) injection. SWAF combines ion-modified smart water with foam to alter rock surfaces from oil-wet to water-wet, facilitating better oil displacement and reducing gas mobility. The novelty of this work lies in the integration of physically constrained Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for data augmentation, expanding the dataset from 1,615 to 13,032 samples while preserving physical realism (e.g., inverse temperature-contact angle relationship), and the use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) as a benchmark for deep learning on tabular data. Furthermore, several advanced machine learning models, including CNN, XGBoost, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), have been utilized to predict contact angle. While CNN serves as a novel deep learning benchmark, tree-based models like XGBoost outperform it for this tabular SWAF dataset, highlighting the value of ensemble methods. The models utilized in this study demonstrated a high degree of adaptability to complex data and varying conditions, resulting in superior performance in predicting contact angle compared to other models. Additionally, these models exhibited robustness to noisy and inconsistent data, enabling accurate predictions even in the presence of discontinuous and irregular data sets. Moreover, the feature importance analysis developed in this work outperforms existing approaches in terms of accuracy, providing a more reliable and trustworthy predictive framework for contact angle estimation. The models were trained and evaluated using nine input parameters including ion concentrations (NaHCO₃, NaCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂, KCl, Na₂SO₄), pressure, temperature, and contact time on a dataset consisting of 13,032 data points, and their performance was evaluated through statistical analysis and graphical representations. The key outcomes of this study can be outlined as follows:

Among the suggested models, the XGBoost model indicated exceptional accuracy, achieving an overall RMSE of 0.09° and R² of 0.99997. The Random Forest and SVR models, following the XGBoost model, showed the next highest levels of accuracy, with RMSE values of 0.13° and 0.32°, respectively. These findings underscore the superior performance of the XGBoost model in predicting contact angle.
