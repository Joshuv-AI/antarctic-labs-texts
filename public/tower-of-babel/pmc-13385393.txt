A polymer-coated nanowire sponge–based contact electrocatalytic system for simultaneous disinfection and removal of multiple micropollutants
Authors: Geng-Sheng Lin, Arshad Khan, Kuldeep Kaswan, Shuai Wang, Manish Kumar Sharma, Ravindra Joshi, Meenakshi Ray, Ping-Yu Yang, Chun-Yo Ting, Ji Gao, Xing Xie, Tsung-Shing Wang, Zong-Hong Lin, Zhaohui Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe removal of diverse contaminants in wastewater remains a major challenge due to low treatment efficiencies, high catalyst costs, and risks of residual chemicals. In this work, we introduce a polymer-coated nanowire sponge (PNS) based contact electro-catalysis (CEC) system for achieving the disinfection of microorganisms and the degradation of multiple micropollutants. Under ultrasonic stimulation, PNS facilitates interfacial electron transfer and generates a high rate of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Its synergistic ROS generation and static charge electroporation achieve over 99% disinfection within 3 minutes while degrading dyes, waste tire leachate, and reducing heavy metal ions in wastewater under ambient conditions. The system also exhibits a high hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) production rate of 20.2 ± 0.1 μmol h⁻¹. Furthermore, PNS demonstrates excellent versatility, ease of recovery, and reusability, highlighting its strong potential for both point-of-use and large-scale wastewater treatment applications.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Materials for energy and catalysis

Introduction

The rapid growth of the global population and socioeconomic development has significantly increased the demand for clean water1, with over 3.6 billion people lacking access to safe water2, and the number is expected to exceed 5.7 billion by 20503,4. However, industrial, agricultural, and urban pollution exacerbate the water crisis. These sources introduce a diverse range of biological pathogens and micropollutants into water systems, including organic compounds, toxic chemical intermediates, antibiotics, and heavy metals. The frequent coexistence of these hazardous substances with harmful microorganisms results in highly complex and persistent contamination in untreated or insufficiently treated wastewater5,6. Therefore, the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG 6)7 aims to improve global access to clean water and sanitation. Various water decontamination strategies, including electrocatalysis8,9, photocatalysis10,11, piezocatalysis12, and biochemical processes,13 have been developed. However, they often rely on expensive catalysts, require complex fabrication processes, and are typically limited to targeting specific types of contaminants, thereby constraining their practical applicability. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a simple, energy-efficient, and residue-free water treatment strategy capable of removing a wide range of micropollutants while providing effective microbial disinfection.

Contact electro-catalysis (CEC) is an emerging redox technology that integrates contact electrification, mechanochemistry, and catalysis14. Compared to conventional catalytic methods, CEC offers distinct advantages, including the use of noble and rare-earth metal-free catalysts, lower costs, and strong reusability, making it a promising and sustainable approach in catalysis and water treatment. Under mechanical stimulation, frequent contact-separation cycles at the interface of dielectric materials facilitate electron exchange15, generating a strong interfacial electric field (~10⁸ V m−1)16,17 that drives redox reactions and promotes the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)18. Additionally, the ability of CEC to accelerate or trigger redox reactions through solid–liquid contact electrification can be further enhanced by ultrasound-induced rapid contact-separation cycles19,20. Leveraging this mechanism, CEC is effective in degrading organic pollutants in aqueous solutions16,21–35, recycling spent lithium-ion batteries36, generating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)37,38, oxidizing methane39,40, reducing CO241, and inactivating algae42. In wastewater treatment, the ability of CEC to produce high levels of ROS (up to 325 μM h⁻¹) under ambient conditions (see Table S1 and Supplementary Data 1) enables either efficient pollutant degradation or microbial disinfection. However, the ROS generation rate of the CEC system needs to be further improved to offer a powerful and sustainable alternative to conventional chemical methods, such as chlorination and advanced oxidation processes (AOPs).

The catalyst material design of the CEC system plays a crucial role in enhancing the ROS generation rate. Recent research has focused on inert dielectric materials, primarily highly electronegative polymers, due to their chemical stability, cost-effectiveness, availability, and strong electron affinity43. Fluorinated materials24,26, such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), have emerged as promising candidates for CEC. Their high surface concentrations of fluorine atoms, the most electronegative element, combined with low ionization energy44, significantly enhance interfacial charge transfer during contact-separation cycles. This enhanced charge accumulates from water, generating strong interfacial electric fields45 that drive redox reactions. Despite these advantages, the inherent hydrophobicity of fluorinated materials poses a major challenge, as it limits their dispersion in aqueous environments. Rather than dispersing evenly, they tend to float on the water surface, forming a thin film that reduces the number of active reaction sites and ultimately limits catalyst loading. Furthermore, most CEC studies have relied on dielectric materials in powder form24, which require filtration for recovery to avoid secondary pollution46. In addition, ROS generation is closely tied to oxygen availability. However, strategies for intrinsically enhancing oxygen availability within CEC systems have yet to be explored. Hence, it is essential to design catalyst materials that simultaneously offer improved water dispersibility, easier recovery, and enhanced oxygen accessibility to boost CEC efficiency25.

In this study, we developed ultrasound-assisted CEC using a synthesized polymer-coated nanowire sponge (PNS), enabling efficient disinfection in a short time and the removal of multiple micropollutants. The PNS-CEC platform demonstrates an exceptionally high H₂O₂ generation rate of 20.2 ± 0.1 μmol h⁻¹. The synergistic effects of the high rate of ROS generation and static charge electroporation enable over 99% disinfection within 3 minutes, alongside the degradation of dyes, waste tire leachate, and heavy metal reduction under ambient conditions. The enhanced performance is attributed to the PNS design, which enables the deposition of PTFE onto copper oxide nanowire (CuOx NW) sponges. This design addresses dispersion challenges in CEC by leveraging the high surface area of Cu sponges, ensuring uniform catalyst distribution in water. Although polymer/metal composites have been shown to enhance CEC performance16, direct metal exposure to water under ultrasonication can cause excessive heavy metal ion leaching, leading to secondary contamination. Our approach mitigates this issue by significantly reducing Cu ion release through the polymer coating. Furthermore, the incorporation of the copper nanowire sponge promotes air encapsulated within PNS, thereby enhancing oxygen availability and subsequently increasing the generation of ROS. Notably, the PNS-CEC system operates without the use of noble or rare-earth metals and avoids generating harmful disinfection by-products. PNS-CEC also demonstrates scalability, reusability, and easy recovery. Hence, it paves the way for advanced catalytic applications in H₂O₂ production, and offers a sustainable, efficient, and cost-effective solution for micropollutant removal and disinfection, serving as a promising candidate for both point-of-use and centralized water treatment facilities.

Results

Performance of the PNS-CEC system

A systematic investigation was conducted on the disinfection and degradation of micropollutants using PNS integrated with ultrasound-assisted CEC for wastewater treatment, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The PNS operates via CEC, generating a high electric field that facilitates electroporation and ROS production, including superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and H₂O₂. It is known that H₂O₂ is a green oxidant widely used in chemical synthesis, environmental remediation, and disinfection. Its industrial production, however, depends on the anthraquinone process, which is energy-intensive and generates hazardous waste47. Therefore, a sustainable and safer production method is urgently needed to produce H₂O₂. This study uses ultrasound with low energy consumption (~0.1 W/cm3) and tunable energy-level capability to assist the PNS-CEC system to improve H₂O₂ and ROS generation rate. The H₂O₂ generation rate reaches 20.2 ± 0.1 μmol h⁻¹, which is much higher than reported systems employing photocatalysis, piezocatalysis, and conventional CEC (Fig. 1b, Table S1, and Supplementary Data 1). The occurrence of contact electrification at the water-PNS interface in the ultrasonication bath is confirmed by the electrical output of a single-electrode triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG), which is immersed in DI water under ultrasonication (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1). The mechanism of solid–liquid TENG is that when PTFE and water come into contact, there is initially no net charge between them. Charge separation occurs during the separation process as electrons transfer from one material to the other. The material that is more tribo-positive becomes positively charged (such as water), while the one that is more tribo-negative (such as PTFE) retains negative charges on its surface48. As the PTFE surface is withdrawn, a potential difference develops, driving electron flow through the external circuit to maintain charge balance. Upon recontact, the direction of charge flow reverses. These periodic micro-contact and separation events, arising from the dynamic interaction between impacting water and the PTFE surface, result in oscillating voltage signals (Supplementary Fig. S2). Around 3750 nC m−2 of negative charges were accumulated on the PTFE surface when in contact with water (Fig. 1d). Therefore, the negative contact-electrification charges on the PTFE surface induce an interfacial electrostatic field that polarizes the underlying CuOₓ/Cu substrate, resulting in a redistribution of free electrons within CuOₓ/Cu to balance the induced potential (Supplementary Fig. S3). In the simulation setup, the PNS surface was modeled by a parametric surface z = f(x, y), interpolated from the cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image (Supplementary Fig. S4) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) three-dimensional height map (Supplementary Fig. S5). A potential of 3.3 V was applied, consistent with the TENG experimental measurements involving contact electrification between PTFE and water. The simulation results (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. S6) demonstrated that an electric field intensity of 5×107 V m−1 was sufficient for effective water disinfection. The synergistic mechanism for a high rate of ROS generation and a high electric field enables the rapid inactivation of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria within 3 min. In addition, the produced ROS also effectively degrades organic pollutants, such as dyes and N-(1,3-dimethylbutyl)-N’-phenyl-p-phenylenediamine-quinone (6PPD-quinone) and reduces toxic heavy metal ions. This method represents a highly promising platform for next-generation water treatment, offering elimination of microbial pathogens and chemical contaminants, and serving as a potential candidate for both point-of-use and centralized water treatment facilities.

Fabrication and characterization of PNS

PTFE was selected for its exceptional chemical inertness, maintaining stability even under harsh conditions, such as exposure to strong acids, strong alkalis, and various solvents. Additionally, its tetrafluoro groups provide a significant advantage due to their highly tribo-negative nature and the low energy level of their lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)49. Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8 illustrate solid–liquid contact electrification and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) results for different polymers. These results confirm that PTFE exhibits the highest contact electrification ability with water compared to polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and polystyrene (PS), aligning with the triboelectric series. This enhanced electrification capability is crucial, as the generation of ROS has been directly correlated with the contact electrification performance of the materials employed50. Furthermore, PTFE exhibits a much lower adhesion force to water droplets in air compared with hydrophilic materials, reflecting its weak confinement of local liquid water and strong affinity toward the gas phase. Consequently, when a bubble approaches the PTFE surface underwater, the thin liquid film separating the bubble from the solid surface becomes unstable and ruptures rapidly. During this bursting process, oxygen is released into the surrounding liquid and interfacial region, thereby enriching the local oxygen concentration at the PTFE/water boundary. This oxygen release, together with the intrinsic aerophilicity of PTFE, facilitates the formation of dynamic three-phase (water–PTFE–air) interfaces with high reactivity, ultimately enhancing ROS generation51. The Cu sponge was selected as the backbone of PNS due to its high porosity52 and sponge structure, which facilitates air entrapment within the interconnected pores and enables easy recovery of PNS after treatment. The use of PNS instead of polymer powder form enhances recovery and contributes to the overall sustainability of the system.

PNS was fabricated using a two-step process (Fig. 2a). In step 1: a commercial Cu sponge underwent pyrolysis at a controlled temperature, leading to form copper oxide nanowires (CuOx NW) on its surface to produce the CuOx NW sponge. In step 2: the CuOx NW sponge was dip-coated with PTFE, followed by thermal curing to ensure strong adhesion, and the final sample was labeled as PNS. Figure 2a presents optical images of the Cu sponge, CuOx NW sponge, and PNS. The color transformation from reddish-brown to black after pyrolysis confirms the successful growth of CuOx NWs on the Cu sponge surface. After PTFE coating, CuOx NW sponge undergoes another distinct color change from black to gray, indicating the deposition of the polymer layer on the surface. After the PTFE coating, PNS exhibits a more hydrophobic surface compared to the pristine Cu sponge and CuOx NW sponge, with the contact angle ranging from 120.6° to 137.3°. The inherent hydrophobicity of the CuOx NW sponge originates from the thermal oxidation of Cu, a temperature-dependent process that governs the sequential phase evolution and morphological development of the CuOₓ nanostructures. During annealing, an initial Cu₂O layer forms on the metallic Cu substrate, followed by the growth of a CuO layer from which CuO NWs subsequently emerge, resulting in a possible characteristic three-layered architecture (Cu/Cu₂O/CuO)53. Notably, Cu₂O is intrinsically hydrophobic, which contributes to the surface hydrophobicity of the CuOx NW sponge54. This interpretation is supported by both X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses (Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10), which confirm the coexistence of mixed copper oxide phases. On the other hand, the strong hydrophobicity of PTFE arises from its nonpolar carbon-fluorine bonds55. All the materials remained hydrophobic, and it was observed that surface texturing, such as increased roughness, amplifies their intrinsic hydrophobicity (details provided in Supporting Information, Note 1 and Table S2). The material exhibits a contact angle within the ideal range (137.3°), which mitigates excessive water repellency while ensuring sufficient interfacial contact between aqueous contaminants and the material for effective treatment. Meanwhile, the material exhibits sufficient hydrophobicity to facilitate easy recovery from the treated water and maintain recyclability across multiple cycles.

Figure 2b presents SEM images of the Cu sponge, revealing its smooth and clean surface with macro-sized porous structure. After pyrolysis, a nanoforest texture emerges as the surface becomes fully covered with CuOx NWs (Fig. 2c). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images display the characteristic needle-like structure of CuOx NW, where the interplanar spacing is 1.8 Å with an hkl index of (202) (Fig. 2d). Following PTFE coating, a uniform size of 0.053 ± 0.018 μm of PTFE particle suspension was applied (Supplementary Fig. S11) to deposit PTFE on the CuOx NWs surface. The coated sponge was subsequently heated in a furnace at 350 °C to ensure firm deposition of PTFE on the surface (Fig. 2e). The microstructure of the PNS surface exhibits ridges, valleys, and some microcracks, along with polymer coverage over the surface pores. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping further reveals heterogeneous interfaces where PTFE is coated onto the CuOx NWs surface (Supplementary Fig. S12). Compared with the elemental mapping of the pristine CuOx NW sponge, the results showed a pronounced increase in fluorine content accompanied by a marked decrease in copper signal intensity after coating, confirming that the PTFE layer covers the CuOx nanowire surface (Supplementary Fig. S13). To gain deeper insight into the structural characteristics of the PNS, cross-sectional SEM and corresponding EDS elemental mapping were conducted for the Cu sponge, CuOₓ NW sponge, and PNS samples, as shown in Supplementary Figs. S14–S16. After thermal oxidation, the Cu sponge surface transformed into a nanoforest-like structure composed of CuOₓ NWs with an average length of approximately 30 μm and an estimated extent of conversion of around 0.75 (details provided in the Supporting Information, Note 2). Subsequent PTFE coating resulted in the formation of a polymer layer with a thickness of around 2.7 μm, as confirmed by cross-sectional SEM (Supplementary Fig. S4). N₂ adsorption–desorption analysis revealed that the specific surface area increased after the growth of CuOx NWs on the Cu sponge, but decreased after coating the CuOx nanowires with PTFE (Supplementary Fig. S17). Specifically, the weight of the pristine Cu sponge was 0.141 ± 0.00 g, which increased to 0.158 ± 0.00 g after thermal oxidation to form the CuOx NW sponge. Following PTFE coating, the total weight further increased to 0.322 ± 0.00 g. From these measurements, the mass ratio of PTFE to CuOx NW sponge was calculated to be approximately 1.03, indicating substantial polymer loading on the sponge surface. The observed reduction in surface area is likely due to partial pore blockage by the PTFE coating, which decreases the accessible surface for gas adsorption.

The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of PNS (Fig. 2f) display characteristic peaks at 1147 and 1203 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the characteristic peaks of stretching vibrations C-F2, and C-F in PTFE, respectively, confirming the successful PTFE coating24–26. Subsequently, the XPS spectra of PNS (Fig. 2g) reveal a significant F 1s peak, further validating the presence of PTFE on the surface. Analysis of the Cu 2p and F 1s regions revealed distinct CuFₓ-related peaks, confirming the coordination of surface Cu species with fluorine atoms derived from PTFE56 (Supplementary Fig. S10). PNS were then immersed in deionized (DI) water to evaluate their uniform water immersion and stability under ultrasonication (Supplementary Fig. S18). When immersed in water, the water with PNS remained clear even after ultrasonication. In contrast, when only CuOx NW sponge was used, the water became turbid due to the disintegration and suspension of the CuOx NWs. This highlights the PNS’s stability in water and its potential for subsequent micropollutant removal and disinfection applications. According to inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis, the Cu²⁺ ion concentration increased slightly from 0.26 to 0.95 ppb mg⁻¹ PNS after ultrasonication (Supplementary Fig. S19). Meanwhile, total organic carbon (TOC) analysis revealed only a marginal rise in organic content, corresponding to 0.026 ppb mg⁻¹ PNS (Supplementary Fig. S20). In addition, ion chromatography (IC) detected an F⁻ ion concentration of 0.0043 ppb mg⁻¹ PNS (Supplementary Fig. S21). These results collectively confirm that both Cu²⁺ leaching from the PNS and potential PTFE release into the solution are minimal, underscoring the structural stability and chemical robustness of the PNS during operation.

To further elucidate the influence of PTFE coating on Cu2+ ion release behavior, the Cu²⁺ concentration normalized by specific surface area was calculated from ICP-MS measurements of the Cu sponge and PNS samples after 5 and 60 min of ultrasonication (Supplementary Fig. S22). The normalized Cu²⁺ release rate per specific surface area was 0.87 ppm × gm−2 min−¹ for the Cu sponge and 0.021 ppm × gm−2 min⁻¹ for the PNS, representing an order-of-magnitude reduction in ion leaching. This result clearly demonstrates that the PTFE coating provides effective protection for the CuOₓ nanowire sponge, significantly suppressing Cu²⁺ ion release and confirming the enhanced chemical stability of the PNS. To further validate this observation, three control conditions were examined: (1) without PNS and without ultrasonication, (2) with PNS but without ultrasonication, and (3) without PNS but with ultrasonication. Under all control conditions, the Cu²⁺ concentration remained negligible ion release in the absence of the sample and ultrasonication system. Besides, in DI water, the Cu2+ ion is extremely low, at approximately 0.27 ppb.

The proposed mechanism for micropollutant degradation involves a synergistic interplay among electron accumulation facilitated by the PTFE layer and enhanced air diffusion enabled by the internal microcracks structure of the PNS (Supplementary Fig. S23). Initiated by ultrasonic waves, cavitation bubbles (CBs) are generated in the solution57. As these bubbles expand and collapse, they produce high-pressure microjets that continuously displace adsorbed water molecules at the PTFE-water interface58, triggering frequent contact electrification and electron transfer. Notably, the CEC reaction energy required for electron transfer at the H₂O/polymer and polymer/O₂ interfaces has been shown to decrease under high-pressure conditions21,25,39,50,59. The two main reaction paths for producing ROS via CEC are the water oxidation reaction (WOR) and the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). For ORR, CBs are initially formed and expanded until they reach a critical size. Upon collapse, these bubbles generate intense localized forces that expel water molecules from the PTFE surface. During this process, the interaction between the electron clouds of fluorine atoms in PTFE and oxygen atoms in water reduces the energy barrier for state transition, facilitating electron transfer from water to PTFE60. Consequently, an electron can transfer from the oxygen atom of a water molecule to the LUMO of PTFE49,61, temporarily generating a negatively charged PTFE* state. At this stage, ions accumulate on the PTFE* surface, forming an electric double-layer (EDL) structure consisting of a diffuse layer and a Stern layer. The PTFE* then donates electrons to oxygen in water, leading to the formation of •O₂⁻ and restoring PTFE to its uncharged state.

Meanwhile, the water oxidation reaction (WOR) occurs as electron-depleted water molecules interact with neighboring water molecules, initiating rapid proton transfer and leading to the formation of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) and •OH via Grotthuss mechanism62 (H₂O⁺ + H₂O → •OH + H₃O⁺). Subsequently, two •OH radicals are combined to form H₂O₂ (•OH + •OH → H2O2)37,63. Additionally, •O₂⁻ can contribute to H₂O₂ generation through the reaction (•O₂⁻ + e⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂)64,65. Upon the violent collapse of CBs, the resulting microjet force propels water onto the PTFE surface, initiating the next cycle of solid–liquid contact. Additionally, O₂ can be further supplied by the air within the PNS sample through microcracks on the PTFE surface66, leading to a pronounced enhancement in H₂O₂ generation. Table S3 summarizes four possible pathways for H₂O₂ generation. H₂O₂ formation via direct oxygen reduction may contribute to the overall production to some extent. However, as demonstrated in a previous study63, H₂O₂ generation was almost completely suppressed when argon gas (Ar) was bubbled through the solution, indicating that O₂ acts as the essential reactant under ambient conditions. Therefore, H₂O₂ formation through the water oxidation pathway is considered negligible in the contact electrocatalytic system.

Micropollutants removal via PNS-CEC

PNS was put into the ultrasonic bath to prepare an ultrasound-assisted PNS-CEC system. To elucidate its redox degradation performance, this system was utilized for the degradation of various contaminants, including dyes (crystal violet (CV) and methyl blue (MB)), waste tire leachate (6PPD-quinone), and the reduction of heavy metal ions (chromium ions, Cr⁶⁺). All the prepared solutions were subjected to ultrasonication at a frequency of 37 kHz and a power of 0.1 W/cm3. Dye degradation samples were collected at ultrasonication intervals of 2, 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min from feed solutions initially containing 5 ppm of dye, as shown in Fig. 3a. These samples were analyzed using UV–vis spectroscopy, which revealed a progressive decline in the characteristic absorbance peaks of CV and MB with increasing ultrasonication time. Notably, the absorbance approached near zero at 60 min, indicating substantial degradation of both dyes. After 60 min, more than 99% of CV and MB were removed. The optical images in Fig. 3a further illustrate this degradation, showing a significant discoloration of the dye solutions, with CV transitioning from its original purple hue to transparent and MB changing from light blue to transparent. Furthermore, the PNS exhibited only slight adsorption of both CV and MB, as indicated by the absence of visible color change in the samples (Supplementary Figs. S24 and S25) and by the minimal concentration decrease under the PNS-only condition (2.13% for CV and 7.84% for MB after 60 min). This minor change is attributed to weak physical interactions between dye molecules and the CuOx surface, in line with previous studies reporting low adsorption affinity of CuO-based materials67. These results confirm that effective removal of CV and MB arises from the synergistic effect of PNS and ultrasonication (PNS-CEC), rather than from adsorption by the PNS alone.

6PPD-quinone, a recently identified emerging micropollutant derived from waste tires, is a widely used antioxidant and antiozonant in rubber manufacturing. Upon tire disposal, it leaches into aquatic environments, posing significant toxicity risks to aquatic organisms68. Ultra performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) analysis revealed that 2 ppm of 6PPD-quinone could be degraded within 10 min (Fig. 3b). Additionally, 5 ppm of Cr6+ was reduced to Cr3+ after 60 min, as observed through UV–vis spectroscopy (Fig. 3c). Control experiments were conducted under three different conditions: (1) without PNS and without ultrasonication, (2) with PNS but without ultrasonication, and (3) without PNS but with ultrasonication. The degradation performance observed under all these control conditions was negligible compared with that of the PNS operated under ultrasonication, underscoring the crucial role of the integrated PNS and ultrasonication system in enabling efficient catalytic activity (Supplementary Figs. S25 and S26). DI water was also examined using UV–vis spectroscopy, and the results showed no significant changes or characteristic peaks in the spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S27). In addition, ICP-MS analysis detected only 0.27 ppb of Cu²⁺ in the DI water (Supplementary Fig. S19), while UPLC-MS/MS measurements showed no detectable signals. These results collectively confirm that DI water itself exerts no measurable influence on the catalytic performance tests. To evaluate the capability of the PNS to simultaneously degrade multiple micropollutants, a mixed solution containing 5 ppm CV, 5 ppm MB, and 5 ppm Cr⁶⁺ was prepared. The results, presented in Supplementary Fig. S28, demonstrate that all micropollutants were effectively removed or reduced after 150 min of treatment, confirming the applicability of the PNS system for simultaneous degradation of organic dyes and inorganic contaminants. Tables S4–S6 list a detailed performance comparison between this study and previously reported methods employing piezocatalysis, photocatalysis, and CEC with other materials. The results demonstrate that the approach presented in this work achieves over 99% removal rate and relatively shorter time for multiple micropollutants, including dye, heavy metal, and 6PPD-quinone.

To clarify the respective roles of the CuOx NWs and the PTFE coating, the removal efficiencies of CV, MB, and 6PPD-quinone, as well as the Cr⁶⁺ reduction performance, were evaluated using two reference samples: (1) CuOx NW sponge and (2) PTFE-coated Cu sponge (PTFE@Cu). As shown in Supplementary Figs. S26, S29, and S30, using the CuOx NW sponge alone resulted in decreases in the degradation efficiencies of CV, MB, and 6PPD-quinone by 32%, 54%, and 59%, respectively, relative to those achieved by the PNS. The Cr⁶⁺ reduction efficiency also declined by 71%. Similarly, for the PTFE@Cu sponge, the degradation efficiencies of CV, MB, and 6PPD-quinone decreased by 20%, 44%, and 46%, respectively, while the Cr⁶⁺ reduction efficiency decreased by 52%. Besides, compared to the nanowire-assisted strategy, direct PTFE coating on the Cu sponge decreased PTFE deposition by 18%, thereby reducing the functional effectiveness of the material. These results collectively highlight the synergistic contributions of both the CuOx NW architecture and the PTFE coating in achieving optimal catalytic performance, emphasizing the importance of incorporating the nanowire structure before PTFE deposition.

We hypothesize that the high ROS generation rate (Fig. 1b) facilitates the degradation of multiple micropollutants. To identify the dominant ROS species involved, specific radical scavengers were individually introduced into the solution. The evolution of CV concentration in the presence of these scavengers, as shown in Fig. 3d, underscores the key reactive species involved in the degradation process. The quenching experiments reveal two key findings: (1) the addition of radical scavengers significantly reduces the efficiency of CV degradation, confirming the generation of ROS in the catalytic process; and (2) both •OH and superoxide radicals •O₂− play significant roles in facilitating the degradation of CV, with •OH being the dominant species in this process. Additionally, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Fig. 3e) further confirms the production of •O2− and •OH radicals. Two protocols were employed here: a 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) solution, as well as a solution containing both DMPO and methanol. Methanol was utilized to quench •OH radicals, enhancing the opportunities for •O2− to react with DMPO. No visible peak was measured during ultrasonication without the presence of PNS, as depicted by the top section of Fig. 3e, indicating no generation of •OH radicals. In contrast, the red curve indicated that quadruplet DMPO-•OH characteristic peaks were yielded when ultrasonication was applied in the presence of PNS. And sextuplet DMPO-•O2- peaks were not detected until introducing methanol (blue curve)21. The presence of •OH and •O₂⁻ was also evaluated using the terephthalic acid assay and the 2,3-bis[2-methyl-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl]−2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide (XTT) method, respectively (Fig. 3f, g). Both assays showed a time-dependent increase in ROS levels for the PNS sample, significantly exceeding those observed for the nonporous PTFE block. The generation of ROS also led to the formation of H2O238, the generation of H2O2 of PNS was confirmed through UV–vis spectroscopy, indicating that the H2O2 production rate is 20.2 ± 0.1 μmol h⁻¹ (Fig. 3h).

To elucidate how the polymer interfacial contact and oxygen accessibility through microcracks influence the PNS-based CEC system, three control experiments were conducted: (1) variation of the PTFE coating amount on the CuOx sponge, (2) ethanol prewetting to displace air trapped in PNS, and (3) fabrication of a nonporous PTFE block as a qualitative limiting control. The extent of PTFE coating has a pronounced influence on H₂O₂ generation. Increasing the PTFE concentration in the coating suspension resulted in greater PTFE deposition on the CuOx framework, which enhanced interfacial contact and led to increased H₂O₂ production (Supplementary Fig. S31). At low coating levels (2 coating layers), the expanded solid–liquid interface facilitated more efficient interfacial charge transfer and oxygen activation, resulting in elevated H₂O₂ yields. However, as the number of PTFE coating cycles increased from 2 (standard PNS) to 5, 10, and 15, the density of surface microcracks progressively decreased, as revealed by SEM imaging (Supplementary Fig. S32). Consistently, N₂ adsorption–desorption analysis revealed a reduction in specific surface area, indicating that the loss of microcracks corresponded to lower accessible catalytic surface (Supplementary Fig. S17). As a result, the H₂O₂ concentration declined with decreasing microcrack density. The reduction in ROS generation is therefore attributed to the loss of accessible active sites arising from reduced specific surface area, despite the increased PTFE coating mass (Supplementary Figs. S33and 34).

To further assess whether air diffusion through the microcrack structure of the PNS contributes to the ROS generation, PNS samples were pretreated by ethanol prewetting to displace air trapped within the porous structure. Compared with untreated PNS, the ethanol-pretreated samples exhibited a distinctly lower H₂O₂ concentration (Supplementary Fig. S35), indicating that air entrapment within the pores provides an additional contribution to ROS generation by enhancing oxygen availability. Finally, to examine an extreme condition in which both internal porosity and microcrack-mediated oxygen access are absent, a nonporous PTFE block was fabricated as a qualitative control. An acrylic substrate with dimensions identical to the PNS was laser cut, and a PTFE film was attached to its surface to eliminate internal porosity. As shown in Fig. 3h, this nonporous PTFE block exhibited the lowest H₂O₂ concentration among all samples, illustrating a conceptual boundary case in which both accessible catalytic surface area and oxygen availability are minimized. This result confirms that both specific surface area and oxygen availability are critical for ROS generation in this system. The overall proposed ROS generation mechanism is summarized in Fig. 3i.

Antibacterial performance of the PNS-CEC system

Waterborne pathogens remain a significant threat to public health, causing widespread illness. Although disinfection methods, such as chlorination69, ultraviolet treatment70, and ozonation71 have improved water quality, their reliance on strong oxidants and harsh conditions contributes to a high carbon footprint and potential health risks, including carcinogenic byproducts72, and microbial resistance.

Electroporation is an effective method for disrupting cell membranes, facilitating the intracellular delivery of substances, such as genes or drugs12,73. In microbial disinfection, strong electric fields induce pore formation in cell membranes, leading to immediate cell death if the damage is extensive74. However, some bacteria exhibit resistance due to rigid outer walls, while others sustain sublethal damage and undergo self-repair, enabling potential regeneration. An alternative approach involves the in-situ generation of ROS, including •OH, H₂O₂, and •O₂⁻, which effectively eliminate pathogens without producing carcinogenic byproducts. However, this method is limited by its relatively slow disinfection rate, as ROS gradually degrades bacterial structures from the exterior inward over an extended period4.

The integration of electroporation and ROS disinfection via CEC enables efficient, residue-free water treatment. H₂O₂ produced via CEC weakens bacterial outer walls, enhancing susceptibility to electroporation. Simultaneously, the strong electric field induced by CEC facilitates electroporation, allowing bacteria to be punctured while exposed to •OH, which oxidizes nucleic acids and degrades cell structures, ensuring complete bacterial destruction from the inside out75. Here, the disinfection performance of PNS was evaluated in a batch reactor using Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) as model bacteria, representing Gram-negative and Gram-positive species, respectively. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thicker peptidoglycan layer, whereas Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane absent in Gram-positive species76. Disinfection was conducted under three control experiments: (1) in the absence of both the PNS and ultrasonication, (2) in the presence of ultrasonication but without the PNS, and (3) with the PNS present but without ultrasonication. The as-prepared bacterial suspension was ultrasonicated at a frequency of 37 kHz and a power of 0.1 W/cm3. Figure 4a–c present the kinetic disinfection results, showing that in the batch reactor, both E. coli and S. aureus were eliminated by over 99% within 5 min when PNS was combined with ultrasonication. In contrast, the effect of disinfection was negligible in all control experiments. A control experiment using a bacterial solution containing only 0.1 ppm Cu²⁺ ions was also conducted to verify that the observed antibacterial effect was not caused by Cu²⁺ ion leaching. The test showed negligible disinfection activity, confirming that the synergistic action of ultrasonication and the PNS is essential for achieving effective antibacterial performance (Supplementary Fig. S36).

The disinfection efficiency showed a gradual increase with the power level, rising from 30% energy level to 100% energy level (Supplementary Fig. S37). As CEC is initiated through the growth and rupture of cavitation bubbles, a process facilitated by ultrasound power, it enhances the formation of such bubbles27,32. When larger ultrasonic power induces a higher number of cavitation bubbles and energy, it consequently boosts the disinfection rate. This trend is further verified by the measured H₂O₂ concentrations, which are increased correspondingly with the applied ultrasonic energy (Supplementary Fig. S38). Additionally, different PTFE loadings were applied in PNS by varying the concentration of the PTFE coating solution (60 wt%, 30 wt%, and 15 wt%). The results indicate that lower PTFE concentrations result in reduced disinfection performance (Supplementary Figs. S39–S41). This decrease is likely due to the reduction of the effective contact area between PTFE and water. The disinfection performance of the nonporous PTFE block was also evaluated (Supplementary Fig. S42). Complete bacterial inactivation was achieved after 30 min of treatment, highlighting the crucial role of air diffusion in accelerating the disinfection process. Pores on the membranes of E. coli and S. aureus were visualized by SEM following fixation of both untreated and PNS-treated samples (Fig. 4d). After disinfection, the bacterial cells exhibited visible damage, with ruptured and fragmented membranes. Additionally, small pore structures were observed on the bacterial surfaces, with some bacteria showing multiple pores. Dye staining experiments further confirmed that electroporation likely led to cell death (Supplementary Fig. S43). Table S7 and Supplementary Data 2 summarize the performance of previously reported methods for disinfection. As a comparison, this work demonstrates over 99% disinfection in a significantly shorter time. The integration of electroporation and ROS disinfection induced by PNS is illustrated in Fig. 4e. This figure highlights how the synergistic effect of electrical disruption and oxidative stress contributes to effective microbial inactivation of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. These findings provide valuable insights into the electrostatic behavior of the system and highlight its potential applications in water treatment technologies.

Scalability and reusability of PNS

In the context of practical industrial wastewater treatment using CEC, evaluating the scalability and reusability of the catalyst is crucial. We envision a large-scale system suitable for continuous flow processing of industrial wastewater, as shown in Fig. 5a–d and Supplementary Fig. S44, demonstrating the feasibility of scaling up this catalytic strategy. As more PNS are incorporated into the reactor, disinfection performance improves, achieving over 99% bacteria removal within 3 min. However, due to the nonuniform energy distribution within the ultrasonication tank (Supplementary Fig. S45), optimizing the number and spatial arrangement of transducers is essential to achieve uniform ROS generation and thereby support the scalability of the PNS CEC system.

To quantitatively evaluate the long-term treatment capability of a single PNS material, a cycling experiment was conducted over 60 consecutive disinfection cycles in a batch reactor. As shown in Fig. 5e, a single PNS sample (0.322 g) effectively treated more than 120 mL of contaminated water while maintaining stable disinfection efficiency without any noticeable decline in performance throughout repeated use. Furthermore, the PNS exhibited no significant decrease in CV degradation performance after ten cycles, maintaining over 98% removal efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S46). These results collectively indicate the excellent reusability and long-term stability of the PNS material.

For effective wastewater treatment using PNS in CEC, both the degradation performance of organic contaminants and the durability of PNS must be considered. No noticeable coloration or morphological changes were observed by the naked eye or SEM analysis before and after the reaction (Supplementary Fig. S47). Furthermore, elemental mapping images confirmed that the PTFE composition remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S48). In addition, XPS analysis was performed, and the spectra exhibited identical characteristic peaks in both cases, indicating that the chemical composition and surface states of the PNS remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S49). These findings indicate that PNS do not participate in the reaction during the degradation process, and their physical and chemical properties remain stable.

Evaluation of the PNS-CEC system for real water applications

To evaluate the effectiveness of the PNS disinfection system in natural water environments, we conducted disinfection tests using river, lake, and stagnant water sources located on or near the campus of National Taiwan University. The initial bacterial concentrations of these real water samples are summarized in Table S8. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, b, the system achieved a microbial inactivation efficiency exceeding 99%. The PNS, activated via ultrasonication, demonstrates characteristics of a truly universal, practically viable, and environmentally friendly water disinfection system. A comprehensive cost analysis was performed to evaluate the economic feasibility of the PNS fabrication process. The preparation cost of the PNS sample was estimated to be approximately 37.4 USD per kilogram (detailed calculations are provided in the Supporting Information, Note 3 and Tables S9–S11). The analysis revealed that material expenses are the dominant cost contributor (~98%), primarily due to the Cu sponge and PTFE solution. Energy and capital costs are negligible on a per-kilogram basis, owing to the large furnace capacity and relatively low energy demand. Labor and maintenance costs represent minor contributions but may increase if the batch size is reduced or furnace efficiency declines. Compared with conventional water treatment technologies (Table S12), the PNS-CEC system offers a broad pollutant treatment spectrum while relying on non-noble metal materials and operating without external oxidants or chemical additives. Although mechanical excitation via ultrasonication is required, such equipment is widely accessible. These characteristics indicate that the PNS-CEC system has the potential to serve as a cost-competitive alternative to conventional water treatment approaches.

Discussion

In this study, we leverage a CEC-based approach that enables the disinfection of biological contaminants and degradation of multiple micropollutants, including dyes, heavy metals, and the emerging contaminant 6PPD-quinone. The superior performance of the ultrasound-assisted PNS-CEC system is attributed to the efficient generation of ROS by well-dispersed PNS-based catalysts, with an H₂O₂ production rate higher than previously reported CEC systems. The PNS exhibits excellent dispersion in aqueous solutions, maintaining high catalytic efficiency without compromising active surface area. Beyond its activity, the system offers practical advantages, such as ease of recovery, scalability, and robust reusability, retaining performance across multiple operational cycles. Notably, its effectiveness has been validated in real-world water treatment scenarios, where it successfully removes microbial contaminants, showcasing its potential for practical and sustainable water purification. Overall, this study establishes a paradigm for the rational design of highly efficient CEC catalysts, providing valuable insight into multiple pollutant degradation strategies. It offers a powerful tool for improving access to clean water, while also guiding future research in CEC, particularly in catalytic design and related strategies to combat the global water contamination crisis.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

The Cu sponge, sourced from Xiamen Tmax Battery Equipments Limited, has a density of 1800 g/m², a pore size of 90 pores per inch (PPI), and a thickness of 3 mm. 60 wt% polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) solution, 37 wt% hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution, methyl blue, crystal violet, potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), 6PPD-quinone, and 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. According to the supplier’s specification, the PTFE solution consists of PTFE dispersed in water. Tert-butanol and p-benzoquinone were obtained from Fisher Scientific. Agar and Luria Bertani (LB) broth were purchased from FocusBio and FGbio, respectively.

Growth of CuOx NWs on Cu sponge

CuOx NWs were grown on Cu sponge using a thermal oxidation process. Before growth, the Cu sponges were cut into 0.7 × 0.7 cm². The sponges were sequentially cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and DI water to remove surface impurities. Subsequently, they were immersed in 1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) for 10 min to remove the oxide layer, followed by thorough washing with DI water. Finally, the sponges were heated in air at 500 °C for 5 h to facilitate nanowire growth. The samples were labeled as CuOx NW sponge.

Preparation of PNS

Two milliliters of 60 wt% PTFE solution was poured into the beaker, and the CuOx NW sponge was immersed in the solution for 1 min. The sample was then dried at 120 °C for 15 min, followed by heating in air at 350 °C for another 15 min. PTFE-coated PNS samples with concentrations of 30 wt% and 15 wt% were prepared using the same procedure. The same method was also applied to fabricate samples with different numbers of PTFE coating cycles (5, 10, and 15 cycles).

Characterization

Surface morphologies and fluorine (F) atom distribution were examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, NovaTM NanoSEM 230) equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The morphology of a CuOx NW was observed using transmission electron microscopy (JEOL JEM-F200 electron microscope). The particle size distribution of the PTFE solution was analyzed with a Laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Coulter LS230, USA). Surface functional groups were identified by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, PerkinElmer, C123672), while chemical bonding was analyzed through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Theta Probe). Surface potential measurements were conducted using amplitude-modulated Kelvin probe force microscopy (AM-KPFM, Bruker ICON). Cu2+ leaching concentration was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7700e).

Preparation of bacterial solutions

E. coli K-12 DH5α and S. aureus ATCC 29213 bacterial cells were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C for 16 h in an incubator with continuous shaking at 5 × g. After incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 3075 × g for 10 min to remove the LB medium from the supernatant. The bacterial pellets were then diluted in PBS to achieve optical densities of 0.06 and 0.3 at 670 nm. Next, the bacterial solutions were diluted with PBS to prepare the concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/ml for antibacterial studies.

Antibacterial experiment

Control samples of bacteria were not given any treatment. In the US group, bacteria were treated directly with US without PNS, and the PNS group did not receive any ultrasonication. In the US + PNS group, the PNS was kept inside the bacteria solution and stimulated with US, simultaneously. After each treatment, bacterial solutions (aliquots of 100 µL) were collected at different time points, like 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min, and spread on solid LB-agar plates, and incubated overnight at 37 °C for colony growth. After incubation, the photographs of the plates were taken, and bacterial colonies were counted using ImageJ software for quantitative analysis of antibacterial activity. Dye degradation experiment

Aqueous solutions of crystal violet (CV) and methyl blue (MB) were prepared at a concentration of 5 ppm. The PNS filter was then immersed in each solution, and the samples were subjected to ultrasonication. Treated dye solutions were collected at specific time intervals of 2, 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. The dye concentrations were subsequently analyzed using UV–vis spectroscopy (JASCO V-670).

6-PPD-quinone degradation experiment

Two ppm 6PPD-quinone solution was prepared, and the sample was immersed in the solution. Under ultrasonication, the treated solutions were collected at intervals of 10, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180 min. The 6PPD-quinone concentrations were subsequently analyzed using UPLC-MS/MS.

Heavy metal reduction experiment

A 5 ppm Cr6+ solution was prepared, and the sample was immersed in the solution. Under ultrasonication, the treated solution was collected at intervals of 10, 30, 90, 150, 210, and 270 min. The heavy metal concentrations were subsequently analyzed using UV–vis spectroscopy.

H2O2 concentration measurement by the potassium permanganate method

In this method, H₂O₂ reacts with potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) in a redox reaction under acidic conditions, during which the characteristic purple-red color of KMnO₄ gradually fades. The concentration of H₂O₂ can be quantified by measuring the change in absorbance before and after the reaction, based on the decolorization of KMnO₄.

Firstly, a stock solution of H₂O₂ (11.66 M) was diluted to prepare standard solutions with concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm, each with a final volume of 50 mL. To prepare the KMnO₄ reaction solution, 5 mL of 2.5 M sulfuric acid and 100 μL of 0.05 M KMnO₄ were added to 44.7 mL of deionized (DI) water and stirred for 5 min. From this solution, 3 mL was taken to measure the absorbance in triplicate for the before-reaction sample (control sample).

Subsequently, 3 mL from the remaining KMnO₄ solution was mixed with 150 μL of each standard H₂O₂ solution (starting with 1 ppm), and the absorbance was measured in triplicate for the after-reaction sample. This procedure was repeated for all other H₂O₂ standards (5, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm). The absorbance at the characteristic wavelength of 525 nm was considered for each sample to construct a standard calibration curve.

To determine the amount of H₂O₂ generated by the PNS and nonporous PTFE blocks, each sample was immersed in 10 mL of DI water in a centrifuge tube and subjected to ultrasonication for varying durations (1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min). At each time point, 150 μL of the sample was collected and mixed with 3 mL of the KMnO₄ reaction solution, and the absorbance was measured in triplicate. The corresponding H₂O₂ concentration at each time point was calculated using the standard calibration curve.

Scavenger test

The active species during the 5 ppm CV degradation reaction at 30 min were investigated by additionally dissolving 0.1 mM trapping agents, including ter-butanol, p-benzoquinone, which could capture •OH, and•O2−, respectively. The absorbance of the solution was measured with a UV–vis spectroscopy.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) analysis

The generation of •OH and •O2− radicals in CEC was analyzed using EPR at ambient temperature. To capture these radicals, DMPO and DMPO/methanol were employed as trapping agents. The experiments followed the above-mentioned degradation procedure, with the only modification by replacing the solution with a 100 mM aqueous DMPO solution (with an additional 1 mM methanol for •O2− detection). After 10 min of ultrasonication, the samples were analyzed using a Bruker EPR-plus spectrometer (Germany). Measurements were conducted in the X-Band (9.842442 GHz) with a 1.25 G amplitude modulation, 31.88 mW microwave power, 100 kHz modulation frequency, and a conversion time of 62.5 ms. The time constant was set to 0.01 ms.

Detection of •OH using terephthalic acid assay

For •OH detection, a 0.1 M NaOH solution was first prepared by dissolving 0.4 g of NaOH in 100 mL of deionized (DI) water. A 3 mM terephthalic acid (TA) solution was then prepared by dissolving 0.0498 g of TA in 100 mL of the NaOH solution. To evaluate •OH generation, the PNS and PTFE block were immersed in DI water within centrifuge tubes and subjected to ultrasonication for varying durations (1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min). At each designated time point, aliquots were collected and mixed with the TA solution in a 1:2 volumetric ratio (TA: sample). The resulting mixtures were analyzed by measuring fluorescence intensity at 425 nm, enabling the quantification of •OH radicals generated under each condition.

Detection of •O2− using XTT assay

To quantify •O2−, a solution of XTT (2,3-bis [2-methyl-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl]−2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide) was prepared by dissolving 0.033 g of XTT powder in 10 mL of DMSO to obtain a 5 mM solution. Further, the PNS and PTFE blocks were immersed in DI water in a centrifuge tube and subjected to ultrasonication for varying durations (1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min). At each time point, the samples were collected and mixed with XTT solution in a 1:2 ratio (XTT: sample). The absorbance was measured at 475 nm to detect the presence of •O2− in each sample.

Total organic carbon (TOC) method

TOC concentrations were measured using a TOC analyzer (Aurora 1030 TOC Analyzer, O.I. Analytical, USA) equipped with an autosampler (Model 1088, O.I. Analytical, USA). Calibration was performed using different concentrations (0.025, 0.5, 1, 5 and 10 ppm) of standard potassium biphthalate (KHP) solutions prior to each measurement. Further, TOC was measured in DI water, 2 ppm 6PPD-quinone, 5 ppm CV, and 5 ppm MB to assess organic carbon level following treatment. Before measurement, each sample was subjected to four different treatments: control (no treatment), US, PNS, and US + PNS. All treatments were carried out for 60 min under identical conditions.

Ion chromatograph (IC) method

Fluoride ion concentration was determined using an IC system (Model: Sykam S 155 Ion Chromatograph, Sykam GmbH, Eresing, Germany), equipped with an S 5250 Sample Injector and a conductivity detector. Calibration was done using Merck fluoride standard solution of different concentrations, such as 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 ppb. Further, fluoride ion leaching from PNS was measured in DI water under various treatment conditions. The leaching experiments in DI water were subjected to three treatment types (US, PNS, and US + PNS). Each treatment was conducted for 0, 5, 10, and 60 min to assess the fluoride ion release in different treatment durations.

Boundary conditions follow the actual tank configuration with eight circular source patches on the bottom (diameter 3.8 cm each). The total acoustic power 720 W was distributed uniformly as 90 W per source. Each source was implemented as exactly one of: normal velocity (vn = v₀), normal acceleration (an = a₀), or pressure amplitude (p = p₀), derived from Z = ρc. For c = 1418 m s−1, the per-source amplitudes are p₀ = 4.74 × 10⁵ Pa (0.474 MPa), v₀ = p₀/(ρc) = 0.335 m s−1, and a₀ = ωv₀ = 7.78 × 10⁴ m s−2 (for c = 1500 m s−1: p₀ = 4.88 × 10⁵ Pa, v₀ = 0.325 m s−1, a₀ = 7.56 × 10⁴ m s−2). The side walls and the non-emitting bottom are sound-hard

The free liquid surface is modeled as sound-soft (p = 0) to represent a pressure-release boundary; a plane-wave radiation condition may be used instead to reduce spurious reflections. Energy-distribution maps are presented as |I| (W m−2) and E (J m−3) across the volume, with averages taken over user-defined subregions where needed.

Measurement of TENG voltage and charge output

For measurement of voltage and charge output, a 4× cm2 single electrode TENG device was prepared using PTFE sheet. The device was stimulated by ultrasound in DI water at different ultrasound energy levels. The voltage and charge output were measured by an electrometer (Keithley, 6514).
