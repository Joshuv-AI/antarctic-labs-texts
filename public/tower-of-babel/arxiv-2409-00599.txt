An unexpected property of 𝐠 \mathbf{g} -vectors for rank 3 mutation-cyclic quivers Thanks: The authors were supported by the University of Alabama, Korea Institute for Advanced Study, and the NSF grant DMS 2042786 and DMS 2302620.
Jihyun Lee, Kyungyong Lee

Abstract
. Let Q Q be a rank 3 mutation-cyclic quiver. It is known that every 𝐜 \mathbf{c} -vector of Q Q is a solution to a quadratic equation of the form ∑ i = 1 3 x i 2 + ∑ 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 3 ± q i ​ j x i x j = 1 , \sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\pm q_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=1, where q i ​ j q_{ij} is the number of arrows between the vertices i i and j j in Q Q . A similar property holds for 𝐜 \mathbf{c} -vectors of any acyclic quiver. In this paper, we show that 𝐠 \mathbf{g} -vectors of Q Q enjoy an unexpected property. More precisely, every 𝐠 \mathbf{g} -vector of Q Q is a solution to a quadratic equation of the form ∑ i = 1 3 x i 2 + ∑ 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 3 p i ​ j ​ x i ​ x j = 1 , \sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}p_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=1, where p i ​ j p_{ij} is the number of arrows between the vertices i i and j j in another quiver P P obtained by mutating Q Q .



1.
Introduction

In the context of cluster algebras,
\mathbf{g}
-vectors, along with
\mathbf{c}
-vectors, serve as fundamental objects. The study of
\mathbf{g}
-vectors is crucial for understanding the properties of cluster variables.

When a quiver
Q
is acyclic, it is known that the positive
\mathbf{c}
-vectors of
Q
are real Schur roots, that is, the dimension vectors of indecomposable rigid modules over
Q

[
1
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
. In particular, every
\mathbf{c}
-vector of
Q
is a real root, hence is a solution to a quadratic equation of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\pm q_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=1,

where
n
is the number of vertices of
Q
and
q_{ij}
is the number of arrows between the vertices
i
and
j
in
Q
. It is also shown
[
2
,
9
]
that the same property holds for
\mathbf{c}
-vectors of rank 3 mutation-cyclic quivers.

For quivers of rank at most 2, explicit formulas for
\mathbf{g}
-vectors are well-understood. This paper focuses on the
\mathbf{g}
-vectors of rank 3 mutation-cyclic quivers.

Let
Q
be a rank 3 mutation-cyclic quiver.
Our main theorem states that for any reduced mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
from
Q
whose first mutation occurs at vertex
k\in\{1,2,3\}
, each column vector
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
in the
G^{\boldsymbol{w}}
-matrix, referred to as a
\mathbf{g}
-vector, satisfies the quadratic equation

q(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+|b_{12}^{[k]}|x_{1}x_{2}+|b_{23}^{[k]}|x_{2}x_{3}+|b_{31}^{[k]}|x_{3}x_{1}=1,

where the coefficients are determined by the number of arrows between vertices
i
and
j
in the quiver
Q^{[k]}
obtained by mutating
Q
at the vertex
k
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
n
be a positive integer. To a quiver
Q
with vertices labeled
1,2,\cdots,n,
we can associate an
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrix, i.e. the matrix is in correspondence with a quiver
Q.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
B=[b_{ij}]
be an
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrix. This
B
will be called an
exchange matrix
. This matrix determines a quiver
Q
as follows:
b_{ij}>0
if and only if
Q
has
b_{ij}
arrows from vertex
i
to vertex
j
. Sometimes we write
B=B(Q)
.

Then, we extend the notion of mutation from quivers to (exchange) matrices.

Definition 2.2
.

(Matrix mutation)

We review the definition of matrix mutations, following
[
3
]
.
Now assume that
M=[m_{ij}]
is an
2n\times n
matrix of integers. Let
\mathcal{I}:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be the set of indices. For
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
,
i_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
, we define the matrix
M^{\boldsymbol{w}}=[m_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}]
inductively: the initial matrix is
M
for
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[\,]
, and assuming we have
M^{\boldsymbol{w}}
, define the matrix
M^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}=[m_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}]
for
k\in\mathcal{I}
with
{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]:=[i_{i},i_{2},\dots,i_{t},k]

by

(2.3)

m_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}=\begin{cases}-m_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}&\text{if $i=k$ or $j=k$},\\
m_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}+\mathrm{sgn}(m_{ik}^{\boldsymbol{w}})\,\max(m_{ik}^{\boldsymbol{w}}m_{kj}^{\boldsymbol{w}},0)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{sgn}(a)\in\{1,0,-1\}
is the signature of
a
. The matrix
M^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}
is called the
mutation of
M^{\boldsymbol{w}}
at index (or label)
k
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]
are called
mutation sequences
, and
n
is the
rank
.
If the mutation sequence
[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
satisfies
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for all
j\in\{1,...,n-1\}
, then it is said to be
reduced
.

Definition 2.4
.

(
C
-matrix,
\mathbf{c}
-vector and
G
-matrix,
\mathbf{g}
-vector)

We also recall the definitions of the
\mathbf{c}
-vector and
\mathbf{g}
-vector as introduced in
[
3
,
4
]
. Let
B
be an
n\times n
initial exchange matrix and
C
be an initial
C
-matrix. Consider the
2n\times n
matrix
M=\begin{pmatrix}B\\
C\end{pmatrix}
and
a mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},\dots,i_{t}]
. After the mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
, i.e., at the indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{t}
consecutively, we obtain
\begin{pmatrix}B^{\boldsymbol{w}}\\
C^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{pmatrix}
. Write their entries as
B^{\boldsymbol{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix},C^{\boldsymbol{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{c}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{w}}&\cdots&\mathbf{c}_{n}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix},
where
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
are the column vectors. The resulting column vectors
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
are called
\mathbf{c}
-vectors
of
B
for
{\boldsymbol{w}},
and the matrix
C^{\boldsymbol{w}}
is called a
C
-matrix
of
B
for
{\boldsymbol{w}}.
And
G^{\boldsymbol{w}}=((C^{\boldsymbol{w}})^{-1})^{T}
matrix is called a
G
-matrix
of
B
for
{\boldsymbol{w}}.
From the matrix
G^{\boldsymbol{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{w}}&\cdots&\mathbf{g}_{n}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\end{bmatrix},
each column vector
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
is called a
\mathbf{g}
-vector
of
B
for any
{\boldsymbol{w}}.

Although this definition of the
G
-matrix is clearly established, it requires computing the inverse of a matrix, which can be cumbersome. Additionally, for proving our main theorem, this approach is less convenient, as it complicates the recursive relationships between
G
-matrices during mutations. Therefore, following the notation developed in
[
8
]
, we adopt a modified approach to defining
G
-matrix mutation that better suits our needs.

Following
[
8
]
, we first extend the notation
[b]_{+}=\text{max}(b,0)
to matrices, writing
[B]_{+}
for the matrix obtained from
B
by applying the operation
b\mapsto[b]_{+}
to all entries of
B.
For matrix index
l,
we denote by
B^{\bullet l}
the matrix obtained from
B
by replacing all entries outside of the
l
-th column with zeros; the matrix
B^{l\bullet}
is defined similarly using the
l
-th row instead of the column.

Note that the operations
B\mapsto[B]_{+}
and
B\mapsto B^{\bullet l}
commute with each other, making the notation
[B]_{+}^{\bullet l}
(and
[B]_{+}^{l\bullet}
) straightforward.
Using this formalism, we can rephrase the sign-coherence condition
[
5
]
for a
C
-matrix as follows :

(2.5)

\text{For every $j,$ there exists the sign}\quad\varepsilon_{j}(C)=\pm 1\quad\text{such that}\quad[-\varepsilon_{j}(C)C]_{+}^{\bullet j}=0.

For each mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
, we then inductively define the
C,G
-matrix of
B
as follows. The following definition provides a recursive method to find the
C
and
G
-matrices by using the previous
C
and
G
-matrices.

Definition 2.6
.

(C,G-matrix mutation)

Let
C
and
G
be the initial
C
and
G
-matrices, both of which are
n\times n
identity matrices. Given a mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
, suppose that
C^{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
G^{\boldsymbol{w}}
are defined. We aim to define
C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}
and
G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}
such that
\mu_{k}(B^{\boldsymbol{w}},C^{\boldsymbol{w}},G^{\boldsymbol{w}})=(B^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]},C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]},G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}).

Then, under the assumption that
C
satisfies (
2.5
), we have

(2.7)

C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}=C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}(J_{k}+[\varepsilon_{k}(C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})B^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}]_{+}^{k\bullet}),\quad G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}=G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}(J_{k}+[-\varepsilon_{k}(C^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})B^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}]_{+}^{\bullet k}),

where we use the notation
J_{k}
to denote the diagonal matrix obtained from the identity matrix by replacing the
(k,k)
-entry with
-1.

3.
Rank 3 mutation-cyclic quivers

Definition 3.1
.

(Mutation-cyclic)
We say that a quiver
Q
is
mutation-cyclic
if for any sequence of mutations
{\boldsymbol{w}}
applied to
Q
, the resulting quiver
Q^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
is cyclic. That is, no matter how many times or in which order mutations are performed on
Q,
the quiver retains the property of being cyclic. Formally, let
\mu_{k}
denote the mutation at vertex
k.
Then
Q
is mutation cyclic if for any finite sequence of mutations
\mu_{i_{1}},\mu_{i_{2}},\ldots,\mu_{i_{t}}
applied to
Q,
the resulting quiver
\mu_{i_{t}}\cdots\mu_{i_{2}}\mu_{i_{1}}(Q)
is cyclic.

In order to state our theorem, we fix
Q
as a rank 3 mutation-cyclic quiver.

Definition 3.2
.

(
A
-matrix)

Let
B
be a
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrix.

The pseudo-Cartan companion
, or
A
-matrix, of
B
is the
3\times 3
symmetric matrix
A=[a_{ij}]
such that
a_{ii}=2
for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, and
a_{ij}=|b_{ij}|
for all
i\neq j.

Remark
.

In
[
10
]
, it is shown that for skew-symmetric cluster algebras, the c-vectors associated with an acyclic seed define a quasi-Cartan companion
1
1

1

The key difference between the pseudo-Cartan companion and the quasi-Cartan companion lies in the definition of the off-diagonal entries. In the quasi-Cartan companion, the off-diagonal entries
a_{ij}
satisfy the condition
|a_{ij}|=|b_{ij}|
for all
i\neq j
while in the pseudo-Cartan companion, they are given by
a_{ij}=|b_{ij}|
.
. Here, we needed to define the pseudo-Cartan companion, denoted by
A
, as a fundamental tool to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 3.3
.

(
A
-mutation)

Let
B
be a
3\times 3
exchange matrix corresponding to rank 3 mutation cyclic quiver.
For each mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
, we inductively define the
A
-matrix of
B
as follows.

Assume
A
is a pseudo-Cartan companion of the initial
B.
Let
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
be a mutation sequence. Suppose that
A^{\boldsymbol{w}}
is defined. We seek to define
A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}
such that
\mu_{k}(A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}.

For each
l\in\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{k\}
, we define a
3\times 3
matrix
D^{(k,l)}=\left[d_{rs}^{(k,l)}\right]
as follows:

d_{rs}^{(k,l)}=\begin{cases}a_{kl}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&\text{if $r=k,s=l$},\\
-1&\text{if $r=s=l$},\\
1&\text{if $r=s\neq l$},\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

These matrices
D^{(k,l)}
are used in the mutation formula to update
A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
to
A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}
. Specifically, after mutating at
k
, we have:

A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[k]}=(D^{(k,l)})^{T}A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}D^{(k,l)}.

We can now state our main results.
When a rank 3 mutation cyclic quiver is given by
Q,
let a reduced mutation sequence be
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
with
i_{r}\in\{1,2,3\}.

For simplicity, let
\mathbf{i}=i_{1},
the first mutation in a mutation sequence. Then after mutating at vertex
\mathbf{i}
, we will have a corresponding
B^{[\mathbf{i}]}=[b_{ij}^{[\mathbf{i}]}].

Theorem 3.1
.

We have
A^{\boldsymbol{w}}=(G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[m]})^{T}A^{[\mathbf{i}]}(G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[m]})
for any reduced mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
with length of at least 1, starting with
\mathbf{i}.

Corollary 3.2
.

Define a quadratic form

q(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}b_{ij}^{[\mathbf{i}]}x_{i}x_{j},

where
b_{ij}^{[\mathbf{i}]}>0
is the number of arrow between
i
and
j
in
\mu_{\mathbf{i}}(Q).

Then for any reduced mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[\mathbf{i},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}]
and for any
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the column vector
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
in the corresponding
G
-matrix is a solution to the following equation :

(3.4)

q(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}})=q\begin{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}\end{pmatrix}\\
=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+|b^{[\mathbf{i}]}_{12}|x_{1}x_{2}+|b^{[\mathbf{i}]}_{23}|x_{2}x_{3}+|b^{[\mathbf{i}]}_{31}|x_{3}x_{1}=1.

, we will check the case where
m=1
and
u\in\{2,3\}
.

LHS

A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\xrightarrow{\text{$\mu_{1}$}}A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}=(D^{(1,l)})^{T}A^{\boldsymbol{w}}D^{(1,l)}

As
l
is not equal to
m
by the Definition
3.3
,
l
could be either 2 or 3 in this case. Thus, we will have two types of
D
matrices, which are
D^{(1,2)},D^{(1,3)}
.

Type 1:

d_{rs}^{(1,2)}=\begin{cases}a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&\text{if $r=1,s=2$},\\
-1&\text{if $r=s=2$},\\
1&\text{if $r=s\neq 2$},\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}\quad\text{so,}\quad D^{(1,2)}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right].

Type 2:

d_{rs}^{(1,3)}=\begin{cases}a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&\text{if $r=1,s=3$},\\
-1&\text{if $r=s=3$},\\
1&\text{if $r=s\neq 3$},\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}\quad\text{so,}\quad D^{(1,3)}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{matrix}\right].

RHS
G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}\xrightarrow{\text{$\mu_{u}$}}G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1,u]}=G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}(J_{u}+[-sgn(\mathbf{c}_{u}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]})b_{1u}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}]_{+}E^{1u}).

Case
\underline{(m,u)=(1,2):}

Let
N_{1}
be
J_{2}+[-sgn(\mathbf{c}_{2}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]})b_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}]_{+}E^{12}.
Then,

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle=J_{2}+[-sgn(\mathbf{c}_{2}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]})b_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}]_{+}E^{12}

\displaystyle=J_{2}+|b_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}|E^{12}

\displaystyle=J_{2}+|-b_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}|E^{12}

\displaystyle=J_{2}+a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}E^{12}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]+\left(a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]

Case
\underline{(m,u)=(1,3):}

Let
N_{2}
be
J_{3}+[-sgn(\mathbf{c}_{3}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]})b_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}]_{+}E^{13}.
Then,

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle=J_{3}+[-sgn(\mathbf{c}_{3}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]})b_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}]_{+}E^{13}

\displaystyle=J_{3}+|b_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}|E^{13}=J_{3}+|-b_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}|E^{13}

\displaystyle=J_{3}+a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}E^{13}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]+\left(a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right].

Observe that

N_{1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]=D^{(1,2)}\quad\text{ and }\quad N_{2}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]=D^{(1,3)}.

Eventually, we will get
A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}
from both cases above :

\displaystyle(D^{(1,2)})^{T}A^{\boldsymbol{w}}D^{(1,2)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{1,3}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2&a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{1,3}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\

0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]=A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}.

\displaystyle(D^{(1,3)})^{T}A^{\boldsymbol{w}}D^{(1,3)}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2&a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2&a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&(a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})^{2}-2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\

a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&a_{12}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}a_{13}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-a_{23}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]=A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[1]}.

Similarly, The remaining cases, when

D^{(m,l)}=N.

By the induction,
4.1
and
4.2
, we can conclude that
A^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[m]}=(G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[m,u]})^{T}A^{\mathbf{i}}(G^{{\boldsymbol{w}}[m,u]})
