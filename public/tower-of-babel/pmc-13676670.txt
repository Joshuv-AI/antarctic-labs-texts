The Impact of Ambient Ozone Exposure on Respiratory Health in the U.S. Populations: A Systematic Review
Authors: Yoojin Cho, Yu Yu, Xinyu Zhang, Michael Kleeman, Michael Jerrett, Ninez A Ponce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOzone, a photochemically generated air pollutant, continues to pose a major concern for public health in the United States, with some 125 million people living in areas that exceed national ambient air quality standards set by the United States Environmental Protection Agency. To fully understand the potential impacts of ambient ozone exposure on respiratory health, we conducted a systematic review of United States epidemiologic and experimental studies published from 1980 to 2026, encompassing both acute and chronic exposure windows. This review focused on three key domains: (1) pulmonary function, (2) asthma-related symptoms and emergency department visits/hospitalizations, and (3) other respiratory-related morbidity and mortality. Findings across diverse study designs demonstrated adverse respiratory effects, with children, older adults, and individuals with pre-existing respiratory conditions being disproportionately affected. This review is among the first to comprehensively synthesize ozone-related respiratory health outcomes including pulmonary functions outcomes across both experimental and population-based observational studies—highlighting consistent decrements in lung function, even at concentrations below current regulatory standards. Additionally, this work incorporated ozone-associated school absenteeism, a sensitive yet underexamined indicator for pediatric respiratory health outcome. The observed associations across multiple outcomes and exposure durations underscored the importance of strengthening ambient air quality standards and prioritizing interventions toward high-risk populations. Introduction

Ground-level ozone (O3) is a secondary air pollutant produced predominantly by photochemical reactions involving nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in the presence of sunlight [1]. Anthropogenic sources, including traffic and industrial emissions, contribute substantially to these precursors. Additionally, wildfires—which are increasingly fueled by human-related climate change—also generate large quantities of precursor pollutants [2, 3]. In the United States (U.S.), O3 concentrations frequently exceed health-protective standards, especially during the warm season. Numerous studies have reported associations between O3 exposure and respiratory health outcomes, including asthma exacerbation and increased emergency department (ED) visits/hospitalizations, and respiratory-related mortality [4–6]. Notably, these associations have been observed even at O3 levels below the current 8-hour National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) of 70 parts per billion (ppb) [7–10]. Despite the expanding body of literature regarding the respiratory impacts of O3 exposure, no prior study has comprehensively synthesized findings across a broader spectrum including pulmonary function indicators such as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR), asthma-related health events and extending to broader respiratory-related morbidity and mortality. Additionally, although school absenteeism is a valuable indicator of children’s overall health and can help assess the broader impacts of O3 exposure among school-aged children, it has been largely overlooked in previous reviews.

This systematic review examined existing studies on the respiratory-related health impacts of O3exposure in the U.S. population. We focused on three major outcome domains: (1) pulmonary function, (2) asthma-related symptoms, ED visits/hospitalizations, and (3) other respiratory-related morbidity and mortality.

Study Method

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigor throughout the review process. We started a literature search using PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/) to identify studies examining the health impacts of O3 exposure, specifically in relation to respiratory outcomes including pulmonary functions, asthma-related symptoms, ED visits/hospitalizations, within the context of the U.S. Search terms combined multiple ozone-related keywords (e.g., “O3,” “ozone,” “trioxygen”) with a broad range of respiratory and clinical outcome terms (e.g., pulmonary function, asthma, respiratory symptoms, emergency department visits, and hospital admissions), and searches were restricted to U.S.-based epidemiological and experimental studies. (More details are provided in the Supplementary Materials). This review encompassed full-text articles published from January 1980 through August 2026. A total of 8,801 records were identified, including 4,957 from PubMed and 3,844 from Web of Science. After removing duplicates, we conducted a first-round screening based on titles and abstracts to exclude those irrelevant to the research question. Full texts of the remaining articles were assessed for eligibility based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Among several exclusion criteria, studies conducted outside the U.S. were excluded to ensure consistency with U.S.-specific O3 regulatory frameworks, exposure metrics, population characteristics, and healthcare contexts, thereby facilitating the synthesis of policy-relevant evidence on O3-related respiratory health impacts in the U.S. Additionally, 18 relevant articles were identified through reference checking of included studies that were not captured by the original search strategies. Following this process, a total of 401 articles were included in the final review (Fig. 1). Three authors conducted screening and eligibility assessments, independently classifying studies for inclusion or exclusion, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached to ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility.

PRISMA flow diagram of the search, screening, and selection process of studies on ozone exposure and respiratory health published between 1980 and 2026

To evaluate the methodological rigor of the studies included in this review, we performed a structured quality assessment using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools―a validated tool tailored to each study design by three authors independently. Any differences in appraisal were resolved through collective discussion, ensuring consistency and methodological rigor in the assessment. Final scores were determined only after agreement was achieved between authors. According to JBI critical appraisal tools, overall study quality was generally rated as low to moderate risk of bias across most outcome domains, indicating that the majority of included studies were methodologically acceptable. Relatively higher risk-of bias quality ratings were more frequently observed among human experimental studies assessing pulmonary function. Because no JBI tool is specifically tailored to controlled experimental designs, these studies were appraised using a modified version of the JBI randomized controlled trial checklist, which may have contributed to this higher risk of bias rating in this domain. Further details can be found in Supplementary materials and figures (Figure S1–S3).

Pulmonary Function

O3 exposure impacts on lung function have been well documented in human experiments and observational studies during the past several decades [11–20]. Pulmonary function indices, such as FEV₁, FVC and indicators of small-airway function which is particularly sensitive to O3 exposure―peak expiratory flow (PEF) and forced expiratory flow between the 25th and 75th percentile of FVC (FEF25–75), which is also known as maximum mid-expiratory flow (MMEF), usually offer sensitive and quantitative markers that reflect both early and clinically significant respiratory changes in response to O3 exposure. In addition to spirometric decrement, other effects such as increased airway reactivity and inflammation or injury including cell counts and cytokine levels such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-6 and neutrophil recruitment in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were also reported. These inflammatory markers serve as biologically plausible intermediates linking O3 exposure to clinical respiratory outcomes.

Human Experimental Studies

The most commonly used format before 2000 to evaluate the short-term or acute O3 exposure on lung function was human experimental study (Table 1 and Table S1). Human tests were usually conducted in an exposure chamber (also called chamber study) and involved healthy adult volunteers who were exposed to controlled O3 concentrations ranging from environmentally relevant levels to those exceeding current ambient standards, sometimes in combination with exercise protocols to simulate real-world ventilation rates. Lung function indices such as FEV₁ and FVC are commonly assessed pre- and post-exposure, alongside inflammation markers and symptoms. Some studies involved square wave exposures where O3 concentration was maintained at a constant value throughout the exposure period [21]. Others conducted triangular wave exposures, where O3 concentration gradually increased to a peak level and then reduced similarly in a stepwise fashion. Other measurements besides the O3 concentration protocol that have been manipulated in chamber studies include exercise (with/without), exposure duration, and exposure method (facemask vs. whole-body exposure in a chamber) [22]. Human experimental studies usually investigated different types of exposure regimens and included a control scenario where participants were exposed to filtered air (FA) with no O3.

Adverse lung function decrements have been consistently observed with controlled exposures to O3 of at least 0.08-ppm (Table 1). In a 1986 University of California (UC) Davis study with 10 highly trained endurance athletes, the participants were randomly exposed to FA, and 0.12-ppm, 0.18-ppm or 0.24-ppm O3 while performing 1-hour competitive exercise [23]. The researchers observed that 0.18- and 0.24-ppm exposures during one hour of heavy exercise were associated with FEV₁ reductions of 5.8–10.5% and FVC reductions of 7.8–9.9%, respectively. Similarly, in another study at the UC Berkeley consisting of 15 healthy young adult students, it was reported that O3 exposure was associated with a 0.5 L, 0.4 L, 0.5 L/s and 0.3 L/s decrease in FVC, FEV1, FEF25−75, FEF75, respectively [17]. In a study conducted at the University of Rochester, participants were exposed to 0.22-ppm O3 for 4 h, with exercise for 20 of every 30 min. Markers of airway inflammation, measured in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), specifically IL-6 and IL-8 were observed to be increased by up to 10-fold and 2-fold, respectively [24]. Similarly, in a Michigan study which exposed 10 adults (5 asthmatics and 5 healthy) to 0.2-ppm O3 observed significant increases in IL-6, IL-8 and other airway inflammatory indicators measured in BALF, but no difference was seen between baseline and postexposure FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC and FEF25−75, suggesting that airway inflammatory responses can occur independent of pulmonary function deficits [25].

As the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) continues to lower the primary O3 NAAQS, recent studies have focused on environmentally relevant concentrations to inform regulatory thresholds (Table 1). In a UC Davis study, researchers examined the lung function effects of time-dependent chamber exposures to O3 for 6.6 h in 30 healthy, nonsmoking young adults using six different experimental protocols: standard square-wave concentration profiles at 0-, 60- and 80-ppb O3 and triangular concentration profiles at 40, 60 and 80-ppb O3 [21]. Decrements in lung function with only 80-ppb exposure protocols were observed. Specifically, the average decrement in FEV1 was 6.1% and 7.0% relative to FA for the square-wave and triangular 80-ppb O3 exposures, respectively. A dose-dependent average decrement in FVC was also observed, with maximum FVC change of 4.9% for square-wave and 5.2% for triangular protocols at 80-ppb O3 relative to FA [26]. In another study, the researchers exposed 59 healthy young adults (19–35 years) to 0, and 0.06-ppm O3 for 6.6 h and found that the participants experienced a greater change in FEV1 and FVC immediately after exposure to 0.06-ppm O3 compared with FA (FEV1: -1.71 vs. -0.002; FVC: -2.32 vs. -1.13) [27]. Additionally, in a recent study conducted at the University of North Carolina, 38 healthy young adults aged 19–34 years without a history of smoking were exposed to clean air (CA) and 0.07-ppm O₃ for 6.6 h with intermittent moderate exercise. A decrease in FEV₁ was observed immediately after exposure to 0.07-ppm O₃ compared with CA (− 1.24% vs. 0.83%) [28].

Considering that human exposure studies with single O3 exposure may underestimate potential toxicity if significant synergy occurs between O3 and other coexisting pollutants, researchers also added several common pollutants to the experimental studies [29–31] (Table 1). In a study with 19 healthy volunteers, researchers from the University of Southern California reported that the participants exposed to O3 at 0.37-ppm in the presence of 100 µg/m3 of sulfate aerosol (H2SO4) and 0.37-ppm sulfur dioxide (SO2) exhibited 3.7% decrements in FEV1 [30]. This finding was corroborated with another study evaluating effects of “acid summer haze” on individuals who exercise extensively outdoors [31]. In the study, 45 adult volunteers were exposed to a mixture of 0.12-ppm O3 and approximately 100 µg/m3 of H2SO4. Similar as the previous study, exposure to O3 alone or O3 and H2SO4 mixture resulted in a FVC reduction, although the difference was not statistically significant. In another UC Santa Barbara study, 10 young adult men were exposed to 0.45-ppm O3 and 0.30-ppm peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN) separately or combined. FEV1 was significantly decreased 20.8% after being exposed to O3 alone for 75 min, and the PAN/O3 exposure induced averaging approximately 10% greater pulmonary function decrements than that was observed during exposure to O3 alone, indicating an interaction effect between PAN and O3 [32].

Exacerbation of pulmonary function impairment induced by O3 exposure during exercise was also reported (Table 1). Exercise may increase minute ventilation, which may potentiate responses to O3 by increasing the absolute amount inhaled and promoting more uniform ventilation. In controlled exposure studies, exercise can also be used to increase the total delivered O3 dose to better approximate exposures over an 8-hour workday. and may help normalize the delivered dose across participants despite differences in resting ventilation and lung size. Additionally, warmer temperatures usually may accelerate the chemical reactions that generate O3 and stimulate increased expired ventilation (VE) during exercise [33]. In a UC Davis study, 10 aerobically trained young adult women were recruited and exercised continuously with exposure to FA, and 0.15-ppm and 0.30-ppm O3 in both moderate (24 °C) and hot (35 °C) ambient conditions [33]. A significant interaction of O3 and ambient heat was observed for FVC and FEV1, as well as for respiratory frequency and alveolar volume. Another study in Santa Barbara exposed 8 young healthy males to 0.4-ppm O3 and 0.4-ppm SO2 at 35 °C-85% relative humidity for 2 h and found that compared to 25 °C-50% relative humidity functional residual capacity (FRC) increased significantly and FEF25−75 exhibited larger decreases in higher temperature environment [33, 34]. These studies indicate that O3 exposure coinciding with ambient heat is likely to result in more severe physiological alterations in lung function. In contrast, another study in University of North Carolina, researchers found that O3-induced deduction in FEV1 were comparable between moderate (22 °C) and elevated (32.5 °C) temperature conditions (FEV1: − 12.4% vs. − 7.5%), while no significant temperature-related modification of the reductions in lung function was observed [35].

Individuals with pre-existing conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and obesity are considered more sensitive to O3 exposure compared to the clinically healthy group (Table 1). In a study consisting of 9 men who had severe COPD and 10 age-matched healthy men, participants were exposed to 0.24-ppm O3 and to FA for 4 h in a random order with 1-week apart. The average decrement in FEV1 among the COPD groups was 19% after exposure to O3, compared with 2% in the healthy group (p < 0.001) [36]. In another University of North Carolina study, the researchers recruited 20 obese and 20 normal weight females to examine whether obesity enhances O3-induced pulmonary dysfunction [37]. Although both groups showed significant decrements in FVC and FEV1 after O3 exposure, the decrement was significantly larger among the obese group (FVC: -12.5%; FEV1: -15.9%) compared to normal weigh group (FVC: -8.0%; FEV1: -11.7%).

Collectively, experimental studies have yielded relatively consistent results, demonstrating that O3 exposure―even at or near current regulatory levels―can lead to measurable lung dysfunction and increased inflammation. These impacts could be enhanced by co-exposure to other pollutants, ambient heat and humidity, physical activity, especially among vulnerable populations.

Observational Studies

Besides experimental studies, a large body of observational studies including longitudinal and cross-sectional studies has been conducted to evaluate the effects of O3 exposure on pulmonary function (Table 1 and Table S1). These studies are critical for understanding real-world health impacts, as they assess individuals under ambient conditions, capture diverse exposure histories, and reflect population-level variation in susceptibility and behavior. While cross-sectional studies assess associations between current lung function and long-term or cumulative exposure, longitudinal studies track within-individual changes over time. These studies complement experimental and semi-controlled investigations by evaluating O3-health associations in broader, more representative populations under ambient exposure conditions. By capturing long-term or repeated exposures, and short-term fluctuations in air quality, observational studies offer valuable insights into the population-level burden of O3-related pulmonary impairment.

Cross-sectional studies were often used to assess O3-related respiratory functions, assuming that current air pollution exposure is highly correlated with previous exposure to make a plausible link with current health status [38]. In a study with participants obtained from the Adventist Health Study of Smog, lifetime O3 exposure was estimated from 20-year residential histories [39]. For each 23-ppb increase in annual average O3, FEV₁ was found to decrease by 6.3% in males whose parents had asthma, bronchitis, emphysema, or hay fever (Table 1). Another study using data from the Second National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) involving 4,300 individuals aged 6–24 years also reported negative associations between annual O3 concentrations with both FEV₁ and FVC [11]. In a study involving 10,251 children aged 8–12 years, the researchers found that higher 3-year average O3 concentrations were statistically significantly associated with reduced FEV₁, FVC, FEV₀.₇₅, FEF₂₅-₇₅, and PEFR (Table 1) [40].

While cross-sectional studies offer valuable insight into population-level effects, longitudinal studies allow for tracking pulmonary changes over time (Table 1). Using the data from Children’s Health Study consisting of 1,759 children aged 12–18 years in 12 Southern California communities, no statistically significant differences were found in growth rates for all the pulmonary function tests including FVC, FEV1, FEF75, or MMEF for each 38.6-ppb or 55-ppb increase in annual average 8-hour maximum and hourly O3 concentration [41, 42], while it was reported that each 36.6-ppb increase in 8-hour maximum O3 was associated with a − 1.21% reduction in PEF growth [15]. Although most studies consistently reported a negative association between long-term O₃ exposure and lung function, some studies reported inconsistent findings. A longitudinal study of cystic fibrosis patients, in which O3 exposure was assigned based on nearest air monitors, found no relationship between annual average O3 and lung function decline [43]. These discrepancies may reflect study-specific factors such as limited statistical power due to small sample size, reduced sensitivity of spirometric endpoints to detect small airway changes in cystic fibrosis patients, or exposure misclassification stemming from assigning ambient O3 levels based solely on residential proximity rather than personal exposure metrics. In addition to investigating the effects of long-term O3 exposure, longitudinal studies have also been employed to assess the impacts of short-term exposure. In a 10-year study of 904 older men from the Normative Aging Study, a 15-ppb increase in 48-hour average O3 was associated with declines in FEV1 (− 1.29%) and FVC (− 1.36%) [44]. In another study of 40 asthmatic adults living near East Moline, Illinois, same-day 8-hour maximum O3 exposure was associated with 2.29% and 2.58% reductions in morning and evening PEFR, respectively [45].

Although the bulk of the data derived from experimental studies have revealed a spectrum of O3 effects on the human respiratory system, relatively few data are available on children, the subgroup that may be at higher risk of O3-induced health impacts (Table 1 and Table S1). Unlike adults, children cannot be enrolled in experimental studies for O3, due to ethical and regulatory restrictions. As a result, real-world observational settings such as summer camps offer a rare and valuable alternative to assess ambient O3 exposure and respiratory outcomes in pediatric populations. These camp-based studies, also called camp studies, recruit school aged children and monitor their lung function, typically through repeated daily measurements of FEV₁, FVC, and PEFR, while simultaneously tracking ambient air pollutants and meteorological variables. In a study of 91 children aged 8–11 in New Jersey, investigators observed statistically significant decreases in FEV₁, FVC, and PEFR associated with 1-, 2-, and 4-hour O3-averages prior to spirometric measurements [46], and negative associations persisted when analyses were restricted to days with O3 levels below 60-ppb or 80-ppb, suggesting that exposures even lower than regulatory criteria may elicit adverse effects. Similarly, a study conducted in California’s San Bernardino Mountains found that each 1-ppb increase in daily average O3 was associated with a 0.39-ml reduction in FEV₁ and a 0.44-ml reduction in FVC [47]. These findings align with a pooled analysis by Columbia University researchers [48], which reported that each 1-ppb increase in previous-hour O3 concentration was associated with a 0.50-ml reduction in FEV₁, indicating that children exposed to O3 under natural conditions experience decreases in FEV1. A recent study recruited the children (n = 2,128) in the Boston-area from Project Viva measured FEV1 and FVC at mid-childhood, early-teens and mid-teens study visits, and FeNO at the early- and mid-teens study visits, and reported that O3 exposure during the first year of life was associated with slower lung function growth between early and mid-adolescence, while no consistent associations between long-term O3 exposure and FEV₁ or FVC from mid-childhood through adolescence were observed [49]. Another recent study in Fresno, California, followed 299 children at approximately 7 and 9 years of age and examined residential exposure to O3 over multiple exposure windows (1 week―12 months). O3 exposure was generally associated with lower FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC ratio across all the exposure windows; however, all 95% confidence intervals included the null [50].

Overall, observational studies provide biologically plausible evidence that O3 exposures contribute to pulmonary function impairments across different populations, with relatively consistent findings for short-term exposures but more mixed evidence regarding the effects of long-term exposure. The convergence of findings across study designs strengthens the epidemiological basis for O3 regulation and reinforces experimental evidence on its respiratory effects.

Asthma-Related Symptoms, ED Visits/Hospitalizations

Asthma, as a chronic respiratory disorder, is a nationwide public health concern affecting nearly 28 million people in the U.S [51]. O3 exposure has been implicated in the exacerbation of asthma, often resulting in ED visits/hospital admissions—key indicators of asthma severity and disease burden. In evaluating existing research, it is important to distinguish between short-term exposure—referring to transient spikes over several hours or days—and long-term exposure, which captures sustained ambient O3 levels over extended periods. The subsections present key collective studies that illustrate the magnitude and consistency of these associations across varying timescales and population groups (Table 2 and Table S2).

Asthma-Related Symptoms

Epidemiological studies have linked O3exposure to increased asthma risk across diverse populations (Table 2). In a California cohort study of approximately 3,000 non-smoking adults, researchers reported a statistically significant association between 20-year cumulative O3 exposure and asthma in men (OR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.03–4.16) but no relationship in women (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.58–1.26) [52]. The researchers also found that higher O3 exceedance frequencies above 60-ppb were statistically significantly associated with increases in asthma risk among men (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 0.99–3.64) but not in women. In a cross-sectional study using the 2001 California Health Interview Survey (CHIS) with 1,487 adult respondents residing in San Joaquin Valley [53], researchers used the respondents’ self-reported daily and weekly asthma symptoms information and O3 concentration measurements from CARB or local air-quality management district monitoring station. It was found that the risk of daily/weekly asthma-related symptoms increased by 23% per 10-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure in the previous year before interview date. In another study linking the 2001 CHIS data and O3 measurements from CARB monitoring stations and the California Department of Transportation, the researchers reported that for each 10-ppb increase in annual average O3, the odds ratio (OR) for daily weekly asthma-related symptoms was 2.09 (95% CI: 1.28–3.41), and the ORs were increased to 2.29 (95% CI: 1.01–5.23) and 3.51 (95% CI:1.45–8.46) after co-adjusting for PM10 and PM2.5, respectively [54]. In another study linking 2001 CHIS data in Los Angeles (LA) and San Diego counties with the annual average O3 concentration measured at the nearest station, researchers observed that for each 10-ppb increase in the annual average O3 exposure prior to the interview date, the risk of poorly controlled asthma increased, particularly among older adults (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 0.91–3.18) and men (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.05–2.94) [55].

The impact of O3 on asthma was also pronounced in children, who tend to spend longer time exercising outdoors (Table 2). In a study with 34,073 U.S.-wide children (ages 3–17), researchers found that for each 5-ppb increase in annual O3 exposure, the OR for current asthma was 1.08 (95% CI: 1.02–1.14) [56]. In another study consisting of 224 children with a history of bronchiolitis in infancy selected from the Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) PATHWAYS Consortium, the researchers estimated O3 exposure generated from a spatiotemporal model based on the geocoded residential address collected at age four. A 2-ppb increase in 2-year average O3 exposure had a relative risk (RR) for current wheeze and asthma of 1.3 (95% CI: 1.0–1.7) and 1.4 (95% CI: 1.1–1.8), respectively [57]. A cohort study followed 3,535 children (ages ~ 9–16) across in Southern California over five years and found that the risk of developing asthma in children playing three or more sports in communities with high O3 concentrations increased by 30%, compared with children playing no sports, but no association was observed in lower-O3 regions [9].

Evidence regarding the association between short-term peaks (over hours to several days) in ambient O3 concentrations and asthma-related symptoms and reliance on rescue medications were also reported (Table 2). In a study using a dataset passively collected by digital sensors monitoring inhaled medication use, the researchers analyzed 456,779 short-acting beta-agonist (SABA) puffs inhaled by 3,386 people with asthma or COPD between 2012 and 2019 across California. These researchers used a deletion/substitution/addition (D/S/A) modeling technique to build the surfaces for O3 [58]. In the multipollutant model, each 1-ppb increase in daily O3 exposure was associated with a RR of 1.005 (95% CI: 1.004–1.007) incremental increase in SABA puffs, and given a 30-ppb increase in O3, the incidence rate ratio on daily SABA puff was 1.161 (95% CI: 1.127–1.233).

In a cohort study of 298 asthmatic children in Michigan (1999–2002), using the measurements from two community-level monitoring sites in the area, researchers reported that for each 16-ppb increase in 1-hour peak-O3 during the previous 3–5 days, the ORs for wheezing, cough and shortness of breath were 1.12 (95% CI: 1.01–1.23), 1.09 (95% CI: 1.00–1.18) and 1.12 (95% CI: 1.01–1.21), respectively [59]. Similarly, a case-crossover study utilizing 2012–2017 electronic health records from over 533,446 patients in Florida and daily O3 data obtained from the US EPA and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention National Environmental Public Health Tracking Network, reported that the risk of acute exacerbation of asthma was found to be 6–7% higher when O3 levels was in the upper quartiles (≥ 36.4-ppb) during lag 0–6 days before the event in April, May and October [51]. The estimated effect of O3-related asthma exacerbations was largest in Hispanics, followed by Black and then Whites for the fourth O3 exposure quartile compared to the first quartile [60].

Asthma-Related ED Visits/Hospitalizations

Many other observational studies have also evaluated associations between both short- and long-term O3 exposure and indicators of asthma severity―ED visits and hospitalizations (Table 2 and Table S2).

Two studies used CHIS data to assess the long-term O3 exposure impacts on asthma-related ED visits/hospitalizations (Table 2). One study used data (n = 1,502) restricted in San Joaquin Valley in 2001 and O3 concentration measurements from the CARB and local air-quality management district monitoring stations, and found that the OR for daily/weekly asthma-related ED visits/hospitalizations was 1.49 (95% CI: 1.05–2.11) for each 10-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure in the previous year before interview date [53]. When stratified by age, there was a 63% increase in ED visits/hospitalizations per 10-ppb increase in O3 among children. Expanding on this, another study used O3 measurements from CARB monitoring stations and the California Department of Transportation. For each 10-ppb increase in annual average O3, the OR for asthma-related ED visits/hospitalizations was 1.35 (95% CI: 0.85–2.14). The ORs increased to 2.89 (95% CI: 1.32–6.34) and 2.48 (95% CI:1.14–5.38) after adjusting for both PM10 and PM2.5, respectively [54]. In a case-crossover study leveraging a dataset of over 405,320 ED visits from 42 U.S. children’s hospitals during 2004–2015, it was observed that each 10-ppb increase in warm-season (May-September) 8-hour maximum daily O3 was associated with asthma-related ED visits (RR = 1.013, 95% CI: 1.008–1.019). O3 exposure restricted to < 70-ppb was also associated with increases in the risk of ED visits for asthma (RR = 1.008, 95% CI: 1.002–1.014) [61].

An association between short-term increases in ambient O3 concentrations and elevated asthma-related ED visits/hospitalizations was also evident, particularly among children, older groups and other vulnerable populations (Table 2). In a case-crossover study linking the daily ED visits among children aged 3–17 from the New Jersy Department of Health and O3 concentrations generated from the Community Multi-Scale Air Quality (CMAQ) Model, it was observed that the associations with O3 were strongest on the same day of the ED visits (RR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.04–1.06) per 15.5-ppb increase in the O3 exposure [62]. In a Washington D.C. study using asthma-related pediatric ED visits (2001–2004) and daily O3 concentrations from local monitoring stations, it was reported that each 10-ppb increase in the same day O3 concentration being associated with a 4.5% and 8.3% increase in asthma-related ED admissions among the 1–17 years and 5–12 age groups respectively [63]. In another study conducted in three U.S. metropolitan areas (Atlanta, Dallas and St. Louis), it was reported that per 28-ppb increase in 3-day moving average of O3 exposure, the RR for asthma-related ED visits was 1.07 (95% CI: 1.04–1.10) in the 5–18 years old, 1.03 (95% CI: 1.01–1.05) in the 0–4 year olds, 1.03 (95% CI: 1.00–1.05) in 19–39 years old, 1.01 (95% CI: 0.98–1.04) in 40–64 years old, and 1.03 (95% CI: 0.97–1.09) in those 65 years and older [64] (Table 2). Similar findings were also observed in a study of over 39 million ED visit records across 17 U.S. states; it was reported that the RR for asthma-related ED visits was 1.06 (95% CI: 1.05–1.06) per 20-ppb increase in O3 on lag days 0–6 [65]. When stratified by age, the RRs for those 0–18, 19–64 and 65 + were 1.04 (95% CI: 1.03–1.06), 1.07 (95% CI: 1.06–1.08), 1.02 (95% CI: 1.00–1.05), respectively. In a case-crossover study in Fresno, California, it was observed that a 5-ppb increase in the concentration of O3 at the lag 0–14 days was associated with a 34% increased risk of having an asthma ED visit. When stratified by age, the ORs for having asthma-related ED visits were 1.42 (95% CI: 1.26–1.60), 1.25 (95% CI: 1.16–1.39), 1.34 (95% CI: 1.19–1.49) and 1.36 (95% CI: 1.06–1.71) for those who were aged 2–5, 6–18, 19–40, 41–64 and 65 + years for each 5-ppb increase in the average O3 concentration of lag 0–14 days, respectively [66]. When stratified by race/ethnicity, the risk increased by 40%, 38% and 27% among Hispanics, Blacks and Whites at the cumulative lag 0–14, respectively (Table 2). Although the evidence from most studies was suggestive of an association between O3 exposure and asthma-related ED visits/hospitalizations, some others reported non-significant results. In a study examining hospital discharge and exposure to O3 for 1991–1994 central LA, neither daily average on admission day nor 8-day average of O3 concentration was found to be significantly related to asthma-related hospital admissions [67]. Similarly, in a recent study using ED visit and inpatient admission data from Stony Brook University Hospital in New York State between 2014 and 2023, no positive associations were observed between daily O3 exposure and asthma-related ED visits or inpatient admissions [68].

Although literature has largely emphasized the respiratory effects of O3 exposure, including asthma exacerbation, an expanding body of evidence implicates both short- and long-term exposure in respiratory mortality and a broader range of adverse respiratory outcomes (Table 3 and Table S3).

Respiratory-Related Mortality

The evidence for adverse health effects associated with long-term exposure to O3 is mixed (Table 3 and Table S3), while the evidence from systematic review and meta-analyses is suggestive of associations with respiratory mortality [69]. In an earlier study of 6,338 nonsmoking Californian from Adventist Health Study, researchers linked O3 concentrations from 348 monitoring stations to 1977–1992 mortality of the cohort and reported a strong association with lung cancer mortality among men (RR = 4.19, 95% CI: 1.81–9.69) when O3 exposures exceeded 100-ppb [70] (Table 3). In a respiratory cancer study among Whites in LA and Honolulu, it was observed that the risk of cancer death was 1.06 (95% CI: 1.04–1.07) for each 10-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure [71]. In a study using the American Cancer Society Cancer Prevention Study II cohort (n = 448,850 subjects with 118,777 death) and correlating with air-pollution data from 96 metropolitan statistical areas in the U.S., it was reported that the RR of respiratory-specific death was 1.04 (95% CI: 1.01–1.07) for each 10-ppb increase in quarterly average (April–September) O3 concentration [8]. In a cohort of over 500,000 U.S. adults aged 50–71, the researchers assigned annual O3 concentrations to each participant’s residence census tract using EPA Fused Air Quality Surface Using Downscaling data and reported that the hazard ratios for respiratory disease and COPD mortality were 1.04 (95% CI: 1.00–1.09) and 1.06 (95% CI: 1.01–1.12) per 10-ppb increase in 8-year average O3 exposure [72]. In another study examining the association between long-term warm-season averaged 1-hour maximum O3 exposure on cause-specific mortality for 22.2 million U.S. Medicare beneficiaries between 2000 and 2008, the researchers found strongest and most consistent increased risks for all respiratory mortality (RR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.03–1.03), COPD-related mortality (RR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.07–1.08) and lung cancer-related mortality (RR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02) [73]. While in a cohort of 79,711 California adults (1982–2000) in LA, the researchers calculated the O3 concentrations from inverse distance square weighting (IDW) models and reported that for each 24-ppb increase in 14-year average O3 exposure, the RR for all-caused respiratory mortality was 1.02 (95% CI: 0.90–1.15) [74] (Table 3).

Short-term O3 exposure has also been associated with elevated respiratory mortality, although significant results are not consistently observed across studies (Table 3). In a national study using daily cause specific mortality counts for 1987–2000 from the National Center for Health Statistics on 95 large urbanized areas and O3 data from US EPA AQS, the researchers reported that a 10-ppb increase in one-week average O₃ exposure was associated with a 0.52% increase in daily cardio-pulmonary mortality [5]. In a case-crossover study consisting of 162,146 deceased subjects aged 35 + from three counties in the eastern Massachusetts during 1995–2002, it was reported that each 10-ppb increase in the average of 8-hour-maximum O3 in the previous four days was associated with 1.96% (95% CI: -1.83–5.90%) increase in deaths from respiratory disorders [75]. Similarly, in a case-crossover study estimating the associations between short-term O3 exposure and all-cause and cause-specific mortality in seven U.S. states from 2000 to 2015, the researchers linked daily O3 exposure levels to over 3 million non-accidental death records based on census tracts or residential addresses and found a 0.41% (95% CI: -0.33–1.15%) increase in respiratory mortality for each 10-ppb increase in O3 exposure at lag 0–2 day [76].

Few studies investigated the association between O3 exposure and respiratory death among infants (Table 3). In a case-control study evaluating the influence of outdoor air pollution on infant all respiratory-caused death in the South Coast Air Basin of California, the researchers identified all infant deaths among infants born alive between 1989 and 2000 and calculated average birth address-based O3 exposure levels during the 2-week, 1-month, 2-month, and 6-month periods before a case subject’s death, but no association between O3 exposure and infant respiratory death was found [77]. A recent study examined monthly O3 exposure in relation to all-cause and cause-specific post-neonatal infant mortality using National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) for all live births (n = 35,665,533) occurring in the contiguous U.S. between 2005 and 2017. Positive associations between average monthly O3 exposure and infant respiratory-related mortality were observed among infants living in Northwest (IRR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.09) or counties with high social vulnerable index (SVI) (IRR = 1.08, 95% CI:1.03–1.13) [78].

Other Respiratory-Related Morbidity

O3 exposure has been implicated in a range of respiratory health endpoints, including bronchitis, pneumonia and COPD, and acute respiratory distress syndrome [79]. Both long-term and short-term O3 exposures contribute to these outcomes, with effect sizes varying across different populations (Table 3 and Table S3).

In a cohort of Medicare recipients (n = 11,084,660) in seven southeastern states linking spatial-temporal model generated annual average O3 exposure from 2000 to 2012, the researchers found that for each 1-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure, the hazard ratios for COPD, pneumonia and lung cancer-related hospitalizations were1.02 (95% CI: 1.02–1.03), 1.03 (95% CI: 1.03–1.03) and 1.004 (95% CI: 1.002–1.005), respectively [80] (Table 3). Similarly, using 2000–2012 Medicare data, it was observed that for each 1-ppb increase in the annual average O3 exposure, there was an increase in annual hospital admission rate for ARDS of 0.24% (95% CI: 0.18–0.31) in single pollutant model and 0.15% (95% CI: 0.08–0.22) when co-adjusting for PM2.5 [81]. In a cross-sectional study of 1,874 current and former smokers with or without chronic obstructive pulmonary disease the SPIROMICS AIR study across seven U.S. cities [82], a 5-ppb increase in 10-year average O3 concentration was associated with a higher risk of respiratory exacerbation (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.12–1.66) and severe exacerbation (OR = 1.37, 95% CI:1.07–1.76). In a 16-year longitudinal study in Washington State, the researchers linked hospital admission and ambient monitoring data and found that for each 10-ppb increase in daily 8-hour maximum O3 at a 3-day lag, the RR for respiratory-related hospital admissions was increased by 2–4% [83]. Using the ED visits data among children (aged 0–4 years) within the Atlanta metropolitan area and O3 concentrations from ambient monitoring networks, the researchers found elevated risk of ED visits related to upper respiratory infection (URI; RR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06) and pneumonia (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.13) for each 28-ppb increase in 3-day average O3 [84] (Table 3). Recently, in the American Cancer Society’s Cancer Prevention Study-II Nutrition Cohort, which included 122,442 participants, each 9.7-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure was associated with a 7% higher risk of incident lung cancer (HR = 1.07; 95% CI: 1.01–1.12). Positive associations were also observed for nearly all lung cancer subtypes except small cell carcinoma, although the 95% CIs included the null [85]. These studies collectively highlight the role of transient O3 spikes in triggering acute respiratory events, reinforcing the need for real-time air quality monitoring and risk communication, especially for vulnerable populations. Although rare, some studies also indicated the acute impacts on restricted activity days and school absence due to some symptom exacerbations that are not perceived as severe enough to search for medical assistance [86]. Using the Health Interview Survey (HIS) 1976–1981, the researchers investigated the association between respiratory-related restricted activity days (RRAD) and minor restricted activity days (MRAD) [87] with 2-week average O3 exposure obtained from EPA’s Storage and Retrieval of Aerometric Data (SAROAD) monitoring network. O3 was observed to be associated with MRAD but not RRAD [88] (Table 3).

Only six studies have examined the association between O3 exposure and school absenteeism in U.S (Table 3). In the northern Nevada study (1996–1998), the researchers included 57 elementary schools with a total of 27,793 student enrollments, and accessed continuously hourly O3 concentrations from six monitoring sites in the Washoe County [89]. It was observed that for each 50-ppb increase in 1-hour maximum O3 concentration in the preceding 14 days, the absence rate would increase 13.01% (95% CI: 3.41–22.61%), indicating the absenteeism numbers for all elementary schools in the area will increase by 160 (95% CI: 42–278) each day due to the increase in O3. Another two studies nested in the Children’s Health Study in Southern California examined type-specific absence incidence data collected by an active surveillance system for the school children aged 9–10 years during January through June 1996 [90, 91]. Hourly-average O3 was measured at central-site monitors in each of the 12 communities. It was observed that an increase of 20-ppb of O3 concentration with 30-day lag period was associated with an increase of 16.3% (95% CI: -2.6–38.9%) for all school absence, 62.9% (95% CI: 18.4–124.1%) for illness-related absence rates, 82.9% (95% CI: 3.9–222%) for respiratory illnesses. An increase of 20-ppb of O3 concentration with 15-day lag period was related to an increase of 45.1% (95% CI: 21.3–73.7%) for upper respiratory illnesses, and 173.9% (95% CI: 91.3–292.3%) for lower respiratory illnesses with wet cough [90]. For each 20-ppb increase in annual average O3 exposure, there was an increase of 30.9% (95% CI: 14.2–50.3%) for all school absence, 25.9% (95% CI: 1.5–56.1%) for illness-related absences and 27.1% (95% CI: 4.5–54.6%) for respiratory illness-related school absence, respectively [91]. In a Utah study using the absence data from 26 elementary schools, 7 middle schools and 3 high schools located in the Salt Lake City School District [92], the researchers modelled O3 concentrations using an IDW method, and reported a positive relationship with prior day O3 exposure and school absence across the full academic year, Winter and Spring quarters. While in another study, researchers recruited 861 children with persistent asthma in seven U.S. urban communities and asked the caregivers to recall the number of days in the past 2 weeks that the child missed school because of asthma [93]. For each 26.7-ppb increase in the 19-day average O3 concentration, the risk ratios for school absence in single-pollutant and co-pollutant models were 1.31 (95% CI: 0.83–2.06) and 1.35 (95% CI: 0.82–2.20), respectively. Similarly, a recent study using the daily school attendance data (2015/16―2017/18) from the Clairton School District of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania and outdoor air pollution data from US EPA AQS reported no association between O3 exposure and school absenteeism. Compared with days with a maximum 8-hour O3 concentration < 55-ppb, days with O3 concentrations ≥ 55-ppb were associated with rate ratios of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.79–1.11) for absenteeism among students with asthma and 1.00 (95% CI: 0.88, 1.27) for non-asthmatic absence [94].

Discussion

We reviewed the literatures regarding the respiratory health outcomes including pulmonary function, asthma-related symptoms, ED visits/hospitalizations, as well as other respiratory-related morbidity and mortality due to O3 exposure, summarizing the findings that both short-term and long-term O3 exposures could contribute to pronounced effects for a broad range of respiratory health outcomes, especially among vulnerable populations such as children, older adults, and individuals with pre-existing health conditions (Fig. 2).

Short-term and long-term O3 exposure effects on respiratory health outcomes

We started the literature review from pulmonary function because it is an objective and important measure of respiratory health and a predictor of cardiorespiratory morbidity and mortality [95], highlighting lung function as a mechanistically informative and policy-relevant outcome. Human experiment studies are usually considered in the context of the US EPA’s revised standards for O3 [96], consistently report small but measurable reductions in lung function, often accompanied by markers of airway inflammation, thus providing important insights into the short-term respiratory effects of O3, particularly under conditions of elevated ventilation such as exercise. However, several limitations are inherent in these experimental designs. First, previous experimental studies primarily evaluate pulmonary function after acute exposure, often in controlled environments that do not fully replicate real-world conditions. Second, these studies are typically conducted on healthy adults, thus limiting their generalizability to vulnerable populations such as children, the older population, and individuals with pre-existing conditions. Third, the intensity and duration of exercise used in these studies often exceed those encountered in daily life, further restricting their applicability [96]. Fourth, experimental studies benefit from repeated measures within the same individuals, strengthening within-person comparisons and offering valuable insights into the role of individual characteristics; however, the studies depend on participants’ collaboration and generally involve relatively small sample sizes and selected study populations, which may limit the generalizability of their findings to broader populations. Furthermore, multiple comparisons (i.e. paired test, two-way ANOVA) in these studies might introduce the risk of spurious findings. Therefore, although valuable for understanding specific biological responses, findings from these experimental studies may not fully represent responses across more diverse populations.

Alternatively, observational studies are more generalizable and can assess O3-related declines in lung function even among children. The camp study design enables researchers to collect repeated lung-function measurements along with standardized outdoor physical activities and examine within-subject variations in pulmonary responses to O3 exposures without ethical constraints. These studies are particularly important given children’s greater vulnerability to air pollution owing to higher ventilation rates per body weight and greater time spent outdoors, providing robust evidence that short-term O3 exposure―even at relatively low ambient concentrations―is associated with reductions in lung function among children. The findings are consistent across diverse settings, exposure metrics, and analytic approaches, and contribute meaningfully to the broader body of epidemiologic evidence on O3-related respiratory health effects in sensitive populations.

Observational studies including longitudinal, case-control, case-crossover and cross-sectional studies also present significant challenges. Most studies relied on administrative health records which constrained exposure assessment to ZIP code–level estimates rather than geocoded address-based estimates, thus limiting spatial resolution for O3 exposure and raising the possibility of O3 exposure misclassification. Also, community-level exposure assessments from fixed-site monitors were often used in previous studies, which might not capture the variability in individual exposure, especially for children or low-income populations with longer time spent performing outdoor activities. For example, among the six studies included in this review that investigated O3-related school absenteeism, air pollution exposures predominantly used community level measurements made by centrally located monitors, rather than using personal exposure assessments. Additionally, without incorporating occupational environment and residential history, potential misclassification of long-term O3 exposure cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, various exposure assessment methods might also contribute to inconclusive results. Most studies use the O3 concentration measured at governmental monitoring sites, restricting the studies limited areas and time periods [59, 90]. A few studies assessed the O3 exposure levels using spatial and/or temporal models such as IDW, however, there is still a lack of well-developed exposure modeling approach for O3 [97]. Further refinements in exposure modeling include the development of advanced exposure assessment models such as chemical transport models or land use regression models combined with monitoring data, land use, traffic and meteorological data are warranted.

Studies that isolate O3 exposure while limiting consideration of other contributing factors may over/underestimate health risks in such exposure contexts. Ambient O3 rarely acts alone. Rather, individuals are typically co-exposed to multiple pollutants, particularly in regions like California, where photochemical smog contains co-occurring pollutants such as PAN. Another pressing concern is the growing need to better characterize O3 exposure from wildfires. Given the increasing frequency and severity of wildfires, it is critical to examine the spatial and temporal impacts of these events on O3 levels to distinguish their effects from conventional emissions. Additionally, factors such as neighborhood air quality, access to green spaces, and dietary habits may also modify individual susceptibility to O3-related health effects. For example, dietary factors may increase susceptibility to pollutant-related health effects independently of socioeconomic status. A growing body of evidence suggests that diets rich in fruits and vegetables [98], antioxidant vitamin supplementation, and dietary fatty acids may help mitigate adverse respiratory effects associated with outdoor air pollution, supporting an important role for oxidative stress in the underlying biological pathways [99–101]. Other vulnerability factors such as obesity and low birth weight also need to be accounted for in the analysis [102]. Further, the adverse effects of O3 exposure might vary over the life-course (including prenatal exposures) such that future research should be directed toward differential effects at different stages of development.

Our review also demonstrated there were limited studies investigating various effects of O3 exposure across vulnerable populations. For example, effects of ethnicity might be confounded with those effects associated with low socioeconomic status. Children from socioeconomically disadvantaged families may experience higher ambient air pollution exposure and greater vulnerability to its health effects due to environmental inequities and co-occurring social and health-related risk factors [103, 104]. A nationwide U.S. study of public schools also found that schools with higher proportions of students from lower-income households, as indicated by eligibility for free or reduced-price lunch, were exposed to higher ambient air pollutant concentrations, particularly NO₂ [105, 106]. Also, few studies have considered factors such as maternal or early-life exposures, which are often shaped by parental socioeconomic status and may influence long-term respiratory outcomes.

For certain respiratory health outcomes such as asthma, in view of how important O3 pollution is as a trigger of exacerbations, one of the key questions is whether short-term peak exposures (e.g., 1-hour peak) versus time-weighted averages over longer time periods (e.g., 1–2 days) are more strongly associated with increased risk of adverse asthma outcomes. Some evidence points to peak exposures being more detrimental than long-term exposure, yet further investigation is needed. Furthermore, the lag time between O3 exposure and asthma impacts remains uncertain. Many studies report stronger adverse health outcomes occurring the day after high O3 exposure rather than on the same day [107]. Also, several studies used case-crossover design to control for many potential individual level confounders (e.g., socioeconomic status, body mass index, tobacco use), given that each case serves as its own control [60]. However, many studies fail to account for other important variables such as maternal age, gestational duration, or family history of asthma or allergy, all of which may confound or modify the relationship between O3 exposure and asthma-related health outcomes.

Several strengths need to be noted. First, unlike many prior reviews that primarily focused on pulmonary function or ED visits/hospitalizations separately, we provided an integrated synthesis of research findings spanning from lung function to respiratory-related mortality, enhancing our understanding of how O3 affects respiratory health across the continuum from early dysfunction to overt disease. Second, this review uniquely included school absenteeism as a meaningful and policy-relevant indicator of O3-related health impacts. Although not as specific as other health indicators (e.g. asthma-related ED visits), school absenteeism captures both minor and intermediate health consequences that are severe enough to prevent them from attending school but not necessarily leading to dramatic consequences such as ED visits/hospitalizations. Therefore, it is a good indicator for school-aged children to reflect their overall health condition and providing a more comprehensive assessment of the adverse impacts of O3 exposure [92, 108]. The inclusion of school absenteeism as an outcome further emphasizes the broader societal and economic consequences of O3 exposure including parental work loss and associated productivity costs [108–110], suggesting that their public health impacts may extend beyond the immediate health of the children.

Conclusions

By integrating evidence from both controlled human exposure experiments and epidemiologic studies conducted in the U.S., this systematic review brings together complementary evidence on O₃-related respiratory health effects across experimental and population-based settings, suggesting relatively consistent associations between O3 exposure and impaired pulmonary function, asthma-related health effects, and other respiratory-related mortality and morbidity including school absenteeism. The associations observed between O3 exposure and multiple respiratory health outcomes—even at concentrations below current regulatory standards—highlight important implications for U.S. air quality policy. These findings suggest that existing O3 standards may not fully protect vulnerable populations and underscore the importance of continued reassessment of regulatory thresholds, particularly in light of climate-driven changes in O3 formation and wildfire-related emissions. In addition to regulatory considerations, the evidence supports targeted public health interventions, improved risk communication, and focused protection of high-risk populations to mitigate O3-related respiratory health impacts.

Future studies are encouraged to incorporate O3 exposures estimated derived from advanced exposure models within multipollutant frameworks to capture the complexity of real-world exposures and individual-level vulnerability, as well as standardizing definitions and improving confounders control which are critical to enhance comparability across the studies. Despite a substantial body of literature, more research efforts are needed and essential for informing equitable air quality standards and guiding targeted public health interventions to effectively reduce the respiratory health burden associated with O3 exposure.
