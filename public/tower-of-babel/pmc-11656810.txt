Assessing the continuum of care in Sub-Saharan African hospitals performing surgery for breast cancer: a secondary analysis of the GlobalSurg 3 study
Authors: Freddy Houéhanou Rodrigue Gnangnon, Ismaïl Lawani, Stephen R Knight, Alexis Parenté, Francis Moïse Dossou, Terrence Totah, Dismand Stephan Houinato, Véronique Blanquet, Pierre-Marie Preux, Ewen M Harrison, on behalf of the GlobalSurg Collaborative and National Institute for Health Research Global Health Research Unit on Global Surgery
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile breast cancer incidence rates in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) are among the lowest worldwide, mortality rates remain among the highest, reflecting particularly poor survival. Only a few studies in SSA have investigated the capabilities of treatment services to adequately provide a continuum of care for breast cancer. Our aim was to assess the availability of diagnostic facilities and adjuvant therapies in hospitals performing breast cancer surgery in SSA.MethodsWe performed a secondary analysis of the GlobalSurg3 study data collected in the SSA region. The GlobalSurg 3 study is a multicenter, international, prospective, observational study of hospitals providing surgical services for cancer patients (including breast cancer) around the world. A total of 47 hospitals from 15 SSA countries and 43 cities were included between April 1, 2018, and Jan 31, 2019.ResultsOne-third of hospitals covered a population greater than two million (n = 17; 36.2%). Ultrasound was available in all hospitals; however, it was not consistently functional in 11 hospitals (23.4%). Only half of the included hospitals (n = 26, 55.3%) had access to a full-time pathologist, whilst the multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach was absent in 42.4% of hospitals. Radiotherapy equipment was only available in nine hospitals (19.1%). Only half of the hospitals (n = 25, 53.1%) had chemotherapy drugs available on site. In nine hospitals (19.1%), patients had to travel more than 50 km to access chemotherapy drugs.ConclusionsOutcomes for breast cancer patients in SSA cannot be improved without significant investments in pathology, surgical and oncological treatment pathways to provide timely diagnostic and effective treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13267-6.Keywords: Breast cancer, Sub-Saharan Africa, Surgery, Mortality, Health system, Diagnosis, Treatment, Access to care

Novelty and impact

To date, most studies aimed at assessing the management of breast cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) use simulated methods due to the absence of robust data. Empirical and observational data on the management of breast cancer in the region are lacking. In this study, we provide country-specific information on diagnostic facilities and adjuvant therapies for breast cancer patients in SSA. We also demonstrate how addressing these inequities is essential to meet the gender equality Sustainable Development Goals of the United Nations.

Introduction

Cancer burden is rising in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1], with breast cancer the leading cause of cancer morbidity in the region [2]. Breast cancer incidence rates in SSA are among the lowest in the world, however mortality rates paradoxically remain among the highest, reflecting the particularly poor survival for patients with breast cancer [2, 3]. For example, the 5-year survival rate for breast cancer exceeds 90% in high-income countries, whereas it is only 40% in South Africa [4]. In addition, half of all breast cancer deaths in women under 50 occur in Sub-Saharan Africa [4].

The management of breast cancer is multidisciplinary and resource intensive. Whilst the diagnosis is primarily based on imaging and pathology, the main pillars of treatment depend on the disease stage and include surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies (chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, targeted therapies and more recently immunotherapy). In SSA, accurate disease classification and staging is usually difficult to achieve due to the lack of appropriate diagnostic tools, and the choice of a treatment plan is often based on the availability of treatment services, with surgery representing the most popular option [5]. On the other hand, with locally advanced or metastatic disease more frequent at the time of diagnosis [6], systemic therapies and radiotherapy have become essential for treatment with both curative and palliative intent.

Several studies have reported the advanced stage of breast cancer at the time of diagnosis in SSA, however there is limited data on the diagnostic, treatment facilities and access to care for patients with breast cancer in the region [7]. Although many reports highlight the need for early detection and downstaging to improve breast cancer patient survival [2, 6, 8], only a few studies have investigated the current capabilities of diagnostic and treatment services to provide quality care to patients in SSA [9].

We carried out this study to assess the availability of diagnostic, surgical and other treatment services in hospitals performing breast cancer surgery in SSA.

Materials and methods

Study design and study period

GlobalSurg (http://globalsurg.org/) is a collaboration of practicing surgeons from around the world, performing research in surgery to foster local, national and international research networks [8]. GlobalSurg 3, a multicenter international prospective observational study of hospitals providing surgical services for cancer patients around the world [8], is the third international study to be conducted by the GlobalSurg collaborative. Any hospital, worldwide, hosting a surgical unit providing surgery (emergency or elective) for one or more of three prevalent cancers (breast, gastric and/or colorectal cancer) was eligible to participate in the GlobalSurg 3 study.

This study is a secondary analysis of data collected as part of the GlobalSurg 3 study. The GlobalSurg 3 study was conducted between April 2018 and January 2019, with validation of data performed until April 23, 2019 based on a validated model that has been used successfully in previous studies [5, 10–14]. A hospital-level survey was also conducted, following a system-based approach adapting the framework for Comprehensive Cancer Centers in LMICs and the methodology has been described in detail elsewhere [15]. Briefly, beta testing was performed at two LMIC hospital sites to ensure survey clarity prior to formal release across collaborating hospitals. For this study, we describe GlobalSurg 3 hospital-level data collected across SSA reporting on the availability of diagnostic and treatment services for patients with breast cancer.

Inclusion criteria

Any hospital providing emergency or elective surgery for breast cancer in SSA was eligible. Included centers had to ensure that data collection is > 90% complete and centers with > 10% missing data were excluded. We used the World Bank criteria [16] to define income groups and categorize the countries of the included hospitals.

Investigators

Data were collected by selected in-country investigators who were responsible for accurate uploading of data via a secure internet-based Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system. Investigators were provided with a data extraction sheet to aid completion, with reminders being sent every four weeks if the survey remained incomplete.

Data points

Hospital infrastructure and process resources identified as core clinical service components to ensure access to quality breast cancer care were captured, such as hospital coverage, the presence of imaging modalities, oncology services, surgical treatment and perioperative care [5]. A detailed protocol for this survey is available in supplementary material 2. We selected nine criteria to define a hospital that could provide a minimum continuum of care for breast cancer patients undergoing surgery.These criteria were adapted from the "Universal patient pathway for breast cancer management" [17]. The criteria were divided into four diagnostic criteria (ultrasound availability, CT scan availability, pathology services availability, waiting time for pathology report) and five therapeutic criteria (MDT availability, radiotherapy availability, radiotherapy type, chemotherapy availability, and postoperative care area availability).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated with R Statistics for Windows, Version 3.5.0. Qualitative variables are presented as proportions, and quantitative variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons of proportions were made using the chi-square test, Fisher's exact test or Yates' corrected test depending on the specific conditions appropriate to each test, while differences between means were assessed using Student's t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the included hospitals

A total of 47 hospitals from 15 countries and 43 cities were included during the data collection period. Of the 15 SSA countries included in the Globalsurg 3 study, six were from Southern Africa, four from West Africa and four from East Africa. Only one country from Central Africa was included (Fig. 1A). In addition, half of the included countries (n = 8) were low-income countries according to the World Bank's classification (Fig. 1B), and the remaining seven countries were classified as middle-income countries.

Diagnostic imaging and pathology services

Ultrasound was available in all hospitals; however, it was not routinely available and/or functioning in one quarter (n = 11, 23.4%). CT scans were available in most hospitals (n = 33, 70.2%) but were non-functional in 12 hospitals (25.5%). Approximately half of hospitals (n = 26, 55.4%) had pathology department available for all patients treated, whilst most hospitals had access to pathology services for more than 75% of their patients (34, 75.6%). Adjuvant oncology therapies

Radiotherapy was available in nine hospitals (20.0%). It was inaccessible in the country of residence for patients attending ten of the 47 hospitals (nine hospitals without any access to radiotherapy and one hospital requiring patients to be transferred abroad for radiotherapy). When radiotherapy was available, both cobalt machines (n = 19, 40.4%) and linear accelerators (n = 19, 40.4%) were used. Opioid pain medication was available in 51.1% (n = 24) of hospitals.

Discussion

According to the United Nations Population Division, the population of SSA is expected to grow rapidly by an annual average rate of 2.7% to reach 2.2 billion by 2050 [18]; constituting a challenge for healthcare systems, especially regarding breast cancer management as breast cancer incidence rises. In this prospective, descriptive study wide variation in the ability of these facilities to provide a quality continuum of care for breast cancer patients were found.

Hospital size and coverage in SSA

More than one-third of the hospitals included in the study covered a population greater than two million people, with the majority defined as tertiary referral hospitals. Our analysis demonstrates that access to care is particularly difficult for cancer patients in SSA. Previously, it has been found that more than one-sixth of the population in SSA lives more than two hours away from a public hospital [18].

Availability and access to breast cancer surgery in SSA

Our results indicate that surgeons are the most accessible specialists involved in breast cancer management in SSA. In most low-income countries, surgeons are often the only available specialists in cancer care and are responsible not only for diagnosis and surgical management, but also for nonsurgical treatment and the provision of palliative care [19].

To improve the technical capacities of hospitals providing cancer care in SSA, there is a great need to train surgeons in a number of cancer surgical procedures [19]. The surgical management of breast cancer has become increasingly complex in recent years, requiring the mastery of a wide range of skills, including oncoplastic surgery, pedicled and perforator flaps for partial or total breast reconstruction, lipomodelling, conservative axillary surgery, assessment and prevention of hereditary risk [20]. A modern breast surgeon must therefore be able to perform these procedures to offer optimum care for patients. In Scotland for instance, treatment by a surgeon specialized in breast cancer surgery has been associated with a significant reduction in mortality [21].

Access to breast cancer imaging in SSA

Unlike CT, ultrasound was available in the majority of SSA hospitals covered by our study. The accuracy of ultrasonography in the preoperative assessment of breast cancer is necessary to provide accurate loco-regional treatment planning [22], but CT is essential for disease staging. In most SSA countries, clinical examination, chest radiography, and ultrasound are often the only available resources for disease staging [19].

Access to pathology in SSA

Only half of the hospitals had a pathology department available all the time. Breast cancer management is often supported by pathology and immunohistochemistry findings [23]. In most low-resource settings, such as SSA, patients with breast cancer rarely obtain a histological diagnosis; therefore, many often receive inappropriate treatment [19]. The greatest challenge in most LMICs is the lack of human resources [19, 23]. In SSA, for instance, there are less than 80 pathologists for every 1 million population, which is 60 times less than recommended [19].

Access to radiotherapy

Access to radiotherapy is probably the most challenging aspect of breast cancer management in SSA and is known as the region of the world with the most limited radiotherapy services [24]. In 2017, half of African countries reported having no access to radiation services [6]. We found radiotherapy was one of the least available oncological therapies. The 2020 WHO World Cancer Report highlighted the significant relationship between the number of available radiotherapy machines and cancer mortality [25]. The limited access to radiotherapy could explain, in part, the excess mortality from breast cancer in SSA for several reasons. First, the majority (up to 90% in some countries) of breast cancers are diagnosed at a late stage in SSA [6], and radiotherapy is a pivotal treatment for the control of locally advanced breast cancers. Radiotherapy can also have an impact on the acceptability of care. Indeed, in a study conducted in Nigeria [26], fear of mastectomy was the primary cause of delay in the management of women with breast cancer. Studies often demonstrate a high rate of mastectomy compared to high-income countries, often due to the absence of radiotherapy, which is the standard of postsurgical care in breast-conserving surgery.

Even when radiotherapy was available, devices often do not comply with international recommendations. We demonstrate that half of the hospitals in our study still use a cobalt accelerator. Cobalt accelerators are suboptimal for radiotherapy treatment in breast cancer care, particularly across SSA. Cobalt's decaying dose rate and higher rates of grade 3 to 5 toxicities limit its use in clinical situations compared with linear accelerators. Moreover, there are security concerns as the active source continues to produce radiation and has a high potential for harm. These therefore require specific security measures for source transfer, disposal and the vault that host such units [27].

Another issue is the fact that the devices sometimes fail. In a previous study, the radiotherapy machine at one of Uganda’s main cancer centers was unavailable for 18 months, leading to only 15% of patients with breast cancer having access to radiotherapy [7].

Access to chemotherapy and systemic therapies

Breast cancer in African and Afro-descendant women is thought to be a more aggressive disease, occurring in younger women with hormone receptor staining associated with poorer prognoses [28]. According to studies carried out in the USA, breast cancer mortality in black women is the highest of all ethnic groups and is 40% higher than in white women [28], creating one of the largest disparities in oncological disease. These aggressive tumors often require excellent access to chemotherapy and radiotherapy to optimize treatment and improve outcomes [6]. Across SSA this is critical, as most breast cancer patients presenting with later stage disease and the role of surgery as a curative measure is vastly reduced [8].

In our study, only half of the hospitals had chemotherapy drugs available on site. Unfortunately, even when chemotherapy is offered, limited availability and treatment costs lead to high rates of sub-optimal treatment from reduced treatment courses and premature termination of cycles [8]. In Eritrea more than 97% of patients received surgical intervention as their only treatment because there were no chemotherapy or radiotherapy options available nationally [8, 29].

Chemotherapy is cost prohibitive for most patients on the African continent [6]. The discovery of HER 2-targeted therapy, such as trastuzumab or pertuzumab, and more recently immunotherapy, such as pembrolizumab, has revolutionized the management of certain subtypes of breast cancer. However, despite the inclusion of some of those systemic therapies in the World Health Organization (WHO) essential drug list, the cost of these life-saving drugs remains, unfortunately, high [6] and therefore inaccessible for most patients in SSA. Tamoxifen, a very low-cost hormonal therapy sometimes available free of charge, is one of the most accessible treatment options across SSA. Unfortunately, in black women, the most common type of breast cancer seems to be triple-negative breast cancer, which is known to be much more aggressive and less sensitive to hormonal therapies [30].

MDT

Our study found that MDTs are not available in most hospitals treating breast cancers in SSA. Even when MDTs were available, there was significant variation in the number of patients discussed. This could partially explain the excess breast cancer mortality in SSA. In some settings, MDT care was reported to intercept 98.8% of all medication errors and improve the quality of care [31]. In the United Kingdom, for example, the introduction of MDTs and the resulting improvement of processes and treatment pathways has reduced breast cancer mortality by 18.0% [5].

One of the most important aspects of MDT care is the involvement of a wide range of team members with different expertise in oncological disease management. In breast cancer services in the UK must include a minimum of MDT core members for breast cancer care include clinical and medical oncologists, breast surgeons, radiologists, pathologists and breast care nurse specialists [31]. We found the most frequently missing practitioners at MDTs were nurse oncology specialists, however it is known that specialist nurses are an important aspect of a multidisciplinary team. An online qualitative survey was conducted in Australia determined that MDT team members in gynecological oncology valued specialist nurse contribution to both the team and the management of patients and identified specialist oncology nurses as “the glue of the team” [32].

Access to palliative care and opiate prescriptions

We demonstrate that access to opiate pain medication likely to be inaccessible in the world’s poorest region, despite this being a relatively inexpensive and deemed an essential medicine [33]. According to a scoping review of palliative care in Africa [34], the limited availability of opioids in Africa is secondary to strict regulations on opioid use and prescription in some countries, while others require specific licenses to prescribe or administer morphine. At a hospital level, training in pain management and the presence of a palliative care team may improve morphine prescription [35].

Pathway and financial access to care

The pathways that patients take to breast cancer treatment in SSA are numerous and often involve obstacles related to both patient- and system-related factors. In addition to diagnostic delays, there are further delays between diagnosis and the commencement of treatment [2, 36]. Understanding these barriers would support global and country-level decision-makers in planning programs to improve oncological treatment [37].

Limitations of the study

Although we examined 47 hospitals from 15 countries, we cannot ensure that our sample is representative of all hospitals treating breast cancer in SSA. In addition, selection bias may be present as included hospitals were all performing breast cancer surgery. Furthermore, certain aspects of breast cancer management were not covered, which include immunohistochemistry, access to immunotherapy and targeted therapy. Moreover, it is important to note that the data for this study were collected in 2019. Consequently, these findings may not fully reflect the current state of breast cancer management in SSA, as significant changes in healthcare infrastructure, treatment availability, or policy implementation may have occurred since then.

However, this work estimates the availability and quality of breast cancer treatment that were not previously known across SSA. This can be used to inform public health authorities on breast cancer management and to improve models for access to breast cancer treatment across SSA.

Conclusion

We obtained information on breast cancer management services from 47 hospitals, 15 countries and 43 cities across SSA and found inequities in access to breast cancer care. Improving breast cancer survival in SSA is an emergency that cannot be challenged without the strengthening of hospital infrastructure and resources [15]. These results also show that a strategy of strengthening hospital capabilities in SSA could markedly reduce breast cancer-related mortality.

Surgery is a key component of breast cancer patient management, with its inclusion in national cancer control plans crucial to reduce the premature mortality from breast cancer observed in SSA [19]. On the other hand, challenges such as late-stage presentation, socioeconomic and cultural barriers, limitations in health care financing, and systemic deficiencies continue to hinder efforts to improve breast cancer outcomes in SSA [38]. Finally, addressing inequities in breast cancer treatment will be essential in meeting gender equality Sustainable Development Goals, as well as health and wellbeing objectives [7].
