P38α deficiency alleviates myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by stabilizing c‑Myc to inhibit ferroptosis
Authors: Xiaohu Ouyang, Shuxin Song, Yalan Dong, Mi Xiang, Liangqingqing Yin, Xunxun Wang, Yan Ma, Yating Lu, Yaxuan Qi, Qingrui Nong, Hao Chen, Ruonan Yu, Alexey Sarapultsev, Xiang Cheng, Shanshan Luo, Desheng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury constitutes a major clinical challenge in ischemic heart disease, and ferroptosis has been recognized as a core driver of cardiomyocyte death during reperfusion. However, the upstream regulatory network governing myocardial ferroptosis remains incompletely defined. The mitogen-activated protein kinase 14 (p38α, MAPK14) is a stress-activated kinase critically involved in cardiac pathophysiology, yet its role in I/R-induced ferroptosis remains unclear. Here, we report that p38α deficiency attenuates ferroptosis and protects against myocardial I/R injury by stabilizing c-Myc. Phosphorylated p38α (p-p38α) was significantly elevated in mouse hearts after I/R and in cardiomyocytes following oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R). Cardiomyocyte-specific p38α knockout markedly reduced ferroptosis, myocardial infarct size, and cardiac dysfunction. Mechanistically, p-p38α interacts with c-Myc under I/R stress via key residues Arg291, Arg300, and Lys304 of c-Myc and promotes the recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1, thereby enhancing ubiquitination at Lys51 (K51) of c-Myc and its proteasomal degradation. Loss of p38α stabilizes c-Myc protein and reverses this process. Furthermore, c-Myc acts as a transcriptional repressor of NCOA4, a core mediator of ferroptosis. I/R-induced downregulation of c-Myc relieves NCOA4 suppression, triggering iron overload, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis. Overexpression of c-Myc or inhibition of STUB1 or NCOA4 abolished the pro-ferroptotic effect of I/R. Pretreatment with the p38α inhibitor VX-745 recapitulated the cardioprotective effects by restoring the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis in vivo. Collectively, our findings identify the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 signaling axis as a regulatory cascade governing myocardial I/R-induced ferroptosis. These findings nominate the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis as a potential therapeutic target in myocardial I/R injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00395-026-01205-5.Keywords: Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, p38α, Ferroptosis, c-Myc, NCOA4

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden [57]. Prompt restoration of coronary blood flow through reperfusion remains the standard strategy for limiting infarct size and preserving cardiac function [57]. However, reperfusion itself triggers a complex wave of secondary injury, collectively termed myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, which exacerbates cardiomyocyte death, myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and contractile dysfunction [45, 57]. Despite major advances in reperfusion therapy, effective interventions that specifically target the molecular basis of reperfusion injury remain limited.

Among the multiple forms of regulated cell death implicated in myocardial I/R injury, ferroptosis has emerged as a particularly important contributor to cardiomyocyte loss during reperfusion [2, 36, 51]. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by phospholipid peroxidation, redox collapse, and dysregulated iron handling [13, 54]. In the heart, growing evidence indicates that ferroptosis intersects with inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial stress, and metabolic dysfunction, thereby amplifying tissue injury after reperfusion [7, 13]. The canonical ferroptotic machinery centers on the system xc⁻ /glutathione/GPX4 axis, whereas ferritinophagy provides an upstream mechanism for mobilizing redox-active iron [48, 53, 55]. NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, a selective autophagy pathway whereby NCOA4 targets ferritin for lysosomal hydrolysis to release sequestered iron, is, therefore, increasingly recognized as a mechanistic bridge between iron homeostasis and ferroptotic execution in cardiovascular disease [13, 17].

The p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is a canonical stress-responsive cascade activated by ischemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation [29]. Recent preclinical studies using mouse models have elucidated the complex, isoform-specific, and sex-dependent roles of cardiac p38 MAPK in the context of myocardial I/R injury [23, 26, 35]. In general, sustained activation of p38α drives cardiomyocyte death and myocardial damage in male mice, whereas p38β activation elicits cardioprotection, an effect reliant on endogenous estrogen signaling in female hearts [29, 32]. Furthermore, transient activation of p38 initiates downstream NF-κB/iNOS/NO signaling pathways, which induce delayed myocardial preconditioning and confer long-term cardiac protection [58]. This suggests that the biological outcomes of p38 signaling depend not merely on protein expression, but also on the magnitude and persistence of kinase activation [27, 39, 40]. During early ischemic stress, low-level transient p38α upregulation delivers mild cardioprotection via phosphorylating Cx43 and HSP27 to stabilize myocardial gap junctions [27, 39, 40]. However, excessive and robust activation of p38α upon reperfusion triggers multiple deleterious signaling cascades, exacerbating irreversible myocardial injury [9, 34]. Furthermore, whether p38α also functions as an upstream regulator of myocardial ferroptosis remains insufficiently defined. This question has become more relevant in light of recent work linking p38-associated stress signaling to ferroptosis regulation in several biological contexts, including redox stress and inflammatory signaling [7, 46]. Thus, p38α may represent an important missing link between stress sensing and ferroptotic execution in the reperfusion heart.

c-Myc is a pleiotropic transcription factor that governs metabolism, cell survival, and stress adaptation, and its activity is tightly regulated at the post-translational level, particularly through ubiquitination-dependent turnover [14, 18]. While c-Myc has been associated with ferroptosis in various pathological conditions, including cancer, renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, and intervertebral disc degeneration, its involvement in ferroptosis induced by myocardial I/R injury remains insufficiently investigated [22, 49, 56]. Likewise, while NCOA4 is recognized as a core ferritinophagy receptor and ferroptosis effector, the upstream transcriptional control of NCOA4 in cardiomyocytes under I/R stress has not been clarified [19, 48]. These unresolved points suggest that an uncharacterized signaling axis may connect p38α activation, c-Myc destabilization, and NCOA4-dependent ferroptosis during reperfusion injury.

In this study, we show that p-p38α is markedly upregulated in I/R-challenged hearts and OGD/R-exposed cardiomyocytes. Cardiomyocyte-specific p38α deficiency alleviates I/R-induced ferroptosis, reduces infarct size, and preserves cardiac function. Mechanistically, p-p38α interacts with c-Myc in the nucleus via Arg291, Arg300, and Lys304 of c-Myc and promotes its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1, leading to enhanced ubiquitination at Lys51 (K51) of c-Myc and its proteasomal degradation. Stabilized c-Myc represses NCOA4 expression, whereas I/R-induced c-Myc loss relieves this repression, driving iron overload and ferroptosis. Pretreatment with VX-745 recapitulates these protective effects in vivo. Together, these findings identify the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis as a previously unrecognized pathway governing myocardial ferroptosis in I/R injury.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 J wild‑type (WT) mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory (Beijing, China). Cardiomyocyte‑specific p38α‑knockout mice (Myh6-Cre, p38α flox/flox; designated as p38α‑CKO) were kindly provided by Huazhong Agricultural University. Briefly, the floxed Mapk14 (p38α) allele was engineered by strategically inserting a loxP site in the intron located upstream of Exon 2 and an additional loxP site within the intron downstream of Exon 3. The expression of Cre recombinase was specifically targeted to cardiomyocytes under the regulation of the endogenous Myh6 promoter. This Cre-mediated homologous recombination results in the deletion of the loxP-flanked Exon 2 to Exon 3 region of Mapk14 exclusively in cardiomyocytes, thereby abrogating the expression of functional p38α. The mice were maintained in an environment with regulated temperature (22–25℃) and humidity (40%-60%), following a 12-h light/dark cycle. They were provided with ad libitum access to food and water. For all experiments, male mice aged 8 to 10 weeks and weighing between 24 and 27 g were utilized.

Myocardial I/R injury model

Pentobarbital sodium was administered to mice by intraperitoneal injection for general anesthesia. After tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, a thoracotomy was conducted, and the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) was ligated with a 7‑0 silk suture. Ischemia was maintained for 45 min, followed by 24 h of reperfusion. Sham-operated mice underwent identical surgery with sutures inserted into myocardium without LAD ligation.

Animal grouping and VX-745 administration

Mice were randomly allocated into four experimental cohorts, each comprising six subjects (n = 6 per group): the sham-operated group (Sham), the sham-operated group treated with VX-745 (HY-10328, MedChemExpress) (Sham + VX-745), the myocardial I/R injury group (I/R), and the I/R group treated with VX-745 (I/R + VX-745). In accordance with the formulation protocol provided by MedChemExpress (MCE), VX-745 powder was freshly dissolved in a vehicle composed of 50% polyethylene glycol 300 (PEG300) and 50% sterile normal saline to prepare the working drug solution. According to established protocols [11, 30], VX-745 was administered intragastrically at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight, 2 h prior to the commencement of myocardial ischemia surgery. To control for potential confounding effects of the solvent, animals in the Sham and I/R vehicle control groups received an equivalent volume of the blank 50% PEG300-saline vehicle via oral gavage at the corresponding time point.

Cell lines, plasmids and lentiviruses

HL-1 cells were purchased from Stem Recell (Shanghai, China; catalog no. STM-CL-6052). Plasmids overexpressing the target genes and the negative control vector (NC-oe) were constructed using the pT3-EF1a backbone to overexpress the mouse p38α (ID 1432), c-Myc (ID 4609), NCOA4 (ID 8031), c-Myc R291A, c-Myc R300A, c-Myc K304A, c-Myc K51R, c-Myc K149R and c-Myc K326R genes. Plasmids expressing short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting the genes of interest and the negative control vector (NC-sh) were constructed using the pLenti‑U6‑shRNA-CMV-GPF-2A-Puro backbone to knockdown the mouse p38α, c-Myc, NCOA4, and STUB1 (ID 10273) genes. The c-Myc knockout lentiviral was constructed using the pLV‑u6‑sgRNA1‑H1‑sgRNA2-EF1a-spCas9-P2A-Puro backbone. These plasmids or lentiviral vectors were packaged into HL-1 cells and neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs). When cells reached 70–80% confluency, two separate transfection/transduction procedures were performed. For plasmid transfection, plasmids were mixed with Opti-MEM (Beyotime, cat. no. C2753) and Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime, cat. no. C0533) before being added to cells. To perform lentiviral transduction, target cells were cultured with lentivirus and polybrene (MCE, cat. no. HY-112735) for the indicated incubation period.

Hypoxia-reoxygenation protocol in vitro

To mimic myocardial I/R injury in vitro, cardiomyocytes were subjected to OGD/R as previously described [50]. Briefly, cells at 70–80% confluence were incubated in pre-warmed serum-and glucose-free DMEM (Gibco, Cat# 11,966–025) in a hypoxic chamber flushed with 100% N₂ at 37 °C for 4 h. For reoxygenation, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM (Gibco, Cat# 11,965–092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Cat# 10,099–141) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15,140–122), and cells were incubated at 37 °C under 17% O₂ and 5% CO2 for 4 h. Normoxic control cells were maintained in complete DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 24 h prior to analysis.

Isolation of neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes

Hearts were harvested, minced in ice-cold Ca2⁺/Mg2⁺-free Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Gibco, Cat# 14,170,112), and digested with 0.125% trypsin (Gibco, Cat# 25,200,072) at 37 °C for 10 min. The supernatant from the first digestion was discarded, and the remaining tissue was sequentially digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase type II (Solarbio, Cat# C8150) at 37 °C. Released cardiomyocytes were collected in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Cat# 11,965,092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Cat# 10,099,141). This digestion-collection cycle was repeated 3–4 times until complete tissue dissociation. The pooled cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, resuspended in fresh complete DMEM, and plated in uncoated 35-mm dishes for 1.5 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 to allow differential adhesion of non-myocytes. Suspended cardiomyocytes were harvested from the supernatant, seeded on laminin-coated (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# L2020) culture dishes, and cultured in complete DMEM at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h prior to downstream experiments.

Adult mouse cardiomyocytes isolation and treatment

8 weeks old male mice were employed for the isolation of primary cardiomyocytes. The isolation protocol was adapted from previously published study, utilizing the Langendorff-free perfusion method [16]. In brief, the mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital and secured on a dissection plate. The inferior vena cava and abdominal aorta were incised to facilitate blood drainage, followed by thoracotomy to fully expose the heart. Pre-warmed ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer was gradually injected into the right ventricular apex at a controlled flow rate, and the ascending aorta was clamped using forceps. The clamped heart was then transferred to a culture dish, where EDTA buffer was continuously perfused into the left ventricle. Subsequently, the heart was transferred to a perfusion buffer and then to a preheated collagenase buffer, maintaining a consistent perfusion rate of 4–5 mL/min until the myocardium appeared pale and softened. Following digestion, the aortic clamp was removed, and the ventricles were sectioned into 1 mm3 pieces and gently pipetted. Digestion was halted by the addition of a stop buffer, and the resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm strainer. Rod-shaped viable cardiomyocytes were enriched through multiple rounds of natural gravity sedimentation, each lasting 20 min, conducted at room temperature. The purified cardiomyocytes were then subjected to a sequential reintroduction of calcium using gradient calcium buffers to prevent calcium overload. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in a laminin-coated plating medium and cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Adenoviral vectors for the knockdown of mouse p38α (Adshp38α), c-Myc (Adshc-Myc), and STUB1 (AdshSTUB1) were developed utilizing the pDC316-U6-shRNA-CMV-EGFP backbone. Concurrently, plasmids for the overexpression of mouse c-Myc (Adc-Myc) and NCOA4 (AdNCOA4) were constructed using the pDC316-CMV-MCS-P2A-EGFP backbone. These constructs were employed to infect adult mouse primary cardiomyocytes at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 virus particles per cell, facilitating both gene knockdown and overexpression experiments.

RNA-seq profiling and analysis

Cardiomyocytes were transfected with shp38α or shControl plasmids. After transfection, samples were prepared and sent to Servicebio (Wuhan, China) for total RNA isolation, mRNA purification, library construction, and high-throughput sequencing. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software to compare the two groups (shp38α vs. shControl), with the threshold set as q < 0.05 and |log₂FC|≥ 1. All differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, which was conducted against the genome background and defined by the hypergeometric test (p < 0.05). A subset of DEGs was selected to generate a hierarchical clustering heatmap for the visualization of gene expression patterns.

Cell viability and LDH assay

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, C0048) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with the CCK-8 reagent at 37 °C for 2 h. Optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytotoxicity was assessed with a Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C0017), which quantifies cell membrane damage by measuring the activity of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released into the culture supernatant, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Western-blotting

Cultured cells or heart tissues were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then homogenized in Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) Lysis Buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1% (v/v) protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, USA). Tissue lysates were prepared from infarcted regions. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein lysates were separated by 10 or 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST (Servicebio, G0004), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Protech, #SA00001-1/2, 1:3000) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Finally, blots were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Invitrogen, USA). Band intensities were quantified by ImageJ software, and values were normalized to the corresponding loading controls. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-p-p38α (Proteintech, 28,796), anti-GPX4 (Proteintech, 30,388), anti-p38α (Proteintech, 14,064), anti-β-Tubulin (Proteintech, 10,094), anti-SLC40A1 (Proteintech, 26,601), anti-ACSL3 (Proteintech, 20,710), anti-HMOX1 (Proteintech, 10,701), anti-c-Myc (MCE, HY-P80626), anti-p–c-Myc (Ser62) (MCE, HY-P80511), anti-STUB1 (Proteintech, 55,430), anti-Ubiquitin (Proteintech, 10,201), and anti-NCOA4 (HUABIO, ER62707).

Real-time PCR assay

Total RNA is isolated from cardiomyocytes or tissues based on trizol (Beyotime, R0016) and then converted into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R201–01). Real- time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed by SYBR Green assay (Vazyme, Q713). The expression levels of the target genes were normalized to the TUBB3 gene and are presented as 2-ΔΔCt values. The forward and reverse PCR primers for TUBB3 (mouse) were 5′- CATCAGCGATGAGCACGGCATA -3′ and 5′- GGTTCCAAGTCCACCAGAATGG -3′, the primers for MYC (mouse) were 5’- TCGCTGCTGTCCTCCGAGTCC-3′ and 5′-GGTTTGCCTCTTCTCCACAGAC-3′, the primers for STUB1 (mouse) were 5’- GCTGGAACAGTATCGAGGAACG-3′ and 5′- TTCCGCTGACACTCCTCCAGTT -3′, the primers for NCOA4 (mouse) were 5′- TGCCATTGGTCTTCAGGCTCCT -3′ and 5′- CAGGCATCGCTGAAGAAACTGC-3′, the primers for TFRC (mouse) were 5’- GAAGTCCAGTGTGGGAACAGGT -3′ and 5′-CAACCACTCAGTGGCACCAACA-3′, the primers for ACSL4 (mouse) were 5’- CCTTTGGCTCATGTGCTGGAAC -3′ and 5′- GCCATAAGTGTGGGTTTCAGTAC -3′.

Infarct size measurements

Surgical procedures for I/R were performed according to established protocols [24]. At 24 h post-surgery, mice were euthanized, and the heart was rapidly excised. The coronary artery was re-occluded at the site of the initial occlusion, and 2 mL of 1% Evans blue (Beyotime, Y025609) was continuously perfused into the inferior vena cava for 3 min. The heart was then removed and washed twice, followed by freezing at − 80 °C for 10 min and cutting into 1 mm-thick sections. Sections were incubated with 1% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC; Beyotime, C0651) at 37 °C for 30 min and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Beyotime, P0099) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the hearts were photographed under a microscope, and the infarct area was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.38 × ; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The remote area was stained blue, the area at risk was stained red, and the infarcted area was stained white. The left ventricular area was also measured using ImageJ software.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 2100 system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) equipped with an MS-400 imaging transducer. Mice were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane, and the chest hair was removed to expose the heart. Mice were placed in a supine position on an echogenic pad to minimize procedural bias and maintained under anesthesia during image acquisition. Echocardiographic measurements were obtained at 24 h after reperfusion in the myocardial I/R group. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) were determined by direct M-mode tracing of the left ventricular endocardial border in the parasternal long-axis view. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) were also calculated. LVEF was defined as the ratio of systolic to diastolic cardiac output. LVFS was calculated as the ratio of the difference between left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD) to LVEDD. All echocardiographic data were acquired and analyzed by a technician blinded to the experimental groups.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

The samples were analyzed by immunohistochemistry using anti-p38α (proteintech, 14,064) and anti-4-HNE (MCE, HY-P81208) antibody. Two independent researchers evaluated the immunostaining results. According to the established procedure, all tissues were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution and then embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin was then sliced into 4-μm thick slices, incubated overnight with the specified primary antibody, and then incubated with the secondary antibody.

Immunofluorescence

Initially, cells were placed on glass coverslips and treated with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min to fix them. After fixation, the cells were treated with PBS solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, ST1723) for 20 min and then incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime, ST2249) solution for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibody. After PBS washing, cells were further probed with fluorescent secondary antibodies (Abcam, ab150077). The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1005). Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1 plus) equipped with a 60 × oil immersion objective lens. The signals were excited using 488 nm and 594 nm lasers, respectively, with emission collected through 500–550 nm and 570–620 nm filters. Images were captured at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels and no additional contrast enhancement or gamma correction was applied. The following primary antibodies were used in this assay: anti-p-p38α (proteintech, 28,796), anti- GPX4 (proteintech, 30,388), anti-cTnT (proteintech, 68,300), anti-c-Myc (proteintech, 16,286), anti-c-Myc (proteintech, 67,447), anti-STUB1 (proteintech, 55,430), anti-NCOA4 (proteintech, 10,968).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated 2’-deoxyuridine 5’-triphosphate nick end labeling assay of myocardial tissue

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated 2’-deoxyuridine 5’ triphosphate nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays were performed using the One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, #E-CK-A422) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cardiomyocytes were stained fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled wheat germ agglutinin lectin. The sections were subsequently photographed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1 plus). Statistical analysis was performed using Image J and GraphPad Prism9.

Transmission electron microscopy

At 24 h after reperfusion, myocardial tissue was fixed with glutaraldehyde overnight. Then it was fixed with 1% osmic acid at 4 °C for 2–3 h, dehydrated with gradient alcohol (50%, 70%, 80%, 85%, 90%, 95%, 100%), soaked overnight with acetone and epoxy resin, and finally embedded and cured with epoxy resin. The tissue was cut into100 nm-thick sections and stained with lead and uranium. The mitochondrial morphology of cardiomyocytes was observed using a frozen trans mission electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher, Glacios). The damaged and undamaged mitochondria were counted in a field of view with a magnification of 1700 times within non-repeating unit areas in the infarct area and quantified using Image J.

Biochemical and ELISA assays

Whole blood (approximately 300 μL) was collected from each animal and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Afterwards, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate serum. The concentrations of creatine kinase and creatine kinase isoenzyme, aspartate aminotransferase, α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase, alanine aminotransferase, urea nitrogen, and creatinine in serum were determined according the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing JianCheng). HL-1 cells were cultured in 6-well plates with a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Cells were harvested and lysed after the corresponding process. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation to detect the level of Glutathione (Beyotime, S0053), Malondialdehyde (Beyotime, S0131), Dehydrogenase (Beyotime, C0019) and 12-HETE (Abcam, ab133034) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Iron, ROS, and lipid ROS levels

Iron, ROS, and lipid ROS levels were detected using Fe2Orange (MCE, HY-D1913), ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033), and BODIPY 581/591 C11 (MCE, HY-D1301). The cells were cultured in 6-well plates and left to rest overnight. After replacing the medium with fluorescent dye, the cells were incubated for 30 min. To remove excess fluorescent dye, rinse twice with PBS and then suspend in 300 μL PBS. Then, the fluorescence intensity of each sample was measured using the Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX, and CytExpert 2.4 software was used to interpret the results.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

For co-immunoprecipitation assay, cells or heart tissue lysates were mixed with ice-cold immune-precipitation buffer (Beyotime, #P0013J) containing protease and phosphorylase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, USA). The mixture was then incubated with the target antibody at 4 °C overnight under constant agitation. Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, #P2012) were incubated with the lysates for 3 h at 4 °C followed by washing, and then lysed with 1 × sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis sample buffer. The supernatant was subsequently used for western-blotting with corresponding antibodies: anti-c-Myc (MCE, YA497), anti-p62-c-Myc (MCE, YA211), anti-p-p38α (proteintech, 28,796), anti-STUB1 (proteintech, 55,430), anti-ubiquitin (proteintech, 10,201).

c-Myc ubiquitination detection

For cellular ubiquitination assays, cells were treated with 10 μM proteasome inhibitor MG132 (MCE, HY-13259) and incubated for an additional 12 h before harvest. Collected cells were lysed in pre-chilled immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (Beyotime, #P0013J) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, MCE, HY-D0843). For cardiac tissue samples harvested post-reperfusion, heart tissues were homogenized using the identical IP buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and 10 mM NEM. All cell and tissue lysates were incubated with the primary antibody against the target protein overnight at 4 °C under constant rotation. Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, #P2012) were then added to the mixture and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. The beads underwent five washes with an IP buffer. After complete removal of the washing solution, 1 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer was supplemented to the beads, and the samples were heated to dissociate bound proteins for subsequent western blotting with anti-ubiquitin antibody (Proteintech, 10,201).

Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN)

HL-1 and NMCMs cells were seeded into 12-well plates and transfected with overexpression plasmids c-Myc and knockdown plasmids c-Myc using Lipo8000™ (Beyotime, #C0533) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, AMCMs cells were seeded into 12-well plates and transduced with c-Myc overexpression and knockdown adenoviruses, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The following day, cells were treated with OGD/R or not. After that, cells were collected for CUT&RUN experiments using Hyperactive pG-MNase CUT and RUN Assay Kit (Vazyme, #HD101-01). Briefly, 1 × 105 fresh cells were collected and washed once in 100 µL of washing buffer, followed by bound to Concanavalin A (ConA) magnetic beads for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Cells were then treated with anti-c-Myc (MCE, HY-P80626) at 4 °C overnight. The following day, cells were washed twice with digitonin (DIG) washing buffer and incubated with pG-MNase enzyme at 4 °C for 1 h. After twice DIG washing, cells were incubated with CaCl2 at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by incubated with a stop buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, beads were gathered, and DNA was eluted from the beads. Finally, qPCR tests were conducted following the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates rather than technical duplicates. For in vivo experiments, n refers to the number of individual animals per group; for in vitro experiments, n refers to independent experiments performed on separate cell preparations or passages. Samples were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during data acquisition and analysis. Prior to parametric statistical analyses, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for multi-group comparisons, while two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was applied for analyses containing two independent variables. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied to assess the relationship between two non-normally distributed continuous variables. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

I/R injury triggers p38α activation and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes

To elucidate the expression pattern of p38α in cardiac tissue, we analyzed public single-cell RNA sequencing data and immunohistochemical data obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. Consistently, these data from mRNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry collectively confirm the widespread distribution of p38α in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1a, Fig. S1a). Subsequently, we developed murine myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) models and in vitro oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) models to replicate cardiac I/R stress (Fig. 1b). Histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed significant myocardial structural damage and inflammatory cell infiltration in I/R-affected hearts (Fig. S1b). In the hearts of mice subjected to I/R, there was a notable upregulation of phosphorylated p38α (p-p38α) and a marked downregulation of the ferroptosis marker GPX4, with a significant inverse correlation observed between p-p38α and GPX4 levels (Fig. 1c, d). Immunofluorescence staining corroborated the increased presence of p-p38α and the reduced levels of GPX4 in I/R-injured myocardium (Fig. 1e, f). Similarly, OGD/R treatment in HL-1 cells and primary neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes (NMCMs) resulted in analogous alterations, characterized by elevated p-p38α, decreased GPX4, and a significant negative correlation between these two proteins (Fig. 1g–k, Fig. S1c–f). The findings indicate that I/R stress simultaneously activates p38α signaling and induces ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes, implying a potential functional association between p38α signaling pathways and myocardial ferroptosis.

Cardiomyocyte‑specific p38α deficiency alleviates myocardial I/R injury

To elucidate the functional role of p38α in I/R injury, we conducted loss-of-function assays in HL-1 cells and developed cardiomyocyte-specific p38α knockout mice (p38α-CKO). HL-1 cells were transfected with shControl, shp38α, oeControl, and oep38α plasmids for 36 h. Western blot analyses revealed that oep38α transfection elevated p38α protein levels, whereas shp38α transfection significantly reduced p38α expression (Fig. S2a, b). Furthermore, p38α knockdown resulted in a marked decrease in phosphorylated p38α (p-p38α) protein levels, while p38α overexpression led to a less pronounced increase in p-p38α (Fig. S2b). Immunohistochemical staining corroborated a significant knockout of p38α protein in the cardiac tissues of p38α-CKO mice compared to the wild-type (WT) group (Fig. S2c). In vitro, p38α knockdown enhanced cell viability and diminished reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in HL-1 cells subjected to OGD/R treatment (Fig. 2a-b). The CCK8 assay demonstrated that NMCMs from p38α-CKO mice exhibited higher activity than those from the WT group following OGD/R exposure (Fig. 2c). In vivo studies demonstrated that p38α-CKO mice exhibited reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and smaller infarct sizes compared to WT mice following I/R injury, as evidenced by H&E and Evans blue-TTC staining (Fig. 2d–e). Echocardiographic assessments indicated that the absence of p38α significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS), suggesting enhanced cardiac contractile function (Fig. 2f, g). Furthermore, the proportion of apoptotic cardiomyocytes, identified through TUNEL-positive staining, was decreased in p38α-CKO mice post-I/R exposure (Fig. 2h–i). Additionally, p38α-CKO mice demonstrated lower serum concentrations of cardiac injury biomarkers, including creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alpha-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (α-HBDH), compared to WT mice (Fig. 2j, Fig. S2d). These data confirm that p38α deficiency protects against myocardial I/R injury by reducing cardiomyocyte death and preserving cardiac function.

Inhibition of p38α protects cardiomyocytes from I/R‑induced ferroptosis

To identify downstream effectors of p38α involved in ferroptosis regulation, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in HL-1 cells. KEGG pathway analysis showed that genes downregulated in shp38α HL-1 cells were significantly enriched in the ferroptosis pathway, thereby nominating ferroptosis-related signaling as a candidate mechanism for subsequent functional validation (Fig. 3a). To further explore the involvement of p38α in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, we performed both in vitro and in vivo loss-of-function experiments. Erastin, a well-recognized activator of ferroptosis, induces this process across various cell types [15]. The CCK8 assay demonstrated that p38α knockdown alleviated erastin-induced ferroptosis in HL-1 cells (Fig. 3b). Additionally, p38α knockdown attenuated the increases in malondialdehyde (MDA) and LDH levels triggered by OGD/R, while restoring glutathione (GSH) levels (Fig. 3c–e). Flow cytometry analyses further confirmed that p38α knockdown significantly reduced Fe 2⁺ production and lipid peroxidation in HL-1 cells subjected to OGD/R (Fig. 3f, g). Western blot analyses demonstrate that p38α knockdown modulates the expression levels of proteins associated with ferroptosis, as evidenced by elevated levels of SLC40A1, GPX4, and ACSL3, and reduced levels of HMOX1 in comparison to the control group following OGD/R (Fig. 3h). Subsequently, we investigated the biological functions of p38α in p38α-CKO mice after I/R injury. Consistent with the in vitro results, the protein levels of SLC40A1, GPX4, and ACSL3 were restored, whereas the protein level of HMOX1 was diminished in p38α-CKO mice relative to WT controls subjected to I/R (Fig. 3i). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed reduced levels of 4-HNE and restored GPX4 expression in p38α-CKO hearts (Fig. 3j, k), and iron staining showed diminished iron accumulation (Fig. 3l). Collectively, these findings establish that p38α inhibition suppresses I/R‑induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes.

p-p38α regulates myocardial I/R-induced ferroptosis through its interaction with c-Myc

The activated p-p38α translocates into the nucleus and modulates the activities of various transcription factors through three distinct mechanisms: direct phosphorylation, indirect regulation mediated by downstream kinases, and modulation of transcriptional complex assembly [21, 37]. To investigate the downstream signaling events of p-p38α, we extracted the expression levels of p-p38α target genes from RNA-seq datasets and visualized their expression trends using a heatmap (Fig. 4a). These target genes included Ets1, Pax6, Mef2d, Atf2, c-Myc, and Mef2a, among which c-Myc has been extensively implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis [10, 43, 44, 49]. Based on this, we hypothesized that p-p38α may regulate ferroptosis through modulating c-Myc expression. Notably, the stability of c-Myc protein in mammalian cells is tightly controlled by phosphorylation at serine 62 (Ser62), a highly conserved residue [12]. OGD/R treatment significantly upregulated c-Myc mRNA expression while concurrently reducing the protein levels of total c-Myc and Ser62-phosphorylated c-Myc (S62-p–c-Myc); these OGD/R-induced effects were rescued by p38α knockdown (Fig. 4b–d). Furthermore, we demonstrated that 10,058-F4, a small-molecule inhibitor of c-Myc, reversed the p38α knockdown-mediated reduction in ROS levels observed after OGD/R exposure (Fig. S3a). Collectively, these findings suggest that c-Myc may function as a downstream effector of p-p38α in the context of OGD/R-induced cellular responses. To further validate this regulatory relationship, we performed c-Myc knockdown and overexpression experiments. HL-1 cells were transfected with shControl, shc-Myc, oeControl, or oec-Myc plasmids for 36 h. Western blot analysis confirmed that oec-Myc transfection significantly increased c-Myc protein levels, whereas shc-Myc transfection markedly downregulated c-Myc expression (Fig. S3b, c). Functional assays revealed that c-Myc knockdown attenuated the p38α knockdown-induced increase in cardiomyocyte viability in the OGD/R model, while c-Myc overexpression exerted the opposite effect (Fig. 4e). Additionally, OGD/R-induced ferroptotic events in cardiomyocytes, including elevated ROS production, lipid ROS accumulation, and iron overload, were alleviated by p38α knockdown; this protective effect was reversed by c-Myc knockdown and further enhanced by c-Myc overexpression (Fig. 4f, Fig. S3d, e). Taken together, these results confirm that c-Myc serves as a downstream target of p-p38α in the ferroptosis signaling pathway.

To explore whether p-p38α regulates I/R-induced ferroptosis through its interaction with c-Myc, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays combined with mass spectrometry were performed. P38α ranked among the top 10 proteins that co-immunoprecipitated with c-Myc (Fig. 4g). Co-IP assays further confirmed that endogenous p-p38α interacts with c-Myc, whereas no interaction was detected with control IgG (Fig. S3f). Immunofluorescence staining revealed distinct co-localization of p-p38α and c-Myc proteins in the nuclei of cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4h). Moreover, the interaction between p-p38α and c-Myc was significantly enhanced in HL-1 cells after OGD/R treatment, whereas p38α knockdown diminished this interaction (Fig. 4i). In contrast, the interaction between p-p38α and S62-p–c-Myc was markedly reduced in OGD/R-exposed HL-1 cells, and p38α knockdown further attenuated this interaction (Fig. 4j). These results collectively indicate that p-p38α regulates myocardial I/R-induced ferroptosis progression through its interaction with c-Myc.

To predict the binding sites between p-p38α and c-Myc, molecular dynamics simulations were performed. Molecular docking analysis constructed a plausible binding model of the p-p38α-c-Myc complex (Fig. 4k). Analyses of the radius of gyration (Rg), root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) consistently confirmed the formation of a stable p-p38α-c-Myc complex (Fig. S4a–d). Binding free energy decomposition analysis was conducted to identify the key amino acids involved in the p-p38α-c-Myc interaction. This analysis identified MET1, LYS284, ARG285, LYS290, ARG291, ARG300, LYS304, LYS313, ARG314, LYS407, and LYS412 of c-Myc as the principal amino acids mediating the interaction (Fig. 4l). To further define the critical binding sites, we focused on residues ARG291 (R291), ARG300 (R300), and LYS304 (K304) as predicted by molecular docking for higher contribution energy (Fig. 4m). Wild-type (WT) and mutant c-Myc-expressing plasmids (c-Myc R291A, c-Myc R300A, c-Myc K304A) were constructed and transfected into c-Myc knockout (KO) HL-1 cells (Fig. S4e). Mutations at these three sites all partially reversed the OGD/R-induced enhancement of the p-p38α-c-Myc interaction, which was further supported by the recovery of c-Myc protein levels (Fig. 4n). Furthermore, compared to WT c-Myc, expression of c-Myc R291A, c-Myc R300A, or c-Myc K304A significantly reversed OGD/R-induced cardiomyocyte damage, as verified by CCK-8 assay and LDH release detection (Fig. 4o, Fig. S4f). Correspondingly, ferroptosis-related indicators, including GSH and MDA levels, were significantly reversed in cells expressing the mutant c-Myc proteins (Fig. 4p, q). The findings collectively indicate that p-p38α modulates myocardial I/R-induced ferroptosis through a direct interaction with c-Myc, specifically targeting key amino acids to influence c-Myc expression.

p-p38α promotes c‑Myc ubiquitination and degradation via the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1

The homeostasis and functional regulation of the c-Myc protein are critically reliant on post-translational modifications (PTMs), with ubiquitination being one of the most pivotal PTMs influencing its biological activities [8]. MG132, a commonly utilized small-molecule proteasome inhibitor, is employed to impede ubiquitination-mediated protein degradation [42]. Administration of MG132 counteracted the OGD/R-induced elevation in ROS levels, indicating that OGD/R may trigger ferroptosis via the proteasomal pathway (Fig. S5a). Furthermore, MG132 effectively mitigated the OGD/R-induced reduction in c-Myc protein levels, mirroring the effects observed with p38α knockdown (Fig. 5a). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that OGD/R treatment significantly expedited c-Myc protein degradation, whereas p38α knockdown substantially inhibited this degradation process (Fig. 5b, c). To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, ubiquitination assays were conducted to assess the impact of p-p38α on the ubiquitination of c-Myc. The findings indicated that the knockdown of p38α significantly attenuated the increase in c-Myc ubiquitination levels induced by OGD/R (Fig. 5d). Ubiquitin molecules are capable of forming polyubiquitin chains through linkage at lysine residues, a process facilitated by E3 ubiquitin ligases, which catalyze the attachment of polyubiquitin chains to substrate proteins, resulting in polyubiquitinated substrates [52]. We summarized the E3 ubiquitin ligases mediating c-Myc ubiquitination over the past five years and screened them based on RNA sequencing results, with their expression trends visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 5e). In comparison to Control HL-1 cells, the expression levels of Trim55, FBXO33, STUB1, Cry2, and Huwe1 were notably downregulated in shp38α HL-1 cells following OGD/R. Among these, STUB1 was selected for further investigation due to its association with the smallest p-value (Fig. 5e). Subsequently, we examined STUB1 mRNA expression in HL-1 cells following OGD/R treatment and observed a significant upregulation of STUB1, which was reversed upon p38α knockdown (Fig. S5b). Western blot results further confirmed that STUB1 protein levels were significantly increased after OGD/R and inhibited by p38α knockdown (Fig. S5c). To further verify the degradative effect of the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1 on c-Myc protein, we performed STUB1 knockdown experiments: HL-1 cells were transfected with shControl or shSTUB1 plasmids for 36 hours, and Western blot analysis confirmed that shSTUB1#2 significantly downregulated STUB1 expression (Fig. S5d). Importantly, STUB1 knockdown significantly reversed the OGD/R-induced decrease in c-Myc protein levels in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 5f). Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis of c-Myc-binding proteins identified STUB1 as a binding partner of c-Myc (Fig. 5g), which was verified by Co-IP assays in HL-1 cells (Fig. S5e). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed a significant proportional colocalization of STUB1 and c-Myc in OGD/R-induced HL-1 cells (Fig. S5f). Co-IP assays in shp38α-transfected HL-1 cells demonstrated that OGD/R treatment significantly enhanced the interaction between STUB1 and c-Myc, whereas p38α knockdown reversed this enhancement (Fig. 5h). Furthermore, mutations at the key binding sites of c-Myc with p-p38α significantly reversed OGD/R-induced c-Myc degradation and markedly reduced c-Myc ubiquitination levels (Fig. 5i). These findings collectively indicate that p-p38α interacts with c-Myc and facilitates its degradation via STUB1.

Moreover, LC/MS analysis identified several lysine residues (K51, K149, and K326) of c-Myc regulated by STUB1-mediated ubiquitination (Fig. 5j). c‑Myc transcriptionally represses NCOA4 to govern ferroptosis

Although we previously showed p38α modulates cardiomyocytes ferroptosis via c-Myc ubiquitination, the c-Myc-ferroptosis regulatory relationship remains unclear. As a pivotal nuclear transcription factor, c-Myc governs cellular processes and participates in pathogenesis when dysregulated [31]. Based on this, we hypothesized c-Myc directly transcriptionally regulates ferroptosis key mediators. Then we examined the expression levels of ferroptosis-related genes in the RNA-seq results. RNA-seq analysis showed ferroptosis-promoting genes NCOA4, TFRC, and ACSL4 were significantly downregulated in shp38α vs. Control HL-1 cells post-OGD/R (Fig. 6a). The qPCR assays confirmed OGD/R elevated their mRNA levels, reversed by p38α knockdown (Fig. 6b). Notably, only NCOA4 mRNA was upregulated by c-Myc deficiency and downregulated by c-Myc overexpression post-OGD/R, while TFRC and ACSL4 were unaffected (Fig. 6c). Western blot further showed OGD/R increased NCOA4 protein, which was abrogated by p38α knockdown (Fig. 6d). c-Myc inhibition enhanced, while its overexpression suppressed, OGD/R-induced NCOA4 upregulation (Fig. 6e). JASPAR database predicted c-Myc-binding sites in NCOA4 promoter (Fig. 6f). CUT&RUN assays showed OGD/R reduced c-Myc enrichment on NCOA4 promoter; c-Myc knockdown further decreased, while overexpression increased, this binding post-OGD/R (Fig. 6g). Act D assays showed OGD/R enhanced NCOA4 mRNA stability; c-Myc knockdown attenuated, while overexpression accelerated, its degradation (Fig. 6h). These data indicated that c-Myc directly binds to the NCOA4 promoter and transcriptionally represses it.

The p38α/c‑Myc/NCOA4 axis regulates ferroptosis in NMCMs and AMCMs

Following the identification that the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis mediates ferroptosis in HL-1 cardiomyocytes subjected to OGD/R, we further explored whether this same regulatory mechanism is conserved in NMCMs and adult mouse cardiomyocytes (AMCMs) (Fig. 7a). To this end, p38α-CKO NMCMs and AMCMs were employed for subsequent experiments. In alignment with our findings in HL-1 cells, the expression of cardiac c-Myc was significantly reduced following OGD/R in NMCMs, whereas this reduction was effectively reversed by the knockout of p38α (Fig. S7a). Conversely, the expression levels of STUB1 and NCOA4 were substantially increased after OGD/R, and this upregulation was significantly mitigated by the deletion of p38α (Fig. S7a). Similar alterations in the expression of c-Myc, STUB1, and NCOA4 were also observed in AMCMs subjected to OGD/R (Fig. S7b). Genetic ablation of p38α ameliorates OGD/R-induced cardiomyocyte injury and ferroptosis in both NMCMs and AMCMs, as evidenced by improved cell viability, reduced LDH release, preserved GSH levels, and decreased MDA accumulation (Fig S7c–f). Co-IP assays demonstrated that the interaction between p-p38α and c-Myc was significantly enhanced in both NMCMs and AMDMs after OGD/R treatment, respectively (Fig. 7b). Subsequently, we examined the ubiquitination status of c-Myc protein in p38-CKO NMCMs and AMCMs after OGD/R. We observed that OGD/R markedly increased c-Myc ubiquitination in both NMCMs and AMCMs. Notably, p38α knockout significantly reduced this effect in both cell types, with lower c-Myc ubiquitination detected in p38α-CKO cells compared with WT controls after OGD/R stimulation (Fig. 7c). We then used immunoprecipitation to detect the interaction between c-Myc and its E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1. The results indicated that OGD/R treatment significantly enhanced the binding of c-Myc to STUB1, and this process was reversed by p38α knockout in both NMCMs and AMCMs (Fig. 7d). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that p38α can regulate c-Myc ubiquitination in both NMCMs and AMCMs during OGD/R.

To further investigate the association between the p38α/c-Myc axis and the downstream protein NCOA4, a series of gene knockdown and overexpression experiments were performed in NMCMs and AMCMs. In particular, we introduced the respective plasmids into NMCMs and infected AMCMs with corresponding adenoviruses to achieve the knockdown of p38α, c-Myc, and STUB1, as well as the overexpression of c-Myc and NCOA4. Western blot analyses verified effective transfection/transduction efficiency and demonstrated significant changes in the expression levels of these target proteins (Fig. S8a–e). In NMCMs and AMCMs, STUB1 knockdown significantly reversed the OGD/R-induced reduction in c-Myc protein levels (Fig. 7e). We further explored the regulatory effect of c-Myc on NCOA4 under OGD/R conditions. The results showed that c-Myc knockdown significantly increased NCOA4 expression, whereas c-Myc overexpression markedly suppressed it (Fig. 7f). CUT&RUN assays demonstrated that OGD/R treatment reduced c-Myc enrichment on the NCOA4 promoter; c-Myc knockdown further decreased this binding, while c-Myc overexpression increased c-Myc enrichment on the NCOA4 promoter post-OGD/R (Fig. 7g). Act D assays demonstrated that OGD/R conditions increased the stability of NCOA4 mRNA. Furthermore, the knockdown of c-Myc further augmented this stability, while the overexpression of c-Myc expedited the degradation of NCOA4 mRNA (Fig. 7h). Together, these results support promoter-level repression of NCOA4 by c-Myc, while also indicating a possible contribution of c-Myc to NCOA4 mRNA degradation.

Finally, we evaluated the role of the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis in OGD/R-induced injury in NMCMs and AMCMs. The results revealed that OGD/R exposure markedly suppressed the viability of both NMCMs and AMCMs; c-Myc knockdown and NCOA4 overexpression exacerbated this inhibitory effect, while p38α knockdown reversed it (Fig. 7i). However, when p38α was inhibited, further c-Myc knockdown or NCOA4 overexpression impaired the protective effect of p38α knockdown on cardiomyocytes (Fig. 7i). We further measured the levels of LDH, GSH, and MDA to assess cardiomyocyte injury and ferroptosis. After OGD/R treatment, GSH levels were significantly decreased, while LDH and MDA levels were increased. Knockdown of p38α reversed these changes, whereas c-Myc knockdown or NCOA4 overexpression exacerbated them. Furthermore, on the basis of p38α knockdown, additional c-Myc knockdown or NCOA4 overexpression abrogated the protective effects induced by p38α knockdown (Fig. 7j, Fig. S8f, g). These results indicate that the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 axis is critically involved in the process of ferroptosis triggered by OGD/R in NMCMs and AMCMs.

Pharmacological inhibition of p38α by VX-745 mitigates myocardial I/R injury

To further evaluate the translational relevance of our findings, we investigated whether pretreatment with VX-745, a p38α inhibitor, could attenuate cardiac I/R injury by suppressing ferroptosis. VX-745 was orally administered to mice 2 h before ischemia surgery (Fig. 8a). Importantly, pretreatment with VX-745 did not induce any significant hepatic or renal toxicity (Fig. 8b). VX-745 treatment dramatically reduced the levels of CK-MB and LDH in I/R-stressed hearts, indicating alleviated cardiomyocyte injury (Fig. 8c). Meanwhile, I/R-induced myocardial infarction size was significantly attenuated in mice treated with VX-745 (Fig. 8d). Furthermore, echocardiography analysis revealed that I/R-induced cardiac dysfunction was greatly ameliorated after VX-745 treatment (Fig. 8e). Concomitantly, VX-745 treatment suppressed I/R-induced cardiac ferroptosis, as evidenced by reduced accumulation of cardiac iron and lipid peroxidation in heart tissues after I/R injury (Fig. 8f, g). The transmission electron microscope (TEM) furnished ultrastructural evidence of ferroptosis, revealing the disassembly of characteristic mitochondrial cristae in cardiomyocytes subjected to I/R injury. Conversely, treatment with VX-745 was observed to mitigate these mitochondrial damages (Fig. 8h).

Subsequently, we explored whether the therapeutic effects of VX-745 on myocardial I/R injury were also mediated through the p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 pathway. WB results demonstrated that VX-745 significantly reversed the I/R-induced degradation of c-Myc protein and the upregulation of STUB1 and NCOA4 proteins (Fig. 8i). In addition, VX-745 inhibited the I/R-induced increases in the interaction between p-p38α and c-Myc, the interaction between c-Myc and STUB1, and the ubiquitination of c-Myc in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 8j–l). Taken together, these results indicate that the VX-745 treatment contributes to protect cardiomyocytes against ferroptosis and myocardial I/R injury, ultimately promoting cardiac function recovery in the animal model.

Discussion

Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury remains a major barrier limiting the full benefit of reperfusion therapy, and ferroptosis is increasingly recognized as a central contributor to cardiomyocyte death during reperfusion [51]. However, the upstream signaling mechanisms that connect ischemia/reperfusion to ferroptosis execution in cardiomyocytes remain incompletely understood [51]. In the present study, we identify a p38α/c-Myc/NCOA4 signaling axis that links stress kinase activation to ferroptosis in the injured heart. Our data expand the established role of p38α in cardiac pathophysiology by placing it upstream of myocardial ferroptosis. Previous work has primarily associated p38α activation with inflammation, apoptosis, and maladaptive remodeling in the ischemic heart [1, 4, 47]. Importantly, the pathological function of p38α is context-dependent; the view that p38α exclusively exerts detrimental effects in ischemic myocardium appears overly simplistic, as moderate p38α activation is indispensable for cardioprotective ischemic preconditioning, which confers resistance against subsequent severe I/R damage [41]. Here, we show that excessive and sustained p38α activation after reperfusion is closely associated with ferroptosis injury, as evidenced by concordant changes in GPX4, lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation, and ferroptosis-related protein expression. Cardiomyocyte-specific p38α deficiency alleviated these changes and improved infarct size and cardiac function, supporting a causal contribution of p38α to ferroptosis-associated myocardial injury.

A central mechanistic finding of this study is that p-p38α interacts with c-Myc and promotes its destabilization under I/R stress. c-Myc is a short-lived transcription factor whose stability is tightly controlled by post-translational modifications, particularly ubiquitination [6]. Although c‑Myc has been linked to ferroptosis in cancer, its role and upstream regulation in myocardial I/R injury have rarely been explored [28]. We found that p-p38α associates with c-Myc in the nucleus under I/R stress, with key residues mapped to Arg291, Arg300, and Lys304 of c-Myc. This interaction promotes c-Myc degradation by enhancing its association with the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1 and increasing ubiquitination at Lys51 (K51) of c-Myc. Notably, this regulatory module is distinct from canonical c-Myc degradation pathways involving GSK3β and FBW7, highlighting a stress-associated c-Myc degradation cascade linked to p38 activation [5, 20]. Our rescue experiments further support that c-Myc is required for p38α-mediated ferroptosis, as c-Myc overexpression mimics, while c-Myc depletion counteracts the effects of p38α inhibition.

We further identified STUB1 as the E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating p38α-dependent c-Myc degradation in cardiomyocytes. STUB1 is a dual co-chaperone and E3 ligase best known for targeting misfolded proteins, but its role in cardiac stress signaling and ferroptosis remains largely uncharacterized [25]. Our study demonstrates that I/R stress upregulates STUB1 in a p38α-dependent manner; STUB1 interacts with c-Myc and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation. Knockdown of STUB1 stabilizes c-Myc and suppresses ferroptosis, phenocopying p38α inhibition. Equally significant, we identified NCOA4 as the downstream effector through which c-Myc influences ferroptosis. NCOA4 functions as a selective autophagy receptor that facilitates ferritinophagy, the mechanism responsible for the degradation of ferritin to liberate labile iron, which constitutes a rate-limiting step in the process of ferroptosis [38]. While NCOA4 is known to promote ferroptosis in multiple systems, its regulation in the heart has not been fully defined. We demonstrate that c-Myc binds the NCOA4 promoter and is consistent with transcriptional repression. Thus, the present data support a model in which c-Myc is primarily a transcriptional repressor of NCOA4. Functionally, this places c-Myc at the intersection of stress signaling and iron mobilization, and links p38α activation to ferroptosis (Fig. 9).

To enhance translational relevance, we validated that pharmacological inhibition of p38α with VX-745 recapitulates the cardioprotective effects of genetic p38α depletion. VX-745 treatment reduced infarct size, improved cardiac function, suppressed ferroptosis, and restored the p38α/c‑Myc/NCOA4 axis in vivo without detectable hepatic or renal toxicity. These results provide preclinical proof‑of‑concept for targeting p38α as a feasible strategy to mitigate myocardial I/R injury via ferroptosis inhibition. Given that p38α inhibitors are already under clinical evaluation for other indications, our findings support the rapid repurposing of these agents for cardioprotection during reperfusion therapy. Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the potential crosstalk between the p38α/c‑Myc/NCOA4 axis and the canonical system Xc⁻-GSH-GPX4 pathway remains to be explored. Second, the cell‑type specificity of this signaling cascade in cardiac fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells requires further characterization. Third, the efficacy of delayed p38α inhibition (therapeutic administration after reperfusion) and long‑term cardiac remodeling effects need to be evaluated in large animal models. Of note, the cardioprotective effects associated with p38α inhibition, as observed in male murine models, are not replicable in pigs or in patients with NSTEMI/STEMI [3, 23, 33]. Finally, clinical studies are needed to confirm the translational value of targeting this axis in patients suffering from I/R injury.

Overall, our findings identify a novel p38α/c‑Myc/NCOA4 signaling cascade that critically regulates myocardial I/R‑induced ferroptosis. Activated p38α promotes c‑Myc degradation via STUB1, leading to NCOA4‑dependent ferroptosis. Targeting this axis, whether through genetic or pharmacological means, effectively inhibits ferroptosis and provides protection against myocardial I/R injury. These findings deepen our understanding of cardiac ferroptosis regulation and provide a promising, mechanism‑based therapeutic strategy for ischemic heart disease.
