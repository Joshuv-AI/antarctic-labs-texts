When (not) to trust Monte Carlo approximations for hierarchical Bayesian inference
Jack Heinzel, Salvatore Vitale

Abstract
The coming years of gravitational wave astrophysics promises thousands of new detections, which can unlock fundamental scientific insights if the information in each observation can be properly synthesized into a coherent picture. State-of-the-art approaches often accomplish this with hierarchical Bayesian inference. However, this typically relies on Monte Carlo approximations that are already very expensive in current data, and may become prohibitively so in the future. In this paper we show how this process can be understood from a first-principles statistical approach. We derive an error estimator E ^ \hat{E} for quantifying the amount of information that is lost due to the Monte Carlo approximation and recommend that this error is limited to no more than E ^ ≲ 0.2 \hat{E}\lesssim 0.2 bits for reliable inference. We also show that the hierarchical likelihood estimator is biased but may be corrected. Finally, we show some practical examples for inference on synthetic gravitational-wave population inference, demonstrating that simple models with strong assumptions can be much more stable to Monte Carlo uncertainty than those with weaker assumptions. We also provide a pip installable package population-error with which analysts can calculate the error statistics E ^ \hat{E} .

II
Gravitational wave population analysis

Suppose we have a collection of
N_{\rm obs}
data
\{d_{i}\}
which each contains a
GW
signal obscured by noise. Our task is to infer the shape of the underlying population of
GW
sources. The most common approach is Bayesian, where we want to infer the Bayesian posterior probability distribution over the underlying population parameters
\Lambda
, based on the observed data.
\Lambda
are called the
hyperparameters
, and they can refer to physical parameters of the population (e.g. the stellar initial mass function or the efficiency of a common envelope ejection), or they can be purely phenomenological (e.g. the powerlaw index in the
BH
mass function).

The Bayesian posterior probability distribution is computed with Bayes’ theorem

p(\Lambda|\{d_{i}\})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)\pi(\Lambda)}{\int\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda^{\prime})\pi(\Lambda^{\prime})\differential\Lambda^{\prime}},

(2)

where
\pi(\Lambda)
is the prior probability distribution over
\Lambda
, and the denominator is called the evidence and ensures the posterior is a normalized probability distribution. The likelihood
\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)
is the probability of observing the dataset, conditioned on the population described by
\Lambda
.

The typical likelihood function used in
GW
population analysis is

\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)\propto\xi(\Lambda)^{-N_{\rm obs}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}p(d_{i}|\Lambda).

(3)

and is derived in Refs.
(
Farr et al. 2015
;
Mandel et al. 2019
;
Vitale et al. 2020
;
Essick and Fishbach 2024
)
.

p(d_{i}|\Lambda)
is the probability of obtaining the data
d_{i}
from the population
\Lambda
, marginalized over the uncertainty in the parameters of that
GW
event
\theta

p(d_{i}|\Lambda)=\int p(d_{i}|\theta)p(\theta|\Lambda)d\theta,

(4)

and
\xi(\Lambda)
is the expected fraction of events which are detected,

\xi(\Lambda)=\int p(\theta|\Lambda)p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)\mathbb{D}[\mathcal{D}]\differential\theta\differential\mathcal{D},

(5)

where
\mathbb{D}[\mathcal{D}]
is an indicator function which is 0 when the data
\mathcal{D}
is undetected and 1 when the data is detected.

However, the integral quantities
\xi(\Lambda)
and
p(d_{i}|\Lambda)
are usually impossible to compute in closed form, and so instead are estimated with a Monte Carlo approach (we discuss the statistical theory of Monte Carlo integration in Section
III
). Specifically, we often use the approximation

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\{d\}|\Lambda)\propto\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)^{-N_{\rm obs}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda),

(6)

where the single event integral quantities are often estimated with

\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)\propto\frac{1}{N_{\rm PE}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm PE}}\frac{p(\theta_{ij}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\theta_{ij}|{\rm PE})}\bigg|_{\theta_{ij}\sim p(\theta|d_{i})}

(7)

with the
\theta_{ij}
drawn from the
PE
(
PE
) posterior
p(\theta|d_{i})
using the prior
\pi(\theta|{\rm PE})
;
1\leq i\leq N_{\rm obs}
indexes the event number and
1\leq j\leq N_{\rm PE}
indexes the individual event samples. We ignore the constant evidence term
\int p(d_{i}|\theta)\pi(\theta|{\rm PE})\differential\theta
, as constant factors in the likelihood are normalized out in the Bayesian posterior.

The selection efficiency is estimated with a large number of simulated sources
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\rm inj}}\sim p(\theta|{\rm draw})
drawn from a fiducial population. The signals are then added to noise from the
GW
detectors. Then, searches
(
Allen et al. 2012
;
Usman et al. 2016
;
Messick et al. 2017
, e.g.)
are run over these data, detecting some subset of the simulated events. We estimate the selection efficiency by

;

\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm inj}}\mathbb{D}[d_{j}]\frac{p(\theta_{j}|\Lambda)}{p(\theta_{j}|{\rm draw})}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm found}}\frac{p(\theta_{j}|\Lambda)}{p(\theta_{j}|{\rm draw})},

(8)

where the indicator
\mathbb{D}[d_{j}]
is 1 when the data segment
d_{j}
is detected by the pipelines, and 0 when it is not. Equivalently the sum may be performed over only the found injections.

We will show in Sec.
III
that Eqs.
7
and
8
are unbiased estimators for the marginalized
p(d_{i}|\Lambda)
in Eq.
4
and for the selection efficiency integral in Eq.
5

We show in Section
III.1
that running any estimator through a nonlinear function, such as
\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)^{-N_{\rm obs}}
, produces a biased estimator. In particular, this means the likelihood estimator is biased, as we discuss further below.

III
Monte Carlo Integration

Equations
7
and
8
are examples of Monte Carlo integrals. In this section, we discuss the basic theory of Monte Carlo integration, the uncertainty in estimators, and biased and unbiased estimation.

Monte Carlo integration is a technique for estimating the value of an integral stochastically. Take many random draws from the domain of integration using a known probability density which is easy to sample, then reweight to the integrand function. Specifically, given an integral

I=\int f(\theta)\differential\theta,

(9)

and
M
independent draws from a distribution
\theta_{i}\sim p(\theta)
, the Monte Carlo estimate of the integral is (see chapter 29 of
MacKay 2003
)

\hat{I}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{f(\theta_{i})}{p(\theta_{i})}.

(10)

Monte Carlo integration is a general technique for estimating nonelementary or high dimensional integrals, because the uncertainty in the estimate is not explicitly dependent on the dimension of the domain of the integral.

We can compute the expectation of the estimator

\langle\hat{I}\rangle=\int\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{f(\theta_{i})}{p(\theta_{i})}\right)p(\theta_{1})\differential\theta_{1}p(\theta_{2})\differential\theta_{2}...p(\theta_{M})\differential\theta_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int\left(\frac{f(\theta_{i})}{p(\theta_{i})}\right)p(\theta_{1})\differential\theta_{1}p(\theta_{2})\differential\theta_{2}...p(\theta_{M})\differential\theta_{M}

(11)

using the fact that integration is linear. Notice that for the
i^{\rm th}
term in the sum, the integrand is independent of all but one of the integration variables
\theta_{i}
, and all others integrate away to 1. Therefore,

\langle\hat{I}\rangle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int\left(\frac{f(\theta_{i})}{p(\theta_{i})}\right)p(\theta_{i})\differential\theta_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int f(\theta_{i})\differential\theta_{i}=\int f(\theta)\differential\theta=I.

(12)

Because the expectation of the Monte Carlo integral is correct (
\langle\hat{I}\rangle=I
), we call the Monte Carlo estimator
\hat{I}
an
unbiased
estimator for
I
. We should note, however, that the simplification in Eq.
12
only works if
p(\theta)>0
wherever
f(\theta)\neq 0
. Otherwise, the estimator is in general
biased
. You cannot re-weight a distribution outside its domain.

Using a similar approach, we can compute the variance of the estimator as

\mathbb{V}[\hat{I}]=\langle(\hat{I}-\langle I\rangle)^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\int\frac{f(\theta)^{2}}{p(\theta)}\differential\theta-I^{2}\right),

(13)

which is just a constant value suppressed by a factor of
M^{-1}
. This gives the Monte Carlo integral the attractive property that the uncertainty can be decreased as much as we like, simply by drawing more samples. How
much
we must increase the number of samples, however, depends sensitively on how similar
p(\theta)
is to
f(\theta)
: the integral in Eq.
13
can be extremely large or even infinite for poorly chosen
p(\theta)
. Indeed, there is a similar issue as Eq.
12
. If there is a finite-measure subset of the domain where
f(\theta)\neq 0
but
p(\theta)=0
, then the variance diverges
for all

M
. Even if the domain where
f(\theta)\neq 0
also has
p(\theta)\neq 0
, if
p(\theta)
becomes too small faster than
f(\theta)
, the variance may still diverge.

Just as we are able to estimate
\hat{I}\approx I
with Monte Carlo integration, we can estimate
\mathbb{V}[\hat{I}]
with

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{I}=\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{1}{M-1}\sum\limits_{i}^{M}\left(\frac{f(\theta_{i})}{p(\theta_{i})}-\hat{I}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{M-1}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{f(\theta_{i})^{2}}{p(\theta_{i})^{2}}-\hat{I}^{2}\right),

(14)

which is also an unbiased estimator:
\langle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{I}\rangle=\mathbb{V}[\hat{I}]
(this fact can be shown by exactly the same procedure as Eq.
12
), provided the variance exists. Note the denominator
M-1
rather than
M
—known as Bessel’s correction—is required for the estimator to be unbiased
(
Kendall and Stuart 1977
)
.
Since Eqs.
7
and
8
are Monte Carlo integrals, they are unbiased estimators for the corresponding integrals:
\langle\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)\rangle=p(d_{i}|\Lambda)
and
\langle\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)\rangle=\xi(\Lambda)

IV.2
Posterior bias

However, the issue with the bias extends beyond the likelihood. Even if the likelihood estimator is unbiased, the
posterior
can be biased. This is for a similar reason as the bias in the likelihood: the posterior is a nonlinear function of the likelihood, and so marginalizing the posterior over the uncertainty in the likelihood will leave some residual bias. Mathematically, this is due to the nonlinearity in the normalization and covariance in the likelihood estimator between multiple locations in parameter space
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}
. Denoting
\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)=\mathcal{L}(\Lambda)
and the scatter
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda)/\mathcal{L}(\Lambda)-1\equiv\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)
,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{p}(\Lambda|\{d_{i}\})\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\pi(\Lambda)\mathcal{L}(\Lambda)[1+\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)]}{\int\differential\Lambda^{\prime}\pi(\Lambda^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\Lambda^{\prime})[1+\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime})]}\right\rangle

(27)

\displaystyle\approx p(\Lambda)\left(1+\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)\rangle-\int\differential\Lambda^{\prime}p(\Lambda^{\prime})\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime})\rangle-\int\differential\Lambda^{\prime}p(\Lambda^{\prime})\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime})\rangle+\int\differential\Lambda^{\prime}\differential\Lambda^{\prime\prime}p(\Lambda^{\prime})p(\Lambda^{\prime\prime})\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime})\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime\prime})\rangle\right)

where we have defined
p(\Lambda)=\pi(\Lambda)\mathcal{L}(\Lambda)/\int\differential\Lambda^{\prime}\pi(\Lambda^{\prime})\mathcal{L}(\Lambda^{\prime})
as the true posterior, and we have used the binomial expansion to second order for the denominator.

\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)\rangle
is proportional to the bias in the likelihood, and is zero for an unbiased likelihood (or
\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm inj}^{-2})
for the corrected likelihood in Eq.
24

\langle\hat{\delta}(\Lambda)\hat{\delta}(\Lambda^{\prime})\rangle

terms between different locations in parameter space.

It may seem surprising that we should worry about nonlinearity in a normalization term that only appears implicitly in most applications. We discuss this in Appendix
A
.

We derive the general bias to leading order in the Appendix
B
. For the
GW

\langle\hat{p}(\Lambda|\{d_{i}\})\rangle=p(\Lambda|\{d_{i}\})\left(1+b(\Lambda)-\int b(\Lambda^{\prime})\differential\Lambda^{\prime}\right)

(28)

where

varies across parameter space and the second term simply ensures the posterior remains normalized. To leading order,

b(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{N_{\rm obs}(N_{\rm obs}+1)\mathbb{V}[\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)]}{\xi(\Lambda)^{2}}-\int C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})p(\Lambda^{\prime}|\{d_{i}\})\differential\Lambda^{\prime}.

(29)

The first term is the bias from the nonlinearity at which the selection effects estimator enters the likelihood;
\mathbb{V}[\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)]
is the variance in the estimator
\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)
. As shown in detail in Appendix
B
, the second term is the
posterior
bias where
C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})
is the covariance in the log-likelihood estimator between points
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}

GW

\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\frac{\hat{C}_{p_{i}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})}{\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda^{\prime})}+\frac{N_{\rm obs}^{2}\hat{C}_{\xi}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})}{\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)\hat{\xi}(\Lambda^{\prime})}

(30)

where

\hat{C}_{p_{i}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N_{\rm PE}-1}\left(\left[\frac{1}{N_{\rm PE}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm PE}}\frac{p(\theta_{ij}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\theta_{ij}|{\rm PE})}\frac{p(\theta_{ij}|\Lambda^{\prime})}{\pi(\theta_{ij}|{\rm PE})}\right]-\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda^{\prime})\right)

(31)

is the covariance between the estimators
\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)
and
\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda^{\prime})
and

\hat{C}_{\xi}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}-1}\left(\left[\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm found}}\frac{p(\theta_{j}|\Lambda)}{p(\theta_{j}|{\rm draw})}\frac{p(\theta_{j}|\Lambda^{\prime})}{p(\theta_{j}|{\rm draw})}\right]-\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)\hat{\xi}(\Lambda^{\prime})\right)

(32)

is the covariance between the selection efficiency estimators
\hat{\xi}(\Lambda)
and
\hat{\xi}(\Lambda^{\prime})

These covariances come from the fact that each estimator is using the

\Lambda

We illustrate the intuition behind the posterior bias in Fig.

. Suppose the true likelihood is a standard normal, and we have an unbiased likelihood estimator scattered by a zero-mean log-Gaussian process. Each draw from the scattering process represents a likelihood estimator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
. We show several draws of the likelihood estimator in the left-hand panel of Fig.

. Notice, due to the form of the log-Gaussian process, the uncertainty is small towards the negative end of the parameter space, and grows larger towards the positive end. We also show the true likelihood (
\mathcal{L}

\langle\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rangle
, red) to show the likelihood estimator is unbiased, as designed.

A schematic for how an unbiased likelihood estimator leads to a biased posterior estimator. We draw likelihood scatter from a zero mean log-Gaussian process where the uncertainty is small for negative

However, the resulting posterior estimator is biased. As shown mathematically in Eq.
27
, this is because of the nonlinearity of the normalization, and we can understand this intuitively from the schematic in Fig.
3
. Consider two cases: one where the likelihood estimator for
x>0
is scattered upwards, and one where the likelihood estimator is scattered downwards. The likelihood estimator is unbiased, so these cases should average out over an ensemble of estimators to the correct likelihood. That is, the amount of upwards scatter balances the amount of downwards scatter in the likelihood estimator. However, when we consider the corresponding
normalized
posterior estimators, this balance is broken. The normalization is larger for the estimator which scattered upwards and smaller for the estimator which scattered downwards. The normalization then down-weights the upwardly scattered estimator and up-weights the downwardly scattered estimators. In general, this means that regions with larger uncertainty in the likelihood will be negatively biased, and regions with smaller uncertainty will be positively biased.
Fig.
3
confirms our intuition: where the likelihood estimator uncertainty is small (
x<0
), the posterior is positively biased. We quantify this bias using the
average
of the posterior estimators, denoted
\langle\hat{p}(x)\rangle
(and dropping the implicit conditioning on data).

For completeness, we include the discussion of general posterior correction in the Appendix
C
and as it applies to some examples in Appendix
F
. That said, in practice it is expensive to include the full posterior correction factor because it requires an integral over the uncorrected posterior. That typically means performing an initial biased inference followed by a second corrected inference. Another drawback is that in the corrected posterior, we lose the ability to estimate the remaining posterior bias and there is often still significant
uncertainty
which actually dominates the total error. For this reason, it is usually enough to use the corrected likelihood but neglect the posterior correction term. In Section
V
, we introduce an estimator to quantify the total error (bias plus uncertainty) in the uncorrected posterior, and the analyst should compute this error statistic to assess the validity of their approximate inference.

V
Robustness measures and uncertainty thresholding

In this section, we introduce a unified statistic
\hat{E}
for evaluating the total error in an approximated posterior. From this discussion, the concepts of accuracy and precision for posterior estimators naturally appear. We also define the accuracy statistic
\hat{A}
and precision statistic
\hat{\Pi}
for estimating these quantities, where the error statistic is the sum
\hat{E}=\hat{A}+\hat{\Pi}
. We note that the precision statistic is proportional to the doubly-integrated Eq. A10 from
Essick and Farr 2022
.

To quantify the error between the posterior estimator and the true posterior, we use the
KL
divergence. Given a reference probability distribution
p
and a test distribution
q
over a space
\Lambda
, the
KL
divergence quantifies the information lost from approximating
p
with
q
. In units of bits
(
MacKay 2003
)

{\rm KL}(p|q)=\int p(\Lambda)\log_{2}\left(\frac{p(\Lambda)}{q(\Lambda)}\right)\differential\Lambda.

(33)

Suppose
q(\Lambda)\propto p(\Lambda)(1+\delta(\Lambda))
for a small perturbation function
\delta(\Lambda)
. Expanding the logarithm and ensuring
q(\Lambda)
is normalized gives
7
7

7

We can also think of Eq.
34

L^{2}

||\delta(\Lambda)-\int p(\Lambda^{\prime})\delta(\Lambda^{\prime})\differential\Lambda^{\prime}||^{2}
on the probability space defined by
p(\Lambda)
. This metric (which approximates the
KL

\delta(\Lambda)-\int p(\Lambda^{\prime})\delta(\Lambda^{\prime})\differential\Lambda^{\prime}=0
over the entire support of

{\rm KL}(p|q)\approx\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}\left[\int p(\Lambda)\delta(\Lambda)^{2}\differential\Lambda-\left(\int p(\Lambda)\delta(\Lambda)\differential\Lambda\right)^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}).

(34)

Here,
p
represents the true posterior and
q=\hat{p}
the estimator, and so
\delta(\Lambda)=b(\Lambda)+\varepsilon(\Lambda)
is the sum of a constant bias term

(defined in Eq.

\varepsilon(\Lambda)

\langle\varepsilon(\Lambda)\rangle=0

\langle\varepsilon(\Lambda)\varepsilon(\Lambda^{\prime})\rangle=C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})

(35)

\hat{\Pi}

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}[\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}\left[\frac{1}{N_{\rm samp}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n})-\frac{1}{N_{\rm samp}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{m})\right]

(37)

\displaystyle\hat{A}[\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}\frac{1}{N_{\rm samp}-1}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}w_{n}^{2}-\frac{1}{N_{\rm samp}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}w_{n}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}{\rm var}[\{w_{n}\}],

(38)

and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n})\equiv\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{n})

and

w_{n}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{N_{\rm obs}(N_{\rm obs}+1)\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}(\Lambda_{n})}{\hat{\xi}(\Lambda_{n})^{2}}-\dfrac{1}{N_{\rm samp}}\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{m})&\text{if using uncorrected likelihood in Eq.~\ref{eq: estimated hierarchical likelihood}}\\
\dfrac{1}{N_{\rm samp}}\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\rm samp}}\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{m})&\text{if using corrected likelihood in Eq.~\ref{eq: unbiased likelihood estimator}}\end{cases}

(39)

where the sum estimates the average covariance between the fixed point
\Lambda_{n}
and another random sample in the posterior
\hat{p}
.

The accuracy statistic
\hat{A}[\hat{p}]
approximates the amount of bias in the posterior estimate
\hat{p}
from the true posterior
p
. Given that we do not have access to an ensemble of posterior estimators nor the true posterior, we can only estimate the bias to leading order. We neglect the higher order terms that should also contribute to the accuracy. A large accuracy statistic indicates that higher order terms would also contribute significantly to the bias, and the posterior estimator is not trustworthy.

Similarly, the precision statistic
\hat{\Pi}[\hat{p}]
measures the average amount of scatter in a theoretical ensemble of posterior estimators. The scatter is the distance between the average posterior estimator (which in general is biased from the true posterior) and the particular realization of the posterior estimator. Again, we do not have access to this theoretical ensemble, and so we can only estimate the precision to leading order based on the estimator we have.
We verify the efficacy of the accuracy and precision statistics for a variety of hierarchical inference problems in Sections
VI
and
VII
.
Additionally, note that the precision statistic is also proportional to the average variance in the difference of the log-likelihoods for two draws from the hyperposterior, discussed in
Essick and Farr 2022
and shown in Eq. A11 therein for the single-event terms.

For a variety of problems we consider, we find that when
\hat{E}[\hat{p}]\lesssim 0.2
bits, the posterior estimator is reliable, and the smaller the error statistic, the more reliable the posterior estimator is. See Fig.
4
for a schematic to illustrate the concepts of accuracy and precision in a posterior estimator.

Notice that the accuracy statistic is on the order of the
square
of the covariance estimator and the precision is on the order of covariance estimator. As the uncertainty in the log-likelihood estimator decreases, the error is dominated by the uncertainty rather than the bias. In practice, though, there are scenarios where the bias can dominate the overall error. Bias and uncertainty in the estimator are due to different aspects of the “noise process” in the likelihood estimator. The bias in the posterior happens due to a changing variance/covariance across the support of the posterior, while the uncertainty in the posterior estimator comes from a variance in the log-likelihood estimator which is not matched by equal
covariance
across the posterior support.

V.1
Thresholding on the uncertainty

Another perspective on the error in the posterior estimator is as a sum of contributions which occur at higher and higher order in the uncertainty of the log-likelihood. The leading order contribution to the error is the precision statistic, which occurs at first order in the covariance of the log-likelihood
C
. The bias occurs at order
C^{2}
, and in principle, there are contributions at third, fourth, etc. order in the covariance.
8
8

8

At higher order, there are also higher order moments which contribute, e.g.
\langle\varepsilon(\Lambda)\varepsilon(\Lambda^{\prime})\varepsilon(\Lambda^{\prime\prime})\rangle
, which must be accounted for.

However we neglect these to describe only the leading order contribution of the precision and the accuracy. If we bound the covariance to be below 1, that bounds the leading order sources of error and also ensures that the contributions at high order in the covariance are suppressed.

Fortunately, the covariance in the log-likelihood must satisfy

|C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})|\leq\sqrt{|C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda)||C_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime})|}\equiv\sigma_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda)\sigma_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda^{\prime}),

(40)

due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, where
\sigma_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda)
is the square root of the variance of the log-likelihood estimator. The same inequality holds for the covariance and variance
estimators

|\hat{C}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})|\leq\hat{\sigma}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda)\hat{\sigma}_{\ln\mathcal{L}}(\Lambda^{\prime}).

(41)

Showing these inequalities does require some careful application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which we do in Appendix
D
.

If we require that the variance in the log-likelihood estimator be bounded below some value (e.g. below 1, as recommended by
Talbot and Golomb 2023
), then this shows that the leading order contributions to error in the posterior estimators are bounded. Now, we do not have access to the true variance of the log-likelihood estimator, but we do have access to an estimator of the variance of the log-likelihood. We can cut out regions of
\Lambda
parameter space wherever our estimator of the variance in the log-likelihood exceeds 1, and we show here that this bounds the error in the posterior estimator.
9
9

9

The uncertainty in the estimator for the variance in the log-likelihood is typically negligible, especially when the variance estimator itself is below 1.
This verifies the conjectured threshold on the log-likelihood put forth by
Talbot and Golomb 2023
.
10
10

10

That being said, there are pathological examples where this argument breaks down: for example, the arguments here fail if the likelihood does not admit a Taylor expansion.

Using the above inequalities of Eqs.
40
and
41
, and a variance threshold
\sigma^{2}_{\rm MAX}
, the precision, accuracy and error statistics are bounded by

\hat{\Pi}[\hat{p}]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\rm MAX}}{\ln(2)}\qquad\qquad\hat{A}[\hat{p}]\leq\frac{\sigma^{4}_{\rm MAX}}{2\ln(2)}\qquad\qquad\hat{E}[\hat{p}]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\rm MAX}}{\ln(2)}+\frac{\sigma^{4}_{\rm MAX}}{2\ln(2)}

(42)

but in practice tend to be much smaller than these bounds (typically by an order of magnitude or so).

While we show the log-likelihood variance threshold suggested in
Talbot and Golomb 2023
guarantees a trustworthy posterior estimator, it only does so within the domain of
\Lambda
which is not below the variance threshold. Furthermore,
Essick and Farr 2022
point out that a threshold on the log-likelihood variance might be sufficient for accurate inference, but also that there are cases where thresholding on the log-likelihood variance is much stronger than necessary; these are the cases where we believe the error statistic is useful (
Essick and Farr 2022
and
Talbot and Golomb 2023
come to similar conclusions and define a statistic similar to our precision statistic). We discuss examples of the variance threshold being stronger than necessary in Section
VII
.

Another common threshold previously used in the literature is based on the effective sample size of a Monte Carlo integral. Somewhat confusingly, there are two related but subtly different definitions of the effective sample size, both of which are used. One definition, defined in
Farr 2019
, is commonly applied to the selection efficiency Monte Carlo integral

N_{{\rm eff},\xi}\equiv\frac{\hat{\xi}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\xi}^{2}}.

(43)

Farr 2019
point out that, when
N_{{\rm eff},\xi}<4N_{\rm obs}
, then the likelihood estimator is extremely biased and so is almost certainly unreliable. However, the converse is not necessarily true: if
N_{{\rm eff},\xi}>4N_{\rm obs}
it is still possible that the likelihood estimator is too uncertain to be trusted. Indeed, the variance in the log-likelihood due to the selection efficiency Monte Carlo integral is
N_{\rm obs}^{2}/N_{{\rm eff},\xi}
, and so for
N_{\rm obs}\gg 4
, we can have a large effective sample size, but a variance which is much larger than one, with no
guarantee
that the posterior estimator is reliable (only the lack of a guarantee that the posterior estimator is
not
reliable).

Historically, some of the first problems due to single-event Monte Carlo integrals (eq.
7
) were encountered by
Abbott et al. 2023b
. To handle such issues, another definition of effective sample size due to
Kish 1965
was used to ensure convergence, where

N_{{\rm eff},p}\equiv\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm PE}}w_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm PE}}w_{j}^{2}}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{p}+\hat{p}^{2}/(N_{\rm PE}-1)}\qquad\qquad\mathrm{where}\qquad\qquad w_{j}=\frac{p(\theta_{j}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\theta_{j}|{\rm PE})}.

(44)

The threshold used by
Abbott et al. 2023b
was to require that
all
single-event Monte Carlo integrals satisfy
N_{{\rm eff},p}>N_{\rm obs}
. Other analyses have used different thresholds, requiring instead that
N_{{\rm eff},p}>10
or some other fixed number.
In particular, the thresholds we refer to as “effective sample size” or “
N_{\rm eff}
” thresholds are

1.

N_{{\rm eff},\xi}>4N_{\rm obs}

2.

N_{{\rm eff},p}>10,30,N_{\rm obs}
depending on the analysis threshold.

Our claim is that these thresholds are not enough to ensure a reliable posterior estimator. Though these may produce reasonable results in some applications, we do not have any theoretical guarantee of reliable results, and we show examples in Section
VII
where these thresholds can be insufficient.

VI
Example with Gaussian hierarchical inference

VI.1
Without selection effects

We now consider a more concrete example with a simple hierarchical inference. To eliminate the effects of bias in the likelihood, we consider an example where there are no selection effects; all signals are detected. However, there is some noise associated with each signal, and this uncertainty must be marginalized over. In particular, our population draws events
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
from an underlying Gaussian population with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
. We note these simulation studies are very similar to those in Sec. III in
Essick and Farr 2022

Suppose there is some noise
n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n})
where we observe
d=\theta+n
. Then, we have a Gaussian likelihood for each event

p(d|\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(d-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)

(45)

For a collection of events
\{d_{i}\}
, we can compute the hierarchical likelihood of Eq.
3
in closed form (ie. we have a “ground truth” for comparison), and also estimate using the traditional Monte Carlo approach of Eq.
6

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\int p(d_{i}|\theta)p(\theta|\Lambda)\differential\theta=[2\pi(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2})]^{-N_{\rm obs}/2}\exp\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\frac{(d_{j}-\mu)^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2})}\right]

(46)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\{d_{i}\}|\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda),\qquad\qquad{\rm where}\qquad\qquad\hat{p}(d_{i}|\Lambda)=\left[\frac{1}{N_{\rm PE}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm PE}}\frac{p(\theta_{ij}|\Lambda)}{\pi(\theta_{ij}|{\rm PE})}\right]_{\theta_{ij}\sim p(\theta|d_{i})}.

(47)

PE

\sigma_{n}

We calculate the posterior on the population hyperparameters
\mu,\sigma
with a uniform hyperprior
\pi(\mu)=U(0,2)

\Lambda

\Lambda^{\prime}

As another example, we consider a similar example as the above, where events are drawn from an underlying Gaussian population

PE

\hat{E}[\hat{p}]\sim 0.05
bits, well within a reliable range and indeed, the ensemble of 4 independent posterior estimators in the left column shows that the results are highly stable.

Since the results with a strong model are stable with no convergence thresholds, they will also be reliable when using the effective sample size threshold. However, analysts should still be careful if they use an effective sample size threshold and check the error statistic is small in order to ensure the systematic uncertainty is acceptable.

When using a weaker model as in Eq.
52
, the resulting hyperposterior will be broader. The posterior estimator is well converged if, for each point in the support, the log-likelihood variance in that point is equally balanced by that point’s average covariance with another point in the posterior. Over a larger hyperposterior support, we do not expect the covariance in the log-likelihood estimator surface to balance the variance. In such cases, the analyst may be forced to threshold on the uncertainty in the likelihood estimator,
a posteriori
rejecting samples where some convergence statistic exceeds some threshold. Common thresholds considered in the literature are based on the effective sample size of individual Monte Carlo integrals
(
Abbott et al. 2023b
;
Farr 2019
, e.g.)
or on the total variance in the log-likelihood estimator
(
Talbot and Golomb 2023
)
.

We showed in Section
V

guaranteed
to bound the overall error in the posterior estimator, and we suggested that thresholds on the effective sample size may not be sufficient. Here, we show two examples to demonstrate that (Fig.

In Fig.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we focus on the estimation of a hierarchical posterior with Monte Carlo integrals. We reconcile two apparently competing ideas for tracking the accuracy of the posterior estimator with a unified statistic
\hat{E}[\hat{p}]
for calculating the average information loss due to the approximation. We show that the likelihood estimator suffers from a bias which we argue can be easily corrected, but that the posterior estimator is also biased and, though it may be corrected in principle (examples in Appendix
F
), it is expensive. In practice, the posterior bias is smaller than the uncertainty in the posterior estimator, and to this end, we derive a precision statistic to quantify this uncertainty (similar to metrics introduced in
Essick and Farr 2022
;
Talbot and Golomb 2023
). We suggest that analysts should ensure their posterior estimator does not suffer from significant Monte Carlo error by calculating the error statistic in post-processing and verifying
\hat{E}[\hat{p}]\lesssim 0.2
bits.

To enable analysts to compute the error statistics, we also publish a
pip
-installable python package called
population-error

(
Heinzel 2025
)
powered by
jax

(
Bradbury et al. 2018
)
.
population-error
takes as input the set of
N_{\rm samp}
samples from the posterior estimator
\hat{p}(\Lambda|\{d_{i}\})
, the population model
p(\theta|\Lambda)
and the
N_{\rm obs}\times N_{\rm PE}
posterior samples and
N_{\rm found}
found injections for computing the accuracy, precision and error statistics.

Even with current catalog sizes of
\mathcal{O}(100)
events, posterior uncertainty is a major systematic source of error. We show in Sec.
VII
that the standard Monte Carlo approach may remain reliable in certain simple models as the number of observations increases, but only under somewhat idealized assumptions (e.g. that simple models will remain appropriate for informative data). It quickly becomes computationally intractable for weak models and large catalogs. Ultimately, a new approach to avoid Monte Carlo uncertainties entirely may become necessary. One idea jointly samples the hierarchical posterior by fitting density estimates to each single event, promising to avoid the single-event Monte Carlo integrals
(
Rinaldi and Del Pozzo 2021
;
Callister et al. 2022
;
Hussain et al. 2024
;
Mancarella and Gerosa 2025
)
. However, it will also be important to remove the Monte Carlo approximation to the selection efficiency, which may be tackled by analytic or machine learning approaches
(
Lorenzo-Medina and Dent 2025
;
Talbot and Thrane 2022
;
Gerosa et al. 2020
;
Callister et al. 2024
)
. Another promising technique proposes avoiding approximate likelihoods entirely via likelihood-free, simulation-based inference
(
Leyde et al. 2024
)
.
Though the impact of uncertainty due to the approximations in these proposals are typically neglected, we argue that our approach (though based on the common Monte Carlo method for likelihood estimation) can be extended to these in the future as long as one can construct an estimate for the covariance in the log-likelihood surface.
