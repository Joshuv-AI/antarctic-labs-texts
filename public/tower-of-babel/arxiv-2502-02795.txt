Improved L p L^{p} bounds for the strong spherical maximal operator
Jonathan Hickman, Joshua Zahl

Abstract
. We study the L p L^{p} mapping properties of the strong spherical maximal function, which is a multiparameter generalisation of Stein’s spherical maximal function. We show that this operator is bounded on L p L^{p} for p > 2 p>2 in all dimensions n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . This matches the conjectured sharp range p > ( n + 1 ) / ( n − 1 ) p>(n+1)/(n-1) when n = 3 n=3 . For n = 2 n=2 the analogous estimate was recently proved by Chen, Guo and Yang. Our result builds upon and improves an earlier bound of Lee, Lee and Oh. The main novelty is an estimate in discretised incidence geometry that bounds the volume of the intersection of thin neighbourhoods of axis-parallel ellipsoids. This estimate is then interpolated with the Fourier analytic L p L^{p} -Sobolev estimates of Lee, Lee and Oh.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The strong spherical maximal operator

Let
n\geq 2
and for a tuple of dilation parameters
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in(0,\infty)^{n}
, define the dilation map

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{r}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{r}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=(r_{1}x_{1},\dots,r_{n}x_{n}).

Let
\sigma
denote normalised (to have mass 1) surface measure on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
. The
strong spherical maximal function
is then given by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}f(x):=\sup_{\mathbf{r}\in(0,\infty)^{n}}|f\ast\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(x)|\qquad\textrm{for $f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$,}

where the dilated measure
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}
is defined via the action

\langle\sigma_{\mathbf{r}},f\rangle:=\langle\sigma,f\circ\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{r}}\rangle\qquad\textrm{for all $f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

Thus, the operator
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
forms maximal averages over concentric axis-parallel ellipsoids and is a natural multiparameter variant of Stein’s spherical maximal function
[
18
]
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
can be interpreted as a singular analogue of the classical strong maximal function (the latter is the analogous multiparameter variant of the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function).

1.2.
The main result

This paper concerns the
L^{p}
mapping properties of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
. Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
n\geq 3
and
p>2
, there exists a constant
C_{n,p}>0
such that

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{n,p}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad\textrm{holds for all $f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

Theorem
1.1
improves upon an earlier result of Lee, Lee and Oh
[
10
]
, which established
L^{p}
-boundedness for
p>2\cdot\frac{n+1}{n-1}
. Moreover, Theorem
1.1
establishes the sharp range of estimates for
n=3
. However, it is likely that the result is not sharp in higher dimensions, and it is natural to conjecture the following.

Conjecture 1.2

(Strong spherical maximal function conjecture)
.

For all
n\geq 2
and
p>\frac{n+1}{n-1}
, there exists a constant
C_{n,p}>0
such that

(1.1)

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{n,p}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad\textrm{holds for all $f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

The conjectural range
p>\frac{n+1}{n-1}
is necessary, in the sense that the inequality (
1.1
) fails for all
p\leq\frac{n+1}{n-1}
. This can be shown using a modified Knapp-type example: we provide a heuristic for this example below in §
1.4
and a precise discussion in Appendix
A
.

For
n=2
, the conjecture is known from
[
11
,
5
,
15
]
. More precisely, the
n=2
case follows by interpolating the endpoint result from
[
15
]
with the Fourier analytic
L^{p}
-Sobolev estimates from either
[
11
]
or
[
5
]
. Theorem
1.1
verifies Conjecture
1.2
in the
n=3
and provides partial progress for
n\geq 4
.

We remark that there is a well-developed and classical theory of such multiparameter maximal functions in the lacunary setting (where the radii
r_{j}
are restricted to powers of
2
) due to Ricci and Stein
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
.

1.3.
A discretised estimate

The main novelty in our approach, compared with the earlier work in
[
10
]
, is to use a geometric argument to prove an
L^{2}
estimate for a discretised variant of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
. To describe the setup, given
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in(0,\infty)^{n}
, we let
E(x;\mathbf{r})
denote the axis-parallel ellipsoid with centre
x
and principal radii
\mathbf{r}
. More precisely,

(1.2)

E(x;\mathbf{r}):=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(y)=0\big\}\quad\textrm{where}\quad F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(y):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(y_{j}-x_{j})^{2}}{r_{j}^{2}}-1.

Furthermore, for
\delta>0
, we consider the
ellipsoidal annulus
(or
homoeoid
)

E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r}):=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(y)|<\delta\big\};

by the regularity of the defining function, when
\mathbf{r}\in[1,2]^{n}
the set
E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})
is comparable to the
\delta
-neighbourhood of
E(x,\mathbf{r})
. In order to study
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
, we consider the localised and discretised operators

(1.3)

M^{\delta}f(x):=\sup_{\mathbf{r}\in[1,2]^{n}}\fint_{E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})}|f|.

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the following.

Theorem 1.3

(Discretised
L^{2}
bound)
.

For all
n\geq 3
and all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{n,\varepsilon}>0
such that

(1.4)

\|M^{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{n,\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

holds for all
0<\delta<1
and all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Owing to both the
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
-loss in the operator norm and the localisation of the radii, Theorem
1.3
does not directly imply Theorem
1.1
. However, the discretised
L^{2}
bound (
1.4
) can be translated into an
L^{p}
–Sobolev bound for a localised variant of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
that we will call
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}
; a precise definition will be given in Section
4.1
. This in turn can be interpolated against
L^{p}
-Sobolev estimates from
[
10
]
, leading to favourable
L^{p}
–Sobolev estimates for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}
in the range
p>2
. Finally, using multiparameter Littlewood–Paley theory, as in
[
10
]
, we pass from
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
and thereby arrive at Theorem
1.1
. The details of these Fourier analytic arguments are presented in §
4
.

1.4.
Methodology

Here we discuss the key ideas of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, which involves a
variable slicing
argument. Variants of this basic approach were recently used by the first author and Jančar to study Stein’s spherical maximal function
[
8
]
, by Chen, Guo and Yang to solve the
n=2
case of Conjecture
1.2

[
5
]
, and by the second author to study planar maximal operators
[
22
]
. That said, the details of the argument used here differ significantly from these works.

The Knapp example.
To motivate the argument, it is useful to first consider the basic Knapp example, which provides the necessary condition
p\geq\frac{n+1}{n-1}
for (
1.1
). Here we give a heuristic for the Knapp example using parameter counting; we provide a more thorough presentation of the example in Appendix
A
, where we also rule out the endpoint case
p=\frac{n+1}{n-1}
. Fix a centre
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with, say,
3/2<|x|<5/2
and a point
\omega\in S^{n-1}
on the unit sphere. We consider all ellipsoids
E(x,\mathbf{r})
centred at
x
; this is an
n
-dimensional family, parametrised by the principal radii
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
. If we impose the condition that
\omega\in E(x,\mathbf{r})
, then this corresponds to a single constraint
F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)=0
on
\mathbf{r}
, where
F_{x,\mathbf{r}}
is the defining function in (
1.2
). Furthermore, if we impose the condition that
E(x,\mathbf{r})
is tangent to
S^{n-1}
at
\omega
, then this corresponds to
n-1
additional constraints. Thus, we have
n
parameters and
n
constraints. We therefore expect that, for many choices of
x
, there should be a choice of
\mathbf{r}
such that
E(x,\mathbf{r})
is tangent to
S^{n-1}
at the specific point
\omega
(this is made precise in Lemma
A.2
).

For
0<\delta<1
, we let
K(\delta)
be a rectangular slab of dimensions
\delta\times\delta^{1/2}\times\cdots\times\delta^{1/2}
centred at
\omega
and lying parallel to the tangent plane to
S^{n-1}
at
\omega
. By formalising the above observations, we may find a set
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of (
n
-dimensional) measure roughly equal to
1
such that for each
x\in F
there exists an ellipsoid centred at
x
which intersects
K(\delta)
on a set of (surface) measure roughly equal to
\delta^{(n-1)/2}
. Taking
f:=\chi_{K(\delta)}
to be the characteristic function of
K(\delta)
and plugging this into (
1.1
), we arrive at the necessarily condition
p\geq\frac{n+1}{n-1}
.

Higher order tangency.
We would be able to strengthen the Knapp example, and thereby disprove Conjecture
1.2
, if it were possible to ensure the ellipsoids
E(x,\mathbf{r})
above are not only tangent to
S^{n-1}
at
\omega
, but are
higher order tangent
(for instance, in addition one of the principal curvatures of the ellipsoid is equal to
1
at
\omega
). However, imposing a higher order tangency condition leads to a situation where the number of constraints is larger than the number of free parameters, which should effectively rule out a counterexample of this kind. Our argument is based on making this simple idea precise.

Duality and exceptional sets.
To prove Theorem
1.3
, we apply a standard duality argument, following Cordoba’s
[
6
]
classical proof of the
L^{2}
boundedness of the Kakeya maximal function (see also
[
3
]
or
[
12
, Chapter 22]
). This reduce matters to bounding expressions of the form

(1.5)

\big\|\sum_{E\in\mathscr{E}}\chi_{E^{\delta}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\sum_{E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}}|E_{1}^{\delta}\cap E_{2}^{\delta}|,

where
\mathscr{E}
is a family of axis-parallel ellipsoids with
\delta
-separated centres and principal radii belonging to
[1,2]
. From this we are immediately led to consider the volume
|E_{1}^{\delta}\cap E_{2}^{\delta}|
for pairs of ellipsoids
E_{1}
,
E_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
. As suggested by our earlier discussion, an unfavourable situation occurs if, generically,
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
have high order tangency, which results in a large intersection between
E_{1}^{\delta}
and
E_{2}^{\delta}
. However, our parameter counting heuristic suggests that higher order tangency should not be generic behaviour.

Fix an ellipsoid
E_{1}
and let
x_{2}
denote a choice of centre for the second ellipsoid. We now consider the set
B_{1}
of all
\omega\in E_{1}
for which there exists some
\mathbf{r}\in[1,2]^{n}
such that
E(x_{2},\mathbf{r})
has high order tangency
with
E_{1}
at
\omega
. By our earlier parameter counting, we expect the set
B_{1}
to be small. In particular, we expect it to be a lower dimensional submanifold of
E_{1}
. Our strategy is to remove the exceptional set
B_{1}
from
E_{1}
, leaving behind the
refined ellipsoid

E_{1}^{\star}:=E_{1}\setminus B_{1}
, so that
|E_{1}^{\delta,\star}\cap E_{2}^{\delta}|
satisfies a favourable bound whenever
E_{2}
is centred at
x_{2}
(here
E_{1}^{\delta,\star}
is essentially the
\delta
-neighbourhood of
E_{1}^{\star}
).

Slicing.
The exceptional set
B_{1}=B_{1}(x_{2})
for
E_{1}
is defined in relation to some choice of centre
x_{2}
. Whilst
B_{1}(x_{2})
itself may be small, the union of these sets over all centres
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is large. However, the key observation which drives the proof is:

For
\ell
a line through the centre of
E_{1}
, we can take the
same
exceptional set
B_{1}(x_{2})
for all
x_{2}\in\ell
.

This suggests a variable slicing argument. We fix a line
\ell
through
0
and consider the ostensibly harder problem of proving uniform bounds for the sliced norms

\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|M^{\delta}_{\star}f\|_{L^{2}(\ell+v)}.

Here the maximal function
M^{\delta}_{\star}
is defined with respect to the refined ellipsoids
E^{\star}
, formed by removing the ‘universal’ exceptional set associated to
\ell+v
. Since our operator is local, such a sliced bound easily leads to a true
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
estimate. By translation invariance, we may further assume
v=0
.

When we apply the duality argument to this sliced formulation, we are led to consider a variant of (
1.5
) where now all the ellipsoids in
\mathscr{E}
are centred on
\ell
and each instance of
E^{\delta}
is replaced with the corresponding refined set
E^{\delta,\star}
. In particular, if
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are higher order tangent, then
E_{1}^{\delta,\star}\cap E_{2}^{\delta,\star}=\emptyset
, which means that the higher order tangent pairs do not contribute to the estimation of the
L^{2}
norm. This leads to a favourable estimate for (
1.5
): see Proposition
2.1
.

Covering by refinements.
Finally, it remains to deal with the contribution to the operator which arises from the exceptional set. To do this, we choose a (finite) set
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of lines through
0
and derive exceptional sets for each
1\leq k\leq n
. We let
M_{k}^{\delta}
denote the maximal operator defined with respect to the refined ellipsoids
E^{k}:=E\setminus B^{k}
, where
B^{k}
is the exceptional set associated to
\ell_{k}
. Given a point
\omega\in E
, by choosing
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
appropriately it is possible to ensure that there exists some
1\leq k\leq n
such that
\omega
lies in the refinement
E^{k}
. This allows us to bound the
M^{\delta}
in terms of the family of maximal operators
M_{k}^{\delta}
.

Notational conventions

Given a list of objects
L
and non-negative real numbers
A
,
B
, we write
A\lesssim_{L}B
or
A=O_{L}(B)
if
A\leq C_{L}B
where
C_{L}>0
is a constant depending only on the objects listed in
L
and a choice of dimension
n
. If
V
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then we let
\mathrm{proj}_{V}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V
denote the orthogonal projection onto
V
.

Structure of the article

In §
2
we carry out the preliminary slicing and duality steps of the argument, and reduce matters to proving volume bounds for intersections between refined ellipsoidal annuli. In §
3
, we prove the required volume bounds and thereby complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In §
4
we combine Theorem
1.3
with the
L^{p}
-Sobolev estimates from
[
10
]
to derive Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in the appendix we discuss the details of the modified Knapp example, which demonstrates the sharpness of our theorem in the
n=3
case.

2.
Slicing and duality

2.1.
Discretised operators

Recall the definition of the ellipsoid
E(x;\mathbf{r})
and the ellipsoidal annulus
E^{\delta}(x;\mathbf{r})
from §
1.3
. In what follows, we will fix a small constant
0<c_{n}<1
, which is chosen depending only on the ambient dimension
n
, subject to the forthcoming requirements of the proof. In order to study
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{st}}
, we consider the localised and discretised operators

(2.1)

M^{\delta}f(x):=\sup_{\mathbf{r}\in\mathfrak{R}}\fint_{E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})}|f|,

where
\mathfrak{R}:=\big[1,1+c_{n}^{2}\big]^{n}
is a restricted set of radii. Note that the above definition is a slight abuse of notion, since the operator
M^{\delta}
was previously defined in (
1.3
). In this previous definition,
\mathbf{r}
ranged over the domain
[1,2]^{n}
rather than
[1,1+c_{n}^{2}]^{n}
. This distinction is harmless, since for each
1\leq p\leq\infty
, these two variants of
M^{\delta}
have comparable
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
operator norm.

Our goal is to prove Theorem
1.3
with
M^{\delta}
now defined as in (
2.1
).

2.2.
Removing the exceptional sets

Clearly, provided the dimensional constant
c_{n}>0
is chosen sufficiently small, we may ensure that

(2.2)

\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|\omega_{k}|^{3}\geq 2c_{n}>0\qquad\textrm{for all $\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with $1/2\leq|\omega|\leq 2$.}

Since the operator
M^{\delta}
is local and translation-invariant, to prove (
1.4
) it suffices to prove the local estimate

\|M^{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

For each
(x,\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)^{n}
, define the affine map

A_{x,\mathbf{r}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\omega\mapsto x+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{r}}\,\omega.

For all
0<\delta<1
, note that
E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})
is the image of the (spherical) annulus
E^{\delta}(0,\mathbf{1})
under
A_{x,\mathbf{r}}
, where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
. We decompose

E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r})\quad\textrm{where}\quad E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r}):=E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})\cap A_{x,\mathbf{r}}\big(\Omega_{k}\big)

for

(2.3)

\Omega^{k}:=\big\{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\omega_{k}|^{3}\geq 2c_{n}\big\}.

Here we use (
2.2
), which ensures that the sets
E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r})
indeed cover
E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})
.
Observe that the sets
E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r})
are translation-invariant, in the sense that

E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r})=x+E^{\delta,k}(0,\mathbf{r})\qquad\textrm{for all $(x,\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)^{n}$.}

Using the above, we define

M_{k}^{\delta}f(x):=\sup_{\mathbf{r}\in\mathfrak{R}}\frac{1}{|E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})|}\int_{E^{\delta,k}(x,\mathbf{r})}|f|\qquad\textrm{for $1\leq k\leq n$,}

so that

(2.4)

M^{\delta}f(x)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}M_{k}^{\delta}f(x).

We now carry out a preliminary ‘slicing’ of the domain. Define

(2.5)

d_{k}=(d_{k,1},\dots,d_{k,n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\textrm{with}\quad d_{k,j}:=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $j\neq k$}\\
0&\textrm{if $j=k$}\end{cases}\qquad\textrm{for $1\leq k\leq n$,}

let
\ell_{k}
denote the
1
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by
d_{k}
, and let
V_{k}:=\ell_{k}^{\perp}
denote the codimension
1
subspace normal to
d_{k}
. By the Fubini–Tonelli theorem and (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\|M^{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}(B(0,1))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(\int_{V_{k}\cap B(0,1)}\|M^{\delta}_{k}f\|_{L^{2}(\ell_{k}(v_{k}))}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\sup_{v_{k}\in V_{k}\cap B(0,1)}\|M_{k}^{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}(\ell_{k}(v_{k}))},

where
\ell_{k}(v_{k}):=\{td_{k}+v_{k}:t\in[-1,1]\}\subseteq\ell_{k}+v_{k}
is a line segment.

2.3.
Duality and reduction to volume bounds

We now apply a standard duality argument, and exploit the translation-invariance of the problem, to reduce matters to the following multiplicity bound.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
1\leq k\leq n
and
0<\delta<1
, we have

\big\|\sum_{E\in\mathscr{E}}\chi_{E^{\delta,k}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\lesssim\log\delta^{-1}\delta^{1/2}\big[\#\mathscr{E}\big]^{1/2}

whenever
\mathscr{E}
is a family of axis-parallel ellipsoids with the following properties:

i)

The centres are
\delta
-separated and lie on
\ell_{k}(0)
;

ii)

The principal radii belong to
\mathfrak{R}
.

The key ingredient in the proof of Proposition
2.1
is the following volume bound.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
and
\delta>0
. Given
x_{j}:=t_{j}d_{k}
for
t_{j}\in[-1,1]
and
\mathbf{r}_{j}\in\mathfrak{R}
for
j=1
,
2
, we have

\big|E^{\delta,k}(x_{1},\mathbf{r}_{1})\cap E^{\delta,k}(x_{2},\mathbf{r}_{2})|\lesssim\log\delta^{-1}\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta+|t_{1}-t_{2}|}.

We stress that the volume bound involves the sets
E^{\delta,k}
, which are each formed by removing some exceptional set from the parent annulus
E^{\delta}
. It is crucial that the exceptional set is removed to avoid degenerate behaviour arising from high order tangencies between the parent annuli.

Once Lemma
2.2
is established, the proof of Proposition
2.1
follows from the standard
L^{2}
Kakeya argument of Córdoba
[
6
]
(see also
[
3
]
or
[
12
, Chapter 22]
).

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Fixing
\mathscr{E}
a family of ellipsoids as in the statement of the proposition and expanding out the square in the
L^{2}
norm, we have

(2.6)

\big\|\sum_{E\in\mathscr{E}}\chi_{E^{\delta,k}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\sum_{E_{1}\in\mathscr{E}}\sum_{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}}|E_{1}^{\delta,k}\cap E_{2}^{\delta,k}|.

For
E_{1}\in\mathscr{E}
, we dyadically partition the sum over
E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}
in (
2.6
) according to the distance between the centres. In particular, define

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{<\delta}

\displaystyle:=\big\{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}:|x(E_{2})-x(E_{1})|<\delta\big\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\big\{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}:\tau/2\leq|x(E_{2})-x(E_{1})|<\tau\big\}

for
\delta\leq\tau\leq 1
, where
x(E)
denotes the centre of an ellipsoid
E
. Since, by hypothesis, the centres
\{x(E):E\in\mathscr{E}\}
form a
\delta
-separated subset of the line segment
\ell_{k}(0)
, we have

\big[\#\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{<\delta}\big]\lesssim 1\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\big[\#\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{\tau}\big]\lesssim\delta^{-1}\tau\qquad\textrm{for all $\delta\leq\tau\leq 1$ and all $E_{1}\in\mathscr{E}$.}

Combining these observations with the volume bound from Lemma
2.2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}}|E_{1}^{\delta,k}\cap E_{2}^{\delta,k}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{<\delta}}|E_{1}^{\delta,k}\cap E_{2}^{\delta,k}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\leq\tau\leq 1\\
\mathrm{dyadic}\end{subarray}}\sum_{E_{2}\in\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{\tau}}|E_{1}^{\delta,k}\cap E_{2}^{\delta,k}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\log\delta^{-1}\delta^{2}\Big(\delta^{-1}\big[\#\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{<\delta}\big]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\leq\tau\leq 1\\
\mathrm{dyadic}\end{subarray}}\tau^{-1}\big[\#\mathscr{E}^{E_{1}}_{\tau}\big]\Big)

(2.7)

3.1.
Controlling the tangency set

Recall that
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\dots,1)
and
E(0,\mathbf{1})=S^{n-1}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fixing
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{r}\in[1/2,2]^{n}
and

\displaystyle E^{\delta}(0,\mathbf{1})\cap E^{\delta}(x,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\big\{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|F_{0,\mathbf{1}}(\omega)|<\delta\textrm{ and }|F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)|<\delta\big\}

\displaystyle=\big(F_{0,\mathbf{1}},F_{x,\mathbf{r}}\big)^{-1}\big([-\delta,\delta]^{2}\big).

For
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in[-1,1]^{2}
let

denote the fibre of the map

.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
u\in[-1,1]^{2}
and
0<\rho<1
, we have

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\gamma_{x,\mathbf{r}}(u)\cap B(\xi,\rho))\lesssim\rho^{n-2}.

Lemma
3.1
is an immediate consequence of the following standard estimate (see, for instance,
[
21
]
). Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
d
-dimensional algebraic variety. Then for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\rho>0
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{d}(X\cap B(\xi,\rho))\lesssim\operatorname{degree}(X)\rho^{d}.

In Lemma
3.1
, the set
\gamma_{x,\mathbf{r}}(u)
is the proper intersection of two ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and thus is an
(n-2)
-dimensional variety of degree at most 4.

Continuing with the above setup, we decompose the sets
E^{\delta,k}(0,\mathbf{1})
for
1\leq k\leq n
according to the size of the angle between the normal vectors
\nabla F_{0,\mathbf{1}}(\omega)
and
\nabla F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)
. To this end, define the
2\times n
Jacobian matrix

J_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial F_{0,\mathbf{1}}}{\partial\omega_{1}}(\omega)&\dots&\frac{\partial F_{0,\mathbf{1}}}{\partial\omega_{n}}(\omega)\\[2.0pt]
\frac{\partial F_{x,\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\omega_{1}}(\omega)&\dots&\frac{\partial F_{x,\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\omega_{n}}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}

and set

\displaystyle\|J_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)\|

\displaystyle:=\big(\det J_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)J_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)^{\top}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\big(|\nabla F_{0,\mathbf{1}}(\omega)|^{2}|\nabla F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)|^{2}-\langle\nabla F_{0,\mathbf{1}}(\omega),\nabla F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)\rangle^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

Note, in particular, that the above quantity vanishes if and only if
\nabla F_{0,\mathbf{1}}(\omega)
and
\nabla F_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)
are parallel. We consider the sets

E_{x,\mathbf{r},<\rho}^{\delta,k}(0,\mathbf{1}):=\big\{\omega\in E^{\delta,k}(0,\mathbf{1}):\|J_{x,\mathbf{r}}(\omega)\|<\rho\big\}\quad\textrm{for $\rho>0$.}

With these definitions, the key result is as follows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Provided
c_{n}>0
is chosen sufficiently small, there exists a dimensional constant

such that the following holds. Let
1\leq k\leq n
and
x:=t\tilde{d}_{k}
for some

and
\tilde{d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

We conclude by showing how Theorem

4.1.
The local maximal operator

We first prove

defined by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}f(x):=\sup_{\mathbf{r}\in[1,2]^{n}}|f\ast\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}(x)|\qquad\textrm{for $f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

For this, we reformulate Theorem

Let

For

It is a simple matter to show that
P(a)=(a-1)^{n-1}(a+n-1)
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, it is clear that
P
is a monic polynomial of degree
n
. By linear dependence relations between the columns,
P(1)=0
and, combining similar observations with the Leibniz rule, we further see that

P(1)=P^{(1)}(1)=\cdots=P^{(n-1)}(1)=0.

Thus,
P(a)=(a-1)^{n-1}(a-\xi)
for some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, it is also clear that
P(-(n-1))=0
, since the sum of the columns of the matrix is the zero vector in this case. Hence we must have
\xi=-(n-1)
.

Using the above we see, for instance, that
|\det J\Phi(3/2,\dots,3/2)|\gtrsim 1
and so there exists some set open set
\Omega\subseteq[1,2]^{n}
containing
(3/2,\dots,3/2)
upon which
\Phi
is a bijection. Let
F:=\Phi(\Omega)
and for each
x\in F
let
\mathbf{r}_{x}
denote the unique
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
such that
\Phi(\mathbf{r}_{x})=x
. By construction, it follows that the ellipsoid
E(x,\mathbf{r}_{x})
satisfies properties i) and ii) and, furthermore,
|F|\gtrsim 1
.
∎
