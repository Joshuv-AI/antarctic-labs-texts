The 3D printed cardiac model: a potential tool in transcatheter complex atrial septal defect closure—a case report study
Authors: Jelle D Nieuwendijk, Abby Geerlings, Jan Baan, Berto J Bouma, Maik J Grundeken, Luis Antonio Moreno-Ruiz, Edgar García, Sheila Vania Sánchez López, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transcatheter closure of atrial septal defects (ASDs) can be complicated by inferior-posterior rim deficiency. Percutaneous closure is possible but demands detailed pre-procedural imaging.Case summaryIn this case, we present a successful percutaneous closure of two secundum ASDs complicated by the absence of the inferior-posterior rim. Pre-procedural simulations were conducted on a printed cardiac 3D model based on cardiac CT imaging.DiscussionAtrial septal defects with an inferior-posterior rim deficiency/absence are associated with an increased risk of device failure in percutaneous closure. Creating a 3D model offers improved understanding of the complex anatomy of the defects and allows pre-procedural simulation of the procedure, which may contribute to successful closure.Keywords: Atrial septal defect, Percutaneous closure, Congenital heart disease, Three-dimensional printing, Case report

Introduction

In atrial septal defects (ASDs), the secundum type is the most common, covering up to 80% of ASDs.1 In less than 10% of the ASD patients, multiple defects of the atrial septum are present.2 In a single secundum ASD, transcatheter device closure is recommended if the defect is ≤38 mm and has a rim of ≥ 5 mm according to the ESC guidelines.1 Regarding ASDs with multiple defects, the ESC guidelines do not provide specific treatment recommendations, but several studies indicate that these patients can be treated with transcatheter closure as well and the results appear to be similar to the closure of a single ASD.3,4 Percutaneous closure of ASDs can however be complicated by a deficient rim (≤5 mm) towards the vena cava inferior (VCI), which is associated with an increased risk of implant failure.5 Transcatheter closure of this type of defect is possible, but adequate measurement of the defect and the surrounding rims is crucial to determine whether a defect is suitable.6,7 Yan et al.8 showed in 29 patients that successful closure of ASDs with a deficient posterior-inferior rim is possible by conducting pre-procedural simulation closures on 3D printed models.

In this case report, we report a patient with two secundum ASDs and absent inferior-posterior rim in the inferior defect, who was treated with transcatheter closure supported by 3D-printing in addition to conventional imaging to optimize the assessment of the anatomy and interventional planning.

Case presentation

The patient concerned a 66-year-old female, known with a secundum ASD and hypertension. The ASD was diagnosed at the age of 52 and in that time considered to be insignificant. For 9 months she complained about exertional dyspnoea, palpitations, non-exertional chest pain, and dizziness. She was referred to our hospital for further evaluation.

On physical examination, the patient had a blood pressure of 117/75 mmHg, a heartrate of 72 bpm, and no murmurs on auscultation. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with an incomplete right bundle branch block, an intermediate heart axis, and Crochetage R waves in lead III and lead aVF.

The transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed a dilated right ventricle with a basal diameter of 46 mm with a normal systolic function. Both atria were dilated, with a left atrial volume index of 41 mL/m2 and a right atrial volume index of 40 mL/m2. A floppy inter atrial septum was seen with the (already known) ASD in the fossa ovalis region, resulting in a left-right shunt. Mild tricuspid regurgitation was observed, and the systolic pulmonary artery pressure was estimated to be 34 (31 + 3) mmHg.

Because of the exertional dyspnoea and chest pain, a coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) was performed, showing signs of coronary artery disease, but the subsequent coronary angiogram showed no significant stenoses.

The CCTA also revealed a second ASD more inferiorly, which partly continues in the junction between the right atrium and VCI, creating a second shunt from the left atrium to the VCI (Figure 1).

The CT scan was segmented using 3D slicer version 5.10.0 (www.slicer.org),9 which was then further processed using Meshmixer (version 3.5.0, Autodesk Inc.). On both atria, a part of the external atrial wall was removed to enable visibility of the atrial septum. The .stl file was prepared for printing in GrabCad. The model was printed using the DLP printer (Origin One, Stratasys, Israel) with a soft photopolymer resin (MED414, A-50, Loctite, Henkel, Germany).

The 3D model was used to conduct multiple simulations of the planned intervention.

Atrial septal occluders and patent ductus arteriosus occluders were tested on the 3D printed model in various sizes, together with the manufacturer. An occluder was considered ‘appropriate’ if it was stable, properly positioned, and not constrained. Ultimately, the Amplatzer septal occluder was selected for the procedure, based on the presence of sufficient rim to anchor the two discs despite the absence of the posterior-inferior rim.

Considering the simulations using the 3D model, percutaneous closure was seen as feasible.

Due to the absence of the inferior-posterior rim, the inferior defect was seen as the most complex defect to close percutaneously and was therefore planned to be closed first in order to allow conversion to surgery in case of procedural failure.

The intervention was guided by a transoesophageal echocardiogram (TOE) and was conducted under general anaesthesia. Two venous accesses were obtained entering the right vena femoralis using two 7 Fr catheters. The superior defect was measured at a diameter of 11 mm on TOE (Figure 2).

Peri-procedural measurements of the inferior defect on fluoroscopy (left image) and of the superior defect on transoesophageal echocardiogram (right image). Ao, aorta; RA, right atrium; LA, left atrium.

The inferior-posterior defect was measured at a diameter of 21 mm (Figure 2) using balloon sizing. Both defects had an eligible size and sufficient rim, with the exception of the inferior-posterior rim of the inferior defect. Invasive pressure measurement showed no pulmonary hypertension with pulmonary artery pressures of 31/7 (19) mmHg.

The inferior-posterior defect was closed first using a 22 mm Amplatzer septal occluder.

Based on the balloon sizing, the rims were considered firm enough to oversize the device with only 1 mm compared to the defect diameter.

The anterior-superior defect was closed using a 13 mm Amplatzer septal occluder, partially covering the inferior placed device. The occluders were not released until correct placement was confirmed using fluoroscopy and TOE. The stability of the devices was tested, and no device luxation could manually be triggered. No residual shunt or venous obstruction of the device was seen on TOE.

Post-procedure the patient was started on dual antiplatelets therapy for 6 months.

Cardiac CT with contrast 1 day post-procedure (Figure 3) showed remaining flow of contrast from the left atrium to the right atrium and VCI, just inferior of the occluder placed in the inferior-posterior defect. This flow is suspected for a small residual shunt.

Final position of the devices. (A) Fluoroscopy, (B) transoesophageal echocardiogram, and (C) CT. LA, left atrium; RA, right atrium.

During admission, the heart rhythm of the patient has been monitored for 24 h following the procedure, and no arrythmias were detected.

The TTE 1 month post-procedure showed reduced dilation of the right ventricle compared to the previous TTE with a basal diameter of 42 mm and a normal systolic function. The closure devices were in place (Figure 4), and no residual shunting was seen on echo doppler. A mild Tricuspid regurgitation was measured and the systolic pulmonary artery pressure was estimated to be 32 (29 + 3) mmHg.

Transoesophageal echocardiogram at 1 month of follow-up; the left image shows the short axis view, the right image the four-chamber view. Ao: aorta; LA, left atrium; LV, left ventricle; RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle.

At 3 months of follow-up, the patient reported complete remission of the palpitations, exertional dyspnoea, and dizziness. The patient still experiences non-exertional chest pain; however, the frequency of occurrence has reduced. The chest pain is atypical and not suspected of a cardiac origin. Therefore, the symptoms will be re-evaluated during follow-up.

Discussion

Anatomy

The anatomical classification of the inferior defect in our case, a defect within the fossa ovalis extending in the VCI, is not clearly defined in current literature. In correspondence with the study by Zaghal et al.10 defining a defect of the fossa ovalis and/or fossa ovalis rim as an ASD rather than a sinus venosus defect (SVD), we define both defects mentioned in this case report as secundum ASDs.

3D model

Regarding complex ASDs, the use of 3D printing of the patient-specific anatomy is increasing.11 He et al.12 conducted multiple percutaneous closures of ASDs in combination with SVDs, reporting successful closure possibly due to improved anatomical understanding of the defects and the possibility of performing simulation procedures on the 3D printed model.

Further endorsing the value of conducting simulation procedures preceding the in-patient procedure, Yan et al.8 found that ASDs with a deficient inferior-posterior rim could be closed successfully in 97% of the patients with a successful preceding in vitro closure on a 3D model.

Within the surgical field of congenital heart disease, the application of the cardiac 3D model in selecting the procedure and allowing simulation procedures has also been reported.13

In the study by Valverde et al.14, the information obtained by the 3D model effected the surgical management in 19 of 40 patients with complex congenital heart disease, indicating its potential value in decision-making.

Although the percutaneous closure was successful, cases have been reported of late occurring complications such as residual shunt after complex ASD closure, emphasizing the need for long-term follow-up in these patients.15

In conclusion, the cardiac 3D model may aid in anatomical understanding and assessing procedural feasibility of complex congenital heart disease, applying to both the surgical and interventional field. This case demonstrates the importance of imaging in complex ASD closure and highlights the possibilities of pre-procedural planning using a cardiac 3D model in percutaneous closure (Figure 5).
