Demographic Variances of Burnout Prevalence Among Healthcare Workers of a Tertiary Hospital in Ghana
Authors: Stephen Tetteh Engmann, Alexander Adjei, Irene Manubea Kwansa, Mawuli Kotope Gyakobo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBurnout among healthcare workers threatens workforce well-being and quality of care. Evidence from Ghana remains limited, particularly regarding demographic and professional differences in tertiary hospitals. This study aimed to examine burnout prevalence and its association with key demographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, and professional category among healthcare workers in a tertiary facility in Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional retrospective study analyzed secondary data from a routine clinical audit of 333 healthcare workers (86 doctors, 233 nurses, and 14 health assistants) at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital. Burnout was measured using the validated Burnout Assessment Tool with sound psychometric properties. Associations between burnout and age, gender, marital status, and profession were examined using descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p≤ 0.05.ResultsAmong the 333 healthcare workers studied, the overall burnout prevalence was 62.4% (n = 208). Women and men had comparable overall burnout prevalence (62.9% versus 61.7%), although women reported higher psychosomatic symptoms. Burnout prevalence declined with increasing age, from 64.4% among those aged 20–29 years to 56.5% among those aged 50–59 years. Single healthcare workers experienced slightly higher burnout prevalence than married workers (64.7% versus 59.6%). Bivariate analysis identified profession as the only demographic factor significantly associated with burnout (χ2 = 6.24, p = 0.044). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, nurses and health assistants had significantly lower odds of burnout compared to doctors (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.26–0.83; p = 0.009).ConclusionBurnout at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital is both substantial and varies by profession, underscoring the need for differentiated responses. Nurses and health assistants had lower odds of experiencing burnout compared to doctors. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, including workload adjustments, structured mental health support, and organizational wellness programs.Keywords: burnout, healthcare workers, demographics, Ghana, stress management

Introduction

In recent years, the issue of burnout among healthcare professionals has attracted a lot of attention, especially because of the consequences it has for patient care and staff well-being.1,2 Burnout is a syndrome characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a diminished sense of personal accomplishment, particularly prevalent among healthcare workers who face demanding emotional interactions with patients.3,4 Burnout affects a significant portion of healthcare workers worldwide, with prevalence rates differing among studies and populations. 5 Over one-third (35–42%) of public health workers experienced burnout, with greater rates noted during the COVID-19 pandemic, according to a comprehensive review and meta-analysis. 5 The prevalence of burnout among healthcare workers has emerged as a significant concern in low- and middle-income countries, including those in sub-Saharan Africa.6-11 There is a rising prevalence of burnout among healthcare providers in sub-Saharan Africa, with reports revealing that rates could be as high as 70% among physicians. 9

In Ghana, Odonkor and Frimpong report high levels of burnout among healthcare workers in Accra, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to assess and support healthcare workers experiencing burnout. 10 Furthermore, female health workers were found to be particularly vulnerable to burnout. 10 A similar study among obstetric providers identified high burnout rates, with two-thirds of providers agreeing that they felt burned out. 12 The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on burnout among healthcare professionals has been notably explored in studies in Ghana.13,14 High burnout rates were reported, and there were significant intentions by healthcare workers to leave the profession due to overwhelming stress and inadequate support.13,14

A comprehensive review of the literature reveals a complex interplay of demographic characteristics that contribute to burnout in healthcare workers.15-19 Montero-Marín et al highlight the importance of sociodemographic and occupational risk factors in understanding the various types of burnout experienced by health care professionals. 16 Their study reveals that individual and contextual factors are critical in shaping burnout experiences. 16 The Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model, proposed by Demerouti et al in 2001, provides a comprehensive framework for understanding employee well-being by examining the balance between job demands and job resources. 20 Job demands represent pressures and challenges employees face, while job resources provide support and tools to meet these demands. 20 Job demands have been found to positively predict burnout; hence, the level of burnout is expected to vary among different health professionals. 21 Li et al extended the Job Demands-Resources model by showing that objective job characteristics at the occupation level influence employee outcomes through perceived job demands and resources, with multilevel analyses revealing that perceived characteristics mediate the relationship between objective job features and employee well-being outcomes. 22

Younger healthcare workers are at an increased risk, particularly when combined with poor health status ratings. 23 Findings from a systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that burnout prevalence peaks at both early and later career stages, and there is a complex relationship between workload, support, and burnout, further complicated by demographic variables. 24 Furthermore, chronic stressors are inherent in healthcare environments, suggesting that inadequate resources and high expectations contribute to the psychological and physical manifestations of burnout. 25

The examination of burnout differences among healthcare workers by demographic factors is crucial. This is because the consequences are severe, with Opoku et al finding that 49.3% of nursing professionals who are predominantly female intended to quit the profession due to burnout-related factors. 14 Marital status may influence burnout experiences, with single healthcare workers reporting different stress responses compared to their married counterparts. 14 In a study in Accra, age emerged as a significant factor associated with burnout, alongside other demographic characteristics including gender, occupation, years of work experience, marital status, and parenthood. 10 In oncology settings, personal demands were identified as critical contributors to burnout, highlighting the need for tailored interventions to mitigate these effects. 26

Burnout significantly impacts patient care, with variations evident across different healthcare professions. 27 Also, healthcare workers with prolonged exposure to job stressors exhibited higher burnout levels. 28 Wright et al contextualised burnout in low- and middle-income countries, calling for more cross-sectional studies to identify evidence-based solutions to this pressing issue. 24 This study aimed to assess burnout prevalence among health workers at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital by profession, age, gender and marital status and recommend targeted wellness interventions to reduce burnout among hospital health workers. There is limited evidence on the prevalence and demographic determinants of burnout among a broader healthcare workforce in a tertiary hospital setting using the relatively new Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT). This study therefore seeks to quantify burnout and identify associated demographic factors among healthcare workers at a Teaching Hospital, thereby providing institution-specific data.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a cross-sectional analysis of secondary data to assess burnout among healthcare workers at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital in Ghana. This 400-bed tertiary healthcare institution, which houses medical and surgical departments, is located in the northern part of the Central Region of Ghana and serves as a teaching hospital for nursing and medical education.29,30 This was a retrospective study utilising data from a previous routine clinical audit by the Department of Internal Medicine and Therapeutics.

Study Population

All health professionals at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital during the period of the clinical audit comprised the study population.

Study Participants and Sampling

The study involved a comprehensive review of all available data on burnout at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital over a period of 2 months from June 2025 to July 2025, following ethical approval granted on 22nd May, 2025. Given the retrospective nature of the study, there was no predetermined sample size. A census of all data available on burnout at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital from the previous clinical audit was used in this study. For this study, 333 healthcare workers with complete and accessible data from the clinical audit, comprising 86 doctors, 233 nurses, and 14 health assistants, were included in the dataset.

Data Extraction

Secondary data on burnout, containing attributes of interest, and demographic data, including age, gender, marital status, and professional designation, were analysed to align with the study objectives. During the clinical audit, burnout was assessed using the self-administered, validated Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT). The BAT has a total of six sections covering the attributes of burnout—mental exhaustion, mental distance, cognitive impairment, emotional impairment, psychological complaints, and psychosomatic symptoms. The core attributes of burnout are mental exhaustion, mental distance, cognitive impairment and emotional impairment. Secondary attributes include psychological complaints and psychosomatic symptoms. The BAT uses a Likert scale to assign scores to the questions in each section as follows: Never (1), Rarely (2), Sometimes (3), Often (4), and Always (5). 31

Exhaustion is “severe loss of energy that results in feelings of both physical and mental exhaustion.” Emotional impairment is “intense emotional reactions such as anger or sadness, and feeling overwhelmed by one’s emotions.” 32 Mental distance is a “strong reluctance or aversion to work, indifference, and cynicism”, whereas cognitive impairment includes memory problems, with concentration and attention deficits. 32 Physical complaints that are either caused by or worsened by a psychological issue but cannot be explained by a physical disorder are referred to as psychosomatic symptoms. 31 Sleep issues, anxiety, and other non-physical symptoms of a psychological issue are referred to as psychological complaints. 31

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic distributions and burnout scores across various groups. Burnout scores were compared for both genders, age groups, single and married healthcare workers and the scores for the different categories of professionals were estimated. The cut-off scores for burnout were set at 25th, 75th and the 95th percentiles, with 4 categories: Low (<25th), Average (25th to < 75th), High (75th to <95th) and Very High (>95th). Average to very high was considered burnout following the cut-off recommended by Schaufeli et al31-33 The intention for this cut-off was to identify healthcare workers experiencing clinically relevant burnout symptoms rather than only severe cases. The exact BAT cut-off values used were based on normative thresholds established by Schaufeli et al. 31 Categorical variables are presented as frequencies/percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviation. The Chi-square test was used to determine the association between the demographic characteristics and burnout. Multivariate logistic regression was done to identify the strength of the association between demographic characteristics and burnout. The significance level was set at p-value ≤ 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The Department of Internal Medicine and Therapeutics, School of Medical Sciences, University of Cape Coast, granted administrative permission to use the data in a letter with reference number IMT/P.14/Vol 1/20. All data were anonymised and kept on a password-protected computer to guarantee privacy and data protection. The Cape Coast Teaching Hospital Ethical Review Committee (CCTHERC) gave ethical approval for the study protocol (reference number CCTHERC/EC/2025/078 and a Federal Wide Assurance Number (FWA) IRB00014450). Given the retrospective nature of the study, individual participant informed consent was waived by the Ethical Review Committee.

Association Between Burnout and Demographic Factors

The only demographic factor found to be associated with burnout in the bivariate analysis using the chi-square test was profession (χ2 = 6.240, p = 0.044) – Table 4. Multiple logistic regression analysis is shown in Table 5. The adjusted regression analysis demonstrated that nurses and health assistants had about 53% lower odds of experiencing burnout compared to doctors [AOR = 0.47 (95% CI = 0.26–0.83), p=0.009]. The logistic regression model demonstrated acceptable goodness-of-fit according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ2 = 6.012, df = 8, p = 0.646). The findings of the logistic regression suggest that doctors may face higher burnout rates.

Discussion

The results reveal a high overall burnout prevalence. Nurses constituted the majority of participants, followed by doctors and health assistants. Burnout was assessed using the self-administered, validated Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT), which represents a significant methodological advancement in burnout measurement, offering a multidimensional symptom structure that differentiates it from traditional instruments like the Maslach Burnout Inventory.32,34 Multiple validation studies have confirmed the BAT’s robust psychometric properties across different contexts. Italian validation studies with teachers and heterogeneous professional samples demonstrated good internal consistency and factorial validity, supporting both four-factor core and two-factor secondary structures.35,36 These studies collectively establish the BAT as a reliable, valid instrument that captures burnout’s multidimensional nature more comprehensively.

The BAT conceptualises burnout through four core dimensions: exhaustion, mental distance, and impaired emotional and cognitive functioning, plus secondary dimensions. 33 Rasch analysis confirms sound psychometric properties and measurement invariance across gender, age, and countries. 37 The tool was developed based on qualitative interviews with practitioners, establishing the four core dimensions and secondary dimensions, demonstrating superior construct validity compared to existing measures. 33 Key insights include the tool’s ability to capture a comprehensive burnout construct through a general factor structure, as confirmed by Redelinghuys & Morgan using bifactor modelling across four countries. 38 These studies collectively establish the BAT as a more theoretically grounded and psychometrically robust alternative to burnout measures.

Similar to findings in this current study, burnout among healthcare professionals in tertiary hospitals is highly prevalent across different domains and settings.39-41 In the Ghanaian healthcare context, burnout research has identified concerning prevalence rates and significant associations with demographic factors, highlighting the need for systematic assessment tools in Ghanaian hospitals. 10 Opoku et al identified key determinants of burnout in a tertiary hospital, which included increasing age, longer duration of practice, female sex, dislike for leadership style and lack of management support. 42 Multiple factors contribute to this high prevalence, including excessive workload, imbalances in duty allocation, physically strenuous work, and resource constraints within hospital settings, which significantly impact the quality of patient care.40,41 A systematic review and meta-analysis from India reports that personal factors such as younger age, female gender, and unmarried status are associated with increased burnout risk among healthcare professionals. 39 The emotionally demanding nature of tertiary hospital environments, combined with high patient loads and organizational pressures, could be the reason for the high prevalence.

Doctors experienced the highest mean burnout score of 70.0 (SD = 16.0) and the highest prevalence (73.3%), with nurses and health assistants having lower odds of experiencing burnout. Research examining burnout differences between doctors and nurses reveals mixed findings and may depend more on work environment and organizational factors than profession type alone.43-45 A meta-analysis of ICU professionals found no significant difference in overall high-level burnout prevalence between physicians and nurses. 45 However, network analysis of Chinese healthcare professionals revealed distinct depression-burnout relationship patterns between doctors and nurses, suggesting different underlying mechanisms. 43 Hence, context also appears crucial in burnout patterns. Systemic constraints such as bureaucratic obstacles, inadequate support systems, and poor working conditions significantly exacerbate burnout risk.46,47 These factors create chronic workplace stress that manifests physiologically through altered biomarkers, including cortisol, melatonin, and thyroid hormones. 1

The gender distribution of this study highlights the gender composition of healthcare professionals within the hospital, where women constituted the majority of the workforce. Gender comparisons showed similar levels of mental exhaustion between men and women, though women reported slightly higher psychosomatic symptoms. Contrary to findings from this study, in which male and female healthcare workers exhibited similar overall burnout scores, in studies from Italy and Taiwan, female healthcare professionals generally experienced higher levels of emotional exhaustion and personal burnout compared to their male counterparts.48,49 However, male healthcare workers reported higher levels of depersonalization. 48 Furthermore, women are more likely to seek support after adverse events, while men are more resistant to seeking help. 48

Regarding marital status, the distribution indicates that a majority of professionals in the hospital were unmarried, which may have implications for work-life balance and stress levels among healthcare providers. Research on burnout differences between single and married healthcare workers shows mixed results. A meta-analysis on the effect of marital status on burnout levels of nurses indicated that single nurses experience higher burnout levels compared to married nurses. 50 However, other research suggests that married healthcare workers had a lower quality of life and potentially higher stress levels than their single counterparts. 51 During the COVID-19 pandemic, marriage was associated with increased personal burnout risk, though work-related burnout showed no significant difference after controlling for other factors. 52 These conflicting findings highlight the complex relationship between marital status and burnout in healthcare settings, suggesting that other factors such as work conditions, family roles, and living conditions may play important mediating roles.

Research on burnout among healthcare workers also reveals varying patterns across age groups. Makkai et al found no significant age-related differences in burnout levels. 53 However, age appears to be a factor, with older healthcare workers experiencing higher levels of emotional exhaustion. 54 Furthermore, according to Tsai and Tsou, young female staff and young doctors/nurses with burnout showed a higher risk of metabolic syndrome. 55 Despite the high prevalence of burnout, there is limited research on this topic in sub-Saharan Africa, and more rigorous epidemiological studies are needed to better understand and address this issue. 9

Limitations and Strengths of the Study

The study provides assessments of burnout among a multidisciplinary healthcare workforce in a Ghanaian tertiary hospital using the Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT), a more comprehensive and psychometrically robust measure than instruments commonly used in previous Ghanaian studies. It further identifies profession-specific differences, thereby generating institution-specific evidence to guide targeted workforce wellness interventions.

However, the study is based on self-reported information with some limitations. Firstly, given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of a census approach, we could not perform a power calculation that could serve as a basis for the estimates presented. Secondly, the study had a significant gender imbalance, with women comprising 64.0% (n=213) compared to 36.0% (n=120) for men, which might affect the generalizability of gender-based findings. Thirdly, the cross-sectional nature of the study restricts the capacity to infer causality from the association found. Furthermore, just like studies that employ secondary data, some key variables were absent. Thus, the interpretation of the findings should be done in the light of these limitations.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The findings demonstrate a substantial burden of burnout and reveal important differences across professional and demographic groups, particularly with nurses and health assistants having lower odds of experiencing burnout compared to doctors. By applying the BAT in a sub-Saharan African healthcare setting, this study extends the evidence base beyond traditional burnout measures and highlights the value of comprehensive burnout assessment for identifying groups that may have higher burnout rates and informing context-specific workforce well-being strategies. Further validation and application of the BAT in African settings could contribute to a better understanding of burnout patterns among healthcare workers in resource-constrained health systems.

There is a need for targeted interventions to address burnout among healthcare workers at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH), particularly for younger and single healthcare professionals. Tailored support programs such as workload adjustments, structured mental health resources, and stress management training should be implemented. Furthermore, improving organisational support systems, such as peer mentorship and employee wellness programs, could help reduce burnout symptoms. Future research should explore additional workplace factors influencing burnout to develop more effective long-term strategies for healthcare professionals. Future studies should explore workplace and organizational determinants of burnout and utilize longitudinal designs to identify causal factors and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions.
