Advances in salivary cortisol monitoring for healthcare applications
Authors: Ganden W. X. Kok, Emily Ortega, Michelle Z. L. Kee, Leon C. Z. Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cortisol is a dynamic endocrine biomarker whose concentration varies with circadian timing, acute stressors, medication, sampling conditions and population characteristics. Saliva provides a non-invasive matrix for repeated measurement of the unbound hormone fraction and is compatible with decentralised point-of-care formats, but salivary cortisol should be interpreted as an adjunctive physiological measure rather than a standalone diagnostic test for psychiatric disorders. This review compares biological sampling matrices and critically evaluates lateral-flow immunoassays, electrochemical, surface-plasmon-resonance and quantum-dot-based cortisol sensors according to recognition chemistry, transduction mechanism, analytical range, sample matrix, validation level and translational readiness. Competitive lateral-flow assays offer rapid, low-cost and discrete measurements, whereas wearable electrochemical and microneedle platforms may support higher-frequency monitoring but face challenges in sample availability, biofouling, calibration drift and long-term stability. Across platforms, very low limits of detection do not by themselves establish suitability for salivary monitoring; physiologically relevant range coverage, authentic-saliva validation, comparison with reference methods and reproducibility are also required. Future progress will depend on standardised collection protocols, selective recognition chemistry, robust digital readout, circadian-aware interpretation and independent clinical validation.

Introduction

The global prevalence of mental disorders presents a major and growing public health concern, with anxiety and depressive disorders being the most frequently diagnosed. In Singapore, recent epidemiological data show a marked rise in prevalence, increasing from 13.4% in 2020 to 17.0% in 2022. Beyond the impact on direct health burden, poor mental health also imposes significant economic costs due to losses in productivity, reduced job performance, and lower employment rates. Current estimates show that symptoms of depression and anxiety account for 2.9% of Singapore's gross domestic product (GDP).1 By 2030, the global economic cost of mental disorders is projected to reach approximately US$6 trillion, while the direct and indirect costs of mental ill health have been estimated to amount to over 4% of global GDP.

The World Health Organization (WHO) established the Special Initiative for Mental Health in 2019, to reduce the treatment gaps and increase access to quality mental health services worldwide. In Singapore, the Inter-agency Taskforce on Mental Health and Well-being was established to promote population-level mental wellbeing and improve service coordination across the sectors. More recently, mental health services have been incorporated into the nationwide “Healthier SG” campaign, to improve mental health support within the community and manage stress-related conditions.

These public health initiatives not only underscore the urgency of making mental health care more accessible but also highlight the value of reliable objective biomarkers that can capture the impact of stress on health. Among many candidate biomarkers, cortisol has received particular attention as a stress biomarker due to its pivotal role in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Understanding cortisol dynamics provides valuable insight into the mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders as well as downstream health consequences. Such research may help clarify the physiological mechanisms associated with stress-related conditions and inform the future development of more individualised monitoring strategies.

This review aims to provide a background on cortisol biology and highlight the link between cortisol levels and both psychiatric disorders and physiological illnesses, emphasising the critical role of cortisol as a biomarker in psychological stress-related research. Specifically, we aim to evaluate saliva as a practical non-invasive matrix for decentralised cortisol monitoring and offer the reader a detailed breakdown of the advantages and limitations of various cortisol sampling matrices such as saliva, blood and sweat. Next, we discuss the disadvantages of salivary cortisol monitoring, along with methods to overcome these limitations, improving its reliability and applicability. Additionally, we examine the translation of salivary cortisol technologies into commercial platforms, ranging from established laboratory immunoassays and reader-based lateral-flow systems to newer smartphone-assisted consumer devices, with particular attention to analytical validation, intended use and regulatory status. We also address the development of point-of-care testing (POCT) for semi-quantitative cortisol level detection and the potential role of rapid POCT in decentralised cortisol measurement. Through a structured investigation, this review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the current state and potential future directions of cortisol monitoring in a clinical setting.

Biology of cortisol

Cortisol, also known as 17-hydroxy-corticosterone, is one of the most extensively studied biomarkers in the field of psychological stress, depression, and anxiety. It is a glucocorticoid hormone produced as the primary product of the HPA axis, a key biological stress response system (Fig. 1). Upon activation, the hypothalamus produces corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which stimulates the secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) by the anterior pituitary. ACTH, in turn, drives the adrenal cortex to produce and release cortisol. Circulating cortisol exerts a negative feedback inhibition on both the hypothalamus and pituitary, hence maintaining homeostatic balance.2

It is characterised by a sharp increase in cortisol levels upon awakening, which peaks within approximately 30–45 minutes after awakening (also known as cortisol awakening response). Cortisol levels then gradually decline throughout the day before falling to its lowest levels in the evening.4 This strong rhythmicity is necessary for synchronising bodily functions with the sleep–wake cycle and metabolic demands, impacting arousal, mood, and immune and inflammatory system functioning.5 Factors such as age and dietary factors can influence the diurnal rhythm of cortisol.6–8 Gender differences can also lead to large variations in cortisol profiles, with women more likely to exhibit higher cortisol awakening response than men.

Cortisol and stress-related psychiatric disorders

While transient HPA axis activation is beneficial and critical for maintaining homeostasis under acute stress, chronic or dysregulated activity has adverse health consequences. Prolonged exposure to elevated cortisol levels desensitizes the HPA axis response over time, consequently impairing metabolic, cardiovascular, and immune functions.9–14 Notably, dysregulation of the HPA axis is also frequently found in stress-related psychiatric disorders.

Some studies and patient subgroups with major depressive disorder have demonstrated elevated basal cortisol or an altered cortisol awakening response, although findings vary according to depression subtype, severity, medication status and sampling design.15–19 Elevated basal cortisol has, in some studies, co-occurred with a blunted response to subsequent stressors, potentially reflecting altered HPA-axis feedback regulation. Population-based research has also emphasised the connection between psychiatric symptoms of depression and cortisol dysregulation. A study on survivors of the 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan observed that adolescents with severe depressive symptoms had a significantly higher morning-to-evening salivary cortisol ratio than their peers with mild symptoms.20,21

Altered cortisol dynamics are also observed in anxiety disorders.22,23 However, findings on the cortisol awakening response were more heterogeneous. While Vreeburg and colleagues showed a significantly higher cortisol awakening response in people with anxiety disorders, Hek and colleagues found the opposite pattern.24 Individuals with long-term anxiety disorders also had a lower cortisol awakening response than controls, consistent with the notion that chronic anxiety may result in downregulation of the HPA axis activity.25 In some longitudinal or treatment studies, changes toward more typical cortisol patterns have coincided with symptom improvement. However, this association does not establish that normalisation of cortisol is a causal mechanism of recovery.26

In sum, these findings indicate that altered HPA-axis activity is observed across several psychiatric conditions, although the direction and magnitude of cortisol changes vary between disorders and study populations. Alterations in cortisol dynamics have also been associated with symptom severity, disease progression and treatment response in psychiatric and metabolic conditions.14 However, the direction and magnitude of these alterations vary across diagnoses, disease subtypes, stages, medications and sampling protocols. Salivary cortisol should therefore be regarded as an adjunctive physiological biomarker that may complement symptom assessment, clinical history and longitudinal monitoring, rather than as a standalone diagnostic or prognostic test for depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder or general psychological stress.27 Unlike self-reported measures, cortisol measurement provides an objective biological index that can be monitored over time, although its interpretation remains dependent on the clinical context and sampling protocol.

Review scope and literature selection

This review used a focused narrative approach to identify literature relevant to cortisol biology, biological sampling matrices and emerging cortisol-sensing technologies. Literature was identified through targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar, supplemented by searches of journal and publisher databases and the reference lists of relevant articles. No formal publication-date restriction was applied, allowing established studies describing cortisol physiology and conventional analytical methods to be considered alongside more recent developments in point-of-care and wearable sensing technologies. Literature available up to 2026 was considered during preparation and revision of the manuscript.

Searches combined “cortisol” with terms relevant to the topics covered in this review, including “saliva”, “salivary”, “lateral flow”, “electrochemical sensor”, “aptamer”, “molecularly imprinted polymer”, “surface plasmon resonance”, “quantum dot”, “wearable”, “microneedle”, “point-of-care”, “smartphone” and “machine learning”. Studies were selected according to their relevance to cortisol sampling, sensing chemistry, analytical performance, biological-matrix validation and the translation of sensing technologies towards decentralised monitoring. For salivary sensing platforms, particular attention was given to studies using authentic human saliva and, where available, comparison with established laboratory methods such as ELISA or LC-MS/MS. Studies using spiked samples or other biological matrices were retained when they provided relevant information on sensing mechanisms, analytical performance or matrix-specific limitations, with the sample matrix clearly identified in the discussion and comparative tables.

Commercial platforms were considered separately from peer-reviewed analytical evidence, with manufacturer sources used primarily to establish current product specifications, intended use and regulatory or commercial status. This review was not conducted as a systematic review or meta-analysis; the literature-selection approach was instead intended to provide representative coverage of the principal cortisol-sampling approaches, sensing technologies and translational considerations discussed herein.

Cortisol sampling methods

Cortisol can be measured through various biological matrices such as saliva, sweat, serum, urine and interstitial fluid. Each sampling method offers distinct advantages and drawbacks, making the choice of technique used highly dependent on the context. The strengths and limitations of each matrix in the clinical setting are highlighted herein.

Serum

Serum cortisol is a clinically established matrix for measuring total circulating cortisol (free plus protein-bound) in many endocrine assessments.28 However, interpretation of total serum cortisol can be affected by variations in cortisol-binding globulin (CBG), and measurement of free cortisol or alternative matrices may be more informative for specific clinical questions.29 Serum cortisol measurement remains important in the investigation of adrenal disorders such as adrenal insufficiency, although the appropriate test and interpretation depend on the clinical context. However, blood sampling is invasive, the measurement of serum cortisol can also show considerable variability due to stress from venipuncture and rapid changes in response to acute stress, resulting in misleading measurements.30,31 Moreover, the need for trained personnel and specialised equipment results in a long turnaround time and limits its suitability for rapid, repeated or decentralised cortisol monitoring.31 Cortisol levels may also have high variability in the absence of highly controlled and standardised settings.30 The need for stringent handling and storage conditions to preserve the viability of the serum cortisol, further increases the logistical burden of serum cortisol measurement. The nominal range for cortisol in serum varies from 61.98–194.28 ng mL−1 in the morning at 8 am, dropping to 23.20–118.52 ng mL−1 in the evening at 6 pm.32

Saliva

Salivary cortisol is widely used for monitoring cortisol levels due to its non-invasive nature and ease of collection. Salivary cortisol has been investigated as a non-invasive surrogate of circulating free cortisol. However, the strength of this relationship depends on the population, concentration range, stimulation condition, sampling interval and analytical method. In a study of 12 endurance-trained men across different exercise intensities, repeated-measures concordance between serum and salivary cortisol was 0.728, although agreement was weaker at higher cortisol concentrations and the salivary cortisol peak occurred later than the serum peak.33 In separate adrenal-stimulation and hydrocortisone studies using LC-MS/MS, salivary cortisone, rather than salivary cortisol, showed the strongest association with serum free cortisol (r = 0.95), reflecting the conversion of cortisol to cortisone by salivary-gland 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2).34 Taken together, these studies demonstrate that the relationship between salivary measurements and circulating free cortisol is context dependent, varying with the salivary analyte measured, study population and experimental conditions.

Saliva samples can be collected without the need for specialised personnel in non-clinical settings, such as at home, enhancing patient compliance and reducing the stress associated with clinical environments.35 Because salivary cortisol predominantly reflects the unbound hormone fraction, it can provide biologically relevant information for selected applications, although this does not necessarily make it more clinically reliable than other matrices for assessing stress-related conditions. Salivary glands express 11β-HSD2, which converts cortisol to cortisone in saliva (Fig. 2).36 This endogenous conversion occurs independently of fasting. Avoiding food, beverages, smoking and toothbrushing before sample collection instead helps to minimise contamination, meal-related changes in salivary flow or pH, and potential assay interference.37–39 These preanalytical factors should therefore be considered separately from cortisol–cortisone interconversion. Because structurally related steroids may cross-react in immunoassays, LC-MS/MS can be used when cortisol and cortisone need to be measured independently.40,41

There are other challenges associated with salivary cortisol monitoring. The low cortisol concentration in saliva is a major challenge as the sensitivity of the biosensor needs to be optimised. The average salivary cortisol ranges from 1.30 ng mL−1 to 3.01 ng mL−1 in the morning and drops to 0.76 ng mL−1 to 1.07 ng mL−1 in the nighttime.42

Sweat

Sweat-based cortisol sampling has emerged as a promising alternative due to its non-invasive nature and potential for high-frequency cortisol monitoring with wearable biosensors. Sweat reflects only the free, biologically active form of cortisol and exhibits a good correlation with serum cortisol levels (r = 0.87), with cortisol concentrations ranging from 141.7 ng mL−1 in the daytime to 8.16 ng mL−1 in the nighttime.43,44

However, systems require further optimisation on their size, accuracy and portability.45 Additionally, a significant drawback of sweat-based cortisol sampling lies in the difficulty of obtaining reliable results when the subject is not perspiring. Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity), physical activity levels and individual variation in sweat production can affect the reliability, repeatability and accuracy of cortisol measurements. Furthermore, sweat-based monitoring may not be accurate due to the residual cortisol left over from past perspiration, while the action of stimulating sweat production itself triggers cortisol secretion, distorting results.36 As such, stringent conditions and operating procedures are needed to obtain accurate cortisol measurements using a sweat-based cortisol sampling method.

Urine

Urinary cortisol is frequently used for assessing cortisol levels, particularly when an integrated measure of cortisol secretion over a 24 h period is required. Urinary cortisol is measured over a 24 hour period and commonly referred to as the 24 h urinary free cortisol (UFC) test. Only the free biologically active form of cortisol can pass through the renal glomeruli into the urine and is therefore reflected in the UFC test.43 This approach provides an integrated estimate of daily cortisol excretion, making it valuable for studying chronic stress states or long-term HPA axis activity, but it requires complete 24 h collection.

The strengths and limitations of urinary cortisol have been highlighted in recent research. One significant advantage is the ability to provide a cumulative measure of cortisol secretion, minimising the impact of acute stress and short-term fluctuations of cortisol levels that can confound other sampling methods, such as saliva or blood.43 While the 24 h UFC test provides a method for non-invasive cortisol measurement, it poses significant drawbacks for cortisol measurement. One significant drawback is the extended duration required for sample collection, spanning a full 24 h period. This prolonged process can be inconvenient for patients, requiring them to carry a specialised urine collection container throughout the day or to remain in a fixed location during the 24 h collection period.46 Additionally, the collected samples must be refrigerated from collection to delivery to a diagnostic laboratory for analysis, further increasing the logistical burden of this method and increasing the turnaround time. Furthermore, urinary cortisol measurements can be influenced by factors such as hydration and renal function, introducing further variations in the measurement.47 The requirement for a 24 h sample collection makes urine highly unsuitable for POC assessment of short-term cortisol changes.

Interstitial fluid (ISF)

ISF is an extracellular fluid that surrounds cells and contains biomolecules like cortisol, predominantly reflecting the free, biologically active form of cortisol. It better reflects free cortisol levels in tissue, as compared to serum cortisol.48 ISF cortisol sampling leverages advancements in wearable biosensors in conjunction with the use of microneedles to obtain ISF in a minimally invasive way, offering the potential for higher-frequency or near-continuous cortisol monitoring, although its analytical and clinical value requires further validation.49,50

Evidence comparing ISF cortisol with circulating cortisol is limited but not absent. Vassiliadi et al. reported moderate correlations between subcutaneous interstitial cortisol and both total and free serum cortisol in mechanically ventilated septic patients (rs = 0.60 and 0.70, respectively).48 However, the study was conducted in a specific critically ill population and showed discordance between interstitial and circulating cortisol measurements in some patients, limiting the generalisability of these findings to other populations and sampling contexts. Variability of ISF composition due to hydration, skin temperature or variability between individuals also impacts cortisol measurements, affecting its reliability, consistency and accuracy. Additional challenges regarding the biocompatibility and biosafety of microneedles need to be addressed for the implementation of a higher-frequency ISF monitoring approach.

Hair

Hair cortisol analysis is a promising method for assessing long-term systemic cortisol exposure, reflecting cumulative HPA axis activity over weeks to months. Human hair grows predictably at a rate of 1 cm per month, while cortisol is incorporated into the hair shaft as it grows. This approach is particularly valuable for studying chronic stress or prolonged physiological states.46

One of the key advantages of hair cortisol sampling is its non-invasive nature, requiring only a small strand of hair to be cut close to the scalp. Additionally, hair samples do not require extensive storage conditions, allowing for storage and transport at room temperature. However, several drawbacks persist, external factors such as hair treatment (e.g. bleaching) and environmental contaminants can affect cortisol concentrations, introducing variability into the measurement.51 Hair cortisol is weakly correlated to serum and salivary cortisol, there is little clinical evidence to support the correlation of hair cortisol levels to stress levels.46 It is evident that hair cortisol only provides values for long-term exposure to stress, while short-term and acute stress responses cannot be detected. This approach is undesirable for POCT applications requiring assessment of short-term changes.

Earwax

One significant benefit of earwax cortisol sampling is its non-invasive nature. Participants reported greater comfort with the self-sampling device, suggesting it does not trigger a local stress response that could affect cortisol levels.52

Earwax cortisol sampling is not without its limitations. One challenge is the potential influence of residual earwax left by previous extraction methods, such as the Reiner-Alexander syringe, which might contaminate subsequent samples and affect accuracy. These findings underscore the need for standardised protocols to ensure consistent and reliable results. Additionally, studies show a moderate correlation (r = 0.39) between hair cortisol concentration and earwax cortisol concentration, but no correlation studies between earwax cortisol concentration and serum or saliva cortisol concentration have been conducted to date.52 Nonetheless, this approach is undesirable for POCT applications requiring assessment of short-term changes.

Comparative assessment of cortisol sampling methods

Taken together, the different biological matrices for cortisol monitoring highlight a central trade-off between clinical reliability, sampling convenience, temporal resolution and suitability for POCT (Table 1). Serum cortisol remains clinically established and useful for precise quantification, but its invasive collection, requirement for trained personnel and susceptibility to venipuncture-induced stress limit its practicality for decentralised stress monitoring. Urinary and hair cortisol provide useful information on cumulative cortisol exposure but lack the temporal resolution needed to capture acute or short-term stress responses. Sweat and interstitial fluid offer promising routes for high-frequency monitoring, particularly through wearable platforms, but their reliability remains affected by sample availability, physiological variability, sensor stability and incomplete clinical validation. In this context, saliva represents a practical compromise between biological relevance and POC compatibility, as it reflects the free, biologically active fraction of cortisol, can be collected non-invasively and is compatible with rapid sensing platforms such as LFIAs.

Saliva as a practical matrix for decentralised cortisol monitoring

Saliva offers a practical balance between biological relevance and compatibility with decentralised cortisol monitoring. Salivary cortisol predominantly reflects the unbound hormone fraction and is not directly affected by variations in cortisol-binding globulin. However, as discussed in Section 2.2, its relationship with circulating free cortisol depends on the analyte measured, population, concentration range, stimulation condition, sampling interval and analytical method. In particular, salivary cortisone may show a stronger association with serum free cortisol under selected adrenal-stimulation conditions because cortisol is converted to cortisone by salivary-gland 11β-HSD2. Saliva should therefore be regarded as a practical matrix for selected monitoring applications rather than as a universally superior replacement for serum, as different biological matrices address different temporal and clinical questions.

Beyond its biological relevance, saliva collection reduces patient discomfort and eliminates the need for venipuncture. This non-invasive approach avoids venipuncture-induced stress artefacts inherent to serum, potentially resulting in artificially elevated serum cortisol levels. This approach is also particularly beneficial for paediatric or geriatric populations where blood-draws are challenging or for individuals with needle phobia. The ease of saliva collection also facilitates repeated sampling over short periods of time, enabling the monitoring of diurnal cortisol variations and acute stress responses without significant burden to the patient, enhancing patient compliance. The tracking of cortisol diurnal rhythm and episodic stress responses may provide complementary information on HPA-axis regulation in stress-related research and longitudinal assessment, but these measurements are not independently diagnostic of burnout or anxiety disorders. Additionally, the use of POCT, such as lateral flow immunoassays in conjunction with salivary cortisol, enables convenient cortisol measurement with minimal training and infrastructure compared with conventional serum assays while being cost-effective.

These practical advantages, together with the ability of salivary cortisol to reflect changes in HPA-axis activity, make it valuable for physiological research and longitudinal monitoring of stress-related conditions. However, its clinical interpretation remains dependent on the sampling protocol, timing, population and intended use. Because salivary cortisol changes following acute psychosocial stress, rapid assays may permit discrete measurements at predefined intervals after a stressor. However, the biological delay between stressor exposure and salivary cortisol response, as well as assay-specific validation, must be considered. The invasiveness of serum sampling and its dependence on trained personnel and laboratory processing limit its practicality for rapid, repeated or decentralised measurements. Flattened diurnal slopes and elevated nocturnal cortisol have been associated with adverse psychiatric and cardiovascular outcomes in some longitudinal studies, although their predictive value varies across populations and study designs.53

Despite its strengths, salivary cortisol faces several limitations. Diurnal variability is a major challenge as cortisol levels vary substantially throughout the day. The timing of sample collection must align with cortisol's circadian rhythm, such as 30–45 minutes post-awakening to capture the cortisol awakening response. Cross-reactivity with cortisone and interference from food contaminants in LFIA also risks false readings. Nevertheless, this challenge can be reduced by having a well-designed, strict protocol in place prior to sampling.

While urine and hair samples are also utilised for cortisol measurement, they present limitations. Urinary cortisol (e.g. UFC) reflects cumulative secretion over time, lacking temporal resolution. Hair cortisol provides insights into long-term exposure but is less effective for detecting acute changes. In addition, sweat analysis remains under-researched and has not been widely adopted in clinical practice.

Emerging strategies in cortisol monitoring

Lateral flow immunoassays (LFIAs)

LFIAs are well suited to POCT because they are low-cost, easy to use and allow for rapid, discrete measurement of various biomarkers, including hormones like cortisol. They operate on the principle of immunochromatography, in which the sample migrates along a membrane by capillary action and interacts with labelled recognition reagents and immobilised capture molecules. In a competitive cortisol LFIA, the resulting test-line intensity decreases as the cortisol concentration increases.54 The intensity of the signal produced at the reactive zones can potentially be used for semi-quantification of cortisol levels in bodily fluids.

LFIAs typically contain:

(1) Sample pad – where the sample (e.g. saliva) is applied.

(2) Conjugate pad – containing anti-cortisol antibodies conjugated to detectable labels (e.g. gold nanoparticles). When the sample migrates through this pad, the antibodies are rehydrated and bind to any cortisol present in the sample.

(3) Nitrocellulose membrane – typically containing a test line, immobilised with a cortisol–protein conjugate (e.g. cortisol-BSA) and the control line, typically containing secondary antibodies that capture any unbound labelled antibodies, serving as an internal control to confirm the assay's validity.

(4) Absorbent pad – wicks excess sample away from the membrane, maintaining capillary flow.

LFIAs for cortisol typically use a competitive assay format because cortisol is too small to bind two antibodies simultaneously, as required in a conventional sandwich assay (Fig. 3). After the sample is applied, it migrates through the conjugate pad and rehydrates the labelled anti-cortisol antibodies. Cortisol in the sample binds to a proportion of these antibodies as the mixture moves along the strip. At the test line, the remaining unoccupied labelled antibodies bind to the immobilised cortisol–protein conjugate. Consequently, increasing cortisol concentrations leave fewer labelled antibodies available for capture at the test line, resulting in a lower test-line intensity. Cortisol concentration can therefore be estimated using an inverse calibration relationship between test-line intensity and analyte concentration. The control line provides an internal indication of adequate sample flow and reagent function.

Commercial translation of salivary cortisol testing has progressed through both dedicated lateral-flow readers and newer smartphone-assisted consumer platforms. SOMA Bioscience markets cortisol-specific lateral-flow devices for use with its Cube LFD Reader, which provides quantitative measurements from saliva samples. Importantly, the platform has also undergone independent comparison with laboratory-based methods. In a preregistered study, SOMA cube measurements showed moderate-to-strong correlations with ELISA, although cortisol concentrations were systematically underestimated and several measurements fell below the device detection range.56 A subsequent study evaluating diurnal salivary cortisol reported significant correlations between the SOMA cube and ELISA across multiple sampling points, with no systematic bias identified by Bland–Altman analysis.57

More recently, smartphone-assisted consumer platforms have extended salivary cortisol measurement to at-home settings. Eli Health currently markets the Hormometer as a saliva-based wellness device with smartphone-assisted readout and a reported analysis time of approximately 20 min.58 Although the manufacturer reports high performance relative to laboratory reference methods, detailed peer-reviewed method-comparison data for the current commercial device remain limited. Nutrix describes cortiSense as a saliva-based sensor integrated with its gSense application, with a reported measurement time of approximately 3 min; however, the product is currently offered for pre-order and is described by the manufacturer as a general-wellness product rather than a medical device. Together, these platforms illustrate the increasing accessibility of salivary cortisol measurement while also demonstrating substantial differences in the extent of independent analytical validation and intended use (Table 2).

Electrochemical sensors

Electrochemical sensors for cortisol monitoring have advanced significantly, offering non-invasive, high-frequency assessments of stress-related biomarkers. These sensors operate by transducing biochemical interactions into measurable electrical signals, enabling the detection of cortisol in various biological fluids such as saliva, sweat and ISF. Electrochemical cortisol sensors commonly employ antibodies, aptamers or molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) as recognition elements. The resulting binding event is converted into an electrical response, although sensor selectivity and stability depend not only on receptor affinity but also on the chemistry and stability of the electrode–recognition interface.

Electrochemical sensors have demonstrated extremely high sensitivity for the detection of salivary cortisol with a detection limit of 2.4 × 10−7 ng mL−1 and a dynamic range of 1.0 × 10−6 ng mL−1 to 10 ng mL−1. Recent innovations also include wearable, textile-based electrochemical sensors that detect cortisol in human sweat.65 A study developed an electrochemical sensor using a polyacrylate-imprinted MIP, demonstrating effective cortisol detection, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 8.00 ng mL−1, falling within the typical physiological concentration of cortisol in human sweat.66 Another device, “StressFit”, combines electromyogram (EMG) monitoring with sweat cortisol detection, allowing for an assessment of physical and biochemical stress indicators, providing a more comprehensive understanding of an individual's stress conditions.67 These devices highlight the potential for integrating such biosensors into wearable textiles for higher-frequency monitoring of cortisol dynamics.

The technologies involved include an antibody-based electrochemical patch utilising antibodies specific to cortisol immobilised on conductive substrates, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) modified carbon screen-printed electrodes.68 Other technologies, such as MIP sensors, employ synthetic polymers formed in the presence of cortisol molecules, creating specific recognition sites complementary to the target analyte. Surface plasmon resonance

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) is an emerging optical sensing technique that can be utilised to detect molecular interactions, such as antigen–antibody binding events. SPR detects changes in the refractive index near the surface of a metal film. This phenomenon occurs when polarised light strikes the metal–dielectric surface at a specific angle, causing free electrons to resonate. The resonance condition is highly sensitive to variations in the local refractive index, making SPR a powerful tool for monitoring biomolecular interactions, including the detection of cortisol in various bodily fluids. In SPR-based cortisol biosensors, the metal surface is typically functionalised with specific recognition elements (e.g. antibodies or aptamers) that selectively bind to cortisol molecules. When cortisol from a sample binds to these recognition elements, it induces a change in the local refractive index at the sensor surface. This change shifts the resonance angle or wavelength of the reflected light, which can be measured to quantify cortisol concentration.72

Stevens et al. developed a portable six-channel SPR biosensor for cortisol detection in saliva using a competitive immunoassay with cortisol-specific monoclonal antibodies. To reduce interference from the complex salivary matrix, the system incorporated an in-line hollow-fibre filtration step that separated small molecules from salivary mucins and other macromolecules before they reached the sensor surface. The platform achieved a detection limit of 1.0 ng mL−1 in saliva, compared with 0.36 ng mL−1 in laboratory buffer, and produced results in less than 10 min. This study demonstrates the potential of portable SPR systems for rapid salivary cortisol measurement while also highlighting the importance of sample conditioning in maintaining analytical performance in complex biological matrices.73

Quantum dots

Quantum dots (QDs) are semiconductor nanocrystals that exhibit unique optical and electronic properties, notably size-tunable fluorescence and high photostability, making them highly suitable for biosensing applications. Quantum dot-based sensors for cortisol typically operate through mechanisms such as fluorescence quenching or electrochemical signal modulation. In the fluorescence quenching approach, QDs are functionalised with specific recognition elements (e.g. aptamers or antibodies) that bind selectively to cortisol molecules. Upon binding, the proximity of cortisol to the QD surface induces fluorescence quenching, leading to a measurable decrease in fluorescence intensity. This change can be correlated with the cortisol concentration in the sample. Researchers have developed such a biosensor with a detection limit of approximately 0.36 ng mL−1 using aptamer-based probes and 0.036 ng mL−1 using antibody-based probes.74 The electrochemical signal modulation approach involves integrating QDs into electrochemical sensors, where the interaction between cortisol and the recognition elements affects the electrochemical properties of the sensors, leading to detectable changes. A study reported an electrochemical aptamer based on functionalised graphene and nitrogen doped carbon QDs for cortisol analysis exhibited a linear detection range from 3.62 × 10−4 to 3.62 ng mL−1 and a low LOD of 1.20 × 10−4 ng mL−1. Both techniques have been successfully applied to detect cortisol in saliva samples, demonstrating their potential for POC testing.75

The optical performance of QDs also depends strongly on their composition and surface chemistry, as the surface coating can influence colloidal stability, fluorescence efficiency and photostability.76 These considerations are particularly important for metal-containing QDs; for example, the toxicity of cadmium-containing QDs is strongly dependent on their composition and surface functionalisation, although the practical relevance of this risk will depend on the degree of user or environmental exposure in the final device format.77

Recognition and interfacial chemistry in cortisol sensing

Across the sensing platforms discussed above, analytical performance depends not only on the transduction mechanism but also on the recognition chemistry and the interface through which molecular binding is converted into a measurable signal (Table 3). Antibodies remain widely used because of their high affinity and compatibility with established immunoassay formats, particularly competitive LFIAs. However, the structural similarity of cortisol to cortisone and other steroids can lead to immunoassay cross-reactivity. In salivary measurements, cortisone cross-reactivity has been shown to contribute to poorer agreement between immunoassay and LC-MS/MS at low cortisol concentrations, while structurally related glucocorticoids such as prednisolone can also interfere with some cortisol immunoassays.40,78 In LFIA systems, performance is further influenced by the chemistry used to conjugate antibodies to labels such as AuNPs, because conjugation conditions can affect particle aggregation, antibody orientation and the accessibility of antigen-binding sites.79 Aptamers and MIPs provide synthetic alternatives to antibody-based recognition, but their analytical behaviour is governed by different chemical principles. Aptamers are sequence-defined nucleic-acid receptors that can be chemically synthesised and readily functionalised for attachment to electrochemical or optical surfaces. Cortisol-specific aptamers have been selected and incorporated into several sensing formats; however, their effective binding behaviour can depend on the immobilisation strategy and local sensing environment because surface attachment can influence aptamer conformation and target accessibility.80–82 MIPs instead generate synthetic recognition sites by polymerising functional monomers around a molecular template. Their relative chemical robustness is advantageous for reusable and wearable sensors, although incomplete template removal, heterogeneous binding sites and non-specific rebinding can reduce selectivity and complicate regeneration.83 Consequently, the performance of aptamer- and MIP-based sensors should be evaluated in the biological matrix for which the device is intended rather than inferred solely from measurements in buffer.

Nanomaterials can further enhance optical or electrochemical transduction but introduce additional interfacial variables. AuNPs provide a strong visible signal in LFIAs, while nanostructured electrode materials can increase the effective sensing area and amplify electrochemical responses. These advantages can nevertheless be offset by particle aggregation, fabrication variability, non-specific adsorption and changes in surface properties during exposure to biological samples.84 Similarly, the optical behaviour of QDs is closely linked to their surface coating and colloidal stability. These considerations are particularly important when comparing sensors with very low limits of detection, because improved buffer-phase sensitivity does not necessarily translate into greater accuracy or reproducibility in authentic saliva. The consequences of matrix interference, biofouling and long-term stability are discussed further in Section 5.

Digital readout and computational interpretation

Digital readout can improve the quantitative interpretation of cortisol POCT by reducing reliance on subjective visual assessment. For LFIAs, smartphone cameras or dedicated readers can quantify test-line intensity and convert the optical signal into an estimated cortisol concentration.85,86 However, image-based measurements can vary with illumination, camera exposure, white balance, viewing angle, focus and device-specific image processing. Reliable smartphone-assisted quantification therefore requires standardised image acquisition or correction methods, together with calibration that remains valid across different phones, strip lots and operating conditions.87–89

Computational methods may further support automated localisation of the test and control lines, background correction, failure detection and quantitative signal interpretation.90 Machine-learning and deep-learning approaches have also been used to classify and quantify LFA results from smartphone images, including automated region-of-interest detection and concentration estimation.89,91,92 The performance of these models depends on the characteristics and diversity of the training data, and validation across different smartphones, assay formats and users is therefore important when considering broader deployment.89,91 Out-of-range measurements, invalid tests and other quality-control failures should also be identified explicitly rather than automatically converted into a physiological interpretation.

For cortisol monitoring, the principal value of digital analysis may therefore lie in improving measurement consistency and supporting interpretation of temporal patterns rather than assigning a universal stress classification to an individual result. Challenges in point-of-care technology for quantification of cortisol

LFIAs have exhibited strong potential for POC, rapid and low-cost detection of cortisol levels. However, their application in semi-quantitative salivary cortisol monitoring faces significant challenges, spanning technical limitations, user-related issues, and validation hurdles. While LFIAs offer rapid results, quantitative salivary cortisol monitoring requires sufficient sensitivity together with reliable discrimination across physiologically relevant concentrations. A 2018 study reported a cortisol LFIA with an LOD of 1.8 ng mL−1 before signal enhancement, while silver enhancement reduced the LOD to 0.5 ng mL−1.55,94 Nevertheless, achieving a low LOD does not necessarily ensure reliable quantification across the physiologically relevant salivary cortisol range. The semi-quantitative nature of many LFIAs, together with calibration variability and matrix-dependent effects, may limit their ability to resolve relatively small changes in cortisol concentration over time. Additionally, cross-reactivity with structurally similar steroids (e.g. cortisone, progesterone) can contribute to biased or inaccurate cortisol measurements.78,95 As such, the selection and optimisation of antibodies with high specificity to cortisol are essential to minimise such interferences. Components of saliva, including mucins, proteins, lipids, electrolytes and cellular debris, can interfere with sensor performance through non-specific adsorption and changes in sample transport. In LFIAs, these components may alter capillary flow, obstruct membrane pores or increase non-specific retention, thereby affecting test-line intensity independently of cortisol concentration. In electrochemical platforms, adsorption of salivary components onto the sensing interface can reduce access to recognition sites, alter interfacial electrical properties and contribute to baseline drift. Sample-conditioning strategies such as membrane filtration, controlled dilution, surfactant-treated pads and antifouling surface coatings may reduce these effects, although their influence on cortisol recovery and assay sensitivity should be evaluated in authentic saliva. Moreover, visual interpretation of test lines introduces subjectivity, which may be reduced through dedicated readers or standardised smartphone-based analysis.96 Dedicated readers can improve quantitative consistency but add cost and instrumentation, potentially reducing some of the portability and affordability advantages of LFIAs.

Cortisol's diurnal rhythm causes cortisol levels to fluctuate greatly throughout the day. Precise sampling times such as 30–45 minutes after awakening – during the cortisol awakening response, may be required to obtain an accurate measurement of cortisol levels in comparison to a reference. However, non-compliance with collection protocols (e.g. delayed sampling, post-waking) introduces variability, as seen in studies where delayed awakening samples missed cortisol spikes.97 Additionally, saliva collection devices may inadvertently collect contaminants, affecting LFIA performance. For example, blood contamination from oral microinjuries can skew results.

Establishing clinically actionable thresholds is further complicated by the strong dependence of salivary cortisol concentrations on sampling time, time since awakening, population characteristics, medication use, recent behaviour and analytical method. For psychiatric and general stress applications, no single salivary cortisol concentration can currently be interpreted as a validated universal threshold for clinical interpretation. Thresholds must instead be defined for a specific intended use, sampling protocol and analytical platform, with validation in the population in which they are to be applied. In this context, repeated measurements and temporal features such as diurnal patterns or within-person changes may provide more informative physiological context than an isolated measurement, although their clinical value also requires prospective validation.

Long-term stability represents a separate challenge for both disposable and reusable cortisol sensors. For LFIAs, changes in antibody activity, nanoparticle conjugate stability, membrane properties and moisture exposure during storage can alter signal intensity and calibration. Electrochemical and wearable sensors additionally require stable recognition layers, electrodes and reference systems over repeated measurements, as well as resistance to mechanical deformation and prolonged exposure to biological fluids. Accordingly, platform evaluation should report relevant storage conditions, shelf life, calibration retention, batch-to-batch reproducibility and, where applicable, the number of validated measurement or regeneration cycles.

Although the simplicity of LFIAs allows for widespread use, this can introduce variability due to differences in user handling, environmental conditions, and subjective interpretation of results. The development of standardised operating procedures is necessary to enhance reproducibility and reduce user-introduced variability. More broadly, these translational challenges are not limited to LFIAs alone. Electrochemical sensors, SPR-based platforms, quantum dot-based sensors and wearable sweat- or interstitial fluid-based systems also face barriers that may limit their use in real-world POCT settings, including matrix interference, biofouling, calibration drift, instrumentation complexity, material reproducibility, sample variability and incomplete clinical validation. Therefore, future cortisol sensing platforms should not be evaluated by analytical sensitivity alone, but also by their reproducibility, ease of use, compatibility with real-world samples, scalability and ability to generate clinically interpretable results.

Translational outlook for point-of-care cortisol monitoring

Interest in rapid and decentralised cortisol measurement has increased because conventional laboratory methods commonly require trained personnel, specialised instrumentation and longer turnaround times.31,36 However, cortisol interpretation is complicated by pronounced circadian and inter-individual variability, making standardised sampling and context-specific interpretation essential.4,7,8 These limitations motivate the development of sensing technologies that can support more accessible and standardised physiological monitoring. Portable point-of-care devices have demonstrated analytical feasibility for rapid cortisol measurement, but their clinical utility remains dependent on validation in authentic samples, comparison with appropriate laboratory reference methods and evaluation in clearly defined intended-use populations.55,85,86 Amongst the biosensing platforms reviewed, LFIAs are particularly suited to applications in which rapid, low-cost and decentralised measurement is prioritised. Electrochemical sensors, SPR and quantum dot sensors offer advantages in analytical sensitivity or temporal resolution, but they involve different requirements for instrumentation, fabrication and validation. The relative value of each platform therefore depends on its intended use rather than analytical sensitivity alone.

In response to the challenges surrounding traditional cortisol measurement methods, there is a clear trend towards developing technologies that facilitate rapid and higher-frequency cortisol monitoring. Among these emerging modalities, LFIA technology has garnered significant attention as a robust alternative for rapid cortisol measurement. The simplicity and portability of LFIAs make them suitable for decentralised research, supervised point-of-care settings and appropriately validated at-home monitoring workflows. Digital readout can be implemented using dedicated lateral-flow readers, as demonstrated by the SOMA cube platform, or through smartphone-assisted image analysis, as used in newer consumer systems such as Hormometer and cortiSense.56,85,86 These approaches can reduce reliance on subjective visual interpretation and facilitate longitudinal aggregation of discrete measurements.88,89 As discussed in Section 4.6, their analytical performance nevertheless depends on robust calibration, reader- or camera-related variability, strip-to-strip and lot-to-lot reproducibility, and independent validation.

Despite these practical advantages, LFIA-based cortisol monitoring remains inherently discrete and must be interpreted in the context of cortisol's pronounced temporal variability. Cortisol follows a diurnal rhythm and changes in response to acute physiological and psychological stimuli; consequently, a single measurement provides only a snapshot of HPA-axis activity.4,97 Serial measurements collected at standardised time points may be used to characterise temporal patterns, but they do not constitute continuous monitoring. Interpretation therefore requires standardised sampling schedules that account for factors such as clock time and time since awakening. Repeated LFIA measurements can be combined with digital records of sampling time and relevant contextual information to examine diurnal or longitudinal patterns, although such patterns remain reconstructed from intermittent measurements rather than measured continuously. In comparison, wearable electrochemical and microneedle-based platforms may support higher-frequency or potentially continuous measurement, depending on the sampling mechanism and demonstrated operating duration.43,49 Such systems can provide greater temporal resolution than discrete LFIAs, but they also face additional challenges including matrix interference, selectivity, biofouling, maintenance of the analytical range, sample availability, power requirements, device longevity, calibration and data interpretation.84 Platform selection should therefore reflect the intended use: LFIAs may be better suited to low-cost, discrete and decentralised measurements, whereas wearable systems may be more appropriate when greater temporal resolution is required and their technical and validation limitations can be adequately addressed. Commercial salivary cortisol platforms also differ substantially in the extent of validation currently available. Reader-based systems such as the SOMA cube have undergone peer-reviewed comparisons with laboratory ELISA methods. Ducker et al. reported moderate correlations between Cube and ELISA measurements but also observed systematic underestimation of salivary cortisol, whereas Mitsuishi et al. subsequently reported significant correlations across five diurnal sampling points without systematic bias in 18 healthy university students.56,57 These findings demonstrate the feasibility of rapid reader-based cortisol quantification while also indicating that agreement with laboratory methods may depend on the study population and measurement protocol.

Newer smartphone-assisted consumer platforms are less extensively characterised in the peer-reviewed literature. Eli Health currently markets Hormometer as a saliva-based wellness device that provides results in approximately 20 min and explicitly states that it is not intended for the diagnosis, treatment or management of disease.58 Nutrix similarly describes cortiSense as a general-wellness product; it is currently available for pre-order with shipment stated for 2026 and is not marketed as a medical device or CE-marked.64 These differences illustrate that commercial availability does not necessarily correspond to equivalent levels of analytical validation, regulatory status or readiness for clinical use. Further evaluation across representative populations, manufacturing lots, operators and real-world use conditions will therefore be important for defining the appropriate applications of these platforms. Additionally, the integration of LFIA devices with digital platforms introduces potential privacy concerns that require rigorous protection measures and compliance with regulatory standards. It is important to ensure that these tools safeguard sensitive health data.

Scalability continues to pose a significant challenge for the successful deployment of LFIA-based cortisol monitoring. The challenge lies in ensuring low batch-to-batch variability during LFIA manufacture. Robust quality control measures and standardised manufacturing protocols are therefore required to preserve signal consistency across production lots.79 In addition, reader-assisted quantification, standardised saliva collection, intended-use-specific quantification and independent validation would further improve reproducibility and help define the appropriate scope of use for LFIA-based cortisol monitoring.

Future research in LFIA technology can extend beyond cortisol monitoring. A promising avenue for future research lies in adapting this technology to quantify other hormones relevant to endocrine and metabolic monitoring. For example, progesterone, insulin and thyroid hormones (T3/T4) are biomarkers relevant to reproductive and metabolic physiology and may represent targets for future multiplexed sensing platforms. Moreover, the development of multiplexed LFIA systems could enable simultaneous measurement of several hormones from a single sample. Their diagnostic or monitoring value would nevertheless depend on analyte-specific accuracy, clinically validated interpretation rules and evidence that the combined measurements improve upon existing testing pathways. Such systems may increase access to repeated hormone measurement, although their value for personal or clinical decision-making must be established prospectively. Their broader utility will ultimately depend on reproducible performance in real-world samples and evidence that multiplexed measurements provide meaningful information beyond existing testing approaches.

Conclusions

Salivary cortisol monitoring offers a practical approach for repeated assessment of HPA-axis activity in stress-related research and selected clinical applications, particularly when combined with POC sensing platforms. Compared with serum, urine, hair, sweat, interstitial fluid and earwax, saliva offers a practical balance between biological relevance, non-invasive collection, patient compliance and compatibility with rapid sensing formats. However, its clinical utility depends on careful control of pre-analytical factors, including sampling time, diurnal variability, food contamination, oral blood contamination and protocol adherence. Saliva should therefore be viewed as a practical matrix for repeated and decentralised physiological measurement, provided that collection timing, preanalytical conditions and interpretation frameworks are carefully standardised.

Among the sensing technologies reviewed, LFIAs remain one of the most practical near-term platforms for salivary cortisol monitoring because of their low cost, portability, ease of use and compatibility with smartphone-assisted readout. Nevertheless, their semi-quantitative nature, susceptibility to user-dependent variability, potential cross-reactivity and inability to provide truly continuous measurements remain important limitations. Electrochemical sensors, SPR platforms, quantum dot-based sensors and wearable microneedle systems offer important advantages in sensitivity, higher-frequency monitoring or multiplexed detection, but many still face challenges in matrix interference, device stability, fabrication complexity, portability and large-scale clinical validation.

Commercial platforms further illustrate the different stages of translation within the field. Established laboratory assays, independently evaluated reader-based systems and newer consumer wellness devices differ substantially in analytical validation, intended use and regulatory status. Commercial availability therefore does not necessarily indicate equivalent analytical performance or readiness for clinical use, highlighting the importance of independent method-comparison studies and clearly defined intended uses when evaluating emerging cortisol-monitoring products.

Overall, the future of cortisol monitoring will depend on matching each sensing platform to a clearly defined intended use—such as laboratory endocrine assessment, repeated decentralised measurement or higher-frequency wearable monitoring—rather than identifying a single universally superior technology. For at-home or decentralised stress monitoring, LFIA-based systems may offer an accessible approach, provided that independent validation, robust calibration and standardised saliva-collection procedures are established. For high-frequency or high-resolution monitoring, wearable electrochemical and microneedle-based systems may provide complementary advantages if issues of biofouling, sensor lifetime, sampling consistency and regulatory approval can be addressed. Future work should prioritise validation in authentic samples and representative populations, comparison with appropriate laboratory reference methods, circadian-aware digital interpretation, protection of sensitive health data and scalable manufacturing with low batch-to-batch variability. Addressing these challenges will be essential for translating cortisol-sensing technologies from analytical research tools into reliable measurement systems capable of supporting clinically interpretable physiological monitoring.
