Knowledge of pharmacy students about autism spectrum disorder in Brazil
Authors: Larissa Couto-Rosa, Paulo Vítor Schultz, Bárbara Brambila-Manso, Kérilin Stancine Santos Rocha, Lorena Rocha Ayres, Dyego Carlos Souza Anacleto de Araújo, Genival Araujo dos Santos Júnior
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmacy students’ and pharmacists’ limited knowledge about Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) can have negative impacts on health care for these individuals. In Brazil, no studies have assessed ASD knowledge among Pharmacy students. This study aims to assess the ASD knowledge among Pharmacy students in Brazil. A cross-sectional survey was conducted with Pharmacy students in Brazil from September 2021 to August 2022. Participants answered online questions about sociodemographic data, contact with people with ASD, and the Brazilian validated version of the Autism Stigma Knowledge – Questionnaire (ASK-Q Brazil), a 49-item questionnaire that assesses ASD knowledge and stigma. Descriptive statistics and the T test were used. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee (Opinion No. 4,464,411). Of the 397 students who participated, most demonstrated adequate knowledge and did not endorse ASD stigma: etiology (99.0%), diagnosis/symptoms (97.0%), treatment (98.7%), and lack of stigma endorsement (100.0%). Those who had a family member with ASD had the highest correct answers in the treatment (p = 0.001; d = 0.45) and diagnosis/symptoms (p = 0.015; d = 0.24) domains. This study showed that almost all students have adequate knowledge about ASD and do not endorse stigma. The results can contribute to the definition of strategies to improve the qualification of future pharmaceutical professionals.Keywords: Knowledge, Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Pharmacy studentsSubject terms: Autism spectrum disorders, Health care

Introduction

People who have Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) have health needs that require the participation of several professionals in the area, including the pharmacist1,2. The training process at the university is important to ensure that professionals are prepared to meet the health needs of these individuals3. However, the literature points to insuficient knowledge about ASD among undergraduate students worldwide4–6. In Brazil, to our knowledge, limited information and study is available regarding the knowledge of Pharmacy students about ASD has not yet been investigated. Assessing the gaps and levels of knowledge among these students can help improve competencies for providing healthcare to people with ASD.

ASD is a neurodevelopmental disorder, with characteristics that involve difficulties in communication, social interaction, and restricted and/or repetitive behaviors7. In recent years, the prevalence of ASD has increased significantly. According to the most recent update of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2023), the prevalence of ASD is 1 in 36 children and, in Brazil, the prevalence is estimated at 2 million individuals8. Due to the prevalence, it is expected that society is prepared to deal with people with ASD. However, the literature indicates gaps in knowledge about ASD in various spheres of the community, such as parents9, education professionals10, healthcare professionals11–13, and university students14.

Notably, lack of knowledge of healthcare professionals about ASD is a barrier to care8. Studies suggest that gaps and/or misconceptions among these professionals can negatively affect health actions and contribute to stigma15,16. Among healthcare professionals, it is essential for pharmacists to have knowledge about ASD, contributing to care through symptom identification, support in therapeutic management, and effective communication with individuals and caregivers17. In turn, gaps in education for pharmacy students, and other health students, may suggest endorsement of stigma and lack of confidence to guide these individuals in clinical practice6,18.

A study conducted with Pharmacy students in the United States (US) found that most students had low familiarity with ASD and lacked instruction on the condition3. In this context, it is essential that Pharmacy students have knowledge about the disorder to ensure that they can properly manage pharmacotherapy, achieve satisfactory outcomes, and improve patient quality of life19,20. Thus, it is important for Pharmacy students to develop competencies for the care of people with ASD, including knowledge about diagnosis/symptoms and treatment3. However, the literature is incipient in evaluating the level of knowledge of health students regarding ASD, especially Pharmacy students3,18,21.

Due to the aforementioned, this study aims to assess the knowledge of Pharmacy students about ASD in Brazil. This work will allow understanding of the knowledge gaps regarding ASD among these future pharmacists, which can be useful in the development of strategies that contribute to the development of skills in the care of people with ASD.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional survey study that aims to assess the knowledge of Pharmacy students regarding ASD, conducted from June 2021 to August 2022. This study followed the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS)22.

Participants and study location

Undergraduate Pharmacy students from both public and private institutions located in Brazil who were 18 years of age or older participated in the study. No exclusion criteria were applied. Invitations to participate were sent on open social media groups related to Pharmacy. Participants were selected by convenience and a snowball recruitment strategy was employed, which is a non-probabilistic method that employs chain referral sampling23,24.

Sample size

Considering a population of 115,693 Pharmacy students enrolled in higher education institutions in Brazil25, a sample size of at least 383 students was required to achieve a 95% confidence interval with a 5% sampling error26. The sample size was calculate using the software G*Power.

Data collection instrument

The questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part contained the items that compose the Brazilian version of the Autism Stigma Knowledge – Questionnaire (ASK-Q Brazil). ASK-Q Brazil was cross-culturally adapted to Brazilian Portuguese in a previous study27. The ASK-Q is a 49-item self-administered questionnaire with dichotomous answers (agree or disagree), divided into four domains: diagnosis/symptoms (18 items), etiology (16 items), treatment (14 items), and stigma (7 items distributed across the other domains). Knowledge is assessed as adequate or inadequate. Each correct answer results in one point, considering that cutoff scores for adequate knowledge are as follows: diagnosis/symptoms (≥ 11 points), etiology (≥ 11 points), and treatment (≥ 10 points) For the items assessing knowledge and stigma, incorrect answers correspond to endorsement of stigma. Thus, the stigma endorsement domain is reverse scored such that higher scores (3–7) reflect lack of stigma endorsement, whereas lower scores (0–2) indicate stigma endorsement28.

The second section of the data collection questionnaire included questions related to sociodemographic data (sex, date of birth, federative unit, type of educational institution, whether the respondent has an ASD diagnosis, whether they have a family member with an ASD diagnosis, and whether they live with someone with ASD), as well as educational information (academic period, whether the topic was addressed in any course, and whether they have taken any course on ASD).

Data collection

The anonymous and self-administered questionnaire was implemented on the Google Forms® online platform version Brasil, from June to November 2021.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel® to Microsoft 365 MSO version 2411. The description of the participants’ profile was performed using descriptive statistics. The T test was used to compare the mean scores according to the variables: having a family member with ASD, living with a person with ASD, type of educational institution, taking a subject that addressed content on ASD, and taking a course that addressed ASD. Bootstrapping procedures were performed (1,000 resamples; 95% BCa CI) to correct deviations from normality in the sample distribution and differences between group sizes29. The confidence interval adopted was 95% and the differences were considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05. To measure the effect size of the comparison between means, Cohen’s d was used, with the following classifications: trivial effect (≥-0.20 and < 0.20), small effect (≥ 0.21 and ≤ 0.39), medium effect (≥ 0.40 and < 0.79), and large effect (≥ 0.80)30,31.

Ethical considerations

This work is part of the research project titled “Evaluation of knowledge of Pharmacy students and Brazilian pharmacists about Autism Spectrum Disorder,” which has been approved (Opinion No. 4,464,411) by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Espírito Santo, Alegre campus, under the registration number CAAE 39222520.6.0000.8151. I confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants signed an informed consent form.

Results

In total, 397 Pharmacy students from 22 of the 27 federative units of the country, representing all regions, participated in the research. Most were women (n = 312; 78.6%), with a mean age of 25 years (minimum age: 19 | maximum age: 61). Regarding type of educational institution, most participants studied in a public institution (n = 268; 67.5%).

When asked about ASD, 1% (n = 4) of the students reported having the disorder, 24.7% (n = 98) reported having a family member with ASD, and 15.6% (n = 62) reported living with someone with ASD. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the knowledge of Pharmacy students about ASD using the ASK-Q questionnaire in Brazil. The results of this study showed that the participants had high rates of correct answers in all domains of the instrument, indicating adequate knowledge about ASD. A study conducted with university students in the US (n = 478), using the ASK-Q, corroborated the results found in this study, as almost all participants had adequate knowledge in the domains of the instrument32. In contrast, another study conducted in the same country, using a questionnaire developed by the authors, identified gaps in the familiarity and knowledge of Pharmacy students on ASD3. In view of the controversial and limited data in the literature, there is a need for further investigations with university students, especially those in the health area, to have a greater understanding of the level of knowledge about ASD.

Although knowledge was found to be adequate in this study, most Pharmacy students were unaware that medications cannot relieve the core symptoms of ASD (communication difficulties, social interaction, and restricted/repetitive behaviors). This result may be associated with some factors, such as lack of knowledge about the core symptoms of ASD and/or a gap in the teaching of pharmacotherapy in ASD. In a study conducted with pharmacists in Palestine (n = 141), more than half of the participants agreed that ASD could be cured with medications33 Other cross-sectional studies conducted with pharmacists in Australia34, in the US35, and with medical students in Palestine6 also showed a lack of knowledge on pharmacotherapy in ASD. In this context, it is essential that the pharmacist in training develops knowledge about ASD to provide adequate care to their future patient, ensuring that pharmacotherapy is appropriately indicated, effective, safe, and convenient.

In this study, we evidenced that students from public institutions had greater knowledge about ASD when compared to students from private institutions. An analysis of the teaching of Pharmacy in Brazil identified that public institutions showed better quality indicators related to the performance of students, professors, and conditions offered for the development of the teaching-learning process36. The scenario of Pharmacy schools in Brazil is diverse, with 429 private institutions and 73 public institutions25. In this national context, although the Brazilian Ministry of Education established the National Curriculum Guidelines for Pharmacy Programs37, the differences between institutions, student profiles, teachers, and teaching methodologies may have influenced these students’ knowledge about ASD. Given the complexity of training Brazilian pharmacists, multifaceted strategies should be designed to fill this gap, which may include fostering professor qualification, teaching-service-community integration of Pharmacy courses, and continuing education.

We highlight that, in this study, having a family member with ASD or living with a person with ASD was a factor that influenced knowledge about this condition. With the recent prevalence of 1 in every 36 children diagnosed with ASD, it is likely that more people will come into contact with the condition, which, in turn, could increase awareness of ASD. In studies conducted in the US using the ASK-Q, a statistically significant association was also found in the diagnosis/symptoms domain among university students who knew someone with ASD5,32. Another study in the US conducted with university students identified that knowing someone with ASD was related to more positive attitudes towards the condition, which suggests greater knowledge and less stigma38. This finding may be related to the fact that people who live in the midst of the theme of ASD are more familiar, either due to interest or exposure to the subject. With the recent prevalence of 1 in every 36 children diagnosed with ASD, it is likely that more people will come into contact with the condition, which, in turn, will contribute to increased awareness of ASD. Therefore, the inclusion of individuals with ASD in everyday settings, especially learning spaces, may be beneficial in providing more knowledge and reducing the stigma associated with the condition5,39.

In our study, most students reported not having taken any subject that addressed ASD. In the study by Khanna et al.. (2014), most students also reported that ASD was not addressed in their courses, and 91% stated that the content should be included in the Pharmacy curriculum3. These data suggest that students may have acquired this knowledge from other sources of information, such as television, books, social media, or online blogs4,40,41. Thus, this information can complement the content of the undergraduate program through visual, dynamic, and interactive formats that accommodate different learning styles and enhance students’ understanding and connection to the real world. In Brazil, it is possible to find social media profiles about ASD with considerable numbers of followers, which generate engagement and recognition42. In this technological era, in which opinion leaders often use their platforms to share topics, media sources can serve as tools to enhance knowledge and influence public perception regarding ASD43.

This study holds both strengths and limitations. The limitations of this study include: (i) the results may not represent all Pharmacy students across Brazil; (ii) there was no control over whether the participants consulted external sources of information when answering the questions, given the use of an online questionnaire was employed; and (iii) convenience sampling was adopted, which may have led to a higher participation rate among individuals more familiar with ASD compared to those less familiar with the subject. Among the strengths are: (i) the originality of the work, being the first study conducted to assess the knowledge of Pharmacy students in Brazil using the ASK-Q; (ii) national coverage, involving all five regions of Brazil, a country with vast continental dimensions and social and cultural differences; and (iii) the use of the ASK-Q Brasil, which was developed with notable psychometric support highlighted by intercultural validity28 and evidence of content validity for Brazilian Portuguese27.

Conclusion

The students had adequate knowledge in all domains of the instrument and did not endorse ASD stigma. The factors associated with a higher level of knowledge were: attending a public institution, having a family member with ASD, or living with someone with ASD. Students who had a family member with ASD demonstrated greater knowledge in the domains of etiology and diagnosis/symptoms, whereas students who lived with someone with ASD showed greater knowledge in the diagnosis/symptoms domain. This study holds important implications for the evaluation of knowledge about ASD among these Pharmacy students in Brazil and can lead to further investigations into the national landscape of this topic, promoting the improvement of student education and the qualification of future pharmacists in providing humanized and accessible care to people with ASD.
