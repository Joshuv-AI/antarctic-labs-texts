ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimPerforming a point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) scan in critical care, along with the incorporation of the findings into a clinical pathway, has now become the standard of care. The Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine (ISCCM) has established an ultrasound network and released the desirable competencies for intensive care unit (ICU) clinicians regarding the same. This survey was performed to study the practice patterns and barriers to utilizing POCUS among various specialists across Indian ICUs.MethodsAn online survey-based, cross-sectional, multicenter, observational study was conducted over a period of 3 months with a questionnaire based on the utility, skills, training, and comfort in performing POCUS in Indian ICUs.ResultsA total of 864 responses were received and analyzed. A total of 67.4% among them were specialists in critical care medicine, and 67.2% had received some form of training in POCUS. Most clinicians reported using POCUS for lung assessments, volume status evaluation, and focused cardiac assessments. Key barriers were inadequate training, limited machines, lack of supervision, time constraints, and medicolegal issues, though 32.4% reported no major obstacles. The majority of participants agreed that POCUS enables more efficient and safer care for critically ill patients. 624/864 respondents strongly agreed that POCUS training is essential for intensivists.ConclusionPoint-of-care ultrasound is being increasingly adopted across various specialties in critical care settings. Highlights

This is the first survey of its kind on point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS) in intensive care units (ICUs) across India, garnering over 800 responses. The survey offers valuable insights into the current practices of POCUS usage at various centers across India. It also highlights the barriers to its adoption and identifies areas with potential for further improvement.

Introduction

The use of point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS) for bedside ultrasonographic assessment, with the integration of findings into patient management plans, is rapidly gaining traction for various indications in critical care settings. Its noninvasive nature, lack of harmful radiation exposure, portability, and ability to be easily repeated make it an invaluable tool in the assessment, diagnosis, and procedural management of critically ill patients, whose condition is constantly evolving.1 As such, ultrasonography is increasingly seen as “the extended hand” of intensivists in the intensive care unit (ICU). Given India's high patient burden, resource limitations, and diverse disease profiles, POCUS offers an accessible, rapid diagnostic tool at the bedside.

The objective of the study was to capture data on the practice patterns of POCUS in critically ill patients and to identify factors affecting its use and its training in Indian ICUs.

It can help identify practice gaps, support evidence-based integration into clinical pathways, and foster standardized training and policy development.

Methods

An Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine (ISCCM) ultrasonography (USG) network initiated an online survey-based, cross-sectional, observational multicentric study. The multiple-choice structured survey questionnaire was drafted, reviewed, and finalized by the network members (Supplementary file). The questionnaire comprised information regarding hospital infrastructure, availability of USG machine, its utilization for various indications and procedures, mode of training, comfort, and barriers to its use, if any. If the USG machine was not available to any physicians, they would jump to the last section of the questionnaire directly.

Approval, including consent waiver from the Medanta Institutional Ethics Committee, was obtained. The questionnaire was then copied to “Google Form,” an online survey tool. The survey link was subsequently circulated to all ISCCM members and non-members through email and other electronic modes of communication, including individual as well as social media, between October 2023 and January 2024. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered.

Any intensivist working in Indian ICUs could participate in the study. Clinicians not working in Indian ICUs were excluded from the study. The questionnaire began with a gatekeeping question on whether the clinician was affiliated with an Indian ICU; respondents were allowed to proceed only if they confirmed this.

Statistical Analysis

The collected data were transferred to an Excel sheet, and descriptive analysis was performed. The analysis included profiling of respondents on different demographic backgrounds and professional parameters. Categorical data was expressed as an absolute number and a percentage.

A total of 864 clinicians, from 26 Indian states, participated in the survey. The top three states with the maximum number of respondents were Maharashtra (13.9%), Delhi (11.8%), and Haryana (10.6%) (Figure S1 – supplementary file). Table ST1 (in the supplementary file) summarizes the availability of the ultrasound machine. Meanwhile, 85 out of 117 clinicians (72.6%) from government hospitals and 22 out of 39 (56.4%) from nursing homes had their own USG machines (Table ST2 – in supplementary file). The clinicians who did not have any USG machines (20/864) skipped to the last section of the questionnaire. Hence, 844 clinicians were considered for further analysis.

Mode of POCUS Training

Among the 844 respondents who had access to a USG machine, 567 (67.2%) had received some form of training in POCUS. Of those trained, the majority of respondents (57.7%) participated in formal USG training programs, workshops, or certification courses, while 37.4% gained their skills within their departments or during their fellowship training (Table ST3 in the supplementary file).

Availability of USG Probes and Frequency of POCUS Examination

The survey recorded and documented the availability of various ultrasound probes (linear, phased array, and curvilinear), the frequency of POCUS use, and the practice of preparing a structured examination report (Table ST4 in the supplementary file). Linear, phased array, and curvilinear ultrasound probes were available to 651 out of 844 participants. Based on the available data, the linear probe was most commonly available. A total of 38.2% (322/844) of respondents reported performing POCUS at admission and whenever there was a change in the patient's clinical condition. Regarding the preparation of structured examination reports, only 25.2% of respondents reported routinely documenting them, while 32.5% do so occasionally. POCUS for Invasive Procedures

Ultrasonography guidance is most strongly preferred for procedures such as central venous catheter insertion (84.8%), hemodialysis catheter insertion (83.6%), thoracocentesis (77.4%), pericardiocentesis (73.8%), and paracentesis (73.7%), where precision is critical in minimizing complications. In procedures like arterial catheter placement (48.1%), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) cannulation (53.4%), abscess drainage (53.8%), and peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) insertion (58.8%), USG is moderately preferred, though a significant proportion of practitioners also consider it an alternative option. Some procedures, including tracheostomy (33.3% preferred, 38.9% alternative) and difficult peripheral venous access (44% preferred, 43.4% alternative), demonstrate a nearly equal split between preferred and alternative categories. In contrast, USG is rarely used for endotracheal intubation (7.9% preferred, 54.7% never used) and lumbar puncture (7.2% preferred, 58.5% never used) (Figure S3 – supplementary file).

Discussion

This nationwide survey offers valuable insights into the current practice patterns, barriers, and perceptions regarding POCUS among critical care clinicians in India. With participation from 864 clinicians across 26 states, the study reflects a broad and diverse representation of practice environments and clinician profiles. We observed that a greater number of clinicians now use POCUS and consider it an important part of the care and management of critically ill. Most responses (66.4%) came from corporate hospitals, potentially indicating greater accessibility to ultrasound machines and better resources. Among the 864 respondents, 20 reported a lack of access to ultrasound machines. This issue was primarily noted in government hospitals and nursing homes, highlighting disparities in equipment availability across different healthcare settings. Clinicians specializing in critical care medicine constituted the largest group of respondents.

In our study, although POCUS is being incorporated into clinical practice by clinicians, only 38.7% of the 864 respondents reported having undergone formal training in the same. The remaining participants either acquired skills through clinical exposure, were self-taught, or had not received any structured training.

The respondents still feel that they lack knowledge or training on POCUS, and this was the most commonly reported reason for not using POCUS. This finding emphasizes the need to intensify training programs. Approximately 30% reported that supervision and feedback about ultrasound examination and findings are lacking. Only 32.4% did not find any sort of inhibition in using POCUS. A significant percentage of respondents feel uncomfortable performing ultrasound assessments across different organ systems. Although consultants and faculty reported slightly higher levels of comfort compared to trainees, a substantial number still lack confidence in performing certain examinations. This trend may contribute to a cycle in which faculty with limited proficiency train trainees, leading to their perception of inadequate learning and the potential perpetuation of inaccurate skills. Our findings seem consistent with those of Wenger et al. and Slemko et al., who also reported that lack of formal training and lack of supervision were among the important barriers to use of POCUS, and that a significant percentage of practitioners are not comfortable in performing certain assessments.2,3 Sternschein et al. suggested that an approach similar to “near-peer training,” where the teachers and students are similarly experienced in a particular subject, can be followed.4 Anyone in the team who has got training regarding basic skills in POCUS can be a “near-peer teacher” to the rest of the team members. However, the near-peer teacher should have received the training session with an expert, and this approach should be backed by strict feedback mechanisms. Saving the images for review with the experts, either in-person or online meeting, and interdisciplinary training sessions involving different departments like cardiology, radiology, critical care, and emergency medicine, are among the various strategies that can help improve the quality and confidence for use of POCUS. The fellowship in critical care ultrasound (FCCU) by ISCCM has been recently initiated and is expected to fill the lacunae existing due to a lack of structured courses and supervised training in India.

A significant percentage of respondents also reported limited access to ultrasound machines, posing another obstacle to the use of POCUS. The reasons for this lack of access—including medico-legal constraints that discourage usage—should be investigated and addressed. In India, acquiring an ultrasound machine and obtaining mandatory registration for its use are strictly regulated under the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PC-PNDT) Act. This legislation, designed to prevent the misuse of ultrasonography, mandates that all doctors using ultrasound register at a specific center. Regulatory concerns were cited as a barrier by 24% of respondents. In 2023, the Central Supervisory Board proposed an amendment to the act aimed at expediting the decision-making process for registration and renewal applications, which is expected to enhance ultrasound accessibility and utilization across various centers.5 We found that more than 70% of respondents prefer to use ultrasound to guide invasive procedures like venous line insertion, thoraco-, pericardio-, and paracentesis. However, for certain other procedures like abscess drainage, suprapubic cystostomy, ECMO cannulation, etc., the percentage of respondents preferring the ultrasound guidance is less. One reason could be that they might not be performing these procedures at their centers themselves. Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine Position Statement for central venous catheterization (CVC) 2020 recommends using USG guidance for CVC placement, wherever available, to improve the success rate, patient safety, and procedural quality and reduce procedure-related mechanical complications.6 In an observational study, the authors concluded that real-time USG-guided percutaneous tracheostomy is an effective and safe procedure even in patients with a deranged coagulation profile.7

A considerable proportion of clinicians are now incorporating POCUS into the evaluation of patients across a range of critical scenarios, including undifferentiated shock, hypoxia, trauma, and cardiac arrest. However, neurological assessments such as optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) measurement, transcranial Doppler (TCD), and venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) remain among the least utilized applications, indicating areas where POCUS adoption could be further expanded. Despite the growing use of POCUS, comprehensive assessments are not consistently performed in these clinical situations. Current ISCCM guidelines recommend an initial rapid cardiac evaluation (RACE), complemented by lung ultrasound (LUS) and a DVT scan when feasible, to aid in determining the underlying type of shock, followed by a detailed echocardiographic assessment. It is recommended to use POCUS evaluation to guide fluid therapy decisions.8 Integrating POCUS into patient discussions, ensuring machine availability, and incorporating it into bedside rounds can promote a more systematic approach to ultrasound-based examinations.

The majority of clinicians agreed that POCUS guidance enhances efficiency, improves safety, and reduces complications during invasive procedures. They also concurred that POCUS should be incorporated as a standard of care in the ICU and that formal POCUS training should be mandatory in critical care education.

More awareness and mandatory structured curriculum in the critical care training programs will help increase the comfort, expertise, and utilization of POCUS across various centers, specialties, and for different clinical indications.

Only 25.2% of the respondents prepare a formal POCUS assessment report every time they perform an examination. Preparing a structured pro forma to document POCUS findings along with other clinical assessment notes would help improve the documentation and serial assessment of the patients.

Given that approximately 38% of respondents cited the lack of prompt ultrasound machine availability and 26% reported insufficient bedside time as barriers to POCUS use, it is essential for institutional leadership to investigate these challenges and foster a more supportive and collaborative clinical environment. The strength of our study is that it is an extensive questionnaire, and a large number of clinicians participated in the survey. However, it is limited by the fact that most of the respondents were those who are already using POCUS at their centers. There is a possibility of a selection bias, as only those using POCUS would have answered in the majority. Having more open-ended questions or conducting interviews would have resulted in an in-depth understanding of the utilization and limitations. Additional variables—such as evaluation of diaphragmatic function, the specific type of ICU, the number of intensivists per shift, or the ICU doctor-to-patient ratio—could also have been incorporated into the survey.

A core curriculum was proposed by Neri et al., which was based on critical problem-based pathways.9 Indian Society of Critical Care Medicine recently released the desired core competencies regarding the use of POCUS expected from a clinical worker in ICUs.10 In a study conducted by Turner et al., it was found that the “simulation-based learner” group was more knowledgeable and confident in bedside ultrasound vs the comparison group, who were informally trained.11 This stresses the need for a structured curriculum for the existing and budding intensivists. With a structured ultrasound fellowship program initiated by ISCCM and certain other POCUS training programs being conducted across India, it is expected that the use of POCUS in critical care in India will improve.

Conclusion

This survey highlights the growing use of POCUS in Indian ICUs, alongside persistent barriers such as limited training, equipment access, and medico-legal concerns. While most clinicians recognize its value in improving patient care and procedural safety, variability in skill and confidence remains. Structured training programs, better equipment availability, and standardized curricula might help to further integrate POCUS into routine critical care practice.
