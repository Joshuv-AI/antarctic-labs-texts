Does the type of biomesh influence recurrence in abdominal wall reconstruction? A long-term follow-up to a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jaiden Punia, Chad G Ball, Andrew W Kirkpatrick, Thomas Clements
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis discussion describes the long-term follow-up of a prospective randomized controlled trial evaluating the performance of the 2 most common biomesh products utilized in the context of complex abdominal wall reconstructions. Although the incidence of hernia recurrence increased over time to 26% (median follow-up 62 mo), both biomesh products performed similarly. Biomesh product choice should remain cost-conscious.

Complex abdominal wall reconstruction remains a challenging technical procedure in a subset of patients who often have important medical comorbidities and have suffered substantially in the domains of both physical and mental health. In 2022, our group published a prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the performance of the 2 most common biomesh products utilized in the context of complex abdominal wall reconstructions.1 The study concluded that, despite significant differences in vendor product costs and claims, the performance of each product was similar.1 In cost-conscious health care systems, this finding was met with widespread acceptance and importance.

Although we noted in the RCT a potentially more technically difficult cohort of hernias in one of the groups — the lower-cost product was associated with more complicated component separation cases — the outcomes and performance were comparable between patient groups and, therefore, we concluded the products were statistically similar.1 This observation most importantly extended to an overall hernia recurrence rate of 14.9% at a mean recurrence interval of 151 days.

Although few studies with longer follow-up are available, unfortunately, it is evident that the hernia recurrence rate typically increases with longer duration of follow-up.2 Despite a median follow-up of 783 days (26 mo) in our RCT, the critical nature of longer-term follow-up (i.e., for evidence of hernia recurrence and complications) was stated and promised in the initial publication.1 Of the 94 original patients (46 and 48 patients randomized to each biomesh group), we completed ongoing evaluation of 93 patients (98.9%). This monitoring occurred via the operating surgeons’ standard practice and comprised both in-person and virtual telemedicine visits. The median extended follow-up interval in these patients was 62 months, with some patients followed for as long as 7 years (RCT study recruitment ended on Aug. 31, 2020; long-term follow-up ended on Aug. 31, 2024). The overall hernia recurrence rate increased from 14.9% at the initial reported RCT follow-up to 25.8% (24/93 patients) in August 2024. Although disappointing, these rates and their associated changes with time fit well within the literature.2 Interestingly, the particular biomesh product that was utilized did not predict recurrence in the long term (p = 0.44). Both products continued to perform similarly. Given preceding discussions regarding the importance of cross-linking within the manufacturing processes of these products1 (one biomesh is cross-linked while the other is not), it still appears as though these claims may be overstated.

Consistent with the preceding published literature, risk factors for hernia recurrence in the RCT included more than 1 previous hernia repair (range 2–5), active abdominal wall infections, and a history of liver transplantation.1 Additional factors, such as repair technique and blood loss, did not appear to affect the rate of recurrence in either group, or overall.1 These observations are consistent with what we observed during the longer-term follow-up of 62 months as well. No additional patient or technical factors became relevant in predicting longer-term hernia recurrence.

Although we still consider that the choice of a mesh product for hernia repairs in general, as well as the specific technique (and location of mesh placement), remains crucial, it appears as though the selection between the 2 primary market-leading biomesh products is less crucial. Despite this observation, it remains important to be vigilant in the initial decision to trigger the use/implantation of a biomesh, as well as cost-conscious in the choice of product. Whether a surgeon practises in a public or private health care system, cost-effectiveness should always remain relevant. There is usually a “best” mesh product that should be employed for the specific presenting patient; sometimes that means no mesh implantation at all, or delaying mesh implantation until the patient improves from an anatomical and local field point of view. In an environment with little ongoing regulatory oversight, broad device classification effect thresholds for initial Health Canada approval, industry proprietary claims, and often surgeon-targeted pressure, these decisions unfortunately remain almost the sole responsibility of the operating surgeon.3 By ensuring our surgical techniques are well founded with sufficient initial training and continuing medical education, our mesh product selections are rooted in science, and our biomesh usage is supported by our local health care systems, we will continue to maintain the trust and confidence of the general public.
