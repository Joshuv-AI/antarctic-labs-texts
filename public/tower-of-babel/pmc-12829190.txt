The combined effect of geriatric nutritional risk index and abdominal obesity on peripheral arterial disease in an elderly hypertensive population: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Chuanli Yu, Chao Yu, Congcong Ding, Huihui Bao, Xiaoshu Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with abdominal obesity may be at risk of a double burden of obesity and malnutrition, which may lead to an increased burden of disease.MethodsA total of 4,735 elderly hypertensive patients aged ≥ 60 years were included in the final analysis in this prospective study, which started from May to August 2018. Cox multivariate regression model was used to explore the association between geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) and new-onset peripheral artery disease (PAD) in different patients, and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze the nonlinear relationship.ResultsThe mean age of patients with hypertension was 67.6 years at baseline. A total of 279 (5.9%) new-onset PAD cases occurrences over a median observation period of 4 years. GNRI was used to assess nutritional risk in elderly patients with hypertension and calculated after weighting serum albumin and weight loss, and the specific formula was as follows: GNRI = [1.489 × serum albumin (g/L)] + [41.7 × (actual weight/ideal weight)]. PAD was defined as ABI ≤ 0.90 on either side of the limb. The full adjusted Cox regression model showed that compared with the upper tertile (GNRI > 112), the hazard ratio of new PAD in the lower tertile (GNRI < 106) was 2.13 (95%CI: 1.54–2.96) in the total population and 2.52 (95%CI: 1.70–3.73) in the abdominal obesity patients, and nonlinear relationships between GNRI and new-onset PAD were found in the total population and patients with abdominal obesity. However, no association or threshold between GNRI and new-onset PAD was found in patients with non-abdominal obesity. The combined effect showed that the malnutrition-abdominal obesity group had a higher risk of new-onset PAD (HR:2.26, 95%CI: 1.21–4.21).ConclusionsAmong Chinese adults with hypertension, a lower GNRI increases the risk of PAD, which may have important implications for risk stratification of PAD, especially in patients with abdominal obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26008-6.Keywords: Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index, Peripheral arterial disease, Abdominal obesity, Hypertension, Epidemiology

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) has already impacted over 230 million people worldwide and is closely associated with the adverse outcomes of cardiovascular diseases and lower limb events [1]. The incidence of PAD escalates as individuals advance in age, particularly within the geriatric demographic [2]. Nevertheless, ranking third among prevalent arteriosclerosis conditions after coronary atherosclerotic disease and stroke, PAD regrettably often goes unnoticed and fails to receive the attention it deserves from individuals, ultimately resulting in grave outcomes such as amputation or fatality. Not only does this impose a substantial economic burden on the nation and households, but it also inflicts psychological and physical anguish upon individuals, while compromising their overall life quality.

Malnutrition frequently arises as a consequence of acute and chronic ailments such as infection, cardiac insufficiency, renal dysfunction, and high blood pressure, and portends an unfavorable prognosis, particularly among the geriatric population. According to the criteria proposed in the 2019 Global Clinical Nutrition Community Consensus report, malnutrition is a combination of at least one phenotypic criterion (weight loss, low body mass index or decreased muscle mass) and one etiological criterion (reduced intake or absorption of food, presence of disease burden/inflammation) [3]. Geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) is a promising and objective nutritional risk screening tool in elderly individuals, which is based on albumin level and body weight changes [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that a low GNRI is closely related to poor prognosis of chronic diseases such as coronary atherosclerotic heart disease [6], heart failure [7], stroke [8], renal insufficiency [9], and diabetes [10]. Similarly, studies have shown that malnutrition is an independent risk factor for amputation and all-cause mortality in patients with Critical limb ischemia or after revascularization [11, 12]. The study by Zhang et al. found that lower GNRI was associated with a reduced risk of hypertension in the general population, but an increased poor prognosis in hypertensive patients. [13]

Hypertension and abdominal obesity have become the primary risk factors for cardiovascular disease, and the absolute risk of hypertension and abdominal obesity is higher in the elderly [14]. However, obesity does not mean good nutrition. With the increase of age and comorbidities, elderly patients may suffer from the double burden of malnutrition and obesity [15]. Compared with the general population, patients with abdominal obesity and malnutrition have a higher risk of all-cause mortality [16]. It is of great clinical significance to pay attention to the nutrition problems of people with double burden.

Nevertheless, there are few studies on people with the double burden of abdominal obesity and malnutrition. Conducting a nutritional risk evaluation and abdominal obesity among individuals with hypertension without PAD at baseline has the potential to enhance risk categorization and mitigate the likelihood of PAD and additional associated complications.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this study, all data were obtained from the database of the Chinese H-Hypertension Registry (registration number: ChiCTR1800017274), which has been reported in other studies [17]. This study is a prospective cohort study that has been ongoing since 2018. The participants were all over 18 years of age and had hypertension, which is defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 140 mmHg, or/and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥ 90 mmHg measured after 5 min of sitting still on three different days, or/and use of antihypertensive medication at baseline. Baseline exclusion criteria included the followings: (1) Inability to provide informed consent due to psychological illness or neurological impairment; (2) Inability to follow up for a long time according to the study protocol or plan to relocate in the short term; (3) Patients evaluated by physicians who were not suitable for enrollment or long-term follow-up (Such as patients who need to be hospitalized). Finally, a total of 14, 343 hypertensive patients from Wuyuan, Jiangxi Province in China were recruited for the prospective study with a median follow-up duration of 4 years. Data exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Those who did not attend the on-site follow-up (n = 4, 901); 2) people under 60 years old (n = 2, 927); 3) missing baseline ankle-brachial index (ABI) (n = 1, 480) and patients with PAD at baseline (n = 124); 4) patients without ABI at the second follow-up (n = 64); 5) patients with missing baseline serum albumin, height, and weight data. In the end, 4,735 elderly hypertensive patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the study

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Anhui Medical University (Ethics NO.CH1059) and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Ethics NO.2018019), and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients volunteered to participate and signed informed consent before participating in this study.

Data collection

The demographic characteristics of the study population were collected by trained researchers. Demographic information included sex, age, lifestyle (smoking status, alcohol drinking status, economic status and condition of exercise etc.), history of disease, and history of medication use. Anthropometric measures included waist circumference, hip circumference, height, weight, SBP and DBP. Blood pressure was measured as the average of three measurements with an electronic sphygmomanometer (OMRON).

All participants would be informed a day in advance that fasting venous blood would be drawn the following morning. Venous blood samples from all study participants were frozen and transported to Shenzhen Biaojia Biotechnology Laboratory for testing. Plasma homocysteine, fasting blood glucose, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein, albumin, uric acid, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured by Beckman Coulter automatic clinical analyzer. Laboratory staffs were blind to the study content.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m). Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, history of diabetes, or use of glucose-lowering drugs. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, and parameters of the equation were measured by Beckman Coulter automatic clinical analyzer [18].

Outcome

The outcome was defined as new-onset PAD during follow-up after the exclusion of patients with PAD at baseline, and the presence of PAD was defined as ABI ≤ 0.90 on either side of the limb [19]. As a rapid and specific measurement method, ABI has been used to screen, diagnose and assess the severity of lower extremity arterial disease [20]. The ABI was calculated as the highest systolic blood pressure at the ankle divided by the highest systolic blood pressure at the ipsilateral upper arm, which were measured by the Omron Colin BP-203RPE III device (Omron Health Care, Kyoto, Japan) after participants rested supine for 5 min. According to the 2017 ESC Guidelines on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Peripheral Arterial Diseases, the lower ABI value of both sides was taken for analysis [19].

GNRI and abdominal obesity

In this study, GNRI was used to assess nutritional risk in elderly patients with hypertension. GNRI was calculated after weighting serum albumin and weight loss, and the specific formula was as follows: GNRI = [1.489 × serum albumin (g/L)] + [41.7 × (actual weight/ideal weight)] [5]. The ratio of actual body weight to ideal body weight can reflect the degree of weight loss. If the actual weight was greater than the ideal weight, the actual weight/ideal weight ratio was set to 1, which indicates no weight loss and can reduce the influence of weight gain. The ideal body weight was calculated according to the Lorentz formula as follows: For male participants, ideal weight (Kg) = 0.75 × height (cm) – 62.5 and for female participants, the ideal weight (Kg) = 0.60 × height (cm) – 40. Based on the above values of GNRI, we divided the GNRI into three equal fraction groups [T1(≥ 112), T2(112, 106) and T3(< 106)]. In addition, according to the degree of serum albumin and weight loss, patients were classified according to the following thresholds: moderate to severe malnutrition risk (M/S risk): < 92; low risk: ≥ 92 to < 98; no risk indicating ≥ 98 [5]. Abdominal obesity was defined as the ratio of waist circumference to hip circumference (WHR) in men > 0.90 and female > 0.85. In contrast, non-abdominal obesity was defined as WHR in men ≤ 0.90 and female ≤ 0.85.

Covariates

In addition to age, sex, BMI, SBP, DBP, smoking status, alcohol drinking status, plasma homocysteine, fasting blood glucose, LDL-C, uric acid and eGFR conventional confounding factors, we also adjusted for variables that contributed more than 10% to the hazard ratio when added to the Cox regression model, such as WHR, HDL-C and history of disease (diabetes, CHD, stroke, heart failure and atrial fibrillation). We further adjusted ALT to account for the possible effect of liver function on nutritional status. Economic status and condition of exercise may have a certain impact on lower limb function and need further adjustment. Considering the possible effect of adding adjustment variables in Table 2 (Model II) on statistical power, we adjusted for medication use (antihypertensive drugs, glucose-lowering drugs, lipid-lowering drugs and antiplatelet drugs) in sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentage (%). One-way ANOVA or χ2 test were used to test the differences of each variable among the three GNRI groups, and T-test or χ2 test were used to test the differences between the two groups. Cox regression equation was used to explore the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of the association between GNRI (continuous and categorical variable) and PAD after adjusting for known confounding factors, and the upper GNRI tertile (≥ 112) and categorical no risk group of GNRI (> 98) were used as a reference. Tests for trend were performed by modeling the tertile classification of GNRI as a continuous variable. At the same time, restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to estimate the potential nonlinear association between GNRI and the relative risk of PAD in the general population and abdominal obesity. When a nonlinear relationship was found, threshold saturation effect analysis and a recursive algorithm were further used to calculate inflection points.

Considering that heart failure, economic status and condition of exercise may have an impact on the risk of PAD, the multiple imputation method was used for imputation to impute missing data for heart failure, economic status and condition of exercise, and further Cox multivariate regression analysis.

In order to further evaluate the potential influence of different populations on GNRI and the relative risk of PAD, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to assess potential effect modifiers. The effects of gender, age (≥ 75 vs. < 75 year), BMI (≥ 24 vs. < 24 kg/m2), SBP (≥ 140 vs. < 140 mmHg), DBP (≥ 90 vs. < 90 mmHg), smoking status, alcohol consumption status, homocysteine (≥ 15 vs. < 15 µmol/L), LDL-C (≥ 2.6 vs. < 2.6 mmol/L), UA (≥ 420 vs. < 420 mmol/L), eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2), stroke (Yes vs. No), diabetes (Yes vs. No) and atrial fibrillation (Yes vs. No) were compared and performed subgroup analyses.

A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All data were analyzed using the statistical packages R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, http://www.r-project.org) and Empower (R) (X&Y Solutions, Inc.; www.empowerstats.com).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

In this research, a total of 4,735 elderly individuals diagnosed with hypertension and aged 60 years or above were ultimately incorporated into the examination, following the exclusion of individuals who did not present at the designated location and those with incomplete data (Fig. 1). The median follow-up was 4 years, and the initial characteristics of the entire population and the subgroups based on GNRI levels are depicted (Table 1). In the overall populace, the average age was 76.60 years, and the average GNRI registered at 109.40. Compared to the group categorized with an upper tertile (T1 group), the group classified with a lower tertile (T3 group) exhibited reduced body mass index (BMI) and albumin levels. Between the GNRI three groups, gender, age, height, BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference and albumin etc. were statistically significant (p < 0.05). In addition, the proportion of abdominal obesity in third class group was 79.6%, 71.6%, and 52.5%, respectively (p < 0.001). However, there were no significant differences in body weight, alcohol drinking status, economic conditions, condition of exercise, and comorbidities of stroke, chronic heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation between GNRI groups (p > 0.05). In supplementary Table 1, grouped by outcome PAD, patients with PAD were older and had lower GNRI, BMI, and albumin levels (p < 0.05).

Association between GNRI and new-onset PAD in total population and patients with abdominal obesity

During 4 years of follow-up, the prevalence of new-onset PAD in all patients was 279 (5.89%), with 183 (5.69%) in abdominal obesity patients and 96 (6.33%) in non-abdominal obesity patients, respectively. Restricted cubic spline was used to visually assess associations between GNRI and the risk of new-onset PAD in different populations (Fig. 2 and 3. As can be seen from Fig. 2, the association between GNRI and the relative risk of PAD showed an L-shaped curve, and the relative risk of PAD increased significantly when GNRI decreased to a certain value. Further stratified analysis showed that compared with non-abdominal obese patients, abdominal obese patients had a higher risk of incident PAD. An L-shaped curve between GNRI and new-onset PAD was observed in abdominal obese patients but not in non-abdominal obese patients (Fig. 3).

In the Cox multiple regression fully adjusted models (Table 2), compared with an upper tertile (GNRI ≥ 112), the hazard ratio of PAD in the lower tertile (GNRI < 106) was increased by 1.13 times (HR: 2.13, 95%CI: 1.54, 2.96) and 1.52 times (HR: 2.52, 95%CI: 1.70, 3.73) and in the total population and abdominal obesity, but 0.45 times (HR: 1.45, 95%CI: 0.79, 2.68) in non-abdominal obesity. According to the clinical cut-off point, compared with the no nutritional risk group (GNRI > 98), the hazard ratio of PAD in the moderate to severe nutritional risk group (GNRI ≤ 92) was increased by 1.50 times (HR: 2.13, 95%CI: 1.19, 5.24) and 3.32 times (HR: 4.32, 95%CI: 1.30, 14.3) and in the total population and abdominal obesity, but 0.95 times (HR: 1.45, 95%CI: 0.71, 5.35) in non-abdominal obesity. In addition, the p for trend test p < 0.05 in all population and patients with abdominal obesity. On the contrary, the p for trend test p > 0.05 in non-abdominal obese patients.

Further adjustment for the use of antihypertensive, glucose-lowering, lipid-lowering, and antiplatelet drugs did not change the hazard ratio between GNRI and PAD in the model II of Supplementary Table 2. Similarly, results remained stable in the model III after multiple imputation and further adjustment for heart failure, economic status and condition of exercise as significant potential predictors of PAD.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, on the whole, we found a significant association between a lower GNRI and new-onset PAD in an elderly hypertensive population, especially those with concomitant abdominal obesity. When GNRI drops to a certain threshold (GNRI < 114), the hazard ratio of new-onset PAD increases significantly in the total population and patients with abdominal obesity. Patients with the double burden of malnutrition and abdominal obesity have a higher risk of PAD.

Malnutrition has been shown to be associated with poor outcomes in various chronic diseases, especially in older populations [21]. The findings of previous studies have demonstrated that at high nutritional risk increases the risk of all-cause mortality of chronic diseases such as heart failure [22, 23], chronic kidney disease [24], type 2 diabetes [10], and coronary artery disease [25]. Therefore, nutritional risk assessment for the elderly is a key factor to improve prognosis, especially for those with acute or chronic diseases. Hypertension is one of the major chronic diseases in China. Although the awareness rate of hypertension has increased and the prevalence rate has decreased, the base of the affected population is still large, and the elderly account for the majority [26]. Studies have suggested that high nutritional risk in people with hypertension increases the risk of chronic kidney disease [17], stroke [27], and even death [28]. This is consistent with our results that a lower GNRI in hypertensive patients predicts an increased relative risk of PAD. In addition, a cohort study in Asian population showed that a lower GNRI increased the incidence of all-cause mortality, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and major adverse limb events (MALE) in PAD patients [29].

However, the predictive power of GNRI varies in different populations, and the optimal threshold of GNRI as a malnutrition risk assessment tool is still controversial. The results of a screening study on the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database showed that GNRI, prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and controlling nutritional status score (COUNT) showed an L-shaped curve with the risk of all-cause mortality in the general population, and PNI had the highest predictive value [30]. In the study of elderly hypertensive patients in China, GNRI showed an L-shaped dose–response relationship with hemorrhagic stroke risk, with an inflection point of 103 [27]. The similar results were seen in GNRI predicting adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with ischemic heart failure [31] and chronic total coronary occlusion [32] after percutaneous coronary intervention, and GNRI significantly improved the predictive value. These studies all suggest that GNRI has a certain cutoff value in identifying people at risk, and the size of the cutoff value varies among different populations. Our study showed a nonlinear relationship between GNRI and the risk of incident PAD in an elderly hypertensive population in rural China, with a GNRI cutoff point of 114, which is significantly above the clinically specified risk threshold 98.

Combined with the characteristics of the population in this study and previous studies, obesity may be the main reason for the significantly higher inflection point of GNRI in this study than in other studies, especially abdominal obesity. In a cohort study of 5,300 Asian people, the obese-malnourished group had the highest risk of heart failure hospitalization and all-cause mortality compared with the normal-weight-well-nourished group, followed by the obese-well-nourished group [16]. Similarly, based on US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data, among hypertensive individuals, the lean-malnourished group had a higher hazard ratio for all-cause mortality, followed by the obese-malnourished group [13]. Studies have shown that obesity and malnutrition affect each other, and that if it occurs early in life, either may increase the risk of the other later in life [33]. However, the double burden of obesity-malnutrition occurs mainly in older age groups, especially abdominal obesity. In this study, abdominal obesity accounted for 68.0%, and it was as high as 79.6% in the upper tertile (GNRI ≥ 112). The results of combined abdominal obesity and GNRI analysis showed that the relative risk of PAD in abdominal obesity with low GNRI was significantly higher than that in non-abdominal obesity with good nutrition in Table 3. In addition, there was an L-shaped association between GNRI and the hazard ratio for incident PAD in abdominal obese patients but not non-abdominal (Fig. 3 and Table 2). In the threshold effect analysis, the inflection point of GNRI was 114 for the total population and abdominal obesity, but it was not found in non-abdominal obesity. Fat accumulation leads to muscle dysfunction and mass loss (sarcopenic obesity) through pathways such as skeletal muscle protein metabolism, mitochondrial function, and insulin resistance, which increases the risk of malnutrition, especially in the elderly [34–36].

Although the mechanism by which malnutrition increases the relative risk of PAD is currently unknown, the following are possible explanations. Inflammation is considered to be the key factor between malnutrition and arteriosclerosis, and the mutual influence of the three is called malnutrition-inflammation-arteriosclerosis syndrome [37]. First, oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation are two major inflammatory responses during the formation of arteriosclerosis, especially in chronic diseases such as hypertension [38]. A prospective study of Framingham offspring showed that low albumin levels increased the hazard ratios for acute myocardial infarction and all-cause death in hypertensive but not non-hypertensive subjects [39]. Not only that, but the underlying inflammation can also accelerate the metabolism of nutrients such as protein, thereby increasing the risk of malnutrition. A study based on a multicenter randomized clinical trial showed that nutritional supplementation did not improve poor outcomes in patients with high baseline inflammation and improved outcomes with low baseline inflammation [40]. Secondly, although studies have shown that albumin has important arterial protective effects such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticoagulant, malnutrition causes the protective effect of albumin on blood vessels to weaken or disappear [41]. Finally, Inflammation and malnutrition promote each other to aggravate the formation of atherosclerosis.

Strengths of this study: 1) This is a prospective cohort study, which provides a reliable basis for clinical practice. 2) The risk of new-onset PAD in people with double nutritional risk was further explored, which suggests that nutritional risk assessment is necessary in people with abdominal obesity. The shortcomings of this study included: 1) this study only focused on elderly hypertensive patients in China, and whether it can be generalized to other populations needs further study. 2) Although we adjusted for as many confounding factors as possible, there are still some unknown residual confounding factors, such as inflammatory markers, infection, dietary habits, etc. However, we have excluded patients with major diseases during the follow-up, which increases the credibility of the results.

Conclusion

In the present study, high nutritional risk was associated with a high relative risk of PAD in elderly hypertensive patients, especially those with concomitant abdominal obesity. As modifiable risk factors, malnutrition and abdominal obesity may improve risk stratification in patients with hypertension.
