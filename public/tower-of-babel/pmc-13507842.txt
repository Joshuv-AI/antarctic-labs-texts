Conservative Management of a Traumatic Deep Palmar Arch Pseudoaneurysm with Delayed Ulnar Motor Dysfunction: A Case Report
Authors: Fernando de Juan Santos, Carlos Villar Yus, Javier Pérez Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic pseudoaneurysms of the palmar arch are rare vascular lesions and are typically managed with surgical repair or endovascular exclusion. Deep palmar arch involvement is particularly uncommon, and neurological deficits are often considered indications for exploration. We report the case of a 26-year-old male with a penetrating palm injury resulting in a bilobulated deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysm, a thenar hematoma, and delayed ulnar motor dysfunction. Because there was no digital ischemia, ongoing bleeding, or lesion expansion, conservative management with structured clinical and radiological surveillance was selected. Serial computed tomography angiography demonstrated early interval thrombosis and vascular stability. Intrinsic hand muscle function progressively recovered, and one-year imaging confirmed complete resolution of the pseudoaneurysm. This case suggests that conservative management may be considered in carefully selected, hemodynamically stable patients without digital ischemia, provided that close clinical and radiological surveillance is feasible.Keywords: Aneurysm, false; Palmar arch; Hand injuries; Conservative treatment; Ulnar neuropathies

INTRODUCTION

Pseudoaneurysms of the palmar arch are uncommon vascular lesions that most frequently involve the superficial arch following penetrating trauma. Deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysms are particularly rare because of their protected anatomical location. Traditionally, management has involved ligation or reconstruction, especially in symptomatic patients or when neurological deficits are present [1]. However, evidence guiding the management of hemodynamically stable patients without digital ischemia remains limited.

We report a rare case of a traumatic deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysm with delayed ulnar motor dysfunction that resolved completely with conservative management under close clinical and radiological surveillance. All procedures were part of routine clinical care and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for publication of clinical data and images was obtained from the patient.

CASE

A 26-year-old male sustained a penetrating injury to the left palm from a screwdriver at work. He presented with swelling and pain in the thenar region, without digital ischemia or hemodynamic instability. Clinical vascular examination revealed normal capillary refill in all digits, preserved skin temperature, and absence of pallor or cyanosis. Handheld Doppler signals were detectable in the digital arteries, supporting preserved distal perfusion. These findings remained stable throughout follow-up. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated a bilobulated pseudoaneurysm measuring approximately 14×20 mm, arising from the deep palmar arch and associated with a large thenar hematoma (Fig. 1A). Three-dimensional reconstruction confirmed its origin from the deep palmar arch with complex branching anatomy (Fig. 1B). Active contrast extravasation and distal ischemia were not observed.

After multidisciplinary discussion, endovascular exclusion was considered technically unfavorable because of the wide neck and complex branching anatomy. Considering the absence of ongoing bleeding or ischemia, conservative management was initiated with limb elevation, analgesia, and close neurovascular monitoring throughout the observation period. Vascular assessment included serial evaluation of capillary refill, skin color and temperature, and the presence of Doppler signals in the digital arteries. No signs of digital ischemia were observed. Neurological monitoring consisted of repeated clinical examinations of intrinsic hand muscle function, particularly the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis muscles, assessment of Froment’s sign, and evaluation of sensation in the ulnar nerve distribution.

During the first 48 hours, clinical reassessments were performed every 8 hours. Subsequently, the patient underwent daily evaluations during the first 2 weeks, followed by weekly outpatient follow-up. Radiological surveillance included repeat CTA at 48 hours, which demonstrated an interval reduction in the pseudoaneurysm size with partial thrombosis and no extravasation (Fig. 2A). Clinical swelling and pain improved accordingly.

Evolution. (A) Computed tomography angiography (CTA) at 48 hours demonstrated interval reduction in size with partial thrombosis and no contrast extravasation. (B) One-year CTA confirmed complete resolution with restoration of normal vascular appearance.

At the initial presentation, no motor deficit was detected on clinical examination, and intrinsic muscle function was preserved. Two weeks after the injury, the patient developed weakness in the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis muscles. The deficit was nonprogressive. Electrophysiological studies demonstrated a moderate-to-severe partial axonal lesion predominantly affecting the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve, with mild sensory involvement. Given the delayed onset and nonprogressive course of the deficit in the context of a large thenar hematoma and thrombosing pseudoaneurysm, compressive neuropathy was considered more likely than primary nerve transection. Considering the early radiological evidence of thrombosis, absence of lesion expansion, lack of ischemia, and potential risk of iatrogenic nerve injury during deep palmar dissection, surgical exploration was not performed. Serial follow-up revealed progressive intrinsic muscle recovery. A 4-month electromyography study demonstrated reinnervation changes.

One-year CTA confirmed complete radiologic resolution of the pseudoaneurysm with restoration of normal vascular anatomy (Fig. 2B).

DISCUSSION

Palmar arch pseudoaneurysms are rare vascular lesions that have traditionally been managed by surgical ligation or reconstruction [1]. Other management strategies reported in the literature include endovascular techniques, thrombin injection, and conservative observation in selected stable cases [2,3]. Deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysms are particularly uncommon because of the protected anatomical location of the deep arch beneath the flexor tendons and intrinsic musculature. Their management may be especially challenging because of the intimate relationship between the deep palmar arch and deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve.

Neurological deficits in this context may arise from several mechanisms, including direct nerve injury, ischemia, or compressive neuropathy secondary to hematoma or mass effect. In the present case, no motor deficits were detected at the initial presentation, and intrinsic muscle function was preserved. The delayed onset and nonprogressive nature of the motor deficit, together with the presence of a large thenar hematoma and subsequent neurological recovery, support a compressive neuropathy mechanism rather than primary nerve transection. This distinction is clinically relevant, as compressive neuropathies may improve with resolution of the underlying mass effect, whereas structural nerve injuries are more likely to require surgical exploration. Electrophysiological studies demonstrated predominant involvement of the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve, with mild sensory abnormalities likely reflecting either partial involvement proximal to the motor branch origin or limited involvement of adjacent sensory fibers. The clinical and electrophysiological findings did not deteriorate during follow-up and progressively improved over time, and vascular examination findings remained stable throughout the observation period, supporting preserved distal perfusion and the absence of digital ischemia.

Surgical exploration of the deep palmar arch is technically demanding because of its deep location and close anatomical relationship with the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve. Dissection of this region carries a significant risk of iatrogenic nerve injury and potential permanent functional impairment. In our patient, this consideration played an important role in the decision to avoid operative intervention in the setting of vascular stability and nonprogressive neurological findings.

In the present case, the pseudoaneurysm exhibited a wide neck and arose from a segment of the deep palmar arch with complex branching anatomy, including multiple inflow and outflow vessels in close proximity. This configuration was considered unfavorable for endovascular techniques, such as coil embolization, because of the risk of nontarget embolization and potential compromise of distal perfusion. Similarly, ultrasound-guided thrombin injection was considered unsuitable because the wide-neck morphology may increase the risk of thrombin migration into the parent vessel and distal embolization. Bosman et al. [2] described a successful ultrasound-guided thrombin injection for a deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysm in a patient with favorable anatomical characteristics. However, comparative data defining the absolute indications for intervention remain limited, and the applicability of thrombin injection appears to depend strongly on lesion morphology and vascular anatomy.

Although conservative management has been described for small, uncomplicated, fully thrombosed pseudoaneurysms, concerns remain regarding distal embolization, lesion expansion, and compressive complications [3]. The relevance of this case does not lie in spontaneous thrombosis per se, but rather in management decision-making in the presence of a deep palmar arch lesion associated with neurological deficits, a scenario in which surgical exploration is frequently considered. In our patient, serial CTA demonstrated early interval thrombosis and progressive reduction in the pseudoaneurysm size without evidence of expansion, active bleeding, or distal ischemia. These radiological findings, together with the stable neurological course, supported continued conservative management.

Although the evidence remains limited, the present case suggests that conservative management may be considered in carefully selected patients with deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysms when the following conditions are met: hemodynamic stability, absence of digital ischemia, early radiological evidence of thrombosis or lesion stability, nonprogressive neurological findings, and high anticipated risk of surgical morbidity due to complex local anatomy. Careful surveillance remains essential, and interventions should be reconsidered if instability, expansion, ischemia, or progressive neurological deterioration develops.

In summary, conservative management may be considered in carefully selected patients with deep palmar arch pseudoaneurysms when serial imaging demonstrates early thrombosis and vascular stability. However, this approach should be adopted with caution and requires close clinical and radiological surveillance.
