Shining light on collective modes in moiré fractional Chern insulators
Nisarga Paul, Ahmed Abouelkomsan, Aidan Reddy, Liang Fu

Abstract
We show that collective excitations and optical responses of moiré fractional Chern insulators (FCIs) drastically differ from those of standard fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states in a Landau level. By constructing a variational wavefunction that incorporates the moiré lattice effect, we capture the collective modes in FCIs across a range of crystal momenta including the roton minimum. Interestingly, new collective modes are found in the long wavelength limit ( 𝒒 → 0 \bm{q}\rightarrow 0 ) at low energy below the excitation continuum, distinct from the FQH case. Some of these modes are optically active and manifest as sharp peaks in optical conductivity at THz frequency. We further show that intraband optical absorption and spectral weight in twisted MoTe 2 {\rm MoTe}_{2} are highly tunable by the displacement field. Our work thus establishes optical spectroscopy as a powerful tool to illuminate the unique collective modes of moiré FCIs.



Introduction—
The fractional quantum anomalous Hall (FQAH) effect observed in two-dimensional moiré materials

,

,

, despite occurring at zero magnetic field,
bears a striking similarity to the fractional quantum Hall (FQH) effect in Landau levels.
In the case of twisted transition metal dichalcogenides (
t
TMDs),
this similarity was understood—and even anticipated
[
6
,

,
8

—by
the close analogy between flat Chern bands
[

and Landau levels. The FQAH states in these partially filled Chern bands, which may be called moiré fractional Chern insulators (FCIs)
[
10
,
11
,
12

, belong to the same phase as the corresponding FQH states in Landau levels. A natural question is whether these moiré FCIs host new phenomena that go beyond decades-long FQH research.

In this work, we show that the low-energy collective modes and optical response of moiré FCIs are drastically different from those of FQH states. For FCIs in twisted TMDs, we find that intraband collective excitations at zero wavevector
q=0
exist below the continuum and are optically active, giving rise to sharp peaks in optical absorption at terahertz frequencies. This is in sharp contrast with FQH states in Landau levels, where all intra-Landau level excitations are optically dark (Kohn’s theorem)
[
13
]
. We further show using a sum rule that the low-energy spectral weight is highly tunable by the displacement field.
Our work suggests low-energy optical spectroscopy as a powerful probe of collective modes in moiré FCIs.

The starting point of our analysis is the continuum model for twisted TMD homobilayers
[
9
]
. For one valley (equivalently spin), it reads
H=H_{\uparrow}+H_{C}
, with
H_{C}
describing Coulomb interactions and

H_{\uparrow}=\frac{\hbar^{2}\boldsymbol{k}^{2}}{2m^{*}}\sigma_{0}+\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+V(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{0}

(1)

where
\{\sigma_{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\}
are the identity and Pauli matrices in the layer-pseudospin space.
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{r})
is a layer Zeeman field that includes both interlayer tunneling and layer potential bias,
and
V(\boldsymbol{r})
is a periodic potential, both varying periodically with the moiré period.

As we shall show below, it is conceptually useful to consider the continuum model above in the large-
J
limit
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
under which the layer pseudospin is locally aligned to the Zeeman field, giving rise to the effective Hamiltonian
H=\tilde{H}+H_{C}
with

\tilde{H}=\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{p}+e\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}))^{2}}{2m^{*}}+\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{r})

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r})
is a
U(1)
gauge field representing a periodically varying magnetic field that encloses one flux quantum per unit cell and
\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{r})
is a periodic scalar potential. This adiabatic approximation maps moiré bands in twisted TMDs to
periodically modulated
Landau levels. The physical picture is that the spatially varying non-coplanar pseudospin texture gives rise to an emergent magnetic field

\boldsymbol{B}_{e}(\boldsymbol{r})=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{A}
that is proportional to the spin chirality

18

In the following, we shall analyze neutral excitations of moiré FCIs using both the original model (
1

2

In each case, we will consider a single Chern band at fractional filling, and assume throughout this work that the gap to remote bands is much larger than the interaction strength. This allows us to neglect band mixing and focus on low-energy intraband excitations.

Collective modes.—
To study collective modes in a moiré FCI, we construct a variational ansatz by acting the density operator on the many-body ground state
|\rm 0\rangle
:

|\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{G}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}}|\rm 0\rangle

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{q}\in
BZ is
crystal momentum
,
\boldsymbol{G}
is a reciprocal lattice vector, the index
\alpha
labels different excited states at wavevector
\boldsymbol{q}
, and
\{c_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{\alpha}\}
is a set of variational coefficients. Here,
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=P\rho_{\boldsymbol{k}}P
where
\rho_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sum_{i}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}
is the density operator at full momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
(ranging to infinity) and
P
is the projection operator onto the underlying Chern band subspace.

Importantly, our ansatz uses a linear combination of density operators at multiple wavevectors that differ by a reciprocal lattice vector, as allowed by the discrete lattice symmetry. This approach goes beyond the single-mode approximation
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, and is crucial to capture the effect from the underlying lattice on the excited states of moiré FCIs, as we shall show below.

The variational energy of Eq. (
3
) is

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}&=\dfrac{\langle\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}|H-E_{0}|\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\rangle}{\langle\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}|\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\rangle}\\
&=\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\langle{0}|\bar{\rho}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}]|{0}\rangle}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})}\end{split}

(4)

where
\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})=\langle 0|\bar{\rho}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}|0\rangle
is the projected static structure factor, and
(H-E_{0})|0\rangle=0
. We also write
\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q})\equiv\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},0,0)
. The variational coefficients
c^{\alpha}_{\bm{G}}
can be found by directly diagonalizing the Hamiltonian in the subspace spanned by
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}}|0\rangle
.

If
\boldsymbol{q}
is a high-symmetry crystal momentum, one expects strong hybridization of degenerate modes that are related by lattice symmetry. It is natural then to use the
lowest star
approximation, restricting to a small set of
\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}
equal in magnitude to
\boldsymbol{q}
. Assuming this set and the Hamiltonian have
C_{n}
symmetry for some
n
, Eq. (
4
) can be simplified to

\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\ell}=\frac{\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q},\ell}^{\dagger}[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q},\ell}]|0\rangle}{\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q},\ell}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q},\ell}|0\rangle}

(5)

where
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q},\ell}\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{2\pi ij\ell/n}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{(j)}}
is a chiral projected density operator with angular momentum
\ell
and
\boldsymbol{q}^{(j)}=R_{2\pi/n}^{j}[\boldsymbol{q}]
are the
n
-fold rotated momenta.

In the following, we will apply the variational ansatz to three cases: (1) ordinary FQH states, (2) the perturbed Landau level (LL) of Eq. (
2

First, we consider the standard FQH effect in the regime where the Coulomb energy

A few features of the magneto-rotons are worth noting for our purposes. First, in the

Next, let us consider the case of LLs perturbed by a weak periodic potential, a special case of Eq. (
2
) with a uniform field. This gives the simplest model for a dispersive Chern band hosting FCIs, provided that the periodic potential is not too strong compared to the Coulomb interaction. Importantly, the periodic potential causes Bragg scattering of collective modes
[

. To calculate the reconstructed excitation spectrum,
we write the potential as
\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{r})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}
, with
|\tilde{V}_{\boldsymbol{G}}|\ll\hbar\omega_{c}
and project into the lowest LL. The projected Hamiltonian, including interactions, is

\bar{H}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}}+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}U(\boldsymbol{q}):\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}:

(6)

where
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is the lowest LL-projected density operator and

is an interaction of strength
U_{0}

for
\boldsymbol{G}_{i}=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}a_{0}}[\cos 2\pi(i-1)/6,\sin 2\pi(i-1)/6]

\sqrt{3}a_{0}^{2}=4\pi\ell_{B}^{2}

The Bragg scattering of magneto-roton modes by the periodic potential is captured by our variational ansatz Eq. (

We note that

Low-frequency optical absorption.—
It is well-known that the intra-LL excitations of FQH states have a vanishing dipole coupling to light in the absence of disorder
[
13
]
. This is because a uniform electric field only couples to electrons’ center of mass, which decouples from other degrees of freedom and has zero intra-LL matrix elements. As a result, optical absorption occurs only at the cyclotron frequency, leaving low-energy collective modes of FQH states optically dark.

In contrast, moiré FCI states only have discrete translation symmetry to which Kohn’s theorem does not apply. Thus, a direct coupling between light and
\boldsymbol{q}=0
collective excitations with angular momentum
\ell=\pm 1
is allowed by symmetry. In particular, low-energy quasielectron-quasihole pairs may be isolated from the continuum and thus undamped, raising an interesting question of whether they are optically active and can be detected as sharp peaks in the optical conductivity at low frequency.

Indeed, our calculations of the optical response to circularly polarized light in the many-body ground state of twisted
{\rm MoTe}_{2}
reveal sharp and prominent peaks at frequencies that correspond to low-lying chiral modes (
\ell=\pm 1
) at
\Gamma
, which are further enhanced by displacement fields
[
29
]
.

To better understand the low-frequency optical response, we derive an intraband
f
-sum rule for the low-energy optical spectral weight for an FCI formed in an isolated Chern band separated from remote bands by a large gap
\Delta
.
In particular, we relate the low-energy spectral weight to a simple ground-state expectation value.

To derive this sum rule, we first perform a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation—a
unitary
transformation
U
—to decouple the low-energy and high-energy subspace by eliminating the off-diagonal matrix elements (i.e., interband terms):

\displaystyle\tilde{H}=UHU^{-1}=\tilde{H}_{1}+\tilde{H}_{2}

(9)

where
P\tilde{H}_{1}P=\tilde{H}_{1}
is the low-energy Hamiltonian acting on a low-energy subspace specified by the projection operator
P
, and
P_{\perp}\tilde{H}_{2}P_{\perp}=\tilde{H}_{2}
with
P_{\perp}=I-P
associated with the high-energy subspace.

To compute the optical conductivity, we find it convenient to relate it to the dynamical structure factor through the continuity equation and fluctuation-dissipation theorem (a thorough discussion for Coulomb systems can be found in Ref.
[
32
]
):

S(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{\hbar A}{\pi e^{2}}\frac{q_{a}q_{b}}{\omega}\real\sigma_{ab}(\bm{q},\omega),

(10)

where
A
is the system area, and the dynamical structure factor is defined as

\displaystyle S(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq 0}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{n0}\right)|\langle n|\rho_{\bm{q}}|0\rangle|^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq 0}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{n0}\right)|\langle\tilde{n}|\tilde{\rho}_{\bm{q}}|\tilde{0}\rangle|^{2}.

Here
n
labels the many-body eigenstate with energy
\hbar\omega_{n}
,
\omega_{n0}=\omega_{n}-\omega_{0}
, and
\rho_{\bm{q}}
is the density operator.
In the second equality, we have rewritten
S
in terms of the many-body eigenstates
|\tilde{n}\rangle\equiv U|n\rangle
for the transformed Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
, and
\tilde{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=U\rho_{\bm{q}}U^{-1}
.

As
\tilde{H}
decomposes into low- and high-energy parts, the structure factor is naturally composed of an intraband part that only involves low-energy eigenstates denoted as
|\tilde{l}\rangle
and an interband part that involves matrix elements between the ground state and high-energy eigenstates denoted as
|\tilde{h}\rangle
,

\displaystyle S(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\neq 0}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{l0}\right)|\langle\tilde{l}|\tilde{\rho}_{\bm{q}}|\tilde{0}\rangle|^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{h}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{h0}\right)|\langle\tilde{h}|\tilde{\rho}_{\bm{q}}|\tilde{0}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle S^{L}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)+S^{H}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega),

with
P|\tilde{l}\rangle=|\tilde{l}\rangle
and
P_{\perp}|\tilde{h}\rangle=|\tilde{h}\rangle
.
Following Eq. (
10
), the optical conductivity can be similarly decomposed:
\sigma=\sigma^{L}+\sigma^{H}
. In particular, we shall focus on the structure factor and optical conductivity at low frequency, which are dominated by the intraband contributions as captured by the low-energy Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{1}

[
33
]
.

We define the intraband optical spectral weight as

W=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\real\sigma^{L}_{aa}(\omega).

(13)

From Eq. (
10
) it follows that

\begin{split}W&=\frac{\pi e^{2}}{\hbar A}\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{l\neq 0}|\langle\tilde{l}|\tilde{\rho}_{\bm{q}}|\tilde{0}\rangle|^{2}\omega_{l0}\\
&=\frac{\pi e^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}A}\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}\langle\tilde{0}|[\tilde{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{L\dagger},[\tilde{H}_{1},\tilde{\rho}^{L}_{\boldsymbol{q}}]]|\tilde{0}\rangle\end{split}

(14)

where
\tilde{\rho}^{L}\equiv P\tilde{\rho}P
is the density operator projected onto the low-energy subspace.

For our system, the isolated Chern band defines a low-energy subspace, separated by a band gap
\Delta
to remote bands. The corresponding Schrieffer-Wolff transformation can be carried out perturbatively in
\lambda/\Delta
:
U=I+\mathcal{O}(\lambda/\Delta)
, where
\lambda H^{\prime}\equiv PHP_{\perp}+P_{\perp}HP
is the interband part of Coulomb interaction with interaction strength
\lambda
. To the zeroth-order approximation in
\lambda/\Delta
, we have
U\approx I
and
|\tilde{0}\rangle\approx|0\rangle
is the ground state of band-projected Hamiltonian
\bar{H}\equiv PHP\approx\tilde{H}_{1}
. Then, the low-energy optical spectral weight simplifies to

\begin{split}W=\frac{\pi e^{2}n}{\hbar^{2}}\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}F({\boldsymbol{q}})\end{split}

(15)

with

F({\boldsymbol{q}})\equiv\dfrac{1}{2N}\langle 0|[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger},[\bar{H},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}]]|0\rangle

(16)

where
\bar{H}
and
\bar{\rho}
are the band-projected Hamiltonian and density operator respectively. Alternatively,
the right-hand side of Eq. (
15
) can be expressed using electron position operator
[
33
]
, which is well defined for systems with open boundary conditions.

As before, we apply this to the three cases of (1) ordinary FQH states, (2) perturbed Landau levels, and (3) twisted TMD homobilayers. In the case of ordinary Landau levels, only the projected interactions
H_{C}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}U(\boldsymbol{q})\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
remain. We may write

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(f_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})+f_{-\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})\right)

using the GMP algebra
[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}]=f_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}
where
f_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}=2ie^{\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\sin(\vec q\wedge\vec q' \ell_B^2/2)
. Using parity symmetry, it follows
F(\boldsymbol{q})
vanishes to order
q^{4}

[
19
,
29
]
, and hence
W=0
in this case, which is also consistent with Kohn’s theorem.

In the case of Landau levels perturbed by a periodic potential, the projected Hamiltonian is
\bar{H}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}}+H_{C}
. The contribution from
H_{C}
vanishes as above, and employing the GMP algebra then yields
[
28
,
34
]

W=\frac{\pi e^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}\left(-V_{\boldsymbol{G}}\cdot\rho({\boldsymbol{G}})\right)(\hat{q}\wedge\boldsymbol{G}\ell_{B}^{2})^{2}

(17)

where
\rho({\boldsymbol{G}})\equiv\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}}|0\rangle=\langle 0|\rho_{\boldsymbol{G}}|0\rangle
is the Fourier component of the charge density in the
exact
ground state
|0\rangle
of
\bar{H}
. It is important to note that
\rho({\boldsymbol{G}})
is generally nonzero because of the periodic potential, leading to nonzero intraband spectral weight
W>0
. Hence the perturbed Landau level serves as perhaps the simplest example of an FCI with nonzero optical spectral weight.

Discussion.—
Our findings suggest new directions for optical experiments on moiré FCIs. The
chiral
fractional quasielectron-quasihole excitations of
t
TMDs, with angular momentum
\ell=\pm 1
and few-meV excitation energy, can be probed directly using terahertz (THz) spectroscopy, or indirectly using inelastic Raman scattering (note that
\ell=\pm 1=\mp 2\mod 3
). We predict that the intraband optical absorption increases with
D
field, which can either be observed directly at low frequencies or indirectly at higher frequencies as a
decrease
in the interband optical absorption with
D
.

The predicted optical response lies at energies of a few meV, corresponding to the THz regime. This scale is challenging but increasingly accessible in van der Waals devices including TMD semiconductors, where recent THz measurements have reached frequency windows of 0.1-1 THz and allow electrostatic control by gates
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
. Since the intraband optical spectral weight can reach
0.1
–
0.6
of the
f
-sum rule limit in the FCI phase, collective mode optical absorption is not expected to be parametrically weak in comparison to interband absorption in twisted TMD homobilayers
[
35
]
.

In addition to magnetorotons, FQH states also host chiral graviton modes at small
\boldsymbol{q}

[
41
,
42
,
43
]
, which can be probed by Raman scattering
[
44
]
. We emphasize that such graviton modes
[
21
,
45
,
46
]
will not be the lowest energy excited states in twisted
{\rm MoTe}_{2}
due to the existence of quasielectron-quasihole excitations that are lower in energy. In the Supplemental Material
[
29
]
, we provide numerical evidence that chiral gravitons in periodically modulated LLs are optically active, and leave its detailed study to the future.

While we have focused on the spin-polarized FCIs at
\nu=1/3
and
\nu=2/3
, our variational approach carries over to the FCI analogs of other Jain sequence states, even-denominator non-Abelian FCIs
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
, non-Abelian spin Hall insulators at
\nu=3

[
51
]
, and potentially composite Fermi liquids
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
. Based on our intraband optical sum rule Eq.

[

, as well as the condensation of

[

, Hall viscosity in FCIs
[

S1
Roton folding in periodic systems and finite-size effects

In this section, we discuss the folding of the roton spectrum in crystalline systems and point out a finite-size effect for the counting of expected folded roton modes based on symmetry considerations. We consider the Hamiltonian of the LLL which takes the form,

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}U(\boldsymbol{q}):\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}:

(S1)

where
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\sum_{j}e^{-|\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/4}e^{-i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}
is the LL-projected density operator in the
n=0
LL in terms of particle guiding center coordinates
\boldsymbol{R}_{j}
. The interaction
U(\boldsymbol{q})
is taken to be bare Coulomb
U(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{U_{0}a_{0}}{2A}\frac{2\pi}{|\boldsymbol{q}|}
. with the interaction strength
U_{0}=e^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon a_{0})
.

As shown by Haldane
[
60
]
, many-body magnetic translations in this case are factorized into a relative and a center of mass (COM) part. The momentum eigenvalues corresponding to the relative motion can be used to label any many-body state. On a finite torus spanned by two vectors
\boldsymbol{L}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{L}_{2}
such that
|\boldsymbol{L}_{1}\crossproduct\boldsymbol{L}_{2}|=2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}N_{\phi}
, the relative momentum takes discrete values,

\boldsymbol{q}\in{\rm BZ_{cont}}=m\boldsymbol{g}_{1}+n\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\>\>,\>\>(m,n)=(0\dots N-1,0\dots N-1)

(S2)

where
N={\rm GCD}(N_{p},N_{\phi})
with
{\rm GCD}
denoting greatest common divisor,
N_{p}
and
N_{\phi}
are the number of particles and flux quanta respectively.
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{2}
are two reciprocal lattice vectors such that
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
. The discretization defines a
many-body
Brillouin zone which we will refer to as
{\rm BZ_{cont}}
. As system size increases (while keeping a fixed filling
\nu
), the mesh defined by
\boldsymbol{q}
becomes denser and larger and in the thermodynamic limit,
\boldsymbol{q}\in R^{2}
as expected from a continuous system. In addition to the relative motion, COM motion gives rise to
q
-fold degeneracy for
\nu=p/q
such any state at momentum
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
is degenerate with other states at
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},q_{2}+mN)
for
m=1,\cdots q-1
.

In Fig
S1
(a), we show the many-body spectrum obtained from diagonalizing equation (
S1
) utilizing the many-body quantum numbers
|\boldsymbol{q}|
at
\nu=1/3
(equivalently
\nu=2/3
). We use a hexagonal torus with
N_{\phi}=27
and equal aspect ratio. We observe as expected the FQH ground state at
|\boldsymbol{q}|=0
. In addition, the magneto-roton branch is clearly visible. Notice that the magneto-roton minima on this cluster occur at the
K
points, defined as
|K|\ell_{B}\approx 1.6
.

To make connection with periodic crystalline system with a well-defined unit cell and discrete translation symmetry, it’s natural to choose the single particle LLL wavefunctions to be magnetic Bloch basis
\phi_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})
parametrized by a Bloch momentum
\boldsymbol{k}

[
61
]
that lives in a Brillouin zone which we will refer to as
{\rm BZ_{Bloch}}
. This is obtained by defining a unit cell spanned by two vectors
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}
that encloses one flux quantum
|\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\crossproduct\boldsymbol{a}_{2}|=2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}
and applying Bloch’s theorem. Again, on a finite torus spanned by two vectors
\boldsymbol{L}_{1}=N_{1}\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{L}_{2}=N_{2}\boldsymbol{a}_{2}
with
N_{1}\times N_{2}=N_{\phi}
, we have

\mathbf{k}\in{\rm BZ_{Bloch}}=m\boldsymbol{g}_{1}+n\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\>\>,\>\>(m,n)=(0\dots N_{1}-1,0\dots N_{2}-1)

(S3)

where
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
.
The many-body Hamiltonian diagonalized with these quantum numbers
does not
reveal the full many-body symmetries of the problem. In such a case, the total momentum
\boldsymbol{K}_{\rm CM}=\sum_{i}^{N_{p}}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
is
folded
relative to the relative momenta
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\rm BZ_{cont}}
as
\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
is defined only modulo reciprocal lattice vectors
\boldsymbol{G}=m\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+n\boldsymbol{G}_{2}
with
\boldsymbol{G}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}

[
62
,
20
]

S1
(a).

To study the low-lying excitations of crystalline systems which do not exhibit continuous translational symmetry and have momentum only defined modulo
\boldsymbol{G}

\boldsymbol{G}

{\rm BZ_{cont}}

(Fig.

(c) the distinct

for the finite system we work with. We observe that
{\rm BZ_{FQH}}
can only fit two
\boldsymbol{G}

S1
(b) at
K_{\rm CM}=\Gamma
.

Unfortunately, this finite-size effect exists for all numerically accessible finite-size systems which poses a challenge in studying the properties of the quasielectron-quasihole states at

\boldsymbol{G}
vectors. On the other hand, the situation is less drastic for the

\boldsymbol{G}

S2
Variational ansatz in periodically modulated Landau levels

In this section, we detail the application of the variational ansatz to the periodically modulated lowest Landau level (LLL). We study the LLL perturbed by a weak periodic potential enclosing one flux quantum per unit cell. We recall the projected Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}}+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}U(\boldsymbol{q})\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}

(S4)

where
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\sum_{j}e^{-|\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/4}e^{-i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}
is the LL-projected density operator in terms of particle guiding center coordinates
\boldsymbol{R}_{j}
, we’ve dropped the normal ordering for convenience, and
A
is system size. It is helpful to distinguish two levels of approximation: (1) the variational approximation and (2) the variational
and
perturbative (
V_{\boldsymbol{G}}\ll
Coulomb) approximation. Both approximations are in the band-projected limit where the cyclotron gap
\hbar\omega_{c}
is the largest energy scale.

(1) Variational.—
Recall that our variational ansatz is

|\phi^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}}|\rm 0\rangle

(S5)

where
\boldsymbol{q}\in
BZ and
\boldsymbol{G}
is a reciprocal lattice vector, which has energy

E^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}-E_{0}=\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\langle{0}|\bar{\rho}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}]|{0}\rangle}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})}

(S6)

where
(H-E_{0})|0\rangle=0
and
\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})=\langle 0|\bar{\rho}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}|0\rangle
is the projected static structure factor. If we also assume that
c_{\boldsymbol{q}+G}^{\alpha\ast}=c_{-\boldsymbol{q}-G}^{\alpha}
, a constraint on our ansatz which is natural in the presence of parity symmetry, then the above can be written as

E^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}}-E_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\langle{0}|[\bar{\rho}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}},[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}]]|{0}\rangle}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c^{\alpha\ast}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}c^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})}.

(S7)

Using the Girvin-Macdonald-Platzman algebra
[
19
]

[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}]=f_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}+\boldsymbol{q}_{2}},\qquad f_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}=2ie^{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\sin(\vec q_1\wedge\vec q_2 \ell_B^2/2),

(S8)

we can simplify the variational energies. It is useful to collect the following expressions:

\displaystyle[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\{\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}},\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\}

(S9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}},[H,\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}]]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}+\frac{1}{4A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\{\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\}+f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\{\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}},\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\}\right)

where we’ve assumed the interaction satisfies
U(\boldsymbol{q})=U(-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})
and
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
. Altogether, the energy without assuming parity symmetry (Eq. (
S6
)) becomes

E_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}-E_{0}=\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\alpha\ast}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}^{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2})+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}\{\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}},\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\}|0\rangle\right)}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\alpha\ast}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}^{\alpha}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})}

(S10)

and the energy assuming parity (Eq. (
S7
)) becomes

E_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}-E_{0}=\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\alpha\ast}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}V_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}+\boldsymbol{G}}|0\rangle+\frac{1}{4A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}T^{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\right)}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\alpha\ast}c_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}^{\alpha}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})}

(S11)

where

T^{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}=f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}(\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2})+\bar{s}(-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},-\boldsymbol{G}_{2},-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}))+f_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}(\bar{s}(-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{G}_{12},0)+\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},0,\boldsymbol{G}_{21})).

(S12)

We remark that Eq. (
S10
) involves the structure factor and a three-density expectation value, while Eq. (
S11
) only involves the structure factor and single-density expectation value. Naturally, the latter is easier to compute, but either may be computed numerically in principle for finite-size systems.

(2) Variational + perturbative.—
The perturbative variational calculation assumes
V

to leading order in

\Gamma

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}}^{(0)}|\hat{V}|\phi_{\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}^{(0)}\rangle

\displaystyle=V_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}\langle\phi_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}}^{(0)}|\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}|\phi_{\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}^{(0)}\rangle

(S15)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}}{N\bar{s}_{0}(\boldsymbol{G})}\langle 0|\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{G}_{i}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{i}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{j}}|0\rangle

where we denote
\bar{s}_{0}(\boldsymbol{G}_{i})

(S16)

(S17)

\displaystyle H\left.\right|_{\Gamma}=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{G}}-\frac{1}{\bar{s}_{0}(\boldsymbol{G})}\begin{pmatrix}0&V_{0}^{*}S_{3}&0&0&0&V_{0}^{*}S_{3}^{*}\\
V_{0}S_{3}^{*}&0&V_{0}S_{3}&0&0&0\\

0&0&0&V_{0}^{*}S_{3}^{*}&0&V_{0}^{*}S_{3}\\
V_{0}S_{3}&0&0&0&V_{0}S_{3}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

S3
Low-energy spectral weight in ordinary Landau levels

It is a well-known corollary of Kohn’s theorem that the low-energy optical spectral weight of an ordinary Landau level system vanishes. For completeness, we review a derivation here, originally due to GMP
[
19
]
. We have

\begin{split}W&=\frac{\pi e^{2}}{2A\hbar^{2}}\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}\langle 0|[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger},[\bar{H},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}}]]|0\rangle\\
&\equiv\frac{\pi e^{2}n}{\hbar^{2}}\lim_{q\to 0}\frac{1}{q^{2}}F(\boldsymbol{q}).\end{split}

(S25)

In an ordinary Landau level, the projected Hamiltonian has only the interaction piece,
\bar{H}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}
. Then
F(\boldsymbol{q})
may be evaluated using the GMP algebra Eq. (
S8
). We quote the result from Eq. (
S9
) (we use
\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q})=\bar{s}(-\boldsymbol{q})
in the parity-symmetric Landau level):

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(f_{-\boldsymbol{q}}^{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\langle 0|\{\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\}|0\rangle+f_{-\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\langle 0|\{\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}},\bar{\rho}_{-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\}|0\rangle\right)

(S26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})f_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{q}}\left(f_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})+f_{-\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})\right)

where
f_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}=2ie^{\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\ell_{B}^{2}/2}\sin(\vec q\wedge\vec q' \ell_B^2/2)
and
\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q})
is the projected structure factor. We expand at small
\boldsymbol{q}
to obtain

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2A}(2i)^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}U(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\wedge\boldsymbol{q}\ell_{B}^{2}/2)^{2}\left(\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})-\bar{s}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})\right)+\cdots

(S27)

where subleading terms are higher order in
|q|
. Next, using parity symmetry, it is possible to show that the leading term above vanishes, and hence that
F(\boldsymbol{q})
vanishes to at least order
q^{4}
. It follows that
W
vanishes identically.

S4
Low-energy spectral weight for modulated Landau levels

In this section, we derive the formula for the low-energy sum rule for modulated Landau levels using another approach based on projected current operators that gives the same final answer. It is first instructive to review the general formalism for projected observables before applying it to a Chern band (or Landau level).

Projection: general formalism.—
Assume our Hamiltonian can be written as

H=T+V,\qquad T=\sum_{n}\varepsilon_{n}P_{n}

(S28)

where
\varepsilon_{n}
are unperturbed energies,
P_{n}
is a projection onto the
n
’th energy eigenspace, and
|V|\ll|\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}|
for all
n,m
. Then one may wish to consider the projected Hamiltonian and projected observables in the
n
’th eigenspace.

Let’s focus on the projected Hamiltonian first. Throughout we use the overbar to denote operators defined only on the projected Hilbert space. We wish to find an operator
\bar{H}_{n}
such that if
H|\psi_{i}\rangle=E_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle
is a solution to the full Schrödinger equation, where
E_{i}=\varepsilon_{n}+O(V)
, then
\bar{H}_{n}|\tilde{\psi}_{i}\rangle=E_{i}|\bar{\psi}_{i}\rangle
, where
|\bar{\psi}_{i}\rangle=P_{n}|\bar{\psi}_{i}\rangle
. The solution to leading order is

\bar{H}_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}+P_{n}VP_{n}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}}P_{n}VP_{m}VP_{n}+\mathcal{O}(V^{3}).

(S29)

Next, given some operator
M
and two not necessarily distinct eigenvectors of
H
denoted
|\psi_{1}\rangle
,
|\psi_{2}\rangle
in the
same
manifold of states near
E_{n}
, we want to find the operator
\bar{M}
, defined only on the projected Hilbert space, such that

\langle\psi_{1}|M|\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}|\bar{M}|\bar{\psi}_{2}\rangle,\qquad|\bar{\psi}_{i}\rangle=P_{n}|\psi_{i}\rangle.

(S30)

The solution to leading order is

\bar{M}=P_{n}MP_{n}+\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{P_{n}MP_{m}VP_{n}-P_{n}VP_{m}MP_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}}+\mathcal{O}(V^{2}).

(S31)

Finally, given some operator
M
and two distinct eigenvectors of
H
denoted
|\psi_{1}\rangle
,
|\psi_{2}\rangle
in
different
manifolds of states near
E_{n}
and
E_{m}
respectively (
n\neq m
), we want to find the operator
\tilde{M}
satisfying

\langle\psi_{1}|M|\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}|\bar{M}|\bar{\psi}_{2}\rangle,\qquad|\bar{\psi}_{1}\rangle=P_{n}|\psi_{1}\rangle,\quad|\bar{\psi}_{2}\rangle=P_{m}|\psi_{2}\rangle.

(S32)

The solution is

\bar{M}=P_{n}MP_{m}+\sum_{\ell\neq m}\frac{P_{n}MP_{\ell}VP_{m}}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{\ell}}+\sum_{\ell\neq n}\frac{P_{n}VP_{\ell}MP_{m}}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{\ell}}+\mathcal{O}(V^{2}).

(S33)

Landau level projected theory.—
Quite generally, we consider electrons in a magnetic field and both 1-body and 2-body potentials:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}H_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}-e{\boldsymbol{A}})+V({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}U({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{r}}_{j})

(S34a)

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{C}

(S34b)

where

and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
describes a uniform magnetic field

Again, we’ll be interested in the low-energy spectral weight for when Galilean invariance is broken and
\Lambda
is intermediate between the strengths of

\hbar\omega_{c}

vanishes. Turning on

For

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}=-\frac{e(\boldsymbol{p}+e\boldsymbol{A})}{mA}

\displaystyle=\frac{ie\omega_{c}}{A}\left(a^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{d}^{*}-a\boldsymbol{d}\right)

where subleading terms are dropped and

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi A}{\hbar}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{\langle 0|j_{a}|n\rangle\langle n|j_{a}|0\rangle}{\omega_{n0}}

Although finite intraband optical spectral weight is expected once continuous translation symmetry is broken, the perturbed Landau level clarifies that optical darkness is not a generic property of FCIs, but instead relies on additional assumptions.

m^{*}=0.62m_{e}
is the effective mass.

S10
Numerical Evaluation of Response Functions

In this section, we discuss efficient numerical methods to evaluate arbitrary response functions in frequency space.

S10.1
(a) Dynamical Response Functions

Quite often, we are interested in evaluating a dynamical response function

I_{B}(\omega)=\sum_{n\neq 0}|\langle 0|B|n\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-(E_{n}-E_{0}))

(S43)

where
|0\rangle
is the many-body state of interest (for example the ground state of a many-body Hamiltonian) with energy
E_{0}
and
|n\rangle
is the
n
-th eigenstate of the Hamiltonian with energy
E_{n}
.
B
denotes the response operator (for example current or density). Let’s define the Green’s function
G_{B}(Z)
,

G_{B}(Z)=\langle 0|\dfrac{B^{\dagger}B}{Z-H}|0\rangle

(S44)

Equation (
S43
) can be recast in the following form

I_{B}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm lim}_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\>{\rm Im}\>G_{B}(\omega+E_{0}+i\eta)

(S45)

where we have made use of the identity
{\rm lim}_{\eta\rightarrow 0}1/(\omega\pm i\eta)=\mathcal{P}(1/\omega)\mp 2\pi i\delta(\omega)
with
\mathcal{P}
denoting the principal value. A finite
\eta
corresponds to broadening the imaginary part from a delta function to a Lorentzian. To evaluate equation (
S43
) or equation (
S45
), one can simply find all the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian. However, this is only possible for small systems. As shown in
[
68
]
, the dynamical correlation function (
S45
) can be evaluated efficiently in the Krylov subspace of the normalized state
|B\rangle=B|0\rangle/\sqrt{\langle 0|B^{\dagger}B|0\rangle}
defined as,

\mathcal{K}^{L}(B)={\rm span}(|B\rangle,H|B\rangle,\dots,H^{L}|B\rangle)

(S46)

This is the same idea behind the standard Lanczos algorithm for finding extremal eigenvalues of a matrix. The orthonormal basis
|v_{n}\rangle
in
L
-th dimensional Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}^{L}(B)
is given by

\begin{split}&|v_{0}\rangle=|B\rangle\\
&H|v_{n}\rangle=b_{n}|v_{n-1}\rangle+a_{n}|v_{n}\rangle+b_{n+1}|v_{n+1}\rangle\\
&a_{n}=\langle v_{n}|H_{n}|v_{n}\rangle,\>b_{n+1}=||H|v_{n}\rangle-a_{n}|v_{n}\rangle-b_{n}|v_{n-1}\rangle||,\>b_{0}=0\\
\end{split}

(S47)

The Hamiltonian expressed in this basis takes the form of a tridiagonal matrix,

H=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{1}&0&0&&0&0\\
b_{1}&a_{1}&b_{2}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&b_{2}&a_{2}&b_{3}&&0&0\\
0&0&b_{3}&a_{3}&&0&0\\
&\vdots&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&&a_{L-1}&b_{L}\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&b_{L}&a_{L}\end{pmatrix}

(S48)

In the Krylov subspace basis (
S47
), the Green’s function (
S44
) is given by

G_{B}(Z)=\langle 0|B^{\dagger}B|0\rangle[(Z-H)^{-1}]_{00}

(S49)

where
00
denotes the element of the first row and first column. We then evaluate the inverse of
(Z-H)^{-1}
by partitioning it into a block
2\times 2
matrix,

\begin{split}Z-H=\begin{pmatrix}Z-a_{0}&(B^{(1)})^{T}\\
B^{(1)}&Z-H^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\\
[(Z-H)^{-1}]_{00}=(Z-a_{0}-b_{1}^{2}[(Z-H^{(1)})^{-1}]_{00})^{-1}\end{split}

(S50)

Evaluating the inverse recursively, we arrive at a continued fraction expression for
G_{B}(Z)
,

\begin{split}G_{B}(Z)=\langle 0|B^{\dagger}B|0\rangle[(Z-H)^{-1}]_{00}\\
=\dfrac{\langle 0|B^{\dagger}B|0\rangle}{Z-a_{0}-\dfrac{b_{1}^{2}}{Z-a_{1}-\dfrac{b_{2}^{2}}{Z-a_{2}-\dots}}}\end{split}

(S51)

To evaluate
G_{B}(Z)
in the many-body state
|0\rangle
, we first calculate
|A\rangle=B|0\rangle/\sqrt{\langle 0|B^{\dagger}B|0\rangle}
then apply
L
Lanczos iterations as given in (
S47
) then we evaluate
G_{B}(Z)
using equation (
S51
). The dynamical response function
I_{A}(\omega)
can be calculated simply through equation (
S45
). The number of Lanczos iterations
L
is increased until convergence is reached. Therefore through this approach, we only need the Hamiltonian
H
, the state
|0\rangle
and the response operator
B
.

S10.2
Example: Optical Conductivity

We apply this method to calculate the regular part of the optical conductivity which we copy below,

\sigma_{\pm}(\omega)=\frac{\pi A}{|\omega|}\sum_{n\neq 0}|\langle 0|j_{\pm}|n\rangle|^{2}[\delta(w-E_{n}+E_{0})-\delta(w+E_{n}-E_{0})]

(S52)

With
j_{\pm}
the current operator for left/right circular polarization In this case, we take
B=j_{\pm}
giving rise to

\begin{split}\sigma_{\pm}(\omega>0)=-\frac{\pi A}{|\omega|}\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm lim}_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\>{\rm Im}\>G_{j_{\pm}}(\omega+E_{0}+i\eta)\\
\sigma_{\pm}(\omega<0)=-\frac{\pi A}{|\omega|}\frac{1}{\pi}{\rm lim}_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\>{\rm Im}\>G_{j_{\pm}}(\omega-E_{0}+i\eta)\end{split}

(S53)

From
\sigma_{\pm}(\omega)
, one can readily compute the real part of longitudinal conductivity
{\rm Re}\sigma_{xx}
or the imaginary part of the Hall conductivity
{\rm Im}\sigma_{xy}
by taking the sum or difference of
\sigma_{+}
and
\sigma_{-}
.
