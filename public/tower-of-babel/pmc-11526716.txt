Folate-engineered chitosan nanoparticles: next-generation anticancer nanocarriers
Authors: Prashant Kesharwani, Kratika Halwai, Saurav Kumar Jha, Mohammed H AL Mughram, Salem Salman Almujri, Waleed H Almalki, Amirhossein Sahebkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChitosan nanoparticles (NPs) are well-recognized as promising vehicles for delivering anticancer drugs due to their distinctive characteristics. They have the potential to enclose hydrophobic anticancer molecules, thereby enhancing their solubilities, permeabilities, and bioavailabilities; without the use of surfactant, i.e., through surfactant-free solubilization. This allows for higher drug concentrations at the tumor sites, prevents excessive toxicity imparted by surfactants, and could circumvent drug resistance. Moreover, biomedical engineers and formulation scientists can also fabricate chitosan NPs to slowly release anticancer agents. This keeps the drugs at the tumor site longer, makes therapy more effective, and lowers the frequency of dosing. Notably, some types of cancer cells (fallopian tube, epithelial tumors of the ovary, and primary peritoneum; lung, kidney, ependymal brain, uterus, breast, colon, and malignant pleural mesothelioma) have overexpression of folate receptors (FRs) on their outer surface, which lets folate-drug conjugate–incorporated NPs to target and kill them more effectively. Strikingly, there is evidence suggesting that the excessively produced FR&αgr (isoforms of the FR) stays consistent throughout treatment in ovarian and endometrial cancer, indicating resistance to conventional treatment; and in this regard, folate-anchored chitosan NPs can overcome it and improve the therapeutic outcomes. Interestingly, overly expressed FRs are present only in certain tumor types, which makes them a promising biomarker for predicting the effectiveness of FR-targeted therapy. On the other hand, the folate-modified chitosan NPs can also enhance the oral absorption of medicines, especially anticancer drugs, and pave the way for effective and long-term low-dose oral metronomic scheduling of poorly soluble and permeable drugs. In this review, we talked briefly about the techniques used to create, characterize, and tailor chitosan-based NPs; and delved deeper into the potential applications of folate-engineered chitosan NPs in treating various cancer types.Graphical AbstractSchematic illustration of target ligand-drug incorporated chitosan nanoparticles and its advantage in treating cancer. Introduction

The solubility and permeability of a drug are essential factors in determining its effectiveness as an anticancer agent. A drug’s solubility determines its ability to dissolve in biological fluids and enter the circulation upon delivery. Drugs with poor solubility usually have limited absorption, and much lesser anticancer efficacy [1–5]. In contrast, drugs that are amphiphilic in nature can spread evenly throughout the body, which guarantees a constant level of exposure to cancerous cells and maximizes the therapeutic efficacy. Next, permeability plays a vital role in determining how well a molecule can cross biological barriers and effectively reach certain tissues, such as tumor locations [6–10]. Amphiphilic molecules could effectively penetrate tumor tissue and generate lethal effects owing to their elevated cell-permeation rate [11–17]. However, certain cancer cells have efflux pumps that aggressively expel medications from inside the cell, resulting in lower drug concentration within the tumor, and subsequently lead toward drug resistance. Despite that, drugs that are greatly soluble and permeable are less likely to be affected by resistance caused by efflux pumps [18–22]. Thus, the significance of solubility and permeability highlights the need for drug development techniques that enhance these characteristics for anticancer medicines, without imparting systemic toxicities. Different strategies, such as using nanoparticle(s) (NP/NPs)–based delivery systems, (liposomes, nanoemulsion, micelles, polymeric NPs, dendrimer, nanocrystals, solid-lipid NPs, and nanodecoys) designing prodrugs, and making chemical alterations, can be applied to enhance the solubility, permeability, targeting capacity, tumor-bioavailability, and overall treatment effectiveness of drugs against cancer cell [23–30].

Chitosan, a biopolymer obtained from chitin, has several physicochemical characteristics that render it as a versatile substance for various applications, such as medication administration, wound recovery, tissue fabrication, and others [31–37]. Biological processes can break down biodegradable substances like chitosan into less complex and non-harmful chemicals. Thus, chitosan's ecologically benign nature and biocompatibility make it suited for use in biomedical applications. In addition, former has amino groups in its composition, which give the polymer a polycationic character [38].Through electrostatic interactions, chitosan can engage with molecules that have a negative charge, such as nucleic acids and proteins. Hence, gene transfer, protein immobilization, and several other applications utilize these interactions [39]. Furthermore, chitosan has mucoadhesive properties, allowing it to stick to mucosal surfaces, such as those in the gastrointestinal system. This characteristic is specially advantageous for drug delivery system(s) (DDSs) designed for oral administration, since it increases the amount of time the drug stays at the absorption site [40]. When dissolved in acidic liquids, chitosan can form films or coatings [41]. Wound dressings, drug delivery patches, and food packaging materials utilize these films for their barrier qualities, antibacterial activity, and controlled release of active components. Chitosan has limited solubility in most organic solvents; however, it can be dissolved in acidic water-based solutions, especially when the pH is below 6 [42]. Various parameters, including the molecular weight, degree of deacetylation (DDA), and pH of the solvent, can alter or affect chitosan's solubility. However, this feature is equally essential for the customizing chitosan-based polymers and DDSs as per various applications [43]. Notably, when crosslinking agents or specific conditions are present, such as high temperatures or the presence of multivalent cations, chitosan can form hydrogels [44]. Chitosan hydrogels are widely used in tissue engineering, pharmaceutical drug delivery and for wound repair to provide a three-dimensional scaffolding for cellular proliferation and sustained release of medicine [45, 46]. Overall, the above-mentioned features of chitosan indicate that it is an important biomaterial for the production of antimicrobial coatings, wound dressings, and food packaging materials, for prolonging their shelf life.

On the other hand, the presence of folate receptors (FRs) in tumors is a well-known occurrence and serves as the foundation for targeted cancer treatments that uses NPs synthesized with folate [47] (Fig. 1). FRs are glycoproteins that facilitate the cellular absorption of folate, which is also known as vitamin B9. They have a vital function in folate metabolism, including DNA synthesis, repair, and methylation. Notably, FRs are often found in modest quantities in normal tissues and are mostly concentrated in certain cell types, including the kidneys, lungs, and placenta [48, 49]. Moreover, several forms of cancer exhibit excessive expression of FRs on their surface. The upregulation is thought to occur because of the heightened need for folate by rapidly developing cancer cells to facilitate DNA synthesis and cell proliferation [50]. Several solid tumors, including ovarian, lung, breast, colorectal, and brain malignancies, often exhibit overexpressed FRs. There are many subcategories of FRs, including FRs alpha (FRα), FRβ, and FRγ [51]. The FRα subtype has been widely researched and is often found to be overexpressed in ovarian and other epithelial malignancies [52]. Conversely, FRβ is found on activated macrophages and is associated with certain blood cancers and inflammatory conditions [53], while FRγ is mostly located in the placenta. The upregulation of FRs in neoplastic cells has substantial implications for both diagnosis and therapy. It facilitates the development of folate-specific imaging agents, enabling non-invasive methods such as positron emission tomography or fluorescence imaging to identify and visualize malignancies [54]. Hence, the abundance of FRs in tumors justifies the formulation or fabrication of chitosan NPs that precisely target these receptors. Apart from effective tumor targeting, chitosan NPs can shield medications from degradation in the challenging gastrointestinal environment, enhance drug absorption via folate–receptor-mediated endocytosis (RME), and selectively target certain cells or tissues, resulting in more efficient therapy with less adverse effects.

This literature review focuses on the formulation, design, characterization, and tailoring techniques for effectively delivering folate-anchored and anticancer drug-incorporated chitosan NPs to kill tumor cells. It also discusses in detail on recent advancements in various folate engineered chitosan-based nano-DDSs in relation to cancer-based therapeutics and/or diagnostic applications.

Systematic survey of literature

We conducted a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of the progress achieved in chitosan-based NPs by using well recognized search engines, including PubMed and Scopus. A search for the phrase "Chitosan NPs" on the search engine resulted in a total of 14,248 articles published between 1989 and 2024. Afterwards, we began our inquiry into the utilization of chitosan NPs for cancer therapy, resulting in a total of 2,006 articles from 1999 to 2024. This data suggests that although humans began using chitosan extensively from 1989, a systematic investigation into its use for cancer treatment commenced in 1999. Afterwards, there was an attempt to gather and organize the current body of information about the excessive expression of the FRs in cancer. We retrieved 1,134 papers from the PubMed database, spanning from 1991 to 2024. Furthermore, our research primarily focused on the application of folate-modified chitosan NPs in tumor targeting. A cumulative total of 65 scientific publications from 2010 to 2024 were obtained. Next, we searched for the abundance of FRs-targeted approaches and folate-modified chitosan NPs to treat cancer in clinical trials. For FRs-targeted approaches, we found 76 articles enlisted in the categories of clinical study and clinical trial (phases I, II, and III). Next, for folate-modified chitosan NPs to treat cancer in the clinical trial study, no result was obtained. These wide ranges of scholarly resources—including articles, books, reviews, and clinical trial reports were collectively used to compile this review.

Synthesis and characterization of ligand-anchored chitosan nanoparticles

Chitosan is a naturally occurring biopolymer that is obtained from chitin, a substance present in the exoskeletons of crustaceans such as crabs, shrimp, and insects [55, 56] (Fig. 2, 3, and 4). This material's versatility stems from its non-toxicity, biodegradability, and compatibility with living organisms. Chitosan consists of repetitive units of D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, connected by β-(1 → 4) glycosidic linkages [57]. The glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine unit often indicates the structure of the former. One method to measure the amount of glucosamine units in chitosan is by looking at its DDA [58]. Because its amino groups are protonated, chitosan dissolves in acidic solutions with a pH below 6.5 [59]. Under neutral and basic circumstances, the amino groups lose their ability to ionize, causing the substance to become insoluble. It is worth mentioning that chitosan is biocompatible and can be broken down by lysozyme in living organisms, which makes it a good candidate for pharmacological and therapeutics applications (Table 1) [60]. Furthermore, the antibacterial characteristics of chitosan are influenced by factors like as its molecular weight, DDA, and the type of microbial strain. [61]. Chitosan exhibits cationic polyelectrolyte properties in acidic conditions because of the existence of protonated amino groups [62]. They are reported to undergo chemical modifications such as N-acylation, carboxymethylation, and grafting to improve its characteristics or include new functions (Fig. 3, and 4) [63]. The incorporation of drug into chitosan can be accomplished by many techniques, which rely on the physicochemical properties of the drug, the intended release pattern, and the specific application. The ionotropic gelation technique is a process that includes the cross-linking of chitosan with polyanions via ionic interactions. To create a cationic polymer, this method entails combining chitosan with an acidic solution. The drug is then combined with the chitosan solution. Subsequently, gel-like particles are formed by ionic interactions when a polyanionic solution, such as tripolyphosphate (TPP) (crosslinker), is introduced to the mixture of chitosan and drug. In particular, the ionotropic gelation methods work best for drugs that are water-loving (hydrophilic), and for producing tiny particles that can release pharmaceuticals in a regulated manner [64]. For incorporating hydrophobic molecules, chitosan and drug are solubilized in an organic solvent via emulsification and solvent evaporation procedure [65, 66]. The mixture is then transformed into a water-based solution that includes an emulsifying agent, such as polyvinyl alcohol. Subsequently, the solvent evaporates, leading to the formation of drug-loaded chitosan particles in a solid state. Furthermore, chitosan matrix characteristics can be modified to manage the release rate, making it beneficial for hydrophobic medicines. Next, coacervation procedures include the partitioning of chitosan and drug molecules from a solution into a solid phase. At first, chitosan and the therapeutics molecule are both solubilized in a common solvent. Afterwards, the chitosan is precipitated and then encapsulates the medication by progressively cooling the solution or adding a non-solvent. On the other hand, spray drying method is a widely applicable technology, particularly for the production of microspheres or microcapsules, and is often used to formulate chitosan-based DDSs in powder form [67]. The drug and chitosan are mixed well, and then the solution is fragmented into little droplets using a spray dryer. When the droplets move through a stream of heated air, the solvent vaporizes, resulting in the formation of drug-incorporated chitosan particles. This method is compatible with both hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules and is often used in the production of pharmaceutical products [68]. The electrospinning technology is used to fabricate nanofibers that encapsulate the drug, enabling its application in transdermal or local delivery systems [69]. At first, chitosan solution is made with the addition of the drug. Subsequently, the drug is absorbed into the chitosan matrix as the solution is electrospun to create fibers. This method is well-suited for topical product and wound healing, offering a substantial surface area for the release of pharmaceutical moiety. Each of these approaches has distinct benefits and constraints, and the picking of a method relies on the unique prerequisites of the DDSs, including the drug kind, intended release pattern, and application (Fig. 3).

The functional characteristics of chitosan NPs are primarily influenced by their structural qualities, such as dimension, surface charge, polydispersity index (PDI), and morphology. Moreover, these characteristics govern or influence the biological activities of the organisms [91]. Their capability to carry a certain amount of active pharmaceutical ingredient and effectively trap them helps regulate the amount of drugs delivered and the adverse effects associated with them. The size of the NPs affects the toxicity associated with drugs. Hence, the manufacturing method has a pivotal role in regulating these characteristics and has the capability to customize chitosan NPs for particular applications. The selection of the procedure is dictated by the desired attributes of the end product [91]. The ionotropic gelation technique utilizes the charge interaction between chitosan and the anionic crosslinking agents, such as sodium TPP to produce an amphoteric ion-pair complex. The generated NPs via ionotropic gelation techniques have improved protein adhesion properties due to this quality. Moreover, the molar proportion of the chitosan and the crosslinker determines the average diameters of the NPs and the kinetics of drug release [91]. Using a mild acid like acetic acid to dissolve chitosan, the temperature at which crosslinking happens, and the speed of the stirring all have an impact on the size distribution of the NPs. However, one major drawback of this technique is the NPs low stability in an acidic environment and their limited capacity to accommodate large molecular-weight drugs [92–94]. Next, chitosan's physicochemical properties, like how it crystallizes, dissolves, and breaks down, are based on its molecular weight and the DDA (Fig. 4). Notably, chitosan that has been completely deacetylated has the highest level of crystallinity, whereas chitosan in a partially deacetylated state has a partly crystalline structure. A greater DDA correlates to a larger density of positive charges, which leads to improved solubility [91]. In addition, a greater cationic charge intensifies the mucoadhesive characteristic of chitosan due to its improved interaction with the negatively charged mucin in the mucus membrane. When it is wet, Mima et al. observed a positive correlation between the DDA and the tensile strength of chitosan, but found no impact on its strength when it is dry. Films fabricated using high-molecular-weight chitosan exhibited superior tensile strength and tensile elongation in comparison to those made from low-molecular-weight [91]. In addition, the degradation of chitosan-based scaffolds used for tissue engineering applications is also significantly affected by the DDA [95]. Rebbouh et al. applied the ionotropic gelation process to produce chitosan NPs for the purpose of delivering the Aah II toxin, which was extracted from the venom of the Androctonus australis hector scorpion. The objective was to formulate a NP-based DDSs for immunizing agent administration. The obtained nanocarriers were perfectly round and had a sleek surface. The entrapment efficiency was found to be 96.66%, while the loading capacity was 33.5%. The Aah II-chitosan NPs exhibited a particle size of 208 nm, a PDI of 0.23, and a zeta potential of + 30 mV, respectively. The tiny size of NPs allowed for the retention of NPs at the injection site, resulting in a depot effect [96, 97]. Chitosan NPs have comparable pharmacokinetics (PK) characteristics to other polymeric NPs because they use the similar drug release principles. Shabouri et al. undertook PK investigations on chitosan NPs in beagle dogs to quantify the bioavailability of cyclosporin-A, by incorporating in various NPs made from polymers like sodium glycocholate (SGC), chitosan, and gelatin. The study suggested that the formulations constituted with chitosan and gelatin NPs exhibited a significantly higher maximum concentration (Cmax), while SGC NPs demonstrated a lower Cmax. Chitosan NPs showed a more than two-fold increase in AUC with respect to SGC and gelatin. The investigator then concluded that the surface zeta potential of the chosen polymers may account for the observed variations in PK parameters. The anionic surface zeta potential on the surface of SGC NPs may impede their ability to stick to the intestinal mucus, in contrast to chitosan NPs [98–100].

In addition to folate, chitosan can form bonds with galactose, mannose, and antibodies to facilitate targeted delivery of incorporated drugs to galactose-, mannose, and antigen-receptor, respectively (Fig. 1). Notably, specific receptors on various cell surfaces, such as galactose recognition and binding domains, identify galactose [72] (Fig. 1, Table 1). Galactose-conjugated chondroitin sulfate is particularly engineered for precise targeting of hepatocytes. Notably, the galactose ligand has a strong affinity for asialoglycoprotein receptors (ASGPR), which are found in large numbers on hepatocytes and different types of human hepatoma cells, with over 500,000 receptors per cell. Similarly, mannose is a fundamental constituent of living cells and has significant potential as a promising option for delivering drugs. Mannosylated chitosan, which is naturally present in the human body, decreases the toxicity associated with unmodified chitosan [72, 101]. Considering the significant role of carbohydrates in several biological processes, mannosylation is a natural and intriguing method for the specific transportation of active substances including anticancer drugs. The presence of mannose receptors on various cell surfaces, including hepatic and lymphatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, nonvascular endothelial cells, human and mouse dendritic cells, and macrophages among others, suggests that mannosylated chitosan-based NP systems are the most viable choice for enhancing the targeting and therapeutic effectiveness of chemotherapeutic agents [72]. Antibody-conjugated chitosan are regarded as efficient ligand systems for the targeted delivery of pharmaceuticals, DNA, and RNA to cells exhibiting elevated levels of particular antigens and receptors. For instance, researchers have used the elevated levels of HER2 expression in 20 percent of breast carcinomas to target the delivery of chitosan-conjugated antibodies. Furthermore, CD4T cells and BRCA1 have been used for the conveyance of chitosan-conjugated antibodies to the desired site [72]. Next, diverse targeting ligands can be affixed to chitosan NPs using many methods, including covalent conjugation, physical adsorption, click chemistry, and affinity interactions. (Fig. 5) [102]. Each of these techniques seeks to firmly bind the ligand to the NPs while preserving their stability and usefulness. The covalent conjugation-based technique modifies the targeted ligand (such as folate, antibodies, or peptides) with reactive groups (such as amino, carboxyl, or thiol groups) suitable for conjugation [103]. On the other hand, chemical coupling takes place, i.e., joining the active ligand with chitosan through functional groups that match. Moreover, the physical adsorption approach entails incubating the targeted ligand with chitosan NPs under appropriate conditions (such as pH and temperature) to promote non-covalent adsorption onto the NPs surface. Next, the adsorption process of linking the targeting ligands can be driven by either electrostatic interactions or hydrophobic interactions. Similarly, the click chemistry method connects azide- or alkyne-functionalized ligands with chitosan NPs modified with complementary reactive groups through bioorthogonal reactions, specifically the copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) or strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) [104]. An affinity-based approach is used to attach a targeted molecule by using affinity interactions, such as biotin-avidin or streptavidin–biotin interactions [105]. This is done by integrating biotinylated ligands into chitosan NPs that are coated with avidin or streptavidin. However, it is possible to genetically modify chitosan by including derivatives such as peptide motifs or fusion proteins. These modifications enable chitosan to selectively attach to certain targeted ligands or receptors [105].

Methods such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) are commonly used to evaluate the morphology of NPs [106]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) or laser diffraction measure the particle size and zeta potential of NPs to assess their stability and surface charge. Next, the techniques like UV–Vis spectroscopy or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and liquid Chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry LC–MS-MS are used to elucidate the drug's encapsulation efficiency of the NPs, and PK characteristic [107]. Methods like dialysis or Franz diffusion cells are useful to further calculate the rate at which the medication releases from the NPs under simulated physiological circumstances [108]. In addition, to confirm the existence of surface alterations or targeting ligands such as folate, methods such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) or X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) are utilized. Furthermore, to evaluate the compatibility of the NPs with living organisms and their potential to cause harm to cells; cell viability tests, such as MTT or alamarBlue assays, can be conducted. Thus, by obtaining the above-mentioned information, formulation scientists can change the properties of chitosan NPs to make them safer and more effective for biomedical uses by carefully preparing and characterizing them.

Mechanisms of drug release from chitosan nanoparticles

The drug's physicochemical properties, NP formulation, and surrounding environmental conditions may all impact its release from chitosan NPs [109]. The primary mechanisms include diffusion, degradation, swelling, erosion, and ion exchange [110] (Fig. 6). The major mechanism that releases medicines or APIs from chitosan NPs is diffusion. This mechanism involves the migration of drug molecules from an area with a greater concentration within the NPs to one with a lower concentration in the surrounding medium [111]. Several parameters, such as the drug, the chitosan matrix, and the environment, can influence the concentration gradient that increases diffusion [112]. Notably, smaller molecules have better diffusion rates than larger molecules because they experience less resistance while crossing the matrix [113]. In addition, the molecule solubility in both the medium and the chitosan matrix can influence the diffusion rate. Pharmaceutical compounds with higher solubility in the matrix have slower rates of release and improved diffusion under watery conditions, whereas hydrophobic molecules have lower diffusion rates due to their reduced solubility in the surrounding medium [114]. Furthermore, the chitosan matrix's permeability could have a substantial effect on the diffusion process. A highly porous matrix facilitates the efficient and quick dispersion of therapeutic compounds [115]. In particular, more cross-linking in the chitosan matrix could slow down drug diffusion by creating a tighter network that makes it harder for drug molecules to move [116]. Additionally, nanocarriers with a larger surface area have the ability to enhance the pace of medicine release owing to the increased area accessible for drug diffusion. In conclusion, diffusion-controlled release is advantageous because it allows for the unhurried and continuous release of drugs, resulting in a steady therapeutic concentration for an extended period [117]. This approach is very beneficial for providing pharmacological medicines that require a steady level of circulation, as well as reducing the frequency of administration, which improves patient compliance.

Another key mechanism known as degradation could liberate pharmaceuticals from chitosan NPs. Chemical, enzymatic, or environmental stimuli may start this process, which degrades the chitosan matrix [119]. As the matrix deteriorates, the drug incorporated within progressively escapes into the surrounding environment. Many body fluids include lysozyme, the primary enzyme responsible for chitosan breakdown [60]. Lysozyme speeds up the breaking down of chitosan's glycosidic bonds, which breaks down the polymer chain [60]. Enzymes typically break down chitosan by removing the β-(1 → 4)-linkages between N-acetylglucosamine and glucosamine units in the polymer [55]. Characteristics such as chitosan DDA concentration, polymer molecular weight, and the presence of certain functional groups affect the enzymatic breakdown rate [120]. A slower rate of degradation is often associated with increased DDA, whereas a lower molecular weight may expedite the process. Overall, breakdown is a necessary mechanism for drug release from chitosan NPs. Many factors impact this process, including enzymatic activity, pH levels, and chitosan's physical and chemical properties. Understanding these characteristics allows formulation scientists to design DDSs that precisely regulate drug release, allowing for a broad variety of medicinal uses.

Swelling is an important step that determines the rate of drug release from chitosan NPs [109]. The process entails assimilation of water or other fluids from the surrounding environment into the polymer matrix, resulting in an increase in NP dimensions [121]. This approach alters the drug's release kinetics by changing the internal structure and porosity of the NPs, which affects the drug's diffusion within them. Swelling occurs when the hydrophilic groups in the chitosan matrix contact the water molecules, causing the matrix to expand [122]. This might increase the mobility of the drug molecules, allowing them to diffuse out of the NPs. The balance of hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions within the polymer network, along with osmotic pressure differences between the interior and exterior of the NPs, determines the degree of swelling. Chitosan with a reduced DDA content has more acetyl groups that attract water, leading to more water absorption and swelling [123]. In contrast, greater DDA results in fewer hydrophilic groups and lesser swelling of matrix, resulting in sustained release. Notably, increasing the cross-linking density may restrict the NPs size and movements by creating a stiffer and less porous structure [122]. In contrast, a reduction in cross-linking density allows for more expansion via swelling. Next, the pH of the surrounding media influences chitosan NP’s swelling behavior [124]. Acidic environments protonate the amino groups in chitosan. This increases the polymer's hydrophilicity, causing it to swell more. Conversely, under neutral or basic conditions, protonation levels fall, resulting in reduced swelling [125]. This occurs due to the protection of the electrostatic charges on the chitosan chains. Furthermore, higher temperatures often increase the kinetic energy of water molecules and the rate of diffusion into the matrix, influencing swelling behavior [126]. As the NPs size increases, the polymer matrix becomes more porous, enabling additional pathways for drug molecules to traverse [127]. This may result in an initial quick release of the medicine, followed by a gradual release as the matrix expands. Overall, variations in swelling qualities caused by altering DDA, cross-linking density, and ambient conditions may affect the rate and duration of drug release. This is particularly useful for developing DDSs with long-term or specialized release kinetics. Furthermore, formulation scientists may tailor swelling to certain environmental stimuli, such as pH fluctuations. For instance, scientists can program NPs to expand and release their therapeutic payload in response to the acidic conditions of a tumor or infection site [128]. To conclude, swelling is critical in the release of medications from chitosan NPs. The chitosan matrix's properties, such as DDA and cross-linking, as well as external conditions like pH and ionic strength, all have an effect. Understanding and regulating these characteristics allows for the development of efficient medicine delivery systems with personalized release patterns.

When the polymer matrix progressively degrades or dissolves, the erosion process releases drugs from chitosan NPs [129]. Erosion refers to the physical removal of material from NPs rather than chemical or enzymatic processes. We can accomplish this by dissolving the chitosan-NPs in a solvent or using mechanical forces [130]. The medication or APIs within the matrix gradually leak out as it deteriorates. DDSs can adjust the erosion process to change the rate of medicine delivery. Notably, surface erosion happens when the coating on the outside of chitosan NPs breaks down or gets smaller over time, which makes the NPs smaller [109]. This erosion is frequently constant and occurs when the matrix dissolves faster than the water enters it. Surface erosion frequently results in a regular and controlled medicine outflow, whereas bulk erosion occurs over the NPs full volume, not just on the surface [131]. Water enters the whole matrix, causing chitosan to dissolve uniformly. Damage to NPs structural integrity may result in bulk erosion and quicker drug release. Notably, high-molecular-weight chitosan contains longer polymer chains, resulting in a slower erosion process than low-molecular-weight chitosan [132]. Furthermore, the mechanism and degree of cross-linking keep the chitosan matrix intact, slowing its erosion. A matrix that is strongly cross-linked is more resistant to disintegration and mechanical stress, resulting in a slower rate of deterioration. Conversely, a reduction in cross-linking density promotes faster deterioration. To conclude, erosion plays an important role in drug release from chitosan NPs. Overall, the intrinsic properties of chitosan, DDA, and molecular weight, as well as extrinsic factors like pH and temperature, regulate this process. By altering these characteristics, we can construct DDSs with precise release patterns, making them suitable for a variety of therapeutic purposes.

Superiority of chitosan-based nanoparticles as drug delivery vehicles for cancer treatment

The restricted solubility of anticancer drugs in physiological environment has prompted the development of many techniques, including colloidal dispersion, nanosuspension, micelles, liposomes, emulsions, nanotubes, prodrugs, and protein conjugates, to enhance drug delivery effectiveness [133]. In addition, researchers have extensively studied polymeric micelles, made from amphiphilic natural or synthetic polymers, to enhance their ability to load hydrophobic compounds [134–139]. A surfactant-free polymeric micelle produced from a carbohydrate-based polymer containing docetaxel (DTX) has been successfully developed and shown to be less hazardous than formulations including surfactants [140]. Cellulose, a naturally occurring polymer made up of β-d-glucose units, has limited solubility in water and other organic solvents. However, chemically changing the hydroxyl groups to hydrophobic groups like methyl or hydroxylpropyl can improve its solubility in water [141]. Methylcellulose (MC), a hydrophobic cellulose derivative, can be destroyed enzymatically to provide low molecular weight methylcellulose. Pluronic block co-polymers are chemically produced in organic solvents, while low-molecular weight methylcellulose (LMwMC) LMwMC–mediated micellar aggregates are created under physical conditions. As a result, they act as a novel form of DDS, improving the solubility and loading efficiency of hydrophobic anticancer drugs [140, 142]. The procedure of surfactant-free solubilization of poorly soluble medicines using chitosan makes use of the unique properties of chitosan to increase the solubility and bioavailability of poorly soluble and permeable molecule, and without the application of surfactants [140]. This approach provides substantial advantages by avoiding potential surfactant issues, such as toxicity or irritating characteristics. Chitosan, a positively charged polymer, may create ionic interactions with negatively charged pharmaceutical compounds. This can lead to the formation of a chitosan-drug complex, which has the potential to improve the drug's solubility [65]. The hydroxyl (-OH) and amino groups (-NH2) of chitosan are reported to form hydrogen bonds with drug molecules. This interaction improves the solubility of weakly water-soluble pharmaceuticals, hence enhancing their absorption and bioavailability. Additionally, many medications with limited solubility occur in crystalline form, which may limit their capacity to dissolve [143]. Chitosan has the potential to convert these drugs into an amorphous state, which often results in enhanced solubility [144, 145]. Chitosan could trap hydrophobic medications in its polymer structure, enabling them to dissolve more readily. A protective coating may progressively release the drug in a controlled manner, enhancing its absorption and utilization by the body [109]. Over a 25-day period, Chung et al. observed that DTX/LMwMC NPs release DTX in a regulated manner. Within the first 24 h, 20% of the medication was released in dissolving media, followed by 50% within two weeks. This demonstrates how their NP method can increase the drug's effectiveness without requiring an initial quick release. The authors argued that the LMwMC NPs consistent drug release pattern was due to both passive diffusion of medicines from the NPs and spontaneous breakdown [140]. By precisely fabricating chitosan-derived NPs, we can achieve a similar release profile [140]. These types of drug delivery vehicles may greatly reduce the toxicological effects associated with maximum tolerated dosage treatment in cancer, and the availability of medication in plasma for a longer period of time could also reduce dosing frequency and the likelihood of drug resistance.

Nanoparticles-based delivery systems advantages in achieving optimal plasma profile for superior anticancer efficacy

Understanding the correlation between the plasma drug profile and the effectiveness of anticancer treatment is essential for comprehending the mechanism by which a drug operates in the body to combat cancer [146]. In order for a medicine to be efficacious, it must reach a therapeutic concentration in the plasma that is adequate to impact cancer cells. It is equally important to sustain this level of concentration for a certain duration in order to achieve a consistent and ongoing anticancer effect [147]. The therapeutic window refers to the range of plasma drug concentrations in which the treatment exhibits efficacy without causing toxicity. Insufficient plasma concentration might render the medicine ineffective, while excessive concentration can lead to severe consequences. It is essential for the medicine to maintain a concentration above a certain threshold in the plasma for a significant period of time in order to effectively exhibit its anticancer effects [148]. In this regard, the anticancer drug incorporated NPs such as chitosan-NPs could be highly beneficial. The plasma levels of the drug also influence its ability to penetrate the tumor tissue. Inadequate penetration may result in less effective outcomes, even if the levels of the substance in the bloodstream are sufficient [149]. To circumvent this, targeted delivery of NPs as per the receptor or antigen being expressed on the surface of cancer cells could be beneficial. Apart from this, NPs can penetrate the tumor by various other mechanism such as endocytosis, paracellular transport, and macropinocytosis [150, 151]. Comprehending the plasma drug profile aids in establishing the most effective dosage and designing optimal drug delivery vehicles that enhances the therapeutic effectiveness while reducing harmful toxic effects. The correlation between plasma levels and effectiveness may determine the frequency of medication administration. For instance, medications with a short half-life may need more frequent administration. Continuous monitoring of plasma levels may assist in promptly changing dosages to ensure effectiveness, particularly for medications with limited therapeutic ranges. Protein binding is critical to the pharmacokinetics of anticancer drugs, influencing their efficacy significantly. When drugs enter the bloodstream, they may bind to plasma proteins such as albumin, alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, and lipoprotein [152]. When a medicine binds to a protein, it forms a drug-protein complex that is often not pharmacologically active [153]. Only the unbound (free) part of the drug molecule can exert its therapeutic influence, including its anticancer potential. Drugs that are highly protein-bound include a higher percentage of the drug in a bound form, reducing the amount of free drug that may reach tissues. This may impair the drug's ability to reach and affect the tumor site. High protein binding medications exhibit a reduced volume of distribution, indicating a primarily circulatory distribution rather than a uniform distribution throughout the body. This may affect their ability to effectively target tumor cells. Drug delivery technologies, such as NPs, may improve anticancer medicine administration by inhibiting protein binding and raising drug concentrations at the tumor site [154]. To conclude, protein binding may reduce the efficacy of anticancer treatments by limiting the amount of unbound drug available to target cancer cells. Understanding and regulating protein binding is critical for improving the efficacy of anticancer treatments.

Biocompatibility, safety, and biodegradability profile of chitosan nanoparticles

Chitosan's biocompatibility as both a molecule and a carrier needs to be carefully studied and dealt with because the way it is made may have an effect on its biocompatibility in the past [38, 155]. Using chitosan as a carrier and mixing it with a drug often limits the availability of positively charged amino groups. The lower charge influences its capacity to interact with cell membranes and the surrounding environment, perhaps decreasing absorption and toxicity. As a result, chitosan that has undergone significant deacetylation has a higher natural inclination to interact with cells, as shown by a prior study [156]. It is also important to look at the unique qualities that chitosan as a polymer offers, like its molecular weight, DDA, and ability to act as a base or salt [38]. We employ numerous assays to measure various aspects of cellular toxicity and a wide range of responses, but the lack of consistency in the assessment conditions complicates meaningful comparisons. However, the literature's information suggests that chitosan solutions frequently display a degree of toxicity, primarily due to dose [156] [157]. Polymer parameters such as molecular weight and DDA [156, 158], as well as the pH of the incubation medium and incubation period [159, 160], impact toxicity in addition to dose. Furthermore, studies typically show no discernible toxicity at doses of up to 1 mg/mL when the polymer serves as the matrix material for DDSs. However, it has been observed on occasion that larger doses do not result in a reduction in cell viability [156, 161–165]. However, the supplied dose continues to dictate the effect [156, 160, 166]. Several studies have also reported the use of chitosan as a coating material [157, 161, 167, 168]. Studies have repeatedly demonstrated that applying chitosan as a coating to a specific core has no substantial toxicity. In fact, certain circumstances have proven the inclusion of the polysaccharide to improve overall toxicity [38]. People often recommend chitosan carriers to deliver drugs across mucosal membranes into the circulation for systemic distribution. Pharmaceuticals' transcellular penetration is dependent on a variety of physicochemical properties, most notably lipophilicity. Mucosal epithelia are often distinguished by the presence of a strongly sealed cell barrier established by close contacts between surrounding cells. This barrier, known as the paracellular route, hinders molecules from moving freely between cells [169–171]. The degree of tightness of these junctions may be measured in cell cultures using a feature known as transepithelial electrical resistance (TER), which assesses the epithelium's barrier properties and integrity [172–177]. Any change in TER implies a modification in how the epithelial barrier functions [177]. Chitosan is known for its ability to temporarily break epithelial tight junctions, which improves medicine penetration as an absorption enhancer. The chitosan impact is due to the interaction of its protonated amino groups with cell membranes. This interaction produces a reversible rearrangement of junction proteins, including the redistribution of cytoskeletal F-actin and the tight junction protein ZO-1. As a result, the connection opens up. Most studies also show that chitosan solutions reduce TER more significantly than chitosan carriers. This is because carriers have fewer exposed chemical groups that may interact with cell surfaces than polymer chains in solution [178, 179]. Nonetheless, some studies have demonstrated that NPs generated a more significant drop in TER than solutions [180]. The pH change of the medium in which the carriers/solutions come into contact with the cells is one possible explanation for these different results. Because chitosan has a pKa of around 6.5, the polymeric chains in solution or carriers will form or not form aggregates depending on the pH of the medium in which they are incubated. The aggregates will have varied sizes, resulting in diverse patterns of interaction with the cells. This evaluation appears in just a few publications on chitosan carriers for drug delivery applications. When PLGA-CS NPs were mixed with Calu-3 cells, levels as high as 0.2 mg/mL did not cause any inflammatory markers to rise [181]. Chitosan microspheres with PLGA NPs did not increase TNF-α production in RAW264.7 macrophages at doses as high as 1.6 mg/mL [182]. Putting the same cell line in contact with chitosan-DNA NPs for 24 h had no effect on IL-6, IL-8, or TNF-α levels, even at a higher dose of 60 µg/mL [182]. The fundamental issue in comparing the results of this test, as with other tests, is the wide variety of conditions, notably the various doses tested. Surprisingly, across a broad range of concentrations, from 0.06 mg/mL to much higher values of 1.6 mg/mL, the data consistently indicated no indication that the tested carriers were inducing any inflammatory responses [169].

Researchers mostly evaluate the safety of chitosan carriers for oral and pulmonary delivery, but they have also investigated other routes. It was found that 72.16 mg/kg of paclitaxel-loaded chitosan micelles given intravenously to mice was the lethal dose (LD50) [183]. Furthermore, research found that giving rabbits an intravenous dose of 6 mg/kg/day for three days had no histological repercussions [183]. In another instance, a nose-only device administered chitosan-graft-spermine/pDNA NPs to mice, but no obvious injury occurred. The lungs' histological examination showed that the pneumocytes did not have any necrosis, metaplasia, or anaplasia. There was also no atelectasis or emphysema. There was no evidence of capillary expansion in the alveolar wall, and only a few damaged endothelial cells were visible. There was no sign of congestion, haemorrhage, or inflammatory cell infiltration. Furthermore, the brain, heart, lung, liver, kidney, and spleen showed no abnormalities [184]. However, the mice experienced some discomfort when fed glycol chitosan/cholanic acid NPs via the trachea (at a dose of 2 mg/kg). From 6 h to 3 days after injection, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), as well as the chemokine MIP-1α, were found in the lungs. These inflammatory markers rose during the first 24 h and then reverted to normal levels [185]. When rats were fed unloaded chitosan microparticles via the trachea, an increased inflammatory response was seen. The dose of particles varied from 2 to 10 mg/kg. The administration of different dosages resulted in a pro-inflammatory response, as evidenced by increased protein levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, increased lactate dehydrogenase activity, increased myeloperoxidase activity, and leukocyte migration in lung tissue. An examination of the bronchoalveolar fluid indicated a high concentration of neutrophils, which are inflammatory cells [186]. Unfortunately, the authors did not examine the animal groups after the inhalations to determine whether there was any healing from the inflammation. It is worth mentioning that the later trial used a dosage range of 2 to 10 mg/kg, while the prior study only tested a dose of 2 mg/kg. The particle composition varies greatly in each study, influencing the observed response. Furthermore, the first research evaluates NPs, while the latter investigates microparticles. The administration of chitosan microparticles at a dose of 2 mg/kg yielded a response nearly identical to the control group in practically every measured parameter [186]. As a result, they are thought to have no inflammatory properties. Testing both systems at the same dose (2 mg/kg) revealed a similar lack of inflammatory effect. The oral delivery resulted in different responses. Researchers tested three different doses (125 mg/kg each) of unloaded alginate and chitosan microparticles on guinea pigs over the course of 10 days. They found that the liver function markers (serum bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase) did not show any negative effects [187]. For 14 days, oral administration of 100 mg/kg of unloaded chitosan/poly-γ-glutamic acid NPs did not result in severe toxicity. The clinical signs of untreated and treated animals were identical. Furthermore, there were no obvious pathological changes in the liver, kidney, or intestine [188]. Investigators validated the hepatotoxicity of these NPs by testing levels of alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase. The therapy showed no changes in the parameters and no histological effect [189]. At the 3-week point, rats given trimethyl chitosan oligomer/DNA NPs orally twice a week for four weeks had moderate diarrhea. However, discontinuing therapy resolved the diarrhea [97]. The authors classified this as an instance of low toxicity. Chitosan is available in a variety of forms, including molecular weights, DDA, and chemical compounds. It is vital to realize that different polymeric characteristics may result in carriers with different topologies, which can cause various responses in both laboratory settings and live organisms. As a result, it is vital to do appropriate testing on different transporters and raw components. According to a previous study, chitosan carriers do not exhibit evident toxicity. However, it is vital to emphasize that neither NPs nor microparticles are fully inactive. Microparticles are quickly taken in by macrophagic cells, which can cause an inflammatory response [190]. About 150 µm chitosan microparticles are suggested as a base for fibroblast development [190]. This finding adds to the evidence of chitosan's medicinal potential.

Muzzarelli [191] demonstrated that fully acetylated chitosan is totally unaffected by the enzyme, such as chitosanase. The presence of three or more consecutive N-acetylated groups determines this. Nwe et al. [192] investigated how the molecular weight and acetylation level of a scaffold affect its efficiency in tissue regeneration. They postulated that lysozyme recognizes N-acetyl glucosamine sequences in chitin and chitosan molecules. As a result, the lysozyme digestibility increases as the polymer chain's N-acetylation level rises. Based on their findings, the chitosan matrix with a high DDA broke down into single chitosan molecules and short chains (oligomers) after being treated with lysozyme for a few days. In contrast, the matrix with low DDA stayed basically constant throughout time. The chitosan's molecular weight, crystallinity, and acetylation level directly influence the rate at which lysozyme degrades a chitosan scaffold. Furthermore, increased concentrations of lysozyme in the degradation medium result in quicker degradation rates. Bagheri-Khoulenjani et al. [193] investigated how chitosan briefly breaks down with varied molecular weights and DDA. The researchers used DDA, molecular weight, crystallinity, and swelling ratio to compare the various classes of chitosan. The data showed that the breakdown of chitosan with increased DDA did not result in any significant changes in molecular weight or DDA. The degradation process included breaking peptide bonds in the polymer's acetoamido side groups, not β-chains. The author found that variations in the location of acetamide groups inside the chitosan molecule affect its breakdown [131, 193].

The inquiry into the breakdown of polymer NPs is fraught with controversy. The majority of research employed an in-vitro degradation method, and all agreed that the micro/NPs are biodegradable. Chitosan NPs, whether crosslinked or not, must swell to accommodate the enzyme solution. It is critical that the solution generate significant swelling in order to create an environment in which the enzyme can efficiently interact with the polymer for destruction [119, 194–196]. A study by Grenha et al. [197] looked at how chitosan NPs with an unknown molecular weight and 86% DDA that had been crosslinked with TPP broke down. The NPs were mixed with lysozyme (0.2 mg/mL, the highest concentration found in human tracheobronchial secretions) in PBS (pH 7.4) and left to sit at 37 °C. The size of the NPs significantly decreased within the first 5 min, and a high enzyme concentration accelerated this degradation process. In a recent study, Poth et al. [198] investigated the process of chitosan breakdown in vitro with acetylation levels ranging from 17 to 42%. Some lysozyme (1.5 μg/mL) and chitosan (42% DDA) were mixed with TPP NPs in a 37 °C solution. Over the course of four days, the TPP NPs lost 40% of their particle size. In contrast, even after 7 days, the size of the chitosan (17% DDA)-TPP NPs remained constant. Over 7 days, increasing lysozyme concentration from 1.5 μg/mL to 150 μg/mL resulted in a 60% reduction in particle size for chitosan (42% DDA)-TPP NPs. Even though chitosan (42% DDA)-TPP NPs were broken down a lot, the enzyme concentration of 150 εg/mL was deemed too high, and no further testing was done after seven days. Kean and Theanu's analysis [199] offers additional insights into the breakdown of chitosan and its derivatives, along with the detrimental effects of the resulting degradation products. We need to conduct extensive research to predict the breakdown of chitosan NPs cross-linked with either TPP or glutaraldehyde, both in the lab and in living organisms. More in vivo research is needed to completely understand the real deterioration of chitosan, identify degradation byproducts, and evaluate their potential toxicity inside the human body.

Rationale behind targeting the folate receptor to treat cancer

FRα is often upregulated in several types of cancer, such as ovarian, breast, lung, and kidney malignancies [200, 201]. In contrast, its expression is generally modest in normal tissues. This differential expression enables a more focused approach, perhaps reducing harm to normal, healthy cells [200, 201]. Cells acquire folate, a vital nutrient for the synthesis and repair of DNA, via folate receptor [201, 202]. Cancer cells, which require an increased amount of folate for quick proliferation and expansion, have a tendency to excessively produce FRα [51, 201]. The folate cycle supports vital metabolic activities and is required for rapidly proliferating cells. The reduced folate carrier (RFC) primarily transports water-soluble B vitamins, known as exogenous reduced folates, into cells under normal physiological settings. The body expresses the RFC, a low-affinity, high-capacity transporter, which operates through a bidirectional anion-exchange process. Within the cell, folates play a critical role in the production of purines and thymidine, which are required for DNA synthesis, methylation, and repair. High-affinity FRs also carry folates. There are four isoforms of the FR (FR&αgr;, FR&βgr;, FRγ, and FR&δgr;) in humans [201, 203–205]. FR&αgr, FR&βgr, and FR&δgr are connected to the cell's outer surface by a glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor, while FRγ is a protein that is released from the cell [201, 206, 207]. A recent study revealed that the FOLR4 gene produces a protein that is incapable of binding to FA or decreased folate. As a result, researchers decided to rename the FR&δgr; as Juno [201, 207]. RME takes the complex formed between the cell surface-receptor and the ligand inside the cell after FA or reduced folate attaches to the FR [201, 203] (Fig. 7). Table 2 displays the distribution of FR isoforms in both normal and malignant tissue, [48, 49] [201, 205–210]. In non-malignant situations, certain polarized epithelia such as the uterus, placenta, choroid plexus, lung, and kidney confine the distribution of FR&αgr; tissue. The expression of FR&αgr; is localized to the apical/luminal surface of polarized cells, preventing interaction with the circulation [201, 203, 204, 206], On the other hand, FR&αgr; is highly expressed in most ovarian, uterine, and ependymal brain tumors, as well as malignant pleural mesotheliomas. Lung, kidney, breast, and colon carcinomas also express it to varying degrees [48, 49, 201, 205–210]. While talking about cancer, it's important to note that FR&αgr; loses its polarized cellular position and FR&αgr; proteins cover the whole surface of the cell. The precise impact of FR&αgr expression on tumors remains uncertain; however, it may include the regulation of folate absorption from the bloodstream [201, 211] or the initiation of signals linked to tumor proliferation [201, 212]. Several studies, both in the lab (in vitro) and on living things (in vivo), have found a link between too much FR production and the progression of ovarian cancer in humans [201, 213–215]. Moreover, studies conducted in live mice with tumor xenografts that express human FR&αgr; have shown that monoclonal antibodies targeting FR&αgr; effectively inhibit tumor development [201, 216]. In addition, the FR&αgr; expression may also promote treatment resistance by increasing the anti-apoptotic capacity of cancer cells [201, 217]. Furthermore, there is data indicating that the overexpression of FR&αgr; remains unchanged during chemotherapy in ovarian and endometrial cancer [201, 218, 219]. This may serve as an indicator for resistance to traditional chemotherapy in ovarian cancer [201, 217, 220]. Only certain types of tumors contain FR, making it a potential predictive biomarker for FR-targeted therapy and a valuable tool for individual patient treatment customization. One of the difficulties in assessing and confirming cancer biomarkers is creating suitable, strong, and feasible measuring tests [221]. Several semiquantitative and quantitative procedures have been used to evaluate FR&αgr; levels in tumor biopsy samples or in the bloodstream. Nevertheless, these methods have inherent difficulties, especially in a clinical environment [217, 222–228] (Table 3).

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles in low-dose oral metronomic chemotherapy

Patients receiving maximum tolerated dose (MTD) therapy for cancer treatment receive the maximum amount of chemotherapy their bodies can withstand (Fig. 8). Although the primary goal of this method is to optimize cancer cell eradication, it frequently necessitates the inclusion of chemobreaks, which are intervals of rest between treatment cycles. These breaks are essential to facilitate the patient's recovery from the detrimental and poisonous side effects of the therapy [139, 230, 231]. Consequently, the efficacy of MTD treatment may decline with time, posing difficulties in disease management and restricting long-term survival prospects. Oral low-dose metronomic chemotherapy is a new option for cancer treatment that is being considered as an alternative to the classic MTD therapy. Metronomic chemotherapy, on the other hand, involves giving small amounts of chemotherapeutic drugs on a regular basis without long breaks. This is different from MTD, which involves giving large amounts of chemotherapy followed by rest periods. This treatment protocol may sustain the necessary level of anticancer medications in the bloodstream, which triggers a response to cancer and hinders the formation of new blood vessels, while also averting the emergence of drug resistance [139, 230, 231]. Notably, even at low concentrations, chemotherapeutic agents may exert strong anticancer effects when administered directly to tumor cells [232]. By attaching specifically to FRs that are overexpressed on cancer cells, folate-anchoring can improve the precise delivery of drug-loaded chitosan NPs. This results in significant anticancer efficacy, even at lower drug doses [54]. Xue et al. formulated chitosan-based NPs of Bay 41–4109 to make the drug stay in the bloodstream longer. The study was done on rats, and the results showed that chitosan NPs had a 3.3-fold higher Cmax, an increase in AUC, and a fourfold increase in absolute bioavailability compared to the Bay 41–4109 solution. In the NPs, the better interaction between the positive charge of chitosan and the negative charge of cell membranes led to increased medication absorption in the intestines. This attribute resulted in an extended duration of drug release in the small intestine. Enoxaparin, which has a very limited capacity to be absorbed orally, was combined with chitosan NPs (Enx-Alg-CS-NPs). This resulted in a threefold increase in the area under the curve (AUC) for enoxaparin when taken orally at a dose of 50 mg/kg in rats. The oral dose had an AUC 20% higher than the intravenous dose of 1 mg/kg. The polymer's ability to adhere to the mucus lining was responsible for the increased absorption of the medication in the rats' intestines [98–100]. Several comparable investigations have shown the PK superiority of chitosan NPs, compared to other polymeric NPs. Notably, low-dose oral metronomic chemotherapy is a therapeutic method that includes giving chemotherapy drugs at much lower dosages repetitively over a long period of time and without long gaps between treatment cycles (Fig. 9) [233]. Through the manipulation of outer surface of chitosan NPs with folate ligands, it becomes feasible to transport substances to certain cells or tissues with precision. Notably, vitamin B12 and folate are found in food as coenzymatically active forms that are bonded to protein. These forms need to be released via processing or digestion before they can be absorbed. Folate is mostly absorbed by active transport in the proximal portion of the small intestine. A glycoprotein called castle's gastric intrinsic factor binds to vitamin B12 and transports it to receptor sites on the brush edge of the ileal mucosal cell. It adheres to these sites when there is calcium and an alkaline pH present, and is subsequently absorbed. Aside from the significant physiological processes of active absorption, a minor portion of ingested folate and B12 is also absorbed passively via diffusion. When ingested, the digestive tract contains FRs, which can enhance the bioavailability of anticancer drug-containing folate anchored-chitosan NPs by boosting their uptake through these receptors [234, 235]. Furthermore, the gastrointestinal tract struggles to absorb many poorly soluble drugs, in this regard, chitosan NPs have the capacity to improve the solubility of drugs that are not easily soluble by enclosing, dispersing, or forming complexes with them [236]. This facilitates the gastrointestinal system's absorption of the pharmaceuticals, thereby enhancing their bioavailability. In addition, chitosan NPs possess mucoadhesive characteristics, enabling them to stick to the mucosal lining of the gastrointestinal system. This adhesion extends the duration of contact between the NPs containing the medicine and the cells lining the intestine, enhancing the drug's absorption [237]. Next, chitosan NPs can disrupt tight junctions in the gut lining, resulting in increase in drug permeation between these cells [238]. Nevertheless, encapsulating drugs inside chitosan NPs protects them from breaking down in the stomach's harsh acidic environment as well as during enzyme breakdown in the digestive tract [239]. This safeguard ensures a higher percentage of the medication remains intact until it reaches the gut's absorption sites, thereby increasing its overall bioavailability. Formulation scientists may also design them to deliver medications gradually over an extended period. Nevertheless, the sustained release profile of the anticancer drug ensures that therapeutic drug levels remain in circulation for an extended period, therefore prolonging the absorption process and enhancing the medicine's effectiveness [109]. However, oral drugs undergo first-pass metabolism in the liver before entering the systemic circulation. Chitosan NPs may partially circumvent this metabolic process by encapsulating the drug into its core, thereby increasing their bioavailability. Notably, folate-anchored chitosan NPs could also be highly beneficial to treat colorectal cancers with overly expressed folate receptor, and there are existing technologies like enteric coating that can precisely modulate it release at colon [51].

Applications of Folate-engineered chitosan nanoparticles in cancer therapy

Folate engineered nanoparticles for breast cancer therapy

5-Florouracil is often used in cancer therapy as a thymidylate synthase inhibitor. Nonetheless, its efficacy in cancer treatment is limited by poor absorption and off-target side effects [241]. Targeted delivery of 5-FU using carefully designed nano-carriers might increase its therapeutic potential while reducing negative effects [242]. Akinyelu and colleagues improved the cytotoxic efficiency of 5-FU against liver and breast cancer cells by encapsulating it in gold NPs (GNP) and functionalizing it with chitosan and FA-conjugated chitosan [243]. The kinetic examination of drug release revealed that the primary mechanism of drug release at both physiological and acidic pH levels was zero order. This notion was recognized as a suitable method for continuous and constant drug delivery, potentially prolonging the effects of pharmaceuticals contained in polymer matrices. The cell toxicity of HepG2, MCF-7, and HEK293 cell lines was assessed using the MTT assay. Free 5-FU, CH@G5-FU-NPs (chitosan-linked gold-fluorouracil NPs), and FCH@G5-FU-NPs (chitosan folate-linked gold-fluorouracil NPs) all increased cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells, however, the effect was concentration dependent. At all dosages examined, FCH@G5-FU-NPs outperformed CH@G5-FU-NPs in terms of cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells. The addition of folate to the carrier may boost particle adherence to MCF-7 cells via FRα present in the surfaces. Subsequently, this interaction might cause folate-RME endocytosis, leading to enhanced internalization of FCH@G5-FU-NPs. In another set of data, CH@G5-FU-NPs and FCH@G5-FU-NPs achieved 74% and 79% encapsulation efficiencies, respectively. NP tracking analysis evaluated the particle size of G-NPs, CH@G5-FU-NPs, and FCH@G5-FU-NPs, revealing a size range of roughly 65.9 nm for G-NPs, 126 nm for CHG5-FU-NPs, and 144 nm for FCHG5-FU-NPs, and was bigger than that observed in TEM analysis. The investigation of cell apoptosis showed that MCF-7 cells treated with FCHG5-FU-NPs had the greatest apoptotic index, followed by a substantial drop in apoptosis levels after folate pre-treatment in the competitive test. This revealed that MCF-7 cells absorbed FCHG5-FU-NPs more effectively, most likely due to the presence of FRs on their membranes. These findings indicated that CHG5-FU-NPs and FCHG5-FU-NPs have significant therapeutic potential for cancer treatment [243] (Fig. 10, Table 4).

Curcumin is very efficient against numerous kinds of malignant cells, including breast carcinoma. Curcumin primarily achieves its anticancer properties by inhibiting the cell cycle and inducing apoptosis in malignant cells. Curcumin's low solubility in aqueous environments hinders its widespread use in cancer therapy. Curcumin has other problems, such as a rapid metabolism and low bioavailability. Esfandiarpour-Boroujeni and his colleagues created folate-customized chitosan NPs loaded with curcumin via a self-assembling technique [244] (Table 4). The FTIR results revealed the presence of curcumin in the particles encapsulated within the chitosan core. They also showed that there were electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions between the molecules of curcumin and the folate-modified chitosan. The NPs produced spherical forms without aggregation, indicating a highly uniform size distribution, according to the FE-SEM examination. They used the DLS to measure zeta potential and saw that the NPs in the water had a negative charge. This demonstrated that the hydroxyl groups in the chitosan were mostly outside of the NPs. After treating L929 and MCF-7 cells with curcumin-loaded, folate-anchored chitosan NPs and free curcumin, they conducted a cell cytotoxicity investigation. Curcumin-loaded chitosan NPs and free curcumin treatment of MCF-7 cells made them less viable as the time of incubation went up. The release investigation revealed a faster release of curcumin from NPs in acidic pH conditions compared to neutral pH conditions. This occurs because, in an acidic environment, the amine group of chitosan undergoes protonation, causing expansion of the polymer matrix. The rapid drug extraction from NPs at acidic pH indicated the NPs pH-responsive nature. NPs conjugated with curcumin and folate-modified chitosan were found to be very useful for targeted treatment because they could only deliver curcumin, specifically to cancer cells [244].

Because of its vasodilating properties, China widely uses ligusticum (LZ), a powerful component of ligusticum [268]. Recent research suggests that LZ not only inhibits tumor metastasis, but also increases the sensitivity of multidrug-resistant (MDR) tumor cells to chemotherapeutic treatments [269]. Modifying chitosan with substances such as folate and arginylglycylaspartic acid polypeptides effectively functions as a receptor-mediated targeted DDS. FA anchored-chitosan (FA-CH) NPs incorporating anticancer drugs increased the cellular accumulation of therapeutic compounds such as gemcitabine and doxorubicin inside FR-positive tumor cells, including HeLa, B16F1, and other cancer cell types. Cheng and colleagues created a new, cost-effective NP-based DDS loaded with LZ to selectively target tumor metastasis and overcome MDR during cancer therapy. An amino-acylation process generated the functional groups of FA-CH, which infrared spectroscopy detected. The appearance of discrete peaks in the HNMR spectrum confirmed the effective conjugation of folate and chitosan. In vitro release tests revealed that the rate of LZ release from the NPs increased as the pH of the dissolving media decreased. This suggested that the release of LZ from FA-CS-LZ-NPs could be apparently more in the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment. When MCF-7 and A549 cells were exposed to FA-CH-LZ-NPs, CH-LZ-NPs, or LZ alone, the cells treated with FA-CH-LZ-NPs absorbed significantly more LZ than those treated with CH-LZ-NPs or LZ solution alone. These data indicated that FA-CH-LZ-NPs successfully targeted and delivered LZ into FR-positive MCF-7 cells. In addition, the authors conducted extensive research on the absorption of LZ within MCF-7 and A549 cells, utilizing a confocal microscope. In MCF-7 cells, FA-CH-LZ-NPs enhanced the LZ fluorescence. In terms of LZ uptake, FA-CH-LZ-NPs outperformed CH-LZ-NPs, and LZ solution, in A549 cells. The MTT test revealed that FA–CH NPs had no significant harmful effects even at doses of 1 mg/mL. Thus, FA-CH-LZ-NPs may serve as a safe method for treating MDR in cancer treatment. This research found that FA-CH-LZ-NPs as a beneficial way to target FR-positive cancer cells, making them a possible approach for overcoming MDR [245].

Methotrexate (MTX), a very effective chemotherapy medicine, treats a variety of malignancies. It cuts down on the tetrahydrofolates that can be used to make pyrimidines and purines when MTX binds strongly to dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) [270, 271]. On the other hand, researchers report that the incorporation of Fe3O4-NPs into chitosan prevents aggregation, protects its surface from oxidation, and provides colloidal stability; thereby improving blood plasma circulation time and lowering RES clearance. Ali and colleagues combined MTX with Fe3O4-NPs using chitosan, and then treated MCF-7 with the former to improve MTX transport and targeting performance. The chitosan-Fe3O4 and MTX@chitosan-Fe3O4 NPs (MTX NPs) measured between 61 and 150 nm, respectively. The MTX NPs adhered to the RME and subsequently emerged as endosomes within the target cells. Subsequently, these endosomes fused with lysosomes that contains proteases, which then cut the peptide link between the NPs and MTX. The cytotoxicity of MTX/CH@Fe3O4 NPs was then investigated and shown to be time dependent in MCF-7 cell lines. It demonstrated that the delivery strategy using chitosan-coated iron oxide NPs for MTX was much more effective on MCF-7 cells than free MTX. In another study, the LDH results indicated that MTX/chitosan-Fe3O4-NPs caused much more membrane damage to MCF-7 cells than free MTX. Further, they assessed the apoptotic properties of the MTX delivery method using an inverted microscope on MCF-7 cells. Overall, the study showed that chitosan conjugation with iron NPs was a beneficial way to deliver MTX due to its biocompatible and biodegradable properties [272].

Microbubbles (MBs), which are gas-filled particles with specific acoustic qualities, are ideal for using ultrasound to direct gene and medication delivery. Abdipour et al. created a novel form of polymeric MBs with a shell composed of polyethylene glycol, PLGA, FA, and chitosan [246] (Table 4). Furthermore, they filled it with gold NPs and incorporated a perfluorohexane core. Additionally, the researchers added MTX to the MBs. Using FTIR analysis, researchers were able to determine and confirm the chemical structure of PLGA-chitosan when combined with PEG and FA. Next, researchers used the MTT test to assess the toxic effects of MTX, MTX-nano-droplets (NDs)-FA, MTX-NDs (nano-droplets), and NDs-FA on cancer cells. All of these samples had the same amount of MTX in them. At all concentrations, free MTX and NDs-FA displayed the lowest cytotoxicity, whereas MTX-NDs-FA had the maximum cytotoxicity. They attribute the findings to the enhanced internalization of MTX due to receptor-mediated opsonization. Next, ultrasonic irradiation validated the active targeting capacity of NDs-FA, followed by flow cytometry analysis. Overall, the polymeric MBs showed great biocompatibility and cellular uptake, suggesting that they are safe and effective when interacting with cells [246].

Using the ionic gelation method, Geetha Kumari and her team tailored cytarabine-loaded FC@CHNPs (folate-modified chitosan NPs) [247] (Table 4). TEM imaging indicated that the particles were nanosized, measuring roughly 50 nm in diameter. The zeta potential value of the FC@CHNPs showed that the particles were positively charged, which kept them from sticking together and suggested a stable nanosuspension. They investigated the release of cytarabine from FC@CHNPs in the lab at pH levels of 6.4 and 7.4, analogous to an acidic tumor and a neutral blood environment. The NPs-cytarabine release kinetics were biphasic, with an initial rapid release followed by a slower release. Afterwards, the anticancer activity of FC@CNP was evaluated using MTT assays on A549 and MCF-7 cell lines. After 72 h of treatment with FC@CNP, MCF-7 cell viability decreased more compared to cytarabine alone. Cell viability deteriorated in a dose-dependent way. In addition, treatment with FC@CNPs resulted in a four-fold drop in bcl2 levels compared to cytarabine alone. This suggests that apoptosis started. The FC@CNP treatment increased the levels of bax, cyt c, and cas9 in MCF-7 cells, which shows that the mitochondrial-dependent intrinsic apoptotic pathway was activated [247].

Yang and his team made a folate-modified, chitosan-coated liposome (FC@CL) that was specifically made to target tumor cells while drugs were being delivered [248] (Table 4). They changed the chitosan (CH) chemically by adding folate, and then they used FTIR and NMR to study the FA-CH conjugates that were made. Afterward, the authors investigated the fluorescein release patterns from liposomes, chitosan-coated liposomes (CCLs), and folate–chitosan-coated liposomes (FCCLs) in vitro using the dialysis technique in PBS. After 24 h, liposomes released approximately 65.57% of fluorescein, while CCLs and FC@CLs released 55.22% and 55.76%, respectively. The chitosan coating on plain liposomes greatly prolonged the release of fluorescein. The extended-release profile of liposomes coated with FA-CH was similar. Next, the investigator evaluated the stability of liposomes and FC@CLs using TEM. When held at 4 ºC, the liposomes remained globular with an estimated size of 200 nm. Overall, the research found that FC@CLs were a potential nanocarrier for targeted medication delivery to tumors [248].

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for lung cancer therapy

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancer forms, accounting for a large proportion of death rates in both primary and metastatic neoplasms [249, 273] (Table 4). Wang and his colleagues studied how well chitosan NPs loaded with gemcitabine (GEM) could fight cancer. The formulation used FA as a targeting agent [250]. Characterization based on particle size showed that adding PEG and FA to the surface of NPs made the particles bigger in GEM@NPs, which confirms that PEG and FA work together to bind. Next, team studied the absorption of NPs by A549 cancer cells and observed a time-dependent trend across all formulations. Cell absorption increased up to 2 h, then plateaued, indicating probable cell saturation. Next, the investigator assessed the cytotoxic reaction using the MTT test to determine the effectiveness of plain GEM, PEG-GEM NPs, and FA-PEG@GEM NPs. At all concentrations investigated, the results indicated that FA-PEG@GEM-NPs were more cytotoxic than both PEG-GEM-NPs and plain GEM. The fact that FA-PEG@GEM-NPs killed more cells may be attributed to the receptor-ligand interaction between FA receptors on the cell surface and FA ligands on the NP surface, which makes it easier for the cells to contact the NPs an invaginated it via RME. They demonstrated that adding FA to the surface of PEG-GEM@NPs increased cell uptake significantly compared to NPs (PEG-GEM@NPs) that did not have FA. At last, the researchers concluded that NPs with appropriate surface modifications may transport bioactive chemicals selectively to tumor locations while limiting possible negative effects [250].

The objective of the work done by Singh and his team was to develop chitosan-folate conjugated multi-walled carbon nanotubes for the purpose of delivering docetaxel specifically to lung cancer cells [251, 273, 274]. Singh et al. synthesized and confirmed the chitosan-folate conjugate using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. Next, analysis was conducted on the multi-walled carbon nanotubes to characterize the NPs and their in vitro release. In order to evaluate the effectiveness of the formulations, the authors conducted laboratory experiments to study the absorption of docetaxel/coumarin-6 loaded multi-walled carbon nanotubes by cells, as well as their toxicity and impact on the cell cycle. The safety and biocompatibility of chitosan-folate conjugated multi-walled carbon nanotubes were evaluated using lung histopathology, in comparison to commercially available docetaxel formulations (Docel™) and acylated nanotubes. The study on cellular internalization showed that lung cancer cells efficiently took in chitosan-folate conjugated multi-walled carbon nanotubes by a process called folate-RME. The IC50 values demonstrated that chitosan-folate conjugated multi-walled carbon nanotubes were 89-fold more effective than Docel™ in inhibiting the growth of A549 cell) [251] (Table 4).

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for ovarian cancer therapy

PNIPAAm, the most extensively studied thermoresponsive polymer, has a lower critical solution temperature. DDSs that use PNIPAAm can effectively and precisely release trapped drug molecules in response to regulated temperature changes [275]. Fathi and team created a distinctive nanosystem based on chitosan (CH) that has the ability to respond to changes in temperature and pH [276]. The nanosystem has a magnetic core that serves as a theranostic agent for delivering erlotinib (ETB), a kind of drug that inhibits tyrosine kinase. Furthermore, they bound MTX to the surface of NPs to specifically target FR-positive ovarian cancer cells. FE-SEM–EDX, which stands for field emission scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, was used to study the structure of MTX-CHC@MNPs. The SEM image of MTX-CHC@MNPs displayed a circular shape with a sleek surface and a size range of 20–60 nm. The MTX-CHC@MNPs satisfactorily enclosed ETB. They determined the encapsulation efficiency to be approximately 86%, and measured the drug loading to be approximately 17%. ETB molecules showed a faster release rate at pH 5.5 as compared to pH 7.4. The protonation of NH2 functional groups in the CH chain may be responsible for the observed phenomenon. This protonation could cause the NPs to swell more, aiding in drug release. Following this, the team assessed the cytotoxicity of different doses of CHC@MNPs, MTX-CHC@MNPs, ETB-loaded MTX-CHC@MNPs, and ETB alone in OVCAR-3 cell lines using the MTT assay. Both MTX-CHC@MNPs loaded with ETB and free ETB (administered at the same dosages) exhibited significantly greater cytotoxic effects compared to CHC@MNPs and MTX-CHC@MNPs. The cell uptake data demonstrated that the combination of MTX-CHC@MNPs was much more efficacious compared to CHC@MNPs alone. This showed that coating the NPs with MTX might potentially enhance the cellular uptake by FR-expressing OVCAR-3 cells. Researchers suggested MTX-CHC@MNPs as a promising intelligent nanocarrier for the specific therapy of FR-positive solid tumors [276].

The USFDA has authorized ETB for the treatment of pancreatic cancer and advanced metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [252] (Table 4). Fathi et al. developed thermosensitive O-maleoyl-modified chitosan micellar NPs. They created these NPs with the goal of delivering ETB to specific sites using FA [252]. The hydroxyl groups of chitosan (CH) changed into maleic anhydride when they were mixed with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). This approach yielded a polymerizable chitosan precursor. The team then made it better by adding NIPAAm to make it more sensitive to temperature and using oleic acid (OA) as a monomer that doesn't react with water. FTIR and NMR spectroscopy validated the successful integration of all functional groups. The zeta potential and particle size of the folate-(PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CH micelle were determined. According to the findings, the micelle had an average particle size of around 63 nm and a zeta potential of -9.8 mV. TEM investigated the morphology of the micelles loaded with FA, revealing their predominant spherical shape. Investigators then treated folate-positive OVCAR-3 cells and folate-negative A549 cells with varying concentrations of ETB alone, (PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CS, folate-(PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CS, and ETB-loaded micelles. The (PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CS and folate-(PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CS NPs had no detectable negative effects on any cell line, demonstrating that the micelles are biocompatible. The folate-(PNIPAAm-co-OA)-g-CS micelles loaded with ETB displayed much higher cytotoxicity than unbound ETB molecules. Certain cells may internalize targeted NPs via RME, resulting in an increase in the number of drug molecules inside the cells. This suggests that folate-modified micelles may be effectively and quickly delivered into particular tumor cells via FRs [252].

Ince et al. made the FA@TQCHNPs molecule, which is made up of radioiodinated FA and thymoquinone (TQ)- incorporated chitosan [253] (Table 4). They then tested it on human ovarian cancer cells to see how it would affect the cells. The average particle size and zeta potential for CHNPs and FA@TQCHNPs were 252 ± 45.88 nm and 344.5 ± 28.64 nm, respectively. SEM images of the synthesized NPs revealed that FA@TQCHNPs had a variety of surface shapes. Most were rod-shaped, but some were also spherical. However, the authors conducted toxicity experiments on several cell lines using five different doses of TQ, TQCS, and FA@TQCS. The results showed that the IC50 values for FA@TQCS were around twice as high as those for TQ and TQCS in SKOV-3 cells. Caco-2 cells treated for 48 h showed IC50 values of 14.66 μg/mL for TQ, 13.99 μg/mL for TQCS, and 13.82 μg/mL for FA@TQCS. SKOV3 cell lines absorbed 131I-FATQCSNPs more readily than 131I-TQ. Next, the binding affinity and specificity of 131I-TQ and 131I-FATQCSNPs were assessed in SKOV3 and Caco-2 cells, demonstrating that 131I-FATQCSNPs had a high affinity for ovarian cells. Nevertheless, FA-linked thymoquinone-chitosan NPs were also shown to have targeted activity on ovarian cell lines in inhibitory experiments [253].

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for prostate cancer therapy

Prostate cancer primarily uses bicalutamide (BCL) as an antineoplastic hormonal agent. This is a nonsteroidal, pure anti-androgen with a particular affinity for androgen receptors. PLGA has many benefits, such as being biodegradable, biocompatible, and simple to break down into its monomer, which lets the body get rid of it quickly through normal metabolic processes. Dhas and colleagues developed FA-conjugated CH-functionalized PLGA NPs (CPN) for efficient BCL administration [254] (Table 4). They compared the characteristics of FA-conjugated and CH-functionalized PLGA NPs (CPN) with those of non-functionalized PLGA NPs (UPN). Next, the investigator confirmed the FA-CH linkage using DSC thermograms, UV absorption spectra, and FTIR spectra. Both optimized BCL-loaded UPN and CPN showed a cumulative proportion of BCL drug releases. The drug release was sluggish, with just 21.82 ± 1.13% of the dose released after 72 h. The slower drug release might be due to the presence of the FA-CS coating, which most likely impedes the drug release mechanism. The cell proliferation test results showed that both NPs and pure BCL had dose-dependent cytotoxicity. The placebo NPs (both CPN and UPN) had no harmful effects on the DU-145 prostate cell lines, suggesting that the produced NPs are safe for human usage. BCL-loaded UPN and CPN showed promising suppression of cell proliferation, with efficiency changing depending on the dose. It's possible that FA conjugation, which makes the NPs better at binding to FRs, is what makes CPN work better than UPN. The cell proliferation test results supported FA-conjugated BCL-loaded CPN's improved effectiveness and cytotoxic effects compared to UPN and pure BCL. It was clear that the made NPs did not interact with erythrocytes because the BCL-loaded UPN and CPN samples did not break down any erythrocytes. In conclusion, FA-CH-functionalized PLGA NPs have benefits over UPN [254].

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for cervical cancer therapy

Sahu and colleagues have developed a new technique for cancer-specific medication delivery using folate-decorated succinylchitosan NPs for improving delivery of doxorubicin (Dox) [256, 267, 277, 278] (Table 4). Investigators linked succinic anhydride to chitosan via amide bonds. The NP sizes were determined using TEM and DLS methods. Research demonstrates that an increase in pH leads to an increase in the average size of NPs. At different pH levels, the remaining amino groups throughout the chitosan chain are protonated and dispersed, which affects their behavior. In contrast, the lack of protonation at higher pH values restricts particle dispersion. After that, they used FESEM to look at the surface shape of both folate-decorated succinyl chitosan NPs and succinyl chitosan. At higher resolution levels, the particles modified with FA revealed a spherical, smooth surface. The MTT assay tested the cytotoxicity of the NPs in HeLa cells and found that cell viability remained unchanged up to a concentration of 60 µg/ml. Furthermore, Dox-loaded NPs were evaluated, and FA-modified NPs had a greater cytotoxic impact on HeLa cells. As time progressed, researchers observed an increased quantity of NPs within the cytoplasm, indicating that cells internalized them instead of merely adhering to their surfaces. A study of the cell cycle showed that Dox-loaded NPs stopped the growth of HeLa cells by halting cell-cycle progression in the G2/M phase. Additionally, this targeted delivery approach may provide a technique for tumor cell identification and labeling applications [267].

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for colon cancer therapy

Soe and his team incorporated a hydrophobic drug, bortezomib (Bor), into FA-conjugated chondroitin sulfate and chitosan (Cs/Chs) and produced Cs/Chs self-assembled NPs (Bor/Cs/Chs-FA) for colorectal cancer therapy [257, 279, 280] (Table 4). TEM was used to determine the structure of CH-Chs NPs, Bor@CH-Chs, and Bor@CH-Chs-FA. The NPs had small particle sizes (about 200 nm) and appeared as distinct, separate globular particles. The release rate of Bor from Bor@CH-Chs and Bor@CH-Chs-FA was measured using a dialysis membrane at pH 7.4 and pH 5.0. Bor release from both Bor@CH-Chs and Bor@CH-Chs -FA increased significantly at acidic pH levels compared to physiological pH. Furthermore, the release rate from Bor@CH-Chs-FA was somewhat slower than that of Bor@CH-Chs. Next, Bor@CH-Chs-FA was tested for cell toxicity in colorectal cancer cell lines expressing FRs (HCT-116 and HT-29) as well as lung cancer cell lines lacking in folate receptors (A549). The absorption of targeted NPs was dependent on folate-RME. They also checked whether the folate ligand was involved in the process of cellular uptake in A549 cells, which didn't show a big difference in uptake with or without FA pre-treatment. Next, the mice model of tumor-bearing xenograft HCT-116 was constructed to investigate Bor@CH-Chs-FA's anticancer activity against colorectal carcinoma in vivo. Bor@CH-Chs-FA inhibited tumor progression by early DNA fragmentation associated with apoptosis, which increased its anticancer effectiveness against folate receptor-overexpressing colorectal carcinoma. The research concluded that the formulated formulations had good potential as therapy options for FR-overexpressing colorectal tumors [257] (Figs. 11 and 12).

Colon cancer is a substantial source of death and morbidity globally. 5-FU is well-known an anti-metabolite agent because it has a structure that is similar to pyrimidines and uses the same metabolic pathways as uracil and thymine [258, 281] (Table 4). Their mechanism of action reveals that 5-FU impairs nucleoside metabolism in both RNA and DNA, and it serves as a treatment for several types of cancer. The bioavailability of 5-FU after oral treatment varies greatly due to uneven absorption in the GI tract and early liver metabolism. Ullah and his colleagues formed NPs by using to chitosan-FA conjugate and incorporated 5-FU via ionic gelation method [258, 282]. Carbodiimide chemistry produced the FA-CH conjugates, and the FTIR and 1H-NMR spectra confirmed the successful conjugation of FA into chitosan molecules. The CH-5-FU NPs measured 208 nm, whereas the FA-CH-5-FU NPs measured 235 nm, respectively. These NPs small size is significant because they can easily enter the permeable tumor vasculature and aggregate inside the tumor site, producing lethal effects on growing cells. The cell toxicity investigation was carried out using Caco-2 cell lines. The IC50 value for CH@5FU NPs was 3.43 µg/mL, whereas FA-CH@5-FU-NPs was 2.67 µg/mL. The encapsulation of 5-FU inside NPs significantly enhanced its cytotoxicity compared to the free drug. The increased cytotoxicity may be attributed to the drug's synergistic activity, as well as the increased hydrophilicity imparted by the FA-CS NPs, which was assisted by the addition of folate. This most likely improved the 5-FU release rate from the NPs [258, 282].

Hesperetin, also known as 5,7,3′-trihydroxy-4′-methoxy flavanone, is a potent bioflavonoid that has demonstrated the ability to inhibit cell growth both in vivo and in vitro, effectively targeting a wide range of cancer types [259] (Table 4). Lazer and his team designed chitosan-FA conjugated, doublecortin-like kinase 1 (DCLK1) functionalized and hesperetin incorporated NPs (CFH-DCLK1 NPs), to specifically target cancer stem cells (CSCs) [259]. During the ionic gelation process, hesperetin was encapsulated in chitosan-FA NPs (CFH). The investigator validated the conjugation of chitosan and FA with DCLK1 using 1H-NMR analysis. The effect of CFH and CFH-DCLK1 NPs on colon CSC targeting was investigated in vitro. The researchers used immunofluorescence to assess DCLK1 levels in HCT116 colon cancer cells before and after injecting CFH and CFH-DCLK1 NPs. This proved that DCLK1 is a good target for getting rid of colon CSCs because it stops signaling for epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which stops cancer cells from spreading and moving. In colon cancer cells, the AO/EtBr staining technique measures apoptotic cell death. The plate treated with CFH-DCLK1 NPs had a larger number of apoptotic cells than the one treated with CFH NPs, resulting in a greater presence of orange-coloured bodies. The invasion experiment showed that CFH and CFH-DCLK1 NPs made cell invasion much less likely than CFH NPs alone and the control group. To quantify apoptosis, researchers used the caspase 3/7 apoptosis test and compared treated plates to controls. The findings showed that CFH NPs caused caspase-3/7 cell death three times faster than the control cells. A real-time RT-PCR test exhibited that cells treated with CFH-DCLK1 and CFH NPs had lower levels of STAT1, NOTCH1, and DCLK1 genes than non-treated cells. Additional investigations showed that CFH-DCLK1 NPs are better at stopping the expression of the NOTCH1, DCLK1, and STAT1 genes [259] (Fig. 13).

Folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles for pancreatic cancer therapy

Pancreatic cancer is a common clinical malignancy among humans. Patients with advanced pancreatic cancer generally have a 3–6 month survival rate, with a less than 5% likelihood of surviving beyond 5 years [283]. Zhou and his team used the coaxial electrospray method to make core–shell NPs that contained folate-chitosan-gemcitabine (FA-CH-Gem) [260] (Table 4). The FA acted as targeted ligands, and chitosan was used to deliver the medication to the tumor. Pancreatic cancer cells primarily exhibit FR on their membranes, while its presence in the cytoplasm is minimal. Notably, COLO357 has the greatest FR expression among the six pancreatic cancer cell lines, hence it was used to confirm the targeting ability of FA-CH-Gem NPs. The results showed that FA-CH-Gem NPs inhibited COLO357 cells much more than CH-Gem NPs or Gem alone. Furthermore, the efficiency of FA-CH-Gem NPs in cell killing was determined by both Gem dosage and exposure time, revealing a time- and dose-dependent connection in pancreatic cancer. Overall, the development of FA-CH-Gem core–shell NPs has allowed accurate, targeted treatment for FR-positive pancreatic cancer, representing a major step forward in the area. These NPs have the ability to improve treatment results, decrease side effects, and limit injury to healthy tissues, holding enormous promise for improving the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients [260].

Trials in progress for folate receptor-targeted therapy and chitosan-based nanoparticles

Because of its high expression in a considerable percentage of tumors, FRs has become a well-known biomarker for tumor cells in some types of cancer. Several diagnostic and therapeutic devices have used FRs to precisely target and scan cancer cells [51]. This section outlines and summarizes the many methods used to achieve this goal in clinical trials, as well as the results obtained after doing so (Table 5). One method for efficiently targeting the tumor is to mix folate with powerful chemotherapeutic medicines to create folate-small molecule-drug conjugates (FA-SMDCs) that target the FRs efficiently [47] (Fig. 14). Other options include FRs-targeting antibodies (either alone or in combination with medications), as well as nanotechnology-folate platforms to supplement these efforts. According to pre-clinical studies, using FRs for targeted therapy or diagnostic purposes has the potential to fundamentally transform the approach to treating many malignancies. These methods, which preferentially target the FRs receptor, might contribute to the development of more complex drug conjugates [47]. Various complementary technologies, such as tiny molecules, NPs, and protein-based approaches, target the FRs, making this particularly significant. On the other hand, the USFDA has allowed the use of chitosan as a biomaterial, but not for therapeutic purposes. This is mostly due to concerns regarding its source, purity, and propensity to provoke an immune response. Because of their diverse uses, clinical investigations into chitosan-based materials and products are currently underway. In this section, we have examined and reviewed FRs-targeted clinical studies for cancer treatment and results, as well as chitosan-based NPs in clinical trials for treating various disorders [70] (Tables 5 and 6).

Challenges and limitations associated with folate engineered chitosan nanoparticles

The production procedure for folate-engineered chitosan NPs poses several challenges and limits. These aspects may be influencing scalability, cost efficiency, and overall product quality. Quality control parameters such as consistent size, surface charge, and folate density over several batches might be difficult to achieve, and variability could have an impact on the efficacy and safety of the NPs [301]. In particular, approaches that work well in a laboratory setting may not be immediately applicable in large-scale industrial operations. Furthermore, raising batch sizes to commercial levels could present new issues, such as changes in mixing effectiveness or temperature control, which could influence the properties of the produced NPs [302]. Chitosan's properties and features might differ depending on its source, such as shrimp or fungus. Differences in molecular weight and deacetylation levels can also influence the physical and chemical properties of NPs [59]. On the other hand, the efficacy and consistency of folate attachment to chitosan are vital for obtaining maximum targeted efficiency. Folate attachment, which is shockingly inconsistent, could explain why targeting abilities aren't always consistent and therapeutic efficacy is low or non-existent in clinical trials. The manufacturing process may include many phases, including chitosan extraction, NP synthesis, folate conjugation, and drug loading. Each process needs thorough monitoring and adjustment to ensure the quality of the outcome. Next, chitosan NPs are potentially sensitive to environmental conditions such as pH and temperature. The instability during storage may jeopardize both the structural integrity of the NPs and the stability of the drugs contained inside them. The NPs require specific storage conditions, such as low temperatures and an inert atmosphere, to maintain their stability over time, and they must undergo accelerated stability testing for optimization [303]. Chitosan is normally biocompatible, but the chemical changes needed for folate conjugation could make it less biocompatible [59]. Because of this, it is important to do a full study on the long-term toxicity of these changed and/or customized NPs. Furthermore, the immune system may perceive modified NPs as antigens, resulting in an immunological response, and this phenomenon could shorten their length of circulation and potency. Apart from these, the size and surface properties of the particles could influence the pace of medication release and their capacity to target particular locations [304, 305]. It may be difficult to achieve a significant and uniform drug loading and encapsulating capacity while keeping the NPs stable. Subsequently, insufficient drug loading may require a greater amount of chitosan, which could prove to be inconvenient and result in systemic toxicities. We must regulate the rate of drug release from the NPs satisfactorily to ensure therapy efficacy. Premature drug release, also known as burst release, can diminish the effectiveness of the treatment and cause severe responses. Furthermore, FR targeting relies on upregulating FRs in certain cancer cells. However, the expression levels of FRs may vary depending on the patient, leading to varying levels of targeting efficiency. Notably, reports indicate that non-specific interactions between NPs and other cells or tissues decrease the concentration of NPs that reach the target location, thereby reducing therapeutic effectiveness and necessitating an increase in dosing frequency. To address these difficulties, a multidisciplinary approach is required, including the integration of knowledge from materials science, chemistry, engineering, pharmacology, and regulatory bodies. It is still possible to overcome these obstacles and achieve substantial progress in the commercial manufacturing of folate-modified chitosan NPs by performing constant research and development and developing strong collaboration between industry and academia.

Conclusion

Although folate-engineered chitosan NPs have enormous promise for a variety of applications, including medicine delivery, cancer treatment, and imaging, they have severe restrictions that prevent broad commercial usage. Chitosan, like any other biomaterial, has a number of problems, including the potential for instability under certain conditions, which may result in aggregate formation or precipitation. Next, correctly controlling the size, arrangement, and surface properties of chitosan NPs may be tricky. To solve this, optimization is often required in synthesis methods to consistently create NPs with the desired properties. Although chitosan NPs have the potential to be drug delivery carriers, adequately and properly anchoring targeting ligands, optimizing drug loading and controlling release kinetics could be problematic. Therefore, it is essential to systematically examine parameters such as the drug's solubility, NP stability, and interactions with chitosan. Dose-dependent toxicity, immunological reactions, and clearance processes are all possible impediments to chitosan NPs efficacy in targeted medication delivery or imaging. Nevertheless, scaling up chitosan NP production from the laboratory to large-scale is a daunting job owing to the requirement to assure uniform quality across batches, cost efficiency, and the potential to expand production capacity. Thus, comprehensively characterizing chitosan NPs is critical for understanding their physicochemical properties and improving their therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, past research focused mainly on material synthesis and assessment in laboratory settings. Despite these drawbacks, FA-chitosan-based nanocomposites have significant promise in biomedicine and cancer treatment. As a result, we should make greater efforts to solve chitosan's limitations and perform thorough research into its myriad possible biological applications and harmful consequences.
