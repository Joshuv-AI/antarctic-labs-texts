Acute otitis media complicated by peripheral facial nerve palsy in an adult: A case report
Authors: Ayesha Afzal, Muhammad Owais, Muhammad Umair Manzoor, Amina Asif, Rahique Shuaib, Muhammad Usman Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute otitis media (AOM) is a common infection, whereas peripheral facial nerve palsy is an uncommon extracranial complication in the post-antibiotic era. We report the case of a 53-year-old immunocompetent man who developed acute suppurative otitis media complicated by ipsilateral peripheral facial nerve palsy following an upper respiratory tract infection. The illness began with sore throat and cough during Hajj and progressed to severe right-sided otalgia, followed by complete right lower motor neuron facial weakness despite sequential empirical oral antibiotics. On return to Pakistan, otoscopy demonstrated a bulging, pulsating opaque tympanic membrane. Laboratory investigations revealed neutrophilic leukocytosis with normal glycated hemoglobin. Computed tomography of the temporal bones showed acute right oto-mastoiditis with middle ear opacification, an air-fluid level within the mastoid, and subtle possible erosive change along the lateral facial nerve canal without intracranial extension. The patient was treated conservatively with intravenous ceftriaxone, oral corticosteroids, and early structured facial physiotherapy. Facial nerve function improved by approximately 70% within 10 days and recovered completely by day 25 without surgical intervention. This case highlights the importance of recognizing facial nerve palsy as a rare but potentially reversible complication of AOM. It also emphasizes the increased burden of upper respiratory tract infections during mass gatherings and the need for early otologic and neurological evaluation in pilgrims presenting with persistent otalgia or new facial asymmetry to facilitate timely treatment and favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Peripheral facial nerve palsy is a rare but potentially serious complication of acute otitis media (AOM) in the post-antibiotic era, most commonly attributed to inflammatory oedema within the facial canal causing neuropraxial compression and localized ischemia, with canal-wall osteitis, bacterial toxin-mediated neuritis, vascular thrombosis, and congenital canal dehiscence proposed as additional mechanisms [1,2]. Although AOM-associated facial palsy is well documented in adult and pediatric populations [[2], [3], [4]], the distinctive temporal progression from upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) to AOM and subsequently severe facial nerve palsy remains insufficiently described. Mass gatherings are associated with a high burden of URTI because of crowding, prolonged close contact, shared accommodation, and physical demands [[5], [6], [7]], with a 2024 analysis of more than 37,000 outpatient encounters reporting URTI as the most common diagnosis (44.6% overall; 46.3% in Mena) [[6], [7], [8]]. We present an unusual case of severe House–Brackmann grade V facial palsy following AOM, with subtle possible erosion of the lateral facial nerve canal on CT, achieving approximately 70% recovery by day 10 and complete recovery by day 25 with medical management alone. This case highlights the importance of early recognition, prompt otological assessment and imaging, and the potential for complete recovery despite severe initial facial nerve dysfunction [1,2,9,10].

Case presentation

In May 2026, a 53-year-old man with a background of hypertension and no history of diabetes mellitus travelled from Pakistan with his family to perform Hajj. Several family members developed sore throats early in the trip, suggesting a shared upper respiratory tract infection. His initial symptoms (sore throat and dry cough) were mild and managed with paracetamol and antihistamines.

Over the following days his cough became productive, though he remained able to complete the core Hajj rituals. On 3 June 2026, he developed severe right-sided otalgia. There was an episode of vertigo, but no hearing loss. He was assessed at a local medical centre and treated symptomatically with paracetamol, ibuprofen, and antihistamines.

His symptoms did not improve. Empirical oral cefixime was started for the worsening productive cough; azithromycin was added three days later. He then developed pain on chewing on the right side, followed by visible facial asymmetry. Oral amoxicillin-clavulanate was substituted while he awaited return travel to Pakistan.

On arrival in Pakistan (10 June 2026), examination showed deviation of the angle of the mouth to the left and incomplete closure of the right eye. He was referred immediately for otolaryngological assessment.

Examination findings

Otoscopy showed a markedly bulging and pulsating right tympanic membrane, consistent with acute suppurative otitis media (Fig. 1). Facial nerve examination showed complete lower motor neuron palsy of the entire right side of the face, graded House-Brackmann V, and Sunnybrook Facial Grading System score was 12 [11,12].Fig. 1Oto-endoscopic images of the right tympanic membrane at presentation, showing a bulging, opaque tympanic membrane with an underlying lobulated soft tissue density, consistent with acute suppurative otitis media.

Laboratory investigations

Complete blood count showed a neutrophilic leukocytosis: white cell counts 15,630/mm³ (reference 4000–10,000), neutrophils 81% (reference 40–80%), absolute neutrophil count 12,660/mm³ , and platelet count 499,000/mm³ (reference 150,000–425,000). Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was 5.6% (non-diabetic range), essentially unchanged from a value of 5.1% recorded in October 2023, confirming the absence of underlying diabetes as a contributing risk factor for facial nerve involvement.

Microbiology

No middle ear aspirate, throat, or sputum culture was obtained, because empirical oral antibiotics (cefixime, then azithromycin, then amoxicillin-clavulanate) had already been initiated sequentially abroad before formal otolaryngological assessment was possible. Once systemic antimicrobial therapy has been started, subsequent culture of middle ear fluid or respiratory secretions is of substantially reduced diagnostic yield, and no fluid was available for sampling at the time of ENT assessment in Pakistan. Consequently, no causative organism was microbiologically confirmed in this case, a limitation this report shares with several published series, in which bacteriological confirmation was similarly obtained in only a minority of patients [9,10].

Imaging

Non-contrast computed tomography of the temporal bones (bone and soft tissue windows) showed opacification of the right mastoid air cells extending into the right middle ear cavity, with an air-fluid level in the right mastoid (Fig. 2). A soft tissue density, lobulated bulge was present at the expected location of the right tympanic membrane. Right middle ear ossicles were intact. Few areas of rarefaction were noted in the tegmen tympani, and subtle erosive change along the lateral aspect of the right facial nerve canal could not be entirely excluded. The sigmoid plate and scutum were intact, and the right inner ear (bony cochlea and semicircular canals) was normal. There was no evidence of intracranial extension. The left temporal bone was normally pneumatized with no abnormality. Diagnosis

Acute right suppurative otitis media with oto-mastoiditis, complicated by ipsilateral peripheral facial nerve palsy (House-Brackmann grade V), in the setting of a preceding upper respiratory tract infection.

Differential diagnosis

Given the acute, unilateral, lower motor neuron pattern of facial weakness occurring in direct temporal association with radiologically confirmed suppurative otitis media, an otogenic cause was considered the most probable explanation. Alternative causes of acute peripheral facial palsy were considered and regarded as less likely: idiopathic Bell's palsy was considered less likely given the clear otoscopic and radiological evidence of concurrent suppurative middle ear disease; Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus) was not supported by the absence of vesicular eruption in the external auditory canal, pinna, or oropharynx; cholesteatoma-associated palsy could not be entirely excluded on imaging alone, as the CT differential for the soft tissue density included both granulation tissue and cholesteatoma, though the acute presentation and rapid resolution with medical therapy alone favored an inflammatory/granulation tissue process; and malignant (necrotizing) otitis externa was considered highly unlikely in a normoglycemic, immunocompetent patient without external canal granulation tissue. Intracranial complications (subdural empyema, sigmoid sinus thrombosis, meningitis) and skull base malignancy were excluded on imaging and clinical grounds.

Treatment

The patient was treated with intravenous ceftriaxone for ten days, oral prednisolone (Deltacortil) at a high starting dose with a gradual taper, an oral antihistamine (bilastine), an oral nasal decongestant preparation, topical xylometazoline nasal drops, and analgesics. Structured facial rehabilitation was started early, comprising approximately 15 min of electrical muscle stimulation followed by supervised facial muscle exercises in each clinic session, supplemented by home exercises performed at least five times daily.

Follow-up and outcome

Repeat otoscopy five days after starting definitive treatment showed a marked reduction in tympanic membrane bulging. By day 10, facial nerve function had improved by approximately 70%; corticosteroids were then tapered, intravenous ceftriaxone was stopped, and a further five-day course of oral amoxicillin-clavulanate was given. By day 25 from the start of definitive treatment, the patient had achieved complete resolution of facial weakness, with normal eye closure, normal oral competence, and full facial symmetry, without myringotomy or any other surgical intervention. Discussion

Peripheral facial nerve palsy remains a rare complication of acute otitis media (AOM) in the post-antibiotic era. Its pathogenesis is primarily attributed to inflammatory oedema within the facial canal causing neuropraxial compression and localized ischemia; other proposed mechanisms include canal-wall osteitis, bacterial toxin-mediated neuritis, vascular thrombosis, and direct extension through a congenitally dehiscent canal [[2], [3], [4]]. In our patient, CT demonstrated a possible subtle erosive change along the lateral facial nerve canal, which may indicate localized osteitis or dehiscence contributing to nerve involvement, although this finding was subtle and could not be definitively confirmed.

Table 1 compares the present case with published AOM-associated facial palsy series. An adult series of 11 patients reported HB grades III–V, bacteriological confirmation in only 1/11 cases, and treatment with parenteral antibiotics plus corticosteroids in all except one patient with diabetes [2]. A mixed pediatric/adult series of 40 patients reported sudden-onset palsy in 95%, predominantly HB grades III–IV, with 90% demonstrating an infra-geniculate lesion and bacterial growth in fewer than half of cultured cases [9]. A systematic review of 15 pediatric studies involving 120 patients reported a mean initial HB grade of 4.68 and complete recovery in 88.5% [1]. The principal novelty of this case is the distinctive clinical sequence of URTI, followed by AOM and subsequently severe peripheral facial nerve palsy, occurring in the setting of a prolonged mass-gathering exposure. Although AOM-associated facial palsy has been described in adult and pediatric populations [[1], [2], [3],9,10,13], to our knowledge, this temporal progression has not been explicitly reported in this particular clinical context. Mass gatherings are associated with a high burden of URTI because of crowding, prolonged close contact, shared accommodation, and physical demands [[5], [6], [7]]. A 2024 analysis of more than 37,000 outpatient encounters at the holy sites identified URTI as the most common diagnosis, accounting for 44.6% of presentations overall and 46.3% in Mina [8]. Cough and sore throat are also frequently reported [6]. Viral URTI may cause Eustachian tube dysfunction, predisposing to secondary bacterial AOM and, rarely, facial nerve involvement. Thus, this case highlights an unusual progression from a common respiratory illness to a rare otological-neurological complication.

Another important learning point was the delayed recognition of facial nerve involvement, during which sequential empirical oral antibiotics were administered without early definitive otological assessment or imaging. Current management includes prompt intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics, systemic corticosteroids, analgesia, treatment of associated nasal or sinus pathology, and close otological monitoring [2,10,14]. Imaging is important to exclude mastoiditis, cholesteatoma, bony erosion, and intracranial extension. Surgical decompression, with or without myringotomy or mastoidectomy, is generally reserved for progressive neurological deterioration, significant radiological nerve compression, or failure of medical therapy [9,14].

The rapid improvement by day 10 and complete recovery by day 25 without surgery, despite an initial HB grade V palsy, further demonstrates the potential for excellent neurological recovery with medical management in appropriately selected patients [1,2,9,10]. Overall, this case contributes to the limited literature by documenting an unusual URTI → AOM → severe facial nerve palsy sequence, highlighting the importance of early recognition and imaging of facial weakness complicating AOM, and demonstrating complete recovery with conservative treatment despite severe initial neurological involvement.

Limitations. This report has several limitations characteristic of real-world case reporting. No middle ear aspirate or culture was obtained, because sequential empirical antibiotics had already been commenced abroad before formal ENT assessment could take place, and the causative organism therefore remains unidentified, a limitation shared with several published series [2,10]. Formal pure tone audiometry and electroneurography/nerve conduction studies were not performed, which would otherwise have allowed more precise quantification of hearing status and neurophysiological prognostication. As with any single case report, causal inference between the URTI and the subsequent otologic and neurological complications cannot be established with certainty, and the findings should be interpreted as a plausible clinical association rather than proof of causation.

Learning points

1.Peripheral facial nerve palsy is an uncommon but clinically important complication of acute otitis media in adults.2.New facial asymmetry in a patient with worsening otalgia warrants urgent otolaryngological referral and evaluation for facial nerve involvement.3.Cross-sectional imaging helps to exclude mastoiditis, cholesteatoma, bony erosion of the facial canal, and intracranial complications, and should be pursued early.4.High-dose intravenous antibiotics and systemic corticosteroids remain the mainstay of medical management.5.Early, structured facial physiotherapy appears to support functional recovery, although its independent contribution cannot be isolated from that of medical therapy in a single case.6.Complete recovery without surgical intervention is achievable with prompt multidisciplinary care, consistent with outcomes reported across published adult and pediatric series.

Patient perspective

The patient described the period of facial weakness as emotionally and physically distressing, occurring at a spiritually significant time during his pilgrimage. He expressed gratitude for achieving full recovery of facial function without residual deficit or the need for surgical intervention and reported satisfaction with the multidisciplinary care received after returning home.
