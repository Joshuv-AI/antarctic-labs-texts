Providing individualised care to high-risk women in the intrapartum period – an interpretive descriptive study
Authors: Mary Curtin, Patricia Leahy-Warren, John Goodwin, Michelle O’Driscoll, Margaret Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch has demonstrated the benefits of providing individualised care tailored to the specific needs of women during labour and birth, a key tenet of Respectful Maternity Care. To achieve this care, support from midwives and obstetricians is essential. There is limited evidence on how women classified as high-risk can receive personalised care during labour and birth.AimThis study aimed to explore how healthcare professionals provide individualised care to high-risk women during labour and birth.MethodsUsing an interpretive descriptive approach, midwives and obstetricians (n = 12) took part in semi-structured, online interviews. Data were analysed using a constant comparative method using open, axial and selective coding.FindingsParticipants articulated a conflict between the clinical responsibility to one woman whilst simultaneously being part of an institution that was required to ensure the safe birth of multiple women. Standardised processes of the institution, such as guidelines, were invoked to care for multiple women. To provide individualised care, participants articulated the need to know the woman in advance of labour, and this increased their willingness to facilitate women’s wishes.ConclusionInstitutional processes can impede the provision of individualised care to high-risk women, particularly if the HCP has not been afforded the opportunity to know the woman prior to labour and birth. Further research is required to understand the nuances of seniority and professional status which may increase facilitation of individualised care requests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09369-7.Keywords: Birth, High-Risk Pregnancy, Midwife, Obstetrician, Qualitative

What is already known

There are benefits to providing individualised care to women during labour and birth. A difficult experience of birth can impact the postnatal period of women and their babies

What this paper adds

Midwives and Obstetricians may have conflicts between the institutional standards and the woman’s individual wishes. There may be individual characteristics of midwives and obstetricians that increase the chance of women’s individualised care requests being materialised

Introduction

The presence of individualised care has previously been recognised in the literature for its ability to ensure women feel special and unique on their own pregnancy journey [28]. Although the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) has not limited access to continuity of care models for women who are considered high-risk, this cohort are more likely to be omitted from this model of care, resulting in a scarcity of continuity of care research for this cohort [14]. This reduces high-risk women’s ability to access care that is individualised and provides relational continuity. Yet, women classified as high-risk are more likely to experience increased levels of distress and negative emotions than women who experience ‘normal pregnancies’ and so may be a group who benefit most from a model of care that is individualised to their specific needs [24, 32].

Humanised practice, which places the physical and psychological elements of birth on equal footing has been identified as a concept that all women can and should achieve, and includes individualised care as a foundational pillar [8]. Humanised practice, which originated in Brazil, is closely aligned to the premise of Respectful Maternity Care [44]. However, humanised practice has been seen to diminish in times of increased clinical activity, identified in the literature as institutional momentum, reducing the veracity of Respectful Maternity Care in practice [9, 25]. Individualised care has been defined by NICE [26] as ‘care and treatment tailored to needs and preferences’. However, this implies individualisation is attainable on the woman’s wishes only yet evidence suggests women’s wishes are not always met in childbirth [5]. This suggests there may be a wider context of external factors requiring consideration in addition to the woman’s health context such as staff skills, competence or the environment that may also contribute to decision made in the clinical setting.

In Ireland, maternity care is mainly obstetric led with a mix of public and private services available depending on availability [41]. The National Maternity Strategy, published by the Department of Health [10] maps out a plan for safe, standardised and high-quality care and has classified women into three risk groups; normal-risk, medium-risk and high-risk. This places all women under the umbrella of risk and moves away from recognising a physiological normality of childbirth or acknowledging that some woman may not have any risk factors at the time of booking. A recent study analysing caesarean section rates in Ireland’s four largest maternity hospitals from 2015–2021 found an increasing caesarean section rate providing further evidence of increased complexity and the growing label of ‘high risk’ in labour and birth [19]. Therefore, there is a need to understand how care is individualised for women who are classified as high-risk in the intrapartum period who birth within a hospital setting. The aim of this study was to explore how midwives and obstetricians provide individualised care to high-risk women in the intrapartum period.

Participants, ethics and methods

A qualitative design was considered most appropriate and aligned with the aim of exploring subjective experiences using an inductive approach. Interpretive description was chosen because of the flexibility it provides to explore complex phenomena that may have implications for clinical practice [34, 35]. Interpretive description is a set of logical steps that reside within qualitative research design and provide the researcher with the opportunity to ‘borrow’ from other conventional approaches. Although it provides flexibility to the researcher within qualitative research design, it does not support the removal of either disciplinary or theoretical underpinnings [17, 34, 37]. The combination of concurrent data collection and analysis and constant comparative analysis are considered central elements of an interpretive descriptive study ([38]; pg. 85). Purposive and snowball sampling were adopted to maximise the likelihood that participants were able to provide in depth contributions [6, 30]. In line with an interpretive descriptive approach, a sample size of 12 was chosen in advance [37, 42]. Three elements were used to support this decision; recommendations of qualitative methodologies, sample sizes of other research with the same research design, and an internal justification [22]. Evidence suggests that an adequate sample size may be within 6–16 participants [42]. Guest et al. [15] suggested a sample of 12 was adequate based on findings that 92% of codes were identified at the 12th interview. Of interest, an interpretive descriptive approach does not propose a sample size, and acknowledges that a small sample may be appropriate to build meaningful accounts of the phenomena of interest [34]. For interpretive descriptive approaches in particular, the premise is that data will never be ‘saturated’ as there will always be another individual experience that has not been accounted for [31, 36]. A poster was developed to advertise the study and sent to Directors of Midwifery in all 19 maternity units in Ireland. The poster was also uploaded to social media platforms to aid recruitment. Inclusion criteria were midwives or obstetricians registered to practice in Ireland, with at least two years’ experience and who currently worked on a labour ward and had experience working with women who were classified as high-risk in the intrapartum period. The parameters of ‘high risk’ were not defined for the participants, to ensure that the experience of the ‘label’ was being explored rather than any specific condition. Potential participants were contacted by the researcher to confirm they met the inclusion criteria and if the participant met the criteria, they were asked to complete a written consent form and a short demographic survey (See Table 1). Semi-structured interviews were conducted online, using an interview guide (Additional file 1), audio and video recorded. Verbatim transcription was completed and data were analysed using a constant comparative analysis and open, axial and selective coding [7]. Constant comparison was applied from participant to participant in the order they were interviewed. Open coding assigns labels to the data to reduce the large amount of data to codes. Axial coding begins to capture the data in a more abstract fashion and moves the data from descriptive to interpretive [34]. Data were analysed using pen and paper to create codes, to increase spontaneity and avoid overanalytical thinking and then entered into NVivo 20 (release 1.7.2) to manage the data. Memos were written after each interview, when a category was formed and if any thoughts of interest arose in the analysis, providing an audit trail. This also created a pathway for reflexivity. Recruitment took place between January and May 2024. Ethical approval was granted for this study by the Social Research Ethics Committee in August 2023 (Log number 2023–178) and is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines and regulations set out by the University. The SRQR reporting guideline was used to draft this manuscript and the SRQR reporting checking has been used and included in additional file 2 [27].

Reflexivity

To reduce the impact of inherent researchers bias, reflexivity is an integral component of qualitative research [29]. This work contributed to the fulfilment of a doctoral degree by MC. MC, MM and PL-W are registered midwives. JG is a registered nurse and MO’D is a clinical pharmacist. All authors work in higher education. To improve critical thinking and analysis, all authors met at regular intervals, and the first author was given feedback throughout the data collection and analytical process. MC maintained a reflexive diary as part of the analytical processes which was revisited at regular intervals.

Findings

Twelve participants were recruited to the study, comprising 8 midwives, 3 consultant obstetricians and 1 non-consultant hospital doctor (NCHD). Most participants were qualified between 11–15 years (n = 5). Four participants (n = 4) had worked on the labour ward for 5 years or less. Six participants were educated to bachelor’s level, five to master’s level and one to doctorate level (see Table 1).

The overarching selective code: Intrapartum positionality – Humanising high-risk birth describes the multitude of variables that contribute to formation of the participants clinical viewpoint. The positionality of the intrapartum caregiver was seen to be integral to influence the direction of intrapartum care. Intrapartum positionality influenced the participants’ willingness to facilitate women’s wishes and provide individualised care in labour and birth.

The selective code was synthesized from two axial codes ‘The needs of the individual Vs the needs of many’ and ‘The hats worn by the healthcare professional’.

‘The needs of the individual Vs the needs of many’ exhibited the conflict that was experienced by healthcare professionals between their clinical responsibility to one woman whilst simultaneously being a part of an institution that was required to ensure the safe birth of multiple women. To provide care for the needs of many, two sub-themes were identified ‘The standard process’ and ‘They come with the rules in their head’. In contrast, to achieve individualised care for women in high-risk birth, the themes identified were ‘knowing the woman in advance of labour’ and ‘The moving line’. The second axial code ‘The hats worn by the healthcare professional’ articulates how the personal attitudes and feelings of a participant contribute to their ability to practice individualised care. The findings of this study are related to the first axial code only as although the two axial codes together formed the selective code and therefore interact they are also conceptually different. ‘The needs of the Individual Vs The needs of the many’ articulate the extrinsic factors that influence the provision of individualised care for high-risk women in the intrapartum period. In contrast, the second axial code ‘The hat’s worn by the healthcare professional’ relate directly to intrinsic factors at play and will be submitted separately for publication.

The standard process

The standard process was the route used by the institution to enact care for high-risk women in labour. Standardising processes included ‘planning ahead’ from the antenatal period preferably, and ‘The guidelines are there for a reason’. The latter created differences between midwives and obstetricians in their approach to the management of intrapartum care.

Planning ahead

Planning ahead ensured, where possible, that unexpected events in the intrapartum period, were avoided and this was an important aspect of providing care to high-risk women. Women were most often identified as high-risk in their pregnancy, so they carried this status with them when they were admitted to the labour ward. This was considered ‘easier’ by participants as it created thinking space for them and this preparation time was thought to result in the care running more smoothly, even if an emergency ensued:

I feel the ones who have been identified high-risk during pregnancy. I find it so much easier because I have time to like, think and there is already, like the doctors already know, everyone, the hospital knows they are coming, and we are ready. We have everything set up for them and then even if there is any emergency, everything comes like smoothly because we know this is what was going to happen. (P8)

It appeared that being categorised as high-risk in pregnancy was also beneficial for inter and intra-professional communications as early acknowledgment of risk meant the obstetric team would have met the woman in the antenatal period. This may contribute to care being considered as ‘easier’ for participants in this study. Regardless, on arrival to the labour ward women required an assessment of their risks and their potential outcomes.

And I suppose affirm the nature of the risk and why it's high-risk, what are the anticipated positive and negative outcomes? What are our goals, I suppose on the labour ward for this woman and for us? And then also as well as the risk, So, what can we anticipate that might go wrong? (P2)

Although positive and negative outcomes were articulated, participants often reiterated the need to anticipate what might go wrong, rather than what might go right. Being busy and feeling useful was often seen when plans were enacted from standardised processes within the institutions, that resulted in tasks being completed regularly. However, participants considered the tasks to be prioritised above everything else which created a lack of flexibility in the intrapartum period, and once the care plan had been set out, it was often perceived to be difficult to deviate from the plan of care. This suggests a lack of flexibility in the standardised processes the participants were expected to follow:

And she's [the woman] supposed to be the decision-maker, but I think a lot of times she doesn't really get that, you know, when once they’re deemed high-risk, they're kind of I suppose assumed to kind of go along with the, the care that has been decided for them. (P4)

This is the plan that's set out for you. (P6)

Practising outside a high-risk setting may play a role in being able to individualise woman’s care requests and being able to alter plans and deviate from standardised processes within the institution. In the quote, the participant, with over 15 years’ experience on the labour ward, was able to use their experience to create flexibility for the woman to ensure that the woman’s voice was prioritised in planning care. Conversely, participant 1 expresses the opinion that a lack of experience may increase fear resulting in participants staying within the boundaries of what is acceptable within the institutions by staying within the agreed processes already established:

So, if a woman doesn't particularly want continuous monitoring or she doesn't want it for a particular period of her labour or whatever. I mean, I will very often adjust my planning around that, I'll clearly discuss with her about what the guidance says, discuss with her what we're after but you know, from using my experience and all the rest of it, actually adjust and say well look we don't have to do continuous monitoring at this point in time. And we put in a different plan that's agreeable with her, for instance. (P12)

As a junior, … They know their stuff. They probably know the guidelines and the evidence better than anyone else, but they're just like there is that fear element, isn't there because you don't know, and you don't have that experience. (P1)

This suggests that perceived rigidity or inflexibility in clinical practice may also be a result of a lack of confidence in clinical decision making or a lack of experience in their professional role which supersedes the knowledge of guidelines and evidence.

The guidelines are there for a reason

Guidelines were used as a boundary for accepted practice but how they were enacted differed between participants in the study. Participants used guidelines to enact the standard processes in the institution, which had the additional benefit of the intrapartum team having an implicit understanding the care plan as it was an established process. Participants suggested guidelines taught HCPs how to ‘toe the line’. Toeing the line implies an obedience to remain on the right side and within the acceptable boundaries of an institutional approach to care. Participant 2 (P2) had substantial experience (11–15 years) on the labour ward and so, did not support a rigid approach to guidelines and instead considered guidelines as a framework to follow the rules, suggesting seniority may be relevant in whether HCPs must ‘toe the line’.

I think they're great to be able to guide your practice, but I don't think they (guidelines) should be dogmatic, and I think sometimes there can be uh, disagreements between different care providers about, you're breaking:/You're going outside of guidelines or cause I suppose they’re a guide on how to ‘toe the line’. (P2)

There was consensus from participants that being a junior member of staff meant that you did have to follow the guidelines, suggesting guidelines were often perceived dogmatic in practice, depending on the status and seniority of the participant within the institution:

You have to follow your guideline or your protocol, but then sometimes women are totally different. (P8)

I’d feel pressure from management to … to do what the hospital wants, to follow the policy. (P4)

To feel the need to align to the guideline or protocol even when recognising it might not be in the best interests of the woman, suggests the prioritisation of the standard process, potentially ahead of the wishes of the woman. One reason guidelines may be followed, even when they did not meet women’s needs, were because they were perceived to be the best practice by some participants. Although Participant 1 uses an absolute (‘100%’) to express their certainty that guidelines are necessary, this regresses to ‘I suppose’ further in the quote suggesting the participant is less sure than first thought. Further, they also question their own statement with the use of ‘aren’t they?’:

Yeah, 100%. The guidelines are there for a reason, aren't they? And people will say they're only guidelines [smile, pause] but, but they are there for a reason, and I suppose the guidelines are best practice and [shrug] well, it's both their documented as best practice and gold standard practice. (P1)

In contrast, senior participants did not perceive guidelines to be gold standard and instead suggested they required interpretation and adaption to meet women’s individualised care needs. Participant 3 was very experienced on the labour ward (> 15 years) and challenged the strength of evidence available, reiterating the need to respect the woman’s wishes for her birth, supporting the previous suggestion that experienced participants may be able to prioritise the woman’s wishes in labour more successfully. If so, there may be more subjectivity to the interpretation and adaptation of guidelines than first considered.

I suppose I would just reemphasize that like guidance is just, is guidance and so you don't have to, it's not like you must do this at all times. It's a guide and it's you know you can interpret it differently and as long as you feel that you're not compromising the care, like the safety of the woman and you’re respecting her wishes, I think it's appropriate to do so. Like it is a, it's a conversation. It's like, it's not just a black or white. Yes, I mean, there are guidelines, and they have particular reasons for that which are underpinned by evidence. But obviously there's varying strength of evidence and, you know, and as kind of clinical situations evolve, you can sometimes, you know, make safe and appropriate choices that are respecting kind of the woman's concerns and, you know, still providing safe care. (P3)

Participants in this study reported that their colleagues were influential in their decision making, using their colleagues counsel for guidance, particularly when women were considering going outside of the guidelines. However, that did not mean participants did not feel liable in the event of a negative outcome. Articulating the response through gritted teeth in the quote below implies the participant (P6) had split loyalties, knowing the information is correct but being less willing to accept it in practice. This participant was also concerned regarding the assignment of blame and their accountability if they supported women’s individualised care requests that did not go to plan. Participant 6 had the least labour ward experience (< 5 years) reiterating a potential connection between a lack of clinical experience and willingness to practice outside of the guideline:

There's a midwife who does a lot of the VBAC [Vaginal birth after caesarean section] clinics and you know, I would often go to her and say, well, the guideline says, you know, vaginal examination every two hours and she kind of sit down and says, [name], it's a guideline. And I would, that now would kind of make me go, OK, but [said through gritted teeth] if something were to happen I, you know, so she, you know, she would be very much say it's a guideline only, but at the same time, in which I fully understand, and I, obviously it is. But at the same time, like if you know, I don't want to be outside of a guideline and be liable for something, you know, like I it would, it's a very, it's kind of a fine line. (P6)

They come with the rules in their head

The second sub-theme is derived from participants’ articulation of how women’s expectations of their birth were managed through a road map that guided women through what would happen to them. Preparation for birth was important for women in the antenatal period and when required, in the intrapartum period. However, some participants suggested that preparation may also be perceived by women as mandatory instructions.

Preparation for birth

Preparation for birth was considered vital for women who were high risk in the intrapartum period. Women with preexisting underlying medical conditions were thought to be requiring specialist preparation because of historical advice they might have received about being pregnant:

Just a kind of preparation and having the information available is key regardless of what's going on, because I think that sometimes, for antenatal care or if people are coming into, if they're having a baby and they've got an underlying medical condition that they've had for their life, they may be, you know, coming in having a baby is going to be a very important thing to them, but they may have always been told they know you have to be careful if you're pregnant. (P3)

‘Being careful’ suggests that women are in danger and may result in increasing women’s being fear of pregnancy and birth. Although women may require additional preparation for labour and birth, some participants thought women were more prepared for birth than in previous generations. Although, the participant below (P1) gave certainty in the statement (‘definitely’), there is also a caveat in the quote above ‘for the most part’ suggesting there may be times that women’s preparation is not of benefit to the participants:

Women are definitely more educated and prepared for labour and birth than we were 20–30 years ago, which is really cool for the for the most part [smiles]. (P1)

Women with little knowledge required information but women who were well prepared with knowledge were sometimes considered a problem also. Women with knowledge may use their birth plan to document their wishes but this may not have been always well received by the intrapartum team.

We (HCPs) still haven't got to a place where we actually are so excited. Here's the one with a massive birth plan. She's all this knowledge in her head. Let's work with her. She's just a problem. (P10)

The birth plan was often the tool used by the participant to support preparation which usually entailed a discussion in the antenatal period to ensure that women could have a conversation in a more relaxed environment. Some participants did not consider these conversations in the intrapartum period to be appropriate due to the potentially highly stressful nature of the labour ward. Some participants expressed concerns that women’s expectations were unrealistic. This may limit women’s input to their birth, whilst maintaining the standardised processes of what is considered acceptable within the institutions:

And then they come in with these unrealistic expectations that haven't been discussed, maybe in clinic or they just haven't got them at all and then trying to explain them in a way they understand in this highly stressful environment. (P9)

Discussion in the antenatal period may be perceived as instructions, and it was suggested women were being prepared by receiving instructions for their labour and birth. Therefore, making alteration to a woman’s preparation and ‘pulling it back’ may suggest a change of what was expected by the woman. Although there is some reference to gaining women’s consent (‘if that’s ok with you’), changing the plan could create inevitable disruption to a woman’s preparation for labour, who may have been told something antenatally, that is no longer considered necessary in the intrapartum period:

OK, this is the way. This is the way it's done. You are continuously monitored. You have an epidural. We are actively involved in the birth of your, you know, as in an obstetrician will be there. But I suppose what I would try and pull it back. If somebody lands (arrives in) to me like, you know, I say absolutely, we do need to monitor your baby continuously if that's OK with you … So, you know, she, you know, the woman can come in thinking. “OK, well, this all has to happen. I have been told I'm not allowed to eat and drink in labour”. You know what I mean? They nearly come with the list of the rules in their head. (P11)

The subjectivity of some of the participants’ approaches to care can also be seen in the consideration of birth plans for labour. It was suggested that some participants disagreed with and mocked women’s plans for their birth, making a choice not to enact them. In contrast, other participants acknowledged the birth plan but considered it to be agreed if it had already been discussed:

The staff, who see the birth plans and go “oh Jesus” or “oh, look at this, look at this. Look what she wants” are not going to do them. Yeah. (P9)

I suppose, antenatal class at the moment is there and they are kind of encouraging women to, to make birth preferences and discuss them antenatally so, if they're already, if they've already been discussed, I don't discuss them again because I don't think there's a point, So they've already been discussed. I'll acknowledge them and say that hey, like seen your birth preferences, read through them. Great. Let's go for it. (P4)

Knowing the woman in advance of labour

The sub-theme ‘knowing women in advance of labour’ was generated from an ideal world which doesn’t exist, building a relationship in labour and she’s not just saying something frivolous.

An ideal world which doesn’t exist

Some participants understood how they would provide individualised care for women and to do this they tried to consider the needs of the individual woman. Placing the woman at the centre, ensured the woman was considered as an individual instead of as cohort of women who have similarity. The preference Participant 2 outlined for care provision was ‘an ideal world’ that support individualised care:

Yeah, I mean, I suppose that when you're thinking about that for individualised care, you are thinking about this woman, not just women like this. (P3)

I suppose, in an ideal world which doesn't exist [smiles] having kind of named midwives or named obstetricians taking care of women and if that could continue through the peripartum period it would be great. (P2)

‘An ideal world’ suggests a reality that is not possible to achieve, yet this was considered the most appropriate way to individualise care for women by several participants. In an ideal world, HCPs would be known to women (‘named’) and would continue throughout the pregnancy and birth journey. It was suggested that not knowing the woman in advance of labour could create problems if the care provider did not agree with the direction the woman had planned for her care:

And then all of a sudden, we're saying, well, actually, I know you were told yes, but actually no. Uhm which, which, which, I think causes issues. Whereas I think if you had someone who met them the whole way along and had that continuity and had that rapport and had that trust, I do think that makes a difference. (P4)

Building a relationship in labour

If a relationship was not established in advance of labour, in addition to the clinical responsibility, some participants explained how they were required to build the relationship and undertake intimate clinical procedures within a short time of meeting. Building the relationship included assessing the information another professional may have already given to the woman, and even if the participant did not agree, enacting it so as not to disrupt the relationship development. Participant 10 showed an awareness of the intimacy that the professional role entails, and this influenced her decision in whether to alter the plan of care. This may be to save the woman from the confusion of conflicting information but also means that the participant does not put themselves in an opposing position to another colleague:

So, first of all you have to have a relationship with her because you're going to be [doing] very intimate things. So, you're trying to build that up. And if she's already down a very negative route, it's very hard to turn some of that back because first of all you want to build a relationship with her, not be going against all the things that's been told. (P10)

A lack of relationship in advance of labour between the woman and the participant may result in some participants having a stronger relationship with the institutions than the woman. Women may be guided to accept recommendations that ensure the smooth running of the institutions rather than what may be the full gamut of choice available for the woman:

So, I still think, I still think we don't really listen to women because I don't think we really give women all of the information. We give them the piece that we want them to hear, to make it easier for us. And I know that sounds very mean, but I think [if] you're working on a busy labour ward, [and] you have 5 inductions - If I sit down with every single one, go through every single thing… (P10)

Participant 10 suggests women may be given the information that benefits the institutions, potentially making it easier to enact standardised processes as women will align to the institutional processes. It also suggests that the decision making for women’s care may be further impacted by the needs of multiple women who are also requiring intrapartum care (you have 5 inductions) rather than the decision-making variables being solely related to the woman’s clinical care.

She’s not just saying something frivolous

Some participants were willing to support women’s individualised care requests, even if this stepped outside of institutional guidance. However, prior to declaring that support, participants wanted to confirm that the woman not only understood her request and took responsibility for the decision but were able to articulate its importance to them. Participant 4 finds care easier when women are informed suggesting a shared responsibility in decision making:

So yeah, I do think it makes a little bit easier when you have an informed woman who is making the decision and understands the implication of that of that decision. She understands the risks associated with that decision. I do think that makes it easier. (P4)

The moving line

The sub-theme ‘the moveable line’ was identified by participants as a route to individualise care but was dependent on the willingness of the participants to do so.

There are limits and boundaries to everything

There were limits to some participants abilities to meet individual women’s needs in labour and birth. This may be due to a medical condition, or the clinical circumstances that the woman presented with. Participants recognised the benefit of telemetry fetal monitoring but suggests that it is not fit to meet the needs of some women in practice:

There are limits and boundaries to everything isn't there and I suppose telemetry [fetal monitoring] is great. It's not great on a woman with a BMI of 50 unfortunately, and I'm not being discriminatory against people's size (P1)

Equipment was not the only limitation identified. Women were limited to the participants’ subjective viewpoint and participants used their own levels of comfort to find their ‘line’. Participant 2 (P2) suggests there is a line that will not be crossed. Yet, if everyone has a ‘line’ and this line is different for everyone it is unclear how women can traverse these subjective differences between the care providers, particularly if they do not know them in advance of labour:

I think lots of people have a line, I think everyone has a line and everyone is in a different place. (P2)

Adaptations in practice were found when some participants were willing to move their line even if the institutional guidance excluded them:

There are, for example, women who really want that kind of supportive care pathway care (low- risk midwifery care) and for reasons that they unfortunately can't control they aren't suitable for that. So that generally like discludes (sic) them from using hydrotherapy, whereas I would make it my absolute business to make sure that I've set up a really nice shower room for them with a birthing stool in it with their lights to try encompass what it was that they wanted, what they want is relaxation of [in] water. So, we can give it to them in different form. I think that's really important. (P1)

Breaking rules and finding loopholes

Sometimes, providing individualised care required breaking the rules. The rules were established through the institutional guidance where participants worked. Participants explained how they broke the rules when women were outside of eligibility criteria. Participants gave examples of how they ‘break the rules’ either by physical assessment or by their use of language:

And I suppose I'm lucky with age that you can actually say, well, OK, but what, what about this individual woman? So her BMI is 35.1. She cannot get in the pool? Well, can she put her leg over the pool, get in and put her leg back over the pool and get out. Then she can. (P10)

So I will talk to them and say this is what the guideline says I'm supposed to recommend X to you. I'll leave it up to yourself, but I would completely respect your decision if you said no (P7)

Age, experience and seniority may be indicators that support high risk women’s individualised care in labour. A participant (P7) expressed the importance of seniority as a supportive mechanism to endorse individualised care requests. Care available in other jurisdictions but not available in Ireland may suggest Ireland is more conservative in its approach than elsewhere. However, participant 7 acknowledges that having seen the practice and being a part of the care, they were willing to endorse the practice themselves. This suggests that being a part of new or changing practice increases its viability and longevity for other women to be able to access it:

So, like I would have had to have a consultant say, well, that's OK, that's fine - do it. In [jurisdiction], I saw a lot of VBACs [Vaginal birth after caesarean] after two and even three previous [caesarean] sections. I just wouldn't have had the balls to endorse that as much, whereas now I'm like she wants it. She's grand. Just support her. But that doesn't really happen in Ireland that much. It's rare. (P7)

Some participants considered guidelines inherently inflexible to meet the woman’s needs and therefore could not meet the needs of women who are high risk, even when the clinical condition of the woman and her baby were within normal parameters:

There always needs to be flexibility in these things particularly when it comes to higher risk people because the rules tend to need to be broken. (P2)

So, we continued pushing and I was very happy to stay on and continue pushing with her for that time because the trace was fine. The woman was well, you know, it was definitely what her choice was. It's outside all guidelines, you know, but it's what the woman wanted … Woman, baby were both fit and well at that point, you know? So, there was no, aside from policy, there was no other clinical indication. (P4)

This placed participants in the position where they needed to break the rules and practice outside guidance to support the woman in labour.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore midwives and obstetricians’ experiences of providing individualised care to high-risk women in the intrapartum period.

To provide non-discriminatory and equal treatment to all women, there was a standardisation of practice to support HCPs to meet the needs of many women in labour equally. The trend for standardisation in healthcare aims to improve care by ensuring that decision making is not based on the subjective opinions of the healthcare professionals and tries to reduce differences in variation and standardised practices are often based on quantitative evidence such as randomised controlled trials [21]. To this end, evidence-based medicine has been criticised for its potential as an obstacle to person-centered care as the insights of the person (or woman) and failing to recognise their knowledge of their body, often found within qualitative evidence [23, 33].

In practice, the assessment of risk, particularly antenatally, and the impact that assessment may have on the choices available to women in the intrapartum period should be made explicit to the woman on completion of that assessment so that the woman has time to consider her position and negotiate, if necessary, prior to the intrapartum stage. To successfully do this, consideration needs to be given to institutions incorporating documentation within the guidelines that explain how the standard of woman-centered care is met and is attainable within the guideline.

Standardisation within the institution, even when implemented with good intent, may have unintended consequences when frontline staff experience a conflict between the policy rules and the woman’s needs. This reflects Tummers et al. [40] theoretical stance of conceptualising coping in public service delivery. Lipsky [20], in their seminal text on Street Level Bureaucracy originally promoted the idea of coping which has been seen to include ‘strategies for survival’, breaking rules or routinising delivery [11, 39]. The implication being that the HCP’s adopt behaviours that do not benefit the woman but instead provide the HCP with cognitive coping strategies such as emotional detachment from the women they care for. Therefore, this potentially reduces the capacity for individualised practice.

Further, in the event the HCP has a conflict, this places the HCP at risk of moral injury. Moral injury has been defined as knowing what to do, but being constrained by the institutional boundaries surrounding them, potentially resulting in burnout [12, 13, 18]. There is some evidence to suggest that for midwives, specifically moral injury may be organisationally mediated [12]. In contrast, there is a lack of evidence regarding moral injury from the perspective of the obstetrician, although there is evidence of increased symptoms of traumatic stress and burnout suggesting that there may be parallels with the midwifery profession [43].

Participants in this study who had less labour ward experience expressed a lack of flexibility in the plans and timeframes allocated within the intrapartum period, reducing their capacity to provide individualised care to women and ‘move the line’. Less labour ward experience may also correlate with being considered ‘junior’, rather than a senior member of the intrapartum team. A key finding of this study was that seniority was also considered to be impactful on the woman’s ability to attain individualised care in the intrapartum period. There is some evidence to suggest that senior midwives act in a supportive capacity to those less experienced, however, this support was not always perceived positively [3]. Nevertheless, the implication of this may be that there is merit in evaluating the support required by junior staff when engaged in complex intrapartum cases with or without senior support. Further research is required to understand the relationship between age, seniority and experience as it is likely nuanced and layered in its complexity.

Women may sometimes be provided with choices that align with the institutional needs and research suggests women may only receive a limited version of what is available and are provided with the options available in the maternity service only [9]. A lack of the full range of choice being made available to women may be perceived as a lack of informed consent. Recent research suggests this meets the threshold for sub-standard care [1]. The implication in practice may be that sub-standard care is less likely a one-off event within high income countries and instead may be embedded within the institutional maternity care services if women’s choices are restricted depending on the institution they attend.

Previous research on humanised practice has suggested that humanisation may be reduced when there is a high clinical workload or the ratio of women to HCPs is increased [2] and this reduction of humanised practice may be a result of the institutional processes being prioritised to maintain a standard of care for multiple women simultaneously [25]. The implication being a clinically busy environment will reduce women’s access to the tenants of respectful maternity care and the decision-making choices surrounding women’s achievement of individualised care are not solely based on the woman’s clinical circumstances but may also be impacted by the wider institutional environment.

In this study, midwives and obstetricians were found to balance the protection of the development of a fledgling relationship with women, with the needs of the institution. The development of a relationship and a connection between human beings were also found in the concept analysis of humanisation in childbirth and has been identified as a pillar of humanisation suggests participants have an understanding and willingness to practice in a humanised manner [8, 9, 16]. However, in a framework for understanding childbirth satisfaction, Brand and Gartland (2024) [4] found that contemporary maternity care for high-risk women does not typically prioritise the relationship between women and their caregiver with evidence in the international literature that it may even be the first occasion they meet. Further, a meta-synthesis of midwives’ experiences of respectful maternity care found hospital managerial rules and the midwives position in the health system a barrier to women-centered individualised care [16]. However, this study suggests that a model of care where midwives and obstetricians build a relationship in advance of labour may not only benefit women but is the preferred option for the professional too. Therefore, the organisational structure of the institution should consider a model of care that supports the development of a relationship between the woman and the HCP in advance of labour and birth. This may have the inadvertent consequences of providing increased flexibility in the practice of the HCP and could ward against HCP’s being siloed into one area of practice for a prolonged period of time.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the inclusion of midwives and obstetricians’ experiences of providing individualised care to high-risk women. This study used purposive sampling to recruit participants. However, ten of the participants were white Irish and this may not be representative sample of the workforce. Finally, as this was a national study in Ireland, the findings should be considered within the context of the Irish healthcare system.

Conclusion

The standardised processes of the institution may inherently impede midwives and obstetricians’ ability to individualise care for women who are classified as high risk in labour. This will be particularly impactful if the plan of care requires stepping away from institutional guidance to facilitate women’s individualised requests. The seniority and professional status of the HCP may also be relevant and requires further exploration in the research. Currently, the delivery of care is weighted to the preference of the institution. However, offering a model of care that supports midwives and obstetricians’ knowing women in advance of their labour may mitigate institutional momentum and increase the likelihood of high-risk women receiving individualised care.
