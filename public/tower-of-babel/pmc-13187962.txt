Bispherical Metal Augment-enabled Controllable Acetabular Distraction for Chronic Pelvic Discontinuity: Mid-term Clinical and Radiographic Outcomes of a Retrospective Single-center Case Series
Authors: Guoyuan Li, Yuhang Wang, Zhengliang Luo, Min Chen, Xiaoqi Zhang, Rui He, Xifu Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe acetabular distraction technique utilizing porous shells and augments is effective for chronic pelvic discontinuity. We report a modified technique employing a novel bispherical augment to achieve controllable distraction and enhanced reconstruction.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of 15 consecutive revision total hip arthroplasties performed for the treatment of chronic pelvic discontinuity at a single institution between 2017 and 2021. A modular revision system of bispherical augment with a highly porous hemispherical shell was used for defect reconstruction and acetabular distraction. The mean follow-up was 5.4 years (4.0-8.1 years). Primary outcomes were implant survivorship free from aseptic loosening and radiographic healing of the discontinuity were assessed, and complications were documented.ResultsOne patient (6.7%) required acetabular revision for aseptic loosening at 3.5 years. One patient required bearing exchange for recurrent dislocation at 3 months postoperatively. Radiographic healing of the discontinuity was achieved in 93.3% (14/15) of hips. One asymptomatic patient had a nonprogressive radiolucent line. Mean Harris scores improved significantly from 28.6 preoperatively to 76.3 at latest follow-up (P = .002). The hip center of rotation was significantly restored inferiorly (41.5 mm-24.4 mm, P = .003) and lower limb discrepancy corrected (24 mm-5 mm, P = .012). Cumulative survival free from revision for aseptic loosening was 90.9% (95% confidence interval: 74%-100%) at mean 5.4 years. No neurovascular complications occurred.ConclusionsThe application of a bispherical augment to enable a modified, controllable acetabular distraction technique provides a reliable alternative method for reconstructing chronic pelvic discontinuity; further larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed.Keywords: Revision total hip arthroplasty, Pelvic discontinuity, Acetabular distraction, Acetabular defect, Augment

Introduction

As total hip arthroplasty (THA) is performed more frequently in younger and more active patients, the incidence and complexity of revision THA, including cases with severe bone loss and pelvic discontinuity, are increasing [1]. Pelvic discontinuity, characterized by separation of the superior pelvis from the inferior hemipelvis [2], poses a significant reconstructive challenge. Although historically representing only 1%-5% of hip revisions, its prevalence is expected to rise with the growing number of THAs in younger populations and higher functional demands [[3], [4], [5]].

The loss of pelvic continuity and extensive acetabular bone defects compromise initial implant stability and anatomical hip center of rotation restoration. Over the past decade, the paradigm for managing pelvic discontinuity has shifted significantly. While earlier approaches included bulk acetabular allografts (associated with high failure rates due to resorption and loosening [6]) and cages (prone to mechanical failure from poor ingrowth and micromotion [7]), contemporary strategies emphasize achieving both rigid initial fixation and long-term biological integration. Techniques like concomitant posterior compression plating, cup-cage constructs, and custom triflange acetabular components (CTACs) remain options, particularly for massive defects [[8], [9], [10]]. However, cup-cages and CTACs often require extensive surgical exposure, carry risks of infection and instability [8,11], and typically necessitate the use of polyethylene liners, limiting bearing surface options [8,9].

A pivotal advancement has been the development and refinement of the acetabular distraction technique [12,13]. This technique utilizes a dedicated distractor or an oversized, highly porous hemispherical shell to apply peripheral distraction and central compression across the discontinuity, promoting stability and bone healing [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Crucially, distraction is increasingly combined with modular porous metal augments (eg, tantalum), allowing intraoperative customization to address complex bone loss patterns [17]. This combined approach (distraction + augments) has demonstrated excellent mid-term survivorship (up to 96.2%) [13,18,19], establishing it as a highly effective and increasingly preferred method for chronic pelvic discontinuity.

Despite the success of distraction techniques, achieving controllable, incremental distraction without over-distraction remains a technical challenge. Over-distraction can lead to neurovascular injury, while under-distraction may compromise stability. Current methods rely on either a separate distractor or an oversized shell, both of which offer limited intraoperative feedback and adjustability. To further optimize the distraction technique and enhance support for the revision shell in severe defects, our team designed a novel bispherical metal augment (Fig. 1). Unlike wedge-shaped augments, its unique geometry aims to maximize bone–implant contact area, facilitate hip center of rotation (HCOR) restoration, and improve biomechanical stability [20,21]. A key innovation is its application within the distraction concept: the bispherical augment's graduated size/thickness parameters allow it to be used as a controllable distractor. By inserting an augment of appropriate thickness into the gap between roof augment and shell before cement setting, the distraction force can be incrementally increased to achieve stable unitization of the hemipelvis without the need for a separate universal distractor. This potentially mitigates the risk of over-distraction and neurovascular injury [17,22] and offers technical convenience. Our bispherical augment differs from prior augment-assisted distraction methods by serving a dual purpose: it not only fills bone defects but also acts as a calibrated spacer to fine-tune the distraction force. This integrated design potentially enhances control and safety.

Bispherical augments.

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of this bispherical augment combined with a modified acetabular distraction technique for chronic pelvic discontinuity reconstruction over a mean 5.4-year follow-up. We hypothesized that this technique would achieve pelvic unitization, restore the anatomical hip center, and yield favorable clinical outcomes with a low complication rate.

Material and methods

Patients

Our hospital's research ethics board approved this study (No. XJS2025-1-5(WK)), which comprised a consecutive series of 15 patients (15 hips) accompanied by chronic pelvic discontinuity treated by the senior author (X.F.S.) between 2017 and 2021 during acetabular revisions. All eligible patients during this period were included, minimizing selection bias. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. All patients were available for retrospective evaluation at an average of 5.4 years (range, 4.0-8.1 years) after surgery. None of the patients were lost to follow-up. The series included 10 females and 5 males, with a mean body mass index of 24 kg/m2 (range, 18-33 kg/m2). The average age at surgery was 69.6 years (range, 53-89 years), and the average number of previous surgeries was 1.3 (range, 1-4). Preoperative diagnoses for the original hip arthroplasty included osteoarthritis (3 hips), osteonecrosis (2 hips), trauma (2 hips), rheumatoid arthritis (4 hips), hip dysplasia (3 hips), and remote septic arthritis (1 hip). Aseptic loosening was the reason for revision in all 15 patients, among which 6 patients were accompanied by severe osteolysis.

Preoperative evaluations

Preoperatively, all patients' erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein levels, and full blood count were assessed to rule out an infection. Anteroposterior (AP) pelvis radiograph and computed tomography with three-dimensional reconstruction were routinely conducted for all patients. Acetabular bone loss was determined using preoperative radiographs and intraoperative findings after socket removal. Acetabular defects were classified using the system described by Paprosky [23]. Two patients had a type IIC bone defect, 3 patients had a type IIIA bone defects, and 10 patients had a type IIIB bone defect, respectively. Although Paprosky IIC defects represent a less severe bone loss pattern, they were included because they were associated with radiographic and intraoperative evidence of pelvic discontinuity, and their management with distraction and augments reflects the spectrum of real-world clinical presentations. Radiological indications for discontinuity included a discrete fracture line visible on radiography, an asymmetrical obturator foramen, or translation of the superior compared to the inferior hemipelvis, observed as a break in the ilio-ischial line [2]. The HCOR was measured on AP radiographs of the pelvis according to the method previously described by O'Neill [24].

Operative details

Patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position. The senior author preferred the posterior approach for THA revisions. Prior skin incisions were made whenever possible. Using this approach, the sciatic nerve was identified and protected. In all cases, the sciatic nerve was identified distally in the adipose tissue deep in the gluteus maximus tendon, traced proximally, and freed from any adhesions before the next operation. Great care and patience were exercised during extraction of the acetabular component to avoid damaging the surrounding host bone. Once the acetabular component was safely removed, meticulous debridement of the acetabulum was performed, and samples were obtained for microbiological examination. Acetabular deficiencies and pelvic discontinuities were carefully assessed. Pelvic discontinuity was identified intraoperatively if movement between the cephalad and the caudal aspects of the pelvis was demonstrated.

The surgical technique for severe acetabular deficiency reconstruction with bispherical augment has been previously described [20]. The anatomical hip center was approximated based on the remaining acetabular landmarks and prepared using hemispheric reamers. To determine the optimal position of the hip rotation center and avoid sacrificing the residual bone of the anterior and posterior columns, acetabular defects were prepared using a reamer to secure bispherical augments for deficiency filling and shell supportation during acetabular distraction. After the spherical treatment of the defects surface, trial augments and trial sockets were inserted with lightly impaction to determine the best contact between the interfaces of bone-augment and augment-socket. Subsequently, the real augment was secured according to the position and orientation of the previous trial. Next, the acetabular component was impacted with the bone cement limited to the augment-cup interface. Attempts were made to maximize the amount of host bone contact with the augments and acetabular component. Particulate graft was placed in the remaining cavity before the revision shell was impacted. The superior and inferior aspects of the hemipelvis were distracted through the discontinuity by placing an acetabular cup 4–6 mm larger than the last hemispherical reamer (Fig. 2). If this distraction of the pelvis could not provide sufficient initial mechanical stability of the cup, a bispherical metal augment with a thickness of 5 mm or 10 mm was wedged into the gap between roof augment and shell with an appropriate depth to further increase the strength of the acetabular distraction (Fig. 3). Augment thickness (5 or 10 mm) was chosen based on intraoperative assessment of distraction resistance. Appropriate depth was defined as sufficient when the entire construct moved as a unit after the acetabulum was maximally distracted. Once the acetabular component was in place, multiple cancellous screws were inserted through the acetabular component into both the ilium and ischium. Ischial screws were placed under direct visualization or fluoroscopic guidance to prevent abduction failure.

The surgical workflow can be summarized as: (1) acetabular exposure and debridement; (2) trial reduction to assess defect and determine augment size; (3) implantation of bispherical augments for defect reconstruction; (4) insertion of a moderately oversized shell (4∼6 mm larger than last reamer) to achieve initial distraction; (5) if manual testing reveals residual instability, a bispherical augment of appropriate thickness (5 or 10 mm) is wedged between the roof augment and shell to incrementally increase distraction until the entire construct moves as a unit; and (6) final screw fixation, with emphasis on at least one screw engaging the ischium posteriorly.

Implants

The bispherical metal augments were made from Ti6Al4V, related design and fabrication processes have been reported [20,21]. A total of 32 bispherical augments were used in these 15 patients, the mean size and thickness of augments used were 56.1 mm (range, 48-66 mm) and 8.5 mm (range, 5-20 mm). Sixteen augments were used for roof defects reconstruction in 10 patients, 1 augment in 5 patients, 2 augments in 4 patients, and 3 augments in 1 patient, respectively. Two augments were used for roof and ischial defects reconstruction in 1 patient. In the other 4 patients, both the roof, ischium and pubis defects required augmentation reconstruction.

Of the 15 patients, a trabecular metal shell (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, USA) was used in 5 patients and a Pinnacle multi-hole shell (DePuy, Warsaw, USA) was used in 10 patients. The mean shell size was 64 mm (range, 54-70 mm). Four LINK classic uncemented stems (LCU, LINK, Hamburg, Germany) and 5 Corail revision stems (DePuy, Warsaw, USA) were used. Femoral components were retained in 5 patients. A ceramic-on-ceramic bearing was used in 14 patients, and a ceramic-on-polyethylene bearing was used in 1 patient.

Postoperative evaluations

Postoperatively, partial weight-bearing walking was performed within the first 12 weeks. Gradual full weight-bearing with crutches was allowed after 12 weeks prior to advancing to weight-bearing as tolerated. AP radiographs were obtained at immediately after surgery, 3 months, 1 year, and then follow-up varied based on the surgeon's discretion to assess for loosening and migration. The most recent radiographs were compared to the initial postoperative radiographs. Pelvic discontinuity healing or unitization of the pelvis was defined as the absence of a fracture line, bone ingrowth with no radiolucent lines into an uncemented cup superior and inferior to the discontinuity site, and with no evidence of component migration or change in position [25]. Loosening was defined as a change in the component abduction angle of greater than 10° or a change in the horizontal or vertical position of greater than 6 mm after correction for magnification [13]. Radiographs were independently assessed by 2 senior authors (R.H. and X.S.), with kappa statistics calculated for interobserver reliability (κ = 0.85). Leg length discrepancy (LLD) was also measured. Clinical outcomes were reported using the Harris Hip Score (HHS). Survivorship of free from revision for aseptic loosening of the acetabular construct was determined using Kaplan-Meier survivorship methods along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Data analysis

All the analyses were performed using SPSS for Mac software (v. 16.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The numerical data collected for this study included age, BMI, HHS values, HCOR, and LLD. Descriptive statistics were calculated. Statistical significance of the improvement in the HHS values, HCOR, and LLD was assessed using a paired sample t-test. Given the small sample size, we also performed nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to confirm the findings from paired t-tests; results were consistent. Kaplan-Meier survivorship was calculated, and a survival plot was created using revision for acetabular construct loosening as the endpoint. Survivorship analysis accounted for competing risks. P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

All 15 patients were available for retrospective clinical and radiographic evaluation after an average of 5.4 years (range, 4.0-8.1 years) postsurgery. Average HHS improved significantly from 28.6 (95% CI: 22.1-35.1) points preoperatively to 76.3 (95% CI: 68.4-84.2) points at latest follow-up (P < .05). The mean vertical distance of HCOR was improved from 41.5 mm (95% CI: 34.8-48.2) preoperatively to 24.4 mm (95% CI: 21.3-27.5) postoperatively (P < .05), and the mean horizontal distance of HCOR was also increased from 33.7 mm (95% CI: 26.9-40.5) preoperatively to 39.5 mm (95% CI: 35.1-43.9) postoperatively (P < .05). The clinically measured LLD in the entire patient cohort after surgery was less than 10 mm in 13 patients and between 10 and 15 mm in 2 patients. Before surgery, 3 patients had a severe limp, 10 had a moderate limp, and 2 had a slight limp. Average LLD was improved significantly from 24 mm (95% CI: 18.2-29.8) preoperatively to 5 mm (95% CI: 2.9-7.1) postoperatively (P < .05). At the latest follow-up, 1 patient had a moderate limp, 9 had a slight limp, and 5 had no limp.

One patient required acetabular constructs revision at 3.5 years postoperatively for aseptic loosening, thought to be due to inadequate ischial fixation. The pelvic discontinuity healed when rerevision surgery was performed, and at the latest follow-up, the acetabular components were stable at 3.7 years post rerevision surgery (Fig. 4). One patient required liner and ceramic head exchange due to recurrent dislocation at 3 months postoperatively, with no recurrence of instability up to the final follow-up (Fig. 5). A continuous translucent line around the acetabular prosthesis was observed in one patient, but there was no displacement of the prosthesis or fracture of the screws, suggesting a suspected loosening. The patient is currently asymptomatic with no further changes in the position of the acetabular component. The asymptomatic radiolucent line was not counted as a failure in survivorship analysis. The remaining 12 patients remained radiographically stable at final follow-up. Using our definition of radiographic healing of the pelvic discontinuity or unitization of the pelvis, the healing rate was 93.3% (14/15) at the final follow-up. With failure due to acetabular components aseptic loosening as the end point, the cumulative survival rate of bispherical augments was 90.9% (95% CI: 74-100) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that the application of a novel bispherical augment combined with a modified, controllable acetabular distraction technique provides reliable and safe reconstruction for chronic pelvic discontinuity, yielding good mid-term clinical and radiographic outcomes with a high implant survival rate (90.9%) and a low complication profile.

Reconstruction of pelvic discontinuity remains demanding, with the primary goals being stable fixation and healing of the discontinuity. The evolution toward biological fixation using highly porous metals has been central to modern strategies [16,17]. The acetabular distraction technique, specifically designed to generate elastic recoil compression across the discontinuity, has emerged as a leading biological solution, demonstrating impressive mid-term survivorship (83-97%) [13,19,22]. Our results, with a 90.9% survival rate at mean 5.4 years, align favorably with these contemporary reports. A detailed comparison is presented in Table 2. The single revision for aseptic loosening in our cohort (attributed to inadequate ischial fixation) mirrors a recognized challenge also reported in other distraction series [13,22], highlighting the critical need for secure fixation points, particularly in the ischium.

Our modified technique builds upon the established distraction principle but introduces the bispherical augment as an integral tool for controllable distraction. Traditionally, distraction is achieved either with a dedicated extra-acetabular distractor [12] or by impacting a significantly oversized shell [13]. The former requires specialized instrumentation, while the latter risks over-distraction, a potential cause of neurovascular injury (eg, sciatic nerve palsy, reported up to 13% [17,22]). Our approach starts with distraction using a shell moderately oversized (4-6 mm) relative to the last reamer. When additional stability is needed, the bispherical augment is wedged into the gap between roof augment and shell, its thickness (5 mm, 10 mm, etc.) acting as a calibrated spacer to incrementally increase the distraction force until stable unitization is achieved. This “augment-mediated” distraction offers precise control, potentially eliminating the need for a separate distractor and significantly reducing the risk of over-distraction and its complications. The absence of neurovascular injuries in our series supports this safety aspect, although meticulous sciatic nerve identification and protection remain paramount.

Beyond its role in distraction, the bispherical augment functions effectively as a standard augment for severe acetabular defect reconstruction. The biomechanical rationale for the bispherical augment, enhanced contact area and initial stability, was previously established through finite element analysis and biomechanical evaluations [21,26]. The current study provides clinical validation of those principles, demonstrating high healing rates and implant survival. This modular approach (shell + augments) allows for customized reconstruction tailored to the defect, maximizing host bone contact, facilitating anatomical HCOR restoration, and correcting LLD, all factors contributing to implant longevity [5,6,20]. Our results confirmed significant improvements in HHS, HCOR position (restored inferiorly by a mean 17.1 mm), and LLD (corrected to a mean 5 mm).

Cup-cages and CTACs technology, due to the need to consider the morphology of acetabular bone defects and the shape matching between the prosthesis and the host bone during surgical installation, it is often difficult to estimate the orientation of the acetabulum, and correction can only be achieved by relying on the installation of a polyethylene liner, thereby ensuring the stability of the hip joint after surgery. However, the design of the bispherical augment facilitates optimal cup positioning without compromising shell-augment interface contact, further enabling the use of ceramic bearings. In current series, our technique allowed the use of ceramic bearings in 93% (14/15) of patients, and meanwhile employing a reasonably sized shell (mean 64 mm). This is particularly relevant given that wear and osteolysis remain significant causes of long-term failure, especially in younger patients [27].

As with any technically demanding procedure, a learning curve exists. Surgeons adopting this technique should be familiar with assessing distraction adequacy and selecting appropriate augment thickness. Preoperative 3 dimensional (3D) planning may facilitate intraoperative decision-making, especially in early cases. It is important to note that all procedures were performed by a single high-volume revision arthroplasty surgeon, which may limit generalizability to less experienced settings.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and lack of a control group limit causal inference. Second, the small sample size, though comparable to other series on this rare condition, reduces statistical power and precision. Third, we used only the HHS; other patient-reported outcome measures (eg, hip disability and osteoarthritis outcome score [HOOS], short form 12 [SF-12]) were not collected. Finally, mid-term follow-up (mean 5.4 years) precludes conclusions about long-term durability. Multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and assess generalizability.

Conclusions

The application of bispherical augments combined with a modified acetabular distraction technique is a reliable alternative method for managing chronic pelvic discontinuity and associated acetabular deficiencies and appears to be able to prevent neurovascular injury. This technique provided favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes, a low complication rate, and a high survival rate of implants at mid-term follow-up. However, the long-term outcomes still need to be examined.
