Serum PCSK9 is not independently associated with dyslipidaemia in type 2 diabetes: a paired cross-sectional study
Authors: Peace Ngozi Okoro, Kolawole Wasiu Wahab, John Kola Olarinoye, Biliaminu Sikiru Abayomi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDyslipidaemia is common in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and contributes substantially to cardiovascular risk. Background

Dyslipidaemia is a critical metabolic abnormality commonly observed in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and contributes substantially to the heightened burden of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in this population [1, 2]. The typical diabetic dyslipidaemia phenotype involves elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triglycerides (TG), reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and often increased total cholesterol (TC) [3]. These alterations arise from insulin resistance, increased hepatic lipogenesis, impaired LDL receptor activity, and chronic low-grade inflammation [4].

Proprotein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin Type 9 (PCSK9) is a key regulator of lipid homeostasis through its post-translational control of the LDL receptor (LDLR). By binding to hepatic LDLR and targeting it for lysosomal degradation, PCSK9 reduces LDL-C clearance from circulation and consequently raises plasma cholesterol levels [5]. Elevated circulating PCSK9 concentrations have therefore been associated with atherogenic lipid profiles and increased cardiovascular risk, making it both a biomarker and therapeutic target in dyslipidaemia management [6, 7].

In patients with T2DM, hyperinsulinaemia and hepatic steatosis may stimulate PCSK9 transcription via SREBP-2 and HNF-1α–dependent pathways, potentially amplifying LDLR degradation and lipid retention [8]. However, the interpretation of serum PCSK9 levels is complex because they are influenced by several physiological and therapeutic factors. PCSK9 exhibits diurnal variation, peaking in the morning in synchrony with cholesterol synthesis and decreasing with prolonged fasting [9]. Furthermore, statin therapy upregulates PCSK9 expression, creating a counter-regulatory effect that can obscure true associations with lipid fractions [10]. These dynamics underscore the importance of controlling for medication exposure and standardizing sample collection times when evaluating PCSK9–lipid relationships.

Another factor relevant to interpreting dyslipidaemia in T2DM is sex-specific variation in HDL-C thresholds. Women typically have higher physiological HDL-C levels; therefore, applying a single cut-off value (< 1.03 mmol/L) may underestimate dyslipidaemia prevalence in females and bias comparative analyses [11].

Despite the expanding global evidence base, data from sub-Saharan African populations remain scarce, and most available studies have not accounted for these methodological and biological complexities. Therefore, the present study compared lipid fractions and serum PCSK9 concentrations between age- and sex-matched adults with and without T2DM, using standardized fasting morning samples, and evaluated whether PCSK9 is independently associated with dyslipidaemia. This design aimed to clarify the relationship between PCSK9 and lipid abnormalities in a Nigerian cohort while acknowledging the influence of circadian, therapeutic, and sex-related factors.

Methodology

Study design and location

This hospital-based, paired cross-sectional study was conducted at the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital (UITH), a 650-bed tertiary care centre in North-Central Nigeria that provides referral services to surrounding states.

Study population

The study included 100 adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) recruited from the Diabetes Clinic and medical wards, and 100 age- and sex-matched apparently healthy controls drawn from hospital staff and patients’ relatives. Matching was performed on a strict 1:1 basis, with controls matched to each T2DM participant by sex and age within ± 2 years, resulting in 100 matched pairs for analysis.

Inclusion criteria for the T2DM group were adults aged 18–65 years with physician-confirmed T2DM, while controls were non-diabetic adults with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) within the normal range. Exclusion criteria included hypothyroidism, Cushing’s syndrome, pregnancy, or age outside the specified range.

Sampling and sample-size justification

T2DM participants were consecutively enrolled (convenience sampling), and controls were purposively selected to match each case. While the initial recruitment target was informed by the regional diabetes prevalence (5.5%) using Fisher’s formula, a post-hoc power analysis was subsequently conducted to verify adequacy for the paired continuous-outcome design.

A total of 100 matched pairs were initially recruited to allow for potential exclusions due to incomplete laboratory data or sample integrity issues. After quality control, 92 pairs had complete measurements for total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, and serum PCSK9, and were included in the primary analyses. Eight pairs were excluded from the final analytic dataset due to missing or unusable biochemical measurements resulting from insufficient sample volume, hemolyzed specimens, and incomplete lipid panel data at the time of analysis.

Given the final analyzable sample of 92 matched pairs, a two-sided paired t-test with α = 0.05 and power = 0.80 was capable of detecting a standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) of 0.30, corresponding to approximately 0.6 mmol/L in LDL-C, which is consistent with a modest but biologically meaningful difference in lipid parameters. This justification ensures that the study had sufficient statistical sensitivity for the primary objective of detecting within-pair differences in lipid fractions.

Data collection

Sociodemographic and clinical information were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire. Physical measurements included weight, height, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and blood pressure. Venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected between 7:00 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. after an overnight fast of at least 8 h under aseptic conditions. Samples were processed immediately, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis.

Because medication histories were not systematically collected, potential exposure to statins, fibrates, or other lipid-lowering drugs could not be accounted for in the models. Similarly, HbA1c data were unavailable for a subset of participants due to reagent interruptions during the study period. These data limitations are explicitly acknowledged as key methodological constraints and are considered in the interpretation of results.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital Ethical Review Committee (Protocol ID No.: UITH/CAT/189/218/190, approval date 06/07/2021).

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. Confidentiality and data protection were maintained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Reporting adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.0 for confirmatory mixed-model and conditional logistic analyses.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median [IQR], depending on distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test). A fully scripted and reproducible workflow was developed to implement data cleaning, paired comparisons, mixed-effects modeling, conditional logistic regression for matched data, sensitivity analyses (sex-specific HDL thresholds and outlier exclusion), and visualization (Bland–Altman and paired-difference plots) (Additional file). The R script also generated analyzable pair counts, missingness summaries, and post-hoc detectable effect sizes to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Key packages included tidyverse, lme4, broom, pROC, effectsize, and DescTools.

Primary comparisons between T2DM and control groups used paired t-tests (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (for skewed data). Effect sizes were expressed as mean difference with 95% confidence intervals.

To account for pairing and inter-individual variability, linear mixed-effects models of the form outcome ~ group + (1 | pair_id) were fitted. Associations between dyslipidaemia (binary outcome) and predictors (PCSK9, group) were evaluated using conditional logistic regression, preserving the matched design.

Multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) and verified with Bonferroni adjustment. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conditional logistic regression respecting the matched study design showed no evidence of an independent association between serum PCSK9 concentrations and dyslipidaemia status.

For each 1 ng/mL increase in PCSK9, the odds of dyslipidaemia were unchanged (mOR = 1.00; 95% CI 0.92–1.10; p = 0.95). Similarly, group assignment (T2DM vs. control) was not significantly associated with dyslipidaemia after accounting for matching (mOR = 1.00; 95% CI 0.66–1.51; p = 0.99) (Table 4). Model discrimination was poor (AUC = 0.51), indicating no predictive utility of PCSK9 alone in this cohort.

Discussion

In this paired cross-sectional cohort, all lipid fractions (TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG) were higher in T2DM than in matched controls, whereas serum PCSK9 concentrations were similar between groups and did not classify dyslipidaemia. These observations are notable given the well-established biology of PCSK9 as an extracellular regulator of LDL receptor (LDLR) degradation and LDL-C levels, and the success of PCSK9 inhibition for lowering LDL-C and improving cardiovascular outcomes in high-risk populations [5–18].

The finding that PCSK9 did not differ between groups and lacked independent association with dyslipidaemia likely reflects a combination of treatment confounding and biological/measurement variability. Statins upregulate circulating PCSK9 via SREBP-2/HNF-1α pathways, even as they lower LDL-C; without medication histories our models could not adjust for this countervailing effect [19, 20]. In addition, PCSK9 exhibits diurnal and nutritional variability—declining with fasting and peaking in parallel with cholesterol synthesis—which can attenuate cross-sectional associations based on single draws [21–23]. Together, these factors offer biologically plausible reasons why serum PCSK9, measured once and unadjusted for therapy, did not track the lipid phenotype in this cohort.

Classically, diabetic dyslipidaemia features high TG, low HDL-C, and small dense LDL driven by insulin resistance [24–27]. By contrast, we observed higher HDL-C in T2DM alongside increases in LDL-C, TC and TG. Several non-exclusive explanations may apply: (i) therapy exposure (e.g., statins, fibrates, or niacin) can modestly raise HDL-C while altering PCSK9 [28], (ii) cohort-specific behaviours (diet, alcohol, physical activity) and sampling conditions (fasting duration) can shift HDL-C, TG and PCSK9 [29], and (iii) HDL functionality may be impaired even when HDL-C is numerically high [30–34]. Thus, elevated HDL-C does not guarantee atheroprotection in T2DM; emphasis on HDL function (cholesterol efflux, anti-inflammatory/anti-oxidative capacity) is increasingly recommended in mechanistic work and some translational studies [30, 31].

In addition, selection and data constraints may have contributed to the observed HDL-C pattern in this cohort. First, medication exposure could not be ascertained, and use of statins, fibrates, or niacin—agents known to increase HDL-C modestly and influence PCSK9 levels—may have been more common in the T2DM group [28]. Second, variations in fasting duration, dietary intake, and alcohol consumption can alter HDL-C and TG levels, particularly in individuals with underlying metabolic disease [29]. Third, recruitment of hospital-based participants who were more likely to be receiving ongoing clinical care may have introduced a selection bias toward individuals with better lipid control. Finally, HDL-C quantification alone does not capture functional capacity; evidence suggests that HDL functionality (e.g., cholesterol efflux, anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative capacity) may be impaired even when HDL-C concentrations appear normal or elevated, especially in T2DM [32–34]. Collectively, these factors may help explain the unexpectedly high HDL-C levels in our study population, underscoring the importance of interpreting HDL-C cautiously and emphasizing functional HDL assessments in future studies.

Recent syntheses underscore that lipid variability itself—rather than single cross-sectional values—tracks microvascular risk in diabetes. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of seven studies (n ≈ 144k), higher variability in TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C was associated with increased odds of microvascular complications (pooled OR per unit variability: TG 1.08 [0.99–1.18], LDL 1.11 [1.02–1.19], HDL 1.09 [1.00–1.18]), with the strongest signals for nephropathy and neuropathy [35]. This aligns with narrative conclusions in the same review that LDL-, HDL-, and TG-variability adversely affect renal outcomes (albuminuria/eGFR decline) [35]. Our single-time-point design cannot capture variability, which may partly explain the absence of a PCSK9–dyslipidaemia association here.

Regarding PCSK9 and glycaemia, contemporary evidence separates pharmacologic inhibition from genetic proxies. Cardiovascular outcome trials (FOURIER with evolocumab; ODYSSEY with alirocumab) did not increase new-onset diabetes or worsen fasting glucose/HbA1c, whereas Mendelian-randomization analyses of LDL-lowering PCSK9 alleles suggest a modest increase in diabetes liability—an inconsistency likely reflecting differences between life-long genetic exposure and time-limited drug treatment [36]. Trial-level estimates show neutral new-onset diabetes risk (e.g., FOURIER HR ~ 1.05; ODYSSEY HR ~ 1.00) [36]. Of note, circulating PCSK9 concentrations per se have not predicted conversion to type 2 diabetes in cohort analyses, supporting our observation that a single PCSK9 measurement has limited standalone prognostic value in unphenotyped T2DM cohorts.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength is the paired design, which reduces confounding by stable individual characteristics and was corroborated by mixed-effects models. We also pre-specified multiplicity control (FDR with Bonferroni checks) and used complementary plots (spaghetti, box-and-jitter, Bland–Altman) to guard against modelling artefacts. Limitations include: (i) absence of key covariates, especially medication history (statins, ezetimibe, fibrates) and glycaemic control indices (HbA1c), precluding full multivariable adjustment; (ii) single-time-point PCSK9 measurement despite known diurnal variation; and (iii) cross-sectional design, which limits temporal inference [37–39].

Clinical implications

Within these constraints, our data suggest that serum PCSK9 alone is unlikely to serve as a reliable biomarker for dyslipidaemia classification in T2DM in routine practice. Current diabetes guidelines continue to emphasise risk-based LDL-C lowering, typically with statins, with escalation to non-statins (including PCSK9 inhibitors) in selected high-risk patients to achieve targets [37]. Our results are consistent with this strategy: they neither refute PCSK9’s role in LDLR biology nor the outcome benefits of PCSK9 inhibitors in appropriate populations, but they caution against using a single PCSK9 measurement as a stand-alone diagnostic or prognostic tool in unphenotyped T2DM cohorts.

Future directions

Prospective studies in T2DM should (i) collect medication exposure and HbA1c, (ii) obtain serial PCSK9 measurements or standardise sampling to diurnal phase and fasting, (iii) include HDL functionality assays, and (iv) test incremental value of PCSK9 beyond established risk markers. Such designs will better determine whether and when PCSK9 adds clinically meaningful information in diabetes management.
