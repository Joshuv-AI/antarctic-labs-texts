Uniformly balanced H H -factors in multicoloured complete graphs
Agnijo Banerjee, Lawrence Hollom

Abstract
A balanced colouring of a graph is one in which every colour appears the same number of times. Given a fixed graph H H on r r vertices and a balanced k k -colouring of the complete graph K n ​ r ​ k K_{nrk} , Hollom (2025) asked the following question: can we always find an H H -factor F F covering all vertices of the complete graph K n ​ r ​ k K_{nrk} such that the inherited colouring of F F is almost balanced? This is known to be the case for palettes of only two colours, or when H H is only a single edge. We answer the above question in full, finding an H H -factor which is at most C r , k C_{r,k} edges away from being balanced, where C r , k C_{r,k} depends only on r r and k k . In fact, we work in the more general setting wherein our palette of colours is a subset of 𝕊 d − 1 \mathbb{S}^{d-1} , and find an H H -factor where the sum of the colours of all edges has bounded Euclidean norm.

1 Introduction

Given a colouring of the complete graph
K_{n}
, questions concerning when we can find specific subgraphs
F\subseteq K_{n}
whose colourings have particular properties are found throughout combinatorics.
For example, Ramsey theory asks for which graphs
F
one can guarantee a monochromatic copy of
F
in
K_{n}
.
More generally, discrepancy theory asks merely for a copy of
F
with one colour occurring as frequently as possible.
Conversely, one may also ask for balanced structures, wherein each colour should occur in
F
(approximately) the same number of times. Indeed, we call an edge-colouring of a graph
balanced
if each colour occurs exactly the same number of times.

We work in the latter setting, letting
F
be an
H
-factor for some fixed graph
H
(that is, a graph consisting of vertex-disjoint copies of
H
) and answer a question of Hollom
[
6
]
, proving the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all integers
k,r\geq 2
there is a constant
C=C_{k,r}
such that the following holds.
For every graph
H
on
r
vertices, integer
n
and balanced
k
-colouring
c\colon E(K_{nrk})\to[k]
, there is an
H
-factor
F
of
K_{nrk}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigg\lvert\lvert E(F)\mathbin{\cap}c^{-1}(i)\rvert-\frac{\lvert E(F)\rvert}{k}\bigg\rvert\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
k
and
r
. In particular, we may take

C=(8kr)^{(8kr)^{k}}.

We remark here that, while we have removed any
n
-dependence from
C
, it seems highly likely that the true value is far smaller than that given above.
Indeed, as will be discussed later, it has been conjectured that the correct value of
C
is
O(k^{2})
when
H
is a single edge.
However, in general, the authors are not aware of any constructions which show that
C
must grow with
k
or
r
.

In fact, we will prove
Theorem

1.1
via a more general result, in which the colours are unit vectors, as follows.
In the following theorem, and throughout this paper,
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix integers
k,r,d\geq 2
, a graph
H
on
r

S\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of size
k
.
Then there is a constant
C=C_{S,r}
such that the following holds.
For every integer

, positive real number
\alpha>0
, and colouring

such that
\lVert\sum_{e\in E(K_{nr})}c(e)\rVert\leq\alpha

H
-factor

of

such that

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq C+\frac{2\alpha}{n}.

Moreover, if
S
is the vertex set of a regular
(d+1)
-simplex in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

1.1 History and known results

There have been several different lines of research which can be viewed in terms of finding balanced subgraphs of coloured graphs.
For example, after a series of partial results, Füredi and Kleitman
[
4
]
showed that, if the edges of
K_{n}
are coloured from the palette
\{1,\dotsc,n-1\}
, then there is a spanning tree with edges having colours summing to
0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
.
This result was later generalised by Schrijver and Seymour
[
9
]
.

In a similar direction, Caro and Yuster
[
2
]
showed that every balanced colouring of
K_{n}
with colours
\{-r,\dotsc,r\}
contains almost zero-sum copies of a fixed spanning graph with bounded maximum degree.
This was then extended by Caro, Hansberg, Lauri, and Zarb
[
1
]
, who worked on the following problem of, given a 2-colouring
f\colon E(G)\to\{-1,1\}
, what conditions on
\lvert f(G)\rvert
guarantee a zero-sum spanning tree (for various graphs
G
, amongst other results).
Moreover, they asked whether every 2-edge-coloured complete graph of order
4n
with equally many edges of each colour contains a perfect matching which also contains equally many edges of each colour.

This question was answered in the affirmative by Ehard, Mohr, and Rautenbach
[
3
]
, and by Kittipassorn and Sinsap
[
7
]
, who proved that such a perfect matching must always exist. The latter also asked whether this result on perfect matchings can be generalised to many colours.

This question was soon answered by means of a counterexample, as Pardey and Rautenbach
[
8
]
, amongst other results, exhibited a 3-colouring of the edges of
K_{6}
with no perfectly balanced perfect matching (i.e. with two edges in each colour). This naturally leads to the question of finding a perfect matching which is merely as close to balanced as possible.
To this end, define the
deviation
of a perfect matching
M\subseteq K_{2kn}
with respect to a balanced colouring
c\colon E(K_{2kn})\to[k]
to be

(1.1)

f_{c}(M)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big\lvert\lvert c^{-1}(i)\mathbin{\cap}M\rvert-n\big\rvert.

The quantity
f_{c}(M)
can be seen to measure how far the colouring of
M
is from being balanced.
Pardey and Rautenbach
[
8
]
thus relaxed the question of
[
7
]
to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3
.

If
n
and
k
are positive integers, and
c\colon E(K_{2kn})\to[k]
is a balanced colouring, then there is a perfect matching
M
of
K_{2kn}
with deviation satisfying

f_{c}(M)\leq O(k^{2}).

They made some progress towards this conjecture, proving that, in the same setting,
f_{c}(M)\leq 3k\sqrt{kn\ln(2k)}
.

Further progress was made towards
1.3
by the second author
[
6
]
, who found a uniform upper bound for
\min_{M}f_{c}(M)
, proving that, for any colouring
c
of
K_{2kn}
,
f_{c}(M)\leq 4^{k^{2}}
for some perfect matching
M
of
K_{2kn}
. The generalisation to arbitrary
H
factors was also considered in
[
6
]
.
Indeed, we may generalise the definition of deviation from (
1.1
) to the following, where
F
is an
H
-factor of
K_{rnk}
, the graph
H
has
m
edges and
r
vertices, and
c\colon E(K_{rnk})\to[k]
is a balanced colouring.

(1.2)

f_{c}(F)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big\lvert\lvert c^{-1}(i)\mathbin{\cap}F\rvert-nm\rvert.

The suggested generalisation to
H
-factors was as follows.

Question 1.4
.

For which graphs
H
on
r
vertices and
m
edges is there a function
h_{H}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
with the following property?

For any
n
and any number
k
of colours, any colour-balanced
c\colon K_{rnk}\to[k]
(that is, such that for every
i
in
[k]
there are equally many edges
e
with
c(e)=i
) contains an
H
-factor
F
with deviation satisfying

(1.3)

f_{c}(F)\leq h_{H}(k).

While
Theorem

1.1
resolves
1.4
in its entirety, the question still remains as to the optimal value of the function
h_{H}(k)
in (
1.3
).
We note here that the bound provided by
Theorem

1.1
is worse than the bound of
4^{k^{2}}
from
[
6
]
in the case of perfect matchings.
Improving these bounds further will be discussed in
Section

6
.

1.2 Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1

We now provide an outline of how we prove
Theorem

1.1
, which consists of deducing this result from
Theorem

1.2
, and then proving the latter.
Throughout this outline, and in the proof itself, we assume that
r
,
k
, and
d
are constant, and
n
is sufficiently large.

First, in
Section

2
, to deduce
Theorem

1.1
from
Theorem

1.2
, we simply note that we can embed the palette
[k]
into
\mathbb{S}^{k-2}
as the vertices of a regular simplex.
Deducing that the colouring arising from
Theorem

1.2
is also balanced in the context of
Theorem

1.1
is then a matter of algebraic manipulations.

We prove in
Section

3
that
Theorem

1.2
holds when
H
is the complete graph
K_{r}
.
In this case, we can use the additional symmetry of
H
to our advantage.
We define the vector
w
as the sum of the colours of the edges of the clique-factor
F
(recalling that the colours are unit vectors), that is

w\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e).

The proof runs by attempting to minimise the square of the Euclidean norm of
w
via local changes, swapping where two vertices are embedded; we will refer to the quantity
\lVert w\rVert
as the
norm of
F
.
We assign a weight
w_{F}(e)
to each edge
e
by taking the inner product of its colour (a unit vector, we recall) and
w
:

w_{F}(e)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\langle c(e),w\rangle.

The crucial observation about this parameter is that

w_{F}(F)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{e\in F}w_{F}(e)=\lVert w\rVert^{2}.

This allows us to prove in
Section

3.1
that, if we start with
H
-factor
F
and apply a swap to reach
F^{\prime}
, there is a constant
L
such that, if
w_{F}(F)-w_{F}(F^{\prime})>L
, then
w_{F}(F)-w_{F^{\prime}}(F^{\prime})>0
, i.e. the norm of
F^{\prime}
is less than the norm of
F
.
We may assume that our
K_{r}
-factor
F
has minimal norm, and thus call a swap as above “contradictory” as it would contradict this assumption.

As the weights in a swap are determined only be the colours of the edges involved, there are only a constant number of potential swaps (looking only at the colours of the edges).
If the norm of
F
is large, then some of these potential swaps must be contradictory, and so it suffices to show that such a swap may actually be performed.

We may associate swaps with points of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by taking the signed sum of the colours (which we recall are unit vectors) added and removed by the swap.
In
Section

3.2
, we show that we can find a vector
w^{\prime}
very close to
w
such that, if

(1.4)

\sum_{e\in F}\langle c(e),w^{\prime}\rangle-\sum_{e\in F^{\prime}}\langle c(e),w^{\prime}\rangle>0,

then
w_{F}(F)-w_{F}(F^{\prime})>L
and the swap is contradictory.

Then, in
Section

3.3
, we prove that there must be a swap satisfying (
1.4
).
Due to the symmetry of
K_{r}
, every edge of the host graph
K_{nr}
which is present in
F
is removed from the
K_{r}
-factor by exactly the same number of swaps.
Likewise, every edge of the host graph which is not in
F
is added to the
K_{r}
-factor by exactly the same number of swaps.
Thus it suffices to show that the average of
\langle c(e),w^{\prime}\rangle
over edges
e\in E(F)
is larger than the average of
\langle c(f),w^{\prime}\rangle
over edges
f\in E(K_{nr})\setminus E(F)
.
This follows from some algebraic manipulations, and so we deduce that there must be a contradictory swap.
The existence of this contradictory swap demonstrates the existence of a
K_{r}
factor of
K_{nr}
whose norm is at most a constant, as required.

In
Section

4
, we consider the case when
S
is the set of vertices of a regular simplex. We then obtain an explicit constant
C
for
Theorem

1.2
in the case
H=K_{r}
, such that if
\lVert w\rVert\geq C
then there must exist a contradictory swap.

We generalise this to
H
-factors in
Section

5
.
If
H
has
r
vertices, then we first find a
K_{r}
-factor
F
using the results of
Section

3
, and then embed an
H
-factor inside of
F
.
As there are only a constant number of possible colourings of
K_{r}
, we may group all copies of
K_{r}
in
F
receiving a given colouring together, and then further subdivide this into blocks of
r!
copies of
K_{r}
, with a constant (
<r!
) number of copies left over.

In each block of
r!
copies of
K_{r}
, all with the same colouring, we embed one copy of
H
into each
K_{r}
, one with each of the
r!
possible injections from
V(H)
to
V(K_{r})
.
The left over copies of
H
are embedded arbitrarily, but there are only a constant number of these.
Thus the
H
-factor inherits the colour-balance of the
K_{r}
-factor up to a constant additive error, proving
Theorem

1.2
.

2 Deducing Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
1.2

To prove
Theorem

1.1
, we embed the
k
colours into a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and apply
Theorem

1.2
.
More precisely, let
Q
be the set of vertices of a regular simplex in
\mathbb{S}^{k-2}
, so
\lvert Q\rvert=k
, and denote the vertices of
Q
as
q_{1},\dots,q_{k}
.
Let
c\colon E(K_{nrk})\to[k]
be the
k
-colouring we are given, and
c^{\prime}\colon E(K_{nrk})\to Q
be the colouring into the vertices of the simplex.
Then, as the
k
-colouring we are given is completely balanced, we know that the value of
\alpha
in
Theorem

1.2
is 0, and so we know that there is a constant
C
depending only on
r
and
k
, and an
H
-factor
F
of
K_{nrk}
such that
\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c^{\prime}(e)\rVert\leq C
.

Say that there are
b_{i}
edges of
E(F)
coloured
i
by
c
.
Then the condition on the colouring becomes

(2.1)

\Big\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}q_{i}\Big\rVert\leq C,

and we need to provide an upper bound on
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big\lvert b_{i}-\lvert E(F)\rvert/k\big\rvert
.
However, as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}=0
, we can set
b_{i}^{\prime}=b_{i}-\lvert E(F)\rvert/k
so that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime}=0
.
Then condition (
2.1
) remains unchanged, and holds for
b_{i}^{\prime}
as well as for
b_{i}
, whereas our goal is now to upper-bound
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert b_{i}^{\prime}\rvert
.
As
\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime}=0
, we know that
2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}b_{i}^{\prime}b_{j}^{\prime}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime 2}
.
Squaring (
2.1
) and noting that, for
i\neq j
,
\langle q_{i},q_{j}\rangle=-1/(k-1)
, we find that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime 2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}b_{i}^{\prime}b_{j}^{\prime}\langle q_{i},q_{j}\rangle=\Big\lparen 1+\frac{1}{k-1}\Big\rparen\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime 2}\leq C^{2}.

Finally, we can note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert b_{i}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\sqrt{k\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime 2}}
, and thus deduce

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert b_{i}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\sqrt{k\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{\prime 2}}\leq\sqrt{k-1}\,C,

and
Theorem

1.1
follows.
∎

3 Finding a balanced clique-factor

As described in the above outline, we first prove an intermediate result, which states that we can find a colour-balanced
K_{r}
-factor.
More precisely, in this section we prove the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix integers
k,r,d\geq 2
and a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of size
k
such that
0
is in the convex hull of
S
.
For every integer
n
, positive real number
\alpha>0
, and colouring
c\colon E(K_{nr})\to S
such that
\lVert\sum_{e\in E(K_{nr})}c(e)\rVert\leq\alpha
, there is a
K_{r}
-factor
F
of
K_{nr}
such that

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq C+\frac{2\alpha}{n},

where
C
is a constant which only depends on
r
and
S
.
In the case that
S
is a regular
(d+1)
-simplex (so
k=d+1
), we may take

C=(8dr)^{(8dr)^{d+1}-1}.

Firstly, note that if
S
does not span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we may pass to a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, for some
d^{\prime}<d
, in which
S
is spanning.
As all of our bounds are increasing in
d
, this allows us to assume without loss of generality that
S
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To minimise
\big\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\big\rVert
for
F
a
K_{r}
-factor, we break up this norm into a sum of inner products.
Indeed, the following definitions are crucial to our arguments.

Definition 3.2
.

Define the
weight
of a colour
s\in S
with respect to a
K_{r}
-factor
F\subseteq K_{nr}
as

w_{F}(s)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\langle s,w\rangle=\sum_{f\in E(F)}\langle s,c(f)\rangle,

where

w\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{f\in E(F)}c(f).

The weight of an edge
e\in E(K_{nr})
is then the weight of the colour of that edge:
w_{F}(e)=w_{F}(c(e))
.
We interpret the weight of a set
X\subseteq E(K_{nr})
as
w_{F}(X)=\sum_{e\in X}w_{F}(e)
.

We may note that the weight of the whole
K_{r}
-factor
F
is

w_{F}(F)=\sum_{e\in E(F)}w_{F}(e)=\!\sum_{e,f\in E(F)}\!\langle c(e),c(f)\rangle=\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\bigg\rVert^{2}=\lVert w\rVert^{2}.

The above equality, despite its simplicity, is in some ways the crux of our entire argument, as will be expanded on in
Section

3.1
.
Assume that we have a
K_{r}
-factor
F
of minimal weight; there can thus be no perturbation of
F
which reduces the total weight.

3.1 Contradictory swaps

We will now consider small perturbations of our
K_{r}
-factor
F
which may decrease the weight.
In particular, we will consider swaps of two vertices.

Definition 3.3
.

For
u,v
vertices in distinct components of
F
, we define the
swap

u\leftrightarrow v
to be the following operation: For each vertex
w\neq u
in the component of
u
, remove the edge
uw
and add the edge
vw
, and for each vertex
w^{\prime}\neq v
in the component of
v
, remove the edge
vw^{\prime}
and add the edge
uw^{\prime}
.
Write the resulting
K_{r}
-factor as
F_{u\leftrightarrow v}

Call the swap
u\leftrightarrow v

contradictory
if
w_{F_{u\leftrightarrow v}}(F_{u\leftrightarrow v})<w_{F}(F)
(noting that this will contradict our assumption that
F
has minimal weight).

Note that any swap removes precisely
2r-2
edges from
F
, and adds
2r-2
distinct edges to form
F_{u\leftrightarrow v}
.
While it may not be obvious at first how one may go about showing that a swap is contradictory in practice, as the weight function
w_{F}
has changed along with the
K_{r}
-factor itself, we now show that, in fact, one can tell whether a swap is contradictory only in terms of the weight function
w_{F}
(or equivalently the vector
w
for
F
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u,v
be vertices of
K_{nr}
in different components of
F
, and let
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
be the sets of edges incident to either
u
or
v
in
F
and
F^{\prime}=F_{u\leftrightarrow v}
respectively.
Then, if

(3.1)

\sum_{e\in E_{1}}w_{F}(e)-\sum_{e\in E_{2}}w_{F}(e)>L,

where
L=8(r-1)^{2}
, then the swap
u\leftrightarrow v
is contradictory.

We note before giving the proof of
Lemma

3.4
that the condition (
3.1
) does not refer to
w_{F^{\prime}}
, only
w_{F}
.

Proof.

We compute the difference
w_{F}(F)-w_{F^{\prime}}(F^{\prime})
.
For ease of notation, for any sets
X,Y\subseteq E(K_{nr})
write
w_{F}(X)=\sum_{e\in X}w_{F}(e)
and

\langle X,Y\rangle\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{e\in X}\sum_{f\in Y}\langle c(e),c(f)\rangle.

Let
E_{0}\mathrel{\coloneqq}E(F)\setminus E_{1}=E(F^{\prime})\setminus E_{2}
.
We then have

\displaystyle w_{F}(F)-w_{F^{\prime}}(F^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle E(F),E(F)\rangle-\langle E(F^{\prime}),E(F^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle E_{1},E_{1}\rangle+2\langle E_{0},E_{1}\rangle-\langle E_{2},E_{2}\rangle-2\langle E_{0},E_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=2(\langle E_{1},E_{0}\mathbin{\cup}E_{1}\rangle-\langle E_{2},E_{0}\mathbin{\cup}E_{1}\rangle)-\langle E_{1},E_{1}\rangle-\langle E_{2},E_{2}\rangle+2\langle E_{1},E_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=2(w_{F}(E_{1})-w_{F}(E_{2}))-\Big\lVert\sum_{e\in E_{1}\mathbin{\cup}E_{2}}\!\!c(e)\;\Big\rVert^{2}.

Note that
\Big\lVert\sum_{e\in E_{1}\mathbin{\cup}E_{2}}c(e)\Big\rVert^{2}\leq(4(r-1))^{2}
as
\lvert E_{1}\rvert=\lvert E_{2}\rvert=2(r-1)
.
Thus, if
w_{F}(E_{1})-w_{F}(E_{2})>8(r-1)^{2}
, then the swap
u\leftrightarrow v
is contradictory, and the lemma is proved.
∎

3.2 Partitioning the space of swaps

The only information we need to determine whether or not a swap is contradictory is the (multi-)set of colours of edges removed and of edges added by the swap.
As a swap removes
2(r-1)
edges and adds
2(r-1)
edges, there are therefore only a constant number of possible swaps considering only the colours of edges removed and added.
Indeed, fix an arbitrary enumeration
S=\{s_{1},\dotsc,s_{k}\}
, and set

Y\mathrel{\coloneqq}\{y\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2(r-1)\}^{k}\mathbin{\colon}y_{1}+\dotsb+y_{k}=2(r-1)\},

thinking of
(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{k})
as representing a collection of edges of which
y_{i}
have colour
s_{i}
.

A swap moves between two vertices of
Y
.
The only relevant information for determining whether a swap satisfies (
3.1
) is the relative counts of colours between the start and end points of the swap, and so we may encode swaps as points in the space

X\mathrel{\coloneqq}\{x\in\{2-2r,3-2r,\dotsc,-1,0,1,\dotsc,2r-3,2r-2\}^{k}\mathbin{\colon}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=0,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert x_{i}\rvert\leq 4r-4\}.

The
weight
of a swap
x\in X
—the difference
w_{F}(E_{1})-w_{F}(E_{2})
as in (
3.1
)—is then given by

\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}w_{F}(s_{i})=\langle g(x),w\rangle,

where

(3.2)

g(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}s_{i}.

We now introduce some more terminology concerning these swaps.

Definition 3.5
.

For
L
as in
Lemma

3.4
, a swap
x\in X
is

•

small
if
|\langle w,g(x)\rangle|\leq L
,

•

increasing
if
\langle w,g(x)\rangle>L
, and

•

decreasing
if
\langle w,g(x)\rangle<-L
.

Let
X_{S}\subseteq X
be the set of all small swaps.

Note that
Lemma

3.4
states precisely that all decreasing swaps are contradictory.
Intuitively, if the swap between
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
is small, then
g(y^{\prime})
lies close to the hyperplane through
g(y)
with normal vector
w
(and vice versa).
We will now perturb this hyperplane slightly to produce a new hyperplane,
W
, so that all small swaps go between two points in the same translate of
W
.
Before we work towards a definition of
W
, we first need another definition.

Definition 3.6
.

We define the
angle

\theta(x)
of a swap
x\in X
as follows:

\theta(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sin^{-1}\bigg\lparen\frac{\lvert\langle g(x),w\rangle\rvert}{\lVert g(x)\rVert\lVert w\rVert}\bigg\rparen,

if
g(x)\neq 0
; otherwise we set
\theta(x)=0
.
i.e.
\theta(x)
is the absolute value of the angle between
g(x)
and the hyperplane with normal
w
.

The hyperplane
W
will contain a set of the form
W_{\theta}\subseteq X
for some angle
\theta
, where

(3.3)

W_{\theta}\mathrel{\coloneqq}\bigg\{x\in X\mathbin{\colon}\frac{|\langle g(x),w\rangle|}{\lVert g(x)\rVert\lVert w\rVert}<\sin(\theta)\bigg\}.

Note that
W_{\theta}
is the set of swaps with angle to
w^{\perp}
less than
\theta
.

W_{\theta}^{\perp}
is then defined to be the space orthogonal to the span of
W_{\theta}
.

The rest of this subsection is dedicated to finding an angle
\theta
for which
W_{\theta}
may be extended to a hyperplane with the following desired properties.
The core ideas of the argument are that, if
\theta
is sufficiently small, then the codimension of
W_{\theta}
is at least 1.
Moreover, as
\theta\to 0
, the angle between
w
and
W_{\theta}^{\perp}
also tends to 0.
Thus, for sufficiently small
\theta
, the set
W_{\theta}
can be extended to a hyperplane which is a good approximation of
w^{\perp}
.

If
\theta
is also not too small in terms of
\lVert w\rVert
, then all small swaps will lie in
W_{\theta}
.
This will give us some range
[\theta_{0},\theta_{1}]
of values for
\theta
in which all the above properties hold.
The final condition we require is that there are no points of
x\in X
so that
g(x)
lies “just outside” of
W_{\theta}
, in the sense that
g(x)\in W_{\theta(1+\zeta)}
for some not-too-large
\zeta
.
If
\theta_{1}/\theta_{0}
is sufficiently large, then we will show that this condition can be made to hold by a simple pigeonhole argument.
We now formalise the above intuition in a series of lemmas.

Throughout all the following lemmas,
S
is any finite subset of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
of codimension zero, and
r
is an integer.
We first provide the statements, and then move on to the proofs.

Lemma 3.7
.

There is a constant
\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(S,r)>0
such that, if
\sin(\theta)\geq\beta_{0}/\lVert w\rVert
, then all small swaps are in
W_{\theta}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

There is a constant
\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}(S,r)>0
such that, for all
\theta<\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
, the codimension of
W_{\theta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is at least 1.

Lemma 3.9
.

There is a constant
\eta=\eta(S,r)>0
such that, if
\phi=\phi(\theta)
is the angle between
w
and the space
W_{\theta}^{\perp}
, then
\sin(\phi)\leq\eta\sin(\theta)
.

Observation 3.10
.

For all
\eta\geq 1
, if
\lVert w\rVert\beta_{1}/\beta_{0}\geq\eta^{\lvert X\rvert}
then there is some angle
\theta
with

\frac{\beta_{0}}{\lVert w\rVert}\leq\sin(\theta)\leq\frac{\beta_{0}\eta^{\lvert X\rvert-1}}{\lVert w\rVert}

such that, for all
x\in X\setminus W_{\theta}
, the angle
\theta(x)
between
g(x)
and
w^{\perp}
satisfies

sin(\theta(x))\geq\eta\sin(\theta)

Indeed,
3.10
follows from considering
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{\lvert X\rvert}\}
in increasing order of
\theta(x)
: if
j
is maximal such that
x_{j}\in W_{\theta}
, then either
\sin(\theta(x_{\lvert X\rvert}))/\sin(\theta(x_{j}))\leq\eta^{\lvert X\rvert-1}
, or there is some
i\geq j
such that
\sin(\theta(x_{i+1}))/\sin(\theta(x_{i}))>\eta
.

We now prove
Lemmas

3.8
,
3.9
and
3.7
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.7
.

Let
x\in X
be a small swap.
We define the constant

(3.4)

\beta_{0}=\frac{L}{\min\{\lVert g(x)\rVert\mathbin{\colon}x\in X,\,g(x)\neq 0\}}.

Then, by definition,
|\langle g(x),w\rangle|\leq L
and
|\langle g(x),w\rangle|=\lVert g(x)\rVert\lVert w\rVert\sin(\theta(x))
.
Therefore

\sin(\theta(x))\leq\frac{L}{\lVert w\rVert\lVert g(x)\rVert}\leq\frac{\beta_{0}}{\lVert w\rVert}\leq\sin(\theta).

We therefore see that
\theta(x)<\theta
, and so
x
is in
W_{\theta}
, as required.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.8
.

There is a finite list of subsets of
g(X)
with codimension 0.
For each such
J\subseteq g(X)
, we claim that there is some
\varepsilon_{J}>0
such that, for any unit vector
v
, there is some
x\in J
with
|\langle v,x\rangle|\geq\varepsilon_{J}
.

Indeed, let
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(v)=\max_{x\in J}|\langle v,x\rangle|
.
Then
f
is a continuous function on a compact space and so attains its minimum: let
v_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be such that
f(v_{0})
is minimal.
It thus suffices to prove that
f(v_{0})>0
.
It is immediate that
f(v_{0})\geq 0
, and
f(v_{0})=0
would imply that
\langle x,v_{0}\rangle=0
for all
x\in J
, contradicting the fact that
J
has codimension 0.
Thus
\varepsilon_{J}>0
.

Then we take

\beta_{1}=\frac{\min_{J}(\varepsilon_{J})}{\max_{x\in X\setminus\{0\}}\lVert g(x)\rVert}.

Now assume for contradiction that, for some
\theta
with
0<\theta<\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
, the set
W_{\theta}
has codimension 0.
Thus there is some
J\subseteq g(X)
of codimension 0 with
J\subseteq W_{\theta}
.
Therefore, by definition of
W_{\theta}
, all
x\in J
have
|\langle x,w\rangle|<\lVert x\rVert\lVert w\rVert\lvert\sin(\theta)\rvert
.
But we know from the definition of
\varepsilon_{J}
that there is some
x\in J
for which
|\langle x,\widehat{w}\rangle|\geq\varepsilon_{J}
.
Therefore

\varepsilon_{J}\leq|\langle x,\widehat{w}\rangle|<\lVert x\rVert\lvert\sin(\theta)\rvert\leq\lVert x\rVert\beta_{1}\leq\min_{J}(\varepsilon_{J}),

a contradiction, as required.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.9
.

There is a finite list of subsets of
g(X)
with codimension at least
1
.
It thus suffices to prove that, for an arbitrary
J\subseteq g(X)
spanning some
U\leq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of codimension at least 1, there is a constant
\eta_{J}>0
such that, if
J\subseteq W_{\theta}
and the span of
W_{\theta}
is also
U
, then
\sin(\phi)\leq\eta_{J}\sin(\theta)
.

We claim that there is some some
\eta_{J}>0
such that, for any unit vector
u\in U
, there is some
x\in J
for which

(3.5)

|\langle u,x\rangle|>\eta_{J}^{-1}\lVert x\rVert.

Indeed, this follows by similar reasoning to that used in the proof of
Lemma

3.8
:
let
B
be the boundary of the Euclidean unit ball in
U
(i.e. the set of unit vectors), and define
f\colon B\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(v)=\max_{x\in J}|\langle v,x\rangle|
.
Then
f
is a continuous function on a compact space, so attains its minimum, and the claim follows.

It remains to show that
\sin(\phi)\leq\eta_{J}\sin(\theta)
under the assumption that
J\subseteq W_{\theta}
and these two sets have the same span.

As
U
has codimension at least 1, there is a space
U^{\perp}\leq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension at least 1.
Let
\pi_{U}
be the orthogonal projection onto
U
and note that, by definition,
\phi
is the angle between the vector
w
and
U^{\perp}
.
It follows that
\lVert\pi_{U}(\widehat{w})\rVert=\sin(\phi)
.
As
J\subseteq W_{\theta}
by assumption, for all
x\in J
we have

|\langle x,\pi_{U}(\widehat{w})\rangle|=|\langle x,\widehat{w}\rangle|\leq\lVert x\rVert\sin(\theta).

Thus, defining
v=\pi_{U}(\widehat{w})/\sin(\phi)
, for all
x\in J
we have

(3.6)

|\langle x,v\rangle|\leq\lVert x\rVert\sin(\theta)/\sin(\phi).

Combining (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), we deduce that
\eta_{J}^{-1}<\sin(\theta)/\sin(\phi)
. Letting
\eta=\max_{J}(\eta_{J})
, the desired result follows.
∎

We may now define the hyperplane
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Assume that
\lVert w\rVert\beta_{1}/\beta_{0}\geq\eta^{\lvert X\rvert}
, and
\theta
is chosen as in
3.10
.
Let
U
be the space orthogonal to both
W_{\theta}
and
w
.
Then
W
is the space spanned by
W_{\theta}\mathbin{\cup}U
.
We know from
Lemma

3.8
that
W_{\theta}
has codimension at least 1, and from
Lemma

3.9
that
w\notin W_{\theta}
.
Thus
W
is indeed a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Define
w^{\prime}
to be the vector normal to
W
with
\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert=\lVert w\rVert
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be the partition of
Y
by translates of
W
.
We now prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
be distinct, and let
y_{1}\in Z_{1}
and
y_{2}\in Z_{2}
.
Then
y_{1}-y_{2}
is an increasing swap if and only if
\langle y_{1}-y_{2},w^{\prime}\rangle>0
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
y_{1}-y_{2}
is increasing, i.e.
\langle y_{1}-y_{2},w\rangle>L
, but
\langle y_{1}-y_{2},w^{\prime}\rangle<0
.
Let
P
be the plane spanned by the vectors
w^{\prime}
and
y_{1}-y_{2}
, and let
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to P
be the orthogonal projection to
P
.
Then, letting
\phi^{\prime}
be the angle between
\pi(w)
and
w^{\prime}
, as
\phi<\pi/2
, we have that
\phi^{\prime}\leq\phi
.

Note that the angle between the vector
y_{2}-y_{1}
and
w
is more than
\pi/2
and the angle between
y_{2}-y_{1}
and
w^{\prime}
is less than
\pi/2
.
Therefore the angle between
y_{2}-y_{1}
and
w
is less than
\pi/2+\phi^{\prime}
, and thus the angle between
y_{2}-y_{1}
and
w^{\perp}
is less than
\phi^{\prime}<\phi
.

However, we know that
\sin(\phi)\leq\eta\sin(\theta)
, and so, by
3.10
, we in fact have that the angle between
y_{1}-y_{2}
and
w^{\perp}
is at most
\theta
.
Thus
y_{1}-y_{2}\in W_{\theta}
, contradicting the fact that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are distinct.
∎

Note that
Lemma

3.8
implies that
\lvert\mathcal{Z}\rvert>1
, and
Lemma

3.11
implies that
\mathcal{Z}
inherits a total order
\prec
from the swaps (noting that the value of
\langle y_{1}-y_{2},w^{\prime}\rangle
is independent of the choices of
y_{i}\in Z_{i}
); write
Z_{1}\prec Z_{2}
if every swap from
Z_{1}
to
Z_{2}
is increasing.
For any such
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, a swap from
Z_{2}
to
Z_{1}
is contradictory.
In the next section, we will prove that there must be such a swap.

3.3 Finding a contradictory swap

We know from the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
that each
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
is the set of vectors
x
such that
\langle w^{\prime},x\rangle=p
for some
p
.
Assume for contradiction that there is no contradictory swap between distinct elements of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Thinking now back in terms of our
K_{r}
-factor
F
, we see that, due to
Lemma

3.11
, every swap which replaces edges
E_{1}
with
E_{2}
must have
\langle w^{\prime},E_{1}\rangle\leq\langle w^{\prime},E_{2}\rangle
, where
\langle v,X\rangle
is shorthand for
\sum_{e\in X}\langle v,c(e)\rangle
.
By symmetry of
K_{r}
, the number of swaps
u\leftrightarrow v
which remove an edge
e\in E(F)
does not depend on
e
, and likewise for adding an edge of
E(K_{nr})\setminus E(F)
.
Thus, comparing average values of
\langle w^{\prime},c(e)\rangle
, we find that

\frac{1}{e(F)}\sum_{e\in E(F)}\langle w^{\prime},c(e)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{e(K_{nr})-e(F)}\sum_{e\in E(K_{nr})\setminus E(F)}\langle w^{\prime},c(e)\rangle.

Noting that
e(F)\leq nr(r-1)/2
, and recalling that
w=\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)
, we deduce

\langle w^{\prime},w\rangle\leq\frac{r-1}{r(n-1)}\big\lparen\langle w^{\prime},E(K_{nr})\rangle-\langle w^{\prime},w\rangle\big\rparen.

Recalling our assumption that
\big\lVert\sum_{e\in E(K_{nr})}c(e)\big\rVert\leq\alpha
, we see that to deduce a contradiction it suffices to prove that

\bigg\lparen 1+\frac{r-1}{r(n-1)}\bigg\rparen\langle w^{\prime},w\rangle>\frac{1}{n}\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert\alpha.

In particular, we know from
3.10
that the angle
\phi
between
w
and
w^{\prime}
satisfies
\sin(\phi)\leq\beta_{0}\eta^{\lvert X\rvert}/\lVert w\rVert
, and so it suffices to prove that

\lVert w\rVert\cos(\phi)>\frac{\alpha}{n}.

If
\lVert w\rVert>2\beta_{0}\eta^{\lvert X\rvert}
(which is automatically true by our assumption that the conditions of
3.10
hold, provided we take
\beta_{1}\leq 1/2
, which we may do) then
\cos(\phi)>1/2
, so it suffices that
\lVert w\rVert>2\alpha/n
.

As
\theta_{0}
,
\eta
, and
\lvert X\rvert
are constants, we may therefore see that there is some constant
C
such that
Theorem

3.1
holds.

4 Finding explicit bounds when
S
is a simplex

In this section, we consider only the case when
k=d+1
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
consists of the vertices of a regular simplex.
This corresponds precisely to the case in
Theorem

1.1
, as shown in
Section

2
.
Our goal is to find an explicit value for the constant
C
in
Theorem

3.1
in this case.

To do this, we find explicit values for the constants
\beta_{0}
,
\beta_{1}
, and
\eta
which we do in
Lemma

3.8
,
Lemma

3.9
, and
Lemma

3.7
respectively.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
S
is a regular simplex, in
Lemma

3.7
we may take
\beta_{0}=L=8(r-1)^{2}
.
That is, if
\theta
is such that
\sin(\theta)\geq L/\lVert w\rVert
, then all small swaps are in
W_{\theta}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
S
is a regular simplex, in
Lemma

3.8
we may take
\beta_{1}=(8dr)^{-d}
.
That is, for
\beta_{1}
as above, for all
\theta<\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
, the codimension of
W_{\theta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is at least 1.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
S
is a regular simplex, in
Lemma

3.9
we may take
\eta=(8dr)^{3d^{2}}
.
That is, for
\eta
as above and
\phi
the angle between
w
and
W_{\theta}^{\perp}
, we have
\sin(\phi)\leq\eta\sin(\theta)
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
.

Recalling the definition of
\beta_{0}
in (
3.4
), we see that it suffices to prove that
\min\{\lVert g(x)\rVert\mathbin{\colon}x\in X,\,g(x)\neq 0\}\geq 1
.
In fact, we claim that

(4.1)

\min\{\lVert g(x)\rVert\mathbin{\colon}x\in X,\,g(x)\neq 0\}=\sqrt{2+\tfrac{2}{d}}.

Indeed, for arbitrary non-zero
x\in X
, we have

\lVert g(x)\rVert^{2}=\bigg\langle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}s_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}s_{i}\bigg\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}x_{i}x_{j}\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle.

We know that

\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-\tfrac{1}{d}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We may also use the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}=0
to deduce that

2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d+1}x_{i}x_{j}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}^{2}.

It therefore follows that

\lVert g(x)\rVert^{2}=\big\lparen 1+\tfrac{1}{d}\big\rparen\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}x_{i}^{2}\geq 2+\tfrac{2}{d},

as required for (
4.1
) to hold.
We may in particular note that
\sqrt{2+\tfrac{2}{d}}\geq 1
, and so we do indeed find that
\beta_{0}=L
suffices, completing the proof of
Lemma

4.1
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

Assume for contradiction that
\theta<\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
and the codimension of
W_{\theta}
is
0
.
Then there are points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}\in X
such that, for all
i
, we have
\theta(x_{i})\leq\theta
, and the parallelotope
P
with vertex set

V(P)=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}g(x_{i})\mathbin{\colon}\xi\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}\bigg\},

has non-zero volume in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We first bound
\text{Vol}(P)
from below.
We note that the set

\Big\{\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{x}g(x)\mathbin{\colon}\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{X}\Big\}

forms a lattice
\Lambda
, and we know from (
4.1
) that the minimal distance between two points of
\Lambda
is greater than 1, and all vertices of
P
lie in
\Lambda
.
The volume of
P
is minimised when it is a fundamental domain of
\Lambda
.
We know that the volume of a regular simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit side length is
\sqrt{(d+1)2^{-d}}/d!
, and so when the side length is
\sqrt{2(d+1)d^{-1}}
, the volume is
\sqrt{(d+1)^{d+1}d^{-d}}/d!
.
The fundamental domain is a parallelotope with volume precisely
d!
times that of this simplex, and thus

\text{Vol}(P)\geq\sqrt{(d+1)^{d+1}d^{-d}}>1.

We now bound
\text{Vol}(P)
from above by the volume of its cross-section along the normal to
w
times the width in direction
w
.
We now claim that

(4.2)

\max\{\lVert g(x)\rVert\mathbin{\colon}x\in X\}\leq 4r.

Indeed, we know by definition that
g(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}x_{i}(j)s_{j}
, where we are writing
x_{i}(j)
to represent the
j^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
x_{i}
. Then, the facts that
\lVert s_{j}\rVert=1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lvert x_{i}(j)\rvert\leq 4(r-1)\leq 4r
allow us to conclude (
4.2
).

Thus the furthest some vertex
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}g(x_{i})
of
P
can be from
0
is at most
4dr
, and so the cross-section of
P
has volume at most
(8dr)^{d-1}
.

For all
x_{i}
, since
\theta(x_{i})\leq\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
, we have from (
3.3
) that

\frac{|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|}{\lVert g(x_{i})\rVert{\lVert w\rVert}}\leq\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1}).

Combining the above with inequality (
4.2
), we find that
|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|\leq 4r\lVert w\rVert\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
. However, every vertex of
P
is a sum of at most
d
of these
x_{i}
so if
x
is a vertex of
P
, we have
|\langle x,w\rangle|\leq 4dr\lVert w\rVert\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
. The width of
P
in direction
w
is thus at most
8dr\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1})
. We thus have

1<\text{Vol}(P)\leq(8dr)^{d}\sin^{-1}(\beta_{1}),

and hence, since
\beta_{1}=(8dr)^{-d}
, we obtain a contradiction and thus the codimension of
W_{\theta}
must be at least
1
. Thus
\beta_{1}=(8dr)^{-d}
suffices (and we note that
\beta_{1}\leq 1/2
, so the assumption of
3.10
is satisfied, as discussed at the end of
Section

3
.)
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

We first note that

(4.3)

\sin(\phi)=\frac{\lVert\pi_{W_{\theta}}(w)\rVert}{\lVert w\rVert},

and so we will find an upper bound on
\lVert\pi_{W_{\theta}}(w)\rVert
.
Indeed, if
y_{1},\dotsc,y_{m}
is an orthonormal basis for
\text{span}(W_{\theta})
, then

(4.4)

\lVert\pi_{W_{\theta}}(w)\rVert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\langle y_{i},w\rangle|.

We therefore take some points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m}\in X
such that
g(x_{1}),\dotsc,g(x_{m})
are a basis for
W_{\theta}
and apply Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation so that
y_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}a_{ij}g(x_{j})
is an orthogonal basis, and track upper bounds for the coefficients
a_{ij}
.
These upper bounds will be derived in turn from a parameter
D
, defined as follows.

We let
D
be the minimum distance from any point in
g(X)
to any hyperplane spanned by points in
g(X)
.
This is the minimum height of a non-degenerate simplex in
g(X)
of any dimension at most
d
.
Since the base of such a simplex consists of
d-1
points of norm at most
4r
, the base has area at most
(8r)^{d-1}
.
We recall from the proof of
Lemma

4.2
that the volume of such a simplex is strictly greater than
1/d!
, and so the height is at least
(d!(8r)^{d-1})^{-1}
.
Thus

(4.5)

D\geq\frac{1}{d!(8r)^{d-1}}\geq(8dr)^{-d}.

Let
g(x_{1}),\dotsc,g(x_{m})
be a basis for
W_{\theta}
consisting of points in
g(X)
.
We now apply Gram-Schmidt orthonormalisation to obtain an orthonormal basis
y_{1},\dotsc,y_{m}
for
W_{\theta}
satisfying

(4.6)

y_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}a_{ij}g(x_{j})

for some constants
a_{ij}
.
We seek to bound these coefficients.

Let
b_{ij}=\langle g(x_{i}),y_{j}\rangle
, so that
g(x_{i})-\sum_{j<i}b_{ij}y_{j}
is orthogonal to all of
y_{1},\dotsc,y_{i-1}
, and so we have, for all
i
, that

(4.7)

g(x_{i})-\sum_{j<i}b_{ij}y_{j}=\Big\lVert g(x_{i})-\sum_{j<i}b_{ij}y_{j}\Big\rVert y_{i}

is a multiple of
y_{i}
.
Since

(4.8)

\lVert g(x_{i})\rVert\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lvert x_{i}(j)\rvert\,\lVert s_{j}\rVert\leq 4r

and
y_{j}
is a unit vector, we have

(4.9)

\lvert b_{ij}\rvert\leq 4r\;\text{ for all }\;i,j

We may then note that, for a fixed index
p
, the vector
g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}
is the altitude of a simplex in
g(X)
, and so

(4.10)

\Big\lVert g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}\Big\rVert\geq D.

We may now rewrite equality (
4.7
) using (
4.6
) to find that

(4.11)

\Big\lVert g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}\Big\rVert\sum_{i\leq p}a_{pi}\,g(x_{i})=g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}=g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}\sum_{j\leq i}b_{pi}\,a_{ij}\,g(x_{j}).

Recalling that
g(x_{1}),\dotsc,g(x_{m})
is a basis, and therefore linearly independent, we may equate coefficients of
g(x_{i})
in (
4.11
).
We may thus deduce that, for all fixed
q<p
, we have

(4.12)

a_{pq}\Big\lVert g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}\Big\rVert=-\sum_{i=q}^{p-1}b_{pi}a_{iq}.

Moreover, by considering the coefficients of
g(x_{p})
in (
4.11
), we may deduce that

\Big\lVert g(x_{p})-\sum_{i<p}b_{pi}y_{i}\Big\rVert a_{pp}=1,

and so inequality (
4.10
) tells us that

(4.13)

\lvert a_{pp}\rvert\leq D^{-1}.

We may now apply inequalities (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) to deduce from (
4.12
) that, again for all
p
and
q
with
q<p
,

(4.14)

\lvert a_{pq}\rvert\leq 4rD^{-1}\sum_{i=q}^{p-1}\lvert a_{iq}\rvert\leq 4prD^{-1}\max_{i<p}\lvert a_{iq}\rvert.

A simple induction applied to (
4.14
) then shows us that

(4.15)

\lvert a_{pq}\rvert\leq(4prD^{-1})^{p-q}\lvert a_{qq}\rvert\leq\big\lparen 4mrD^{-1}\big\rparen^{m},

where we have used from (
4.13
) that
\lvert a_{qq}\rvert\leq 4mrD^{-1}
.

We can now combine the above results to produce the desired upper-bound on
\sin(\phi)/\sin(\theta)
.

First, we may rearrange the definition (
3.3
) of
W_{\theta}
and apply inequality (
4.8
) to find that, for all
i
,

\sin(\theta)\geq\frac{|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|}{\lVert g(x_{i})\rVert\lVert w\rVert}\geq\frac{|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|}{4r\lVert w\rVert}.

Next, we may use inequality (
4.4
) along with equality (
4.3
) to find that

\sin(\phi)=\frac{\lVert\pi_{W_{\theta}}(w)\rVert}{\lVert w\rVert}\leq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\langle y_{j},w\rangle|}{\lVert w\rVert},

and thus, combining the above two inequalities,

(4.16)

\frac{\sin(\phi)}{\sin(\theta)}\leq\frac{4r\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\langle y_{j},w\rangle|}{\max_{i}|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|}.

We know from (
4.6
) that

|\langle y_{j},w\rangle|\leq\sum_{\ell\leq j}a_{j\ell}|\langle g(x_{\ell}),w\rangle|\leq m\,\max_{j,\ell}\lvert a_{j\ell}\rvert\;\max_{i}|\langle g(x_{i}),w\rangle|,

which, when combined with inequalities (
4.16
) and (
4.15
), tells us that

\frac{\sin(\phi)}{\sin(\theta)}\leq 4rm^{2}\big\lparen 4mrD^{-1}\big\rparen^{m},

which is the constant bound we require.
We may recall that
m\leq d
and substitute in the lower bound for
D
found in (
4.5
) to conclude that we may take

\eta\leq 4d^{2}r\big\lparen 4dr(8dr)^{d}\big\rparen^{d}\leq(8dr)^{3d^{2}}.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

4.3
.
∎

It remains to compute the bound on the constant
C
in
Theorem

3.1
.

In the proof of
Theorem

3.1
it was necessary only that
\lVert w\rVert
satisfies the assumption in
3.10
and that
\lVert w\rVert>2\alpha/n
. Thus, to obtain a value of
C
, we need only that if
\lVert w\rVert\geq C
then
\lVert w\rVert
satisfies the assumption in
3.10
. Thus we can take

C=\frac{\beta_{0}\eta^{|X|-1}}{\beta_{1}},

for
\beta_{0}
,
\beta_{1}
, and
\eta
as in the above three lemmas.

Since we have
|X|<(4r)^{k}=(4r)^{d+1}
, we can take

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=8(r-1)^{2}\Big\lparen(8dr)^{3d^{2}}\Big\rparen^{(4r)^{d+1}}(8dr)^{d}

\displaystyle\leq(8dr)^{2+3d^{2}(4r)^{d+1}+d}

\displaystyle\leq(8dr)^{(8dr)^{d+1}-1},

as claimed in
Theorem

3.1
.

5 Moving from a clique-factor to an
H
-factor

In this section, we deduce
Theorem

1.2
from
Theorem

3.1
.
We know from
Theorem

3.1
that there is a constant
C_{K_{r}}
depending on
d,k,r,S
such that, for every integer
n
and colouring
c\colon E(K_{nr})\to S
such that
\lVert\sum_{e\in E(K_{nr})}c(e)\rVert\leq\alpha
, there is a
K_{r}
-factor
F^{\prime}
of
K_{nr}
such that

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F^{\prime})}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq C_{K_{r}}+\frac{2\alpha}{n}.

Given such a
K_{r}
-factor
F^{\prime}
, let
J_{1},\dotsc,J_{n}
be the copies of
K_{r}
in
F^{\prime}
.
Each
J_{i}
inherits a colouring
c_{i}\colon E(K_{r})\to S
induced by
c
.
As
\lvert S\rvert=k
, there are at most
k^{\binom{r}{2}}
distinct colourings amongst
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n}
.

We now find an
H
-factor that is a subgraph of
F^{\prime}
, recalling that
\lvert V(H)\rvert=r
and so we can place one copy of
H
inside each
K_{r}
.
Roughly, amongst all those copies of
K_{r}
given the same colouring by
c
, we want to cover each edge by a copy of
H
an equal number of times.
We do this approximately, and then account for the error term, which we can show is constant.

For a given
c^{\prime}\colon E(K_{r})\to S
, let
X_{c^{\prime}}\subseteq[n]
be the set of those
i
such that
c_{i}=c^{\prime}
.
Arbitrarily partition each
X_{i}
as

X_{c^{\prime}}=Y_{c^{\prime},1}\mathbin{\cup}\dotsb\mathbin{\cup}Y_{c^{\prime},h_{c^{\prime}}}\mathbin{\cup}Z_{c^{\prime}},

where
\lvert Y_{c^{\prime},1}\rvert=\dotsb=\lvert Y_{c^{\prime},h_{c^{\prime}}}\rvert=r!
, and
\lvert Z_{c^{\prime}}\rvert<r!
(so
h_{c^{\prime}}=\lfloor\lvert X_{c^{\prime}}\rvert/r!\rfloor)
.

For each
Y_{c^{\prime},j}
, let
f\colon Y_{c^{\prime},j}\to S_{r}
be a bijection to the set of permutations of
[r]
, which of course has
r!
elements.
Then for each element
t\in Y_{c^{\prime},j}
, embed a copy of
H
in
J_{t}
, embedding vertices at locations determined from the permutation
f(t)
.

For the copies of
K_{r}
in
Z_{c^{\prime}}
, we choose embeddings of
H
arbitrarily.
As
\lvert Z_{c^{\prime}}\rvert<r!
, this will only contribute a constant error term.
Let this
H
-factor of
X_{c^{\prime}}
be
F_{c^{\prime}}
.
For any individual copy of
H
embedded in
J_{i}
(a copy of
K_{r}
), we have

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(e)-\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}\sum_{e\in E(J_{i})}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq 2\binom{r}{2}.

Thus, noting that the two sums cancel out when summed over a set
Y_{c^{\prime},j}
in the above embeddings, we have

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F_{c^{\prime}})}c(e)-\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}\sum_{i\in X_{c^{\prime}}}\sum_{e\in E(J_{i})}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq 2r!\binom{r}{2}.

Let
F
be the
H
-factor of
K_{n}r
consisting of the union of all
F_{c^{\prime}}
s. Then, summing over all classes, we have

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)-\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}\sum_{e\in E(F^{\prime})}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert\leq 2k^{\binom{r}{2}}r!\binom{r}{2}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\;\bigg\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}\left(C_{K_{r}}+\frac{2\alpha}{n}\right)+2k^{\binom{r}{2}}r!\binom{r}{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}C_{K_{r}}+2k^{\binom{r}{2}}r!\binom{r}{2}\right)+\frac{2\alpha}{n},

and so

C=\frac{e(H)}{e(K_{r})}C_{K_{r}}+2k^{\binom{r}{2}}r!\binom{r}{2}

will do for a graph
H
in
Theorem

1.2
.

Finally, in the case when
S
is a regular simplex, we may substitute in the value of
C_{K_{r}}
from
Theorem

3.1
and replace
k
with
d+1
to find that

\displaystyle C\leq C_{K_{r}}+2(d+1)^{r^{2}}r^{r+2}\leq(8dr)^{(8dr)^{d+1}-1}+(8dr)^{r^{2}}\leq(8dr)^{(8dr)^{d+1}},

as required, completing the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

6 Concluding remarks

We have proved a uniform upper bound on the optimal balance of an
H
-factor in a
K_{n}
equipped with a balanced
r
-colouring.

It seems likely that the constants we obtain in
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
are also far from optimal, and improving the dependencies here would be of some interest.
In particular, it is not immediately clear that
C
must depend on both the number of colours and the size of the graph
H
, and very little is known about lower bounds for these problems.

There are also many other directions that remain to be understood, and many potential avenues for improving our main results.
Indeed, one may note that, when there are only two colours, significantly more is known.
For example, it is shown in
[
5
]
that one can find colour-balanced copies of spanning graphs
H
with only a bound on the maximum degree of
H
, and one can even take the host graph
G
to be merely a graph of sufficiently large maximum degree rather than a complete graph.
With this in mind, we ask the following question, which, if answered affirmatively, would give a significant generalisation of
Theorem

1.1
.

Question 6.1
.

For fixed integers
k,r,d,\Delta\geq 2
, is there a constant
C=C_{k,r,\Delta}
such that the following holds?
For every integer
n
, set
S\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of size
k
, colouring
c\colon E(K_{n})\to S
such that
\lVert\sum_{e\in E(K_{n})}c(e)\rVert\leq 1
, and every graph
H
on
n
vertices with maximum degree at most
\Delta
, there is a bijection
f\colon V(H)\to V(G)
such that

\bigg\lVert\sum_{e\in E(H)}c(f(e))\;\bigg\rVert\leq C,

where, if edge
e
connects vertices
u
and
v
, then
f(e)
is understood to be the edge of
K_{n}
between
f(u)
and
f(v)
.

It would also be of interest to consider, in the context of
Theorem

1.2
, whether one is required to fix a finite set
S
of colours in advance.

Question 6.2
.

What upper bounds can be placed, for fixed integers
d,r
and fixed
r
-vertex graph
H
, on
\big\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\big\rVert
for
F
an
H
-factor of a complete graph
K_{rn}
with
c\colon E(H_{rn})\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
?
Can there be an upper bound independent of
n
?

There are of course many other potential avenues of investigation besides the above two questions.
For instance, one could ask
6.1
when
H
is restricted to be a tree, or in
6.2
, one could ask for an upper bound on
\min_{F}\big\lVert\sum_{e\in E(F)}c(e)\big\rVert
over all
H
-factors
F
in terms only of
H
and the size of the image of
c
.
