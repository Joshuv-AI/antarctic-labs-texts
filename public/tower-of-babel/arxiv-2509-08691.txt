Power and limitations of distributed quantum state purification
Benchi Zhao, Yu-Ao Chen, Xuanqiang Zhao, Chengkai Zhu, Giulio Chiribella, Xin Wang

Abstract
Quantum state purification, which mitigates noise by exploiting multiple noisy copies of unknown states, has applications in quantum communication and computation with imperfect devices. Despite its importance, the fundamental capabilities and limitations of purification in distributed quantum systems remain largely unexplored. Here, we systematically study distributed quantum state purification under local operations and classical communication (LOCC). We prove that no nontrivial two-to-one LOCC purification protocol exists for three fundamental sets: all pure states, all maximally entangled states, and the four Bell states. This no-go theorem demonstrates that no LOCC protocol can reduce noise for every state within these sets, even probabilistically. In stark contrast, we show that single-state purification is achievable, and we provide an explicit analytical LOCC protocol for individual target states. For arbitrary state sets, we develop an optimization-based algorithm that systematically designs LOCC purification protocols, demonstrating its efficacy through concrete examples. These results delineate the fundamental boundaries of LOCC-based purification and provide practical strategies for noise reduction in distributed quantum information processing.

These approaches are designed to work in scenarios where a single copy of the noisy quantum state is available. When multiple copies are available, instead, it is sometime possible to remove part of the noise through quantum state purification

;

;

(also known as a

\psi=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
into a mixed state
\mathcal{N}(\psi)

Then, the purification protocol is applied to approximately restore the initial pure state; mathematically, the protocol is described by a completely positive trace non-increasing (CPTN) map
\mathcal{E}
(also known as a
quantum operation
) that probabilistically yields a higher-fidelity state
\sigma_{\psi}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})/\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})]

n

\mathcal{N}(\psi)

\sigma_{\psi}
has a higher fidelity to the initial state
\psi

\mathcal{N}(\psi)

F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)\geq F(\mathcal{N}(\psi),\psi)

F(\rho,\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}^{2}
denotes quantum fidelity.

Most existing purification protocols have considered a global setting, in which arbitrary joint operations are allowed on the

-copy state

;

;
Cirac et al. 1999
;
Barenco et al. 1997
;

In distributed quantum computing, it is important to consider a scenario where the state
\psi

are distributed to multiple processors. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, these processors have access only to the assigned subsystems and are restricted to performing local operations and communicating through classical channels.
Up to now, however, this scenario has remained largely unexplored. Is it possible to find distributed purification protocols that purify quantum states using only LOCC operations?

In this work, we investigate the feasibility of distributed quantum state purification under depolarizing noise, which is a canonical quantum noise process defined as

{\cal N}^{\gamma}(\psi)=(1-\gamma)\psi+\gamma\frac{I}{d}
,
where
\gamma
denotes the noise level,
I
is the identity matrix, and
d
is the Hilbert space dimension. Specifically, we consider the scenario where an unknown state
\psi
is drawn from the set
{\cal S}_{P}
of all pure states, and the noisy state
\mathcal{N}^{\gamma}(\psi)
is shared between remote parties. We show that no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol exists in this setting. We further relax the input set to the Bell states set
{\cal S}_{B}
, consisting of four Bell states, and again find that no
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol exists. Based on the no-go theorem for the Bell states set, similarly, we arrive at the no-go theorem for the maximally entangled states set
{\cal S}_{MES}
.
Importantly, these
2\rightarrow 1
no-go results do not extend to the general
n\rightarrow 1
setting. For a straightforward strategy, if two parties share infinitely many copies of the noisy state
{\cal N}^{\gamma}(\psi)
, they could implement tomography to determine the state first, and then apply a purification protocol tailored to that state.
Then, the problem reduces to purifying a known quantum state with LOCC operations. To purify a known state, we construct an analytical LOCC protocol. Notably, when the target state is the Bell state, the task reduces to entanglement distillation

;
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Zang et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2021
. For the state sets with finite size, we further introduce an optimization-based algorithm for designing LOCC purification protocols. These results establish fundamental limitations and constructive methods for distributed state purification, offering a new avenue for reducing noise in distributed quantum architectures.

No-go theorem for distributed purification protocol.
—
We start with some notations. Denote a pure state as
|\psi\rangle
, and the corresponding density matrix is denoted as
\psi=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
. The Bell states are defined as

|\Phi\rangle^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)
, and
|\Psi\rangle^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle\pm|10\rangle)
, with corresponding Bell density matrices
\Phi^{\pm},\Psi^{\pm}
, respectively. The distributed
n\rightarrow 1
purification protocol is denoted as
{\cal E}_{A^{n}B^{n}A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
, where
A^{n}B^{n}=A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}\cdots A_{n}B_{n}
is the input system, and
A^{\prime}B^{\prime}
is the output system. In this work, we only consider the case of a 2-qubit system.

Suppose we have a noise state
{\cal N}(\psi)
, where
\psi
is some pure state and
{\cal N}
is a given noise channel. Let
{\cal E}
be an
n\rightarrow 1
CPTN-LOCC map. Then the output state is

\sigma_{\psi}\coloneqq\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}}{p_{\psi}}=\frac{{\cal E}({\cal N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})}{\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal E}({\cal N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})]}.

(1)

where
\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}={\cal E}({\cal N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})
stands for the unnormalized output state, and
p_{\psi}=\operatorname{Tr}[\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}]
is the success probability of getting the state
\sigma_{\psi}
. If the output state
\sigma_{\psi}
is closer to the target state
\psi
, i.e.,
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)\geq F({\cal N}(\psi),\psi)
, then the CPTN-LOCC map
{\cal E}
is a
distributed purification protocol
for the pure state
\psi
over noise
{\cal N}
. If the state
\psi
is sampled from a set of bipartite pure states
{\cal S}
, and the map
{\cal E}
can increase the fidelity for every state
\psi
in the set
{\cal S}
, then we say the CPTN-LOCC map
{\cal E}
is a
distributed purification protocol
for the set
{\cal S}
over noise
{\cal N}
. The formal definition is given in the following.

Definition 1

(Distributed purification protocol)

An
n\rightarrow 1
CPTN map
{\cal E}
is a distributed purification protocol for the state set
{\cal S}
over a noisy channel
{\cal N}
if the protocol
{\cal E}
can be realized by LOCC operations and increases the fidelity for every state in the set
{\cal S}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})\geq F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi)),\;\forall\,\psi\in{\cal S}.

(2)

There is a special case called
trivial distributed purification protocol
, which refers to the protocol that preserve the fidelity for every state in the set, i.e.,
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)=F({\cal N}(\psi),\psi),\;\forall\,\psi\in{\cal S}
. For example, the partial trace channel is a trivial purification protocol. On the contrary, the
nontrivial purification protocol
is defined as a protocol that can strictly increase the fidelity over some states, which is
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)>F({\cal N}(\psi),\psi),\;\exists\,\psi\in{\cal S}
. Obviously, for a given state set
{\cal S}
and noise channel
{\cal N}
, the
n\rightarrow 1
distributed purification protocol
{\cal E}
is not unique, and different protocols have different performances. To fairly compare the purification protocols, we define the
average purification fidelity
to quantify the performance of different purification protocols, which only takes the successful cases into account. Specifically, sample quantum states from the state set uniformly, apply the purification protocol, rule out all the failure cases, and the average fidelity of the successful cases can be computed by
\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})=\frac{1}{|{\cal S}|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F(\psi,\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}}{\bar{P}})
, where
\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}
is the unnormalized purified state, and
\bar{P}
is the average successful probability. Clearly, the performance of purification protocol depends on the average successful probability
\bar{P}
. Thus, with a given average success probability
\bar{P}
, we define the
optimal distributed purification protocol

{\cal E}^{*}
as a protocol that achieves the
optimal average purification fidelity
, which is

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{\rm LOCC}^{*}(n,\bar{P};{\cal S},{\cal N})

\displaystyle=\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E}^{*})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\cal E}}\big\{\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},

\displaystyle{\cal N},{\cal E})\,\big|\,{\cal E}\in{\rm LOCC}\cap{\rm CPTN},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal E}({\cal N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})]=\bar{P}\big\}.

(3)

The definition of average purification fidelity is also valid for the continuous state set by replacing the summation with the integral. The more formal definitions refer to Supplemental Material
B
.

From the definition, the performance of distributed purification protocols depends on the state set. The most general state set is all 2-qubit pure states
{\cal S}_{P}
, i.e., the universal set. Surprisingly, our first main result reveals that there does not exist a
2\rightarrow 1
nontrivial distributed purification protocol.

Theorem 1

(No-go theorem for the pure state set)

For the depolarizing noise channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma}
with noise level
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there is no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol for the set
{\cal S}_{P}
that contains all 2-qubit pure states.

To prove Theorem
1
, we first relax the LOCC operation set to the positive partial transpose (PPT) operation set.
Then we assume there exists a non-trivial purification protocol
{\cal E}
, and the protocol
{\cal E}
must satisfy a bunch of linear constraints.
By utilizing the symmetry property of the state set
{\cal S}_{P}
and Schur’s lemma, we reform and simplify the linear constraints, and the problem becomes an SDP problem. We then prove that the optimal solution to the SDP corresponds to the trivial purification protocol, implying there is no nontrivial distributed purification protocol. The detailed proof is shown in the Supplemental Material
C
.

Here, we would like to emphasize that the
2\rightarrow 1
no-go theorem for the pure state set
{\cal S}_{P}
does not imply the
n\rightarrow 1
no-go theorem. For a straightforward strategy, if two parties share infinitely many copies of the noisy state
{\cal N}^{\gamma}(\psi)
, where
\psi
is an arbitrary pure state, they could implement tomography to achieve the density matrix of the noisy state. Since the noise channel
{\cal N}^{\gamma}
is global depolarizing noise,
{\cal N}^{\gamma}(\psi)=(1-\gamma)\psi+\gamma\frac{I}{4}
, the two parties can deduce the pure state
\psi
from the density matrix of the noisy state. Now the problem reduces to purifying a known quantum state with a distributed purification protocol. Such a problem will be demonstrated in the next section.

The proof of Theorem
1
is highly dependent on the symmetry of the pure state set
{\cal S}_{P}
. It is natural to ask whether a nontrivial distributed protocol exists if we consider a less symmetric state set. Here we take the Bell state set
{\cal S}_{B}
into consideration, i.e.,
{\cal S}_{B}=\{\Phi^{+},\Phi^{-},\Psi^{+},\Psi^{-}\}
. In distributed quantum computing, operations are applied to the quantum systems locally, and thus it is natural to assume the noise is introduced locally. Thus, in the following parts, we will consider the noise model as bi-local depolarizing noise, and denote it as
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}={\cal N}_{A}^{\gamma_{1}}\otimes{\cal N}_{B}^{\gamma_{2}}
. Due to the property of depolarizing noise and maximally entangled states, applying depolarizing channels
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
locally on maximally entangled states is equivalent to applying global depolarizing channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma^{\prime}}
with parameter
\gamma^{\prime}=1-(1-\gamma_{1})(1-\gamma_{2})
. The detailed proof can be found in Supplemental Material
F
. If we consider the Bell states set
{\cal S}_{B}
and bi-local depolarizing channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
, the non-trivial purification protocol still does not exist. Formally, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(No-go theorem for four Bell states)

For the bi-local depolarizing noise channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
with noise levels
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, there is no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol for the set
{\cal S}_{B}
that contains 4 Bell states.

To prove this theorem, we first relax the operation set from LOCC to the PPT operation set. Thus, the optimal average purification fidelity
\bar{F}_{\rm PPT}^{*}(n,\bar{P};{\cal S},{\cal N})
can be efficiently calculated via a semidefinite program (SDP) as shown in the Supplemental Material
E
.
Then we prove that the optimal PPT purification protocol for the maximally entangled states is trivial, i.e., the fidelity of the purified state is not increased. Since LOCC operation is a strict subset of PPT operation, we directly arrive at that there is no nontrivial LOCC purification protocol for the Bell state set
{\cal S}_{B}
. The detailed proof is shown in Supplemental Material
G
.

If we apply the local unitaries
W_{AB}=U_{A}\otimes V_{B}
to the Bell states set, we could obtain a new set
{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}}
. Since the local unitary transformed set is equivalent to the Bell states set, we could arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 3

For the bi-local depolarizing noise channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
with noise levels
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, there is no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol for the set
{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}}
that contains 4 arbitrary orthogonal maximally entangled states.

Combining Theorem
2
and Corollary
3
, it is straightforward to arrive at a statement that there does not exist a nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
purification protocol for the union set
{\cal S}_{U}={\cal S}_{B}\cup{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{1}}\cup\cdots{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{k}}\cdots\cup{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{n}}
, where
W_{AB}^{k}
refers to local unitary operation.
If there exists a nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
purification protocol for the union set
{\cal S}_{U}
, it means the purified fidelity strictly increases for some states, i.e.,
F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})>F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi),\exists\,\psi\in{\cal S}_{U}
. Then, by removing some states from the union set, we obtain
F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})>F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi),\exists\,\psi\in{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{k}}
, which contradicts the no-go theorem shown in Corollary
3
. Therefore, there must be no nontrivial purification protocol for the union set
{\cal S}_{U}
.
If all the local unitary operations are considered, the complementary set becomes the maximally entangled state set

\displaystyle{\cal S}

{}_{MES}={\cal S}_{B}\cup{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{1}}\cup\cdots{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{k}}\cdots\cup{\cal S}_{B}^{W_{AB}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\{|\Phi\rangle\!\langle\Phi|\;|\;|\Phi\rangle=U_{A}\otimes V_{B}|\Phi^{+}\rangle,\forall\;U_{A},V_{B}\in SU(2)\},

and we arrive at the no-go theorem for maximally entangled states set
{\cal S}_{MES}
.

Corollary 4

(No-go theorem for maximally entangled states set)

For the local depolarizing noise channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
with noise levels
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, there is no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol for the set
{\cal S}_{MES}
that contains all maximally entangled pure states.

Purifying a known state with LOCC protocol.
—
We have seen different no-go theorems for the distributed state purification. Then what can we do if we know more information about the noisy states? The simplest case is that the state set
{\cal S}
only has one pure state
|{\cal S}|=1
and the state is known. This case is trivial in the global purification setting, because the global purification protocol can directly prepare the target state
\psi
. However, such a problem is nontrivial in the distributed computing paradigm, as it is impossible to prepare the state
\psi
via an LOCC protocol as long as
\psi
is entangled.
In Theorem
5
, we show that a nontrivial distributed purification protocol exists if the target state
\psi
is known.

Figure 2:
Diagram for the
2\rightarrow 1
analytical distributed purification protocol for noisy state
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi)
. This purification protocol has two phases. In phase 1, the local unitaries map the noisy state
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi)
to
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi_{\alpha})
. Phase 2 can purify the noisy state
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi_{\alpha})
for arbitrary
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Theorem 5

(Distributed purification protocol for known states
)

For a given state
\psi
, which is corrupted by the bi-local depolarizing noise channel
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}
with noise level
\gamma\in[0,0.4]
, there always exists a nontrivial LOCC purification protocol, such that the purified state
\sigma_{\psi}
has a strictly higher fidelity, i.e.,
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)>F({\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi),\psi)
.

To prove the theorem, we propose an analytical
2\rightarrow 1
distributed purification protocol to purify the state corrupted by bi-local depolarizing noise, which is shown in FIG.
2
. When two parties, Alice and Bob, possess two copies of shared noisy states
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi)
, they apply some local unitaries
U
and
V
on their own systems respectively (as shown in Phase 1), mapping the noisy state
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi)
to
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi_{\alpha})
, where
|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle=\alpha|00\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}|11\rangle
with
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
Then, for the noisy state
{\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma,\gamma}(\psi_{\alpha})
with arbitrary
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the circuit shown in Phase 2 can non-trivially purify it. Specifically, Alice and Bob apply local rotation gates and CNOT gates on their own parts, and
make measurements on systems
A_{2},B_{2}
. Denote the measurement results as
m_{A}
and
m_{B}
respectively, and Alice and Bob exchange the results via classical communication. If
m_{A}=m_{B}=0
, then the purification is successful and outputs the purified state
\sigma_{\psi}
; otherwise, the purification fails and the process described above needs to be repeated. To verify the effectiveness of the proposed protocol, we derive the fidelity of the purified state and the target state
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)
and show that
F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)-F({\cal N}_{AB}^{\gamma}(\psi),\psi)\geq 0
holds for arbitrary states with
\gamma\in[0,0.4]
. More proof details are shown in Supplemental Material
H
.

The conventional distributed purification protocols, such as DEJMPS
Deutsch et al. 1996
and BBPSSW
Bennett et al. 1996
, can only purify the noisy maximally entangled state. While the proposed protocol, as shown in FIG.
2
, can not only purify maximally entangled states, but also works for non-maximally entangled states. Note that non-maximally entangled states are also important resources in distributed quantum information processing tasks such as entanglement catalyst
Jonathan and Plenio 1999
and the verification of Hardy’s paradox
Hardy 1992
;
Hardy 1993
.

Purification protocol via optimization.
—
In a more general case, where the state set contains more than one pure state, i.e.,
|{\cal S}|\geq 2
, the proposed protocol shown in FIG.
2
no longer works, and we have to propose an LOCC purification protocol for every new state set. This motivates us to introduce an automatic algorithm to design the distributed purification protocol. Specifically, Alice and Bob share
n
-copy of noised states
{\cal N}_{AB}(\psi)
, and the state
\psi
is sampled from a set of pure states
{\cal S}
. Alice and Bob apply local unitaries
U
and
V
on their own systems, respectively. After measuring all the systems except the first pair, the two parties check the measurement results via classical communication. If the measurement results are all 0, the purification process is successful, and the first pair state is the purified state
\sigma_{\psi}
; otherwise, the purification fails.

In this algorithm, the optimal local unitaries
U
and
V
, that satisfy the constraints, are unknown. To solve this problem, we utilize the parametrized quantum circuits (PQCs)
Benedetti et al. 2019
to represent the local unitaries
U(\bm{\theta})
and
V(\bm{\zeta})
, and apply gradient-based optimization method
Cerezo et al. 2021
to search the optimal parameters
\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}
in the PQCs
U(\bm{\theta})
and
V(\bm{\zeta})
. The optimization method has been successfully applied to various tasks, such as quantum chemistry
Kandala et al. 2017
;
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
McClean et al. 2017
;
Nakanishi et al. 2019
, quantum information analysis
Chen et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Zhao and Fujii 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
, and quantum data compression
Romero et al. 2017
;
Cao and Wang 2021
.

To optimize the parameters in the PQCs, we have to design a cost function that describes our requirements. In the case of a distributed purification protocol, there are two requirements. For the first one, the average of purified fidelity should be as large as possible, i.e.,
\max_{U,V}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})
. For the second one, the purification protocol should increase the fidelity for every purified state in the set, i.e.,
F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})\geq F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi)),\;\forall\,\psi\in{\cal S}.

Based on the requirements, we have the following cost function

C=-\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})+\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}S(F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi))-F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})),

(4)

where
S(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-ax}}
is sigmoid function with positive real coefficient
a
. The first summation term
\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})
corresponds to the first requirement. The second summation is the punishment term. When
F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi))-F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi})\geq 0
, it implies the purified state has lower fidelity, then
S(F(\psi,{\cal N}(\psi))-F(\psi,\sigma_{\psi}))\geq 1/2
. The more the fidelity drops, the greater the punishment will be. The penalty strength can be modified by tuning the parameter
a
. With the chosen ansatz and the cost function Eq. (
4
), we can execute the optimization algorithm to train the parameters. More algorithm details please refer to the Supplemental Material
I
.

Discussion.
—
In this work, we investigate distributed quantum state purification as a method for mitigating the effect of noise. We systematically characterize its fundamental limitations and operational capabilities. In particular, we prove that no nontrivial
2\rightarrow 1
LOCC purification protocol exists for three representative state sets: the pure states state
{\cal S}_{P}
, the Bell state set
{\cal S}_{B}
, and the maximally entangled state set
{\cal S}_{MES}
. For the special case where the target set contains one known pure state, we construct an analytical purification protocol. For a general set with finite size, we introduce an optimization-based algorithm to design LOCC purification protocols. These results identify both no-go regimes and constructive possibilities, establishing distributed state purification as a viable strategy for enhancing noise resilience in quantum networks.

For future work, it would be interesting to identify the necessary and sufficient conditions for state sets that admit a nontrivial distributed purification protocol. Another promising direction is to determine the optimal
n\rightarrow 1
purification protocol under LOCC constraints, as well as to study the power and limitations under the multipartite entanglement regime.

Appendix B
Formal definitions of average purification fidelity and optimal distributed purification protocol

To fairly compare the purification protocols, we define the
average purification fidelity
to quantify the performance of different purification protocols.
The average purification fidelity only takes the successful cases into account. Specifically, sample quantum states from the state set uniformly, apply the purification protocol, rule out all the failure cases, and the average fidelity of the successful cases can be computed by
\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})=\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}\frac{p_{\psi}}{\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}p_{\psi}}F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)
. Dividing
|{\cal S}|
from the numerator and denominator, respectively, we arrive at
\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})=\frac{1}{|{\cal S}|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}\frac{p_{\psi}}{\bar{P}}F(\sigma_{\psi},\psi)
. Since the output state is
\psi=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}}{p_{\psi}}
, we have
\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})=\frac{1}{|{\cal S}|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F\Big(\psi,\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}}{\bar{P}}\Big)
. Formally, the average purification fidelity is defined in the following.

Definition 2

(Average purification fidelity)

For a given pure state set
{\cal S}
, noise channel
{\cal N}
and the
n\rightarrow 1
distributed purification protocol
{\cal E}
, the average purification fidelity is defined as

\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})=\frac{1}{|{\cal S}|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}F\Big(\psi,\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}}{\bar{P}}\Big),

(13)

where
\tilde{\sigma}_{\psi}
is the unnormalized purified state.

Clearly, the performance of purification protocol depends on the average successful probability
\bar{P}
, which was also discussed in Ref.
Yao et al. 2025
. Thus, with a given average success probability
\bar{P}
, we define the
optimal purification protocol
as a protocol that achieves
optimal average purification fidelity

\bar{F}^{*}
. Since this work focus on the distributed purification protocol, we have to constraint the operation as LOCC. The
optimal distributed purification protocol
can be defined formally in the following.

is
a protocol that achieves the optimal average purification fidelity:

\displaystyle=\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E}^{*})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\cal E}}\big\{\bar{F}(n;{\cal S},{\cal N},{\cal E})\,\big|\,{\cal E}\in{\rm LOCC}\cap{\rm CPTN},\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{\psi\in{\cal S}}\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal E}({\cal N}(\psi)^{\otimes n})]=\bar{P}\big\}.

Lemma 8

{\cal N}_{A}^{\gamma_{1}}\otimes{\cal N}_{B}^{\gamma_{2}}
is equivalent to applying global depolarizing channel

\gamma^{\prime}=1-(1-\gamma_{1})(1-\gamma_{2})

Proof.

Denote
\Phi_{AB}^{MAX}

\rho_{A}=\rho_{B}=I/d
, where

is the dimension of the subsystems
A
and
B

Note that the success case can be defined flexibly. For example, one can define the success case as measurements of ancillary systems are all 1 or all 0. In our case, we only define the measurement as all 0 as the successful case. The algorithm may achieve higher fidelity by inducing ancilla qubits.

The choice of the unitary ansatz should be made carefully, for the trade-off between trainability and expressibility. If the ansatz we choose is very complex and the number of parameters is large, it may express more quantum states. However, at the same time, it will be harder to train, preventing us from achieving the optimal result. On the contrary, if the ansatz is simple and the number of parameters is small, then it is very easy to train. But the global optimal results may not be expressed by the ansatz. Meanwhile, the barren plateaus, referring to a phenomenon in which the gradient decreases exponentially with respect to the increase of quantum system size, is another important problem we have to take into consideration. Choosing a suitable quantum ansatz and setting the starting parameters carefully can also circumvent barren plateaus to some extent
