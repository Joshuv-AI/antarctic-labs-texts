Knotted folds induced by homochiral all-parallel (or all-antiparallel) double-helices
Authors: Stephen T Hyde, Myfanwy E Evans, Callum A R Metzke, Neave Taylor, Ozlem Keskin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany biomolecules and their synthetic counterparts preferentially assemble into double-helices with a preferred handedness and parallelism, such as all-antiparallel, right-handed double-helical polymorphs of DNA and RNA favored under ambient conditions by Watson–Crick duplexing. We analyze knots formed by double-helices which are exclusively all-parallel (or exclusively all-antiparallel) and all right-handed (or all left-handed) . Most simpler knot enantiomers are unrealizable. Among the nearly 500 distinct knot enantiomers with up to 10 crossings, fewer than 10% can be formed by such double-helices. Simplest designs for the folding of admissible knot enantiomers via duplexing of complementary base pairs in single-stranded RNA (or DNA) are enumerated for those knots.Keywords: knots, double-helical assemblies, duplexing of RNA

All knots can be constructed from networks of double-helices. We show that knots fall into two classes: those which can be built from double-helices which are simultaneously homochiral and either all-parallel or all-antiparallel (homoparallel) and those which cannot. Most knots cannot be realized by homoparallel, homochiral double-helices. Given that the thermodynamic ground state of many biopolymers, such as DNA or RNA, is comprised of double-helices with a preferred chirality and parallelism, the most favored knotted assemblies of those biopolymers are constrained to a small subset of all knots. Simpler theoretical designs for the arrangements of complementary base pairs along a single closed strand of RNA which lead to homochiral and homoparallel double-helical folds for all of those knots are given.

Introduction

Knots are an everyday phenomenon in the macroscopic world of ropes and strings. Some molecules also form knots (1), both in vivo (2), and in vitro (3); other molecules, such as topoisomerases, can untie knots (4). Their presence at (bio)molecular length scales begs the question: which knots are most likely in molecular tangles? A simple answer emerges if the entanglement results from folding of a single molecular strand into a double-helix, or a network of multiple double-helices, resulting in a knot. In many cases the constituent helices are all of the same flavor: the A- and B-helices of DNA and RNA are exclusively right-handed and all-antiparallel (5, 6)), A- and B-forms of amylose molecules (the major component of starch) contain parallel, right-handed double-helices (7), and synthetic foldamers and helicates such as aromatic oligoamides, which assemble into antiparallel left-handed double-helices (8). More complex “branched” double-helices, comprised of multiple helices fused at three-way (or higher) junctions are also possible.

A single closed helically wound strand can form a so-called torus knot (9). Torus knots are wound around a single helical axis, which is unbranched. Introducing three-way (or higher) strand junctions allows us to wind a double-helix around a set of axes whose edges form a branched network. Any such “branched” double-helix is built from simpler constituent double-helices, which are free of strand junctions are wound around just one of the edges of the network. Branched double-helices may also form knots if the parities of twists on constituent helices are set to ensure the winding is composed of just one closed string. In general, those knots are beyond the class of torus knots. Here, we explore knots which can be realized by (branched or unbranched) double-helices.

We focus on the subclass of double-helical windings whose constituent (unbranched) helices are exclusively right-handed, or exclusively left-handed. We call such windings “homochiral.” Generic “heterochiral” double-helical windings are composed of a mix of right- and left-handed constituent double-helices. If a knotted double-helical winding is oriented by adding arrows around the loop, the mutual orientation of the pair of woven strand segments within each constituent helix is either parallel or antiparallel. If the constituent double-helices are exclusively parallel, or exclusively antiparallel, the winding is “homoparallel.” Just as branched double-helical windings are either homo- or heterochiral, they are also either homo- or heteroparallel. Motivated by the observation that double-helical molecular structures often favor a specific hand and parallelism, we analyze (branched and unbranched) double-helices which are simultaneously homoparallel and homochiral.

We show that homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings are related to standard constructions of knot theory. Seifert surfaces of knots give all-antiparallel windings (which may be homo- or heterochiral). Gluing a pair of Seifert surfaces along their knotted boundary gives a tubular surface whose skeleton describes an all-antiparallel winding of the knot. Complementary all-parallel windings of the same knot are realized on “anti-Seifert” skeletons, which are generalized 2D duals to the Seifert skeleton. We demonstrate that all homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings can be constructed from planar knot diagrams whose crossings have equal 2D writhe, a feature of a restricted class of knots known to knot theorists as “positive” (and “negative”) knots, as described below. In general, those knot diagrams describe heteroparallel and/or heterochiral windings, but they are readily tweaked to form homoparallel, homochiral windings whose planar diagrams contain additional crossings due to nontrivial embeddings of their winding skeleton, which reticulate higher-genus tori rather than the sphere. Indeed, any positive knot enantiomer can be realized as an all-parallel, all-right-handed (P.R) winding, or an all-antiparallel, all-left-handed (A.L) winding. Similarly, any negative knot enantiomer can result from homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings of flavor P.L or A.R. Explicit constructions of those windings for all admissible knots with up to 10 crossings are given via their skeletons and twist vectors, whose coefficients are the numbers of half-twists per double-helix on each edge of the skeleton, signed according to their handedness. Lastly that winding data are translated into circular fold diagrams and associated fold labels for each admissible knot enantiomer. That data encode explicit blueprints for the arrangements of complementary base pairs along a closed strand of (for example) RNA such that its most stable fold (assuming classical Watson–Crick base-pair duality) is that knot enantiomer and those designs are compared with laboratory syntheses of knots.

From planar knot diagrams to double-helical windings

Peter Guthrie Tait, the pioneer of knot theory, introduced a number of fruitful concepts to explore knots. In particular, any knot diagram—a drawing of the knot in the plane which distinguishes over- from under-crossings—partitions the plane into closed regions, which can be colored black and white so that two black and two white regions meet at each crossing in a checkerboard fashion (10). A pair of “Tait skeletons” can be constructed from the checkerboard diagram, by placing vertices in each black or white domain, and joining adjacent vertices within like-colored regions by edges, as in Fig. 1c and d (10). Two Tait skeletons emerge, one confined to white domains, the other to black. For example, Fig. 1a shows a minimala planar diagram of the negative enantiomer of the trefoil knot (31−), which induces the pair of Tait skeletons shown in Fig. 1 c and d. (Our convention for labeling knot enantiomers will be explained below.) One of those skeletons is branched, with multivalent vertices: its graph contains a pair of trivalent vertices and three edges, forming the θ3-graph. Since that skeleton’s edges join black regions of the checkerboard, we refer to it as the black Tait skeleton. Its complementary white Tait skeleton is an unbranched loop (a 1-bouquet graph, B1), with three edges linked by three divalent vertices. Either Tait skeleton can be used to reconstruct the original knot diagram of 31− as a double-helical winding, as follows. We tubify each edge of the skeleton and merge those tubules into a smooth surface. Since each crossing in the planar diagram is equivalent to the planar projection of a double-helix with a single half-twist, winding a unit-twist double-helix around each tubified edge of the skeleton, then fusing adjacent pairs of loose ends of the constituent helices recovers the trefoil enantiomer, as shown in Fig. 1e and f. The handedness of constituent helices (right, r or left, l) depends on the orientation of the skeleton’s edge with respect to the crossing in the original planar diagram. Each tubified edge of the branched black skeleton hosts a crossing which can be seen as a right-handed double-helix, with a single half-twist, t=+1, as in Fig. 1e, equivalent to the branched double-helical winding of tubified edges in Fig. 1g. On the other hand, tubified edges of the unbranched white skeleton host three co-axial left-handed double-helices with a single half-twist, t=−1, connected in series, equivalent to a single unbranched double-helix with twist t=−3 (Fig. 1f and h). Notice all three unit-twist double-helices wound on the tubified black skeleton have chirality r, whereas all three helices on the white skeleton have chirality l. Both Tait windings are therefore homochiral, with flavors R and L. Each unit-twist double-helix is either antiparallel (a) or parallel (p). Like chirality, parallelism is dependent on the orientation of the Tait edge through each crossing. Inspection shows that both windings of 31− are homoparallel; all constituent (right-handed) double-helices wound on the tubified black skeleton are antiparallel (Fig. 1e), while all constituent (left-handed) helices wound on the tubified white skeleton are parallel (Fig. 1f). In summary, both the branched (black) double-helical winding and the unbranched (white) windings shown in Fig. 1g and h are homoparallel and homochiral double-helical windings, with flavors A.R and P.L,b respectively. The opposite trefoil enantiomer, 31+, can be formed by swapping all over/under crossings in Fig. 1a. By the same reasoning as above, that enantiomer can be formed by double-helical windings on the same black and white Tait skeletons—the θ3 and B1 graphs with opposite chirality to those forming 31−; i.e. homoparallel, homochiral windings of flavors A.L and P.R, respectively.

More generally, any planar diagram of a knot can be reconfigured as a double-helical winding, composed of fused double-helices wound around each tubified edge of a Tait skeleton defined by the planar diagram. Figure 2a and d shows double-helical windings wrapped on their corresponding black and white Tait skeletons derived from a minimal planar diagram of the (achiral) alternating figure-of-eight knot (41). Like the trefoil example, the pair of branched double-helical windings on the black and white Tait skeletons generated by that diagram of 41 are homochiral: one is L (Fig. 2a), the other R (Fig. 2d). Consider next the Tait windings derived the nonminimal diagram of 41 containing 5 crossings in Fig. 2b and d. That diagram gives a black Tait skeleton whose three edges (ignoring the divalent vertices) form the θ3-graph. The edge with interstitial divalent vertices hosts three coaxial r double-helices, forming a double-helix with ti=3 half-twists. The remaining two edges host unit-twist l helices (ti=−1). In contrast, the white Tait skeleton (Fig. 2e), whose graph structure—again ignoring divalent vertices—is that of the tetrahedron edge-graph (tet), hosts a pair of coaxial r double-helices, forming a single helix with twist ti=2 and three l double-helices (ti=−1). It follows that the 5-crossing planar diagram of 41 induces a pair of heterochiral double-helical windings (denoted r&l) on either Tait skeleton.

Similarly, the pair of Tait windings of 819+ in Fig. 2c and f are heteroparallel. In contrast, the pair of Tait windings of the nonminimal diagram of 41 in Fig. 2b and e are homoparallel: the winding on the black Tait skeleton is A; that on the white skeleton is P.

The examples in Figs. 1 and 2 demonstrate that knotted double-helical windings on Tait skeletons can assume a variety of flavors: (i) homoparallel and homochiral, (ii) heteroparallel and homochiral, (iii) homoparallel and heterochiral, or (iv) heteroparallel and heterochiral, depending on the knot diagram. In fact, minimal diagrams of alternating knots inevitably induce homochiral double-helical windings on either Tait skeleton, whereas Tait windings due to minimal diagrams of nonalternating knows are heterochiral.

Tait’s black and white skeletons can be drawn on the page without crossed edges; it is a planar embedding. However, it turns out to be more fruitful to embed the skeletons on the surface of a 2D sphere. Intuitively, an embedding on the sphere can be done by drawing a large loop around the exterior of the skeleton diagram and tightening that loop to a single point, gathering the region of the plane containing the skeleton into the surface of a ball. Formally, the map from the plane onto the sphere is the inverse of the conformal map of a sphere onto the plane formed by projecting all points on the sphere from a source located at the north pole of the sphere onto a plane which lies below the sphere. Under that map, the outermost vertices of white Tait skeletons merge to form a single vertex, as described in the captions to Figs. 1 and 2. From here on, we define Tait skeletons as graph embeddings on the sphere with specific cyclic ordering of edges around each vertex corresponding to the cyclic arrangement of edges on the sphere. (The vertices of those skeletons can be thought of as rigid disc-like hubs whose radiating spokes on the sphere describe the skeleton’s edges.) Graph embeddings are related to so-called ribbon graphs (11, 12). More generally, any winding skeleton consists of a graph, G (whose cyclic edge ordering is arbitrary) and an embedding on an orientable surface of genus γ. Since a Tait skeleton can reticulate the sphere (i.e. be traced on the sphere’s surface without intersections except at graph vertices) γ=0 for any Tait skeleton. Shortly, we introduce other non-Tait skeletons, which reticulate a torus (for which γ=1) or multitorus whose genus, γ>1. The concept of a winding skeleton as a graph embedding is key to describing double-helical tangles, such as knots or links, since the entanglement of a network of double-helices winding around edges of a graph G is usually not preserved if γ is changed. However, fixing γ and the twists ti on each tubified edge of G fixes the entanglement.

Whereas the surface of genus γ hosts the embedded skeleton, the tubified skeleton hosts the double-helical winding. That surface too is orientable. Its genus, which we call the “tubified genus” (gtub) is equal to the cyclomatic number of the underlying graph G of the Tait skeleton. Beyond planar diagrams and Tait skeletons to more complex double-helical windings

Toggling any helical twist axis passing through a crossing in a planar knot diagram from black to white exchanges both the chirality (r↔l) and parallelism (a↔p) of the associated unit-twist double-helix. Therefore if a black Tait skeleton hosts a homoparallel, homochiral double-helical winding (with flavor, say, A.R), its dual white skeleton also hosts a homoparallel, homochiral winding (with flavor P.L). (The trefoil example in Fig. 1 is one example.) Conversely, if a Tait skeleton hosts a heteroparallel and/or heterochiral double-helical winding, so does its dual (cf. Fig. 2).

Clearly, Tait’s construction fails to produce homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings in general. Nevertheless some knot diagrams whose black and white Tait windings are not homoparallel and/or homochiral can be reconstructed by homoparallel, homochiral windings on a dual pair of “gray” skeletons, which include both black and white axes. Gray skeletons can be usefully comprehended as “partial duals” of a Tait skeleton in the following sense (12). Whereas the full dual of a black Tait skeleton results by switching all black edges to white, a partial dual results by switching a subset of the black edges. Indeed, any combination of black and white edges leads to an admissible “gray” winding skeleton, as described below. Further, any gray skeleton is a graph embedding on a substrate of genus γ>0, in contrast to the black or white Tait skeletons, for which γ=0. A knot diagram with N crossings admits a total of 2N skeletons (each with N edges) since any edge may be either black or white. Two of those skeletons are the dual Tait skeletons and the remaining 2N−1 gray skeletons also form 2N−1 dual pairs. If a gray skeleton has (N−x) white edges and x black edges, where 0<x<N, its dual has x white edges and (N−x) black edges. Just as for black and white Tait skeletons (for which x=0 or N), the pair of gray skeletons are 2D duals of each other with respect to the surface of genus γ>0, where γ depends on x. Whereas black or white (Tait) skeletons can be drawn on the page without intersecting edges, gray (non-Tait) skeletons cannot be drawn on the page without edge intersections, since γ>0. That complication makes it more difficult to portray double-helical windings on gray skeletons, but provided we ignore crossings induced by projections onto the page, skeletons and their associated double-helical windings can be usefully sketched on the page, as we show below.

Generic gray skeletons hosting helical windings can be constructed from a checkerboard coloring of a planar knot diagram by a multistep process. Consider a gray skeleton induced by some configuration X of black and white edges. As for the Tait skeleton, each of those edges passes through a single crossing of the knot diagram and describes a twist axis for an associated double-helix of unit twist, either positive or negative. One further step leads to black, white or gray winding skeletons for any setting. Shrink each of the C(X) (green) circles to a disc-like vertex, retaining its connections to bridging (yellow) edges. That operation morphs the trivalent skeleton to a multivalent skeleton with C(X) (green) vertices (each a shrunken disc) and N (yellow) edges. In order to conserve the winding trajectory of the knotted wound strand from one tubified edge of the gray skeleton to the next, the cyclic ordering of (yellow) edges around each (green) vertex corresponds to that around its precursor circle, effecting a 2D embedding of the multivalent graph. Whereas the cyclic ordering of edges of Tait skeletons can be realized by embedding the skeleton on the page (i.e. γ=0), those for all other 2N−2 skeletons cannot. Rather, all gray skeletons must be embedded on higher-genus substrates of genus γ≥1.

The value of γ for any gray skeleton induced by a specific setting of black and white edges, X can be determined with the help of Euler’s relation for a map on an orientable surface of genus γ; V(X)−E+F(X)=2(1−γ(X)), where E is the number of edges in the skeleton, F(X) the number of faces traced out on the genus-γ (poly)torus and V(X) the number of vertices. E is dependent only on the number of crossings (N) in the original knot diagram (and therefore independent of the setting X). Since there is a single edge for each crossing, E=N. V(X) is equal to the number of circles for the configuration X (C(X)). The number of faces F(X) can be counted from a drawing of the skeleton, taking care to order the edges according to their arrangement around a loop in the preliminary skeleton and to ignore intersecting edges in a drawing on the page, which are an artifact. Any winding skeleton can be realized in 3D space in many ways, guided by its embedding on the substrate of genus γ and any embedding of that substrate in space. A suite of 2N double-helical windings in space follow, formed by winding unit-twist double-helices around each tubified edge of the embedded skeleton. For example, four distinct windings follow from the single minimal planar diagram of 31−, whose projections are shown in Fig. 4m–p, along with their associated parallelism and chirality. (The apparent crossings in the windings in Fig. 4o and p are unavoidable, given that their gray skeletons lie on γ(X)=1 tori.) The parallelism and chirality of double-helical windings on distinct (gray or black) skeletons vary, since twist axes associated with each crossing (the state X) differ between skeletons.

Equipped with winding skeletons for all 2N settings of X, we now explore the possibility of generating knotted double-helical windings which are simultaneously homoparallel and homochiral. Recall that the chirality of any unit-twist double-helix associated with a crossing in a knot diagram can be toggled between l and r by toggling the skeleton edge through that crossing between black and white, from which it follows that an exclusively homochiral double-helical winding—either L or R can be constructed from any knot diagram of any knot enantiomer, whether that diagram is alternating, or minimal or neither.d Similarly, the parallelism at any crossing can be switched between parallel and antiparallel by toggling the skeleton edge from back to white, so any knot diagram admits a setting of edges X which induces an all antiparallel double-helical winding (or all-parallel, P, for the dual setting X′).

The skeleton hosting the all-antiparallel (A) double-helical winding is recognized in knot theory in another guise. It is identical to the “Seifert graph” (13), from which a “Seifert surface” can be constructed by replacing its vertices by discs and its edges by twisted ribbons. Indeed, the Seifert graph is constructed from a planar knot diagram by a specific subset of the smoothings introduced above (Fig. 3). Those Seifert smoothings are those whose concave arcs inherit a coherent orientation from the orientation of their precursor arcs, corresponding to the cases in Fig. 3a, c, e, g. More generally, any skeleton hosting a double-helical winding can be considered as the “spine” of two surfaces: the oriented, boundary-free surface of genus gtub(X), formed by tubification of that skeleton, or the bounded “ribbon surface” of genus g(X), which may be oriented or—more commonly—nonoriented. A generic ribbon surface is formed by attaching a ribbon to each edge j of the skeleton, then twisting the ribbon by tj half-turns, where tj is the number of half-twists which result by merging all coaxial unit-twist helices wound on the tubified edge j. Although the ribbon surface formed from the A skeleton (the Seifert surface) is orientable, all other ribbon surfaces derived from the remaining 2N−1 skeletons are nonorientable, since the presence of even a single parallel double-helix in the winding renders the surface nonorientable. (That follows from the requirement that any strand must exit a crossing then traverse an odd number of other crossings before returning to the original crossing in order to form a parallel double-helix at that crossing. The twisted ribbons contained in that closed path therefore contain an odd number of twists, classified in Ref. (15) as a type-1 walk, which implies that the ribbon surface is nonorientable.)

Among the (2N) tubified skeletons related to any knot diagram (all of which are orientable), the dual of the Seifert-skeleton (which we call the “anti-Seifert” skeleton) is of interest, since its tubification hosts an all-parallel double-helical winding. Since the A skeleton has even tubified genus, Eq. 4 implies that the tubified genus of the P skeleton is even or odd, of matching parity to N, the number of crossings N in the knot diagram. (That follows from, since the A skeleton has even genus). Like the Seifert surface, the ribbon surface formed from the anti-Seifert skeleton is closely related to the tubified P surface, albeit differently.g Recall that the tubified A skeleton is the oriented double-cover of the oriented Seifert surface. In contrast, the tubified P skeleton can be constructed by gluing the (oriented) double-cover of the (nonoriented) anti-Seifert ribbon surface to an annulus along the pair of disjoint loops bounding both surfaces.h Conversely, the tubified P skeleton remains intact on cutting around the knotted double-helical winding on its surface (e.g. Fig. 6b), in contrast to the tubified A skeleton, which falls into two pieces.

Knots induced by homoparallel, homochiral branched double-helices

It turns out that the construction of generic winding skeletons leads to a simple criterion which determines whether a knot (or its enantiomer) can be realized by double-helical windings which are simultaneously homoparallel and homochiral. To explain that criterion, we introduce a third feature of knot crossings in addition to its chirality and parallelism, namely its writhe. Each crossing in an oriented planar knot diagram has an associated 2D writhe, wr, which can assume two possible values: wr=+1 (positive writhe) or wr=−1 (negative writhe), as illustrated in Figure 3. Note that crossings with wr=+1 are either (i) parallel and right-handed p.r or (ii) antiparallel and left-handed (a.l), whereas crossings with wr=−1 are (iii) antiparallel and right-handed (a.r) or (iv) parallel and left-handed (p.l). In contrast to chirality and parallelism, switching the color of a skeleton edge (black↔white) conserves the writhe associated with that crossing, wr.

Consider first a planar knot diagram that contains both positive and negative writhes. The unit-twist helices associated with positive writhes have flavors p.r and/or a.l whereas the constituent helices with negative writhe have flavors p.l and/or a.r. Clearly, no amount of edge switching can result in homoparallel, homochiral windings, since no specific flavor—whether p.r, a.l, p.l, or a.r—is common to both positive and negative writhes. On the other hand, an isowrithe planar knot diagram, all of whose crossings have identical writhe can induce homoparallel, homochiral windings. Either all of its crossings are related to helices with the same flavor, in which case the associated winding is already homoparallel and homochiral, or helices of different flavors are present. In the latter case, those flavors must be some combination of p.r and a.l of all writhes are equal to +1. Switching edges associated with the p.r helices renders the entire winding homoparallel and homochiral (with flavor A.L). Alternatively, switching the subset of a.l edges realizes a second solution, with flavor P.R. Similarly, isowrithe diagrams whose writhes are all equal to −1 allow homoparallel, homochiral windings, with flavors A.R or P.L. Conversely, consider any homoparallel, homochiral winding. In that scenario, all crossings share the same flavor, one of p.r, a.l, p.l, or a.r. It follows that all crossings have equal writhe. (For example, any crossing in a homoparallel, homochiral P.R winding must be of the type in Fig. 3b or that in Fig. 3f, both of which have the same writhe, wr=+1.)

We note in passing that the sign of W for a minimal diagram of a knot affords a useful convention to distinguish many (but far from all) chiral knot enantiomers, including those which result from homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings. Planar oriented diagrams of knot enantiomers with positive W characterize the “+” knot enantiomer, such as 819+ in Fig. 2b whereas negative W characterizes the “−” knot enantiomer, e.g. 31− in Fig. 1. (This convention is consistent with that in Ref. (22).) Since positive writhe is associated with p.r and a.l crossings and negative writhe with p.l and a.r flavors, if a knot (K) can be wound by homoparallel, homochiral double-helices, its negative enantiomer (K−) can only be realized by dual windings with flavors A.R or P.L and its positive enantiomer (K+) only by dual windings with flavors A.L or P.R. Conversely, A.L or P.R homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings can only give the positive enantiomer of a knot and A.R or P.L windings result in negative enantiomers only.

In summary, any knot can be realized by many different double-helical windings. Indeed, any planar diagram of a knot with N crossings has up to 2N distinct double-helical windings, formed by combining pairs of strand fragments into different sets of double-helices. (Including nonminimal diagrams for which N exceeds the minimal crossing number of the knot, N(min).) Most of those windings wrap on skeletons which reticulate the torus, bitorus or a more complex oriented surface of genus γ>1. Consequently, they cannot be drawn on the page without spurious crossings, in contrast to their Tait windings (for which γ=0). The overwhelming majority of knot windings contain a mixture of left- and right-handed double-helices, and/or a mixture of parallel and antiparallel helices. Among the total of 249 prime knotsi with N(min)≤10, just 42 can be realized by double-helical windings which are simultaneously homoparallel and homochiral. Counting both enantiomers of chiral prime knots, there are 478 cases with up to 10 crossings. Therefore, just 9% (42) are realizable as homoparallel, homochiral double-helical windings with a specific flavor.

Designing, classifying, and distinguishing windings: duplexed folds and their labels

Each knotted homoparallel, homochiral double-helical winding can be viewed as a fold of a molecular loop composed exclusively of double-helices wound on each edge of its underlying skeleton, such the theoretical duplexed structure of single-stranded RNA induced by Watson–Crick complementary nucleotides arranged along the strand. The fold “topology,” which is the relative arrangement of duplexed domains along the string, follows from the winding and can be conveniently described by a canonical fold label, L (a digit string), or its associated circular fold diagram. Related “chord” or “circuit” diagrams have been used previously to characterize folds of proteins and synthetic polymer chains, among others (26–28). Here we encode a knotted fold by thickened chords within a circle, or ribbons, bridging nonadjacent sites around its perimeter. The circle represents the unfolded (closed, single-stranded) backbone of the molecular chain; each ribbon encodes a constituent double-helix (with parallelism A or P) and signed twist tj of the constituent double-helix wrapped around the tubified edge (with index j) of the underlying skeleton. Thus a skeleton with e edges leads to a diagram with e ribbons. Antiparallel constituent double-helices are represented by untwisted ribbons, consistent with the opposite orientations of each strand within that helix, whereas parallel double-helices are represented by once-twisted ribbons. The width of each ribbon scales with the absolute twist of its constituent helix, |tj|. R helices (for which tj>0) are represented by dark ribbons, L helices (tj<0) by lighter ribbons.

The cyclic sequence of ribbon ends around the perimeter of the circular diagram encodes the topology of its associated duplexed fold. For example, the Tait windings for 819+ in Fig. 9 give A.L and P.R windings (whose topological data are listed in Fig. 9), whose circular diagrams shown in Fig. 12.

Given the prevalence of double-helical folds in biochemistry, it is of some interest to translate these knotted double-helical windings into hypothetical secondary structures formed by folding of a closed single-strand of (for example) single-stranded (ss)RNA. DNA and RNA and synthetic analogs adopt a huge variety of secondary helical structures, depending on the chemical environment and their sequence of nucleic acids. Classical Watson–Crick base pairing results in antiparallel double-helices, characteristic of the A- and B- and Z-forms, of which the classic B-form is most commonly realized by double-stranded DNA. Natural RNA forms a wide range of secondary structures in vivo, containing pseudoknots, bulges, hairpins and stem loops, which are (often structurally complex and dynamic) embeddings of unknotted strand regions (see, for example, (29)). Double-helical domains within those structures are commonplace, where the A-form is most prevalent, with 11–12 nucleotides per full-twist (i.e. twice the number of half-twists, our ti) within the antiparallel, right-handed double-helix. The relative flexibility of RNA allows a single stand to adopt a range of nontrivial folded secondary structures, driven by interactions between nonadjacent nucleotides on the strand. A- (and B-) forms of RNA fold into antiparallel, right-handed (A.R) double-helices , whereas the Z-form consists of antiparallel, left-handed (A.L) helices (30, 31). Therefore, duplexed folds of single-stranded (ss)RNA containing (homochiral and homoparallel) A.R double-helices can be thought of as “ground states” of duplexed ssRNA, ignoring kinetic obstacles. Most, if not all, known ssRNA folds are unknotted (32) (possibly forming pseudoknots (33), which correspond to double-helical windings which induce the trivial knot, 01). Nevertheless, ssRNA (or DNA) folds resulting in bona fide knots cannot be ruled out, either in vivo, or engineered in vitro. In either case, if a strand fold is driven by Watson–Crick complementarity alone, thermodynamics (as opposed to kinetics) favors homoparallel and homochiral A.R double-helices. Since 3-way (or higher) junctions are possible in folded (DNA and) RNA, branched double-helical windings are possible. If those folds are knotted, they are therefore likely to form one of the knots listed in Tables 1 and 2, notwithstanding kinetic obstacles. And since right-handed, antiparallel helices are favored under conditions, the negative ( −) enantiomers of those knots are more readily formed than their + enantiomers. (Should the Z-form be favored, positive ( +) enantiomers of those knots are expected.)

These theoretical fold designs data are related to continiung efforts to synthesize molecular knots from synthetic DNA, pioneered by Seeman and colleagues (34, 35). The construction strategy captured by fold labels outlined above mimics Seeman’s, who proposed a number of designs to engineer DNA knots via duplexing of complementary base pairs, including 31,41,52,63,76,821,946, and 949 (36). His designs for 31(−), 52(−), and 949(−) knots induce A.R branched double-helices, consistent with the suite of admissible knots realizable by homoparallel and homochiral double-helices listed in Tables 1 and 2. (We note that Seeman’s designs for 31− and 52− correspond to our simplest windings, listed in Supplementary material, whereas his 949− design does not, as discussed in Supplementary material.) Their other designs were heterochiral and required both B- (R) and Z-forms of DNA (L). They also reported the successful synthesis of the 31− knot in a single strand of RNA (37), to our knowledge the earliest report of knotted RNA in vivo or in vitro. More recently, knotted RNA forming both trefoil enantiomers (31+ and 31−) have been synthesized, via engineering of hybrid DNA-RNA junctions (38). However, the resulting RNA knots are not formed by duplexing within a RNA strand; rather they are engineered with supporting DNA crossover junctions, so these designs are more complex than the simpler theoretical schema introduced here, which relies only on Watson–Crick duplexing.

We note that experimental evidence of the formation of genuine knots in RNA in vivo—beyond so-called pseudoknots, which form the trivial unknot—is scarce at best (32, 39, 40). Deeply knotted folds, with higher crossing numbers, are likely to be far rarer than simpler knots, such as the trefoil, 31. Evidently, any bona fide knotted fold can only be realized from an unknotted closed strand by cutting then regluing the strand: a process which is effected by topoisomerases in vivo and known to be possible for both DNA and RNA (41). A simple measure of the degree of cutting and regluing to form a knot is its unknotting number: the minimum number of strand crossing changes needed to untangle (some diagram of) the knot and give an unknotted loop. That number is at most 4 for all of the knots in Tables 1 and 2. In fact, most 9- and 10-crossing cases have an uncrossing number of 3 and a half of those 32 knots have uncrossing numbers of 1 or 2. So, although the relevance of the theoretical branched homoparallel and homochiral double-helical knotted folds explored in this article to biomolecular structures in vivo remains unknown, the topic deserves more thorough investigation at the biomolecular scale in living systems. Further, the design and synthesis of complex nano-machinery relies on molecular catenation and entanglement (42), for which knots provide the simplest examples. The fundamental dichotomy between knots which can be realized by homoparallel, homochiral double-helices and those which cannot is therefore likely to be of broad relevance.
