Effectiveness of Snoezelen Therapy in a Sensory-adapted Dental Environment on Anxiety of 6–12-year-old Children Undergoing Dental Treatment: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Varunika V Sahai, Shivayogi M Hugar, Niraj S Gokhale, Chandrashekhar Badakar, Krishna Kajave, Snehal S Kumthekar
Published: 2026

Introduction

Dental anxiety (DA) has a negative impact on the mental, physical, emotional, and psychological health of the child, which harmfully affects children's oral health-related quality of life. The child's uncooperative behavior leads to prolonged appointment duration, which is even more stressful for the child. It results in an overall unpleasant experience primarily for the child as well as the dentist. This leads to complete avoidance of dental visits, leading to neglected dental care. Thus, DA negatively impacts the dental treatment quality.1

A study on 400 children showed that anxiety rates were highest in children aged 6–12 years. Where approximately 61.5% had severe DA and 23% had mild-to-moderate anxiety.2

Dental anxiety can be managed by nonpharmacological or pharmacological interventions. Nonpharmacological behavior guidance techniques are the most fundamental techniques, which focus on the improvement of the child's behavior by utilizing psychological aspects, and sometimes multiple sessions may be required to sustain initial treatment response.3 If these techniques do not show proper results, a pediatric dentist may shift to using pharmacological means to manage the child. However, pharmacological interventions do not address the root cause of DA but only aim at carrying out dental treatment in a relatively short time.1

As research continues to evolve, there is a growing emphasis on developing innovative, patient-centered strategies to further optimize anxiety reduction in dental settings. The ambiance of a dental operatory plays an important role in aggravating the anxiety of a child patient.4 Visual sights in the operatory, especially the sight of needles or the pungent smell and taste of dental materials and cleaning agents used in the operatory, sounds, and vibrations of the air-rotor can be inducing factors of anxiety in children.5

Conventional behavioral guidance techniques, such as tell-show-do (TSD), modeling, distraction, and play therapy, have shown effective management of children's negative behaviors and anxiety during dental treatment. Even though these approaches are valuable chairside, they often fail to address one of the most critical factors of provoking anxiety in children, i.e., the environment of the dental office itself. The physical surroundings, atmosphere, and even the sensory stimuli within the dental setting can significantly influence a child's emotional response. Addressing the dental office environment by creating a more child-friendly, comforting, and less intimidating space could play a key role in alleviating anxiety. It helps in ameliorating the complete dental experience for children.5

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) has advocated various newer nonpharmacological behavior guidance techniques for management of kids having DA and kids with special healthcare needs. These techniques are dental treatments of a child in a sensory-adapted dental environment (SADE) inspired by the Snoezelen environment.6

It is a novel technique to reduce DA in children. Sensory-based treatment aims to enhance sensory processing and self-regulation, upsurge adaptive function, and alter responses to external stimuli. In SADE, the child is presented with a controlled multisensory stimuli room, which is a combination of specialized dimmed lighting, calming music, and pleasant smells addressing the visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile, and gustatory stimuli.7 The Snoezelen therapy activates the primary senses of vision, tactile, and aroma, along with patient-centered therapy.8 It produces a psychologically optimistic impact on the child, leading to a tranquil state of mind, causing alleviation of anxiety.9 The Snoezelen environment is a combination of visual stimulation via chromotherapy, auditory stimulation via audio therapy, olfactory stimulation via aromatherapy, tactile stimulation via play therapy, and gustatory stimulation.

In order to acknowledge the inimitable challenges posed by pediatric dentists to the problem of DA, this trial was designed to provide insights on the efficacy of a SADE on the DA of young kids undergoing dental procedures. Hence, an attempt was made to carry out research that compares the conventional behavior guidance techniques, which involve the utilization of a special effects environment.

Aim

To evaluate and compare the effectiveness of a SADE and regular dental environment (RDE) to alleviate anxiety in children undergoing dental treatment.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was an in vivo, randomized controlled, double-arm, parallel-group study conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research and Ethics Committee with research number ECI/NEW/INST/2021/2435. Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the institutional review board. The trial was registered in Clinical Trial Registration of India (registration number: CTRI/2024/04/065178). The trial was conducted over a period of 6 months from August 2024 to January 2025.

Randomization and Blinding

All 60 samples were allocated randomly into two equal groups of 30 each by simple random sampling using the lottery method to ensure randomization. The study was a single-blinded study where the statistician was blinded.

The air diffuser and Bluetooth speaker were switched off. No toys were provided to the child during dental treatment. Clinical Procedure

The principal investigator (PI) performed dental treatment following standard operating protocol. Before starting the treatment, TSD was done to explain the dental procedure to the child. This protocol was followed in both groups.

Children requiring dental treatment in group I and in group II were treated in RDE and SADE, respectively. Preoperative anxiety scores were recorded using Venham's picture test (VPT). Heart rate was recorded using a pulse oximeter. The behavior of the child was assessed by Frankl's behavior rating scale by a skilled pediatric dentist.

After complete recording of case history, anxiety and behavior scores, and obtaining the informed consent, the children were allocated to the control or experimental group.

Children having class I caries in permanent molars were treated in both groups. In a group, isolation was done with a nonflavored rubber dam sheet. While in group II, a flavored rubber dam sheet was used. After which, in both groups, class I cavity preparation was carried out using an airotor and round carbide bur. After 15 seconds of etching and water rinsing, a bonding agent was applied, and curing was done for 20 seconds. The resin-based composite filling material (Shofu Beautifil Bulk Restorative Composite) was then used to restore the prepared cavity, after which the restoration was polished and occlusion was checked with an articulating paper for any occlusal irregularities.

Postoperative anxiety scores were recorded using VPT, heart rate using a pulse oximeter, and behavior was recorded by Frankl's behavior rating scale by a qualified pediatric dentist.

Statistical Analysis

The data collected were compiled, entered, and tabulated on the Excel sheet. Then the collected data underwent statistical analysis tests using IBM SPSS software (version 20.0, Chicago IL, USA). Metric continuous data was presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs test was applied for intragroup comparison of VPT scores pre- and postoperatively. While intergroup comparison of VPT scores was done by the Mann–Whitney U test. For statistical significance, the probability value of <0.05 was considered.

Discussion

Regular dental settings often evoke feelings of unease, an unfamiliar atmosphere, harsh lighting, and unpleasant sounds of dental equipment. For children, these components of the dental operatory act as a sensory stimulant and thus induce anxiety.10 Such a setting is characterized by monochromatic color schemes and subdued lighting, creating an austere atmosphere that can exacerbate patient anxiety. These rooms are gloomy and monotonous, which in turn causes an undesirable effect on the child. Especially for a child patient, an environment with a calming effect should be created.11

The development of SADE overcomes problems of a regular dental setting. It is a more personalized approach to pediatric healthcare that involves designing specialized environments that accommodate the unique needs and likings of children.10

A SADE is characterized by multisensory integrated behavior guidance methods, which involve the combination of five sensory stimuli combined in a single environment. Each of five sensory modifications incorporated in the dental operatory has a distinct impact on brain function, collectively contributing to a more calming experience and thus helping in the reduction of anxiety.9

In the current trial, modification of the dental environment was done by using blue, green, and pink-colored projected lights for visual stimulation (chromotherapy); the sound of ocean waves, i.e., white noise via Bluetooth speakers for auditory stimulation (audio therapy); providing the child with stress balls (squeezy toys) for tactile stimulation; lavender essential oil for olfactory stimulation (aromatherapy); and flavored rubber dam sheets for gustatory stimulation.

The colors blue, green, and pink provide a feeling of calmness, serenity, and tranquillity.12 Research on children's color preferences has consistently shown a strong inclination towards the colors blue, green, and pink, and given the high impact of these colors on human psychology, we selected these colors for visual stimulation via chromotherapy in the current trial.13–15

For auditory stimulation, white noise was used. Based on Robert and Sowray's cross-sensory mechanism, audio stimulation reduces oral anxiety by concealing pain impulses.16 Therefore, introducing white noise in the dental operatory masks sounds of the drill. Thus, it was used as part of SADE.

Existing research on SADE focuses on special health children. To order, provide tactile stimulation to these children; a friendly immobilization wrap is employed to mimic a “hugging” effect. This wrap not only provides a sense of security but also ensures children's safety through application of deep pressure, a technique known to be calming and organizing.17 AAPD explicitly advises against the practice of restraints or protective stabilization for children with normal development or cooperative behavior.18 Given that participants in this study were typically developing young individuals, we adhered to these guidelines and refrained from using butterfly wraps. Instead, we employed active distraction techniques using stress balls to provide tactile stimulation, promoting a positive and noninvasive dental experience. The rationale for using stress balls for tactile stimulation was to squeeze the toy and distract the child's focus away from the ongoing dental procedure.19

In the current trial, we have used lavender essential oil as a part of aromatherapy in SADE. Aromatherapy via use of lavender oil has both physiological and psychological effects on the human body. Through the sense of smell, the psychological stimulus produces a physiological effect.20 For gustatory stimulation, we opted to replace nonflavored rubber dams with flavored alternatives, providing a more comfortable and pleasant treatment environment.

In the current trial, we included typically developing children between the age range of 6 and 12 years old suffering from dental fear and anxiety (DFA). Our study centered on participants of this age group, as they possess sufficient cognitive maturity to comprehend and articulate their experiences of pain and anxiety. This also enabled the use of self-reporting scales, which were deemed more reliable due to the children's ability to provide accurate and informed responses. To assess anxiety in our pediatric population, we employed the VPT, a well-established psychometric tool. A pulse oximeter was used to objectively measure anxiety since worry-induced features increase heart rate and are directly caused by sympathetic activation. Therefore, utilizing both physiological and psychometric techniques for evaluation can have a crucial part in offering both a qualitative and quantitative assessment of anxiety.

In the present study, it was found that children receiving dental treatment in SADE showed a significant reduction in anxiety as compared to children receiving dental treatment in RDE. These results of our study were highly statistically significant with both psychological and physiological parameters.

A randomized controlled study on 22 children with autism and 22 typically developing children was performed. Findings of this study suggested that SADE was more beneficial for children with autism as compared to typically developing children. Notably, the results of the study by Cermak et al. diverged from our own findings, where dental treatment under SADE was found highly efficacious for children having DA.8

A trial by Fathima et al. proved that there was a notable change in anxiety levels and behavior postoperatively for children undergoing dental treatment in SADE.10 The results of this study were in accordance with our study, where anxiety levels reduced significantly in the SADE group and behavior changes were not significant.

Ganapathy et al. in 2024 revealed that Snoezelen's approach works well in managing a child's DFA.21 For evaluation of the effect of desensitization by Snoezelen in pediatric patients, Anjali et al. conducted a study in 2023. The results of this study stated that there was no significant difference in anxiety of children when a virtual game was compared with a Snoezelen environment.22 Fux-Noy et al. concluded that the multisensory waiting environment has no significant effect on the anxiety of children.23 However, the results of these studies contradicted the findings of our study, which highlighted the significant benefits of Snoezelen therapy in a SADE.

The limitation of our trial was a smaller sample size, so results cannot be extrapolated to a broader population. Blinding was not done, as SADE was visible to participant and investigator. The futuristic ideas of our study is to investigate the effects of SADE on pain perception of children undergoing more complex treatments like pulpectomies, root canal treatments, and extraction.

Conclusion

Our study thus concluded that a SADE is quite helpful at lowering anxiety levels during dental procedures. This is a new approach that can effectively be used as a less dangerous and more successful supplement to help regulate children's behavior in dental operatory.

Clinical Significance

A SADE stands to significantly enhance patients' experiences during dental procedures. Identifies effective strategies to alleviate anxiety and improve young patients' comfort level and cooperation and enhances greater satisfaction with dental visits. Moreover, this knowledge empowers clinicians to tailor anxiety management approaches according to individual patient preferences and requirements, thereby optimizing treatment outcomes.
