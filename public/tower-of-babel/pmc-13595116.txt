A fatal posterior-fossa chloroma masquerading as tuberculous meningitis after peripartum BCR-ABL1 inhibitor cessation - a case report
Authors: Bhvika Zutshi, Sandeep Garg, Praveen Bharti, Amit Kumar, Annesha Chakraborty, Saarthak Miglani, Ajay Kumar Meena, Arpit Singh Chahal, Sankha Sekhar Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracranial myeloid sarcoma complicating chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is exceptional; when its cerebrospinal-fluid profile simultaneously mimics tuberculous meningitis (TBM), the diagnostic trap becomes fatal. A 27-year-old postpartum woman with Ph⁺ CML, initially presenting in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia lymphoid blast crisis, developed rapidly progressive posterior-fossa syndrome following peripartum tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) discontinuation. CSF revealed lymphocyte-predominant pleocytosis, hypoglycorrhachia, hyperproteinorachia, and adenosine deaminase 33 U/L, with negative CBNAAT, malignant cytology, and flow cytometry; BCR-ABL1 P210 registered an International Scale of 49.196%. Contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated a dural-based, homogeneously enhancing, diffusion-restricting cerebellopontine mass with pachymeningeal involvement, consistent with granulocytic sarcoma. Death from tonsillar herniation occurred within one week, before tissue confirmation. Three imperatives emerge: CSF adenosine deaminase elevation lacks specificity for TBM in hematological malignancy; Ph⁺ CML can undergo myeloid CNS relapse following peripartum TKI cessation; and a posterior-fossa dural mass in leukemia demands urgent tissue diagnosis.Keywords: Myeloid sarcoma, Granulocytic sarcoma, Chloroma, Chronic myeloid leukemia, Lineage switch, Adenosine deaminase, CSF, Meningioma mimic, Ph⁺ leukemia

Introduction

Myeloid sarcoma (MS), a tumefactive extramedullary proliferation of myeloid blasts recognized as a distinct WHO entity complicates 2.5-9.1% of AML cases and may also herald blastic transformation of CML [1]. CNS involvement occurs in approximately 3.25% of all myeloid sarcomas; among intracranial cases, CML accounts for only approximately 10.4% versus 68.8% for AML [2]. When dural-based, CNS MS closely mimics meningioma radiologically, and in tuberculosis-endemic settings its CSF profile may closely simulate TBM. We describe a fatal postpartum case that uniquely combines five independently rare clinicopathological features: Ph⁺ CML presenting in lymphoid blast crisis, presumptive lymphoid-to-myeloid lineage switch at CNS relapse, TKI cessation during pregnancy, a dural-based posterior-fossa mass radiologically consistent with granulocytic sarcoma, and death from tonsillar herniation before tissue confirmation.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old woman, one-month postpartum following uncomplicated vaginal delivery, presented to our institution in April 2026. She had been diagnosed with Ph⁺ CML manifesting as Philadelphia chromosome-positive B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) lymphoid blast crisis in December 2024, treated with vincristine, daunorubicin, and L-asparaginase (nine cycles) plus imatinib maintenance until April 2025. She was thereafter lost to follow-up with her TKI self-discontinued during pregnancy. Two months of low-grade fever, headache, and emesis preceded admission, markedly worsening from postpartum day 3; progressive gait deterioration over three weeks had left her bedridden, with three days of altered sensorium. Examination revealed meningismus and bilateral grade 4 papilledema. A household tuberculosis contact was present.

Investigations: CSF at opening pressure 18 cm H₂O demonstrated 120 cells/mm³ (80% lymphocytes), glucose 34 mg/dL, protein 249 mg/dL, and ADA 33 U/L. CSF CBNAAT (M. tuberculosis), malignant cytology, and CSF flow cytometry were all negative. Hemogram showed a myeloid left shift (myelocytes 12%, promyelocytes 3%) without circulating blasts. Peripheral blood flow cytometry identified approximately 10.5% CD13/CD33⁺, CD38⁺, CD34⁻ precursor myeloid cells with absent lymphoid phenotype (CD19⁻, CD22⁻, HLA-DR⁻), consistent with maturing granulocytic cells in uncontrolled chronic-phase disease rather than a circulating blast population. BCR-ABL1 quantitative RT-PCR detected the P210 transcript at IS 49.196% (observed BCR-ABL1/ABL1 ratio 67.117%); P190 and P230 were absent, indicating no molecular response.

Non-contrast CT head demonstrated an ill-defined, lobulated, iso-to-hyperdense extra-axial lesion (2.4 × 2.6 × 2.4 cm) along the left tentorium cerebelli with adjacent white-matter edema. Contrast-enhanced 3-Tesla MRI identified an extra-axial lesion in the left cerebellopontine cistern along the left tentorium, isointense on T1/T2, hyperintense on FLAIR, with homogeneous post-contrast enhancement, pachymeningeal thickening, and restricted diffusion (low ADC), without blooming artifact, perilesional edema, or midline shift (Fig. 1), favoring granulocytic sarcoma.

Management and Outcome: Given lymphocyte-predominant pleocytosis, hypoglycorrhachia, elevated ADA and a household tuberculosis contact, empirical anti-tubercular therapy, intravenous dexamethasone, acetazolamide and pyridoxine were initiated for presumed TBM, along with hydroxyurea; stereotactic biopsy was planned. The patient deteriorated rapidly and died from tonsillar herniation within one week, before biopsy was achievable. Autopsy was declined by the next of kin.

Discussion

Extramedullary blast crisis in CML is uncommon; in a population-based series, only 1 of 15 patients (6.7%) who developed extramedullary disease had CNS involvement [3]. A meta-analysis of intracranial myeloid sarcomas confirmed that CML accounts for only 10.4% of intracranial cases and identified a survival benefit for patients receiving chemotherapy or radiotherapy [2]. Leukemic cells reach the dura via transit from skull marrow through Virchow-Robin spaces to the subarachnoid surface, explaining the pachymeningeal enhancement observed here.

Our patient’s CML initially presented in lymphoid blast crisis yet relapsed with a myeloid peripheral shift and a presumptive myeloid extramedullary tumor. In the largest dedicated lineage-switch series (n=33), 28 patients switched from ALL to AML; only one was a CML lymphoid-to-myeloid blast-phase conversion [4]. Anastasi and colleagues established that BCR/ABL can simultaneously involve multiple hematopoietic lineages, consistent with transformation of a multipotent leukemic stem cell capable of either myeloid or lymphoid expression [5]. The P210 transcript, the canonical CML marker, present in approximately one-third of adult Ph⁺ ALL — is compatible with this multipotent origin, though transcript type alone cannot exclude de novo Ph⁺ B-ALL. To our knowledge, a CML presenting in lymphoid blast crisis that relapses as a CNS myeloid sarcoma following TKI cessation in pregnancy has not been previously described.

The decision to initiate empirical ATT was clinically defensible but illustrates a documented diagnostic trap. In a 10-year retrospective review of 137 patients with CSF ADA ≥10 IU/L, hematological malignancy was the most frequent diagnosis (26.2%), preceding TBM (19.0%), with ADA levels not differing significantly between groups [6]. In tuberculosis-endemic settings, a genuine household contact and TBM-like CSF powerfully anchor clinicians to an infective diagnosis, delaying recognition of CNS granulocytic disease. A negative CBNAAT with raised CSF ADA should mandate active exclusion of hematological malignancy before TBM is accepted.

TKIs are teratogenic and cessation before conception is standard practice; however, stopping treatment in a patient without sustained deep molecular response risks disease escape. In the STIM trial, 61% of patients in complete molecular remission experienced molecular recurrence after imatinib discontinuation [7]. Our patient’s BCR-ABL1 IS of 49.196% at presentation confirms wholly uncontrolled disease throughout pregnancy, a consequence of TKI cessation compounded by loss to follow-up. Structured monitoring and prompt postpartum TKI reinstitution are essential in any incompletely responding patient.

Dural-based chloromas are typically iso-to-hyperdense on CT and show homogeneous post-contrast enhancement with a dural tail, closely resembling meningioma [8]. Features favoring a hematological mimic include T2 isointensity, restricted diffusion and leptomeningeal extension in a patient with a known myeloid neoplasm; dural-based MS is a recognized meningioma mimic and has been specifically described in this context [9]. Management of CNS Ph⁺ disease requires a CNS-penetrant TKI; dasatinib crosses the blood-brain barrier and has demonstrated efficacy in CNS Ph⁺ leukaemia [10], combined with intrathecal chemotherapy, cranial radiotherapy and, where feasible, allogeneic transplantation. The prognosis of posterior-fossa MS is dismal and an immediate hazard is tonsillar herniation, the terminal event here - underscoring the imperative for early tissue diagnosis and neurosurgical decompression when mass effect threatens.

The central limitation is the absence of histopathological confirmation: the patient died before biopsy and autopsy was declined. The diagnosis remains clinico-radiological. Negative CSF malignant cytology and flow cytometry argue against overt leptomeningeal infiltration but do not exclude dural or parenchymal disease. Future documentation of analogous cases should include stereotactic biopsy with myeloperoxidase, CD33, and CD34 immunohistochemistry, and repeat bone-marrow cytogenetics at relapse.

Conclusion

In tuberculosis-endemic settings, hematological malignancy is a frequent cause of elevated CSF ADA; a negative CBNAAT should prompt active exclusion of CNS granulocytic disease before anchoring on TBM. Ph⁺ CML presenting in lymphoid blast crisis can relapse as a myeloid CNS sarcoma following TKI cessation; the P210 transcript is consistent with a multipotent leukemic stem cell. A dural-based, homogeneously enhancing posterior-fossa mass in a patient with CML demands early tissue diagnosis, urgent management of raised intracranial pressure to avert tonsillar herniation, and initiation of CNS-directed, myeloid-appropriate therapy. Structured TKI monitoring and prompt postpartum reinstitution are essential in any incompletely responding CML patient.

Patient perspective

A patient perspective could not be obtained as the patient was deceased. Her brother, as next of kin, expressed the hope that sharing her clinical course might help other clinicians recognize this pattern earlier; a brief statement is on file and available to the Editor-in-Chief on request.
