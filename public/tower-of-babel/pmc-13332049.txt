Detection of benign prostatic hyperplasia using RGB prostate images and deep learning
Authors: Rohit Srivastava, Rishita Kumar, Surya Kant, Harish Kumar Shakya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the identification of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) through a deep learning-based analysis of RGB prostate histopathological images. Adaptive Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is selectively applied to the L-channel in the LAB color space to enhance tissue visibility while preserving chromatic fidelity. At the architectural level, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are integrated with Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) layers, enhanced further through spatial and temporal attention mechanisms. This hybrid design facilitates both localized pattern recognition and the modeling of long-range contextual dependencies across tissue regions. To mitigate class imbalance and prevent overfitting, the training regime incorporates two key strategies: an adaptive focal loss function and a comprehensive image augmentation protocol. The proposed model achieved an AUC of 0.7220 on the validation set and an AUC of 0.73 on the test set. While the precision for normal tissue classification remained high, the recall for BPH detection highlighted the need for improvement in sensitivity. The proposed CNN–BiLSTM–Attention architecture demonstrates potential as a diagnostic aid in digital pathology, offering interpretable insights and forming a foundation for enhancing histological classification systems. Future work will focus on improving recall performance for BPH detection and expanding the architecture to support multi-class prostate disease grading frameworks. This study utilizes an RGB histopathological dataset consisting of 176 prostate images, each appropriately annotated. The model demonstrates moderate classification performance and a moderate true-positive rate for detecting Normal samples. The model, however, has a low sensitivity in the detection of the cases of BPH as indicated by the relatively low recall values.

Introduction

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) exists as a non-invasive prostate gland growth that affects numerous older males at a high rate. BPH incidence rises dramatically among men during aging because clinical prevalence counts show that 50% of men in their 50s to 60s have BPH and more than 80% of men after age 70 develop the condition. The non-malignant prostate growth leading to BPH enlarges the prostate which generates lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) including frequent urination as well as urgent and inhibited voiding, weak urinary streams, and repeated night-time bathroom use.

Lower urinary tract symptoms from prostate enlargement lead to major reductions in patient quality of life, resulting in extensive healthcare expenses and a growing need for specialized urological care. The diagnosis of BPH needs to be both accurate and prompt, as its detection facilitates timely therapeutic intervention and helps distinguish BPH from prostate cancer, such as adenocarcinoma. The standard diagnostic approach to BPH evaluation depends on clinical history along with physical examinations, particularly digital rectal examination (DRE), as well as tests for serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA), uroflowmetry, post-void residual volume measurement, and transabdominal or transrectal ultrasound evaluations. Medical practitioners face significant limitations in these conventional diagnostic procedures due to subjectivity and inter-observer variability, along with limited sensitivity in detecting early morphological changes in prostatic tissue1.

While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior visualization of soft tissues, its implementation in routine diagnosis is limited by high costs and lack of availability, especially in resource-limited or developing regions. As a result, there is a growing demand in modern healthcare for advanced automated diagnostic tools that leverage widely available imaging techniques to assist clinicians in accurately identifying BPH.

However, the application of such AI techniques for BPH classification using RGB-based prostate histopathological or imaging data remains underexplored. Limited research interest, coupled with a lack of large, publicly available datasets related to benign prostatic conditions, has contributed to a significant knowledge gap. Compared to malignant conditions like prostate adenocarcinoma, benign conditions such as BPH receive relatively less attention in the research community, hindering progress in developing reliable automated diagnostic tools.

The study aims to address the existing research gap by developing a novel deep learning-based diagnostic system capable of identifying Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) from RGB prostate images. We utilize the “A Diagnostic Imaging Dataset for Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia Classification” dataset, which includes 176 clinically annotated RGB images labeled as either Normal or BPH. These images were collected in a clinical setting in Bangladesh and correspond to RGB histopathological images obtained from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides. The staining process highlights cellular nuclei and cytoplasmic structures, enabling clear visualization of glandular morphology and stromal patterns relevant to BPH diagnosis.

Our research serves as a foundation for benign prostatic condition analysis using low-cost imaging modalities that are scalable, making the system suitable for deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments.

The proposed methodology integrates multiple technical innovations. The preprocessing pipeline begins with the extraction of quality-assessed image patches based on tissue content and contrast criteria. This is followed by adaptive Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to enhance glandular structures and mitigate lighting inconsistencies across the dataset.

The classification model architecture combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for spatial feature extraction with Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) layers, allowing the system to model sequential dependencies across patches. An attention mechanism is incorporated to prioritize the most relevant regions, enhancing the model’s ability to integrate spatial and contextual information. This hybrid CNN-BiLSTM-Attention architecture improves both feature richness and decision interpretability.

To address class imbalance—a common issue in medical datasets—the model employs an adaptive focal loss function. This loss function dynamically reweights hard-to-classify samples within each training batch, enhancing learning stability and performance.

Generalization is further improved through extensive data augmentation techniques. These include geometric transformations (e.g., rotations, flips), brightness adjustments, and zooming operations to simulate variations encountered in clinical imaging2.

The proposed model demonstrates modest improvement over conventional CNN baselines while maintaining moderate overall classification performance, achieving results comparable to baseline accuracy while maintaining competitive recall and Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics. Notably, the model achieves this without relying on test-time augmentation strategies or multiple-view data, highlighting its simplicity and efficiency. These findings validate the feasibility of using lightweight deep learning architectures for BPH detection from individual RGB prostate images, an approach that is particularly advantageous in resource-constrained clinical settings and telemedicine environments. Compared to previous studies on CNN-only models for malignant prostate, our new model joins attention strategies to address benign prostate cancer using RGB histology data which is not often examined.

The medical field has undergone significant transformation with the advent of Deep Learning (DL), which enables automatic feature extraction and supports accurate image classification and segmentation. In particular, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have demonstrated strong performance in medical imaging tasks such as tumor detection3, diabetic retinopathy grading4, and lung disease diagnosis5. By learning features directly from raw pixel data, DL models eliminate the need for handcrafted features and enable end-to-end disease detection systems.

Despite the rapid advancements in DL for medical imaging, its application in diagnostic systems for Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) remains underexplored and underutilized. Most artificial intelligence (AI) research in prostate disease diagnostics focuses on prostate cancer detection, while BPH–a highly prevalent but non-malignant condition–has received comparatively little attention. This disparity underscores a significant gap in the current medical AI landscape and highlights the need for further research into DL-based approaches for BPH diagnosis.

Researchers such as Litjens et al.6 developed a comprehensive automated system for prostate cancer detection using multi-parametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging (mpMRI). Their method leveraged deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to reconstruct intricate spatial patterns in mpMRI volumes, resulting in significant improvements in distinguishing malignant from benign tissue regions. Similarly, Bulten et al.7 proposed a deep learning (DL) model for Gleason grading on whole-slide histopathology images. Their AI system surpassed expert pathologists in both consistency and accuracy, setting a new benchmark for prostate cancer grading automation.

In contrast, AI-driven advancements for diagnosing Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) remain relatively limited. Existing studies on BPH predominantly utilize traditional machine learning (ML) techniques with narrow application scopes. Other efforts in BPH classification have experimented with fuzzy logic systems, decision trees, and logistic regression models trained on small, private datasets. These datasets typically lack diversity in patient demographics and imaging variability, which reduces generalization. Furthermore, these conventional ML methods depend heavily on domain-specific feature engineering and require extensive preprocessing steps. As a result, such systems risk introducing bias and exhibit reduced effectiveness when exposed to unseen or real-world clinical data.

To date, there exists no complete deep learning pipeline that employs unprocessed RGB prostate tissue images for the automated diagnosis of BPH. This represents a significant gap in current research. Filling this gap could catalyze the creation of affordable, explainable, and non-invasive diagnostic solutions suitable for real-time use in rural clinics or telehealth platforms. Addressing this unmet need could transform prostate disease diagnosis by democratizing access to advanced screening technologies.

The work proposed in this study introduces a pioneering solution that addresses the above shortcomings. We present an interpretable, lightweight deep learning architecture specifically tailored for analyzing RGB prostate images to detect BPH. Our model integrates multiple key components including spatial feature extraction, temporal sequence modeling through bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) layers, and an attention mechanism for dynamic feature prioritization89. This architecture is further optimized using an adaptive focal loss function to address class imbalance–commonly observed in medical datasets.

By combining interpretability with clinical-grade precision, our system offers both transparency and performance. It lays a moderate foundation for subsequent research into benign urological disease classification and encourages wider exploration of AI applications in underrepresented medical domains.

Patch-based learning is commonly used in histopathological image analysis to process high-resolution slides by dividing them into smaller regions that preserve important diagnostic structures.

The medical imaging research field has extensively proven this approach as effective. Campanella et al.10, together with Hou et al.11 showed how utilizing patch-based learning improves performance measurements for both prostate and breast cancer diagnosis in histopathology work. The system’s training approach limited its model guidance to the parts of the images containing diagnostic value, which led to better performance and more accurate extrapolation to new data samples. The capability to augment data, together with expanded training datasets, supervision, and multiple-instance learning support, makes patch-based strategies particularly valuable when dealing with partial labels12.

Patch-based learning provides clinical interpretations and other distinct advantages, together with its performance scalability features. Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) medical condition that displays localization features of enlarged gland tissue with thickened epithelium and expanded stroma. The tissue slide shows specific features that exist within various smaller subregions, although these features might not appear in their entirety throughout the entire slide. The model benefits from processing image patches since such an approach helps it concentrate on identifying essential diagnostic clues that might disappear in full-image data.

This work extends the quality-aware patch selection method through a developed algorithm for quality-based patch selection. The proposed filtering procedure selects patches from a tissue sample based on the removal of regions with background patterns and insufficient contrast and light exposure conditions. A diagnostic-content-rich area selection process operates with brightness, tissue-density and contrast measures as a heuristic. The preselection process guarantees meaningful histological features for training therefore it eliminates training noise while boosting both convergence and interpretability of the model.

The preprocessing technique that proves most successful for addressing these issues is Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE). The CLAHE algorithm improves regional picture contrast levels, maintaining a sufficiently low signal-to-noise ratio compared to standard histogram equalization processing. When CLAHE operates in small image tiles, it enhances tissue features using adaptive contrast, which proves vital for medical diagnostic analysis. This methodology has demonstrated impressive achievements when used to analyze retinal images, along with analyzing skin lesions in dermoscopy and classifying liver tissues41314.

The histopathological RGB images in prostate are still not easy to analyse as sometimes the slight morphological variation between BPH and normal tissue can be hard to identify. The presence of difference in the intensity of staining, illumination, and the preparation of the slide may add another source of variability, which complicates the process of automated classification. These reasons encourage the application of contrast enhancement methods like CLAHE to enhance the local tissue visibility. The critical diagnostic elements, such as gland dilatation and epithelial thickening, together with stromal expansion, tend to have weak expression patterns that are irregularly spread throughout the section. Bright-field RGB imaging faces two major detection obstacles from lighting inconsistencies, in addition to unpredictable color patterns across the images. The generation of greater visibility for faint characteristics depends on the implementation of CLAHE technology.

The preprocessing framework of our study implements a dynamic CLAHE version. Each image patch receives unique tile size and clip-limit parameter adjustments, which depend on its individual brightness and intensity distribution. The system uses an optimal contrast enhancement method that protects against image over-saturation and detail destruction. Rao et al.14 presented research that utilized an altered CLAHE technique for both improving image contrast and enhancing boundary definition which resulted in superior performance during kidney segmentation with CNN. The research confirms the importance of adaptive preprocessing methods for medical imaging when they are used to enhance deep learning accuracy in organ-level analysis.

The preprocessing system of our workflow enables CLAHE alongside various approaches that optimize data quality. Human liver sclerosing particles will undergo brightness thresholding to erase overly bright or dark areas followed by tissue-content filtering for background elimination and Gaussian smoothing for noise removal and pixel intensity normalization for standardizing input data values.

Results

The following section provides an extensive assessment of the CNN-BiLSTM-Attention model implementation for classifying BPH from histopathological image patches. The evaluation includes a thorough examination of training performance together with classification metrics and result visualization and deep analysis of performance indicators such as precision and recall and AUC-ROC and class-wise analytic.

Training and validation performance

The learning process utilized a total of 50 epochs with a batch size of 16, employing early stopping and learning rate reduction based on validation AUC scores. The peak validation AUC attained was 0.7220, while the final epoch reported a validation accuracy of 63.31%. Discussion

The findings of the research point to the fact that the suggested CNN–BiLSTM–Attention-based model has potential in terms of the classification of BPH by analyzing RGB histopathological images. The model is a hybrid architecture that combines several deep learning elements that examine spatial texture patterns using convolutional layers and contextual dependencies using a Bidirectional LSTM. Also, the attention mechanism forms dynamically weight-based temporal features improvements with the most discriminative, allowing the network to concentrate on the diagnostically important areas. The combination of convolutional feature extraction, sequential modeling and attention-guided learning also helps to enhance the interpretability and provide a more targeted analysis when performing histopathology.

The proposed CNN–BiLSTM–Attention model achieved a test ROC-AUC of 0.73, indicating moderate discriminatory capability between normal and BPH tissue patches. While these findings demonstrate the feasibility of deep learning-based analysis of RGB prostate images, the results should be interpreted as preliminary rather than clinically definitive, and further validation on larger and more diverse datasets will be necessary before potential clinical deployment.

The model achieved a test AUC of 0.73 and high precision for the BPH class (0.92); however, the corresponding recall remained low (0.32). A large number of true BPH instances remain unidentified because the model exhibits a distinctive behavior of recognizing positive samples when it classifies, while misdiagnosing many existing BPH cases. Moderate precision enables minimal false positive diagnoses, yet the low level of recall creates issues since it might delay proper treatment for patients. The model demonstrated this behavior partly due to both restricted sample quantity and natural class distribution imbalance between BPH and normal samples, where we implemented class weighting along with data enhancement strategies. Further development of the model is necessary to enhance its sensitivity, although this work needs to maintain current levels of precision. The comparatively low recall for the BPH class indicates that the model is unable to identify some proportion of true BPH cases. Although the proposed framework demonstrates feasibility for automated BPH classification, the current sensitivity is insufficient for clinical screening or autonomous diagnosis. The present model should therefore be considered exploratory and supportive in nature, with future work focused primarily on reducing false negatives. At its current stage, the proposed model should be regarded as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous diagnostic system. It may assist pathologists by identifying tissue regions warranting closer review; however, the present recall is insufficient for standalone screening applications. Clinical deployment would require higher sensitivity, multicenter validation, and calibration under routine pathology workflows. Although the accuracy is moderate and presupposes that the predicted cases of BPH are typically accurate, the low sensitivity limits the applicability of the model in the screening-based clinical practices, in which the number of positive cases detected is crucial. The work in the future will then be based on enhancing sensitivity by using larger and more diverse datasets, optimization of thresholds, and multi-scale learning of features.

The model training process together with the performance results confirmed the effectiveness of the preprocessing pipeline. The local tissue morphology became more distinguishable when CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) was applied to the L-channel of LAB color space. The RGB format of the dataset benefited from this preprocessing method since it normally lacks H&E stain standardization. During training the extraction of quality-controlled informative patches by a multi-criteria filter based on standard deviation and contrast and mean intensity and tissue content enriched model learning.

Using BiLSTM for sequential modeling enabled the network to detect extended visual dependencies throughout extracted patch sequences because it replicated pathologist diagnostic processes when observing continuous tissue structures. The temporal attention mechanism improves this ability by focusing on feature vectors from the most pertinent patches in ways that correspond to clinical observations of hyperplasia occurring only at particular locations.

Nevertheless, some limitations persist. Despite the use of patch-based learning and augmentation, the dataset remains limited in size and source diversity. Because all images originate from a single dataset and a relatively small cohort, the learned feature distribution may not generalize well to other institutions, scanners, or preparation conditions. Accordingly, the current findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. The research adopts images from a clinical facility but these 176 images create restrictions for model exposure to different visual data patterns. None of the applied patch-based augmentation techniques can replace a large and balanced dataset for complete dataset augmentation. The implementation of RGB images without standardized staining techniques results in inconsistent color patterns throughout the data thereby reducing effectiveness of generalized learning. The present pipeline fails to specifically represent glandular morphology together with nuclear patterns because these elements are essential for detecting BPH during pathology assessment. Future research should focus on developing explicit morphological prior models plus transformer-based global context modules as they show promise for next-level development15.

This study introduces a novel and resilient deep learning methodology aimed at the identification of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) using RGB histopathological prostate images. The complete workflow is meticulously designed to address core challenges in medical image analysis, including visual inconsistency of BPH regions, class imbalance, and intra-image color variations that often compromise model performance.

The pipeline begins with a rigorous patch extraction strategy focused on preserving morphological details and tissue quality. A sliding window with 50% overlap is applied, guided by statistical thresholds on standard deviation, mean intensity, contrast, and tissue percentage. These criteria ensure the selection of structurally informative patches. To enhance visibility of textural features while preserving color fidelity, each selected patch undergoes Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) applied on the luminance channel of the image in LAB color space.

The core classifier is a hybrid neural architecture that integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) units, and dual attention mechanisms. The CNN backbone is responsible for extracting rich hierarchical spatial features. This is followed by a spatial attention module, which highlights the most informative regions in the feature maps. The refined features are then reshaped into temporal sequences and passed through a BiLSTM block to capture bidirectional contextual dependencies.

A temporal attention mechanism is employed to dynamically assign weights to time steps based on their relevance, allowing the model to focus on critical sequence patterns. Finally, the attended representation is passed through fully connected layers to yield a binary classification output indicating the presence or absence of BPH. The implementation of adaptive focal loss played a crucial role in addressing the class imbalance inherent in the dataset. By dynamically adjusting class weights during each training batch, the loss function placed increased emphasis on hard-to-classify and minority samples. This was further supported by the use of diverse image augmentation techniques, including rotation, shearing, and color perturbations, which collectively enhanced the model’s generalization ability while mitigating overfitting.

Empirical results substantiated the effectiveness of the proposed classification framework. The model achieved a validation AUC score of 0.7220 and a test AUC of 0.73, indicating moderate discriminative performance. During training, the model attained a training accuracy of 69% and a validation accuracy of 63.31%, suggesting a stable and moderately generalized learning capability.

In terms of precision and recall trade-offs, the model exhibited high precision scores for BPH prediction, whereas recall values were comparatively lower. This trend reflects a conservative classification behavior that favors reducing false positives over maximizing sensitivity–a valuable trait in high-stakes medical diagnosis. While the conservative approach may overlook some true positives (i.e., false negatives), it ensures fewer incorrect BPH diagnoses, thereby reducing unnecessary psychological and clinical follow-up consequences. Such trade-offs align with accepted practices in clinical screening systems, where minimizing false alarms often takes precedence over exhaustive detection. The collected data highlights both the advantages and limitations of utilizing RGB-based histopathological images for medical diagnostics. RGB imaging offers notable ease of acquisition and compatibility with standard image processing pipelines, especially when compared to hyperspectral or multimodal scanning systems. However, its restricted spectral range and sensitivity to staining inconsistencies pose significant challenges for reliable feature extraction and model generalization.

The proposed framework establishes a foundational approach for future investigations, including the integration of multimodal data sources such as histopathology with genomic or radiological information. Additionally, the framework presents opportunities for extending to semi-supervised learning scenarios using partially labeled datasets, and to federated learning settings that uphold privacy across multiple clinical data centers.

This study presents a preliminary deep learning framework for BPH detection using RGB histopathological images. Although the proposed architecture proves to be an analytical structure on an automated basis, the present sensitivity is not yet strong enough to conduct a clinical screening, and additional research on the subject is needed to enhance the detection of BPH-positive cases. It successfully integrates clinical requirements with computational rigor by leveraging structured data preprocessing, attention-enhanced model design, statistically robust evaluation, and domain-informed engineering practices. Future enhancements will aim to expand the current binary classification system into a multi-class framework capable of performing Gleason grading, as well as develop stain-invariant representations to improve generalizability across laboratories. The ultimate objective is to enable real-time integration into clinical decision-support systems for high-impact, practical deployment.

Methods

Dataset description

This research uses the “Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) Diagnostic Imaging Dataset,” which Kaggle provides freely to the public. The dataset features 176 RGB images from prostate gland histopathological examinations, with BPH and Normal tissue being the two disease categories. The clinical diagnostic center in Bangladesh contributed these images, which provide an authentic dataset that replicates medical image practices and population demographics in the region.

The dataset used in this study consists of two equally distributed classes labeled as BPH (Positive Class) and Normal (Negative Class).

The BPH (Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia) class is defined as the positive class due to the presence of benign glandular enlargement, which may include both stromal and epithelial hyperplasia. In contrast, the Normal (Negative Class) refers to healthy prostate tissue without any observable hyperplastic changes.

All images in the dataset are in RGB (Red, Green, Blue) color format, stored as .jpg files with medium to high resolution as shown in Fig. 5. Unlike grayscale representations, RGB images preserve essential tissue-level characteristics, including color saturation, hue variations, and ductal staining patterns. These features play a critical role in identifying subtle morphological differences between healthy and hyperplastic tissue, which would otherwise be diminished or lost during grayscale conversion. The dataset contains a total of 176 prostate images in RGB (Red, Green, Blue) format, comprising 94 images labeled as benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and 82 images representing healthy prostate tissue. The original sample is thus close to being balanced on the image level though not exactly equal. Several patches are created of every image after patch extraction to train the model. In this step, class balancing is applied at the patch level using sampling and training-time class weighting to guarantee contributions of both classes are equal in the optimization process. The dataset was compiled through patient biopsy examinations conducted under clinical supervision. Pathologists captured RGB (Red, Green, Blue) images from histopathological slides that underwent conventional H&E (Hematoxylin and Eosin) staining. This staining technique vividly highlights cellular nuclei and cytoplasmic structures, providing rich visual detail. The resulting image data clearly illustrates tissue alterations associated with Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH), including the crowding of acinar glands, stromal enlargement, and irregular glandular pattern formations. In contrast, the normal class images contain uniformly spaced glands with minimal stromal cell proliferation, representing healthy prostate tissue architecture.

Because the dataset incorporates both morphological and chromatic features, it is well-suited for deep learning-based classification. The models are capable of learning glandular hyperplasia patterns, variations in epithelial cell density, and changes in staining intensity–all of which are indicative of BPH. The use of RGB imaging, rather than grayscale or binary alternatives, allows the network to leverage subtle color differences and spatial textures that are essential for accurate diagnostic classification.

The data in this study is anonymized histopathological images that are taken out of a publicly available data repository in Kaggle. No personally identifiable patient information is contained in the dataset. In this study, the secondary analysis of the previously gathered medical images was the only type of research that did not require any form of direct contact with human subjects or access to confidential patient information. Hence, no further ethical consent was needed to have the current study.

CLAHE-based contrast enhancement

Important tissue patterns like glandular structure and epithelial textures from RGB slides become difficult to recognize due to contrast variations between uneven staining and scanner inconsistencies, and lighting artifacts. We apply Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) before the deep learning model input as a preprocessing approach to highlight delicate tissue patterns.

Traditional global histogram equalization methods differ from CLAHE because it adjust contrast locally through their tile-based approach to histogram equalization. By applying this method, the approach prevents noise amplification while maintaining vital structural details that are needed for accurate classification of BPH.

Data augmentation

Deep learning models need extensive quantities of varied training data for successful generalization capability. The datasets consisting of medical images together with histopathological analysis typically possess limited quantity and show an imbalance between different sample categories. A lopsided class distribution pattern during model training makes the system prone to develop biased algorithms that fail to properly identify minority categories (BPH or Normal).

A data augmentation method acts as our solution to develop synthetic training samples and expand training dataset diversity. Model architecture

The complete workflow of the CNN–BiLSTM–Attention model appears in Fig. 7. The model sequence consists of convolutional blocks that integrate batch normalization and ReLU activation mechanisms, followed by alternating max pooling operations. A spatial attention module is applied to emphasize important regions within the feature map. The spatially weighted features are then reshaped into sequential representations, which are passed to a Bidirectional LSTM layer to capture both forward and backward temporal dependencies. Architectural configuration

All experiments in this study were conducted using Kaggle’s cloud-based GPU infrastructure. The implementation was carried out using Python version 3.10 with TensorFlow 2.12.0 as the primary deep learning framework, alongside the high-level Keras API for model construction and training.

Data preprocessing and manipulation were facilitated using standard Python libraries including NumPy, OpenCV, Pillow (PIL), and Matplotlib. Training was executed on a single NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU with 16 GB VRAM, provided via Kaggle’s notebook environment. Although the platform does not explicitly disclose CPU or memory specifications, TensorFlow GPU acceleration utilized cuDNN and CUDA 11.x drivers from Kaggle’s backend.

Real-time data augmentation and batch-level loading/shuffling were managed using tf.data pipelines, while ImageDataGenerator was used for on-the-fly preprocessing to enhance computational efficiency. TensorFlow’s automatic memory management system was employed for GPU resource optimization, and hardware utilization was monitored via notebook-level tools.

Model training progress and evaluation metrics such as accuracy, loss, and AUC were tracked and visualized using Matplotlib, while model checkpoints were saved to Kaggle’s working directory to ensure recoverability in case of interruptions.

BPH classification pipeline

Table 4 presents a complete deep learning approach to classify Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) within RGB histopathological prostate images. High-quality patch extraction initiates the workflow, which is followed by preprocessing and augmentation steps, and then proceeds to sequence modeling through a CNN-BiLSTM-Attention classification network.

Each image is divided into fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$224 \times 224$$\end{document} pixel-sized overlapping patches with a stride of 112 pixels. The analysis of each patch requires the calculation of four quality measures: standard deviation, mean intensity, tissue percentage, and contrast. Only patches that meet all specified quality thresholds are retained. The enhancement process for valid patches is conducted in the LAB color space by applying adaptive Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L$$\end{document}-channel. The adjusted input patches are normalized to a stable range of [0, 1] for model processing. The selected dataset contains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M = 15$$\end{document} top-quality patches from each image. The original dataset undergoes multiple image augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, zooming, shearing, brightness adjustment, and color shifting to expand its size. The merged combination of original and augmented patches forms the final dataset, which is then split into training, validation, and testing sets in proportions of 70:15:15. During training, class weights are utilized to address imbalances in class proportions. The core structure of the proposed model consists of multiple layers of Conv2D \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} BatchNormalization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} ReLU \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} MaxPooling blocks, which perform spatial feature extraction. Following this, a spatial attention mechanism is applied, which combines average and max pooled projections using a convolutional attention gate to generate an attention map. This map is then applied to the CNN features.

The spatially weighted feature maps are reshaped and passed into a Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) unit, enabling the network to capture both forward and backward contextual dependencies. Subsequently, a temporal attention mechanism computes attention scores across time steps, allowing a context vector to be generated through a weighted summation.

In the final stage, fully connected layers with dropout and batch normalization are used to maintain generalization capabilities. A sigmoid activation function is applied to produce the probability of BPH presence. Spatial attention mechanism

To emphasize salient spatial regions, a spatial attention map is computed by applying average pooling and max pooling operations across the channel axis:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\text {avg}} = \text {AvgPool}(X), \quad M_{\text {max}} = \text {MaxPool}(X)$$\end{document}These pooled maps are concatenated and passed through a convolution layer with a sigmoid activation to generate the spatial attention mask:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_s = \sigma (\text {Conv}(M_{\text {avg}} \parallel M_{\text {max}}))$$\end{document}The original feature map is then reweighted using element-wise multiplication:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X' = M_s \odot X$$\end{document}

Reshaping for BiLSTM

The attention-refined features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X' \in \mathbb {R}^{H' \times W' \times C'}$$\end{document} are reshaped into a 2D matrix suitable for sequence modeling:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = \text {Reshape}(X') \in \mathbb {R}^{T \times d}, \quad T = H' \cdot W', \quad d = C'$$\end{document}Each timestep corresponds to a spatial location, and d is the feature dimension per timestep.

Bidirectional LSTM

The reshaped sequence is processed by a Bidirectional LSTM to capture contextual dependencies from both temporal directions:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t = \left[ \overrightarrow{\text {LSTM}}(s_t), \; \overleftarrow{\text {LSTM}}(s_t) \right] , \quad t = 1, \dots , T$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document} denotes the concatenated forward and backward hidden states for timestep t.

Temporal attention mechanism

To aggregate important temporal features, we employ an attention mechanism. Each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document} is first passed through a dense layer with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tanh$$\end{document} activation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_t = \tanh (W h_t + b)$$\end{document}The attention scores are then computed using a softmax normalization:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _t = \frac{\exp (e_t)}{\sum _{k=1}^{T} \exp (e_k)}$$\end{document}The context vector is a weighted sum of the BiLSTM outputs:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = \sum _{t=1}^{T} \alpha _t h_t$$\end{document}

Fully connected layers

The context vector c is then passed through two fully connected (dense) layers, each followed by ReLU activation and dropout regularization. Finally, a sigmoid activation provides the probability of the positive class (BPH):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&z_1 = \text {Dropout}(\text {ReLU}(W_1 c + b_1)) \\&z_2 = \text {Dropout}(\text {ReLU}(W_2 z_1 + b_2)) \\&\hat{y} = \sigma (W_3 z_2 + b_3) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y} \in [0,1]$$\end{document} represents the predicted probability for the BPH class as shown in Table 4.
