Ray transform of symmetric tensor fields on Riemannian manifolds with conjugate points
Sean Holman ∗ and Venkateswaran P. Krishnan †

Abstract
. In this article, we study the microlocal properties of the geodesic ray transform of symmetric m m -tensor fields on 2-dimensional Riemannian manifolds with boundary allowing the possibility of conjugate points. As is known from an earlier work on the geodesic ray transform of functions in the presence of conjugate points, the normal operator can be decomposed into a sum of a pseudodifferential operator ( Ψ \Psi DO) and a finite number of Fourier integral operators (FIOs) under the assumption of no singular conjugate pairs along geodesics, which always holds in 2-dimensions. In this work, we use the method of stationary phase to explicitly compute the principal symbol of the Ψ \Psi DO and each of the FIO components of the normal operator acting on symmetric m m -tensor fields. Next, we construct a parametrix recovering the solenoidal component of the tensor fields modulo FIOs, and prove a cancellation of singularities result, similar to an earlier result of Monard, Stefanov and Uhlmann for the case of geodesic ray transform of functions in 2-dimensions. We point out that this type of cancellation result is only possible in the 2-dimensional case.



1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
be an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with boundary. A problem of significant interest in seismology and geometric inverse problems is the unique determination of the metric
g
from the knowledge of the boundary distance function
d_{g}
on
\partial M\times\partial M
. A region in the interior of the manifold with “high metric” will not be seen by the boundary distance function since it takes into account only the shortest paths connecting boundary points. Due to this reason, unique determination of
g
from
d_{g}|_{\partial M\times\partial M}
in general is not possible, and some additional restrictions on the metric
g
are required. One assumption is to restrict the metric under consideration to be
simple
. By this we mean the boundary
\partial M
is strictly convex and for any
x\in M
,
\exp_{x}:\exp_{x}^{-1}M\to M
is a differomorphism. A model
n
-dimensional simple manifold with boundary is a strictly convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Euclidean metric. Another obstruction to uniqueness always present regardless of the restrictions on the metric
g
results from the fact that any isometry of the manifold
M
fixing the boundary will give the same boundary distance function. Due to this, one can determine the Riemannian metric (if at all possible) given the boundary distance function only up to diffeomorphisms fixing the boundary, in which case, we call such Riemannian manifolds boundary distance rigid. Michel conjectured that any simple Riemannian manifold is boundary distance rigid. While this conjecture in its full generality remains open, significant progress has been made in the last few decades; see
[
14
,
12
,
19
,
29
,
34
]
for a few groundbreaking works in this direction.
Linearization of the boundary distance function near a fixed simple metric
[

or linearization of the lens data (which consists of the length of geodesics together with the scattering relation) in the general case
[
31

leads to the integral geometry problem of determining a symmetric
2
-tensor field

from the knowledge its geodesic ray transform

. The geodesic ray transform associates to

I_{2}f(\gamma)=\int\limits_{0}^{l(\gamma)}f_{ij}(\gamma(t))\dot{\gamma}^{i}(t)\dot{\gamma}^{j}(t)\mathrm{d}t,

where

is a geodesic connecting boundary points with

-tensor field of the form

denotes the symmetrized covariant derivative; see (

) below. This non-injectivity should be expected due to the natural obstruction to uniqueness in the non-linear problem caused by isometries fixing the boundary mentioned above. The natural conjecture is that on a simple Riemannian manifold

,

In the above problem, one can relax the assumption that the rank of the tensor field be

(m-1)
-tensor field

-injective. This conjecture has been proven under curvature assumptions that include the Euclidean case

,

]
, for the case of simple metrics when
m=0,1

[

,

, and for the case of simple metrics in two dimensions for

[

]
and any

[

]
. For tensor fields of rank

in dimension

, this problem in its full generality remains open even for simple Riemannian manifolds. Nevertheless, important progress has been made in the last few decades; see
[
18
,
27
,
29
,

,
36
,

,

,

[

]
,

, the transform

acting on symmetric 2-tensor fields was studied from a microlocal analysis point of view. In particular, it was shown that on simple Riemannian manifolds, the transform

is a pseudodifferential operator (

For the case of real-analytic simple metrics, using tools from analytic microlocal analysis, the integral geometry problem was settled (without any assumptions on curvature) in
[
29
]
. A significant breakthrough result was achieved by Uhlmann and Vasy in
[

[

We also mention a remarkable work of Guillarmou, who showed that negatively curved manifolds with strictly convex boundaries, and more generally manifolds with hyperbolic trapped sets and no conjugate points are deformation lens rigid, in particular, boundary distance rigid. For a list of open problems in the area, see
[

, and for a detailed contemporary reference with related results, see

.

One approach to understanding the geodesic ray transform, is through the study of the normal operator
\mathcal{N}_{m}=I^{*}_{m}I_{m}
, where
I^{*}_{m}
is the formal
L^{2}
adjoint of
I_{m}
. This has been the main object of study in several of the works mentioned in the previous paragraph.
When there are no conjugate points, more precisely, when the manifold
(M,g)
is simple, it is known that the normal operator is a pseudodifferential operator (
\Psi
DO) of order
-1
, elliptic on the solenoidal component
{}^{s}\!f
of the symmetric
m
-tensor field
f
(see
[
28
]
for the case
m=2
and
[
24
]
for any
m\geq 1
).
Consequently, the wavefront set
\mbox{WF}(^{s}\!f)
of the solenoidal component of
f
can be recovered from the knowledge of the geodesic ray transform.

In the presence of conjugate points, these assertions are no longer true.
The normal operator is no longer a
\Psi
DO. In
[
32
]
, the authors showed that for the case
m=0
, in the presence of only fold-type conjugate points, the normal operator acting on functions is a sum of a pseudodifferential operator (
\Psi
DO) and a Fourier integral operator (FIO), and they computed the principal symbol of both these operators. They also showed a cancellation of singularities result for the geodesic ray transform in 2-dimensions.
In a follow-up work for the geodesic ray transform in 2-dimensions
[
13
]
, the restriction of fold-type conjugate points in
[
32
]
was removed to allow all possible types of conjugate locus to arrive at a cancellation of singularities result similar to the one in
[
32
]
. Cancellation of singularities in the presence of conjugate points for the attenuated geodesic ray transform was also considered in
[
8
]
. These works were subsequently generalized by the first author and Uhlmann in
[
9
]
with only the additional assumption of no singular conjugate points and they showed that the normal operator is a sum of a
\Psi
DO and several FIOs with each FIO corresponding to conjugate pairs of a given order. We note that all the works mentioned above are for weighted and non-weighted cases of the geodesic ray transform acting on functions (i.e. the case
m=0
).

The primary purpose of this paper is to study the geodesic ray transform of symmetric
m
-tensor fields on 2-dimensional Riemannian manifolds in the presence of conjugate points. To the best of our knowledge, ours would be the first work addressing the microlocal analysis of the normal operator associated to the geodesic ray transform acting on symmetric tensor fields on Riemannian manifolds with conjugate points.
We are interested in undertaking a microlocal study of the transform
I_{m}
in this set-up, which is a natural follow-up to
[
9
]
, although that work included no restriction on dimension. In contrast to
[
9
]
, we restrict the dimension of the Riemannian manifold to be
2
for several reasons. First, due to the restriction on dimension, we place no restrictions on the type of conjugate points. We recall, in dimensions 3 and higher,
[
9
]
required the assumption of no singular conjugate pairs. However, singular conjugate pairs do not occur in 2-dimensions, and so this hypothesis is not necessary for us. Second, as already mentioned above, we explicitly compute the principal symbol of the
\Psi
DO and FIO parts of the operator
I_{m}^{*}I_{m}
in this work, and our computation relies on the stationary phase method
[
5
]
. In 2-dimensions, the computation of these symbols is easier as it is a finite sum, in comparison to higher dimensions, where the sum should be replaced by an integral. Finally, in 2-dimensions we can prove a result on cancellation of singularities result similar to
[
13
]
. We hope to address the analysis of the normal operator
I_{m}^{*}I_{m}
acting on symmetric
m
-tensor fields in the presence of conjugate for higher dimensions in a future work.

In Section
2
, we begin with preliminaries and then show in Subsection
2.3
that a similar decomposition of the normal operator into
\Psi
DO and FIO parts to that in
[
9
]
holds for the tensor case. In Section
3
we compute explicitly the principal symbol of the
\Psi
DO and FIO components of
I^{*}_{m}I_{m}
. Finally, in Section
4
we construct a parametrix recovering the solenoidal component of
f
modulo FIOs, and show a cancellation of singularities result, similar in spirit to the work of
[
13
]
. We emphasize that our cancellation of singularities result is only for interior singularities and we cannot address cancellation of singularities at boundary points with our current techniques.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The geodesic ray transform

In this section, we introduce some notation, definitions and results that will be used in this paper. Most of what we give in this section is standard (e.g. see
[
21
]
).

Let
(M,g)
be a compact 2-dimensional Riemannian manifold with boundary and volume form
\mathrm{d}V_{g}
. The tangent bundle of
M
will be denoted by
TM
with projection map
\pi:TM\rightarrow M
, and
SM\subset TM
will denote the unit sphere bundle. For points
v\in TM
we will sometimes use the notation
(x,v)
which indicates that
x=\pi(v)
. For
v\in TM\setminus\{0\}
, we will write
\gamma_{v}
for the maximally extended geodesic with initial condition
\dot{\gamma}_{v}(0)=v
. Note that this also includes the condition
\gamma_{v}(0)=\pi(v)
. The outward/inward pointing unit vector fields will be denoted by

\partial_{\pm}SM=\{v\in SM:\pi(v)\in\partial M,\pm\langle\nu,v\rangle_{g}>0\}

where
\nu
is the unit outer normal on the boundary
\partial M
. The set of smooth functions on
M
will be denoted
C^{\infty}(M)
, functions compactly supported in the interior
M^{\rm int}
of
M
will be
C_{c}^{\infty}(M^{\rm int})
, and distributions on
M
(the dual space of
C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
) will be
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
. Distributions that are compactly supported within
M^{\rm int}
will be denoted
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(M^{\rm int})
.

We assume that
(M,g)
is non-trapping, in the sense that geodesics starting in
M
will meet the boundary in finite time in both directions. We also assume that the boundary
\partial M
of
M
is strictly convex. By this, we mean that the second fundamental form of the boundary defined by

\Pi(v,v)=\langle\nabla_{v}\nu,v\rangle_{g}\mbox{ for }v\in T(\partial M)

is positive definite. Here
\nabla
is the covariant derivative induced by the Levi-Civita connection on
(M,g)
. Since the boundary is strictly convex, the functions
\tau_{\pm}:SM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which map
v\in SM
to the positive (resp. negative) time when
\gamma_{v}
first intersects
\partial M
are smooth on the interior of
SM
. We will also use the notation
D_{t}
for the covariant differentiation of a vector field along a curve parameterized by
t
.

Let
f
be a symmetric covariant tensor field of rank
m
. In local coordinates
(x^{1},\cdots,x^{n})
,
f
can be written as

f(x)=f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(x)\mathrm{d}x^{i_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}x^{i_{m}},

with Einstein summation convention assumed. We write
C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
,
C_{c}^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
, and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M^{\rm int}})
for the space of smooth, smooth compactly supported, distribution valued, and compactly supported distribution valued symmetric
m
tensor fields, respectively. Smooth tensor fields can be considered as functions on the tangent space
TM
, and for this we will use the notation

\langle f,v\rangle=f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}v^{i_{1}}\cdots v^{i_{m}}

for any
v\in TM
. The tensor field
f
being symmetric means that for any permutation
\sigma
of the indices,

f_{i_{\sigma(1)}\cdots i_{\sigma(m)}}=f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}.

We will use the usual musical operators to equate covectors and vectors as well as symmetric tensors with indices up and down. For example,

f^{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}=g^{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots g^{i_{m}j_{m}}f_{i_{1}\cdots j_{m}},

and

\flat_{g}(v)_{i}=v^{j}g_{ij},\quad\sharp_{g}(u)^{i}=u_{j}g^{ij}.

The Hodge star operator given by the metric
g
will be denoted by
\ast_{g}
. In the 2-dimensional case,
\ast_{g}
maps
1
-forms to
1
-forms by the formula

\ast_{g}(f)_{i}=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}(g)}\epsilon_{ji}g^{jl}f_{l}

where
\epsilon
is the alternating tensor. Given a covector
\xi\in T^{*}M
, the Hodge star gives a method to find a vector
\xi^{\perp}
annihilated by
\xi
through the formula
\xi^{\perp}=\sharp_{g}(\ast_{g}(\xi))
. This notation
\xi^{\perp}
will be used below.

We introduce the following pointwise product on tensors

\langle f,h\rangle_{g}=f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}h^{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}},

and the corresponding
L^{2}
inner product on the space of rank
m
symmetric tensor fields

(2.1)

\langle\langle u,v\rangle\rangle_{g}=\int\limits_{M}\langle u,\overline{v}\rangle_{g}\ \mathrm{d}V_{g},

The space
L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
is the closure of
C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
under the norm corresponding to this inner product. The covariant derivative of a tensor field is given in local coordinates by

(2.2)

f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m};k}=\nabla_{k}f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}=\frac{\partial f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}}{\partial x^{k}}-\sum\limits_{p=1}^{m}\Gamma_{ki_{p}}^{r}u_{i_{1}\cdots i_{p-1}ri_{p+1}\cdots i_{m}}

where
\Gamma_{ki}^{r}
are the Christoffel symbols. The covariant derivative, and other differential operators introduced below, extend to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
using weak derivatives. Then, the Sobolev spaces
H^{k}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
for integer
k\geq 0
are the subspaces of
f\in L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
such that all components of
\nabla^{l}f
are in
L^{2}
in any coordinates with the natural inner product and norm. For non-integer
s>0
,
H^{s}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
can be defined via interpolation (see
[
35
, Chapter 4]
for more information on the definition of Sobolev spaces).

We introduce an operation
d
on the space of symmetric tensor fields, called the symmetrized covariant derivative. If
f
is a smooth symmetric rank
m
tensor field, then
\mathrm{d}f
is a rank
m+1
symmetric tensor field defined to be the symmetrization of the covariant derivative
\nabla
. That is,
\mathrm{d}=\sigma\nabla
where
\sigma
is the symmetrization. With respect to the
L^{2}
inner product defined in (
2.1
), the formal
L^{2}
adjoint of
d
is equal to
-\delta
, where the divergence
\delta
is defined by

(\delta f)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m-1}}=g^{jk}f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m-1}j;k}.

A tensor field
f
is called
solenoidal
if
\delta f=0
.

In the case of vector fields,
\delta
is the standard divergence and for this case, the Helmholtz decomposition is classical. A similar decomposition for symmetric tensor fields has played a role in analysis of the tensor ray transform for decades
[
21
]
. Sharafutdinov
[
21
, Theorem 3.3.2]
proved this decomposition for the case of symmetric tensor fields in
H^{k}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})
when
k
is an integer
at least
1
. Although the result in
[
21
, Theorem 3.3.2]
only explicitly includes
k\geq 1
, it is possible to extend to include
k=0
as well with little extra work
[
26
, Theorem 3.6]
. We will now review the decomposition, sketching its proof.

Suppose that
f\in C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})
for
k\geq 0
and consider the boundary value problem

(2.3)

\delta du=\delta f,\quad u|_{\partial M}=0.

As demonstrated in
[
21
]
, this is an elliptic boundary value problem which satisfies the Shapiro-Lopatinkii conditions (these are called the complementing condition in
[
1
]
; see also
[
35
, Proposition 11.9]
) and there is a unique solution
u\in C^{\infty}(S^{m-1}\tau_{M}^{\prime})
. Therefore, for
k\geq 1
, the unique solution
u
satisfies

(2.4)

\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})}\leq C\|\delta du\|_{H^{k-1}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})}=C\|\delta f\|_{H^{k-1}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{k}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})}.

Note that the standard Shapiro-Lopatinskii result gives the first step of this estimate only for
k\geq 1
. To obtain the same estimate for
k=0
, we have

\|du\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}^{2}=\langle f,du\rangle_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\|du\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\Rightarrow\|du\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}.

The first equality above follows from the weak formulation of (
2.3
). Using the equality

(2.5)

\langle u(\gamma_{v}(t)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle du(\gamma_{v}(s)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(s)\rangle\ ds

which holds for
v\in\partial_{-}SM
since
u|_{\partial M}=0
, we can establish a Poincaré-type inequality for
u
. Indeed, using Santaló’s formula and (
2.5
)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}^{2}&\leq C\int_{SM}\langle u(x),v\rangle^{2}\ dSM(x,v)=\int_{\partial_{-}SM}\int_{0}^{\tau_{+}(v)}\langle u(\gamma_{v}(t)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\rangle^{2}\ dt\ d\partial_{-}SM(v)\\
&\leq C\int_{\partial_{-}SM}\int_{0}^{\tau_{+}(v)}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\langle du(\gamma_{v}(s)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(s)\rangle\ ds\right)^{2}\ dt\ d\partial_{-}SM(v).\end{split}

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the fact that
\tau_{+}(v)
is bounded by hypothesis, we then have

\|u\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}^{2}\leq C\int_{\partial_{-}SM}\int_{0}^{\tau_{+}(v)}\langle du(\gamma_{v}(s)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(s)\rangle^{2}\ ds\ d\partial_{-}SM(v)

for a new constant
C
. Applying Santaló’s formula a second time then proves

\|u\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq C\|du\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}.

Note the constant
C
may have changed from the previous step. Using the generalized Korn’s inequality as well (
[
20
, Theorem 2.2]
), implies

(2.6)

\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{k}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})}

for
k=0
. Considering also (
2.4
), we have established (
2.6
) for all integers
k\geq 0
.

Given
u
the unique solution of (
2.3
), if we define

{}^{s}\!f=f-du,

then
{}^{s}f
is solenoidal and we obtain the solenoidal decomposition

f={{}^{s}}\!f+du.

Note additionally by (
2.6
) that

(2.7)

\|^{s}\!f\|_{H^{k}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{k}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}

for any
k\geq 0
. By continuity, we can extend the above construction to allow
f\in L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
, in which case
{}^{s}\!f
and
du\in L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
are projections onto the space of solenodal tensor fields and potential tensor fields, respectively. These spaces are orthogonal in
L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
, which leads to

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}=\|^{s}\!f\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}^{2}+\|du\|_{L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}^{2}.

By interpolation, we also obtain (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) with
k
replaced by any
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
s\geq 0
.

The arguments above result in the Helmholtz decomposition for symmetric tensor fields which we now record in a theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\delta
and
d
be the differential operators defined above with domains
D(\delta)=L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
and
D(d)=H^{1}_{0}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
. Then the space
L^{2}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})
admits an orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})=\operatorname{Ker}(\delta)\oplus\operatorname{Ran}(d),

known as the solenoidal-potential decomposition. If,
f\in H^{s}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
for
s\geq 0
and

f=\,^{s}\!f+du

is the solenoidal-potential decomposition of
f
, then

\|^{s}\!f\|_{H^{s}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}

and

\|u\|_{H^{s+1}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})}

for a constant
C>0
independent of
f
.

We will write
\pi_{S}
for the projection of
L^{2}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
onto the solenoidal part as described in Theorem
2.1
.

We now precisely define the ray transform of symmetric
m
-tensor fields as follows: It is initially defined on the space of smooth symmetric
m
-tensor fields

I_{m}:C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau_{M}^{\prime})\to C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)

as

(2.8)

I_{m}f(v)=\int\limits_{\tau_{-}(v)}^{0}\langle f(\gamma_{v}(t)),\dot{\gamma}_{v}(t)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

To define the backprojection operator, we introduce the mapping
F:SM\rightarrow\partial_{+}SM
given by

(2.9)

F(v)=\dot{\gamma}_{v}(\tau_{+}(v)).

The backprojection operator
I_{m}^{*}:C^{\infty}(\partial_{+}SM)\to C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
is then defined in local coordinates by

(2.10)

(I_{m}^{*}h)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(x)=\int\limits_{S_{x}M}h(F(v))v_{i_{1}}\cdots v_{i_{m}}\mathrm{d}S_{x}(v).

Note that
I_{m}^{*}
is the formal adjoint of
I_{m}
with respect to the
L^{2}
inner product (
2.1
). Additionally, both
I_{m}
and
I_{m}^{*}
can be extended as continuous maps on the respective
L^{2}
spaces, and to compactly supported distribution spaces.

2.2.
Fourier integral operator and its principal symbol

Our main results give the principal symbols of the pseudodifferential and Fourier integral parts of the operator
\mathcal{N}_{m}
. Given this, we now introduce Fourier integral operators (FIOs). As a first reference on the topic we recommend
[
5
]
, although this only considers FIOs acting on scalars. Because

acts on tensor fields, we must also consider FIOs acting on tensor fields, or, more generally, Fourier integrals taking values in a vector bundle. For a general treatment of FIOs, which includes this case, see
[
10
]
.

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth manifolds of dimension
n_{X}
and
n_{Y}
respectively, and with non-vanishing densities
|\mathrm{d}v_{X}|
and
|\mathrm{d}v_{Y}|
. Let
E_{X}
and
E_{Y}
be smooth vector bundles over
X
and
Y
respectively with
E_{X}
having a fiberwise metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E_{X}}
. For our purposes,
E_{X}
and
E_{Y}
will be the symmetric tensor bundles
S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M}
, but let us continue to consider the general case for now. Finally, let
\mathcal{C}\subset T^{*}X\setminus\{0\}\times T^{*}Y\setminus\{0\}
be a canonical relation (see
[
5
]
for the definition of canonical relation). An FIO of order
m
is an operator
A:C^{\infty}_{c}(E_{Y})\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(E_{X})
such that the Schwartz kernel
K_{A}
of

can be locally represented in the form

(2.11)

K_{A}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi(x,y,\theta)}a(x,y,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\ |\mathrm{d}v_{Y}|^{1/2}\ |\mathrm{d}v_{X}|^{1/2},

where
\varphi(x,y,\theta)
is a non-degenerate phase function which locally defines
\mathcal{C}
and

is an amplitude function of order
\mu=m+n/2-(n_{X}+n_{Y})/4
taking values in
\mathrm{Hom}(E_{Y},E_{X}^{*})
(
E_{X}^{*}
is the dual bundle to
E_{X}
). The technical terms in the previous sentence will be explained below.

Remark 2.2
.

This definition differs slightly from
[
10
, Section 25.2]
, where FIOs acting on sections of vector bundles are identified with distributions in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X\times Y,\Omega_{1/2}\otimes\mathrm{Hom}(E_{Y},E_{X}))
. However, it is not clear how to identify such distributions with operators from sections of
E_{Y}
to sections of
E_{X}
without some additional structure. We have chosen our convention,
a
taking values in
\mathrm{Hom}(E_{Y},E_{X}^{*})
, to avoid this. Indeed, for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(E_{Y})
and
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(E_{X})
, we have

\langle K_{A},(h|\mathrm{d}v_{Y}|^{1/2})\otimes f|\mathrm{d}v_{X}|^{1/2}\rangle=\int_{X}\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\varphi(x,y,\theta)}\langle a(x,y,\theta)f,h\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\theta|\mathrm{d}v_{Y}||\mathrm{d}v_{X}|.

where
\pi_{X}
and
\pi_{Y}
are projections onto the respective components. The relationship between
K_{A}
and
A
is given, for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{1/2}\otimes E_{Y})
and
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{1/2}\otimes E_{X})
, by

\langle K_{A},h\otimes f\rangle=\langle Af,h\rangle.

These relationships are required to make sense of Definition
2.3
.

Non-degeneracy of the phase funciton
\varphi
means that it is a real-valued, homogeneous function of degree
1
in
\theta
,
\mathrm{d}_{x,y,\theta}\varphi\neq 0
for all
(x,y,\theta)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and at the set of points
(x,y,\theta)
such that
\partial_{\theta}\varphi(x,y,\theta)=0
, the set
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\left(\partial_{x,y,\theta}\varphi\right)(x,y,\theta)\}
for
1\leq j\leq n
is linearly independent. The phase function
\varphi
defines
\mathcal{C}
in the sense that,
locally on
\mathcal{C}
,

(2.12)

\mathcal{C}=\Big\{(\mathrm{d}_{x}\varphi(x,y,\theta),-\mathrm{d}_{y}\varphi(x,y,\theta))\in T^{*}X\setminus\{0\}\times T^{*}Y\setminus\{0\}\ :\ \mathrm{d}_{\theta}\varphi(x,y,\theta)=0\Big\}.

The function
a
is called an amplitude function and it satisfies an amplitude estimate, depending on its order
\mu
, defined as formula
(
2.14
) below. Note that (
2.11
) is of the form

(2.13)

K_{A}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\varphi(z,\theta)}a(z,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\ |\mathrm{d}v_{Z}|^{1/2},

where
z=(x,y)\in X\times Y=Z
and
|\mathrm{d}v_{Z}|
is the product density. We call this type of integral a Fourier integral and will introduce the next few concepts for expressions of this form. The phase function
\varphi
in (
2.13
) also defines a Lagrangian manifold by

\Lambda=\Big\{\mathrm{d}_{z}\varphi(z,\theta)\in T^{*}Z\setminus\{0\}\ :\ \mathrm{d}_{\theta}\varphi(z,\theta)=0\Big\}.

Note that, when (
2.13
) is given by an FIO as described above, then the canonical relation
\mathcal{C}
given by (
2.12
) is related to
\Lambda
through multiplication by
-1
in the
T^{*}Y
component.

For a vector bundle
E_{Z}
on
Z
, a function
a\in C^{\infty}(Z\times\mathbb{R}^{n};E_{Z})
is said to be an amplitude function if for each compact
K\subset Z
and multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
, we have the following estimate:

(2.14)

\lvert\partial_{z}^{\beta}\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}a(z,\theta)\rvert\leq C_{K,\alpha}(1+|\theta|)^{\mu-|\alpha|}

for some real
\mu
. The norm on the left side of this estimate can be taken to be any fibrewise norm on the space
E_{Z}
in local coordinates. The space of functions satisfying an estimate of the above form will be denoted
S^{\mu}(Z\times\mathbb{R}^{n};E_{Z})
and
\mu
is called the order of the amplitude function. For the application of FIOs, we will take
E_{Z}=\mathrm{Hom}(E_{Y},E_{X}^{*})
, or more specifically in our case
E_{Z}=\mathrm{Hom}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M},(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})^{*})
. It is also possible to define amplitudes on a conic manifold taking values in a vector bundle. In particular,
S^{\mu}(\Lambda;\Omega_{1/2}\otimes M_{\Lambda})
is the space of amplitudes defined on
\Lambda
with values in the product bundle of the set of half-densities
\Omega_{1/2}
and the Keller-Maslov line bundle
M_{\Lambda}
over
\Lambda
. For details of these definitions, see
[
5
]
.

In the sections below, we use the method of stationary phase to compute the principal symbols of Fourier integrals, and therefore the principal symbol for corresponding FIOs. We now state the definition for the principal symbol of a Fourier integral.

Definition 2.3
.

[
5
, Definition 4.1.1]

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{Z}}
be open and let
K_{A}
be a Fourier integral of order
m
defined by a non-degenerate phase function
\varphi
and an amplitude
a\in S^{m-(n/2)+(n_{Z}/4)}(Z\times\mathbb{R}^{n};E_{Z})
as in (
2.13
). Let
\psi(z,\alpha):Z\times\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth, homogeneous of order
1
in
\alpha
, and such that, for fixed
\alpha
,
\{d_{x}\psi(x,\alpha)\}\subset T^{*}Z
intersects
\Lambda
transversely at
\alpha
. Suppose
p_{L}:T_{\alpha}(T^{*}Z)\rightarrow\mathrm{Ker}(D\pi|_{\alpha})
is the projection along
T_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\psi
restricted to
T_{\alpha}\Lambda
, and
\omega
is the volume form in
\mathrm{Ker}(D\pi|_{\alpha})
. Finally, let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Z;\Omega_{1/2}\otimes E_{Z}^{*})
. The principal symbol of
K_{A}
is the element in

S^{m+(n_{Z}/4)}(\Lambda,\Omega_{1/2}\otimes M_{\Lambda}\otimes E_{Z})/S^{m+(n_{Z}/4)-1}(\Lambda,\Omega_{1/2}\otimes M_{\Lambda}\otimes E_{Z})

given by

E_{Z}^{*}\ni u(\pi(\alpha))\to e^{\mathrm{i}\psi(\pi(\alpha),\alpha)}\langle K_{A},ue^{-\mathrm{i}\psi(x,\alpha)}\rangle|p_{L}^{*}\omega|^{1/2},

for
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

When we apply Definition
2.3
to the case of FIOs, we identify the Lagrangian
\Lambda
with the canonical relation
\mathcal{C}
by changing the sign of the
T^{*}Y
coordinate. Note that the principal symbol of an FIO will take values in the vector bundle
\Omega_{1/2}\otimes M_{\mathcal{C}}\otimes\mathrm{Hom}(E_{Y},E_{X}^{*})
over
\mathcal{C}
.

2.3.
Geodesic ray transform as an FIO

It is well-known that
I_{m}
and
I_{m}^{*}
are Fourier integral operators of order
-1/2
. For the case
m=0
(the scalar transform) this can be shown using the fact that the Schwartz kernel of
I_{m}
is a weighted delta type distribution in the space
\partial_{+}SM\times M

[
13
]
, and the same follows follows for each component of the tensor transform. We will discuss the canonical relations of
I_{m}
and
I_{m}^{*}
below (see (
2.24
) and (
2.25
)), but first introduce some general considerations useful for analysis of the normal operator

(2.15)

\mathcal{N}_{m}=I_{m}^{*}I_{m}.

In
[
9
]
, the authors consider the push-forward and pull-back of half-densities by a mapping
G:X\rightarrow Y
between manifolds with dimensions
n_{X}
and
n_{Y}
, respectively. We will use a similar definition, but without the complication of half-densities.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G:X\rightarrow Y
be a smooth submersion between manifolds which are given densities
|\mathrm{d}\mu_{X}|
and
|\mathrm{d}\mu_{Y}|
. Then the push-forward
G_{*}:C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
and pull-back
G^{*}:C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(X)
are defined by

\int\limits_{Y}h(y)G_{*}[f](y)|\mathrm{d}\mu_{Y}(y)|=\int\limits_{X}h(G(x))f(x)|\mathrm{d}\mu_{X}(x)|=\int\limits_{X}G^{*}[h](x)f(x)|\mathrm{d}\mu_{X}(x)|.

The result
[
9
, Lemma 1]
remains true in this situation and we have that
G_{*}
and
G^{*}
are both FIOs of order
(n_{Y}-n_{X})/4
. We will require this result for the case of
F
defined by (
2.9
).
For this mapping, we have
F_{*}
and
F^{*}
are FIOs of order
-1/4
and canonical relations given by

(2.16)

\mathcal{C}_{F_{*}}=\left\{(\xi,DF|_{v}^{t}\xi)\ :\ v\in SM,\ \xi\in T^{*}_{F(v)}\partial_{+}SM\setminus\{0\}\right\},

(2.17)

\mathcal{C}_{F^{*}}=\left\{(DF|_{v}^{t}\xi,\xi)\ :\ v\in SM,\ \xi\in T^{*}_{F(v)}\partial_{+}SM\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

Unlike the situation in
[
9
]
, which considered the geodesic ray transform on scalar fields, the operators
I_{m}
and
I^{*}_{m}
cannot be written as the composition of a pull-back and a push-forward. However, we can modify the framework of
[
9
]
slightly by introducing the operator
\pi^{*}:C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(SM)
defined by

(2.18)

\pi^{*}[h](v)=\langle h,v\rangle

with adjoint given, in local coordinates, by

(2.19)

\pi_{*}[f](x)=\int\limits_{S_{x}M}f(x,v)v_{i_{1}}\cdots v_{i_{m}}|\mathrm{d}S_{x}M(v)|,

which maps
\pi_{*}:C^{\infty}(SM)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
. We note that this is an abuse of notation since
\pi^{*}
and
\pi_{*}
defined by (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) are not the push-forward and pull-back of the projection
\pi:SM\rightarrow M
, although they play the same role that the push-forward and pull-back did in
[
9
]
.

Using Santaló’s formula, we have,

(2.20)

I_{m}=F_{*}\circ\pi^{*},\ I_{m}^{*}=\pi_{*}\circ F^{*}

by a calculation very similar to that found in
[
9
]
. We would also like to be able to apply
[
9
, Lemma 1]
to conclude
\pi_{*}
and
\pi^{*}
are FIOs, but it does not apply because these are not the push-forward and pull-back of a submersion. However, we have the identity

\int\limits_{M}\langle h,\pi_{*}[f]\rangle_{g}\ \mathrm{d}V_{g}=\int\limits_{SM}\langle h,v\rangle f\ |\mathrm{d}SM|=\int\limits_{SM}\pi^{*}[h]f\ |\mathrm{d}SM|.

If we take coordinates

on
SM
, where

are coordinates on
M
, then if

is supported within this coordinate neighborhood the integrals above are equal to

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{(x,\alpha)}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{y}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\xi}}e^{i\eta\cdot(x-y)}h_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}(y)v^{i_{1}}\cdots v^{i_{m}}f(x,\alpha)J(x,\alpha)\ \mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\eta

in the coordinate domain. Note that
v
in this equation depends on
\alpha
. From (
2.21
), we see that

\pi_{*}
and
\pi^{*}
are FIOs of order
-1/4
with canonical relations

(2.22)

\mathcal{C}_{\pi_{*}}=\Big\{(\eta;D\pi|^{t}_{w}\eta):w\in SM,\eta\in T^{*}_{y}M\setminus\{0\}\Big\},

(2.23)

\mathcal{C}_{\pi^{*}}=\Big\{(D\pi|^{t}_{w}\eta;\eta):w\in SM,\eta\in T^{*}_{y}M\setminus\{0\}\Big\},

It is possible to use (
2.16
), (
2.17
), (
2.22
), (
2.23

\mathcal{C}_{I_{m}}=\left\{(\xi;\eta)\ :\ w\in SM,\ \eta\in T^{*}_{y}M\setminus\{0\},\ D\pi|_{w}^{t}\eta=DF|_{F(w)}^{t}\xi\right\},

The operator

fails to satisfy the Bolker condition globally because geodesics passing normally to

The canonical relations (

show that

\mathcal{N}_{m}=I_{m}^{*}I_{m}=\Psi_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{m,j}

where

is an FIO with canonical relation given by one connected component of the set

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{J}&=\Bigg\{\Big(\gamma(a),\flat_{g}(D_{t}J(a));\gamma(b),\flat_{g}(D_{t}J(b))\Big)\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt:\ \mbox{$J$ is a Jacobi field along $\gamma$ with $J(a)=J(b)=0$}\Bigg\}.\end{split}

3.
Principal symbols of the pseudodifferential and Fourier integral components of the normal operator

In this section, we will calculate the principal symbols of the diagonal and conjugate parts of the normal operator
\mathcal{N}_{m}
using Definition

. From (
2.26
), we see that
\mathcal{N}_{m}
decomposes into a sum of pieces the first of which is a
\Psi

at a point

\phi(\pi(\alpha))\mapsto e^{i\psi(x_{0},y_{0},\alpha)}\langle K_{\mathcal{N}_{m}},e^{-i\psi(x,y,\alpha)}\phi(x,y)\rangle|p_{L}^{*}\omega|^{1/2}

where

is the Schwartz kernel of
\mathcal{N}_{m}
,

is homogeneous of order 1 in

and for fixed
\alpha

\Lambda
transversally at

and

Also,
\phi
is a half density on

with values in

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}^{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}j_{1}\cdots j_{m}}(x_{0},\xi,x_{0},\xi)

4.
Constructing a parametrix and cancellation of singularities

Let us write the decomposition (
2.26
) as

(4.1)

\mathcal{N}\equiv\Psi+A

where
A=\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}
is a sum of FIOs. In fact, we may assume a little bit more about the decomposition (
4.1
) as we record in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

It is possible to choose
\Psi
and
A
in (
4.1
) which are symmetric and map solenoidal to solenoidal and potential to potential components. Furthermore, they can both be chosen to vanish on the potential part of the solenoidal decomposition.

Proof.

Symmetrising both sides of (
2.26
) and using the fact that
\mathcal{N}
is symmetric, we see that
\Psi
and
A
can be made symmetric. Since the canonical relation of
A
, given by
(
2.27
)
, is symmetric the symmetrisation of
A
remains a sum of FIOs with the same canonical relations, which are themselves symmetric. Then composing on both sides with the projection
\pi_{S}
onto the solenoidal part, which is a pseudodifferential operator, and using the fact that
\pi_{S}\mathcal{N}\pi_{S}\equiv\mathcal{N}
we see
\Psi
and
A
can be chosen to respect the solenoidal decomposition. Finally, since
\mathcal{N}
vanishes on the potential part of the solenoidal decomposition,
\Psi
and
A
can be chosen to do so as well by considering the projection of (
4.1
) onto the potential part.
∎

As shown in the concluding part of §
3
,
\Psi
is elliptic on solenoidal tensors and specifically the operator

(4.2)

C=\Psi+\mathrm{d}\Lambda\delta

is elliptic where
\delta
is the divergence defined with respect to the metric
g
and
\Lambda
is a scalar symmetric elliptic
\Psi
DO of order
-3
. To avoid problems with the boundary, we will consider a pseudodifferential parametrix for
C
within an open manifold
\widetilde{M}
compactly contained in the interior of
M
. For example, we can take
\widetilde{M}
to be the set which is at least a distance
\epsilon
from
\partial M
. Given such an
\widetilde{M}
, let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
be equal to one on
\widetilde{M}
. In the following a
parametrix
of
C
is an operator
T
such that

(4.3)

TC=\varphi(\mathrm{Id}+K)

where
K
is an operator on
M
with smooth Schwartz kernel. We also will use the notation

(4.4)

TC\equiv\mathrm{Id}

to denote (
4.3
). This notation and the term parametrix will also be used for other operators. Similar to (
4.4
), we will write
f\equiv g
if
f
and
g
are equal modulo a smooth function on
M
.

Next we show how a parametrix for
C
behaves when acting on
\mathcal{N}
. The proof follows the method used in
[
3
]
for the case without conjugate points.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a symmetric parameterix
T
for
C
which respects the solenoidal decomposition. Also, we have

(4.5)

T\mathcal{N}\equiv\pi_{S}+TA.

Proof.

First, note that
C
is symmetric and respects the solenoidal decomposition by (
4.2
) and Lemma
4.1
. Because of this, as a parametrix for
C
,
T
can be chosen with the same properties.

To prove (
4.5
) we follow the proof of
[
3
, Proposition 3]
but with necessary changes to include the possibility of conjugate points. Let us first define the operator

(4.6)

B=\mathcal{N}+\mathrm{d}\Lambda\delta

and let
S_{D}
be the solution operator for the Dirichlet problem

\delta du=f,\quad u|_{\partial M}=0

taking
f
to
u
. Then applying
\mathrm{d}S_{D}
on the right we have

\mathrm{d}\Lambda=(B-\mathcal{N})\mathrm{d}S_{D}

and substituting this into (
4.6
) using
(
2.26
) yields

\Psi+A+\mathrm{d}\Lambda\delta\equiv\mathcal{N}+(\Psi+A+\mathrm{d}\Lambda\delta-\mathcal{N})\mathrm{d}S_{D}\delta.

Next, note that
\mathcal{N}\mathrm{d}S_{D}\delta=I^{*}I\mathrm{d}S_{D}\delta=0
and so, using also (
4.2
),

C+A\equiv\mathcal{N}+(C+A)\mathrm{d}S_{D}\delta.

Applying
T
to the previous equation and rearranging yields

T\mathcal{N}\equiv\mathrm{I}-dS_{D}\delta+TA-TAdS_{D}\delta.

The last term on the right side of the last equation is zero by Lemma
4.1
, and since
\mathrm{I}-dS_{D}\delta=\pi_{S}
we obtain the conclusion (
4.5
).
∎

Armed with Lemma
4.2
, we now present a result on cancellation of singularities which can occur in the presence of conjugate points. This is a version of
[
13
, Theorem 4.3]
for the case of tensor fields. The statement uses microlocal Sobolev spaces which we first define as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
V\subset T^{*}M
be an open conic set and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
is in
H^{s}(V)
if for any function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(M)
which is homogeneous of degree
1
in the fiber variable and whose support is contained in
V
,
\phi(x,D)f\in H^{s}(M)
. The same definition applies for
f\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
, in which case we use the notation
H^{s}(V,S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
.

We now give the cancellation of singularities result.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that
\gamma_{0}
is a geodesic in
M
with conjugate points
x_{0}
and
y_{0}\in M^{\rm int}\cap\gamma_{0}
. Let
V_{i}\subset T^{*}M^{\rm int}
,
i=1
,
2
, be sufficiently small conical neighbourhoods around the wavefront elements in
\Lambda_{J}
, see (
2.27
), corresponding to the conjugate pair and
\mathcal{V}\subset T^{*}\partial_{+}SM
a sufficiently small conical neighbourhood around
\dot{\gamma}_{0}\cap\partial_{+}SM
. For
i=1
or
2
, let
f_{i}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
with
\mathrm{WF}(f_{i})\subset V_{i}
. Then

I_{m}(f_{1}+f_{2})\in H^{s}(\mathcal{V})

if and only if

(4.7)

\pi_{S}f_{1}+TAf_{2}\in H^{s-1/2}(V_{1},S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M}).

The previous statement is also true with
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
switched.

Proof.

First, suppose that
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are taken sufficiently small so that
C_{I}V_{1}
and
C_{I}V_{2}\subset\mathcal{V}
. Then we have

(4.8)

WF(I_{m}(f_{1}+f_{2}))\subset C_{I}V_{1}\cup C_{I}V_{2}\subset\mathcal{V}.

Now, suppose that

I_{m}(f_{1}+f_{2})=h.

Then

T\mathcal{N}(f_{1}+f_{2})=TI^{*}h,

and by (
4.5
)

(4.9)

\pi_{S}f_{1}+TAf_{2}+\pi_{S}f_{2}+TAf_{1}\equiv TI_{m}^{*}h.

Now we will microlocalise (
4.9
). Indeed, let us take a second pair of cones
W_{i}
slightly larger that
V_{i}
and let, for
i=1
or
2
,
\phi_{i}\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
equal to
1
on
W_{i}
and with support in a conic neighbourhood of
W_{i}
. Then
f_{i}\equiv\phi_{i}(x,D)f_{i}
and in the following we will abuse notation for the action of
\phi_{i}(x,D)
on
f_{i}
by simply writing
\phi_{i}f_{i}
. The conjugate pair
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
is associated with one component of the set (
2.27
) corresponding with operator
A_{j}
, and so we obtain

(4.10)

\phi_{1}\pi_{S}\phi_{1}f_{1}+\phi_{1}TA_{j}\phi_{2}f_{2}\equiv\phi_{1}TI_{m}^{*}h.

Now suppose that
h\in H^{s}(\mathcal{V})
. Then since
I^{*}
is an FIO of order
-1/2
whose cannonical relation is a local graph and
T
is a pseudodifferential operator of order
1
,
\phi_{1}TI_{m}^{*}h\in H^{s-1/2}(V_{1},S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
which proves the “only if” part of the statement.
Assume now (
4.7
) and that
\tilde{\phi}_{1}\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
is a symbol of order
0
equal to
1
on
V_{1}
with support contained in
W_{1}
. Then by (
4.7
) we have that the left-hand-side of (
4.10
) is in
H^{s-1/2}(V_{1},S^{m}\tau^{\prime}_{M})
. If we apply
I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}C
to (
4.10
), by the mapping properties of
I_{m}
and
C
we find that the resulting left-hand-side will be in
H^{s+1}(\mathcal{V})
. Therefore, applying
I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}C
to the right-hand-side of (
4.10
) we obtain

H^{s+1}(\mathcal{V})\ni I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}C\phi_{1}TI_{m}^{*}h\equiv I_{m}\tilde{\phi}^{2}_{1}[\phi_{1},C]TI_{m}^{*}h+I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}I_{m}^{*}h.

Considering the supports of
\tilde{\phi}_{1}
and
\phi_{1}
, we see that the first term on the right side above is smoothing and so

(4.11)

H^{s+1}(\mathcal{V})\ni I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}C\phi_{1}TI_{m}^{*}h\equiv I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}I_{m}^{*}h\equiv(\tilde{\phi}_{1}I_{m}^{*})^{*}(\tilde{\phi}_{1}I_{m}^{*})h

Suppose that
\psi\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}\partial_{+}SM)
is a symbol of order
0
equal to one on
\mathcal{V}
and supported in a slightly larger cone. By (
4.8
),
\psi(x,D)h\equiv h
and (
4.11
) implies

\psi I_{m}\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{2}I_{m}^{*}\psi h\in H^{s+1}(\mathcal{V})=(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}I_{m}^{*}\psi^{*})^{*}(\tilde{\phi}_{1}I_{m}^{*}\psi)h.

From the discussion after (

),
\tilde{\phi}_{1}I_{m}^{*}\psi(x,D)
satisfies the Bolker condition if

is sufficiently small and therefore
\mathcal{N}_{\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}I_{m}^{*}\psi^{*}}=(\tilde{\phi}_{1}^{*}I_{m}^{*}\psi^{*})^{*}(\tilde{\phi}_{1}I_{m}^{*}\psi)

is a graph (see
[
13

), we can apply the FIO composition calculus to determine the principal symbol of

which, considering the definition (

(note

∎

Similar to

If there is a geodesic with a conjugate pair in

and support contained in the projection of

is sufficiently small that this projection is contained in a coordinate patch, and that in these coordinates

\widetilde{f}_{i_{1}\cdot i_{m}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}h,&\mbox{if $i_{1}=\cdots=i_{m}=2$.}\\

be the solenoidal part, as in Theorem

.

Now, by Lemmas

.
∎
