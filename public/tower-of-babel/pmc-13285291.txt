Multi‑scale immune and spatial profiling of sclerosing adenosis and surrounding breast tissue identifies an immune‑cold field phenotype associated with breast cancer risk
Authors: Nicole Cruz-Reyes, Hongling Liang, Melody Stallings-Mann, Laura Pacheco-Spann, Bryan M McCauley, Yuanhang Liu, Robert A Vierkant, Jessica L Fischer, Denice L Gehling, Lisa R Seymour, Amy C Degnim, Mark E Sherman, Stacey J Winham, Derek C Radisky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBenign breast disease (BBD) is common and confers heterogeneous increases in breast cancer risk; however, risk prediction relies mainly on histopathology and clinical factors. Sclerosing adenosis (SA) is a proliferative BBD lesion associated with an approximately two-fold increase in risk, yet most women with SA never develop breast cancer. We hypothesize that the immune–stromal microenvironment of SA and its surrounding lobular field relates to subsequent invasive breast cancer.MethodsIn a nested case–control study within a BBD cohort, we profiled 24 sclerosing adenosis (SA) biopsies (9 developing invasive breast cancer within 15 years, cases; 15 cancer-free at ≥ 15 years, controls). We integrated whole-tissue NanoString gene-expression profiling with multiplex immunofluorescence (MxIF) imaging of SA lesions and surrounding morphologically normal lobules. We measured immune and stromal biomarkers in SA lesions and adjacent lobules, with image analysis masked to case–control status, integrated these data with whole-tissue gene expression, and summarized both microenvironment patterns and the proximity of immune cells to proliferating epithelium.ResultsSA biopsies from women who later developed cancer showed a low-immune, high-stromal gene-expression program, whereas controls were enriched for immune signatures. Stromal densities of CD8⁺, CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ cells in both lobular stroma and SA lesions mirrored this axis and were markedly lower in cases than controls. Unsupervised clustering identified immune-cold and immune-hot lobule types and four SA lesion field archetypes; immune-hot lobules and immune-hot/epithelium-proliferative lesion fields were enriched in controls. Spatial analyses further showed that immune-hot lobules have stromal immune cells positioned closer to proliferating epithelium and enriched CD27–CD8 microclusters, whereas SA lesions from cases exhibit greater immune-to-Ki67 distances, fewer boundary-proximal CD8⁺ sentinels, and depletion of CD27–RUNX3 and RUNX3–CD8 microclusters.ConclusionsThese findings support an association of an immune-cold SA lesion embedded within an immune-cold lobular field phenotype with subsequent invasive breast cancer risk in women with SA, and suggest that spatially organized, RUNX3-rich immune microenvironments may contribute to epithelial surveillance. Validation in larger cohorts will be needed to confirm generalizability and clarify lesion-specific versus field-wide contributions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02293-w.Keywords: Benign breast disease, Sclerosing adenosis, Breast cancer risk, Tumour microenvironment, Multiplex immunofluorescence, NanoString, Spatial analysis, Immune microenvironment

Background

Benign breast disease (BBD) is a common diagnosis, affecting more than one million women per year in the United States [1, 2]. Sclerosing adenosis (SA) is a common proliferative lesion characterized by expansion and distortion of lobular units in a fibrotic stroma; it is identified in approximately one-quarter of benign breast biopsies in some series and has been associated with a roughly two-fold increase in breast cancer risk [3, 4]. Yet most women with SA never develop breast cancer. Thus, the key clinical challenge is to distinguish higher- from lower-risk SA at the time of the benign biopsy, when surveillance and prevention decisions are made.

Risk assessment after a BBD diagnosis has traditionally relied on broad histologic categories, nonproliferative changes, proliferative disease with or without atypia, and atypical hyperplasia, with progressively higher relative risks across categories [5–7]. Current stratification frameworks incorporate age, family history and these broad histologic groupings, sometimes combined with clinical risk models that incorporate biopsy findings [7–9]. Women with biopsy-proven BBD have a sustained increase in breast cancer risk compared with the general population, but there is substantial heterogeneity within histologic categories and limitations for using morphology alone to predict who will progress [2, 9, 10]. Even among women with the same broad BBD diagnosis, risk varies according to additional features such as mammographic density, extent of lobular involvement and co-existing benign proliferative changes [11–15]. Together, these observations suggest that a missing dimension for risk modeling may lie in lesion-specific and field-level biology—including stromal activation and local immune surveillance, features not captured in current BBD risk models [7–10, 13].

In invasive breast cancer, the tumor microenvironment is a key determinant of prognosis and treatment response. High densities of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, particularly CD8⁺ T cells, are associated with improved survival across cohorts and molecular subtypes and predict benefit from chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade [16–19]. Single-cell and spatial analyses have further highlighted the importance of tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells and organized immune niches, with TRM-like CD8⁺ populations and related gene signatures correlating with favorable outcomes [20–22]. Complementary work in breast tissue has emphasized that even histologically normal breasts contain substantial resident immune populations that may participate in early recognition and control of atypical epithelial clones [23–27].

By comparison, the immune and stromal microenvironments of benign breast lesions, and their relation to subsequent cancer risk, have been much less systematically characterized. Normal breast lobules contain organized myeloid and lymphoid infiltrates, with cytotoxic T cells and dendritic cells closely apposed to the basal epithelium and higher densities of adaptive immune cells in lobules with lobulitis, consistent with an active mucosal-type immune system in normal breast tissue [28]. Quantitative studies of premalignant tissue at the time of benign biopsy further indicate that BBD lobules have higher densities of CD8⁺ T cells, CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, CD20⁺ B cells, CD68⁺ macrophages and other effectors than normal donor tissue, and that reduced B-cell infiltration—particularly the absence of CD20⁺ cells—is associated with increased subsequent breast cancer risk [23]. Additional work has demonstrated decreased CD56⁺ NK cells and MICA expression in lobules with fibrocystic and proliferative changes [29], increased immune-cell densities in normal/benign lobules from BRCA1/2 mutation carriers and from women with BBD compared with normal donors [30], and a positive association between CD45 expression and somatic mutational burden in BBD tissue, with higher mutation burden observed in women who did not develop cancer [31]. Collectively, these studies suggest that immune cell composition and activation are altered years before cancer develops and vary across benign histologies and inherited risk states. At the same time, most prior work has relied on bulk or semi-quantitative measures, considering immune cells primarily as totals or densities. These observations raise the possibility that risk-relevant information is encoded less in immune abundance per se than in spatial organization, proximity to proliferating epithelium, and integration with stromal programs.

Parallel concepts from field cancerization support a multiscale view of risk, in which genetic, epigenetic and metabolic alterations extend beyond focal lesions into histologically benign tissue and benign histologic patterns. Similarly, mammographic density may reflect a broader field of altered susceptibility [32]. These field-level changes include stromal and extracellular matrix remodelling and alterations in epithelial gene expression, but the contribution of the immune microenvironment, both within lesions such as SA and in the surrounding lobular field, remains incompletely defined. Moreover, SA is typically assessed as an isolated lesion, despite arising in the context of morphologically normal lobules that may vary widely in epithelial content, stromal composition, immune infiltration and proliferative activity [7, 9, 10].

To address these gaps, we asked whether immune and stromal features of SA and its surrounding lobular field help distinguish women with higher versus lower subsequent breast cancer risk. Building on prior work linking reduced immune infiltration in BBD tissue with increased risk of subsequent breast cancer [23], we hypothesized that SA biopsies from women who later develop cancer would show reduced immune gene-expression signatures and immune cell densities in SA and adjacent lobules. We tested this hypothesis in a nested case–control study integrating NanoString gene-expression profiling of SA tissue with multiplex immunofluorescence imaging of adjacent lobules and SA lesion fields from women who did and did not subsequently develop invasive breast cancer.

Methods

Study cohort

This study was conducted under the approval of the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB #75–87). Women with BBD were identified through a review of Mayo Clinic pathology archives [7, 15]. Women with prior diagnosis of breast cancer or previous breast surgeries were excluded. SA was confirmed histologically on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections using established criteria [3, 4]. At cohort annotation, index biopsies were assigned an overall BBD histologic impression category (non-proliferative versus proliferative disease without atypia); in this analytic set, biopsies were categorized as non-proliferative or proliferative disease without atypia. SA was the index proliferative lesion used for cohort selection; other benign non-atypical changes may co-occur in BBD biopsies and were not used as exclusion criteria. For this nested case–control study, we selected women whose BBD biopsies contained SA and had sufficient archived tissue available for analysis [7, 33]. Cases were defined as women with SA who developed invasive breast cancer within 15 years of their BBD biopsy. Controls were women with SA who were cancer-free for ≥ 15 years following biopsy (two controls developed breast cancer only after > 27 years). Age at biopsy, biopsy year and follow-up time were abstracted from clinical records. For each patient, a representative formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) block containing SA was chosen for NanoString gene expression profiling and multiplex immunofluorescence (MxIF) imaging.

NanoString gene expression profiling and preprocessing

Total RNA was extracted from whole FFPE tissue sections containing the SA lesion using standard protocols, and gene expression was quantified using NanoString IO360 and BC360 panels [33–35]. Raw counts were normalized using NanoString internal positive controls and housekeeping genes according to the manufacturer’s recommendations to yield expression values on a comparable scale across samples. For downstream analyses, we constructed a sample by- gene- matrix for the 24 SA biopsies (9 cases, 15 controls). For probes repeated across the two panels, mean values of the two measurements were used. This yielded a matrix of approximately 1,200 unique genes × 24 samples. All subsequent analyses used these gene level- expression values.

Gene expression signatures

To summarize coordinated variation in immune and stromal programs, we defined composite gene expression signatures a priori guided by canonical T-cell–inflamed/IFN-γ–related immune profiles and TGFβ/ECM fibroblast activation signatures described in the tumor microenvironment literature. The immune signature comprised core T-cell, chemokine, cytolytic effector, checkpoint, and antigen-presentation markers (CD3D, CD3E, CD4, CD8A, CD8B, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, GZMB, PRF1, CCR5, IFNG, ICOS, LAG3, PDCD1, HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1), which overlap with published IFN-γ/T-cell–inflamed signatures and are widely used as indicators of cytotoxic and helper T-cell activity and antigen presentation [36–39]. The stromal/ECM/TGFβ signature comprised genes involved in TGFβ signaling, collagen and integrin networks, matrix proteolysis, proteoglycan deposition, and fibroblast activation (ITGAV, SMAD5, PTEN, NF1, COL11A1, COL5A1, MMP9, MMP14, TGFB1, TGFB3, VCAN, FAP), overlapping with COL11A1- and FAP-centered activated fibroblast signatures that mark desmoplastic stroma across solid tumors [40–42]. All genes were pre-specified and present on the NanoString IO360/BC360 panels; signature scores were calculated as the mean of normalized expression values for member genes.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (MxIF) and image analysis

MxIF was performed on FFPE sections containing both morphologically normal lobules and adjacent SA lesion [43]. The panel included antibodies against pancytokeratin (panCK) (catalog #M3515, 1:150, Agilent Technologies (Dako), Santa Clara, CA, USA), CD8 (catalog #HPA037756, 1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), CD68 (catalog #HPA048982, 1:500,Sigma-Aldrich), RUNX3 (catalog #ab224641, 1:2000, Abcam Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), CD27 (catalog #ab131254, 1:2000, Abcam) and Ki67 (catalog #M7240, 1:100, Agilent Technologies), together with a nuclear stain to identify individual cells. Regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to individual morphologically normal lobules and to discrete fields sampling the SA lesion were annotated by a breast pathologist. Within each ROI, panCK was used to segment epithelial and stromal compartments, and cell phenotypes were quantified as densities (cells per mm²) in epithelial, stromal and combined (“total”) compartments. Each biological lobule or lesion field was represented by three linked annotations (epithelial, stromal and total) sharing a common ROI identifier and sample identifier.

Per-sample stromal immune densities

To obtain per-sample stromal immune cell densities, we restricted the analysis to the stromal compartment (“Stromal” tissue category) and grouped ROIs by patient. For each patient and compartment, we summed the number of marker positive cells across all ROIs and divided by the summed stromal tissue area, yielding densities in units of cells per mm². We computed stromal densities for CD8+, CD68+ and RUNX3+ cells in (i) lobular stroma and (ii) SA lesion stroma separately. For two women, SA was present in the diagnostic specimen but not in the specific block selected for analysis (which was chosen for lobular content); these samples therefore contributed only to lobule-level analyses. Additional variation in denominators across figures reflects metric-specific biological inclusion requirements (e.g., presence of qualifying lobules, stromal immune cells, or Ki67⁺ epithelium), rather than technical quality exclusion.

Clustering of lobules and sclerosing adenosis lesion fields

To identify recurrent lobule phenotypes, we constructed a lobule-level matrix from the MxIF data. We restricted analysis to the “Total” tissue category for each lobule, so that each biological lobule was represented by a single row linked to its sample identifier. Lobules were clustered by k-means on z-scored panCK⁺, CD8⁺, CD68⁺, RUNX3⁺, CD27⁺, Ki67⁺ and total cell densities. These seven features correspond to the full MxIF panel (plus total cell density) and provide a compact summary of immune infiltration, epithelial content/proliferation, and overall lobule cellularity. We explored k-means solutions with k = 2–4 using elbow plots of within cluster sum of squares and inspection of PCA projections and cluster centroids. Lobules lay predominantly along a single gradient from small, immune-cold, Ki67-low lobules to larger, immune-rich, Ki67-high lobules, and increasing k beyond 2 mainly subdivided this continuum into intermediate states without yielding qualitatively new microenvironments. Given 198 lobules from 24 women, we therefore focused on k = 2 as a parsimonious, stable and biologically interpretable partition into immune-cold versus immune-hot lobules. One cluster (cluster 0) comprised smaller, less cellular, immune-poor lobules; the other (cluster 1) comprised larger, more cellular, immune-rich lobules with higher Ki67. For clarity, we refer to these as immune-cold lobules (cluster 0) and immune-hot lobules (cluster 1) throughout the text. To summarize lobule composition at the patient level, we grouped lobules by sample and counted the total number of lobules and the number assigned to the immune-hot cluster. For each sample, the fraction of immune-hot lobules was defined as the number of immune-hot lobules (cluster 1) divided by the total number of lobules profiled for that patient.

An analogous approach was used to define lesion archetypes within SA lesions. For lesion fields, PCA revealed a more multimodal distribution than for lobules, with separable tendencies toward immune-poor, immune-rich, epithelium/Ki67-high and macrophage-dominant states. We compared k-means solutions with k = 4–6 and found that k = 2 or 3 merged clearly distinct phenotypes (for example, immune-hot and epithelium proliferative fields), whereas k > 4 mainly fragmented these patterns into small subclusters. At k = 4, cluster centroids corresponded consistently to immune-cold, immune-hot, proliferative and macrophage-rich SA fields, and each cluster contained a sufficient number of fields for case–control comparisons. We therefore used k = 4 for the main lesion field analyses. Specifically, the four centroids corresponded to: (i) epithelium dense, Ki67-high fields (cluster 0), (ii) immune-rich, CD27/CD8/RUNX3-high fields (cluster 1), (iii) CD68-dominant fields with relatively low panCK (cluster 2) and (iv) low-density, immune-poor fields (cluster 3). For clarity, we refer to these as proliferative SA fields (cluster 0), immune-hot SA fields (cluster 1), macrophage-dominant SA fields (cluster 2) and immune-cold SA fields (cluster 3). At the patient level, we defined a “hot lesion fraction” as the fraction of lesion fields assigned to immune-hot, epithelium/proliferation-high and macrophage-dominant archetypes. For each sample, this was calculated as the number of lesion fields in clusters 0, 1 or 2 divided by the total number of lesion fields profiled for that patient.

Spatial metrics and microcluster definitions in lobules

Nearest-neighbor analyses were performed on single-cell data derived from the MxIF segmentations of morphologically normal lobules. Separate nearest-neighbor tables were available for CD8⁺, CD68⁺, CD27⁺, RUNX3⁺ and epithelial Ki67⁺ cells. Each row corresponded to one index cell and included its lobule identifier, tissue category (stromal or epithelial), x–y coordinates and distances to the nearest cell of each other marker and to the epithelial–stromal boundary. Lobule identifiers were matched to the lobule level density table and then linked to NanoString samples and case status.

Prior work in invasive breast cancer has shown that the fraction of CD8⁺ T cells within ~ 20 μm of tumor cells, a distance roughly corresponding to one cell diameter, is strongly associated with response to therapy and survival and is more prognostic than shorter or longer distance cutoffs. Specifically, CD8⁺ cells within 20 μm of tumor cells (“N20-CD8⁺” cells) strongly predicted pathological complete response and long-term outcomes, supporting this peritumoral zone as a critical interaction distance for effective T-cell–mediated immunosurveillance [44], and similar boundaries have been found in melanoma [45], pancreatic [46], and colorectal cancers [47]. Guided by these findings, we hypothesized that in benign breast tissue, boundary-proximal CD8⁺ cells and direct CD8⁺ coverage of proliferating epithelium would mark an immune-engaged lobular microenvironment.

To quantify boundary-proximal CD8⁺ cells, we used the distance from the tissue category edge (epithelial–stromal interface). For each lobule with at least one stromal CD8⁺ cell, the boundary CD8 fraction was defined as the proportion of stromal CD8⁺ cells located within 10 μm of the epithelial–stromal interface. Lobules without stromal CD8⁺ cells were excluded from this metric. To assess immune coverage of proliferating epithelial cells, we generated “count-within” tables for epithelial Ki67⁺ cells that recorded the number of neighboring CD8⁺ cells within a 20 μm radius. For each lobule containing at least one Ki67⁺ epithelial cell, we defined a CD8 coverage index as the fraction of Ki67⁺ cells with at least one CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Lobules without Ki67⁺ epithelial cells did not contribute to this metric. These two CD8-based proximity metrics were pre-specified as primary spatial endpoints; other distance-based and microcluster metrics were considered exploratory.

To capture local immune microclusters, we used nearest-neighbor distances between stromal immune phenotypes. For each lobule, we defined the CD27–CD8 microcluster fraction as the proportion of stromal CD27⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm and the RUNX3–CD8 microcluster fraction as the proportion of stromal RUNX3⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Lobules lacking the relevant index cell type (CD27⁺ or RUNX3⁺) were excluded from the corresponding calculation.

Spatial metrics and microclusters in sclerosing adenosis lesion fields

Nearest-neighbor analyses were similarly performed for SA lesion fields using single-cell data derived from MxIF segmentations. For CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ cells, we computed median distances from stromal immune cells to their nearest Ki67⁺ epithelial cell, and for epithelial Ki67⁺ cells we computed median distances to their nearest CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ neighbors. These metrics were summarized at the lesion field level. Boundary-proximity metrics for CD8⁺ cells in SA lesions were defined analogously to lobules. For each lesion field with at least one stromal CD8⁺ cell, the lesion CD8 boundary fraction was defined as the proportion of stromal CD8⁺ cells located within 10 μm of the epithelial–stromal interface. Lesion fields without stromal CD8⁺ cells were excluded.

To extend the Ki67-centric coverage analysis to SA lesions, we used nearest-neighbor distances from epithelial Ki67⁺ cells to CD8⁺ cells. For each lesion field containing at least one Ki67⁺ epithelial cell, we defined a lesion CD8 coverage index as the fraction of Ki67⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. For microcluster analyses in lesions, we defined the CD27–RUNX3 microcluster fraction as the proportion of stromal CD27⁺ cells with a RUNX3⁺ neighbor within 20 μm, the RUNX3–CD8 microcluster fraction as the proportion of stromal RUNX3⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm, and the CD27–CD8 microcluster fraction as the proportion of stromal CD27⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Lesion fields without the relevant index cells were excluded from the corresponding microcluster metrics.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3) using dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, clubSandwich, sandwich, and related packages. Group comparisons of NanoString signatures and per-sample stromal immune cell densities were assessed using Welch’s t-tests. Nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were computed in parallel as sensitivity analyses but were not used for primary inference. Associations between continuous features were summarized using Spearman correlation coefficients, with Pearson correlations reported for signature–signature relationships.

For principal component analysis (PCA), we used NanoString-normalized counts. Results were similar when we applied a log₂(count + 1) transform, so we present analyses based on the normalized but otherwise untransformed values. Each gene was standardized across samples (z-scores), and PCA was performed using prcomp without further centering or scaling. Variance explained by each component was calculated as the proportion of total variance in the standardized matrix.

For exploratory differential expression, we compared normalized counts between cases and controls using Welch’s two-sample t-tests. Log₂ fold change was calculated as log₂(mean(case) + 1) − log₂(mean(control) + 1) to stabilize estimates for low-abundance genes. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Consistent with sample size and exploratory aims, interpretation emphasized gene-set–level patterns rather than individual genes.

Because spatial metrics are derived from multiple lobules or lesion fields sampled within the same patient, within-patient correlation violates independence assumptions of naïve per-ROI/per-lobule tests. Therefore, for all spatial metrics and microcluster analyses presented in Figs. 6 and 7, we fit cluster-robust regression models with patient-level clustering. Models were fit using ordinary least squares with CR2–type cluster-robust standard errors (bias-reduced linearization), which provide accurate small-sample inference for nested designs. For binary contrasts (e.g., case vs. control, immune-cold vs. immune-hot lobules), spatial outcomes were regressed on the contrast of interest with patient identifier included as the clustering variable. Statistical significance was determined from robust Wald tests using the cluster-robust variance–covariance matrix.

Results

Patient cohort and NanoString profiling of sclerosing adenosis reveal an immune–stromal axis associated with breast cancer risk

Cases were defined as women who subsequently developed invasive breast cancer within 15 years after the SA biopsy (n = 9), whereas controls were women who remained cancer-free for more than 15 years (n = 15). Age at biopsy was similar between groups, while controls tended to have earlier biopsy years and longer follow-up (Table 1).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of these profiles revealed that the first two principal components explained 19.6% and 11.3% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 1a). To characterize this axis more formally, we performed exploratory differential expression analysis between cases and controls using Welch’s t tests on gene-level expression (Fig. 1b). As expected, given the modest sample size, no individual gene met a stringent false discovery rate threshold after multiple testing correction (all BH-FDR q > 0.05; Supplemental Table S2). Nevertheless, several genes showed nominal, directionally consistent differences in expression. PTEN, NF1, ITGAV, and the innate immune adaptor LY96 were upregulated in SA biopsies from cases (for example, PTEN log₂ fold change [case vs. control] = 0.59, P = 8.4 × 10⁻⁴, q = 0.655), whereas immune effector and trafficking genes such as CCR5, CD8A, and ICOS were downregulated (CCR5 log₂ fold change = − 0.48, P = 4.4 × 10⁻³, q = 0.655; CD8A log₂ fold change = − 0.47, P = 1.1 × 10⁻², q = 0.655; ICOS log₂ fold change = − 0.65, P = 7.0 × 10⁻³, q = 0.655). These trends prompted us to summarize expression into higher-level immune and stromal signatures rather than focusing on single genes.

We defined an immune signature as the mean z score across canonical T-cell and antigen-presentation genes (CD3D, CD3E, CD4, CD8A/B, CXCL9–11, CXCL13, GZMB, PRF1, CCR5, IFNG, ICOS, LAG3, PDCD1, and HLA-DRA/DRB1). SA biopsies from controls exhibited significantly higher immune signature scores than those from cases (mean 0.16 versus − 0.27; Welch’s t test P = 0.0035, BH-FDR q = 0.007; Fig. 1c). Conversely, a stromal/ECM/TGFβ signature built from ITGAV, SMAD5, PTEN, NF1, COL11A1, COL5A1, MMP9, MMP14, TGFB1, TGFB3, VCAN, and FAP was elevated in cases relative to controls (0.30 versus − 0.18; P = 0.030, BH-FDR q = 0.030; Fig. 1d).

When plotted against one another, immune and stromal signatures were moderately anti-correlated across samples (Pearson r = − 0.50; P = 0.012; Spearman ρ = −0.44, P = 0.034; Fig. 1e). Notably, SA biopsies from women who later developed breast cancer clustered in the low-immune/high-stromal quadrant, whereas biopsies from women who remained cancer-free more frequently occupied the reciprocal high-immune/low-stromal quadrant. Together, these analyses define a NanoString-derived immune–stromal axis within SA tissue that is associated with subsequent breast cancer risk and provides a natural scaffold for integrating the spatial MxIF data.

Stromal immune cell densities in lobules and lesions track the NanoString immune signature and case status

The gene expression profiles pointed to a coherent immune–stromal axis in SA tissue: controls showed higher expression of T-cell effector and trafficking genes (CD8A/B, GZMB, PRF1, CXCL9/10/11, CXCL13, ICOS, PDCD1, LAG3, HLA-DRA/DRB1), whereas cases showed higher expression of ECM/TGFβ/stromal programs (ITGAV, SMAD5, TGFB1/3, COL11A1, COL5A1, VCAN, MMP9/14) and the innate adaptor LY96. In the exploratory gene-level contrasts, CD27 was nominally lower in cases (P = 0.0099), but did not reach significance after multiple testing correction (BH-FDR q = 0.65); RUNX3 showed a weaker nominal trend (P = 0.083; q = 0.79). Guided by this immune–stromal pattern, we designed a parsimonious MxIF panel that (i) captures the dominant immune axis (CD8 for cytotoxic T cells, CD27 for B/T memory and costimulation), (ii) reads out the myeloid/stromal–immune interface (CD68 for macrophages), (iii) tracks epithelial content and proliferation (panCK to segment epithelium, Ki67 for cycling epithelium) and (iv) includes RUNX3, which was nominated by the NanoString contrasts and is a lineage- and context-informative transcriptional node that sits at the intersection of T-cell differentiation and epithelial tumor-suppressive pathways [48–50]. This marker set operationalizes the bulk immune–stromal signal at single-cell resolution, enabling us to test whether cell abundance and spatial organization recapitulate the NanoString axis. For each lobular and lesion region, we quantified CD8⁺, CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ cells in epithelial, stromal and total compartments by MxIF and computed per-sample stromal densities by summing cells across all stromal regions and normalizing by the summed stromal area.

In morphologically normal lobules, stromal immune cell densities were consistently lower in patients who later developed breast cancer than in controls (Fig. 2a). Compared with controls, cases exhibited approximately four-fold lower stromal CD8⁺ density (mean ± s.d. 39.5 ± 18.6 vs. 157.6 ± 138.5 cells/mm²; Welch’s t-test P = 0.010, BH-FDR q = 0.010), 2.6-fold lower stromal CD68⁺ density (74.9 ± 34.7 vs. 197.0 ± 120.8 cells/mm²; P = 0.004, q = 0.006) and 3.4-fold lower stromal RUNX3⁺ density (234.0 ± 140.6 vs. 792.2 ± 540.9 cells/mm²; P = 0.003, q = 0.006). Here, BH-FDR q values were computed across the three stromal marker density contrasts shown in Fig. 2a. These differences persisted despite heterogeneity in the number of lobules sampled per patient, indicating that the background lobular microenvironment in cases is globally more immune-cold (immune-depleted) than in controls.

We then examined stromal immune densities in the SA lesion itself, restricting to samples with at least one SA lesion field annotated (controls n = 12, cases n = 8). Although lesion-level differences were less pronounced than those observed in lobules, SA lesions in controls still harbored higher stromal densities of CD8⁺ (142.9 ± 118.2 vs. 59.1 ± 37.0 cells/mm²; P = 0.038, BH-FDR q = 0.040), CD68⁺ (386.5 ± 343.8 vs. 103.3 ± 68.8 cells/mm²; P = 0.017, q = 0.040) and RUNX3⁺ cells (979.4 ± 800.8 vs. 385.5 ± 376.0 cells/mm²; P = 0.040, q = 0.040) than lesions in cases (Fig. 2b). BH-FDR q values were computed across the three stromal marker density contrasts shown in Fig. 2b. Thus, both background lobules and the SA lesion tend to be relatively immune-cold in women who subsequently develop breast cancer.

To directly relate these spatial features to the NanoString immune signature defined in SA tissue, we correlated per-sample stromal densities with the immune signature. Stromal CD8⁺, CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ densities in lobular stroma were positively correlated with the immune signature (CD8⁺: Spearman ρ = 0.58, P = 0.005, BH-FDR q = 0.008; CD68⁺: ρ = 0.56, P = 0.008, q = 0.008; RUNX3⁺: ρ = 0.54, P = 0.001, q = 0.004), as was stromal CD8⁺ density in the SA lesion (ρ = 0.56, P = 0.008, q = 0.008; Fig. 2c). Here, BH-FDR q values were computed across these four immune-signature correlation tests. In contrast, these stromal immune densities showed either weak or negative correlations with the NanoString stromal/ECM/TGFβ signature.

A correlation heatmap summarizing these associations revealed a coherent pattern (Fig. 2d). Because this panel is intended as a compact, rank-based effect-size summary across multiple variables, we computed Spearman correlations among the per-sample stromal immune densities (lobular stroma and SA lesion stroma), NanoString signature scores, and case status (coded 1 for cases, 0 for controls). Under this framework, per-sample stromal immune densities in lobular stroma showed strong negative correlations with case status (for example, lobule stromal RUNX3⁺: ρ = −0.72; lobule stromal CD8⁺: ρ = −0.60; lobule stromal CD68⁺: ρ = −0.65) and strong positive correlations with the NanoString immune signature (RUNX3⁺: ρ = 0.54; CD8⁺: ρ = 0.58; CD68⁺: ρ = 0.56). Stromal immune densities in SA lesions exhibited more moderate but concordant associations with case status (CD8⁺: ρ = −0.42; CD68⁺: ρ = −0.50; RUNX3⁺: ρ = −0.42) and with the immune signature (ρ = 0.20–0.58), linking higher stromal immune infiltration to an immune-high/stromal-low NanoString profile and to control status. These findings indicate that the nanostring immune–stromal axis in SA tissue is not merely a bulk expression artifact but is mirrored by differences in stromal immune cell infiltration in both background lobules and the SA lesion.

Within-patient lesion–lobule contrasts show modest, parallel changes in immune cell composition

We next leveraged the repeated-measures structure of the data to compare morphologically normal lobules with the corresponding SA lesion within each biopsy. For each marker (CD8⁺, CD68⁺, RUNX3⁺, panCK⁺) and compartment (stroma, epithelium, total), we computed area-weighted mean densities in lobules and SA lesion fields and defined lesion–lobule differences (Δ = lesion − lobule).

In the stromal compartment, both cases and controls tended to show higher CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ stromal densities in SA lesions than in their own lobules, with only modest differences in the magnitude of these increases between outcome groups (Fig. 3a, top). Notably, none of the lesion–lobule delta contrasts shown in Fig. 3a or Fig. 3b differed significantly between cases and controls. Stromal CD8⁺ changes were more variable, with small average increases or decreases in both groups. When we considered total (epithelium plus stroma) RUNX3⁺ density, controls on average showed a decrease from lobules to lesions, whereas cases showed a modest increase (Fig. 3a, bottom), indicating a subtle divergence in how RUNX3⁺ cells are redistributed between compartments, despite higher absolute RUNX3⁺ densities in both lobules and lesions from controls (Fig. 2).

In the epithelial compartment, both groups generally showed a loss of immune cells when moving from lobules to SA lesions (Fig. 3b): epithelial CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ deltas were predominantly negative, consistent with relatively immune-poor lesion epithelium compared with lobular epithelium. Epithelial panCK⁺ densities also tended to decrease from lobules to lesions in most patients, with a larger mean decrease in cases than in controls, although this between-group difference did not reach statistical significance.

To assess whether lesion–lobule differences carried information beyond absolute densities, we correlated all Δ features with case status and the NanoString immune and stromal signatures (Fig. 3c). In contrast to the strong correlations observed for absolute stromal immune densities, lesion–lobule deltas showed only weak and heterogeneous associations (|Spearman ρ| typically < 0.3, all P > 0.2 for case status and signatures). This pattern suggests that progression risk is better captured by the overall immune tone shared by lobules and lesions than by whether SA lesions gain or lose immune cells relative to each woman’s baseline lobular context.

Lobules segregate into immune-cold and immune-hot types that are differentially distributed between cases and controls

Given the strong associations between stromal immune densities and case status at the sample level (Fig. 2), we asked whether individual lobules could be grouped into recurrent phenotypic types based on their MxIF profiles. We focused on the total compartment for each lobule and considered seven features: CD27⁺, CD68⁺, CD8⁺, Ki67⁺, panCK⁺, RUNX3⁺, and total cells, capturing adaptive and innate immune infiltration, epithelial content and proliferation, and overall lobule cellularity. We clustered lobules using k-means (k = 2) on z-scored features (Methods), and visualized the resulting partition in a PCA projection (Fig. 4a). One cluster comprised lobules with lower panCK⁺, immune, and proliferation markers, whereas the other comprised larger, more cellular, immune-rich lobules. To facilitate multi-marker comparison on a single axis, we expressed lobule-type centroid profiles as marker densities normalized to the immune-cold centroid for each feature. Under this normalization, immune-hot lobules were elevated across immune markers (CD8⁺, CD68⁺, CD27⁺, RUNX3⁺), epithelial content (panCK⁺), proliferation (Ki67⁺), and overall cellularity (Fig. 4b; cluster robust hot–cold contrasts for each feature, Supplemental Table S3a). We therefore refer to these as immune-cold lobules (less cellular, immune-poor) and immune-hot lobules (highly cellular, proliferative, immune-rich). Representative MxIF images illustrate these phenotypes: immune-cold lobules show sparse immune and RUNX3 staining, whereas immune-hot lobules display dense CD8⁺, CD68⁺, RUNX3⁺, and CD27⁺ infiltration and abundant Ki67⁺ epithelium (Fig. 4e, f).

We next examined how these lobule types were distributed across cases and controls. Because multiple lobules were sampled within each biopsy, lobule-level counts are descriptive and do not represent independent observations; therefore, we emphasize patient-level summaries (Fig. 4c, d) and cluster-robust models for inference. Across all 198 lobules, immune-hot lobules were strongly enriched in controls: 47 of 51 immune-hot lobules (92%) arose in women who remained cancer-free, whereas only 4 immune-hot lobules (8%) arose in women who later developed breast cancer. In contrast, immune-cold lobules were more evenly distributed (69/147 [47%] from cases and 78/147 [53%] from controls). At the patient level, the mean fraction of immune-hot lobules per sample was 0.32 in controls and only 0.05 in cases (Fig. 4c). Several controls had a majority of their lobules classified as immune-hot (fraction 0.4–1.0).

We next asked whether the proportion of immune-hot lobules per biopsy tracked with case status (Fig. 4d). Across the 24 biopsies, cases were enriched for samples with few or no immune-hot lobules, whereas controls more often had a mixture that included a substantial immune-hot component. Notably, immune-hot lobules were rare among cases (only 3 women had a fraction of immune-hot lobules larger than 0, across 9 women), so we interpret the patient-level association as descriptive and the regression as exploratory. In a simple exploratory logistic regression with case status as the binary outcome and the fraction of immune-hot lobules as the only predictor, higher immune-hot fraction was strongly associated with lower odds of being a case (logit[P(case)] = 0.53 − 7.76 × immune-hot fraction; in-sample AUC 0.78, 95% CI 0.60–0.95). Given the modest sample size, we interpret this model as a summary of effect size rather than as a predictive tool.

We then compared lobule-type composition with the NanoString immune signature. Because this cohort is small (n = 24 biopsies; 9 cases), these models are exploratory and intended to compare complementary signals rather than to build a validated predictor. In exploratory logistic models, the immune signature alone separated cases and controls moderately, and models using the fraction of immune-hot lobules showed a similar level of discrimination. A model combining both features improved in-sample separation, and the immune-hot lobule fraction was only weakly correlated with the NanoString immune signature (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.20, P = 0.36). Together, these results suggest that spatial heterogeneity in lobule immune state carries information about progression risk that is partially independent of bulk SA gene expression, but this complementarity will require validation in larger independent cohorts.

SA lesion fields form four archetypal microenvironments that differ between cases and controls

We next asked whether regions of interest (ROIs) sampling the SA lesion itself (“fields”; Methods) could be grouped into recurrent spatial archetypes and whether these differed between women who did or did not progress to breast cancer. For each lesion field, we used the total compartment and extracted CD27⁺, CD68⁺, CD8⁺, Ki67⁺, panCK⁺, RUNX3⁺, and total cell densities from the MxIF dataset. After these features were z-scored across all lesion fields (n = 119), we clustered lesion fields using k-means (k = 4; Methods) and visualized the resulting partition in a PCA projection (Fig. 5a). Comparison of lesion-field centroid profiles as marker densities normalized to the immune-cold centroid for each feature revealed four biologically interpretable SA field archetypes (Fig. 5b; cluster-robust contrasts for each feature, Supplemental Table S3b): immune-cold fields (low immune and Ki67), immune-hot fields (CD27/CD8/RUNX3-high), proliferative fields (panCK/Ki67-high), and macrophage-rich fields (CD68-dominant). Representative MxIF images from archetypal fields illustrate the three principal lesion field types: proliferative (Fig. 5e), immune-cold (Fig. 5f), and immune-hot (Fig. 5g).

We then quantified how these lesion archetypes were distributed between cases and controls (Fig. 5c). Across all fields, immune-cold SA fields were markedly enriched in cases (41/55 fields, 75%) compared with controls (23/64 fields, 36%). In contrast, immune-hot and proliferative SA fields were more common in controls. Among control lesion fields, 15/64 (23%) were immune-hot and 22/64 (34%) were proliferative, whereas among case fields only 3/55 (5%) were immune-hot and 11/55 (20%) were proliferative. Macrophage-rich SA fields were observed exclusively in controls (4/64 [6%]) and absent in cases. Overall, 37/64 (58%) control fields belonged to the immune-hot or proliferative archetypes, compared with only 14/55 (25%) of fields in cases.

To summarize lesion composition at the patient level, we computed, for each sample with at least one SA lesion field (controls n = 12, cases n = 8), a hot lesion fraction defined as the fraction of fields assigned to immune-hot, proliferative or macrophage-dominant SA archetypes. This fraction varied widely among controls (median 0.67, interquartile range 0.33–0.95) but was generally lower among cases (median 0.12, interquartile range 0.00–0.24; Fig. 5d). In an exploratory logistic regression with case status as the outcome and hot lesion fraction as the predictor (logit[P(case)] = 1.18–4.60 × hot fraction), higher hot lesion fraction was associated with lower odds of being a case and yielded moderate in-sample discrimination (AUC 0.84, 95% CI 0.66–1.00; DeLong method). Given the modest sample size, we interpret this model as an effect-size summary rather than a validated predictor. The model estimated a high probability of being a case when no hot lesion fields were present (P(case) ≈ 0.77 at hot fraction = 0), which fell as the hot fraction increased (≈ 0.51 at 0.25, 0.24 at 0.5, 0.09 at 0.75 and 0.03 at 1.0). AUC for the logistic model case vs. control ~ fraction hot lesion fraction was 0.84 (95% CI 0.66–1.00; DeLong method), estimated in-sample using pROC.

Interestingly, the hot lesion fraction showed only a weak correlation with the NanoString immune signature derived from SA tissue (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.12, P ≈ 0.60), suggesting that bulk immune gene expression and the spatial distribution of lesion field archetypes capture distinct aspects of the microenvironment. This parallels the lobule-level analysis, in which the immune-hot lobule fraction was also only weakly correlated with the immune signature yet improved in-sample discrimination when combined with it (Fig. 4d). In exploratory logistic models with case status as the outcome, the NanoString immune signature alone separated cases and controls moderately, and adding the hot lesion fraction further improved in-sample discrimination (Fig. 5d). These findings indicate that lesion field archetypes, particularly the relative abundance of immune-hot and proliferative fields versus immune-cold fields, provide complementary information about progression risk beyond bulk SA gene expression.

Spatial immune architecture around epithelium and proliferating cells differs by lobule type and case status

To define how spatial relationships between stromal immune cells and epithelium differed between women who did and did not develop breast cancer, we used nearest-neighbor analyses at the single-cell level to derive lobule-level metrics. For each morphologically normal lobule, we summarized the median distance from stromal CD68⁺ cells to their nearest panCK⁺ epithelial cell (CD68⁺–panCK⁺ distance). Lobules from women who subsequently developed breast cancer showed larger median CD68⁺–panCK⁺ distances than lobules from controls (median 54.32 μm vs. 38.54 μm; Fig. 6a); however, this case–control contrast did not reach statistical significance after accounting for within-patient clustering (cluster-robust P = 0.16, BH-FDR q = 0.19; Supplemental Table S4). Thus, the epithelial proximity of lobular macrophages shows a directional trend by case status, but is sensitive to within-patient sampling variation.

We next examined how spatial immune proximity to proliferating epithelium varied with lobule type. For each lobule, we computed the median distance from stromal CD68⁺ and CD8⁺ cells to their nearest Ki67⁺ cell. Immune-hot lobules showed markedly shorter CD68⁺–Ki67⁺ and CD8⁺–Ki67⁺ distances than immune-cold lobules, with median CD68⁺–Ki67⁺ distances of 197.82 μm versus 302.77 μm and median CD8⁺–Ki67⁺ distances of 179.96 μm versus 279.36 μm, respectively (Fig. 6b). To further characterize the organization of adaptive immune microenvironments, and guided by studies in breast, melanoma, pancreatic, and colorectal cancers showing that CD8⁺ T cells within ~ 20 μm of tumor cells mark a functionally engaged peritumoral zone [44–47], we prespecified CD8-centered microcluster metrics based on a 20 μm interaction radius. We first quantified T/B-cell–rich microclusters by measuring, for each lobule, the fraction of stromal CD27⁺ cells with at least one CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Immune-hot lobules had a substantially higher fraction of CD27⁺ cells engaged in such CD27–CD8 microclusters than immune-cold lobules (median 0.28 vs. 0.13; n = 51 vs. 143 lobules; cluster-robust P = 0.01, BH-FDR q = 0.035; Fig. 6c and Supplemental Table S4), indicating that immune-hot lobules are not only more immune-dense but also enriched for locally clustered adaptive immune niches. We then focused on RUNX3⁺ cells, which showed the strongest per-sample association with case status among the stromal markers in both lobules and SA lesions (Fig. 2a, b,d) and contributed prominently to the immune-hot archetypes (Figs. 4b and 5b). For each lobule, we calculated the fraction of stromal RUNX3⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Lobules from controls exhibited a higher median prevalence of RUNX3–CD8 microclusters than lobules from cases (median fraction 0.25 vs. 0.15; n = 125 vs. 73 lobules; Fig. 6d), but this case–control contrast was attenuated after accounting for within-patient clustering and did not reach statistical significance (cluster-robust P = 0.06, BH-FDR q = 0.105; Supplemental Table S4).

Because many stromal immune cells lie near the epithelial–stromal boundary, we then asked whether boundary-proximal CD8⁺ cells differ between cases and controls. Using distance from the tissue category edge, we defined, for each lobule with at least one stromal CD8⁺ cell, the boundary CD8 fraction as the proportion of stromal CD8⁺ cells located within 10 μm of the epithelial–stromal interface. Lobules from controls showed a numerically higher boundary CD8 fraction than lobules from cases (median 0.45 vs. 0.34; n = 124 vs. 72 lobules; Fig. 6e), but this contrast was not statistically significant after accounting for within-patient clustering (cluster-robust P = 0.10, BH-FDR q = 0.14; Supplemental Table S4). Thus, while the direction is consistent with reduced epithelial-edge CD8⁺ sentinels in cases, this metric is sensitive to within-patient sampling.

We also asked whether individual proliferating epithelial cells are adequately covered by nearby immune cells. For each lobule containing at least one Ki67⁺ epithelial cell, we calculated a CD8 coverage index defined as the fraction of Ki67⁺ cells with at least one CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Although many lobules in both groups had Ki67⁺ cells completely devoid of nearby CD8⁺ cells, lobules from controls showed, on average, higher CD8 coverage of Ki67⁺ cells than lobules from cases (Fig. 6f). However, after accounting for within-patient clustering, this case–control contrast did not reach statistical significance (cluster-robust P = 0.38, BH-FDR q = 0.38; Supplemental Table S4).

In lobule-level spatial analyses, adjusting for patient-level clustering substantially attenuated many naïve associations (Supplemental Table S4). With CR2 robust SEs, only spatial metrics involving stromal immune proximity to proliferating epithelium (CD8→Ki67 and CD68→Ki67 distances) and CD27–CD8 microclusters remained statistically significant, and these associations also remained below BH-FDR q < 0.05 within the Fig. 6 spatial-metric family. Case–control and lobule-type contrasts for other spatial metrics were no longer significant, indicating that much of the apparent lobule-level heterogeneity reflects within-patient sampling variation rather than independent biological differences.

Taken together, these spatial analyses show that immune-hot lobules are characterized by immune cells—particularly CD8⁺ T cells, CD68⁺ macrophages, CD27⁺ cells and RUNX3⁺ cells—positioned close to proliferating epithelium and organized into mixed microclusters (Fig. 6c, d). Several lobule-level spatial metrics also showed directional case–control trends consistent with reduced epithelial surveillance in cases (for example, lower RUNX3–CD8 microclusters, fewer boundary-proximal CD8⁺ cells and lower CD8 coverage of Ki67⁺ epithelium), but these contrasts were attenuated after accounting for within-patient clustering and did not reach statistical significance (Supplemental Table S4). Representative MxIF images of sentinel-rich, well-covered immune-hot lobules from controls (Fig. 6g) visually illustrate short CD8⁺/CD68⁺–Ki67⁺ distances and abundant CD27/RUNX3/CD8 microclusters. Together, these findings add a spatial and epithelial surveillance dimension to the immune-competent lobular field versus immune-cold lobular field phenotypes identified earlier.

SA lesions in cases lack boundary-hugging CD8 sentinels and RUNX3-rich microclusters

Having established that cases exhibit an immune-poor lobular field and that immune-hot lobules are characterized by tighter stromal immune proximity to proliferating epithelium and enriched CD27–CD8 microclusters (Fig. 6), we next asked whether SA lesions themselves exhibit analogous spatial differences between cases and controls. Using nearest-neighbor data from stromal CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ cells, we quantified, for each SA lesion field, the median distance from stromal immune cells to proliferating epithelial cells (Ki67⁺). Lesion fields from controls showed substantially smaller CD8⁺–Ki67⁺ and CD68⁺–Ki67⁺ distances than fields from women who subsequently developed breast cancer (Fig. 7a, b). In patient-clustered models, the CD68⁺–Ki67⁺ contrast remained significant (cluster-robust P = 0.0228, BH-FDR q = 0.035), whereas the CD8⁺–Ki67⁺ contrast was nominally significant but slightly above the BH-FDR threshold (P = 0.0432, q = 0.052; Supplemental Table S5). Representative boundary sentinel–rich control lesions illustrate these findings in CD8⁺/CD68⁺ proximity to Ki67⁺ epithelium (Fig. 7g).

We then examined whether SA lesions differ in the organization of stromal CD27⁺ and RUNX3⁺ immune populations. For each lesion field, we estimated the fraction of stromal CD27⁺ cells with a RUNX3⁺ neighbor within 20 μm (CD27–RUNX3 microclusters) and the fraction of stromal RUNX3⁺ cells with a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm (RUNX3–CD8 microclusters). Both measures were markedly reduced in lesions from cases compared with controls (Fig. 7c, d). In patient-clustered models, the CD27–RUNX3 microcluster fraction (cluster-robust P = 0.0075, BH-FDR q = 0.027) and RUNX3–CD8 microcluster fraction (P = 0.0091, q = 0.027) remained statistically significant (Supplemental Table S5). CD27–CD8 microclusters showed a similar directional trend but with a weaker effect size, suggesting that a distinctive feature of control lesions is the presence of mixed RUNX3-rich adaptive immune clusters.

We next asked whether CD8⁺ T cells in SA lesions also hug the epithelial–stromal boundary and whether they effectively cover proliferating epithelial cells. For each lesion field with at least one stromal CD8⁺ cell, we calculated a CD8 boundary fraction, defined as the proportion of stromal CD8⁺ cells lying within 10 μm of the epithelial–stromal interface. Control lesions showed higher boundary CD8 fractions than lesions from cases (Fig. 7e). After accounting for within-patient clustering, this difference remained statistically significant (cluster-robust P = 0.0235, BH-FDR q = 0.035; Supplemental Table S5). Thus, in lesions from controls, a larger share of stromal CD8⁺ cells occupy boundary-hugging sentinel positions at the epithelial edge.

Finally, we quantified CD8-mediated immune coverage of proliferating epithelial cells within SA lesions. Using nearest-neighbor distances from epithelial Ki67⁺ cells to CD8⁺ cells, we defined, for each lesion field with at least one Ki67⁺ cell, a CD8 coverage index as the fraction of Ki67⁺ cells that had a CD8⁺ neighbor within 20 μm. Coverage values were generally low and zero-inflated in both groups, reflecting the fact that many individual Ki67⁺ cells had no nearby CD8⁺ cell (Fig. 7f). Control lesion fields (n = 36) had a mean coverage of 0.10 (median 0.0), whereas case lesion fields (n = 6) had a mean of 0.056 (median 0.0). The direction of effect was consistent with the lobule-level trend, but the group difference did not reach statistical significance in patient-clustered models (cluster-robust P = 0.515, BH-FDR q = 0.515; Supplemental Table S5).

Compared with lobules, where many naïve spatial associations attenuated after accounting for within-patient clustering (Supplemental Table S4), lesion-level spatial metrics were more robust to within-patient clustering (Supplemental Table S5). Using patient-clustered models with robust (CR2) standard errors, contrasts involving stromal immune proximity to proliferating epithelium (CD8→Ki67 and CD68→Ki67), RUNX3-associated microclusters (CD27–RUNX3 and RUNX3–CD8), and boundary-proximal CD8 infiltration remained nominally significant; after BH-FDR correction within the Fig. 7 family, all remained below q < 0.05 except CD8→Ki67 distance (q = 0.052). The Ki67-centric CD8 coverage index was the exception: it remained non-significant and was most sensitive to patient-level heterogeneity, consistent with its low, zero-inflated distribution and the small number of Ki67-positive lesion fields in cases. Overall, these cluster-robust models indicate that most lesion-level spatial architectures (Fig. 7b–e) reflect robust case–control differences rather than per-field sampling variability, whereas most lobule-level spatial metrics were more sensitive to within-patient sampling (Supplemental Table S4).

Taken together, these lesion-level spatial findings reinforce and extend the multiscale boundary sentinels and coverage picture seen in normal lobules. In controls, SA lesions sit in an immune-engaged microenvironment in which stromal CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ cells lie close to proliferating epithelium, a large fraction of CD8⁺ cells cluster at the epithelial–stromal interface, and stromal CD27⁺ and RUNX3⁺ cells form abundant RUNX3-rich microclusters with CD8⁺ T cells. In contrast, SA lesions in women who progress are immune-cold and spatially under-surveilled, with proliferating epithelial cells surrounded by immune cells at greater distances, fewer boundary-hugging CD8 sentinels, and a marked paucity of CD27–RUNX3 and RUNX3–CD8 microclusters. Together with the lobule-level data, these results suggest that both the lobular field and the SA lesion itself must be immune competent, boundary engaged and spatially organized to constrain progression.

Discussion

In this nested case–control study of women with SA, we combined bulk NanoString profiling with spatially resolved MxIF to characterize the SA microenvironment and its surrounding lobular field in relation to subsequent invasive breast cancer. We found a consistent immune–stromal axis across modalities: SA biopsies from women who later developed cancer showed reduced immune gene expression and lower stromal densities of CD8⁺, CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ cells, together with higher expression of stromal/ECM/TGFβ programs, whereas biopsies from women who remained cancer-free were enriched for immune signatures and immune-dense stroma. At the tissue level, morphologically normal lobules segregated into immune-cold and immune-hot archetypes (Fig. 4), and SA lesion fields into four reproducible microenvironments (immune-cold, immune-hot, epithelium/Ki67-high and macrophage-rich; Fig. 5). Immune-hot lobules and immune-engaged/proliferative SA fields were strongly enriched in controls, whereas cases were dominated by immune-cold lobules and lesions. Spatial metrics added a further layer: in controls, both lobules and SA lesions contained CD8⁺ and CD68⁺ cells positioned close to epithelium and proliferating cells, with abundant RUNX3-rich microclusters and CD8⁺ boundary sentinels lining the epithelial–stromal interface (Figs. 6 and 7). In contrast, women who progressed had globally immune-poor fields and lesions in which immune cells sat farther from Ki67⁺ epithelium, boundary proximal CD8⁺ cells were depleted, and RUNX3-rich clusters were rare, while Ki67-centric CD8 coverage showed greater patient-level heterogeneity. Together, these findings support a microenvironmental risk phenotype characterized by immune-cold SA embedded within an immune-cold, stromally activated lobular field. Conversely, women who remained cancer-free appeared to maintain an immune-engaged, RUNX3-rich microenvironment at multiple scales, both across morphologically normal lobules and within SA lesion fields, in which proliferating epithelial cells are more tightly and spatially surveilled.

An immune–stromal field phenotype associated with subsequent breast cancer

A striking feature of our data is that case–control differences extend well beyond the SA lesion itself. In morphologically normal lobules, stromal densities of CD8⁺, CD68⁺ and RUNX3⁺ cells were markedly lower in women who later developed cancer than in controls, and these stromal densities tracked closely with an SA-derived immune gene expression signature (Fig. 2). SA lesions in cases similarly showed fewer stromal immune cells and a higher stromal/ECM/TGFβ NanoString signature. When integrated, these observations argue for a broadly altered field microenvironment in women who progressed: the background lobular bed is immune-cold and stromally rewired, and SA lesions arising in this context fail to recruit or organize robust immune infiltrates. Importantly, because this study is restricted to biopsies containing SA, our data cannot determine whether the immune-cold field phenotype is specific to SA-bearing breasts or represents a more general breast-wide host/tissue state associated with risk. The similarity in direction of immune and stromal differences in both morphologically normal lobules and SA lesions, together with the relatively modest lesion–lobule deltas (Fig. 3), suggests that much of the risk-associated signal reflects a field-level microenvironment rather than an SA-specific stromal or immune response. Future studies that include benign biopsies without SA and that compare multiple benign histologic subtypes within the same cohort will be required to resolve lesion-specific versus field-wide contributions to risk.

In contrast, women who remained cancer-free tended to have immune-competent lobules and SA lesions embedded within an immune-engaged stromal field. These biopsy-scale differences were captured not only by bulk expression and absolute densities, but also by simple summary metrics: the fraction of immune-hot lobules per biopsy and the fraction of immune-hot/proliferative lesion fields (hot lesion fraction) each explained a substantial proportion of the variation in case status and added predictive information beyond the NanoString immune signature alone (Figs. 4 and 5). Although our sample size is modest and these models are exploratory, the convergence of bulk, density-based and archetype-based measures supports the concept that an immune-cold field microenvironment is associated with higher breast cancer risk in women with SA.

Relationship to sclerosing adenosis risk assessment

SA is a common proliferative lesion in benign breast biopsies, characterized by expansion and distortion of lobular units in a fibrotic stroma. Large cohort studies, including analyses from the Mayo BBD cohort, have linked SA to an approximately twofold increase in subsequent breast cancer risk, although the magnitude of risk has varied and SA is generally considered a risk marker rather than a direct precursor lesion [4, 7, 33]. Existing risk models for women with BBD incorporate age, family history and broad histologic categories (non-proliferative disease, proliferative disease with or without atypia, atypical hyperplasia), sometimes combined with mammographic density and other factors [4, 7–10]. These models treat the microenvironment largely as a black box: stroma, resident immunity and spatial architecture are not explicitly captured. Our results suggest that explicitly incorporating quantitative microenvironment features may complement existing BBD risk models and improve risk discrimination, but this will require validation in larger cohorts.

Prior studies examining infiltrating immune cells in BBD have generally used bulk or semi-quantitative measures and have not identified strong associations between total immune infiltrates and subsequent invasive breast cancer risk [23, 51]. Our findings help reconcile these observations. By focusing specifically on SA and its immediate lobular context, and by using single-cell spatial metrics, we show that risk-associated differences are encoded not only in immune abundance but in the loss of spatially organized immune niches that position immune cells near epithelium and proliferating cells. Immune-cold lobules and SA fields in cases are characterized by low immune densities and increased distances between immune cells and epithelium, fewer boundary-proximal CD8⁺ cells and depleted mixed CD27/RUNX3/CD8 microclusters (Figs. 6 and 7). Such higher-order spatial features are unlikely to be captured by earlier bulk assessments of immune infiltrates, potentially explaining why those studies did not identify strong risk associations.

More broadly, our results extend the concept of field cancerization in the breast [32]. Histologic features of BBD and radiologic markers such as mammographic density have been interpreted as manifestations of larger fields of altered epithelium and stroma that predispose to cancer [10, 15]. Here we show that immune and stromal states also exhibit field-like behavior: multiple lobules within the same biopsy share a common immune-cold, stromal-high phenotype in women who subsequently develop breast cancer, whereas controls more often display a heterogeneous field containing immune-hot lobules and immune-engaged SA lesions.

Tissue-resident immunity, RUNX3 and microenvironmental control

Our observations fit within, and extend, an emerging literature on tissue-resident immunity in breast tissue. In invasive breast cancer, tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, particularly CD8⁺ T cells, have been linked to improved survival and response to systemic therapy [16–19]. Single-cell analyses have identified CD8⁺ T-cell subsets with a tissue-resident memory (TRM) phenotype that correlate with favorable prognosis and response to immune checkpoint blockade [21, 22, 26]. Even histologically normal breast tissue harbors abundant resident immune cells, including T cells, B cells and macrophages, which are thought to participate in early recognition and control of atypical epithelial clones [25].

In this context, the immune-hot lobules we observe in controls, which are rich in CD8⁺, CD68⁺, RUNX3⁺ and CD27⁺ cells and with immune cells positioned close to Ki67⁺ epithelium and the epithelial–stromal boundary (Fig. 6), may represent lobules with robust tissue-resident immunity that continually surveil proliferating epithelial cells. Conversely, the predominance of immune-cold lobules in women who progressed suggests that these breasts lack a widespread network of resident immune cells capable of effective surveillance. SA lesion fields in controls also often adopt immune-hot or epithelium/Ki67-high archetypes with dense adaptive infiltrates (Fig. 5), implying that these lesions are noticed by the immune system and embedded in a dynamic microenvironment, whereas SA lesions in cases remain relatively immune-cold.

The particularly strong associations we observe for RUNX3-positive cells suggest that a RUNX3-enriched niche may be an important component of this protective microenvironment. RUNX3 has been implicated as a tumour suppressor in breast cancer, where it can be inactivated through genetic and epigenetic mechanisms and modulate oestrogen receptor signalling [48, 52, 53]. It also sits at the interface of immunity, inflammation and cancer, influencing T-cell differentiation and stromal–epithelial crosstalk [49, 50]. In our cohort, stromal and total RUNX3⁺ cell densities in both lobules and SA lesions were markedly higher in controls than in cases, and RUNX3 contributed strongly to the definition of immune-hot lobule and lesion archetypes (Figs. 2, 4 and 5). Moreover, RUNX3-rich microclusters that include CD8⁺ and CD27⁺ cells were depleted in both lobules and lesions from cases (Figs. 6 and 7). Across modalities, RUNX3 consistently marks immune-engaged lobular and SA microenvironments, appearing in high-density stromal infiltrates, in immune-hot archetypes, and in mixed RUNX3–CD8 and CD27–RUNX3 microclusters, suggesting that RUNX3-enriched immune niches may be a critical component of protective field immunity in benign breast tissue. Although our immunophenotyping does not resolve specific TRM subsets or regulatory populations, these patterns raise the possibility that RUNX3-enriched immune/stromal niches support both effective immune surveillance and tumour-suppressive signalling to epithelium, whereas their loss in cases may permit atypical clones within SA to escape both immune and epithelial-intrinsic control.

At the same time, the NanoString stromal signature in cases was enriched for TGFβ and ECM-related genes that have been linked to immunosuppressive, pro-tumour stroma in other contexts [25, 50]. The combination of stromal activation with reduced immune infiltration and RUNX3 depletion may create a microenvironment that physically separates immune cells from epithelium, dampens effector function and fosters clonal expansion within SA.

Microenvironmental repertoires and risk stratification

A conceptual advantage of our spatial approach is that it allows us to consider the repertoire of microenvironments within a given biopsy, rather than focusing solely on the properties of a single lesion. Morphologically normal lobules are partitioned into two reproducible phenotypes: an immune-cold, less cellular lobule and an immune-hot, highly cellular/proliferative lobule (Fig. 4). SA lesion fields are partitioned into four archetypes: immune-cold, immune-hot (T/B-cell-rich), epithelium/Ki67-high and macrophage-rich (Fig. 5). Cases were dominated by immune-cold lobules and SA fields, whereas controls more often exhibited a diverse microenvironmental repertoire that included immune-hot lobules and immune-engaged SA fields.

At the patient level, simple metrics such as the fraction of immune-hot lobules and the hot lesion fraction captured meaningful variation in case status and provided complementary information to the NanoString immune signature. These observations suggest that what distinguishes higher- and lower-risk SA is not the presence of any single bad lesion type, but the absence of protective microenvironments, particularly immune-rich lobules and immune-engaged SA fields, and a tendency toward a homogeneous, immune-cold field. In other words, progression risk appears to track depletion of a protective immune-engaged repertoire rather than the emergence of a single distinctive high-risk lesion subtype. From a translational perspective, this raises the possibility that relatively simple immunohistochemical panels (for example, CD8, CD68, RUNX3, pan-cytokeratin and Ki67) scored at the lobule and lesion-field level, or quantified across whole-slide images to capture both overall immune tone and heterogeneity, could approximate these archetypes and be integrated into existing BBD risk models.

Clinical implications for risk stratification and prevention

If validated, microenvironmental profiling of SA and its surrounding lobules could refine risk stratification for women with BBD beyond traditional clinicopathologic variables. A woman with SA whose biopsy shows an immune-cold, stromal-high field lacking immune-hot lobules and lesion fields may have a meaningfully different risk profile from a woman with SA in an immune-competent field, even if their histologic diagnoses are identical. Incorporating field-level immune and stromal features into risk models could help identify women who may benefit from more intensive surveillance, chemoprevention or future microenvironment-modulating interventions.

Our data also raise the broader concept of microenvironmental prevention. In invasive breast cancer and other malignancies, strategies aimed at enhancing local immune tone, normalizing stromal signalling or promoting tissue-resident memory T-cell populations are being actively explored [20–22, 26]. If an immune-cold SA in an immune-cold field proves to be a reproducible risk phenotype, analogous interventions could eventually be tested in high-risk benign settings to determine whether shifting the field toward an immune-competent, RUNX3-rich state reduces progression risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample size is modest (24 SA biopsies) with limited numbers of lobules and lesion fields per patient, and all samples were derived from a single institution. Although patterns were consistent across multiple modalities, all associations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, independent cohorts and in other BBD histologies. Our NanoString and MxIF panels, while focused, do not capture all relevant immune and stromal subsets, and our classification of immune-hot versus immune-cold lobules and lesion fields is necessarily coarse. Larger sample size future studies incorporating more extensive immunophenotyping and spatial transcriptomics could refine these archetypes, resolve specific effector and regulatory populations, and link them to functional states.

We analyzed a single time-point biopsy per patient and cannot assess temporal dynamics. Because controls were defined by absence of invasive breast cancer within 15 years after the index biopsy, some may develop breast cancer after this window (as occurred in two women). Immune states may also fluctuate over time. Longitudinal imaging and serial sampling would be valuable to determine whether immune-cold fields are stable features or transient states, and whether microenvironmental shifts precede histologic progression. Fourth, while we adjusted for case status and used internal expression signatures, we did not explicitly model all potential confounders such as hormonal exposures, parity or systemic inflammatory conditions that might influence tissue immunity. Fifth, because all biopsies in the present study contained SA, we cannot determine whether the immune-cold field phenotype we describe is specific to SA-bearing breasts or reflects a more general benign breast microenvironment associated with risk. Finally, MxIF is currently a specialized research technology. Translation into routine pathology practice will require demonstrating that simpler, lower-plex assays can recapitulate the key risk-associated patterns we describe. Although we used patient-clustered models for spatial metrics and report BH-FDR q values for families of related spatial comparisons (Supplemental Tables S3–S5), the modest cohort size means that all spatial and archetype associations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Despite these limitations, the convergence of bulk immune–stromal signatures, stromal immune densities, lobule and lesion archetypes and spatial sentinel metrics on a coherent immune-cold field phenotype provides a robust biological signal. This work offers a framework for linking benign breast microenvironments to cancer risk and highlights the potential of spatially informed, microenvironment-based markers to improve risk stratification for women with BBD.

Conclusions

In women with SA, we identify a microenvironmental phenotype characterized by immune-cold SA lesions embedded within an immune-cold, stromally activated lobular field that is associated with subsequent invasive breast cancer. In contrast, women who remain cancer-free more often display immune-hot, RUNX3-rich lobules and SA fields in which stromal immune cells cluster near proliferating epithelium and form mixed RUNX3-positive immune microclusters. These findings highlight the potential of multiscale immune and spatial features in benign breast tissue to refine risk stratification, but they require validation in larger, independent cohorts.
