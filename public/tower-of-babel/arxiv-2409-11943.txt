Uniform resolvent estimates, smoothing effects and spectral stability for the Heisenberg sublaplacian
Luca Fanelli, Haruya Mizutani, Luz Roncal, Nico Michele Schiavone

Abstract
. We establish global bounds for solutions to stationary and time-dependent Schrödinger equations associated with the sublaplacian ℒ \mathcal{L} on the Heisenberg group, as well as its pure fractional power ℒ s \mathcal{L}^{s} and conformally invariant fractional power ℒ s \mathcal{L}_{s} . The main ingredient is a new abstract uniform weighted resolvent estimate which is proved by using the method of weakly conjugate operators –a variant of Mourre’s commutator method– and Hardy’s type inequalities on the Heisenberg group. As applications, we show Kato-type smoothing effects for the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, and spectral stability of the sublaplacian perturbed by complex-valued decaying potentials satisfying an explicit subordination condition. In the local case s = 1 s=1 , we obtain uniform estimates without any symmetry or derivative loss, which improve previous results.

1.
Introduction

Many physical systems evolve within the laws of constrained dynamics, operating within specific geometric settings, where the movement is restricted to predetermined directions within the tangent space. This fundamental premise has spurred the advancement of the sub-Riemannian geometry, which starts with the famous paper by Cartan
[
11
]
. A fundamental aspect of sub-Riemannian structures lies in their ability to regain the “missing directions” within the tangent space by iteratively computing commutators of vector fields that characterize the partial differential operator under consideration. Among the large amount of physical applications, we mention Quantum Mechanics, non-Holonomic Mechanics, the Physiology of Neurovision, etc.
The typical framework for modeling such systems involves the so called non-abelian Carnot groups (or
stratified Groups
), which are non-abelian Lie groups
\mathbb{G}
, (i.e. groups which are also Riemannian manifolds, with a non-commutative group law) with a Lie algebra (i.e. the tangent space at the identity) which exhibits a layered structure, mirroring the underlying physical context.
The toy model is given by the well known
(2d+1)
-dimensional
Heisenberg Group

\mathbb{H}^{d}
, introduced by Weil to describe his theoretical approach to Quantum Mechanics based on Group Representation Theory. Naturally connected to the sub-Riemannian structure of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, the
sublaplacian

\mathcal{L}
is the hypoelliptic operator defined as the sum of the squares of suitable horizontal vector fields which generate, together with their commutators, the Lie Algebra (see the definition
(​​

2.3
)
below).

The purpose of the present manuscript is to describe how the sub-Riemannian (in particular, the anisotropic) structure of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
affects to the global properties of solutions to Schrödinger equations associated with
\mathcal{L}
, as well as its pure fractional power
\mathcal{L}^{s}
and conformally invariant fractional power
\mathcal{L}_{s}
. We mainly focus on the so-called
uniform resolvent estimates
for the stationary problem and
Kato-type local smoothing effects
for the time-dependent problem.

Main results

We first recall some definitions and basic notions of the functional framework on the Heisenberg group (see e.g. the standard textbook
[

Let

Let us also equip

(
2.1)

\displaystyle|(z,t)|_{\mathbb{H}}:=(|z|^{4}+t^{2})^{1/4}

For

These vector fields satisfy the commutation relations

(

[X_{j},X_{k}]=[Y_{j},Y_{k}]=0,\qquad[X_{j},Y_{k}]=-4\delta_{jk}T,

Setting the horizontal gradient

2.1.2.
Spectral stability

In the last decades, Spectral analysis of non-self-adjoint operators has been extensively studied (see e.g.
[
13
,
14
,
19
,
20
,
28
]
and references therein). A natural and typical question in this context is the spectral stability, namely under what conditions on the perturbations are the spectrum of perturbed operators the same as that of the unperturbed one? It is worth mentioning that, in contrast with the self-adjoint case, many classical tools such as the comparison principle and the spectral decomposition theorem are not available in the non-self-adjoint setting in general. Instead, suitable resolvent estimates (combined with the Birman–Schwinger principle) have been shown to be very useful to prove the spectral stability or certain spectral radius bounds for eigenvalues (see
[
24
]
).

Our theorem can be also used to show the spectral stability for the Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{L}+V
with a complex-valued potential

as follows. Recall that
\mathcal{L}
is the positive self-adjoint operator on

associated to the quadratic form

q_{0}[\psi]:=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}|\nabla_{\!\mathbb{H}}\psi|^{2}dzdt,\qquad D(q_{0})=H^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d}),

with purely continuous spectrum
[0,\infty)
. Let

be a measurable function subordinated to
\mathcal{L}
, with bound less than one, viz. there exists
0\leq a<1
such that

(
2.11)

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}|V||\psi|^{2}dzdt\leq a\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}|\nabla_{\!\mathbb{H}}\psi|^{2}dzdt,

for all
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
. Then the quadratic form

q_{V}[\psi]:=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}V|\psi|^{2}dzdt,\quad D(q_{V}):=\Big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\ |\ \int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}|V||\psi|^{2}dzdt<\infty\Big\}

is relatively bounded with respect to
q_{0}
, with bound less than 1, and we can define an
m
-sectorial
differential operator

associated to the closed quadratic form

Theorem 2.10

(Spectral stability)
.

Suppose that

is a measurable function satisfying
(​​

2.11
)

Proof.

We write

with

and

2.2.
Known results

We here recall some known results and compare our theorems with them. In
[
2
]
, Bahouri, Barilari and Gallagher proved following Strichartz-type estimates:

\|e^{-i\tau\mathcal{L}}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}_{\tau}L^{q}_{z}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}

for any
2\leq p,q\leq\infty
,
2/p+2d/q=d+1
and cylindrical

. This estimate with the choice

and Hölder’s inequality particularly imply

\|w(t)e^{-i\tau\mathcal{L}}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{H}^{d})}\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}

for any cylindrical
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
and
w\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. In
[
35
]
, Măntoiu proved several space-time estimates with loss of derivatives for the sublaplacians on stratified Lie groups, which, in the setting of Heisenberg groups, particularly implies, for
1/2<s\leq 1
,

\|{\langle}|(z,t)|_{\mathbb{H}}{\rangle}^{-s}|(z,t)|_{\mathbb{H}}^{-s}{\langle}\mathcal{L}{\rangle}^{-s/2}\mathcal{L}^{(1-s)/2}e^{-i\tau\mathcal{L}}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{H}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}.

Note that the term
{\langle}\mathcal{L}{\rangle}^{-s/2}\mathcal{L}^{(1-s)/2}
represents the derivative losses if
s>1/2
.
Compared with these two results, the interesting point of our theorems is that, on one hand, we obtain uniform estimates
without any symmetry or derivative loss
((i) and (ii) in Theorems
2.1
and
2.8
), and, on the other hand, full derivative losses with respect to
\mathcal{L}
are not necessary and spherical ones are enough ((iii) and (iv) in Theorems
2.1
and
2.8
). In particular, we recover a similar uniform resolvent estimates and local smoothing effects to the Euclidean case under cylindrical symmetry (Theorems
2.4
and
2.9
).

3.
An abstract uniform resolvent estimate

In this section, we study a uniform resolvent estimate in an abstract setting, which will play an essential role in the proof of the main theorems.

We first recall basic facts on the functional calculus for self-adjoint operators used in the paper (see
[
38
]
on the spectral theory for self-adjoint operators). For any self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{H}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
and any Borel measurable function
\varphi(\lambda)
on
{\mathbb{R}}
which is finite almost everywhere, a densely defined closed operator
\varphi(\mathcal{H})
is defined via the spectral decomposition theorem as

(
3.1)

\begin{split}{\langle}\varphi(\mathcal{H})f,g{\rangle}&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\lambda)d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda)f,g{\rangle}\\
D(\varphi(\mathcal{H}))&:=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\ |\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda)f,f{\rangle}<\infty\big\},\end{split}

where
E_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the spectral measure associated with
\mathcal{H}
. Here we list a few basic properties:

•

\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits E_{\mathcal{H}}=\sigma(\mathcal{H})
;

•

D(\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}})+\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}}))=D(\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}}))\cap D(\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}}))
and
\overline{\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}})+\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}})}=(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})({\mathcal{H}})
. In particular, they coincide with each other if one between
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
is bounded;

•

D(\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}})\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}}))=D((\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2})({\mathcal{H}}))\cap D(\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}}))
and
\overline{\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}})\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}})}=(\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2})({\mathcal{H}})
. In particular, if
\varphi_{2}
is bounded then
\varphi_{1}({\mathcal{H}})\varphi_{2}({\mathcal{H}})=(\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2})({\mathcal{H}})
;

•

\varphi(\mathcal{H})^{*}=\overline{\varphi}(\mathcal{H})
. In particular,
\varphi(\mathcal{H})
is self-adjoint if
\varphi
is real-valued;

•

If
\varphi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, then
\varphi(\mathcal{H})\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2})
and

(
3.2)

\displaystyle\|\varphi(\mathcal{H})\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}.

Let
Q=2d+2
be the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
and

(
3.3)

A:=-i\left(z\cdot\partial_{z}+2t\partial_{t}+\frac{Q}{2}\right)=-iz\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}-i2t\partial_{t}-i(d+1)

be the self-adjoint generator of the dilation group

e^{i\tau A}f(z,t)=e^{\frac{Q}{2}\tau}f(e^{\tau}z,e^{2\tau}t),\quad\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The operator
e^{i\tau A}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
. Moreover, it is easy to see that
e^{-i\tau A}\mathcal{L}e^{i\tau A}=e^{2\tau}\mathcal{L}
(see Lemma
3.5
below). In particular,
e^{i\tau A}
leaves
H^{m}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
invariant for any
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

From this point on, we will consider
{\mathcal{H}}
to be a non-negative self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
, with domain
H^{2s}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
, satisfying the conditions
(H1)
,
(H2)
and
(H3)
in Section
2
.

Remark 3.1
.

Hypothesis
(H3)
is necessary in order to ensure a joint spectral measure associated to
\mathcal{L}
and
{\mathcal{H}}
, and to define the operator
\phi(\mathcal{L},{\mathcal{H}})
for any measurable, almost everywhere finite function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
. We refer to Appendix
B
for more details. It follows that if
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
are of polynomial growth, then
[\phi_{1}(\mathcal{L}),\phi_{2}({\mathcal{H}})]=0
on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
. In particular,
(H3)
implies
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{L}
commute.

Let us define the operators

(
3.4)

S_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{L}^{s/2}{\langle}\mathcal{L}{\rangle}^{-\alpha/2},\qquad A_{\alpha}:={\langle}\mathcal{L}{\rangle}^{-\alpha/2}A{\langle}\mathcal{L}{\rangle}^{-\alpha/2},

for any
\alpha\geq 0
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
s>0
,
1/2<\mu\leq 1
and
\alpha\geq 0
and assume
(H1)
,
(H2)
and
(H3)
. Then

(
3.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{\sigma\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}}\|{\langle}A_{\alpha}{\rangle}^{-\mu}S_{\alpha}({\mathcal{H}}-\sigma)^{-1}S_{\alpha}{\langle}A_{\alpha}{\rangle}^{-\mu}\|\leq\kappa({\mathcal{H}},s,\mu),

where
\kappa({\mathcal{H}},s,\mu)
is given by

(
3.6)

\kappa({\mathcal{H}},s,\mu)=\inf_{b>0}e^{2b}\left\{K_{1}b^{-1/2}+K_{2}b^{1/2}D\left(2,b^{1/2}\right)+K_{3}b^{\mu-1/2}D\left(\frac{1}{\mu-1/2},b^{\mu-1/2}\right)\right\}^{2}

where
K_{1}:=(2C_{1}^{2}C_{2}^{-1})^{-1/2}
,
K_{2}:=(2C_{1}^{-1}s)^{1/2}\max\{s,2e^{-(1+s/2)}\}
,
K_{3}:=\frac{2-\mu}{\mu-1/2}(2C_{1}s)^{-1/2}
and

(
3.7)

D(p,x):=e^{-x^{p}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{\tau^{p}}d\tau

is the generalized Dawson’s integral.

Remark 3.3
.

Expanding the Maclaurin series in
(​​

3.7
)
, one obtains the bound

D(p,x)<x^{1-p}(1-e^{-x^{p}})
for

and

, with which we can give a more explicit estimate for

:

\kappa({\mathcal{H}},s,\mu)<\inf_{b>0}\left\{K_{1}b^{-1/2}e^{b}+(K_{2}+K_{3}b^{2\mu-2})(e^{b}-1)\right\}^{2}.

Moreover, in case of
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}
,
C_{1}=C_{2}=1
and one can thus calculate an explicit upper bound of

and

(see for instance

), but there appear to be no results on the best constant (or even an explicit expression of the constant) for smoothing effects in the case of the inhomogeneous propagator
\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}F(s)ds

Lemma 3.11

(Gronwall’s inequality)
.

Appendix A
The fractional powers of the sublaplacian, and their spectral theory

In this appendix we give the definition of the fractional sublaplacians
\mathcal{L}^{s}
and
\mathcal{L}_{s}
. Our exposition here will be concise—for more details we refer the reader to the book by G.B. Folland
[
23
]
, as well as
[
22
,
42
,
43
]
for some short recap. Note that our notation and definitions may slightly differ from other present in the literature.

After recalling the convolution between two functions
f
and
g
on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}

(f*g)(z,t):=\int_{{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}f\left(z-z^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}\right)g(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})dz^{\prime}dt^{\prime},

we define the
\lambda
-twisted convolution as

(f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}g^{\lambda})(z):=(f*g)^{\lambda}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}f^{\lambda}(z-z^{\prime})g^{\lambda}(z^{\prime})e^{\frac{i}{2}\lambda(x^{\prime}y-xy^{\prime})}\,dz^{\prime},

where
f^{\lambda}
stands for the Euclidean inverse Fourier transform of
f
in the vertical variable
t
:

f^{\lambda}(z):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z,t)e^{i\lambda t}\,dt.

It is worth commenting that the group Fourier transform of
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
is given by the Weyl transform
W_{\lambda}
of
f^{\lambda}
. However, since for the purposes of this work we do not use the Fourier analysis on the Heisenberg group, again we refer the reader to
[
23
]
for this topic.

Let us introduce also the scaled Laguerre functions of type
(d-1)
:

\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(z):=L_{k}^{d-1}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|\lambda||z|^{2}\Big)e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda||z|^{2}},

where
L_{k}^{d-1}
are the Laguerre polynomials of type
(d-1)
(see
[
47
, Chapter 1.4]
). The functions
\{\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
form an orthogonal basis for the subspace consisting of radial functions in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, namely functions invariant under the action of the group
\textbf{SO}(2d)
of all orthogonal matrices of order
2d
and determinant
1
.
In this way, we can write the so-called
special Hermite expansion
of a function

g
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
as

g(z)=(2\pi)^{-d}|\lambda|^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(z),

and therefore give the spectral decomposition of the sublaplacian:

\mathcal{L}f(z,t)=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+d)|\lambda|(f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda})(z)\right]e^{-i\lambda t}|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda.

The pure fractional sublaplacian
\mathcal{L}^{s}
can be then defined for
s\geq 0

via the spectral decomposition

(
A.1)

\mathcal{L}^{s}f(z,t)=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big((2k+d)|\lambda|\big)^{s}(f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda})(z)\right]e^{-i\lambda t}|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda,

whereas the conformal fractional sublaplacian
\mathcal{L}_{s}
can be defined for
0\leq s<d+1
by

(
A.2)

\mathcal{L}_{s}f(z,t)=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2|\lambda|)^{s}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1+s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1-s}{2})}(f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda})(z)\right]e^{-i\lambda t}|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
in
(​​

A.2
)
was introduced by Branson, Fontana and Morpurgo
[
10
]
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
(more precisely, see
[
10
, (1.33)]
), as the counterpart of the conformal fractional Laplacian from Riemannian geometry. The operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
occurs naturally in the context of CR geometry and scattering theory on the Heisenberg group: when we identify
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
as the boundary of the Siegel’s upper half space in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d+1}
, they have the important property of being conformally invariant (see for instance
[
10
]
). The operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
differs completely from
\mathcal{L}^{s}
since there is no geometry involved in the latter, and these operators only coincide in the limit as
s\to 1
, namely
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}^{1}=\mathcal{L}
. It is known that
\mathcal{L}_{s}
also possesses an explicit fundamental solution of the form
c_{d,s}|(z,t)|_{\mathbb{H}}^{-Q+2s}
with some constant
c_{d,s}>0
(see for instance
[
42
, Section 3]
), while an explicit expression for the fundamental solution for for
\mathcal{L}^{s}
is not known.

Equivalently we can also define
\mathcal{L}_{s}
and
\mathcal{L}^{s}
via the abstract spectral theorem, which comes handy for the purposes of this work. Using the formula
(​​

3.1
)
, for
\mathcal{L}^{s}
we can write

{\langle}\mathcal{L}^{s}f,g{\rangle}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{s}d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{\mathcal{L}}}(\lambda)f,g{\rangle},\qquad D(\mathcal{L}^{s}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\ |\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{2s}d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{\mathcal{L}}}(\lambda)f,f{\rangle}<\infty\}.

On the other hand,
(​​

A.2
)
means that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
corresponds to the spectral multiplier

(2|\lambda|)^{s}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{(2k+d)|\lambda)}{2|\lambda|}+\frac{1+s}{2}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{(2k+d)|\lambda|}{2|\lambda|}+\frac{1-s}{2}\big)},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Thus, we can write (at least formally)

(
A.3)

\mathcal{L}_{s}:=(2|T|)^{s}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{2|T|}+\frac{1+s}{2}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{\mathcal{L}}{2|T|}+\frac{1-s}{2}\big)}.

To define rigorously the RHS of this formula, we need to exploit the joint spectral theory (see Appendix
B
below) for
\mathcal{L}
and
-iT
, using the fact that they strongly commute. Indeed, if we denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{T}
the Fourier transform in
t
and by
\eta
the Fourier variable in
t
, that is
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{-1}T\mathcal{F}_{T}=i\eta
, then
(​​

2.4
)
yields

\mathcal{L}={\mathcal{F}}_{T}^{-1}(-\partial_{z}^{2}-4i\eta N+\eta^{2}|z|^{2})\mathcal{F}_{T}
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\eta):=-\partial_{z}^{2}-4i\eta N+\eta^{2}|z|^{2}
for short. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\eta)
and
\eta
clearly commute strongly, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}-\sigma)^{-1}(-iT-w)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{T}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\eta)-\sigma)^{-1}(\eta-w)^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{T}=(-iT-w)^{-1}(\mathcal{L}-\sigma)^{-1}

for any
\sigma,w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence there exists a unique spectral measure
E_{\mathcal{L},-iT}
such that, for any measurable, a.e. finite function
\phi
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we can define the operator
\phi(\mathcal{L},-iT)
via the spectral theorem:

(
A.4)

\displaystyle{\langle}\phi(\mathcal{L},-iT)f,g{\rangle}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\phi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{L},-iT}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})f,g{\rangle},

\displaystyle D(\phi(\mathcal{L},-iT))

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\ \left|\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\phi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})|^{2}d{\langle}E_{\mathcal{L},-iT}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})f,f{\rangle}<\infty\right.\right\}.

In particular, we can therefore define
\mathcal{L}_{s}
choosing
\phi=\Phi_{s}
in the above formula, with

\Phi_{s}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):=(2|\lambda_{2}|)^{s}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2|\lambda_{2}|}+\frac{1+s}{2}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2|\lambda_{2}|}+\frac{1-s}{2}\big)},

getting again formula
(​​

A.3
)
.

Now, the three conditions
(H1)
,
(H2)
and
(H3)
are easily verified for both
\mathcal{L}^{s}
and
\mathcal{L}_{s}
. Indeed, the homogeneity can be checked directly by a change of variables in
(​​

A.2
)
and
(​​

A.1
)
, after observing that

[(e^{i\tau A}f)^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}](z)=e^{-(2d+2)\tau}[f^{\lambda/e^{2\tau}}*_{\lambda/e^{2\tau}}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda/e^{2\tau}}](e^{\tau}z).

Condition
(H2)
for
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is trivial. For
\mathcal{L}_{s}
with
0<s<d+1
, Stirling’s formula

\Gamma(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{t}}\left(\frac{t}{e}\right)^{t}\left(1+O(t^{-1})\right),\quad t\to\infty,

yields that the function

m(r)=\frac{\Gamma\left(r+\frac{1+s}{2}\right)}{r^{s}\Gamma\left(r+\frac{1-s}{2}\right)}

on
[d/2,\infty)
satisfies
c_{s}\leq m(r)\leq C_{s}
with some constants
c_{s},C_{s}>0
. Using this fact with
r=\frac{2k+d}{2}
and formulas
(​​

A.1
)
and
(​​

A.2
)
, we obtain

c_{s}\|\mathcal{L}^{s/2}f\|^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{s}^{1/2}f\|^{2}\leq C_{s}\|\mathcal{L}^{s/2}f\|^{2}

since both
\mathcal{L}_{s}^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{-s/2}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-s/2}\mathcal{L}_{s}^{1/2}
correspond to the spectral multiplier

\sqrt{\frac{(2|\lambda|)^{s}\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1+s}{2}\big)}{(2k+d)^{s}|\lambda|^{s}\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1-s}{2}\big)}}=\sqrt{m\left(\frac{2k+d}{2}\right)},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}.

Condition
(H2)
with
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}_{s}
thus holds.
Condition
(H3)
with
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}^{s}
is trivial. For
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{L}_{s}
, condition
(H3)
follows from Proposition
B.1
(2) in Appendix
B
with
\varphi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(\Phi_{s}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})-\sigma)^{-1}
and
\psi(\lambda_{1})=(\lambda_{1}-w)^{-1}
. Equivalently, we can also see this from
(​​

A.1
)
and
(​​

A.2
)
, since both
(\mathcal{L}_{s}-\sigma)^{-1}(\mathcal{L}-w)^{-1}
and
(\mathcal{L}-w)^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{s}-\sigma)^{-1}
correspond to the spectral multiplier

\left[(2|\lambda|)^{s}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1+s}{2}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{2k+d}{2}+\frac{1-s}{2}\big)}-\sigma\right]^{-1}[(2k+d)|\lambda|-w]^{-1},\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}^{s}
and
\mathcal{L}_{s}
leave
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits N
invariant, since they are invariant under rotations
z\mapsto Uz
with
U\in\textbf{SO}(2d)
, and
N
is a sum of generators of the rotations (see Remark
2.3
).

Finally, it is also worth mentioning that there is yet an another way to characterize the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
though an extension problem (see
[

,
43
]
).

Appendix B
Joint spectral theory for commuting self-adjoint operators

The purpose of this appendix is to record the joint spectral theory for two commuting self-adjoint operators. We refer to textbooks
[
6
]
for details.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be two self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space
X
such that they strongly commute:

[({\mathcal{H}}_{1}-\sigma)^{-1},({\mathcal{H}}_{2}-w)^{-1}]=0,\quad\sigma,w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}},

or equivalently
[E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(\Omega_{1}),E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}(\Omega_{2})]=0
for any Borel measurable sets
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then there exists a unique spectral measure
E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
such that

E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})=E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(\Omega_{1})E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}(\Omega_{2})

for any
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})
and that
E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}
diagonalize
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
simultaneously, namely

{\langle}H_{j}f,g{\rangle}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\lambda_{j}d\mu_{f,g},\quad f\in D({\mathcal{H}}_{j}),\ g\in X,\ j=1,2,

where
\mu_{f,g}={\langle}E_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})f,g{\rangle}
(see
[
6
, Theorems 5.2.6 and 5.5.1]
). For measurable
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying
|\varphi|(\lambda)<\infty
for a.e.
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we then define the operator
\varphi({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2})
by

\displaystyle{\langle}\varphi({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2})f,g{\rangle}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\varphi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})d\mu_{f,g},\quad f,g\in D(\varphi({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2})),

\displaystyle D(\varphi({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}))

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in X\ \left|\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\varphi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})|^{2}d\mu_{f,f}<\infty\right.\right\}.

In what follows, we put
J_{\varphi}=\varphi({\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2})
for short.

Proposition B.1
.

J_{\varphi}
is a densely defined closed operator on
X
satisfying the following properties.

(1)

For
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
D(\alpha J_{\varphi}+\beta J_{\psi})=D(J_{\varphi})\cap D(J_{\psi})
and

\overline{\alpha J_{\varphi}+\beta J_{\psi}}=J_{\alpha\varphi+\beta\psi}
.
In particular, if one of
\varphi
and
\psi
belong to
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, then
{\alpha J_{\varphi}+\beta J_{\psi}}=J_{\alpha\varphi+\beta\psi}
.

(2)

D(J_{\varphi}J_{\psi})=D(J_{\varphi\psi})\cap D(J_{\psi})
and
\overline{J_{\varphi}J_{\psi}}=J_{\varphi\psi}
. In particular, if
\psi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, then
J_{\varphi}J_{\psi}=J_{\varphi\psi}
.

(3)

J_{\overline{\varphi}}=(J_{\varphi})^{*}
, where
\overline{\varphi}
is the complex conjugate of
\varphi
.

Proof.

The proof is completely the same as of the single case
\varphi({\mathcal{H}})
. See
[
6
, Theorems 5.4.3–5.4.7]
for the proof of the single case.
