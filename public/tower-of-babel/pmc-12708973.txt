Increased Risk of Hematologic Malignancy in Moderate to Severe Psoriasis in Relation to the Use of Systemic Immunosuppressants: A Nationwide Population-Based Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Yu Ri Woo, Kyunghee Chae, Seowon Song, Subin Lee, Yu Jin Lee, Hoon Kang, Sukil Kim, Jung Eun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between cancer and the use of systemic immunosuppressants in psoriasis treatment has not well established. The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between the systemic immunosuppressants used in the treatment for psoriasis and the risk of certain cancers in Korean patients with moderate to severe psoriasis.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted involving 93,152 patients with moderate to severe psoriasis and 205,850 matched controls in Korea, using merged data from the National Health Insurance System, Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service, and Korea National Cancer Incidence Database from 2008 to 2018.ResultsThe study observed a lower incidence of any cancer in moderate to severe psoriasis patients (2.4%) compared to the general population (2.99%). However, there was a higher risk of hematologic cancers, particularly Hodgkin's lymphoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukemia, and cutaneous T cell lymphoma. Notably, methotrexate doses of ≥ 17.5 mg/week increased the risk of hematologic cancer risk by 7.546 times and cutaneous T cell lymphoma risk by 9.038 times, but cyclosporine and corticosteroids use did not show a significant association with increased incidence of hematologic cancers. Meanwhile, use of cyclosporine, methotrexate and corticosteroid did not significantly affect the risk of skin cancer among patients with psoriasis.ConclusionThis study reveals an increased risk of hematologic cancers, such as cutaneous T cell lymphomas, associated with high-dose immunosuppressant use in moderate to severe psoriasis, underscoring the need for careful treatment management.Keywords: Psoriasis, Neoplasms, Hematologic Neoplasms, Immunosuppressive Agents, Methotrexate, Cyclosporine, Corticosteroid

INTRODUCTION

Psoriasis, a common chronic inflammatory disease primarily manifesting on the skin, affects a significant portion of the global population.1 The severity of psoriasis can vary, ranging from mild cases that can be effectively managed with topical therapy to moderate to severe cases that may necessitate extensive treatment including phototherapy, oral immunosuppressants or biologic therapies.1,2,3 In addition to its cutaneous manifestations, psoriasis is a prevalent disease with significant systemic implications, resulting in considerable health challenges and disability.4,5,6

The potential link between psoriasis and an increased risk of cancer remains a subject of ongoing research and debate. A recent meta-analysis of 112 observational studies indicated that the prevalence of any cancer in patients with psoriasis was 4.78%, with a relative risk (RR) of 1.21.7 This analysis revealed increased risks for specific types of cancer, including keratinocyte cancer (RR, 2.28), and lymphomas (RR, 1.56).7

The elevated risk of certain cancers, particularly hematologic malignancies, in individuals with psoriasis prompts questions about the underlying causes. Whether this increased risk is attributable to the disease itself, or if systemic treatments for psoriasis, particularly immunosuppressants, could be contributing factors remains poorly elucidated. These treatments, which are essential in managing moderate to severe psoriasis, have been previously reported to be associated with an increased prevalence of lymphoma in general.8,9,10

Although there is a reported increase in the prevalence of cancer among psoriasis patients, many previous studies have not thoroughly examined the potential influence of psoriasis medication on cancer risk. This lack of detailed investigation highlights the need for more focused research into the relationship between systemic treatments for psoriasis and the risk of developing cancer.

In this study, we aim to explore the association between the use of systemic treatments, including immunosuppressants like cyclosporine, methotrexate and corticosteroids, and the risk of cancer in Korean patients with moderate to severe psoriasis. Through an extensive retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the National Health Insurance System (NHIS), Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service (HIRA), and Korea National Cancer Incidence (KNCI) databases,11 this investigation aims to clarify the risks of cancer associated with various psoriasis treatments in moderate to severe psoriasis patients. By identifying into both the any cancer risk and the specific risks associated with different drug types and dosages, this study seeks to provide a clearer understanding of how treatment strategies for psoriasis might influence cancer risk. This study will help inform clinicians and patients about the potential risks associated with these therapies, guiding more informed treatment decisions and highlighting the need for cancer screening in this vulnerable population.

METHODS

Data source

We conducted a retrospective nationwide cohort study on patients with moderate to severe psoriasis using a merged dataset in Korea. This dataset combined data from the NHIS database from 2007 to 2018, the HIRA database from 2007 to 2018, and the KNCI Database from 2007 to 2017. By merging data from the NHIS, HIRA, and the KNCI database over the period from 2007 to 2018, this study provides a robust and detailed perspective on the patterns and outcomes of treatments for moderate to severe psoriasis and cancer patients. This broad coverage merged database ensures a more accurate representation of the population, enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the study’s findings.

Study population

In this study, individuals primarily diagnosed with psoriasis based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th revision, diagnosis codes were eligible for inclusion (Fig. 1). Moderate to severe psoriasis was defined as patients who had ICD-10 codes (L40.x) for psoriasis and were prescribed systemic medications such as methotrexate, cyclosporine, or acitretin, or received phototherapy based on Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System codes following their psoriasis diagnosis. Additionally, those diagnosed with Korean Classification of Diseases-8 codes for severe psoriasis (L40.00) were also classified as having severe psoriasis.

Study flowchart.

ICD = International Classification of Diseases, KCD = Korean Classification of Diseases, NHIS = National Health Insurance System.

Exclusions included individuals who received medications for psoriasis or phototherapy before their psoriasis diagnosis (n = 15,329), patients under 18 years old (n = 3,602), patients diagnosed with psoriasis within a one-year look-back period (n = 37,208), patients diagnosed with psoriasis after 2017 (n = 19,856), and patients diagnosed with cancer prior to or within one year of their moderate to severe psoriasis diagnosis (n = 2,149). Ultimately, a total of 93,152 patients with moderate to severe psoriasis were included in the study.

A cohort of 331,569 individuals from the general healthy population, using the NHIS database, was utilized. These individuals were matched 1:3 by 10-year age group and sex with the 93,152 moderate to severe psoriasis patients. This resulted in a total of 205,850 individuals in the general population cohort and 91,557 in the moderate to severe psoriasis cohort. The patients with moderate to severe psoriasis and the general healthy populations were followed up until the diagnosis of any cancer or until December 31, 2018. Diagnosis of each cancer was classified by the ICD-10 codes for each cancer (Supplementary Table 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Catholic University of Korea.

Additionally, we conducted an analysis on 93,152 patients with moderate to severe psoriasis to investigate the impact of immunosuppressive treatments on cancer risk. This analysis divided the psoriasis patients into two groups: those who developed cancer (n = 2,249) and those who did not (n = 90,903), with further analysis performed on these groups. The cut-off values for high and low doses of each drug were based on the median values observed in the study population and typical prescription increments in Korean clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was presented with means ± standard deviation for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Differences between groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was utilized to assess the cancer risk in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis, adjusting for confounders such as age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, heart failure, angina, acute ischemic stroke, bronchitis, chronic kidney disease, and transplanted organ. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and exempted by Institutional Review Board of Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea (PC19ZNSE0128). No informed consent was required from patients due to the nature of public data from NHIS, HIRA, and the KNCI.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The study included a cohort of 91,557 patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, matched for age and sex with 205,850 individuals from the general population. The moderate to severe psoriasis group exhibited a marginally lower rate of comorbidities compared to the general population (Supplementary Table 2).

Incidence and risk of cancer in moderate to severe psoriasis compared to general population

Regarding cancer incidence, the general population had a rate of 2.99%, while it was 2.40% in the moderate to severe psoriasis patients, corresponding to an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of 0.900 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.857–0.945) for cancer development in the psoriasis group (Table 1). Although any cancer incidence was lower in the moderate to severe psoriasis patients, the risk varied by cancer type. Notably, the risk of hematologic cancers was higher in the psoriasis group, with an aHR of 2.257 (95% CI, 1.823–2.794), including increased risks for Hodgkin's lymphoma (aHR, 4.958; 95% CI, 1.224–20.091), non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (aHR, 2.847; 95% CI, 2.132–3.802), leukemia (aHR, 1.843; 95% CI, 1.240–2.738), compared to the general population (Table 1). We further examined the risk of cutaneous T cell lymphoma among non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and found the increased risk of cutaneous T cell lymphoma (aHR, 14.958; 95% CI, 7.036–31.798) in moderate to severe psoriasis patients compared to the general population (Table 1). The subclassification of cutaneous T cell lymphoma based on ICD-10 codes and the incidence of cutaneous T cell lymphoma was described in Supplementary Table 3.

Characteristics of moderate to severe psoriasis patients who developed cancer

In the cohort of moderate to severe psoriasis patients, those who developed cancer (n = 2,249) had a higher average age of 54.9 years, compared to 43.1 years in those without cancer (n = 90,903). This suggests that older age and longer disease duration may be more prevalent in the cancer group (Table 2). The group with cancer also showed a higher prevalence of comorbidities than those without cancer. On average, cancer developed approximately 4.2 years after the diagnosis of psoriasis. The use of immunosuppressive medication, including cyclosporine, and methotrexate, was more common among patients who did not develop cancer compared to those who did (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This comprehensive retrospective cohort study offers significant insights into the complex relationship between psoriasis, its treatments, and the risk of cancer. Our findings are grounded in an extensive analysis of data from the NHIS, the HIRA, and the KNCI database, providing a robust and detailed perspective on the patterns and outcomes of moderate to severe psoriasis treatments in Korea.

Our study found that while the incidence of any cancer in patients with moderate and severe psoriasis (2.4%) was lower compared to the general population (2.99%), there was a notable increase in specific types of cancers. Particularly, the risk of hematologic cancers, including Hodgkin's lymphoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukemia and cutaneous T cell lymphoma, was higher in our psoriasis cohort. This finding aligns with a study by Jung et al.,12 which explored the association between moderate to severe psoriasis and the development of hematologic malignancies.

Furthermore, the increased risk of hematologic malignancies observed in our study is supported by findings from multiple international cohorts and systematic reviews. Bellinato et al.13 conducted a meta-analysis of over 25 observational studies involving more than 2 million psoriasis patients. Their analysis demonstrated a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.27 for lymphoma, with particularly increased risks for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (HR, 6.22), highlighting a significant association between psoriasis and lymphoproliferative malignancies. Chiesa Fuxench et al.14 analysed a large UK population-based cohort of 198,366 patients with psoriasis and matched controls, reporting an aHR of 1.06 for overall cancer and 1.34 for lymphoma. Similarly, Vaengebjerg et al.7 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 112 observational studies and fond increased risks for keratinocyte cancer (relative risk [RR], 2.28), Hodgkin’s lymphoma (RR, 1.87), non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (RR, 1.48), and lung cancer (RR, 1.26). These studies differ somewhat in population demographics and exposure definitions, but all consistently report elevated risks for hematologic malignancies among patients with psoriasis. While discrepancies exist in the findings regarding overall cancer risk, potentially due to differences in treatment patterns, surveillance intensity, or environmental cofactors, the concordant signal regarding hematologic malignancies underscores the need for vigilant cancer screening in this patient population.

A central focus of our study is the examination of how psoriasis treatments, particularly immunosuppressants, influence cancer risk. Interestingly, the study observed that the risk of cancer varied not just by the type of treatment but also by the dosage. In patients with moderate to severe psoriasis, we found that higher doses of cyclosporine (≥ 250 mg per day) were associated with a 1.446-fold increased risk of any cancer compared to lower doses (< 250 mg per day) of cyclosporine in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis. This 1.446-fold increase in any cancer risk is a critical point and suggests that while cyclosporine is effective in managing psoriasis, its high-dosing use needs careful consideration due to the associated any cancer risks in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis.

Cyclosporine is an immunosuppressive agent primarily used in organ transplantation and autoimmune diseases like psoriasis.15 Its main mechanism of action involves inhibiting T-cell activation and proliferation by blocking the activity of calcineurin, a key enzyme in the immune response pathway.16 This suppression of the immune system, while beneficial in controlling psoriasis, may also reduce immune surveillance, allowing malignant cells to proliferate unchecked and interfere with DNA repair mechanisms.17,18

One of the key observations in our study was the increased risk of hematologic cancers associated with immunosuppressive drug use. Specifically, we found a substantial increased risk of hematologic cancers in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis who were prescribed with methotrexate. Methotrexate, an antimetabolite and a structural analogue of folic acid, has been extensively documented for its effectiveness in treating psoriasis.19 It works by inducing the apoptosis of keratinocyte proliferation, a hallmark of psoriasis, thereby reducing inflammation and lesion formation.20 The potential oncogenic mechanisms of methotrexate in increasing the risk of hematologic cancers in psoriasis patients can be understood through several pathways. In patients with moderate to severe psoriasis undergoing methotrexate treatment, the drug's immunosuppressive properties may compromise the immune system's capacity to detect and eliminate malignant cells, potentially increasing the risk of hematologic cancers. Methotrexate's propensity to cause chromosomal breaks in vivo could lead to chromosomal instability, a condition that might specifically predispose these patients to oncogenic mutations associated with hematologic malignancies.21 Although our current dataset does not allow for direct identification of Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)-related cases, the association between methotrexate use and EBV-related lymphomas is also well-documented. Methotrexate can reactivate latent EBV and likely contributes to the increased risk of lymphoma.22 These interferences could escalate the risk of hematologic cancers in individuals with moderate to severe psoriasis.

The oncogenicity potential of methotrexate has been a subject of long-standing debate. While methotrexate is used in the treatment of several hematologic cancers, various types of solid cancers and also of inflammatory disorders,23 this study provides evidence that methotrexate use in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis is linked to an increased cancer risk. Notably, this risk is higher in the group receiving higher doses, as compared to those receiving lower doses, among psoriasis patients. Furthermore, methotrexate has also been associated with an increased risk of cancer in patients with rheumatoid arthritis,24,25 another condition that often necessitates the use of immunosuppressive drugs.

Additionally, we found that the use of immunosuppressive drugs does not affect the risk of skin cancer. Although phototherapy for psoriasis is generally known to increase the risk of skin cancer, the use of PUVA treatment,26 which is widely believed to contribute to this increased risk, has been rare in Korea since 2007. Additionally, twice-weekly NBUVB treatments, conforming to the guidelines of the Korean health insurance system, do not appear to increase the risk of skin cancer. This observation is consistent with the results of previous studies.27,28 Moreover, the study confirms that immunosuppressive medications, used in patients with moderate to severe psoriasis, do not significantly affect the increased risk of skin cancer in these patients.

Given the increased risk of certain cancers, regular cancer screening should be an integral part of the management plan for patients with moderate to severe psoriasis, particularly those on high-dose immunosuppressive therapy. The study highlights areas for further research, especially in understanding the mechanisms by which psoriasis treatments may influence cancer risk.

One of the primary limitations of this study is its retrospective nature. While the large dataset provides a comprehensive overview, retrospective studies inherently rely on existing records and data, which can lead to potential biases or missing information. Despite rigorous statistical adjustments, there remains the possibility of residual confounding. Variables not captured in the databases, such as lifestyle factors including smoking, alcohol use, and body weight, as well as family history of cancer, other autoimmune disorders, and detailed treatment adherence, could influence cancer risk and are not accounted for in the analysis. Another limitation is the possibility of diagnostic misclassification between psoriasis and mycosis fungoides (MF), a type of cutaneous T cell lymphoma. Patients misdiagnosed with psoriasis who actually have MF may be treated with methotrexate, potentially inflating the observed incidence of cutaneous T cell lymphoma in this cohort. Additionally, our dataset did not include detailed severity metrics such as PASI scores, making it difficult to separate the effects of dose from disease severity.

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study in Korea, drawing from the extensive data of the NHIS, HIRA, and KNCI databases, reveals a complex relationship between psoriasis, its treatments, and cancer risk. Key findings include a higher risk of specific hematologic cancers such as Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas, leukemia, and cutaneous T cell lymphoma. The study highlights a dose-dependent increase in cancer risk associated with immunosuppressive treatments like cyclosporine and methotrexate, underscoring the necessity for cautious use and vigilant monitoring, particularly at higher doses. Our study highlights the importance of carefully using methotrexate in treating moderate to severe psoriasis due to its potential cancer risks. These insights are crucial for refining treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes in psoriasis management.
