Tagged vector space, Part I: Dirac notation as originally intended
Filippus S. Roux

Abstract
A generalization is provided for the notion of tags, as used in various formulations of physical scenarios. It leads to the definition of tagged vector spaces, based on a set of axioms for tags and their extractors. As an application, such a tagged vector space is used to provide, in the context of quantum optics, a formal mathematical description for the Dirac notation that is closer to its intended usage compared to current mathematical formulations: it provides a one-to-one mapping between kets and bras and allows operators to operate either to the left or to the right. The canonical commutation relations for the quadrature and ladder operators are derived as consequences of the axioms of the tagged vector space. These axioms also lead to a symplectic phase space with the Wigner function and the Weyl transform emerging naturally.



1
Introduction

When the formalisms of quantum physics, such as the Dirac formalism
[
1
]
and the operator formalism, were originally invented, the underlying mathematical foundations for these formalisms did not exist to the extent they do today. These formalisms were intended to perform consistent calculations for the purpose of modeling physical scenarios; a purpose which they eventually fulfilled quite successfully. Since then, the formal mathematical aspects of these formalisms were addressed in ever increasing levels of abstraction, to the point where much of the foundations of quantum formalism have been developed to a highly sophisticated level
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

While the resulting mathematical formulation is suitably rigorous, it represents the usage in a way that differs from what was intended when the original formalisms were invented
[
6
,
7
]
. For example, the idea of a Dirac state vector
[
1
,
8
]
, as represented by a ket
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
, is that it is completely agnostic about any coordinate system. Yet, in terms of current mathematical physics representations, a ket is simply taken as an element of a Hilbert space, for which the space of square integrable functions
L^{2}
is often taken as a suitable representation. These functions carry explicit dependencies on some independent variables. In the physics context, such independent variables represent something physical, such as coordinates in configuration space or the Fourier domain variables. In fact, arbitrary coordinate systems can be defined with the aid of unitary transformations. With such freedom in defining the coordinate system, all related by unitary transformations, various functions in
L^{2}
can represent the exact same physical state vector, provided that the relationships among their independent variables, as defined in terms of the coordinate systems, are taken into account. The opposite situation is where one and the same function in
L^{2}
can actually represent different physical state vectors when the independent variables are associated with different coordinate systems.

This variability is captured in terms of the original formulation of Dirac state vectors in terms of kets, the formulation of which is accomplished by multiplying the function by the intended coordinate basis, also expressed in terms of kets and integrating over the independent variables. Symbolically, it is represented by

\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\triangleq\int\left|{\mathbf{x}}\right\rangle\psi(\mathbf{x})\ {\rm d}\mathbf{x},

(1)

where we leave the careful definition of the independent variables and the integration domain unspecified for the time being.

Being elements of a Hilbert space, these kets can be used in the evaluation of inner products with the aid of dual Dirac state vectors, represented by bras
\left\langle{\psi}\right|
. Such bras were considered to have a one-to-one association with their corresponding kets, leading to a symmetric formulation. The bras are given by the Hermitian adjoint of the kets, and visa versa. The inner product is then represented by
\langle{\psi}|{\psi^{\prime}}\rangle
, producing a complex numerical value as result.

Another aspect of the Dirac formalism is that it was suppose to allowed one to apply an operator to a ket
\hat{A}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\left|{\phi}\right\rangle
to produce a new state, or some transformed version of that state (the result is not always normalized, but should at least be normalizable to still be an element of the Hilbert space). When the Hermitian adjoint is applied to such an equation, one obtains the equivalent operation expressed in terms of the bras
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}=\left\langle{\phi}\right|
. In other words, the original idea was that the operator (or its Hermitian adjoint) can also be applied to the bras, thus operating toward the left.

While the resulting formalism with its modus operandi, worked well within the quantum physics context, it led to no small amount of discomfort in terms of a suitable formal mathematical foundation
[
6
]
. (See also
[
7
]
, and references therein.) Part of the problem can be traced to conceptual differences between the two communities in terms of how they view the notation and the mathematical processes that are being represented. Such conceptual differences don’t actually have anything to do with either the underlying mathematics or the physics that it tries to model. (As demonstrated here, it is possible to provide a formalism that adheres to the prescriptions as employed by the physics community while still being mathematically rigorous.)

In formal mathematics, it is preferred to view processes in terms of mappings from a domain to a target space. For example, the inner products are viewed as mappings from the space of state vectors (kets) to the complex plane. Hence, it is represented as

\langle{\phi}|{\psi}\rangle\equiv\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\{\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\{\cdot\}
is a linear functional, generally associated with a tempered distribution
\phi
. Moreover, an operator
\hat{A}
is a mapping from the space of state vectors back to itself, or to some other vector space. The representation of the adjoint process
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}=\left\langle{\phi}\right|
is therefore awkward when viewed in the context of formal mathematics
[
6
]
.

The operators and state vectors that were eventually defined in the mathematical representation of the Dirac formalism conformed to the conceptual approach that is fashionable in formal mathematics. Unfortunately, it does not capture the original conceptual understanding that was intended with the Dirac formalism and its associated operator formalism.

Our goal is to provide a suitable mathematical formulation that captures the intended conceptual understanding of the Dirac formalism, without deviating (too much) from the usual conceptual approach that is fashionable in formal mathematics. However, to do so, we first need to define some new tools.

1.1
Motivation

The “new tools” are in fact merely a generalization of some “old” well-known tools. To introduce the new tools, we’ll first present some examples of these well-known tools.

1.

The first example is the simple case of a three-dimensional vector

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{x}x+\mathbf{e}_{y}y+\mathbf{e}_{z}z,

(3)

where
\mathbf{e}_{x}
,
\mathbf{e}_{y}
and
\mathbf{e}_{z}
are the unit vectors in the
x
-,
y
-, and
z
-directions, respectively, and
x
,
y
, and
z
represent the numerical values of the three components along these directions. To extract a particular component from the total vector, one performs the dot-product of the total vector with the unit vector along that direction. For example,

\mathbf{e}_{x}\cdot\mathbf{v}=x.

(4)

2.

Another example that differs only slightly from the previous example comes from the field of differential geometry
[
9
]
. Here, we encounter a vector field defined as

X=X^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{1}}+...+X^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{n}},

(5)

at some point, where the partial derivatives take over the role of the unit vectors, and the components are given by
X^{n}
. At the same time, we also have 1-forms

\omega=\omega_{1}{\rm d}x^{1}+...+\omega_{n}{\rm d}x^{n},

(6)

where the components are given by
\omega_{n}
, and the differentials
{\rm d}x^{n}
serve in the role of the unit vectors. To extract a component from one of these two kinds of composite quantities, we use a unit vector of the other kind of quantity, because

{\rm d}x^{n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{m}}\right)=\delta^{n}_{m},

(7)

where the result represents the Kronecker delta.

3.

A
qubit

[
10
]
is a normalized state vector in a two-dimensional Hilbert space. It is often represented in terms of Dirac notation by

\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\left|{0}\right\rangle\alpha+\left|{1}\right\rangle\beta,

(8)

where
\left|{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|{1}\right\rangle
are the elements of the two-dimensional basis, and
\alpha
and
\beta
are complex coefficients. Normalization requires that
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. The two basis elements are mutually orthogonal
\langle{0}|{1}\rangle=0
. It thus allows one to extract a specific component, such as

\langle{0}|{\psi}\rangle=\alpha.

(9)

4.

The final example looks completely different. Here, a generating function
[
11
]
is formed as a linear combination of an infinite discrete set of functions
\{g_{n}(\mathbf{x})\}
, indexed by integers
n
, and multiplied by an auxiliary variable (the generating parameter) raised to the power of
n
:

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x},\mu)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{n}g_{n}(\mathbf{x}).

(10)

The auxiliary variable
\mu
serves as the generating parameter. The result is one composite function with an additional dependence on the auxiliary variable. The individual functions are extracted from the generating function with the aid of a differential operation

\frac{1}{n!}\left.\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial\mu^{n}}\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x},\mu)\right|_{\mu=0}=g_{n}(\mathbf{x}).

(11)

Even though these examples are different and originate in different subfields of mathematics and physics, there are some common aspects that can be identified in all of them. In all cases, different mathematical entities (components or functions) are combined into a composite entity by labelling the individual entities in some way so that they can be extracted from the composite entity by a suitable operation. This notion is obviously quite powerful, because it reappears in different forms in different contexts.

We refer to the formal devices with which the labelling is done as
tags
. In these examples, the tags are diversely represented either by unit vectors, partial derivatives, basis vectors, or auxiliary variables raised to the
n
-th power. In all these cases the tags are indexed by a discrete index. While the first three examples have finite numbers of distinct tags, the last example has a countable infinite number of tags.

Tags usually don’t represent anything physical in applications of these mathematical scenarios. They are often purely formal devices to alleviate calculation complexity. On the other hand, the index with which these tags are labelled often has a physical meaning, such as the physical direction in space, as found in the first example.

In some of the examples, the representation of the composite quantity in terms of the tags and their components (or coefficients) is not unique. For example, the same vector given in (
3
) can also be represented as

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{x}^{\prime}x^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}_{y}^{\prime}y^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}_{z}^{\prime}z^{\prime}=\sum_{n}\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\prime}c_{n}^{\prime},

(12)

in terms of different unit vectors
\mathbf{e}_{n}^{\prime}
with associated components
c_{n}^{\prime}
, provided that they are related to those in (
3
) by

\mathbf{e}_{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{n}\mathbf{e}_{n}U_{nm}~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~c_{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{p}U_{mp}^{{\dagger}}c_{p},

(13)

where the matrices are unitary so that

\sum_{m}U_{nm}U_{mp}^{{\dagger}}=\delta_{np}.

(14)

Hence, a vector can be represented in terms of a different set of tags by inserting a Kronecker delta decomposed into a unitary matrix and its Hermitian adjoint

\mathbf{v}=\sum_{n}\mathbf{e}_{n}c_{n}=\sum_{mnp}\mathbf{e}_{n}U_{nm}U_{mp}^{{\dagger}}c_{p}=\sum_{m}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{\prime}c_{m}^{\prime}.

(15)

A similar freedom in definition exists for the qubits; any two mutually orthogonal states can be used as a basis. Transformations of tags are also found in differential geometry. However, it does not work for generating functions. We refer to this property as the
unitary invariance
of the composite entity’s representation in terms of tags.

The process whereby the tagged entities are extracted is performed with the aid of
extractors
. In the first example, the extractors are the same as the tags, namely the unit vectors

\mathbf{e}_{m}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}=\delta_{mn}.

(16)

In the second example, they are the differentials

dx^{m}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{n}}\right)=\delta^{m}_{n}.

(17)

The third example uses the dual state vectors associated with the basis elements

\langle{m}|{n}\rangle=\delta_{mn}.

(18)

In the last example, they are provided by the differential process defined in (
11
)

\frac{1}{m!}\left.\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial\mu^{m}}\mu^{n}\right|_{\mu=0}=\delta_{mn}.

(19)

In what follows, we generalize these notions and provide some formal mathematical definitions. Then we use it to provide a mathematical description for the Dirac notation.

2
General notation

To generalize these notions, we represent the tags by
\tau_{n}
and the extractors by
\chi_{n}
where
n
represents an index ranging over the elements in an
index space
. The formal extraction process then becomes

\chi_{m}\tau_{n}=\delta_{mn}~~~~{\rm for}~~~m,n\in\mathbb{N}~({\rm or}~\mathbb{Z}).

(20)

In general, tags can be indexed by any set, depending on the context. It can also be a continuous variable taken from the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
, or even by a function space for the definition of functionals
[
12
]
. If the index space is
\mathbb{R}
, the tags and extractors become functions of the continuous index
\tau(\nu)
and
\chi(\nu)
. The extraction process then leads to a Dirac delta function instead of a Kronecker delta

\chi(\mu)\tau(\nu)=\delta(\mu-\nu).

(21)

The whole point of the tags is to form linear combinations of entities serving as coefficients. Hence,

\mathbf{F}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau_{n}F_{n},

(22)

where the coefficients
F_{n}
are elements in a
coefficient space
. The extraction process produces

\chi_{m}\mathbf{F}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\chi_{m}\tau_{n}F_{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta_{mn}F_{n}=F_{m}.

(23)

For the continuous case, the linear combinations are integrals

\mathbf{F}=\int\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu,

(24)

where
F(\nu)
is a
coefficient function
. The extraction process then becomes

\chi(\mu)\mathbf{F}=\int\chi(\mu)\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\int\delta(\mu-\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=F(\mu).

(25)

Due to the tag in the integrand of the integral in (
24
), the integral cannot be evaluated. Therefore, we cannot say much about the integration measure in (
24
). However, when the extractor removes the tag and produces the Dirac delta function in the integrand, the integral becomes associated with a tempered distribution.

2.1
Unitary invariance

The unitary invariance in the (finite) discrete case implies that

\mathbf{F}=\sum_{n}\tau_{n}F_{n}=\sum_{m,n,p}\tau_{n}U_{nm}U_{mp}^{{\dagger}}F_{p}=\sum_{n}\tau_{m}^{\prime}F_{m}^{\prime}.

(26)

In the continuous case, it is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\int\tau(\nu)U(\nu,\eta)U^{{\dagger}}(\eta,\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\eta\ {\rm d}\mu

(27)

\displaystyle=\int\tau^{\prime}(\eta)F^{\prime}(\eta)\ {\rm d}\eta,

where the
unitary kernels
satisfy the condition

\int U(\nu,\eta)U^{{\dagger}}(\eta,\mu)\ {\rm d}\eta=\delta(\nu-\mu),

(28)

the transformation of the tags is represented by

\tau^{\prime}(\eta)=\int\tau(\nu)U(\nu,\eta)\ {\rm d}\nu,

(29)

and the transformation of the coefficient function is given by

F^{\prime}(\eta)=\int U^{{\dagger}}(\eta,\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu.

(30)

2.2
Associated and unassociated tags and extractors

Based on unitary invariance, a scenario in which composite quantities are formed as linear combinations of tags actually allows infinitely many different possible sets of tags, all related via unitary transformations. It also means that the simple relationships given in (
20
) and (
21
) on which the extraction process is based can become more general. For those in (
20
) and (
21
), we say that the extractors are
associated
with their tags. In the case of
unassociated
extractors, the more general operations produce

\chi_{m}\tau_{n}^{\prime}=U_{m,n}~~~~~{\rm or}~~~~~\chi_{m}^{\prime}\tau_{n}=U_{m,n}^{{\dagger}},

(31)

in the finite discrete case, and

\chi(\mu)\tau^{\prime}(\nu)=U(\mu,\nu)~~~~~{\rm or}~~~~~\chi^{\prime}(\mu)\tau(\nu)=U^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\nu),

(32)

in the continuous case. To see how this general extraction process works, we start with the last expression in (
27
) and then apply
\chi(\mu)
on it. Performing the calculation, we represent the transformed tag as given in (
29
), leading to

\displaystyle\chi(\mu)\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\int\chi(\mu)\tau^{\prime}(\nu)F^{\prime}(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\int\chi(\mu)\tau(\eta)U(\eta,\nu)F^{\prime}(\nu)\ {\rm d}\eta\ {\rm d}\nu

(33)

\displaystyle=\int\delta(\mu-\eta)U(\eta,\nu)F^{\prime}(\nu)\ {\rm d}\eta\ {\rm d}\nu=\int U(\mu,\nu)F^{\prime}(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu.

Here, we left the transformation of the coefficient in integral form to demonstrate (
32
) by comparing the second and last expressions. It thus follows that the extractors are also related by unitary transformations. Compared to (
29
), they transform as

\chi^{\prime}(\eta)=\int U^{{\dagger}}(\eta,\nu)\chi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu.

(34)

The unitary kernels in (
32
) are in general tempered distributions. Usually the bare extractor would not operate on a bare tag. Instead, the extractor operates on a linear combination of tags, which is expressed in terms of an integral, as in (
24
). The unitary kernel is thus produced under the integral, which provides a suitable definition for the distribution.

2.3
Unbiased tags and extractors

There are special cases of unassociated tags and extractors that are referred to as being
unbiased
. A set of tags and a set of extractors are called (mutually) unbiased when the magnitude of the bare extraction process between them is independent of their respective indices. For example, let
\{\tilde{\chi}\}
represent a set of extractors that is unbiased with respect to a set of tags
\{\tau\}
. Then

\left|\tilde{\chi}(\mu)\tau(\nu)\right|=c,

(35)

for all
\mu
and
\nu
, where
c
is a constant that is independent of
\mu
and
\nu
. It implies that the unitary kernel is a complex phase factor

\tilde{\chi}(\mu)\tau(\nu)=\exp\left[{\rm i}\phi(\mu,\nu)\right],

(36)

where
\phi(\mu,\nu)
is a real monotonic function of
\mu
and
\nu
.

An important case is where this complex phase factor is the
Fourier kernel

\tilde{\chi}(\mu)\tau(\nu)=\exp(-{\rm i}\mu\nu).

(37)

In what follows, we always assume that unbaised sets of tags and extractors produce the Fourier kernel. When
\tilde{\chi}
is applied to a linear combination of tags defined in terms of a coefficient function
F(\nu)
, the result is the Fourier transform of
F(\nu)
:

\tilde{\chi}(\mu)\mathbf{F}=\int\tilde{\chi}(\mu)\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\int\exp(-{\rm i}\mu\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\mathcal{F}\{F\}(\mu).

(38)

2.4
Adjoint composite entity

We always write the extractor on the left-hand side of the tag on which it operators. So the extractor always operates toward the right. However, we can also think of it as a process where the tag “operates” toward the left on the extractor. So, while we always write the extactor on the left and the tag on the right when they operate on each other, the identity of which one is operating on the other is ambiguous. The roles of the tag and the extractor can therefore be exchanged. The latter can be combined into an
adjoint
composite entity, represented as

\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}=\int G^{*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu,

(39)

where
G^{*}(\mu)
is the coefficient function. Applying it to a tag on its right, we get

\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\tau(\nu)=\int G^{*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\tau(\nu)\ {\rm d}\mu=\int G^{*}(\mu)\delta(\mu-\nu)\ {\rm d}\mu=G^{*}(\nu).

(40)

In the process, the tag “extracted” the coefficient of the extractor from the composite entity. Note that the order of the extractor and the tag remained the same (extractor on the left and tag on the right), and we can still think of this process in terms of the extractor operating on the tag (which would be the preference in mathematics). The result remains the same.

2.5
Identity and other operators

Based on the convention of the order in which extractors and tags are written when they operate on each other, we can write them in the opposite order as
\tau(\nu)\chi(\mu)
when we do not want them to operate on each other. This allows us to define an
identity operator

\mathbb{I}
in terms of tags and their associated extractors, integrated (or summed) over the entire index space

\mathbb{I}\triangleq\int\tau(\nu)\chi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\int\tau(\nu)\delta(\nu-\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu.

(41)

It is then easy to show that
\mathbb{I}\tau=\tau
and
\chi\mathbb{I}=\chi
.

A straight-forward generalization of the identity operator provides us with a way to define arbitrary operators as

\hat{A}\triangleq\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu,

(42)

where
A(\mu,\nu)
is a kernel. Note that we use the hat
\hat{\cdot}
to represent such an operator. Conversely, according to our notation, a hat always implies a kernel
dressed
on the left by a tag and on the right by an extractor. The term dressed implies an integration (or summation) over a shared index. When an operator is applied to a composite entity (a linear combination of tags), it produces

\displaystyle\hat{A}\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\tau(\xi)F(\xi)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\xi

(43)

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\delta(\mu-\xi)F(\xi)\ {\rm d}\xi\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu=\int\tau(\nu)F^{\prime}(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\mathbf{F}^{\prime},

where

F^{\prime}(\nu)=\int A(\nu,\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu,

(44)

is the transformed coefficient function, as produced by the action of the operator.

The same operator can also be applied to an adjoint composite entity (a linear combination of extractors). In that case, it operates toward the left to produce

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}

\displaystyle=\int G^{*}(\xi)\chi(\xi)\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\xi

(45)

\displaystyle=\int G^{*}(\xi)\delta(\xi-\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int G^{*}(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu=\int G^{\prime*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu=\mathbf{G}^{\prime{\dagger}},

where

G^{\prime*}(\mu)=\int G^{*}(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu,

(46)

is the transformed coefficient function of the adjoint composite entity. It demonstrates that operators defined in terms of dressed kernels can operate in either of the two directions with impunity.

When considering scenarios where an operator is sandwitched between elements from both types of linear combinations, such as
\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}\mathbf{F}
, the order of evaluation is unimportant, regardless of whether the operator implements mappings to subsets of the respective coefficient spaces or not. To illustrate this property, we consider the extreme case of a projection operator. Such a projection operator can be represented as

\hat{P}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{{\dagger}}=\int\tau(\nu)P(\nu)P^{*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu,

(47)

where
\mathbf{P}
is a linear combination of tags with a coefficient function
P(\nu)
and
\mathbf{P}^{{\dagger}}
is the adjoint of
\mathbf{P}
. It follows that

\hat{P}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{P}\int P^{*}(\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu=\mathbf{P}\alpha,

(48)

and

\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{P}=\int G^{*}(\nu)P(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\mathbf{P}^{{\dagger}}=\beta^{*}\mathbf{P}^{{\dagger}},

(49)

where
\alpha
and
\beta^{*}
are two (in general) unrelated complex numbers produced by the inner products between the coefficient functions. The choice of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}
affects the values of these complex numbers but not the identify of the resulting elements
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}^{{\dagger}}
unto which the projections are performed. In both cases, the remaining overlap then leads to the unambiguous result

\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{P}\mathbf{F}=\int G^{*}(\nu)P(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\int P^{*}(\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu=\beta^{*}\alpha.

(50)

For a general operator, it becomes

\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}\mathbf{F}=\int G^{*}(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu,

(51)

in which the order of integration is unimportant. It follows that the overlap of operators by composite entities is an
associative
process.

2.6
Operator trace

In terms of tags and extractors, an
operator trace
is defined as the process whereby the (left-most) tag is flipped around to the other side to be placed on the right-hand side of the (right-most) extractor, which then operates on the tag to produce the Dirac delta function. (In case the tag and extractor are not associated, the appropriate unitary kernel is produced instead of the Dirac delta function.) Hence,

\displaystyle\tr\{\hat{A}\}

\displaystyle\triangleq\tr\left\{\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu\right\}=\int A(\nu,\mu)\chi(\mu)\tau(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu

(52)

\displaystyle=\int A(\nu,\mu)\delta(\mu-\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu=\int A(\nu,\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu.

In this way, an operator trace is converted into a
kernel trace
. In the case of multiple operators, the extractor of a preceeding operator operates on the tag of a subsequent operator so that all tags and extractors are removed in the trace process.

The operator trace thus defined involves the entire space of all possible linear combinations of the tags. As a result, it tacitly assumes that the operators are defined on that entire space. Often such traces of operators on that space are divergent. Traces that produce divergent results are not considered to be physically meaningful. In the case where tags are used to model physical scenarios, the domain on which operators are defined is restricted by the physical entities on which they operate. In such cases, traces would normally include such entities so that the trace would evaluate to a finite result, due to the coefficient functions that define these entities.

3
Tagged vector spaces

The discussions in the preceeding section provide the definitions of the notation and concepts related to tags, serving as the background for the formal definition of tagged vector spaces. For a formal definition for a
tagged vector space
, we need to specify two things. The one is the
index space

\mathcal{I}
. It is the space whose elements are used to label the tags and the extractors. This index space remains the same even when we transform the tags or extractors via unitary transformations. The other thing is the
coefficient space

\mathcal{C}
whose elements serve as the coefficients in the linear combinations of the tags (or extractors). Since the labels of the coefficients need to be the same as those of the tags, the elements of the coefficient space are functions of the index space

\mathcal{C}=\left\{F:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}\right\}.

(53)

The range of these functions
\mathcal{T}
is usually either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, but it could also be something more complex. Here, we assume a continuous index space (such as
\mathbb{R}
).

The set of tags
\{\tau\}
and the set of their associated extractors
\{\chi\}
, which are both labelled by the index space, are defined with two basic axioms:

•

orthogonality
:

\chi(\mu)\tau(\nu)=\delta(\mu-\nu),

(54)

•

completeness
:

\int\tau(\nu)\chi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=\mathbb{I}.

(55)

Due to the abstract nature of these tags and extractors, no further properties are imposes at this point.

For a given set of tags
\{\tau\}
, the tagged vector space is then defined as

\Upsilon(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})=\left\{\mathbf{F}=\int\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu:\nu\in\mathcal{I},F\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

(56)

However, unitary invariance allows us to define the elements of this tagged vector space in terms of any other set of tags
\{\tau^{\prime}\}
that is related to
\{\tau\}
via a unitary transformation.

Every tagged vector space is associated with an
adjoint tagged vector space
, defined in terms of the same
index space

\mathcal{I}
and a
coefficient space

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that may be the same as that of the tagged vector space. (Scenarios where
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\neq\mathcal{C}
may be rare, but can be useful in certain situations.) The linear combinations in the adjoint tagged vector space are formed with the associated set of extractors
\{\chi\}
. Hence,

\Upsilon^{\prime}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=\left\{\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}=\int G^{*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu:\mu\in\mathcal{I},G^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\right\},

(57)

Again, thanks to unitary invariance, the elements of the adjoint tagged vector space can be expressed in terms of any other set of extractors
\{\chi^{\prime}\}
that is related to
\{\chi\}
via a unitary transformation.

3.1
Operators

Given a tagged vector space and the associated adjoint tagged vector space, as defined above, we define operators as given in (
42
), with the requirement that an operator maps elements of the tagged vector space back to the same tagged vector space, and, provided that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{C}
, it also maps elements of the adjoint tagged vector space back to the same adjoint tagged vector space. (It is possible to generalize the definition of operators to scenarios where this requirement is not satisfied. However, we do not consider such scenarios here.) So, given an operator
\hat{A}
, then with
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{C}
, we have

\hat{A}:\Upsilon\longrightarrow\Upsilon~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\hat{A}:\Upsilon^{\prime}\longrightarrow\Upsilon^{\prime}.

(58)

Stated differently,
\mathbf{F}\in\Upsilon
if and only if
\hat{A}\mathbf{F}\in\Upsilon
, and
\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\in\Upsilon^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathbf{G}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}\in\Upsilon^{\prime}
. The domain of these operators is, by definition, the total tagged vector space and the total adjoint tagged vector space. (Although this restriction on the definition of operators may at this point seem overly limiting, we’ll see below that it provides a sufficiently large set.)

3.2
Adjoint

If
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{C}
, then there exists a bijective mapping between the tagged vector space and the adjoint tagged vector space that we call the (Hermitian)
adjoint
, represented by

\displaystyle\textdagger:

\displaystyle\Upsilon(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Upsilon^{\prime}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}=\int\tau(\nu)F(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{F}^{{\dagger}}=\int F^{*}(\nu)\chi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu,

where
F^{*}
represents the complex conjugate of
F
, belonging to the same coefficient space. The mapping is performed by replacing the tags by their associated extractors, and also replacing the coefficient functions by their complex conjugates.

The same process can be performed in reverse

\displaystyle\textdagger:

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{\prime}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Upsilon(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{{\dagger}}=\int F^{*}(\mu)\chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{F}^{{\dagger}{\dagger}}\equiv\mathbf{F}=\int\tau(\mu)F(\mu)\ {\rm d}\mu.

It provides an equivalent mapping (an involution) from the adjoint tagged vector space to the tagged vector space in which extractors are replaced by their associated tags, and coefficient functions by their complex conjugates.

When applied to operators, the mapping produces
\hat{A}\longmapsto\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{A}

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\nu)A(\nu,\mu)\ \chi(\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\int\tau(\mu)A^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\nu)\ \chi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu.

(61)

The adjoint kernel
A^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\nu)
is the transposed complex conjugate of the kernel
A(\nu,\mu)
. The tags and extractors are replaced by each other and their order is inverted. When the adjoint mapping is applied to a product of operators, the order of the operators is likewise inverted. The same applies when composite entities are involved. For example

\left(\hat{A}\mathbf{F}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{F}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\left(\mathbf{F}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{F}.

(62)

Note that the way
\hat{A}
appears in (
43
) and (
45
) does not have anything to do with the adjoint. If
A^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\nu)=A(\nu,\mu)
, the operators is
self-adjoint
; the domain of the operators are always defined to be the same, according to (
58
).

4
Application: Dirac notation for quantum optics

The new tools that we needed are now in place. As a demonstration of the above definitions, we apply them in quantum theory to provide a formal definition of the Dirac notation in the context of quantum optics. For this purpose, we introduce a new notation for the tags and extractors to conform to the Dirac notation. The result provides a formal mathematical description for quantum optics expressed in terms of Dirac notation that captures the original intent of this formalism more accurately, while at the same time avoids any awkwardness in terms of formal mathematics.

4.1
Bras and kets

Here, we focus on the continuous case. The index space is the real numbers
\mathcal{I}\equiv\mathbb{R}
and we choose the coefficient space to be the Schwartz space of one-dimensional complex functions
\mathcal{C}\equiv S(\mathbb{R})
.

Instead of
\tau(\nu)
, we represent the tags in terms of Dirac notation as kets
\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\equiv\tau(\nu)
and their associated extractors as bras
\left\langle{\mu}\right|\equiv\chi(\mu)
, so that the two axioms now read

•

orthogonality
:

\langle{\mu}|{\nu}\rangle=\delta(\mu-\nu),

(63)

•

completeness
:

\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\left\langle{\nu}\right|\ {\rm d}\nu=\mathbb{I}.

(64)

Here
\langle{\mu}|{\nu}\rangle
represents the process where the extractor operates on the tag. These tags and extractors cannot represent normalized (or normalizable) state vectors on their own. The tagged vector space is defined as

\Upsilon=\left\{\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\psi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu:\nu\in\mathbb{R},\psi\in S(\mathbb{R}),\|\psi\|=1\right\},

(65)

and the adjoint tagged vector space is defined as

\Upsilon^{\prime}=\left\{\left\langle{\phi}\right|=\int\phi^{*}(\mu)\left\langle{\mu}\right|\ {\rm d}\mu:\mu\in\mathbb{R},\phi^{*}\in S(\mathbb{R}),\|\phi\|=1\right\}.

(66)

We also define an
inner product
, represented as

\displaystyle\langle{\phi}|{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{*}(\mu)\langle{\mu}|{\nu}\rangle\psi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\nu=\int\phi^{*}(\mu)\delta(\mu-\nu)\psi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\nu

(67)

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{*}(\nu)\psi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu,

being equivalent to an inner product between the coefficient functions. The tagged vector space thus leads to an
inner product space
, with elements given by normalized
state vectors
denoted by kets
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
. (Strictly speaking, we could have defined these inner products already for arbitrary tagged vector spaces and their adjoint tagged vector spaces, but the notation was not suitably developed at that stage.) One can convert the inner product space to a complete
Hilbert space
that is defined in terms of a coefficient space given by
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. However, for physical states, the coefficient functions need to be in
S(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, we maintain the tagged vector space simply as an inner product space.

Although the inner product between state vectors corresponds to an inner product between their coefficient functions, which implies that the inner product space defined in terms of the tagged vector space is isomorphic to the coefficient space in terms of which it is defined, it is important to note that it does not
physically
represent the same space. The reason is that there does not exist a unique mapping between the state vectors in the inner product space and the elements of the coefficient space, because of the unitary invariance in the definition of the state vectors. All representations of a given element
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
in terms of different tags via unitary transformations represent the same physical state vector of a system, and therefore the same element in the inner product space. The same applies for the elements of the adjoint tagged vector space
\left\langle{\phi}\right|
, representing
adjoint state vectors
. (Here, we avoid the term “dual state vectors” because of its meaning in formal mathematics as a linear functional.)

The inner product implies a norm given by the
Euclidean norm
. The additional conditions for the normalization of the coefficient functions in the definitions of the tagged vector space and the adjoint tagged vector space in (
65
) and (
66
) are expressed in terms of this norm. As a result,
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
represents a
normalized
state vector in that
\|\psi\|^{2}=\langle{\psi}|{\psi}\rangle=1
. By implication, the coefficient functions are normalized. Similarly,
\left\langle{\phi}\right|
represents a
normalized
adjoint state vector. These conditions are required in the quantum context to ensure
conservation of probability
. The norm in turn implies a metric given by the
Euclidean metric
and the metric also leads to an associated metric topology.

4.2
Operators

The
density operator
for a pure state is defined as

\hat{\rho}_{{\rm pure}}\triangleq\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|\equiv\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\psi(\nu)\psi^{*}(\mu)\left\langle{\mu}\right|\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu,

(68)

consistent with the notation in (
42
). To generalize it to arbitrary states, which can include mixed states, we also allow ensemble averages of such pure states, leading to the general definition

\hat{\rho}\triangleq\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\rho(\nu,\mu)\left\langle{\mu}\right|\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu,

(69)

where
\rho(\nu,\mu)
represents a self-adjoint positive semi-definite Hilbert-Schmidt kernel. Its Hilbert-Schmidt norm represents the
purity
of the state. The normalization of the density operator is imposed by requiring that its operator trace equals 1. According to (
52
), it implies that

\tr\{\hat{\rho}\}=\int\rho(\nu,\mu)\langle{\mu}|{\nu}\rangle\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu=\int\rho(\nu,\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=1.

(70)

General operators are defined as in (
42
), in a way similar to density operators,

\hat{A}\triangleq\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle A(\nu,\mu)\left\langle{\mu}\right|\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\mu.

(71)

However, the kernel
A(\nu,\mu)
of a general operator need not be a Hilbert-Schmidt kernel. Nevertheless, according to (
58
), they are defined to map elements of the inner product space back to the same inner product space. Unfortunately, even if we restrict the elements of the coefficient space to be normalized Schwartz functions, the conditions do not restrict the space of possible kernels by much. In general, these kernels form a subset of the
Schwartz kernels
. We also relax the restriction on normalization by allowing a subsequent normalization process, so that the kernels may include those that are not
trace preserving
.

These operators (including density operators) are thus defined as operators (operating toward the right) that map elements from the tagged vector space back to the same tagged vector space, as shown in (
58
). The process is shown in (
43
), but with the tags and extractors represented by kets and bras, respectively. The operators are such that, even allowing for a possible non-trace-preserving process, we still have
\|\psi^{\prime}\|<\infty
. For those cases where
\|\psi^{\prime}\|=1
, the operator is a unitary operator, often used to represent physical processes involved in the evolution of physical states.

The same operators are also defined as operators (operating toward the left) that map elements from the adjoint tagged vector space back to the same adjoint tagged vector space, also shown in (
58
). The process is likewise shown in (
45
) by replacing the tags and extractors by kets and bras, respectively.

When the adjoint is applied to such an operator process, we get

\left\langle{\psi^{\prime}}\right|=\left(\left|{\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle\right)^{{\dagger}}=\left(\hat{A}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\right)^{{\dagger}}=\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}.

(72)

As with
\hat{A}
, the adjoint
\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}
can operate either toward the right or toward the left. So, we also have

\left|{\phi^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left(\left\langle{\phi^{\prime}}\right|\right)^{{\dagger}}=\left(\left\langle{\phi}\right|\hat{A}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}\left|{\phi}\right\rangle.

(73)

Operators are
never
placed inside kets or bras because that would lead to confusion. The symbol inside kets and bras are used as labels to distinguish them and do not represent anything on which an operator can operate. Although we generally use the same symbol for the coefficient function to label the state vector, this is not always the case. For example, the kets and bras representing Fock states are usually labelled with the integer for the occupation number. Operators are defined only to operate on state vectors and adjoint state vectors. As such, the operator is placed outside the ket or the bra on which it operates on the side of the straight edge:
\hat{A}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
or
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{A}
.

4.3
Probability distributions and their moments

The normalization imposed on the state vectors are required for the conservation of probability, as incorporated into the formalisms of quantum theory. In the tagged vector space formulation, it implies that the modulus squared coefficient function acts like a probability distribution

|\psi(\nu)|^{2}\equiv P(\nu)
, so that

\int|\psi(\nu)|^{2}\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\int P(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu=1.

(74)

The different moments of such a probability distribution play significant roles in measurements (or observations). These moments are defined by

\mathcal{M}_{n}\triangleq\int\nu^{n}P(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\int\nu^{n}|\psi(\nu)|^{2}\ {\rm d}\nu.

(75)

In the context of quantum theory (as represented here in terms of a tagged vector space), a convenient method to compute such moments is to define
moment operators
as diagonal operators

\hat{M}_{n}\triangleq\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle\nu^{n}\left\langle{\nu}\right|\ {\rm d}\nu.

(76)

When such an operator is sandwiched between the bra and ket of the same state vector, it produces

\displaystyle\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{M}_{n}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\psi^{*}(\mu)\langle{\mu}|{\nu}\rangle\nu^{n}\langle{\nu}|{\xi}\rangle\psi(\xi)\ {\rm d}\mu\ {\rm d}\nu\ {\rm d}\xi

(77)

\displaystyle=\int\nu^{n}|\psi(\nu)|^{2}\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\mathcal{M}_{n}.

The product of any two moment operators produces another moment operator with the sum of their powers
\hat{M}_{m}\hat{M}_{n}=\hat{M}_{m+n}
. It thus suffices to define a
first moment operator
. All the higher moments can then be produced as products of multiple first moment operators, so that

\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{M}_{1}^{n}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\int\psi^{*}(\nu)\nu^{n}\psi(\nu)\ {\rm d}\nu\equiv\mathcal{M}_{n}.

(78)

Provided that these moment operators are only applied to state vectors whose coefficient functions are elements of Schwartz space, these moments are always finite and therefore defined on the entire inner product space. However, although finite, they can take on any value. It is therefore not possible to specify a constant that would be larger than all the possible values that would be obtained for all the elements in the coefficient space. Therefore the moment operators are
unbounded
. So, while these operators are defined on the entire inner product space when the coefficient space is given by Schwartz space, thus satisfying the conditions for (
58
), they would not be defined on the entire Hilbert space when the coefficient space is
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The first moment operator assumes a specific representation of the tags as labelled by the index space. If we apply a unitary transformation to define the operator in terms of a different set of tags, the result is not a first moment operator for the new set of tags. In other words, if

\left|{\mu}\right\rangle^{\prime}=\int\left|{\nu}\right\rangle U(\nu,\mu)\ {\rm d}\nu~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\left\langle{\mu}\right|^{\prime}=\int U^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\xi)\left\langle{\xi}\right|\ {\rm d}\xi,

(79)

then

\int U^{{\dagger}}(\mu,\nu)\nu U(\nu,\xi)\ {\rm d}\nu\neq\mu.

(80)

Therefore, if we want to compute the first moment with respect a different set of tags that is related to the original set of tags via a unitary transformation, we need to define a dedicated first moment operator for that different set of tags. As a result, it is common to define two separate first moment operators for two mutually unbaised sets of tags.

4.4
Quadrature operators

An important example of such a pair of first moment operators is the
quadrature operators
used in quantum optics. To define the quadrature operators, we use two different mutually unbiased sets of tags (and extractors). The indices for these two sets of tags are labelled by the variables
q\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively, so that the tags are indicated as
\left|{q}\right\rangle
and
\left|{p}\right\rangle
, respectively, and their associated extractors by
\left\langle{q}\right|
and
\left\langle{p}\right|
, respectively. The axioms are then represented by

•

orthogonality
:

\langle{q}|{q^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta(q-q^{\prime})~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\langle{p}|{p^{\prime}}\rangle=2\pi\delta(p-p^{\prime}),

(81)

•

completeness
:

\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\left\langle{q}\right|\ {\rm d}q=\mathbb{I}~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\int\left|{p}\right\rangle\left\langle{p}\right|\ \frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}=\mathbb{I},

(82)

•

unbiased
:

\langle{q}|{p}\rangle=\exp({\rm i}qp)~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\langle{p}|{q}\rangle=\exp(-{\rm i}qp),

(83)

where we include a third set of axioms for the mutually unbiased property. Note that the axioms for the
p
index space incorporate factors of
2\pi
to anticipate the definition of the Fourier transform. The quadrature operators are now defined as first moment operators

\hat{q}=\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\left\langle{q}\right|{\rm d}q~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\hat{p}=\int\left|{p}\right\rangle p\left\langle{p}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}.

(84)

The domains of definition for these operators are as expressed in (
58
) in terms of the definitions of the spaces in (
65
) and (
66
).

4.5
Quadrature operator commutation

It is now of interest to consider the commutation of these quadrature operators, as defined in terms of their tags and extractors. The definition of these operators in terms of a tagged vector space allows us to avoid having to define one of the operators in terms of a derivative, which would imply that it could only operate toward the right. Instead, it allows these operators to operate either to the right or to the left.

Consider now the commutation
[\hat{q},\hat{p}]
. In terms of (
84
), it becomes

\displaystyle[\hat{q},\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\langle{q}|{p}\rangle p\left\langle{p}\right|{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}-\int\left|{p}\right\rangle p\langle{p}|{q}\rangle q\left\langle{q}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q

(85)

\displaystyle=\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\exp({\rm i}qp)p\left\langle{p}\right|{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}-\int\left|{p}\right\rangle p\exp(-{\rm i}qp)q\left\langle{q}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q,

where we used (
83
). To make the two terms more compatible, we insert identities resolved in a
q
-basis in (
82
) on the side of
\hat{p}
in each term, and then relabel the
q
-indices to bring the two terms into the same form. The result reads

\displaystyle[\hat{q},\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q^{\prime}

(86)

\displaystyle-\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)q^{\prime}\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}.

Next, we employ integration by parts on the integration over
p
in the second term

\displaystyle\int\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\exp({\rm i}qp)\frac{p}{-{\rm i}q^{\prime}}\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\right]_{p=-t}^{p=t}

(87)

\displaystyle+\int\exp({\rm i}qp)\frac{qp}{q^{\prime}}\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p){\rm d}p

\displaystyle-\int\exp({\rm i}qp)\frac{1}{-{\rm i}q^{\prime}}\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p){\rm d}p.

The term with the limit produces a potentially divergent result. Below, we’ll consider it separately. Substituting this result back into the full expression, we get

\displaystyle[\hat{q},\hat{p}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|{\rm d}q\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q^{\prime}

(88)

\displaystyle-{\rm i}\hat{\Delta}-\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)\frac{qp}{q^{\prime}}\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)q^{\prime}\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)\frac{1}{-{\rm i}q^{\prime}}\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)q^{\prime}\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm i}\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}-{\rm i}\hat{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm i}\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\delta(q-q^{\prime})\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}-{\rm i}\hat{\Delta}={\rm i}\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\left\langle{q}\right|{\rm d}q-{\rm i}\hat{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm i}\mathbb{I}-{\rm i}\hat{\Delta},

where

\hat{\Delta}\triangleq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\left\langle{q^{\prime}}\right|\right]_{p=-t}^{p=t}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}.

(89)

This term indicates that we need to consider the domain of definition of these operators. This domain is determined by the state vectors on which these operators can operate to produce state vectors that are still elements of the same domain. Such state vectors are defined by

\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\int\left|{q}\right\rangle\psi(q){\rm d}q~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\left\langle{\psi}\right|=\int\psi^{*}(q)\left\langle{q}\right|{\rm d}q,

(90)

where
\psi(q)
and
\psi^{*}(q)
are Schwartz functions, as specified for the coefficient space in the definition of the tagged vector space. When we consider the application of
\hat{\Delta}
on such state vectors, either toward the right
\hat{\Delta}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
or toward the left
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{\Delta}
, we get (for example)

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\langle{q^{\prime}}|{q^{\prime\prime}}\rangle\psi(q^{\prime\prime})\right]_{p=-t}^{p=t}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}{\rm d}q^{\prime\prime}

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)p\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\psi(q^{\prime})\right]_{p=-t}^{p=t}{\rm d}q{\rm d}q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\left[\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|{q}\right\rangle\exp({\rm i}qp)p\tilde{\psi}(p)\right]_{p=-t}^{p=t}{\rm d}q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|{q}\right\rangle\left[\exp({\rm i}qt)t\tilde{\psi}(t)+\exp(-{\rm i}qt)t\tilde{\psi}(-t)\right]{\rm d}q,

where

\tilde{\psi}(p)=\int\exp(-{\rm i}q^{\prime}p)\psi(q^{\prime}){\rm d}q^{\prime}

(92)

is the Fourier transform of
\psi(q^{\prime})
. If
\psi(q^{\prime})
is a Schwartz function, then so is
\tilde{\psi}(p)
. It thus follows that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t\tilde{\psi}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t\tilde{\psi}(-t)=0.

(93)

The same happens for
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{\Delta}
. Therefore, the terms produced when
\hat{\Delta}
either operates toward the right on state vectors (elements of the tagged vector space), or toward the left on adjoint state vectors (elements of the adjoint tagged vector space), are zero. We end up with

[\hat{q},\hat{p}]={\rm i}\mathbb{I},

(94)

where the equation is understood as being restricted to the domain of definition for these operators. The commutation of each operator with itself is easily shown to be zero
[\hat{q},\hat{q}]=[\hat{p},\hat{p}]=0
.

4.6
Ladder operators

One final stage in the development that we need to consider is the definition of
ladder operators
(creation and annihilation operators) in terms of tags and extractors. We define the ladder operators in terms of the quadrature operators as

\hat{a}\triangleq{\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(\hat{q}+{\rm i}\hat{p}\right)~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}\triangleq{\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(\hat{q}-{\rm i}\hat{p}\right).

(95)

Based on those of the quadrature operators, the commutation relations of the ladder operators are

[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}]=\mathbb{I}~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~[\hat{a},\hat{a}]=[\hat{a}^{{\dagger}},\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}]=0.

(96)

We now derive the expressions for ladder operators in terms of tags and extractors. For this purpose, we use identity operators to express both terms using the same types of tags. Considering the annihilation operator, we have

\displaystyle\hat{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(\hat{q}+{\rm i}\hat{p}\right)={\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left(\int\left|{q}\right\rangle q\left\langle{q}\right|\ {\rm d}q+{\rm i}\int\left|{p}\right\rangle p\left\langle{p}\right|\ \frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}\right)

(97)

\displaystyle=

The implication of (

We can show that

.

Considering an abitrary linear transformation of the quadrature operators

\hat{q}\rightarrow\hat{q}^{\prime}=a\hat{q}+b\hat{p}~~~~~{\rm and}~~~~~\hat{p}\rightarrow\hat{p}^{\prime}=c\hat{q}+d\hat{p},

where

(104)

5
Conclusions

The remaining aspects of the quantum optics formalism can now be derived in a straight forward way from that which is laid down so far. See, for example Chapter 3 in
[
12
]
. Subsequently, we can define a number operator in terms of the ladder operators and compute its eigenstates. They are the Fock states and are represented in terms of tags (and extractors) with the Hermite-Gauss functions as their coefficient functions. One can also consider the (right) eigenstates of the annihilation operator (or the left eigenstates of the creation operator), to obtain the coherent states, with Gauss functions as their coefficient functions. Displacement operators and squeezing operators are readily defined as exponentiated operators in terms of ladder or quadrature operators.

Although, we can proceed to use the above expressions in terms of tags and extractors to obtain the associated expressions for the Fock states, we already have such expressions obtained from the basic relationships of the ladder operators from standard quantum theory. The same applies for the coherent states.

We found that the ladder operators are naturally represented in terms of Weyl transforms of complex combinations of the unbiased indices. These complex quantities thus represent the Wigner functions of the ladder operators. One can proceed to relate the kernels of arbitrary operators, expressed in terms of tags and extractors, to their Wigner functions. Products of operators thus defined leads to Moyal star products of their Wigner functions. In this way, the quantum formalism defined in terms of tagged vector spaces thus naturally leads to a Moyal formalism.

The tagged vector space formalism allows us to maintain the general usage of Dirac notation and its associated operators, as employed among physicists. At the same time, it provides a structure in which the rigorous mathematical formulations of quantum theory is incorporated, rendering the tagged vector space formalism mathematically well defined.

Here in Part I, we have focused on the one-dimensional case where the index space is
\mathbb{R}
, representing the particle-number (quantum) degree of freedom in the context of quantum optics. To include the other degrees of freedom (such as the spatiotemporal degrees of freedom), the index space needs to be defined as a
function space
. It then leads to a functional representation of the tagged vector space, which in turn leads to the notion of
functional integration
. This topic is addressed in Part II.
