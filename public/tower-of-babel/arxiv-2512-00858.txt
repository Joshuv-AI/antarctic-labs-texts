On Lemniscate starlikeness of analytic functions and its application to special functions
Saiful R. Mondal, Ahmad K. Al Abdulaali

Abstract
. This paper investigates the lemniscate starlikeness of analytic functions by deriving specific conditions on their power series coefficients. The study utilizes the Cauchy product of power series along with key inequalities involving the Pochhammer symbol and the Gamma function. The derived results are further applied to a number of special functions, providing parameter restrictions under which these functions become lemniscate starlike. The findings extend and refine several earlier results in this domain.



1.
Introduction

We consider the analytic and univalent functions
f
defined in the unit disk
\mathcal{U}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}

and normalized by
f(0)=0,\quad f^{\prime}(0)=1
. We denote such classes by
\mathcal{S}
. For a function
f\in\mathcal{S}
have the power series form

(1)

\displaystyle f(z)=z+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}.

Given
0\leq\alpha<1
,
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\alpha)
, the class consisting of functions
f\in\mathcal{S}
with analytic charaterization

\real\left\{\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right\}>\alpha\quad(z\in\mathcal{U}),

is known as starlike of order
\alpha
. Similarly,
f
is said to be in the class of functions
convex of order
\alpha
, denoted by
K(\alpha)
, if

\real\left\{1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right\}>\alpha\quad(z\in\mathcal{U}).

For two analytic functions
f
and
g
defined on
\mathbb{D}
, the function
f
is said to be
subordinate
to
g
, denoted by
f\prec g
, if there exists an analytic function
w:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
satisfying
w(0)=0
and
f(z)=g(w(z))
. Moreover, if the function
g
is univalent, then
f\prec g
if and only if
f(0)=g(0)
and
f(\mathbb{D})\subset g(\mathbb{D})
.

Consider the function
\phi_{l}(z):=\sqrt{1+z}
, which maps
\mathbb{D}
univalently onto the region illustrated in Figure
1
. This region is commonly referred to as the
lemniscate domain
; see
[
28
,
27
]
.

Figure 1.
Image of
\phi_{l}(\mathbb{D})
.

A function
f\in\mathcal{S}
is lemniscate starlike if
\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\prec\sqrt{1+z}
. This is equivalent to say

(2)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}}{(f(z))^{2}}-1\right|<1.

The lemniscate starlike can be further refined by considering
\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\prec\sqrt{1+cz}
,
0<c\leq 1
. We denote this class as
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(q_{c})
. The class
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(q_{c})
is the main attraction of our study. This class was introduced and studied in
[
27
]
.

The
cross-product
(Cauchy product) of the power series

g_{1}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n},

which serves as the main analytical tool in this study, is defined by

g_{3}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n},\quad\text{where}\quad c_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}b_{\,n-j}.

In this study, we derive the conditions on the coefficient
a_{k}
of the power series (
1
) for a function
f\in\mathcal{S}
such that
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(q_{c})
, where
c\in(0,1]
. The main result in this regard is presented in
Section

3
. This result is further applied to several special functions, and the corresponding outcomes are discussed in various subsections of
Section

4
. Specifically,
Section

4.1
provides examples involving exponential functions, while
Section

4.2
addresses the case of confluent hypergeometric functions. The lemniscate starlikeness of the Bessel and Struve functions is analyzed in
Section

4.3
and
Section

4.5
, respectively. The result related to Bessel functions refines and extends the findings reported in
[
31
]
. The product of Bessel functions is examined in
Section

4.4
, and an illustrative example involving the error function is discussed in
Section

4.6
. For clarity and readability, a brief literature review of each special function under consideration is included in its respective subsection.

Moreover, several fundamental results involving Pochhammer symbols and Gamma functions, which play an essential role in the development of the main theorem and its applications, are provided in
Section

2
. Finally, the study concludes by listing several additional special functions that can also be investigated using the established main result. Owing to the similarity of the arguments, detailed computations for these cases are omitted.

2.
Fundamental results

We begin this section by proving certain fundamental results necessary for the framework of our study. Furthermore, we state auxiliary results from the literature that will facilitate the application of the main theorems obtained herein.

Lemma 1
.

For
n\in N
,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n\leq\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}.

The equality holds at
n=3
.

Proof.

First, we test the cases numerically for
n=1,2,
and
n=3
.

(1)

n=1
:
\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n=1.25
,
\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}=1.73801

(2)

n=2
:
\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n=3
,
\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}=3.02069

(3)

n=3
:
\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n=5.25
,
\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}=5.25
.

Clearly, the inequality (
3
) holds for
n=1,2
, and both sides are equal for
n=3
.

Suppose that the inequality (
3
) is true for
m=n\geq 3
, that is,

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}}{4}+m\leq\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{m}{3}}.

Now,

\displaystyle\frac{(m+1)^{2}}{4}+(m+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{4}+\frac{3m}{2}+\frac{5}{4}<\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{m}{3}}+\frac{m}{2}+\frac{5}{4}<\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{m+1}{3}}.

To prove the last part of the inequality, for real
x\geq 3
, define the function

H(x)=\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{x+1}{3}}-\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{x}{3}}-\frac{x}{2}-\frac{5}{4}.

Clearly, the inequality
H(x)>0
is equivalent to showing
H(m)>0
for all integers
m\geq 3
.

The derivatives of
H(x)

H^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\ln(21/4)}{3}\,\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{x}{3}}\left(\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{1/3}-1\right)-\frac{1}{2}.

Let
C=\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{1/3}-1\approx 0.73
and

B=\tfrac{\ln(21/4)}{3}\approx 0.55
. Then

H^{\prime}(x)=BC\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{x/3}-\frac{1}{2}.

Since
\tfrac{21}{4}>1
, the term
\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{x/3}
grows exponentially with
x
.
Thus for
x\geq 3
,
H^{\prime}(x)
is strictly positive. Therefore
H(x)
is strictly increasing on
[3,\infty)
.

Numerically,

H(3)=\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{4/3}-\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)-\frac{11}{4}\approx 1.08>0.

Since
H(3)>0
and
H(x)
is increasing for
x\geq 3
, it follows that

H(m)>0\quad\text{for all integers }m\geq 3.

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For
k\in[1,n]
for a fixed
2\leq n\in N
,

(4)

\displaystyle n(k+1)-k^{2}\leq\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}.

Proof.

First, denote the function

(5)

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(k):=n(k+1)-k^{2}.

It is straightforward to verify that

\Psi_{n}(k)=k(n-k)+n>0,\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\;k\in[1,n].

Differentiating with respect to
k
yields

\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(k)=n-2k,

which vanishes at
k=\tfrac{n}{2}
. Furthermore,

\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(k)=-2<0,

demonstrating that
\Psi
is strictly concave and hence attains its maximum at
k=\tfrac{n}{2}
. Consequently, the maximal value of
\Psi
is given by

(6)

\Psi_{n}\!\left(\tfrac{n}{2}\right)=\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n.

From
Lemma

1
, it follows that

\Psi_{n}\!(k)=\frac{n^{2}}{4}+n\leq\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{3}}.

This completes the proof. ∎

We need the following basic information about the generalized hypergeometric function: The generalized hypergeometric function is denoted by

{}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};z)

and is defined by the power series:

{}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},\ldots,b_{q};z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{p})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}\cdots(b_{q})_{n}}\cdot\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\ldots(a+n-1)
is the well-known Pochammer symbol
[
23
]
. The convergence of the generalized hypergeometric series depends on the relation between
p
and
q
:

•

If
p\leq q
:
The series converges for all finite
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence, the function is entire.

•

If
p=q+1
:
The series converges for
|z|<1
. It may also converge at
|z|=1
under the condition:

\real\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\right)>0.

•

If
p>q+1
:
The series diverges for any
z\neq 0
, except in special cases that allow analytic continuation.

Denote
\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0\}
. The following identity for
b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}
is useful in the sequel.

Lemma 3
.

For
b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}
,

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}=\dfrac{2^{2n}\left(b-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}{n!(b)_{n}(2b-1)_{n}}.

Proof.

To prove the identity, we need a few basic relations.
For
0\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}=n(n-1)(n-2)\ldots(n-k+1)=(-1)^{k}(-n)_{k},

\displaystyle(b)_{n-k}(b+n-k)_{k}=(b)_{n}\implies(b)_{n-k}=\frac{(b)_{n}}{(b+n-k)_{k}}.

Thus, we can rewrite the right-hand side of (
7
) as

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}=\frac{1}{n!(b)_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(-n)_{k}(b+n-k)_{k}}{(b)_{k}}.

Our next verification is the identity,
(-1)^{k}(b+n-k)_{k}:=(1-b-n)_{k}
which is trivially true for
k=0,1
. Now, using mathematical induction along with basic properties of the Pochhammer symbol
(a+1)_{m}=(a+1)(a)_{m-1}
and
(a+m)(a)_{m}=(a)_{m+1}
, one can easily verify the identity.

This gives us a closed form of the right-hand side of (
7
) as

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}=\frac{1}{n!(b)_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{k}(1-b-n)_{k}}{k!(b)_{k}}=\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(-n,1-b-n;b;1)}{n!(b)_{n}}.

The classical Chu–Vandermonde summation formula provides a closed form for a terminating hypergeometric series
[
26
]
:

{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(-n,\,\alpha;\,\beta;\,1\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{k}\,(\alpha)_{k}}{(\beta)_{k}\,k!}=\frac{(\beta-\alpha)_{n}}{(\beta)_{n}},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Using this closed form (
9
) can be rewrite as

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}=\frac{(2b-1+n)_{n}}{n!(b)_{n}(b)_{n}}.

Next, recall below two identities of the Pochhammer symbol as

(11)

\displaystyle(a)_{2n}=(a)_{n}(a+n)_{n};\quad(a)_{2n}=2^{2n}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)_{n}\left(\frac{a+1}{2}\right)_{n}.

Finally, setting
a=2b-1
, we have

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}=\frac{(2b-1)_{2n}}{n!(2b-1)_{n}(b)_{n}}=\frac{2^{2n}\left(b-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}{n!(2b-1)_{n}(b)_{n}}.

∎

Lemma 4
.

For
b\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}
such that
\real(b)\geq-1/2
, then

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}}\right|\left|\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}\right|\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n!|b|^{n}}.

Proof.

Suppose
b\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{-}
with
b=\alpha+i\beta
. It is trivially holds that
|(b)_{1}|=|b|
. Now, for
k\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle|(b)_{k}|^{2}=|b|^{2}|b+1|^{2}\ldots|b+k-1|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})((\alpha+1)^{2}+\beta^{2})\ldots((\alpha+k-1)^{2}+\beta^{2})\geq(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})^{k}=(|b|^{k})^{2}

provided
\alpha=\real(b)\geq-1/2
. This together, we have
|(b)_{k}|\geq|b|^{k}
for
k\geq 1
. Similarly, it follows that
|(b)_{n-k}|\geq|b|^{n-k}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
.

Now, from the binomial theorem, we know

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}x^{k}=(1+x)^{n}\implies\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}=\frac{2^{n}}{n!}.

Finally, we conclude that for
\real(b)>-1/2

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{k!\,(b)_{k}}\right|\left|\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,(b)_{n-k}}\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,\left|b\right|^{k}}\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,\left|b\right|^{n-k}}=\frac{1}{|b|^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{1}{(n-k)!}=\frac{2^{n}}{n!|b|^{n}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Lemniscate starlike of analytic functions

Now we state and prove our main theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be real or complex sequence such that

(14)

\displaystyle a_{1}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|<1.

Suppose that
f(z):=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
. Now, for
c\in[0,1]
if

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}-c\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|<c,

then
zf^{\prime}(z)/f(z)\prec\sqrt{1+cz}
.

Proof.

Now, if
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
, then

\displaystyle(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}=\left(z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}na_{n}z^{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=z^{2}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1)a_{n+1}z^{n}\right)^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle=z^{2}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)(n-k+1)a_{k+1}a_{n-k+1}\right)z^{n}\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle(f(z))^{2}=\left(z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=z^{2}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n+1}z^{n}\right)^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle=z^{2}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k+1}a_{n-k+1}\right)z^{n}\right).

From (
3
) and (
3
), it follwos that

\displaystyle(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}-(f(z))^{2}=z^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(n(k+1)-k^{2}\right)a_{k+1}a_{n-k+1}\right)z^{n}.

Thus for
|z|<1
in together with
Lemma

2
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}-(f(z))^{2}}{z^{2}}\right|<\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|n(k+1)-k^{2}\right||a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|<\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg[\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|\bigg].

Simpliarly

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(f(z))^{2}}{z^{2}}\right|>1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}}{(f(z))^{2}}-1\right|=\frac{\left|\tfrac{(zf^{\prime}(z))^{2}-(f(z))^{2}}{z^{2}}\right|}{\left|\tfrac{(f(z))^{2}}{z^{2}}\right|}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|n(k+1)-k^{2}\right||a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|}{1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg[\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|\bigg]}{1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|}.

Since the denominator is positive, the follwoing inequality holds

\displaystyle 0<\dfrac{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg[\left(\frac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|\bigg]}{1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|a_{k+1}||a_{n-k+1}|}<c,

provided (
15
) holds. This completes the proof of the result.
∎

Example involving exponential function

Example 4.1

4.2.
Example involving confluent hypergeometric functions

A particularly important case is obtained by setting
p=0
and
q=1
, leading to the
confluent hypergeometric limit function

(19)

{}_{0}F_{1}(;b;z)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(b)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

This function is sometimes denoted by
\Phi(b;z)
in the classical literature.

The function
{}_{0}F_{1}(;b;z)
is the unique analytic solution normalized by
{}_{0}F_{1}(;b;0)=1
of the second-order linear differential equation

(20)

z\,y^{\prime\prime}(z)+b\,y^{\prime}(z)-y(z)=0.

Since the parameters satisfy
p=0<q+1=2
, the defining series converges for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, and hence
{}_{0}F_{1}(;b;z)
is an
entire function
.

One of the most remarkable properties of
{}_{0}F_{1}
is its close relation to Bessel functions. For
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(21)

{}_{0}F_{1}(;\nu+1;z)=\Gamma(\nu+1)\,z^{-\nu/2}\,I_{\nu}(2\sqrt{z}),

where
I_{\nu}(z)
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind. Similarly,

(22)

{}_{0}F_{1}(;\nu+1;-z)=\Gamma(\nu+1)\,z^{-\nu/2}\,J_{\nu}(2\sqrt{z}),

with
J_{\nu}(z)
being the Bessel function of the first kind.
Thus,
{}_{0}F_{1}
provides a hypergeometric framework for Bessel functions and, more generally, for many special functions arising in analysis.

Some special evaluations of
{}_{0}F_{1}
include:

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{1}(;1;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}=I_{0}(2\sqrt{z}),\quad{}_{0}F_{1}\!\left(;\tfrac{1}{2};-\tfrac{x^{2}}{4}\right)=\cos(x),\quad{}_{0}F_{1}\!\left(;\tfrac{3}{2};-\tfrac{x^{2}}{4}\right)=\frac{\sin(x)}{x}.

This illustrates how
{}_{0}F_{1}
interpolates between trigonometric and Bessel functions.

Now consider our next example as
\mathtt{F_{2}}(z):=z{}_{0}F_{1}(\quad;b;z)
. Then, the corresponding sequence
a_{n}=1/((n-1)!(b)_{n-1})
, and from Lemma
2
we have
for
b>0

(23)

\displaystyle\delta(b):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{(b)_{k}k!(b)_{n-k}(n-k)!}\right)={}_{1}F_{2}\left(b-\frac{1}{2};b,2b-1;4\right)-1

Using Mathematica software, we can numerically compute the root of the equation

(24)

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{2}\left(b-\frac{1}{2};b,2b-1;4\right)-1=1

is
b=2.75885
. Further, from the
Figure

2
it is clear that
\delta(b)<1
.

\delta(b)
for

.

Hence
\delta(b)<1
for

. This leads to the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

For

,

\,{}_{1}F_{2}\left(b-\frac{1}{2};b,2b-1;2\sqrt[3]{42}\right)-c\,_{1}F_{2}\left(b-\frac{1}{2};b,2b-1;4\right)<1,

In particular,

The proof the

4.3.
Example involving Bessel functions

We recall some information about Bessel functions and their connections with geometric function theory.
Bessel functions of the first kind, denoted by
J_{\nu}(z)
, are solutions to Bessel’s differential equation:

z^{2}w^{\prime\prime}(z)+zw^{\prime}(z)+(z^{2}-\nu^{2})w(z)=0,

where
\nu
is a real or complex parameter. These functions are central to many problems in mathematical physics, including heat conduction, wave propagation, and problems in circular and cylindrical domains. In the unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
,
J_{\nu}(z)
is analytic because it has the power series expansion:

J_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu},

which converges absolutely in the entire complex plane.

For applications in geometric function theory, it is often convenient to consider the
normalized form
of the Bessel function:

f_{3}(\nu,z):=2^{\nu}\Gamma(\nu+1)z^{1-\nu/2}J_{\nu}(\sqrt{z}),

which satisfies the normalization conditions
f_{3}(\nu,0)=0
and
f_{3}^{\prime}(\nu,0)=1
.
Similarly, the modified Bessel function of the first kind,
I_{\nu}(z)
, is defined by:

I_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu},

and has a normalized form analogous to that of
J_{\nu}(z)
as

f_{4}(\nu,z)=2^{\nu}\Gamma(\nu+1)z^{1-\nu/2}I_{\nu}(\sqrt{z}).

In the study of the geometric properties of the function
f_{3}(\nu,z)
, several contributions have been made in the literature
[
25
,
5
,
29
]
. Selinger
[
25
]
demonstrated that the function
f_{\nu}(z)
is starlike in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
for all
\nu\geq 0.75
. This range was subsequently improved by Baricz and Ponnusamy
[
5
]
, who showed that starlikeness holds for
\nu\geq-0.432619635\ldots
or within the interval
-0.875\leq\nu<-0.719669914
. It was also noted that the constant
-0.875
is optimal in the sense that it is the smallest value of
\nu
for which
f_{\nu}
remains starlike in
D
. A variety of techniques were utilized in
[
5
]
to verify the starlikeness of
f_{3}(\nu,z)
for
\nu\geq-0.875
, although a gap remains where the result has not been fully validated. By synthesizing all the results in
[
5
]
concerning both
f_{3}(\nu,z)
and
f_{4}(\nu,z)
, it can be concluded that

Theorem 4.2
.

[
5
]

For
\nu\in[-0.875,-0.719669914)\cup(-0.4373468657,\infty)
the functions
f_{3}(\nu,z)
and
f_{4}(\nu,z)
are starlike in
\mathbb{D}
.

Our next consideration is the generalized Bessel function, introduced in
[
13
,
14
]
. The generalized and normalized Bessel functions have the series form

\displaystyle\mathtt{U}_{\nu}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\eta^{n}}{4^{n}(\kappa)_{n}}\;\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad 2\kappa=2p+b+1\neq 0,-2,-4,-6,\ldots;

which is the solution of

(26)

\displaystyle 4z^{2}\mathtt{U^{\prime\prime}}(z)+4\kappa z\mathtt{U^{\prime}}(z)+\eta z\mathtt{U}(z)=0.

For
b=c=1
, the function
\mathtt{U}_{\nu}
represents the normalized Bessel function of order
p
, while for
b=-\eta=1
, the function
\mathtt{U}_{\nu}
represents the normalized modified Bessel function of order
p
. The Spherical Bessel function can also be obtain by using

,
\eta=1

The inclusion of

in various subclasses of univalent functions theory is extensively studied by many authors
[

and some references therein. Recently, the lemniscate starlike, convexity and other properties of

The function

when

p
when

Let us consider

\displaystyle\mathtt{F_{3}}(\kappa,\eta,z):=z\mathtt{U}_{p}(z).

It can be shown that

(27)

\displaystyle 4zf_{4}^{\prime\prime}(z)+4(\kappa-2)zf_{4}^{\prime}(z)+(cz-4\kappa+8)f_{4}(z)=0.

Now we have the following result regarding the lemniscate starlike property of generalised Bessel functions:

Theorem 4.3
.

For real
\kappa>0
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}

\,{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\kappa-\frac{1}{2};\kappa,2\kappa-1;\frac{|\eta|\sqrt[3]{21}}{2^{2/3}}\right)-1<c\,_{1}F_{2}\left(\kappa-\frac{1}{2};\kappa,2\kappa-1;|\eta|\right)\leq 2c.

Then, the function
\mathtt{F_{3}}(\kappa,\eta,z)
is lemniscate starlike.

Proof.

If we denote

a_{k}=\frac{(-\eta)^{k-1}}{4^{\,k-1}(k-1)!\,(\kappa)_{k-1}},

then

\displaystyle\mathtt{F_{3}}(\kappa,\eta,z):=z\mathtt{U}_{p}(z)=z+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}.

In order to prove the result by applying

3.1

14

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{|\eta|^{k}}{4^{k}k!|(\kappa)_{k}|}\frac{|\eta|^{\,n-k}}{4^{\,n-k}(n-k)!|(\kappa)_{n-k}|}<1,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}-c\right)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{|\eta|^{k}}{4^{k}k!|(\kappa)_{k}|}\frac{|\eta|^{\,n-k}}{4^{\,n-k}(n-k)!|(\kappa)_{n-k}|}<c.

Consider first the sum on the left-hand side of (

Since

Applying

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\eta|^{n}}{4^{n}}\frac{2^{2n}\left(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}{\left(2\kappa-1\right)_{n}\left(\kappa\right)_{n}n!}

Therefore, the inequality (

Next, consider the sum in (

Proceeding as before, we have

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{n}{3}}-c\right)\frac{|\eta|^{n}}{4^{n}}\frac{2^{2n}\left(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{n}}{\left(2\kappa-1\right)_{n}\left(\kappa\right)_{n}n!}

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{2}\!\left(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2};\,2\kappa-1,\,\kappa;\,\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{3}}|\eta|\right)-1<c\;{}_{1}F_{2}\!\left(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2};\,2\kappa-1,\,\kappa;\,|\eta|\right).

Thus, both hypotheses of

are satisfied for

whenever condition (

S_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\Gamma(n+3/2)\,\Gamma(n+\nu+3/2)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu+1},

which is entire. The modified Struve function is given as

L_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+3/2)\,\Gamma(n+\nu+3/2)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu+1}.

Struve functions have gone through with several generalization

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\nu,b,\eta}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\eta)^{n}}{\Gamma(n+3/2)\,\Gamma\left(n+\nu+\tfrac{b+2}{2}\right)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+\nu+1}.

The function
\mathcal{W}_{\nu,b,\eta}(z)
is the solution of the differential equation

(49)

\displaystyle z^{2}w^{\prime\prime}(z)+bzw^{\prime}(z)+(\eta z^{2}-\nu^{2}+(1-b)\nu)w(z)=\frac{4z^{\nu+1}}{2^{\nu+1}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\nu+b/2\right)}.

For inclusion in geometric function theory, both

\displaystyle\mathtt{W}_{\nu,b,\eta}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{\nu}\sqrt{\pi}z^{-\nu}\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{b+2}{2}\right)\mathcal{W}_{\nu,b,\eta}(\sqrt{z})=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tfrac{(-\eta)^{n-1}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{n-1}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{n-1}4^{n-1}}z^{n},

and

(51)

\displaystyle=2^{\nu}\sqrt{\pi}z^{-\nu}\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{b+2}{2}\right)\mathcal{U}_{\nu,b,\eta}(\sqrt{z})=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tfrac{(-\eta)^{n-1}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{n-1}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{a(n-1)}4^{n-1}}z^{n}

here

Applying identity (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tfrac{|\eta|^{k}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{k}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{k}4^{k}}\tfrac{|\eta|^{n-k}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{n-k}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{n-k}4^{n-k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{|\eta|^{n}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{n}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{n}4^{n}}\right)^{2}-1

\displaystyle=\left[\,{}_{1}F_{2}\!\left(1;\tfrac{3}{2},\ \kappa_{s};\;\frac{|\eta|}{4}\right)\right]^{2}-1.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac{21}{4}\right)^{n/3}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tfrac{|\eta|^{k}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{k}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{k}4^{k}}\tfrac{|\eta|^{n-k}}{\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)_{n-k}\,\left(\kappa_{s}\right)_{n-k}4^{n-k}}

\displaystyle=\left[\,{}_{1}F_{2}\!\left(1;\tfrac{3}{2},\ \kappa_{s};\;\frac{\sqrt[3]{21}|\eta|}{4\;\sqrt[3]{4}}\right)\right]^{2}-1.

4.6.
Example of Error functions

The
error function
, denoted by
\operatorname{erf}(z)
, is an important transcendental function that arises in diverse areas such as diffusion theory, statistics, and heat conduction. It is defined by the integral representation.

(53)

\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

This function is entire and odd, satisfying
\operatorname{erf}(-z)=-\operatorname{erf}(z)
. The complementary error function is given by
\operatorname{erfc}(z)=1-\operatorname{erf}(z)
. Both
\operatorname{erf}(z)
and
\operatorname{erfc}(z)
play essential roles in probability and mathematical physics, particularly in the formulation of the Gaussian integral and cumulative distribution functions of the normal law.

By expanding
e^{-t^{2}}
in its Maclaurin series and integrating term by term, one obtains the series representation

(54)

\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}z^{2n+1}}{n!\,(2n+1)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(z-\frac{z^{3}}{3}+\frac{z^{5}}{10}-\frac{z^{7}}{42}+\cdots\right),

which converges absolutely for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. This confirms that
\operatorname{erf}(z)
is an entire function of order
1
and type
2
. Its derivative is given by

\operatorname{erf}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-z^{2}},

which shows that
\operatorname{erf}(z)
is monotonic and bounded on the real line, with
\operatorname{erf}(z)\to 1
as
z\to+\infty
and
\operatorname{erf}(z)\to-1
as
z\to-\infty
.

In the context of geometric function theory, we consider the following normalization:

(55)

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{5}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{z}\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{z})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}z^{n}}{(n-1)!\,(2n-1)}.

Now, for
\mathtt{F}_{5}
, the right-hand side of hypothesis (
14
) is equivalent to

(56)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(2k+1)}\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,(2(n-k)+1)}.

Use of the identity (
31
) gives

(57)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(2k+1)}\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,(2(n-k)+1)}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!\,(2n+1)}\right)^{2}-1=1.13935>1.

Thus,
Theorem

3.1
is not applicable on
\mathtt{F}_{5}
.

Recall,
\mathfrak{H}\ast\mathfrak{K}
, the well-known Hadamard product of two analytic functions

\mathfrak{H}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
and
\mathfrak{K}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}z^{k}
. Then

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{H}\ast\mathfrak{K})(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}b_{k}z^{k}.

Let us now consider the function
\mathtt{F}_{6}(z)=\mathtt{F}_{5}(z)\ast(e^{z}-1)
. Clearly,

(58)

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{6}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}z^{n}}{(n-1)!\,(2n-1)n!}.

In this case, the numerical sum indicates that
the right-hand side of hypothesis (
14
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\,(2k+1)(k+1)!}\frac{1}{(n-k)!\,(2(n-k)+1)(n-k+1)!}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!\,(2n+1)(n+1)!}\right)^{2}-1=0.402721.

Similarly, for
c=1
, the right-hand side of hypothesis (
15
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{21^{\tfrac{k}{3}}}{4^{\tfrac{k}{3}}\,k!\,(2k+1)(k+1)!}\frac{21^{\tfrac{n-k}{3}}}{4^{\tfrac{n-k}{3}}\,(n-k)!\,(2(n-k)+1)(n-k+1)!}

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{21^{\tfrac{n}{3}}}{4^{\tfrac{n}{3}}\,n!\,(2n+1)(n+1)!}\right)^{2}=0.407896.

Hence, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.8
.

The function
\mathtt{F}_{6}(z)\in S^{\ast}(q_{1})
. In general,
\mathtt{F}_{6}(z)\in S^{\ast}(q_{c})
for
c_{0}\approx 0.577889<c\leq 1
. Here,
c_{0}
is the root of the equation

\,{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2};\frac{3}{2},2;\frac{\sqrt[3]{21}}{2^{2/3}}\right){}^{2}-c\,_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2};\frac{3}{2},2;1\right){}^{2}=1.
