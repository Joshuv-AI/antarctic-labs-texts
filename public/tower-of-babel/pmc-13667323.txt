Calcified Plaque Detachment: Alternative Calcified Plaque Modification After Coronary Intravascular Lithotripsy
Authors: Daisuke Naito, Akiko Matsuo, Takeru Kasahara, Keisuke Ota, Shinsaku Matsunaga, Norio Nakajima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCalcified plaque (CP) detachment (CD) and calcium fracture (CF) can occur following coronary intravascular lithotripsy (IVL). The extent to which CF alone facilitates stent expansion during CP modification with IVL remains unclear.AimsThis study aimed to investigate the clinical implications of IVL‐induced CD.MethodsOptical coherence tomography was performed to assess coronary CP immediately after IVL in 45 consecutive patients for whom images were available. CD was defined as partial or complete separation of the CP from either the coronary artery intima or media. The study population was divided into CD and non‐CD groups.ResultsCD was observed in 22 patients (49%) without associated major dissection, intramural hematoma, or slow flow, whereas CF was observed in 35 patients (78%). No significant differences were found in baseline characteristics between the groups. The stent expansion index was not significantly different between the groups (77.6% vs. 83.2%, p = 0.17). Across increasing calcium arc grades, CD frequency decreased significantly (p = 0.01), whereas CF frequency increased (p = 0.02).ConclusionsCD frequently occurs in patients undergoing coronary IVL, with CP separation from the vessel wall proceeding safely and without coronary flow impairment. In addition to CF, CD may contribute to CP modification, facilitating lumen enlargement and successful stent deployment.

Introduction

Coronary artery calcification (CAC) remains a challenge in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), although the evolving technology of coronary stents, including drug‐eluting stents, has drastically reduced the incidence of in‐stent restenosis. Severe CAC is closely associated with procedural failures, such as coronary dissection, perforation, and incomplete stent expansion, which can cause late adverse events such as in‐stent restenosis and stent thrombosis [1]. Modern technological devices dedicated to modifying severe CAC, such as rotational atherectomy (RA) and orbital atherectomy (OA), have facilitated stent delivery and expansion. However, procedure‐related complications, including in‐hospital death, cardiac tamponade, periprocedural myocardial infarction, and emergency surgery, are more frequently observed with the use of these devices than with conventional procedures [2, 3].

Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has been recently introduced; this technique employs a novel balloon‐based device that generates acoustic pressure waves to disrupt calcification and improve vascular compliance. Unlike RA and OA, IVL is independent of wire bias and can modify both superficial and deep calcifications. It has a lower rate of serious complications (such as perforation and slow flow) than atherectomy because the device is balloon‐based [4, 5, 6, 7]. IVL enhances vascular compliance in invasive treatment for CAC by creating calcium fractures (CF), facilitating adequate stent expansion without causing major coronary artery dissection (CAD) [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Conventional balloon angioplasty often requires high‐pressure inflation, whereas RA and OA require aggressive ablation, both of which are likely to cause critical complications. CF creation serves as a cornerstone of calcified plaque (CP) modification by IVL and can be identified using optical coherence tomography (OCT) [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. OCT also often reveals CP separation from the vessel wall without causing lumen narrowing immediately after IVL, differing from complications typically observed after conventional balloon angioplasty, such as CAD [10]. This study highlights this phenomenon—CP detachment (CD)—and its clinical implications and relationship with CF in stent expansion.

Methods

Study Population

This single‐center prospective observational study included consecutive patients who underwent OCT‐guided PCI for de novo calcified and stenotic coronary arteries at Kyoto City Hospital in Kyoto, Japan, between July 2023 and August 2025. Eligible patients met the criteria of both the Shockwave C2 Coronary IVL system (Shockwave Medical, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and the Japanese Association of Cardiovascular Intervention and Therapeutics treatment guidelines [12]. Patients with acute coronary syndrome, total occlusion, hemodynamic instability, or severe renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), except for those receiving renal replacement therapy, and patients in whom adequate OCT images could not be obtained were excluded. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants and was approved by the Kyoto City Hospital Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 780). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study Procedure

PCI was performed via the radial or femoral approach using a 6‐Fr or 7‐Fr guiding catheter. Intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time > 200 s. Following coronary angiography and confirmation of moderate or greater calcification, an OCT catheter (Dragonfly, Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was advanced distally to the target segment. After an intracoronary bolus injection of isosorbide dinitrate, pre‐procedural OCT images (pre‐PCI OCT) were acquired using the ILUMIEN OPTIS system (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at a motorized pullback speed of 36 mm/s over 75 mm (OPTIS™, 180 frames/s), with contrast media infused using either an injector pump or forceful hand injection. In cases where the OCT catheter could not cross the lesion, pre‐dilatation with small balloons (diameter: 1.5–2.0 mm) or guide catheter extension was employed at the operator's discretion.

The IVL balloon catheter (Shockwave C2 Coronary IVL Catheter; Shockwave Medical Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), which contained multiple lithotripsy emitters enclosed within an integrated balloon, was advanced over a standard coronary guidewire to the target lesion and connected to an IVL generator (Shockwave Medical Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a dedicated connector cable. The IVL balloon size was selected at a 1:1 ratio to the reference vessel diameter, in accordance with current recommendations [7]. After positioning the IVL balloon at the target lesion, inflation was initiated at 4 atm. Each treatment cycle consisted of ten pulses, after which the balloon was inflated to 6 atm. This process was repeated until complete balloon expansion was achieved, and the entire lesion length was treated accordingly. When the lesion preparation remained insufficient after delivering a maximum of 80 pulses per catheter, an additional IVL balloon was used. Post‐IVL OCT was performed. Second‐generation drug‐eluting stents were deployed and optimized using noncompliant balloon dilation to ensure full stent expansion, whereby post‐dilation facilitates CF displacement and widening. This approach is recommended in the Disrupt CAD study [7]. The final OCT scan (post‐PCI) was performed at the end of the PCI procedure.

OCT Image Analysis

OCT images were acquired using the ILUMIEN OPTIS system before pre‐PCI, post‐IVL, and post‐PCI, as previously described. Cross‐sectional OCT images were analyzed at 1‐mm intervals using Ultreon 2.0 (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA). In pre‐PCI OCT analysis, the minimum lumen (MLA) and mean reference lumen areas (RLA) were assessed. The reference site was identified as the location with the largest luminal area, either proximal or distal to the stenotic lesion. The mean reference lumen diameter was calculated using the formula: √(average of the proximal and distal reference areas/π) × 2. The percentage of the stenotic area (%) was defined as follows: [(mean RLA − MLA)/mean RLA] × 100. The ratio of the IVL balloon diameter to the mean reference diameter (IVL/R) was calculated to determine the effect of the expansion force.

OCT‐derived calcifications are defined as signal‐poor or heterogeneous regions with sharply delineated borders. An eruptive calcified nodule was identified as a CP protruding into the vessel lumen with a fibrous cap disruption. Superficial calcification was defined as calcification with a leading edge located within 500 µm of the lumen surface [13, 14]. Maximum calcium thickness was defined as the largest distance between the luminal edge and the outer border of the calcium deposit within the lesion. The maximum calcium arc per lesion and the total CP length were recorded. The calcium score of the target lesion was calculated for each lesion based on the maximum angle, thickness, and length of the CP, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 and higher scores indicating greater calcification severity [15].

CD, OCT‐derived major CAD, intramural hematoma, and CF were evaluated in post‐IVL OCT analysis. CD was defined as the separation of the CP, either at the intima or media, either partially or completely, without involvement of non‐CP or normal vessel layers. This separation delineated the contour of the CP and created a gap between it and the adjacent non‐CP components of the media or intima without connecting to a false lumen or causing lumen narrowing (Figure 1). Major CAD was defined as a dissection with an extensive lateral (> 60°) and longitudinal (> 2 mm) extension that involved deeper vessel layers (media or adventitia) and resulted in lumen narrowing in either the CP or non‐CP segments. An intramural hematoma was identified by a double‐lumen appearance with a glistening intimomedial membrane separating the false and true lumens [16]. CF was defined as a gap created within the calcified region [14]. In the post‐PCI OCT analysis, the minimum stent diameter (MSA) and the maximum and minimum stent diameters were measured for each segment. Stent circularity was evaluated using the eccentricity index, which was calculated as the ratio of the minimum to maximum stent diameter [16]. The stent expansion index was calculated as the MSA divided by the average of the proximal and distal reference luminal areas [17].

Angiographic Analysis

Angiographic images were acquired in multiple projections to optimize the visualization of all coronary segments. Target lesion calcification was graded based on angiographic findings. Moderate calcification was defined as radiopacities visible only during cardiac motion before contrast injection, while severe calcification was defined as visible radiopacities without cardiac motion before contrast injection, typically appearing on both sides of the arterial lumen. Angiographic dissections after IVL were classified as A to F according to the criteria established by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute; dissections classified as C to F were defined as significant CAD [18]. Slow or no flow was defined as Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 2 flow with delayed distal vessel filling (≥ 3 cardiac cycles) or a mean TIMI frame count > 50 frames [19].

Statistical Analysis

The study population was divided into CD and non‐CD groups after IVL. Culprit lesions were classified into three grades of CP severity according to the maximum continuous circumferential arc of calcium derived from OCT: 181°–270°, 271°–359°, and 360° [20]. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables are expressed as median and interquartile range for non‐normally distributed data and as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data. Comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and unpaired t‐test, respectively. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman rank correlation coefficients. The Kendall rank correlation coefficient was applied to assess the relationship between CP arc eccentricity and CD or CF occurrence. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

OCT Analysis

The comparative OCT findings between the CD and non‐CD groups are presented in Table 3. All pre‐procedural parameters were statistically similar, including minimal lumen area at calcium and maximum calcium thickness (1.59 vs. 1.60 mm2; 1297.3 vs. 1245.2 μm, respectively), except for calcium arc, which tended to be smaller in the CD group than in the non‐CD group (271.8° vs. 311.4°, p = 0.02). CF occurred at a similarly high frequency in both groups (16 (72.7%) vs. 19 (82.6%), p = 0.43). Post‐IVL MLA values did not differ significantly between groups. Relationship Between CD and CF

CF was observed in 35/45 patients (77.8%), whereas CD was identified in 22 (48.9%), following IVL. Four patients exhibited neither CF nor CD: two had eruptive calcified nodules, and one exhibited a signal‐free shadow behind the CP, hindering CD identification. The numbers of CF and CD are shown in Table 2; neither of these correlated with MSA or stent expansion rate at maximum calcium. No significant relationship was found between CD and CF (p = 0.43). CD occurrence significantly decreased with increasing circumferential arc‐dependent CP severity, whereas CF occurrence showed a significant positive correlation (Figure 2).

Discussion

The present study found that CD was identified using OCT immediately after IVL in approximately half of the study population. CD did not result in lumen narrowing or impaired coronary flow; with increasing calcium arc–dependent CP severity, CD occurrence significantly decreased, whereas CF occurrence significantly increased.

Presumed Mechanism of CP Detachment

CF has often received attention when IVL is used to modify CP in PCI. CD was identified and highlighted as an overlooked OCT finding after IVL; this provides a perspective distinct from coronary dissection caused by conventional high‐pressure balloon dilatation or coronary atherectomy, such as in RA or OA, as lumen narrowing or impeded coronary flow did not occur. CD represents a phenomenon in which the CP becomes cleanly detached from the fibrous tissues of the coronary plaque, forming gaps between the CP and non‐CP that restore coronary flow (Figure 3).

(b) Schematic cross‐section showing CD creation around the CP. Multiple IVL mechanisms such as compression, spalling, superfocusing, shearing, and squeezing stress have been reported [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These fracturing effects occur when shock waves encounter tissues with different acoustic impedances [4]. Recent studies have emphasized the role of calcification adjacent to fibrous tissues in plaque biomechanics. A calcium plate within the plaque can cause stress concentrations that amplify the stress near the calcium plate‐fibrous tissue insertion owing to the large impedance difference. When the IVL fractures the calcium plate, shock waves travel through the CP and may cause debonding at the calcium‐noncalcium plate junction [21]. In this study, the incidence of CD had an inverse relation with the degree of calcium. This suggests that in CPs with smaller circumferential arcs, shockwaves have a greater impact on the interface between the CP and fibrous tissue rather than the CP itself, promoting debonding and gap formation following subsequent low‐pressure balloon dilation. This process may allow sufficient dilation of the IVL balloon without CF. Indeed, Gupta et al. reported similar phenomena as base fracture with eccentric CPs [10]. It is considered that base fractures result in CDs in eccentric CPs. Meanwhile, in CPs with larger circumferential arcs, shockwaves may travel through CPs and act mainly on the boundary between blood and CPs, i.e., the surface of CP.

Clinical Implications of CD

CFs increased in proportion to the grade of CP arc severity, consistent with previous findings [20]. In contrast, CDs increased as CP arc grade decreased, although CD frequency did not show a statistically significant relationship with CFs. These observations suggest that CD may complement CF in widening the lumen during CAC treatment (Figure 4). Other studies have also reported that the presence of CF did not affect MSA, supporting our hypothesis [7, 10].

Calcified fractures (CFs) increase with the severity of the calcified plaque (CP) arcs, whereas CP detachment (CDs) is more common in plaques with eccentric arcs. Larger circumferential CPs are ideal targets for IVL to effectively generate CFs. However, when IVL is applied to smaller circumferential CPs, CDs create gaps between CPs and noncalcified tissue, which may sufficiently widen the stenotic lumen to allow adequate coronary stent expansion, even without CFs. Regarding the outcome of this study, MSA and stent expansion rates were lower owing to the smaller mean reference diameter and BMI compared with those in other studies, although the CF rate was comparable [7, 9]. Additionally, these vessels with small diameters might have led the operators to prefer relatively small‐diameter IVL balloons or high‐pressure balloons. CDs involve debonding between different tissue types (calcification plates and fibrous tissues) induced by shock waves, rather than by barotraumatic forces. This mechanism aligns with the findings that CDs were not accompanied by major dissection, intramural hematoma, or slow/no flow. Conversely, devices such as cutting or scoring balloons, which rely on high‐pressure inflation to create CFs, and atherectomy tools such as RA and OA frequently induce barotrauma‐related dissection, intramural hematoma, and distal embolism, which are strongly associated with no/slow flow complications [2, 3].

Unlike IVL, RA and OA require specialized operator skills, and even skilled handling cannot eliminate the wire bias. IVL may serve as a potential alternative to RA or OA for CP modification to facilitate stent delivery and expansion. However, long‐term outcomes following IVL remain uncertain, and no direct comparisons between IVL and atheroablation devices have been reported to date. Further investigations are warranted to determine the long‐term clinical benefits of IVL. After obtaining robust evidence that CP modification by IVL, including CD and CF, safely maintains the lumen and restores coronary flow, IVL followed by a drug‐coating balloon could be a future stent‐free strategy for CP.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the analysis was conducted at a single center with a small number of patients. Additionally, no control group was included, and neither CP severity nor morphology could be adjusted. Therefore, larger multicenter prospective studies are required to validate our findings. Direct comparisons between IVL, conventional balloon angioplasty, RA, and OA under identical conditions were also not conducted, and reproducing CD under coronary flow and left ventricular wall motion after IVL remains difficult, both in vivo and in vitro. Additionally, a signal‐free shadow behind the CP in OCT can interfere with CD identification. Also, given the three‐dimensional structures of CF and CD, it is difficult to accurately count their number on the two‐dimensional plane of OCT cross‐section images. Finally, although favorable acute outcomes were demonstrated, the study did not evaluate long‐term results.

Conclusions

CD and CF were identified using OCT imaging after IVL. CD involves clean debonding between the CP and non‐CP components without impairing coronary flow. CD frequency decreased in proportion to CP arc severity, whereas CF frequency increased. CD may safely contribute to lumen expansion and stent deployment similar to CF.
