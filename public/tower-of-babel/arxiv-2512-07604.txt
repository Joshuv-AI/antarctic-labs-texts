Diverse stages of star formation in the IRAS 18162-2048 region
R. Fedriani, G. Anglada, A. Caratti o Garatti, J.F. Gómez, J. Masqué, M. Osorio, B. Stecklum, A.R. Rodríguez-Kamenetzki, R. Galván-Madrid, C. Carrasco-González, G. Blázquez-Calero, A.F. Placinta-Mitrea, A. Sanna, R. Cesaroni, L. Moscadelli, T.P. Ray, D. Coffey, G.A. Fuller

Abstract
Context. High-mass star formation remains a major open problem in astrophysics, particularly regarding the transition between deeply embedded protostars and the onset of ionising radiation capable of producing photodissociation regions (PDRs) and compact H ii regions. Aims. We aim to characterise the excitation and ionisation conditions of the high-mass star-forming region IRAS 18162 − - 2048, where the parsec-scale jet HH80 − - 81 lies. Methods. We obtained adaptive optics–assisted integral field spectroscopy in the near-infrared (IR) K K band ( 1.93 − 2.47 ​ μ ​ m 1.93-2.47\penalty\ \mathrm{\mu m} ) with VLT/SINFONI, complemented by VLA X and C bands (3 − - 6 cm) and ALMA band 3 ( ∼ \sim 3.3 mm) observations. We analysed the continuum and line emission to derive visual extinction and excitation conditions, and kinematics of the gas of the region. Results. The near-IR continuum reveals two infrared sources, IRS 2 and IRS 7, while the main protostellar core IRAS 18162-2048 remains undetected up to 2.47 ​ μ ​ m 2.47\penalty\ \mathrm{\mu m} . IRS 7 shows a peculiar Hydrogen Recombination Line (HRL) Br γ \gamma profile with a narrow emission component superimposed on a broad absorption feature, consistent with a B2/B3 zero-age main-sequence (ZAMS) star. Extended H 2 emission exhibits a ‘sawtooth’ pattern in the excitation diagram, characteristic of UV radiation in a PDR rather than shock excitation. The radiative transfer model Cloudy reproduces the H 2 ro-vibrational populations for T gas = 600 T_{\mathrm{gas}}=600 K and n H = 7.9 × 10 3 ​ cm − 3 n_{\mathrm{H}}=7.9\times 10^{3}\penalty\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} . VLA X and C bands observations reveal a compact radio source previously reported as a ‘stationary condensation’ (SC) and coincident with IRS 7. For the first time, we detect IRS 7/SC in mm wavelengths. The spectral index in the 3 − - 6 cm and 3.3 mm regime is consistent with optically thin free–free emission. Conclusions. Our near-IR and radio observations reveal that IRS 7/SC is a B2/B3 ZAMS star that has begun to photoionise its environment, giving rise to an extended PDR and a compact H ii region. The coexistence of this source with the deeply embedded protostar IRAS 18162-2048 and other bubble-like structures in the field, suggests a multigenerational star-forming environment. Future James Webb Space Telescope observations targeting the H 2 pure rotational lines ( 3 − 28 ​ μ ​ m 3-28\penalty\ \mathrm{\mu m} ) and other HRLs less affected by extinction will be essential to characterise the cooler molecular and ionised gas to fully disclose the formation history of the region.



Emergence of UV Feedback

1
Introduction

The formation of low-mass (
M_{*}<2\penalty\ M_{\odot}
) and high-mass (
M_{*}>8\penalty\ M_{\odot}
;
L_{\mathrm{bol}}>10^{3}\penalty\ L_{\odot}
) stars has traditionally been studied along separate pathways
(
11
;
38
;
76
, e.g.,)
. Yet it is increasingly clear that stars of different masses can form together, evolve on different timescales, and interact dynamically with their common environment
(see
12
, for a review)
. In such clustered settings, massive (proto)stars strongly influence their surroundings through a variety of feedback processes, from powerful jets, winds, and outflows, to intense UV stellar radiation that rapidly produces compact H
II
regions. Unlike their low-mass counterparts, massive protostars reach the zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) while still accreting
(
61
)
, irradiating and shaping their natal cloud even during their earliest growth phases. At the same time, several characteristics of star formation appear to scale smoothly across the mass spectrum, such as the ubiquity of accretion disks
(
9
;
2
)
and associated collimated jets and outflows
(
7
;
4
;
65
)
.

Feedback from massive stars can disrupt local gas reservoirs but also has the potential to induce or accelerate subsequent star formation. Theoretical work and observations over the past decades have pointed toward feedback-driven triggering in bright-rimmed clouds, expanding H
II
regions, and wind-blown bubbles
(
47
;
74
;
31
, e.g.,)
. In addition, protostellar jets and molecular outflows themselves may compress and destabilize dense gas, seeding the next generation of young stars
(
39
;
59
, e.g.,)
. Understanding the interplay between accretion, feedback, and clustered formation, especially in high-mass protostellar regions, therefore remains central to building a complete theory of star formation.

The high-mass star-forming region IRAS 18162-2048 (
d\sim 1.4
kpc,
L_{\mathrm{bol}}\sim 10^{4}\penalty\ L_{\odot}
;
3
) offers a unique opportunity to investigate these processes in action. Its central main protostar, IRAS 18162-2048, is a deeply embedded,
20-30\penalty\ M_{\odot}
protostar driving the spectacular
\sim 15
pc (in projection) HH 80–81 multiwavelength jet
(
52
;
20
;
54
;
8
;
55
)
. The surrounding cluster contains numerous young stellar objects (YSOs)
with circumstellar masses (most likely tracing disks) between
0.003
and
\sim 5\penalty\ M_{\odot}
identified in high-resolution ALMA band 6 observations
(
16
;
3
)
. Mid-infrared imaging (
8
–
13\penalty\ \mu
m) also revealed embedded sources such as IRS 2 and IRS 7 which may be more evolved sources
(
72
)
. The region therefore presents a hint of multigenerational star formation, where massive protostellar accretion, disk/jet activity, and emerging UV-driven photoionisation may coexist within a compact environment.

Despite the wealth of existing observations, the region IRAS 18162-2048 continues to pose fundamental questions. The origin of the bright H
2
emission, the nature of compact cm-mm sources in the vicinity of the main protostar, and the degree to which newly formed massive stars irradiate and reshape their environment remain unclear. Are these signatures solely the result of shock activity from ongoing accretion and outflows, or do they instead mark the ignition of young OB-type stars and the development of compact H
II
regions and PDRs? To address these questions, we combine near-infreared (IR) integral field spectroscopy with radio observations to map the molecular and ionised gas to identify emission mechanisms. The paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
we detail the observations taken and the data reduction procedures, in Section
3
we present our results, while in Section
4

5

3.2
The Hydrogen Recombination Line Brackett Gamma

Line profiles can provide key diagnostics of the physical mechanisms at work in YSOs. For example, inverse P-Cygni profiles (i.e., strong red-shifted continuum absorption) are associated with infalling material in molecuar emission at large scales
(
84
)
, whereas P-Cygni profiles (i.e., strong blue-shifted continuum absorption) are linked to strong ejection activity for ionised winds close to the central sources
(
57
)
. The Br
\gamma
line in YSOs has been observed to trace both jets close to the protostar
(
19
)
and accretion processes within circumstellar disks
(
40
)
. A prominent P-Cygni profile was observed in the high-mass YSO IRAS 13481
-
6124
(
33
)
, where the line traces powerful jets close to the massive protostar. In our case, we detect Br
\gamma
emission only at the position of IRS 7, showing a peculiar line profile. The line exhibits a clear emission core superimposed on a broad absorption feature (see Fig.
3
). We fitted a double Gaussian profile to the Br
\gamma
line, accounting for both the emission and absorption components. The emission component is unresolved at our spectral resolution of
\sim 75\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
and peaks at
\sim 0\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
in the local standard of rest. These two facts suggest that it could be tracing an H
ii
region; while the absorption feature displays a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of
\sim 750\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
. Considering only the absorption component, we estimate an equivalent width (EW) of
2.2\pm 0.2\penalty\ \AA
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first detection of an HRL in the region of IRAS 18162-2048.

The observed line profile is characteristic of an OB-type zero-age main-sequence (ZAMS) star.
13
conducted a high spectral resolution (
\mathcal{R}\sim 10000
, corresponding to
\sim 30\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
) survey of deeply embedded young massive star candidates and established that O- and B-type stars can be empirically distinguished by their Br
\gamma
equivalent width, with a boundary at
EW=5\penalty\ \AA
, and by the presence or not of a number of emission lines. Although our EW measurement could nominally place IRS 7 in the O-type regime by the classification scheme of
13
, this value may be affected by veiling, which tends to reduce the apparent depth of absorption features. We can confidently rule out an O-type classification for IRS 7, as we do not detect the characteristic C
iv
lines at 2.069, 2.078, and 2.083
\mathrm{\mu m}
, nor the He
i
lines at 2.058, 2.112, and 2.113
\mathrm{\mu m}
, the N
iii
line at 2.115
\mathrm{\mu m}
, or the He
ii
line at 2.185
\mathrm{\mu m}
, all of which were found in the spectra of O-type sources by
13
. We therefore classify IRS 7 as a B2/B3 ZAMS star
(see
13
, and references therein for details on the classification scheme)
. This is later confirmed by our radio observations (see Sect.
3.5
).

We interpret the broad absorption component as originating from the stellar photosphere
(
13
, found full width at zero intensity of
800\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
for the Br
\gamma
for other sources in their survey)
, while the narrow unresolved emission core most likely traces ionised gas associated with an incipient H
ii
region surrounding IRS 7. This interpretation is consistent with the presence of extended continuum and H
2
emission in the vicinity, which together indicate that IRS 7 is already influencing its immediate environment through both radiative and mechanical feedback.

3.3
Extinction and Excitation conditions

Line ratios involving transitions that originate from the same upper energy level can be used to estimate the extinction along the line of sight
(
58
, e.g.,)
. Ideally, these lines should be widely separated in wavelength to maximise sensitivity to reddening. However, our spectral setup covers only the
K
band, from 1.93 to 2.47
\mathrm{\mu m}
. To estimate the visual extinction,
A_{V}
, we use the H
2

1-0
Q(3)/
1-0
S(1) line ratio, which offers the highest S/N among the available line pairs. We follow the same approach described by
25
, but see also
83
, adopting the near-IR extinction law from
67
.

Since our data provide spectra for every pixel, we can construct a spatially resolved
A_{V}
map. Figure
4
(left panel) shows the resulting pixel-by-pixel extinction distribution. For instance, at the position of IRS 2, the
1-0
Q(3) line is not detected with sufficient S/N, leading to unreliable extinction estimates in that region, and therefore has been masked (gray region in the figure). Across the main H
2
bow-shaped structure, the average extinction is
\sim 12
mag, consistent with the value of
12.4
mag reported by
72
using an independent method.

The H
2
line ratio
1-0
S(1)/
2-1
S(1) provides insights into the excitation mechanism of the emitting gas (Fig.
4
middle panel). For shock excitation, this ratio is expected to exceed
\sim 10
, whereas for purely UV-pumped emission it is typically
\sim 1.7

(
14
)
. After correcting the fluxes for extinction using our derived
A_{V}
values, we obtain an average ratio of
\sim 5.5
.
This clearly indicates that the H
2
emission in the region is inconsistent with purely shock-driven thermal excitation and instead points to UV fluorescence as the dominant process (see Sect.
3.4
). However,
15
showed that in dense PDRs, collisional processes can alter the level populations, leading to deviations from those expected for purely UV-excited gas. Therefore, we are probably observing an intermediate case between UV excited and shocked H
2
.

Velocity maps provide key insight into the kinematics associated with a given emitting species. The right panel of Figure
4
presents the radial velocity map of the H
2

1-0
S(1) line with respect to the systemic velocity of the region, which is the strongest and best isolated transition in our dataset. We used the Python package
bettermoments

(
77
)
to fit Gaussian profiles to the line emission across the field, considering only pixels with S/N
>3
. The average radial velocity within the main emitting region is
\sim 1\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
, indicating negligible bulk motion along the line of sight. Such low velocities are consistent with two scenarios: (i) a potential outflow oriented nearly in the plane of the sky, resulting in minimal radial velocity components, or (ii) PDR-excited H
2
emission. In shock–driven H
2
emission associated with protostellar jets, velocity shifts of tens to hundreds of
\mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}
are typically observed
(
35
, e.g.)
and evidence of blue- and red-shifted components
(
34
, e.g.)
. In contrast, PDR–excited H
2
usually exhibits very small velocity gradients close to the systemic velocity
(
15
;
51
)
. Combined with the excitation analysis presented earlier, this velocity structure strongly supports a PDR origin for the observed H
2
emission rather than shock–driven outflow activity (see also Sect.
3.4
).

Together, the extinction, excitation, and velocity maps analyses provide a consistent picture in which UV radiation from nearby massive (proto)stars plays a key role in shaping the observed near-IR emission.

3.4
H

Ro-vibrational Diagrams

The combined effects of UV radiation and collisional excitation produce two limiting regimes for the ro-vibrational level populations. In dense and/or hot environments, such as shocked gas in protostellar outflows, frequent collisions drive the level populations toward a thermal (Boltzmann) distribution, resulting in ro-vibrational diagrams that follow a straight line in a logarithmic excitation plot
(
17
, e.g.,)
. In contrast, low-density gas exposed to intense UV radiation exhibits a distinct distribution, with populations that do not decrease monotonically with excitation energy. In excitation diagrams, this manifests as a characteristic ‘sawtooth’ pattern, which serves as a diagnostic signature of UV-pumped H
2
emission in PDRs
(
14
;
45
)
. Such diagrams allow a clear distinction between collisional and radiative excitation mechanisms and provide constraints on both the gas temperature and the ortho-to-para ratio (OPR).

We construct a ro-vibrational diagram using the dereddened fluxes from Table
1
, adopting
A_{V}=12
mag. To model the emission, we fit a two-temperature component while leaving the OPR as a free parameter. Figure
5
shows the resulting ro-vibrational diagram for the observed H
2
lines, together with the fit obtained using the PDR Toolbox
(
62
)
. The ‘sawtooth’ pattern characteristic of PDRs is clearly visible, confirming the dominance of UV pumping in the emission, instead of a near-linear thermal distribution. The best-fit model yields a cold component with
T_{\mathrm{cold}}\sim 400
K, a hot component with
T_{\mathrm{hot}}\sim 3000
K, a total H
2
column density
N(\mathrm{H}_{2})\sim 2\times 10^{23}\penalty\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}
, and an OPR
\sim 2.75
.

The derived hot-component temperature is higher than typically observed in protostellar outflow knots, where values of
T_{\mathrm{hot}}\sim 2000
K are more common, and the ro-vibrational diagrams usually follow a near-linear thermal distribution
(
18
;
25
)
. This further supports the conclusion that UV-pumping is the dominant excitation mechanism in the H
2
emission for which
T_{\mathrm{cold}}
is a better tracer of the kinetic temperature of the gas. The fitted OPR is close to the local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) value of
\sim 3
, consistent with the expected rotational levels in dense gas exposed to strong UV fields
(
73
)
.

4.1
IRS 7: A B2/B3 Zero Age Main Sequence Star Powering an H
ii
and a PhotoDissociation Region

Our results strongly indicate the presence of sources of UV photons within the region of IRAS 18162-2048. The Br
\gamma

(
13

. In addition, the presence of a PDR is revealed by the H

1-0

2-1

4

2.47\penalty\ \mathrm{\mu m}
(Fig.

IRAS 18162-2048 has been extensively observed at mm and cm wavelengths. The optical component was first identified by
43

68
detected free-free emission in the region. Later,
52
presented high-resolution VLA observations at 6 cm, revealing for the first time what
53

1000\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}}

(

8

For the first time, IRS 7/SC has been detected in the millimetre regime using our ALMA
\sim 3.3

\alpha=0.19\pm 0.27

Specifically, the absence of measurable proper motions, the compact morphology, the measured spectral index, the Br
\gamma

4.3
Multigenerational star formation in IRAS 18162-2048

The high-mass star-forming region IRAS 18162-2048 presents convincing evidence for multigenerational star formation, with young protostars, compact H
ii
regions, and evolved feedback-driven structures coexisting within the same molecular environment. In addition to the well-studied collimated jet driven by the main protostar IRAS 18162-2048, several bubble-like cavities have now been identified across the region (cf. H
2
continuum subtracted image shown in Fig. 16 from
8
and Fig. 2 from
55
). These include bubble-like structures centred on IRS 4, IRS 3, and a third source southeast of MHO 2360, as well as the bubble revealed in this study around IRS 7/SC (see Fig.
1
). Such morphology is more consistent with the cumulative action of multiple photoionising sources over time than with purely shock-excited bow shocks from protostellar outflows. Although we cannot rule out that some of this molecular emission emerges from outflow shocked material.

Our VLA detections provide further support for a more evolved stellar population embedded within the region. Both IRS 4 and IRS 3 are clearly detected at cm wavelengths, suggesting the presence of ionised gas. Although the lack of equivalent ALMA or near-IR IFU coverage prevents a full characterisation of these sources, the spatial coincidence between radio peaks and bubble H
2
structures strongly hints at past or ongoing H
ii
regions. We also calculate the number of Lyman continuum photons for the two sources as done for IRS 7/SC (see Sect.
4.1
). We obtain
\log_{10}(\dot{N}_{i}/\mathrm{s^{-1}})\sim 43.82
for IRS 4 and
\log_{10}(\dot{N}_{i}/\mathrm{s^{-1}})\sim 43.83
for the source in the proximity of IRS 3 (see Table
2
), both consistent with B2/B3 ZAMS stars. In fact, these sources may represent an older generation of stars as they are clearly detected in the H
\alpha
image of
8
, see their Figure 1, and therefore had time to disperse their surroundings and lower the extinction. This structure should be considered in conjunction to the newly detected PDR around IRS 7/SC, which our combined near-IR and radio diagnostics favour a compact photoionised nebula. In this case, however, IRS 7/SC may be in a younger evolutionary stage in comparison to the aforementioned sources.

Taken together, the presence of multiple ionised bubbles and compact radio sources indicates that several massive or intermediate-mass stars have already reached the stage of producing photoionising feedback. Meanwhile, the main protostar IRAS 18162-2048 appears to be significantly younger. Estimates for massive protostars undergoing high accretion rates (
10^{-4}
–
10^{-3}\penalty\ M_{\odot}\penalty\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}
) predict the development of a compact H
ii
region at ages
\sim 10^{5}
yr
(
60
;
23
;
29
;
56
)
. Although
3
suggested the presence of an incipient hypercompact H
ii
region towards the main protostar, observational evidence remained inconclusive. Our results are consistent with IRAS 18162-2048 still being younger than
\sim 10^{5}
yr and not in the ZAMS stage yet, i.e., prior to fully developing a stable photoionised region, while other sources in its vicinity have already reached this evolutionary stage.

This age spread opens the possibility that a triggered star formation process operated in the region IRAS 18162-2048. The spatial distribution of the bubbles appears to surround the central region where the main protostar IRAS 18162-2048 resides. Although speculative at this stage, the geometry
toward the main protostar IRAS 18161-2048 may be suggestive of compressed gas being funnelled inward by previous generations of feedback-driven expansion.
Such a sequence of older stars carving cavities, followed by collapse of swept-up material forming younger objects, has been proposed in other high-mass star-forming regions
(
49
;
78
;
50
;
46
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, the current data do not yet allow a firm conclusion, and dedicated molecular-line kinematic studies and higher-resolution radio and infrared spectro-imaging of the outer bubbles will be required to confirm or refute this scenario.

In summary, the high-mass star-forming region IRAS 18162-2048 is emerging as a clustered, sequential star-forming environment where feedback from earlier massive stars has shaped the cloud and may have influenced the birth of the next generation, including the main protostar IRAS 18162-2048. The system therefore provides an excellent laboratory to study the interplay between feedback and star formation in a massive protocluster context.

5
Conclusions

We have presented near-IR integral field spectroscopic observations of the high-mass star-forming region IRAS 18162–2048 obtained with VLT/SINFONI, complemented by new VLA X and C bands data and ALMA band 3 observations. Our study resolves the inner
\sim
10000 au of the system, revealing a complex interplay between massive and intermediate-mass young stellar objects, ionised and molecular gas, and UV-irradiated material. Through spectral and morphological diagnostics, we identified clear evidence of a compact H
ii
region and a PDR excited by a B2/B3 type ZAMS star, most likely by IRS 7/SC. Below we summarise the main conclusions:

•

•

Large and small scales near-IR and radio observations reveal multiple compact bubbles associated with sources such as IRS 7, IRS 4, and IRS 3, southeast to MHO 2360 indicating previous episodes of massive-star feedback and the presence of H
ii
regions. The morphology and spatial distribution of these bubbles suggest that earlier generations of stars influenced the cloud structure and potentially helped trigger subsequent star-formation events. While further high-resolution observations are required to firmly establish this scenario, IRAS 18162–2048 stands out as an active, multigenerational, feedback-rich star-forming complex.
