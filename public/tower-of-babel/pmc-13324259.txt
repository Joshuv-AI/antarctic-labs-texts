A comprehensive assessment of fuel reformulation strategies in Delonix regia biodiesel to improve CI engine characteristics with RSM optimization
Authors: I Syed Sadiq Nawaz, M A Asokan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this research was to examine how the performance and emission characteristics of a B20 blend of diesel and gulmohar biodiesel could be improved using two approaches: partial hydrogenation and the addition of di-tert-butyl peroxide. The ignition quality and emission characteristics of Delonix regia biodiesel are influenced by the proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acid methyl esters. Hydrogenated Delonix regia biodiesel was produced using a high-pressure autoclave reactor, and gas chromatography (GC) analysis was performed to quantify changes in fatty acid methyl ester composition, particularly the reduction of polyunsaturated components resulting from partial hydrogenation. Further, the experimental results were analysed using desirability function optimization in Design-Expert 13 software. The cetane enhancer, DTBP, was blended with B20 at a concentration of 2 ml/L. A diesel engine was used to evaluate the performance and emission characteristics of base diesel, GB20, HGB20, and GB20 + DTBP fuels. Compared with the GB20 blend, the modified GB20 fuels showed reductions in NOx emissions of 10.8% for HGB20 and 12.8% for GB20 + DTBP. In addition, the brake thermal efficiency at full load increased by 7.1% for HGB20 and 2.5% for GB20 + DTBP. Compared with diesel fuel, emissions from the modified biodiesel blend specifically carbon monoxide, HC, and smokes were significantly reduced. Moreover, improvements in CI engine performance were achieved through the development of a CCD model using response surface methodology (RSM). The desirability function yielded an accuracy value of 0.918 and identified hydrogenated GB20 at higher loads as the optimal operating condition. The investigation indicated that hydrogenated GB20 is the most advantageous blend among the tested fuels and can operate efficiently as a diesel substitute.Keywords: Delonix regia, Autoclave, Di-tert-butyl peroxide, CCD, NOx emissionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Scientists have been looking into possible alternative energy sources that can mitigate environmental degradation and rising energy prices due to the continuous exhaustion of non-renewable conventional fossil fuels. Charcoal, nitrogen oxides, carbon dioxide, and hydrocarbons are some of the dangerous gases released when chemicals are burned insufficiently. Power generation can be achieved through either renewable or non-renewable resources. Seventy to Eighty% of the global energy supply originates from fossil fuels, according to energy statistics1. Biodiesel presents numerous benefits compared to traditional diesel, such as renewability, biodegradability, enhanced oxygen content for superior combustion, reduced Carbon monoxide, HC, and smoke emissions and compatibility with current engine systems. Its application can bolster rural economies by utilising indigenous feedstock, including non-edible Gulmohar crops2. As an added bonus, the gulmohar seed can detoxify the body and improve circulation in addition to alleviating inflammation, otalgia, and thoracic diseases. It has carminative qualities as well. The main industry that uses this chemical is the tannery. This is the oil that is used to make things like detergent, light bulbs, and medicine3. To address the demand for conventional fuels and comply with emission standards, it is essential to explore alternative sustainable biofuel supplies. The diverse types of biofuels employed in internal combustion (IC) engines include bio-oils, biodiesel, and bio-alcohol4. The transesterification process with catalytic technology is extensively employed in biodiesel synthesis since it may be performed under very modest circumstances. These variables may hinder the transesterification reaction rate, therefore making biodiesel synthesis economically unfeasible and diminishing its production5.

Juliflora biodiesel results from high temperatures that induce the hydrolysis of triglycerides, leading to an increased concentration of fatty acids6. Trivalent long-chain alcohols are formed when different lengths of fatty acids are connected to a glycerin molecule. The charge that tiglycerides offer is crucial in trans-esterification. Esterification, which comprises fatty acid re-esterification with methanol to produce a methyl ester that is monovalent, produces the undesirable byproduct glycerine by breaking these bonds7. Gulmohar biodiesel shown raised brake thermal efficiency and lower specific fuel consumption in CI engines compared to B20 blends, according to the tests8. Adding 1% to 2% BD to EHN allowed for full combustion due to the higher concentrations of CN and latent heat of vaporization. When used under ideal conditions, the cetane improver greatly enhances DTBP’s performance, oxidation stability, and other emissions9. Higher IP reduced the physical delay period and improved the performance of the engine10. This alternative must satisfy the criteria of being economical, easily accessible, highly efficient, and, most importantly, renewable. The ignition delay is reduced and the laminar flame speed upsurges with higher cetane11. Increased IP led to improved atomization, while sophisticated IT addressed the issues posed owing to elevated bulk modulus of compressibility of biodiesel12. Notably, under partial and medium load conditions, biodiesel blends exhibit enhanced combustion stability and less soot emissions relative to diesel. Biodiesel can achieve improved NOx–smoke trade-offs compared to diesel when used with cetane boosters like DTBP or through engine modifications such as thermal barrier coatings. Moreover, biodiesel possesses a greater oxygen content, leading to enhanced combustion and emissions reductions of carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), and smoke13. One way to shorten the time it takes for a diesel engine to start is to mix biodiesel and DTBP together14,15. DTBP is a chemical that works well to cut down on NOx emissions. At higher temperatures, the two gases nitrogen as well as oxygen in the air must react to make NOx. It is crucial to examine the effects of DTBP on NOx generation, which was influenced by the net HRR and the mean temperature16. There is a linear relationship between the amount of DTBP additive in diesel fuel and the ignition delay. Heat release rate data shows that the addition of DTBP begins the reaction and raises combustion temperatures during the early phases of ignition17. We added DTBP as a cetene improver to the Juliflora fuel in amounts ranging from 1% to 2% by volume. BTE increased by 9.44% and exhaust opacity values decreased by up to 15% when cetane enhancer additive was used with juliflora biodiesel18,19.

The application of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) combined with the desirability function represents a powerful and comprehensive optimization strategy for multi-response systems, especially in engine testing where multiple performance and emission parameters must be simultaneously evaluated. Central composite design and desirability to identify the optimal combination of engine load and biodiesel blend that maximizes engine efficiency while minimizing harmful emissions20. The key advantage of using the desirability function in RSM lies in its ability to balance trade-offs among multiple conflicting objectives, offering a holistic solution that would be difficult to obtain using traditional single-objective optimization methods. This approach not only ensures optimal engine performance but also supports environmental sustainability, making it highly suitable for biodiesel-diesel blend applications in compression ignition engines21. Researchers have looked at how various methods of increasing the cetane number affect the test fuels’ combustion and emission properties. However, biodiesel blends that include partially hydrogenated oils and DTBP improve combustion characteristics and reduce emissions.

This study presents to systematically assess and compare two fuel reformulation strategies partial hydrogenation and di-tert-butyl peroxide (DTBP) addition utilized in Delonix regia biodiesel blends under uniform compression ignition engine operating circumstances. This work’s originality is in the direct evaluation of their comparative effectiveness on performance, combustion, and emission characteristics, focusing specifically on the trade-off between NOx and brake thermal efficiency. Additionally, Response Surface Methodology combined with a desirability function is utilized for multi-objective optimization to determine the optimal operating conditions, thereby providing a realistic and scalable approach for improved biodiesel application in CI engines.

Materials and methodology

Test samples preparation

Biodiesel was produced using the transesterification reaction technique. The chemical interaction between alcohol and triglycerides, which are the lipid components of oils, is characterized by the presence of a catalyst that facilitates the transesterification reaction. Catalysts can be homogeneous bases, such as NaOH and KOH. This mechanism facilitates the reaction between methanol and triglycerides present in the oil. Acidic ionic liquids and sulfuric acid are less commonly used as acid catalysts. To enhance the triglyceride conversion process, biodiesel was produced from gulmohar seed oil using a combination of methanol and sodium hydroxide. The process involves three reversible reaction steps that lead to the formation of glycerol, monoglycerides, and diglycerides. The primary molecular component of biodiesel, FAME, is produced during the entire process. Physicochemical characteristics of the test fuels are illustrated in Table 1.

The batch manufacturing process used partial hydrogenation in an SS316 autoclave reactor for delonix regia. A 600 ml volume of Delonix regia oil was mixed with 1.5 g of palladium catalyst (0.4% w/w of oil) for partial hydrogenation. This catalyst concentration does not represent the biodiesel fraction in the test fuel. A pressure of 10 bar was applied to the hydrogen gas supply throughout the reaction, and the reactor temperature maintain constant at 150° C. Finally, palladium catalyst was filtered out of the hydrogenated biodiesel. DTBP was purchased from a local market in 100 ml containers. The DTBP used in this study had a purity of 98%, a molecular formula of C₈H₁₈O₂, and a molecular weight of 146.23 g/mol. It is a colorless to pale yellow liquid with a density of approximately 0.79 g/cm³ at 25 °C and a boiling point of around 110 °C. DTBP exhibits good thermal stability at ambient conditions. and decomposes at temperatures above 100 °C, producing free radicals that promote early ignition and reduce ignition delay. Even at lesser concentrations, such as 2000 ppm DTBP, the GB20 blend properties are unaffected. This study examined diesel, the base fuel, three different GB20 blended fuels, and one other fuel. According to the blending concentration utilized as test fuels, the chosen fuels are diesel, GB20, HGB20, and GB20 + DTBP. The partial hydrogenation was carried out in a high-pressure autoclave reactor using a palladium-based catalyst under controlled temperature, pressure, and reaction time. These parameters were deliberately selected to selectively hydrogenate polyunsaturated fatty acid methyl esters while avoiding complete saturation. This controlled hydrogenation reduced the degree of unsaturation, leading to improved cetane number and oxidation stability, which subsequently influenced combustion behavior.

Experimental setup

The experiments were conducted on a single-cylinder, four-stroke, water-cooled compression ignition engine operated at a constant speed of 1500 rpm. The engine load was varied from 0 to 100% using an eddy current dynamometer, while injection pressure, injection timing, and compression ratio were maintained constant throughout the experiments. For each test fuel, the engine was allowed to reach steady-state operating conditions before recording performance, combustion, and emission parameters. All measurements were repeated three times, and the average values were reported to ensure repeatability and reliability of the experimental results. The levels of CO, HC, and NOx were determined using the AVL DI GAS 444 N, commonly referred to as the Five Gas Analyzer. The opacity of smoke was measured by this AVL 437 C smoke meter. Result and discussion

Brake specific fuel consumption

Figure 3 displays the contour plot depicting the fluctuation of BSFC in relation to engine load and fuel types tested. BSFC is a vital metric of engine efficiency, indicating the fuel necessary to generate a unit of brake power. Reduced BSFC values indicate enhanced fuel efficiency. Regardless of the test fuels, the plot clearly shows a trend where BSFC falls with increasing load.

Effect of load and test fuels on brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC).

The BSFC stays comparatively high at lower loads (25–40%), particularly for diesel and GB20 test fuels. Incomplete combustion and reduced brake thermal efficiency under light load circumstances are the causes of this rise. On the other hand, BSFC dramatically drops at greater loads (over 70%), with the lowest values seen for hydrogenated GB20 test fuels and close to maximum load (100%) as well. This pattern suggests that enhanced air-fuel mixing, higher in-cylinder temperatures, and better combustion conditions all contribute to the engine’s increased efficiency under heavier loads.The combination of high load (85–100%) with hydrogenated B20 is the ideal range for BSFC, as shown by the blue zone, where BSFC reaches its lowest value of roughly 0.355%. On the other hand, base GB20 and low load (25–40%) exhibit the lowest performance, with BSFC peaking at approximately 0.625%, indicated in red. These findings suggest that using test fuels with optimized composition (maybe blends with a moderate to lower proportion of biodiesel) and operating at higher loads can greatly improve Engine performance in compliance with BSFC. The reduction in BSFC and the improvement in BTE observed for hydrogenated GB20 are consistent with earlier studies on hydrogenated biodiesel blends, where reduced unsaturation and improved cetane number enhanced combustion efficiency9,13 The presence of a periodic or nonlinear interaction impact between fuel mix qualities and engine running conditions is suggested by the parabolic and wave-like contour pattern along the fuel axis. Overall, the findings show that test fuel composition and engine load have a considerable influence on BSFC.

Brake thermal efficiency

Figure 4 illustrates the contour plot of Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE) as a function of engine load and fuel type. BTE is a crucial performance indicator that assesses how effectively the chemical energy of fuel is transferred into operational mechanical work. All test fuel types exhibit a clear and steady rise in BTE as engine load increases, according to the contour plot. As shown in the blue area, BTE values are low at lower loads (25–40%), ranging from about 17–22%, particularly for unaltered fuels. Suboptimal combustion conditions, such as incomplete combustion, a lower in-cylinder temperature, and a longer ignition delay at low engine loads, are to blame for this decreased efficiency. All gasoline blends show a discernible improvement in BTE as the load rises toward 85–100%, with maximum values hitting at 35.4% (as shown in the red-orange zone).

Effect of load and test fuels on brake thermal efficiency.

Better atomization, increased combustion efficiency, and a favorable air-fuel ratio that encourages full combustion are the causes of this improved performance at higher loads. Furthermore, higher cylinder pressures brought on by increased thermal input result in more efficient energy conversion22. It’s interesting to see that although load has a greater impact on BTE, the modified test fuel type also has an impact. For example, in the mid-load range (55–70%), hydrogenated and DTBP test blends exhibit a marginally higher BTE than the extreme fuel compositions (pure diesel and GB20). This implies that fuels that are modestly blended may offer a balance between oxygen content and combustion quality, improving engine thermal efficiency23. Similar improvements in BTE (5–8%) for hydrogenated or cetane-enhanced biodiesel fuels were reported by Musthafa and Asokan and Chacko et al., confirming the role of improved ignition quality in energy conversion efficiency. Further evidence that engine load is more important than fuel blends in influencing BTE comes from the relatively equal contour spacing along the vertical (fuel) axis as opposed to the more fast gradient along the horizontal (load) axis.

Combustion duration

The combustion duration contour plot (in crank angle degrees) versus engine load and test fuel types is displayed in Fig. 5. The time interval (measured in degrees of crank angle) between the beginning and conclusion of the primary combustion event is known as the combustion duration. It is essential in determining the engine’s overall performance, heat release rate, and combustion efficiency24. According to the plot, combustion time rises gradually with engine load, with values ranging from about 37.2° CA at low load (25%) to 50.2° CA at high load (100%). Longer combustion times are caused by higher in-cylinder pressures and temperatures at higher loads, as well as larger fuel injection amounts. Particularly for test fuels, the combustion duration stays shorter (blue to light-green zones) at lower loads, indicating more thorough and effective combustion. The duration progressively moves into the yellow-orange band as the load rises beyond 70%, signifying longer combustion times. This might be because of the accompanying thermal inertia at greater load circumstances and the delay in full oxidation of the increased amount of fuel supplied.

Effect of load and test fuels on combustion duration.

In contrast to the effect of load, the variation in combustion duration appears to be less significant across the different test fuels. Nonetheless, a minor impact is apparent: fuels with GB20 blend indices often exhibit marginally longer combustion times for all loads. This might be because biodiesel has a higher viscosity and less volatility, which can prolong the combustion process by delaying atomization and mixing. Compared to fuel blends, engine load has a greater influence on combustion duration, as shown by the contour spacing and color gradients. However, especially at medium loads, selecting the appropriate blend (hydrogenated GB20) may help to shorten combustion duration and increase thermal efficiency. In conclusion, as engine load grows, combustion duration lengthens, and a modest GB20 blend might provide the best balance between timing and full combustion. Conversely, reducing emissions requires modified B20 blends to minimize combustion duration within allowable bounds.

Ignition delay

The contour plot of ignition delay (ID) in degrees of crank angle in relation to test fuel type and engine load is shown in Fig. 6. The time period that exists between the initiation of fuel injection and the commencement of combustion is known as the ignition delay. Peak pressure, heat release rate, combustion phasing, and overall engine efficiency are all greatly impacted25,26. The plot clearly shows that as engine load increases, ignition delay decreases, going from about 11.2° CA at 25% load to as low as 4.97° CA at 100% load. Greater pressures and temperatures within the cylinder at greater loads are thought to be the cause of this tendency since they accelerate auto-ignition and fuel-air mixing. As a result, more steady and thorough combustion occurs at larger loads since the interval between injection and ignition is shortened.

Effect of load and test fuels on ignition delay.

Another important factor is the impact of the test fuel composition. Particularly at low engine loads, the base blend values, which probably correspond to fuels with a larger biodiesel content, exhibit longer igniting delays. This is explained by the fact that biodiesel has a lower volatility and a higher viscosity, which prevents atomization and evaporation and delays ignition. Furthermore, compared to diesel, biodiesel usually has a lower cetane number and a greater flash point, both of which lengthen the ignition delay. The mid-to-high range of test fuels show comparatively shorter ignition delays, however, particularly when used with medium to high engine loads. This might be the result of the blend’s advantageous diesel ignition properties and the biodiesel’s optimal oxygen content balance, which improves the mixture’s ignition propensity. Although load has a greater effect, the contour gradient shows that both engine load and fuel type have a considerable impact on ignition delay. High loads and mixed fuels at the diesel end of the spectrum result in the shortest igniting delays, whereas low load conditions with a modified cause the longest delays27. The shorter ignition delay and higher peak cylinder pressure obtained with HGB20 align well with findings reported for DTBP-blended and hydrogenated biodiesel fuels16, where enhanced radical formation and reduced chemical delay promoted faster combustion.

Cylinder pressure (CPmax)

The maximum cylinder pressure (CPmax) contour plot, which is impacted by changes in engine load and test fuel type, is shown in Fig. 7. A crucial determinant of the engine’s power production, thermal efficiency, and structural integrity, CPmax is a fundamental indicator of in-cylinder combustion behavior. With readings ranging from roughly 55.95 bar at 25% load to 84.25 bar at 100% load, the plot shows that CPmax grows noticeably with increasing engine load. This steady increase in cylinder pressure with load is anticipated since higher loads require more fuel to be injected, which increases heat release, improves combustion energy, and raises peak pressures. In terms of test fuel impacts, it has been found that fuels with blend hydrogenated and DTBP added B20 blends typically show somewhat higher CPmax values than the extremes. Because biodiesel has more oxygen available and diesel components have better volatility, this behavior implies that moderate biodiesel blends increase combustion.

Effect of load and test fuels on cylinder pressure (CPmax).

Higher in-cylinder pressures are the result of a more thorough and effective combustion process. Conversely, under all load situations, base fuels show lower CPmax values. This is probably because biodiesel has a lower heating value and a higher viscosity, which might slow down combustion and lessen the severity of pressure development. Similar to diesel, blends with very low biodiesel concentration also exhibit somewhat lower CPmax, perhaps as a result of comparatively lower oxygen availability during combustion. High load conditions (85–100%) with modified fuel blends yield the most favorable region (red zone) for CPmax, indicating excellent combustion performance in this range.

Carbon monoxide (CO) emissions

Figure 8 shows a contour plot of carbon monoxide (CO) emissions, expressed in g/kW h, for various engine loads and test fuel types. CO is a byproduct of incomplete combustion, and its emission level is an important measure of combustion efficiency and oxygen availability during the combustion process28. The contour graphic clearly shows that CO emissions drop with increasing engine load. At low loads (25–40%), CO emissions are relatively significant, reaching up to 3.47 g/kW h, especially for base fuels. The higher CO level at minimal loads is mostly due to reduced combustion temperatures and limited oxygen supply, resulting in partial fuel oxidation. Particularly for modified fuel blends, CO emissions dramatically decrease as engine load rises toward 85–100%, reaching values as low as 0.85 g/kW h. Better combustion conditions greater in-cylinder pressure and temperature encourage full combustion, which in turn minimizes the generation of CO are the cause of this decrease in CO emissions at higher loads. Hydrogenated B20 Blend exhibit the lowest CO emissions under all load circumstances when it comes to test fuel influence. This is explained by the fact that biodiesel contains more oxygen, which promotes the oxidation of CO to CO₂. In contrast, GB20 and diesel that contain more have slightly higher CO emissions, especially when there is little load. This is because of either inadequate atomization. The blue areas that predominate on the plot’s right side are areas of ideal combustion, where a high engine load and an oxygen-rich fuel blend reduce the production of CO29.

Effect of load and test fuels on carbon monoxide (CO) emissions.

Hydrocarbon (HC) emissions

Figure 9 shows a contour plot of unburned hydrocarbon (HC) emissions, measured in g/kW h, under various engine loads and test fuel blends. HC emissions are generally caused by incomplete combustion, quenching at the combustion chamber walls, and the existence of dense fuel-air zones30. As a result, HC emission levels vary depending on engine running circumstances and fuel qualities. The plot clearly indicates a decreasing trend in HC emissions as engine load increases, with values ranging from a maximum of 1.47 g/kW h at 25% load to a low of 0.45 g/kW h at full load (100%). At lower loads, combustion temperatures and in-cylinder pressures are insufficient to ensure complete fuel oxidation, resulting in increased HC production.

Effect of load and test fuels on hydrocarbon (HC) emissions.

On the other hand, higher pressure and temperature, enhance combustion efficiency and reduce ignition delay at higher loads, leading to a more thorough fuel burn and lower HC emissions. The plot’s transition from red/yellow zones at low load to deep blue at high load regions serves as a visual representation of this improvement. Modified biodiesel blends consistently provide lower HC emissions than either extreme pure diesel or B20 when the impact of test fuel blends is examined. This is because biodiesel’s oxygenated composition promotes fuel oxidation during combustion. However, because to poor atomization and decreased volatility, greater biodiesel blends may show somewhat higher HC under low loads, leading to wall impingement and quenching. Modified blended fuels have the lowest HC emissions (0.45–0.6 g/kW h) under high load conditions, which makes this range ideal for reducing unburned hydrocarbons. In terms of emissions, the reduction in CO, HC, and smoke emissions for modified GB20 fuels corroborates earlier observations that oxygenated biodiesel fuels promote complete oxidation28.

NOx emissions

The contour plot for nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions, measured in g/kW h, as a function of engine load and test fuel mixes is shown in Fig. 10. The presence of oxygen during combustion and high in-cylinder temperatures are the main causes of NOx emissions. They are a key environmental concern due to their participation in smog generation and respiratory health consequences31. The plot illustrates the intricate relationship between fuel type and load. In contrast to what is usually expected, NOx emissions for fuel blends at B20 and HB20 reach up to 5.52 g/kW h at lower engine loads (25–40%). This red zone indicates a combustion state where localized high-temperature zones are promoted by relatively lean mixes and the natural oxygen content of biodiesel, speeding up the generation of thermal NOx even at lower loads. NOx emissions start to decrease when engine load rises beyond 60%, especially for modified biodiesels compared to base B20. Shorter ignition delays, richer mixes, and quenching effects that restrict peak combustion temperatures and hence reduce thermal NOx generation may be the cause of this decrease in NOx at higher loads (deep green to blue zone)32. Furthermore, NOx generation is greatly impacted by test fuel mixtures. Biodiesel blend B20 have increased NOx emissions, particularly at moderate engine loads, due to their increased oxygen concentration and cetane number, which can result in hotter and more thorough combustion. Although biodiesel blends are often associated with increased NOx emissions, the present study demonstrates a measurable NOx reduction for hydrogenated GB20 and DTBP-added GB20, particularly at higher loads. This behavior agrees with previous studies reporting that improved cetane number and moderated heat release rates can suppress peak combustion temperatures and thereby limit thermal NOx formation9,31.

Smoke emissions

The contour plot of smoke opacity (%), a crucial measure of particulate matter (PM) emissions, as impacted by fuel type and engine load is shown in Fig. 11. Incomplete combustion is the primary contributor to the emission of smoke, which is greatly influenced by the fuel’s oxygen availability, carbon-to-hydrogen (C/H) ratio, and aromatic content33. The contour map clearly shows a gradient from blue (low opacity ~ 0.02%) at lower loads to red (high opacity ~ 0.49%) at full load, indicating that smoke emissions rise as engine load increases. This pattern is anticipated as greater loads necessitate greater fuel input, which results in richer air-fuel mixes, less air available, and ultimately incomplete combustion all of which create an environment that is conducive to the creation of soot.

Effect of load and test fuels on smoke emissions.

When it comes to the impact of test fuel blends, it has been found that blends with a B20 blend tend to emit more smoke than other test blends. This is because biodiesel’s oxygenated composition improves combustion and lowers soot production. In particular, smoke emissions are low for all fuel blends (blue to cyan zone) at low to moderate loads (25–60%), indicating efficient combustion. Particularly for modified GB20 blends, where the absence of oxygenated molecules causes incomplete combustion and increased particulate matter, smoke emissions increase at high loads (over 80%).It’s interesting to note that, particularly at low-to-moderate loads, a minimum smoke emission is seen at Hydrogenated GB20 blend. This suggests that a well-balanced blend of biodiesel and diesel can provide the advantages of sufficient volatility and natural oxygen, promoting cleaner burning and less smoke production.

Overall parameters with desirability function.

A strong and all-encompassing optimization technique for multi-response systems is the application of Response Surface Methodology in conjunction with the desirability function, particularly in engine testing where several performance and emission characteristics need to be assessed concurrently. The best engine load and biodiesel blend combination that improves engine efficiency while minimizes hazardous emissions was determined in this study using the desire function. Every response variable, including pollutants such as NOx, HC, CO, and smoke, brake thermal efficiency, and brake specific fuel consumption, was converted into a dimensionless desirability scale with a range of 0 (totally undesired) to 1 (entirely desirable). To direct the optimization process, these distinct desirability values were then combined to create a composite desirability index.

The optimization results revealed that the optimal operating state occurred at a high load of 99.18% and a fuel blend level of 3, which most likely corresponds to a mid-level mixture of hydrogenated biodiesel with diesel. The optimal situation resulted in a high BTE of 33.49%, suggesting efficient energy conversion. Concurrently, the BSFC was reduced to 0.406 g/kWh, indicating improved fuel utilization. Emissions were also greatly reduced, with NOx at 4.27 g/kWh, HC at 0.49 g/kWh, CO at 1.04 g/kWh, and smoke at 0.43%, all falling below permissible levels. Furthermore, combustion aspects like ignition delay (4.97° CA) and combustion duration (47.05° CA) were within acceptable limits, indicating reliable engine functioning. The desirability function’s main advantage in RSM is its ability to balance trade-offs between numerous conflicting objectives, providing a holistic solution that would be impossible to get with traditional single-objective optimization approaches.

Conclusion

This research examines the performance and emission parameters of biodiesel–diesel blends using a CI engine and fuel modification techniques. The present study analyses the aforementioned fuel reformulation alternatives with respect to the NOx and the brake thermal efficiency (BTE) trade-offs. The combustion and emission parameters of test fuels were evaluated and the findings are presented below.

Modified gulmohar biodiesel blends showed improved physicochemical properties, including increased cetane number and heating value, thereby achieving better performance and combustion with lower emissions. This study demonstrated that a CI engine could operate efficiently and exhibit superior combustion characteristics when fueled with modified biodiesel B20 blends. The addition of DTBP at a concentration of 2000ppm improved the BTE and CP were 2.5%, 3% respectively. Similarly, it reduced BSFC, CO, and HC emissions by 10.3%, 11.4%, and 4.3%, respectively compared to B20. The hydrogenated biodiesel B20 blend showed further improvement in CI engine characteristics compared to DTBP addition. For HB20, BTE and CP improved by 7% and 6%, respectively, while BSFC, CO, and HC were reduced by 16.5%, 6%, and 13%, respectively compared to the base fuel. In regard with the characteristics of smoke and nitrogen oxide emissions, HB20 has an advantage over DTBP addition since it has more oxygen and no nitrogen content. Compared with earlier works that evaluated individual cetane enhancement techniques, the present study uniquely provides a direct comparison between partial hydrogenation and DTBP addition under identical engine conditions. The results clearly indicate that hydrogenated GB20 offers a superior NOx–BTE trade-off, which has not been comprehensively reported in earlier studies.

A potential strategy for converting the fuel into an eco-friendly and sustainable energy source is to incorporate both fuel modification strategies. The RSM optimization analysed the experimental results and produced regression models for engine responses, yielding a desirability value of 0.918. This strategy not only optimizes engine performance but also promotes environmental sustainability, making it ideal for biodiesel-diesel blend applications in compression ignition engines.

The present study is limited to a single-cylinder CI engine operating at constant speed and fixed injection parameters, with analysis restricted to B20 blends. Long-term durability, injector wear, and higher blend ratios were not considered. Future studies should address multi-cylinder engines, endurance testing, advanced optimization, and sustainability assessment.
