Unveiling the Influence of Amplifying Language-Specific Neurons
Inaya Rahmanisa, Lyzander Marciano Andrylie, Mahardika Krisna Ihsani, Alfan Farizki Wicaksono, Haryo Akbarianto Wibowo, Alham Fikri Aji

Abstract
Language-specific neurons in LLMs that strongly correlate with individual languages have been shown to influence model behavior by deactivating them. However, their role in amplification remains underexplored. This work investigates the effect of amplifying language-specific neurons through interventions across 18 languages, including low-resource ones, using three models primarily trained in different languages. We compare amplification factors by their effectiveness in steering to the target language using a proposed Language Steering Shift (LSS) evaluation score, then evaluate it on downstream tasks: commonsense reasoning (XCOPA, XWinograd), knowledge (Include), and translation (FLORES). The optimal amplification factors effectively steer output toward nearly all tested languages. Intervention using this factor on downstream tasks improves self-language performance in some cases but generally degrades cross-language results. These findings highlight the effect of language-specific neurons in multilingual behavior, where amplification can be beneficial especially for low-resource languages, but provides limited advantage for cross-lingual transfer. 1 1 1 The code and dataset are made available at https://github.com/tauimbz/lang-task-neuron.



1
Introduction

Figure 1:
Illustration of our methodology. There are three sequential stages involving: 1). Identification of language-specific neurons, 2). Finding the optimal amplifying steering factor, and 3). Evaluation of the optimal amplifying steering factor on downstream tasks to understand its impact on the models’ behavior.

Multilingual large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities. However, they still continue to exhibit inconsistencies in downstream tasks (i.e., often producing biased or inaccurate outputs across different linguistic contexts), especially involving low-resource languages
Dang et al. 2024
. Understanding the root cause of these inconsistencies remains challenging due to limited transparency in how LLMs represent language-specific features and perform cross-lingual transfer.

In elucidating that issue, a few recent studies have begun to interpret model inner workings in multilingual contexts, revealing neuron-level correlations with language features and suggesting the existence of language-specific neurons
Kojima et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
.
These neurons have been shown to affect multilingual capability following deactivation and signs of language-steering capabilities in generation. Interventions typically use fixed activation values, called steering factor, to control behavior. Most prior works use zero as the steering factor to deactivate targeted neurons. However, the impacts of actively steering these neurons in downstream tasks, particularly related to improving performance or enabling task transfer, remain unclear as recent studies have focused only on self-language interventions
Mondal et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
.

This work addresses the gap by identifying and analyzing two types of neurons: language-specific neurons and language-activated (less-specific) neurons. We then compare various steering factors derived from different settings to evaluate their effect on the steerability of these neurons and analyze their impact on downstream task performance using neurons specific to the target language (self-intervention) and to other languages (cross-intervention). We evaluate 18 languages including low-resource ones like Quechua, Haitian Creole, and Swahili, across models primarily trained in English and Chinese (Qwen), English (Gemma), and Southeast Asian languages (SeaLLMs) using XCOPA, XWinograd, and Include-lite for reasoning and knowledge tasks, and FLORES-200 for translation.

Several significant findings from this study are as follows:

•

Comparison of different steering factors using the proposed Language Steering Shift (LSS) reveals that patched steering factors are the most effective and statistically outperform test-time intervention factors. Amplifying language-specific neurons with these factors consistently directs outputs toward all target languages, achieving over 90% average success in language shifting.

•

Optimal amplification of language-specific neurons in self-intervention can reduce perplexity and improve task performance for certain languages, with gains reaching up to
10\%
in some models, showing improved results for low-resource languages compared to
Mondal et al. 2025
.

•

Cross-lingual amplification can slightly reduce perplexity and enhance performance between closely related languages, but it generally leads to performance degradation in others, indicating limited effectiveness in cross-lingual transfer.

2
Related Work

Recent studies have explored neuron activations in language
Kojima et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
, task
Leng and Xiong 2025
;
Song et al. 2024
, and mixed contexts
Wang et al. 2024
, often by inhibiting neurons to assess their roles.
Kojima et al. 2024
showed that intervening on language-specific neurons can steer output language. Common intervention methods include activation patching
Zhang and Nanda 2024
, model editing
Meng et al. 2023
, and suppression via zeroing or percentile thresholds
Mondal et al. 2025
. While
Mondal et al. 2025
has explored self-language intervention on downstream tasks, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet focused on comparing amplification factors and provided improving insights into self- and cross-lingual interventions in downstream tasks.

3
Methodology

This section describes our method for identifying language-specific and language-activated neurons, introduces the Language Steering Shift (LSS) score to quantify language steering, and details our approach for determining the steering factors.

3.1
Neuron Identification

As illustrated in Figure
1
, initially, we define neurons as the outputs of the activation function within the FFN modules in LLMs as they have been found to store knowledge or features
Dai et al. 2022
;
Tang et al. 2024
.

Identifying Language-Activated Neurons.

As a baseline method, we adapt a method from
Wang et al. 2024
to define language-activated neurons, i.e., neurons whose activation value exceeds 0 throughout every sentence
s
in the dataset of language
k
, denoted
S_{k}
, allowing a single neuron to be language-activated for multiple languages. The set of language-activated neurons in layer
i
for language
k
, denoted by
A_{k}(i)
, is described by:

A_{k}(i)=\left\{j\;\middle|\;\texttt{act}(i,j;s)>0,\;\;\forall s\in S_{k}\right\}\,.

For neuron
j
,
\texttt{act}(i,j;s)
is the activation value of the
j
-th neuron in the
i
-th layer when processing sentence
s
. These neurons are not further filtered, resulting in relatively large number of neurons and overlapping activations across languages. We refer to these as
baseline neurons
.

Language-Specific Neurons.

language activation probablity entropy
(LAPE) method
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
was adopted to identify language-specific neurons.
The probability of activation for the
j
-th neuron within the
i
-th layer when processing language
k
, denoted by
p^{k}_{i,j}
, is estimated according to:

\hat{p}^{k}_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}(\texttt{act}({i,j})>0)\mid\text{language }k\text{ tokens}\right]\,.

The expectation is taken over all tokens from sentences in language
k
, where
\mathbb{I}(.)
is an indicator function that returns
1
if the condition is true and
0
otherwise; and
\texttt{act}(i,j)
denotes the activation value of the
j
-th neuron in the
i
-th layer.
Let
\mathbf{p}_{i,j}=[p^{1}_{i,j},\dots,p^{k}_{i,j},\dots,p^{|L|}_{i,j}]
represent a list of activation probabilities for a set of languages
L
. These probabilities are then normalized to yield the L1-norm version, denoted by
p^{\prime}_{i,j}
.
Using this normalized version, the LAPE score of the
j
-th neuron in the
i
-th layer is formally defined as:

\text{LAPE}_{i,j}=-\sum_{k=1}^{|L|}p^{\prime k}_{i,j}\log(p^{\prime k}_{i,j})\,.

We filter neurons whose language activation probability exceeds the
m
-th percentile. Finally, we select neurons that fall within the bottom
n\%
of LAPE scores.

3.2
Language Steering Shift (LSS)

We propose
Language Steering Shift (LSS)
score to quantify the extent to which same-meaning answers are steered to the target language.
Let
L
be the set of languages used. For every language
k\in L
, we evaluate the dataset for language
k

by performing: 1) a no intervention scenario, and 2) an intervention scenario where neurons corresponding to language
l
are intervened where
l\in L\setminus\{k\}
. The LSS score for evaluating language
k
when intervened upon by neurons specific to language
l
, denoted by
\texttt{LSS}(k,l)
, is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\texttt{LSS}(k,l)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left(\delta(k,l)|_{\texttt{int}}-\delta(k,l)|_{\texttt{non}}>0\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\delta(k,l)

\displaystyle=\log p(\texttt{ans}_{l})-\log p(\texttt{ans}_{k})\,,

where
\log p(\texttt{ans}_{x})
denotes the log-probability assigned by the model to the answer in language
x
; the symbols
|_{\texttt{non}}
and
|_{\texttt{int}}
represent the no intervention and intervention scenario, respectively;
\mathbb{E}[.]
is the expectation value across all sentences in the dataset.

Intuitively, this metric measures the tendency of a model to produce the correct answer in target language
k
given an incomplete sentence written in source language
l
after intervening neurons corresponding to language
k
. For example, given the English sentence “Dublin is the capital city of
Ireland
”, the LSS score measures how much it shifts toward the Chinese translation, “Dublin is the capital city of
爱尔兰共和国
”, when intervened using
zh
neurons. The underlined words represent the answers being analyzed.

3.3
Determining steering factors

We steer language-specific neurons in each layer with amplifying steering factors, which were determined in the identification stage (patched) and test-time intervention stage.

Patched Steering Factors.

Let
v_{j,k}
be the variable that contains the activation value of neuron
j
that is specific to language
k
. In the identification stage involving the multilingual FLORES-200 dataset, the steering factors are computed from the mean and median of activation values across all sentences within the dataset
S_{k}
for language
k
. As a result, a steering factor is specific to neuron
j
and language
k
, to which we patch the language-specific neurons.
This process is formally described as:

v_{j,k}\leftarrow\texttt{agg}\{s\in S_{k}|a_{j,s}\}\,,

where
\texttt{agg}\in\{\texttt{max},\texttt{median}\}
; and
a_{j,s}
represents the activation value of neuron
j
averaged across all tokens in sentence
s
during the identification stage. We refer to these steering factors as patch max (
pmax
) and median (
pmedian
).

Test-Time Intervention Steering Factors.

Let
v_{j,k,m}
be the variable containing the activation of neuron
j
for language
k
when processing sentence
m
, i.e., a sentence from the test dataset. We define the steering value as the maximum over activation values within the sentence. Using this value, we apply two operations to the language-specific neurons: addition (
+max
) to assign with a value surpassing its activation range, and replacement (
=max
) to assign the maximum activation. This computation is formally represented as:

\texttt{steer}_{j,m}\leftarrow\texttt{max}\{t\in T(m)|a_{j,m,t}\}\,,

\textbf{{=max}}\textrm{ : }v_{j,k,m}\leftarrow\texttt{steer}_{j,m}\,,

\textbf{{+max}}\textrm{ : }v_{j,k,m}\leftarrow v_{j,k,m}+\texttt{steer}_{j,m}\,,

where
\texttt{steer}_{j,m}
is the steering factor for neuron
j
during inference on sentence
m
;
T(m)
is the set of tokens in sentence
m
;
and
a_{j,m,t}
is the activation value of neuron
j
associated with token
t
on sentence
m
.
As a comparison of our amplifying factors, we also performed replacement with
0
and
10p
(10th percentile) as a replacement of
max
, which has been similarly
demonstrated by
Mondal et al. 2025
to deactivate neurons. We refer to these as
=0
and
=10p
, respectively.

3.4
LSS Scores and Optimal Amplifying Steering Factor

Result of the Language Steering Shift (LSS) score shows that LAPE neuron amplification yields high scores for many languages, often reaching 100%, as shown in Table
1
for the intervention on
id
. These strong results are consistent across other languages, as detailed in Appendix
E
. However, interventions involving certain language pairs may result in lower LSS scores. For example, translation between
id
and
ms
in either direction tends to produce scores capped at 50% in most models. This is likely due to the high similarity between the two languages, which makes it more difficult for the models to distinguish between them. Notably,
en
remains easy to steer to, suggesting a strong influence that is possibly due to its dominance and widespread presence in LLM training data.

The optimal amplifying factors, defined as those that result in the highest aggregated LSS scores, are found to be
pmax
and
pmedian
. A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons reveals that the patched steering factors
pmax
(mean = 0.9204) and
pmedian
(
\texttt{mean}=0.9095
) significantly outperform the other factors (
p<0.001
); Further results are provided in Appendix
E
. However, the difference between the two is not statistically significant (
p=0.98
), indicating that both are equally effective as optimal amplifying factors.

Test-time intervention factors,
+max
and
=max
, perform well mainly for high-resource languages such as
en
,
pt
,
it
, and
fr
, but show limited effectiveness for other languages, with the lowest performance observed for
ta
in Qwen and Gemma despite its substantial token presence. The deactivating factor
=0
shows variation in results, with successful steering in some languages (e.g.,
en
,
nl
, and
it
), while
=10p
demonstrates difficulty in steering, often resulting in LSS scores close to
0\%
across many languages, align with findings from
Mondal et al. 2025
.

Compared to Baseline neurons, LAPE neurons yield higher average LSS scores across languages as illustrated in Figure
2
. This suggests that LAPE is more effective at steering language, likely due to its stronger language specificity. In contrast, Baseline neurons show greater variability (see Appendix
E
); due to their lower specificity, deactivating these neurons with
=10
and
=10p
sometimes result in higher LSS scores than amplifying them. This contrast further supports LAPE neurons as more reliable and consistent for steering towards corresponding language.

Experimental Settings

Identification of Language-Activated Neurons and Language-Specific Neurons.

We identify language-activated (Baseline) and language-specific (LAPE) neurons by feeding the LLMs sentences from the
dev
split of FLORES-200 dataset
Team et al. 2022
containing
30{,}000
word-tokens per language. We assess 18 languages to ensure broader coverage of language specificity:
en
,
nl
,
id
,
ms
,
vi
,
jp
,
zh
,
fr
,
pt
,
ru
,
et
,
ht
,
it
,
qu
,
sw
,
ta
,
th
, and
tr
in small and large models, namely Qwen2.5 (0.5B and 7B), Gemma2 (2B and 9B), and SeaLLMs-v3 (1.5B and 7B), totaling 6 models (see Appendix
B
for details). For identifying LAPE neurons, we use a language activation probability threshold (
m
) set at the 95th percentile and a LAPE score threshold (
n
) of 1%. All hyperparameter values are adopted from
Tang et al. 2024
as they have been demonstrated to effectively capture language-specific neurons.

Finding the Optimal Steering Factor.

We assess the determined steering factors on LSS score for the small models using a reconstructed MLAMA
Kassner et al. 2021
, a multilingual fact-probing dataset, so that each language data includes the answers of every other languages and filters the rows to ensure that no answers in different languages were the same.
15 out of 18 languages are used due to limitation in the dataset.

Evaluating the Optimal Steering Factor on Downstream Tasks.

We evaluate the steering factor for commonsense reasoning, which is considered one of the most difficult tasks for LLMs
Li et al. 2022
, using XCOPA
Edoardo M. Ponti and Korhonen 2020
and XWinograd
Muennighoff et al. 2022
. For factual knowledge, we use Include-lite
Romanou et al. 2024

Mondal et al. 2025
, who focused only on self-language intervention, we extend the analysis to cross-lingual intervention.

The
devtest
split of FLORES-200 is used to measure perplexity and BLEU for translation from English (
en
) to every other languages. Translation tasks are performed using 2 types of prompts:
targeted
and
non-targeted
. Targeted prompts explicitly mention the target language, whereas non-targeted prompts do not. For example, when translating to
id
, the targeted prompt begins with
"Translate from English to Indonesian."
, whereas the non-targeted prompt starts with
"Translate from English into the target language."

Knowledge and Reasoning Tasks

Overall performance of knowledge and reasoning tasks intervention as presented in Table
2
shows that
pmax
self-intervention can improve accuracy up to 10%, with consistent gains observed in Include-lite. XCOPA and XWinograd self-intervention may show less consistent improvements with only modest changes in some models. In contrast, cross-language interventions generally lead to performance drops on average. These results suggest that amplifying LAPE neurons in self-language on knowledge and reasoning tasks is potentially beneficial for enhancing performance, though their effectiveness in cross-lingual transfer appears limited.

Include-lite Evaluation.

The result in Figure

6
Conclusion

This study proposes the Language Steering Shift (LSS) metric to compare steering factors of language-specific (LAPE) neuron intervention and identify the optimal one, then evaluates its impact in downstream tasks and language modeling. Optimal amplification steers outputs toward target languages with over
90\%
success on average.
Self-language intervention modestly improves or maintains performance in language modeling measured by perplexity, translation, knowledge, and reasoning tasks. Cross-language intervention generally lowers performance, though gains can appear between closely related languages in perplexity and BLEU scores for translation. Compared to less specific language-activated neurons, LAPE neurons yield clearer, more consistent improvements, suggesting that greater neuron specificity enhances multilingual performance.
Overall, these findings suggest that amplifying self-language LAPE neurons can enhance a model’s language capabilities, although not always improving task accuracy. Furthermore, the consistent degradation from cross-language interventions highlights their limited effectiveness for cross-lingual transfer.

Limitations

This study is limited to decoder-only models in 0.5B to 9B parameters and limited sets of tasks. Methods to identify language-specific neurons are also limited to LAPE, there are still gaps on the effect of amplification on neurons obtained by fine-grained attribution methods such as Integrated Gradients.
