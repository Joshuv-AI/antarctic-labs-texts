Multifunctional nanotherapeutics for targeted modulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress to potentiate cancer therapy
Authors: Shuang Tian, Jingying Wang, Yitong Yu, Yueyuan Liu, Yi Liu, Jiameng Li, Yanfei Shang, Yueyuan Zhao, Yu Zhang, Yan Fang, Kai Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), arising from the disruption of proteostasis within the tumor microenvironment, represents a fundamental driver of tumorigenesis, immune evasion and resistance against conventional therapies. In recent years, the precise modulation of ERS through the application of nanotechnology has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the molecular mechanisms underlying ERS and discusses how engineered nanotherapeutics can selectively target the endoplasmic reticulum through approaches such as ligand conjugation, peptide modification or membrane fusion to induce sustained ERS. These nanotherapeutics initiate ERS by mechanisms including calcium ion dysregulation, overproduction of reactive oxygen species and direct activation of unfolded protein response signaling pathways. Persistent ERS subsequently facilitates immunogenic cell death by promoting the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which enhance the maturation of dendritic cell and promote the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Moreover, combining ER-targeted nanotherapeutics with established therapeutic modalities, such as photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy and chemodynamic therapy, has demonstrated synergistic antitumor efficacy and improved immune responses. Despite these advances, several critical challenges remain, particularly in terms of delivery efficiency, targeting specificity and systemic biocompatibility. Future research should emphasize the integration of nanotechnology with systems immunology and cancer metabolism, as well as the incorporation of artificial intelligence and single-cell omics to optimize the design and translational potential of ER-targeted nanotherapeutics. Collectively, these interdisciplinary strategies offer considerable potential to overcome therapeutic resistance and to promote the advancement of precision oncology.Keywords: Endoplasmic reticulum stress, Nanomaterials, Immunogenic cell death, Tumor microenvironment, Cancer immunotherapy, Combination therapy

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite significant advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [1]. Conventional treatment modalities, such as surgical resection, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, have long constituted the foundation of clinical oncology. Nevertheless, these approaches are often constrained by systemic toxicity, suboptimal pharmacokinetics, and the eventual emergence of therapeutic resistance [2]. As a result, increasing attention has been directed toward the development of precision delivery systems designed to enhance tumor specificity while minimizing off-target effects, thereby improving overall clinical outcomes [3,4].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a complex and dynamic ecosystem characterized by hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress and profound metabolic reprogramming. These pathological conditions disrupt protein homeostasis within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), resulting in the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins and the subsequent activation of ER stress (ERS) [5]. At the same time, the elevated biosynthetic demands of malignant cells impose increased pressure on protein-folding machinery, thereby generating intrinsic vulnerabilities that can be exploited therapeutically [6]. This intrinsic vulnerability is further intensified by oncogene-driven constitutive signaling, which exacerbates ER overload and promotes continuous activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), an adaptive pathway designed to restore proteostasis [7]. However, persistent or unresolved ERS can shift the cellular signaling from pro-survival toward pro-apoptotic pathways, thereby presenting a compelling target for therapeutic intervention. These pathophysiological insights have stimulated the development of nanotechnology-enabled strategies for the precise modulation of ERS. Engineered nanomaterials possess the capability to directly engage ER-resident signaling pathways or enhance endogenous stress signals within the ER, thereby triggering apoptotic cascades and promoting immunogenic cell death (ICD) [8]. This dual mode of action, which induces both direct cytotoxicity and robust antitumor immune activation, underscores the potential of ERS as a central regulatory node for next-generation anticancer strategies [9,10].

Over the past decade, subcellular organelle-targeted modalities have undergone a paradigm shift, driven by the development of multifunctional nanoplatforms that exhibit substantial potential for improving oncological outcomes. These multifunctional nanotherapeutics, typically ranging from 1 to 100 nanometers in diameter, are particularly well-suited to exploit the aberrant physiology of tumors through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, while simultaneously enabling precise delivery to subcellular compartments [11,12]. These nanotherapeutics possess tunable physicochemical properties that address many limitations associated with conventional treatments, including adjustable optical characteristics, magnetic responsiveness and customizable surface chemistry, which together facilitate deep tumor penetration, improved pharmacokinetics and reduced systemic toxicity. Such attributes collectively contribute to enhanced biosafety, enhanced tumor selectivity and optimized therapeutic indices, thereby advancing both the efficacy and precision of anticancer interventions [13]. Furthermore, the integration of nanotechnology with diagnostic modalities has significantly broadened the scope of precision oncology. While traditional diagnostic techniques largely rely on imaging and histopathological evaluation, the advent of immunohistochemical profiling of tumor-specific biomarkers now enables molecular classification and predictive diagnostics, allowing for more individualized treatment planning [14,15]. Despite these advances, conventional chemotherapeutics remain limited by multidrug resistance (MDR), systemic toxicity, and nonspecific biodistribution. These challenges underscore the urgent need for the development of targeted nanotherapeutic alternatives capable of circumventing these obstacles and improving therapeutic outcomes [16,17].

Recent work has increasingly recognized that the fate of a tumor, whether it adapts or succumbs, can hinge on how ERS is managed [18]. Therefore, a key emerging strategy in cancer therapy is the precise modulation of ERS, not merely inducing stress, but finely tuning its intensity, duration and subcellular targeting, so as to flip the cellular response from survival to immunogenic death, transform tumors into “in situ vaccines”, and reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment. This review systematically evaluates the emerging field of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics, with emphasis on three interrelated therapeutic approaches. It explores how precise modulation of ERS pathways can enhance antitumor immune responses by promoting ICD and restoring immunosurveillance. It further discusses the application of photodynamic therapy (PDT) in amplifying intracellular oxidative stress, thereby inducing sustained ER dysfunction. In parallel, photothermal therapy (PTT) is examined as a strategy to disrupt ER homeostasis through localized hyperthermia, contributing to both cytotoxicity effects and immune activation. These ER-focused strategies, when integrated into rationally designed nanoplatforms, offer the potential for spatiotemporally controlled and synergistic therapeutic outcomes. The review also highlights the potential of integrating these nanotherapeutic modalities into combination therapy regimens, facilitating the strategic shift of ERS from promoting tumor survival to inducing cell death (Scheme 1). Such approaches aim to overcome existing therapeutic limitations, enhance antitumor immune responsiveness, and advance the development of personalized cancer treatments.

Roles of ERS in oncology

ERS plays a pivotal role in cancer biology by integrating signals from protein homeostasis, metabolic status, and the TME. In oncology, ERS exhibits a dual nature: while moderate or adaptive stress promotes tumor cell survival, proliferation, and therapy resistance, excessive or unresolved stress can trigger apoptotic pathways and ICD, reshaping antitumor immunity. Understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms governing ERS induction is therefore crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets [19]. Moreover, the oncologic relevance of ERS modulation extends beyond direct cytotoxicity, encompassing regulation of tumor metabolism, immune evasion and sensitivity to combination therapies. Finally, delineating the key signaling pathways that control ERS in cancer provides a rational framework for designing interventions that precisely shift ERS from pro-survival to pro-death outcomes.

Cellular and molecular mechanisms governing the induction of ERS

As a central organelle in eukaryotic cellular organization, the ER plays a pivotal role in orchestrating protein biogenesis, encompassing synthesis, folding and conformational maturation. In addition, the ER tightly regulates intracellular calcium homeostasis with precise spatiotemporal control and governs essential post-translational modifications, including N-linked glycosylation and disulfide bond formation [20,21]. Structurally, the ER membrane is continuous with the outer nuclear envelope, while its cytoplasmic surface is studded with ribosome that mediate co-translational protein import. The ER lumen maintains continuity with the perinuclear space, enabling integrated nucleocytoplasmic communication and coordination of cellular processes [22,23]. ER proteostasis is upheld by a network of proteases, oxidoreductase chaperones and calcium flux regulators, which collectively ensure proper protein quality control. However, this finely tuned balance is susceptible to disruption by physiological fluctuations or pathological insults, leading to a condition known as ERS [24,25]. Under such stress conditions, the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins activates the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway, which retrotranslocates aberrant peptides to the cytoplasm for ubiquitin-proteasome mediated degradation [26,27]. In cancer cells, factors such as genetic instability, transcriptional dysregulation and metabolic reprogramming generate a hostile microenvironmental that sustains chronic ERS [28,29]. To mitigate such proteotoxic burden, eukaryotic cells have evolved the UPR, a conserved ER-nuclear signaling axis that transiently suppresses global protein translation synthesis, reprograms gene expression, and orchestrates cell fate decisions to either restore ER homeostasis or initiate apoptotic pathways.

Protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) constitute the triad of transmembrane ERS sensors in mammalian cells. Under homeostatic conditions, these sensors remain in an inactive state through their association with the chaperone BiP (also known as GRP78) [30]. Upon the onset of ERS, BiP exhibits increased affinity for misfolded proteins, leading to its dissociation from the sensors and enabling their activation through oligomerization, autophosphorylation or proteolytic cleavage, depending on the specific signaling branch [31]. The resulting UPR is initially adaptive, functioning to restore proteostasis and preserve cellular viability. However, when ERS is prolonged or exceeds the cellular buffering capacity, the UPR shifts into a terminal phase characterized by the activation of pro-apoptotic signaling pathways and the onset of irreversible cellular damage [32].

In cancer, hyperproliferative activity gives rise to a TME characterized by elevated metabolic demands, hypoxia, nutrient depletion and extracellular acidosis [33]. These pathological conditions, together with lipid imbalance and calcium dyshomeostasis, impose substantial ERS not only on tumor cells but also on adjacent stromal and immune cell populations. In particular, the protein-folding capacity of the ER is markedly compromised in both malignant cells and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, leading to sustained and compartmentalized activation of ERS signaling pathways [34]. This pervasive proteotoxic burden throughout the TME highlights the therapeutic importance of targeting ERS pathways to achieve more effective cancer treatment.

Oncologic relevance of ERS modulation

ERS represents a proteostatic crisis that results from an imbalance between the cellular demand for protein folding and the processing capacity of the ER. This evolutionarily conserved stress response initiates a biphasic regulatory program in which the initial adaptive phase, mediated by UPR signaling, promotes the restoration of proteostasis, while sustained or excessive stress beyond compensatory thresholds leads to the activation of apoptotic cell death pathways. Within the TME, ERS triggered by hypoxia or nutrient deprivation activates several protein quality control pathways, including the translocation of misfolded proteins from the ER to the cytosol for degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, the engagement of ERAD through recruitment of the 26S proteasome, and the induction of autophagy-lysosomal pathways that facilitate large-scale clearance of organelles [35,36]. The UPR enables cells to adapt to proteostatic stress by dynamically regulating the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins. The accuracy and responsiveness of this signaling axis are critical determines of cellular survival during ERS. Mechanistically, the UPR is mediated by a tripartite network of transmembrane sensors. IRE1α facilitates the unconventional splicing of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA. PERK phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) to attenuate global protein synthesis. ATF6 is activated through regulated proteolytic cleavage, allowing it to translocate to the nucleus and initiate transcription of stress-responsive genes [37,38]. The ERAD machineryplays a central role in resolving ERS by facilitating the clearance of misfolded proteins. However, when activation of the UPR is prolonged, it can paradoxically intensify ERS through dysregulated inter-organelle communication. Emerging evidence indicates that sustained UPR signaling in tumor cells may propagate stress signals to adjacent cells within the TME, including stromal and immune populations, thereby disrupting local immune function and contributing to immune evasion [39].

ERS exerts a dichotomous influence on tumor cell behavior. Under moderate or transient stress conditions, activation of the PERK-eIF2α, IRE1α-XBP1 and ATF6 signaling axes promotes an adaptive response. This includes the upregulation of molecular chaperones such as GRP78, attenuation of global protein synthesis and induction of autophagy. Together, these mechanisms enhance the ability of neoplastic cells to withstand unfavorable microenvironmental conditions, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation and exposure to chemotherapeutic agents. Concurrently, activation of the IRE1α-XBP1 signaling axis contributes to immune modulation within the TME by promoting the differentiation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and dendritic cells (DCs) toward a tolerogenic phenotype. This pathway also enhances the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and stimulates the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokins, thereby facilitating the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME [40,41]. In contrast, under conditions of persistent or excessive ERS, the cellular response shifts from adaptive survival to programmed cell death. This transition is primarily mediated by the upregulation of C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), a pro-apoptotic transcription factor downstream of the PERK signaling pathway. CHOP activation promotes intracellular calcium efflux, increases the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and triggers the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade. These events ultimately lead to apoptotic or pyroptotic cell death, depending on the cellular context and the severity of the stress. TRAF3-interacting protein 3 (TRAF3IP3) has been reported to drive excessive ERS in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells through activation of the PERK-ATF4-CHOP signaling axis, ultimately leading to apoptotic cell death [42]. Concurrently, ERS fpromotes the translocation of calreticulin to the cell surface, the extracellular release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and the efflux of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). These damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) collectively facilitate the recruitment and activation of DCs and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), thereby initiating a potent antitumor immune response [43,44]. These dichotomous outcomes highlight the therapeutic potential of finely modulating the intensity of ERS. Moderate activation of ERS may support tumor immune evasion and contribute to therapeutic resistance. In contrast, deliberate amplification of ERS beyond the adaptive threshold can induce selective tumor cell death while concurrently promoting antitumor immune responses, thereby offering a promising strategy for cancer therapy.

ERS orchestrates a complex immune evasion program within the TME by reprogramming diverse immune cell populations. In T lymphocytes, sustained activation of the PERK-eIF2α-CHOP signaling cascade leads to global suppression of protein translational and upregulation of inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) (Fig. 1). These changes contribute to T cell exhaustion and impaired effector function, ultimately weakening antitumor immunity [45]. In TAMs, activation of the IRE1α-XBP1 signaling pathway promotes polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype. This state is characterized by elevated secretion of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), both of which contribute to the suppression of local immune responses [46]. In DCs, ERS-driven activation of IRE1α initiates regulated IRE1-dependent mRNA decay (RIDD), resulting in the degradation of transcripts encoding major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules and co-stimulatory surface proteins. This impairs antigen presentation capacity and disrupts the priming of effective T cell responses [47]. And in neutrophils, ERS triggers the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETosis) and facilitates the release of immunosuppressive mediators that interfere with the cytotoxic functions of effector lymphocytes [48]. Collectively, these ERS-mediated alterations across diverse immune cell populations contribute to the establishment of a profoundly immunosuppressive TME, which facilitates immune evasion and supports sustained tumor progression.

Signaling pathway for modulating ERS in oncology

Multiple signaling pathways converge to regulate ERS in cancer cells, including the PERK‑eIF2α‑ATF4, IRE1α‑XBP1, and ATF6 branches of the unfolded protein response, with BiP/GRP78 serving as a central sensor and chaperone that modulates these pathways. Fine‑tuning the activity of BiP and its downstream signaling allows selective activation of pro-death or immunogenic programs, providing a mechanistic basis for designing targeted nanotherapeutics and combination strategies that shift ERS from a tumor-promoting to a tumor-suppressing role (Table 1).

BiP in ERS signaling

The mammalian ER harbors two major molecular chaperones, BiP and GRP78, both of which are constitutively expressed under normal physiological conditions and are markedly upregulated in response to proteostatic disturbances. BiP, a member of the heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) family, possesses an N-terminal ER-targeting signal sequence that distinguishes it from its cytosolic homologs. In cancer cells, the expression of BiP is significantly upregulated during ERS, and the protein undergoes stress-dependent nucleo-cytoplasmic translocation [49]. Under homeostatic conditions, BiP functions as a key regulatory chaperone by maintaining the transmembrane stress sensors IRE1α, PERK and ATF6α in an autoinhibited state through stoichiometric binding. Upon the onset of ERS, BiP dissociates from these sensors due to its preferential binding to accumulated unfolded proteins, leading to the activation of the three major signaling cascades of the UPR. The determination of cell fate, between adaptive survival and programmed apoptosis, is tightly regulated by the UPR signaling axis. This regulatory system functions as a stress rheostat that integrates both the severity of proteostatic disruption and the duration of ERS signaling. Sustained activation of the UPR, typically extending beyond 6 h, often shifts this regulatory balance toward pro-apoptotic signaling. This mechanistic transition has important implications for therapeutic strategies that seek to exploit ERS in cancer treatment [31,50,51]. For example, NP-Co2 is a cobalt (III)-cyclolamin-based prodrug nanoparticle encapsulated within a poly(2-HD-co-HPMDA)-mPEG (PHHM) copolymer, which enables selective drug release in response to the elevated glutathione (GSH) levels characteristic of the TME. Upon systemic administration, NP-Co₂ preferentially accumulates in the ER, where it induces robust ERS, as evidenced by significant upregulation of BiP expression and activation of the canonical BiP/p-PERK/p-eIF2α/CHOP signaling cascade. The resulting ERS promotes ICD, which enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) by reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment and facilitating the transition of immunologically “cold” tumors into a “hot” phenotype. This combined therapeutic strategy leads to substantial suppression of breast cancer growth and metastasis [52].

PERK-eIF2α pathway in ERS signaling

Phosphorylation of eIF2α at the evolutionarily conserved Ser51 residue represents a key molecular hallmark of ERS activation. This modification leads to global attenuation of protein translation while simultaneously permitting the selective translation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) mRNA through a mechanism known as leaky ribosomal scanning [53]. Histone deacetylases (HDACs), which catalyze the reversible removal of acetyl groups from both histone and non-histone proteins, have attracted increasing interest as therapeutic targets owing to their ability to remodel epigenetic landscapes and alter transcriptional programs in malignancies. Recent studies suggested that treatment of breast cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma cells with specific HDAC inhibitors induces phosphorylation of eIF2α at Ser51 through activation of the PERK pathway-mediated. This is followed by upregulation of activating ATF4 and its downstream effectors, ultimately leading to apoptosis or cell cycle arrest [54]. Accumulating evidence indicates that HDAC inhibitors exert broad-spectrum antitumor effects, including the induction of cell cycle arrest, activation of mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic pathways, and suppression of both metastatic dissemination and tumor-associated angiogenesis [55]. For example, in p53-deficient human colon carcinoma cells, pharmacological inhibition of HDACs has been shown to activate the ERS sensor PERK, leading to its autophosphorylation and subsequent phosphorylation of eIF2α at Ser51 residue. Activation of the PERK-eIF2α axis initiates a transcriptional cascade involving ATF4, ATF3 and CHOP, which collectively driving apoptosis through intrinsic pro-death signaling pathways.

The PERK signaling axis plays a central role in coordinating cellular adaptive responses to ERS through the modulation of redox homeostasis and attenuation of global protein synthesis [56,57]. During ERS, the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins within the ER lumen triggers the dissociation of the molecular chaperone BiP/GRP78 from PERK, facilitating its homodimerization and subsequent autophosphorylation [58]. Upon activation, PERK phosphorylates eIF2α at Ser51, thereby redirecting translation toward a subset of stress-responsive mRNAs, most notably that of ATF4 [59]. ATF4 subsequently induces the expression of CHOP, a pro-apoptotic transcription factor that integrates ERS signals into apoptotic outcomes under conditions of sustained or irreversible stress [60,61]. In addition to its role in apoptosis, phosphorylated eIF2α has been shown to enhance the translocation of calreticulin to the cell surface, thereby facilitating ICD and promoting antitumor immune responses, which are associated with improved clinical outcomes [62,63]. In addition to its role in promoting apoptosis, the ATF4-CHOP signaling axis also contributes to metabolic reprogramming and promotes autophagic flux, serving as a secondary adaptation mechanism under conditions of chronic stress [64,65]. The functional duality of the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway has been elucidated in genetic models deficient in PERK, eIF2α or ATF4, where ERS fails to induce CHOP expression, thereby impairing the downstream execution of apoptosis [[66], [67], [68]]. These findings highlight the context-dependent nature of PERK signaling, which can shift from transient activation that favors cell survival through translational reprogramming, to sustained activation that drives programmed cell death via CHOP-mediated transcriptional programs. This functional bifurcation renders the PERK pathway as a critical regulatory hub that links ER homeostasis to cancer cell fate determination and therapeutic outcomes.

ICD functions as a critical interface linking tumor cell apoptosis to the initiation of effective antitumor immune responses [69]. Recent evidence has identified the PERK signaling axis as a key modulator of ER proteostasis and immune evasion within the TME. Specifically, genetic ablation of PERK in B16-F10 melanoma cells resulted in a 3.2-fold increase in the expression of MHC class I and II antigen presentation machinery, thereby enhancing tumor immunogenicity and potentially improving immune recognition. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of PERK using the selective inhibitor GSK2656157 in immunocompetent syngeneic mouse models elicited a substantial expansion of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ CTLs and yielded a 68% reduction in tumor burden compared to control treatments [70]. In addition to its direct effects on tumor cells, PERK signaling plays a pivotal role in regulating the composition and functional state of tumor-associated myeloid populations, thereby contributing to the remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME. Targeted deletion of PERK in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) facilitated their differentiation into immunostimulatory myeloid subsets, which in turn augmented CD8⁺ T cell-mediated tumor clearance [71]. Similarly, inhibition of PERK in TAMs reprograms their immunosuppressive phenotype toward a pro-inflammatory state, thereby enhancing T cell infiltration and attenuating tumor progression [72]. Mechanistically, pharmacological blockade of PERK interferes with ERS adaptation in malignant cells, leading to reduced tumor cell viability and the induction of ICD. This immunogenic cascade is characterized by the production of type I interferons (IFNs) by tumor-infiltrating DCs, which subsequently promote the activation and clonal expansion of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T CTLs.

IRE1α-XBP1 pathway in ERS signaling

IRE1α represents the most evolutionarily conserved sensor within the UPR and functions as a bifunctional type I transmembrane protein. It integrates a luminal domain that detects ERS with cytosolic serine/threonine kinase and endoribonuclease (RNase) domains that mediate downstream signaling events [73,74]. Under conditions of ERS, the accumulation of misfolded proteins leads to the dissociation of the ER chaperone BiP from the luminal domain of IRE1α. Which facilitates IRE1α oligomerization and trans-autophosphorylation, resulting in its conformational activation. The activated IRE1α then initiates its RNase function, leading to the unconventional splicing of XBP1 mRNA and the production of its transcriptionally active spliced isoform (XBP1s) [75]. The XBP1s also contributes to the induction of pro-apoptotic mediators, including CHOP which functions as a critical determinant of cell fate under conditions of sustained ERS [[76], [77], [78]]. While CHOP is expressed at low basal levels under physiological conditions, its transcriptional upregulation through the IRE1α-XBP1 signaling axis transforms it into a molecular switch that marks the transition from adaptive to apoptotic signaling [[79], [80], [81]]. Functionally, CHOP promotes apoptosis through a dual mechanism involving the suppression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, such as BCL-2 and MCL-1, and the amplification of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling. These pathways converge to drive mitochondrial dysfunction and trigger cell death [82,83].

In addition to its canonical role in mediating XBP1 mRNA splicing, activated IRE1α also participates in inflammatory and apoptotic signaling cascades through its interaction with TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2). This interaction facilitates the recruitment and activation of apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1), which in turn phosphorylates and activates downstream mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK), including JNK and p38. The resulting signaling cascade establishes a regulatory platform that modulates the balance between autophagy and apoptosis in a context-dependent manner [84]. Under conditions of persistent or irreparable ERS, IRE1α becomes hyperactivated and undergoes extensive oligomerization, facilitating its functional shift from a pro-survival mediator to a pro-apoptotic effector via regulated RIDD. By selectively cleaving a subset of ER-associated mRNAs and microRNAs, RIDD contributes to the attenuation of global protein synthesis and depletes transcripts encoding proteins essential for cell survival. Ultimately, this maladaptive branch of IRE1α signalingleads to the activation of caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways, thereby committing the cellular decision toward death in response to sustained ERS [85,86].

The IRE1α-XBP1 signaling axis plays a critical role in regulating tumor-associated immune responses, particularly by modulating the phenotype and function of innate immune cells within the TME. Genetic deletion of Xbp1 has been shown to enhance the phagocytic capacity of TAMs and compromise tumor cell viability in murine models of colorectal cancer (CRC), thereby revealing an immunosuppressive role of XBP1 within this specific tumor context [87]. Although prevailing studies suggest that activation of the IRE1α-XBP1s branch of the UPR facilitates tumor progression by alleviating ERS and promoting cellular adaptation, emerging evidence indicates that the functional outcomes of this pathway display considerable heterogeneity across different cancer types [88]. For instance, enforced overexpression of the spliced form of XBP1 has been shown to paradoxically suppresses tumor growth in xenograft models of germinal center B-cell-like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (GCB-DLBCL), which stands in stark contrast to its well-documented pro-tumorigenic role in other malignancies [89]. This functional divergence suggests that the biological outcomes of IRE1α-XBP1 signaling are not universally oncogenic, but rather depend on multiple contextual factors, including the intensity and duration of ERS, as well as the immunological and metabolic characteristics of the TME [90]. XBP1 expression can enforce differentiation programs and restrict malignant proliferation, highlighting the lineage-specific nature of UPR outcomes. In contrast, tumors experiencing severe hypoxic stress tend to rely more heavily on the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis to maintain redox balance and translational homeostasis, and recent in vivo studies demonstrate that loss or pharmacological inhibition of PERK can shift stressed tumor cells toward ICD, thereby enhancing T cell–mediated tumor clearance [91]. Moreover, recent research indicates that context-dependent activation of ERS pathways, including IRE1α–XBP1, can either suppress or promote anti-tumor immunity depending on tumor type and microenvironmental conditions, underscoring the necessity of tailoring ERS-targeted strategies and nanotherapeutic design to specific cancer contexts [92].

Building on this paradigm, recent therapeutic innovations have sought to exploit the dual role of IRE1α-XBP1 signaling in immune modulation and redox homeostasis. One such innovation involves the development of a nanoemulsion formulation termed as α-T-K, designed for the concurrent inhibition of ERS and oxidative stress. This formulation co-encapsulates KIRA6, a selective inhibitor of IRE1α RNase activity, and α-tocopherol, a lipid-soluble antioxidant, within a biocompatible nanoemulsion delivery system [93]. Treatment with α-T-K has been shown to markedly reduce the expression of ERS markers, including XBP1, thereby confirming its capacity to attenuate UPR signaling. Concurrently, this dual blockade induces profound metabolic reprogramming within TAMs, driving their polarization from an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype toward a pro-inflammatory M1-like state. This phenotypic shift enhances antigen presentation, facilitates infiltration of CTLs, and stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Functionally, α-T-K not only delays tumor progression in hypoxic tumor models but also significantly enhances the antitumor efficacy of PD-1 ICB [94].

ATF6 in ERS signaling

As an ER-resident type II transmembrane sensor, ATF6 functions as a key transducer of the UPR. Under conditions of ERS, dissociation of the molecular chaperone BiP from the luminal domain of ATF6 facilitates its translocation to the Golgi apparatus, where it undergoes sequential proteolytic cleavage of its transmembrane domain by site-1 and site-2 proteases [95]. This processing event releases the cytosolic basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor domain, which subsequently translocates into the nucleus to initiate transcriptional programs that promote the restoration of ER homeostasis [[96], [97], [98]]. Once translocated into the nucleus, the cleaved ATF6 fragment binds directly to ERS response elements (ERSEs) within the promoters of target genes. This binding induces the transcriptional upregulation of various ER-resident chaperones and protein foldases, which collectively enhance the protein folding capacity of the ER and relieve proteotoxic stress [99]. In parallel, ATF6 promotes the expression of other key components of the UPR, including CHOP and XBP1, through ERSE-dependent transcriptional reprogramming [100,101]. Importantly, ATF6 also contributes indirectly to the regulation of CHOP by enhancing XBP1 transcription, thereby establishing a hierarchical regulatory framework that integrates multiple UPR branches. This coordinated network enables cells to balance adaptive and apoptotic responses in the context of persistent or severe ERS.

As a representative example of nanotechnology-based therapeutic strategies, the PAZ@Fe-MOF nanocomposite has been developed through the coordination of Fe(III)-tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (TCPP), ZrOCl2 and benzoic acid, enabling the encapsulation of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor pazopanib (PAZ) within its framework [102]. This engineered nanostructure features a highly porous architecture that affords both high drug-loading capacity and pH-responsive release behavior, thereby ensuring selective activation within the acidic TME. Under acidic conditions, the Fe-based metal-organic framework (MOF) catalyzes the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), leading to the generation of ROS and resulting in potent cytotoxic effects that facilitate tumor cell eradication. Upon cellular internalization by M2-polarized macrophages, PAZ@Fe-MOF preferentially accumulates within the ER, where it mediates a significant downregulation of ATF6 expression. This suppression of ATF6 signaling disrupts the downstream TGFBR1-SMAD3 pathway, leading to reduced transcription of TXNDC5 which is a key mediator of M2 macrophage polarization. As a result, the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs is effectively inhibited, and the macrophage population undergoes functional reprogramming toward an immunostimulatory state. In addition, the MOF-based nanoagent enables dual mitochondrial damage by simultaneously promoting oxidative stress and calcium overload, thereby enhancing its efficacy in synergistic antitumor therapy.

A growing body of evidence highlights that ERS signaling exhibits substantial heterogeneity across cancer types, which has critical implications for the design and application of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics. In solid tumors such as CRC, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), chronic ERS is typically induced by a hostile TME, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress. In CRC and NSCLC, the IRE1α-XBP1 axis predominantly functions in a pro-tumorigenic manner, promoting survival, angiogenesis, and therapy resistance. In contrast, PDAC is more reliant on the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 axis for maintaining adaptive homeostasis, making this pathway a viable target for therapeutic disruption. Meanwhile, hematological malignancies display markedly different ERS dynamics. In multiple myeloma, which is characterized by high secretory demands, the IRE1α-XBP1 axis is constitutively activated and essential for cell survival, rendering it highly susceptible to pharmacological inhibition. Interestingly, in GCB-DLBCL, the same IRE1α-XBP1 pathway has been implicated in anti-tumorigenic functions, potentially through the enhancement of ICD and suppression of proliferation. Furthermore, basal expression levels of ERS markers such as GRP78/BiP and CHOP differ significantly between tumor types, with solid tumors often exhibiting high GRP78 as a protective adaptation and hematologic malignancies showing elevated CHOP under stress, which predisposes them to apoptosis. These divergent ERS profiles underscore the necessity of tailoring nanotherapeutic interventions to the dominant UPR branches specific to each cancer type. For instance, nanocarriers designed to inhibit PERK may be more effective in hypoxic solid tumors like PDAC, whereas those modulating IRE1α signaling could be more appropriate for treating myeloma. Conversely, in cancers where sustained ERS is detrimental to tumor viability, strategies that amplify or prolong ERS may be beneficial.

Approaches for targeting ERS in cancer

As a central organelle in eukaryotic cells, the ER undergoes stress when the accumulation of misfolded proteins disrupts proteostasis, thereby activating the UPR and potentially leading to apoptosis. This tightly regulated process has been closely associated with anticancer therapeutic strategies, inspiring the development of ER-targeted drug delivery systems designed to exploit ERS for selective tumor cytotoxicity. Recent advances in this field have leveraged a diverse array of nanomaterials, including organic, inorganic and biologically derived nanoparticles, to perturb ER homeostasis and induce ERS in malignant cells, ultimately enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Efforts to target ERS typically begin with the identification of ER-localized molecular signatures, followed by the development of targeted delivery strategies based on a range of small molecules or peptides (Table 2). For instance, methyl sulfonamide derivatives serve as ER-targeting moieties by selectively binding to sulfonamide receptors located on the ER membrane. Similarly, phosphoethanolamine facilitates ER localization through low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor-mediated endocytosis. In addition to these small-molecule approaches, several peptide-based ligands possess intrinsic ER-targeting capabilities, enabling precise cargo delivery through specific interactions with ER-associated receptors or intracellular transport mechanisms. These delivery strategies, which rely on molecular recognition, receptor-mediated trafficking and peptide-receptor interactions, collectively illustrate the versatility and adaptability of ER-targeted platforms in the field of nanomedicine [117].

Small molecule-based ligand for ER targeting

Targeting the ER has emerged as a compelling therapeutic strategy for inducing apoptosis and enhancing antitumor immune responses. Engineered nanotherapeutics functionalized with small-molecule ligands enable precise ER localization, thereby facilitating controlled induction of ERS and ICD. For example, the alkylphospholipid analog edelfosine preferentially accumulates in the ER of PDAC cells, where it induces sustained ERS and activates CHOP-dependent apoptotic signaling, thereby underscoring its therapeutic potential in ER-targeted cancer therapy [118,119]. Similarly, sulfonyl-based ligands have been shown to leverage ATP-sensitive potassium channels located on the ER membrane to achieve high targeting specificity [120]. Beyond direct molecular targeting, surface-functionalized nanomaterials have emerged as versatile platforms for achieving more precise subcellular delivery to the ER. For instance, stimuli-responsive polymeric nanoparticles such as Ds-sP/TCPP-TER NPs, have been engineered by integrating the ER-targeting ligand tetraethylenepentamine (TER) with the photosensitizer TCPP, thereby enabling precise ER localization and enhancing the induction of ERS along with the associated therapeutic effects [121].

Vitamin E (α-tocopherol) has also been employed as a small-molecule modulator to facilitate the selective accumulation of nanotherapeutics within the ER [122]. A redox-responsive tocopheryl-SS-DM1 conjugate (Toc-SS-DM1) was synthesized and incorporated into PEGylated phospholipid nanovesicles functionalized with a pH-low insertion peptide (pHLIP) which is designed to respond to both the acidic TME and elevated intracellular GSH levels. Upon internalization via pHLIP-mediated membrane insertion, the lipophilic tocopherol moiety facilitates membrane intercalation and directs targeted accumulation within the ER, where tocopherol-induced ROS production further amplifies ERS and extends intrareticular retention [123]. Building upon these advances, a pH- and cathepsin B-activatable nanoplatform (PCANER) has been developed to enable spatiotemporally controlled induction of ERS and ICD [124]. This system is constructed by conjugating an ER-targeted photosensitizer PPaER, derived from pheophorbide-a (PPa) modified with a p-nitrobenzenesulfonamide moiety, to an ultra pH-sensitive amphiphilic block copolymer via a cathepsin B-cleavable spacer (Fig. 2A). The resulting micelles remain stable during circulation but undergo acid-triggered disassembly within the TME, followed by cathepsin B-mediated cleavage and subsequent release of PPaER. After lysosomal trafficking and relocation to the ER, irradiation with NIR initiates sequential lysosomal disruption and pronounced ERS, thereby activating GRP78-dependent type II ICD and promoting pyroptotic cell death.

Protein-based NanoICD systems have further expanded the potential of ER-targeted therapies through a modular design framework [125]. In this system, acrylamide functions as a neutral polymerization backbone to maintain colloidal stability, while cationic monomer N-(3-aminopropyl)-methacrylamide facilitates cellular uptake and promotes lysosomal escape via the proton sponge effect. ER targeting is achieved through the integration of N-(2-((4-methylphenyl)sulfonamido)ethyl)acrylamide, a ligand that directs nanotherapeutics localization to the ER lumen. To maintain structural integrity and prolong ERS signaling, the supramolecular architecture is further stabilized through crosslinking with N,N'-methylenebisacrylamide (Fig. 2B). Importantly, co-encapsulation of catalase within this system enables the decomposition of immunosuppressive tumor-derived H2O2, thereby contributing to the reprogramming of TME, enhancing CTLs infiltration and mitigating PD-1/PD-L1-mediated immune evasion [126].

Given that over 50% of malignancies harbor mutations in p53, which disrupt immune regulation and contribute to chemoresistance, strategies aimed at inducing ERS while simultaneously degrading mutant p53 (mutp53) have garnered considerable interest [127]. Although ERS-induced apoptosis typically proceeds via p53-independent pathways, the presence of mutp53 can exert dominant-negative effects that impair ERS-mediated cell death responses [128]. To address this, a prodrug-based nanoparticle (FP NPs) has been engineered through the self-assembly of dichlorinated cisplatin and fluplastatin, offering a dual mechanism of action targeting both mutp53 degradation and ERS amplification [129]. These nanoparticles are designed to target the ER and preferentially accumulate within the ER lumen through fluplastatin-mediated localization. Upon accumulation, the system induces ROS-mediated ERS, promotes CHOP/HSP70-dependent degradation of mutp53, and triggers DNA mismatch-associated apoptosis pathways.

This section highlights the application of small-molecule ligands for ER targeting, underscoring their structural tunability, favorable membrane permeability, and ease of incorporation into nanoplatforms. These properties make them attractive tools for modular design and functional enhancement of ER-targeted nanotherapeutics. However, despite these advantages, the ER specificity of small-molecule ligands remains suboptimal. Their targeting precision is often compromised by metabolic instability and off-target distribution in vivo, which may diminish therapeutic efficacy and increase systemic toxicity. These limitations point to a critical need for the development of ligands with improved selectivity, enhanced metabolic stability, and more robust in vivo validation to fully realize their potential in ER-targeted nanomedicine.

Peptide-based ligand for ER targeting

Peptide-based targeting ligands have emerged as a versatile and highly specific approach for subcellular delivery, including precise localization to the ER. Comprising short amino acid sequences, these ligands can be rationally designed or identified through combinatorial screening techniques such as phage display to selectively recognize receptors or retention signals associated with ER membranes. Among the most widely employed motifs is the KDEL sequence (Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu), which binds to KDEL receptors to facilitate the retention or retrieval of proteins and therapeutic agents within the ER lumen. When conjugated to nanotherapeutics, KDEL and other ER-affinitive peptides facilitate efficient ER localization by leveraging the retrograde transport pathway from the Golgi apparatus back to the ER [130]. A representative example of this strategy is the development of a cancer cell membrane (CCM)-coated biomimetic nanoparticle system, termed EMPP-LOV, designed for the encapsulation and targeted delivery of lovastatin (LOV) [131]. Incorporating the KDEL peptide, this nanotherapeutics facilitates precise subcellular localization within carcinoma cells (Fig. 3A). Following efficient internalization and escape from lysosomal degradation, the nanotherapeutics preferentially accumulate within the ER, where they induce sustained ERS and activate pro-apoptotic signaling cascades, ultimately leading to apoptotic cell death.

The tumor suppressor protein p53 functions as a central regulator of apoptosis in response to DNA damage and cellular stress [132]. However, its therapeutic efficacy in cancer treatment is often compromised by the hypoxic conditions of the TME, which lead to p53 destabilization and functional inactivation [133]. To address this challenge, an ER-targeted liposomal delivery system, termed PAR-Lipo, was designed by incorporating the cationic peptide pardaxin (Par) into the lipid bilayer. This modification facilitates clathrin-mediated endocytosis, enabling the efficient co-delivery of plasmid DNA and ERS-inducing stimuli directly to the ER compartment. Upon internalization, the ER-targeted delivery of PAR-Lipo induces ERS, which promotes ER membrane expansion and enhances the spatial proximity between the ER and the nuclear envelope. Despite its effectiveness, the functionality of PAR-Lipo is significantly compromised under hypoxic tumor conditions, where gene delivery efficiency is markedly reduced due to oxygen-dependent degradation of p53 protein [134]. To address this limitation, a self-oxygenating nanoplatform, termed PFOB@Lipo, was subsequently developed [135]. This system encapsulates perfluorooctyl bromide (PFOB), a perfluorocarbon compound capable of dissolving and storing molecular oxygen, into Par-functionalized liposomes (Fig. 3B). Upon exposure to low-frequency ultrasound selectively applied at the tumor site, PFOB releases its stored oxygen, thereby alleviating local hypoxia through targeted reoxygenation. This reoxygenation strategy restores the functional stability of p53 and acts synergistically with PAR-Lipo-induced ERS to enhance the overall therapeutic efficacy of the nanotherapeutics.

The nanoplatform CB + miR + R/PGA-SLN—CSW is constructed from solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) that co-encapsulate three therapeutic agents, including a selective inhibitor (CB-5083) of valosin-containing protein (VCP), a microRNA (miR-142) known to suppress PD-L1 expression, and a Toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist (R848) that functions as an immune adjuvant. These SLNs are further coated with a pH-responsive polyglutamic acid-polyethylene glycol (PGA-PEG) shell and surface functionalized with a cocktail of tumor-targeting peptides directed against EGFR, PD-L1 and the estrogen receptor (Fig. 3C) [136]. Upon exposure to the acidic TME, the coating undergoes conformational changes that facilitate controlled release of the therapeutic payload and expose the surface-bound peptides, thereby enhancing tumor-specific cellular uptake via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Following internalization, CB-5083 inhibits the ATPase activity of VCP, leading to the accumulation of misfolded proteins and the subsequent induction of ERS.

Peptide-based ligands offer high specificity for ER targeting and have demonstrated robust ERS-mediated therapeutic effects across diverse nanoplatforms. Their modularity, biocompatibility, and ability to exploit endogenous transport pathways make them particularly attractive for subcellular delivery applications. However, their clinical potential remains constrained by several factors, including limited peptide stability in vivo, variability in receptor expression across tumor types, and the synthetic complexity associated with constructing multifunctional nanoparticle systems. Moving forward, efforts to improve ligand stability, streamline nanoparticle synthesis, and conduct comprehensive in vivo validation will be essential for translating peptide-based ER-targeting strategies into clinically viable therapeutics.

Biological membrane functionalization strategies for ER targeting

Modulating intracellular trafficking pathways has emerged as a compelling approach to address persistent barriers in the development of effective siRNA delivery systems [[137], [138], [139]]. A representative example involves the construction of an ER membrane-decorated hybrid nanocomplex (EhCv/siRNA), which significantly enhances siRNA transfection efficiency and gene-silencing potency [140]. In this system, a cationic siRNA carrier (Cv) is cloaked with ER-derived membranes, allowing the nanoparticles to hijack endogenous ER-Golgi-ER recycling pathway instead of the canonical endosome-lysosome route (Fig. 4A). This alternative trafficking route allows the system to bypasses lysosomal degradation, thereby increasing cytosolic availability of siRNA and improving gene silencing outcomes. Mechanistic investigations further revealed that ER-resident membrane proteins not only facilitated cellular uptake but also redirected intracellular trafficking to favor ER-Golgi cycling.

Expanding upon this intracellular targeting concept, several nanoplatforms have been rationally designed to deliver therapeutic agents directly to the ER lumen, where they exacerbate protein misfolding and intensify ERS, ultimately inducing selective apoptotic responses in tumor cells [141]. For instance, shape-engineered nanocarriers such as CCM-coated nanorods (CRs) and nanospheres (CSs) exhibit distinct intracellular behaviors and therapeutic outcomes [142]. These particles enter cells primarily via caveolin-mediated endocytosis, with CRs exhibiting preferential accumulation within the ER, thereby enabling efficient delivery of DOX to the nucleus (Fig. 4B). Building on the concept of ERS-induced immunogenicity, a redox-responsive nanoplatform (GCT@CM NPs) was developed by encapsulating phosphorus dendrimer-copper(II) complexes (1G3—Cu) and toyocamycin within a CCM-cloaked polymeric matrix (Fig. 4C) [143]. These nanoparticles remain inert under physiological conditions but undergo disassembly in the reductive TME, resulting in GSH depletion and the controlled release of Toy. Simultaneously, the 1G3—Cu component serves as a T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agent, while the CCM coating facilitates homotypic tumor targeting, enhancing selective accumulation at the tumor site.

Leveraging the intrinsic fusogenic compatibility between liposomes and ER membranes, ER-mimicking liposomes have been formulated using a customized phospholipid composition that closely resembles the native ER membrane [144,145]. A novel class of ER-liposomes was formulated employing a tailored lipid composition consisting of phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidylserine [146]. These ER-liposomes exhibit selective tropism for ER membranes and associated intracellular networks, entering cells predominantly through scavenger receptor- and LDL receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once internalized, they undergo intracellular trafficking via a microtubule-dependent retrograde pathway, with sequential localization to early endosomes and the Golgi apparatus prior to ER targeting.

Biological membrane functionalization offers a powerful strategy for precise intracellular trafficking, enabling nanotherapeutics to leverage endogenous ER-targeting pathways for enhanced therapeutic delivery. By mimicking natural membrane components, these systems can achieve improved biocompatibility, immune evasion, and subcellular specificity. However, practical challenges such as the complexity of membrane isolation, batch-to-batch variability, and difficulties in large-scale production pose significant barriers to clinical translation. Moving forward, future efforts must strike a balance between biomimetic design and manufacturability, with a focus on improving reproducibility, stability, and scalability to advance membrane-functionalized ER-targeting nanomedicines toward clinical application.

Synergistic mechanisms of targeting ERS in cancer therapy

Tumor cells exhibit elevated levels of ERS, primarily due to oncogene-driven hyperactivation of protein synthesis and increased secretory demands within the TME [[147], [148], [149], [150]]. These conditions overwhelm the protein-folding capacity of the ER, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins and subsequent activation of the UPR. Expression levels of UPR-related markers serve as reliable indicators of ERS severity and have been closely associated with tumor immunogenicity. To therapeutically exploit this vulnerability, various nanomaterials have been engineered to specifically target the ER. These systems employ either active targeting strategies based on ligand-receptor recognition or passive targeting approaches that leverage the unique physicochemical characteristics of the TME. By directing therapeutic agents to the ER, these nanotherapeutics are capable of deliberately inducing ERS. Moreover, combinatorial strategies that integrate ERS-inducing nanotherapeutics with ICB or other immunotherapies have demonstrated significant potential in improving ICD and eliciting robust antitumor immune responses (Table 3). These synergistic approaches offer a compelling paradigm for enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy by harnessing ERS as both a cytotoxic stimulus and a modulator of antitumor immune responses (Fig. 5).

ERS-mediated calcium dyshomeostasis

Intracellular calcium ions (Ca2+) function as highly dynamic and versatile secondary messengers that regulate a broad spectrum of fundamental cellular processes, including proliferation, metabolism, differentiation, and programmed cell death. The maintenance of calcium homeostasis depends on the precise spatial and temporal regulation of Ca2+ concentrations across various subcellular compartments, which is achieved through coordinated mechanisms such as ER-mediated calcium release, extracellular calcium influx, and calcium-dependent activation of intracellular signaling cascades that modulate gene expression, enzymatic activity, and organelle function. Disruptions in calcium signaling perturbs these regulatory networks, thereby triggering cellular stress responses or contributing to the onset of pathological conditions. This dual functionality highlights the role of Ca2+ not only as a pivotal mediator of physiological signaling but also as a potential therapeutic target in pathologies characterized by calcium dysregulation, such as cancer [[151], [152], [153]].

To therapeutically harness calcium signaling within the TME, a pH-responsive nanomodulator designated as AuNCs@mSiO₂@MnO₂ was developed by co-encapsulating curcumin (a Ca2+-mobilizing agent), CaCO₃ and MnO₂ within nanoparticles cloaked with CCM [154]. In the acidic conditions characteristic of the TME, CaCO₃ undergoes decomposition, leading to the simultaneous release of Ca2+ and curcumin, while also neutralizing intracellular acidity. The induced calcium overload within the ER results in sustained ERS, subsequently triggering ICD and promoting the activation of antitumor immune responses (Fig. 6A). In parallel, MnO₂ catalyzes the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 into molecular oxygen, thereby alleviating tumor hypoxia and simultaneously generating ROS through Fenton-like reactions. The combined effects of ER-localized Ca2+ overload and ROS accumulation induce synergistic oxidative stress, which further amplifies ICD signaling and enhances the overall antitumor immune response. Distinct from direct calcium delivery strategies, cuprous oxide nanoparticles (CONPs) induce ERS by interfering with copper chaperones such as ATOX1 and CCS, in addition to promoting ROS accumulation. This interference disrupts intracellular copper trafficking, leading to concurrent metal dyshomeostasis and ROS-mediated mitochondrial stress [156]. Such comparative insights highlight the versatility of nanomaterial-designed ERS inducers that modulate diverse ion signaling pathways. Moreover, calcium overload has also been leveraged to drive pyroptosis using the CS-HAP@KAE nanoparticle system [157]. By releasing Ca2+ from hydroxyapatite and perturbing homeostasis with kaempferol, this nanotherapeutics activates the STING-IRF3 signaling axis to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” phenotypes.

Drawing inspiration from viral tropism, a biomimetic nanoplatform termed artificial tobacco mosaic virus (ATMV) was developed by structurally reprogramming the natural TMV [[158], [159], [160]]. This system integrates RGD-modified single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNT-RGD) as tumor-targeting scaffolds, along with π-π stacked chondroitin sulfate-modified dendrons (CSMDs) that emulate the structural functions of viral capsids [161]. Upon cellular internalization, ATMV induces ER dilation and Ca2+-mediated apoptosis, while the acidic conditions trigger CSMD matrix degradation and doxorubicin (DOX) release, resulting in nuclear DNA damage. By uniting calcium overload-induced oncolysis with DNA-targeted chemotherapy, this design exemplifies the therapeutic potential of biomimetic nanotechnology in achieving precise and synergistic anticancer effects (Fig. 6B).

ERS-induced ROS generation

The excessive accumulation of intracellular ROS has been widely recognized as a potent trigger of ERS. Leveraging this mechanism, various nanomaterials have been deliberately engineered to elevate intracellular ROS levels and disrupt ER homeostasis, thereby initiating ERS and activating downstream apoptotic signaling cascades [162]. These nanomaterials generate ERS-inducing ROS through diverse mechanisms. For example, two-dimensional arsenene nanosheets exploit their intrinsic surface redox activity to selectively inhibit the thioredoxin-like protein TXNL1, resulting in the collapse of cellular redox balance and activation of ROS-mediated ERS pathways (Fig. 7A) [163]. In another approach, RGD-functionalized calcium phosphate nanoparticles (RGD-CaPO/DOX) were designed to selectively target integrin αvβ3-overexpressing ovarian cancer cells [164]. Upon internalization and lysosomal trafficking, the acidic environment induces nanoparticle dissolution and the simultaneous release of DOX and Ca2+ (Fig. 7B). The resulting mitochondrial ROS overproduction combined with ER calcium overload cooperatively activates caspase-3-dependent apoptosis. Another similar study was also conducted by fabricating electrospun curcumin/gelatin hybrid nanofibers (Cc/Glt NM) to achieve sustained local induction of ROS and ERS in solid tumors [165]. This localized delivery system enabled prolonged release of curcumin, leading to persistent intracellular ROS accumulation and subsequent ERS via activation of the Bip/PERK/eIF2α signaling axis.

Alternatively, “chemodynamic-like” nanotherapeutics such as Cu-IXZ@DSF whave been designed to exploit the acidic TME for catalyzing Fenton-like reactions and releasing chemotherapeutics that disrupt mitochondrial respiration, thereby creating a self-amplifying loop of oxidative and ERS [166]. This nanoplatform enables the co-delivery of copper ions, disulfiram (DSF) and the proteasome inhibitor ixazomib through a tumor-selective cascade (Fig. 7C). Within the reductive TME, elevated GSH levels trigger nanoparticle disassembly, releasing ixazomib to inhibit proteasomal function and induce protein accumulation, which activates ERS via the BiP/PERK axis. Concurrently, the released copper ions react with DSF metabolites to generate Cu(DTC)₂ in situ, which promotes ROS production and ixazomib-mediated proteotoxicity, synergistically leading to mitochondrial apoptosis through activation of the caspase-3/9 pathways.

ERS-induced autophagy

ERS orchestrates multiple modalities of cell death through its three canonical signaling branches, which are activated in response to diverse cellular stressors and engage in a reciprocal feedback relationship with ER homeostasis. Under conditions of mild or transient ERS, autophagy is preferentially engaged as a cytoprotective mechanism that facilitates the removal of misfolded proteins and damaged organelles. This adaptive process, known as ERS-resolution autophagy, is characterized by LC3 lipidation and the formation of autophagosomes, followed by fusion with lysosomes to restore proteostatic balance within the ER. In contrast, sustained or excessive ERS surpasses the cellular adaptive capacity and reprograms autophagy toward a pro-death function. Under these conditions, persistent activation of the PERK-ATF4 signaling axis leads to elevated expression of CHOP, a key transcription factor that drives the transition from protective autophagy to autosis. This form of autophagy-dependent cell death is frequently observed in therapy-resistant malignancies [[167], [168], [169]]. Reflecting the importance of this shift, the quantitative thresholds that define the duration and intensity of ERS required to induce CHOP-mediated autosis remain an important focus for future investigations.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a central regulator of cellular metabolism, is intimately linked to the control of autophagy. Under stress conditions such as nutrient deprivation or hypoxia, mTOR activity is suppressed, which in turn initiates autophagic responses aimed at maintaining intracellular homeostasis [170]. In the context of tumor therapy, both ERS and autophagy exhibit dual roles depending on their duration and intensity, either contributing to cell survival by mitigating stress or facilitating cell death through sustained activation [54,171]. Therefore, a more refined understanding of the ways in which nanotherapeutics influence this intricate balance is essential for advancing nanomedicine-based therapeutic strategies [172]. For example, silica-based nanotherapeutics have demonstrated this dual regulatory potential [173,174]. Their physical presence disrupts ER homeostasis by imposing mechanical stress and sequestering ER-resident chaperones, a combination that gives rise to persistent ERS and activates both the PERK-eIF2α and IRE1α-XBP1 signaling pathways. Autophagy subsequently emerges in a biphasic pattern in which the early response serves a protective role through engagement of the IRE1α-JNK-BECN1 pathway, whereas continued stress eventually drives the system toward excessive autophagic flux, leading to caspase-independent cell death. This mechanism has been further refined through the use of mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) engineered to encapsulate propranolol (PRN), creating a composite nanotherapeutic system termed PRN@MSN [175]. While MSNs inherently promote autophagosome formation, PRN concurrently inhibits autophagosome-lysosome fusion, together establishing a self-amplifying blockade of autophagic flux. This dual mechanism is accompanied by intracellular ROS overproduction, which serves as a key amplifier of cellular stress responses. Consequently, ERS is exacerbated, leading to the downregulation of the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and a corresponding increase in PRN-mediated apoptotic signaling.

Other strategies have employed exogenous triggers to shift the cellular response toward lethality. For instance, copper-based nanocrystals (Cu@Cu2-xO NCs) exhibit near-infrared (NIR) photothermal activity, which induces substantial ROS generation and elicits endoplasmic ERS. This combination synergistically enhances autophagy and thermal ablation effects, thereby amplifying antitumor efficacy [176]. In contrast, the graphene oxide-based nanostructures (ER-GO—NPs) have been developed by integrating the ERS inducer DOX, the DNA-damaging agent cisplatin, and dansyl-functionalized sulfonamide ligands with selective affinity for ER membrane receptors [177]. This multifunctional design enables precise ER accumulation and elicits robust ERS through activation of CHOP-mediated apoptotic pathways. When co-administered with chloroquine (CQ), a pharmacological inhibitor of autophagy, the ER-GO—NP system effectively blocks cytoprotective autophagy and redirects the cellular response toward apoptosis, as indicated by elevated expression of late-stage apoptotic markers.

ERS promotes ICD

Compared with conventional therapeutic modalities, tumor immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative strategy that activates the host immune system and establishes durable immune memory [178,179]. A hallmark of ICD involves the translocation of the ER chaperone CRT to the cell surface, where it functions as an “eat-me” signal by engaging CD91 receptors on DCs. Concurrently, DAMPs such as ATP and HMGB1 are released or exposed, serving as “find-me” signals through interaction with receptors including P2 × 7 and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4). Together, these molecular events promote the maturation of DCs and the activation of CTLs, which are essential for the effective elimination of tumor cells [180,181].

Given that ERS plays a fundamental role in initiating these immunogenic signals, considerable efforts have been directed toward designing of nanotherapeutics that utilize diverse triggers to amplify ERS-driven immunogenicity [182,183]. One representative approach involves the use of PEG-modified bimetallic gadolinium-MOF (Gd-MOF-5), which are engineered to release Gd³⁺ and Zn²⁺ ions in response to the acidic TME [184]. The released Gd³⁺ ions inhibit scramblase activity, thereby preventing the externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) and counteracting PS-mediated immunosuppressive signaling (Fig. 8A). Meanwhile, the intracellular accumulation of Zn²⁺ promotes ERS-mediated ICD through enhanced CRT exposure and ATP secretion, leading to the activation of DCs. In another study, a triblock copolymer was synthesized through reversible addition-fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization, comprising poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), poly(vinylenediamine) (PDEA) and PEG [185]. This nanocarrier was employed to encapsulate the immune adjuvant R848 and, upon encountering to the acidic TME, underwent structural activation that facilitated mitochondrial accumulation and disruption of intracellular redox homeostasis (Fig. 8B). These perturbations triggered ERS and upregulated the expression of gasdermin D, thereby inducing pyroptosis and amplifying ICD.

Conversely, polymer-based nanotherapeutics often exploit intracellular ROS generation and GSH depletion to disrupt the redox balance within the ER. In one approach, a fourth-generation polyamidoamine (PAMAM G4) dendrimer was functionalized with tertiary amine moieties and a seven-membered heterocyclic compound (C7A), resulting in a multifunctional construct termed G4P-C7A [186]. This nanotherapeutics preferentially accumulates within the ER and mitochondria, where it promotes ROS generation, facilitates the release of ATP and HMGB1, activates DCs and confers prophylactic immunity against tumor challenge. To further enhance tumor selectivity and ICD induction, a ruthenium-based chemotherapeutic agent, KP1339, was encapsulated into diselenide-bridged mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles (MONs) to create MON@KP1339 [187]. These nanotherapeutics were further camouflaged with 4T1 tumor cell membranes to generate CM@MON@KP1339, thereby enabling homotypic targeting. Upon intravenous administration, the elevated intracellular GSH levels within the TME triggered the disassembly of the nanotherapeutics, resulting in KP1339 release and the induction of ROS-mediated ERS.

Furthermore, ERS serves as a critical nexus linking proteotoxic signaling to immune activation by coordinating the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and DAMPs [[188], [189], [190]]. For example, the natural xanthone derivative α-mangostin, which exerts intrinsic antitumor effects through pro-apoptotic activity as well as ERS induction via disruption of proteostasis [[191], [192], [193], [194]]. When encapsulated within poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles, α-mangostin promotes the exocytosis of antigen-rich exosomes (αM-Exos) that are enriched in ERS-induced DAMPs such as Hsp70 [195]. These exosomes exhibit intrinsic immunostimulatory activity owing to the presence of Hsp70, a stress-inducible chaperone that facilitates lymph node trafficking via scavenger receptor recognition and enhances DCs uptake through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Upon subcutaneous administration, αM-Exos enable the coordinated delivery of tumor antigens and endogenous adjuvants, resulting in effective DCs maturation, robust activation of CTLs and expansion of memory T cell populations (Fig. 8C).

Synergistic integration of ERS modulation with established oncologic therapeutic modalities

Monotherapeutic strategies have often shown limited clinical efficacy, primarily due to the intrinsic heterogeneity of tumor cell populations and the emergence of adaptive resistance mechanisms during treatment progression [[196], [197], [198]]. In response to these challenges, this section offers a comprehensive analysis of efforts to integrate ERS-targeting nanotherapeutics with established immunotherapeutic modalities, including ICB and therapeutic cancer vaccines. These combinatorial strategies are designed to potentiate antitumor efficacy through multiple interrelated mechanisms, such as the induction of ERS-mediated ICD, improved presentation of tumor-associated antigens, and the activation of CTLs. By simultaneously alleviating immunosuppression within the TME and stimulating systemic antitumor immune responses, these approaches offer the potential to elicit durable immunological memory against malignant cells. This integrated therapeutic paradigm highlights the synergistic relationship between localized ER homeostasis and systemic immune activation, providing a rational paradigm for overcoming the limitations commonly associated with conventional monotherapies in oncology.

Synergistic integration with PDT

PDT represents a clinically validated modality for tumor treatment that relies on the activation of to generate ROS with high spatial and temporal precision, thereby minimizing off-target cytotoxicity (Fig. 9A) [199]. Despite these advantages, the therapeutic efficacy of PDT is frequently compromised by the hypoxic nature of the TME and the inherently short half-life of ROS [200,201]. Recent advances have highlighted the therapeutic potential of strategies that perturb ER homeostasis, thereby inducing ERS as a complementary cytotoxic mechanism in cancer therapy. In this context, emerging research has focused on the development of nanotherapeutics that simultaneously target the ER and integrate PDT, with the goal of enhancing ICD and potentiate antitumor immune responses through synergistic mechanisms [202,203].

To enable efficient generation of ROS within the ER compartment, a multifunctional nanoplatform termed Cy7.5-G@GPM has been developed [204]. This system is constructed using molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) nanosheets conjugated with glucose-modified hyperbranched PEG, resulting in the formation of glucose-functionalized MoS2 nanocarriers (GPM) [205]. The presence of glucose moieties facilitates tumor-specific accumulation of the nanotherapeutics through interaction with glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), which is frequently overexpressed on the surface of malignant cells. The nanocarrier architecture enables the hydrophobic encapsulation of glibenclamide, a pharmacological agent known to modulate ER calcium channels, along with Cy7.5, a fluorescent dye that preferentially localizes to the ER. Upon exposure to NIR light at 808 nm, the system release its therapeutic payload and achieves selective localization within the ER lumen. This targeted activation leads to substantial generation of ROS, which in turn leads to the upregulation of CHOP, the downregulated expression of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, and the activation of caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathways. Beyond the direct induction of apoptosis, the modulation of autophagy offers a complementary therapeutic approach. TCPP-TER-Zn@RSV nanosheets have been assembled via π-π stacking between the ER-targeting photosensitizer TCPP-TER-Zn and resveratrol (RES), a polyphenolic compound known to augment ER-selective autophagy [206]. Upon irradiation at 638 nm, these nanosheets generate ROS within the ER lumen, causing ER damage and the subsequent activation of ER-phagy, a selective autophagic process responsible for the removal of damaged ER components (Fig. 9B).

In addition to conventional photosensitizer-mediated PDT targeting the ER, a more advanced ER-targeted nanoplatform, termed UCSMRER, has been developed to further improve therapeutic efficacy [207]. This system is built upon upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) as the central core, which is subsequently coated with a GSH-responsive mesoporous silica layer that functions as a stimuli-sensitive drug release component, incorporating both the photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6) and RES (Fig. 9C). To enable organelle-specific delivery, the nanoparticle surface is further functionalized with N-p-Tosylglycine, a targeting ligand known for its high affinity toward ATP-sensitive potassium channels localized on the ER membrane. Upon irradiation with NIR light at 980 nm, the UCNP core efficiently converts NIR energy into ultraviolet (UV) emissions, which in turn activate the embedded Ce6 to produce ROS within the ER lumen. The targeted generation of ROS within the ER not only induces apoptosis in tumor cells but also activates ERS pathways and promotes ICD, thereby enhancing the overall immunostimulatory potential of the treatment.

Additional nanotherapeutics have been developed following similar design principles to achieve precise ER-targeted PDT. For instance, UCNPs-Ce6@RuR@mSiO2@PL-HA (UCRSPH) and SA-CaO2 nanoparticles, both employing UCNPs as the core structure, have demonstrated enhanced PDT efficacy by enabling precise targeting of the ER [208]. In these constructs, Ce6 serves as the primary photosensitizer, while ruthenium red (RuR) is incorporated for itsspecific affinity toward calcium channel proteins localized on the ER membrane, thereby facilitating organelle-specific accumulation and phototoxicity. Upon exposure to NIR light at 980 nm, Ce6 is activated to generate singlet oxygen, thereby initiating oxidative stress within tumor cells. The incorporation of both RuR and calcium peroxide (CaO2) further amplifies cytotoxicity by inducing sustained cytoplasmic calcium overload, thereby perturbing intracellular calcium homeostasis. Concurrently, the release of oxygen from CaO₂ alleviates tumor hypoxia, collectively augmenting the overall efficacy of PDT [209].

ER-targeted PDT nanoplatforms exemplify a promising strategy in which localized generation of ROS, regulation of intracellular calcium signaling, and mitigation of tumor hypoxia collectively enhance ERS, promote ICD, and strengthen antitumor immune responses. These therapeutic systems, by integrating photosensitizers with calcium modulators and oxygen-generating elements, are capable of eliciting more sustained and potent antitumor effects. Nevertheless, many current designs are based on structurally intricate constructs, such as UCNP-based or multicomponent assemblies. Such complexity presents significant challenges for large-scale manufacturing, raises potential concerns regarding long-term biodistribution and biodegradability, and poses barriers to clinical translation. To address these limitations, future research should focus on the rational design of simplified, biocompatible nanoplatforms with clearly validated in vivo mechanisms of action, thereby ensuring that improvements in therapeutic efficacy are not achieved at the expense of safety, manufacturability, or translational feasibility.

Synergistic integration with PTT

PTT is a light-triggered therapeutic modality that employs photothermal agents (PTAs) to convert absorbed optical energy into localized hyperthermia, thereby inducing tumor cell death through mechanisms such as membrane disruption and protein denaturation [210]. Despite its clinical promise, the therapeutic efficacy of PTT remains constrained by the shallow tissue penetration of light and the uneven distribution of heat within tumor masses, often resulting in incomplete tumor ablation [211]. In addition, the excessive heat generated during PTT is known to induce the overexpression of heat shock proteins (HSPs), which in turn confer the thermotolerance to malignant cells and attenuating overall therapeutic outcomes [212]. In response to these challenges, the induction of ERS has emerged as a promising sensitization strategy to potentiate the antitumor efficacy of PTT.

To overcome the thermoresistance associated with PTT, a multifunctional nanotherapeutics designated as PES-Au@PDA was developed. This system comprise gold nanospheres (AuNS) coated with polydopamine (PDA) shell and loaded with the HSPA5 inhibitor Pifithrin-μ (PES) [213]. The PDA coating facilitates NIR-responsive photothermal conversion, thereby enabling localized heating that simultaneously triggers the release of PES to enhance CHOP-mediated apoptotic signaling and attenuates thermotolerance (Fig. 10A). Similarly, gold nanobipyramids coated with titanium dioxide (NBP/TiO₂) demonstrate efficient photothermal conversion while concurrently disrupting autophagosome-lysosome fusion. This disruption impairs cathepsin B maturation, exacerbates proteotoxic stress, and ultimately activates ERS-driven apoptotic pathways [214].

ERS, when combined with PTT, has been extensively utilized to induce ICD. To harness this synergistic mechanism, a nanoscale therapeutic system designated as HAuNS-Met@MS was designed [215]. The system incorporates metformin within PLGA microspheres functionalized with hollow gold nanoshells, enabling robust NIR-induced hyperthermia. In parallel, co-administration of 2-deoxyglucose (2DG) suppresses glycolysis and mitigates heat-induced thermotolerance, thereby amplifying ERS and facilitating the release of DAMPs, which subsequently promote DCs maturation and T cell priming (Fig. 10B). However, in certain tumors such as specific subsets of triple‑negative breast cancer (TNBC), constitutive activation of the UPR facilitates cellular adaptation to ERS and contributes to therapeutic resistance [216]. To improve targeting specificity, gold nanorods (AuNRs) were functionalized with the CXCR4 antagonist peptide E5, forming AuNRs-E5 nanoparticles capable of CXCR4-mediated tumor homing and inhibition of metastasis-associated signaling pathways [217] Under NIR irradiation, AuNRs-E5 generate localized hyperthermia that disrupts ER calcium homeostasis, activates the UPR, and induces ICD via the release of DAMPs, ultimately eliciting robust systemic antitumor immune responses [218].

PTT have also demonstrated the capacity to target specific functions of the ER, particularly in calcium homeostasis. The dual-enhanced calcium nanogenerator (DECaNG) integrates curcumin into calcium phosphate (CaP)-doped hollow mesoporous copper sulfide (HMCuS) nanoparticles, enabling the simultaneous induction of photothermal ablation and intracellular calcium overload to potentiate ERS-mediated tumor cell death (Fig. 10C) [219]. Upon NIR irradiation, the HMCuS core generates localized hyperthermia while simultaneously promoting CaP decomposition and the release of Ca2+. Curcumin further disrupts ER calcium reservoirs, collectively leading to elevated cytosolic and mitochondrial Ca2+ concentrations [220]. This calcium dysregulation induces mitochondrial dysfunction and activates caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathways, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy in preclinical models of breast cancer.

Overall, the integration of ERS modulation with PTT has demonstrated synergistic effects in promoting apoptosis, inducing ICD, and intensifying subcellular stress responses, particularly at the level of organelles such as the ER and mitochondria. These combined strategies potentiate antitumor efficacy through mechanisms including calcium overload, heat-induced proteotoxicity, and disruption of ER homeostasis. However, several translational challenges persist. The structural complexity of multicomponent nanotherapeutics, limited tissue penetration of NIR light, intertumoral heterogeneity in UPR signaling, and the potential risk of Ca2+-mediated toxicity collectively hinder clinical scalability. To overcome these limitations, future research should focus on developing streamlined nanotherapeutic systems with tunable ERS-inducing capacity, improved photothermal responsiveness, and tumor-specific adaptation. These refinements will be essential to achieve a balance between therapeutic potency and safety, thereby enhancing the clinical viability of ERS-PTT combinatorial strategies.

Synergistic integration with sonodynamic therapy

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has emerged as a compelling alternative to PDT, employing low-intensity ultrasound in combination with sonosensitizers to induce the generation of cytotoxic ROS. A key advantage of SDT lies in its superior tissue penetration, which expands its applicability in the treatment of deep-seated tumors [221,222]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of SDT is often impeded by the hypoxic characteristics of the TME, as ROS production is highly dependent on the availability of molecular oxygen [223]. To address this limitation, recent studies have focused on the development of multifunctional nanotherapeutics that integrate both hypoxia-alleviation mechanisms with ER-targeting strategies. These systems exert their therapeutic effects either through the augmentation of intratumoral oxygen levels via catalase-like enzymatic activity or the oxygen-carrying components, or through the modulation of ERS related signaling pathways to potentiate apoptosis, autophagy and ICD.

A representative example is the PLGA-PEG-FA nanocarrier, fabricated via a two-step emulsification process to co-encapsulate the sonosensitizer IR780 and the oxygen-releasing agent perfluorohexane (PFH), resulting in IRO@FA nanoparticles [224]. Upon ultrasound irradiation, IR780 produces ROS that induce intracellular oxidative damage, while PFH undergoes an ultrasound-triggered phase transition to release oxygen. The combination of exogenous oxygen supply and ROS generation effectively alleviates tumor hypoxia, augments sonodynamic efficacy, and induces ERS. In another study, THPP@NPs were constructed by encapsulating the sonosensitizer 5,10,15,20-tetrakis (4-hydroxyphenyl) porphyrin (THPP) into poly(Cys-Cys-TK) nanoparticles with GSH-responsive disulfide linkages [225]. This nanotherapeutic is designed to undergo tumor-specific dissociation within the reductive TME, thereby enabling controlled release of the encapsulated sonosensitizer. Upon ultrasound irradiation, THPP produces substantial levels of ROS while simultaneously depleting intracellular, leading to amplified oxidative stress. The resultant redox imbalance activates ERS, upregulates heat shock protein A5 (HSPA5), and triggers CHOP-mediated apoptotic pathways. Persistent ERS subsequently induces caspase-3 activation, which triggers secondary pyroptosis (Fig. 11A). The combined induction of apoptosis and pyroptosis synergistically enhances the efficacy of SDT and helps circumvent tumor resistance to oxidative stress.

The integration of ERS, autophagy, and ferroptosis has been achieved through the development of a multifunctional (MOF-based nanotherapeutic platform, termed GDMCN2 [226]. This system features iron-based MOF nanocages coated with a covalent organic framework (COF) shell, enabling the co-encapsulation of gemcitabine (GEM) and the sonosensitizer sinoporphyrin sodium (DVDMS). Upon ultrasound activation, GDMCN2 releases GEM and activates DVDMS, resulting in ROS generation that promote the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore and disrupt ER calcium homeostasis, thereby initiating ERS (Fig. 11B). Sustained ROS accumulation and GSH depletion inhibit glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), promoting lipid peroxide (LPO) accumulation and triggering autophagy-dependent ferroptosis. The combined effects of ROS-mediated cytotoxicity, ERS-induced autophagy, ferroptotic cell death, and GEM-mediated genotoxic stress synergistically enhance antitumor efficacy, mitigate chemoresistance, and increase tumor immunogenicity.

Overall, the integration of SDT with ERS modulation represents a promising therapeutic approach that combines improved tissue penetration with enhanced oxygen availability and the activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, and ICD. These multifunctional nanotherapeutics reflect a rational design philosophy that seeks to maximize the efficiency of ROS generation and to stimulate ERS capable of amplifying antitumor immune response. Despite these advantages, most existing systems rely on complex, multicomponent constructions that present considerable obstacles for scalable synthesis, long-term biocompatibility assessment, pharmacokinetic control, and eventual regulatory approval. In addition, the individual contributions of the various cell death pathways remain insufficiently defined, and the ERS intensities required to shift cellular outcomes toward apoptosis, pyroptosis, or ferroptosis have yet to be clearly determined. Future investigations should emphasize the systematic clarification of ERS-mediated death mechanisms, the development of simplified and more biocompatible material platforms, and the evaluation of therapeutic benefits in clinically relevant tumor models, thereby ensuring both therapeutic effectiveness and translational suitability.

Synergistic integration with chemodynamic therapy

Compared with conventional chemotherapeutic regimens, chemodynamic therapy (CDT) offers several distinctive advantages, including reduced systemic toxicity, improved therapeutic selectivity, and enhanced tumor specificity. The antitumor effects of CDT primarily rely on the localized generation of highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) through Fenton or Fenton-like reactions within the TME. These radicals induce oxidative damage to essential cellular components such as DNA, lipids and proteins, thereby promoting tumor cell death [[227], [228], [229]]. However, the efficiency of these reactions is highly dependent on specific physicochemical conditions, including acidic pH and the availability of H2O2, which are often suboptimal within the TME. These limitations have driven the development of advanced nanotechnology-based combination strategies to potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of CDT [230].

Copper selenide (Cu₂Se) nanocubes (NCs) have been introduced as versatile catalysts that not only substitute for Fe²⁺ in Fenton-like reactions but also function as effective PTAs in the second near-infrared (NIR-II) window [231]. To enhance ROS production, CaO₂ nanoparticles serving as endogenous sources of H2O2 and Ca2+ were co-encapsulated with Cu₂Se within a thermoresponsive lauric acid (LA) shell, resulting in the formation of the composite nanotherapeutics (Cu₂Se-CaO₂)@LA [232]. Upon NIR-II irradiation, the melting of the LA shell facilitates the controlled release of Cu₂Se and CaO₂, thereby initiating robust ROS production and inducing intracellular calcium overload. The elevated Ca2+ concentrations disrupt mitochondrial and ER homeostasis, subsequently activating the PERK-eIF2α signaling axis and triggering ERS. By combining photothermal enhancement, Fenton-like catalytic activity, endogenous generation of H2O2 and Ca2+, and immune activation mediated through ERS, this multifunctional nanotherapeutics elicit a robust and synergistic antitumor response. In another study, a FA-conjugated liposomal system has also been developed to co-deliver copper peroxide (CP) and chloroquine, forming a targeted nanotherapeutics referred to as CC@LPF [233]. The incorporation of chloroquine disrupts lysosomal integrity and exacerbate ERS, while CP catalyzes the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 to generate cytotoxic·OH, thereby inducing oxidative damage and promoting apoptosis (Fig. 12A) [234].

In addition to the induction of ICD, CDT can be further potentiated by amplifying ERS. To this end, acetylated PAMAM G5 dendrimers (G5-NHAc), functionalized with phenylboronic acid (PBA) and conjugated to toyocamycin (Toy), were encapsulated within a pH-responsive iro-tannic acid coordination network to construct the nanotherapeutics G5.NHAc-Toy@TF [235]. Under acidic TME conditions, the nanostructure undergoes disassembly, releasing Fe³⁺ ions that catalyze Fenton-like hydroxyl radical (·OH) production, deplete intracellular GSH, suppress GPX4, and initiate ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 12B). Concurrently, Toy functions as an inhibitor of IRE1α RNase activity, thereby preventing the splicing of XBP1 and attenuating the adaptive UPR, rendering tumor cells more vulnerable to ERS-induced apoptosis. This dual strategy synergistically couples ferroptosis with pharmacological amplification of ERS, resulting in enhanced antitumor efficacy.

A complementary strategy involves the construction of a pH-responsive nanoreactor comprising a Hemin-Zinc (He-Zn) coordination complex, which catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic ·OH radicals, thereby inducing CDT [236]. To further enhance therapeutic efficacy, a covalently linked pH-sensitive prodrug, hyaluronic acid-dopamine-bortezomib (HA-DA-BTZ) was synthesized and integrated into the system (Fig. 12C). This prodrug enables preferential tumor accumulation and is engineered to release bortezomib (BTZ), a potent proteasome inhibitor, in response to acidic conditions of the TME [237]. Upon activation, He-Zn-mediated ROS generation amplifies both oxidative stress and ERS, collectively triggering apoptotic signaling through convergent pathways.

Overall, the integration of (CDT with ERS modulation significantly broadens the therapeutic scope of Fenton-based strategies. By enhancing intracellular ROS generation, these nanotherapeutics initiate multiple cell death pathways, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, and ICD, while concurrently reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment. The reviewed nanotherapeutics exemplify rational design principles, incorporating tumor-specific targeting, TME-responsive activation, and coordinated induction of cellular stress. However, many of these systems rely on complex, multicomponent architectures that may limit large-scale production, complicate biocompatibility assessments, and pose challenges for regulatory approval. Additionally, the efficacy of CDT remains influenced by heterogeneous redox conditions within the TME, and the individual contributions of ERS, ferroptosis, and ICD remain insufficiently quantified. Moving forward, the development of simplified, mechanistically transparent designs with robust in vivo validation will be critical for advancing CDT-ERS strategies toward clinical translation.

Synergistic integration with immunotherapy (IMT)

Modulation of ERS has emerged as a compelling strategy to bridge intrinsic cellular stress pathways with the induction of potent antitumor immunity [238,239]. In addition to its well-established role in triggering ICD, ERS exerts diverse immunomodulatory effects that collectively augment the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies. Specifically, ERS facilitates the surface exposure of CRT and promotes the extracellular release of DAMPs, thereby enhancing DCs-mediated antigen presentation and the subsequent activation of CTLs. This immunogenic reprogramming effectively transforms the TME into an in situ vaccination niche, amplifying both local and systemic antitumor immune responses. Moreover, ERS contributes to the destabilization of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, thereby attenuating immune evasion and potentiating the therapeutic efficacy of ICB [240]. In addition, ERS-induced inflammatory signaling pathways promote the maturation of DCs and T cell infiltration within the TME, effectively reversing the immunosuppressive phenotype characteristic of poorly immunogenic or “cold” tumors [241]. By simultaneously augmenting tumor antigenicity, facilitating efficient antigen presentation, and promoting adaptive immune priming, ERS-targeted interventions serve as a potent adjunct to current immunotherapeutic strategies.

Tumor vaccines offer significant clinical potential by eliciting durable, tumor-specific immune responses. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of conventional vaccine strategies remains limited due to the intrinsically low immunogenicity of TAAs and the profoundly immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME [242,243]. To address the limitations, the integration of immunostimulatory adjuvants with TAAs has gained increasing attention. A representative example is the R848-GA@TCLs nanoparticle vaccine, which demonstrates the effectiveness of this strategy. In this system, the TLR7/8 agonist R848 is covalently conjugated to gallic acid (GA), facilitating its interaction with tumor cell lysates and enabling spontaneous self-assembly into immunogenic nanoparticles [244]. The Toll-like receptor activation by R848, coupled with the processing of stress-associated antigens, further engages ERS pathways, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and amplifying pro-inflammatory signaling.

Because the administration of TAAs alone often results in suboptimal immune responses, the co-delivery of immunoadjuvants has become an essential strategy for enhancing vaccine efficacy. To this end, a pH-responsive diblock copolymer nanocarrier was fabricated to co-encapsulate TAAs along with poly (I:C), a potent dsRNA-based adjuvant [245]. The acid-triggered release within endolysosomal compartments facilitates efficient antigen cross-presentation and enhances CTLs responses. (Fig. 13A) [246]. Furthermore, a self-assembled, iron-free nanoactivator (DAR) was developed by co-assembling DOX, tannic acid (TA), and the photosensitizer IR820 through synergistic π-π stacking interactions and electrostatic forces (Fig. 13B) [247]. DAR exhibits pH-responsive swelling and rupture witin lysosomes, facilitating the release of endogenous iron ions that catalyze Fenton reactions and trigger oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis. Concurrently, the generation of ROS induces ERS, thereby enhancing ICD, promoting CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and stimulating IFN-γ secretion. These effects collectively establish a positive feedback loop that reinforces the interplay between ferroptosis and antitumor immunity.

ICB has emerged as a transformative strategy in cancer immunotherapy, offering durable clinical benefits and prolonged survival across a range of malignancies [248]. Despite these advances, a substantial subset of patients exhibit limited responsiveness to ICB, primarily due to insufficient tumor immunogenicity and the presence of immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME. To address these challenges, the combination of tumor vaccines with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has shown synergistic potential by augmenting antigen-specific T cell responses and reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME. In this context, a pH-responsive vesicular nanotherapeutic was developed using PEG-b-poly(diisopropylaminoethyl methacrylate-co‑butyl methacrylate) (PEG-DB) polymers was devised to co-deliver neoantigensalong with the cGAS-STING agonist cGAMP and the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A [249]. This combinatorial strategy effectively induces robust DCs maturation and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. Moreover, activation of the STING pathway may transiently trigger ERS within DCs, thereby enhancing antigen processing and presentation. The delivered neoantigens elicit potent innate and adaptive immune responses, while co-administration with anti-PD-1 antibodies significantly augments antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cell activation and promotes pronounced tumor regression.

ER-targeted manganese-based nanocomplexes (Mn/OPDMA NCs) were further developed through coordination between Mn²⁺ and the zwitterionic polymer OPDMA (Fig. 13C) [250]. These nanocomplexes demonstrate extended systemic circulation, deep tumor penetration, and intrinsic ER-targeting capacity, enabling the precise delivery of Mn²⁺ into the ER. This targeted release effectively activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which promotes M1-type macrophage polarization, DCs maturation, and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. In a related strategy, pH-responsive nanoparticles composed of poly(beta-amino ester) (PBAE) were employed to encapsulate cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs), which function as STING agonists [251]. These nanoparticles facilitate endosomal escape and enable cytosolic delivery of CDNs following endocytosis, thereby overcoming limitations associated with the poor cellular uptake and entrapment of free CDNs. In B16-F1 melanoma models, the combination of nanotherapeutics with anti-PD-1 ICB significantly enhanced DC activation, boosted CD8⁺ T cell responses, and improved tumor suppression.

Overall, the integration of ERS modulation with IMT represents a powerful strategy to enhance antitumor immune responses. By promoting ICD, enhancing DCs maturation, activating the cGAS-STING pathway, and improving tumor antigen presentation, ERS serves as a key amplifier of both innate and adaptive immunity. This approach shows strong synergy with ICB and cancer vaccines, particularly in immunologically “cold” tumors. However, challenges remain, including the complexity of multicomponent nanotherapeutics, difficulty in mechanistically isolating the effects of ERS from other immune modulators, and the variability of TME. Moving forward, the development of simplified, clinically translatable systems with clearly defined mechanisms will be essential to fully realize the potential of ERS-IMT synergy in cancer treatment.

Synergistic integration with multiple therapies

An expanding body of evidence underscores the therapeutic promise of combining ERS-targeted interventions with other complementary treatment modalities to overcome the limitations of monotherapies and elicit more durable antitumor effects [252]. Within this context, it is essential to further explore and systematically optimize the synergistic potential of ERS modulation when integrated with diverse therapeutics to achieve enhanced clinical outcomes. A representative example is the development of a multifunctional ER-targeted nanophotoreducer, which incorporates indocyanine green (ICG), CpG oligonucleotides, catalase, gold nanorods and KDEL peptides into a combo nanoplatform termed ERsNP (Fig. 14A) [253]. Upon irradiation with NIR light, RsNP simultaneously induces PTT and PDT, where localized heating and ROS production synergistically provoke ERS and promote apoptotic tumor cell death. The inclusion of catalase facilitates the decomposition of intratumoral H2O2, alleviating hypoxia and further enhancing PDT efficacy. Meanwhile, KDEL-mediated ER retention ensures precise subcellular targeting, amplifying ERS and promoting ICD.

Further advancing this multimodal therapeutic paradigm, a novel sonodynamic nanodroplet system (PMPS NDs) has been developed to enhance tumor-targeted accumulation and tissue penetration while effectively inducing ICD [254]. The PMPS molecule is synthesized by amidating protoporphyrin IX with 4-methylphenylsulfonylurea, a structural modification that enables selective binding to sulfonylurea receptors localized in the (Fig. 14B). Upon ultrasound activation, PMPS NDs generated singlet oxygen, inducing robust ERS and promoting the immunogenic release of DAMPs, including CRT and HMGB1. This immunogenic signaling facilitated DCs maturation and CTLs activation. When combined with anti-PD-L1 ICB, PMPS NDs further alleviated immunosuppression and achieved synergistic inhibition of pancreatic cancer metastasis. In the context of NSCLC spinal metastasis, RGDyK-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with zinc protoporphyrin (ZnPP@MSN-RGDyK) were developed to enhance tumor targeting and immunomodulation. These nanoparticles downregulated PD-L1 expression via augmented ubiquitination while simultaneously producing ROS under laser irradiation, thereby amplifying PDT-induced ICD and strengthening antitumor immune responses [255]. In this system, the RGDyK peptide facilitates enhanced tumor targeting while promoting the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of PD-L1 on cancer cells. Concurrently, zinc protoporphyrin serves as a photosensitizer that generates ROS upon laser irradiation, thereby intensifying PDT-induced ICD and promoting the release of TAAs (Fig. 14C). This dual-action nanotherapeutics thereby enhances CTLs activation through simultaneously suppressing PD-L1-mediated immune evasion and increasing antigen availability. Furthermore, administration of ZnPP@MSN-RGDyK effectively sensitizes spinal metastases of NSCLC to ICB by reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment to favor antitumor immunity.

The integration of ERS modulation with multiple therapeutic modalities including PTT, PDT, SDT, CDT and IMT, offers a powerful strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy against solid tumors. These multifunctional nanoplatforms leverage the capacity of ERS to trigger diverse forms of programmed cell death, such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, and ICD, while simultaneously amplifying ROS production and facilitating antigen presentation. By coordinating cellular stress responses with immune activation and TME remodeling, these combinatorial nanotherapeutics significantly improve antitumor immunity and therapeutic durability. One of the key advantages of such multimodal systems is their ability to overcome the limitations of monotherapies, such as poor immunogenicity, resistance to oxidative stress, or inadequate tissue penetration. Additionally, the inclusion of targeting moieties ensures spatial precision in stress induction, minimizing off-target effects and systemic toxicity. However, the translational success of these complex systems remains challenged by several factors. Many of the reviewed nanotherapeutics involve structurally intricate, multicomponent formulations that complicate reproducibility, large-scale manufacturing, and clinical regulatory approval. Moreover, the safety of materials under prolonged exposure and their long-term biodistribution and clearance remain inadequately characterized. Mechanistically, the precise contributions of individual pathways such as ERS, ferroptosis, and ICD often overlap and are not fully delineated in vivo, making it difficult to optimize therapeutic windows without risking excessive toxicity or therapeutic variability due to tumor heterogeneity.

Conclusions and outlooks

This review highlights the critical role of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics in the context of tumorigenesis and cancer therapy. It provides a comprehensive analysis of the molecular pathways underlying ERS and discusses how engineered nanotherapeutics can selectively localize to the ER through diverse targeting strategies. These nanotherapeutics initiate ERS via multiple mechanisms, including calcium ion dysregulation, excessive generation of ROS, and direct activation of UPR signaling cascades. This review summarizes the fundamental mechanisms by which various nanomaterials manipulate ERS to induce a shift from cellular repair processes to apoptotic elimination, offering insights into their potential to enhance cancer treatment efficacy.

Although ERS-modulating nanotherapeutics have demonstrated encouraging preclinical efficacy across various tumor models, their clinical translation remains in its early stages, with only a few ER-targeted platforms advancing beyond in vivo rodent studies. While some ERS-inducing agents such as proteasome inhibitors (e.g., bortezomib) have achieved FDA approval in hematologic malignancies, nanocarriers specifically designed to modulate ERS have not yet received Investigational New Drug (IND) approval. Most of the currently available data pertain to proof-of-concept studies involving lipid, polymeric carriers, and inorganic-based nanomaterials, which have been shown to enhance drug retention in the ER, potentiate ERS-induced ICD, and improve antitumor immunity. However, clinical validation is still lacking.

A comprehensive understanding of nanocarrier classification is essential for guiding the rational design and clinical translation of ERS-targeted cancer therapies. The most frequently employed nanocarriers can be broadly categorized into lipid, polymeric, inorganic and biomimetic platforms, each exhibiting unique physicochemical traits and therapeutic profiles. Lipid-based carriers, including liposomes and lipid nanoparticles, benefit from excellent biocompatibility and membrane fusion ability, making them suitable for ER targeting via membrane-mimicking strategies; however, they can suffer from instability and variable drug retention. Inorganic nanoparticles such as mesoporous silica, gold or MOFs offer structural rigidity, high drug-loading capacity, and customizable surface functionalities, yet face concerns related to long-term accumulation, biodegradability, and cytotoxicity, which challenge their clinical advancement. Polymeric micelles provide modular architecture, controlled release behavior, and versatile functionalization options, although potential immunogenicity and issues with degradation must be carefully managed. Biomimetic nanocarriers, including membrane-coated nanoparticles and exosome-like vesicles, offer intrinsic targeting abilities, immune evasion, and efficient ER trafficking through endogenous pathways, yet limitations in reproducibility, large-scale manufacturing, and regulatory standardization present significant barriers. Importantly, across all categories, clinical translation requires rigorous evaluation of key benchmarks such as tumor accumulation (ideally ≥5% ID/g), acceptable toxicity thresholds (≤10% organ damage relative to controls), and validated ER localization. This comparative framework underscores the necessity for platform-specific optimization and highlights the potential of hybrid designs to overcome current limitations. By strategically integrating material properties with ER-targeting requirements, future nanocarriers may more effectively balance therapeutic efficacy, safety, and translational feasibility.

Several critical translational challenges hinder the progress of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics. First, many advanced platforms present concerns regarding long-term biocompatibility, off-target ERS in healthy tissues, and unclear metabolic clearance, all of which necessitate rigorous toxicological profiling. Second, achieving tumor-selective ER targeting remains difficult due to the lack of highly specific ligands and the dynamic nature of ER architecture, which varies with tumor progression. Third, the complexity of multi-component nanoplatforms complicates large-scale synthesis, GMP compliance, and batch-to-batch reproducibility. Additionally, few studies address interpatient heterogeneity or ERS resistance, which is particularly relevant in tumors with constitutive UPR activation such as TNBC or pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The lack of standardized biomarkers to quantify ERS intensity and therapeutic response further complicates clinical evaluation.

Despite encouraging advances in preclinical research, the successful translation of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics into clinical practice remains constrained by the lack of standardized quantitative metrics to evaluate delivery efficacy, therapeutic selectivity, and systemic safety. To address this gap, it is essential to adopt established translational benchmarks widely recognized in the nanomedicine field, enabling more objective assessment of clinical viability. Current evidence in nanomedicine suggests that clinically promising nanoplatforms should achieve tumor accumulation levels of at least 5% injected dose per gram (ID/g), which is significantly higher than the typical 2%–3% ID/g observed for non-targeted nanocarriers in murine models. In addition to tumor homing, successful ER‑specific delivery should be supported by quantitative intracellular data, such as colocalization coefficients between nanoparticle signals and ER markers, alongside measurable ERS activation of at least a twofold increase in canonical markers such as CHOP, ATF4, or BiP. Safety assessments should likewise follow quantitative standards, with systemic toxicity evaluated via serum indicators within 10% deviation from physiological baseline and without significant histopathological abnormalities in major organs. Therapeutic efficacy benchmarks in vivo typically include tumor growth inhibition of ≥60% and survival extension of ≥30% in syngeneic models, accompanied by characteristic biochemical evidence of ICD such as elevated calreticulin exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 translocation. By integrating these quantitative and regulatory benchmarks, future studies on ERS-targeted nanomedicines can be designed with improved clarity, reproducibility, and clinical alignment. Such standardization is imperative for bridging the gap between experimental promise and therapeutic implementation.

The development of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics is intrinsically grounded in multidisciplinary collaboration, integrating advances from tumor immunology, cancer metabolism, and nanomedicine. From an immunological perspective, ERS profoundly influences the tumor immune microenvironment by altering antigen presentation pathways, modulating cytokine secretion profiles, and promoting immune cell exhaustion. Simultaneously, findings from metabolic reprogramming have elucidated how ERS intersects with nutrient-sensing pathways, redox homeostasis, and mitochondrial integrity, thereby exerting critical control over tumor cell survival and adaptability. Nanotechnology serves as a powerful complement to these domains by enabling subcellularly precise drug delivery, real-time biosensing, and the implementation of synergistic therapeutic regimens. This integrative framework facilitates the rational design of ER-targeted nanotherapeutics that leverage immunometabolic vulnerabilities and adapt to the spatial and temporal complexities of the TME. Addressing these challenges systematically will be crucial to advancing ERS-targeted nanomedicine from conceptual innovation to clinical utility.

Looking forward, the advancement of ERS-targeted nanotherapeutics is expected to be propelled by multidisciplinary innovation to bridge these gaps. The integration of artificial intelligence with single-cell omics technologies is poised to enable high-resolution profiling of ERS heterogeneity across malignant, stromal, and immune cell compartments, thereby facilitating the identification of cell type specific vulnerabilities and the rational optimization of therapeutic targeting strategies. In parallel, the convergence of materials science, computational biology, and clinical pharmacology is anticipated to foster the development of nanoplatforms engineered for programmable, stimuli-responsive drug release, improved tumor specificity, and favorable pharmacokinetic properties. These integrated approaches not only support the design of precision nanomedicines but also deepen mechanistic insights into ICD and immune modulation. Collectively, such a multidisciplinary framework offers a compelling path toward overcoming adaptive resistance, reducing systemic toxicity, and enhancing the overall therapeutic efficacy of ERS-based interventions in oncology.
