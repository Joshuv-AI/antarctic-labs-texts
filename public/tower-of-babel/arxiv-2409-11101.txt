Abstract
. Let the complex reflection group G ⁡ ( m , p , n ) G(m,p,n) act on the unit polydisc 𝔻 n \mathbb{D}^{n} in ℂ n . \mathbb{C}^{n}. A 𝚯 n \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n} - contraction is a commuting tuple of operators on a Hilbert space having 𝚯 ¯ n := { 𝜽 ⁡ ( z ) = ( θ 1 ​ ( z ) , … , θ n ​ ( z ) ) : z ∈ 𝔻 ¯ n } \overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}(z)=(\theta_{1}(z),\ldots,\theta_{n}(z)):z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n}\} as a spectral set, where { θ i } i = 1 n \{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} is a homogeneous system of parameters associated to G ⁡ ( m , p , n ) . G(m,p,n). A plethora of examples of 𝚯 n \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n} -contractions is exhibited. Under a mild hypothesis, it is shown that these 𝚯 n \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n} -contractions are mutually unitarily inequivalent. These inequivalence results are obtained concretely for the weighted Bergman modules under the action of the permutation groups and the dihedral groups. The division problem is shown to have negative answers for the Hardy module and the Bergman module on the bidisc. A Beurling-Lax-Halmos type representation for the invariant subspaces of 𝚯 n \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n} -isometries is obtained.

The notion of a quotient domain
\Omega/G
for a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
by a finite complex reflection group
G

[
3
]
. After that there is a flurry of activity involving this notion, for instance, see
[
6
,
7
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17

18

22

. A
complex reflection
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a linear mapping
\sigma:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\sigma
has finite order in
GL(n,{\mathbb{C}})
and the rank of
I_{n}-\sigma
is
1.
A group
G
generated by complex reflections is called a
complex reflection group
.
Let
G
be a finite complex reflection group which acts (right action) on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol{z}={\sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{z},\,\,\text{~for~}\sigma\in G\text{~and~}\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{D}^{n}.

(1.1)

The group action extends to the set of all complex-valued functions on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
by
\sigma(f)(\boldsymbol{z})=f({\sigma}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{z})
and a function
f
is said to be
G
-
invariant
if
\sigma(f)=f
for all
\sigma\in G.
There is a system of
G
-invariant algebraically independent homogeneous polynomials
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
associated to a complex reflection group
G,
called a homogeneous system of parameters (hsop) or basic polynomials associated to
G.

In fact, Chevalley-Shephard-Todd theorem provides a characterization of finite complex reflection groups in terms of hsop. It states that a finite group
G
is generated by complex reflections if and only if the ring of
G
-invariant polynomials

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]^{G}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]:\sigma(f)=f\text{~for all~}\sigma\in G\}

forms a polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}]

[
27
, p.282]
. The polynomial map

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}):\mathbb{D}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})

is a proper holomorphic map and the domain
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is biholomorphically equivalent to
the quotient
\mathbb{D}^{n}/G

[
31
,
3
]
. So we refer to the domains of the form
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
by
quotient domains.

The irreducible finite complex reflection groups were classified by Shephard and Todd in
[
27
]
. They proved that every irreducible complex reflection group belongs to an infinite family
G(m,p,n)
indexed by three parameters, where
m,n,p
are positive integers and
p
divides
m,
or, is one of 34 exceptional groups. Although for certain values of
m,p
and
n,

G(m,p,n)
can be reducible, for example,
G(2,2,2)
is the dihedral group of order
4
which is isomorphic to the product of two groups of order
2.
We note that
G(m,p,n)=

n\times n
monomial matrices whose nonzero entries are
m
-th root of unity with product a
\frac{m}{p}
-th root of unity, where
m,p,n
are positive integers and
p
divides
m.
It is known that

contains complex reflections of two types when

\xi_{m}=e^{2\pi i/m}
we have

(1)

transposition like reflections
\sigma_{ij|k}
for

and

such that the

-th component and

-th component of

are
\xi_{m}^{k}z_{i}
and
\xi_{m}^{-k}z_{j},
respectively and any other component remains the same.

(2)

The diagonal reflection
\sigma_{i|k}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and

acts by
\sigma_{i|k}\cdot(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},\xi_{m}^{k}z_{i},z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n}).

The group
G(m,m,n)
contains only transposition like reflections. In other words, the above discussion provides an explicit description of the action of
G(m,p,n)
on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. For a detailed study on

we refer to
[
24
, Chapter 2]
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}
denote the symmetrization map
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}

s_{i}(z)=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<\ldots<k_{i}\leq n}z_{k_{1}}\cdots z_{k_{i}}\mbox{~for~}i=1,\ldots,n.

For

That is,

Theorem 2.5
.

Let

Then the following are equivalent:

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}
-contraction, then
(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})
is a
\Gamma_{n-1}
-contraction,
where
\gamma_{j}=\frac{n-j}{n}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1.

Proof.

For
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots z_{n-1}],
we note that
f\circ\pi\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
and by hypothesis

\displaystyle\|f(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(f\circ\pi)(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f\circ\pi\|_{\infty,\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty,\pi(\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n})}\leq\|f\|_{\infty,\Gamma_{n-1}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

For a commuting tuple
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
of operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H},
let
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T}):=(\theta_{1}(\mathbf{T}),\ldots,\theta_{n}(\mathbf{T})).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be an tuple of commuting contractions. Then
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T})
is a
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}
-contraction if and only if
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
satisfies von Neumann’s inequality for all
G
-invariant polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be a commuting tuple of contractions satisfying the analogue of von Neumann’s
inequality for all
G
-invariant polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].

For a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}],
we note that

\displaystyle\|f(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T}))\|=\|(f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta})(\mathbf{T})\|\leq\|f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\infty,\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n}}=\|f\|_{\infty,\boldsymbol{\theta}(\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n})},

since
f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a
G
-invariant polynomial, the above inequality holds by hypothesis, and it shows that

\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}(\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n})
is a spectral set for
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T}),
thus
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T})
is a
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}}
-contraction.

Conversely, let
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be a tuple of commuting contractions such that
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T})
is a

{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}
-contraction and
q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
be
G
-invariant. So there is a
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
such that

f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}=q.
By hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\|q(\mathbf{T})\|=\|f(\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{T}))\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty,\overline{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}}=\|f\circ\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\infty,\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n}}=\|q\|_{\infty,\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{n}}.

∎

We note down the following corollary which will be relevant in the sequel.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be a commuting tuple of contractions on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying von Neumann’s inequality for all
G
-invariant polynomials in

\mathcal{M}
be a common invariant

is a

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}.

Proof.

For a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}],
we note that

\displaystyle\|f(\theta_{1}(\mathbf{T})|_{\mathcal{M}},\ldots,\theta_{n}(\mathbf{T})|_{\mathcal{M}})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f(\theta_{1}(\mathbf{T}),\ldots,\theta_{n}(\mathbf{T}))|_{\mathcal{M}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f(\theta_{1}(\mathbf{T}),\ldots,\theta_{n}(\mathbf{T}))\|

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}}.

Since

is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction on
\mathcal{H}
by Proposition
2.8
, the last inequality holds. Hence we have the desired conclusion.
∎

3.
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitaries and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometries

We start with the following obvious generalizations of definitions in
[
2
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
be commuting operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. We say that
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is

(i)

a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-
unitary
if
T_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
are normal operators and the joint spectrum

\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
of
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is contained in the distinguished boundary of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}.

(ii)

a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-
isometry
if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
containing
\mathcal{H}
and a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitary
(\widetilde{T}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{T}_{n})
on
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{H}
is invariant for
\widetilde{T}_{i},i=1,\ldots,n

and
T_{i}={\widetilde{T}_{i}}|_{\mathcal{H}},i=1,\ldots,n.

(iii)

a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-
co-isometry
if
(T_{1}^{*},\ldots,T_{n}^{*})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry.

(iv)

a
pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry
if
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry and
T_{n}
is a pure isometry.

The proof of the following theorem works along the lines of Agler and Young
[
2
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
T_{j},\,j=1,\ldots,n,
be commuting operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitary;

(ii)

T_{n}^{*}T_{n}=I=T_{n}T_{n}^{*},\,(T_{n}^{p})^{*}T_{j}=T_{n-j}^{*}
and
(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})
is a
\Gamma_{n-1}
-contraction,
where
\gamma_{j}=\frac{n-j}{n}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
;

(iii)

there exist commuting unitary operators
U_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

T_{j}=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\ldots<k_{j}\leq n}U_{k_{1}}^{m}\ldots U_{k_{j}}^{m}\mbox{~for~}j=1,\ldots,n-1\mbox{~and~}T_{n}^{p}=\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\big)^{m}.

Proof.

Suppose (i) holds. Let
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitary. By the spectral theorem for commuting normal operators, there exists a spectral measure
E(\cdot)
on
\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
such that

T_{j}=\int_{\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})}z_{j}E(d\boldsymbol{z}),\,j=1,\ldots,n,

where
z_{1},...,z_{n}
are the co-ordinate functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Let
\tau
be a measurable right inverse of the restriction of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
to
\mathbb{T}^{n},
so that
\tau
maps distinguished boundary of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
to
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Let
\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})
and

U_{j}=\int_{\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})}\tau_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})E(d\boldsymbol{z}),\,j=1,\ldots,n.

Clearly,
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
are commuting unitaries on
\mathcal{H}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\ldots<kj\leq n}U_{k_{1}}^{m}\ldots U_{k_{j}}^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\ldots<k_{j}\leq n}\int_{\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})}\tau_{k_{1}}(\boldsymbol{z})\ldots\tau_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{z})E(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})}\theta_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})E(d\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{j},

for
j=1,\ldots,n
. Hence (i) implies (iii).

Suppose (iii) holds. Then clearly,
T_{n}^{*}T_{n}=I=T_{n}T_{n}^{*}
and
(T_{n}^{p})^{*}T_{j}=T_{n-j}^{*},j=1,\ldots,n.
Since

(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction by Proposition
2.8
, it follows from Lemma
2.7
that
(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})
is a
\Gamma_{n-1}
-contraction, where
\gamma_{j}=\frac{n-j}{n}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1.
Hence (iii) implies (ii).

Suppose (ii) holds. Since
T_{n}
is normal, it follows from Fuglede’s theorem that

T_{n-i}^{*}T_{n-i}=(T_{n}^{p})^{*}T_{i}T_{i}^{*}T_{n}^{p}=T_{i}T_{i}^{*}.

Since
T_{i}
’s are commuting operators,

T_{n-i}^{*}T_{n-i}=(T_{n}^{p})^{*}T_{i}T_{n-i}=(T_{n}^{p})^{*}T_{n-i}T_{i}=T_{i}^{*}T_{i}.

Thus
T_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
is normal for
i=1,\ldots,n.
Therefore, the unital
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
generated by
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
is commutative and by the Gelfand-Naimark’s theorem is
*
-isometrically isomorphic to
C(\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})).
Let
{\hat{T}}_{i}
be the image of
T_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n,
under the Gelfand map. By definition, for an arbitrary point
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
in
\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
,
{\hat{T}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\theta_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n.
By the properties of the Gelfand map and the hypothesis we have,

\overline{\hat{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\hat{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})=1=\hat{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})\overline{\hat{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\mbox{~and~}\overline{\hat{T}_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})^{p}}\hat{T}_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\overline{\hat{T}_{n-i}({\boldsymbol{z}})}\mbox{~for~}\boldsymbol{z}\in\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}).

Thus we obtain
|\theta_{n}|=1,{\bar{\theta}_{n}}^{p}\theta_{i}=\bar{\theta}_{n-i}.
Now
(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})
is a
\Gamma_{n-1}
-contraction implies

\|f(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty,\Gamma_{n-1}}\mbox{~for~}f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}],

which is equivalent to the fact that
\|f\|_{\infty,\Gamma_{n-1}}^{2}-f(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})^{*}f(\gamma_{1}T_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}T_{n-1})
is a positive operator for
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].
An application of the Gelfand transform yields

\|f\|_{\infty,\Gamma_{n-1}}^{2}-\overline{f(\gamma_{1}\hat{T}_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\hat{T}_{n-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}))}f(\gamma_{1}\hat{T}_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}),\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\hat{T}_{n-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}))\geq 0\mbox{~for~}f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]

and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}).
This proves that
(\gamma_{1}\theta_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\theta_{n-1})
is in the polynomially convex hull of
\Gamma_{n-1}.
Since
\Gamma_{n-1}
is polynomially convex,
(\gamma_{1}\theta_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\theta_{n-1})
is in
\Gamma_{n-1}.
Therefore, by Theorem
2.5
,
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
is in the Shilov boundary of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
and hence
\sigma(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\subset\partial\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
. This proves (ii) implies (i).
∎

To proceed further, we introduce some spaces of vector-valued and operator-valued functions for our exposition, we recall them following
[
2
]
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be a separable Hilbert space and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
be the
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded operators on
\mathcal{E},
with the operator norm. Let
H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
be the Hardy space of analytic
\mathcal{E}
-valued functions on
\mathbb{D}
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
be the Hilbert space of square integrable
\mathcal{E}
-valued functions on
\mathbb{T},
with their usual inner products. Let
H^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
be the space of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
-valued bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
and
L^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
the space of bounded measurable

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
-valued functions on
\mathbb{T},
each with appropriate version of the supremum norm. For

\varphi\in L^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
let
T_{\varphi}
be the Toeplitz operator with symbol
\varphi,
given by

T_{\varphi}f=P(\varphi f),~~~~~f\in H^{2}(\mathcal{E}),

P:L^{2}(\mathcal{E})\longrightarrow H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
being the orthogonal projection. In particular
T_{z}
is the unilateral shift operator on

H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
(
z
denotes the identity function on
\mathbb{T}
).

We need the following lemma to characterize a a pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
be functions in
L^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
and
M_{\Phi_{i}},\,i=1\ldots,n
, denote the corresponding multiplication operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{E}).
Then
(M_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,M_{\Phi_{n}})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction if and only if
(\Phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(z))
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction for all
z
in
\mathbb{T}.

Proof.

Note that
\|M_{\Psi}\|=\|\Psi\|_{\infty}
for
\Psi\in L^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
and

f(M_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,M_{\Phi_{n}})=M_{f(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n})}
for all polynomials
f\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}].
So

\displaystyle\|f(M_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,M_{\Phi_{n}})\|=\|M_{f(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n})}\|=\|f(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n})\|.

Thus

\displaystyle\|f(M_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,M_{\Phi_{n}})\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n},}

if and only of

\|f(\Phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(z))\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n},}\text{~for all~}z\in\mathbb{T},

which completes the proof.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

An interesting case in the above Lemma occurs when
\dim\mathcal{E}=1.
In this case,
(\Phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(z))
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction means that
(\Phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(z))\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
which is true as
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
is polynomially convex.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
T_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n,
be commuting operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry if and only if there exist a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
and a unitary operator
U:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
and functions
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n-1}
in
H^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
and operators
A_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})\,i=1,\ldots,n-1
such that

(i)

T_{i}=U^{*}T_{\Phi_{i}}U,\,i=1,\ldots,n-1,\,T_{n}=U^{*}T_{z}U
;

(ii)

(\gamma_{1}\Phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}(z))
is a
\Gamma_{n-1}
-contraction for all
z
in
\mathbb{T},
where
\gamma_{i}=\frac{n-i}{n}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
;

(iii)

\Phi_{i}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}A_{\ell}^{(i)}z^{\ell}

for some
A_{\ell}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
satisfying
{A_{\ell}^{(i)}}^{*}=A^{(n-i)}_{p-\ell}
for
\ell=0,\ldots,p
and
i=1\ldots n-1.

(iv)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}[A_{\ell}^{(i)},A_{k-\ell}^{(j)}]=0
for
k=0,\ldots,2p
and
i,j=1\ldots n-1,
where
[P,Q]=PQ-QP
for two operators
P,Q.

Proof.

Suppose
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry. So, there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitary
(\tilde{T}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{T}_{n})
such that
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{K}
is a common invariant subspace of
\tilde{T}_{i}
’s and
T_{i}=\tilde{T}_{i}|_{\mathcal{H}},\,i=1,\ldots,n
. From Theorem
3.2
, it follows that

{\tilde{T}}_{n}^{*}{\tilde{T}}_{n}=I\mbox{~and~}{\tilde{T}}_{i}^{*}{\tilde{T}_{n}}^{p}={\tilde{T}}_{n-i},\,1\leq i\leq n-1.

By compression of
\tilde{T}_{i}
to
\mathcal{H}
, we have

T_{n}^{*}T_{n}=I\mbox{~and~}T_{i}^{*}T_{n}^{p}=T_{n-i},\,1\leq i\leq n-1.

Since
T_{n}
is a pure isometry and
\mathcal{H}
is separable, there exists a unitary operator
U:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
for some separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, such that
T_{n}=U^{*}T_{z}U
, where
T_{z}
is the shift operator on
H^{2}(\mathcal{E}).
Since
T_{i}
’s commute with
T_{n}
, there exist
\Phi_{i}\in H^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
such that
T_{i}=U^{*}T_{\Phi_{i}}U
for
1\leq i\leq n-1.
Since
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-contraction, an appeal to Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
3.3
yields part (ii).
The relations
T_{i}^{*}T_{n}^{p}=T_{n-i}
imply that

T_{\Phi_{i}}^{*}T_{z}^{p}=T_{\Phi_{n-i}},\,1\leq i\leq n-1.

The last relations in turn yield

\Phi_{i}(z)^{*}z^{p}=\Phi_{n-i}(z)\mbox{~for all~}z\in\mathbb{T},\,i=1,\ldots,n-1.

If
\Phi_{i}(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}A_{\ell}^{(i)}z^{\ell}
are in
H^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1.
Then
{\Phi_{i}(z)}^{*}z^{p}=\Phi_{n-i}(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
implies

\sum_{\ell\geq 0}{A_{\ell}^{(i)}}^{*}z^{p-\ell}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}{A_{\ell}^{(n-i)}}z^{\ell}\mbox{~for all~}z\in\mathbb{T}.

By comparison of the coefficients, we obtain

A^{(i)}_{\ell}=A^{(n-i)}_{\ell}=0\mbox{~for~}\ell>p\mbox{~and~}{A^{(i)}_{\ell}}^{*}=A^{(n-i)}_{p-\ell}\mbox{~for~}\ell=0,\ldots p,\,i=1,\ldots,n-1.

This shows that

\Phi_{i}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}A_{\ell}^{(i)}z^{\ell}\mbox{~for some~}A_{\ell}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{~satisfying~}{A_{\ell}^{(i)}}^{*}=A^{(n-i)}_{p-\ell}

for
\ell=0,\ldots,p
and
i=1\ldots n-1.

This proves part (iii).

Now since
T_{i}
’s commutes, we have
T_{\Phi_{i}}T_{\Phi_{j}}=T_{\Phi_{j}}T_{\Phi_{i}}
and consequently,

\sum_{k=0}^{2p}\big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}A_{\ell}^{(i)}A_{k-\ell}^{(j)}\big)z^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{2p}\big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}A_{\ell}^{(j)}A_{k-\ell}^{(i)}\big)z^{k}\mbox{~for all~}z\in\mathbb{T}.

Comparing the coefficients of
z^{k}
for
k=0,\ldots,2p
we get part (iv).

Conversely, let
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
be a tuple satisfying conditions (i) to (iv). Consider the tuple
(T_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,T_{\Phi_{n-1}},T_{z})
of multiplication operators on
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
with symbols
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n-1},z,
respectively. The condition (iv) implies that
T_{\Phi_{i}}
’s are commuting operators. A part of condition (iii) shows that
(T_{z}^{p})^{*}T_{\Phi_{i}}=T_{\Phi_{n-i}}^{*}
by repeating analogous calculations as above. Thus, it follows from part (ii) of Theorem
3.2
, that
(T_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,T_{\Phi_{n-1}},T_{z})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-unitary and so is
(U^{*}T_{\Phi_{1}}U,\ldots,U^{*}T_{\Phi_{n-1}}U,U^{*}T_{z}U)
. Now,
T_{i}
’s,
1\leq i\leq n
, are the restrictions to the common invariant subspace
H^{2}(\mathcal{E})
of
(T_{\Phi_{1}},\ldots,T_{\Phi_{n-1}},T_{z})
and hence
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry. Since
T_{n}
is a shift,
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is a pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometry.
∎

Example 3.6
.

and
G(p,p,2),\,\Phi_{1}(z)^{*}z^{p}=\Phi_{1}(z)

where

and
A_{1}^{*}=A_{1}.

We now characterization of pure
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
-isometries in terms of the parameters
associated with them. For simplicity of notation, let
(T_{\mathbf{\Phi}},T_{z})
denote the
n

Theorem 3.12
.

Let

be a closed subspace of

and

Proof.

We will prove only the forward direction as the other part is easy to see. Let
\mathcal{M}\neq\{0\}
is invariant under

\mathcal{M}
is invariant under

and hence the Beurling -Lax-Halmos theorem

Definition 4.1
.

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a Hilbert module over an algebra
\mathcal{A}
if the map
(f,h)\to f\cdot h,

f\in\mathcal{A},h\in\mathcal{H},
defines an algebra homomorphism
f\mapsto T_{f}
of
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),
where
T_{f}
is the bounded operator defined by
T_{f}h=f\cdot h.

A closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a submodule of the Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
if
\mathcal{M}
is closed under the action of
\mathcal{A}.
If in addition,
T_{f}^{*}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
f\in\mathcal{A},
then
\mathcal{M}
is called a reducing submodule of
\mathcal{H},
where
T_{f}^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
T_{f}.
A reducing submodule
\mathcal{M}
is called
minimal
or
irreducible
if only reducing submodules of
\mathcal{M}
are
\{0\}
and
\mathcal{M}
itself. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}
admits a reducing submodule if and only if there exists an orthogonal projection
P
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
PT_{f}=T_{f}P
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}.

Definition 4.2
.

A Hilbert module
\mathcal{H}
over
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
-valued holomorphic functions on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n},
is said to be an

if

(1)

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
is dense in

and

(2)

\mathcal{H}
possesses a
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
-valued reproducing kernel on

The module action in an analytic Hilbert module is given by pointwise multiplication, that is,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}(\boldsymbol{h})(\boldsymbol{z})=p(\boldsymbol{z}){\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\,\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega.
Note that if the
i
-th component of
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{H}
is

then the
i
-th component of

Let
G
be a finite group which acts on the open unit polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{n}.
Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space consisting of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
with a

K(\sigma\cdot z,\sigma\cdot w)=K(z,w)\text{~for all~}z,w\in\mathbb{D}^{n}\text{~and~}\sigma\in G.

Let

be the set of equivalence class of irreducible representations of

For each
\varrho\in\widehat{G},
we define the linear map

\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{\varrho}f)(z)=\frac{\deg\varrho}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_{\varrho}(\sigma^{-1})f(\sigma^{-1}\cdot z),

(4.1)

where

is the character of the representation

Remark 4.3
.

The

If

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}.
We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
S:\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm triv}(\mathbb{D}^{2})\to\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm sign}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
be a linear surjection satisfying
SM_{s_{i}}=M_{s_{i}}S
for
i=1,2.
Then
S=M_{\varphi},
where
\varphi=cJ_{\boldsymbol{s}}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

Let
S(1)=f
for some
f\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm sign}(\mathbb{D}^{2}).
Then
SM_{s_{i}}=M_{s_{i}}S
for
i=1,2
yields
Sg=gf
for
g\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm triv}(\mathbb{D}^{2}).
Since
S
is a surjection there exists
h\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm triv}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
such that
Sh=J_{\boldsymbol{s}}=z_{1}-z_{2}.
Thus
Sh=hf=J_{\boldsymbol{s}}hf_{\rm triv}
for
f_{\rm triv}\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm triv}(\mathbb{D}^{2}).
This forces
hf_{\rm triv}=1.
This is impossible, unless
h
and
f_{\rm triv}
are both scalars. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.21
.

Let
S:\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm triv}(\mathbb{D}^{2})\to\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{\rm sign}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
be defined by
Sf=J_{\boldsymbol{s}}f.
Then
S
does not have a bounded inverse for
\lambda=1,2.

Proof.

Noting that
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}]\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbb{D}^{2}),
consider the map
T:\mathbb{P}_{\rm sign}(\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}])\to\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}
defined by
Tf=\frac{f}{J_{\boldsymbol{s}}}.
If
T
is bounded, then there exists
K>0
satisfying

\|Tf\|\leq K\|f\|\mbox{~for all~}f\in\mathbb{P}_{\rm sign}(\mathbb{C}[z]).

Recall that

\displaystyle\|z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}\|^{2}=\frac{m!n!}{(\lambda)_{m}(\lambda)_{n}}.

(4.8)

Set
e_{m,0}(z_{1},z_{2})=z_{1}^{m}-z_{2}^{m}.
Then
(Te_{m,0})(z_{1},z_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}z_{1}^{m-k}z_{2}^{k-1}
for
m\geq 1,
and boundedness of
T
implies that

\displaystyle\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}z_{1}^{m-k}z_{2}^{k-1}\|^{2}\leq K^{2}\|e_{m,0}\|^{2}.

(4.9)

Putting
\lambda=2,
it follows from Equation (
4.9
) that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{(m+1-k)k}\leq\frac{2K^{2}}{m+1}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{(1-\frac{k}{m+1})k}\leq 2K^{2}\mbox{~for~}m\geq 1.

(4.10)

Note that
1-\frac{k}{m+1}<1.
Hence
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{(1-\frac{k}{m+1})k}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}.
Thus, Equation (
4.10
) cannot hold for all
m\geq 1.

To prove the assertion for
\lambda=1,
we simply note from Equation (
4.8
) that Equation (
4.9
) reduces to
m^{2}\leq 4K^{2}
for all
m\geq 1.
This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.22
.

In general, the map
f\mapsto V_{n}f
, where
V_{n}(z)=\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})
, from
\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{triv}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
to
\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}_{sign}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is one-one and one ask whether this map is onto or not to have a similarity. It turns out that this is the same is asking whether
V_{n}
is a ’divisor’ in the sense of
[
8
,
26
]
- that is, if
f\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
and
f/V_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
, where
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is the algebra of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, then is it true that
f/V_{n}\in\mathbb{A}^{(\lambda)}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
? The Proposition above shows that the division problem fails for

when
