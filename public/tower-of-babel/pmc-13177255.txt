Advanced Adsorption Mechanisms of Copper Ions in Wastewater Utilizing a Tailored COF-MWCNTs Nanocomposite: A Comprehensive Investigation into the Synergistic Interactions and Environmental Applications
Authors: Sameh A. Elbanna, Inas A. Ahmed, Nora M. Hilal, Mahmoud F. Mubarak, Hanan A. Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The persistent contamination of water by toxic heavy metals threatens the environment and public health. Herein, a covalent organic framework-multiwalled carbon nanotube (30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs) nanocomposite was synthesized via a modulator-solvent-induced polymerization method for efficient Cu2+ removal from wastewater. X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy/transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM), S BET, and spectroscopic characterizations confirmed the successful integration of the porous COF with conductive MWCNTs, providing accessible active sites and enhanced surface properties. Batch adsorption experiments revealed rapid uptake, reaching equilibrium within 120 min with optimal performance at pH 3.0. The maximum adsorption capacity reached 55.6 mg/g, and the adsorption increased with temperature, indicating an endothermic process. The pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Freundlich isotherm model have adequately described the adsorption process of Cu2+ ions, suggesting that chemisorption was the predominant mechanism for these ions’ adsorption. The nanocomposite maintained stable performance over five cycles without significant capacity loss. These results demonstrate the synergistic effect of COF porosity and MWCNTs conductivity, highlighting the nanocomposite as a robust and reusable adsorbent for wastewater remediation. The observed optical properties (band gap of 1.6 eV and suppressed PL) suggest potential for future photocatalytic applications, although such studies are beyond the scope of this work.

Introduction

Development of innovative materials for environmental cleanup has received a lot of attention lately due to the increasing demand for sustainable and efficient solutions to water pollution. − Among various pollutants, heavy metal ions, such as copper (Cu2+), present a serious risk to aquatic ecosystems and the health of people. − Conventional techniques for extracting copper ions from wastewater, including ion exchange and chemical precipitation, often suffer from high costs, inefficiency, and secondary pollution. − Therefore, there is an urgent need for innovative materials and technologies that can effectively and economically address this challenge. , The class of covalent organic frameworks (COFs) has shown great promise of materials for environmental applications due to their high crystallinity, tunable porosity, and functional diversity. − COFs are made from natural building materials through covalent bonding, resulting in extremely well-organized and porous materials that offer enormous surface areas and selective adsorption properties. − The remarkable potential for electricity, durability, and huge surface area of multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) is well-known. − The integration of COFs with MWCNTs can improve the composite material’s general efficiency. Recent studies have shown that combining COFs containing other substances like metal oxides and carbon-based nanomaterials, can significantly improve their adsorption and photocatalytic properties. − As an illustration, an examination by Jamadi et al. demonstrated that incorporating MWCNTs into a composite structure improved its microwave absorption performance as a result of the components’ combined effects. Similarly, Xu et al. stated that the mix of ferrite nanocrystals and polyaniline (PANI) in a composite matrix enhanced its electromagnetic properties. These findings highlight the possibility of hybrid materials dealing with environmental obstacles through advanced adsorption and catalytic processes. Despite their promising performance, hybridization of COFs with carbon-based nanomaterials, such as graphene oxide, reduced graphene oxide, activated carbon, and carbon nanotubes, also presents several limitations that must be acknowledged. Pure COFs often suffer from structural fragility, limited water stability, and a potential collapse of the porous network under practical conditions. When integrated with MWCNTs, the composites may experience aggregation due to the strong π–π interactions between nanotubes, which can reduce the accessible surface area and hinder mass transfer. Additionally, the synthesis of COF-based hybrids typically requires precise control of the reaction environment, modulator concentration, and solvent composition, making large-scale production challenging. Controlling the growth of COF on conductive carbon substrates without blocking pores, while preserving electron and ion transport, is a key challenge. Achieving a high accessible surface area and reproducible composite compositions can also significantly influence the adsorption performance. Recent reviews and reports describe COF-carbon hybrids that excel in specific tasks (e.g., dye removal, photocatalysis, or electro sorption), but many of these studies either (i) rely on graphene/GO supports (which tend to give very high uptake for dyes but under different conditions), (ii) report complex postsynthetic functionalization steps, or (iii) do not present a systematic optimization of COF loading on carbon nanotubes. These limitations highlight the need for optimized COF loading and improved integration strategies, challenges that the present study aims to address.

The present study aims to develop a novel COF-MWCNTs nanocomposite to get copper ions out of wastewater through an advanced adsorption process. The innovation of this research lies in the tailored synthesis of a COF-MWCNTs composite using a modulator-solvent induced polymerization approach that is not simultaneously demonstrated in the cited works, which ensures the formation of a highly crystalline and porous COF structure integrated with MWCNTs. This method not only enhances the composite’s adsorption capability and surface area but also facilitates the rapid transfer of electrons, lowering the electron–hole pair turnover rate and raising the total adsorption efficiency. Anther innovative objective of this study is to explicitly establish a systematic optimization of COF loading (10–50 wt %) through a quantitative loading-structure-performance relationship showing an optimum composition at 30 wt % COF. This optimization demonstrates the trade-off between creating more adsorption sites (higher COF content) and avoiding pore-channel blockage and aggregation that reduce accessibility and conductivity, an aspect rarely quantified in earlier COF-carbon reports followed by practical adsorption performance metrics for Cu2+removal from wastewater including reusability; a combination that is not previously reported. This study also aims to address the structural and morphological characteristics of the COF-MWCNTs nanocomposite utilizing sophisticated analytical techniques such as surface area (S BET), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Furthermore, unlike previous COF-carbon adsorbents, this study systematically correlates optical properties (band gap energy and photoluminescence (PL) suppression) with adsorption performance revealing a synergistic charge-transfer-assisted chemisorption mechanism. The combination of PSO-dominant kinetics, intraparticle diffusion contribution, and positive thermodynamic parameters (ΔH° and ΔS°) supports the novelty of the adsorption mechanism. Overall, this work provides a new synthetic approach, a structurally distinct COF-MWCNTs interface, and mechanistic insights not previously reported for COF-based metal-ion adsorbents. While the nanocomposite exhibits favorable optical properties (narrow band gap, low PL), its actual photocatalytic performance was not evaluated here and remains a subject for future investigation.

Results and Discussion

Assessment of the COF-MWCNT Nanocomposite

The 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite designed and selected to be used in this study to represent the optimal formulation for achieving the highest Cu2+removal performance was thoroughly characterized using various analytical techniques. The results of these characterizations are discussed in detail below to understand the structural, morphological, and optical properties of the nanocomposite.

X-ray Diffraction

To verify the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s crystalline structure and phase purity, XRD examination was carried out. The XRD patterns (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information) showed characteristic peaks at 2θ angles of 7.89°, 13.25°, 15.55°, and 20.50° corresponding to the highly ordered and crystalline nature of the COF structure indicating the successful polymerization. These peaks are associated with the COF’s in-plane reflections (100) and (110) and higher order planes (200) and (001), respectively, arising from ordered pore lattice. − The presence of MWCNTs was confirmed by the presence of a strong (002) peak recorded at ∼26° and a weak (100) peak near 43° characteristics of in-plane graphitic carbon ordering. ,, The XRD patterns of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite exhibit both the COF and the MWCNTs peaks with lower intensity confirming successful integration. The absence of any additional peaks suggests that the composite is free from impurities and that the COF and MWCNTs are well-integrated, forming a homogeneous structure.

FE-SEM

FE-SEM was used to examine the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s morphological characteristics (Figure ). The SEM image; Figure a revealed a uniform spherical morphology of the COF particles in the nanoscale range. The uniform distribution of COF particles and the integration of MWCNTs (Figure b) within the COF matrix was clearly evident, as can be seen in the FE-SEM images of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite (Figure c,d). The nanocomposite showed a well-defined circular form with particle sizes in the nanoscale providing a porous and interconnected structure that increases the surface area and enhances its adsorption capacity. This successful integration is further evidenced by the absence of large MWCNTs aggregates, which would not be achievable without the acid functionalization pretreatment that introduces surface groups compatible with COF interactions.

This pattern clearly indicates a mesoporous structure with slit-shaped pores formed through the assembly of plate-like COF layers and the incorporation of multiwalled carbon nanotubes.

Overall, these textural characteristics demonstrate that the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite possesses an interconnected porous network and high surface accessibility that make it a promising candidate for adsorption, catalysis, and environmental remediation applications.

Influence of COF Loading on the Performance of COF-MWCNTs Nanocomposite

To better understand the contribution of the COF content to the final performance of the nanocomposite, different COF loadings (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 wt %) were integrated with a fixed amount of MWCNTs and evaluated in terms of textural properties and adsorption capability (q max) (Table ). Increasing the COF loading from 10 to 30 wt % resulted in a significant enhancement in S BET from 286.4 to 412.6 m2/g and total pore volume from 0.181 cm3/g to 0.276 cm3/g. This trend reflects the formation of additional porous COF domains on the nanotube network creating more accessible adsorption sites. At higher COF loadings (40–50 wt %), a decrease in S BET and pore volume was observed, which is attributed to aggregation of COF particles and partial obstruction of MWCNTs channels. This structural congestion negatively affected copper ion experimental adsorption capacity reducing the performance from 52.4 mg/g (30 wt % loading) to 44.1 mg/g at 50 wt % loading. These results indicate that an optimized COF loading around 30 wt % provides the best synergistic balance between high porosity, good dispersion, and efficient electron/ion transfer pathways. Both insufficient COF loading (lack of active sites) and excessive COF loading (pore blockage and reduced conductivity) negatively impact adsorption efficiency. Therefore, the selected loading used in this study represents the optimal formulation for achieving the highest Cu2+removal performance.

Comparison of S BET with Reported COF-Carbon Composites

The S BET of the synthesized 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite (412.6 m2/g) falls within the high range compared with other COF-Carbon hybrids reported in the literature (See Table S1 in the Supporting Information). For example, ultrathin COF/rGO composites typically show S BET between 320–380 m2/g due to partial restacking of graphene layers. COF-CNT hybrids reported by Jiang et al. and others generally range from 250-360 m2/g depending on CNT curvature and COF nucleation density. Some highly engineered COF-graphene systems exhibit higher S BET near 500 m2/g; however, these often involve additional exfoliation or postsynthetic functionalization steps not required in the present synthesis. The S BET obtained in our work, therefore, represents a favorable balance between high porosity and preserved nanotube channels, confirming the successful in situ growth of the COF structure on MWCNTs.

UV–Vis Spectroscopy

By employing ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy, the optical characteristics and band gap energy of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite were assessed (See Figure S2 in the Supporting Information). The 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite showed a wide absorption band in the visible region of the spectrum (Figure S2), indicating that the nanocomposite can efficiently absorb visible light. Also, the UV–vis spectrum of the nanocomposite revealed a band gap energy of 1.6 eV, suggesting favorable optical properties for visible-light absorption. The optical band gap (E g) of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was determined using the Tauc method based on the UV–vis absorption data. The Tauc relation for an indirect allowed transition is expressed by eq as follows 1 (αhν)1/2=A(hν−Eg) where α is the absorption coefficient, hν is the photon energy, and A is a proportionality constant. A Tauc plot of (αhν)1/2 versus hν was constructed, and the linear portion of the curve was extrapolated to intercept the energy axis (hν). The extrapolation yielded an energy band gap of 1.60 eV confirming the value reported from the absorption edge analysis. The measured band gap energy is not only relevant for photocatalytic applications but also influences the adsorption behavior of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite. Such a narrower band gap promotes higher electron mobility which enhances surface polarization and strengthens electrostatic attraction between the nanocomposite and Cu2+ions. The presence of MWCNTs further improves the conductivity and facilitates charge transfer across the interface. These electron-transfer pathways contribute to chemisorption-assisted uptake of Cu2+, which agrees with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model observed later in this study. Therefore, the optical band structure is directly connected to the enhanced adsorption efficiency of the nanocomposite.

Photoluminescence (PL) Spectroscopy

The efficiency of electron–hole pair separation and recombination in the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was evaluated using PL spectroscopy. A notable decrease in the PL intensity of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite compared with its individual components indicates efficient separation of electron–hole pairs, which is a favorable characteristic for materials with potential photocatalytic activity (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information). This behavior confirms the efficient charge separation promoted by the conductive MWCNTs framework. Reduced recombination enhances the availability of free electrons on the adsorbent surface, which interact with copper ions and facilitate both surface complexation and partial redox-assisted binding. The PL suppression is consistent with the band gap characteristics of the nanocomposite and therefore provides direct evidence for the enhanced chemisorption capacity reflected in the adsorption kinetics. These results demonstrate that the optical behavior of the composite is closely associated with its adsorption performance. It is important to emphasize that while the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite exhibits promising optical characteristics (narrow band gap of 1.6 eV and suppressed PL emission), no photocatalytic experiments were conducted in this study. Any potential photocatalytic application such as visible-light-driven degradation of organic pollutants or photoreduction of metal ions remains purely speculative at this stage and requires dedicated experimental validation in future work.

Optical Properties and Adsorption Link

Overall, the UV–vis and PL results reveal that the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite possesses a narrowed band gap, broad visible-light absorption, and suppressed radiative recombination. These optical characteristics enhance electron mobility and surface polarization, which in turn strengthen electrostatic attraction and facilitate chemisorption of Cu2+ ions. The strong correlation between optical properties and adsorption efficiency supports the dual-role mechanism of the composite, where both structural porosity and charge-transfer capability contribute to the good adsorption capacity observed experimentally.

The discussed characterization results give a thorough explanation of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s technical, morphological, and optical characteristics. The successful integration of COF and MWCNTs, combined with enhanced surface area and optical properties, makes this composite a highly intriguing substance for possible photocatalytic applications and effective for Cu2+ ion removal from wastewater.

FTIR Spectroscopy and Composite Formation Mechanism

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the chemical structure of the COF, acid-treated MWCNTs (MWCNTs), and the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite to elucidate the nature of interactions between the components. The FTIR spectrum of the pristine COF (Table S2 in the Supporting Information) exhibits a characteristic peak at 1620 cm–1 assigned to the CN stretching vibration from the imine linkages formed between aldehyde and amine precursors. It also shows bands at 1580 cm–1 and 1510 cm–1 corresponding to aromatic CC stretching vibrations, along with a band at 1250 cm–1 assigned to C–N stretching. The aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending vibration band is observed at 810 cm–1. Notably, the absence of peaks at 3300–3500 cm–1 (N–H stretching from p-phenylenediamine) and 1680 cm–1 (CO stretching from 1,3,5-triformylbenzene) confirms the successful condensation reaction and complete conversion of the precursors into the COF structure.

The FTIR spectrum of acid-treated MWCNTs (Table S2) shows a broad band at 3200–3600 cm–1 assigned to the O–H stretching vibration from the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. , The three characteristic bands observed at 1720 cm–1, 1580 cm–1, and 1220 cm–1 are respectively due to the CO stretching from carboxyl groups (−COOH), CC stretching from the graphitic structure, and C–O stretching from carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. These peaks confirm the successful functionalization of MWCNTs with oxygen-containing groups through acid treatment. The FTIR spectrum of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite (Table S2) exhibits peaks from both components. All characteristic COF peaks (1620, 1580, 1510, 1250, and 810 cm–1) are preserved. The broad O–H stretching band (3200–3600 cm–1) from MWCNTs is present but with reduced intensity and a slight shift to 3250 cm–1. The carboxyl CO peak at 1720 cm–1 shows a reduced intensity and broadening.

The changes observed in the composite spectrum provide insights into the integration mechanism. The shift and intensity reduction of the O–H stretching band (3200→3250 cm–1) and broadening of the CO peak suggest hydrogen bonding between carboxyl/hydroxyl groups on acid-treated MWCNTs surface and the imine nitrogen atoms and aromatic rings of the COF structure. The preservation of aromatic peaks from both the COF (1580, 1510 cm–1) and MWCNTs graphitic structure indicates intact aromatic systems that can engage in π–π stacking interactions, facilitating nanocomposite stability. The nanoscale morphology observed in TEM shows MWCNTs threading through the COF matrix, suggesting significant physical entanglement enhanced by the porous COF structure. The FTIR analysis confirms that the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite formation involves multiple noncovalent interactions (hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking) rather than covalent bonding, which is consistent with the mild synthesis conditions (ultrasonication in ethanol at ambient temperature). This noncovalent integration strategy preserves the intrinsic properties of both components; the high S BET and functional groups of COF and the high conductivity and mechanical strength of MWCNTs enhance the overall adsorption performance. The SEM and TEM analyses further support this mechanism by showing uniform distribution of MWCNTs within the COF matrix, maintained porosity of the composite structure, intimate contact between COF particles and MWCNTs surfaces, and without phase separation or aggregation. These combined characterization results confirm successful composite formation through complementary physical and chemical interactions, resulting in a stable, integrated nanocomposite with enhanced properties for copper ions adsorption.

Selection of the Optimal COF-MWCNT Composition

To justify the composite formulation used in this study, several COF loadings (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 wt %) were examined. As shown in Table , the adsorption performance improved with increasing COF content up to 30 wt %, primarily due to the creation of additional active sites and improved surface area. Above 30 wt % loading, however, excessive COF domains caused pore congestion and aggregation on the MWCNTs surface, reducing the adsorption efficiency. The 30 wt % COF formulation exhibited the best balance between porosity, functional group accessibility, and electron-transport pathways. Therefore, this optimized 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs composition was selected as the model material for all adsorption experiments.

Outcomes of Batch Adsorption Tests

For the sake of clarity, all adsorption experiments were performed under standardized conditions unless otherwise stated. A fixed solution volume of 100 mL containing Cu2+at the initial concentration of 50 mg/L was used. The adsorbent mass was 5 g/L, and all tests were conducted at 25 °C using a shaking speed of 150 rpm. Contact time was varied between 5 and 120 min for kinetic measurements, while pH, temperature, and dosage were varied one parameter at a time during the parametric studies. These conditions have now been fully described to ensure the complete reproducibility of the adsorption results.

The Impact of Contact Time

An essential component of adsorption procedures is the contact time. − In this study, the impact of contact duration on the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s ability to adsorb copper ions was investigated by conducting batch adsorption experiments at various time intervals ranging from 5 to 120 min (Figure a). All the tests utilized 100 mL of copper ion solution with a starting concentration of 50 mg/L. At room temperature, the mixture was agitated at a constant 150 rpm, and samples were taken at regular intervals to monitor the adsorption progress over a range of contact times to determine the time required to reach equilibrium. The findings demonstrated that in the early phases, the adsorption capacity increased quickly with significant adsorption occurring within the first 30 min. The adsorption capacity reached 30.8 mg/g within 30 min suggesting that the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite is rapidly absorbing copper ions. This rapid adsorption is ascribed to the large surface area and porous structure of the composite, which provides several active sites for the adsorption of copper ions.

The adsorption capacity continued to increase with increasing time, reaching a maximum of 45.2 mg/g at 120 min, with no significant change in adsorption beyond that time, indicating that the adsorption process reached equilibrium within 120 min. The quick adsorption kinetics seen in this investigation aligns with results for comparable COF-based composites that have been reported in the literature. For instance, a study by Xu et al. reported rapid adsorption for a COF-based composite in the removal of organic pollutants. The high adsorption capacity and rapid kinetics of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite render it a material with prospects for real-world uses in water treatment, where quick contamination removal is crucial.

Effect of Temperature

Because it impacts the kinetic energy of the adsorbate molecules, temperature is a crucial factor that impacts both the adsorption mechanism and the adsorbent–adsorbate connection. ,, The impact of temperature on copper ion adsorption in this investigation by the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was examined over a range of temperatures from 25 to 50 °C. The increase in adsorption capacity with temperature, indicates an endothermic process; however, to validate this behavior, thermodynamic parameters (ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°) were determined. A Van’t Hoff plot of ln K c versus 1/T (Section ) revealed a positive ΔH° value confirming an endothermic adsorption mechanism. ,, A positive ΔS° value suggests increased randomness at the solid-solution interface, likely due to the displacement of coordinated water molecules surrounding Cu2+ions. Negative ΔG° values across all temperatures indicate that the adsorption is spontaneous and becomes more favorable at higher temperatures. The observed increase in the adsorption capacity with temperature can be ascribed to multiple causes. To begin with, higher temperatures increase the copper ions kinetic energy, facilitating their movement toward the adsorbent surface and enhancing the adsorption rate. Second, the adsorption process is endothermic, meaning that the rise in entropy drives the adsorption, which is favorable at higher temperatures. This is consistent with the findings reported in the literature for similar adsorption processes. , At 25 and 50 °C, the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s adsorption capacities were found to be 42.1 and 50.3 mg/g, respectively. This pattern suggests that greater temperatures increase the composite’s effectiveness likely because of the improved diffusion of copper ions into the composite’s porous structure. The 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s extensive surface area and porous nature provide a large number of adsorption-active sites that become more accessible at higher temperatures (Figure b). The findings also imply that both chemical and physical interactions play a role in the process of adsorption. The fact that the adsorption capacity increases as temperature rises suggests that adsorption involves chemical interactions as well as physical interactions, which are amplified at higher temperatures. This is supported by the findings of other studies, which have shown the both chemical and physical interactions affect heavy metal-ion adsorption.

Effect of Adsorbent Dosage

As shown in Figure c, the dosage of 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was varied from 1.0 to 10.0 g/L in order to examine its impact on the adsorption effectiveness of copper ion. The findings demonstrated that as the dose of the adsorbent was increased, the adsorption capacity increased up to 5 g/L of adsorbent and then plateaued at higher dosages. The highest adsorption capacity was 48.7 mg/g at a dosage of 5 g/L of adsorbent. The greater availability of active sites for copper ion adsorption is responsible for the observed rise in the adsorption capacity with adsorbent dosage. Higher adsorption capacity is the result of increased surface area and active sites accessible for adsorption as the amount of 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite increases. At dosage higher than 5 g/L, the adsorption capability somewhat declined, indicating that the additional adsorbent did not contribute significantly to further adsorption. The restricted availability of copper ions in the solution and active site saturation are probably the causes of this plateau effect. For comparable adsorption procedures, the results are consistent with those documented in the literature. For instance, one study by Maleki et al. showed that up to a certain point, a composite material’s adsorption capability increased as the dosage of the adsorbent increased, after which it plateaued. Similarly, Ding et al. revealed that the adsorption effectiveness of heavy metal ions increased as the dosage of the adsorbent increased, peaking at an optimal dosage. The optimal adsorbent dosage of 5 g/L for the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite suggests that this amount provides a balance between adsorption efficiency and material usage. This optimal dosage can be used to design practical water treatment systems, where the goal is to maximize adsorption efficiency while minimizing material costs.

Impact of Initial pH

The adsorption efficiency was strongly impacted by the solution’s initial pH because it affects both the ionic form of the adsorbate and the surface charge of the adsorbent. , The impact of pH on the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s ability to adsorb copper ions was investigated across pH values between 2.0 and 7.0. The findings demonstrated that the experimental adsorption capability peaked at acidic pH values, reaching a maximum of 52.4 mg/g at pH 3.0 (Figure d). This indicates that the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite is most effective in acidic conditions. This pattern can be explained by the exterior chemistry of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite and the way copper ions behave in various pH ranges. At higher pH results, a higher concentration of hydroxide ions (OH–) exists, which can result in the precipitation of copper hydroxide and competition for adsorption sites. This decreases the number of active sites available for copper ions adsorption on the adsorbent surface, thereby causing the adsorption capacity to drop. Examples include Maleki et al. who showed that the adsorption capacity of a composite material for heavy metal ions increased at lower pH values due to enhanced electrostatic interactions. Similarly, Ding et al. found that as pH increased the copper ion adsorption effectiveness dropped because copper and hydroxide ions compete with one another for adsorption sites.

To further elucidate the pH-dependent behavior, the speciation of Cu2+ in aqueous solution was calculated using Visual MINTEQ software (See Figure S4 in the Supporting Information). At pH < 5.3, Cu2+ is the dominant species (>95%), with negligible Cu­(OH)2 precipitation. The critical precipitation pH for Cu­(OH)2 under our conditions (initial [Cu2+] = 50 mg/L) was found to be approximately 5.3. Therefore, the observed decline in adsorption capacity above pH 3.0 cannot be attributed to bulk precipitation but rather to two concurrent effects: (i) increased competition between H+ and Cu2+ for surface binding sites at pH < 3.0, and (ii) partial formation of soluble Cu­(OH)+ complexes at pH 4.0–5.0, which have lower affinity for the adsorbent surface. Above pH 5.3, precipitation increasingly interferes with the true adsorption measurements. This speciation analysis confirms that the optimal pH 3.0 represents the best compromise between minimal H+ competition and maximal Cu2+ availability as free divalent ions.

Adsorption Kinetic Models

To identify the rate-controlling steps of Cu2+ adsorption onto 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite, the experimental data were fitted to the pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), and intraparticle diffusion (IP-D) kinetic models. The linearized eqs (eqs S1–S3) are provided in the Supporting Information. The PFO model assumes that the adsorption rate is proportional to the number of unoccupied sites, while the PSO model assumes that chemisorption involving electron sharing or exchange between the adsorbent and Cu2+ ions is rate-limiting. As shown in Table and Figure a,b, both models yielded high correlation coefficients (R 2 = 0.987 for PFO, 0.999 for PSO). However, the calculated equilibrium adsorption capacity (q e,cal) from the PSO model (48.7 mg/g) is in excellent agreement with the experimental value (48.9 mg/g), whereas the PFO model significantly underestimated q e,cal (45.2 mg/g). Therefore, the PSO model provides the most representative description of the adsorption kinetics, confirming that chemisorption is the dominant rate-controlling step. This implies that the Cu2+ ions interact strongly with the nitrogen-rich COF framework and oxygen-containing functional groups on MWCNTs through electron sharing or surface complexation.

Application in Real Wastewater

To evaluate the practical applicability of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite, adsorption experiments were performed using real wastewater collected from an electroplating facility. The physicochemical characteristics of wastewater are summarized in Table S3. The initial Cu2+ concentration was 42.6 mg/L, along with competing ions such as Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, and Pb2+. The adsorption tests were conducted under optimized conditions (pH = 5.5, adsorbent dosage = 5 g/L, and contact time = 60 min). The removal efficiency of Cu2+ reached 91.3% and an adsorption capacity of 48.6 mg/g, which is slightly lower than that observed in synthetic solutions (≈97.8% and an adsorption capacity of 52.4 mg/g), indicating a minor competitive effect. However, the high removal efficiency confirms the strong selectivity of the adsorbent toward Cu2+ ions even in the presence of competing ions, which is attributed to strong coordination interactions with nitrogen- and oxygen-containing functional groups. The slight decrease in performance can be attributed to the competition for active sites by divalent ions (Ca2+, Mg2+) and complexation with dissolved organic matter. These results demonstrate the excellent anti-interference capability and potential applicability of the developed nanocomposite for real wastewater treatment.

Adsorption Mechanism

The adsorption of Cu2+ ions onto the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite proceeds through a synergistic multistep mechanism involving surface complexation, electrostatic attraction, π-interaction-assisted binding, pore diffusion, and charge-transfer-enhanced chemisorption (Figure ). The mechanism is supported by the combined structural, spectroscopic, kinetic, thermodynamic, and optical characterization results. First, the mesoporous structure and high specific surface area of the composite (S BET = 412.6 m2/g) provide abundant accessible adsorption sites and interconnected transport channels. The hybrid micro/mesoporous network formed by COF domains grown on the MWCNTs framework enables rapid external surface adsorption followed by intraparticle diffusion into internal pores, consistent with the multilinear intraparticle diffusion model and the fast initial uptake observed within the first 30 min. Second, the COF framework contains nitrogen-rich imine (CN) linkages and aromatic rings, while the acid-treated MWCNTs contribute oxygen-containing functional groups such as −COOH and −OH. These functional groups act as primary coordination centers for Cu2+ ions through Lewis acid–base interactions and surface complexation. Cu2+ ions can coordinate with imine nitrogen atoms and oxygen donor atoms, forming inner-sphere surface complexes. The dominance of the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (R 2 = 0.999) supports the idea that chemisorption involving electron sharing or exchange is the rate-controlling step. Third, electrostatic interactions contribute significantly, particularly under acidic conditions. At pH ≈ 3.0, partial protonation of surface functional groups occurs while sufficient negatively polarized oxygen sites remain available, creating a strong Coulombic attraction between the adsorbent surface and Cu2+ ions. The strong pH dependence of adsorption capacity confirms the role of surface charge effects in the uptake mechanism. Fourth, π–π and cation−π interactions also assist adsorption. The conjugated aromatic structures of both the COF skeleton and MWCNTs sidewalls provide delocalized π-electron systems that can interact with hydrated Cu2+species and their coordination spheres. These interactions help to stabilize Cu2+ near the surface and promote subsequent coordination with donor atoms. Fifth, the optical and electronic properties of the composite further enhance adsorption. UV–vis and PL analyses show a narrowed band gap (1.6 eV) and suppressed electron–hole recombination, indicating improved charge mobility and interfacial charge transfer through the MWCNTs network. This conductive pathway increases surface polarization and facilitates charge-transfer-assisted binding between Cu2+ ions and functional groups. The reduced PL intensity confirms efficient electron separation, which supports chemisorption and possible partial redox-assisted interactions at the interface. Thermodynamic parameters reinforce this interpretation. The positive enthalpy change (ΔH° > 0) indicates an endothermic process typical of activated chemisorption, while the positive entropy change (ΔS° > 0) suggests release of hydration water molecules from Cu2+during surface complex formation. The negative ΔG° values confirm spontaneous adsorption. Overall, Cu2+adsorption on the COF-MWCNTs nanocomposite occurs through a cooperative mechanism involving (i) rapid external surface binding, (ii) diffusion into mesopores and COF channels, (iii) coordination with N/O functional groups (chemisorption), and (iv) charge-transfer and π-interaction stabilization enabled by the conductive MWCNTS-COF interface. This synergistic mechanism explains the high capacity, fast kinetics, and good reusability of the nanocomposite.

Conclusions

The 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was successfully synthesized via a novel approach that combines in situ COF growth on MWCNTs with an optimized COF loading (10–50 wt %). This combination resulted in the formation of a stable nanocomposite facilitated by noncovalent interactions including hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking, as confirmed by FTIR analysis. The nanocomposite exhibited a highly crystalline structure, well-accessible mesoporous network, and enhanced S BET of 412.6 m2/g. The adsorbent achieved a maximum experimental adsorption capacity of 52.4 mg/g under optimal conditions (pH 3.0, 25 °C, adsorbent dose of 5 g/L, and contact time of 120 min). Langmuir isotherm fitting confirmed a theoretical maximum capacity (q max) of 55.6 mg/g for Cu2+ remediation, indicating an excellent potential for practical application. Adsorption capacity increased with temperature, indicating an endothermic nature. Kinetic studies revealed that the pseudo-second-order model adequately describes the adsorption process confirming the chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. Isotherm data were well-described by the Freundlich model. The reusability test demonstrated that the nanocomposite’s adsorption capability can be significantly maintained even after five cycles highlighting its sustainability. The nanocomposite’s good adsorption efficiency, rapid kinetics, and high reusability combined with favorable optical properties (band gap energy of 1.6 eV), position it as a promising candidate for wastewater treatment and environmental remediation. Future studies will focus on scaling the nanocomposite for large-scale applications and exploring its potential for removing additional organic pollutants and heavy metals. In addition, given its favorable optical properties, future work should include dedicated photocatalytic experiments (e.g., degradation of dyes or reduction of Cr­(VI)) to determine whether the material functions as an effective photocatalyst, a hypothesis that remains untested in the present study.

Materials and Methods

Materials

1,3,5-Triformylbenzene (TFB, 97%), p-phenylenediamine (PDA, 97%), benzoic acid (99.5%), 1,4-dioxane, acetic acid (99.8%), sulfuric acid (98%), nitric acid (65%), methanol, ethanol, DMF, and THF were used without further purification. Multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs; 10–30 nm diameter, 5–40 μm length, >95% purity) acted as a conductive backbone to enhance electron mobility and create accessible adsorption channels and were employed as the carbon support.

Methods

Preparation of COF Particles

A modulator-solvent induced polymerization approach was used for the preparation of COF particles. 0.10 mmol of TFB dissolved in 2.0 mL of 1,4-dioxane and ultrasonicated for 5 min (Solution A). 0.15 mmol of PDA (0.15 mmol) and benzoic acid (0.30 mmol) were dissolved in 2.0 mL of 1,4-dioxane and ultrasonicated for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution (Solution B). Benzoic acid was used as a modulator controlling nucleation rate and preventing COF aggregation, while 1,4-dioxane was used as a reaction solvent producing uniform COF domains. For polymerization initiation, 200 μL of solution A were mixed with 200 μL of solution B in a 2 mL reaction vial and ultrasonicated for 2 min to ensure homogeneity. Then, the condensation reaction occurred upon heating the vial at 80 °C for 15 min to initiate imine link formation due to the interaction between TFB and PDA as COF aldehyde and amine monomers, respectively. The obtained precipitate was cooled to room temperature, centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min, and washed three times with DMF to remove the unreacted monomers and three times with THF to remove residual DMF and oligomers. After that, the product was soaked in fresh THF for 12 h to ensure the complete removal of trapped species. The final COF product was obtained by drying the purified COF particles in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 24 h.

Synthesis of 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs Nanocomposite

Pretreatment of Multiwalled Carbon Nanotubes (MWCNTs)

Prior to composite formation, the as-received MWCNTs were subjected to acid treatment to introduce oxygen-containing functional groups (carboxyl and hydroxyl) on their surface, which facilitates better integration with the COF matrix and enhances dispersibility. This was done by dispersing 1.0 g of pristine MWCNTs in a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and nitric acid in a total volume of 100 mL in a round-bottom flask. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion and then refluxed at 80 °C for 4 h under continuous magnetic stirring. After being cooled to room temperature, the mixture was diluted with 500 mL of deionized water and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane. The collected MWCNTs were washed repeatedly with deionized water until the filtrate pH reached neutral (pH 6–7). The acid-treated MWCNTs were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h and stored in a desiccator until use. The acid treatment introduces carboxyl (−COOH) and hydroxyl (−OH) groups on the MWCNTs surfaces, as confirmed by FTIR analysis.

COF Integration with Acid-Treated MWCNTs

The COF-MWCNTs nanocomposite was synthesized through ultrasound-assisted dispersion and solvent evaporation methods with an optimized mass ratio. The mass ratio was optimized through preliminary experiments where different COF loadings (10–50 wt %) were tested to achieve maximum adsorption capacity while maintaining good dispersion. The 30 wt % COF loading resulted in the highest BET surface area (412.6 m2/g), optimal pore volume (0.276 cm3/g), and the maximum Cu 2 + adsorption capacity (52.4 mg/g), while higher loadings caused partial pore blocking and reduced ion accessibility. Therefore, the COF-MWCNTs nanocomposite with a fixed mass ratio of 30 wt % COF to 70 wt % MWCNTs was selected as the optimal and representative composition for the adsorption experiments reported in this study.

For the integration of the prepared COF particles with acid-treated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), they were combined in a solution of ethanol. First, 0.7 g of MWCNTs was dispersed in 80 mL of ethanol using ultrasonication for 30 min. Then, 0.3 g of COF particles was added to the MWCNTs dispersion (Mass ratio of 30 wt % COF: 70 wt % MWCNTs). The mixture was then subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min, followed by stirring for 6 h at room temperature. This step is crucial for achieving a homogeneous distribution of the components within the composite structure, physical entanglement of COF particles with MWCNTs, and potential hydrogen bonding between COF functional groups and oxygen-containing groups on the acid-treated MWCNTs surface. The final 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was formed by transferring the mixture to a Petri dish and drying it in a vacuum for 24 h at 60 °C to evaporate the ethanol solvent. This drying process helps to consolidate the nanocomposite structure, ensuring that the COF particles and MWCNTs are firmly integrated, thereby enhancing the overall performance of the nanocomposite for adsorption applications. The dried nanocomposite was gently ground using a mortar and pestle to obtain a fine powder and then stored in a sealed container in a desiccator.

Nanocomposite Characterization

The synthesized 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite was comprehensively characterized using different analytical tools. FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the range 4000–400 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1. The crystalline composition of the composite had been verified having a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer and utilizing XRD. The morphological features and particle size distribution were examined by utilizing a Zeiss Supra 55 device for high-resolution SEM. The detailed internal structure and particle morphology were further investigated by employing a JEOL JEM-2100F microscope and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). The Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer was used to obtain N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms to ascertain the distribution of pore sizes and specific surface area. A Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer was used to perform UV–vis spectroscopy in order to evaluate the composite’s optical characteristics and band gap energy. The efficiency of electron–hole pair separation and recombination was evaluated by using photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy with a Horiba Jobin Yvon Fluorolog-3 spectrometer. A comprehensive knowledge of the structural, morphological, and optical characteristics of the nanocomposite was made, which is crucial for its application in the adsorption of copper ions in this study and provides insights into its potential for future photocatalytic applications.

Tests of Batch Adsorption

Preparation and Analysis of Cu2+ Solutions

A stock solution of Cu2+ (1000 mg/L) was prepared by dissolving an accurately weighed amount of copper­(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O, analytical grade) in deionized water. Working solutions of desired concentrations (10–100 mg/L) were obtained by the appropriate dilution of the stock solution. The concentration of Cu2+ ions before and after adsorption was determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS, PerkinElmer A Analyst 400) at a wavelength of 324.8 nm. Calibration was performed using a series of standard Cu2+ solutions, and the calibration curve exhibited excellent linearity (R 2 > 0.999). Calibration data for Cu2+ determination by AAS are presented in Table S4 in the Supporting Information. The detection limit of the method was approximately 0.02 mg/L and all measurements were conducted in triplicate with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of less than 3%, ensuring high analytical precision and reproducibility.

To assess the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s adsorption efficiency to remove copper ions from wastewater, a sequence of tests on batch adsorption was conducted. Contact time, temperature, adsorbent dosage, and the solution’s initial pH were among the variables that were examined to determine their impacts on the adsorption efficiency.

Effect of Contact Time

The contact time experiments were performed to determine the time required to reach adsorption equilibrium. A fixed amount of 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite (5 g/L) was added to the solution of copper ions with a starting concentration of 50 mg/L. The adsorbent dosage was fixed at 5 g/L, corresponding to a solid–liquid ratio (S/L) of 0.005 g/mL (i.e., 0.5 g in 100 mL solution). The selected dosage (5 g/L) was based on preliminary optimization to ensure sufficient active sites while avoiding particle aggregation and mass transfer limitations. The mixture was agitated at a steady speed of 150 rpm at 25 °C. Samples were taken at regular intervals ranging from 5 to 240 min to monitor the adsorption progress. According to the data, the adsorption rate was high in the first 60 min, and the elimination of copper ions increased significantly. After around 120 min, the adsorption process stabilized, and the adsorption capacity did not show a significant change.

The Impact of Temperature

Batch tests were performed at 298, 308, and 318 K to examine the effect of the temperature change on the adsorption process. A starting copper ion solution concentration of 50 mg/L and 5 g/L of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite were used in the experiments. Each temperature setting involved 120 min of stirring of the mixture at 150 rpm. The findings demonstrated that the adsorption capacity increased in tandem with temperature, indicating an endothermic adsorption mechanism and that higher temperatures promote copper ions adsorption.

Effect of Adsorbent Dosage

By varying the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite dosage from 1.0 to 10.0 g/L in copper ions solution at an initial concentration of 50 mg/L, the impact of adsorbent dose on the adsorption effectiveness was examined. For 120 min, the experiments were carried out at 25 °C while being continuously stirred at 150 rpm. According to the findings, the adsorption capacity increased as the dosage of the adsorbent increased, reaching a plateau at higher dosages. This indicates that there is an optimal amount of adsorbent required to achieve a maximum adsorption efficiency.

Effect of Initial pH

The initial pH of the solution is a critical parameter affecting the adsorption process. , Experiments were conducted at different pH values ranging from 2.0 to 7.0 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH to ascertain the ideal pH for the elimination of copper ions. A fixed amount of 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite (5 g/L) was added to 50 mg/L of copper ions solution in 100 mL. At room temperature, the mixture was stirred for 120 min at 150 rpm. The findings demonstrated that at acidic pH levels, the adsorption effectiveness was at a maximum, with a significant decrease in the adsorption capacity with pH increase. The optimal pH for maximum copper ion removal was found to be around 3.0. The 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite’s adsorption behavior was better understood thanks to these batch adsorption tests, highlighting the importance of optimizing experimental conditions to achieve efficient copper ion removal from wastewater.

Regeneration and Reusability Test

To assess the reusability and economic feasibility of the 30 wt % COF-70 wt % MWCNTs nanocomposite, adsorption–desorption cycles were conducted using 0.1 M HCl as the regenerating agent. Preliminary tests with different HCl concentrations (0.05–0.5 M) indicated that 0.1 M provides an optimal balance between the desorption efficiency and structural stability. The nanocomposite was used for five consecutive cycles of copper ions removal. After each adsorption cycle, the spent adsorbent was gathered, washed with distilled water to remove unbound ions, and then treated with 50 mL of 0.1 M HCl under stirring for 120 min to desorb Cu2+ ions. The regenerated adsorbent was subsequently washed to neutral pH and dried for 5 h at 80 °C before reuse.
