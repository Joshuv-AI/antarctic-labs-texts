Identification of Filifactor Alocis in Periodontal Biofilms using Polymerase Chain Reaction Technique: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Kaliaperumal Poorana, Nallasivam Lavanya, Munuswamy Jeeva Rekha, Rajendran Maheaswari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aim:Contribution of organisms causing periodontal disease remains unclear. Strong association has emerged between the presence of gram-positive Filifactor alocis (F.alocis) and periodontitis. Hence, we aimed to identify the presence of F.alocis in periodontal biofilms through PCR technique in health and disease.Settings and Design:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 45 participants divided into three equal groups based on their periodontal conditions, namely, healthy, generalized chronic periodontitis (GCP) and generalized aggressive periodontitis (GAP).Methods and Materials:Subgingival plaque samples were obtained, and real-time PCR was used to identify the presence of F.alocis. The clinical parameters, plaque index, sulcus bleeding index, probing pocket depth, and clinical attachment level were determined.Statistical Analysis:The statistical analysis was done using the software SPSS (Statistical package for social science). All the data were analyzed using the ANOVA test.Results:The difference between mean plaque index, sulcus bleeding index, probing pocket depth, and clinical attachment level was statistically significant between healthy and periodontitis (chronic and aggressive) sites. F.alocis was identified in all samples. The mean cycle threshold (CT) values of healthy, GCP, and GAP were 38.01, 30.85, and 28.92, respectively, and the results are statistically significant.Conclusion:The load of organism was significantly higher in the GAP group followed by the GCP group and lowest in the healthy group. Bacterial load was highest in teeth with deep pockets. Hence, F.alocis seems to be a powerful diagnostic marker organism for periodontal disease, and it should be considered an important periodontal pathogen.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is a biofilm-induced chronic inflammatory disease which leads to the annihilation of the periodontium.[1] The tissue damage in periodontitis is umpired by disparaging host immune responses coordinated by a team of periodontal pathogens in subgingival biofilm.[2] Although the etiological role of bacteria is proven, impacts of individual species or clusters of organisms are vague.

There are meagre data to consider that definitely cultivable bacteria bestow more to the pathogenesis of periodontitis than fastidious microbes. It is also unclear that the conceded periodontal pathogens P. gingivalis, P. intermedia, and T. forsythia are apt diagnostic markers to discriminate health and disease[345] as greater than 50% of the phylotypes documented in the oral cavity are yet to be cultured.[67] This raises queries on the relative significance of these organisms in the etiopathogenesis of periodontal disease.

The dogma that gram-negative microbes preponderate is no longer plausible with the substantial escalation of gram-positive microbes in deep unhealthy sites. As documented by some studies, gram-positive organisms like Filifactor alocis (F.alocis) are found in larger quantities than gram-negative organisms. F.alocis is a fastidious, gram-positive, obligate anaerobic rod, present in human gingival sulcus.[8] F.alocis is attributed as the second most prevalent in chronic periodontitis and third most prevalent in generalized aggressive periodontitis and suggested to be an excellent marker organism for periodontal disease.[9] F.alocis should be charted along with the 12 species used for routine diagnostics of periodontitis-associated bacterial flora.[10] It can be used as a potential marker for periodontal disease activity, since it is found in large numbers in chronic periodontitis subjects than people with healthy periodontium.[7]

F.alocis is a new emerging periodontal pathogen which has recently gained reputation in the field of periodontology due to its documented evidence in pathogenesis of various forms of periodontal diseases such as chronic periodontitis, aggressive periodontitis, and periimplantitis by its inherent virulence characteristics. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have provided the evidence for F.alocis as a main periodontal pathogen, and it has been successfully isolated in DM (diabetes mellitus) and GDM (gestational diabetes mellitus) with periodontitis.[1112]

Unique virulence potential of F.alocis is contributed by its robust characteristics such as resistance to oxidative stress, production of collagenases and proteases, induction of epithelial cell apoptosis, extracellular matrix degradation, suppression of neutrophilic granulocytic reaction, and inhibition of compliment activation.[1314]

Markers of periodontal disease activity are studied through numerous diagnostic methodologies. By monitoring the presence or absence of pathogens in a patient, one can predict the recurrence of disease and also can improve the prognosis. The “gold standard” culture methods to detect periodontal pathogens have inherent advantages and also numerous limitations. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) overwhelms the limitations and is an exceptionally sensitive and specific technique for exploration of subgingival plaque biofilm.[15] Hence, we aimed to identify the presence of F.alocis in periodontal biofilms through PCR technique in healthy, generalized chronic periodontitis (GCP) and generalized aggressive periodontitis (GAP).

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by Institutional Ethics Committee. After getting the written informed consent, the participants were recruited in the study.

Study population

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was done among the out-patients from the Department of Periodontics. We have recruited 45 participants for the purpose of the study, and out of which, 15 were healthy controls, 15 had generalized chronic periodontitis (GCP), and the last 15 had generalized aggressive periodontitis (GAP). We have included systemically healthy participants between 20–50 years of age with at least a total of 20 natural teeth and a minimum of 5 teeth in a quadrant. Pregnant, lactating mothers, participants with habit of betel-nut, pan masala, tobacco chewing, smoking, and alcohol consumption, who had received any antibiotic therapy and any surgical or nonsurgical therapy in the last 6 months were excluded.

Operational definition

Healthy controls: Participants with the Probing Pocket Depth (PPD) of ≤3 mm.

Generalized chronic periodontitis: A minimum of two sites per quadrant with the PPD of ≥4 mm.

Generalized aggressive periodontitis: Participants with a minimum of two interproximal sites per quadrant with PPD of ≥4 mm for ≤25 years and PPD of ≥6 mm for 26-35 years.[16]

Procedure

After getting the approvals and the consent, complete medical history and oral examination was done. The study included 45 patients aged 20–50 years, divided into three groups: healthy periodontium (n = 15), generalized chronic periodontitis (GCP) (n = 15), and generalized aggressive periodontitis (GAP) (n = 15). Clinical parameters such as Sulcus Bleeding Index, Plaque Index, Probing Pocket Depth, and Clinical Attachment Level were used to evaluate the subjects. Subgingival plaque biofilm was collected from each subject using sterile paper points (Densply®), number 30, inserted into the depth of the pocket [Figure 1]. After 30 seconds of insertion, the paper points were removed and transferred to a microcentrifuge tube kept in the ice and transported to the microbiological laboratory for downstream analysis immediately.

(a) Subgingival plaque sample collection using paper points (b) Transfer of paper points to micro-centrifuge tube

Microbiological analysis

Microbial counts of F.alocis in the collected samples were assessed by analyzing the expression of 16S rRNA gene sequences by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) technique.

DNA isolation

Subgingival plaque samples were retrieved, and 1 ml of lysozyme enzyme was added. Lysozyme enzyme solution was prepared using Tris HCl, EDTA, tritonex, and lysozyme (20 mg per sample). After adding the enzyme, the tube was incubated at 37°C for 30 min in a water bath. 20 μL of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate was added to the sample and incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the bath. After incubation, an equal volume of phenol chloroform solution was added and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min.

The supernatant was collected and transferred into new Eppendorf tubes, and equal volume of Chloro-iso-amine alcohol was added and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and transferred into a new Eppendorf tube. A volume of 1/10th of sodium acetate and 300 μL of 100% ethanol (absolute) was added to the Eppendorf tube and stored at -50º C overnight. The following day, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and discarded, and the pellet was air dried. A total of 30–50 μL of DNA/RNAse free water was added to elute the DNA.

Real-time PCR technique

Real-time PCR was done to identify the presence of F.alocis. The primers and procedure of real-time PCR were as suggested by Siqueira and Rocas.[17] The reaction mix of 20 μL contains 10 μL of SYBR Green Supermix, 1 μL of forward and 1 μL of reverse primer at 10 μL concentrations, 2 μL of DNA template, and 6 μL of sterile distilled water. This was loaded in a 96-well plate and F. alocis was identified with the amplification of 16S rRNA. The primer sequences used in the study were as follows,

Forward Primer: 5’-CAGGTGGTTTAACAAGTTAG TGG-3’.

Reverse Primer: 5’- CTAAGTTGTCCTTAGCTGTC TCG-3’.

PCR conditions were, initial denaturation of 95°C for 10 mins, denaturation of 95°C for 15 sec, annealing of 50°C for 2 mins, and extension of 60°C for a minute and 40 cycles. The amplification was performed in a thermal cycler (7900 HT RTPCR, Applied Biosystem, UK) with SYBR green fluorophore. The PCR yielded a product of 310bps. Negative controls without DNA were used for quality control. A melting curve analysis was made, and the results were analyzed with the RQ manager and cycle threshold (CT) calculated [Figure 2].

Real time PCR amplification plot shows the peak of F.alocis and Cycle threshold values

Statistical analysis

The data were collected in paper-based forms and entered in Microsoft Excel, and the analysis was done using SPSS version 15. Continuous variables like plaque index, sulcus bleeding index, PPD, and clinical attachment level in the groups were reported as mean with standard deviation (SD). Real-time CT values for the identification of F.alocis were expressed as mean with SD. The independent t test was used to find the difference in plaque index, sulcus bleeding index, PPD, and clinical attachment level between the groups. ANOVA was used to find the difference in CT values of F.alocis between the three groups. A CT value of 30 had been taken as a cutoff for the identification of F.alocis. A P value of <0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

RESULTS

In a total of 45 participants, the mean plaque index in whole mouth for healthy, GCP, and GAP was 0.71 ± 0.17, 2.59 ± 0.15, and 0.83 ± 0.22, respectively. At the selected site was 0.67 ± 0.16, 2.60 ± 0.20, and 0.84 ± 0.22 in all the three groups mentioned. There was a significant difference in whole mouth mean plaque index as well as in the selected sites between the three groups studied with the P value of <0.001, but the post hoc test reveals no significant difference between healthy and GAP (whole mouth: P = 0.12, selected site: P = 0.07).

The mean Sulcus bleeding index for whole mouth for healthy group, GCP, and GAP was 0.74 ± 0.16, 3.71 ± 0.28, and 4.11 ± 0.26, respectively. The same at the selected region was 0.51 ± 0.18 in healthy, 3.89 ± 0.19 in GCP, and 4.01 ± 0.21 in GAP. The difference between all the three groups was highly significant with the P value of <0.001. However, the post hoc test reveals at selected region; there were no significant difference observed with the P value of 0.35.

The mean (SD) PPD for whole mouth for healthy group, GCP, and GAP was 2.73 ± 0.21, 5.92 ± 0.35, and 6.23 ± 0.45 mm, respectively. The same at selected sites were 2.71 ± 0.22, 5.76 ± 0.58, and 6.27 ± 0.14 mm for healthy, GAP, and GCP. Significant difference in sulcus bleeding index was measured between all the three study groups (P ≤ 0.05).

The mean clinical attachment level (CAL) for healthy group was 0 mm, for chronic periodontitis group was 5.78 ± 1.22 mm, and for aggressive periodontitis was 6.27 ± 0.58 mm. There was no significant difference in CAL between GAP and GCP (P = 0.17). DISCUSSION

Periodontitis is a ubiquitous microbial disease instigated by multifaceted biologic mechanisms between putative periodontal pathogens and the susceptible host leading to the devastation of periodontal tissues. Identification of the key pathogen opens the gateway for the successful treatment and prediction of prognosis of the periodontal disease. This study found that the mean CT values of F.alocis in healthy periodontium were high followed by GCP and then GAP. It indirectly reflects the bacterial load.

In our study, we found a low CT value in the GAP group than the GCP group. However, in contrast to our results, a study in south Indian subpopulation reported a high bacterial load in GCP than GAP. This could be due to the involvement of different strains of F.alocis, which might cause the disease in GAP and GCP. The PCR identifies the presence or absence of F.alocis and does not differentiate the strains. The above study suggested that high bacterial load in GAP leads to increased enzyme activity and destruction of periodontium resulted in increased PD and CAL.[1218]

The flora in periodontal pocket is characterized by the presence of innumerable phylotypes of bacteria of varied characteristics and more than 50% of them are not yet cultured. It is possible that the traditional prototype of a principally gram-negative flora concomitant with disease may undergo radical changes when more sophisticated diagnostic methods are used to validate this not yet cultured bacteria. This alters the therapeutic modality of treating the periodontal disease, which may lead to a more tangible benefit for the patient.

A recent study by Ashwini et al.[11] in GDM subjects with periodontitis demonstrated a decrease in loads of P.gingivalis and F.alocis after nonsurgical periodontal therapy. Another study showed co-occurrence of F.alocis with a red complex organism; T.forsythia in diabetic subjects leads to increased severity of periodontal disease in them.[12]

The relevance of F.alocis is supported by several other epidemiological studies conducted in the past years using DNA-based techniques. F.alocis was detected in generalized aggressive periodontitis (GAP) patients as well as in chronic periodontitis (CP) patients with prevalence rates varying between 45%[19] and 90%,[10] liable on the methods employed. Some authors propose F.alocis as a marker organism for periodontal disease[10] and even for the change from periodontal health to disease.[4] For the present study, PCR technique was employed for microbial analysis. More interestingly, PCR has also unveiled a number of uncultured and unidentified periodontal pathogens in health and disease in many epidemiologic studies.[42021]

Traditional culture methods are labor intensive, needs a skilled personnel, stringent sampling, and time consuming. In addition, maintaining bacterial vitality is difficult and inability to detect low numbers of microorganisms were the disadvantages.[22] Other diagnostic tests like dark field microscopy detect motile periodontal pathogen and immunodiagnostic methods like immunofluorescence assay, flow cytometry, show false-positive results, and cross-reactions. Hence, PCR overwhelms the above restrictions and is sensitive to detect a single copy of the target DNA.[23]

Kumar PS et al.[7] employed 16S PCR amplification using universal 16S primers of dental plaque samples, followed by cloning and sequencing to allow an open-ended and quantitative exploration of the bacterial populations present in periodontal health and disease. Bao et al., in 2022,[24] used label-free quantification proteomics to study the extracellular vesicle (EV) proteins in F.alocis strain ATCC35896 and identified 28 EV proteins.

The detection of high bacterial load of the organism in GAP and GCP is substantial evidence that proposes an association of F.alocis in periodontal disease. A low bacterial load in healthy controls who had a probing depth of less than 3 mm with healthy periodontium does not require laborious therapeutics. Periodontal status of a person depends on oral hygiene, immune response, and genetic background of the host they should also be considered. In addition, a deep diseased periodontal pocket in GAP and GCP favors the existence of increased quantities of bacteria and that any organism should be isolated more persistently from these patients.

The current study used a highly sensitive technique for the identification of F.alocis. Very few studies are available unveiling the presence of F.alocis in periodontal biofilms of Indian population. Our study is one of the earlier studies to elucidate the existence of F.alocis in South Indians through highly authentic RT-PCR technique. Further studies are needed to study the oral microbiome which is found to be responsible for the pathogenesis of disease by disrupting the balance in the symbiotic relationship among the organisms by its unrestrained virulent characteristics.

CONCLUSION

The prevalence of F.alocis in both GAP and GCP patients was found to be elevated as compared to healthy control. F.alocis can be a potential diagnostic marker in periodontal disease. The putative pathogens cause dysbiosis, and the role of putative periodontal pathogen F.alocis, causing dysbiosis need to be evaluated in future studies for preventive, predictive, and personalized treatment, beneficial for patients and future large scale research is the need of the hour. In addition, omics studies have opened a new gate to explore the unknown facts which play a strategic role in interaction within microbe–microbe and host–microbe.
