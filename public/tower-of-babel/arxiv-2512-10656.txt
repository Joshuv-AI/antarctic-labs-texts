Token Sample Complexity of Attention
Léa Bohbot Cyril Letrouit Gabriel Peyré ENS, PSL Univ. LMO, Univ. Paris-Saclay, CNRS CNRS, ENS, PSL Univ. François-Xavier Vialard LIGM, Univ. Gustave Eiffel, CNRS

Abstract
As context windows in large language models continue to expand, it is essential to characterize how attention behaves at extreme sequence lengths. We introduce token-sample complexity: the rate at which attention computed on n n tokens converges to its infinite-token limit. We estimate finite- n n convergence bounds at two levels: pointwise uniform convergence of the attention map, and convergence of moments for the transformed token distribution. For compactly supported (and more generally sub-Gaussian) distributions, our first result shows that the attention map converges uniformly on a ball of radius R R at rate C ⁡ ( R ) / n C(R)/\sqrt{n} , where C ⁡ ( R ) C(R) grows exponentially with R R . For large R R , this estimate loses practical value, and our second result addresses this issue by establishing convergence rates for the moments of the transformed distribution (the token output of the attention layer). In this case, the rate is C ′ ​ ( R ) / n β C^{\prime}(R)/n^{\beta} with β < 1 2 \beta<\tfrac{1}{2} , and C ′ ​ ( R ) C^{\prime}(R) depends polynomially on the size of the support of the distribution. The exponent β \beta depends on the attention geometry and the spectral properties of the tokens distribution. We also examine the regime in which the attention parameter tends to infinity and the softmax approaches a hardmax, and in this setting, we establish a logarithmic rate of convergence. Experiments on synthetic Gaussian data and real BERT models on Wikipedia text confirm our predictions.



1
Introduction

The remarkable success of Transformer models in natural language processing and beyond has been driven by attention mechanisms that can flexibly handle very long input sequences
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
;
Devlin et al., 2019
)
. Recent large-context Transformers extend context windows from
100
K to
10
M tokens
(
Reid et al., 2024
;
Hooper et al., 2024
)
, allowing models to attend over entire books or codebases in a single forward pass. This trend raises fundamental questions about how attention mechanisms behave in the regime of extremely large sequences, and how much benefit is gained by providing ever more tokens as input. In this work, we introduce the notion of
token sample complexity
to characterize the precision gained by feeding additional tokens into an already-trained attention model. Classical statistical sample complexity focuses on how a model’s performance changes as the amount of training data increases. In contrast, we assume the transformer is already trained and examine a different asymptotic setting: for a fixed input distribution, we let the number
n
of tokens in the context grow. As this input dimension
n
increases, our objective is to understand how the resulting attention outputs behave in this limit. We quantify here how many tokens are required for the attention outputs to approach their limiting behavior as
n\to\infty
.

We formalize the infinite-token limit of a Transformer’s self-attention as a continuous operator on probability measures. Following
(
De Bie et al., 2019
;
Pevny and Kovarik, 2019
)
, we view a sequence of
n
tokens as an empirical distribution
\hat{\mu}_{n}
that approximates an underlying token distribution
\mu
. We define the attention mechanism in the limit of infinitely many tokens as an operator
\Gamma
acting on
\mu
, providing a continuous reference that finite-
n
attention approximates. This measure-theoretic perspective was pioneered by
(
Sander et al., 2022
)
and extended to study Transformers as interacting particle systems
(
Lu et al., 2019
;
Geshkovski et al., 2024
;
Geshkovski et al., 2025
;
Castin et al., 2025
;
Burger et al., 2025
;
Bruno et al., 2025
;
Zimin et al., 2025
;
Chen et al., 2025
)
.

We use this infinite-token limit to formalize
token sample complexity
: the rate at which finite-
n
attention approaches its infinite-limit counterpart. With compactly supported token distributions, one naturally expects parametric convergence at rate
1/\sqrt{n}
. This is not what we observe experimentally. On both synthetic Gaussian data and BERT applied to Wikipedia, convergence is slower, following sub-parametric rates
n^{-\beta}
with
\beta<1/2
. The exponent
\beta
depends on the
attention horizon
(how far the kernel reaches) and the
spectral reach
(the scale of token variance): when these grow large, as with hardmax attention, convergence can slow down dramatically, even approaching logarithmic rates.

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we develop the notion of
token sample complexity
for attention mechanisms: the rate at which attention outputs converge to their continuous limit as
n
grows. We analyze this at two levels: (i) the almost-sure convergence of the
attention map
itself at a given query
x
, and (ii) the convergence in law of the
transformed distribution
for random inputs. More precisely, our key contributions are the following.

In Section
3
, we quantify attention map convergence. Proposition
3.3
establishes pointwise convergence at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
with exponential dependence on attention horizon and spectral reach. Theorem
3.4
provides uniform convergence on balls
B_{R}
for sub-Gaussian tokens, with explicit quantitative estimation of the rate.

In Section
4
, we analyze moment convergence, directly relevant for downstream tasks relying on aggregate statistics. Theorem
4.4
gives rates for the moment of any Lipschitz or quadratic function of the attention, covering mean and covariance. For compact support, Proposition
4.1
yields
C(R)/\sqrt{n}
with
C(R)
exponential in radius
R
, becoming uninformative for large
R
. This reveals a fundamental trade-off: by tracking a more relevant characteristic distance, in Theorem
4.4
we obtain polynomial (rather than exponential) dependence on scale, with a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\beta})
where
\beta<1/2
depends on attention horizon and spectral properties of the token distribution, which better captures real-data behavior.

In Section
5
, we complete our analysis by deriving convergence rates for infinite horizon (hardmax) attention in the Gaussian case: Proposition
5.1
shows that convergence becomes only logarithmic in
n
, of order
1/\sqrt{\ln n}
.

In Section
6
, we illustrate our theoretical findings on synthetic data. These results are further motivated by real-data experiments: using a BERT model
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
on long text inputs (Wikipedia documents), we empirically observe the predicted deceleration of convergence rates. Observed rates remain strictly slower than
1/\sqrt{n}
, demonstrating that the sub-parametric convergence regime governs attention behavior in practice.

1.2
Background and related work

Classical PAC sample complexity.
In statistical learning theory,
sample complexity
refers to the number of training samples
N
required for a learning algorithm to achieve a probably approximately correct (PAC) solution
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
)
. The classical question is: given i.i.d. examples of fixed dimension, how many samples are needed so that, with high probability, the learned model’s error is within
\varepsilon
of the optimum? Classical results show
N
scales with hypothesis class complexity (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity), but crucially assumes each sample has fixed, finite dimension. This is the regime of standard PAC theory: we collect more fixed-size data points (images, sentences) to improve generalization.

Token sample complexity (this work).

In our setting, we consider an entirely different kind of complexity question: the model is
already trained
, and we are not collecting new independent samples; instead, we are increasing the number of tokens in a single input sequence. Modern Transformer encoders like BERT process an input as a single sequence of tokens (e.g. words or subword tokens in a sentence) of length
n
. Importantly,
n
is not fixed—the architecture allows variable-length input, and indeed self-attention inherently permits an unbounded number of tokens by design
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
. Thus, we can think of
n
as a parameter of the problem instance, akin to an input dimension that can grow.

This inference-time perspective aligns with a growing interest in processing extended contexts at inference. In-context learning (ICL) exemplifies this: Transformers solve tasks often requiring more tokens than seen during training
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Sander et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. Similarly, chain-of-thought prompting elicits multi-step reasoning purely through extended input sequences
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
. As these methods push
n
beyond training-time lengths, understanding how attention accuracy scales with
n
during inference becomes a natural question.

Transformers and self-attention

A Transformer model is built from layers that mix content across positions via
self-attention
and process each position with feed-forward networks
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
. In a Transformer encoder, an input sequence of
n
tokens is represented as row vectors
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(typically after an initial embedding and positional encoding). A self-attention head computes output vectors
z_{i}
as weighted aggregates of a value matrix
V=[x_{1};\dots;x_{n}]
using affinities between tokens. In the common softmax attention, the attention weight from token
i
to token
j
is

a_{ij}=\frac{\exp((x_{i}W^{Q})\cdot(x_{j}W^{K})^{\top}/\sqrt{d})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}\exp((x_{i}W^{Q})\cdot(x_{j^{\prime}}W^{K})^{\top}/\sqrt{d})},

where
W^{Q},W^{K}
are learned projection matrices for queries and keys. Then
z_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x_{j}W^{V})
(with
W^{V}
the value projection). The multi-head attention mechanism repeats this process with multiple sets of
(W^{Q},W^{K},W^{V})
and concatenates the results. Crucially, the self-attention mechanism has
no intrinsic limitation on
n
: the sums run from
1
to
n
, and aside from computational or memory constraints, one can feed as many tokens as desired. This is in stark contrast to, say, fixed-size CNNs or RNNs that might struggle with very long dependencies. Attention’s power lies in its ability to capture long-range interactions, but this comes at the expense of a quadratic cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
in sequence length, motivating research into more efficient variants.

Sparse attention and efficient variants

Our results also give insight into the error created when the input tokens are randomly sub-sampled, which can be viewed as a simple model of attention sparsification. In practice, more advanced sparsification strategies are used, such as restricting tokens to local neighborhoods as in Longformer
(
Beltagy et al., 2020
)
and BigBird
(
Zaheer et al., 2020
)
, or explicitly projecting keys and values to lower-dimensional subspaces as in Linformer
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
. Although our findings do not cover those methods, they offer an initial step toward a theoretical understanding of such approaches.

Transformers with arbitrarily many tokens

To mathematically analyse convergence rate in the large
n
limit, it is crucial to define the limit mean field model, which corresponds to defining attention as operating over a probability distribution of tokens. This mean field model was initially presented in
(
De Bie et al., 2019
)
and
(
Pevny and Kovarik, 2019
)
, followed by its formulation for Transformers in the work of
(
Vuckovic et al., 2020
)
,
(
Sander et al., 2022
)
,
(
Geshkovski et al., 2024
)
, and
(
Geshkovski et al., 2025
)
. It can also be coupled with a neural ODE framework to model infinitely deep transformers, as studied for instance in
(
Sander et al., 2022
)
, note however that our contributions do not address this setting and focus on a single attention layer.
Similarly,
(
Geshkovski et al., 2024
)
,
(
Geshkovski et al., 2025
)
and
(
Castin et al., 2025
)
develop a
Transformer PDE
for standard softmax attention and prove it is the mean-field limit of an
n
-particle system as
n\to\infty
. The paper
(
Geshkovski et al., 2025
)
models tokens as points evolving on the
sphere

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, to mimic the normalization layers in real Transformers, and connects the resulting equations to various models in mathematical physics. Recent works that build on this mean-field view also include
(
Karagodin et al., 2024
)
(clustering with causal masking) and
(
Bruno et al., 2025
)
(meta-stable clustering). Notably,
(
Castin et al., 2025
)
prove that the Transformer PDE preserves Gaussianity, reducing the dynamics to mean and covariance evolution. In our work, we focus on a single attention layer in the infinite-token limit, using the Gaussian case as a tractable baseline to derive explicit convergence rates and build intuition for extensions to Gaussian mixtures and other sub-Gaussian distributions.

2
Mathematical preliminaries

2.1
Definition of self-attention

We fix
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Vectors live in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathrm{softmax}(w)_{i}\;:=\;\frac{e^{w_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{w_{j}}}\,.

Definition 2.1

(Single-head self-attention)
.

Let
Q,K,V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and set
A:=K^{\top}Q/\sqrt{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For a sequence
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
, the single-head self-attention map
f:\,(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{n}
is

f(X)_{i}\;=\;V\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}(X)\,x_{j},\qquad P_{ij}(X)\;=\;\mathrm{softmax}(x_{i}^{\top}A^{\top}x_{j}\big).

Definition 2.2

(Multi-head self-attention)
.

Let
H_{\text{heads}}
divide
d
and set
k=d/H_{\text{heads}}
. For each head
h\in\{1,\ldots,H_{\text{heads}}\}
, let
Q^{(h)},K^{(h)},V^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and an output projection
W^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. Denoting by
f^{(h)}
the single-head map associated with
\big(Q^{(h)},K^{(h)},V^{(h)}\big)
, the multi-head operator is

f_{\mathrm{MH}}(X)\;=\;\sum_{h=1}^{H_{\text{heads}}}W^{(h)}\,f^{(h)}(X)\;\in\;(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}.

Permutation equivariance and measure modeling.

Self-attention without positional information is equivariant to reindexing: for any permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
,

f\big(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}\big)\;=\;\big(f(X)\big)_{\sigma(1:n)}.

Hence, one may represent the sequence via its empirical distribution

\hat{\mu}_{n}\;:=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},

and analyze attention at the level of probability measures. This naturally leads to formalizing the infinite-token limit by viewing attention as an operator on such measures.

Definition 2.3

(Continuous (mean-field) self-attention)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of Borel probability measures. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\;:=\;V\,\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}\,y\;d\mu(y)}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}\;d\mu(y)}\;\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\qquad F(\mu)\;:=\;(\Gamma_{\mu})_{\#}\mu\;\in\;\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k}).

When
\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}}
,
F(\hat{\mu}_{n})
coincides with the empirical law of the discrete self-attention outputs.

2.2
Centering and exponential tilting

Reduction to the centered case.

All our results are stated for centered sub-Gaussian distributions, but they extend straightforwardly to non-centered sub-Gaussian distributions, up to adding some constants (related to the mean of the distribution) to the bounds. To keep the presentation as simple as possible, we focus on the centered case in the paper.

Attention as exponential tilting (importance sampling)

Let
X\sim\mu
be a token and assume the denominators below are finite. Then

\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\;=\;V\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,X\,e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}\right]}\;=\;V\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{Ax}}[X],\qquad d\mathbb{Q}_{Ax}=\frac{e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}]}\,d\mathbb{P}\,.

where we use the
Esscher transform
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined by the tilted measure

d\mathbb{Q}_{t}:=\frac{e^{\langle t,X\rangle}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle t,X\rangle}]}\,d\mathbb{P}.

Thus, the empirical (finite-
n
) attention is the Monte Carlo importance-sampling estimator of this tilted expectation.

2.3
Sub-Gaussian background

Sub-Gaussian models provide a convenient level of generality for our purpose: they let us move beyond the compact support setting while strictly containing it as a particular case (see Hoeffding’s lemma in
(
Hoeffding, 1963
)
). Sub-Gaussian distributions are extensively used in statistics due to their exponential-type tails and sharp concentration properties for linear and Lipschitz functionals
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
;
Vershynin, 2018
;
Wainwright, 2019
, Ch. 2)
.
They naturally model bounded random variables, sensor noise, and high-dimensional data with controlled tail behavior. They also cover mixtures of Gaussian embeddings that have been recently studied in the context of Transformers.

We adopt the standard log-moment generating function (log-MGF) characterization of sub-Gaussian vectors to formalize our theoretical framework.

Definition 2.4

(Sub-Gaussian vector)
.

A centered random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix

\Sigma\succ 0
if

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\{\langle t,X\rangle\}\right]\;\leq\;\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,t^{\top}\Sigma\,t\right).

(1)

This matrix formulation of sub-Gaussianity captures potential anisotropy in the distribution—when
\Sigma
is ill-conditioned, different directions exhibit varying tail behaviors. While an equivalent scalar formulation
\Sigma:=\sigma^{2}I
exists for isotropic cases, the matrix parameterization provides greater generality. Remarkably, we can link the sub-Gaussianity matrix parameter through the classical inequality
Cov(X)\preceq\Sigma
, with a tight bound for Gaussian variables.

Furthermore, the sub-Gaussian condition (
1
) implies several useful facts: exponential concentration for linear and Lipschitz functionals, closure under linear transformations
X\mapsto BX
, and stability under convex combinations and exponential tilting. This last property is particularly relevant for transformer architectures, where attention mechanisms reweight token representations through exponential softmax operations based on key-query similarities. Adopting sub-Gaussian tokens is therefore both technically practical and theoretically justified, allowing us to leverage the rich toolbox of high-dimensional concentration theory.

3
Convergence of the attention map

We analyze the convergence of empirical attention when tokens follow a sub-Gaussian distribution in the sense of Definition
2.4
. This allows us to quantify how unbounded yet light-tailed token distributions affect this pointwise convergence.

In this section, we examine how fast
\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}
converges to zero. We first establish that
\bigl\|\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}
grows at most linearly in Euclidean norm. Then we provide both a pointwise high-probability convergence for any token
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a uniform convergence rate over all tokens in a ball of radius
R
. In both cases we highlight the dependence of the estimated rate on the attention parameters and the token distribution properties.

3.1
At most linear growth of the attention map

We start with a preliminary result that quantifies the growth of the attention map norm with the query norm. Lemma
3.1
establishes that the attention map
\Gamma_{\mu}(x)

cannot grow faster than linearly in the query norm
\|x\|_{2}
. This linear growth property is crucial for controlling the behavior of attention mechanisms and will be instrumental in establishing convergence rates.

Let us denote by
\|\Sigma\|_{2}:=\lambda_{\max}\!\bigl(\Sigma\bigr)
the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes both the matrix norm and the Euclidean norm (clear from the context).

Lemma 3.1

(At-most linear growth of
\Gamma
)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
. For any matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
and any
x,t\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define

\Gamma_{\mu}(x):=V\,\nabla K(Ax),\qquad K(t):=\ln\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle t,X\rangle}].

Then

\bigl\|\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;2\,\|V\|_{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{2}\,\bigl\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\bigr\|_{2}\,\|x\|_{2}\;.

Proof.

Following the proof in
(
Bobkov and Götze, 2025
)
, we define the auxiliary function

P(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle t,\Sigma t\rangle-K(t)
which satisfies
P(t)\geq 0
by definition of the sub-gaussiannity. By Jensen’s inequality, we have
K(t)\geq 0
which implies
P(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle t,\Sigma t\rangle
. Moreover, by convexity of
K(t)
, we have
P^{\prime\prime}\leq\Sigma
. By Taylor expansion, this implies

0\leq P(h+t)=P(t)+\langle\nabla P(t),h\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\Sigma h\rangle\,.

Minimizing over
h
the r.h.s. leads to

\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla P(t),\Sigma^{-1}\nabla P(t)\rangle\leq P(t)\,.

Therefore, we get by the triangle inequality

\|\nabla K(t)\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}=\|\Sigma t-\nabla P\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}\leq\|t\|+\sqrt{2P(t)}\leq 2\|t\|_{\Sigma}\,,

where we used the Mahalanobis norm
\|\cdot\|_{S}
defined for a positive definite matrix S by
\|x\|_{S}=x^{\top}Sx=\|S^{1/2}x\|_{2}^{2}
.

Converting this estimate for the standard Euclidean norm leads to

\displaystyle\|\nabla K(Ax)\|_{2}=\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}\nabla K(Ax)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\right\|_{\rm op}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\nabla K(Ax)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}Ax\|_{2}.

Coming back to the attention map, we have
\Gamma_{\mu}(x)=V\,\nabla K(Ax)
, which yields the claimed linear growth.
∎

Gaussian distributions saturate this sub-Gaussian bound:
K(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma t
exactly, so
\nabla K
is linear and Lemma
3.1
is tight up to constants. As a result, the class of Gaussian distributions is preserved layer-wise
(
Castin et al., 2025
)
, which allows to follow the evolution of tokens by tracking only means and covariances. This makes Gaussians a calibrating case for both growth bounds and deviation inequalities (see for example
(
Wainwright, 2019
, Sec. 2.1)
, and
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Sec. 2.5)
).

Lemma 3.2

(Exact linearity under Gaussian inputs (Castin and al. 2025))
.

If
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, then for all
x
, the log-MGF is quadratic and the population attention map is
exactly linear
:

\Gamma_{\mu}(x)=V\,\nabla K(Ax)=V\,\Sigma Ax.\qquad K(t):=\ln\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle t,X\rangle}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\,t^{\top}\Sigma t.

Hence the Gaussian family is preserved by attention, and first/second moments determine the dynamics.

Note that, when
\mu
has a compact support, the transformed distribution of the tokens stays bounded.

3.2
Pointwise convergence of the attention map

Here we prove that the pointwise concentration of the attention map exhibits the classical
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
asymptotic rate for empirical processes, combined with an exponential dependence on
\|\Sigma^{1/2}Ax\|_{2}^{2}
that captures how attention mechanisms amplify signals along high-variance directions. This pointwise analysis provides relevant insights into how individual attention outputs stabilize with increasing context length, with potential applications in attention sketching and approximation methods.

Proposition 3.3

(Pointwise convergence of the attention map for sub-Gaussian tokens)
.

Let
X\sim\mu
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
. For any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the empirical attention computed from
n
i.i.d. tokens satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;q_{\Sigma,V,d,\delta}\cdot\frac{e^{\frac{5}{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}Ax\|_{2}^{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

(2)

where
q_{\Sigma,V,d,\delta}:=(2+\sqrt{3})\|V\|_{2}\,\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}\cdot\;q_{d,1-\delta}^{1/2}.

Here
q_{d,1-\delta}
denotes the
(1-\delta)
-quantile of the
\chi^{2}(d)
distribution, which satisfies in particular
q_{d,1-\delta}\leq d+2\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta))}+2\ln(1/\delta).

The proof of Proposition
3.3
can be found in Appendix
B.5
. It combines classical tools of statistics with properties of sub-Gaussian distributions.

In the particular case of Gaussians, a sharper estimation can be found in Appendix
C.1
.

3.3
Uniform convergence of the attention map

In Proposition
3.3
, the pointwise bound controls the error at a single, fixed query
x
. While this result achieves precise control in
\delta
, and a sharp exponential rate, the event of probability
\delta
for which
\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}
is not controlled depends a priori on the query
x
, and thus Proposition
3.3
does not ensure global convergence of the attention map. Indeed, when attention processes many tokens across the space of query, we need simultaneous control for all
x
in this space: a single bad query can ruin global behavior of the attention operator across the distribution.

Establishing an explicit quantitative estimation of this uniform convergence (Theorem
3.4
) is substantially more challenging: while classical empirical process theory ensures uniform convergence over
\mathcal{F}=\{y\mapsto ye^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}\}
, extracting quantitative bounds from these results cannot be done off-the-shelf. Our main contribution is therefore to establish such explicit estimations of the rates through a refined analysis.

Theorem 3.4

(Uniform convergence of the attention map for sub-Gaussian tokens)
.

Let
X\sim\mu
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
. For any
R>0
,
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for
n\geq n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R):=4e^{2R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\Bigr)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;q_{(\Sigma,A,V,R,d,\delta)}\cdot\frac{e^{8\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}}{\sqrt{n}},

(3)

where
q_{(\Sigma,A,V,R,d,\delta)}:=\frac{C\,\sqrt{d}}{\delta}\|V\|_{2}\cdot\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}\cdot(R\|A\|_{2}\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}d+\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)^{1/2}\|A\|_{2}+5^{d/2}\sqrt{d}).

Note that, despite the substantially more delicate analysis, the exponential rate in
R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}
remains the same as in Proposition
3.3
up to a constant factor, though the pre-exponential factor is now less precise in
\delta
and carries additional dependence on
R
,
A
and
\Sigma
. The proof of this result can be found in Appendix
B.2
.

For Gaussians, a better estimate on the rate can be derived.

Proposition 3.5

(Uniform convergence of the attention map for Gaussian tokens)
.

Let
X\sim\mu
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
. For any
R>0
,
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for
n\geq n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R):=4e^{2R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\Bigr)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;q_{(\Sigma,A,V,R,d,\delta)}^{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\frac{e^{R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}}{\sqrt{n}},

(4)

where
q_{(\Sigma,A,V,R,d,\delta)}^{\mathcal{N}}
is defined in Appendix
C.3
.

In order to extract quantitative bounds from classical results of empirical process theory, our proof of Theorem
3.4
proceeds in three steps: (i) symmetrize and bound the supremum of the class, reduce it along dual directions, and verify sub-Gaussian increments on a product space
G=B_{1}\times\mathcal{F}
; (ii) control both a Lipschitz envelope and an unbounded class envelope
F(y)=\|y\|_{2}e^{R\|A^{\top}y\|_{2}}
via high-order moments, chaining methods (Dudley’s entropy integral) and covering numbers bounds; and (iii) handle the ratio structure of attention without a uniform delta method, since the empirical denominator can get arbitrarily close to zero, even if with small probability. The full proof of Theorem
3.4
can be found in Appendix
B.4
(see also Appendix
C.3
for specific derivations to the Gaussian case). A similar bound for the covariance matrix can be derived as a corollary to Theorem
3.4
(see Appendix
B.6
for proof).

Finally, for compactly supported distributions of tokens, we show the following bound in Proposition
3.6
(see Appendix
B.7
for proof).

Proposition 3.6

(Uniform convergence of the attention map for compactly supported tokens)
.

Let
X\sim\mu
be centered, with
\operatorname{Supp}(\mu)\subset B_{R_{0}}
. For any
R>0
,
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for
n\geq n_{min}(\delta,A,R,R_{0}):=4e^{2RR_{0}\|A\|_{2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\Bigr)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\bigl\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{q_{V,A,R_{0},d,\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{2RR_{0}\|A\|_{2}},

(5)

where
q_{V,A,R_{0},d,\delta}:=\|V\|_{2}\cdot\frac{c\,\sqrt{d}\,R_{0}(2\,R_{0}^{2}\|A\|_{2}+R_{0}\,\|A\|_{2}+2)}{\delta}.

4
Convergence of the transformed distribution

So far, we have analyzed the convergence of a single attention vector
\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}
computed for queries in a fixed compact set. However, it is also important to consider the convergence of the output distribution itself. The convergence of the output distribution is particularly relevant for downstream tasks that rely on aggregate token statistics, such as classification or sequence-level predictions. Moreover, in deep transformer architectures, attention layers are stacked: the inputs to subsequent layers depend on the entire reweighted distribution produced by earlier attention mechanisms. Understanding distributional convergence is therefore crucial for analyzing error propagation through the network depth.

To characterize the convergence of the transformed distribution, we estimate the rate at which empirical moments
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[f(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))]
converge as
n\rightarrow\infty
for any Lipschitz function
f
. We extend this analysis to quadratic functions, enabling us to capture both the mean and covariance of the transformed distribution. These first two moments are natural quantities to study, particularly because they fully characterize Gaussian distributions.

4.1
Compactly supported token distributions

We begin by analyzing the case where the token distributions is bounded, a particular instance of the sub-Gaussian framework. In appearance, this case exhibits more favorable convergence behavior.

From Proposition
3.6
, we deduce the following estimation in the compact support setting.

Proposition 4.1

(Lipschitz functional convergence rate for compactly supported tokens)
.

Let
X\sim\mu
centered with
\operatorname{Supp}(\mu)\subset B_{R_{0}}
, and let
f:\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)
be a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
L_{0}
. Recall the definition of the empirical distribution
\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
. Let us denote by
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}
the expectation w.r.t.
\mu
, and
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}
the expectation w.r.t. the empirical measure. For
n
i.i.d. tokens, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[f(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[f(\Gamma_{\mu}(X))]\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot L_{0}\cdot\left(q_{V,A,R_{0},d,\delta}\,e^{2\cdot R_{0}^{2}\|A\|_{2}}+q^{\prime}_{V,R_{0},d,\delta}\right)

where
q_{V,A,R_{0},d,\delta}
is defined in Proposition
3.6
, and
q^{\prime}_{V,R_{0},d,\delta}:=\|V\|_{2}\,q_{d,1-\delta}^{1/2}R_{0}^{2}
.

Our motivation for examining this specific case is based on two key observations. First, tokens pass through a normalization layer before the attention mechanism,
making them effectively compact-supported, and thus we expect the parametric rate predicted by theory to manifest in practice.
Second, this assumption is standard in the theoretical study of transformers
(
Geshkovski et al., 2025
)
, providing additional motivation to investigate it within this framework.

Surprisingly, this simplified framework does not align well with practical observations, particularly for models like BERT. Standard bounded-support analyses assuming tokens lie in a ball of radius
R
yield parametric rates
C(R,\Sigma,A)/\sqrt{n}
, where
C(R,\Sigma,A)
grows exponentially in the product of
R
and attention parameters (such as
\|\Sigma A\|_{2}
). However, for such bound to be informative we need to compare
R
with the relevant scales of the problem: the number of tokens
n
, the variance of the distribution, and the norm of attention matrices. Critically, when the product
R\cdot\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}
becomes large relative to
\sqrt{\ln n}
, the exponential growth of
C(R,\Sigma,A)
overwhelms the
1/\sqrt{n}
decay, and we lose our upper convergence bound. This motivates extending our analyses to the sub-Gaussian setting to get better constants that captures real convergence behavior.

4.2
Sub-Gaussian tokens

We analyze the convergence of empirical attention when tokens follow a sub-Gaussian distribution in the sense of Definition
2.4
. This allows us to quantify how unbounded yet light-tailed token distributions affect both pointwise attention outputs and their low-order moments.
We obtain a convergence rate estimate of
O(n^{-\beta})
with
\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}
, where
\beta
depends on the attention parameters and the token distribution.

Definition 4.2

(Spectral reach and attention–reach coupling)
.

Let
X
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
. Recall
A=K^{\top}Q
. We define:

1.

The Spectral Reach

\|\Sigma\|_{2}\;:=\;\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma).

2.

The Attention Horizon

H\;:=\;\bigl\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\bigr\|_{2}.

3.

The Attention–Reach Coupling (ARC)

\Psi\;:=\;\|\Sigma\|_{2}\,H^{2}.

Remark 4.3

(Intuition for spectral reach, attention horizon, and ARC)
.

The spectral reach
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
, the largest eigenvalue of the covariance, measures the maximum scale of variance in the token distribution. The attention horizon
H
quantifies the effective range of attention: intuitively, how distant tokens can still influence a given query, as determined by token geometry (
\Sigma
) and attention weights (
A
). The product
\Psi=\|\Sigma\|_{2}H^{2}
, the
Attention–Reach Coupling (ARC)
, combines these two factors: larger values mean attention operates over tokens with greater variance spread over wider distances.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)
the Hilbert space of measurable functions

g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|g(x)\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty.

Theorem 4.4

(Lipschitz functional convergence rate for sub-Gaussian tokens)
.

Let
X
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
and define
\Psi
as above. Let
f:\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mu)
be a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
L_{0}
. Let us denote by
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}
the expectation w.r.t.
\mu
, and
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}
the expectation w.r.t. the empirical measure. For
n
i.i.d. tokens, with
n\geq\max(n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R),30)
, where
n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R)
defined in Proposition
3.5
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[f(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[f(\Gamma_{\mu}(X))]\bigr\|\;\leq\;P(\sqrt{\ln n},L_{0})\cdot O\,\!\bigl(n^{-\frac{1}{2(1+32\cdot\Psi)}}\bigr),

where
P
is a polynomial function.

We highlight the particular case of the mean of the distribution, which is a corollary of the previous result (with
f={\rm id}
).

Corollary 4.5

(Mean convergence rate for sub-Gaussian tokens)
.

Let
X
be centered and sub-Gaussian with parameter matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
and define
\Psi
as above. Let us denote by
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}
the expectation w.r.t.
\mu
and
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}
the expectation w.r.t. the empirical measure. For
n
i.i.d. tokens, with
n\geq\max(n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R),30)
, with
n_{min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R)
defined in Proposition
3.5
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)]\bigr\|\;=\;P(\sqrt{\ln n})\cdot O\,\!\bigl(n^{-\frac{1}{2(1+32\cdot\Psi)}}\bigr).

where
P
is a polynomial function.

The same result holds for Gaussian distribution, with a sharper rate.

Proposition 4.6

(Mean convergence rate for Gaussian tokens)
.

For Gaussian tokens using the same notation we have,

\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)]\bigr\|=O\!\left((\ln n)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\;n^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\Psi)}}\right).

Remark 4.7
.

Corollary
4.5
establishes a fundamental trade-off between the complexity of the linear transformation (captured by
H^{2}=\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|^{2}
) and the sub-Gaussian parameter (captured by
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
) in determining the convergence rate. When
H^{2}
is large relative to
\frac{1}{\|\Sigma\|_{2}}
, we have
\Psi\gg 1
and the convergence rate can be
n^{-\varepsilon}
where
\varepsilon
approaches
0
, which is slower than the standard parametric rate. Conversely, when
H^{2}
is small relative to
\frac{1}{\|\Sigma\|_{2}}
,
\Psi\ll 1
and we recover the standard
n^{-1/2}
rate.

A similar convergence rate holds for the covariance matrix.
Let

\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}(X)):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x_{i})-\bar{\Gamma}_{n}\big)(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x_{i})-\bar{\Gamma}_{n}\big)^{\top},\quad\bar{\Gamma}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x_{i}),

and

\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\mu}(\Gamma_{\mu}(X)):=\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)\Gamma_{\mu}(X)^{\top}\big]-\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)]\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)^{\top}].

Our main result regarding the convergence rate of the covariance is the following.

Proposition 4.8

(Covariance convergence rate for sub-Gaussian tokens)
.

With the notation
\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))
and
\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\mu}(\Gamma_{\mu}(X))
defined above, for
n
i.i.d. tokens with
n\geq\max(n_{\min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R),30)
, where
n_{\min}(\delta,\Sigma,A,R)
is defined in Proposition
3.5
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big\|\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))-\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\mu}(\Gamma_{\mu}(X))\big\|_{2}\ \leq\ P(\sqrt{\ln n})\,O\,\!\bigl(n^{-\frac{1}{2(1+16\cdot\Psi)}}\bigr).

(6)

The proof follows broadly the same steps as the one of Theorem
4.4
. It can be found in Appendix
B.6
.

5
Hardmax regime

To complete our analysis, we study the case of an infinite horizon regime (the ‘hardmax’ attention).
This limit case is practically relevant because, in real Transformer architectures, matrix norms tend to be significant, creating conditions similar to this limiting regime.

In this section, we take a simplified setting of Gaussian distribution in dimension
d=1
. The key insight is that in the wide horizon regime (where
\|A\cdot\sigma\|_{2}\gg 1
), the softmax attention mechanism behaves essentially like a hard-max selector (
A=\infty
) , concentrating attention weights on extreme token values. As a consequence, the empirical attention output reduces to a combination of the sample
maximum
and
minimum
. This phenomenon leads to remarkable cancellation effect and the convergence rate is governed by classical Gaussian extreme value theory.

In the whole Section
5
, we assume
d=1
, i.e., we work in the one-dimensional setting.

5.1
Convergence rate of hardmax attention

We consider a set of tokens
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Let
M_{n}^{+}:=\max_{j\leq n}X_{j}
and
M_{n}^{-}:=\min_{j\leq n}X_{j}
be the maximum and minimum of the token set
(X_{i})_{i\in[1,n]}
.

The hardmax-attention is defined by,

\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}(X_{i}):=\begin{cases}M_{n}^{+}&\text{if }X_{i}>0,\\[2.0pt]
M_{n}^{-}&\text{if }X_{i}<0.\end{cases}

This corresponds to a limit behavior where
A=+\infty
, and thus the attention map
\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}(X_{i})
selects only the token with same sign as
X_{i}
and largest modulus.

The empirical mean is

\bar{\Gamma}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\Gamma}_{i}=\frac{I_{+}}{n}M_{n}^{+}+\frac{I_{-}}{n}M_{n}^{-}

where
I_{+}=\#\{i:X_{i}>0\},
and
I_{-}=n-I_{+}
denote the number of positive and negative tokens
X_{i}
. Note that
I_{+}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

Our main result in the hardmax regime derives the bias and standard deviation of the absolute value of the empirical mean, both of which are of order
1/\sqrt{\ln(n)}
.

Proposition 5.1

(Hardmax convergence rate)
.

As
n\to\infty
, taking the expectation w.r.t.
X_{1},...,X_{n}:

\mathbb{E}[|\bar{\Gamma}_{n}|]=\frac{\ln(4)\,\sigma}{2\,\sqrt{2\ln n}}\,[1+o(1)]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[\bar{\Gamma}_{n}^{2}]=\frac{\pi^{2}\,\sigma^{2}}{24\,\ln n}\,[1+o(1)]

Therefore, the expectation decreases exactly at the rate
\sigma/\sqrt{\ln n}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Notice that the symmetry of
X
yields
\mathbb{E}[\bar{\Gamma}_{n}]=0
.

The next subsection is devoted to the proof of this result.

5.2
Proof of Proposition
5.1

We decompose the empirical mean into two components:

\bar{\Gamma}_{n}=\frac{I_{+}}{n}\,M_{n}^{+}+\frac{I_{-}}{n}\,M_{n}^{-}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(M_{n}^{+}+M_{n}^{-}\bigr)\;+\Bigl(\tfrac{2I_{+}-n}{2n}\Bigr)\bigl(M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}\bigr).

Setting

V_{n}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(M_{n}^{+}+M_{n}^{-}),\quad U_{n}:=\Bigl(\tfrac{2I_{+}-n}{2n}\Bigr)\bigl(M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}\bigr),

(7)

we get
\bar{\Gamma}_{n}=V_{n}+U_{n}
.

The key insight is that
V_{n}
captures the main asymptotic behavior while
U_{n}
represents a smaller-order deviation term.

Before proving Proposition
5.1
, we establish several technical results on extreme values of Gaussian random variables.

5.2.1
Extreme value analysis

In this subsection, we derive precise asymptotic rates for
V_{n}
and establish bounds on
U_{n}
. Extreme value theory provides weak convergence of the maximum and minimum (Lemmas
B.17
and
B.18
in the Appendix). To obtain convergence in expectation, we establish uniform integrability of both extremes in Lemma
5.3
.

Let
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
be the scaling parameters of extreme values defined by

a_{n}:=\sigma(\sqrt{2\ln n}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\ln n}\,(\log\log n+\log 4\pi))\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{n}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\ln n}}.

Lemma 5.3

(Uniform Integrability of Extremes)
.

Let
Z_{n}=\frac{M_{n}^{+}-a_{n}}{b_{n}}
and
Y_{n}=-\frac{M_{n}^{-}+a_{n}}{b_{n}}
. Then
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are uniformly integrable.

The definition of uniform integrability is recalled in Appendix
B.8
.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

We establish the result for
Z_{n}
in several steps. The same argument applies to
Y_{n}
by symmetry.

Step 1
:
From equation (1.5) in
(
Tanguy, 2015
)
, we have the concentration inequality:

\mathbb{P}(|M_{n}^{+}-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{+}]|>t)\leq 6e^{-ct\sqrt{\ln n}}

(8)

Step 2:
We show that
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n}|]<\infty
.

Let
M_{n}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
M_{n}^{+}
and set
Z_{n}^{\prime}=\frac{M_{n}^{\prime}-a_{n}}{b_{n}}
. From Step 1:

\sup_{n}\frac{1}{b_{n}}\mathbb{E}[|M_{n}^{+}-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{+}]|]<\infty\,.

(9)

Using the convexity of
|\cdot|
and Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{n}^{\prime}}[|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{\prime}|]\geq|Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}^{\prime}]|\,.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n}|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{\prime}|]+|\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}^{\prime}]|

From (
9
), we can deduce that
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{\prime}|]<\infty
.
Indeed

|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{b_{n}}|M_{n}-M_{n}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{b_{n}}|M_{n}-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}]|+\frac{1}{b_{n}}|M_{n}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{\prime}]|.

Since, on the other hand
\sup_{n}|\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]|<\infty
, this gives
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n}|]<\infty
.

Step 3:
We transfer the concentration around
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{+}]
to a concentration around
a_{n}
.

Define
\delta_{n}=\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{+}]-a_{n}
. Then
|\delta_{n}|=O(b_{n})
, so
|\delta_{n}|\to 0
. From the event inclusion

\{|M_{n}^{+}-a_{n}|>t\}\subset\{|M_{n}^{+}-\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{+}]|>t-|\delta_{n}|\}

and (
8
), we deduce

\mathbb{P}(|M_{n}^{+}-a_{n}|>t)\leq 6e^{-c(t-|\delta_{n}|)\sqrt{\ln n}}\,.

For
n
large enough, it holds for all
t\geq 1
that
t-|\delta_{n}|\geq t/2
, giving

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{n}|>t)\leq 6e^{-c^{\prime}t}\,.

Step 4:
Using the tail integral formula,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[|Z_{n}|\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{n}|>A\}}\big]=\int_{A}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{n}|>x)\,dx\leq\int_{A}^{+\infty}6e^{-c^{\prime}x}dx=\frac{6}{c^{\prime}}e^{-c^{\prime}A}\to 0

as
A\to\infty
, uniformly in
n
.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Asymptotic behavior of
V_{n}
)
.

There holds

\mathbb{E}|V_{n}|=\frac{\ln(4)\,\sigma}{2\,\sqrt{2\ln n}}\,[1+o(1)]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{2}]=\frac{\sigma^{2}\pi^{2}}{24\,\ln n}\,[1+o(1)]\,.

Proof.

The random variables
Z_{n}=\frac{M_{n}^{+}-a_{n}}{b_{n}}
and
Y_{n}=-\frac{M_{n}^{-}+a_{n}}{b_{n}}
jointly converge weakly to the joint distribution of two independent Gumbel random variables, which we denote
G_{+}
and
G_{-}
(see Lemma
B.17
in the Appendix).

By Lemma
5.3
, both
|Z_{n}|
and
|Y_{n}|
are asymptotically uniformly integrable. Since
|Z_{n}-Y_{n}|\leq|Z_{n}|+|Y_{n}|
, it follows that
|Z_{n}-Y_{n}|
is also asymptotically uniformly integrable. Combining the weak convergence of the sum (Lemma
B.18
) with the dominated convergence theorem (
(
van der Vaart, 1998
, Theorem 2.20, Section 2.5)
), we conclude that
|Z_{n}-Y_{n}|
converges in expectation.

Recall that
V_{n}=\frac{M_{n}^{+}+M_{n}^{-}}{2}
, which can be rewritten as
V_{n}=\frac{\sigma(Z_{n}-Y_{n})}{2\sqrt{2\ln n}}
. We can therefore deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln n}|V_{n}|}{\sigma}\right]\to\mathbb{E}\left[|G_{+}-G_{-}|\right].

(10)

Since
L:=G_{+}-G_{-}
follows a centered logistic law, we have

\mathbb{E}[|L|]=\ln 4,

which gives:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln n}}{\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}|V_{n}|=\ln 4\,.

The variance result follows similarly using
\text{Var}(L)=\pi^{2}/3
.
∎

Lemma 5.5

(Upper Bound on
U_{n}
)
.

Let
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
defined in (
7
), it holds:

\mathbb{E}[|U_{n}|]\leq\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[U_{n}^{2}]=O\Bigl(\frac{\ln n}{n}\Bigr)\,.

Proof.

Using
\text{Var}(2I_{+}-n)=n
and
\mathbb{E}[|M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}|^{2}]=O(a_{n}^{2})
, we have:

\mathbb{E}[|U_{n}|]\leq\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{2I_{+}-n}{2n}\right|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}[|M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}|^{2}]\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}}\cdot 2a_{n}=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}\,.

For the second moment, using that
\mathbb{E}[|M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}|^{4}]=O(a_{n}^{4})
, we get

\mathbb{E}[U_{n}^{2}]\leq\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{2I_{+}-n}{2n}\right|^{4}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}[|M_{n}^{+}-M_{n}^{-}|^{4}]\Bigr)^{1/2}=O(\frac{\ln n}{n})

which concludes the proof.
∎

5.2.2
Conclusion

We first establish the convergence of the mean. Using the triangle inequality,

|V_{n}|-|U_{n}|\leq|\bar{\Gamma}_{n}|\leq|V_{n}|+|U_{n}|\,.

Taking expectations and using
\mathbb{E}|U_{n}|=o(\mathbb{E}|V_{n}|)
from Lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
:

(1-o(1))\,\mathbb{E}|V_{n}|\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}|\bar{\Gamma}_{n}|\;\leq\;(1+o(1))\,\mathbb{E}|V_{n}|

which provides the announced equivalent for the mean in Lemma
5.4
.

For the variance, we decompose:

\text{Var}(\bar{\Gamma}_{n})=\text{Var}(V_{n})+\text{Var}(U_{n})+2\,\text{Cov}(U_{n},V_{n})\,.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

|\operatorname{Cov}(U_{n},V_{n})|\;\leqslant\;\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U_{n})\,\operatorname{Var}(V_{n})}.

In addition, from Lemma
5.5
,
\mathbb{E}[U_{n}^{2}]=O(\ln n/n)
, and from Lemma
5.4
,
\text{Var}(V_{n})=\Theta(1/\ln n)
. This gives
\text{Var}(U_{n})=o(\text{Var}(V_{n}))
and
|\operatorname{Cov}(U_{n},V_{n})|=|\operatorname{Cov}(U_{n},V_{n})|\leq\sqrt{O\!\left(\frac{\ln n}{n}\right)\,\Theta\!\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=o\!\left(\frac{1}{\ln n}\right)
from which
\text{Var}(\bar{\Gamma}_{n})\sim\text{Var}(V_{n})
follows. Since
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\Gamma}_{n})=\mathbb{E}(V_{n})=0
, this concludes the proof.

5.3
Discussion

Cancellation phenomenon

In the wide-horizon regime, softmax behaves like a hard maximum that focuses on extremes. For Gaussian inputs, the maximum and minimum are asymptotically
\pm\sqrt{2\ln n}
, so the mechanism selects either one with roughly equal probability as
n
grows, and their contributions cancel, yielding a mean near zero. This symmetry-driven cancellation is specifically Gaussian and implies a slow decay of order
\sigma/\sqrt{\ln n}
, much quicker than any fixed polynomial rate. Observe that real token embeddings (e.g., in BERT) do not display the above saturation. Our interpretation is that their distributions can easily deviate from Gaussian symmetry through skewness, kurtosis, anisotropy, or dependence for example. These departures disrupt the tail balance, removing the cancellation and the characteristic
1/\sqrt{\ln n}
behavior.

6
Experiments

In this section, we present experiments on both synthetic Gaussian data and real-world Wikipedia tokens using BERT embeddings (
1
M and
20
M tokens). We describe our experimental procedures and design choices, and show that the observed convergence rates are consistent with our theoretical analysis.

Monte carlo experimental protocol

For each Monte Carlo experiment with
n
tokens, we begin by sampling
n
points from a
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\alpha,\Sigma)
. We then calculate the attention matrix for these tokens and compute the empirical mean
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X)]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. We measure the error
\left\|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}[\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}[\Gamma_{\mu}(X)]\right\|_{2}
and
\big\|\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\hat{\mu}_{n}}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(X))-\operatorname{Cov}_{X\sim\mu}(\Gamma_{\mu}(X))\big\|_{2}
, and repeat this entire procedure for
R_{\text{rep}}
iterations to obtain average error values and associated uncertainty ranges.

The procedure described above constitutes one Monte Carlo experiment for a fixed number of tokens
n
. We repeat this process for increasing values of
n
to analyze convergence behavior as
n
grows.

Convergence rate analysis

The theoretical convergence rate follows
O(n^{-\beta})
with
\beta=\frac{1}{2(1+\Psi)}
. We estimate this rate empirically by performing linear regression on log-scale error values as a function of
n
across all Monte Carlo experiments, yielding
\hat{\beta}
. Theory predicts that the convergence slope depends on the horizon parameter
H^{2}=\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
. For synthetic experiments, we have access to both
A
, and
\Sigma
and vary both. For fixed token distribution with a given covariance
\Sigma
, we can only test the influence of the attention horizon through the attention parameter
A
(rescaling a fixed matrix).

Modelisation of a limit distribution

For synthetic datasets, the limit distribution
\mu
is continuous. For real token distributions,
\mu
is discrete, requiring truncation at
N_{\max}
tokens to construct
\hat{\mu}_{\text{lim}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\max}}\delta_{x_{i}}
as an approximation for the infinite-token limit. We then choose
n_{\max}
as the maximum number of tokens sampled uniformly among those
N_{\max}
tokens in Monte Carlo experiments to estimate mean and covariance. To ensure
\hat{\mu}_{\text{lim}}
approximates a continuous distribution, we require
N_{\max}\gg n_{\max}
, while
n_{\max}
must be large enough for Monte Carlo convergence to be reached.

Number of tokens required for convergence

To evaluate the number of tokens
n_{max}
needed to achieve convergence, we use dimension 1 as a baseline, where Monte Carlo typically converges with
20-30
samples. For
n
samples in dimension
d
, Monte Carlo convergence rates are governed by CLT fluctuations that scale as
\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{n}}
. To maintain the same accuracy level as in dimension 1, dimension
d
therefore requires
d
times more samples than dimension 1. Under this scaling, the experiment in Figure
5
is equivalent to taking
\sim 300
tokens in dimension 1. In our first BERT experiments we choose
N_{\max}=1\text{M}\gg n_{\max}=10\text{k}
and in our second experiments
N_{\max}=20\text{M}\gg n_{\max}=200\text{k}
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced the notion of
token sample complexity
to quantify how attention outputs converge as sequence length
n
increases. We established in Theorem
3.4
that attention maps achieve uniform convergence on bounded query regions at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
for sub-Gaussian tokens. For statistical moments of the attention output distribution, Proposition
4.1
yields parametric rates in
1/\sqrt{n}
under compact support assumptions, but with prefactors scaling exponentially in radius
R
and attention parameters, rendering these bounds impractical at realistic data scales. Extending to sub-Gaussian token distributions, Theorem
4.4
reveals polynomial (rather than exponential) dependence on attention parameters and a sub-parametric rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\beta})
with
\beta<1/2
, better aligned with practical behavior. We also derived exact logarithmic convergence rates of order
1/\sqrt{\ln n}
for the infinite attention horizon limit. Experiments on BERT using Wikipedia text confirmed our theoretical results, with empirical rates strictly below
1/\sqrt{n}
, establishing that sub-parametric convergence characterizes attention on real data.

Appendix A
Tools of empirical process theory

Before diving into the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we recall a few results and definitions from empirical process theory that will be useful to the proof.

First, recall the definition of the Rademacher complexity.

Definition A.1

(Rademacher complexity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\{f:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a class of (measurable) functions and

S=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\sim\mu^{n}
an i.i.d. sample.
Let
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables, independent of
S
, with

\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{i}=-1)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. The
empirical
Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
S
is:

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\!\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,f(Y_{i})\right\|_{2}\right].

The (expected) Rademacher complexity at sample size
n
is

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mu^{n}}\big[\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{F})\big].

If
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
is parameterized by
\Theta
, replace

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
with
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}
.
For
d=1
, this reduces to the usual scalar definition.

Intuitively, the Rademacher complexity quantifies the ability of the function class
\mathcal{F}
to fit random noise on the specific sample
S\sim\mu
.
Notice that the expression resembles the empirical expectation we aim to bound. The symmetrization step consists in artificially introducing Rademacher samples
\varepsilon_{i}
to relate our supremum over
\mathcal{F}
to a measure of the richness of the class, that relies on measuring the alignment between function outputs
f(Y_{i})
and random noise.

Next, we recall some useful definitions about sub-Gaussian processes and the Dudley’s entropy integral, central in the proof Theorem
3.4
.

Definition A.2

(Sub-Gaussian
\psi_{2}
norm)
.

For a real-valued random variable
Z
, its
\psi_{2}
(sub-Gaussian) norm is

\|Z\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\Bigl\{\sigma>0:\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{Z^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2\Bigr\}.

For a random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\langle X,v\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}},

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|v\|_{2}=1\}
. We say
X
is sub-Gaussian if
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
.

Definition A.3

(Sub-gaussian increments)
.

Consider a random process
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
on a metric space
(T,d)
. We say that the process has
sub-Gaussian increments
if there exists
K\geq 0
such that

\|X_{t}-X_{s}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq Kd(t,s)\quad\text{for all }t,s\in T,

(11)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
denotes the sub-Gaussian norm.

Definition A.4

(
\varepsilon
-net and covering numbers)
.

Let
(T,d)
be a metric space. Consider a set
T^{\prime}\subset T
and a number
\varepsilon>0
. A subset
\mathcal{N}\subset T^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-net of
T
if balls of radius
\varepsilon
centered at points in
\mathcal{N}
cover
T^{\prime}
.

The smallest cardinality of an
\varepsilon
-net of
T^{\prime}
is called the covering number of
K
and is denoted
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,T^{\prime},d)
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,T^{\prime},d)
is the smallest number of closed balls with centers in
T^{\prime}
and radius
\varepsilon
whose union covers
T^{\prime}
.

Theorem A.5

(Dudley’s entropy integral)
.

(see
[
Vershynin, 2018
, Theorem 8.1.3, p. 221, 2nd ed.]
)
Let
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered random process on a metric space
(T,d)
with sub-Gaussian increments as in (
11
), with
K\geq 0
its sub-Gaussian parameter. Then, there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that:

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]\leq CK\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(T)}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,T,d)}\,d\varepsilon,

(12)

where
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,T,d)
denotes the covering number of
T
at scale
\varepsilon
introduced in Definition
A.4
.

Definition A.6

(Data-dependent distance)
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be a sample of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\mathbb{P}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
denote the empirical measure.
For any measurable
g
, define the empirical
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
norm under the samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
by

\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\;:=\;\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g(Y_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

For the indexed class
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{x}:\,x\in B_{R}\}
, the data-dependent distance between
f_{x}
and
f_{x^{\prime}}
is

\|f_{x}-f_{x^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\;=\;\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f_{x}(Y_{i})-f_{x^{\prime}}(Y_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Lemma A.7

(Empirical Lipschitz control)
.

Assume there exists a measurable envelope
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
such that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B_{R}
and all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|f_{x}(y)-f_{x^{\prime}}(y)\|_{2}\;\leq\;L(y)\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Then, for any samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
,

\|f_{x}-f_{x^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\;\leq\;\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Proof.

By the pointwise Lipschitz condition and the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}
,

\|f_{x}-f_{x^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|f_{x}(Y_{i})-f_{x^{\prime}}(Y_{i})\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Y_{i})^{2}\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}=\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

The claim follows by taking square roots.
∎

Lemma A.8

(Bound on empirical Lipschitz envelope)
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. with law
\mu
. Assume there exists a function
L
as defined in Lemma
A.7
such that
\mathbb{E}[L(Y_{1})^{2}]<\infty
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\big]\;\leq\;\big(\mathbb{E}[\|L(Y_{1})\|_{2}^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\;=:\;\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{\mu})}.

Proof.

By Cauchy–Schwarz applied to
Z=\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}
:

\mathbb{E}\big[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\big]\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}.

Using the fact that
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Y_{i})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[L(Y_{i})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[L(Y_{1})^{2}].

∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
3.4

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
3.4
is to analyze the numerator and denominator of the attention ratio separately, and then combine the results. We will prove this Theorem in three parts. We first establish a concentration in expectation and subsequently derive a high-probability bound via Markov’s inequality. These steps rely on classical tools from the empirical processes literature.

Part 1: Uniform concentration of the numerator of
\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)

Recall that the continuous attention map is given by

\Gamma_{\mu}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y\,e^{\langle Ax,Y\rangle}]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle Ax,Y\rangle}]}.

Consider the numerator. It is the expectation of
y\mapsto y\,e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}
, for an
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, over the sub-Gaussian random vector
Y
.

Define the function class
\mathcal{F}:=\{f_{x},\,x\in B_{R}\}
, where,
\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f_{x}:y\mapsto y\,\exp^{\langle Ax,\;y\rangle}\in L^{2}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(13)

Our goal is to prove a uniform concentration result over the function class
\mathcal{F}
, indexed by
x
in the ball
B_{R}
.

Let

\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{x}(Y_{i})\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}[f_{x}(Y)]\|_{2},

and define the envelope function of the class
\mathcal{F}
,

F:y\mapsto\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\|f_{x}(y)\|_{2}=\|y\|_{2}\,e^{R\|A^{\top}y\|_{2}}.

(14)

Proposition B.5

(Bound on numerator)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq C\,(R\|A\|_{2}\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|_{2}d}+5^{d/2})\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|_{2}}\,}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot e^{\,8R^{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

We will first bound
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]
, and then apply
Markov’s inequality to obtain a high-probability concentration bound.

The proof follows two main steps. First, we use a symmetrization lemma (Lemma
B.6
below) to relate
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]
to the Rademacher complexity of the function class
\mathcal{F}
. Next, we use Dudley’s entropy integral together with the Lipschitz property of
f_{x}
to bound this Rademacher complexity via covering numbers.

Step 1: Symmetrization of
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]

We first derive a symmetrization result that will allow us to link
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]
to the Rademacher complexity of the class
\mathcal{F}
defined in Section
A
.

Lemma B.6

(Symmetrization of
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]
)
.

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}\right]\leq 2\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}).

Proof.

Let
(X_{1},...,X_{n})
i.i.d. copies of
Y
independent of
Y
.

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Y_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{Y}[f(Y)]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(Y_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}}[f(X_{i})])\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i})\right]\right\|_{2}.

Using Jensen inequality, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i}))\right]\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i}))\right\|_{2}\right].

Let
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
be i.i.d. Rademacher signs, independent of
S=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
. The random vector of components
\varepsilon_{i}(f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i}))
has the same
joint distribution as the vector with components

f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i}))\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y,\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(f(Y_{i})-f(X_{i}))\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{Y,\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f(Y_{i})\right\|_{2}\right]:=2\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}).

This yields
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
.
∎

Step 2: Bounding the Rademacher complexity
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}
)

Now that we have bounded
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]
by the Rademacher complexity, the next step of the proof is to use Dudley’s entropy integral to bound this Rademacher complexity
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}
).

In order to do that, we will use some useful definitions introduced in Section
A
, as well as the Dudley’s entropy integral Theorem
A.5
.

Recall that the Rademacher complexity we want to bound is:

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{S,\varepsilon}[\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\underbrace{\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,f_{x}(Y_{i})\right\|_{2}}_{:=\|Z_{x}\|_{2}}]=:\mathbb{E}_{S}[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\![\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\left\|Z_{x}\right\|_{2}]}_{(\star)}].

The strategy of the rest of the proof is to apply Dudley’s Theorem
A.5
to
(\star)
. In the one-dimensional case, Dudley’s theorem can be applied directly to the scalar process
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\,f_{x}(Y_{i})
, which is a sub-Gaussian process conditioned on the
Y_{i}
with respect to the associated empirical norm
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\;:=\;\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Y_{i})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}
(see Lemma
B.4
). In the present setting, however, the process takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To use the same concentration inequality, we therefore project it onto scalar directions—that is, we use the dual norm considering:

Z_{(z,x)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\langle z,f_{x}(Y_{i})\rangle:=z^{\top}Z_{x}.

(15)

We must then verify that this scalar process
Z_{(z,x)}
remains sub-Gaussian, though, now, with respect to an appropriate metric
d_{\mathcal{G}}
defined on the product space
(\mathcal{G},d_{\mathcal{G}})
, where
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{F}\times B_{1}
(Lemma
B.3
). Once this sub-Gaussian property is established, Dudley’s theorem
A.5
can be applied to
Z_{(z,x)}
and the rest of the analysis will require bounds on:

(i)

the moments of the envelope function
F
(Lemma
B.8
),

(ii)

the Lipschitz constant of functions in
\mathcal{F}
, (Lemmas
B.7
and
B.10
), and

(iii)

the covering number of
\mathcal{G}
(Lemmas
B.1
and
B.2
).

In order to do this we will need the definition
A.6
of the empirical distance and Lemmas
A.7
and
A.8
introduced in Section
A
, which we will be referring to.

Lemma B.7

(Bound on Lipschitz constant for numerator)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}:=\{f_{x},\,x\in B_{R}\}
as defined in (
13
). With an abuse of notations, denote
f:(x,y)\mapsto y\,e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}
. Then,
f(\cdot,y)
is Lipschitz with respect to
x
. Let
y\mapsto L(y)
be its Lipschitz constant. Consider
Y\sim\mu
, a sub-gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of matrix parameter
\Sigma
, and scalar parameter
\sigma:=\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|_{2}}
. Then,
L
verifies

\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{\mu})}\leq C||A||_{2}\,\sigma^{2}\,d\,\exp\!\big(2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big),

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Consider
f_{t}(y):=y\,e^{\langle t,y\rangle}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
- we will then apply the result to
t=Ax
using
\nabla_{t}f(t,y)=A^{\top}\nabla_{x}f_{x}(y)
.
The Jacobian of
f(\cdot,y)
at
t
is

\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\;=y\,y^{\top}\;e^{\langle t,y\rangle}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,.

Let

\|\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(P)}:=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|\nabla_{t}f(t,Y)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{4}\,e^{2\langle t,Y\rangle}\big]\Big)^{1/2},

where we used the fact that
YY^{\top}
is a rank one matrix, hence
\|YY^{\top}\|=\|Y\|_{2}^{2}
.

By Cauchy–Schwarz and the sub-Gaussian property of
Y
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(P)}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\big]\Big)^{\!1/4}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}e^{4\langle t,Y\rangle}\Big)^{\!1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\big]\Big)^{\!1/4}\,\exp\!\Big(2\,t^{\top}\Sigma t\Big).

Use
t^{\top}\Sigma t=x^{\top}A^{\top}\Sigma A\,x\leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
, and take the supremum over
\|x\|_{2}\leq R
:

\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R}\|\nabla_{x}f_{x}(y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(P)}\ \leq\ ||A||_{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\Big)^{\!1/4}\ \exp\!\big(2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big).

To express the moment factor,

\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\Big)^{\!1/4}=\Big(\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\Big)^{\!1/8}\Big)^{2}\underbrace{\leq}_{\star}(c\,\sigma\,\sqrt{d})^{2},

where
\star
is an extension to dimension
d
of a classical bound on sub-Gaussian moments, with
c
a universal constant (see
[
Vershynin, 2012
, Lemma 5.5]
).

Hence

\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{\mu})}:=\ \sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R}\|\nabla_{x}f_{x}(y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(P)}\ \leq C||A||_{2}\,\sigma^{2}\,d\,\exp\!\big(2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Then, we will need a bound on the envelope function of the class
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma B.8

(Bound on envelope function)
.

Let
F
be the envelope function defined in equation (
14
), and
Y\sim\mu
, a sub-gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of parameter
\sigma=\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|_{2}}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]^{1/2}\leq C5^{d/2}\,\sqrt{d}\,\sigma\,e^{\,8R^{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]^{1/2}=\mathbb{E}\!\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{2}\,e^{\,2R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}}\big]^{1/2}\ \leq\ \big(\mathbb{E}\|Y\|_{2}^{4}\big)^{1/4}\;\big(\mathbb{E}e^{\,4R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}}\big)^{1/4}.

The first term
\big(\mathbb{E}\|Y\|_{2}^{4}\big)^{1/4}
is controlled by the same argument used for
\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|Y\|_{2}^{8}\Big)^{\!1/4}
in Lemma
B.7
. Now, let
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be the unit sphere and
U\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
an
\varepsilon
-net with

|U|\leq(1+2/\varepsilon)^{d}
. By a standard estimate (e.g.
[
Vershynin, 2018
, Lemma 4.4.1, p. 116, 2nd ed.]
) we have,

\|g\|_{2}=\sup_{\|u\|=1}\langle g,u\rangle\ \leq\ \frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\max_{u\in U}\langle g,u\rangle,

hence for
g=A^{\top}Y
,

e^{\,4R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}}\ \leq\ \sum_{u\in U}\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{4R}{1-\varepsilon}\,\langle Au,Y\rangle\Big).

Taking expectations and using the sub-Gaussian moment generative function bound of equation (
1
) with
t=\tfrac{4R}{1-\varepsilon}Au
,

\mathbb{E}\,e^{\,4R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}}\ \leq\ (1+2/\varepsilon)^{d}\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big\|\tfrac{4R}{1-\varepsilon}\,\Sigma^{1/2}A\Big\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=(1+2/\varepsilon)^{d}\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{8R^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Finally, for
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
,

\big(\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\ \leq\ C5^{d/2}\,\sqrt{d}\,\sigma\,e^{\,8R^{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
A.7
holds for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. That is for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B_{R}
,

\|f_{x}-f_{x^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\ \leq\ \|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

We formally prove in Lemma
B.3
that the empirical process defined in equation (
15
) is a sub-Gaussian process, with respect to the distance associated to the product space
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{F}\times B_{1})
defined in Lemma
B.3
. We also need a bound on the covering number of
\mathcal{G}
. Corresponding Lemmas with their proofs can be found in Appendix
B.1
.

Now that we have gathered all the necessary tools to bound the Rademacher complexity
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
we can end the proof of Proposition
B.5
.

Proof of Proposition
B.5

Let
R_{L,F}=2R\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}+2\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}
and apply Theorem
A.5
to the centered sub-Gaussian process
Z_{(z,x)}
with respect to the metric space
(\mathcal{G},d_{\mathcal{G}})
, in order to bound
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{(z,x)\in\mathcal{G}}Z_{(z,x)}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{(z,x)\in\mathcal{G}}Z_{(z,x)}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{G})}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{G},d_{\mathcal{G}})}\,d\delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{R_{L,F}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{2\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}},B_{R},\|\cdot\|_{2}\Bigr)}\,+\,\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{2\|F(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}},B_{1},\|\cdot\|_{2}\Bigr)}\,d\delta,

where we used Lemma
B.1
and
B.2
.

Taking the expectation over
Y_{i}
, and using the symmetrization Lemma
B.6
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{(z,x)\in\mathcal{G}}Z_{(z,x)}|Y_{1},...,Y_{n}]\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}(\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{J})

where

\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Big[\int_{0}^{R_{L,F}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{2\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}},B_{R},\|\cdot\|_{2}\Bigr)}\Big]d\delta,

and

\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\Big[\int_{0}^{R_{L,F}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{2\|F(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}},B_{1},\|\cdot\|_{2}\Bigr)}\,d\delta\Big]\Big).

For the term
\mathcal{I}
, set the change of variable
u=\frac{\delta}{2R\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}}
for
0\leq u\leq 1
. For the second term
\mathcal{J}
, set
u=\frac{\delta}{2\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}}

\mathcal{I}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1+\frac{\|F\|_{n}}{R\,\|L\|_{n}}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\left(Ru,B_{R},\|\cdot\|_{2}\right)}\,2R\,\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\,du\right],

\mathcal{J}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1+\frac{R\,\|L\|_{n}}{\,\|F\|_{n}}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}\left(u,B_{1},\|\cdot\|_{2}\right)}\,2\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}\,du\right],

where we can denote
\|F\|_{n}:=\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}
and
\|L\|_{n}:=\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}
in the integrand to alleviate the notations.

Recall that
\ln\mathcal{N}\left(\varepsilon,B_{r},\|\cdot\|\right)\leq d\ln\left(1+\frac{2\,r}{\varepsilon}\right)
(see Corollary 4.2.11. in
[
Vershynin, 2018
]
), hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\leq 4\sqrt{2d}\,R\,\mathbb{E}[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}]+2\sqrt{d\ln 3}\,\mathbb{E}[\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}].

(16)

The same steps for the second term give,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\leq 4\sqrt{2d}\,\mathbb{E}[\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}]+2R\,\sqrt{d\ln 3}\,\mathbb{E}[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}].

(17)

Detailed derivations of (
16
) and (
17
) can be found in Appendix
B.3
.
Finally,

\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]\leq\frac{c\,\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(R\,\mathbb{E}[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}]+\,\mathbb{E}[\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}]\Big).

(18)

Applying Lemma
A.8
,
\mathbb{E}[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}=\left(\mathbb{E}[L(Y_{1})^{2}]\right)^{1/2},
and using Lemma
B.7
, along with Lemma
B.8
it comes,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c\,\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big(R\,C_{1}\,\|A\|_{2}\sigma^{2}\,de^{2R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}+5^{d/2}C_{2}\sigma\,\sqrt{d}\,e^{\,8R^{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\,d\,(R\|A\|_{2}\sigma\sqrt{d}+5^{d/2})\sigma\,}{\sqrt{n}}\,e^{\,8R^{2}\,\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}=:C_{0}(\Sigma,\,A,\,\delta,\,R).

for some positive constant
C
.

To conclude, we apply the Markov inequality, which yields, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F}}]\leq\frac{C_{0}(\Sigma,\,A,\,\delta,\,R)}{\sqrt{n}}.

This ends the proof of Proposition
B.5
.
∎

Part 2: Uniform concentration of the denominator empirical process

An analogous result as Proposition
B.5
holds for the denominator with an identical proof using the function class

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\{h_{x}:y\mapsto e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle},x\in B_{R}\}.

Proposition B.9

(Bound on denominator)
.

For all
R>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F^{\prime}}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\delta}\cdot\frac{\|A\|_{2}\,\sigma\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{n}}e^{2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

The strategy of the proof follows the one of Proposition
B.5
, in dimension 1. The first step of symmetrization is identical. Then, we detail a few differences compared to the numerator.

First, the Lipschitz constant calculation slightly differs, as shown in Lemma
B.10
below.

Lemma B.10

(Bound on Lipschitz constant for denominator)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, and
L
defined in Lemma
A.7

\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{\mu})}\leq C\|A\|_{2}\sigma\sqrt{d}\,\exp\!\big(2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big),

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Here, we apply the exact same proof as for Lemma
B.7
, with
h(t,y):=e^{\langle t,y\rangle}
, using
\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|Y\|_{2}^{4}\Big)^{\!1/4}\ \leq C\sigma\sqrt{d}
.
∎

Then, the sub-Gaussian process under consideration is now

Z_{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}h_{x}(Y_{i}),

(19)

(see Lemma
B.4
for justification).

In dimension 1, we apply Theorem
A.5
to the sub-Gaussian process
Z_{h}
- defined in equation (
19
) - with respect to the metric space
(\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})})
to bound
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}Z_{h}]
. Note that Dudley’s entropy bound extends to
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}|Z_{h}|]
using that
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}|Z_{h}|]=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}\max\{Z_{h},-Z_{h}\}
(both statement can be found in the literature). Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}|Z_{h}|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{F^{\prime}})}\sqrt{\ln N(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{\prime},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n}))}\,d\delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(B_{R})\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}}\sqrt{\ln N(\frac{\delta}{\|L\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}},B_{R},\|\cdot\|_{2})}\,d\delta,

where we used Lemma
B.2
.

Taking the expectation over
Y_{i}
, and using the symmetrization Lemma
B.6
(see Appendix
B.3
for details),

\displaystyle E[\|\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P}\|_{\mathcal{F^{\prime}}}]\leq\frac{C\,\|A\|_{2}\,\sigma\sqrt{d}}{\delta\,\sqrt{n}}\,e^{2\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}:=C_{0}^{\prime}(\Sigma,A,R;\delta).

(20)

Finally we also conclude using Markov inequality.
∎

Part 3: Uniform concentration of the attention map (ratio bound)

The last step of the proof is to combine Propositions
B.5
and
B.9
to bound the attention’s ratio. In the sequel,
x\in B_{R}
is fixed, and thus omitted in the notation most of the time. For the sake of clarity, we simplify the notation for both empirical and continuous attention using
N_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}e^{\langle Ax,y_{i}\rangle}
,
D_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\langle Ax,y_{i}\rangle}
, and
N:=\int ye^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}d\mu(y)
,
D:=\int e^{\langle Ax,y\rangle}d\mu(y)
. Then, we can write:
\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)=\|V\|_{2}\cdot\frac{N_{n}}{D_{n}}
and
\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}(x)=\|V\|_{2}\cdot\frac{N}{D}
.

Putting everything on the same denominator yields:

\displaystyle\|\frac{N_{n}}{D_{n}}-\frac{N}{D}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{N_{n}D-ND+ND-ND_{n}}{DD_{n}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|N_{n}-N\|_{2}}{D_{n}}+\frac{\|N\|_{2}\,|D_{n}-D|}{DD_{n}}.

To bound below the denominator
D_{n}
, note that
D=\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle Ax,Y\rangle}]\geq 1
by Jensen’s inequality. By Cantelli’s inequality, using the fact that
D=\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]
, it comes that, for any
\varepsilon>0
:

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}\leq 1-\varepsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(D_{n}\leq D-\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma_{D_{n},x}^{2}}{\sigma_{D_{n},x}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}},

where
\sigma_{D_{n},x}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(D_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}(e^{\langle Ax,Y\rangle})\leq\frac{1}{n}\,e^{2R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}
(using the definition
2.4
of sub-Gaussian distribution).

Taking
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
, in order for the event
D_{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
to be verified with probability
1-\delta_{1}
,
n
must be greater than

n\geq n_{min}(\delta_{1},\Sigma,A,R):=4e^{2R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta_{1}}-1\Bigr).

Hence, with probability
1-\delta_{1}
,

\|\frac{N_{n}}{D_{n}}-\frac{N}{D}\|_{2}\leq 2(\|N_{n}-N\|_{2}+\|N\|_{2}\,|D_{n}-D|).

(21)

By Proposition
B.5

B.5
Proof of Proposition
3.3

Proof.

Let
\mu
be as in the statement of Proposition
3.3
, and let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
. We let
A=K^{\top}Q
where
K
and
Q
are the key and query matrices in the attention mechanism, and consider the random vector
Z_{j}(x):=\left(e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle}x_{j},e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

We introduce notation for the numerator and denominator of the attention maps
\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)
and
\Gamma_{\mu}(x)
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{n}(x)}{D_{n}(x)}\quad\text{where}\quad N_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle}x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad D_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}\quad\text{where}\quad N(x)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}X\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad D(x)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}\right]\in\mathbb{R}

where
X\sim\mu
.

Step 1: Joint Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and Delta Method

Under the assumptions that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}X\|_{2}\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}]<\infty
, the strong law of large numbers ensures almost sure convergence

N_{n}(x)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}N(x),\qquad D_{n}(x)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}D(x)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The random vectors
Z_{j}(x)=(e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle}x_{j},e^{\langle Ax,x_{j}\rangle})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
are i.i.d. with mean
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}(x)]=(N(x),D(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Assuming
\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{1}(x)\|^{2}]<\infty
, the multivariate central limit theorem yields

\sqrt{n}\left(\begin{pmatrix}N_{n}(x)\\
D_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}N(x)\\
D(x)\end{pmatrix}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}_{d+1}\left(0,\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{1}(x))\right)

where
\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{1}(x))=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(x)&\Sigma_{12}(x)\\
\Sigma_{21}(x)&\Sigma_{22}(x)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)},
and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{11}(x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}(e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{\top}Xe^{2\langle Ax,X\rangle}]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[Xe^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}]\bigr)^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{22}(x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle})=\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle Ax,X\rangle}]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}]\bigr)^{2}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{12}(x)

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{21}(x)^{\top}=\operatorname{Cov}(e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle}X,e^{\langle Ax,X\rangle})=\mathbb{E}[Xe^{2tX}]-\mathbb{E}[Xe^{tX}]\,\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Applying the multivariate delta method to
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{*}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by
g(a,b)=a/b
, we obtain,

\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{N_{n}(x)}{D_{n}(x)}-\frac{N(x)}{D(x)}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}_{d}\left(0,\operatorname{Cov}(x)\right),

where the asymptotic covariance is denoted
\operatorname{Cov}(x)
and is equal to

\operatorname{Cov}(x)=\nabla g_{(N(x),D(x))}\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{1}(x))\nabla g_{(N(x),D(x))}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

The gradient of
g
at
(N(x),D(x))
is

\nabla g_{(N(x),D(x))}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{D(x)}I_{d}&-\frac{N(x)}{D(x)^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)},

where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix.
The variance
\operatorname{Cov}(x)
expands as

\operatorname{Cov}(x)=\frac{1}{D(x)^{2}}\Sigma_{11}(x)-\frac{1}{D(x)^{3}}\left(N(x)\Sigma_{12}(x)^{\top}+\Sigma_{12}(x)N(x)^{\top}\right)+\frac{1}{D(x)^{4}}\Sigma_{22}(x)N(x)N(x)^{\top}.

Step 2: Chi-Square Bound

From the asymptotic normality, the squared Euclidean norm converges in distribution to a chi-squared random variable

\left\|\sqrt{n}\,\operatorname{Cov}(x)^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x))\right\|_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{d}\chi^{2}(d).

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sqrt{n}\,\operatorname{Cov}(x)^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x))\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq q_{d,1-\delta}\right)\geq 1-\delta

(26)

where
q_{d,1-\delta}:=F_{\chi^{2}(d)}^{-1}(1-\delta)
is the
(1-\delta)
-quantile of the
\chi^{2}(d)
distribution.

To derive the desired inequality for
\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}^{2}
, we use a spectral inequality.

Step 3: Spectral Bound

Let
\tilde{z}_{n}=\sqrt{n}(\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x))
. Applying the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem, we get

\|\tilde{z}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))\|\operatorname{Cov}(x)^{-1/2}\tilde{z}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

This yields the event inclusion

\left\{\|\operatorname{Cov}(x)^{-1/2}\tilde{z}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q_{d,1-\delta}\right\}\subset\left\{\|\tilde{z}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))q_{d,1-\delta}\right\}.

(27)

From (
26
) and (
27
), we obtain that for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\tilde{z}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))q_{d,1-\delta}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Therefore:

\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))^{1/2}q_{d,1-\delta}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\quad\text{with probability }1-\delta.

This spectral inequality allows us to convert bounds on quadratic forms back to Euclidean norm bounds, completing the bridge between the chi-squared concentration and our desired result.
The quantile
q_{d,1-\delta}
can be upper bounded using the Laurent-Massart concentration inequality for chi-squared random variables (see
[
Laurent and Massart, 2000
, Section 4.1 Lemma 1]
) stating that, for any
x>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\chi^{2}(d)\geq d+2\sqrt{dx}+2x)\leq e^{-x}.

Setting
x=\ln(1/\delta)
in this inequality yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\chi^{2}(d)\geq d+2\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta)}+2\ln(1/\delta)\right)\leq\delta.

By definition of the quantile function,

q_{d,1-\delta}\leq d+2\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta)}+2\ln(1/\delta).

This yields our refined bound, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))^{1/2}\bigl(d+2\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta)}+2\ln(1/\delta)\bigr)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Step 4: Moment Generating Function Representation

We express the variance components using the moment generating function (MGF)
M(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{t^{\top}X}]
and its derivatives. For a centered sub-Gaussian
X
with parameter
\Sigma
:

\displaystyle M(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle t,X\rangle}],\quad M^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{E}[Xe^{\langle t,X\rangle}],\quad M^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}e^{\langle t,X\rangle}].

The variance components become

\displaystyle\Sigma_{11}(x)

\displaystyle=M^{\prime\prime}(2Ax)-M^{\prime}(Ax)M^{\prime}(Ax)^{\top}.

\displaystyle\Sigma_{22}(x)

\displaystyle=M(2Ax)-M(Ax)^{2}.

\displaystyle\Sigma_{12}(x)

\displaystyle=M^{\prime}(2Ax)-M^{\prime}(Ax)M(Ax).

Since
D(x)=M(Ax)
and
N(x)=M^{\prime}(Ax)
, the covariance
\operatorname{Cov}(x)
can be written as

\operatorname{Cov}(x)=\frac{M^{\prime\prime}(2Ax)}{M(Ax)^{2}}-\frac{M^{\prime}(Ax)M^{\prime}(2Ax)^{\top}+M^{\prime}(2Ax)M^{\prime}(Ax)^{\top}}{M(Ax)^{3}}+\frac{M(2Ax)}{M(Ax)^{4}}\,M^{\prime}(Ax)M^{\prime}(Ax)^{\top}.

Let
X
be
\Sigma
-sub-Gaussian with
\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)}
. For any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and unit vector
u
:

\displaystyle|\langle u,M^{\prime}(t)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}e^{t^{\top}\Sigma t}.

(28)

\displaystyle\langle u,M^{\prime\prime}(t)u\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\|\Sigma\|_{2}e^{t^{\top}\Sigma t}.

(29)

Let us prove this result. Let
Y=\langle u,X\rangle
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

|\langle u,M^{\prime}(t)\rangle|=|\mathbb{E}[Ye^{\langle t,X\rangle}]|\leq(\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}])^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle t,X\rangle}])^{1/2}.

Since
X
is
\Sigma
-sub-Gaussian,
Y
is
\sigma_{u}
-sub-Gaussian with
\sigma_{u}=\sqrt{u^{\top}\Sigma u}\leq\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{1/2}
. Thus
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\leq\|\Sigma\|_{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle t,X\rangle}]\leq e^{2t^{\top}\Sigma t}
.

The second inequality follows similarly using the fourth moment bound
\mathbb{E}[Y^{4}]\leq 3\|\Sigma\|_{2}^{2}
for sub-Gaussian random variables.

Step 6: Main spectral bound

We will prove the following spectral bound for attention variance.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lambda_{\max}(\operatorname{Cov}(x))\leq(2+\sqrt{3})\|\Sigma\|_{2}e^{5(Ax)^{\top}\Sigma Ax}.

Let
u
be a unit vector and
t=Ax
. Using
M(t)\geq 1
, and
\frac{M(2t)}{M(t)^{4}}\leq\frac{1}{M(t)^{2}}\leq 1
,

|u^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}(x)u|\leq|u^{\top}M^{\prime\prime}(2t)u|+|u^{\top}M^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+2|u^{\top}M^{\prime}(2t)||u^{\top}M^{\prime}(t)|.

Substituting the bounds from (
28
) and (
29
), with
p(t):=t^{\top}\Sigma t
,

|u^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}(x)u|\leq\|\Sigma\|_{2}\left(\sqrt{3}e^{4p(t)}+e^{2p(t)}+2e^{5p(t)}\right).

Since the exponential terms are dominated by
e^{5p(t)}
, we obtain

|u^{\top}\operatorname{Cov}(x)u|\leq(2+\sqrt{3})\|\Sigma\|_{2}e^{5p(t)}.

Since this holds for all unit vectors
u
, the result follows.

Step 7: Conclusion

Combining our results, we obtain that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}_{n}}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{C(\Sigma,A,V,x,\delta,d)}{\sqrt{n}},

where

C(\Sigma,A,V,x,\delta,d)=(2+\sqrt{3})\|V\|_{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{2}\,e^{\frac{5}{2}(Ax)^{\top}\Sigma Ax}\bigl(d+2\sqrt{d\ln(1/\delta)}+2\ln(1/\delta)\bigr)^{1/2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.1

C.2

Lemma C.4

(Bound on Lipschitz constant for numerator for Gaussian distribution)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and
L
defined in Lemma
A.7
, for
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, and
x\in B_{R}
,

\|L\|_{L^{2}(P)}\leq c(\Sigma,A,R)\exp\!\big(R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big),

where
c(\Sigma,A,R)
is a polynomial function in
\|A\|_{2}
,
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
,
R
, and
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Consider
f(t,y):=y\,e^{\langle t,y\rangle}
- we will then apply the result to
t=Ax
using
\nabla_{t}f(t,y)=A^{\top}\nabla_{x}f_{x}(y)
.

The Jacobian of
f(\cdot,y)
at
t
is

\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\;=y\,y^{\top}\;e^{\langle t,y\rangle}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

Let
\|\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(\mu)}:=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\|\nabla_{t}f(t,Y)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{4}\,e^{2\langle t,y\rangle}\big]\Big)^{1/2},

where we used the fact that
YY^{\top}
is a rank one matrix, hence
\|YY^{\top}\|=\|Y\|_{2}^{2}
.

Notice that, for any measurable function
g
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(Y)e^{\langle t,y\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\int g(y)e^{\langle t,y\rangle}\phi_{\Sigma}(y)\,dy=\int g(y)\exp\left(\langle t,y\rangle-\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y\right)\frac{dy}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\int g(y)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(y-\Sigma t)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(y-\Sigma t)+\frac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma t\right)\frac{dy}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma t}\int g(y)\phi_{\Sigma}(y-\Sigma t)\,dy=e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma t}\mathbb{E}\left[g(Y+\Sigma t)\right].

Using this property, we can write:

\|\nabla_{t}f(t,y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(\mu)}=e^{t^{\top}\Sigma t}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}\big[\|Y+\Sigma t\|_{2}^{4}\big]\Big)^{1/2},

Note that
t^{\top}\Sigma t=x^{\top}A^{\top}\Sigma A\,x\leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
, and take the supremum over
\|x\|_{2}\leq R
:

\|L\|_{L^{2}(P)}:=\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R}\|\nabla_{x}f_{x}(y)\|_{\mathrm{L^{2}}(\mu)}\ \leq\ c(\Sigma,A)\ \exp\!\big(\,R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}\big),

where
c(\Sigma,A)
is a polynomial function in
\|A\|_{2}
,
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
, and
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)
.
∎

Lemma C.6

(Bound on envelope function for Gaussians)
.

For
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, and
x\in B_{R}
,

\big(\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\ \leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{2}\,\Bigl(2^{d+1}d\,+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,2^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,(2R\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2})^{d+1}\Bigr)\,e^{R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

Write
Y:=\Sigma^{1/2}Z
, where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and notice that
2R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}\leq 2R\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}\|Z\|_{2}:=t\|Z\|_{2}
, where
t:=2R\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}
. Then:

\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\!\big[\|Y\|_{2}^{2}\,e^{\,2R\|A^{\top}Y\|_{2}}\big]\ \leq\ \|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}e^{\,t\|Z\|_{2}}]=\|\Sigma\|_{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}e^{\,t\|Z\|_{2}}].

As
\|Z\|_{2}\sim\chi_{d}
, writing
c_{d}:=\frac{2^{1-\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}
, one can explicitly compute:

\displaystyle E[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}e^{\,t\|Z\|_{2}}]

\displaystyle=c_{d}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}e^{-r^{2}/2}e^{tr}\,dr=c_{d}\,e^{t^{2}/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}e^{-(r-t)^{2}/2}\,dr.

Making the change of variable
r-t=s
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}e^{-(r-t)^{2}/2}\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{-t}^{\infty}(s+t)^{d+1}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|s|+t)^{d+1}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\underbrace{2^{d}(|s|^{d+1}+t^{d+1})}_{(\star)}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds,

where we use
(a+b)^{d+1}\leq 2^{d}(a^{d+1}+b^{d+1})
in
(\star)
.

Then use symmetry to compute

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|s|^{d+1}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds=2\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{d+1}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds=2\cdot 2^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+2}{2}\right)=2^{\frac{d+2}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+2}{2}\right).

Combining these results, we obtain that,

\displaystyle E[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}e^{\,t\|Z\|_{2}}]

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}\,2^{d}(2^{\frac{d+2}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+2}{2}\right)+\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^{d+1})\,e^{t^{2}/2}=(2^{d+1}d\,+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,2^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,t^{d+1})\,e^{t^{2}/2}.

Finally, with
t=2R\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}
,

\big(\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\ \leq\|\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{2}\,\Bigl(2^{d+1}d\,+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,2^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,(2R\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2})^{d+1}\Bigr)\,e^{R^{2}\|\Sigma^{1/2}A\|_{2}^{2}},

which is exactly the stated inequality.
∎
