THE FOCUSING ENERGY-CRITICAL NONLINEAR SCHRÖDINGER SYSTEM WITH POWER-TYPE GROWTH NONLINEARITIES IN THE RADIAL CASE
LUIZ GUSTAVO FARAH, MAICON HESPANHA

Abstract
. This work is concerned with a coupled system of focusing nonlinear Schrödinger equations involving general power-type nonlinearities in the energy-critical setting for dimensions 3 ≤ d ≤ 5 3\leq d\leq 5 in the radial setting. Our aim is to demonstrate the scattering versus blow-up dichotomy in the radial case. To achieve this, we first prove the existence of ground state solutions using the concentration-compactness method combined with variational techniques. We then establish finite-time blow-up through a convexity argument and prove scattering by applying the concentration-compactness and rigidity method.

Introduction

This paper investigates the Cauchy problem for the focusing energy-critical
l
-component nonlinear Schrödinger system

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\alpha_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}+\gamma_{k}\Delta u_{k}-\beta_{k}u_{k}=-f_{k}(\mathbf{u}),\quad k=1,\ldots,l,\\
\mathbf{u}(0,x)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(x),\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},...,u_{l})
with
u_{1},...,u_{l}
complex-valued functions on the variables
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
,
\Delta
is the Laplacian operator,
\alpha_{k},\gamma_{k}>0
,
\beta_{k}\geq 0
are real constants. The nonlinearities
f_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{l}\to\mathbb{C}
exhibit
p
-type growth and satisfy certain assumptions, which will be detailed later.

We are interested in the energy-critical case, where the
{\dot{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-norm of the solutions to (
1.1
) are invariant under the scaling symmetry

g(t,x)\mapsto g_{\lambda}(t,x):=\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{2}}g(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

A direct calculation shows that
p=\frac{d+2}{d-2}
, where

denotes the power of the nonlinearity
f_{k}
,
k=1,...,l
. In particular, for dimensions
d=3
,
d=4
and
d=6
, the nonlinearities are quintic, cubic and quadratic, respectively.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in studying the dynamics of nonlinear coupled Schrödinger systems with nonlinearities exhibiting power-like growth. For instance, in the specific case where
l=p=2
,
f_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})=2\overline{u}_{1}u_{2}
and

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u_{1}+\Delta u_{1}=-2\overline{u}_{1}u_{2},\\
\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u_{2}+\kappa\Delta u_{2}=-u^{2}_{1},\end{cases}

where

. This system arises as the non-relativistic limit of a nonlinear Klein–Gordon system, see
[
21
]
for further details.

The work
[

]
also addresses the local and global well-posedness and the existence of

The study of nonlinear Schrödinger systems with quadratic interactions is not limited to two-wave interactions. In particular, models exhibiting quadratic-type nonlinear growth for the case

Another example, where

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u_{1}+\Delta u_{1}-u_{1}+\left(\dfrac{1}{9}|u_{1}|^{2}+2|u_{2}|^{2}\right)u_{1}+\dfrac{1}{3}\bar{u_{1}}^{2}u_{2}=0,\\

Motivated by
[

]
-
[

, we aim to provide sufficient conditions on the interaction terms

to study the dynamics of energy-critical systems,
p=\frac{d+2}{d-2}

. This research extends the subcritical framework,
\frac{d+4}{d}\leq p<\frac{d+2}{d-2}
, established in
[
38
]
. To this end, we will impose the following assumptions on the nonlinear terms

(H1)
.

The nonlinearities satisfy

\displaystyle f_{k}(\mathbf{0})=0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,l.

(H2)
.

We have, for any
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
, that

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{m}}[f_{k}(\mathbf{z})-f_{k}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})]\right|+\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}_{m}}[f_{k}(\mathbf{z})-f_{k}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})]\right|&\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{l}|z_{j}-z_{j}^{\prime}|^{\frac{4}{d-2}},\qquad k,m=1,\ldots,l.\end{split}

(H3)
.

There exists a function
F:\mathbb{C}^{l}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that

f_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\overline{z}_{k}}(\mathbf{z})+\overline{\frac{\partial F}{\partial z_{k}}}(\mathbf{z}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,l.

(H4)
.

There exists positive constants
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{l}
such that for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}

Im\sum_{k=1}^{l}\sigma_{k}f_{k}(\mathbf{z})\widebar{z}_{k}=0,

(H5)
.

The function
F
is homogeneous of degree
\frac{2d}{d-2}
, that is, for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{l}
and
\lambda>0
,

F(\lambda\mathbf{z})=\lambda^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}F(\mathbf{z}).

(H6)
.

The function
F
satisfies the following inequality

\left|\mathrm{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{u})\;dx\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!\mathbf{u}\!\!\big\bracevert\!\!)\;dx.

(H7)
.

The function
F
is real valued on
\mathbb{R}^{l}
, that is, if
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
then

F(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, all the functions
f_{k}
are non-negative on the positive cone in
\mathbb{R}^{l}
, that is, for
y_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,l
,

f_{k}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{l})\geq 0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,l.

(H8)
.

The function
F
can be written as the sum
F=F_{1}+\cdots+F_{m}
, where
F_{s}
,
s=1,\ldots,m
is super-modular on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
,
1\leq d\leq l
and vanishes on hyperplanes, that is, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
i\neq j
and
k,h>0
, we have

F_{s}(y+he_{i}+ke_{j})+F_{s}(y)\geq F_{s}(y+he_{i})+F_{s}(y+ke_{j}),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},

and
F_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})=0
if
y_{j}=0
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

As we will show, assumptions
(H1)
–
(H2)
suffice to prove local well-posedness in the energy space (see Theorem
1.3
below). Moreover, assumptions
(H3)
-
(H4)
guarantee that solutions to system (
1.1
) conserve both mass and energy, which are
given, respectively, by

(1.4)

M(\mathbf{u}(t)):=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\sigma_{k}\alpha_{k}}{2}\|u_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and

(1.5)

E(\mathbf{u}(t)):=K(\mathbf{u})+L(\mathbf{u})-2P(\mathbf{u}),

where
K
,
L
and
P
are defined as

(1.6)

K(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad L(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\beta_{k}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad P(\mathbf{u})=\hbox{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{u}(t))dx.

The functionals
K
and
P
are referred to as the kinetic and potential energies, respectively.

To study the asymptotic behavior of the global solutions to the energy-critical system (
1.1
), under assumptions
(H1)
-
(H8)
, we well also impose a mass resonance condition. More precisely, we say that the system satisfies the mass-resonance condition if

(1.7)

\mathrm{Im}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2\gamma_{k}}f_{k}(\mathbf{z})\overline{z}_{k}=0,\quad\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{l},

where
\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
are the parameters appearing in (
1.1
).

This condition simplifies the application of localized virial-type arguments. Indeed, let
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define

(1.8)

V(t)=\int\phi(x)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx.

Taking the time derivative, we obtain

(1.9)

V^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u_{k}\widebar{u}_{k}dx-4\int\phi(x)\mathrm{Im}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2\gamma_{k}}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})\widebar{u}_{k}dx,

where the last term vanishes due to the mass-resonance condition (
1.7
).

Remark 1.1
.

Note that systems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) satisfy assumptions
(H1)
-
(H8)
. Indeed, their associated potential functions are given respectively by
F(z_{1},z_{2})=\widebar{z}_{1}^{2}z_{2}
and
F(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{36}|z_{1}|^{4}+\frac{9}{4}|z_{2}|^{4}+|z_{1}|^{2}|z_{2}|^{2}+\frac{1}{9}\widebar{z}_{1}^{3}z_{2}
. Moreover, using the mass-resonance condition (
1.7
), one can verify that both systems satisfy this condition if, and only if,
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
and
\sigma=3
, respectively.

Our main goal is to establish a classification of the scattering versus blow-up dichotomy in the radial setting. Before presenting our results, we first recall the known results for the Cauchy problem associated with the single nonlinear Schrödinger equation

(1.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u=\lambda|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

The local well-posedness was first established by
[
8
]
. Furthermore, to investigate the asymptotic behavior of radial solutions, the authors in the seminal work
[
26
]
developed the concentration-compactness and rigidity method, proving scattering below the energy threshold for radial initial data in dimensions
3\leq d\leq 5
. These results were later extended to the non-radial setting in dimensions
d\geq 5

[
28
]
, and more recently in dimension
d=4

[
12
]
.

Turning back to our problem, we start with the following definition.

Definition 1.2

(Solution)
.

A solution to the system (
1.1
) is a function
\mathbf{u}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{l}
, defined on a non-empty time interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, with

, such that
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{C}_{t}^{0}\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{x}^{1}(K\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathbf{L}_{t,x}^{d}(K\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all compact interval

, and satisfies the Duhamel formula

(1.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{k}(t)=U_{k}(t)u_{k0}+i\int_{0}^{t}U_{k}(t-s)\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}f_{k}(\mathbf{u})ds,\\
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}=(u_{10},...,u_{l0}),\end{array}\right.

where
U_{k}(t)
denotes the corresponding unitary group defined by
U_{k}(t)=e^{it\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}(\gamma_{k}\Delta-\beta_{k})}
,

, and

. The interval

is referred to as the lifespan of the solution
\mathbf{u}
. We say that
\mathbf{u}
is a maximal-lifespan solution if the solution cannot be extended to an interval
J\supset I
strictly larger than
I
. The solution is said to be global if
I=\mathbb{R}
.

To state our local well-posed result we introduce the norms

and

for an interval

as follows

(1.12)

\|\cdot\|_{S(I)}:=\|\cdot\|_{L_{I}^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}L_{x}^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}}\,\hbox{ and }\ \|\cdot\|_{X(I)}:=\|\cdot\|_{L_{I}^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}L_{x}^{\frac{2d(d+2)}{d^{2}+4}}}.

Note that
(\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2},\frac{2d(d+2)}{d^{2}+4})
is an admissible pair as defined in Definition
2.5
below. Furthermore, by the Sobolev embedding (see Lemma
2.8
below), it follows that

(1.13)

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{S}(I)}\lesssim\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{X}(I)}.

By following the strategy for the energy critical Schrödinger equation (

) (see, for example,
[

or
[

, Theorem 2.5]
, and for a textbook exposition,

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

and suppose that assumptions
(H1)
-
(H2)
are satisfied. For
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists

It is well known that the ground state solution to the single nonlinear Schrödinger equation is closely linked to the sharp constant in the critical Sobolev inequality (see, for example,
[
51
]
). Our first goal is to is to investigate the existence of a special solution for system (
1.1
). More precisely, a standing wave solution for (
1.1
) is a particular solution of the form

(1.14)

u_{k}(t,x)=e^{i\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2}\omega t}\psi_{k}(x),\quad k=1,...,l.,

where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{k}
are real-valued functions decaying to zero at infinity. Substituting into (
1.1
), we find that
\psi_{k}
satisfies the equation

(1.15)

-\gamma_{k}\Delta\psi_{k}+b_{k}\psi_{k}=f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),\quad k=1,\ldots,l,

where
b_{k}:=\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\omega+\beta_{k}\geq 0
. The action function associated with (
1.15
) is

(1.16)

S(\boldsymbol{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\gamma_{k}|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int b_{k}|\psi_{k}|^{2}\right]-\int F(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})dx.

A ground state solution for (
1.17
) is a nontrivial critical point of the functional
S
that also minimizes it. According to
[
38
, Lemma 4.3]
, nontrivial solutions to (
1.15
) exist only if
\omega=0
and
\beta_{k}=0
for
k=1,\ldots,l
(and thus

). Consequently, equation (
1.15
) simplify to

(1.17)

-\gamma_{k}\Delta\psi_{k}=f_{k}(\boldsymbol{\psi}),\quad k=1,\ldots,l,

with the corresponding action functional expressed as

(1.18)

S(\boldsymbol{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

The preceding discussion leads to the following definition.

Definition 1.4
.

A function
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
weak solution
of (
1.17
) if for any
\mathbf{g}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(1.19)

\gamma_{k}\int\nabla\psi_{k}\cdot\nabla g_{k}dx=\int f_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})g_{k}dx,\quad k=1,\ldots,l.

Moreover, a weak solution
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
ground state
of (
1.17
) if

S(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\inf\{S(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$});\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}\in\mathcal{C}\},

where where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of all non-trivial solutions of (
1.17
). The set of all ground state of (
1.17
) will be denote by
\mathcal{G}
.

The existence of this special solution is established in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

The set
\mathcal{G}
is non-empty, and thus there exists at least one ground state solution
\boldsymbol{\psi}
for system (
1.17
).

The proof of previous theorem is inspired by the recent work of the second author
[
22
]
. We employ the concentration-compactness principle from
[
36
]
, constructing a sequence of probability Radon measures and excluding the possibilities of vanishing and dichotomy to establish compactness. As a consequence, we also obtain the optimal constant for some critical Sobolev inequality (see Corollary
3.9

Once the existence of ground state solutions is established, we can set the threshold for our dichotomy. We begin by studying the global existence and scattering of radial solutions. In this part, we work with initial data in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which means that we do not have control over the
L^{2}
norm. Consequently, as in the analysis of ground state solutions, we set all
\beta_{k}=0
in (
1.1
) and instead work with the system

(1.20)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\alpha_{k}\partial_{t}u_{k}+\gamma_{k}\Delta u_{k}=-f_{k}(\mathbf{u}),\quad k=1,...,l,\\
\mathbf{u}(0,x)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(x).\end{array}\right.

Note that in this case, the energy is given by

(1.21)

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}(t))=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0}):=K(\mathbf{u}_{0})-2P(\mathbf{u}_{0}).

Our goal is to prove that, under the mass-resonance condition (
1.7
), if the initial data has both energy and kinetic energy strictly below those of a ground state, then the corresponding solution to (
1.20
) is global and scatters. To this end, we adopt the Kenig-Merle approach introduced in
[
26
]
on the radial setting. Our result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
3\leq d\leq 5
and assume that
(H1)
-
(H8)
and the mass-resonance condition (
1.7
) hold. If
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is radially symmetric and
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is any ground state solution satisfying

(1.22)

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})<\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi})

and

(1.23)

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then the corresponding solution
\mathbf{u}
with initial data

at

for the system (
1.20
) is global. Moreover, there exists
\mathbf{u}^{+},\mathbf{u}^{-}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm}\|u_{k}(t)-u_{k}^{\pm}\|_{\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}}=0,\quad k=1,...,l.

The above result may be extended to
d\geq 5
without the radial assumption by employing the strategy in
[
28
]
. This will be explored in future work.

Finally, we investigate blow-up in finite time. Here, we consider initial data in
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which gives us control over the

norm. Next we state the local theory in this space.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
3\leq d\leq 5
and suppose that assumptions
(H1)
-
(H2)
are satisfied. For
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists

Since we can also work with the

norm, there is no need to impose the mass-resonance condition (

), and we can work directly with the original system (
1.1
). The following result asserts that if the initial data has energy below that of the ground states, but kinetic energy exceeding that of the ground states, then the maximal interval of existence of the corresponding solution must be finite.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
3\leq d\leq 5
. Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
\mathbf{u}
be the corresponding solution of (
1.1
) defined in the maximal time interval of existence, say,
I
. If
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is radially symmetric functions satisfying

(1.24)

E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi})

and

(1.25)

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})>K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

where
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is any ground state solution, and

is the energy defined in (

The condition
3\leq d\leq 5
in the above result is required only due to Theorem

, though the proof method applies to all dimensions

. To prove the above theorem, we employ the convexity method, which, roughly speaking, consists of deriving a contradiction by working with the virial identities for the function (

). Note that the second term in the identity (

) vanishes only under the mass-resonance assumption (

). To address this, we adopt a modification of the method, as in

, which involves discarding the second term in (

(1.26)

\mathcal{R}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}2\mathrm{Im}\int\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u_{k}\widebar{u}_{k}dx.

2.1.
Notation

In this subsection we introduce some notation used throughout the paper. The letter
C

3.
Existence of ground state solution

This section is devoted to prove the existence of ground state solutions as stated in Theorem
1.5
. We begin by noting that we do not assume the potential function
F
is strictly positive away from the origin. The following result establishes a property of the potential energy
P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
that allows us to address this issue by placing our problem in an appropriate set, rather than
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}:=\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\,\,P(\boldsymbol{\psi})>0\}
. If
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of all non-trivial solutions of (
1.17
), then
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Assume
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{C}
. By inserting
\mathbf{g}=\boldsymbol{\psi}
in (
1.19
) we obtain

\int|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}dx=\int f_{k}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\psi_{k}dx,\quad k=1,...,l.

Using Lemma
2.3
(ii) we get

(3.1)

K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\left(\frac{2d}{d-2}\right)P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Since
\psi
is non-trivial, it follows that
P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})>0
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{N}
as desired.
∎

Now, let us introduce the functionals

(3.2)

J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}):=\frac{K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}}{P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in\mathcal{N}.

and

(3.3)

\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}):=K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})-2P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Remark 3.2
.

Using (
3.1
), we observe that
S(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\frac{2}{d-2}P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
. Thus, by the definition of
J
, we obtain

J(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\frac{K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}}{P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})}=\left(\frac{2d}{d-2}\right)^{\frac{d}{d-2}}P(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{2}{d-2}}=\frac{2}{d-2}d^{\frac{d}{d-2}}S(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})^{\frac{2}{d-2}}.

Hence, a non-trivial solution to (
1.17
) is a ground state if and only if it has the least energy
\mathcal{E}
among all solutions, which is equivalent to minimizing
J
.

3.1.
Critical and localized Sobolev-type inequalities.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that the vector
\mathbf{u}
is a real-valued function. First, applying Lemma
2.3
and Sobolev’s inequality, we obtain

|P(\mathbf{u})|\leq\int|\mathrm{Re}F(\mathbf{u})|dx\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}\leq C_{0}K(\mathbf{u})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}

where

is a positive constant depending on
\gamma_{k}
. Hence, we obtain the following critical Sobolev-type inequality

(3.4)

P(\mathbf{u})\leq CK(\mathbf{u})^{\frac{d}{d-2}},

for some positive constant

. In particular, there exists a constant

such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq J(\mathbf{u}),\quad\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{N}

and we define the optimal constant by

(3.5)

C_{\rm opt}^{-1}:=\inf\{J(\mathbf{u});\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{N}\}.

As stated in Remark
3.2
, a ground state solution exists once the above infimum is achieved. Let
({\mathbf{u}_{m}})\subset\mathcal{N}
be a minimizing sequence for (

J(\mathbf{u}_{m})\to C_{\rm opt}^{-1}
. A straightforward calculation shows that
K(\big\bracevert\!\mathbf{u}\!\big\bracevert)\leq K(\mathbf{u})
, and combined with

, it follows that
J(\big\bracevert\!\mathbf{u}\!\big\bracevert)\leq J(\mathbf{u})
. Thus, we can assume, without loss of generality, that minimizing sequences are non-negative. Moreover, by the homogeneity of the functionals

and
P
, it also follows that

, where

is given by

(3.6)

Thus, solving the minimization problem (

) is equivalent to solving (
3.6
). Finally, the functionals

and

are also invariant under the transformation

(3.7)

\mathbf{u}\mapsto\mathbf{u}^{R,y}=R^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}\mathbf{u}\left(R^{-1}(x-y)\right)

where

, which ensures ensures that our minimization problem is invariant under dilations and translations.

3.2.
Concentration-compactness

As previously noted, the next step in addressing the minimization problem (

[

. We begin with a modified version of Lemma 1.7.4 from
[

regarding the concentration function, defined as follows

Q_{m}(R):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B(y,R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx,\quad R>0.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{m})\subset{\mathbf{L}}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\mathbf{u}_{m}\geq 0
and
\int F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx=1
, for any

. Let
Q_{m}(R)
be the concentration function of

defined by (

). Then, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is

such that

Q_{m}(R)=\int_{B(y,R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx.

Proof.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Given
R>0
, there exists a sequence
(y_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

Q_{m}(R)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{B(y_{j},R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx>0.

Thus, one must find
j_{0}
such that if
j\geq j_{0}
then
\int_{B(y_{j},R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx\geq\epsilon
, for some
\epsilon>0
. We claim that the sequence
(y_{j})
is bounded. Indeed, if it were not, then up to a subsequence, the balls
B(y_{j},R)
and
B(y_{i},R)
would be disjoint for all
i\neq j
. Then

1=\int F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx\geq\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}\int_{B(y_{j},R)}F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx=\infty,

which is a contradiction. Therefore, the sequence
(y_{j})
admits a convergent subsequence with limit
y=y(m,R)
and the proof is completed by applying the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

We now introduce notation for the concentration-compactness framework. Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space. We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{b}(X)
the space of all bounded continuous functions on

, and by
\mathcal{C}_{c}(X)
the space of all continuous functions with compact support on

. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}_{+}(X)
denotes the Banach space of all non-negative measures on

,
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(X)
the space of all finite (or bounded) measures and
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}
the space of all probability measures. Given two measures
\nu
and
\mu
, we write
\nu\ll\mu
to indicate that
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
. For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(X)
, the quantity
|\mu|:=\mu(X)
is called the mass of
\mu
. Let us introduce some notions of convergence in the context of measures.

Definition 3.5
.

(i)

A sequence
(\mu_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}_{+}
is said to converge vaguely to
\mu
in
\mathcal{M}_{+}(X)
, denoted by
\mu_{m}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\mu

\int_{X}fd\mu_{m}\rightarrow\int_{X}fd\mu
for all

.

(ii)

A sequence
(\mu_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(X)
is said to converge weakly to
\mu
in
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(X)
, denoted by
\mu_{m}\rightharpoonup\mu
, if
\int_{X}fd\mu_{m}\rightarrow\int_{X}fd\mu
, for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(X)
.

(iii)

A sequence
(\mu_{m})\subset\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}
is said to be uniformly tight if, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset

such that
\mu_{m}(X\backslash K_{\epsilon})\leq\epsilon
for all
m
. We also say that a set

is vaguely bounded if
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\int_{X}fd\mu\right|<\infty

. For a complete proof we refer the reader to

Lemma 3.6

(Concentration-compactness lemma I)
.

Suppose that
(\nu_{m})
is a sequence in
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, there is a subsequence, still denoted by
(\nu_{m})
, such that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

(Vanishing) For all
R>0
it holds

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu_{m}(B(x,R))\right)=0.

(ii)

(Dichotomy) There is a number
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
R>0
and a sequence
(x_{m})
with the following property: given

\nu_{m}(B(x_{m},R))\geq\lambda-\epsilon,

\nu_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(x_{m},R^{\prime}))\geq 1-\lambda-\epsilon,

for
m
sufficiently large.

(iii)

(Compactness) There exists a sequence
(x_{m})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for each
\epsilon>0
there is a radius
R>0
with the property

\nu_{m}(B(x_{m},R))\geq 1-\epsilon,

for all
m
.

To determine a minimizer for (
3.6
), the next step is to construct an appropriate sequence of probability Radon measures. By Lemma
3.6
, up to a subsequence, this sequence will satisfy one of the three conditions outlined. We rule out vanishing and dichotomy, ensuring the sequence’s compactness. Consequently, we achieve vague convergence in
\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. This convergence enables the application of what we call the concentration-compactness lemma II, inspired by the limit case lemma in
[
36
]
. In essence, this lemma ensures dilation invariance for the minimization problem.

Lemma 3.7

(Concentration-compactness lemma II)
.

Let
(\mathbf{u}_{m})\subset\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a sequence such that
\mathbf{u}_{m}\geq 0
and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\mathbf{u}_{m}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u},&\hbox{in}&\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\mu_{m}:=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{km}|^{2}dx\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\mu&\hbox{in}&\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\nu_{m}:=F(\mathbf{u}_{m})dx\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\nu,&\hbox{in}&\mathcal{M}_{+}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).\\

Then,

(i)

There exists an at most countable set
J
, a family of distinct points
\{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\,j\in J\}
, and a family of non-negative numbers

(3.11)

\nu=F(\mathbf{u})dx+\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}.

(ii)

Moreover, we have

(3.12)

\mu\geq\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}dx+\sum_{j\in J}b_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}

fore some family
\{b_{j};\,j\in J\},b_{j}>0,
such that

(3.13)

a_{j}\leq I^{-\frac{d}{d-2}}b_{j}^{\frac{d}{d-2}},\quad\mbox{for all}\quad j\in J.

In particular,
\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}^{\frac{d-2}{d}}<\infty

Proof.

The proof follows the same approach as in the quadratic case presented in
[

, Lemma 3.6]
, with suitable modifications for our context.

Proof of Theorem

.

We begin by applying Theorem

to obtain a nontrivial minimizer

of (

Taking

and applying Lemma

The following inequality holds

(4.15)

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{U}(t-a_{2})\mathbf{w}(a_{2})\|_{\mathbf{S}([a_{2},s])}&\lesssim\|\mathbf{U}(t-t_{0})\mathbf{w}(t_{0})\|_{\mathbf{S}([a_{2},s])}+\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{v})-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}([a_{2},s]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\quad+\|\nabla\mathbf{e}\|_{\mathbf{L}_{t}^{2}\mathbf{L}_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{v})-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}([a_{2},s]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\epsilon.\end{split}

The quantity
\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{v})-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}([a_{2},s]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}
can therefore be controlled, as before, via a continuity argument. As a consequence, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{v})-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I_{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{2}\epsilon,

which implies

\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{S}(I_{2})}\leq C_{2}\epsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\nabla\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{X}(I_{2})}\leq cA^{\prime}+C_{1}\epsilon+C_{2}\epsilon.

By iterating this procedure, we get

\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{v})-f_{k}(\mathbf{u})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{n}\epsilon,

and

(4.16)

\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{S}(I_{n})}\leq C_{n}\epsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\nabla\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{X}(I_{n})}\leq cA^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\epsilon

provided that (
4.3
) and (
4.5
) hold with
t_{0}
replaced by
a_{j}
. Indeed, evaluating the integral equation (
4.10
) at
t=a_{n+1}
and proceeding as in (
4.11
)-(
4.12
), we have

(4.17)

\begin{split}\|U_{k}(t-a_{n+1})w_{k}(a_{n+1})\|_{{S}(I)}&\lesssim\|U_{k}(t-t_{0})w_{k}(t_{0})\|_{{S}(I)}+\|\nabla e_{k}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\quad+\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{u})-f_{k}(\mathbf{v})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\leq\varepsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}C_{j}\varepsilon\end{split}

and

(4.18)

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[t_{0},a_{n+1}]}\left\|w_{k}(t)\right\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}&\lesssim\left\|w_{k}(t_{0})\right\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}+\|\nabla e_{k}\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\quad+\|\nabla[f_{k}(\mathbf{u})-f_{k}(\mathbf{v})]\|_{L_{t}^{2}L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}([t_{0},a_{n+1}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\leq cA^{\prime}+\varepsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}C_{j}\varepsilon.\end{split}

Therefore, by choosing
\epsilon_{0}
sufficiently small, depending on
M
,
A
,
A^{\prime}
, and
d
, we can proceed with the inductive argument. Finally, the estimates (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) follow directly from (
4.2
), (
4.16
), and (
4.18
).
∎

The preceding result allows us to establish a useful continuity result.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Let

4.2.
Variational estimates

Next we will establish some coercivity lemmas adapted to our setting.

Lemma 4.7

(Coercivity I)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
\mathbf{u}
be a solution of (
1.20
) with maximal existence interval
I
. Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{G}
be a ground state. Suppose that

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})<(1-\tilde{\delta})\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi}).

(i)

If

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then there exists
\tilde{\delta}_{1}=\tilde{\delta}_{1}(\tilde{\delta})
such that

K(\mathbf{u}(t))<(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

for all
t\in I
.

(ii)

If

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})>K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then there exists
\tilde{\delta}_{2}=\tilde{\delta}_{2}(\tilde{\delta})
such that

K(\mathbf{u}(t))>(1+\tilde{\delta}_{2})K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

for all
t\in I
.

Proof.

From the energy conservation and the sharp inequality (
3.24
), we deduce

(4.19)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{\frac{d}{d-2}},\quad\mbox{for all}\quad t\in I.

Let
G(t)=K(\mathbf{u}(t))
,
a=\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
,
b=2C_{\rm opt}
and
q={\frac{d}{d-2}}
. Using (
3.25
), we get

\gamma=(bq)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}=\left(2C_{\rm opt}\frac{d}{d-2}\right)^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}=K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Thus, Lemma
2.9
yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma 4.8

(Coercivity II)
.

Under hypothesis of Lemma
4.7
we have

(i)

If

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})<K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then there exists
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\tilde{\delta})>0
such that

(4.20)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t))\geq\delta^{\prime}K(\mathbf{u}(t)),

for all
t\in I
. Moreover,
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
.

(ii)

If

K(\mathbf{u}_{0})>K(\boldsymbol{\psi}),

then there exists
\delta^{\prime\prime}=\delta^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\delta})>0
such that

K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq-\delta^{\prime\prime}K(\mathbf{u}(t)),

for all
t\in I
.

Proof.

We will show item (i). The second one is proved in an analogous way. Using the sharp inequality (
3.24
) and Lemma
4.7
, we deduce

1-\frac{2d}{d-2}\frac{P(\mathbf{u}(t))}{K(\mathbf{u}(t))}\geq 1-\frac{2d}{d-2}C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{\frac{2}{d-2}}=1-\left[\frac{K(\mathbf{u}(t))}{K(\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right]^{\frac{2}{d-2}}\geq 1-(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})^{\frac{2}{d-2}}=:\delta^{\prime}.

Multiplying both sides by
K(\mathbf{u}(t))
we get the result.

Next we claim that the energy of the solution in non negative in case (i). Indeed, if
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\geq\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})>0
(see Remark
3.2
) the result is trivial. On the other hand, if
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})<\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
, since

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})=K(\mathbf{u}(t))-2P(\mathbf{u}(t))=\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-2}{d}\left[K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t))\right],

inequality (
4.20
) implies that
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
.

∎

Lemma 4.9

(Energy trapping)
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathbf{u}
be the corresponding solution of (
1.20
) with maximal existence interval
I
. If
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta)\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi})
and
K(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta^{\prime})K(\boldsymbol{\psi})
, then

K(\mathbf{u}(t))\sim\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}(t)),\quad\mbox{for all}\quad t\in I.

Proof.

By (
3.25
) and
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq(1-\delta)\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\psi})
we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}(t))&\leq K(\mathbf{u}(t))+2|P(\mathbf{u}(t))|\\
&\leq K(\mathbf{u}(t))+2C_{\rm opt}|K(\mathbf{u}(t))|^{\frac{d}{d-2}}\\
&\leq\left(1+2C_{\rm opt}[(1-\tilde{\delta}_{1})K(\boldsymbol{\psi})^{\frac{2}{d-2}}]\right)K(\mathbf{u}(t)).\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}(t))&\geq\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-2}{d}[K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t))]\\
&\geq\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t))+\frac{d-2}{d}\delta^{\prime}K(\mathbf{u}(t))\\
&=\frac{2}{d}\left(1+\frac{d-2}{d}\delta^{\prime}\right)K(\mathbf{u}(t)).\end{split}

Combining both inequalities, we get the result.
∎

4.3.

We start by setting
E_{C}
to be the threshold

E_{C}=\sup\left.\begin{cases}0<E<\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}):\,\,\mbox{for every radial}\,\,\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\,\mbox{such that}\\

where
\psi
is any ground state solution for system (

, the number

is well-defined and satisfies

, where

.

)
, we reach a contradiction by choosing suitable
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

We can assume that the frequency scale function
\lambda(t)
is bounded from below, i.e.,
\lambda(t)\geq A_{0}>0
, as detailed in
[
26
, Section 5]
. Furthermore,
\lambda(t)
can be adjusted so that
\inf_{t\in I_{+}}\lambda(t)\geq 1
for at least half of the maximal lifespan, following the arguments in
[
28
, Section 4]
.

Next we will prove that the critical solution must be global.

Proposition 4.15
.

Suppose
E_{C}<\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
and let
\mathbf{u}_{C}
be the solution established in Theorem
4.12
. Then,
I_{C}=\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
I_{C}=(-T_{C}^{-},T_{C}^{+})
. We will prove the result for
T_{C}^{+}
, with the case for
T_{C}^{-}
following similarly. Assuming
T_{C}^{+}<\infty
, we claim that

(4.37)

\lambda(t)\rightarrow\infty,\quad\hbox{as}\quad t\rightarrow T_{C}^{+}.

Otherwise, there is a sequence
t_{i}\uparrow T_{C}^{+}
such that
\lambda(t_{i})\rightarrow\lambda_{0}<\infty
such that, by the compactness of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
, the sequence
\mathbf{v}_{i}:=\lambda(t_{i})^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}\mathbf{u}_{C}\left(t_{i},\lambda(t_{i})^{-1}x\right)\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
in
\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Now, since
\inf_{t\in[0,T_{C}^{+}]}\lambda(t)\geq 1
, and
\lambda_{0}>0
, we conclude that

\mathbf{u}_{C}\left(t_{i},x\right)=\lambda(t_{i})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\mathbf{v}_{i}(t_{i},\lambda(t_{i})x)\rightarrow\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\mathbf{v}(T_{C}^{+},\lambda_{0}x)
. Let
h(t,x)
be the solution of (
1.20
) with initial data
\lambda_{0}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\mathbf{v}(T_{C}^{+},\lambda_{0}x)
, in a interval
(T_{C}^{+}-\delta,T_{C}^{+}+\delta)
, with
\|h\|_{S((T_{C}^{+}-\delta,T_{C}^{+}+\delta))}<\infty
and a sequence of solutions
\mathbf{h}_{i}(t,x)
with initial data at
T_{C}^{+}
equal to
\mathbf{u}_{C}(t_{i})
with

\sup_{i}\|\mathbf{h}_{i}\|_{S(T_{C}^{+}-\delta/,T_{C}^{+}+\delta/)}<\infty.

However,
\mathbf{u}_{C}(t+t_{i}-T_{C}^{+})=\mathbf{h}_{i}(t)
, which contradicts Lemma
4.3
, since
T_{C}^{+}<\infty
.

Now, fix
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}
to be radial, equal
1
for
|x|\leq 1
and equal
0
for
|x|\geq 2
and set
\varphi_{R}(x)=\varphi(x/R)
. Define

X_{R}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\varphi_{R}(x)|u_{Ck}|^{2}dx,\quad t\in[0,T_{C}^{+}).

Thus, applying Hölder’s inequality, Hardy’s inequality, and (
4.27
), we get

(4.38)

|X^{\prime}_{R}|\leq\frac{2}{R}\left|\mathrm{Im}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\nabla\varphi_{R}\nabla u_{Ck}\cdot\bar{u}_{ck}dx.\right|\lesssim K(\mathbf{u}_{C})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\frac{|u_{Ck}|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Let
\mathbf{v}
be defined as in (
4.33
). For any
R>0
, using a change of variables yields, for each
k=1,\ldots,l
,

\begin{split}\int_{|x|<R}|u_{Ck}(t,x)|^{2}dx&=\lambda(t)^{-2}\int_{B(0,\epsilon R\lambda(t)}|v_{k}(t,z)|^{2}dz\\
&\quad+\lambda(t)^{-2}\int_{B(0,R\lambda(t))\backslash B(0,\epsilon R\lambda(t))}|v_{k}(t,z)|^{2}dz\\
&:=A+B\end{split}

Moreover, by Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding

A\leq\lambda(t)^{-2}(\epsilon R\lambda(t))^{2}\|v_{k}\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}}^{2}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}R^{2}K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),

which can be controlled by choosing
\epsilon>0
small enough. Also, by (
4.37
) and the pre-compactness of
\mathcal{K}
, we have

B\leq R^{2}\|v_{k}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(|x|\geq\epsilon R\lambda(t))}^{2}\rightarrow 0,\quad\hbox{as }t\rightarrow T_{C}^{+}.

Therefore, for all
R>0
,

(4.39)

\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{|x|<R}|u_{Ck}|^{2}dx\rightarrow 0\quad\hbox{as }t\rightarrow T_{C}^{+},

which implies that
\lim_{t\to T_{C}^{+}}X_{R}(t)=0
.
Therefore, in view of the (
4.38
), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus implies

X_{R}(0)\lesssim T_{C}^{+}K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\quad\mbox{and}\quad X_{R}(t)\lesssim(T_{C}^{+}-t)K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Therefore, making
R\rightarrow\infty
we obtain that
\mathbf{u}_{C}(t)\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for all
t\in[0,T_{C}^{+})
, and
\|\mathbf{u}_{C}(t)\|_{\mathbf{L}_{2}}^{2}\lesssim(T_{C}^{+}-t)K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
. Finally, by mass conservation, we must have
\mathbf{u}_{C}\equiv 0
which contradicts (
4.27
). ∎

We now finalize the contradiction argument by showing that the solution
\mathbf{u}_{C}
cannot exist. To this end, we note that precompactness of the flow, modulo the invariances of the equation, occurs only for the trivial solution.

Proposition 4.16
.

Suppose
E_{C}<\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
and let
\mathbf{u}_{C}
be the solution established in Theorem
4.12
. Then
\mathbf{u}_{C}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that, using Sobolev embedding and the precompactness of
\mathcal{K}
, for each
\epsilon>0
there exists
R(\epsilon)>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

(4.40)

\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{|x|>R(\epsilon)}|\nabla u_{Ck}|^{2}+|u_{Ck}|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}\leq\epsilon.

Let
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a radial function such that
\phi(x)=|x|^{2}
for
|x|\leq 1
and
\phi(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 2
. Define

V_{R}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}|u_{Ck}|^{2}R^{2}\phi\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)dx.

Then, using (
4.27
), Hardy’s inequality, and Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

(4.41)

|V^{\prime}_{R}(t)|\lesssim 2R\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\mathrm{Im}\int\left|\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)\bar{u}_{ck}\nabla u_{Ck}dx\right|\lesssim R^{2}K(\mathbf{u}_{C})\lesssim R^{2},

for all
t\geq 0
and
R>0
. Moreover, applying Lemma
2.3
and noting that for
|x|\leq R
we have
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\phi(x)=2\delta_{ij}
,
\Delta\phi(x)=2d
, and
\Delta^{2}\phi(x)=0
, we deduce

(4.42)

\begin{split}V_{R}^{\prime\prime}(t)&=4\sum_{1\leq j,m\leq d}\mathrm{Re}\int\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{m}\partial x_{j}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\partial_{x_{j}}\bar{u}_{ck}\partial_{x_{m}}u_{Ck}\right]dx\\
&\quad-\int\Delta^{2}\phi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|u_{Ck}|^{2}\right)dx-\frac{8}{d-2}\int\Delta\phi F(\mathbf{u}_{C})dx+8\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{|x|>R}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{Ck}|^{2}dx\\
&\quad-8\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{|x|>R}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{Ck}|^{2}dx+\frac{16d}{d-2}\int_{|x|>R}F(\mathbf{u}_{C})dx-\frac{16d}{d-2}\int_{|x|>R}F(\mathbf{u}_{C})dx\\
&=8\left[K(\mathbf{u}_{C})-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}_{C})\right]+O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\int_{R\leq|x|\leq 2R(\epsilon)}|\nabla u_{Ck}|^{2}+|u_{Ck}|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}\right).\\
\end{split}

Observe that by selecting
R=R(\epsilon)
, we can control the last term using (
4.40
). Hence, by choosing
\epsilon
sufficiently small and applying conservation of energy, Lemma
4.8
, and Lemma
4.9
, we obtain

(4.43)

V_{R}^{\prime\prime}(t)\gtrsim K(\mathbf{u}_{C})\gtrsim\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{C0}).

Integrating in
t
and using (
4.41
) along with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we obtain

t\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{C0})\lesssim V_{R}^{\prime}(t)-V_{R}^{\prime}(0)\lesssim|V_{R}^{\prime}(t)-V_{R}^{\prime}(0)|\lesssim R^{2}(\epsilon).

Dividing both sides by
t>0
and letting
t\to\infty
, it follows that
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{C0})=0
. By conservation of energy, this implies
\mathbf{u}_{C}\equiv 0
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our global well-posedness and scattering result.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By Theorem
4.16
, it follows that
E_{C}=\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
. Let
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be radial and satisfy (
1.22
) and (
1.23
), thus the corresponding solution is global with
\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|u_{k}\|_{S(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
. For the scattering part, observe that, by applying Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
2.2
, we obtain that

\left\|\int_{t}^{\infty}U_{k}(t-s)f_{k}(\mathbf{u})ds\right\|_{\dot{\mathbf{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\rightarrow 0\quad\hbox{as }t\rightarrow\infty.

Then, for each
k=1,\ldots,l
, if we define

u_{k}(t)=U_{k}(t-a)u_{k}(0)+\int_{a}^{t}U_{k}(t-s)f_{k}(\mathbf{u})ds

and

u_{k}^{+}=U_{k}(-a)u_{k}(0)+\int_{a}^{\infty}U_{k}(-s)f_{k}(\mathbf{u})ds,

we obtain the desired result.
∎

5.
Blow-up in finite time

To begin, we note that the well-posedness theory in
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
stated in Theorem
1.7
can be established using an argument similar to that of Theorem
1.3
, with the addition of the norm
\mathbf{X}(I)
in the definition of the ball for the contraction mapping argument (see
[
26
, Theorem 2.5]
). Moreover, if the initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is radially symmetric, then the corresponding solution
\mathbf{u}
, defined on its maximal existence interval
I
, is also radially symmetric.

As mentioned in the Introduction, we follow the strategy from
[
24
]
and work with the function
\mathcal{R}
defined in (
1.26
). Furthermore, if we assume
\phi
is radially symmetric, a simple calculation (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Lemma 2.9]
) allows us to express
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)
as

(5.1)

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)=4\int\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx-\int\Delta^{2}\phi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx-\frac{8}{d-2}\mathrm{Re}\int\Delta\phi F(\mathbf{u})dx.

Now, we introduce the functional

\tau(\mathbf{u}(t))=K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2d}{d-2}P(\mathbf{u}(t)).

This functional is often referred to as the "Pohozaev" functional due to its close connection with the Pohozaev identities (see
[
38
, Lemma 4.3]
). By employing the energy definitions in (
1.5
), we can rewrite

(5.2)

\tau(\mathbf{u}(t))=\frac{d}{d-2}E(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{2}{d-2}K(\mathbf{u}(t))-\frac{d}{d-2}L(\mathbf{u}(t))

where
L(\mathbf{u})
is defined in (
1.6
). The following lemma establishes a key property of the Pohozaev functional, namely that it is strictly negative for solutions of (
1.1
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.8
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\tau(\mathbf{u}(t))\leq-\delta<0,\quad t\in I.

Proof.

We adopt the approach from
[
13
, Theorem 1.3]
. We begin by observing that (
3.3
) and (
3.1
) imply

(5.3)

K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=\frac{d}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Moreover, from the sharp inequality (
3.24
) and conservation of the energy, we have

(5.4)

K(\mathbf{u})=E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-L(\mathbf{u})+2P(\mathbf{u})\leq E(\mathbf{u}_{0})+2|P(\mathbf{u})|\leq E(\mathbf{u}_{0})+2C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u})^{\frac{d}{d-2}}.\\

Define
f(r)=E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-r+2C_{\rm opt}r^{\frac{d}{d-2}}
for
r>0
, and let
G(t)=K(\mathbf{u}(t))
. From (
5.4
), it follows that

f\circ G(t)=E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-K(\mathbf{u}(t))+2C_{\rm opt}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{\frac{d}{d-2}}\geq 0.

Applying Lemma
2.9
with
\delta=0
,
a=E(\mathbf{u}_{0})
,
b=2C_{\rm opt}
,
q=\frac{d}{d-2}
and
\gamma=\left(\frac{2C_{\rm opt}d}{d-2}\right)^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}
, from (
3.25
), we obtain

a<\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\gamma\Leftrightarrow E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),

and, from relation (
5.3
), it follows that

G(0)>\gamma\Leftrightarrow K(\mathbf{u}_{0})>\frac{d}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Hence, considering (
1.24
) and (
1.25
), we can apply Lemma
2.9
to obtain

(5.5)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))>K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}),\quad\quad t\in I.

The assumption (
1.24
), combined with the conservation of energy and (
5.3
), yield

\frac{d}{2}E(\mathbf{u}(t))=\frac{d}{2}E(\mathbf{u}_{0})<\frac{d}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})=K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})<K(\mathbf{u}(t)),\quad t\in I

and consequently, from (
5.2
),

\tau(\mathbf{u}(t))<0,\quad t\in I.

Now, let us show that there is
\theta>0
, such that

(5.6)

\tau(\mathbf{u}(t))<-\theta K(\mathbf{u}(t)),\quad t\in I.

Indeed, if
E(\mathbf{u}_{0})\leq 0
the result follows immediately. On the other hand, suppose
E(\mathbf{u}_{0})>0
and that (
5.6
) does not hold. Then, there exist sequences
(t_{m})\subset I
and
\theta_{m}\rightarrow 0
such that

\frac{-\theta_{m}}{d-2}K(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))\leq\tau(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))<0,

which implies

E(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))=\frac{d-2}{d}\tau(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))+\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))+L(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))\geq(1-\theta_{m})\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t_{m})).\\

Using the conservation of energy again, (
5.3
) and (
5.5
) imply that

E(\mathbf{u}_{0})=E(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))\geq(1-\theta_{m})\frac{2}{d}K(\mathbf{u}(t_{m}))>(1-\theta_{m})\frac{2}{d}K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\geq(1-\theta_{m})\mathcal{E}(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}).

Taking the limit as
m\rightarrow\infty
leads to a contradiction with (
1.24
). Therefore, the result follows from (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) by setting
\delta=\theta K(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})
.
∎

Another tool to prove Theorem
1.8
is the following straightforward result. Since its proof involves a direct computation, we omit the details. For a similar statement in dimension 4, the reader may refer to
[
22
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some constant
c>0
, define
\chi(x)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
a radial function satisfying

\chi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|x|^{2},&0\leq|x|\leq 1,\\
c,&|x|\geq 3.\end{array}\right.

Assume also that
\chi^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 2
and
0\leq\chi^{\prime}(x)\leq 2r
, for all
r\geq 0
. Let
\chi_{R}(x)=R^{2}\chi(x/R)
. Then

(i)

If
|x|\leq R
,

(5.7)

\Delta\chi_{R}(x)=2d\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Delta^{2}\chi_{R}(x)=0.

(i)

If
|x|\geq R
,

(5.8)

\Delta\chi_{R}(x)\leq C\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\Delta^{2}\chi_{R}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}},

where
C
is a constant independent of
R
.

Finally, we prove our blow-up result.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let
I=(T^{-},T^{+})
be the maximal lifespan interval of
\mathbf{u}
. We will prove only that
T^{+}<\infty
, since the case
T^{-}>-\infty
can be handled in a similar manner. By choosing
\phi(x)=\chi_{R}(x)
as defined in Lemma
5.2
and using (
1.26
) and (
5.1
), we obtain

\mathcal{R}(t)=2\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathrm{Im}\int\alpha_{k}\nabla\chi_{R}\cdot\nabla u_{k}\bar{u}_{k}dx

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)&=8\tau(\mathbf{u})+4\int(\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}-2)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|\nabla u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx-\int\Delta^{2}\chi_{R}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx\\
&\quad-\frac{8}{d-2}\hbox{Re}\int(\Delta\chi_{R}-2d)F(\mathbf{u})dx\\
&=:8\tau(\mathbf{u})+\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{3}(t).\end{split}

By construction, since
\chi_{R}^{\prime\prime}\leq 2
for all
r\geq 0
, it follows that
\mathcal{R}_{1}\leq 0
. Then, applying mass conservation together with (
5.7
)–(
5.8
), we obtain

\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)\leq\int|\Delta^{2}\chi_{R}|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx\leq CR^{-2}\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)dx\leq CR^{-2}Q(\mathbf{u}_{0}).

Moreover, from (
5.7
) and Lemma
2.3
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{3}&=-\frac{8}{d-2}\hbox{Re}\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}(\Delta\chi_{R}-2d)F(\mathbf{u})dx\\
&\leq C\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}|\mathrm{Re}F(\mathbf{u})|dx\\
&\leq C\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}\sum_{k=1}^{l}|u_{k}|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}dx\\
&=C\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(|x|\geq R)}^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}.\end{split}

Next we recall the radial the radial Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (see, for instance,
[
45
, page 323]
)

\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}|f|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}\leq CR^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-2}}\|f\|^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-2}}_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}^{\frac{2}{d-2}}.

Then, for
d>3
and
\epsilon>0
, by Young’s inequality, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{3}&\leq C\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{l}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-2}}\|u_{k}\|^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-2}}_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(|x|\geq R)}^{\frac{2}{d-2}}\\
&\leq C_{\epsilon}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}\left(\frac{1}{d-2}\right)M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}+\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{d-2}\right)K(\mathbf{u}),\\
\end{split}

where
C_{\epsilon}
is a constant depending on
\epsilon
,
\alpha_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
. Also note, from (
5.2
), that

\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{d-2}\right)K(\mathbf{u})\leq\frac{d}{d-2}\epsilon E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-{\epsilon}\tau(\mathbf{u}).

Putting together the above estimates, we obtain

(5.9)

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq\left(8-\epsilon\right)\tau(\mathbf{u})+CR^{-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\epsilon}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}\frac{1}{d-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}+\frac{d}{d-2}\epsilon|E(\mathbf{u}_{0})|.

Therefore, for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, Lemma
5.1
yields

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\left(8-\epsilon\right)\delta+CR^{-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\epsilon}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}+\frac{d}{d-2}\epsilon|E(\mathbf{u}_{0})|.

Therefore, by fixing
R
large enough and then
\epsilon
small enough, we obtain
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq-2\delta
. Integrating this inequality over
[0,t)
leads to

(5.10)

\mathcal{R}(t)\leq-2\delta t+\mathcal{R}(0).

For
d=3
, we have the estimate

\mathcal{R}_{3}\leq CR^{-4}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{2}K(\mathbf{u}),

which, by following the same argument, yields

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\left(8-{CR^{-4}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{2}}\right)\delta+CR^{-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})+CR^{-4}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})|E(\mathbf{u}_{0})|,

and this also implies (
5.10
) provided that
R
is chosen sufficiently large.

On the other hand, by Hölder’s inequality,

(5.11)

\begin{split}|\mathcal{R}(t)|&\leq 2R\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\int|\chi^{\prime}(|x|/R)||\nabla u_{k}||u_{k}|dx\\
&\leq CR\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}(\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L^{2}})\\
&\leq CRM(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{1/2}K(\mathbf{u}(t))^{1/2}.\\
\end{split}

By combining (
5.10
) with the fact that
\mathcal{R}(0)/\delta<T_{0}
can be ensured by taking
T_{0}
sufficiently large, we conclude that

(5.12)

\mathcal{R}(t)\leq-\delta t<0,\quad t\geq T_{0}.

Thus, by applying (
5.11
) and (
5.12
), we deduce that

\delta t\leq-\mathcal{R}(t)=|\mathcal{R}(t)|\leq CRM(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{1/2}K(\mathbf{u})^{1/2},

which implies that, for some positive constant
C_{0}
,

(5.13)

K(\mathbf{u}(t))\geq C_{0}t^{2},\quad t\geq T_{0}.

Now, since
\epsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily small, it follows from (
5.9
) and (
5.2
) that

(5.14)

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq\left(\frac{8d}{d-2}\right)E(\mathbf{u}_{0})-\left(\frac{15}{d-2}\right)K(\mathbf{u})+CR^{-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\epsilon}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}},

where we have once again used the conservation of energy and the fact that
L(\mathbf{u})\geq 0
. Now, we can choose
T_{1}>T_{0}
such that

C_{0}\left(\frac{15}{2(d-2)}\right)T_{1}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{8d}{d-2}\right)E(\mathbf{u}_{0})+CR^{-2}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})+C_{\epsilon}R^{-\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}M(\mathbf{u}_{0})^{\frac{2(d-1)}{d-3}}.

From (
5.13
) and (
5.14
), we obtain
