Safety and efficacy profile of S-005151 (Redasemtide), in patients with chronic liver diseases: phase 2 trial
Authors: Atsunori Tsuchiya, Yusuke Watanabe, Naruhiro Kimura, Hiroyuki Abe, Toru Setsu, Takeshi Yokoo, Hiroteru Kamimura, Akira Sakamaki, Hiroaki Saito, Masaki Tominaga, Tatsuhiko Sato, Nobutaka Kitamura, Takahiro Tanaka, Yoshitomi Kanemitsu, Masahiro Ishizawa, Haruna Miyazawa, Mototsugu Tanaka, Hiroyuki Ishikawa, Kenichi Harada, Yoshihiko Tomita, Katsuto Tamai, Shuji Terai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsCirrhosis is a disease of impaired liver function and fibrosis caused by long-term liver damage. However, to date, no drugs have been approved to improve liver fibrosis. We report the results of a phase 2 study of S-005151 (generic name: Redasemtide), a partial peptide of High-Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), in patients with chronic liver disease.Approach and resultsThis single-center, non-randomized, single-arm, open-label study was performed in Japan in patients with chronic liver disease (cohort A: 5 patients, cohort B: 5 patients) caused by HCV, HBV, MASH, or alcohol, with MR elastography of ≥ 4 kPa and Child–Pugh score up to 7 points. The primary endpoint was safety; secondary endpoints were efficacy against liver injury, function, and fibrosis.One adverse event (dysphonia) was observed in cohort A and one (fever) in cohort B, both of which were mild drug-related adverse events. S-005151 was well-tolerated. Regarding efficacy, there was a trend toward improvement post-treatment, with a decrease in transaminase and improvement in tissue inflammation scores in some cases; however, there was no significant improvement in hepatic dysfunction. Regarding liver fibrosis, there was a rapid and stable decrease in serum type IV collagen 7S levels, improvement in MR elastography findings, and an increase in platelet counts in some cases; 5 of 10 patients showed a trend toward improvement in liver fibrosis.ConclusionsS-005151 is well-tolerated in patients with chronic liver disease and may have therapeutic effects, in reducing liver damage and improving liver fibrosis.Trial registrationjRCT, jRCT 2031200232, Registered 4 December 2020 (https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/latest-detail/jRCT2031200232).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41232-026-00421-9.

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a disease associated with chronic liver damage that results in fibrosis, impaired liver function, and the development of liver cancer. The most common causes of cirrhosis worldwide are viral hepatitis, alcohol consumption, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Furthermore, 10.6 million cases of decompensated cirrhosis and 112 million cases of compensated cirrhosis, respectively, are recorded each year [1, 2].

As cirrhosis progresses, liver function declines, and portal hypertension, jaundice, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and infection rates increase. In 2019, cirrhosis was associated with 2.4% of deaths worldwide [3]. Currently, liver transplantation is the only way to save the lives of patients with advanced disease, but there is a shortage of donors, major surgery is required, and life-long immunosuppressive therapy is needed [4]. Therefore, new drugs to ameliorate cirrhosis are expected [5–9].

S-005151, a partial peptide of High-Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), is a candidate for the treatment of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis [10, 11]. Regarding HMGB1, numerous studies have reported that it functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) in the setting of liver injury. However, HMGB1 has two opposite roles, which can be induced by box A and box B [12]. Box A of HMGB1 blocks the spread of inflammation to the surrounding tissues and ameliorates fibrosis. However, box B induces tissue remodeling and activates inflammatory reactions by binding to toll-like receptors (TLRs) and/or receptors for advanced glycation end products on the surface of target cells [13]. Circulating HMGB1 has been shown to stimulate bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), mobilizing them into the peripheral blood and promoting their recruitment to necrotic tissues. Furthermore, MSCs mobilized by HMGB1 and accumulated at sites of tissue necrosis have been shown to suppress inflammatory responses while exerting differentiation potential not only toward mesenchymal lineages but also toward ectodermal epithelial tissues, thereby contributing to tissue regeneration [12, 14]. We have shown that S-005151, which is derived from box A of HMGB1, reduces liver damage and fibrosis in a cirrhotic mouse model [10]. Furthermore, S-005151 improves steatosis, liver injury, and fibrosis in MASH model mice in the short term and suppresses the development of liver tumors in the long term [11]. The precise biological activity of this agent has not yet been fully elucidated. However, in animal experiments, we have recently performed spatial transcriptomic analyses in a mouse model of liver injury and demonstrated several notable changes, including a reduction in activated hepatic stellate cells, a shift of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and alterations related to hepatocellular lipid metabolism [15]. Based on these potential synergistic effects, we hypothesized that this agent could exert therapeutic benefits in chronic liver disease. We, therefore, aimed to utilize S-005151 in this study to confirm its safety and estimate its potential efficacy in patients with chronic liver disease, particularly those with compensated cirrhosis.

Materials and methods

Study oversight

All study-related documents were designed by the trial team of the Division of Gastroenterology and Hepatology of Niigata University and the Clinical and Translational Research Center of Niigata University Medical and Dental Hospital, Niigata, Japan. This trial was approved by Niigata University Medical and Dental Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB reference number: I020-003) and registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (registry number: jRCT2031200232). This investigator-initiated clinical trial was funded by Shionogi & Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan), and complied with the study protocol (S-005151CLD) and the Japanese Good Clinical Practice guidelines. In conducting the clinical trials, the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed, and the human rights, welfare, and safety of the patients were secured to the maximum extent possible. In addition, documents or records pertaining to the clinical trial were preserved and are appropriately maintained. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All authors had access to the study data and have reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Study design

This was a single-center, non-randomized, single-arm, open-label study of patients with chronic liver disease and comprised two cohorts (A and B), with a target number of 10 patients (5 in each cohort) to undergo treatment (Fig. 1). Pre-observation period (84 days)

Patients whose eligibility for the study was confirmed at pre-test 1, equivalent to day − 84, and who were determined to be eligible, were provisionally enrolled and followed up for 84 days to obtain patient information and check the transition of liver disease status and living conditions. During this pre-observation period, patients were mainly instructed on their living conditions every 4 weeks. There is no definitive evidence-based rationale for selecting an 84-day period, as clinical trials in patients with cirrhosis remain limited. However, many patients with a clinical condition similar to those enrolled in the present study are routinely followed at approximately 84-day intervals in real-world clinical practice. Therefore, we adopted this timeframe to evaluate changes over a clinically relevant and practical observation period.

Treatment period (28 days; 4 weeks)

At the end of the pre-observation period, eligibility was again confirmed (pre-test 2: equivalent to day 1); once the patients were determined to be eligible, they were enrolled in the main study and started receiving the study drug. Safety evaluations were conducted weekly throughout the treatment period, and drugs were administered as follows: cohort A (five cases), treatment period weeks 1–4: 1 dose/week (4 doses total); cohort B (five cases), treatment period, week 1: 1 dose/day, 4 days in a row; weeks 2–4: 1 dose/week (7 doses total), each dose of 1.5 mg/kg (weight equivalent) of S-005151 was administered once daily by intravenous administration over 90 min, according to body weight before enrollment. In the non-clinical pharmacology study using the CCl₄-induced liver injury model, the investigational product was administered a total of eight times (twice weekly for 4 weeks) [10]. Based on this background, and considering feasibility in real-world clinical practice, the dosing schedule for cohort A was designed as once weekly administration for 4 weeks, which we considered to be a practical and clinically achievable regimen. For cohort B, the dosing schedule was informed by an ongoing clinical development program in patients with recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, in which the first week consisted of four consecutive days of administration. Based on this experience, cohort B was designed to explore, in an exploratory manner, the potential necessity and impact of initial consecutive dosing by administering the investigational product on consecutive days during the first week. Regarding dose selection, the maximum dose that had previously been confirmed to be tolerable in elderly individuals was 1.5 mg/kg. Therefore, 1.5 mg/kg was selected as the dose for both cohorts in the present study. The study schedule consisted of the following steps and was common to each cohort.

Follow-up period (140 days)

Safety and efficacy were evaluated every 4 weeks after completion of study drug administration.

Participants

The trial was conducted from 23 December 2020 (date of first patient consent) and 17 November 2022 (date of last patient observation, including follow-up).

Inclusion criteria

At the time of provisional registration (pre-test 1), equivalent to day − 84, after obtaining patient background information, we included patients meeting the following criteria: (1) male or female patients aged 20–80 years; (2) patients who were able to understand the study and provide written consent to participate of their own free will before examination; (3) patients with any of the following chronic liver diseases or a history of chronic liver disease: alcoholic steatohepatitis who abstained from alcohol for at least 6 months, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis who have been on exercise or diet therapy for at least 6 months, chronic hepatitis B who tested negative for HBV DNA for at least 6 months on nucleic acid analog therapy, and patients with chronic hepatitis C who had achieved sustained virologic response (SVR) with antiviral therapy for at least 12 months; (4) patients with a Child–Pugh score of ≤ 7 at pre-test 1; and (5) patients whose liver stiffness on magnetic resonance (MR) elastography was ≥ 4 kPa in pre-test 1 (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Target patients (A) and effectiveness evaluation implementation schedule (B). At the time of full registration (pre-test 2: equivalent to day 1), (1) patients with any of the following chronic liver diseases or a history of chronic liver disease: alcoholic steatohepatitis and abstained from alcohol for at least 6 months prior to the pre-test, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis who had been on exercise or diet therapy for at least 6 months prior to the pre-test, chronic hepatitis B who were continuously negative for HBV DNA for at least 6 months prior to the baseline test with nucleic acid analogs, and chronic hepatitis C who had achieved SVR with antiviral therapy for at least 12 months prior to pre-test 1; (2) patients whose Child–Pugh score fluctuated within 1 point between pre-test 1 and 2; (3) patients whose liver stiffness result on MR elastography was ≥ 4 kPa in pre-test 2; and (4) patients who underwent liver biopsy within the past 6 months from the date of enrollment were included.

The following inclusion criterion was common to pre-tests 1 and 2: (female subjects only) patients who agreed to use contraceptive methods from the time of consent until 3 months after the last dose.

Exclusion criteria

At the time of provisional registration, pre-test (1), equivalent to day − 84, we excluded those (1) patients scheduled to concomitantly use banned drugs or therapies during the clinical trial period; (2) who received regenerative medicine (regenerative medicine products such as cell engineering products, cell therapy drugs, gene therapy drugs, and gene therapy products) in the past; (3) who previously received S-005151; (4) who received any other investigational drug within 90 days of obtaining consent; and (5) female patients who were pregnant or intended to become pregnant during the study period, lactating female patients, or male patients whose partners intended to become pregnant.

Common to both provisional and full registration (pre-test 1 and 2), we excluded patients (1) with autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis or primary sclerosing cholangitis, or liver transplantations; (2) with hepatic encephalopathy; (3) with recurrent infections and fever; (4) with moderate or severe ascites; (5) with gastroesophageal varices requiring treatment during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed within the past 6 months from the date of consent; (6) who underwent interferon therapy within 6 months prior to obtaining consent; (7) who were receiving anticoagulants; and (8) with active malignancy (including a history of cancer with a disease-free period of ≤ 5 years; however, lesions equivalent to intraepithelial or intramucosal carcinomas that were treated endoscopically and were deemed curable were not included in active malignancies); (9) with severe cardiovascular, renal, respiratory, or hematologic diseases that the investigator or sub-investigator considered inappropriate for this study; (10) with or with a history of severe drug allergy (shock, anaphylaxis, angioedema) or with a history of severe allergic disease; and (11) patients deemed inappropriate for this study by the investigator or sub-investigator.

Study assessment

Safety

The occurrence and frequency of adverse events, vital signs, electrocardiograms, and laboratory test results were assessed. Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. The relatedness of each adverse event to the study drug was assessed by the investigators based on clinical judgment, taking into account the temporal relationship between study drug administration and the onset of the event, the presence of alternative etiologies (including underlying disease or concomitant medications), the clinical course after continuation or discontinuation of the study drug, and the biological plausibility of a causal relationship. Causality was categorized according to standard pharmacovigilance practice (e.g., related, possibly related, or unrelated). This study was designed primarily as a safety-focused, exploratory trial to estimate potential efficacy signals and to inform the selection of appropriate evaluation strategies for subsequent, larger-scale clinical studies.

Efficacy

The following were evaluated in an exploratory manner. We assessed whether post-treatment median values improved relative to the baseline (day 1) median values. By observing various perspectives, we were able to evaluate treatment effects comprehensively and on a case-by-case basis and to consider which items can be effectively used for evaluation when moving to the next phase of treatment.

Schedule of patient visits

Patients visited the hospital monthly during the pre-treatment and follow-up periods, and weekly during the treatment period, for blood tests. Liver stiffness measurements such as MR elastography (MRE), US elastography, and FibroScan were performed approximately every 12 weeks. Liver biopsies were performed before S-005151 administration and at the end of the trial period (Fig. 2B).

Liver biopsy and evaluation

Percutaneous liver biopsy was performed using a 17-G needle (MAJIMA needle, Top. Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with a LOGIQ S8 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Liver biopsies were performed at two time points: prior to treatment and within 7 days after completion of the study, and the results were compared. The biopsy was performed on the right lobe of the liver, using the following technique: the MAJIMA needle was inserted into the liver; the inner needle was pulled out and the stylet was inserted; and negative pressure was produced by aspiration with a syringe. Under these aspiration conditions, the needle was turned 2–3 times to ensure that the tissue was aspirated, and the needle was removed to complete the procedure. The tissue was immediately fixed in a container containing 10% formalin and a paraffin block was prepared. Tissues cut at a thickness of 4 μm were subjected to hematoxylin–eosin (HE), Azan, and Elastica van Gieson (EVG) staining, and the necroinflammatory score of the modified Histology Activity Index (HAI) was calculated. Sirius Red staining was performed, the fibrosis area ratio was calculated, and the stage of fibrosis was determined [16, 17]. One liver pathologist performed a summary evaluation at the conclusion of the cohort A and B trials.

FibroScan

Vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) was measured using a LOGIQ S8 (GE healthcare) integrated with a FibroScan. The right lobe of the liver was identified beforehand, and all cases were measured with the M-probe until 10 valid measurements were obtained. The median of the 10 valid measurements (to the first decimal place) was used as the liver stiffness by VCTE in this trial. Measurements were performed at a different location when the number of unsuccessful measurements exceeded 40%.

US elastography

Two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) was performed using a LOGIQ S8 (GE Healthcare). A 10-mm diameter region of interest (ROI) was established 1–6 cm from the liver surface, avoiding vessels and masses, and the right lobe was observed intercostally to measure liver stiffness from six sites: three anterior and three posterior regions. The median value (to the first decimal place) of the six measurements was adopted as the liver stiffness using 2D-SWE.

MR elastography (MRE)

MRE was performed using a 3.0-T clinical whole body MR scanner (Discovery 750w; GE Healthcare) and a 32-element phased-array coil. A passive driver was positioned on the right rib cage at the xiphoid process level and attached to a waveform generator. Four slices of MRE were obtained, including the level of the porta hepatis during 16 s of breath-holding. The parameters of 2D spin-echo echo-planar-MRE sequence were as follows: TE, 59.2 ms; TR, 1000 ms; slice thickness, 10 mm; field-of-view, 42 × 42 cm; matrix, 64 × 64; scan plane, axial; magnetization encoding gradients frequency, 80 Hz; driver frequency, 60 Hz; driver amplitude, 50%. The driver amplitude was set to 30% for weights below 45 kg and 70% for weights above 75 kg. Wave images and a stiffness map with crosshatching were generated automatically on the operating console.

The ROI was placed on the right lobe, avoiding the lateral area just below the heart that is affected by the heartbeat. A slice in which the right lobe appeared large on the stiffness map was selected, referring to the region of uniform waves in the wave image. To avoid lesions, large intrahepatic vessels, and crosshatching (an “X” on the stiffness map that automatically indicates areas unsuitable for measurement based on signal-to-noise ratio and wave parallelism) as much as possible, a freehand ROI was drawn on the stiffness map. The ROI was surrounded as much as possible by a freehand drawing on a stiffness map. To avoid edge effects (i.e., reflections and partial volume effects), measurements were taken 1 cm inside the liver contour. Liver stiffness by MRE was measured by two experienced abdominal radiologists.

Preparation of S-005151

The investigational drug S-005151 (redasemtide trifluoroacetate) was manufactured by Shionogi & Co., Ltd. S-005151 was a lyophilized white powder formulation containing 25 mg S-005151 per vial. Ten S-005151 vials were packaged and labeled with the development number (S-005151), dosage form, content of the active ingredient, contents, serial number, date of manufacture, storage method, indication that the product was for investigational use, and the name, title, and address of the coordinating investigator.

S-005151 was stored at 2 °C–8 °C in a light-shielded and sealed container. The investigational drug provider provided the investigational drug to a manager appointed by the head of the site in accordance with an agreement between the investigational drug provider and the site. The investigational drug manager stored and managed the investigational drug in accordance with the “Procedures for the Management of Investigational Drugs.”

For adjustment, 5 mL of saline solution was added to one vial of the investigational drug and dissolved thoroughly by shaking the vial to prevent foaming. The concentration of S-005151 after dissolution was 5 mg/ml. The required amount of S-005151 was determined by body weight measured before day 1 of administration. The required amount of dissolved solution was drawn off to obtain 1.5 mg/kg and mixed with saline to obtain a final concentration of 0.1 to 2.7 mg/mL. S-005151 was promptly administered after preparation. If the solution was not administered promptly, the prepared saline bag was stored at 2–8 °C and administered in 90 min within 24 h. The investigational drug was then promptly administered after confirming that it had reached room temperature.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were performed at Niigata University after database lock. For the safety endpoints, the frequency and rate of adverse events that occurred after the start of study drug administration were summarized for each cohort. The numbers of subjects who experienced serious adverse events, adverse events leading to discontinuation of the study drug, adverse events with predictable or known predictability, and adverse events (AEs) for which a causal relationship with the study drug could not be ruled out were summarized in the same manner. For the efficacy endpoints, as a rule, summary statistics were calculated according to data type. For items observed as continuous variables, the number of cases, arithmetic mean, standard deviation, and minimum, median, and maximum values were calculated. For items observed as discrete variables, the number of cases and percentages for each category were calculated. To explore the multidimensional structure of laboratory variables and to facilitate interpretation of treatment effects, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. To quantitatively evaluate temporal changes in liver injury and fibrosis after treatment, the daily effect size was calculated for ALT, type IV collagen 7S, and MRE. The mean ± standard error (SE) of the daily effect size and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each parameter over the predefined observation periods. To estimate treatment efficacy by comparing pre- and post-treatment changes, patients were classified into the “improved or remained unchanged” group and the “worsened” group based on pre-treatment trends. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was applied using each laboratory parameter measured from day − 84 to day 1 before treatment as the dependent variable and time (days) as the independent variable. Patients with a positive regression coefficient for days were classified into the worsened group, whereas those with a negative or zero coefficient were classified into the improved or remained unchanged group.

Due to the exploratory nature of this trial and the small sample size, no confirmatory statistical hypothesis testing was performed. Data are presented in tables according to cohort and subject. All analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Trial population, baseline, and treatment characteristics

Of the 18 provisionally registered cases, 10 were enrolled in the study, excluding four cases of pre-enrollment ineligibility, one of withdrawal of consent, and three of discontinuation and dropout before the start of treatment (Fig. 1). The 10 patients enrolled in the study consisted of five from cohort A (A-1–A-5) and five from cohort B (B-1-B-5). There were no cases of discontinuation or dropout, and all procedures were completed. Four males and six females aged 56–74 years with hepatic involvement due to the hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, alcohol, or MASH participated in the trial. Only one patient in cohort A had Child–Pugh Grade B (score 7), while the others had Child–Pugh Grade A (score 5, eight patients; score 6, one patient). Safety outcomes

Adverse events occurred in 3/5 (60%) patients in cohort A, 3/5 (60%) patients in cohort B, totaling 10 cases (cohort A + cohort B). Adverse events in cohort A (five patients) included one case of fever (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), chills (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), gingival abscess (moderate, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 2, recovery), conjunctivitis (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), dysphonia (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), and bleeding after liver biopsy (severe, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 3, recovery). Adverse events in cohort B (five patients) included one case of fever (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), epigastric pain (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery), gingival swelling (moderate, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 2, recovery), and contact dermatitis (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery).

Serious adverse events occurred in 1/5 (20%) patients in cohort A and 0/5 (0%) patients in cohort B. This adverse event was one case of post-liver biopsy hemorrhage, which occurred during the follow-up period, but the patient recovered without treatment and the event was not causally related to the study drug.

Adverse drug reactions related to drug administration occurred in 1/5 (20%) patients in cohort A and 1/5 (20%) patients in cohort B. Adverse drug reactions in cohort A included one patient with dysphonia (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery). Cohort B had one case of fever (mild, CTCAE v5.0 toxicity grade 1, recovery). Adverse drug reactions related to drug administration were transient and resolved quickly on the day of administration (Supplementary Table S2).

Secondary outcomes

We evaluated parameters related to liver injury, liver function, and fibrosis. In addition, the following data were compared with data from the first day of administration (day 1). First, the evaluation of liver injury showed a decreasing trend in AST and ALT levels (Supplementary Fig. S1). There were no cases of worsening in the pre-/post-comparison of the modified HAI necroinflammatory scores of the liver biopsy tissue. Four of 5 patients in cohort A improved by 1 point, and 2 of 5 patients in cohort B improved by 1 and 2 points, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S7).

As for liver function, the ALBI score, MELD score, and Child–Pugh score did not appear to have changed substantially, possibly because many patients did not have poor liver function initially (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Finally, for liver fibrosis, serum markers P-III-P, type IV collagen-7S, hyaluronic acid (Supplementary Fig. S2), M2BPGi, and ATX showed a decreasing trend. In particular, type IV collagen-7S showed a remarkable decline post-treatment. Formulae to assess fibrosis were calculated using FIB-4 and APRI, both of which showed an improving trend (Supplementary Fig. S3). FibroScan (VCTE), US elastography, and MRE also showed a decreasing trend (Supplementary Fig. S4). Liver biopsy assessments of the fiber area with Sirius Red staining showed a decreasing trend, while fibrosis-stage assessment showed improvement in 3/5 cases in cohort A and 1/5 cases in cohort B (Supplementary Fig. S7B). A decreasing trend in IgG and an increasing trend in Plt were also observed (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Next, to more clearly demonstrate treatment effects, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on 14 promising laboratory parameters (AST, ALT, IgG, platelet, P-III-P, IV collagen-7S, hyaluronic acid, M2BPGi, ATX, FIB-4, APRI, MRE, VCTE, 2D-SWE) measured at day 1. As shown in the table below, the cumulative contribution ratio up to the third principal component reached 79%, indicating that the first three components adequately captured the overall variance. Denial of spontaneous recovery

Next, to verify whether data for these patients may have improved even without treatment, we analyzed representative measurements of ALT, which is related to liver injury, and those of type IV collagen-7S and MRE, which are related to fibrosis, by dividing patients into groups that improved or remained unchanged before treatment and groups that worsened. The criteria for classifying patients into the “improved or remained unchanged” group and the “worsened” group prior to treatment were defined as follows. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was applied using each laboratory parameter measured from day − 84 to day 1 before treatment as the dependent variable and time (days) as the independent variable. Patients with a positive regression coefficient for days were classified into the worsened group, whereas those with a negative or zero coefficient were classified into the improved or remained unchanged group. The numbers of patients in the improved or remained unchanged group versus the worsened group were 7:3 for ALT, 3:7 for type IV collagen 7S, and 8:2 for MRE. Consequently, not only those that had improved or remained unchanged before treatment in terms of ALT, type IV collagen-7S, and MRE liver stiffness, but also those who tended to worsen before treatment demonstrated a tendency to improve (Fig. 6A-C). Effective cases

There were variations among the individual tests and cases. The following results present case-by-case evaluations of representative cases exploring the characteristics thereof.

Case 1: A-1: 69-year-old female, treated for hepatitis C, with a background of MASH

After administration of S-005151, a decrease in ALT and type IV collagen 7S levels was observed. The IgG levels also showed a decreasing trend. In particular, serum type IV collagen 7S dramatically decreased immediately after S-005151 administration and improved from 5.4 ng/ml before administration to approximately 3.3 ng/ml at the end of the study. The liver tissue was fully removed without fragmentation, and fibrosis improved from stage 5 to stage 3. In addition, MRE revealed a decreasing trend in fibrosis markers and other parameters post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Case 2: A-2: 62-year-old female with a background of alcoholic liver disease

Slight decreases in ALT and serum type IV collagen 7S levels were observed after administration. In particular, type IV collagen 7S levels dramatically decreased immediately after drug administration and improved from 10.0 ng/ml before administration to approximately 7.9 ng/ml at the end of the study. In addition, platelets tended to rise after administration (from 56,000/μL before administration to over 60,000/μL more often, rising to a maximum of 79,000/μL). The tissue showed improvement in both stages (from stage 5 to stage 2) and the area of fibrosis (from 6.1 to 3.5%). MRE was not deemed appropriate for evaluation because of the overall poor imaging in this case owing to iron deposition in the liver (Supplementary Fig. S5). Although it is not known whether this was related, at the end of the trial, the HbA1c value improved from 7.7% at the time that the trial was conducted to 6.6% (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Case 3: A-3: 69-year-old female, treated for hepatitis C, with a background of MASH

A decrease was shown in the levels of type IV collagen 7S and M2BPGi. In particular, type IV collagen 7S levels decreased dramatically immediately after drug administration and improved from 16.7 ng/ml before treatment to 7.8 ng/ml by the end of the study. Platelet levels increased from 32,000/μL before administration to 67,000/μL at the end of the study. The tissue was strongly fragmented, especially before treatment, but there was improvement in the fibrotic area. In fragmented tissues, the fibrous portion may have been detached during biopsy or fixation, and the specimen may have been prepared without the fibrous portion. As a result, the fibrous portion may have been evaluated as if it should be present but was not, resulting in lower estimates. MRE findings also improved. The patient was Grade B with a Child–Pugh score of 7, but her liver function did not improve (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Case 4: B-2: 57-year-old male with a background of MASH

Markers related to fibrosis and inflammation, such as type IV collagen 7S, P-III-P, ATX, M2BPGi, and IgG, showed a decreasing trend post-treatment, suggesting an effect thereof. Especially, type IV collagen 7S dramatically decreased immediately after S-005151 administration and improved from 7.0 ng/ml before treatment to 5.2 ng/ml at the end of the study. Although the tissue showed no improvement in either the stage or area of treatment, the fibrotic area may have been estimated to be low because the area before treatment was quite fragmented; however, post-treatment, it was relatively unfragmented. On MRE, the measurement immediately before administration (day 1) was not imaged as well as at day − 84, which may have led to a lower estimate of the pre-treatment evaluation. Although these tissue and MRE results did not match the blood data, we believe that the blood data better reflected the patient’s condition (Supplementary Fig. S12).

Case 5: B-3: 69-year-old male with a background of alcoholic liver disease

Post-treatment, AST, ALT, type IV collagen 7S, P-III-P, IgG, and other markers related to fibrosis and inflammation showed decreasing trends, suggesting that the treatment had an effect. The tissue showed considerable fragmentation before treatment, but relatively little fragmentation thereafter. The tissue showed improvement in the rate of fibrotic area. All MR images were clear and reflected an improvement in fibrosis during the follow-up. A decrease in MRE along with a decrease in fibrosis markers was confirmed post-treatment, and it could be inferred that the treatment was effective (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Discussion

We evaluated the safety and efficacy of S-005151 in patients with chronic liver disease, which was mainly compensated cirrhosis. One case of dysphonia was observed in cohort A and one case of fever was observed in cohort B. Both cases were mild, and the patients recovered on the day of administration, suggesting that there were no serious adverse drug reactions associated with the administration of S-005151 to these patients.

Three major effects of this drug were evaluated: liver injury (inflammation), liver function, and fibrosis. As for liver injury, AST and ALT data showed a decreasing trend after administration, and the necroinflammatory score of the modified HAI in the tissues also improved in 6 of 10 cases. These results suggest that S-005151 has a similar effect in reducing liver damage as observed in animal experiments using mice. The tendency of IgG to decrease may also have been involved in this reduction in liver damage; however, its effect on B cells is unknown to date. Additionally, there is no apparent evidence of improvement in liver function in terms of ALBI, MELD, or Child–Pugh scores, and it remains to be verified in future clinical trials whether this is because the patients were mainly compensated cirrhosis patients with good liver function. One patient with Child–Pugh Grade B (A-3) showed little improvement in liver function during the study period despite a decrease in liver stiffness. The patient had a large shunt vessel, which may have been difficult to regenerate owing to a lack of effective portal vein blood flow. It remains to be determined how further improvement in liver function can be made.

Regarding fibrosis, the platelet serum markers P-III-P, type IV collagen-7S, hyaluronic acid, M2BPGi, ATX, FIB-4, APRI, MRE, and Sirius Red staining on liver biopsy to assess the fibrotic area and score fibrosis showed an overall trend toward improvement, and there was a tendency for many of these items to move in tandem within individual cases. In this study, we measured the levels of several fibrosis markers. This is because it is currently unknown which marker provides the most reliable and sensitive evaluation for use in a clinical trial, owing to large daily fluctuations [18]. However, in this clinical trial, we confirmed that three markers (platelets, P-III-P, and type IV collagen/7S) and two indices (FIB-4 and APRI) showed improvement in some cases post-treatment, which were presumed to be treatment-related changes. Among the three markers, type IV collagen 7S was considered particularly reliable for evaluating fibrosis improvement in effective cases, as it reflects basement membrane remodeling associated with active hepatic fibrogenesis and sinusoidal capillarization and was often markedly reduced and stabilized early after treatment compared with the other markers. We believe that the timing of the fibrosis evaluation is very important. Among the cases evaluated as having improved in this study, there were cases with further improvements in type IV collagen/7S and MRE data after the end of the trial. For example, in cases A-1, A-2, and B-2, type IV collagen improved before, at the end of, and after treatment as follows: A-1, 5.4 ng/ml, 3.4 ng/ml, 2.7 ng/ml (3 months after the clinical trial); A-2, 10.0 ng/ml, 7.9 ng/ml, 5.4 ng ml (6 months after the clinical trial); B-2, 7.0 ng/ml, 5.2 ng/ml, 4.3 ng/ml (9 months after the clinical trial). Although not all patients were followed up after the clinical trial, there was one case (A-3) in which the MRE clearly improved thereafter, and the trend of fibrosis improvement may have been more apparent if the clinical trial period had been extended for several more months.

In a previous study by Tamai et al. at Osaka University, it was discovered that High-Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), a nuclear gene expression regulatory protein, is released into the bloodstream in large amounts upon severe tissue injury, stimulating Platelet Derived Growth factor Receptor α (PDGFRα)-positive mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in the bone marrow to mobilize into the bloodstream and accumulate specifically in the injured tissue [12, 14, 19, 20]. In contrast, HMGB1 has a binding domain for lipopolysaccharide (LPS), an endotoxin, and HMGB1 bound to LPS activates TLRs and induces a strong inflammatory response [21, 22]. In their joint research, Osaka University and StemRIM Co., Ltd. discovered S-005151, a partial peptide of HMGB1, which does not induce an inflammatory response and mobilizes MSCs into the blood. This peptide was created using only BoxA by omitting BoxB, the LPS-binding domain of HMGB1 [10, 11]. The peptide was tested for efficacy in a liver cirrhosis model, the heart, and the skin [10–12, 14, 19, 20]. Clinical trials are being conducted in Japan for epidermolysis bullosa, acute cerebral infarction, and knee osteoarthritis, and dosages have been determined based on these studies. The peptide was undetectable in the blood within a very short time, and we expect that further target cells and the mechanism of action will be clarified in the future. It remains unclear whether the anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects are independent of each other, or whether the improvement in fibrosis is mediated through a reduction in inflammation. This issue has not yet been conclusively determined and should be addressed in future studies. We believe that spatial analysis using human liver tissue and liver tissue from animal experiments may help clarify the effects of drugs in more detail in the future.

This investigator-initiated clinical trial has some limitations. First, this is a single-arm study with a small number of patients; we are in the process of increasing the size of the study and verifying the results with those of controls. Various analyses were performed to broadly evaluate liver injury, liver function, and fibrosis, and to estimate efficacy. Some tests could be evaluated stably, while others were difficult to evaluate owing to fluctuating values. Liver biopsy and MRE data were included, but some cases were difficult to evaluate because of differences in the site of the liver biopsy and problems such as fragmentation of the tissue and loss of fibrous portions during biopsy. MRE seemed to be very useful in cases where the evaluation could be performed effectively [23]. However, there were also cases in which evaluation was difficult and cases in which conditions were different among the four times that elastography was performed. These aspects should also be considered in future studies. Iron deposits in the liver and a loose fit of the passive driver are considered possible causes of this poor imaging [24]. Poor breath-holding may also be an important factor in the poor shooting conditions. By conducting clinical trials with these points in mind, we believe that a more accurate MRE may allow for a more accurate clinical trial than a liver biopsy [25]. Liver biopsy was found to be more difficult to evaluate than expected, especially in patients with cirrhosis, because of the higher risk of bleeding and other problems; the more fibrotic the patient, the more fragmented the tissue [26]. In addition, this was a single-arm, Phase IIa, investigator-initiated trial, and statistical significance could not be used to determine efficacy. Therefore, an 84-day pre-observation period was allowed, after which S-005151 was administered and evaluated by follow-up. The results showed that there were changes immediately after administration, such as a decrease in type IV collagen/7S, platelet count, and ALT levels, which strongly suggested that S-005151 had a positive effect. In addition, the results showing a tendency towards improvement post-treatment in the pre-treatment worsening group, including ALT and type IV collagen/7S results, are thought to be a natural consequence of the treatment effect. The responses to type IV collagen/7S and P-III-P were slightly different between cohorts A and B. Cohort B, which had S-005151 more frequently administered during the early stages of treatment, did not show an increased response to treatment. Whether this is due to differences in administration methods or background liver disease remains to be clarified. Our impression from conducting the trial was that the effect was stronger and clearer in patients whose causes had been strictly eliminated through abstinence from alcohol and hepatitis C treatment and in patients with stiff liver to the extent that MRE data were greater than 6 kPa. In this trial, patients were observed for 140 days after administration, and in some cases, fibrosis markers and liver stiffness on MRE improved further after the trial, suggesting that S-005151 may have a long-lasting effect. In this trial, 4–7 doses were administered for 1 month; however, it is necessary to further examine the duration of administration, administration interval, follow-up period, and dosage in the future. In addition, several patients reported that they felt more comfortable post-treatment, and some patients showed improvement in diabetes (patient A-2: HbA1c 7.7% immediately before treatment, 6.6% at the end of the study, and 6.3% 1 month after the end of the study) without changing obvious body weight change, suggesting the possibility of effects other than fibrosis improvement. These observations may be explained, at least in part, by an improvement in systemic and hepatic inflammation induced by this agent, which could have contributed to improved insulin resistance. However, as pointed out by the reviewer, HbA1c values in patients with cirrhosis may be influenced by anemia or altered erythrocyte lifespan associated with portal hypertension and hypersplenism. Therefore, the observed reduction in HbA1c may not necessarily reflect improved glycemic control alone. Alternative markers, such as glycated albumin, may provide a more accurate assessment of glycemic status in this population. These phenomena cannot be proven yet because of the lack of a control group and the small size of the study, but they should be evaluated in future clinical trials.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study are attractive because this drug is safe and may provide a new tool to address the unmet medical need to improve liver fibrosis. We also obtained information regarding evaluation methods and timing. We hope that this drug will be further investigated in many patients and will contribute to the successful treatment of liver fibrosis.
