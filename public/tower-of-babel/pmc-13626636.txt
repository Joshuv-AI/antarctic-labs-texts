A novel approach to HPV16 VLP construction and production with improved accessibility and particle sorting potential
Authors: Morgan Williamson, Caleb Boren, Rahul Kumar, Lauren Gollahon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human papillomavirus (HPV), a member of the Papillomaviridae family, infects a broad range of animal hosts and is responsible for the majority of cervical cancers and a substantial proportion of oropharyngeal cancers. Current treatment strategies for HPV-associated cervical disease depend largely on disease stage, with preinvasive and early invasive lesions commonly managed through surgical excision or radiation. Additionally, more advanced cases often require chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or combination regimens. Prophylactic vaccination remains the most effective preventive approach, and currently available vaccines, including Gardasil, Gardasil-9, and Cervarix, rely on L1-based virus-like particles (VLPs) to induce protective immunity. At the same time, increasing interest has emerged in the use of nanomaterials and viral capsids as platforms for targeted drug and gene delivery, alongside the development of higher-throughput methods for nanoparticle and viral characterization. Modified HPV capsids have shown promise for cell-specific targeting and more efficient cargo delivery with reduced off-target cytotoxicity. In this study, the HPV16 structural proteins L1 and L2 were fused to the N- and C-terminal halves of the Venus fluorophore, respectively, using a specially designed linker intended to preserve capsid assembly while reducing VLP aggregation associated with current production methods. The resulting bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)-based VLPs demonstrated reduced aggregation and improved total particle yield. In addition, the particles generated a detectable YFP signal, supporting fluorescence-based monitoring of VLP assembly and providing a potential basis for high-throughput particle characterization and sorting. This intrinsic fluorescent signal may also reduce reliance on separately packaged reporter constructs for downstream analysis.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV), a member of the Papillomaviridae family, can infect a wide range of animal hosts and remains one of the most clinically significant oncogenic viruses in humans (Van Doorslaer, 2022). It has been determined that approximately 5% of all cancers worldwide can be attributed to HPV infection, accounting for 99.7% of cervical cancers, 70% of all oropharyngeal cancers, and a significant portion of other anogenital cancers (Meng et al., 2025; de Martel et al., 2017; Okunade, 2020). Structurally, HPV is a non-enveloped, icosahedral virus approximately 50–60 nm in diameter that contains an ∼8-kb double-stranded DNA genome, placing it in Group I of the Baltimore classification system (Mlynarczyk-Bonikowska and Rudnicka, 2024). The capsid is composed of about 360 L1 molecules arranged into pentamers, with 12–72 L2 molecules (de Villiers et al., 2004), or, more recently, 12–36 L2 molecules (Guan et al., 2017). HPV types are categorized by >10% differences in the L1 major capsid protein. HPV specifically targets heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), a type of glycosaminoglycan present in the extracellular matrix, rendering most epithelial and mucosal cells permissive to infection (Cagno et al., 2019).

Cell disruption or wounding is required for both native HPV and HPV VLP infection. Because heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) are displayed on disturbed and cancerous cells, HPV VLPs may inherently target a broad range of tumor types. One group has exploited the HPV capsids’ native ability to recognize HSPGs displayed on various tumor cell types (Kines and Schiller, 2022). This same group previously demonstrated that HPV VLPs preferentially infect tumor cells and did not enter normal cells, primary keratinocytes lacking HSPGs, or undisturbed tissue types (Kines et al., 2016). When conjugated differently, the same particle platform was shown to induce long-lasting adaptive ant-tumor immunity without the need for tumor-specific antigens (Kines et al., 2021), and to effectively treat both local and distant tumors in combination with immunotherapy (Huis In 't Veld et al., 2023). Furthermore, papilloma particles were shown to remain functional across different tumor pH and temperature conditions, which may vary with tumor density and location (Knapp et al., 2022). These studies also note that systemic delivery of HPV16 PsVs was well tolerated in mice, with no observed toxicity or significant immune effects. These works strongly suggest that HPV15 PsVs/VLPs are highly specific vectors for drug and gene therapy. With this growing evidence, continued improvement of high-yield, high-quality HPV VLP production methods will be important for supporting broader experimental and therapeutic applications.

Native HPV is not cultivatable by traditional viral culture methods in vitro. HPV replication is intimately linked to the differentiation of stratified squamous epithelium, and viral protein expression depends on cell cycle and immune signaling in differentiated cells. As a result, fully formed viral progeny only exist in limited amounts during natural infection. Many lines of research have therefore led to current papillomavirus production methods. Most literature using HPV16-VLPs uses the methods and recommended materials provided by Buck, Schiller, and colleagues, who developed the optimized coding sequences. These methods required the purchase of plasmids with the optimized codons under a special SV40 large T-dependent system that achieves high copy number only in 293 TT cells. The 293 TT cell line can be purchased or generated in-house by transfecting an additional SV40 large T-expressing sequence. Alternatively, coding sequences can be cloned into parent vectors under a general promoter to improve accessibility across other producer cell lines. Through related approaches, all grounded in the same core construct, critical HPV research has continued to advance. At the same time, concerns have recently been raised regarding producer cell lines that express Large T, including spontaneous packing of Large T-coding regions and the potential delivery of oncogenic DNA into healthy cells.

Understanding the native HPV capsid assembly and its co-evolution within human host systems is important for developing modified VLP models that remain efficient, reproducible, and biologically relevant. Existing VLPs used in the current vaccine platform have been L1-only and have required multivalent protection. A multivalent particle with different L1 types can assemble at equimolar concentrations to provide protection against multiple HPV types, including heterologous HPV types (Wang et al., 2020). L1 proteins assemble into pentamers that interlock primarily through disulfide bonds between Cys175 and Cys428, a conserved trait among HPV types. In addition, cryo-electron microscopy maps of HPV16 revealed numerous other L1 interactions that contribute to stable capsid formation (Guan et al., 2017). Most research studies use L1-only VLPs as they provide sufficient structural similarity for many experimental models while requiring less input material for production. Although L1 alone allows stable capsid formation, it does not fully recapitulate infectivity that seems to require L2 in native infection. L1-only VLPs are sometimes modified through the conjugation of GFP fused to a flexible Glycine/Serine linker. In host systems expressing both L1 and L2, HPV VLPs are able to assemble in multiple configurations, and native particles have been reported to contain roughly 12–36 molecules of L2. Because L1 is typically produced in excess, these systems can generate a large number of L1-only VLPs, contributing to heterogeneous particle populations, even after purification. This heterogeneity poses an obstacle to analytical techniques. In certain environments, L1-only VLPs can bind together and form larger mega-VLP structures, limiting some experimental preparations. Incorporation of L2 binding alters capsid architecture subtly, as L2 binding between L1 pentamers brings the proteins closer together and changes capsid diameter by 2–5 nm. Such slight differences are somewhat difficult to resolve without highly calibrated equipment and techniques not commonly used in cell biology settings.

Harvesting, isolating, and purifying viruses for experimental studies through ultracentrifugation-based methods requires costly equipment and supplies and carries an increased risk of particle deformation, aggregation, and contamination. Current standard approaches for assessing virus concentration, infectivity, and efficiency are often time-consuming and typically terminal for both the virus and the host cell model. The methods commonly used to determine infection efficiency, including qPCR and immunoassays, require collecting infected cells at specific time points and can become somewhat tedious and restrictive. Flow cytometry is a highly sensitive, gentle, and high-throughput method for detecting fluorophores within cell populations. With that said, its use for viral applications remains somewhat limited by a minimum detection threshold that is often above the size range of most viruses. Additionally, higher-resolution cytometers are not yet standard across all institutions. At the same time, live-cell and in vivo fluorescence imaging technologies continue to advance, including bimolecular fluorescent complementation (BiFC) to monitor protein-protein interactions in real time. In BiFC systems, fluorescent proteins are split into two nonfluorescent fragments, typically an N-terminal and a C-terminal half and fused to two proteins of interest. When tagged proteins interact, the fluorophore is reconstituted and emits a fluorescent signal and then stops when the interacting proteins dissociate.

The attachment of complementary BiFC fluorophore halves to the HPV16 structural proteins offers the potential to monitor productive capsid assembly in real time while distinguishing particles containing both L1 and L2 from those containing L1 alone. YFP emission generated by BiFC interaction may provide information on particle assembly, aggregation state, and intracellular trafficking, including the presence of fully assembled particles and particles undergoing disassembly or removal from the extracellular space via endocytic processes. This approach may reduce dependence on terminal sample collection and virus-specific antibody conjugates for immunocytochemistry, while also allowing the timing of VLP harvest to be assessed with relatively minimal equipment, including bright-field scopes equipped with filter inserts or filter wheels. Because the BiFC signal can be detected across multiple imaging platforms, this design may improve accessibility for construct validation and monitoring in laboratories without specialized nanoparticle instrumentation. In addition, particle populations containing both L1 and L2 may be distinguished from L1-only particles during multiple production steps. As fluorescence-based cell sorting, flow virometry, and nanoparticle detection technologies continue to advance, the BiFC signal may further support more efficient discrimination and isolation of particles containing both structural proteins, potentially reducing reliance on density gradient separation and the yield loss associated with those workflows. Intensity variation between particles may also provide a future basis for distinguishing populations with relatively high versus low L2 incorporation in higher-sensitivity flow systems.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

To detect HPV capsid protein binding or assembly/disassembly in live cells in real time using fluorescent microscopy, each protein was fused to one-half of the fluorescent protein Venus. To fuse the protein portions, various PCR techniques were used. PCR cycling conditions are reported in the supplementary (ST1), with denaturation and extension temperatures, 95 °C and 72 °C, respectively, remaining the same for all reactions. Q5 high fidelity polymerase master mix (NEB #M0492) was used for all reactions, allowing a 15 s/kB extension rate for plasmid DNA. 10 ng of template DNA, and a final concentration of 0.5 mM for each primer were added for all PCR reactions. Overlap extension PCR primers were designed for each gene and ordered for synthesis by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). L1 and L2 were copied from the expression vector p16sheLL (Addgene plasmid # 37320; http://n2t.net/addgene37320; RRID:Addgene_37320) (Buck et al., 2006) (Figures 1A, 2). The C-terminus half of Venus was copied from pBiFC-VC155 expression vector (Addgene plasmid # 22011; http://n2t.net/addgene:22011; RRID: Addgene_22011) (Shyu et al., 2006), and the N-terminus half of Venus was copied from pBiFC-VN173 (Addgene plasmid # 22010; http://n2t.net/addgene:22010; RRID: Addgene_22010) (Shyu et al., 2006). PCR fragments were separated on 1% agarose gel in TAE buffer. PCR fragments were extracted and purified with Qiagen Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen; #28706X4) before further cloning steps and before ligation into backbone plasmid. 2 μL (100 pg) from the ligation reaction was transformed into 40 μL of MAX Efficiency DH5α Competent E. coli cells (Invitrogen; #18258012) by heat shock. Average transformation efficiency for pBiFC-L1-VC155 and pBiFC-L2-VN173 were 7.03 × 109 and 6.25 × 109, respectively. Individual colonies were picked, expanded in liquid LB + AMP100 for 12–16 h, and mini-prepped using QIAprep Spin Miniprep kit (Qiagen; #27104). Promising clones of the correct size were sent to Plasmidsaurus (Eugene, OR) for sequencing.

Cell culture for VLP-producing cells

293Ts were cultured in filtered DMEM (Gibco; #12100046) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Gibco; #12662029) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco; #15070063). Cells were grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and appropriate humidity. The plasmids were transfected into 293T cells plated for ∼70% confluency after 24 h 1 h prior, cells were given fresh complete media. Plasmid DNA was complexed to LipoD293T transfection reagent in serum-free high-glucose DMEM at a ratio of 3:1 lipid:DNA. The final concentration of DNA was 0.5 μg per well in a 6-well plate or per 900 K cells. 0.5 μg of positive control p16sheLL or 0.25 μg pBiFC-L1-VC155 + 0.25 μg pBiFC-L2-VN173 was transfected per well. The lipid/DNA mixture was added to the cultures and incubated for 22 h. The transfection media was then removed; cells were rinsed with PBS and replenished with fresh complete media. 56–72 h after transfection, cells were collected accordingly for protein analysis or fluorescent imaging.

Immunoblotting

293T cells transfected with both plasmids were removed from the culture dish with 0.25% trypsin with EDTA (Gibco; #15050065) and pelleted by centrifugation at 4.0 × 1,000 rpm. Cell pellets were washed once with cold PBS to remove excess media and trypsin. Cell lysis for reducing assays was facilitated using RIPA buffer (Pierce) containing 0.1% SDS, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1X HALT protease inhibitor cocktail (100X) (ThermoScientific). Total protein lysate was collected from the supernatant of the cell lysis reaction after 15 min incubation at 4 °C with mixing and centrifugation at 12 × 10,000 rpm. Protein concentration was determined from the BCA curve shown in the previous section. Samples were mixed 1:1 with 2X Laemmli Buffer + 2-BME and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min 40 μg per well was loaded onto 8%–12% SDS-PAGE gel. 5 μL of PageRuler pre-stained protein ladder (ThermoScientific; #26616). Gels ran at 130V for ∼45min. Separated proteins were semi-dry transferred (BioRad TransBlot Turbo) onto nitrocellulose membranes with transfer buffer containing 20% methanol. Membranes were blocked at 4 °C overnight with 5% non-fat milk. Primary antibodies were diluted in the blocking agent and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Anti- HPV16 L1 (CamVir1) mouse monoclonal (abcam, ab69) at a dilution of 1:2000, Anti-HPV16 L2 (2JGmab#5) mouse monoclonal (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-65709) at a dilution of 1:500 in blocking buffer. The anti-E6 mouse monoclonal antibody was specific for types 16 and 18 at a 1:250 dilution. Loading control was THE β-tubulin mouse monoclonal antibody (GeneScript, #A01410), diluted 1:2,000. The primary antibody was rinsed thrice with PBST. The secondary antibody used was HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#A1014; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), diluted 1:2000 and added for 1–2 h at room temperature. The secondary antibody was washed off with PBST, and membranes were briefly soaked in enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity, Thermo Fisher Scientific #34095) before imaging. Blots were imaged on the Li-COR Odyssey XF Imaging System using the chemi channel and 800 channel for the ladder.

Immunocytochemistry

Production of VLPs in 293T producer cells and transfection efficiency were determined by fixed-cell imaging of BiFC signal and fluorescent antibody detection. 293T cells were seeded and transfected in the same manner as described in the previous section, except for the additional use of No.1.5 glass cover slips coated with Poly-D-lysine 0.1 mg/mL (Gibco; #A3890401). 48 h post-transfection, the culture media were removed, and the cells were rinsed with PBS. Cells were fixed with 4% formalin for 15 min, permeabilized for 20 min in 0.01% Triton-X, and blocked for 2 h in 3% BSA with three PBS washes between each step. Indirect immunofluorescence used the same primary antibodies as in the immunoblotting steps. Anti-HPV16 L1 (CamVir1) diluted 1:2,000, or Anti-HPV16 L2 (2JGmab#5) diluted 1:500 in 3% BSA was added to the cells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next morning, cells were rinsed with DPBS and incubated for 2 h with Goat anti-Mouse IgG2a conjugated to Alexa633 (Invitrogen; #A-21050) diluted 1:1,000 in 3% BSA. Cells were rinsed and immediately imaged on an Olympus BX51 or a Leica microscope using filters for FITC and Texas Red.

Maturation and harvest of VLPs

293T cells were removed from the culture dish with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco; #15050065) and pelleted. All media and trypsin were rinsed with PBS, and cells were pelleted again. Lysis buffer for capsid maturation made with DPBS (Gibco; #14190144) + 9.8 mM MgCl2. Sterile DPBS +9.8 mM MgCl2 was supplemented with 2.5% Ammonium sulfate buffer, 0.2% Brij-58 (ThermoFisher Scientific; #28336), 0.2% benzonase endonuclease (Millipore Sigma; #101697), 0.1% RNAse mix (Qiagen), and 100X Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific; #78429) diluted to 1X final concentration. Cell pellets were incubated in 1.4 pellet volumes of lysis buffer in a 37 °C water bath overnight. The following day, the supernatant was collected after 5 min on ice and 5 min at 5,000 x g. The remaining pellet was clarified three times with 1.2 pellet volumes of DPBS +9.8 mM MgCl2. The supernatant from each clarification step was pooled. A discontinuous sucrose gradient was created using 3 dilutions of a 60% w/v Optiprep™ (Iodixanol) Density Gradient Medium (MilliPore Sigma; CAS# 92339–11–2). The gradient was gently overlaid, starting with 39%, then 31%, and then 27% iodixanol in DPBS +0.85 M NaCl, in 2 mL steps. The gradient was allowed to diffuse overnight at 4 °C before the clarified supernatant was gently layered on top. Ultracentrifugation occurred at 4 °C for 8 h at 40,000 rpm in SW 41 Ti swinging bucket rotor (Beckman Coulter, #331362). Compatible thin-wall polypropylene tubes (14 × 89mm; Beckman Colter #331372) were used. A 25-gauge needle connected to a 1 mL or 3 mL sterile syringe was used to pierce the side wall of the tube at a 45° angle to collect fractions containing significant VLP content in ∼1 mL steps.

Electron microscopy

150-mesh copper-coated grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences, EMS150-Cu) were pre-treated with 1% alcian blue in 3% glacial acetic acid for 10 min to neutralize the negative charge. Grids were rinsed 5X and excess wicked away with filter paper. 10 mL of VLPs in Optiprep™ were allowed to attach to the grid surface for 10 min, then washed 1X in DI water. Excess liquid was wicked away with filter paper. 10 mL of L1 antibody-conjugated, 10 nm gold particles (Abcam; ab201808) was dropped onto grids for 2 min for immunogold labeled grids. Excess antibody was blotted away. Grids were negatively stained with 2% Uranyl acetate for 45 s. Grids were allowed to dry for at least 4 h before imaging on the Model H7650 Transmission Electron Microscope (TTU CASM, Hitachi) at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

Flow cytometry

The transfection efficiency of pBiFC-L1-VC155 and pBiFC-L2-VN173, and the efficient intracellular assembly of the resulting proteins in 293 T cells, were confirmed using total population flow cytometry. BiFC signal was detected using the BL1 channel on the Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Invitrogen Catalog Number A24858). Side- and forward-scatter heights plotted the main cell population away from apoptotic bodies. Voltages used to gate the cells: FSC = 190, SSC = 290, BL1 = 300. The main population was further separated into singlets and doublets with a side scatter area vs. side scatter height density plot. Only the singlet cell population was included in the fluorescent channel counts.

Extraction and analysis of encapsidated DNA

HPV Pseudovirions and VLPs have been known to package plasmids of sizes similar to the wild-type genome. 20 mL of the virus sample was processed using a PCR purification kit (Qiagen; #28104) to isolate viral DNA. This method was previously described by the lab of Cellular Oncology at NIH (Cardone et al., 2014). In case the capsids could escape primary purification, 20 mL of particles were incubated in 10 mM DTT before the DNA purification step to disassemble the capsids. The resulting elution showed no bands on a 1% agarose gel. Thus, the amount of DNA was either 0 or extremely low. To rule out the second option, the eluted sample was amplified with multiple primer sets (ST1). Because the plasmids used to make the virions are around 6 kB, L1 Fwd and 155 Rev were used to assess excess pBiFC-L1-VC155 plasmid encapsidation. Primer pairs L2 fwd and 173 Rev were used to detect encapsidated pBiFC-L2-VN173. M13 universal primers were used for nonspecific DNA amplification. Finally, SV40 large T antigen primers were used to check for SV40 genetic material since most 293Ts contain integrated portions of the large T gene, which was used to immortalize the cell line originally. All primer sequences are listed in ST1.

Dynamic light scattering with malvern zetasizer nano ZSP

Malvern Zetasizer nano ZSP was used to measure particle size by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). Particle motion is related to particle size with the Stokes-Einstein equation. Measurements consisted of 10 s of light collection, repeated 9 times for each light detector. Forward and backscatter angles of 12.8° and 173° were detected on each read. The size measurement was calibrated against 10 nm beads. The 39% Optiprep™ solution was measured for background signal. The protein size setting was tested using 1 mg/mL BSA, and protein aggregation was normalized between BSA samples with or without 10 mM DTT.

Software and statistical analyses

All DNA components were designed and analyzed in SnapGene (version 6.2.2). All protein models were visualized in ChimeraX (version 1.5). Theoretical protein structures predicted by AlphaFold2 (Version 2.3.2) on Google Colab with AlphaColab (Mirdita et al., 2022). Fluorescent image processing and fluorescent intensity were measured in FIJI. Graphic illustration and statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism (v9.5.1) unless mentioned to be automatically generated from associated software.

Expression of recombinant proteins in 293T producer cells

The total lysate in reducing buffer, from 293T cells transfected with p16sheLL, was expected to show some L2. However, since the proteins are co-expressed under a single promoter and for the reasons described above, L1 was expected to be more abundant. 293T cells transfected with pBiFC-L1-VC155 and pBiFC-L2-173 were expected to have relatively equal amounts of L1 and L2 in the total reduced lysate since each gene is under a separate and identically strong promoter (CMV).

Results from SDS-PAGE-separated immunoblots mostly matched the expected weights from Table 1. The negative control sample that was transfected with pBiFC-VC155 and pBiFC-VN173 showed no expression of either L1 or L2. Cell lysates transfected with the positive control p16sheLL showed moderate L1 expression around 55 kDa, as expected and reported by other groups (Buck et al., 2004). The same positive control sample did not show L2 expression, which was surprising. The expression efficiency of p16sheLL was expected to be slightly lower overall. Due to differences in large T antigen expression in the host cell, the amount of L2 produced may be below the limit of detection for immunoblotting. Alternatively, since the binding site of L2 is often hidden in mature capsids, little to no L2 signal is expected if incomplete disassembly of already folded and assembled L1:L2 complexes or immature capsids.

293T cells transfected with pBiFC-L1-VC155 and pBiFC-L2-VN173 showed strong expression of L1 around 60 kDa, as predicted in the previous section. The same population showed L2 expression at approximately 70 kDa, as predicted by the initial modeling. The loading control b–Tubulin was detected in all three lysates around 50 kDa with equal loading (Figure 8).

Discussion

A large part of creating a new nanoparticle is developing methods for both constructing and characterizing said nanoparticle. This is especially important when testing the particles for applications to Human Health. One problem with recent advances in nanotechnology and viral vector technology is the lack of live-cell and real-time nano-tracking of particles during in vitro production and investigations.

Discussion of considerations for new capsid construction and validation

Producer cell choice and accessibility of the platform

HPV16-BiFC VLPs were harvested and purified as described by other research groups (Buck et al., 2006; Buck et al., 2004; Chen et al., 2000; Kim et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022; Schellenbacher et al., 2017; Slupetzky et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2022). In this study, 293T cells were selected as the host cell model for plasmid transfection and intracellular viral capsid formation, as they are widely used as producers for a range of VLP systems (Han et al., 2025; Hołubowicz et al., 2025). Unlike current methods that rely on 293 TT cells (Cerqueira et al., 2017), the modified coding regions used in this project were placed under a general CMV promoter. Because CMV is a broadly active mammalian promoter, its use may improve construct compatibility across commonly available producer cell lines and reduce the need for specialized SV40 large T-expressing systems. Additionally, this design has the potential to increase accessibility of HPV16 VLP production by reducing dependence on specialized producer systems. Following production, isolated VLPs were analyzed for the presence of the Large T coding region as a quality control step, as unintended packaging of producer cell-associated sequences would be undesirable in downstream applications (De et al., 2023). Although HeLa cells represent another easily transfected producer cell type, there is a substantial risk of packaging E6/E7 genomic material associated with that cell line. Because of this, HeLa cells are not ideal for producing empty VLPs intended for therapeutic use. However, HeLa cells may still be useful in experimental settings where larger portions of the HPV genome are intentionally packaged for infectious studies.

Evidence that BiFC modification does not disrupt capsid maturation

Successful, real-time capsid assembly was characterized by cell swelling and a peak in BiFC signal intensity in 293T cells after ∼56 h (Figure 15). In this figure, clearly swollen cells are overlaid with BiFC signal, supporting the interpretation that productive capsid formation occurred during this time window. Previous studies reported that 293 TT cells, which contain a stable extra copy of large T antigen and express p16sheLL at high copy number, “swell up like beach balls” (Buck et al., 2004). In the present study, flow data from standard 293T cells showed less size variation for the positive control p16sheLL vector, and immunoblotting confirmed a slightly lower protein yield, likely reflecting lower large T expression in the regular 293T background. By 96 h, the flow data showed substantial cellular debris, suggesting that cells loaded with capsids had begun to burst following transient transfection with both constructs.

Capsid maturation was further supported by the use of an alkaline lysis buffer optimized with ammonium sulfate, which allowed maturation when lysis occurred for 24–36 h at 37 °C (Kopitak et al., 2025). Formation of the HPV icosahedral structure depends on disulfide bond formation between L1 subunits, a process that occurs relatively slowly (Buck et al., 2013). Because there were no significant changes in the overall charge of L1 pentamers or the total capsid, the pH of all buffers used throughout lysis, storage, and dilution was maintained. The icosahedral pattern is initiated by disulfide bonding between residues Cys175 and Cys428 (Wang et al., 2020). L1 trimers are often seen in reducing Western blot analysis without appropriate enzymatic digestion or reduction of samples containing assembled viral particles or partially assembled polypeptide fragments (Trejo-Cerro et al., 2026). In this study, the consistent appearance of L1 dimers and trimers in L1 immunoblots (Figure 19) suggested that BiFC-constructed capsids retained disulfide bond behavior similar to that expected of normally assembling particles. Together, these findings support the conclusion that BiFC modification did not disrupt the normal capsid properties required for proper assembly and maturation.

It remains unclear whether L1-VC155 and L2-VN173 molecules produced in separate cells can assemble during lysis and maturation. Native HPV capsid assembly occurs intracellularly, although cell-free assembly of papillomavirus-like particles has also been reported (Cerqueira et al., 2016). Thus, proper maturation and lysis could increase the efficiency of these plasmids in producing fully formed particles. Excess L2 expression compared to native and p16sheLL, which involves L1 and L2 at near-equimolar concentrations, could facilitate maximum L2 saturation per particle and, concurrently, maximum fluorophore intensity per particle, but this potential remains unexplored and is beyond the detection methods provided in this study.

Heterogeneity of the transfected population and implications for particle composition

Each cell in the transfected population falls into one of four categories, yielding a range of expression levels. This includes 293T cells with no plasmid uptake, pBiFC-L1-VC155 only, pBiFC-L2-VN173 only, or both pBiFC-L1-VC155 and pBiFC-L2-VN173. Immunoblotting of total cell lysate was used to quantify total protein; however, this does not directly inform the estimation of fully formed capsid yield. The use of fluorescent imaging (Flow and ICC) enabled more quantification of the BiFC signal over time. ICC also showed overlap between BiFC signal and L1 antibody staining relative to L1 antibody staining alone, allowing estimation of the ratio of L1/L2-containing versus L1-only capsids produced in 293T cells. This ratio provides a more informative estimate of transfection efficiency, particularly for the subset of cells that successfully received both plasmids. Because L1 particles can assemble into pentamers and support loose capsid formation with or without L2 (Chen et al., 2000), there is substantial potential for a high proportion of L1-only VLPs relative to L1/L2 VLPs, both across the overall transfected population and within individual cells expressing both plasmids. Mixed particle populations of this type remain less well characterized. However, there is currently no evidence to suggest that a heterogeneous mixture of VLP types is inherently less effective for packaging or targeting, or that it introduces additional safety concerns. Practical separation of L1-only and L1/L2-containing particles remains challenging, as the structural difference between these capsid types is limited to only a slight reduction in diameter associated with increased capsid tightness. Because L2 is the minor capsid protein, variation in L2 incorporation also does not substantially alter total particle weight.

Imaging and flow-based detection of BiFC capsid formation

Overall, the ratio of L1-only to L1/L2 capsids became more consistent at higher magnification and with more specific excitation/emission filters. The highest fluorescence detection was recorded with the Attune flow cytometer, which is well-suited for high-throughput flow virometry applications. The wavelengths of the BL1 filter, together with the separation of cells as they pass through the laser in the optical cell, increased the recorded transfection efficiency and apparent L1/L2 capsid production. The Attune measured approximately 18% of the total population as L1/L2 capsid-producing cells, whereas the Nikon, Olympus, and Leica scopes averaged approximately 11.1%, 7.9%, and 3.8%, respectively. This difference is not unexpected, as flow cytometry measures the entire population, whereas microscopy requires averaging across multiple fields of view.

A specific YFP filter, such as the one used on the Attune and Nikon systems, provided the most accurate approximation of VLP formation efficiency, which was expected. The Olympus scope with a FITC filter was the next-best option. When a YFP filter is unavailable, the BiFC signal can still be detected within the FITC range, and accuracy improves with increased magnification. The GFP filter in the Leica had the least spectral overlap with YFP, so the number of BiFC-positive cells with sufficient intensity above the background is lower.

In this specific comparison, the phase-contrast and brightfield images captured by the Leica were substantially better for cell border visualization and improved the accuracy of cell counting per image relative to the Olympus system. In the Olympus BX-51 instruments available in the department, dust particles on the phase-contrast lenses partially obstructed the camera view, as shown in Figure 13, where multiple dark spots and lower contrast between cell borders are visible compared with Figure 14. For Alexa 633 detection, both PI and Texas red filters performed with similar accuracy. TRITC detection of Alexa 633 was also tested on the Olympus BX-51 using the same filter wheel as PI and FITC, but TRITC showed the lowest accuracy because of reduced spectral overlap, so PI was used for subsequent samples.

Importantly, BiFC VLP capsid formation remained detectable after formalin fixation and Triton X permeabilization, with sufficient preservation for L1 antibody binding in immunocytochemistry applications. Together, these findings indicate that BiFC VLP capsid formation can be imaged across multiple spectral ranges and instrument types, making this method widely accessible while remaining ideal for modern, high-throughput instruments.

Discussion of particle size, yield, and aggregation

Particle size and morphology

It is important to note that 2D flattening of a 3D icosahedral particle can make it appear larger in TEM images. For this reason, diameters measured from 2D TEM images were used primarily to provide a rough assessment of particle uniformity, including sphericity and flattening, across the population. The relatively small error bars among diameter measurements taken within the same particle suggest that individual particles were fairly uniform in shape. Similar variation across diameter directions was also observed in p16sheLL particles, indicating that BiFC-VLPs retain an icosahedral morphology comparable to that of the positive control and undergo a similar degree of flattening during 2D imaging.

Among individual particles, the observed size distribution ranged from approximately 55–100 nm for BiFC-VLPs and 35–105 nm for p16sheLL VLPs. The addition of the BiFC components to the capsid proteins increased the overall particle size by approximately 1.5-fold relative to p16sheLL capsids, which averaged ∼50–55 nm. Notably, the overall size range was broader in the p16sheLL group than in the BiFC group.

Estimation of particle yield and MOI relevance

Particle count per unit area of the grid was used to generate a rough estimate of the concentration of viruses per mL recovered following ultracentrifugation. Estimating the number of HPV VLPs produced in each sample allows comparison with theoretical yield and with other methods of particle measurement, which together can help validate characterization of these novel particles and provide an approximate total titer. As described in the previous sections, transfection efficiency was first used to estimate the number of cells containing the virus. This calculation was then extended by averaging the number of viruses observed per grid area, which was used to estimate the number of viruses present per 10 μL of the 1 mL fraction collected. The final step was to divide the estimated number of viruses per mL by the concentration of virus-containing cells per mL loaded onto the density gradient.

Scale bar proportion was also used to calculate the total area imaged on the grid. Approximately 20 viruses were counted within a 9.08 × 10−6 mm area, and this ratio was compared with x/3.05 mm, representing the full grid area. Based on this estimate, 67.2 × 105 BiFC virus particles were calculated per 20 μL of sample loaded onto the grid, corresponding to an estimated yield of 33.59 × 107 particles in a 1 mL fraction. Using these values, 2 μL of the undiluted VLP-containing fraction was added per 100,000 cells to maintain a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ∼6.7. This falls within the suggested MOI range of 0.1–10 (Shabram and Aguilar-Cordova, 2000).

Aggregation behavior and sample-state dependence

The attachment of BiFC proteins to the capsid resulted in slight differences in apparent 2D shape distortion compared with p16sheLL-produced capsids. However, a large proportion of the particles showed similar distortion patterns, suggesting that BiFC-VLP assemblies remained structurally uniform across the sample. Nearly all BiFC replicates showed an average-diameter peak at 100% intensity, indicating highly similar light scattering behavior across the population and suggesting relatively uniform particles with acceptable purity and concentration. BiFC-VLP samples were prepared in the same manner as p16sheLLVLPs, stored at −80 °C, and handled as suggested in the original protocol provided by (Buck et al., 2005). In contrast, most p16sheLL samples showed two intensity-derived size peaks and lower PDI in backscatter results. Taken together, these findings suggest that p16sheLL samples contained either larger VLP aggregates or incompletely separated VLPs associated with producer cell lysates through electrostatic interactions. Viral particle aggregation is not unusual, and complications arising from it are still being reported in research applications (Pradhan et al., 2022).

Some grids were not washed after the viral attachment step during TEM preparation. Because this particle construct was novel, its charge and interaction properties were unknown, and excessive washing could have disturbed the sample. Grids were therefore prepared both with and without washing after application of the virus supernatant. Addition of BiFC appeared to significantly reduce particle aggregation relative to both washed and unwashed p16sheLL samples (Figures 29–31). Particle adhesion and aggregation also affected downstream measurements. This was reflected in the Zetasizer data, where p16sheLL-containing samples in aqueous solution showed two distinct peaks, including one near 4,000 nm that corresponded to the sizes of mega-HPV L1 assemblies observed on unwashed p16sheLL VLP-loaded TEM grids. These large assemblies appeared capable of maintaining recognizable higher-order structural patterning despite variation in total particle size, consistent with the preservation of icosahedral assembly features. It is possible that the distorted star-like appearance observed between L1 “mega-pentamers” resulted in part from sample processing and 2D flattening of a 3D particle. Although the mega-VLPs were broadly reminiscent of native structure, clear aberrations were still present, including greater spacing between mega-pentamers and formations in which some mega-pentamers appeared to be absent. The largest aggregate particle recorded across samples measured 10 microns in diameter, representing an approximately 1000-fold increase over the single particles measured in Sections 3.6 and 3.7.

Adhesion, aggregation, motility, protein folding, and overall survival of most viruses are controlled by hydrophobic and Van der Waals forces (Bormashenko et al., 2021). Viruses and nanoparticles that are larger than the atomic scale but remain submicron in at least one dimension occupy the mesoscale. For reference, the total molecular weight estimated from various calculations mentioned in Sections 2 and 3 fell within the natural range (106 Da–109 Da) for most viruses, and BiFC-fused VLPs had a median estimated weight of 2.5 × 108 Da. Mesoscale particles are significantly affected by spontaneous fluctuations in the system’s equilibrium (Bormashenko et al., 2021), which is important for determining measurement methods and constraints. In Table 2 from Section 3, temperature was kept constant across all samples to reduce variation in the system’s immediate kinetic energy. Viruses are also known to remain stable in aqueous environments and to be strongly influenced by water-mediated surface interactions (Gassilloud and Gantzer, 2005). It is therefore plausible that the large mega-VLP aggregates observed in p16sheLL samples prepared on unwashed grids reflected the absence of a DI water wash step. The p16sheLL grids washed with DI water after sample application may have allowed especially large particles, which had greater surface area for hydration and interaction, to move more readily into the aqueous phase.

The absence of mega-VLPs in both the washed and unwashed BiFC-VLP preparations suggests that the addition of the HPPAPKED linker and/or Venus fluorophore may have slightly altered the electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions in a way that reduced particle aggregation. Small clusters ranging from 3–5 particles were still observed in both p16sheLL and BiFC TEM images, and correct capsid formation was evident in individual BiFC VLPs. The addition of Venus and, potentially, the linkage of the fluorophore, capsid binding, and assembly are only inhibited at high aggregate concentrations. Correct capsid formation of individual particles is still unhindered by fluorophore linkage to the capsid proteins.

Zetasizer forward-scatter data for BiFC-VLPs included several noisy samples with lower-quality polydispersion indices, which could initially suggest fluorescent light interference from intact capsids displaying BiFC signal. Because this construct is novel, it was difficult to generate a truly appropriate positive control for DLS. In addition, the 633 nm wavelength emitted and detected by the Zetasizer lies outside the detection range of the Venus/BiFC signal, making it unlikely that the fluorophore itself was the primary source of noise. A more likely explanation is that BiFC samples contained relatively few large or aggregated particles, which are the principal targets of forward-scatter detection (Markova et al., 2022). In the aqueous environment of Zetasizer cuvettes, BiFC VLPs exhibited very little aggregation, whereas under the dry, fixed conditions required for TEM, particles occasionally appeared as aggregates up to 300 nm in diameter. During TEM preparation, particles may have formed doublets, triplets, or quadruplets because of water loss, charge attraction to the pre-treated acidic grids, or other processing-related effects. Notably, no larger aggregates were observed in the BiFC group after TEM preparation. In contrast, p16sheLL VLPs appeared to aggregate under both aqueous and dry preparation conditions, suggesting reduced stability across these conditions and raising concerns for downstream vaccine manufacturing workflows.

Future directions with flow virometry, resonant mass measurement, and field flow techniques

Weight- and buoyancy-based sorting potential

Mesoscale particles are most often screened using low-throughput mass spectrometry (throughput 104). Flow cytometry and flow virometry rely on multiple physical principles to separate particles based on size, acoustic and hydrodynamic focusing, and fluorescent activation (Tabler and Tilton, 2024). FACS can increase throughput and selection potential to 109+, limited primarily by the number of particles in the sample and the instrument’s resolution (Robinson et al., 2026). As manufacturers and emerging technologies move toward nanoscale and microscale applications, the effects of surface tension, hydrodynamic properties, and other small-scale microenvironmental forces become increasingly important on a per-particle basis. Applications such as flow virometry and resonant mass analysis of very small particles must account for factors such as flow pressure, buoyancy, surface tension, the flow medium, and other related factors (Tabler and Tilton, 2024).

Newer theories in flow engineering allow reducing the lower limit of detection by adding Resonance Mass Measurement (RMM). RMM is another validated tool (in addition to or in place of Zetasizer instruments) commonly used to measure quantity and size (Krueger et al., 2019). Particle buoyant mass is measured by the change in frequency of a resonator as particles interact with it. This measurement is based on Archimedes’ Principle, which describes the buoyant force on an object, accounting for hydrostatic pressure and the object’s surface tension. The addition of buoyancy separation to bacterial cell recombinants was successfully selected, with higher throughput efficiency than current flow cytometers (Schmitt et al., 2018). Subvisible cell phenotypes had different densities that were directly proportional to the formation of a reporter enzyme. These advances suggest that extremely small particles and small weight fluctuations can be appropriately gated, with consideration of Archimedes’ Principle.

The addition of BiFC components to L1 and L2 increases the total weight of L1/L2-containing capsids, producing a greater weight difference than that observed for L1-only particles or unmodified p16sheLL particles. A linear trend was observed in both modified and unmodified VLP total weight (kDa) across the minimum and maximum L2 composition range (Figure 32). Simple linear regression showed that both groups differed significantly from a slope of 0 (F = 204.5, DF = 1, 19, p < 0.0001), and that the slopes also differed significantly between groups (F (1,38) = 12.03, p = 0.0013). Within each group, rows were compared using a 2-way ANOVA (F (20,20) = 33). Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0024 indicated a significant difference in the weight of L1-only particles and particles with 360 L1 + 12 L2 molecules (minimum L1/L2 weight) in both groups. Although BiFC modification shifts the total weight profile of the VLP relative to the p16sheLL construct, the incremental difference per additional L2 molecule remains too small to support conventional size-based separation. Only the particles at either extreme (L1 only and L1 + 32 L2) are significantly different in weight enough to be detected by nano detection methods.

Charge-based separation potential using field flow fractionation

Field-Flow Fractionation is another candidate for modifying current flow cytometer approaches for nanoparticle sorting (Giordani et al., 2023). A significant difference in charge was observed between L1/L2 BiFC VLPs and the other groups (L1-only BiFC VLPs or p16sheLL VLPs), which could enable separation of fully formed capsids based on charge using adapted liquid chromatography techniques. At physiological pH (7.3–7.4), the overall charge of L1 without BiFC is ∼ -3, whereas the BiFC-modified L1 protein is approximately +27. The L2 protein also shifts in overall charge from −6 to −9 with BiFC addition. The overall charge of the BiFC-modified fully formed capsids is much more positive compared to the native negatively charged particles, and L1/L2 is significantly less than L1-only charged particles in the BiFC-modified construct.

Current flow cytometer and field-flow fractionation instruments also share similar principles of sample uptake and laminar flow, making combined or adapted workflows conceptually plausible. Separation of particles based on charge and molecular weight could be achieved by adding a charged membrane and a crossflow step following initial sample injection, but before fluorescent detection through the optical cell. Under these conditions, charge differences between L1-only and L1/L2-containing particles could promote retention of one population while allowing the other to pass through first, generating temporal and spatial separation within the membrane-containing region of the flow path. Subsequent FACS-based analysis on an instrument capable of nanoparticle detection could then provide secondary confirmation of a fully functional, separated VLP population.

An additional advantage of integrating these approaches is that the environmental forces acting on mesoscale particles would remain more consistent across sorting conditions, potentially reducing variability introduced by differences in sample preparation and storage between purification and downstream analytical methods. In the context of HPV16 VLP formation, the hydrostatic modifications provided by BiFC conjugation are potentially different enough from specialized separation of VLPs containing both L1 and L2 on the basis of overall particle charge.

Conclusion

This project demonstrated the production of correctly formed HPV16-like capsids modified with a fluorescent BiFC tag, with the potential to allow for particle monitoring and sorting applications. Overall, the incorporation of BiFC enabled efficient detection of intracellular VLP production in producer cell populations across multiple excitation/emission filter combinations and imaging platforms while preserving capsid formation and reducing aggregation relative to similar constructs. The findings suggest that this construct could offer practical advantages in studies requiring accessible monitoring of VLP assembly, characterization of mixed particle populations, and improved recovery of usable particles from aqueous suspension.

If further studies confirm immunogenicity and in vivo functionality, the BiFC-VLPs could provide a useful platform for vaccine-oriented applications. In that regard, these particles could potentially be used to protect against tumor development in tissues with similar extracellular matrix components by priming the adaptive immune system, as seen in previously mentioned vaccine models. Combining BiFC-VLPs with a reported chimeric L2 epitope-presenting L1 protein (Slupetzky et al., 2007; Sanchooli et al., 2018) may provide a route toward broader cross-type protection in addition to type-specific protection associated with L1. Since VLPs were confirmed to be empty of genetic material, they may serve as a foundation for future gene and chemotherapeutic packaging for targeted delivery. The reduced aggregation of BiFC-modified particles further suggests that packaged therapies may be distributed more uniformly across targeted cell populations, while improving flow properties and reducing technical limitations associated with large aggregates. Additionally, the lack of aggregation should also facilitate a higher yield of usable viruses suspended in aqueous solutions (required for vaccine dosing, storage, and administration).

Although flow virometry was not available in this study, the combined use of TEM and Zetasizer analyses strongly suggests that this new construct may support more accurate measurement and future sorting of particle populations, with possible advantages in flow rate and reduced clogging in downstream applications. This work establishes and validates a novel HPV-16 VLP with a fluorophore attachment, preserving protein loop domains and structural flexibility while expanding the particle’s potential utility for monitoring, sorting, and future translational applications.
