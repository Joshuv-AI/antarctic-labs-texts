A robust matrix-free approach for large-scale non-isothermal high-contrast viscosity Stokes flow on blended domains with applications to geophysics
Andreas Burkhart, Nils Kohl, Barbara Wohlmuth, Jan Zawallich

Abstract
We consider a compressible Stokes problem in the quasi-stationary case coupled with a time dependent advection-diffusion equation with special emphasis on high viscosity contrast geophysical mantle convection applications. In space, we use a P2-P1 Taylor–Hood element which is generated by a blending approach to account for the non-planar domain boundary without compromising the stencil data structure of uniformly refined elements. In time, we apply an operator splitting approach for the temperature equation combining the BDF2 method for diffusion and a particle method for advection, resulting in an overall second order scheme. Within each time step, a stationary Stokes problem with a high viscosity contrast has to be solved for which we propose a matrix-free, robust and scalable iterative solver based on Uzawa type block preconditioners, polynomial Chebyshev smoothers and a BFBT type Schur complement approximation. Our implementation is using a hybrid hierarchical grid approach allowing for massively parallel, high resolution Earth convection simulations.

Introduction

The Navier–Stokes equations coupled with an energy conservation equation
Gassmöller et al. 2020
can be used in geophysics to model the convective flow of the Earth’s mantle as a viscous fluid. Since mantle convection takes place on a very long timescale (
\approx 10^{8}
years), a common approach is to simplify the instationary Navier–Stokes equations to the stationary Stokes equations and argue that viscous relaxation and seismic waves effectively take place on a faster timescale than mantle convection
Gassmöller et al. 2020
. Further simplifications for a buoyancy driven fluid include the Boussinesq approximation
Boussinesq 1903
and the (truncated) anelastic liquid approximation
Jarvis and Mckenzie 1980
;
Schubert et al. 2001
.
Such simplified Earth convection models can be classified into compressible
Heister et al. 2017
;
Gassmöller et al. 2020
or incompressible
Kronbichler et al. 2012
;
Liu and King 2019
approaches. Many of the physical parameters driving the convective processes of a mantle convection simulation are hard to constrain and are thus only known up to a significant uncertainty. Prominent examples are the mantle viscosity for which estimates range from
10^{19}\operatorname{Pa}\operatorname{s}
in the asthenosphere to
10^{23}\operatorname{Pa}\operatorname{s}
in the lower mantle
(
Fowler 2004
, Chap. 5.7)
, and the temperature at the core mantle boundary (CMB) for which estimates range from
2750\operatorname{K}\pm 250\operatorname{K}

Jeanloz and Morris 1986
to
4500\operatorname{K}\pm 1000\operatorname{K}

da Silva et al. 2000
;
Jeanloz 1990
;
Alfè et al. 1999
.

Of particular geophysical interest in terms of understanding geothermal events and the vertical motion of the Earth’s lithosphere (uplift and subsidence)
Bunge and Glasmacher 2018
is the study of rising hot material (called
plumes
) and sinking cold material (called
slabs
) inside the Earth’s mantle. However, verifying the results of a given mantle convection model is a difficult task as we can only compare with observations based on the current state of the Earth’s mantle, e.g., via seismic tomography which in turn introduces further uncertainty
Freissler et al. 2024
. Comparing the result of a forward mantle convection simulation with real observations starting with our understanding of the mantle’s current state as an initial state is also infeasible due the extremely long time scales at play.

The aforementioned uncertainties and the fact that past states of the Earth’s mantle can no longer be observed, motivates the study of inverse mantle convection problems using the adjoint method to facilitate reconstructions of the Earth’s mantle going backwards in time
Colli et al. 2020
. Solving an inverse problem however typically includes a feedback loop using the result of forward model calculations to generate synthetic data and thus still requires fast solvers for the forward problem.

The viscosity inside the Earth’s mantle exhibits very steep gradients
(
Fowler 2004
, Chap. 5.7)
and in general does not only depend on the location but also on the pressure, temperature and strain rate
Gassmöller et al. 2020
. This results in high contrast nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE) models and thus poses a significant numerical and mathematical challenge in particular to the iterative solvers typically used
Kronbichler et al. 2012
;
Heister et al. 2017
;
Dannberg et al. 2022
;
Drzisga et al. 2018
;
Jodlbauer et al. 2024
;
Wichrowski and
Krzyżanowski 2022
.

In this work, we focus on a compressible forward mantle convection model using the truncated anelastic liquid approximation (TALA) coupled with a variation of the time dependent energy conservation equation presented in
Gassmöller et al. 2020
. We consider a space and temperature dependent nonlinear viscosity model. For the time dependent case, we use a semi-implicit time stepping scheme to linearize the resulting subproblems. In particular for the Stokes subproblem, we present an iterative flexible GMRES (FGMRES)
Saad 1993
outer Krylov solver preconditioned with an Uzawa type block preconditioner
Drzisga et al. 2018
. The individual solver parts use geometric multigrid (GMG) approaches where applicable and are implemented in a matrix-free fashion in the finite element framework HyTeG

Kohl et al. 2019

Starting from the coupled compressible Stokes equations presented in

All scalar values

Equations (

Spatial Discretisation

In the following, we describe the spatial discretisation, including how the Earth’s mantle is approximated via a mesh and blending, the finite element method, and the resulting discrete variatonal problems.

4.1
Mesh and Blending

We discretise
\Omega

Bergen et al. 2006

In case of the annulus, we start with the generation of an unstructured grid by dividing a hollow convex regular polygon into congruent isosceles trapezoids. The trapezoids are then divided into layers of equal height. The trapezoids in these layers are then in turn subdivided into triangles. The generation of the unstructured hollow spherical mesh works in a similar way. We start with a triangular truncated pyramid mesh, based on the hollow icosahedron. These truncated pyramids are again divided into layers of equal height into congruent truncated pyramids, which are then in turn subdivided into tetrahedrons in each layer.

\tilde{x}_{i}\in\tilde{\Omega},\,i\in I_{\tilde{x}}:=I_{\operatorname{Inner}}\dot{\cup}I_{\operatorname{Surf}}\dot{\cup}I_{\operatorname{CMB}},

(8)

which we split into inner, surface and CMB positions.

The Taylor–Hood element pairing as introduced in
Taylor and Hood 1973
, is known to be a discrete inf-sup stable pairing for the Stokes problem under certain conditions on the domain and mesh
Verfürth 1984
;
Girault and Raviart 1986
;
Ern and Guermond 2004
and is one of the default element choices for geophysical applications
Thieulot and Bangerth 2022
.

5
Discrete Stokes Problem and Solver

Taking into account blending, we define the discrete function spaces

\displaystyle V_{h}=\Big\{

\displaystyle v_{h}\mid v_{h}=\tilde{v}_{h}\circ B^{-1}\text{ for some }

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{h}\in\left(C^{0}\left(\tilde{\Omega}\right)\right)^{\operatorname{d}}\text{ such that }\forall\tilde{K}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\,\left.\tilde{v}_{h}\right|_{\tilde{K}}\in\left(\mathcal{P}_{2}\left(\tilde{K}\right)\right)^{\operatorname{d}}\Big\},

\displaystyle Q_{h}=\Big\{

\displaystyle q_{h}\mid q_{h}=\tilde{q}_{h}\circ B^{-1}\text{ for some }

\displaystyle W_{h}=\Big\{

\displaystyle w_{h}\mid w_{h}=\tilde{w}_{h}\circ B^{-1}\text{ for some }

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{h}\in C^{0}\left(\tilde{\Omega}\right)\text{ such that }\forall\tilde{K}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\,\left.\tilde{w}_{h}\right|_{\tilde{K}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left(\tilde{K}\right)\Big\},

with

representing the space of multivariate polynomials up to global degree

on an element
\tilde{K}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}

5.1
Linear algebraic formulation

We seek to find approximate solutions
u_{h}^{n+1}=\tilde{u}_{h}^{n+1}\circ B^{-1}\in V_{h}
,
p_{h}^{n+1}\in Q_{h}
to (
7
) that satisfy the boundary and pressure constraints

\displaystyle\forall i\in I_{\operatorname{CMB}}\,\left.\left(u_{h}^{n+1}\cdot n\right)\right|_{B\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right)}=0,\,\,\,\forall i\in I_{\operatorname{Surf}}\,\tilde{u}^{n+1}_{h}\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right)=u_{\operatorname{Surf}}\left(B\left(\tilde{x}_{i}\right)\right),\,\,\,\int_{\Omega}p_{h}^{n+1}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}=0.

Let
\varphi_{0}^{u},\ldots,\varphi_{N_{u}-1}^{u}
and
\varphi_{0}^{p},\ldots,\varphi_{N_{p}-1}^{p}
denote the velocity and pressure nodal basis functions associated with the blended Taylor–Hood element on
\Omega
.

Neglecting the boundary conditions at first, the discrete variational Stokes problem can be written as a linear system using
{\textbf{f}}^{\,n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
,
{\textbf{g}}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}}
,
{B},{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times N_{u}}
, and symmetric, positive semidefinite
{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}\times N_{u}}
, where

\displaystyle{A}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}2\eta\left(x,T_{h}^{n+1}\right)\nabla_{s}\varphi_{j}^{u}:\nabla_{s}\varphi_{i}^{u}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}-\frac{2}{\operatorname{d}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\cdot\varphi_{j}^{u}\right)\left(\nabla\cdot\varphi_{i}^{u}\right)\operatorname{d\text{$x$}},

\displaystyle{B}_{ij}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\cdot\varphi_{j}^{u}\right)\varphi_{i}^{p}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}},\quad{C}_{ij}=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\ln\rho\cdot\varphi_{j}^{u}\varphi_{i}^{p}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}},

\displaystyle{\textbf{f}}^{\,n+1}_{i}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\text{Ra}}{\text{Pe}}\rho\alpha{T_{d,h}^{n+1}}g\cdot\varphi_{i}^{u}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}},

resulting in an asymmetrical generalised saddle point problem with block structure

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{A}&{B}^{T}\\
{B}+{C}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\,\,{\textbf{u}}^{n+1}\,\,\\
\,\,{\textbf{p}}^{n+1}\,\,\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\,\,{\textbf{f}}^{\,n+1}\,\,\,\\
\,\,{\textbf{g}}\,\,\,\end{pmatrix}

(9)

and coefficient vectors
{\textbf{u}}^{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
,
{\textbf{p}}^{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}}
.

As a second step, we assume the velocity degrees of freedom to be ordered such that, similar to (
8
), we can split up

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{n+1}=:\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix},\quad{\textbf{f}}^{\,n+1}=:\begin{pmatrix}{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{\,n+1}\\[2.0pt]
{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{\,n+1}\\[2.0pt]
{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{\,n+1}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

between surface, CMB and inner degrees with
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{n+1},{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{\,n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Surf}}\right|}
,
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{n+1},{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{\,n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{CMB}}\right|}
and
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{n+1},{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{\,n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Inner}}\right|}
, respectively. Splitting up

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{A}_{SS}&{A}_{SC}&{A}_{SI}\\
{A}_{CS}&{A}_{CC}&{A}_{CI}\\
{A}_{IS}&{A}_{IC}&{A}_{II}\\
\end{pmatrix}:={A},\quad\par\begin{pmatrix}{B}_{IS}&{B}_{IC}&{B}_{II}\end{pmatrix}:={B},\quad\par\begin{pmatrix}{C}_{IS}&{C}_{IC}&{C}_{II}\end{pmatrix}:={C},

into blocks of matching dimensions results in a block system matrix of the form

\displaystyle{M}:=\begin{pmatrix}{A}_{SS}&{A}_{SC}&{A}_{SI}&{B}_{IS}^{T}\\[2.0pt]
{A}_{CS}&{A}_{CC}&{A}_{CI}&{B}_{IC}^{T}\\[2.0pt]
{A}_{IS}&{A}_{IC}&{A}_{II}&{B}_{II}^{T}\\[2.0pt]
\bar{B}_{IS}&\bar{B}_{IC}&\bar{B}_{II}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(N_{u}+N_{p}\right)\times\left(N_{u}+N_{p}\right)}.

(11)

with
\bar{B}:=B+C\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times N_{u}}
.

The pressure solution is only determined up to a constant (compare
Ern and Guermond 2004
;
Boffi et al. 2008
) and the surface normal on the CMB in general does not align with the coordinate axes. Hence the free-slip boundary condition cannot be enforced individually in each dimension and needs to be treated in a different fashion than the surface boundary condition. To address this, as a final step, we introduce a discrete projection operator

\displaystyle P:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{S}&0&0&0\\
0&P_{v}&0&0\\
0&0&I_{I}&0\\
0&0&0&P_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(N_{u}+N_{p}\right)\times\left(N_{u}+N_{p}\right)}

(12)

with identity matrices
I_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Surf}}\right|\right)\times\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Surf}}\right|\right)}
,
I_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Inner}}\right|\right)\times\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{Inner}}\right|\right)}
and projections
P_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times N_{p}}
,
P_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{CMB}}\right|\right)\times\left(\operatorname{d}\left|I_{\operatorname{CMB}}\right|\right)}
, respectively ensuring a unique pressure solution and that the free-slip boundary condition at the CMB is discretely fulfilled. Note that in practice
P_{p}
is implemented such that it only enforces that the vector
\textbf{p}^{n+1}
has arithmetic mean zero. This saves computational effort during the solving process and an additive constant
c_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
to ensure
\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{h}^{n+1}+c_{p}\right)\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}=0
can be determined in a post processing step.

Applying
P
to
{M}
in a similar fashion to a left and right preconditioner, whilst realising the Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{\operatorname{Surf}}
by a standard block row modification results in a system of the form

\displaystyle P\begin{pmatrix}I_{S}&0&0&0\\[2.0pt]
{A}_{CS}&{A}_{CC}&{A}_{CI}&{B}_{IC}^{T}\\[2.0pt]
{A}_{IS}&{A}_{IC}&{A}_{II}&{B}_{II}^{T}\\[2.0pt]
\bar{B}_{IS}&\bar{B}_{IC}&\bar{B}_{II}&0\end{pmatrix}P\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{p}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=P\begin{pmatrix}{\textbf{u}}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{\text{Int}}\\[2.0pt]
{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{CMB}}^{\,n+1}\\[2.0pt]
{\textbf{f}}_{\operatorname{Inner}}^{\,n+1}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{g}\end{pmatrix}

(13)

where
{\textbf{u}}_{\operatorname{Surf}}^{\text{Int}}
denotes the interpolation
I_{h}\left(u_{\operatorname{Surf}}\right)
of the surface boundary condition. Note that
\textbf{u}^{n+1}
and
\textbf{p}^{n+1}
in (
13
) are only determined up to the projections
P_{v}
and
P_{p}
, respectively, hence (
13

(
Saad 1993
, Sec. 2.1)
and starting with a consistent (projected) initial guess and right-hand side, this problem is circumvented, leading the approximated solution to automatically fulfill the discrete free-slip boundary condition and to have pressures of mean zero. This approach however requires careful application of the pressure and free-slip projections throughout the solver implementation. In particular, we apply these projections as part of the block preconditioner (see Sec.
5.2.1
), multigrid prolongation, restriction and smoother (see Sec.
5.2.2
).

5.2.1
Uzawa Type Block Preconditioners

We make use of Uzawa type block preconditioners
Drzisga et al. 2018
for the compressible Stokes system. In particular, we consider the inexact Uzawa, adjoint inexact Uzawa, and symmetric Uzawa block preconditioners
(
Drzisga et al. 2018
, Sec. 3)
, as defined for a symmetrical saddle point system, i.e., for
C=0
. Omitting the time step for a clearer notation, a single update step of these block preconditioners for the saddle point system can be written as
(
Drzisga et al. 2018
, Sec. 3.1)
:

Inexact Uzawa:

\begin{split}&\textbf{u}_{k+1}:=\textbf{u}_{k}+\sigma\hat{A}^{-1}\left(\textbf{f}-A\textbf{u}_{k}-B^{T}\textbf{p}_{k}\right)\\
&\textbf{p}_{k+1}=\textbf{p}_{k}-\omega\hat{S}^{-1}\left(\textbf{g}-B\textbf{u}_{k+1}\right)\end{split}

(16)

Adjoint inexact Uzawa:

\begin{split}&\textbf{p}_{k+1}=\textbf{p}_{k}-\omega\hat{S}^{-1}\left(\textbf{g}-B\textbf{u}_{k}\right)\\
&\textbf{u}_{k+1}:=\textbf{u}_{k}+\sigma\hat{A}^{-1}\left(\textbf{f}-A\textbf{u}_{k}-B^{T}\textbf{p}_{k+1}\right)\end{split}

(17)

Symmetric Uzawa:

\begin{split}&\hat{\textbf{u}}:=\textbf{u}_{k}+\sigma\hat{A}^{-1}\left(\textbf{f}-A\textbf{u}_{k}-B^{T}\textbf{p}_{k}\right)\\
&\textbf{p}_{k+1}=\textbf{p}_{k}-\omega\hat{S}^{-1}\left(\textbf{g}-B\hat{\textbf{u}}\right)\\
&\textbf{u}_{k+1}:=\hat{\textbf{u}}+\sigma\hat{A}^{-1}\left(\textbf{f}-A\hat{\textbf{u}}-B^{T}\textbf{p}_{k+1}\right)\end{split}

(18)

Here,
\hat{A}
and
\hat{S}
are representing nonsingular approximations of
A
and the Schur complement
S=BA^{-1}B^{T}
with respect to the symmetrical system.
The Uzawa method can also be used as a saddle point system solver
Elman and Golub 1994
and smoother
Drzisga et al. 2018
. The adjoint inexact Uzawa matches the block triangular preconditioner used in
Kronbichler et al. 2012
. Regarding the convergence of the block preconditioner, the relaxation parameters
\omega,\sigma>0
should be chosen appropriately such that
\sigma\hat{A}\geq A
and
\omega\hat{S}\geq S

Drzisga et al. 2018
;
Jodlbauer et al. 2024
. Note that in the block preconditioners above,
B
can be replaced by
B+C
while not replacing
B^{T}
by
(B+C)^{T}
, resulting in a non-symmetrical approach with a slightly higher convergence rate for some examples.

5.2.2
Approximation of

In order to approximate

On the coarsest grid, we use a non-matrix free CG solver preconditioned by an algebraic multigrid (AMG) method implemented in the freely available software package PETSc

under a 2-clause BSD license.

5.2.3
Approximation of the Inverse Schur Complement

A common choice, see
Burstedde et al. 2008
;
Geenen et al. 2009
;
Burstedde et al. 2009
;
Furuichi et al. 2011
;
Kronbichler et al. 2012
;
Burstedde et al. 2013
;
May et al. 2015
;
Isaac et al. 2015
;
Gmeiner et al. 2016
, for the Schur complement approximation
\hat{S}
in case of sufficiently smooth viscosities, is the inverse-viscosity scaled mass matrix
M_{1/\eta}
(or a lumped version of it), i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{M}^{-1}:=M^{-1}_{1/\eta}.

(19)

The mass approximation was shown to be spectrally equivalent to the Schur complement in the isoviscous
Verfürth 1984
and variable viscosity case
Grinevich and
Olshanskii 2009
, but is known to deliver poor convergence results in case of high viscosity contrasts
Rudi et al. 2015
;
Rudi et al. 2017
. In our tests, we solve
\hat{S}_{M}
up to a relative tolerance of
\operatorname{tol}_{\operatorname{invMass}}>0
.

Another choice are BFBT (or least-squares commutator) type inverse Schur complement approximations
Elman 1999
;
Elman et al. 2005
;
Elman et al. 2006
;
May and Moresi 2008
;
Furuichi et al. 2011
;
Isaac et al. 2015
;
Rudi et al. 2015
;
Rudi et al. 2017
of the form

\displaystyle(BA^{-1}B^{T})^{-1}=\left(B{C}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(B{C}^{-1}A{D}^{-1}B^{T}\right)\left(B{D}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}

for suitable symmetric positive definite matrices
C,D
. Prominent examples are the diag(A)-BFBT approximation, where
C=D=\operatorname{diag}(A)
, and the weighted BFBT approximation

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{w}^{-1}:=\left(B{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(B{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}^{-1}A{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}^{-1}B^{T}\right)\left(B{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}

(20)

with
{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}
as the (potentially lumped) vector mass scaled with the square root of the viscosity, which have been shown to be a suitable and algorithmically robust choice, especially for geodynamic applications with high viscosity contrasts
Rudi et al. 2015
;
Rudi et al. 2017
. In particular, the weighted BFBT approximation has been shown to preserve its efficacy in combination with higher order space discretisations using an asymmetric rescaling of the viscosity close to Dirichlet boundaries for mesh independent algorithmic robustness
Rudi et al. 2017
. Similar to
Rudi et al. 2015
;
Rudi et al. 2017
, we replace
\left(B{M}_{\sqrt{\eta}}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}
by solving an inverse viscosity scaled Poisson problem with Neumann boundary conditions up to a relative or absolute tolerance of
\operatorname{tol}_{\operatorname{wBFBT}}>0
(whichever applies first) with a CG solver preconditioned by a GMG approach which we denote by
K^{-1}_{1/\sqrt{\eta}}
and introduce a potentially asymmetric scaling of the viscosity near Dirichlet boundaries via

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{w}^{-1}\approx K_{1/\sqrt{\eta_{l}}}^{-1}\left(B{M}_{\sqrt{\eta_{l}}}^{-1}A{M}_{\sqrt{\eta_{r}}}^{-1}B^{T}\right)K_{1/\sqrt{\eta_{r}}}^{-1},

where

\displaystyle\eta_{l}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}a_{l}^{2}\eta\left(x\right),&x\in D_{\eta}\\
\eta\left(x\right),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\eta_{r}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}a_{r}^{2}\eta\left(x\right),&x\in D_{\eta}\\
\eta\left(x\right),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for some
a_{l},a_{r}>0
. Here,
D_{\eta}=\bigcup\left\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\partial K\cap\Gamma_{\operatorname{Surf}}\neq\emptyset\right\}
. The scaled vector masses
{M}_{\sqrt{\eta_{r}}}
and
{M}_{\sqrt{\eta_{l}}}
are solved up to a relative tolerance of
\operatorname{tol}_{\operatorname{VectorMass}}>0
using a CG solver preconditioned by a GMG approach.

We propose a V-cycle BFBT variation of the diag(A)-BFBT approximation

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{V}^{-1}:=\left(B\hat{A}_{C}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(B\hat{A}_{C}^{-1}A\hat{A}_{C}^{-1}B^{T}\right)\left(B\hat{A}_{C}^{-1}B^{T}\right)^{-1},

(21)

replacing
\operatorname{diag}\left(A\right)
with
\hat{A}_{C}
, representing a comparatively computationally cheap
A
block GMG V-cycle using only
m_{V}=1
pre- and post smoothing step with a Chebyshev smoother of degree
\deg_{V}=1
. On the coarse grid, we again solve up to a relative tolerance of
\operatorname{tol}_{\operatorname{coarse}}>0
.

In order to apply a V-cycle BFBT approximation, it is still necessary to solve an approximate Schur complement operator of the form
B\hat{A}_{C}^{-1}B^{T}
. Since a relatively large tolerance
\operatorname{tol}_{\operatorname{V-BFBT}}\approx 0.1
is sufficient for a solver suitable for geophysical applications with realistic viscosity contrasts, we use a CG solver preconditioned by
M_{1/\eta}
which can be efficiently solved up to this comparatively large relative tolerance.

The combination of an outer Krylov solver loop and a block preconditioner is a well-known solver structure that has been successfully used in the past to solve Stokes problems (compare
Kronbichler et al. 2012
,
Heister et al. 2017
) and generalised Stokes problems
Jodlbauer et al. 2024
, even in a high contrast setting
Wichrowski and

.

6
Discrete Temperature Problem and Solver

Similar to the weak formulation of (
7
), dividing (
5
) by
\rho\left(x\right)C^{p}
, multiplying by a test function
w\in H_{0}^{1}\left(\Omega\right)
,
integrating over the domain, using integration by parts, using the Gauß divergence theorem, and eliminating the boundary integral of the diffusion term due to the vanishing boundary of the test function, admits a variational problem of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}s^{n+1}T^{n+1}w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par+\tau^{n+1}\Bigg(\par\int_{\Omega}u_{*}^{n+1}\cdot\nabla T^{n+1}w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par+\int_{\Omega}\frac{k}{\operatorname{Pe}C^{p}}\nabla T^{n+1}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{w}{\rho\left(x\right)}\right)\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\operatorname{Di}\alpha}{C^{p}}T^{n+1}\left(u_{*}^{n+1}\cdot g\right)w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par\Bigg)\par=\tau^{n+1}\Bigg(\par\int_{\Omega}s^{n}\hat{T}^{n}w_{h}\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par-\int_{\Omega}s^{n-1}\hat{T}^{n-1}w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{H}{C^{p}}w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\operatorname{Pe}\operatorname{Di}}{\operatorname{Ra}\rho\left(x\right)C^{p}}2\eta\left(x,T_{*}^{n+1}\right)\dot{\varepsilon}\left(u_{*}^{n+1}\right):\dot{\varepsilon}\left(u_{*}^{n+1}\right)w\operatorname{d\text{$x$}}\par\Bigg)

(22)

for all
w\in H_{0}^{1}\left(\Omega\right)
with

\displaystyle s^{n+1}:=\frac{2\tau^{n+1}+\tau^{n}}{\tau^{n+1}+\tau^{n}}\par,\quad\par s^{n}:=\frac{\tau^{n+1}+\tau^{n}}{\tau^{n}}\par,\quad\par s^{n-1}:=\frac{\tau^{n+1}\cdot\tau^{n+1}}{\tau^{n}\left(\tau^{n+1}+\tau^{n}\right)}.

Replacing
u_{*}^{n+1}\in V
,
T^{n+1},T_{*}^{n+1},\hat{T}^{n},\hat{T}^{n-1}\in W
in (
5
) by
u_{*,h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
,
T_{h}^{n+1}
,
T_{*,h}^{n+1}
,
\hat{T}_{h}^{n}
,
\hat{T}_{h}^{n-1}\in W_{h}
and the test function
w
by

\displaystyle w_{h}\in W_{0,h}:=\Big\{

\displaystyle g_{h}\mid g_{h}=\tilde{g}_{h}\circ B^{-1}\text{ for some }\tilde{g}_{h}\in C^{0}\left(\tilde{\Omega}\right)\text{ with }

\displaystyle\left.\tilde{g}_{h}\right|_{\left(\partial\tilde{\Omega}\right)}=0,\left.\tilde{g}_{h}\right|_{\tilde{K}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left(\tilde{K}\right)\,\forall\tilde{K}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\Big\}

leads to the discrete variational advection diffusion problem we solve in practice.

Given
u_{*,h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
and
T_{*,h}^{n+1},\hat{T}_{h}^{n},\hat{T}_{h}^{n-1}\in W_{h}
, we seek to find an approximation
T_{h}^{n+1}\in W_{h}
, that fulfills the discrete variational advection diffusion problem for all test functions
w_{h}\in W_{0,h}
.

To solve the linear system arising from the discrete variational advection diffusion problem, we use an FGMRES outer loop preconditioned by a CG solver solving the symmetric, positive semidefinite mass and diffusion part of the left-hand-side. The advection-diffusion system is solved up to an absolute tolerance of
\operatorname{tol}_{T}>0
. The application of the preconditioner is carried out up to machine precision in an exact way in each FGMRES iteration. The Dirichlet boundary conditions at the surface and CMB are again enforced in a similar manner to (
13
).

8
Conclusion

In this study, we investigated numerical schemes to solve the TALA, a common approach to model the convective flow in the Earth’s mantle. While approximating the spatial structure via blending, the proposed approach uses a splitting method between the time dependent equation for the temperature and the time independent Stokes system for the velocity and the pressure. Our approach combines a BDF2 scheme with variable time step resulting in a second order convergence in time. While a frozen velocity approach for tackling the compressible case is quite popular, we use the physically more accurate implicit version of the compressible mass balance. Although this results in a non-symmetric off-diagonal block in the saddle point Stokes formulation, our iterative solver is robust with respect to this term. The overall code scales well enough such that high-resolution simulations are possible. Solving the saddle point problem with
2.11\cdot 10^{9}
unknowns while having
6.7\cdot 10^{8}
unknowns in the temperature needs 69% of the overall computation time. Therefore, different approaches to solve the saddle point problem have been investigated. They showed that when only a small error reduction is needed per time step, all solvers perform well, while a larger reduction is fastest reached with the V-cycle BFBT approximation. High-resolution simulations with
2.7\cdot 10^{9}
degrees of freedom in space and 6166 steps in time showed the effect of plate movement on the creation of slabs, plumes, and convection cells.

11.3
Nondimensionalisation of the Governing Equations

Starting with a rescaling of the spatial and temporal variables in
11.3.1
, we provide the nondimensional form of momentum, mass and energy balance equations in
11.3.2
,
11.3.3
and
11.3.4
, respectively.

11.3.1
Spatial and Temporal Derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}=\frac{1}{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{i}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\nabla=\frac{1}{d}\tilde{\nabla}:=\frac{1}{d}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_{\operatorname{d}}}\end{pmatrix}\par

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\tilde{t}}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{t}}=\frac{u_{0}}{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{t}}

(25)

11.3.2
Momentum Conservation Equation

We start with the dimensional momentum conservation equation

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\tau+\nabla p_{d}+\rho\alpha T_{d}g=0.

(26)

Using (
25
), we get

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\tau

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\eta\left(\nabla u+\left(\nabla u\right)^{T}-\frac{2}{\operatorname{d}}\left(\nabla\cdot u\right)I\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{u_{0}\eta_{0}}{d^{2}}\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\eta}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{u}+\left(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{u}\right)^{T}-\frac{2}{\operatorname{d}}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{u}\right)I\right)

\displaystyle=:-\frac{u_{0}\eta_{0}}{d^{2}}\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\tau},

\displaystyle\nabla p_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}\eta_{0}}{d^{2}}\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{p}_{d},

\displaystyle\rho\alpha T^{d}g

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}\eta_{0}}{d^{2}}\frac{\operatorname{Ra}}{\operatorname{Pe}}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{T}^{d}\tilde{g}.

After dividing by
\frac{u_{0}\eta_{0}}{d^{2}}
and dropping the tilde in notation, the nondimensional momentum conservation equation reads

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\tau+\nabla p_{d}+\frac{\operatorname{Ra}}{\operatorname{Pe}}\rho\alpha T^{d}g=0.

(27)

11.3.3
Mass Conservation Equation

We start with the dimensional mass conservation equation

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot u-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\cdot u=0.

(28)

Using (
25
), we get

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot u=-\frac{u_{0}}{d}\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{u},\quad\quad\quad-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\cdot u=-\frac{u_{0}}{d}\frac{\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\rho}}{\tilde{\rho}}\cdot\tilde{u}.

After dividing by
\frac{u_{0}}{d}
and dropping the tilde in notation the nondimensional mass conservation equation reads

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot u-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\cdot u=0.

(29)

11.3.4
Energy Conservation Equation

We start with the dimensional energy conservation equation

\displaystyle\rho C^{p}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla T\right)-\nabla\cdot\left(k\nabla T\right)-\alpha T\rho\left(u\cdot g\right)-\rho H-\tau:\varepsilon=0.

(30)

Using (
25
), we get

\displaystyle\rho C^{p}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla T\right)=\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{C}^{p}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{T}}{\partial\tilde{t}}+\tilde{u}\cdot\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{T}\right),

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(k\nabla T\right)=-\frac{k_{0}\Delta T}{d^{2}}\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\left(\tilde{k}\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{T}\right)=-\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}\frac{1}{\operatorname{Pe}}\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\left(\tilde{k}\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{T}\right),

\displaystyle-\tau:\varepsilon=-\frac{\eta_{0}u_{0}^{2}}{d^{2}}\tilde{\tau}:\tilde{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}\frac{\operatorname{Pe}\operatorname{Di}}{\operatorname{Ra}}\tilde{\tau}:\tilde{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle-\alpha T\rho\left(u\cdot g\right)=-\alpha_{0}\Delta T\rho_{0}u_{0}\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{T}\tilde{\rho}\left(\tilde{u}\cdot\tilde{g}\right)=-\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}\operatorname{Di}\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{T}\tilde{\rho}\left(\tilde{u}\cdot\tilde{g}\right),

\displaystyle-\rho H=-\rho_{0}H_{0}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{H}=-\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{H}.

After dividing by
\frac{\rho_{0}C^{p}_{0}\Delta Tu_{0}}{d}
and dropping the tilde in notation, the nondimensional energy conservation equation reads

\displaystyle\rho C^{p}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla T\right)-\frac{1}{\operatorname{Pe}}\nabla\cdot\left(k\nabla T\right)-\operatorname{Di}\alpha T\rho\left(u\cdot g\right)-\rho H-\frac{\operatorname{Pe}\operatorname{Di}}{\operatorname{Ra}}\tau:\varepsilon=0.

(31)
