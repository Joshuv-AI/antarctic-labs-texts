The Metabolic Legacy of Acute Pancreatitis: Post-Pancreatitis Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Serge Chooklin, Serhii Chuklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus after acute pancreatitis (PPDM-A) is increasingly recognized as a distinct metabolic complication rather than a simple subtype of type 2 diabetes. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on PPDM-A, with emphasis on terminology, diagnostic timing, epidemiology, pathophysiology, risk factors, clinical phenotypes, screening, prevention, and long-term follow-up. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies. PPDM-A may develop after mild, moderately severe, or severe acute pancreatitis, although the risk is greatest in patients with pancreatic necrosis, recurrent attacks, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, obesity, dyslipidemia, fatty liver disease, metabolic comorbidities, and marked in-hospital glycemic variability. A major diagnostic challenge is distinguishing transient stress hyperglycemia and previously unrecognized diabetes from incident PPDM-A. Formal diagnosis should generally be established no earlier than 90 days after the index episode of acute pancreatitis to minimize misclassification due to transient stress-related dysglycemia. Follow-up assessment should combine fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c, whereas a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may provide greater sensitivity during early recovery, particularly when HbA1c is unreliable, results are borderline or discordant, or isolated postprandial dysglycemia is suspected. Current evidence indicates that PPDM-A develops through interacting pathogenic domains, including pancreatic endocrine injury with impaired β-cell reserve, metabolic dysregulation characterized by insulin resistance and persistent inflammation, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction with nutritional consequences, and emerging mechanisms involving gut microbiome alterations, bile acid–FGF19 signaling, and extracellular vesicle-mediated communication. Available data support a trajectory-based model in which some patients recover normal glucose metabolism, some develop persistent intermediate dysglycemia, and others progress to overt diabetes over months or years. Structured post-discharge surveillance, risk-stratified follow-up, assessment of exocrine dysfunction, and prevention of recurrent pancreatic injury are essential for improving early detection, reducing diagnostic misclassification, and optimizing long-term clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is increasingly recognized not as an exclusively acute and self-limiting condition, but as a disease that may initiate persistent metabolic, functional, and systemic consequences. Among these, post-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus following AP (PPDM-A) is particularly important because of its frequency, diagnostic complexity, and unfavorable clinical course. In a nationwide population-based cohort, patients with post-pancreatitis diabetes had higher risks of severe hypoglycemia and all-cause mortality than those with type 2 diabetes, supporting the concept that diabetes arising after pancreatitis represents a distinct clinical phenotype rather than simply coincidental or misclassified type 2 diabetes.1

The long-term burden of AP extends beyond disturbances in glucose metabolism. Population-based evidence indicates that mortality may remain elevated after resolution of the acute episode,2 while systematic evidence demonstrates persistent impairment in several domains of health-related quality of life, including physical functioning, bodily pain, general health, vitality, and mental health.3 PPDM-A may further contribute to this burden through the need for prolonged metabolic monitoring, pharmacological treatment, management of hypoglycemia, and assessment of concomitant exocrine pancreatic dysfunction and nutritional abnormalities. These consequences are likely to increase healthcare utilization and reduce patients’ ability to resume normal social and occupational activities. Nevertheless, the direct economic burden attributable specifically to PPDM-A has not been adequately quantified, representing an important area for future health-economic research.

A foundational review by Petrov characterized post-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus as a clinically important form of secondary diabetes and summarized its epidemiology, risk factors, pathogenesis, and management.4 However, several gaps remain between this conceptual framework and its application specifically to patients recovering from AP. First, previous literature has frequently considered diabetes secondary to acute and chronic pancreatitis together, despite differences in their clinical course and opportunities for early surveillance. Second, inconsistent use of the terms new-onset diabetes after pancreatitis, post-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus, type 3c diabetes, and PPDM-A continues to limit comparability between studies. Third, earlier syntheses could not incorporate the more recent prospective evidence on the timing and evolution of dysglycemia after AP, emerging prediction models, or translational data concerning inflammatory, metabolic, exocrine, body-composition, and microbiome-related pathways. Consequently, there remains a need for an AP-specific synthesis that connects current biological evidence with practical decisions concerning diagnostic timing, risk stratification, and longitudinal follow-up.

The timing of diabetes assessment is central to the identification of PPDM-A. Hyperglycemia detected during hospitalization or shortly after AP may reflect a pre-existing but previously unrecognized disorder, transient stress hyperglycemia, nutritional support, medication effects, or persistent diabetes initiated or unmasked by pancreatic injury. To reduce this diagnostic uncertainty, PPDM-A should generally be classified only after a post-acute interval of at least 90 days, when the immediate inflammatory and metabolic disturbances have subsided. Prospective multicenter evidence based on serial glycemic assessment indicates that incident diabetes may become detectable at different time points during the first year after AP.5 A single normal test early in recovery therefore does not reliably exclude subsequent dysglycemia. HbA1c is practical for longitudinal surveillance, whereas fasting plasma glucose and the oral glucose tolerance test may provide complementary information when HbA1c is inconclusive or when early postprandial dysglycemia is suspected. These considerations support repeated rather than one-time assessment after AP.

PPDM-A is also mechanistically heterogeneous. The relative contribution of pancreatic endocrine injury, systemic and local inflammation, insulin resistance, altered incretin responses, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, nutritional disturbances, changes in body composition, and pre-existing metabolic susceptibility may vary among patients. Because the exocrine and endocrine compartments of the pancreas are functionally interdependent, exocrine dysfunction after AP may coexist with endocrine impairment; maldigestion, nutritional deficiencies, and altered incretin signaling may further destabilize glucose homeostasis.4 Importantly, the evidentiary strength of the proposed mechanisms is not equivalent. Clinical cohort data most consistently support the roles of endocrine pancreatic injury, inflammatory and metabolic stress, exocrine dysfunction, and adverse metabolic phenotypes. Experimental findings concerning differential islet injury and glucose-homeostasis responses provide biological support for heterogeneity in disease development.6 By contrast, microbiome-derived signatures and other multi-omic biomarkers, although promising for risk prediction and mechanistic characterization, remain translational and are not yet sufficiently validated for routine clinical decision-making.7

The epidemiology of PPDM-A also requires cautious interpretation. Reported incidence estimates vary substantially across studies because of differences in AP etiology and severity, baseline metabolic characteristics, exclusion of pre-existing diabetes, diagnostic criteria, timing and frequency of testing, duration of follow-up, and access to post-discharge care.8 For clinical context, a widely cited systematic review and meta-analysis estimated a pooled incidence of diabetes after AP of 23% (95% CI 13–35%), although between-study heterogeneity was substantial.9 Such methodological heterogeneity makes it difficult to determine how much of the observed variation represents true geographical or population-level differences. Evidence from regions with limited access to structured metabolic follow-up remains particularly sparse, raising the possibility that PPDM-A is underdiagnosed in routine practice. Accordingly, harmonized definitions, standardized follow-up intervals, and broader geographical representation are needed to establish the global burden of PPDM-A more accurately.

Against this background, the present review provides an updated, AP-specific synthesis of PPDM-A. Its principal contribution is the integration of diagnostic timing, longitudinal glycemic trajectories, clinically supported risk factors, mechanistic evidence of differing maturity, and emerging prediction approaches within a single clinically oriented framework. The review aims to clarify terminology and the ≥90-day diagnostic principle; summarize current evidence on incidence, risk factors, and geographical variability; distinguish established mechanisms from exploratory translational findings; and outline a risk-stratified approach to metabolic surveillance after AP. By linking recent evidence to practical post-discharge decision-making, this review seeks to support earlier recognition of PPDM-A while avoiding the premature classification of transient peri-acute hyperglycemia as persistent diabetes.

Methods

This article was developed as a narrative, evidence-based review focused specifically on PPDM-A as a distinct clinical entity. The objective was to synthesize current epidemiological, mechanistic, and clinically relevant evidence concerning dysglycemia developing after AP and to interpret these findings within contemporary diabetes-classification frameworks and current diagnostic standards.

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar for publications from January 2010 through July 2026. Earlier landmark publications were eligible for inclusion when identified through reference-list screening or citation tracking and considered necessary to explain the development of terminology, classification, or clinical concepts. The search strategy used PubMed Medical Subject Headings, where applicable, and database-appropriate free-text terms. The principal search concept combined “acute pancreatitis” with terms related to subsequent glycemic abnormalities, including “post-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus”, “PPDM-A”, “new-onset diabetes after acute pancreatitis”, “incident diabetes after acute pancreatitis”, “prediabetes after acute pancreatitis”, “dysglycemia after acute pancreatitis”, and “glucose metabolism after acute pancreatitis”. Additional targeted searches combined “acute pancreatitis” with “glycemic variability”, “pancreatic necrosis”, “exocrine pancreatic insufficiency”, “risk factors”, “prediction”, “screening”, “surveillance”, “treatment”, and “outcomes”. Search terms and syntax were adapted to the indexing and search functions of each database (Supplementary Table S1).

The electronic searches identified 288 records: 142 from PubMed/MEDLINE, 97 from Scopus, and 49 from Google Scholar. After the removal of 88 duplicate records, 200 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 106 records were excluded because they did not address diabetes or persistent disturbances of glucose metabolism developing after AP. These records predominantly concerned chronic pancreatitis without separately reported AP data, diabetes following pancreatic surgery, pancreatic cancer-associated diabetes, pre-existing diabetes as a risk factor for AP, or other AP outcomes without post-acute glycemic assessment. Ninety-four potentially relevant publications were retained for full-text assessment.

To improve coverage of the heterogeneous terminology used for PPDM-A and to identify relevant sources that were not retrieved by the primary electronic search, the reference lists and forward citations of key eligible publications, foundational reviews, and relevant clinical guidelines were examined. Targeted supplementary searches were also undertaken for specific diagnostic, mechanistic, and management questions addressed in the review. These supplementary methods identified 49 additional sources for detailed assessment. Consequently, 143 full-text sources were evaluated during the overall selection process.

Full-text eligibility was determined according to the relevance of the population, exposure, outcomes, and reported data to the objectives of the review. Fifty-three sources were not retained in the final synthesis. The principal reasons for exclusion were the absence of separately extractable AP-specific findings; assessment limited to acute-phase stress hyperglycemia without adequate post-discharge follow-up; insufficient information on the timing or definition of diabetes; predominant focus on another pancreatic disease or intervention; substantial overlap with a more complete publication; or limited additional contribution beyond evidence already represented by more comprehensive sources. Clinical studies were eligible when they included individuals with documented AP and reported newly detected dysglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes, new-onset diabetes after pancreatitis, new-onset prediabetes after pancreatitis, PPDM, or PPDM-A during follow-up. Although the review primarily addressed adult populations, studies involving pediatric, adolescent, or mixed-age populations were eligible when they provided directly relevant evidence regarding age-specific risk, diagnosis, or outcomes. Translational and mechanistic investigations were eligible when they directly addressed endocrine dysfunction, glucose metabolism, or putative pathways linking AP with subsequent dysglycemia.

Eligible evidence included prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, clinical trials, translational or mechanistic investigations, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus documents, and clinical guidelines. Sources addressing broader pancreatogenic diabetes, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, diabetes classification, glucose-monitoring standards, or long-term consequences of AP were included as contextual evidence when they contributed directly to the interpretation, diagnosis, surveillance, or management of PPDM-A.

Sources were excluded when they focused exclusively on chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer-associated diabetes, cystic fibrosis-related diabetes, pancreatic surgery-related diabetes, or another non-AP cause of pancreatogenic diabetes without providing transferable evidence relevant to PPDM-A. Additional exclusion criteria were mixed pancreatitis populations without separately interpretable AP data; established pre-existing diabetes without separate analysis of incident post-AP dysglycemia; isolated stress hyperglycemia during the index AP admission without subsequent assessment; and insufficiently described definitions of AP, diabetes, or the timing of glycemic evaluation.

Results

Terms and Classification of PPDM-A

In the contemporary literature, dysglycemia and diabetes developing after AP are described using several partially overlapping terms. This variability is not merely semantic, as it directly influences cohort definition, incidence estimates, comparability of outcomes across studies, and approaches to follow-up after AP. In the AP setting, the most commonly used terms include post-pancreatitis diabetes mellitus (PPDM), PPDM-A, new-onset diabetes after pancreatitis (NODAP), and post-AP diabetes (PAPD) (Figure 2). In practice, PAPD is conceptually aligned with PPDM-A and is generally defined by excluding pre-existing diabetes and applying standard diagnostic criteria only after a post-event interval, most commonly ≥ 90 days after AP resolution.10 An important consequence of this terminological inconsistency is misclassification in routine clinical practice, where many patients are labeled as having type 2 diabetes despite a clear temporal association with pancreatitis and a potentially distinct risk profile. Terminology, diagnostic timing, and classification of PPDM-A.

Recent gastroenterology guidance has improved conceptual clarity by defining NODAP as an umbrella term for diabetes developing in a previously non-diabetic individual after pancreatitis and by distinguishing it from PPDM according to the availability of biochemical evidence around the pancreatitis episode.14 Within this framework, NODAP requires evidence that diagnostic criteria for diabetes were not met before and/or during the early peri-event period. By contrast, the term PPDM may be applied when such baseline biochemical documentation is unavailable or inconclusive, as is frequently the case in retrospective cohorts and administrative database studies.14 For a review focused specifically on AP, the clinically relevant subtype is PPDM-A, defined as diabetes identified after an AP episode in an individual without confirmed pre-existing diabetes.

A related classificatory challenge is the frequent use of the terms pancreatogenic diabetes and type 3c diabetes mellitus (T3cDM) in discussions of diabetes occurring after AP. In the AP-specific context, these terms should not be used interchangeably with PPDM-A. PPDM-A is primarily an event- and time-anchored construct that refers to diabetes first recognized after AP, whereas the designation pancreatogenic diabetes generally implies stronger evidence of pancreatic causality beyond temporal association alone, including structural pancreatic abnormalities, exocrine dysfunction, and exclusion of autoimmune diabetes.4,15 This distinction is particularly important in AP cohorts, where detailed exocrine testing, autoimmune markers, and standardized pancreatic imaging descriptors are often unavailable. Consequently, most studies rely on pragmatic PPDM-A definitions rather than strict phenotyping of pancreatogenic diabetes.

The principal challenge in PPDM-A classification is timing. Hyperglycemia during the acute phase of AP is common and may reflect stress physiology, systemic inflammation, nutritional instability, and treatment-related factors rather than persistent diabetes. Accordingly, classifying in-hospital hyperglycemia as incident diabetes may substantially overestimate the true burden of PPDM-A.16 To reduce this risk, contemporary definitions generally apply a delayed diagnostic window, most often > 90 days after AP onset or index admission, in order to distinguish persistent dysglycemia from transient stress hyperglycemia and metabolic instability during early recovery.17–22 Recent original studies further support this approach by showing that prediction of PPDM-A is more informative when based on longitudinal post-AP follow-up models rather than isolated acute-phase glucose measurements, including imaging- and radiomics-based models and multivariable Cox regression frameworks.20–22

This time-based separation is consistent with current expert recommendations, which advise against establishing NODAP, and similarly NOPAP (new-onset prediabetes after pancreatitis), during the pancreatitis episode or within approximately the first 3 months thereafter, because early glycemic abnormalities are often transient and may be confounded by acute illness.14 In this context, the use of a delayed diagnostic window is not simply a methodological preference but a necessary prerequisite for valid PPDM-A classification.

In addition, glycemic abnormalities after AP are increasingly recognized as dynamic trajectories rather than a single binary outcome. Prospective longitudinal studies indicate that some individuals normalize glycemia after the acute episode and remain stable, some develop delayed deterioration, and others exhibit fluctuating glycemic patterns over time, suggesting that a single early assessment may misclassify long-term metabolic status.23 More recent original evidence also supports dynamic risk stratification after AP. In a 3-year follow-up cohort, in-hospital glucose variability, particularly the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE), was independently associated with subsequent PPDM-A, whereas cumulative PPDM-A incidence increased over time from 16% at 1 year to 27.3% at 3 years.24 These findings further illustrate the limitations of relying on a single inpatient glucose measurement for durable classification.

From a pragmatic AP-specific perspective, post-event glycemic states may be classified into three clinically relevant categories: (1) transient stress hyperglycemia related to the acute episode and resolving during recovery; (2) previously unrecognized diabetes, most commonly type 2 diabetes, identified during AP evaluation and supported by pre-AP records and/or interpretable HbA1c values; and (3) PPDM-A, in which diabetes is first established after the acute event and persists beyond the early recovery period. This framework may reduce definitional contamination and improve comparability across studies by separating acute-phase dysglycemia from persistent post-AP endocrine sequelae.10,23,24

Recent international standards further support the need for explicit post-AP classification by recognizing pancreatic diabetes as an umbrella category that includes diabetes developing after pancreatitis and by recommending active surveillance for dysglycemia after pancreatic injury.15,25 In the AP setting, this has direct practical relevance because it links terminology to a minimum follow-up requirement: screening for diabetes should be performed within 3–6 months after AP and repeated periodically thereafter, rather than assuming that discharge glucose values are sufficient for definitive classification.25

Epidemiology and Burden of PPDM-A

The burden of PPDM-A is defined not merely by the occurrence of incident diabetes, but by the prolonged period during which disturbances in glucose homeostasis may emerge, evolve, and persist after hospital discharge. In a substantial proportion of AP survivors, glycemic impairment becomes apparent months to years after the index event rather than during the initial hospitalization alone. This extended post-acute period creates a long clinical “risk tail”, characterized by repeated health-care encounters, rehospitalizations, and an increased likelihood of unfavorable long-term outcomes. Reported epidemiologic estimates vary considerably across studies, largely because of differences in diagnostic ascertainment (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, or oral glucose tolerance testing), the strategy used to confirm diabetes outside the acute stress period, and the rigor with which investigators exclude previously unrecognized diabetes that predated AP. Accordingly, incidence estimates should be interpreted in light of contemporary diagnostic standards and the principle of delayed confirmation after recovery, rather than being treated as directly comparable across cohorts.25

The temporal nature of PPDM-A burden is clearly demonstrated in both routine-practice datasets and longitudinal cohort studies. In AP populations followed over extended periods, diabetes is frequently diagnosed well beyond the index episode, with mean or median intervals of approximately 2–3 years after AP, and with some patients developing diabetes substantially later. This pattern indicates that clinically relevant incident PPDM-A continues to accumulate long after discharge and that studies with short follow-up systematically underestimate the true burden of disease.26 Such delayed accrual supports the broader concept that AP should not be regarded solely as an acute inpatient disorder, but rather as a condition with important long-term metabolic sequelae (Figure 3).Figure 3Natural history trajectories and clinical course of PPDM-A.A flowchart of post-acute pancreatitis outcomes, mechanisms and surveillance over time.The flowchart outlines the management of conditions after an acute pancreatitis (AP) episode. A major determinant of this long-term burden is the persistence of pancreatic and metabolic vulnerability after discharge. Recurrent episodes of AP occur in a clinically meaningful proportion of patients and may progressively increase endocrine risk through repeated inflammatory injury and gradual depletion of metabolic reserve.27 From an epidemiologic perspective, this implies that the burden of PPDM-A cannot be adequately captured by discharge diagnoses alone and instead requires structured longitudinal surveillance.

In a widely cited systematic review and meta-analysis including 14 studies, the pooled incidence of diabetes after AP was 23% (95% CI 13–35%), although heterogeneity across studies was substantial. Importantly, insulin-treated diabetes accounted for a considerable proportion of cases, with a pooled incidence of 15% (95% CI 9–23%), indicating that dysglycemia after AP is not confined to mild abnormalities of glucose regulation but often reflects clinically significant endocrine dysfunction requiring intensive treatment.9 Subsequent evidence syntheses have likewise demonstrated a time-dependent increase in cumulative incidence, with higher estimates in studies employing longer follow-up durations, consistent with progressive post-AP accrual of cases.28,29

Prospective and protocolized cohorts further emphasize the breadth of the at-risk population, including patients without overt necrotizing disease. In the DORADO cohort, a substantial proportion of participants without baseline diabetes developed new-onset prediabetes or diabetes after AP, although incidence estimates differed according to whether combined fasting glucose plus HbA1c criteria or HbA1c-alone thresholds were used.30 Longitudinal trajectory analyses provide additional insight by showing that, over 24 months, the cumulative incidence of new-onset prediabetes or diabetes rises substantially and that patients follow distinct glycemic trajectories, including stable normoglycemia, persistent impairment, and progressive deterioration. These findings underscore that PPDM-A risk is dynamic and heterogeneous rather than fixed.23 Shorter prospective studies also identify an early post-discharge signal, with incident diabetes developing in a notable minority during the first year; however, they simultaneously illustrate the strong dependence of incidence estimates on the timing of case definition and the adequacy of baseline exclusions.31 More recent original longitudinal data further reinforce the concept of time-dependent burden accumulation: in one 3-year follow-up cohort, cumulative PPDM-A incidence increased from 16% at 1 year to 27.3% at 3 years, while in-hospital glucose variability, particularly the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE), independently predicted subsequent PPDM-A. These findings highlight that the epidemiologic burden continues to unfold after discharge and is not adequately captured by a single early glycemic assessment.24

A critical methodological issue is that glycemic abnormalities observed during the index admission should not automatically be classified as PPDM-A. Hyperglycemia in the acute phase may reflect stress physiology, systemic inflammation, nutritional fluctuations, and treatment-related effects. To reduce misclassification, contemporary expert recommendations emphasize baseline metabolic assessment, including HbA1c and, ideally, evaluation within the broader context of pancreatic function, during hospitalization or at the first outpatient review, followed by structured reassessment after clinical recovery.14 This principle is central to epidemiologic interpretation, because cohorts that count early stress hyperglycemia as incident diabetes are likely to overestimate PPDM-A burden and reduce comparability with studies that apply delayed confirmation windows.

One of the most consistent epidemiologic observations in PPDM-A is the presence of a severity gradient. Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates a substantially higher incidence of diabetes after severe AP than after mild or moderately severe disease, and a markedly increased incidence among patients with pancreatic necrosis compared with those without necrosis. Etiology also appears to modify risk, with alcohol-related AP generally associated with a greater subsequent diabetes burden than biliary AP.9 Real-world propensity score-matched analyses support this gradient, showing higher rates of incident diabetes after severe AP than after mild AP, together with a persistent excess risk over several years of follow-up, even though the risk is often greatest during the early post-discharge period.32

These findings support a practical distinction between early and late diabetes detection after AP. Early abnormalities may represent stress-related dysglycemia or previously unrecognized diabetes that becomes evident during AP-related care, whereas later-onset cases are more compatible with sustained endocrine sequelae arising from post-AP pancreatic injury and metabolic remodeling. From an epidemiologic standpoint, this distinction is highly consequential, because longer follow-up consistently produces higher cumulative incidence estimates. Studies with limited follow-up should therefore be understood as capturing only a portion of the total burden of PPDM-A.24,28,29

The burden of PPDM-A extends beyond the prevalence of hyperglycemia to encompass adverse clinical outcomes and increased health-care utilization. In population-based matched cohort analyses, post-pancreatitis diabetes has been associated with higher all-cause mortality and a greater risk of hospitalization than type 2 diabetes, with particularly pronounced excess risks for infectious and gastrointestinal causes of hospitalization and death.12 Additional population-based evidence suggests a less favorable overall clinical course than that observed in type 2 diabetes, including a higher risk of hypoglycemia and increased mortality. Collectively, these data support the view that PPDM-A represents a high-risk diabetes phenotype rather than a routine form of type 2 diabetes occurring in an individual with a prior history of AP.1

Misclassification remains a major source of epidemiologic uncertainty and, in itself, contributes to disease burden at the systems level. In real-world datasets, many cases of PPDM are coded as type 2 diabetes, thereby obscuring the true incidence of the condition, weakening risk stratification, and complicating resource allocation for AP survivors.33 This issue is particularly relevant in PPDM-A research, where case ascertainment often relies on administrative coding combined with incomplete exclusion of pre-existing diabetes. Consequently, between-study heterogeneity may reflect not only biological differences across AP populations, but also major methodological differences in how PPDM-A is identified and labeled.

Importantly, studies focused primarily on diabetes onset during the index hospitalization may fail to capture the longer-term burden that determines population-level outcomes. For example, in a tertiary-center AP cohort, in-hospital PPDM onset, as defined by that study, was not independently associated with short-term adverse inpatient outcomes, highlighting that metrics such as length of stay or intensive care utilization do not adequately reflect the delayed morbidity and post-discharge health-care utilization associated with PPDM-A.34

Overall, heterogeneity in published estimates of PPDM-A burden is expected and appears to be driven predominantly by differences in diagnostic definitions and analytic windows, particularly whether acute-phase dysglycemia is included; the extent of baseline screening for occult pre-existing diabetes, including admission HbA1c availability; reliance on laboratory-confirmed diagnoses versus administrative coding; the duration and intensity of follow-up; and the underlying AP case-mix, including severity, pancreatic necrosis, and recurrence burden.9,14,23–25,32 Recognition of these sources of variation is essential for accurate interpretation of the literature and for the design of future PPDM-A studies using clinically meaningful, reproducible, and methodologically robust endpoints.

Pathophysiology of PPDM-A

The pathophysiology of PPDM-A is heterogeneous and cannot be attributed to a single pathway. Current evidence supports an interaction between pancreatic endocrine injury, inadequate β-cell compensation, insulin resistance, persistent inflammatory–metabolic stress, exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, and pre-existing metabolic susceptibility. The relative contribution of these components may vary according to AP severity and etiology, recurrence burden, structural pancreatic damage, nutritional status, body composition, and baseline glucose homeostasis.14,15,25

Importantly, the supporting evidence is not uniform. Pancreatic endocrine injury, impaired β-cell reserve, insulin resistance, inflammatory–metabolic stress, and exocrine dysfunction have the strongest support from clinical cohorts and longitudinal human studies. Body-composition phenotypes, immune profiles, and alterations in enteroinsular signaling represent emerging human associations or phenotype modifiers. Major pathophysiological mechanisms of PPDM-A.

Pancreatic Endocrine Injury and Inflammatory–Metabolic Stress

Human metabolic studies indicate that PPDM-A frequently involves both impaired insulin secretion and insulin resistance. Some patients who subsequently develop dysglycemia show increased fasting insulin or C-peptide concentrations during early recovery, consistent with compensatory hyperinsulinemia. However, insulin secretion may remain insufficient when interpreted relative to the prevailing degree of insulin sensitivity, as reflected by a reduced disposition index.30 Alterations in amylin, pancreatic polypeptide, glucagon, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) have also been described, suggesting that post-AP endocrine dysfunction may extend beyond an isolated defect in insulin secretion.30,35

Direct and indirect injury to the endocrine pancreas is a biologically plausible and clinically supported component of PPDM-A. Acute necroinflammation may damage β cells, disrupt islet architecture and microcirculation, and alter the local pancreatic microenvironment. Nevertheless, PPDM-A may also occur after mild or non-necrotizing AP, indicating that absolute loss of endocrine tissue is not the sole explanation and that functional impairment of surviving islets and pre-existing β-cell reserve are also relevant.10,15,16,28 Experimental differences in glucose homeostasis and islet injury among mouse strains exposed to comparable AP-related injury further support host-dependent variation in endocrine susceptibility, although these findings cannot be translated directly into clinical risk estimates.6 This interpretation is consistent with the integrated physiology of the pancreas, in which endocrine function depends on local vascular integrity, stromal remodeling, and communication between acinar, ductal, immune, and islet cells.36

Structural and inflammatory remodeling after AP may additionally involve ductal and acinar pathology with secondary effects on endocrine function. Across diseases of the exocrine pancreas, ductal abnormalities, inflammatory infiltration, stromal changes, and fibro-fatty replacement have been associated with islet remodeling and endocrine dysfunction.37 Although this evidence is not exclusively AP-specific, it supports a model in which PPDM-A may result from persistent dysfunction of the islet microenvironment as well as from direct β-cell loss.36,37

Persistent inflammatory activation may provide an additional link between AP and subsequent dysglycemia. Prospective human data have identified post-AP cytokine profiles associated with later new-onset prediabetes.38 These findings support the biological relevance of inflammatory–metabolic interactions but do not establish circulating cytokines as causal mediators or validated clinical biomarkers.

Experimental studies provide further biological plausibility. Molecular regulators of pancreatic inflammation have been shown to affect glucose tolerance together with inflammatory and pancreatic-injury phenotypes in experimental models.39 Altered gastrin-releasing peptide concentrations have also been associated with abnormal glucose metabolism after pancreatitis.40 However, these findings remain less firmly established than pancreatic endocrine injury, impaired β-cell compensation, or insulin resistance and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent clinical importance.

A clinically relevant bridge between inflammation and insulin resistance is cytokine-associated lipolysis. Interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α have been associated with glycerol and free fatty acid concentrations after AP, suggesting that persistent inflammatory signaling may increase circulating lipid flux and insulin demand.41 In patients with limited β-cell reserve, this mechanism could facilitate progression from compensated insulin resistance to overt dysglycemia. Nevertheless, the available evidence remains observational and does not establish that modification of individual cytokines would prevent PPDM-A.30,41

In-hospital glycemic variability may reflect the combined effects of pancreatic endocrine stress, systemic inflammation, altered nutrition, treatment exposure, and reduced metabolic reserve. Greater glucose variability during the index admission has been associated with subsequent PPDM-A. Current evidence therefore supports glycemic variability primarily as a clinically accessible marker of metabolic instability and future risk rather than as an independently established causal mechanism.24,42

The relative contributions of β-cell dysfunction, insulin resistance, inflammation, and structural pancreatic injury are being investigated in prospective AP cohorts. The Diabetes RElated to Acute Pancreatitis and Its Mechanisms study was designed to characterize longitudinal glucose metabolism, β-cell function, insulin sensitivity, and pancreatic-related factors after AP.43 Such prospective phenotyping is required to distinguish causal pathways from cross-sectional associations.

Metabolic Susceptibility, Exocrine Dysfunction, and Human Phenotype Modifiers

Body composition represents a clinically relevant but still developing domain. Visceral adiposity may contribute to insulin resistance and inflammatory activity, whereas reduced skeletal-muscle reserve may impair peripheral glucose disposal. In patients with hypertriglyceridemic AP, computed tomography-derived measures of visceral adipose tissue and muscle reserve have been associated with subsequent PPDM-A.44 These findings are particularly relevant to populations in which hypertriglyceridemic AP is common, but they provide stronger evidence for risk enrichment than for independent causality.10,22,44

Hypertriglyceridemic AP illustrates the interaction between pancreatic injury and pre-existing metabolic susceptibility. Hydrolysis of excess triglycerides may generate high local concentrations of free fatty acids and aggravate pancreatic and microvascular injury, while affected patients frequently have coexisting dyslipidemia, visceral adiposity, fatty liver disease, or impaired glucose regulation. Subsequent diabetes may therefore reflect the combined effects of pancreatic injury and an adverse metabolic background rather than either component alone.10,44

Exocrine pancreatic dysfunction represents another clinically supported component of PPDM-A. A prospective multicenter longitudinal study demonstrated that EPI may remain present 12 months after AP, confirming that exocrine dysfunction can persist well beyond the acute episode.45 Potential metabolic consequences include maldigestion, irregular nutrient delivery, nutritional deficiencies, loss of muscle reserve, and disruption of nutrient-dependent enteroinsular signaling.36,45

Population-based evidence further supports an association between exocrine pancreatic dysfunction and later diabetes. Exocrine pancreatic dysfunction, approximated by pancreatic enzyme dispensing, was associated with an increased risk of new-onset diabetes, including after mild AP.46 However, this relationship may be bidirectional: exocrine dysfunction may contribute to dysglycemia through maldigestion and altered enteroendocrine responses, but it may also act as a marker of more extensive or persistent pancreatic injury. Observational studies cannot fully distinguish these possibilities.45,46

Alterations in pancreatic and gut-hormone responses may further modify the PPDM-A phenotype. An impaired GIP response with relatively preserved GLP-1 secretion has been reported in PPDM, while pancreatic polypeptide deficiency has been proposed as a contributor to hepatic insulin resistance.30,35 These hormonal patterns have been evaluated in relatively limited populations and are not sufficiently validated to distinguish PPDM-A from type 2 or autoimmune diabetes in routine practice. They should therefore be regarded as emerging physiological phenotypes rather than established diagnostic biomarkers.30,35

Immune-cell profiles also remain an emerging field. An association between B-lymphocyte proportions and subsequent PPDM-A has been reported, but the evidence is based on limited cohorts without standardized sampling or broad external validation.13 Immune-cell signatures should therefore remain candidate prognostic markers rather than established mechanistic determinants.

Advanced imaging and radiomic features may capture pancreatic injury, adipose distribution, muscle reserve, and ductal anatomy more comprehensively than conventional clinical variables.19–22 Their present contribution is predominantly predictive rather than mechanistic: an association between an imaging-derived feature and PPDM-A does not establish that the feature itself causes diabetes. These approaches are therefore considered more appropriately within PPDM-A risk prediction than among established pathophysiological mechanisms.19–22

Exploratory Translational Pathways

The gut microbiome–metabolome–immune interface represents a promising but preliminary area of PPDM-A research. Prospective observational evidence indicates that buccal and rectal microbiota profiles recorded during AP may be associated with post-discharge diabetes, and microbiome-based classifiers have shown potential for risk prediction.7 However, microbiota composition may be influenced by antibiotic exposure, nutrition, AP severity, medication use, geography, and pre-existing metabolic disease. It remains uncertain whether dysbiosis contributes directly to PPDM-A, reflects AP severity, or acts as a marker of a broader metabolic disturbance.7

Extracellular vesicles and exosomal microRNAs have been proposed as potential links between pancreatic inflammation, intercellular signaling, and endocrine dysfunction.47 PPDM-A-specific evidence remains very limited, and no exosomal-miRNA panel has been externally validated for diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection. Differences in sample processing, isolation, and quantification further restrict interpretation. Exosomal microRNAs should therefore be presented as hypothesis-generating candidates rather than established biomarkers or clinically actionable mechanisms.47

Hepatobiliary physiology may also modify metabolic recovery after biliary AP. A nationwide cohort identified associations between cholecystectomy, recurrent biliary events, and subsequent PPDM risk.18 This observation raises the possibility that alterations in hepatobiliary physiology, including bile acid–FGF19 signaling, may be relevant; however, the available clinical evidence does not establish FGF19 as a causal mediator of PPDM-A.18

Overall, PPDM-A is best interpreted using an evidence-ranked multiple-hit model. AP may reduce endocrine reserve and disrupt exocrine–endocrine integration, whereas baseline insulin resistance, limited β-cell compensatory capacity, body composition, and other host characteristics influence whether glycemia subsequently normalizes, remains intermittently abnormal, or progresses to diabetes.14,15,25 Recurrent pancreatic injury may further reduce functional reserve and increase the probability of persistent dysglycemia.

This hierarchy has direct implications for clinical interpretation. Diagnostic and surveillance decisions should be based primarily on standardized glycemic criteria and clinically supported risk factors, including pancreatic injury burden, recurrence, baseline metabolic status, glycemic variability, exocrine dysfunction, and nutritional trajectory.14,15,25 Microbiome, immune, exosomal-miRNA, and other multi-omic signatures should remain within the research setting until their reproducibility, incremental clinical value, and external validity have been demonstrated.

Risk Factors and Predictors of PPDM-A

The risk of PPDM-A is best conceptualized as the consequence of interaction between two major domains: pre-existing metabolic susceptibility and the severity, distribution, and persistence of pancreatic and systemic injury induced by the episode of AP itself (Figure 5). The first domain includes obesity, prediabetes, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, family history of diabetes, and related metabolic comorbidities, whereas the second encompasses pancreatic necrosis, organ failure, microvascular injury, sustained inflammatory stress, and structural sequelae such as ductal disruption after necrotizing AP. Risk factors, early predictive signals, and high-risk phenotypes in PPDM-A.

At the individual-patient level, many predictors of PPDM-A overlap with those used in type 2 diabetes risk assessment; however, in PPDM-A their effect is modified by the burden of pancreatic injury. Baseline insulin resistance and limited β-cell reserve appear to increase vulnerability, whereas the AP episode functions as the precipitating insult that unmasks or accelerates dysglycemia. Across study designs, obesity and dyslipidemia have emerged among the most reproducible associations, together with comorbidities linked to systemic inflammation and impaired glucose metabolism, such as chronic kidney disease and chronic liver disease. In a large systematic review and meta-analysis, post-AP dysglycemia or diabetes was more likely among individuals with obesity, with pooled odds ratios approaching 1.85, and among those with chronic kidney disease, in whom the odds were approximately doubled. Similar directional associations were also reported for cirrhosis and dyslipidemia, although heterogeneity remained substantial for some exposures.29

Beyond generalized adiposity, gland-specific and regional fat burden may be particularly relevant. Emerging evidence suggests that intrapancreatic fat deposition may increase the risk of PPDM-A, consistent with the concept that local lipotoxicity amplifies endocrine vulnerability in a pancreas already injured by AP.16 Imaging-based and phenotype-oriented studies further indicate that adipose distribution, especially visceral adiposity, may provide more informative risk stratification than body mass index alone.11,44 At the same time, registry-based analyses suggest that PPDM-A should not be viewed simply as type 2 diabetes arising after AP. Compared with typical type 2 diabetes populations, PPDM-A may occur relatively more often in individuals without marked obesity.14 Clinically, this implies that a lean phenotype does not exclude substantial risk when pancreatitis-related injury is present.

Prospective cohort data are consistent with these observations. In AP survivors, new-onset diabetes has been associated with obesity, defined as body mass index of at least 30 kg/m2, higher Balthazar E scores at admission, and more extensive early necrosis on 72-hour computed tomography, particularly when parenchymal necrosis exceeds 50%.31 More detailed lipid phenotyping may further improve prognostic precision. In a retrospective cohort with long-term follow-up, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol independently predicted subsequent PPDM-A alongside body mass index and fasting glucose, suggesting that broader atherogenic dyslipidemia, rather than triglycerides alone, may track endocrine risk after AP.22

Clinical features reflecting the effective “dose” of pancreatic injury and systemic inflammatory stress are among the most robust predictors of PPDM-A. Across multiple cohorts, moderately severe or severe AP, pancreatic necrosis, organ failure, intensive care unit admission, infected necrosis, prolonged hospitalization, and hospital readmissions repeatedly cluster with greater subsequent diabetes risk. In the meta-analysis by Zahariev et al, moderately severe or severe AP was associated with markedly higher odds of post-AP dysglycemia or diabetes compared with mild AP, with a pooled odds ratio of approximately 4.32, whereas pancreatic necrosis demonstrated an even stronger pooled association, with an odds ratio of about 5.53.29 These findings are consistent with broader pancreatitis-focused syntheses that identify necrotizing AP as a particularly high-risk phenotype, although effect estimates vary across datasets and according to the definitions applied.48

Real-world tertiary-center data further reinforce the prognostic importance of necrotizing injury and imaging-derived severity markers. In a United Kingdom cohort of 401 patients, PPDM occurred in 11% and was strongly associated with both the presence and extent of necrosis; computed tomography severity index categories remained independently predictive in multivariable analyses, supporting the clinical utility of imaging-based severity markers for routine risk stratification.34 At the same time, large administrative datasets and high-level evidence syntheses have noted inconsistency in the independent effect of AP severity when severity is incompletely captured or when recurrence burden is not adequately modeled.14 AP severity should therefore be regarded as a powerful but context-dependent predictor rather than a solitary determinant of risk.

In addition to the extent of necrosis, its anatomical distribution may also influence diabetes risk. Clinical observations summarized in recent pancreatitis-focused reviews suggest that necrosis involving the pancreatic tail may confer greater risk than necrosis confined to the head or body, which is biologically plausible given the regional distribution of islet tissue.49 Organ failure and critical illness may further amplify risk through prolonged catabolism, excess stress-hormone signaling, skeletal muscle loss, and lipotoxicity, thereby worsening insulin resistance and accelerating β-cell exhaustion even in the absence of extensive necrosis.

The etiology of AP may shape PPDM-A risk both directly, through distinct metabolic milieus, and indirectly, through differences in severity and recurrence patterns. Alcohol-related AP has been associated with increased PPDM risk in pooled analyses.29 Hypertriglyceridemic AP may represent a particularly high-risk subtype, and recent body-composition studies in this setting support the importance of the adipose–muscle phenotype in determining risk.44 In biliary AP, long-term metabolic risk may be reduced when the inciting biliary cause is definitively corrected and recurrent events are prevented. In a nationwide cohort that modeled cholecystectomy as a time-dependent exposure after first AP admission, delayed cholecystectomy was associated with a higher risk of PPDM, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.36, as well as increased insulin use, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.16. These findings support the concept that recurrent biliary events before definitive treatment may amplify metabolic risk.18 Nevertheless, this interpretation should remain cautious, since delayed surgery may also correlate with greater severity of the index episode and with perioperative selection factors.

Recurrence of AP is one of the most clinically meaningful risk amplifiers for PPDM-A, because each additional episode may contribute cumulative pancreatic injury. Recurrent AP has been associated with higher PPDM risk in both observational and pooled analyses.18,29 Contemporary syntheses further support a recurrence-related dose–response relationship, with multiple recurrent attacks conferring substantially greater risk than a single episode.14,16 These observations underscore that prevention of recurrence should be regarded as a central component of PPDM-A risk reduction, even after apparent clinical resolution of the initial AP event.

A particularly informative structural predictor after necrotizing AP is disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome (DPDS). Observational studies have linked DPDS to recurrent pancreatic fluid collections and to new-onset diabetes, highlighting the prognostic significance of persistent morphological disruption after the acute event.50 This is directly relevant to PPDM-A risk assessment because DPDS may reflect ongoing pancreatic injury burden and repeated inflammatory complications well beyond the index hospitalization.

EPI, or broader EPD, represents another important predictor domain and may function both as a co-phenotype and as a marker of more extensive pancreatic injury. The 2026 Standards note that concurrent EPI, abnormal pancreatic imaging, and absence of type 1 diabetes-associated autoimmunity support classification of pancreatic diabetes in the setting of pancreatitis, and they recommend consideration of EPI screening, for example by fecal elastase measurement, in this context.25 Population-level data suggest that exocrine dysfunction may precede and predict PPDM-A. In a nationwide cohort study, EPD, approximated by pancreatic enzyme dispensing as a time-dependent exposure, was associated with a substantially higher risk of new-onset diabetes, including in analyses restricted to patients with mild AP.46 The association persisted in sensitivity analyses designed to reduce bias and reverse causality, strengthening its prognostic relevance.46 Whether EPI or EPD is independently causal or primarily a surrogate of pancreatic injury burden remains uncertain; clinically, however, it clearly identifies a population at elevated risk.

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is another clinically relevant modifier that may lower the threshold for persistent dysglycemia after AP by increasing systemic insulin resistance and inflammatory tone. Multicenter retrospective cohort data have identified NAFLD as an independent risk factor for new-onset diabetes after AP.51 As with other metabolic predictors, this association should be interpreted in the context of potential confounding by visceral adiposity and baseline metabolic dysfunction, rather than as evidence of a fully isolated causal role.

Regional differences in AP etiology and metabolic case-mix should also be considered when interpreting PPDM-A risk. The current evidence base is geographically uneven, and risk estimates derived predominantly from Western cohorts may not be directly transferable to all populations. East Asian studies have placed particular emphasis on hypertriglyceridemic AP and associated metabolic phenotypes. Chinese cohort data have linked subsequent diabetes risk to visceral adiposity, reduced muscle reserve, dyslipidemia, and coexisting fatty liver disease.44,51 These findings suggest that insulin resistance and lipotoxicity may assume greater relative importance in populations in which hypertriglyceridemic AP is prominent. Nevertheless, differences in diagnostic definitions, follow-up intensity, and patient selection preclude firm conclusions regarding true biological variation between regions. Broader geographical representation and externally validated prediction models are therefore required.

Among early hospital-based predictors, admission glucose and, especially, in-hospital glycemic variability appear particularly promising. A prospective longitudinal cohort demonstrated that greater glucose variability during the first 72 hours of hospitalization predicted adverse HbA1c trajectories over the subsequent two years and anticipated new-onset diabetes after discharge.52 Evidence syntheses summarizing these findings indicate that a high glycemic lability index during the first 72 hours predicts NODAP or PPDM-A more strongly than admission hyperglycemia alone.14 Subsequent retrospective studies likewise found strong predictive value for the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE), with time-dependent receiver operating characteristic areas under the curve remaining high through 12 to 36 months of follow-up.24 Stress hyperglycemia itself may also carry prognostic significance, particularly when it persists beyond the earliest phase of AP.11,53 A practical caveat, however, is that HbA1c may be unreliable in settings associated with altered erythrocyte turnover, such as blood transfusion, hemorrhage, hemolysis, or kidney failure, making plasma glucose-based criteria preferable in some early post-AP contexts.25

Imaging-based predictors are increasingly important for quantifying injury burden and refining individualized risk estimation. Conventional computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging can directly characterize necrosis, persistent fluid collections, duct disruption, pancreatic atrophy, and body-composition features, and these clinically interpretable findings are the most immediately useful imaging contributors to risk stratification. By contrast, radiomics-based models should currently be viewed as exploratory extensions of this framework rather than equivalent forms of evidence. Several retrospective nomograms and machine-learning tools have shown promising discriminatory performance using combinations of adiposity metrics, admission glucose, inflammatory severity markers, and recurrence history.20,21,54 However, most such studies remain single-center or retrospective, are vulnerable to overfitting and feature instability, and have not yet demonstrated the external validation and transportability required for routine PPDM-A prediction. Earlier, more parsimonious models based on readily available metabolic variables, including age, body mass index, glucose, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, therefore remain more practice-proximal at present.55

A complementary imaging-related domain is pancreaticobiliary junction anatomy on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography. In a retrospective cohort, pancreaticobiliary junction type and junction angle independently predicted PPDM-A in multivariable Cox analysis, and the addition of these anatomical features to clinical and metabolic predictors improved model discrimination.22 Although promising, these findings require external validation and harmonization of imaging protocols and feature-extraction approaches before they can be broadly applied in routine practice.

Simplified bedside scores have also been proposed. Trieu et al developed the CAPS score using a limited set of clinical variables, including history of cirrhosis, admission albumin, history of prediabetes, and moderately severe or severe AP, and demonstrated moderate predictive performance in validation analyses. This approach illustrates both the practical appeal and the current limitations of simplified tools.56

Certain exposures may behave differently in PPDM-A than in classical type 2 diabetes epidemiology. A large administrative study of first-episode AP reported that statin use was associated with a lower hazard of diabetes after AP, an observation highlighted in pancreatitis-focused reviews as potentially differing from the usual association between statins and diabetes in general populations.11,53 Although this does not constitute causal evidence, it reinforces the importance of deriving PPDM-A prediction from pancreatitis-specific cohorts rather than extrapolating from general diabetes epidemiology. By contrast, tobacco smoking appears to be more consistently associated with elevated PPDM-A risk, and mechanistic follow-up data summarized in clinical guidance suggest a dose-dependent relationship between smoking burden and more rapid decline in C-peptide, reflecting worsening β-cell function in pancreatitis populations.14 These findings support explicit incorporation of pack-year exposure into risk assessment and follow-up planning.

Practice-ready prediction tools and biomarker signals are best regarded as extensions of the broader risk-factor framework rather than as a separate conceptual domain. Among the predictors most immediately applicable to clinical care, one of the strongest candidates is not a specialized molecular biomarker, but in-hospital glycemic variability derived from routine glucose measurements. Emerging AP-specific cohort data indicate that larger in-hospital glycemic excursions are independently associated with later PPDM-A, supporting the use of routine inpatient glucose patterns as an early risk signal.24 This is especially attractive from an implementation perspective because such data are already collected in standard clinical care, can be integrated into electronic health records, and can be directly linked to an actionable response, namely intensified post-discharge glycemic reassessment anchored to the diagnostic window of at least 90 days. Syntheses of metabolic sequelae after pancreatitis further support this approach by suggesting that indices of glycemic lability are more informative for later risk stratification than admission hyperglycemia alone.14 By contrast, complex biomarker panels, advanced immune signatures, and radiomic classifiers remain investigational adjuncts rather than tools ready to carry the same decision-making weight as validated clinical predictors.

Clinically actionable prediction of PPDM-A is therefore best achieved through an integrated, AP-specific framework that combines several domains: baseline metabolic susceptibility, including obesity, prediabetes, dyslipidemia, NAFLD, smoking, and inflammatory-metabolic comorbidity; severity of the AP course and overall injury burden, including necrosis, organ failure, intensive care admission, and prolonged inflammatory stress; recurrence burden and persistent structural complications such as disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome; exocrine dysfunction, including EPI or EPD, with its associated nutritional and enteroinsular consequences; and early measurable signals such as in-hospital glycemic variability, persistence of stress hyperglycemia, and quantitative imaging features, including CT- or MRI-derived body composition and, where available, pancreaticobiliary junction anatomy on MRCP. This AP-specific approach is aligned with current classification standards for diabetes associated with exocrine pancreatic disease while avoiding inappropriate extrapolation from non-AP forms of pancreatogenic diabetes.25,29

At present, therefore, the elements of PPDM-A prediction that are truly ready for routine use remain largely based on readily available clinical and laboratory variables, whereas many emerging biomarker classes, including multi-omics panels and complex cytokine signatures, remain at the research stage. Even biomarker-focused discussions emphasize that candidate signals are highly sensitive to pre-analytical and analytical factors, including sample handling, processing time, storage conditions, and method standardization, all of which substantially limit portability across institutions.30 Until assay standardization and incremental clinical utility are convincingly demonstrated, such markers should be viewed as exploratory adjuncts rather than practice-ready tools.

This pragmatic perspective is consistent with the fact that universally accepted screening protocols for diabetes after AP are still lacking, even though structured surveillance is increasingly supported by cohort data and by conceptual frameworks addressing the long-term sequelae of pancreatitis. A clinically coherent strategy is intensified metabolic reassessment during the first year after discharge, followed by annual surveillance thereafter, because a substantial proportion of incident dysglycemia and diabetes emerges during early follow-up.5,14,16

Clinical Phenotypes and Natural History of PPDM-A

PPDM-A should be viewed not as a single clinicopathological entity, but as a heterogeneous spectrum of post-AP disturbances in glucose homeostasis. Following an episode of AP, patients may exhibit markedly different glycemic courses. In some individuals, hyperglycemia emerging during the acute illness is transient and resolves during convalescence. In others, persistent yet relatively stable dysglycemia develops, frequently within the range of prediabetes. In a clinically important subgroup, however, glycemic control progressively deteriorates over months or years, ultimately resulting in overt diabetes. This trajectory-based concept is supported by longitudinal and cohort studies demonstrating substantial interindividual variability in both the timing and severity of post-AP dysglycemia22,23,56 (Figure 3).

Accordingly, clinically meaningful phenotyping of PPDM-A cannot rely on the severity of hyperglycemia alone. A more informative framework should incorporate several complementary dimensions: the timing of dysglycemia onset after AP, the trajectory of glycemic change over time (transient, stable, or progressive), the degree of glycemic variability, the relative contributions of insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion, and the presence of coexisting EPD or malabsorption that may influence glucose excursions and therapeutic requirements. Consideration of these parameters helps explain why some patients resemble a relatively mild type 2 diabetes phenotype, whereas others develop metabolically unstable disease with earlier insulin dependence despite similar HbA1c levels.1,14,33

A major methodological challenge in defining PPDM-A is the differentiation of true incident diabetes developing after AP from two common alternatives: previously unrecognized diabetes that predated the AP admission and was first detected during hospitalization, and transient stress- or treatment-related hyperglycemia during the index episode. Contemporary pancreatology guidance emphasizes that inpatient fasting or random glucose measurements obtained during AP are insufficient for this distinction, because stress hyperglycemia and iatrogenic hyperglycemia are common in acute illness.14 For this reason, incident new-onset diabetes after pancreatitis (NODAP), which clinically corresponds to PPDM-A when attributed to AP, should not be adjudicated earlier than 3 months after the index event and should be diagnosed using standard glycemic criteria.14 This temporal safeguard is essential, as it directly determines classification into transient versus persistent phenotypes and substantially influences observed natural-history patterns.

Even when uniform diagnostic definitions are applied, the timing of clinically recognized PPDM-A remains highly variable. Rather than representing an immediate dichotomous outcome after hospital discharge, diabetes often accumulates gradually over time. This pattern is reflected in studies reporting delayed median time to diagnosis and is consistent with at least three broad natural-history pathways: remitting dysglycemia, persistent low-grade dysglycemia, and progressive dysglycemia culminating in diabetes.10,22,56 Importantly, clinically significant PPDM-A may also develop after mild AP, indicating that its natural history cannot be reduced to an oversimplified model in which severe necrosis alone leads directly to insulin-deficient diabetes.14

Prospective studies with repeated follow-up assessments further support the existence of early and delayed phenotypes within PPDM-A. In one prospective investigation, diabetes identified within the first month after AP was categorized separately from diabetes diagnosed later, allowing a more nuanced appreciation of post-AP glycemic evolution. Although most cases arising within the first year were early-onset, a smaller late-onset subgroup was also observed, and the two groups differed clinically, including in age distribution, suggesting that even within the first post-AP year PPDM-A is not phenotypically uniform.31 From a clinical perspective, this distinction is relevant because early dysglycemia may reflect a composite of stress-related metabolic perturbation, unmasked pre-existing dysmetabolism, and early AP-related endocrine injury, whereas later-onset diabetes more strongly suggests a progressive post-pancreatitis metabolic process.

Medium- and longer-term follow-up data indicate that delayed-onset PPDM-A constitutes a substantial proportion of cases and frequently emerges well beyond the immediate recovery period. In a retrospective cohort with extended observation, the median interval from the first AP episode to PPDM diagnosis was approximately 32.6 months, with a wide interquartile range, underscoring pronounced interpatient variability in time to onset.22 Similarly, a prospectively assembled cohort that excluded diabetes diagnosed during the index hospitalization reported a median time to diagnosis of 27.8 months after AP admission.56 Collectively, these findings support a natural-history model in which delayed phenotypes are common and long-term metabolic surveillance is clinically warranted.

The strongest direct evidence for phenotypic heterogeneity in PPDM-A comes from trajectory-based analyses. In a 24-month prospective cohort with repeated glycemic assessments, distinct post-AP glycemic trajectories were identified, including a progressive pattern characterized by an increasing probability of deranged glucose metabolism, alongside other groups with stable or reversible abnormalities.23 This observation has major implications for phenotype definition, as it indicates that post-AP dysglycemia is inherently dynamic and that classification based on a single measurement at one time point may misrepresent the patient’s true long-term course.

Another clinically relevant phenotypic dimension is glycemic variability during and after the index AP admission. In-hospital glucose dynamics may identify patients at increased risk of progressive PPDM-A even before diabetes can be diagnosed by conventional criteria. A prospective longitudinal study demonstrated that glucose variability during the early phase of AP predicted the two-year probability of new-onset diabetes.52 Subsequent follow-up work further showed that the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE) was independently associated with later PPDM-A, with a threshold of approximately 5.1 mmol/L discriminating risk over time.24 These findings suggest that glycemic variability should be incorporated into phenotype characterization not merely as a marker of metabolic instability, but also as an early indicator of an adverse glycemic trajectory.

An additional phenotypic axis is defined by the temporal relationship between exocrine and endocrine pancreatic dysfunction after AP. In a nationwide cohort of patients after a first pancreatitis attack, EPD, approximated by pancreatic enzyme dispensing, was associated with a higher risk of new-onset diabetes in time-dependent analyses, including models restricted to mild AP.46 Clinically, this supports the recognition of a PPDM-A phenotype in which exocrine dysfunction either precedes or parallels endocrine decline and may signal increased likelihood of progression from transient or stable dysglycemia to overt diabetes. These observations also reinforce the importance of systematically assessing exocrine dysfunction when characterizing PPDM-A phenotypes and anticipating their clinical course.25

Phenotypes linked to recurrent pancreatic or biliary injury provide further support for an injury-accumulation model of disease evolution. In a nationwide cohort of patients with a first AP attack, delayed cholecystectomy was associated with a higher risk of PPDM, and recurrent biliary events demonstrated a graded relationship with subsequent diabetes development.18 These data are consistent with the concept that repeated pancreatic or biliary insults contribute cumulatively to later endocrine dysfunction. Clinically, this pattern may define a subgroup characterized by progressive risk accrual, earlier treatment intensification, and more complex long-term management.

At the clinical level, PPDM-A may manifest as a more metabolically unstable disorder than typical type 2 diabetes, at least in a subset of patients. Nationwide population-based data have shown that PPDM is associated with higher risks of severe hypoglycemia and all-cause mortality than type 2 diabetes.1 Treatment trajectories also appear to differ, as population-based evidence indicates that patients with PPDM are more likely to progress to insulin therapy and are frequently misclassified, which may delay recognition of the underlying phenotype and confound natural-history comparisons.33 These observations emphasize that PPDM-A phenotyping should encompass not only biochemical criteria, but also the longitudinal clinical behavior of the disease, including instability of glycemic control, susceptibility to hypoglycemia, and therapeutic progression.

Metabolic comorbidity patterns may provide additional refinement of susceptibility phenotypes and natural-history subgroups. In a nationwide cohort, pre-existing gout was associated with an increased risk of PPDM, whereas PPDM itself was associated with a higher subsequent risk of gout, suggesting bidirectional metabolic-inflammatory interactions that may influence both susceptibility to and progression of dysglycemia after AP.57 Although such comorbidity patterns do not define PPDM-A in themselves, they may help identify patient subgroups with greater metabolic complexity, competing risks, and potentially more rapid disease progression.

Taken together, available evidence indicates that PPDM-A is best understood as a dynamic continuum of glycemic trajectories after AP rather than as a fixed post-discharge endpoint. Some patients experience transient dysglycemia that resolves during recovery, others develop persistent intermediate abnormalities, and a clinically important subgroup progresses to overt diabetes over months to years. Prospective and retrospective studies consistently demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in time to onset, including delayed phenotypes with median diagnostic intervals of approximately 2–3 years after the index AP episode.10,22,23,56 Accurate phenotyping therefore requires strict temporal safeguards, particularly deferral of incident PPDM-A adjudication until at least 3 months after AP and avoidance of reliance on inpatient glucose values alone, so that natural-history analyses reflect true post-AP diabetes rather than stress hyperglycemia or previously unrecognized pre-existing diabetes.14

Diagnostics and Screening of PPDM-A After Discharge

Post-discharge evaluation of patients after AP should pursue three principal objectives: first, to confirm whether dysglycemia persists beyond the immediate post-acute period; second, to distinguish true PPDM-A from pre-existing or previously unrecognized diabetes; and third, to tailor the choice of diagnostic tests and the intensity of surveillance according to the individual risk profile and nutritional phenotype, as discussed in the preceding sections.

With respect to timing, a pragmatic approach is to perform an early outpatient reassessment approximately 4–12 weeks after discharge, with earlier evaluation in patients who exhibit symptoms suggestive of hyperglycemia, marked inpatient dysglycemia, dehydration, or ongoing metabolic instability. This initial reassessment should then be followed by structured surveillance. Available evidence supports repeated glycemic testing during the first 1–2 years after AP, when the incidence of new dysglycemia is highest, followed by annual testing in individuals at increased risk15,16,58 (Figure 6). Risk-stratified diagnostic and long-term follow-up framework for PPDM-A.

Risk-stratified surveillance may be further refined by incorporating inpatient glycemic dynamics. In a cohort with extended follow-up, the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE) recorded during hospitalization independently predicted subsequent PPDM-A, and a threshold of ≥5.1 mmol/L identified a subgroup with a markedly higher cumulative incidence over time.24 Although such markers are not diagnostic in themselves, they may help identify patients who warrant earlier reassessment and closer follow-up after discharge. Emerging post-discharge prediction models support the same general principle, namely, that readily available clinical variables can be used to identify those most likely to benefit from intensified surveillance.56

Selection of diagnostic tests after discharge should follow standard diabetes criteria while taking into account confounders specific to the post-AP setting. According to the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care 2026, diabetes is diagnosed by any of the following: HbA1c ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), 2-hour plasma glucose during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), or random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in the presence of classic symptoms; confirmatory repeat testing is recommended unless hyperglycemia is unequivocal and symptomatic.25 In patients recovering from AP, the OGTT is particularly informative when HbA1c and fasting glucose are borderline or discordant, as postprandial dysglycemia may precede fasting abnormalities. A practical diagnostic framework after discharge is therefore based on HbA1c combined with fasting plasma glucose, with selective use of OGTT for clarification of uncertain cases.25

Interpretation of HbA1c requires particular caution in recently hospitalized patients after AP. Although HbA1c is valuable for assessing chronic glycemic exposure, its reliability may be compromised by transfusion, anemia, hemolysis, acute blood loss, erythropoietin therapy, and advanced kidney disease or dialysis, all of which may occur in the post-hospitalization setting. In such circumstances, the ADA Standards recommend reliance on plasma glucose-based criteria rather than HbA1c alone.25 Accordingly, when HbA1c validity is uncertain in the early post-discharge period, diagnostic emphasis should shift toward fasting plasma glucose and/or OGTT. Short-term glycemic markers such as fructosamine or glycated albumin may occasionally be considered, but their interpretation must remain cautious, particularly in the presence of hypoalbuminemia, systemic inflammation, or nutritional instability.59

At present, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) should be regarded as an adjunctive tool for phenotyping and safety assessment rather than as a formal diagnostic modality for PPDM-A. The ADA 2026 Standards state that current evidence is insufficient to support the use of CGM for screening or diagnosing dysglycemia in individuals without established diabetes.25 Nevertheless, in selected patients recovering from AP, CGM may provide clinically useful information by documenting glycemic variability, detecting occult hypoglycemia, and characterizing postprandial excursions when standard laboratory measures are inconclusive or when glycemia appears particularly labile. This may be especially relevant in view of population-based data showing a higher risk of severe hypoglycemia in PPDM than in type 2 diabetes.1 Even so, CGM findings should be interpreted as complementary data reflecting interstitial rather than plasma glucose and not as substitutes for diagnostic plasma-based testing.60

When PPDM-A is suspected, post-discharge evaluation should extend beyond glucose thresholds alone and adopt a mechanism-oriented approach. Hyperglycemia after AP should be interpreted in the broader context of pancreatic structural injury, exocrine dysfunction, and endocrine reserve. Imaging findings such as necrotic sequelae, pancreatic atrophy, ductal abnormalities, or persistent collections may help link dysglycemia to the burden of pancreatic injury. In a tertiary-center cohort, the presence and extent of necrosis as well as CT-based severity indices were associated with both PPDM development and long-term insulin requirement.34 EPD is similarly relevant from a diagnostic and prognostic perspective. In a nationwide cohort, EPD, approximated by repeated pancreatic enzyme dispensing, was strongly associated with subsequent new-onset diabetes, including in patients with mild AP and in lagged analyses, supporting its use as a marker for intensified surveillance and more refined phenotyping.46 Recent syntheses likewise support combining standard glycemic testing with assessment for EPI, when clinically indicated, together with selective autoimmune testing in atypical cases to minimize diagnostic misclassification.48

Advanced imaging-based analytic tools may eventually enhance screening triage after discharge, although they are not yet part of standard clinical practice. For example, a CT radiomics nomogram integrating radiomic features with clinical variables, including AP severity, recurrence, and admission glucose, demonstrated better discrimination for incident PPDM-A than clinical variables alone.54 This signal is encouraging, but it should be interpreted as preliminary because the current radiomics literature is still dominated by retrospective model development rather than prospective implementation or broad external validation. At present, such approaches are best viewed as potential triage enhancers within a risk-stratified surveillance framework rather than as replacements for laboratory-based follow-up or for clinically transparent predictors such as necrosis, recurrence, exocrine dysfunction, and inpatient glycemic patterns.

Endocrine phenotyping may be further supported by measurement of insulin and/or C-peptide, interpreted in conjunction with contemporaneous glucose levels, to estimate residual secretory reserve. In atypical clinical presentations, pancreatic autoantibody testing, including GAD65, IA-2, and ZnT8, may be warranted to exclude autoimmune diabetes. Conceptual definitions of PPDM emphasize that confirmed type 1 diabetes, diabetes predating the first AP episode, and transient stress hyperglycemia during or soon after AP should not be classified as PPDM-A.14,61 This distinction is critical both for diagnostic validity and for the development of an appropriate follow-up strategy.

Immune and inflammatory biomarkers may, in the future, contribute to more refined risk stratification, but they currently remain investigational. One study reported that the percentage of B lymphocytes differed between patients who later developed PPDM-A and those who remained normoglycemic after AP, with this variable retained in multivariable prediction together with recurrent AP and other clinical factors.13 These findings are hypothesis-generating and useful for future mechanism-oriented studies, but they do not yet support routine biomarker-guided surveillance and should not be interpreted as having evidentiary weight comparable to longitudinal glycemic assessment, pancreatic imaging sequelae, or exocrine dysfunction. Structured glycemic surveillance based on validated diagnostic criteria therefore remains the clinical priority.

Overall, post-discharge diagnostics and screening for PPDM-A should be structured, repeated, and context-sensitive. The most robust clinical strategy is one that avoids diagnosing PPDM-A during the window of stress hyperglycemia, performs formal reassessment after recovery, typically at or beyond 3 months, continues surveillance for months to years using standard glucose-based criteria with selective OGTT and cautious interpretation of HbA1c, and incorporates AP-specific determinants, including disease severity, pancreatic necrosis, recurrence, imaging sequelae, and exocrine dysfunction, in order to define the intensity of follow-up.14,15,25

Prevention of PPDM-A and Risk Modification

Prevention of PPDM-A should be framed as a structured, pancreas-centered strategy of post-acute care aimed at limiting cumulative pancreatic injury and attenuating subsequent endocrine decline after an index episode of AP. Unlike conventional models of diabetes prevention, prevention of PPDM-A must focus primarily on mechanisms directly linked to the course and aftermath of pancreatitis, particularly prevention of recurrent AP, early recognition of EPD or insufficiency, and risk-adapted metabolic surveillance after discharge. Preventive measures should therefore begin once the acute episode has stabilized and oral nutrition has been re-established, and they should be incorporated into standardized post-AP follow-up pathways rather than reduced to nonspecific lifestyle counseling alone.14,53

A central principle in PPDM-A prevention is that repeated pancreatic injury appears to amplify the risk of subsequent diabetes. On this basis, etiology-directed secondary prevention of recurrent AP should be regarded as one of the most clinically actionable strategies for reducing PPDM-A risk. Cohort and population-based data support an association between recurrence burden and PPDM, including in hypertriglyceridemia-induced AP, in which a higher number of recurrent attacks was significantly associated with post-pancreatitis diabetes.62 These findings support a practical post-discharge model centered on identification and elimination of recurrent triggers, including definitive treatment of biliary disease, reduction of triglyceride levels, alcohol abstinence, and withdrawal of pancreatitis-inducing medications where appropriate, combined with close follow-up to minimize repeated pancreatic insults.61

In biliary AP, timely cholecystectomy represents a particularly important pancreas-directed preventive intervention. In a nationwide cohort of patients with a first episode of AP, delayed cholecystectomy and recurrent biliary events before surgery were associated with a higher risk of incident PPDM and with greater subsequent need for insulin therapy.18 These observations suggest that timely definitive treatment of biliary etiology is relevant not only to recurrence prevention but also to reducing repeated pancreatic injury that may accelerate endocrine deterioration after AP. Although residual confounding cannot be excluded, the clinical implication is clear: prevention of recurrence and reduction of endocrine risk should be integrated within the same longitudinal care pathway.

Risk stratification during the index hospitalization and immediately after discharge may further strengthen prevention planning. Clinical commentary in pancreatology has identified discharge protocols as an underused opportunity to improve recognition and follow-up of diabetes risk after AP, particularly in patients who are otherwise lost in fragmented post-hospital care.53 A surveillance-oriented model for metabolic sequelae after pancreatitis has proposed structured follow-up with repeated glycemic assessment together with evaluation of exocrine function, including fecal elastase testing in appropriate settings, thereby redefining prevention as early identification and early response rather than delayed recognition of established disease.14 In addition, simple inpatient tools such as the CAPS score, which incorporates cirrhosis, albumin, history of prediabetes, and moderately severe or severe pancreatitis, may help identify individuals who require intensified glucose monitoring and earlier optimization of modifiable risk factors after discharge.56

The severity and pattern of pancreatic injury during the index episode should also inform the intensity of preventive follow-up. Prospective studies indicate that patients who later develop new-onset diabetes after AP more often exhibit high-injury phenotypes, including extensive pancreatic necrosis on early computed tomography.31 This supports a prevention framework in which follow-up is guided not solely by broad severity categories, but also by more specific indicators of tissue destruction, likelihood of recurrence, and persistent structural sequelae. Contemporary prediction models likewise identify admission glycemia and adiposity-related parameters as clinically relevant correlates of PPDM-A risk, thereby supporting closer surveillance and targeted risk modification in susceptible phenotypes.20

Another major pancreatitis-specific target for risk modification is early recognition of EPD or EPI, given that exocrine sequelae may precede and predict later diabetes after pancreatitis. In a nationwide cohort study, newly developed EPD after pancreatitis was strongly associated with incident diabetes, including in analyses incorporating lag periods to reduce reverse causation.46 Although these findings do not establish that treatment of EPI prevents PPDM-A, they strongly support a prevention-oriented approach in which exocrine dysfunction and maldigestion are actively sought rather than passively documented. In clinical practice, this requires structured assessment for steatorrhea, bloating, weight loss, and nutritional decline, together with targeted evaluation of micronutrient status, particularly fat-soluble vitamins when clinically indicated, as part of post-AP risk modification.46,63

Nutritional risk modification in PPDM-A should be individualized and grounded in the metabolic and digestive consequences of AP recovery. The aim is not merely to promote generic healthy eating or weight loss, but to prevent both metabolic deterioration and nutritional compromise during the post-pancreatitis period. In some AP survivors, obesity and visceral adiposity represent important risk signals for PPDM-A and justify structured weight-oriented intervention.31,64 In others, however, reduced intake, maldigestion, sarcopenia, or micronutrient deficiencies may predominate and require a different preventive strategy. Observational studies in post-AP populations linking habitual vitamin and vitamer intake with markers of insulin resistance and β-cell function support the value of systematic nutritional assessment and selective correction of deficiencies as part of a broader prevention plan.65 Dietary counseling may also need to be tailored to pancreatic fat burden, since associations between carbohydrate intake and insulin-related traits after AP appear to differ according to the degree of intra-pancreatic fat deposition.66

Lifestyle-based risk modification remains relevant in PPDM-A, but it should be explicitly linked to prevention of recurrent pancreatic injury and to the specific metabolic vulnerability that follows AP rather than framed as a generic diabetes prevention message. Alcohol abstinence and smoking cessation are particularly important because they may reduce recurrent pancreatic injury and thereby indirectly lower the risk of PPDM-A.61,63 Physical activity and optimization of body composition are also plausible preventive strategies in AP survivors with adiposity-driven risk, including those with ectopic fat accumulation, but such interventions should be individualized according to nutritional status, exocrine function, and the phase of clinical recovery.64,66

Meta-analytic data further support a prevention strategy that is both etiology-specific and attentive to comorbidity burden. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that higher risk of PPDM was associated with alcoholic AP, hypertriglyceridemia-induced AP, obesity, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and liver disease.29 These associations should not be interpreted as proof of direct preventable causality, but they do provide a pragmatic basis for stratified surveillance and targeted management after AP, including control of dyslipidemia, reduction of triglyceride levels, and coordinated management of major comorbid conditions in patients at elevated risk of PPDM-A.29

Additional AP-specific signals from the index hospitalization may help refine surveillance intensity and preventive planning after discharge. In hypertriglyceridemic AP, larger in-hospital glycemic excursions of long amplitude were independently associated with subsequent PPDM, and a threshold has been proposed for identifying patients at particularly high risk who may benefit from intensified follow-up.24 At present, such measures should be regarded as aids to risk stratification rather than validated therapeutic targets. Prospective data also suggest that older age may be associated with earlier onset of diabetes after AP, supporting closer early surveillance in older AP survivors.31

Pharmacologic prevention of PPDM-A remains insufficiently evidence-based, and no medication can currently be recommended specifically for primary prevention of PPDM-A on the basis of randomized trial data. Observational studies suggest that certain exposures, such as statin therapy, may be associated with a lower incidence of PPDM after a first attack of AP; however, these findings remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation before they can be translated into preventive recommendations.67 Likewise, anti-inflammatory and microbiota-directed interventions remain investigational in this setting. In this context, the historical experience with probiotic prophylaxis in severe AP, which raised substantial safety concerns, remains an important reminder that even biologically plausible interventions in pancreatitis require rigorous, safety-focused evaluation before incorporation into prevention pathways.68

Overall, the most defensible current model for prevention of PPDM-A is a structured post-AP program that integrates four interrelated components: etiology-specific prevention of recurrent AP, risk-stratified glycemic surveillance after discharge, active detection and management of exocrine dysfunction and maldigestion, and individualized nutritional and lifestyle modification aligned with the patient’s post-AP phenotype and recovery status. Treatment of PPDM-A

The treatment of PPDM-A should be conceptualized as a post-acute, phenotype-informed management strategy rather than as a uniform extension of type 2 diabetes care. Two pathophysiological dimensions recurrently shape therapeutic decision-making in this setting: variable degrees of insulin deficiency resulting from pancreatic endocrine injury and heterogeneous insulin resistance that may either predate AP or develop during recovery. Accordingly, safe and effective management cannot rely solely on glycemic indices, but must also incorporate the post-AP clinical phenotype, including nutritional status, weight trajectory, dietary regularity, and the presence of EPD/EPI, all of which may substantially affect treatment tolerability and glycemic variability5,15,23 (Figure 7). Because glucose-lowering therapy in PPDM-A often requires close coordination with nutritional support and exocrine management, multidisciplinary follow-up involving both endocrinology and gastroenterology is preferable whenever feasible, particularly in insulin-treated individuals and those with nutritional vulnerability.11

Glycemic targets in PPDM-A should generally align with contemporary standards of diabetes care, but their application should place particular emphasis on hypoglycemia prevention and overall treatment safety. For many nonpregnant adults, an HbA1c target of <7% remains appropriate; however, less stringent targets, such as <8%, are often more suitable when the dominant clinical concerns include severe hypoglycemia risk, marked glycemic instability, malnutrition, low body weight, substantial comorbidity burden, or limited life expectancy.69 In PPDM-A, HbA1c alone may fail to capture clinically important glycemic lability, especially in the presence of irregular food intake or malabsorption. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) therefore has particular value in this population, as it permits assessment of time in range (70–180 mg/dL), time below range, nocturnal hypoglycemia, and day-to-day glycemic variability, thereby improving both treatment titration and safety.42,69 In high-risk settings, including prior severe hypoglycemia, recurrent nocturnal hypoglycemic episodes, cognitive impairment, or solitary living, CGM with alert functions should be viewed primarily as a safety intervention rather than as a convenience technology.69

A pragmatic therapeutic approach to PPDM-A begins with determining whether insulin deficiency is the dominant metabolic feature. When the clinical presentation is characterized by severe hyperglycemia, ketosis or diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar states, progressive weight loss, pronounced glycemic variability, or suspected impairment of endogenous insulin secretion, insulin therapy should be considered early rather than deferred until later stages of treatment failure.11,15 This approach is further supported by population-based data showing that PPDM is associated with higher risks of severe hypoglycemia, major adverse cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality than type 2 diabetes, underscoring the need for closer surveillance and individualized treatment goals from the outset.1 When insulin is required in PPDM-A, treatment should be regarded as highly safety-sensitive, because the risk of hypoglycemia may be amplified by irregular oral intake, nausea, abdominal pain, carbohydrate malabsorption, and impaired counterregulatory responses. In clinical practice, long-acting basal insulin analogues are often favored as the most stable therapeutic foundation, with cautious titration guided by self-monitoring of blood glucose or CGM.70 Rapid-acting prandial insulin can then be introduced when needed, but dosing should be individualized according to actual meal timing and quantity, with consideration of postprandial administration or conservative correction strategies in patients with inconsistent appetite or intake. Because EPI and maldigestion may lead to erratic nutrient absorption, optimization of exocrine support and stabilization of dietary intake are often prerequisites for the safe titration of prandial insulin.45,63

Emerging cohort data specific to AP suggest that glycemic variability during the index hospitalization may also inform the intensity of subsequent diabetes surveillance and therapeutic planning. In a hospital-based cohort of patients with AP followed for 3 years, larger in-hospital glycemic excursions, quantified as the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE), were independently associated with subsequent development of PPDM-A.24 At present, these findings are best interpreted as tools for post-discharge risk stratification and follow-up planning rather than as a basis for defining specific inpatient glycemic intervention targets.

In patients in whom insulin resistance appears to predominate and endogenous insulin secretion is relatively preserved, metformin often represents a rational first-line pharmacologic foundation, provided that it is tolerated and not contraindicated. This is particularly relevant in individuals with overweight or obesity, features of metabolic syndrome, or relatively mild hyperglycemia after AP.11 Notably, in nationwide data on PPDM, metformin exposure was associated with lower risks of severe hypoglycemia, major adverse cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality, supporting its role as a foundational therapy when clinically feasible.71 In practice, gastrointestinal intolerance may substantially limit its use in post-pancreatitis populations; however, slow dose escalation, extended-release formulations, and simultaneous management of maldigestion may improve tolerability.63,70 When tolerated, metformin is often continued after insulin initiation, as this may reduce insulin requirements and contribute to improved overall metabolic control.70

Other non-insulin glucose-lowering agents should be used selectively and only after careful clinical phenotyping. Sulfonylureas and glinides are generally less attractive options because the inherent risk of hypoglycemia is magnified in the setting of irregular food intake and marked glycemic variability.70 Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists may be appealing in some type 2 diabetes contexts, but their role in PPDM-A remains insufficiently defined because of limited disease-specific evidence, frequent gastrointestinal adverse effects, and persistent concern regarding use in individuals with a history of pancreatitis. Their use should therefore be individualized and cautious rather than routine.11,63 Thiazolidinediones may improve insulin resistance, but their utility is often constrained by edema, heart failure risk, weight gain, and fracture risk, all of which may be particularly problematic in PPDM-A populations characterized by frailty or substantial comorbidity.70

In patients with PPDM-A and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease, treatment should remain consistent with contemporary diabetes guidelines favoring agents with proven cardiorenal benefit, while incorporating additional PPDM-A-specific safety considerations.69,70 In particular, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors may be appropriate when cardiorenal indications are compelling; however, the risk of ketoacidosis may be heightened in PPDM-A phenotypes marked by relative insulin deficiency, reduced caloric intake, dehydration, or intercurrent illness. Careful patient selection, explicit sick-day education, maintenance of adequate nutrition and hydration, and temporary drug discontinuation during high-risk periods are therefore essential when these agents are prescribed.69

Because exocrine dysfunction and EPI are common after AP and may substantially destabilize diabetes management, integration of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) should be regarded as a clinically important component of PPDM-A care. By improving digestion and nutrient absorption, PERT may reduce glycemic variability, enhance tolerance of oral glucose-lowering medications, and support nutritional recovery, thereby indirectly lowering the risk of hypoglycemia.45,72 Exocrine dysfunction should therefore be actively evaluated and treated within PPDM-A care pathways rather than approached as a separate or secondary issue. This requires attention to symptoms of maldigestion and steatorrhea, longitudinal body-weight trends, and targeted monitoring for fat-soluble vitamin deficiency when clinically indicated.45,63 When PERT is initiated, therapeutic response should be assessed clinically through stool pattern, gastrointestinal symptoms, and weight trajectory, with dose adjustment when maldigestion persists.59

Nutritional and lifestyle interventions should be considered integral therapeutic components of PPDM-A rather than general supportive advice. In this setting, dietary regularity directly influences glycemic variability, the safety of insulin administration, and the tolerability of glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy. Nutritional management should prioritize preservation or restoration of lean body mass, adequate protein and caloric intake when clinically appropriate, and correction of micronutrient deficiencies, while avoiding iatrogenic exacerbation of maldigestion or unintended weight loss.63,73 Although a fiber-rich dietary pattern may be beneficial in selected patients, its implementation should be individualized, particularly in those with poorly controlled EPI or persistent steatorrhea.74

Taken together, the management of PPDM-A cannot be reduced to the selection of a single glucose-lowering agent. Effective treatment requires coordinated control of hyperglycemia, proactive prevention of hypoglycemia, active recognition and treatment of exocrine dysfunction, nutritional rehabilitation, and stabilization of dietary intake patterns that directly influence both medication safety and glycemic variability. PPDM-A is therefore best approached as a distinct post-AP clinical entity in which endocrine, exocrine, and nutritional care must be integrated within a structured longitudinal follow-up model.5,15,45,69

Important unresolved questions remain in the therapeutic management of PPDM-A, including the optimal timing for treatment initiation after the acute episode, the best way to phenotype insulin deficiency versus insulin resistance in routine practice, the comparative effectiveness of non-insulin agents in this population, and the extent to which treatment should be guided by exocrine dysfunction, nutritional frailty, and glycemic variability rather than HbA1c alone. Accordingly, current management is still based largely on extrapolation from broader diabetes and diabetes-of-the-exocrine-pancreas literature, and dedicated prospective PPDM-A treatment studies remain a major unmet need.11,15,63,69

Complications of PPDM-A and Long-Term Follow-up

The long-term consequences of PPDM-A extend beyond the conventional complication paradigm applied to type 2 diabetes. In this setting, risk is shaped not only by chronic hyperglycemia but also by the broader metabolic aftermath of AP, including EPD/EPI, maldigestion, micronutrient deficiency, body-weight instability, sarcopenia, and pronounced glycemic lability. Accordingly, morbidity in PPDM-A is frequently driven by wide glucose fluctuations and episodes of symptomatic or severe hypoglycemia in addition to sustained hyperglycemia. These factors may contribute to rehospitalization, delayed functional recovery, reduced work capacity, and increased mortality. Population-based comparisons between PPDM and type 2 diabetes support this interpretation, demonstrating a disproportionate burden of severe hypoglycemia and higher all-cause mortality in PPDM, consistent with a metabolically fragile phenotype of pancreatitis-related diabetes.1 In PPDM-A, this vulnerability is likely further amplified by fluctuating caloric intake, variable nutrient absorption, and impaired counterregulatory responses in a subset of patients, particularly in those with endocrine insufficiency and coexisting EPI.46, 63.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has particular value in the long-term management of PPDM-A because it captures glycemic instability more effectively than HbA1c alone. Studies in pancreatogenic diabetes phenotypes have identified clinically relevant nocturnal hypoglycemia together with marked postprandial hyperglycemic excursions, underscoring time-of-day susceptibility and meal-related variability as major therapeutic targets.42 In routine PPDM-A care, CGM-derived metrics, including time in range, time below range, and coefficient of variation, may therefore be useful not only for treatment adjustment but also for complication prevention, especially in patients with recurrent hypoglycemia, discordant HbA1c values, or unstable nutritional intake.60,69

An important longitudinal consideration in PPDM-A is that glycemic variability may represent both a complication and a harbinger of subsequent adverse outcomes. Emerging AP-specific evidence suggests that glucose instability during the index hospitalization is associated with future development of PPDM-A. In a hospital-based AP cohort with 3-year follow-up, the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE) during hospitalization independently predicted later PPDM-A, indicating that early dysglycemia may identify patients who warrant intensified surveillance after discharge.24 Prospective longitudinal studies also demonstrate heterogeneity in post-AP glycemic trajectories over time, reinforcing that classification at a single post-discharge time point is insufficient.23 Although interventional thresholds remain undefined, these findings support a follow-up strategy in which early glycemic lability after AP is regarded as a marker of risk for brittle long-term metabolic trajectories.

The long-term burden of PPDM-A is not limited to glucose fluctuations. Persistent inflammatory and metabolic disturbances after AP may promote chronic catabolic signaling, weight instability, and sarcopenia, each of which can aggravate insulin resistance, diminish physiologic reserve, and increase vulnerability to treatment-related hypoglycemia. In a cohort evaluated at a mean of approximately 30 months after a first AP episode, interleukin-6 was associated with the lipolysis marker glycerol, and the increase in glycerol was substantially greater in individuals with abnormal glucose metabolism after pancreatitis than in those with normal glucose metabolism, supporting a link between persistent inflammatory signaling and long-term metabolic fragility.41 In parallel, glycemic variability has been linked to greater short-term risk and may plausibly contribute to cumulative vascular injury, further emphasizing that management should target glucose volatility rather than HbA1c in isolation.42

Notably, short-term inpatient outcomes do not adequately reflect long-term PPDM-A risk. In a tertiary-care AP cohort with a median follow-up of approximately 22 months, new-onset PPDM during hospitalization was not an independent predictor of length of stay, intensive therapy unit admission, or death in multivariable analyses.34 This observation highlights a key principle of PPDM-A follow-up: the absence of major adverse inpatient events should not be interpreted as evidence of low long-term metabolic risk, because much of the clinically relevant burden becomes apparent later through recurrent metabolic instability, hypoglycemia, and cumulative complications.

With regard to microvascular complications, including retinopathy, chronic kidney disease, neuropathy, and diabetic foot disease, the evidence base derived from rigorously defined PPDM-A cohorts remains less developed than that for type 2 diabetes, partly because of heterogeneous case definitions and inconsistent exclusion of pre-existing dysglycemia. Nevertheless, the pragmatic clinical approach is to assume that PPDM-A carries clinically meaningful microvascular risk for a given degree of glycemic exposure and to implement standard evidence-based screening pathways while accounting for the heightened susceptibility of these patients to hypoglycemia and malnutrition.25,75,76 Thus, the distinguishing feature of PPDM-A is not a fundamentally different microvascular screening strategy, but rather the need to individualize glycemic targets and therapeutic intensification in order to minimize iatrogenic harm in patients with labile glycemic control, fluctuating intake, or poor nutritional reserve.77

Macrovascular risk in PPDM-A should likewise be addressed through a dual framework that incorporates both traditional diabetes-related atherothrombotic risk and the broader post-pancreatitis clinical milieu, including smoking, alcohol exposure, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome, deconditioning, and sarcopenia. Even if differences in major adverse cardiovascular events between PPDM and type 2 diabetes are not consistently large across datasets, the excess burden of severe hypoglycemia and mortality in PPDM supports a rigorous guideline-concordant approach to cardiovascular prevention, including blood pressure control, lipid lowering, smoking cessation, and structured physical activity, while tailoring glycemic goals to reduce hypoglycemia in nutritionally vulnerable patients.1,78

A pancreatitis-specific and potentially modifiable determinant of long-term PPDM-A risk is prevention of recurrence. Recurrent AP episodes, particularly recurrent biliary events, appear to increase the likelihood of subsequent PPDM-A and of later insulin-requiring disease phenotypes. In a nationwide cohort of patients after a first AP episode, delayed cholecystectomy was associated with a higher risk of PPDM and subsequent insulin use, and recurrent biliary events before cholecystectomy further increased PPDM risk.18 Long-term care in PPDM-A should therefore explicitly incorporate recurrence prevention, including etiology-specific management and biliary risk control where relevant, as an integral component of metabolic risk reduction.

Nutritional complications are central to the long-term course of PPDM-A. EPI may persist or newly develop after AP, including after episodes initially classified as mild, and may directly destabilize diabetes management through maldigestion, malabsorption, weight loss, and micronutrient deficiency. In a prospective multicenter cohort, more than one-third of participants had objective evidence of EPI at 12 months after AP, and severe EPI was observed even after index mild AP.45 These findings support proactive surveillance for EPI/EPD during PPDM-A follow-up rather than reliance on symptom-triggered testing alone. Clinically important domains include steatorrhea, bloating and flatulence, weight loss, protein-calorie malnutrition, and deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins, all of which may impair functional status and increase susceptibility to hypoglycemia during glucose-lowering therapy.63,73

Longitudinal data further support the integration of exocrine surveillance into diabetes follow-up after pancreatitis. In a nationwide cohort study, EPD was associated with a substantially increased risk of new-onset diabetes, and the temporal separation between EPD and incident diabetes suggested that exocrine abnormalities may precede overt dysglycemia in some patients.46 Although these data do not establish causality, they strengthen the rationale for combined endocrine-exocrine follow-up in PPDM-A and for routine nutritional assessment that includes monitoring of weight trajectory, dietary quality, symptom burden, and targeted micronutrient testing when clinically indicated.79

Bone health should also be recognized as a major domain of long-term complications in PPDM-A. Post-pancreatitis metabolic sequelae commonly include nutritional deficits, reduced physical activity, persistent inflammation, and exocrine dysfunction, all of which may contribute to osteoporosis and fragility fractures. Integrative frameworks of pancreatitis sequelae identify skeletal complications as clinically meaningful long-term outcomes, and the associated risk may not be fully captured by bone mineral density measurements alone.14 This issue is particularly relevant in PPDM-A when EPI contributes to deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins, especially vitamin D, as well as to weight loss and reduced muscle mass. Bone-health surveillance and correction of nutritional deficiencies should therefore be incorporated into complication prevention rather than relegated to a purely supportive role.14,79

Sarcopenia and impaired physical function are likewise both consequences and amplifiers of adverse post-AP trajectories, with direct relevance to PPDM-A. Loss of skeletal muscle compromises glucose disposal and may worsen insulin resistance, whereas low energy reserves and irregular nutritional intake increase vulnerability to hypoglycemia during treatment. Recent data in AP populations associate sarcopenia with worse clinical outcomes and support structured rehabilitation strategies combining nutritional support with progressive physical activity.80 In long-term PPDM-A care, sarcopenia should therefore be viewed as both a metabolic and a functional complication domain, with implications for adherence, self-management capacity, and fall risk. Additional clinical risk-factor studies in pancreatitis cohorts further support the inclusion of sarcopenia-focused assessment in routine follow-up planning.81

Because many clinically important harms evolve gradually, long-term follow-up in PPDM-A should prioritize structured surveillance rather than opportunistic glucose testing alone. Recent studies have evaluated risk-stratification tools for predicting PPDM-A after discharge, including in-hospital glucose-variability markers, CT radiomics models, and clinical nomograms.19,20,24,54 Although these tools are designed to predict incident PPDM-A rather than late complications per se, they are highly relevant to follow-up planning because early identification of high-risk patients may facilitate timely nutrition and EPI evaluation, earlier use of CGM, and specialist referral.

A structured surveillance framework for metabolic sequelae after pancreatitis is particularly applicable to PPDM-A because dysglycemia and exocrine dysfunction may emerge at different times and in variable sequence. The proposed metabolic sequelae surveillance programme recommends initiating surveillance at approximately 6 months after pancreatitis diagnosis in order to reduce overdiagnosis related to transient stress hyperglycemia, with at least annual follow-up during the early surveillance period; in-hospital or early outpatient baseline assessment may further help distinguish pre-existing from new-onset abnormalities.14 In practical PPDM-A care pathways, screening at approximately 3 months after discharge, when stress hyperglycemia has generally resolved and HbA1c interpretation becomes more reliable, using HbA1c and/or oral glucose tolerance testing is also reasonable, followed by repeated assessment during the first year and annual surveillance thereafter, given that diabetes risk may persist for years after a single AP episode.5,23,49

Quality of life and functional recovery should be regarded as core long-term endpoints in PPDM-A rather than secondary considerations. Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, including pain, dyspepsia, diarrhea, and steatorrhea, together with fatigue, sleep disturbance, reduced exercise tolerance, and psychological burden after severe illness, may indirectly worsen nutritional status, adherence, and glycemic control. Studies of post-pancreatitis recovery also suggest that return to work and broader psychosocial outcomes are clinically meaningful and should be incorporated into long-term care planning through patient education, behavioral support, and individualized goal setting.82

Taken together, these considerations support a multidisciplinary long-term follow-up model for PPDM-A. Endocrinology input is needed for glycemic management and complication screening; gastroenterology or pancreatology input is important for pancreatic sequelae, including EPI/EPD, pain, and recurrence prevention; nutrition specialists are essential for dietary optimization and pancreatic enzyme or vitamin strategies; and physiotherapy and mental health support should be incorporated when indicated. In practical terms, early reassessment after discharge once acute-phase confounding has subsided, repeated evaluations during the first year, and annual long-term monitoring aligned with standard diabetes complication pathways provide a coherent framework for sustained risk reduction in PPDM-A.14,76,77

Special Patient Groups in PPDM-A

Special patient subgroups in PPDM-A illustrate that this disorder should not be viewed simply as “type 2 diabetes developing after hospitalization for pancreatitis”, but rather as a heterogeneous post-pancreatitis metabolic syndrome shaped by varying contributions of insulin deficiency, insulin resistance, EPD/EPI, nutritional vulnerability, and recurrent pancreatic injury. Contemporary diabetes standards support etiologic classification when the clinical context strongly suggests diabetes of the exocrine pancreas, while maintaining the use of standard diagnostic criteria, including fasting plasma glucose, oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT), and/or HbA1c, and acknowledging that HbA1c may be less reliable after critical illness or in nutritionally vulnerable patients.25 In PPDM-A, this etiologic framework has important practical value because it improves recognition of glycemic lability and hypoglycemia risk, prompts systematic assessment of exocrine function and nutritional status, and supports longitudinal surveillance for progressive endocrine dysfunction after pancreatic injury.14,15

One of the most clinically actionable subgroups is PPDM-A with predominant exocrine dysfunction, in which maldigestion, weight loss, micronutrient deficiency, sarcopenia, and brittle glycemia frequently coexist (Figure 5). In a nationwide cohort analysis following a first episode of AP, EPD was associated with a substantially increased risk of subsequent new-onset diabetes, and this association remained significant in lag analyses designed to minimize reverse-causation bias. Importantly, the signal was also observed after mild AP, indicating that clinically meaningful exocrine-predominant risk is not restricted to severe or necrotizing disease.46 This phenotype warrants explicit nutritional surveillance because malabsorption and reduced oral intake can destabilize glucose control, increase susceptibility to hypoglycemia during therapy, and contribute to functional decline.14,63 In practical terms, these patients should be assessed not only for glycemic abnormalities but also for weight trajectory, gastrointestinal symptom burden, and objective evidence of EPI/EPD, since endocrine and exocrine sequelae after AP often evolve in parallel rather than independently.45

A second underrecognized subgroup comprises patients who develop PPDM-A after mild, non-necrotizing AP. Because mild AP accounts for the majority of clinical presentations, there is a persistent but misleading assumption that long-term metabolic sequelae are negligible in this setting. However, cohort and surveillance data indicate that diabetes risk remains clinically relevant even after apparently uncomplicated AP, and such patients may easily be overlooked or misclassified as having routine type 2 diabetes if the history of pancreatitis is not incorporated into diagnostic reasoning.5,46,53 This subgroup is clinically heterogeneous, as some patients may demonstrate a phenotype dominated by insulin resistance rather than overt insulin deficiency, which may further obscure recognition of PPDM-A.53 For this reason, structured post-discharge metabolic testing, including OGTT where feasible, is preferable to passive follow-up even in patients without necrosis or prolonged organ failure.5,25

Another important subgroup consists of patients with early glycemic instability during the index AP admission, including stress hyperglycemia and marked inpatient glucose variability. These individuals may not yet fulfill durable diagnostic criteria for PPDM-A during hospitalization, but they appear to represent a high-risk phenotype for later diabetes and more brittle metabolic trajectories. Recent risk-stratification studies have identified inpatient glycemic variability as a strong predictor of subsequent PPDM-A, supporting intensified follow-up in patients with large glucose excursions even when glucose levels appear to normalize by discharge.24 Prospective longitudinal evidence further supports the concept of heterogeneous glycemic trajectories after AP, indicating that a single normal or borderline measurement shortly after discharge does not reliably exclude long-term endocrine risk.23 This subgroup is particularly important in follow-up planning because short-term inpatient outcomes do not accurately reflect long-term metabolic vulnerability.34

A clinically distinct subgroup is formed by patients with recurrent AP or repeated AP-related events, who follow a cumulative-injury trajectory. Recurrent episodes appear to amplify diabetes risk and may reopen a period of metabolic vulnerability after each attack. In cohorts of hypertriglyceridemic AP, the number of recurrences has been significantly associated with PPDM-A risk, supporting the view that recurrence burden should be incorporated into risk-adapted surveillance rather than assuming a single stable trajectory after the initial episode.62 A similar implication emerges in biliary AP, where delayed cholecystectomy and increasing numbers of recurrent biliary events before surgery have been associated with progressively higher PPDM risk, supporting an “event-reset” approach to surveillance after each relapse.18 More broadly, recurrence after a first AP episode is not uncommon, which reinforces the need for continued surveillance beyond the initial post-discharge period.83

A further high-risk subgroup includes patients with severe pancreatic injury, particularly those with necrotizing pancreatitis, persistent organ failure, infected necrosis, or the need for invasive intervention during the index episode. These patients sustain a greater biological burden of pancreatic injury and are therefore more likely to develop persistent endocrine insufficiency, concomitant exocrine dysfunction, weight loss, and unstable glucose profiles during recovery. Long-term follow-up studies after AP have shown that pancreatic necrosis and disease severity are independent risk factors for subsequent pancreatic endocrine insufficiency.84 In patients with infected necrotizing pancreatitis requiring intervention, follow-up data from randomized trials indicate that long-term endocrine and nutrition-related sequelae remain common despite modern step-up treatment strategies.85 Clinically, this subgroup often requires earlier multidisciplinary involvement, including endocrinology, pancreatology, and nutrition support, together with closer glucose monitoring because the combination of tissue injury, EPI, and fluctuating intake substantially increases the risk of hypoglycemia during treatment escalation.15,45

Another diagnostically challenging subgroup comprises younger or non-obese patients without overt metabolic syndrome, in whom PPDM-A may be underrecognized because clinicians often implicitly associate post-pancreatitis dysglycemia with older age or classic type 2 diabetes phenotypes. In such cases, anchoring on body habitus may delay recognition and defer appropriate endocrine-exocrine evaluation. This subgroup is clinically important because dysglycemia after AP may develop in younger individuals and even after a single episode, particularly when structural pancreatic abnormalities or recurrent attacks are present, underscoring the need for proactive follow-up rather than symptom-driven testing alone.1,86,87

A further pragmatic subgroup is the “multiple-hit” PPDM-A phenotype, in which pancreatic injury coexists with metabolic and systemic vulnerability factors that amplify risk without displacing pancreatitis as the primary etiologic driver. AP-focused cohort studies and predictive models have identified recurrence burden, disease severity, inflammatory and laboratory markers, and metabolic comorbidity signals as clinically relevant correlates of post-AP diabetes risk in selected populations.20,24,39,56,62 These patients may appear predominantly “metabolic” at first glance, yet the pancreatitis history remains central to pathogenesis and follow-up planning. In practice, this subgroup is best managed through intensified surveillance, recurrence prevention, and integrated nutritional and cardiometabolic care rather than being treated as having routine type 2 diabetes alone.

Another underappreciated subgroup includes patients with psychiatric comorbidity or marked psychosocial vulnerability, in whom risk may be amplified by medication effects, disordered eating, alcohol or substance exposure, fragmented continuity of care, and reduced adherence to long-term monitoring. Although direct PPDM-A-specific outcome data for this subgroup remain limited, surveillance-oriented pancreatitis frameworks identify psychiatric disorders and psychosocial factors as clinically relevant modifiers of long-term metabolic trajectories.14,82 In PPDM-A, these considerations justify closer care coordination, simplified monitoring strategies, and proactive scheduling of follow-up, because discontinuity of care may delay diagnosis and worsen glycemic instability.

Finally, a clinically important subgroup is technology-eligible PPDM-A with heightened vulnerability to hypoglycemia, particularly patients with EPI and weight loss, marked glycemic variability, nocturnal hypoglycemia, or discordance between HbA1c and day-to-day glucose patterns. Although recommendations for diabetes technology are not specific to PPDM-A, current guidance for other specific diabetes types supports the use of CGM in pancreatogenic diabetes to improve safety and detect otherwise unrecognized hypoglycemia.60 In PPDM-A, this supports early consideration of CGM in selected high-risk phenotypes, not only to refine glycemic targets, but also to reduce hypoglycemia risk and facilitate safer adjustment of nutrition and glucose-lowering therapy.50,69

Taken together, these special patient groups support an AP-specific approach to PPDM-A that avoids automatic relabeling as routine type 2 diabetes, explicitly integrates exocrine function and nutritional status into interpretation of dysglycemia, and applies risk-adapted longitudinal surveillance informed by readily available clinical signals, including disease severity, pancreatic necrosis, inpatient glycemic variability, recurrence burden, baseline metabolic profile, nutritional reserve, and psychosocial vulnerability. This phenotype-based framework is particularly important because the risk and management complexity of PPDM-A may be substantial even after a single apparently uncomplicated episode of AP.

Discussion

PPDM-A should be regarded as a distinct post-acute metabolic syndrome rather than a simple extension of type 2 diabetes arising after hospitalization for pancreatitis. Across the literature reviewed, its clinical significance rests on three consistent observations: PPDM-A is relatively common, biologically heterogeneous, and associated with clinically important long-term morbidity. A major challenge in both research and clinical practice is diagnostic attribution. In many published cohorts, pre-AP glycemic status has not been characterized with sufficient rigor, limiting the ability to distinguish truly incident PPDM-A from previously unrecognized diabetes. Even when HbA1c is available, its interpretation may be confounded in the peri-hospital period by anemia, transfusion, altered erythrocyte turnover, or recent critical illness.25 Accordingly, well-defined post-discharge reassessment windows are essential if persistent diabetes is to be differentiated from transient stress-related dysglycemia. Without such temporal standardization, both epidemiologic estimates and clinical classification remain vulnerable to substantial misclassification bias.

The most biologically coherent interpretation of PPDM-A is a multi-hit model in which pancreatic endocrine injury sustained during AP interacts with systemic insulin resistance, persistent low-grade inflammation, altered body composition, exocrine dysfunction, and disruption of the gut-pancreas axis. This framework is more consistent with the available evidence than any single-mechanism explanation and helps account for the considerable phenotypic diversity observed across patient populations. In this context, the emerging association between microbiome signatures, AP severity, and subsequent diabetes further supports the concept of a broader post-pancreatitis metabolic network, although a causal relationship has not yet been established.7,88

From a clinical standpoint, EPI is especially important because it provides a mechanistic link between pancreatic injury and several downstream determinants of adverse metabolic outcomes, including maldigestion, weight loss, micronutrient deficiency, altered incretin signaling, and glycemic instability. Prospective multicenter data demonstrating persistent and heterogeneous exocrine trajectories at 12 months after AP strongly support follow-up models that integrate endocrine, nutritional, and exocrine assessment rather than approaching these domains in isolation.45 This integrated perspective is particularly relevant in PPDM-A, where malnutrition and unstable intake may amplify hypoglycemia risk and complicate interpretation of conventional glycemic markers.

Although current prediction tools and early glycemic phenotyping strategies are promising, they remain pre-implementation. Instruments such as CAPS and markers such as in-hospital glucose variability may help identify individuals who require intensified surveillance, but they still require external validation, harmonized endpoint definitions, and calibration across different clinical settings before they can be considered reliable decision-support tools.24,56 At present, they are better viewed as research-enabling or risk-enrichment approaches than as mature instruments for routine care.

For diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring, conventional glycemic criteria remain fundamental, but PPDM-A requires context-sensitive interpretation of these measures. HbA1c may be unreliable in selected patients recovering from severe illness, whereas OGTT may be particularly informative when early postprandial dysregulation is suspected despite non-diagnostic fasting indices.25 Continuous glucose monitoring may have particular value in patients with fluctuating dietary intake, weight loss, coexisting EPI, or suspected glycemic lability; however, it should be regarded primarily as a monitoring instrument that refines risk assessment and treatment safety rather than as a diagnostic test.89

The most immediately actionable advance in PPDM-A is therefore unlikely to be a single biomarker. Rather, it lies in improving the overall architecture of care: harmonized case definitions, explicit reassessment windows after discharge, integrated endocrine-exocrine-nutrition follow-up, and risk-adapted use of monitoring tools.24,45,56 In practical terms, individualized management should be guided by beta-cell reserve, nutritional status, exocrine function, and vulnerability to hypoglycemia, while continuing to apply established principles of cardiovascular and renal risk reduction where appropriate.69,75

Overall, the field appears ready to move beyond largely descriptive retrospective incidence studies toward prospective, mechanism-informed investigations with rigorous baseline phenotyping, standardized outcome definitions, and external validation of predictive models.90 Such studies will be essential not only to define biologically meaningful PPDM-A subgroups, but also to translate emerging mechanistic and epidemiologic insights into management strategies capable of improving real-world clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This review focuses specifically on PPDM-A; however, the underlying evidence base remains substantially heterogeneous with respect to study design, patient populations, follow-up intensity, and endpoint definition. A major limitation across the field is the absence of fully standardized diagnostic time windows after AP and the inconsistent distinction between truly incident diabetes and previously unrecognized dysglycemia. As a result, published incidence estimates, risk-factor associations, and outcome comparisons cannot be interpreted as directly equivalent across studies.

A large proportion of the available data derives from retrospective cohorts, registry-based analyses, and administrative datasets. Although these sources provide important scale and epidemiologic breadth, they are intrinsically limited by incomplete characterization of pre-AP glycemic status, insufficient granularity regarding AP phenotype and severity, residual confounding related to comorbidities and treatment exposures, and marked variability in post-discharge surveillance. These limitations are particularly consequential in PPDM-A, where diagnosis is highly dependent on timing, structured reassessment after resolution of the acute phase, and careful exclusion of transient stress-related dysglycemia.

Another important limitation is the lack of harmonization in glycemic testing strategies and in the evaluation of emerging biomarkers, microbiome signatures, metabolomic profiles, and predictive tools. HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, OGTT, and CGM-derived metrics are not applied consistently across studies, which complicates cross-study comparisons and weakens efforts to define uniform diagnostic or prognostic algorithms. In parallel, most biomarker-based and prediction-oriented studies remain single-center, exploratory, or internally validated only. External validation in diverse populations, combined with transparent reporting and standardized endpoints, remains necessary before these approaches can be incorporated with confidence into routine clinical care.

Conclusions

PPDM-A is a frequent and clinically meaningful consequence of acute pancreatitis and should be recognized as a distinct post-acute dysglycemia phenotype rather than routinely classified as type 2 diabetes. Its development reflects varying contributions from pancreatic endocrine injury, insulin resistance, inflammatory–metabolic stress, EPD and/or EPI, and pre-existing metabolic susceptibility. Structured surveillance after discharge is therefore warranted. Diagnostic classification of PPDM-A should be deferred until at least 3 months (≥90 days) after the acute pancreatitis episode, when transient stress hyperglycemia has resolved, to reduce diagnostic misclassification.

Implementation remains challenging because responsibility for follow-up is often fragmented between hospital specialists and primary care, while access to multidisciplinary pancreatic clinics, OGTT, EPD/EPI assessment, and nutrition services is inconsistent. A feasible approach is a tiered pathway embedded within existing care that integrates scheduled glycemic reassessment with evaluation for EPD/EPI and nutritional risk: routine follow-up for lower-risk patients and intensified specialist follow-up for those with necrotizing or recurrent AP, marked glycemic variability, EPD/EPI, malnutrition, or substantial metabolic vulnerability. Standardized discharge documentation, automated reminders, and predefined referral criteria may improve implementation without requiring dedicated multidisciplinary clinics for all AP survivors.
