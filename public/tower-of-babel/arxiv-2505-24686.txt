Synergistic Motifs in Gaussian Systems
Enrico Caprioglio, Pedro A. M. Mediano, Luc Berthouze

Abstract
High-order interdependencies are central features of complex systems, yet a mechanistic explanation for their emergence remains elusive. Currently, it is unknown under what conditions high-order interdependencies, quantified by the information-theoretic construct of synergy, arise in systems governed by pairwise interactions. We solve this problem by providing precise sufficient and necessary conditions for when synergy prevails over low-order interdependencies in the weak interaction regime, namely, we prove that antibalanced (highly frustrated) correlational structures in Gaussian systems are sufficient for synergy-dominance and that antibalanced interaction motifs in Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes are necessary for synergy-dominance. We validate the applicability of these analytical insights beyond the weak interaction regime, as well as in Ising, oscillatory, and empirical networks from multiple domains. Our results demonstrate that pairwise interactions can give rise to synergistic information in the absence of explicit high-order mechanisms, and highlight structural balance theory as an instrumental conceptual framework to study high-order interdependencies.

Notation and preliminaries.
—Let
G
be a signed graph with adjacency matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
W_{ii}=0
. Harary’s structure theorem
[

states that a graph

is

(

\bm{w}^{k}
be the set of all closed walks

define the weight of a walk as
\sigma(w)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}W_{w_{i},w_{i+1}}
. This theorem implies that if

is

then every closed walk of length
k
satisfies
\mathrm{sign}(\sigma(w))=(-1)^{k}
.

Let
\textbf{X}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{N}\}
be a multivariate process of size
N
and let
\textbf{X}_{-i}=\textbf{X}\setminus{X_{i}}
denote the same system with the
i
th
variable removed. The O-information can be written as
[
42
]
:

\displaystyle\Omega(\textbf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}TC(\textbf{X}_{-i})-(N-2)TC(\textbf{X}),

(1)

where the total correlation
TC(\textbf{X})=\sum_{j}H(X_{j})-H(\textbf{X})

[
46
]
quantifies the strength of correlations within the system as a whole
[
15
,
42
]
. A negative sign (

) indicates that high-order interdependencies dominate (synergy-dominance); a positive sign (
\Omega>0
) instead indicates that low-order interdependencies dominate (redundancy-dominance)

,

,

]
.

Static Gaussian systems.
—Information-theoretic quantities can be studied analytically in Gaussian systems, allowing us to derive precise conditions for synergy-dominance. In this regime, the O-information can be conveniently written in terms of blocks of the covariance matrix as

\displaystyle\Omega(\textbf{X})=\frac{N-2}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma)\Big]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma_{-i})\Big],

(2)

where
\Sigma_{-i}
indicates the system’s covariance matrix with the
i
th
variable removed (see Supplemental Material (SM)
[
47
]
for step-by-step derivations). Without loss of generality, in what follows we consider standardized covariances
\Sigma
(i.e., unit-variance correlation matrices). To study the signed patterns in
\Sigma
using SBT, we represent the correlational structure of Gaussian systems as a
complete signed
graph
G
with adjacency matrix
W=\Sigma-\mathbb{I}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. We simplify Eq. (
2
), following a similar approach as in
[
54
]
, by expanding the log determinants using the Mercator series

\log\big[\det(\Sigma)\big]=-\sum_{k=2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}].

(3)

Importantly, this approximation converges only if the spectral radius of
W
, denoted as
\rho(W)
, is less than one. As noted in
[
54
]
, this applies for the case of weakly coupled systems, which are common across the complex systems literature
[
55
,
56
,
57
]
. Using the trace identity
\sum_{i}^{N}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=(N-k)\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]
for
k=2,3
(see
[
47
]
) we can use Eq. (
3
) to isolate the lowest order term in
\Omega
:

\Omega(\textbf{X})=\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]+R_{4}(W).

(4)

where
R_{4}(W)\leq\mathcal{O}(\rho(W)^{4})
as
\rho(W)\to 0
.

This approximation yields an important insight: Since
\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]=\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{3}}\sigma(w)
, whenever
|\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]|>|R_{4}(W)|
we have that
X
is synergy-dominated if the triangles (i.e., length-3 walks) are antibalanced. In fact, the lowest order term in Eq. (
4
) is, up to normalization and sign, equivalent to the structural balance-energy
U

[
3
]

U=-\frac{1}{\binom{N}{3}}\sum_{\{i,j,k\}}{W_{ij}W_{jk}W_{ik}},

(5)

a measure of balance that is minimized (maximised) if each triangle
\{i,j,k\}
in the network is balanced (antibalanced). In what follows we study the higher-order corrections in Eq. (
4
) and use SBT to provide a stronger condition, proving that systems with antibalanced correlational structures must be synergistic. To do this, we introduce a trace identity valid for any
k
.

Proposition 1
—For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=N\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}|w|\sigma(w),

where
|w|
is the number of unique nodes encountered along the closed walk

. See proof in the SM

]

We use this identity to eliminate the explicit dependence on
W_{-i}
in the higher-order terms of Eq. (
4
), and derive the key theorem for static systems:

Theorem 1
—If
G
is antibalanced, then
\Omega(\textbf{X})<0
.

To prove this, insert Eq. (
3
) into Eq. (
2
) and apply Proposition 1 to obtain

\displaystyle\Omega(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\Bigg[\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{2k}\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}(|w|-2)\sigma(w)\Bigg].

(6)

If
G
is antibalanced, every nonzero closed walk
w\in\bm{w}^{k}
in
G
has
(-1)^{k-1}\sigma(w)<0
, and therefore
\Omega<0
.

This result furnishes an exact sufficient condition for synergy-dominance and allows us to identify the signed patterns in the correlation matrix (i.e., closed walks
w
in
W
) which contribute synergistically. The sign of each summation term in Eq. (
6
) depends on both the parity of
k
and the sign of the walk’s weight
\sigma(w)
. The
synergistic motifs
, i.e., the closed walks that contribute negatively in Eq. (
6
), thus correspond to even-length closed walks with
\sigma(w)>0
and odd-length closed walks with
\sigma(w)<0
(see examples in Fig.
1
).

Notably, while our proof assumes
\rho(W)<1
, our extensive numerical analyses (see
Experimental validation
section and SM
[
47
]
) have not found any redundancy-dominated antibalanced correlational structures even when
\rho(W)\geq 1
.

Dynamical Gaussian systems.
—We now ask which patterns of interactions in the coupling matrix of a linear dynamical system can lead to synergistic correlational structures. For example, in diffusive processes with only positive couplings, two variables that both correlate positively with a third tend to be correlated
[
58
]
. Synergy-dominated systems instead (Fig.
1
) break this and hence the un- or anti-correlated pair must be negatively coupled. We therefore conjecture that antibalanced interaction motifs are required for dynamical Gaussian processes to be synergy-dominated.

Following a similar approach as Ref.
[
54
]
, we consider Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) processes:
d\textbf{X}(t)=-\textbf{X}(t)\cdot(\mathbb{I}-A)dt+d\bm{\mathcal{W}}(t)
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the interaction (or coupling) matrix and
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
is a Wiener process with identity covariance. For symmetric, Schur stable
A
, the stationary covariance can be written as (see
[
54
,
59
,
60
]
)

\tilde{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}-A)^{-1}.

(7)

Then, by normalizing
\tilde{\Sigma}
we obtain the correlation matrix
\Sigma
. With this closed-form expression, we prove our conjecture exactly for
N=3
and in the weak interaction regime for arbitrary
N
. More precisely, we call weak interaction the regime where
\rho(A)\ll 1
(implying
\rho(W)<1
), and strong interaction the regime with
0<\rho(A)<1
where the system is still Schur-stable (so information-theoretic quantities are well defined) but correlations are strong and
\rho(W)>1
is allowed.

Theorem 2
—Let
X
be a stable OU process with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
. If
\Omega(\textbf{X})<0
, then
A
is antibalanced.

To derive this, let
a=\Sigma_{12},b=\Sigma_{13},c=\Sigma_{23}
. From Eq. (
2
), the condition
\Omega<0
simplifies to
2abc<(ab)^{2}+(ac)^{2}+(bc)^{2}-(abc)^{2}
. To connect this to the interactions
A_{ij}
, we use Eq. (
7
) to write the same condition as
-2xyz>(xy)^{2}+(xz)^{2}+(yz)^{2}-(xyz)^{2}
, where
x=A_{12},y=A_{13},z=A_{23}
(see SM
[
47
]
). For Schur-stable
A
, the RHS is always positive, so this expression is satisfied only if
xyz<0
. Thus, antibalanced interactions are
necessary
for synergy-dominance when
N=3
.

For systems of arbitrary dimension, elements in
\tilde{\Sigma}
are more complex. In the weak interaction regime, Eq. (
4
) is dominated by the cubic term when
A
is balanced. The SM
[
47
]
provides analytical bounds on
R_{4}(W)
in the balanced case, and numerical tests to identify the range of
\rho(W)
for which
|\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]|>|R_{4}(W)|
. Under this condition, we prove that antibalanced interaction motifs are necessary for the system to be synergy-dominated by studying the sign relationships between
A
and
W
.

Theorem 3
—Let
X
be a stable OU process with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times{N}}
. In the weak interaction regime, antibalanced interaction motifs in
A
are necessary for
\Omega(\textbf{X})<0
.

To prove this, we first show in the weak interaction limit that if
A
is balanced then
\Omega(\textbf{X})>0
. For Schur stable
A
, the Neumann series of Eq. (
7
), after normalization, yields

\displaystyle W_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(A^{l})_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}},\quad i\neq{j},\;d_{i}=(\tilde{\Sigma})_{ii}>0\,.

(8)

The denominator is positive, so the sign of
W_{ij}
is the same as that of
\sum_{l}(A^{l})_{ij}
i.e., the contribution of all length-
l
interaction walks from
i
to
j
. If
A
is balanced, Harary’s structure theorem for balance guarantees that any walk from node
i
to
j
satisfies
(A^{l})_{ij}>0
if
i
and
j
are in the same set, and
(A^{l})_{ij}<0
otherwise. Consequently,
\mathrm{sign}\left(\sum_{l}(A^{l})_{ij}\right)=\mathrm{sign}(A_{ij})
, which implies that
W_{ij}
and
A_{ij}
must share the same sign. In the weak interaction regime
|\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]|>|R_{4}(W)|
, such that
\mathrm{sign}(\Omega(\textbf{X}))=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}])
. Thus, for balanced
A
we have
\mathrm{sign}(\Omega(\textbf{X}))=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{tr}[A^{3}])>0
and the stationary dynamics of the resulting OU process is ensured to be redundancy-dominated. Consequently, we conclude that antibalanced interaction motifs (e.g., antibalanced triangles of interactions), which leave
A

not
balanced, are
necessary
for the system to be synergy-dominated.

Conclusion.
—We have established, for the first time, analytical sufficient and necessary conditions for the emergence of high-order behaviours from low-order mechanisms, providing a theoretical ground to a rapidly growing line of research
[
4
,
3
,
5
,
77
,
16
,
68
,
78
,
79
,
80
]
. Specifically, we proved that antibalanced correlations are sufficient for synergy-dominance in Gaussian systems and that antibalanced interaction motifs are necessary for synergy-dominance in OU processes. While our analytical results are derived in the weak interaction regime (except for Theorem
2
which is valid for any
\rho(W)
), numerical simulations and empirical data analyses show that the same qualitative behaviour strongly persists beyond the weak interaction regime. Our results are therefore directly applicable whenever multivariate dynamics are modelled as approximately stationary linear processes with Gaussian statistics, which is a widely used approach in information-theoretic analyses of multivariate continuous data in neuroscience and biology. Moreover, our analyses of Ising and SL oscillator networks, together with the comparison between Gaussian and nonlinear Kraskov estimators on empirical datasets, indicate that the same relationship between antibalanced motifs and synergy extends beyond linear Gaussian models. These results provide a novel mechanistic link between low-order mechanisms and high-order interdependencies, establishing SBT as a natural framework for modelling and analysing synergistic complex systems with pairwise interactions. The expansion of the O-information in terms of signed closed walks has immediate implications for empirical analyses: (
i
) We expect our motifs analysis to point to faster and more principled algorithms for detecting synergistic subsystems in real datasets whenever the Gaussian approximation is justified
[
81
,
42
,
43
]
. (
ii
) The leading, normalised term of the expansion is mathematically identical to the structural balance-energy
U
, explaining the similarities and uncovering novel connections between two previously disconnected lines of work, namely information-theoretic
[
40
,
41
,
43
]
and SBT-based studies
[
4
,
7
,
6
,
8
]
. We demonstrate this connection explicitly in the SM
[
47
]
by reanalysing an existing SBT-based study using the O-information instead of
U
. Finally, our Ising results motivate exploring frustrated quantum magnets, including spin liquids
[
82
,
83
]
, using quantum measures of high-order interdependencies
[
84
,
85
]
.

Derivation of Static Gaussian systems analytical results

I.1
Gaussian O-information derivation

Let
\textbf{X}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{N}\}
be a multivariate Gaussian process of size
N
with correlation matrix
\Sigma
, and let
\textbf{X}_{-i}=\textbf{X}\setminus{X_{i}}
denote the same system with the
i
th
variable removed. The differential entropy of a Gaussian system can be written as
[
2
]

H(\textbf{X})=\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[(2\pi e)^{N}\det(\Sigma)\Big].

(S1)

Using this expression, we write the total correlation (
TC
) as

\displaystyle TC(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}^{N}\Big[H(X_{j})\Big]-H(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\big[(2\pi e)^{N}\big]-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[(2\pi e)^{N}\det(\Sigma)\Big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma)\Big].

Then, the O-information can be written as,

\displaystyle\Omega(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}TC(\textbf{X}_{-i})-(N-2)TC(\textbf{X}),

(S2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma_{-i})\Big]\Big\}-(N-2)\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma)\Big]\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-2}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma)\Big]-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\log\Big[\det(\Sigma_{-i})\Big]

(S3)

as reported in Eq. (
2
) in the main text.

I.2
Proof of trace identities

To isolate the leading term in Eq. (
S3
), we used the trace identity
\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=(N-k)\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]
valid for
k=2,3
. To derive these equations, recall that, given a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times{N}}
with zeros along the diagonal, we have
(M^{k})_{ii}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k}-1}M_{i\,j_{1}}M_{j_{1}\,j_{2}}\dots M_{j_{k-1}\,i}
. Then we can write the trace of a subsystem
W_{-i}^{2}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[W^{2}]-\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}W_{ji}-\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ji}W_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[W^{2}]-2\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}W_{ji},

since
W
is symmetric and
\mathrm{tr}[W^{1}]=0
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{3}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\\
i\neq j\neq k\end{subarray}}W_{ij}W_{jk}W_{ki}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\\
i\neq j\neq k\end{subarray}}W_{ji}W_{ik}W_{kj}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\\
i\neq j\neq k\end{subarray}}W_{jk}W_{ki}W_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]-3\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\\
i\neq j\neq k\end{subarray}}W_{ij}W_{jk}W_{ki}.

So that when summing over
i
we obtain
\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{2}]=(N-2)\mathrm{tr}[W^{2}]
and
\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{3}]=(N-3)\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]
. While it is tempting to generalize
\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=(N-k)\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]
to any
k
, this identity already breaks down for
k=4
, since for
k\geq 4
closed walks on the graph with adjacency matrix
W
can have repeated vertices. Thus, we introduce a general trace identity valid for any
k
(Proposition 1).

Proposition 1
—For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=N\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}|w|\sigma(w),

where
\bm{w}_{i}^{k}
is the set of closed walks of length
k
that include element
i
at least once and
|w|
is the number of unique nodes encountered along the closed walk
w
.

Proof of Proposition 1
—We begin by writing the trace of
W_{-i}^{k}
(the submatrix
W
with row and column
i
removed) in terms of closed walks. The total sum of the weights of all closed walks of length
k
in the full graph
G
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\,(W^{k})_{ii}=\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)

If vertex
i
is removed, the trace
\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]
corresponds to the sum of weights of all closed walks of length
k
that
do not
visit vertex
i
at any point. Therefore,
\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]
can be written as the total sum of all walks’ weights minus the sum of the weights of the walks that
do
include vertex
i
:

\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]=\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w),

(S4)

where
\bm{w}_{i}^{k}
is the set of all closed walks of length
k
that include element
i
at least once. Next, we take the sum over all elements
i\in\{1,,\dots,N\}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w)\right)

\displaystyle=N\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w)

For the first term on the RHS, we know that
\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)=\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]
. For the second term,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w)
, consider any closed walk
w\in\bm{w}^{k}
. This walk
w
visits
|w|
distinct nodes in general (only for
k=2,3
it always visits exactly
2
and
3
nodes, respectively). For example,
w=\{1,2,1,2,1\}\in\bm{w}^{4}
has
|w|=2
. When we sum over all
i
, this walk
w
will be included in the inner sum
\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w)
exactly
|w|
times (once for each distinct node it visits). Therefore, the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{w\in\bm{w}_{i}^{k}}\sigma(w)
is equivalent to
\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}|w|\sigma(w)
. Substituting these expressions back into the equation, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{tr}[W_{-i}^{k}]

\displaystyle=N\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}\sigma(w)-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}|w|\sigma(w)

\displaystyle=N\mathrm{tr}[W^{k}]-\sum_{w\in\bm{w}^{k}}|w|\sigma(w),

(S5)

which completes the proof.
\blacksquare

II.1
Step-by-step proof of Theorem 2

Theorem 2
—Let
X
be a stable OU process with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
. If
\Omega(\textbf{X})<0
, then
A
is antibalanced.

Proof of Theorem 2
—Let
\mathrm{corr}(X)=\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}\;X\;\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}
. We start by showing that, for any interaction matrix
A
with self-interactions, i.e.,
A_{ii}\neq 0
, there is a matrix
B
with
B_{ii}=0
and
B_{ij}=A_{ij}/\sqrt{(1-A_{ii})(1-A_{jj})}
such that the stationary correlation of the OU process
\Sigma=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-A)^{-1})=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-B)^{-1})
.

Let us define the matrix
\Xi
with elements
\Xi_{ii}=\sqrt{1-A_{ii}}
such that
(\mathbb{I}-A)=\Xi\;(\mathbb{I}-B)\;\Xi
. For any positive diagonal matrix
D
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{corr}(DXD)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(DXD)^{-1/2}\;DXD\;\mathrm{diag}(DXD)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=D^{-1/2}\;\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}\;D^{-1/2}\;DXD\;D^{-1/2}\;\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}\;D^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}D^{-1}DXDD^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(X)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}(X),

since the diagonal matrices commute and
D^{-1}D=DD^{-1}=\mathbb{I}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-A)^{-1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}(\Xi^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-B)^{-1}\Xi^{-1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-B)^{-1}),

such that
\Omega(A)=\Omega(B)
since
\mathrm{sign}(A_{ij})=\mathrm{sign}(B_{ij})
. Next, we prove the theorem for the case with no self-interactions i.e.,
A_{ii}=0
. Let us write
(\mathbb{I}-A)^{-1}=C\;(\det(\mathbb{I}-A))^{-1}
, where
C=\mathrm{adj}(\mathbb{I}-A)^{\mathsf{T}}
. Then, each element of
\Sigma
can be written as

\Sigma_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{C_{ij}}{\sqrt{C_{ii}C_{jj}}}&\text{if }i\neq j\\
1&\text{if }i=j\end{cases}

(S11)

where for every pair
i,j\in\{1,2,3\},\;i\neq{j},
and
k=\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{i,j\}
we have
C_{ii}=1-A_{jk}^{2}>0
, and
C_{ij}=A_{ij}+A_{ik}A_{jk}
.
Recall the synergistic condition for a static Gaussian system of size
N=3
. Using
a=\Sigma_{12},b=\Sigma_{13},
and
c=\Sigma_{23}
,
\Omega<0
if

2abc<(ab)^{2}+(ac)^{2}+(bc)^{2}-(abc)^{2}.

Let
x=A_{12},\,y=A_{13},\,z=A_{23}
. After substituting the elements from Eq. (
S11
) into the synergistic condition, the LHS becomes

\displaystyle 2abc=2\frac{(x+yz)(y+xz)(z+xy)}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})(1-z^{2})},

while the RHS becomes

\displaystyle(ab)^{2}+(ac)^{2}+(bc)^{2}-(abc)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+yz)^{2}(y+xz)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})(1-z^{2})^{2}}+\frac{(x+yz)^{2}(z+xy)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})^{2}(1-z^{2})}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(y+xz)^{2}(z+xy)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}(1-y^{2})(1-z^{2})}+\frac{(x+yz)^{2}(y+xz)^{2}(z+xy)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}(1-y^{2})^{2}(1-z^{2})^{2}}\,.

To simplify these, we multiply both sides by the common denominator
(1-x^{2})^{2}(1-y^{2})^{2}(1-z^{2})^{2}
which is always positive and non-zero for Schur-stable
A
. Taking the difference between the RHS and LHS we obtain

\displaystyle(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})(x+yz)^{2}(y+xz)^{2}+(1-x^{2})(1-z^{2})(x+yz)^{2}(z+xy)^{2}+(1-y^{2})(1-z^{2})(z+xy)^{2}(y+xz)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-(z+xy)^{2}(y+xz)^{2}(x+yz)^{2}-2(z+xy)(y+xz)(x+yz)(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})(1-z^{2})>0

This expression can be written in the form
k_{1}^{2}k_{2}>0
, where

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=(-1+x^{2}+y^{2}+2xyz+z^{2}),

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=(xyz)^{2}-(xy)^{2}-(xz)^{2}-(yz)^{2}-2xyz.

Since
k_{1}^{2}
is always positive, we require
k_{2}
to be positive for the condition to be satisfied, or equivalently

-2xyz>+(xy)^{2}+(xz)^{2}+(yz)^{2}-(xyz)^{2}.

For Schur-stable A, the RHS is always positive, so this expression is satisfied only if
xyz<0
. Thus, we conclude that if
\Omega(\textbf{X})<0
then
A
is antibalanced. The same conclusion holds for the case with self-interactions, i.e.,
A_{ii}\neq 0
, since
\Sigma=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-A)^{-1})=\mathrm{corr}((\mathbb{I}-B)^{-1})
where
B_{ii}=0
.
\blacksquare

II.2
Sufficiency condition for cubic dominance

In this section, we study the upper bound of the higher-order terms
|R_{4}(W)|
in the expansion of

(Eq. (

II.2.1
Notation

In what follows, we use the short notation

to denote the sum over even

\displaystyle S_{\text{odd}}

Practical bound

First, we obtain the lower bounds for

III
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process implementation and numerical results

III.1
Methodology

To test the relationship between the O-information and antibalanced structures in the interaction matrix, we computed
\Omega
using the closed-form stationary covariance
\tilde{\Sigma}
of the OU process with interaction matrix
A
(all-to-all couplings) for all possible sign patterns in
A
. More specifically:

1.

we constructed the set
\bm{G}_{N}
of all possible non-isomorphic complete signed graphs of size
N
(see details below),

2.

for each of these graphs
G_{i}\in\bm{G}_{N}
, we sampled
1000
Schur stable matrices with the particular sign patterns in the off-diagonals given by the adjacency matrix of the graph
G_{i}
(see details below),

3.

finally, we computed the O-information using Eq. (
S2
) and the correlation matrix obtained for each
A
.

Note, as highlighted in the main text, in this work we restrict our analysis to
complete
signed interaction networks (all-to-all couplings). This choice allowed us to explore the full range of possible signed patters: from the most balanced to the most antibalanced.

It should be noted that there are many ways to sample Schur stable interaction matrices
A
. Here, we show in Fig.
S8
additional results for interaction matrices with different average spectral radii
\rho(A)
and with or without self-interactions. Importantly, as shown in Fig.
S8
, our results remain unaffected. For different interaction strengths, the main qualitative differences we observe are (
i
) the behaviour of the lower bound and (
ii
) the maximum mean value of
\Omega
. In the strong–interaction regime, approximately defined here as
\rho(A)>0.5
(see Fig. S4) when the number of antibalanced triangles is small the system displays much higher values of
\Omega
than for the weak interactions case. As the number of antibalanced triangles increases, the lower bound rapidly approaches a minimum; this effect can be clearly observed for
N>6
and
\rho(A)>0.5
. These nonlinear behaviours can be explained by the dominance of nonlinear contributions in the strong interactions regime, where
|\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{tr}[W^{3}]|>|R_{4}(W)|
does not hold. In contrast, in the weak interactions regime (approximately
\rho(A)<0.5
) both the mean and the lower bound decrease approximately linearly as the number of antibalanced triangles increases (see the example for
N=8
and
\rho(A)\approx 0.37
). Additionally, we note that the slight increase of the empirical lower bound for large numbers of antibalanced triangles (for
N>6
) is a finite-sampling effect. Although our sampling algorithm guarantees Schur stability of the interaction matrices for any number of antibalanced triangles, it does not sample matrices uniformly conditional on a fixed number of frustrated triangles. Consequently, the configurations with the largest number of antibalanced triangles and lowest
\Omega
can be under-represented, and the empirical minimum of
\Omega
can be slightly higher than the true minimum.

Non-isomorphic complete signed graphs.
We constructed the set
\bm{G}_{N}
using the non-isomorphic simple graphs dataset publicly available in
[
9
]
. This dataset contains the set of all non-isomorphic simple graphs of size
N<10
, which are unsigned and non-complete. Thus, we obtained the set of all non-isomorphic
complete
signed graphs by substituting the missing edges with a negative edge (we illustrate this in Fig.

Sampling Schur stable matrices
. Let
\mathbf{S}_{N}
be the set of adjacency matrices of all possible non-isomorphic unweighted complete signed graphs in
\bm{G}_{N}
. To generate a Schur stable matrix
A
with a desired sign pattern
S_{i}\in\mathbf{S}_{N}
, we can sample a random symmetric matrix
M
, multiply it element-wise by
S_{i}
, and then divide each element by the largest absolute eigenvalue to ensure stability, such that
A=(M\circ S_{i})/\lambda_{\max}
where
\circ
denotes element-wise multiplication between matrices. This method ensures that the largest absolute eigenvalue of the resulting interaction matrix
A
will be less than (but very close to) unity, i.e., the spectral radius of the resulting matrix
A
is
\rho(A)<1
. To study interaction matrices with smaller spectral radii we can add a parameter
\epsilon
, such that

A=\frac{M\circ S_{i}}{\lambda_{\max}+\epsilon},

(S19)

For Fig.
2
in the main text we use
\epsilon=0.4
to fix the spectral radius of the interaction matrices for
N=8
to be
\rho(A)=0.907\pm 0.009
. To see how the spectral radius affects the O-information, see in Fig.
S8
the case for
N=7
with
\epsilon=0.4
and with
\epsilon=0.001
.

IV
Experimental validation: model implementations and empirical datasets

Here, we describe more in details the synthetic and empirical datasets used to validate our analytical results. For both the Ising and SL models, we used all possible non-isomorphic complete signed graphs
\bm{G}_{N}
to construct the coupling structures, as we did for the OU process numerical study (see Sec.
III.1
).

IV.1
Ising Model

Model definition.
We consider an Ising model of
N
binary spins
s_{i}=\{-1,+1\}
with no external field. The Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}J_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}

(S20)

where the sum runs over all spin pairs
\langle i,j\rangle
. Here, we consider all-to-all coupling schemes only (as in the OU process study) and allow the off-diagonal terms
J_{ij}
to be either positive or negative:
J_{ij}\in\{1,-1\}
. The Boltzmann weight of a micro-state
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{N})
at inverse temperature
\beta
is
b(s)=e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}(s)}
. For
N\leq 10
, the state space
S=\{-1,+1\}^{N}
can be enumerated exhaustively, allowing us to compute the partition function
Z=\sum_{s\in{S}}b(s)
and the probability of each state as
p(s)=b(s)/Z
.

Information theoretic quantities for the Ising model.
With the state space probability distribution, we can compute the entropy of the full system:
H=-\sum_{s}p(s)\log{p(s)}
. Similarly, we obtain the entropy of the subsystems
H_{-i}
by evaluating
H_{-i}=-\sum_{s_{-i}}p_{-i}(s_{-i})\log{p_{-i}(s_{-i})}
for each
i
, where
s_{-i}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{N})
denotes a state with the element
i
removed, whose probability is obtained by marginalising over spin
s_{i}
i.e.,
p_{-i}(s_{-i})=\sum_{s_{i}=\pm 1}p(s_{-i},s_{i})
. With these expressions, we can compute the total correlation using
TC=\sum_{i}{H_{i}}-H=N-H
(since the entropy of a spin is
H_{i}=1
in the absence of an external field), and the total correlation of the subsystems using
TC_{-i}=\sum_{j\neq{i}}{H_{j}}-H_{-i}=(N-1)-H_{-i}
. Then, the O-information is obtained via
\Omega=\sum_{i}TC_{-i}-(N-2)TC
.

Model parameters.
As for the OU process study (see Sec.
III.1
), we computed the O-information of the Ising model described above for each possible coupling structure (
N<10
). For simplicity, we fixed the couplings at
|J_{jk}|=1
, such that the only free parameter is the inverse temperature
\beta
. In Fig.
S9
, we show additional results for
N\in\{5,7,9\}
and for
\beta\in\{0.01,0.1,1\}
. All computations here are exact (i.e., no sampling).

IV.2
System of Stuart-Landau oscillators

Oscillator model.
The intrinsic (uncoupled) dynamics of a Stuart-Landau (SL) oscillator is described by the normal form of a supercritical Hopf bifurcation

\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}=Z_{j}(a+i\omega-|Z_{j}|^{2})+\beta\eta_{1}+i\beta\eta_{2}

(S21)

where
Z_{j}=Z_{1}+iZ_{2}
denotes the state of oscillator
j
,
a
is the bifurcation parameter, and
\omega=2\pi f
is the angular frequency (
f_{j}
denotes the intrinsic oscillator’s frequency). At
a=0
, the system undergoes a supercritical bifurcation. In the subcritical regime (
a<0
), if
a
is sufficiently close to
0
, the system exhibits damped oscillations induced by the complex Gaussian noise
\eta_{1}+i\eta_{2}
with standard deviation
\beta
. When
a\ll 0
, the system is overdamped and oscillations decay very quickly. In the supercritical regime (
a>0
) the system exhibits a stable limit cycle with frequency
f_{j}
.

Coupled oscillators model.
The coupled system of SL oscillators of size
N
can be written as:

\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}=Z_{j}(a+i\omega-|Z_{i}|^{2})+K\sum_{k\neq{j}}A_{jk}\big(Z_{k}-Z_{j}\big)+\beta\eta_{1}+i\beta\eta_{2},

(S22)

where
K
is the global coupling parameter and
A
is the interaction matrix. Again, we consider all-to-all interactions and allow both positive and negative interactions
A_{jk}=\pm 1
. In the results we present here and the main text, all oscillators are identical: same intrinsic frequency and same bifurcation parameter.

Information theoretic quantities for the SL model.
Using the simulated real parts
\operatorname{Re}Z_{j}(t)
, we compute the O-information using the
HOI
Python package
[
10
]
. For the entropy estimators we used the Gaussian copula method implemented in
HOI
, as it is commonly used to study fMRI and EEG data
[
11
]
. Similarly to
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
, signals were band-pass filtered with a
4
th-order Butterworth filter (
35
–
45\mathrm{Hz}
) before computing the information theoretic quantities. In Fig.
S10
we show additional results for
N=4,5
, demonstrating that our conclusions hold when different parameters are used, or if unfiltered data is used to compute the information-theoretic quantities.
