Exponential Lindbladian fast forwarding and exponential amplification of certain Gibbs state properties
Zhong-Xia Shang, Dong An, Changpeng Shao

Abstract
We investigate Lindbladian fast-forwarding and its applications to estimating Gibbs state properties. Fast-forwarding refers to the ability to simulate a system of time t t using significantly fewer than t t queries or circuit depth. While various Hamiltonian systems are known to circumvent the no fast-forwarding theorem, analogous results for dissipative dynamics, governed by Lindbladians, remain largely unexplored. We first present a quantum algorithm for simulating purely dissipative Lindbladians with unitary jump operators, achieving additive query complexity 𝒪 ⁡ ( t + log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) log ⁡ log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}\left(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right) up to error ε \varepsilon , improving previous algorithms. When the jump operators have certain structures (i.e., block-diagonal Paulis), the algorithm can be modified to achieve exponential fast-forwarding, attaining circuit depth 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( t + log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) log ⁡ log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) ) \mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)\right) , while preserving query complexity. Using these fast-forwarding techniques, we develop a quantum algorithm for estimating Gibbs state properties of the form ⟨ ψ 1 | e − β ⁡ ( H + I ) | ψ 2 ⟩ \langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I)}|\psi_{2}\rangle , up to additive error ϵ \epsilon , with H H the Hamiltonian and β \beta the inverse temperature. For input states exhibiting certain coherence conditions —e.g., ⟨ 0 | ⊗ n ​ e − β ⁡ ( H + I ) ​ | + ⟩ ⊗ n \langle 0|^{\otimes n}e^{-\beta(H+I)}|+\rangle^{\otimes n} —our method achieves exponential improvement in complexity (measured by circuit depth), 𝒪 ( 2 − n / 2 ϵ − 1 log β ) , \mathcal{O}(2^{-n/2}\epsilon^{-1}\log\beta), compared to the quantum singular value transformation-based approach, with complexity 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ϵ − 1 ​ β ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{\beta}) . For general | ψ 1 ⟩ |\psi_{1}\rangle and | ψ 2 ⟩ |\psi_{2}\rangle , we also show how the level of improvement is changed with the coherence resource in | ψ 1 ⟩ |\psi_{1}\rangle and | ψ 2 ⟩ |\psi_{2}\rangle .



I
Introduction

Quantum computing has emerged as a revolutionary computational paradigm, offering the potential to solve problems that are intractable for classical computers
Shor 1994
;
Grover 1996
;
Feynman 2018
. Among its most transformative applications is the simulation of quantum systems
Feynman 2018
. The development of quantum simulation algorithms follows a central theme of reducing computational complexity.
In the context of Hamiltonian simulation, where the goal is to simulate
e^{-iHt}
up to error
\varepsilon
, techniques have evolved from product formulas
Lloyd 1996
;
Childs et al. 2021
and the linear combination of unitaries (LCU)
Berry et al. 2015
to quantum signal processing (QSP)
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Low and Chuang 2017b
, achieving optimal additive query complexity
\mathcal{O}(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}),

which matches the known theoretical lower bound
Berry et al. 2007
;
Berry et al. 2014
.
The lower bound of
\Omega(t)
arises from the no fast-forwarding theorem for general Hamiltonian simulation
Berry et al. 2007
;
Atia and Aharonov 2017
, which states that no quantum algorithm can simulate
e^{-iHt}
for arbitrary Hamiltonians
H
with query complexity sublinear in
t
. Furthermore, Ref.
Chia et al. 2023
rules out the possibility of fast-forwarding even in terms of circuit depth through parallel queries.
However, these results do not exclude the possibility of fast-forwarding in special cases. In fact, the exponential speedup in Shor’s factoring algorithm can be understood as an example of exponential fast-forwarding
Atia and Aharonov 2017
. Other notable examples include efficiently diagonalizable Hamiltonians, commuting local Hamiltonians, and quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians
Atia and Aharonov 2017
;
Gu et al. 2021
.

Hamiltonian evolution models the dynamics of closed quantum systems. When a system interacts with a large environment, a widely used model for describing the dynamics of open quantum systems is the Lindbladian
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
. In recent years, there has been rapid growth in quantum computing research focused on simulating Lindbladian dynamics, which has various applications, including Gibbs state preparation
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
, ground state preparation of quantum Hamiltonians
Ding et al. 2024a
;
Lin 2025
;
Zhan et al. 2025
;
Shang et al. 2024a
, dissipative quantum state engineering
Guo et al. 2025
, quantum optimization
Chen et al. 2024
;
Basso et al. 2024
, and solving differential equations
Shang et al. 2024b
.

Various Lindbladian simulation algorithms have been proposed
Kliesch et al. 2011
;
Childs and Li 2017
;
Schlimgen et al. 2021
;
Pocrnic et al. 2025
;
Ding et al. 2024b
;
Yu et al. 2024
, with the current state-of-the-art algorithms
Cleve and Wang 2017
;
Li and Wang 2023
achieving a multiplicative query complexity of

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t\,\text{polylog}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
. Since Hamiltonian dynamics is a special case of Lindbladian dynamics, any universal Lindbladian simulation algorithm must obey the same complexity lower bound as in Hamiltonian simulation, namely

\Omega(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}),

which rules out fast forwarding in the general case (i.e. simulating general Lindbladians of an evolution time
t
using sublinear cost in
t
). In fact, even for purely dissipative Lindbladians (i.e., with no internal Hamiltonian), fast forwarding is excluded by a dissipative version of the no fast-forwarding theorem
Childs and Li 2017
.
Nevertheless, exploring special cases that admit Lindbladian fast forwarding remains an important direction, as it may improve the efficiency of Lindbladian simulation in practically relevant cases, and deepen our understanding of the similarities and differences between coherent and dissipative quantum dynamics.

Beyond quantum simulation, a natural question is whether quantum computing can offer advantages in fundamental tasks such as understanding thermal equilibrium, and preparing the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta H}
, where
\beta\geq 0
is the inverse temperature. Gibbs states characterize the static properties of quantum systems, and understanding them is not only essential for studying phases of matter in condensed matter physics
Alhambra 2023
, but also has significant implications for practical applications in machine learning
Amin et al. 2018
and optimization
Brandao and Svore 2017
;
Van Apeldoorn et al. 2017
.

However, establishing a quantum advantage in Gibbs state preparation is highly nontrivial. At high temperatures, Gibbs states are close to the maximally mixed state and exhibit no entanglement
Bakshi et al. 2024
, making them efficiently simulable on classical computers
Yin and Lucas 2023
. In contrast, at moderately low temperatures (e.g.,
\beta=\Omega(\log n)

Rajakumar and Watson 2024
), Gibbs states can already have large overlap with ground states, rendering their preparation QMA-hard
Kempe et al. 2004
.
Only recently have exponential quantum advantages been established in this area. These include Gibbs sampling tasks whose classical intractability is connected to the collapse of the polynomial hierarchy
Rajakumar and Watson 2024
;
Bergamaschi et al. 2024
, as well as results that relate Gibbs state preparation to universal quantum computation
Rouzé et al. 2025
. Exploring further advantages and applications of quantum algorithms for Gibbs-related tasks remains an active and promising research direction.

In this work, we investigate the possibility of Lindbladian fast-forwarding and explore its applications to estimating properties of Gibbs states. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

In the first part of this work, we explore the quantum simulation of purely dissipative Lindbladians with unitary jump operators. We first present a quantum algorithm for simulating such Lindbladians with additive query complexity

\mathcal{O}\left(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right),

(1)

as stated in Theorem
1
. This improves upon the previously known multiplicative complexity
\mathcal{O}(t~\text{polylog}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
from
Cleve and Wang 2017
;
Li and Wang 2023
. Our approach is based on implementing the linear combination of unitaries (LCU) technique
Childs and Wiebe 2012
in the vectorization space, where a density matrix
\rho=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|
is treated as a vector
\vec{\rho}=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}|i\rangle|j\rangle
. Thanks to the dissipative nature of the Lindbladian, there is no need for the oblivious amplitude amplification procedure typically required in LCU-based Hamiltonian simulation
Berry et al. 2015
.

Next, we show that when the jump operators are block-diagonal Pauli operators, this simulation algorithm can be adapted to enable exponentially fast forwarding — specifically, a circuit depth of

\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(t+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)\right),

(2)

while maintaining the same query complexity as before (see Theorem
2
). This improvement is enabled by the ability to perform parallel Pauli matrix multiplication coherently on quantum computers via the tableau representation, which allows the Lindbladian queries to be executed in parallel. While parallel queries in Hamiltonian simulation have been known to yield mild improvements in special cases
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Haah et al. 2021
, to our knowledge, this work demonstrates for the first time an exponential separation between circuit depth and query complexity in Lindbladian simulation.

In the second part of this work, we apply this exponentially fast-forwardable class of Lindbladians to propose a quantum algorithm (see Theorem
3
) for estimating Gibbs state properties of the form
\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I)}|\psi_{2}\rangle
, which we refer to as the Gibbs coherence amplitude (GCA). For a general GCA quantum estimator, a lower bound of
\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{\beta})
can be obtained where
\epsilon
is the additive estimation error. The lower bound of
\epsilon
follows from the lower bound of amplitude estimation at
\beta=0

Brassard et al. 2002
;
Bennett et al. 1997
, and the lower bound of
\beta
follows from the lower bound of the degree of the polynomial approximation to the
e^{-\beta x}
function
(
Montanaro and Shao 2024
, Theorem 1.5)
. Previously, the popular quantum singular value transformation (QSVT)
Gilyén et al. 2019
combined with the amplitude estimation techniques
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Aaronson and Rall 2020
;
Grinko et al. 2021
;
Rall and Fuller 2023
achieves a near-optimal complexity
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{\beta})
(
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
hides
\log(\epsilon^{-1})
factor) which suggests little space for general further improvements. However, our motivation is that if we only care about special cases, whether the possibility of significant improvements could exist. We believe focusing on special cases of hard problems will be one of the major directions for future quantum algorithm design. Answering this question will help us to understand what properties of Gibbs states are more accessible to quantum algorithms, which is crucial for identifying impactful quantum applications and rigorously establishing quantum advantages. Here, we give an affirmative answer to the question. For certain GCAs, such as
|\psi_{1}\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
, our algorithm has the total runtime and circuit depth

\mathcal{O}\left(2^{-n/2}\epsilon^{-1}\log\beta\right),

(3)

achieving exponential improvements in both
\beta
and
n
compared with QSVT. For general choices of
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
, we also provide a complexity result, which turns out highly depends on the quantum coherence resources
Streltsov et al. 2017
—namely, the number of Hadamard gates—required to prepare
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
.

In contrast to previous algorithms
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
based on simulating Lindbladians with the Gibbs state as a static steady state, our approach is dynamical. We leverage the non-diagonal matrix encoding (NDME) and channel block encoding (CBE) techniques introduced in
Shang and Zhao 2024
;
Shang et al. 2024b
, treating vectorized off-diagonal density matrix blocks as pure states. Specifically, we apply a Pauli encoding, mapping
I\mapsto|0\rangle
and
X\mapsto|1\rangle
. Assuming knowledge of the Pauli decomposition
H=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
(which holds for many Hamiltonians in physics and chemistry
Coleman 2015
;
McArdle et al. 2020
), we construct tailored Lindbladians which is within the regime of our exponential fast-forwarding, such that their dissipative evolution effectively implements
e^{-\beta(H+I)}
on these Pauli-encoded states. The benefit of the Pauli encoding is that the
2^{n/2}
exponentially large vector norm of the vectorized Pauli matrices can help exponentially amplify certain GCAs, achieving the complexity in Eq.
3
.

II.1
Lindbladian fast forwarding

The Lindblad master equation or Lindbladian, describes the dynamics of open quantum systems when the environment is large enough to enable the Markovian approximation. Lindbladian dynamics is a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map, and is given in its general form
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]=-i[H_{s},\rho(t)]+\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}\left(F_{i}\rho(t)F_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),F_{i}^{\dagger}F_{i}\}\right),

(4)

where
\rho(t)=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}(t)|i\rangle\langle j|
is the density matrix of the system,
H_{s}
is the system internal Hamiltonian, and
F_{i}
’s are quantum jump operators with jump strength
g_{i}\geq 0
. In the above,
[A,B]:=AB-BA
denotes the commutator, and
\{A,B\}:=AB+BA
denotes the anticommutator for any operators
A
and
B
.

In this work, we consider the simulation of purely dissipative Lindbladians with
H_{s}=0
and
F_{i}
as unitary operators, i.e.,

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{d}[\rho(t)]=\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}\left(F_{i}\rho(t)F_{i}^{\dagger}-\rho(t)\right).

(5)

We define the Lindbladian norm

\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i},

(6)

which matches the norm definitions in the previous Lindbladian simulation algorithms
Cleve and Wang 2017
;
Li and Wang 2023
.

Given access to the following two unitary operators

\displaystyle U_{g}:~U_{g}|0\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{\frac{g_{i}}{\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}}}|i\rangle,

(7)

\displaystyle U_{F}:~U_{F}=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes I+\sum_{i=1}^{M}|i\rangle\langle i|\otimes F_{i},

(8)

we have Theorem
1

B

Discussion of results:
Unitary channels have been well studied and shown to have important roles in quantum information and quantum computing
Gutoski and Johnston 2014
;

;

;

, with the Pauli channel as the typical example for modeling noise in quantum error correction

. Theorem

shows that, instead of doing general Lindbladian simulations, resulting in multiplicative complexity
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t\,\text{polylog}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
(
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
hides

factors) in previous state-of-the-art works, if we only focus on special Lindbladians, i.e. purely dissipative Lindbladians with unitary jump operators (Eq.
5
), we can achieve an improved additive complexity
\mathcal{O}(t+\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
with a strictly linear dependence on
t
. Also, while our results in Theorem
1
seem similar to the results in QSP-based Hamiltonian simulation
Low and Chuang 2017b
, however, our method, as will be clearer in the following method overview section, is actually closer to the LCU
Childs and Wiebe 2012
technique in a modified sense.

The fast forwarding for the Lindbladian Eq.
5
with block-diagonal Pauli jump operators Eq.
13

2
, is non-trivial compared to the Hamiltonian simulation cases. Note that the underlying reason for exponential fast forwarding of Hamiltonians

;
Gu et al. 2021
—such as commuting local Hamiltonians and quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians—is that they are efficiently diagonalizable. In contrast, as evidenced by Theorem
3
below, the Lindbladian dynamics considered here cannot be efficiently diagonalized for

when viewed in the vectorization picture (see Appendix
B.2
), and the Clifford hierarchy increases as
R
grows
Cui et al. 2017
. As we will show later, the main reason for fast forwarding in this case is the ability to compute the product of
K
Pauli operators in
\log(K)

These different origins for exponential fast forwarding lead to different complexity results. In Theorem
2
, exponential fast forwarding appears in circuit depth, while the query complexity remains unchanged compared to Theorem
1
. As will become clear later, this exponential separation between query complexity and circuit depth arises from the parallel access to
V_{F}
, meaning increasing the number of ancilla qubits (Eq.
22
). We can also understand Theorem
2
as a conservation of space-time complexity. Our algorithm suggests that for the special Lindbladians we considered here, one can transfer the time complexity (circuit depth) to the space complexity (increasing ancilla qubits) without compromise, i.e., without increasing the overall space-time (query) complexity. This conservation of space-time complexity is surprising since in most of the situations we met in quantum computing, reducing circuit depth by increasing qubit numbers always implies an increase in the overall space-time complexity. One example is exactly the Hamiltonian simulation. The no parallel fast-forwarding theorem in Ref
Zhang et al. 2024
suggests that to reduce the circuit depth for general Hamiltonian simulation, an exponentially larger number of ancilla qubits is needed. Another example is in quantum searching and quantum phase estimation
Kitaev 1995
. When we use naive repeated measurements and the Hadamard test
Aharonov et al. 1998
(equivalent to increasing space complexity) to replace Grover’s algorithm
Grover 1996
and quantum phase estimation, we will sacrifice the quadratic quantum speedup
Burchard 2019
, and the Heisenberg limit will go down to the standard quantum limit
Ni et al. 2023
.

II.2
Estimating Gibbs coherence amplitude

Given an
n
-qubit Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}Q_{i},

(23)

where
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
Q_{i}
are
n
-qubit Pauli operators from
\{\pm 1\}\times\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, its Gibbs state at inverse temperature
\beta
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta)},

(24)

with
\mathcal{Z}(\beta)=\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})
the partition function. Without loss of generality, we will set
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}=1
.

In this work, we consider the problem of estimating the quantity
\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I)}|\psi_{2}\rangle
for arbitrary
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
, which we call the Gibbs coherence amplitude (GCA). We let
|\psi_{1}\rangle=U_{1}|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
be prepared by a quantum circuit
U_{1}
from
|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
and let
|\psi_{2}\rangle=U_{2}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
be prepared by a quantum circuit
U_{2}
from
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
. For the convenience of the design of our quantum algorithms, we ask
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
to be constructed from the universal gate set
\{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{S},T,CNOT\}
with a circuit depth
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
respectively, and we define
D=D_{1}+D_{2}
. We use
n_{h_{1}}
and
n_{h_{2}}
to denote the number of Hadamard gates in
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
respectively and define
n_{h}=n_{h_{1}}+n_{h_{2}}
. We assume the Hamiltonian Pauli decomposition in Eq.
23
and the gate decomposition of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are known a priori, whose information will be utilized to construct a quantum algorithm for our purpose with complexity summarized in Theorem
3
. The detailed analysis can be found in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a quantum algorithm that can estimate
\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I)}|\psi_{2}\rangle
up to additive error
\epsilon
with success probability at least
1-\delta
, and with circuit depth and total runtime

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(2^{-(n-n_{h})/2}\epsilon^{-1}\left(M\log(\beta)+D\right)\log\left(\delta^{-1}\right)\right).

(25)

Discussion of results:

At infinite temperature (i.e.,
\beta=0
), estimating GCA becomes the standard amplitude estimation problem (i.e., estimating
\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle
), which has a known lower bound of
\Omega(\epsilon^{-1}D\log(\delta^{-1}))
on the total runtime
Zwierz et al. 2010
;
Bennett et al. 1997
. This is also the lower bound on circuit depth when considering the Heisenberg limit
Burchard 2019
.
In comparison, our algorithm in Theorem
3
achieves a complexity of

\mathcal{O}(2^{-(n-n_{h})/2}\epsilon^{-1}D\log\left(\delta^{-1}\right))

which can exponentially improve the complexity in the regime where
n-n_{h}=\Theta(n)
—for instance, when
n_{h}=\sqrt{n}
or
n_{h}=3n/4
. Since a linear number of Hadamard gates is allowed, with no restrictions on magic states
Gottesman 1998
or entanglement
Horodecki et al. 2009
, to our knowledge, no existing classical algorithms can efficiently simulate quantum systems in this regime
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
;
Orús 2014
. We want to mention that the result in Theorem
3
also indicates that the absolute value of the estimated GCA is upper bounded by
2^{-(n-n_{h})/2}
, which will be clear in Appendix
C
.

The maximal improvement of our algorithm is
2^{-n/2}
, which corresponds to the case where
|\psi_{1}\rangle
is a
+/-
basis and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
is a
0/1
basis. This result highlights the importance of optimizing quantum algorithms for special cases, rather than focusing on worst-case complexity lower bounds.
Interestingly, the dependence on the number of Hadamard gates is particularly noteworthy. Hadamard gates can generate quantum coherence
Streltsov et al. 2017
and, when combined with Toffoli gates, can encode the GapP function related to the number of solutions to polynomial equations over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
into quantum amplitudes
Dawson et al. 2005
. Therefore, at infinite temperature, the improvement shown here may indicate a super-quadratic quantum speedup for special GapP instances, although classical algorithms for these cases still require careful examination.

in terms of query complexity is
\Omega(\sqrt{\beta})
as shown in Ref
(
Montanaro and Shao 2024
, Theorem 1.5)
, which comes from the quadratically fast polynomial approximation to monomial functions
(
Sachdeva et al. 2014
, Theorems 4.1 and 5.3)
. Our result actually obeys this lower bound since our fast-forwarding Lindbladian simulation algorithm, Theorem
2
, is only for circuit depth. We remark that if the spectral norm
\|H\|
is known, the QSVT approach allows for spectral amplification
Low and Chuang 2017a
, which can mitigate the exponentially decaying factor
e^{-\beta}
, yielding exponentially better scaling in
\beta
compared to our approach (Appendix
), and our approach will then only have the
2^{-n/2}
advantage. However, in general, it is infeasible to determine the spectral norm of
H
in advance, as estimating the ground energy of quantum Hamiltonians is known to be QMA-hard
Kempe et al. 2004
.

We can also compare our approach of using Lindbladians for Gibbs-related problems with recently proposed Lindbladian-based Gibbs state preparation algorithms
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Ding et al. 2025
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
. Unlike those approaches, which encode Gibbs states as the steady states of Lindbladians, our method encodes the Gibbs operator
e^{-\beta H}
into the Lindbladian dynamics.
The main limitation of our approach is that it does not allow full preparation of the Gibbs state or support sampling tasks
Rajakumar and Watson 2024
;
Bergamaschi et al. 2024
. However, for the task of GCA estimation, our method appears to offer significant advantages, which can be summarized in three aspects.
First, estimating GCA from the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
requires knowledge of the partition function
\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta(H+I)})
, since

\langle\psi_{1}|\rho_{\beta}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I)}|\psi_{2}\rangle}{\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta(H+I)})},

as discussed in
Bravyi et al. 2021
.
Second, the complexity of these Gibbs state preparation algorithms depends on the mixing time of the Lindbladians, which is generally expected to scale exponentially with
\beta
due to the inherent hardness of ground state preparation.
Finally, the complexity reduction of
\mathcal{O}(2^{-(n-n_{h})/2})
is unlikely to be seen their approach.

III
Methods overview

In this section, we briefly give an overview of the main ideas behind our algorithms, with more details deferred to the appendices.
Before the overview, we introduce the notion of vectorization. The mapping from an
n
-qubit operator
O=\sum_{ij}o_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|
to a
2n
-qubit vector
\ket{O}=\sum_{ij}o_{ij}|i\rangle|j\rangle
is called
vectorization
. One can also interpret
\ket{O}
as the Choi vector of
O
, e.g., see
Choi 1975
. Any general linear transformation
O\rightarrow\sum_{i}A_{i}OB_{i}^{\dagger}
can be re-expressed in the vectorized picture as
\ket{O}\rightarrow\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}^{*}\ket{O}
. Here,
B_{i}^{\dagger}
and
B_{i}^{*}
denote the Hermitian conjugate and complex conjugate of
B_{i}
, respectively.

III.1
Lindbladian fast forwarding

When Lindbladians are purely dissipative and the jump operators are unitaries (Eq.
5
), the solution at time
t
in the vectorization picture has the form

\displaystyle\ket{\rho(t)}=e^{-T}\exp\left(T\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}\right)\ket{\rho(0)},

(26)

where
p_{i}=g_{i}/\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}
and
T=\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}t
. The exact dynamics
\exp(T\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*})
can be well approximated, up to error
\varepsilon
, by a Taylor truncation of degree
Gilyén et al. 2019

K=\mathcal{O}\left(T+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right).

(27)

The Taylor expansion takes the form

\displaystyle\ket{\rho(t)}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}\right)^{k}\ket{\rho(0)}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\vec{i}_{k}}p_{\vec{i}_{k}}F_{\vec{i}_{k}}\otimes F_{\vec{i}_{k}}^{*}\ket{\rho(0)},

where
\sum_{\vec{i}_{k}}p_{\vec{i}_{k}}F_{\vec{i}_{k}}\otimes F_{\vec{i}_{k}}^{*}
denotes the expansion of
(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*})^{k}
(see the formal definition in Appendix
B.1
). Since
F_{i}
are unitaries, the Taylor truncation in the vectorization picture takes the form of an LCU, and corresponds to a quantum channel with unitary Kraus operators in the original (operator) picture.

A purification of this quantum channel can be implemented by a quantum circuit composed of
U_{g}
(Eq.
7
) and
U_{F}
(Eq.
8
). Both the query complexities for
U_{g}
and
U_{F}
are
\mathcal{O}(K)
, and the queries to
U_{F}
are made adaptively. Since
U_{F}
is a control operator including
M+1
terms, given access to all
F_{i}
operators, the circuit depth to implement
U_{F}
is
\mathcal{O}(M)

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
(which is also true for
U_{g}
). As a result, the overall circuit depth becomes

\mathcal{O}\left(M\left(T+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)\right).

(29)

This establishes the main idea of proving Theorem
1
. Further details, including the number of ancillas are provided in Appendix
B.1
.

As shown in Eq.
29
, our algorithm has an additive scaling between
T
and
\varepsilon
.
This is a remarkable feature and, to the extent of our knowledge, is the first time for an LCU-like simulation algorithm to achieve additive scaling.
Specifically, existing LCU-based algorithms for simulating Hamiltonians
Berry et al. 2015
or Lindbladians
Li and Wang 2023
also first approximates the evolution operator by a truncated Taylor or Dyson series, and then attempts to implement the truncated series by LCU.
However, if the expansion is performed over a long time interval, then the
1
-norm of the coefficients could be exponential in
T
, leading to an inefficient LCU implementation.
To resolve this issue, existing algorithms breaks the long time interval into
T
many short ones, implementing the truncated series by LCU on each short interval, and applies an oblivious amplitude amplification
Berry et al. 2015
technique in each step to ensure that the success probability does not decay exponentially.
Although this is a time-efficient approach, the overall complexity becomes multiplicative, as the overall cost would be the cost per step (which is
\mathcal{O}(\log(T/\varepsilon))
) times the overall number of steps (which is
\mathcal{O}(T)
).
As a comparison, our algorithm does not need to perform such time discretization and can directly implement the long-time Taylor series.
This is because the Lindcladians we consider are purely dissipative, leading to an extra rescaling factor
e^{-T}
in the expansion in Eq.
28
and a constant
1
-norm of the coefficients.
Therefore our algorithm avoids the time discretization and oblivious amplitude amplification steps, is a global algorithm and thus has an additive scaling.

For the fast-forwarding (Theorem
2
), note that each unitary in the LCU form of the Taylor truncation in the vectorization picture is a product of up to
K
operators of the form
F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}
. When we restrict each
F_{i}
to be of the form given in Eq.
13
, the result of the product
F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}
can be efficiently inferred using the multiplication rules of Pauli matrices. To realize the corresponding (purification of the) quantum channel on a quantum computer, assuming Eq.
13
, each Kraus operator can be constructed with up to
\log(K)
layers of
V_{F}
(Eq.
14
) through the binary encoding from Eq.
II.1
. Note that, given a classical tableau
i\rightarrow x_{F_{i}}
, the circuit depth of constructing
V_{F}
is also
\mathcal{O}(M)
by converting the reversible classical circuit into the quantum circuit
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. The reduction in depth from
K
to
\log(K)
arises from the observation that the product of
K
Pauli operators can be computed in logarithmic depth: first group the
K
operators into
K/2
pairs and multiply them in parallel, then group the resulting
K/2
operators into
K/4
pairs, and so on.
As a result, while the number of queries to
V_{F}
remains
K
, the circuit depth can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(\log K)
. Note that each
F_{i}
is not directly a single Pauli operator but consists of
R
Pauli blocks; hence, the parameter
R
also contributes to the overall simulation complexity.

Combining these components, the total circuit depth of the fast-forwarding algorithm is

\mathcal{O}\left(M\log\left(T+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)+R\right).

(30)

See Appendix
B.2
for further details about the proof of Theorem
2
, including the number of ancillas.

III.2
Estimating Gibbs coherence amplitude

Consider the following
(n+1)
-qubit quantum channel:

\mathcal{C}[\rho]=\sum_{i}\begin{pmatrix}K_{i}&0\\
0&L_{i}\end{pmatrix}\rho\begin{pmatrix}K_{i}^{\dagger}&0\\
0&L_{i}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix},

(31)

where
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}K_{i}&0\\
0&L_{i}\end{pmatrix}\right\}
is a set of Kraus operators. When
\rho_{S}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&S\\
S^{\dagger}&\cdot\end{pmatrix}
, we have

\rho_{\mathrm{out}}:=\mathcal{C}[\rho_{S}]=\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&\sum_{i}K_{i}SL_{i}^{\dagger}\\
\sum_{i}L_{i}S^{\dagger}K_{i}^{\dagger}&\cdot\end{pmatrix},

(32)

from which, we can find that if we focus on the upper-right block, we can get rid of the CPTP restrictions. The expectation value of the operator
X\otimes I_{n}
with respect to
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}
is

\mathrm{Tr}\big((X\otimes I_{n})\rho_{\mathrm{out}}\big)=2\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sum_{i}K_{i}SL_{i}^{\dagger}\right)\right]=2^{n/2+1}\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\langle\Omega|\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}\ket{S\rangle}\right],

(33)

where
\ket{\Omega}=2^{-n/2}\sum_{i}\ket{ii}
is the
2n
-qubit Bell state. To access the imaginary part, we can consider the observable
Y\otimes I_{n}
.

Our algorithm for estimating
\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}|\psi_{2}\rangle
is based on constructing a quantum channel such that

\langle\Omega|\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}\ket{S\rangle}\propto\langle\psi_{1}|e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}|\psi_{2}\rangle.

(34)

Due to vectorization, the identity operator can be interpreted as the Bell state with exponentially large norm
2^{n/2}
. Therefore, if we estimate the left-hand side of Eq.
33
, the result will be exponentially amplified, reducing the estimation complexity. This is the origin of the
2^{-n/2}
factor in Theorem
3
. However, the norm of
\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}\ket{S\rangle}
can be small due to the dissipative nature of quantum channels.

We refer to
\rho_{S}
as the non-diagonal density matrix encoding (NDME) of
S
, and the way of encoding operations into
\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}
as the channel block encoding (CBE)
Shang and Zhao 2024
;
Shang et al. 2024b
(see formal definitions in Appendix
C
). In our setting, we adopt the Pauli encoding where
I
is mapped to
\ket{0}
and
X
to
\ket{1}
. For example, when
S\propto I+X
, the vectorized form
\ket{S\rangle}
corresponds to
\ket{+}
. This encoding choice significantly simplifies our analysis and, more importantly, allows us to relate the norm
\left\|\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}\ket{S\rangle}\right\|
to the quantum coherence resource
Streltsov et al. 2017
, giving rise to the
2^{n_{h}/2}
factor in Theorem
3
.

More concretely, since GCA takes the form
\langle+|^{\otimes n}U_{1}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}U_{2}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
, we construct the CBE of
U_{1}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}U_{2}
using three successive channels
\mathcal{C}_{U_{1}^{\dagger}}\circ e^{\mathcal{L}_{H}\beta}\circ\mathcal{C}_{U_{2}}.

For
\mathcal{C}_{U_{1}^{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{U_{2}}
, since
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are composed from basic gates in the universal set
\{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{S},T,\mathrm{CNOT}\}
, we first build basic channels corresponding to each gate and then compose them to form the desired channel. As a result, the circuit depths of
\mathcal{C}_{U_{1}^{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{U_{2}}
are
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, respectively. Under the Pauli encoding, the Hadamard gate becomes special since we prove that the optimal CBE construction can only encode
\mathcal{H}/\sqrt{2}
, while for other gates, 1 to 1 encoding is possible. Therefore,
n_{h}
Hadamard gates will exponentially (
2^{-n_{h}/2}
) dissipate the norm of
\sum_{i}K_{i}\otimes L_{i}^{*}\ket{S\rangle}
.

For
e^{\mathcal{L}_{H}\beta}
, which provides a CBE of
e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}
, we directly utilize fast-forwardable Lindbladians with
R=2
. For any Hamiltonian
H
, we show that there exists such a Lindbladian with carefully chosen jump operators, such that its evolution for time
\beta
simulates a CBE of
e^{-\beta(H+I_{n})}
. As demonstrated in Theorem
2
, this simulation has circuit depth
\mathcal{O}(M\log\beta)
.

After preparing (a purification of)
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}
, we apply the amplitude estimation algorithm
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Aaronson and Rall 2020
;
Grinko et al. 2021
;
Rall and Fuller 2023
to estimate either
\mathrm{Tr}((X\otimes I_{n})\rho_{\mathrm{out}})
or
\mathrm{Tr}((Y\otimes I_{n})\rho_{\mathrm{out}})
with Heisenberg-limit scaling. Putting all components together yields the total circuit depth or runtime stated in Theorem
3
. See Appendix
for full details.

IV
Summary and outlook

In summary, we found that for purely dissipative Lindbladians with unitary jump operators, their simulation can be achieved with additive complexity, as stated in Theorem
1
, improving upon the multiplicative complexity of existing Lindbladian simulation algorithms. Furthermore, when the jump operators are restricted to block-diagonal Pauli operators, we presented a quantum simulation algorithm that enables exponential fast-forwarding in circuit depth while preserving the query complexity (Theorem
2
). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result on Lindbladian fast-forwarding.

An application of these fast-forwarding Lindbladians is the estimation of Gibbs state properties, such as the Gibbs Coherence Amplifier (GCA). By leveraging fast-forwarded Lindbladian simulations, we encode the GCA into the non-diagonal blocks of density matrices using several new techniques, including Non-Diagonal Matrix Encoding (NDME), Coherence-Based Encoding (CBE), and Pauli encoding. Under certain conditions related to quantum coherence, the encoded GCA can be exponentially amplified. As a result, compared with the advanced QSVT-based approaches for estimating GCA, our algorithm can achieve exponential advantages in two parameters (Theorem
3
).

There are three directions that merit further investigation.

•

First, it is important to identify more classes of Lindbladians that support fast-forwarding, to explore the general conditions enabling Lindbladian fast-forwarding, and to understand how these differ from their Hamiltonian counterparts.

•

Second, it is also important to uncover the physical significance and potential applications of GCA, particularly in regimes where our algorithm offers exponential advantages. Furthermore, by replacing the Pauli encoding with alternative encoding schemes, we can also explore what other types of quantum resources—beyond coherence—may be relevant to the complexity of estimating GCA.

•

Third, it is interesting to explore whether the new techniques introduced in this work, including both the fast-forwarding and vectorization techniques (e.g., NDME, CBE), can inspire the discovery of new quantum algorithms and novel applications.

,
Aaronson and Rall 2020
,
Grinko et al. 2021
,

Lemma 1

(Amplitude estimation, absolute value)
.

Given two quantum states
|S_{1}\rangle=U_{1}|0^{n}\rangle

|S_{2}\rangle=U_{2}|0^{n}\rangle

\epsilon
-additive error estimation of

\displaystyle U_{G}=(I-2U_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|U_{1}^{\dagger})(I-2U_{2}|0\rangle\langle 0|U_{2}^{\dagger}),

(35)

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log(\delta^{-1}))
times.

1

Lemma 2

(Amplitude estimation, entire estimation)
.

Given two quantum states
|S_{1}\rangle=U_{1}|0^{n}\rangle
and
|S_{2}\rangle=U_{2}|0^{n}\rangle

\epsilon
-additive error estimation of
\text{Re}(\langle S_{1}|S_{2}\rangle)
and
\text{Im}(\langle S_{1}|S_{2}\rangle)

and

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle+|1\rangle\right)\otimes|0^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\mathcal{H}\text{ on ancilla}}

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(|0\rangle\otimes\left(I+U\right)|0^{n}\rangle+|1\rangle\otimes\left(I-U\right)|0^{n}\rangle\right).

Appendix B
Lindbladian simulation

B.1
Proof of Theorem
1

We consider the simulation of the Lindbladian

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{d}[\rho(t)]=\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}\left(F_{i}\rho(t)F_{i}^{\dagger}-\rho(t)\right),

(39)

where
F_{i}
are unitary operators. In the vectorization picture, the Lindbladian has the form

\frac{d\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}}{dt}=L_{d}\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}\left(F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}-I\right)\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}.

(40)

The solution is

\displaystyle\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\exp(L_{d}t)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle}=\exp\left(t\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{i}tI\right)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle}

(41)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}t\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}-\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}tI\right)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle}

\displaystyle=e^{-T}\exp\left(T\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}\right)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle},

where
p_{i}=g_{i}/\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}
and
T=\|\mathcal{L}_{d}\|_{L}t
.

We can approximate Eq.
41
via the Taylor truncation

\displaystyle\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle\approx e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}\right)^{k}\ket{\rho(0)\rangle}

(42)

\displaystyle=e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\otimes F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{*}\ket{\rho(0)\rangle},

where
\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\otimes F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{*}
is the expansion of
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}F_{i}\otimes F_{i}^{*}\right)^{k}
and
K=\mathcal{O}(T+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})})
.
For Eq.
42
, we have two observations. First, the 1-norm is bounded by 1 because

\displaystyle e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}=e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}\right)^{k}=e^{-T}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}<e^{-T}e^{T}=1.

(43)

Second, each term
F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\otimes F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{*}
in the vectorization picture corresponds to a unitary transformation in the original picture

\displaystyle F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\rho(0)F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{\dagger}.

(44)

Therefore, the Lindbladian dynamics can be directly and approximately interpreted as a quantum channel in the Kraus-sum representation.

Based on Eq.
42
, we now present a quantum algorithm for Lindbladian simulation. We consider a
(K+K\log(M+1)+n)
-qubit circuit, where:

•

The first
K
qubits represent the first environment, labeled as
a
;

•

The next
K\log(M+1)
qubits represent the second environment, which is further decomposed into
K
groups, each with
\log(M+1)
qubits, labeled as
b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{K}
;

•

The remaining
n
qubits represent the system governed by the Lindbladian dynamics.

The implementation of the Lindbladian simulation requires three different unitary blocks. Without loss of generality, and for simplicity, we assume that the initial state is a pure state:
\rho(0)=|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|
for some state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
.

We start from the initial state
|0\rangle_{a}|0\rangle_{b_{1}}\cdots|0\rangle_{b_{K}}|\psi_{0}\rangle
, and first apply a unitary circuit
U_{T}
on the first environment such that

U_{T}|0\rangle_{a}=C\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sqrt{e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}}\,|\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{k}\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{K-k}\rangle_{a},

(45)

where the normalization constant
C
is given by

C=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{K}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}}}.

(46)

Next, we introduce a unitary operator
U_{g}
with the property

U_{g}|0\rangle_{b_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{p_{i}}|i\rangle_{b_{k}}.

(47)

By treating the qubits in the first environment as control qubits (controlled on the
\ket{1}
state), we apply a series of controlled-
U_{g}
operations to the state in Eq.
45
. This produces the following state:

C\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sqrt{e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}}\,|\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{k}\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{K-k}\rangle_{a}\left(\bigotimes_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{p_{i}}|i\rangle_{b_{l}}\right)\otimes|0\rangle_{b_{k+1}}\cdots|0\rangle_{b_{K}}.

(48)

The third type of unitary we require is
U_{F,k}
(just
U_{F}
in Eq.
8
with
k
denoting ancilla systems), defined as

U_{F,k}=|0\rangle\langle 0|_{b_{k}}\otimes I+\sum_{i=1}^{M}|i\rangle\langle i|_{b_{k}}\otimes F_{i},

(49)

where
I
and each
F_{i}
act on the
n
-qubit Lindbladian system. We sequentially apply
U_{F,k}
for
k=1
to
K
, resulting in the final state:

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sqrt{e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}}\,|\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{k}\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{K-k}\rangle_{a}\left(\bigotimes_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{p_{i}}|i\rangle_{b_{l}}\right)\otimes|0\rangle_{b_{k+1}}\cdots|0\rangle_{b_{K}}\otimes F_{\vec{i}_{k}}|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sqrt{e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}}\,|\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{k}\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{K-k}\rangle_{a}\sum_{\vec{i}_{k}}|\vec{i}_{k}\rangle_{b_{1\cdots k}}\otimes|0\rangle_{b_{k+1}}\cdots|0\rangle_{b_{K}}\otimes\sqrt{p_{\vec{i}_{k}}}F_{\vec{i}_{k}}|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(50)

By tracing out all the environment systems, we obtain
\tilde{\rho}(t)
, a good approximation to the true density matrix
\rho(t)
. This algorithm effectively implements the following quantum channel:

\tilde{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{A}[\rho(0)]:=C^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\vec{i}_{k}}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\,p_{\vec{i}_{k}}\,F_{\vec{i}_{k}}\rho(0)F_{\vec{i}_{k}}^{\dagger}.

(51)

For the complexity of this algorithm, since each
U_{F,k}
is applied sequentially for
k=1
to
K
, while all control-
U_{g}
operations can be implemented in parallel, the circuit depth is determined by the query complexity of
U_{F}
. Noting that both
U_{g}
and
U_{F}
have query complexity
K
, the overall query complexity is
\mathcal{O}(K)
, and the circuit depth is also
\mathcal{O}(K)
.

We now present the error analysis, specifically, we prove that
\|e^{\mathcal{L}_{d}t}-\mathcal{A}\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon
.
First, we have

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{L}t}[\rho]-\mathcal{A}[\rho]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-C^{2}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\rho F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{\dagger}+\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\otimes F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{*}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-C^{2}}{C^{2}}\mathcal{A}[\rho]+\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}\frac{C^{2}}{C^{2}-1}\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}F_{\vec{i_{k}}}\rho F_{\vec{i_{k}}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-C^{2}}{C^{2}}\mathcal{A}[\rho]+\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}\mathcal{B}[\rho]

for some quantum channel
\mathcal{B}[\cdot]
. During the derivation, we used the relation

\displaystyle\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}=\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{i_{k}}}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}p_{\vec{i_{k}}}=\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}.

(53)

Following the definition of the diamond norm, we have

\displaystyle\|e^{\mathcal{L}t}-\mathcal{A}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma}\left\|\left(e^{\mathcal{L}t}\otimes\mathcal{I}\right)(\sigma)-\left(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{I}\right)(\sigma)\right\|_{1}

(54)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\sigma}\left\|\frac{1-C^{2}}{C^{2}}(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{I})[\sigma]+\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}(\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{I})[\sigma]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\sigma}\left\|\frac{1-C^{2}}{C^{2}}(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{I})[\sigma]\right\|_{1}+\left\|\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}(\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{I})[\sigma]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=2\frac{C^{2}-1}{C^{2}}=2\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}.

This upper bound can be further bounded by Taylor’s theorem as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}e^{-T}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=e^{-T}\left(e^{T}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{T^{k}}{k!}\right)=e^{-T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(T-\tau)^{K}}{K!}e^{\tau}d\tau\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(T-\tau)^{K}}{K!}d\tau=\frac{T^{K+1}}{(K+1)!}.

Following the proofs in Lemmas 57-59 of Ref
Gilyén et al. 2019
, we have

\displaystyle\|e^{\mathcal{L}t}-\mathcal{A}\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\frac{T^{K+1}}{(K+1)!}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{because }\quad K=\mathcal{O}\left(T+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}{\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right).

(56)

This leads to the results in Theorem
1
.

. The core idea is to organize the

2^{s}

.

where

C.1
Non-diagonal density matrix encoding

Here, we introduce the
non-diagonal density matrix encoding
(NDME) technique. The key idea is that, given an arbitrary
n
-qubit matrix, we can encode it into a non-diagonal block of an
(n+1)
-qubit density matrix.

Definition 3

(Non-diagonal density matrix encoding (NDME)
Shang and Zhao 2024
,
Shang et al. 2024b
)
.

Given an
(n+1)
-qubit density matrix
\rho_{M}
and an
n
-qubit matrix
M

\rho_{M}
has the form

\rho_{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&\gamma M\\
\gamma M^{\dagger}&\cdot\end{pmatrix},

(81)

with
\gamma\geq 0
, we say that
\rho_{M}
is a
\gamma
-NDME of
M
.

NDME allows us to bypass the Hermitian and positive semi-definite constraints of density matrices and instead use density matrix elements to encode general matrix information.
