Gain/Amplification 17p as a Potential Favorable Prognostic Factor in Multiple Myeloma: A Real‐World Settings Retrospective Study
Authors: Teng Wang, Siyuan Cui, Jingyi Wang, Zhaoxia Liu, Kui Liu, Zhenzhen Wang, Xinyu Tang, Di Liu, Yan Wang, Ruirong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a genetically heterogeneous malignancy in which cytogenetic abnormalities critically influence prognosis. The prognostic significance of 17p gain/amplification (17p+), particularly relative to del(17p) and the high‐risk 1q21 gain/amplification (1q21+), remains insufficiently defined.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 125 MM patients treated at a single center between 2015 and 2024. Cytogenetic profiles were assessed using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models evaluated the impact of 17p+ on progression‐free survival 1 (PFS1), time to next treatment (TTNT), and overall survival (OS).ResultsCompared with del(17p), 17p+ was associated with significantly longer PFS1 (HR 0.21, p < 0.01), TTNT (HR 0.18, p < 0.01), and OS (HR 0.25, p < 0.05). In patients receiving proteasome inhibitor–based therapy or not undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation, 17p+ remained favorable for PFS1 (HR 0.20, p < 0.05, HR 0.21, p < 0.01). Multivariate Cox models confirmed 17p+ as an independent protective factor for PFS1 (HR 0.08), TTNT (HR 0.07), and OS (HR 0.02) (all p < 0.001). Among patients with 1q21+, co‐occurrence of 17p+ improved PFS1 (HR 0.47) and TTNT (HR 0.36) (p < 0.05), though no significant OS benefit was observed.Conclusions17p+ is associated with markedly improved clinical outcomes in MM and may partially mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of 1q21+. These findings highlight the relevance of 17p+ in cytogenetic risk stratification and personalized treatment strategies and support further validation in multicenter cohorts.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.Keywords: cytogenetic abnormalities, gain/amplification 1q21, gain/amplification 17p, multiple myeloma

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a clonal plasma cell malignancy characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of plasma cells. The majority of patients eventually relapse and develop resistance to current treatments [1]. MM is the second most common hematologic malignancy, with its incidence increasing with age. In recent decades, the global incidence of MM has demonstrated an upward trend, with significant variations in both incidence and mortality rates across different countries and regions [2, 3].

MM is a genetically complex and highly heterogeneous malignancy, with cytogenetic abnormalities closely linked to its pathogenesis, progression, drug resistance, and relapse [4]. In established risk stratification systems such as the International Staging System (ISS) and Mayo Stratification of Myeloma and Risk‐Adapted Therapy (mSMART), cytogenetic features serve as key prognostic indicators [5, 6, 7]. Chromosomal instability underlies the high prevalence of these abnormalities in MM [8]. Notably, del(17p), gain/amp(1q21), and translocations involving chromosome 14 (t(4,14), t(14,16), t(14,20)) are strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes [9, 10]. Additional loci, such as 6p22 and 9p21, have been identified, though their prognostic significance remains under debate [11]. Comprehensive evaluation of cytogenetic abnormalities and their incorporation into risk stratification frameworks are essential for advancing personalized and precision MM therapy.

Chromosome 17p mutations primarily affect the expression of the tumor suppressor gene TP53. For example, 17p− is often associated with mutations or deletions in TP53, which can adversely affect the prognosis of MM patients [12]. The gain or amplification of 17p (gain/amp(17p), 17p+) remains poorly studied, and its relationship with MM prognosis is not well understood. In a previous report, we described a patient with MM who was 17p+ and t(4,14) and who demonstrated a favorable prognosis [13]. Therefore, the impact of gain/amp(17p) on the prognosis of MM patients, particularly in the context of specific treatments or conditions, warrants further investigation.

On the basis of the clinical data of MM patients at our medical center from 2015 to 2024, we analyzed the impact of cytogenetic abnormalities, particularly 17p+, in combination with other concurrent cytogenetic abnormalities, as well as the effects of different treatment regimens on progression‐free survival 1 (PFS1), time to next treatment (TTNT), and overall survival (OS) rates. This study aims to provide a comprehensive perspective for guiding personalized treatment strategies and prognostic assessment in MM patients.

Methods

Patient Samples

Inclusion criteria required patients to meet the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) consensus criteria for MM [14]. Clinical data collected included age, sex, disease classification, date of initial diagnosis, prognostic scores (ISS, R‐ISS, D‐S, and mSMART), treatment regimens, and comprehensive laboratory results (including bone marrow cytology, FISH, immunofixation electrophoresis, and imaging such as CT, x‐ray, or MRI). Data on relapse timing, survival status, and mortality were also captured for subsequent prognostic analyses.

Study endpoints included PFS1, defined as the time from initiation of first‐line therapy to disease progression or death, TTNT, the time from treatment initiation to the need for subsequent therapy, and OS, calculated from diagnosis to death or last follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Kaplan–Meier curves assessed the impact of individual factors on PFS1, TTNT, and OS. Univariate Cox regression compared survival outcomes across groups [15]. For normally distributed data (Shapiro–Wilk test, p > 0.05) with equal variances (F test, p > 0.05), Student's t‐test was used, otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test (n < 5), with Cramer's V used for correlation analysis [16].

Multivariate Cox regression identified independent prognostic factors for PFS1, TTNT, and OS. Categorical variables were coded as dummy variables. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals (p > 0.05) [17]. Time‐dependent covariates violating the assumption (p < 0.05) were adjusted using the coxph() function in R, and model fit was evaluated using AIC and BIC [18]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with p‐values adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg method. HR > 1 indicated risk, while HR < 1 indicated protection. All analyses were performed in R version 4.1.2.

Results

Gain/Amplification of 17p as a Protective Factor for MM Prognosis

This retrospective study included 125 MM patients treated at our center between May 2015 and November 2024, with complete clinical and laboratory data. Cytogenetic analysis by FISH identified abnormalities including gain/amplification of 1q21 (1q21+), del(1p), add(6p), add(13q), 17p+ or 17p–, and mutations in chromosomes 4, 11, 14, 16, and 21. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 66 years, and 64.8% were classified as mSMART high‐risk [19]. Amyloidosis occurred in 4.0%, plasma cell leukemia in 5.6%, and extramedullary disease in 9.6%. The frequencies of 17p–, 17p+, 1q21+, and t(4,14) were 7.2%, 13.6%, 56.8%, and 16%, respectively. ASCT was performed in 20.8% of patients.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with normal cytogenetics had significantly longer PFS1, TTNT, and OS than those with 17p– (PFS1: HR = 0.28, TTNT: HR = 0.31, OS: HR = 0.30, all p < 0.01). Compared to 17p– patients, those with 17p+ had significantly better outcomes (PFS1: HR = 0.21, TTNT: HR = 0.18, OS: HR = 0.25, all p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between normal and 17p+ groups (p > 0.05).

Gain/Amplification of 17p Is Associated With Certain Cytogenetic Abnormalities

In previous Kaplan‒Meier analysis, 17p+ was found to be a potential protective prognostic factor compared with 17p−. To further investigate the factors influencing the 17p+ mutations, we performed a statistical analysis by comparing these cases with other clinical factors or cytogenetic abnormalities. The results revealed that 17p+ status was significantly associated with 1q21+ and 4p16 abnormalities, and deletion of 20q12 (p < 0.01, p < 0.01, p < 0.05, Table S1). The Cramer's V values for these associations were 0.308, 0.287, and 0.276, respectively (Table S1). Although 17p+ status may be positively correlated with 1q21+ and 4p16 abnormalities, and deletion of 20q12, the correlation coefficients were all < 0.5.

Prognostic Differences Between Gain/Amplification of 17p or Deletion of 17p in Different Treatment Regimens

Patients who received a proteasome inhibitor (Pi)‐based treatment regimen as part of their initial therapy had a significantly longer OS than those who did not receive Pi‐based therapy. However, there was no significant difference in PFS1 between the two groups (PFS1: HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.34–1.41, p > 0.05, Figure 1A, OS: HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.20–0.91, p < 0.05, Figure 1B). Among all patients who received Pi‐based initial treatment, patients with 17p+ mutations resulted in a significantly longer PFS1 than del(17p), but there was no significant difference in OS (PFS1: HR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05–0.77, p < 0.05, Figure 1C, OS: HR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.08–1.93, p > 0.05, Figure 1D).

In addition, in this study, patients who underwent ASCT had significantly prolonged OS and PFS1 (PFS1: HR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.23–0.87, p < 0.05, Figure 2A, OS: HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.13–1.01, p < 0.05, Figure 2B). In the real world, the majority of patients are unable to undergo ASCT for various reasons. Among patients who did not receive ASCT, 17p+ mutations did not significantly affect OS, but it significantly prolonged PFS1 (PFS1: HR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.64, p < 0.01, Figure 2C, OS: HR = 1.67, 95% CI: 0.10–1.24, p > 0.05, Figure 2D). It should be noted that, due to the limited sample size, survival analysis for patients with 17p+ or 17p− status who underwent ASCT in our cohort could not be reliably performed.

Gain/Amplification of 17p May be an Independent Protective Factor Affecting Prognosis in MM Patients

To further explore the independent prognostic factors for MM in this cohort, we included clinical baseline data and cytogenetic abnormalities, and established Cox proportional hazards regression models for PFS1, TTNT, and OS. Before constructing the Cox models, we verified the proportional hazards assumption for all covariates via the Schoenfeld residuals method (Table S2). For the PFS1 model (bone lesions, p < 0.05, Figure S1A, del(13q14), p < 0.05, Figure S1B, add(11q13), p < 0.05, Figure S1C), and the TTNT model (bone lesions, p < 0.05, Figure S2A, add(11q13), p < 0.05, Figure S2B), time‐dependent covariates were adjusted. After correction, no significant difference was observed in the AIC and BIC between the corrected and uncorrected models, and the corrected models exhibited good balance and model fit (Table S3A,B,C,D, all p > 0.05).

According to the TTNT regression model (Figure 3B), the following factors were identified as independent prognostic factors: The absence of plasma cell leukemia (HR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.06–0.70, p < 0.05), the absence of amyloidosis (HR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.05–0.93, p < 0.05), the absence of EMD (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.12–0.63, p < 0.01), the receipt of ASCT (HR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.12–0.67, p < 0.01), and cytogenetic abnormalities, including 17p abnormalities (17p+ vs. 17p−: HR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.02–0.27, p < 0.001, 17p normal vs. 17p−: HR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.08–0.56, p < 0.01), and 1q21 abnormalities (1q21 3 copies vs. 1q21 normal: HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.95, p < 0.05).

(B) Gain/amplification of 17p as an independent protective factor for TTNT. (C) Gain/amplification of 17p as an independent protective factor for OS. 17p−: Deletion 17p, 17p+: Gain/amplification 17p; ASCT: Autologous stem cell transplantation.

In the OS regression model (Figure 3C), the following factors were identified as independent prognostic factors: The absence of renal failure (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.10–0.54, p < 0.01), the absence of amyloidosis (HR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.01–0.80, p < 0.05), the absence of EMD (HR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.28, p < 0.001), the receipt of ASCT (HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.08–0.97, p < 0.05), and cytogenetic abnormalities, including 17p abnormalities (17p+ vs. 17p−: HR = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.004–0.16, p < 0.001, 17p normal vs. 17p−: HR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03–0.32, p < 0.001), 1q21 abnormalities (1q21 normal vs. 1q21 3 copies: HR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.04–0.36, p < 0.001), 13q14 abnormalities (del(13q14) vs. 13q14 normal: HR = 4.74, 95% CI: 4.74–17.36, p < 0.05), and 16q23 abnormalities (16q23 normal vs. 16q23 abnormal: HR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.37, p < 0.01).

Gain/Amplification of 17p May Reverse the Adverse Prognostic Impact of Gain/Amplification of 1q21

Our previous correlation analysis, revealed a positive correlation between 17p+ and 1q21+ (Table S1). 1q21+ mutations is a well‐known high‐risk cytogenetic abnormality in MM. Therefore, we further investigated the prognostic impact of the combination of 17p+ and 1q21+ in MM. Compared with 1q21+ without 17p+, the presence of 17p+ combined with 1q21+ significantly prolonged PFS1 (PFS1: HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.17–0.99, p < 0.05, Figure 4A) and TTNT (TTNT: HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.14–0.91, p < 0.05, Figure 4B). However, the combination of 17p+ and 1q21+ did not significantly affect OS (OS: HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.13–1.39, p > 0.05, Figure 4C).

Prognostic impact of 17p+ with a 1q21 abnormality on MM outcomes. PFS1 (A), TTNT (B), and OS (C) in MM patients with 17p+ with 1q21+ and without 1q21+. 17p+: gain/amplification 17p, 1q21+: gain/amplification 1q21.

In the PFS1 regression model (Figure 3A), the following factors were identified as independent prognostic factors: The absence of plasma cell leukemia (HR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.06–0.52, p < 0.01), the absence of amyloidosis (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.06–0.96, p < 0.05), the absence of EMD (HR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.75, p < 0.01), the receipt of ASCT (HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.16–0.80, p < 0.05), and cytogenetic abnormalities, including 17p abnormalities (17p+ vs. 17p−: HR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.25, p < 0.001, 17p normal vs. 17p−: HR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.44, p < 0.001), 1q21 abnormalities (1q21 3 copies vs. 1q21 normal: HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.96, p < 0.05), 13q14 abnormalities (del(13q14) vs. 13q14 normal: HR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.36–3.59, p < 0.01), and 16q23 abnormalities (16q23 normal vs. 16q23 abnormal: HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.14–0.98, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study performed a cytogenetic analysis of 125 MM patients from a real‐world retrospective cohort in China, with a particular focus on the relationship between 17p+ status and prognosis. The results showed that patients with 17p+ status exhibited significantly longer PFS1, TTNT, and OS compared with those with 17p− status, suggesting that 17p+ status may serve as a protective prognostic factor. Notably, this effect was observed even in patients harboring other high‐risk cytogenetic abnormalities, such as 1q21+, as defined by the mSMART guidelines [5]. This finding contrasts sharply with the conventional view that 17p abnormalities, particularly 17p−, are associated with poor prognosis and it may provide a novel perspective for risk stratification in MM.

The onset and progression of MM are associated with various primary and secondary genetic events [12]. Primary genetic events include translocations involving chromosome 14 (e.g., t(4,14), t(11,14), t(14,16), t(14,20)) and hyperdiploidy of odd‐numbered chromosomes (such as chromosomes 3, 5, 7, 9, etc.) [20]. Secondary genetic events are typically more complex and include chromosomal copy number abnormalities (e.g., del(13q), del(17p), add(1q21)), as well as MYC related translocations or somatic mutations in driver genes (such as KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and TP53) [21, 22]. Notably, the TP53 gene is located on the short arm of human chromosome 17 (17p13.1), and the P53 protein it encodes plays a crucial role in cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and apoptosis. Mutations or deletions of TP53 (e.g., del(17p)) are closely linked to the onset and progression of various malignancies [23]. Gain/amplification of 17p (17p+) reflects an increase in the copy number of this region, but its prognostic significance in MM remains unclear. It is possible that 17p+ either retains or partially enhances the normal function of TP53, or alternatively, it may reflect the increased expression of other potential protective genes located on 17p (such as PER1) [24], which could exert a positive impact on tumor biology.

In addition, the clonal heterogeneity of MM often gives rise to secondary high‐risk cytogenetic aberrations during disease onset or progression [25]. These high‐risk aberrations typically do not occur in isolation, and the mSMART risk stratification system has categorized two such aberrations as “double‐hit MM” and three as “triple‐hit MM” [6]. These genetic changes not only underlie the high heterogeneity of MM but also critical determinants of a patient's response to various treatments and long‐term prognosis. 1q21+ is one of the most common cytogenetic abnormalities in MM [26], and its adverse prognostic impact is well, as approximately 80% of relapsed/refractory MM patients exhibit this abnormality [27]. Our study found that the presence of 17p+ in conjunction with 1q21+ was associated with longer PFS1 and TTNT compared with the presence of 1q21+ alone. These findings suggest that 17p+ may partially mitigate the adverse prognostic effect of 1q21+, potentially providing a valuable addition to the current cytogenetic risk stratification in MM.

Furthermore, in our cohort, we observed a positive association between 17p+ and 1q21+ and 4p16 abnormalities, and del(20q12), although the correlations were relatively weak (all p < 0.5). It has been reported that in high‐risk MM, 1q21+, and 17p− may be associated via specific genomic events such as translocations [28].

Our study found that among patients who received PI‐based therapy and did not undergo ASCT, those with gain/amplification of 17p exhibited a significant prolongation of PFS1, although the effect on OS was not significant. This finding, based on cytogenetic data, offers a potential therapeutic strategy for identifying patients who are ineligible for transplantation, as well as elderly or frail patients who may benefit from a more personalized treatment approach.

At initial diagnosis, transplant‐eligible patients typically receive induction and consolidation therapy followed by high‐dose melphalan (HDM), autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), and maintenance therapy. When available, daratumumab combined with bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone (D‐VTd) represents the standard regimen for induction and consolidation [29]. For transplant‐ineligible patients, the daratumumab, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (DRd) regimen is generally preferred [30]. Meanwhile, elderly or frail patients are more prone to treatment interruptions due to treatment‐related adverse events, and this regimen demonstrates improved tolerability in these populations [1]. In our study, among patients receiving PI‐based therapy without ASCT, those with gain/amplification of 17p exhibited significantly prolonged PFS1, although OS was not significantly impacted. These findings suggest that cytogenetic profiling may help identify transplant‐ineligible, elderly, or frail patients who could benefit from more personalized therapeutic strategies.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, this is a single‐center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, which may introduce selection bias. Second, in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, cytogenetic abnormalities including t(4,14), t(14,16), and t(14,20) may not have produced robust results due to limited sample size. Besides, there are no clinical data in our center that simultaneously include both 17p+ alterations and next‐generation sequencing results, therefore, we are unable to discuss the gene expression characteristics of 17p+ cases. These findings require further validation in larger, multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that MM patients with 17p+ mutations, compared with those with 17p− mutations, MM patients with 17p+ mutations presented long‐term benefits in various outcome measures, including PFS1 (HR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.09–0.67, p < 0.01, Figure 5A), TTNT (HR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.06–0.55, p < 0.01, Figure 5B), and OS (HR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.07–0.89, p < 0.05, Figure 5C). Among MM patients receiving PI‐based treatment or those not receiving ASCT, 17p+ patients showed significantly longer PFS1 compared with 17p− patients (PI‐based treatment: HR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05–0.77, p < 0.05, Figure 1C, non‐ASCT: HR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.64, p < 0.01, Figure 2C). Furthermore, Cox multivariate regression analysis identified 17p+ as an independent protective prognostic factor relative to 17p− (PFS1: HR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.25, p < 0.001, Figure 3A, TTNT: HR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.02–0.27, p < 0.001, Figure 3B, OS: HR = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.004–0.16, p < 0.001, Figure 3C). In addition, when 17p+ mutations occur in MM patients with high‐risk 1q21+ mutations, concurrent 17p+ mutations may partially mitigate the adverse prognostic effect of 1q21+ (PFS1: HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.17–0.99, p < 0.05, Figure 4A, TTNT: HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.14–0.91, p < 0.05, Figure 4B), although no significant effect was observed on OS (OS: HR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.13–1.39, p > 0.05, Figure 4C). While further validation in multicenter studies with larger cohorts is warranted, these findings underscore the potential clinical relevance of 17p+ mutation status within the cytogenetic context of MM for informing precision treatment strategies.
