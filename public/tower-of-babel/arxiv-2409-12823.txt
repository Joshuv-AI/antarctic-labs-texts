Symplectic fermions in general domains
David Adame -Carrillo

Abstract
We review the basic features of a logarithmic conformal field theory that arise in the context of the scaling limit of lattice models. The theory of interest is the symplectic fermions, whose central charge is − 2 -2 . We provide an explicit construction of its space of fields as a logarithmic Fock space, and discuss its logarithmic structure as a representation of the Virasoro algebra. The construction of the correlation functions is presented following the ideas of the bootstrap approach. The text aims to be accessible to readers with little or no expertise in conformal field theory.



1Introduction

Statistical mechanics and conformal field theory.

It is a well-established conjecture
that the scaling limit of two-dimensional critical models of statistical mechanics can be described by two-dimensional conformal field theories (CFTs).
Yet, making precise mathematical sense of such a statement has proven to be challenging in most cases.
This conjecture has inspired many beautiful mathematical works on the emergence of conformally covariant objects in the scaling limit of probabilistic models.

The results in the literature that are more relevant for the present contribution are those regarding multipoint correlation functions.
One example are the works on the dimer model by Kenyon (
[
Ken00
,
Ken01
]
),
in which the author proved the scaling limit of the dimer height function to be the gaussian free
field (GFF),
an object that is well-known to carry the free boson CFT in its correlation kernels—see, e.g.,
[
KaMa13
]
.
Another instance of such results are the series of outstanding works

(
[
Hon10
,
CHI15
,
CHI21
]
) in which the correlation functions of all Ising primary fields with general boundary conditions were proven to converge to conformally covariant functions in the scaling limit.

A key feature of any two-dimensional CFT is that its space of fields enjoys a rich algebraic structure.
In particular, the set of local fields constitutes a representation of the Virasoro algebra, an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra that encodes the conformal symmetries of the theory.
The correlation functions of all the (infinitely many) fields are conjecturally expected to be recovered from the scaling limit of the corresponding discrete model too.

Recently (
[
ABK24
]
), and for the first time, the full structure of a CFT has been obtained as the scaling limit of a discrete model.
The results can be summarized as follows.
Following the ideas developed in
[
HKV22
]
, the space of lattice local fields of the discrete gaussian free field (DGFF),
can be rendered a Virasoro representation.
Moreover, this space is in one-to-one correspondence with —i.e., it is isomorphic to— the space of local fields of the free boson CFT.
Via this correspondence, the (suitably renormalized) correlation functions of DGFF lattice local fields converge, in the scaling limit, to the CFT correlation functions of the corresponding free boson local fields.

Logarithmic CFT.

In certain critical lattice models, non-local observables give rise to logarithmic singularities in correlation functions.
The CFTs that appear in the scaling limit of these models are known as logarithmic conformal field theories (logCFTs).
Besides featuring fields that produce logarithmic singularities in correlation functions, the space of fields of a logCFT exhibits a more intricate algebraic structure than non-logarithmic CFTs.
See
[
CrRi13
]
for a review on logCFT.

A noteworthy example of this phenomenon are crossing probabilities in critical percolation.
In fact, the CFT arguments that Cardy used in his original derivation of the celebrated Cardy–Smirnov formula (
[
Car92
,
Smi01
]
) can be accommodated only
in a logarithmic CFT;
see, e.g.,
[
MaRi07
]
.
More recently, the understanding of percolation as a logCFT has seen further developments:
certain probabilistic observables have been identified as CFT fields that produce logarithmic dependencies within correlation functions—see
[
Cam24
,
CaFe24
]
.

A prominent example of a logarithmic CFT is the symplectic fermions.
The theory was first expounded in the full plane by Kausch in
[
Kau00
]
, building on earlier works as
[
Gur93
,
Kau95
,
GaKa99
]
.
It was subsequently reformulated in the language of vertex operator algebras (VOA) in
[
Abe05
]
),
and has ever since been studied by the VOA community; see
[
DaRu17
]
and references therein.

Associated to every CFT there is a —typically real— parameter
c
called
central charge
that is encoded in the Virasoro structure of the space of fields.
In essence, the central charge determines how a system responds to the introduction of a macroscopic length scale (
[
DMS97
, Section 5.4]
).
For the symplectic fermions, this parameter takes the value
-2
.
What makes this theory particularly appealing are its deep connections with several discrete probabilistic models that have long been predicted to fall within the universality class of CFTs at central charge
c=-2
.

Dimers, spanning trees, dense polymers and sandpiles.

In the recent work
[
AdRu25
]
, the
fermionic discrete gaussian free field
(fDGFF) has been proven to converge in the scaling limit to the symplectic fermions CFT in the sense of
[
ABK24
]
.
This result is consistent with longstanding predictions that several discrete models should be described by (logarithmic) CFTs at
c=-2
, particularly in light of the various connections between the fDGFF and these models.
Motivated by these developments, the goal of this text is therefore twofold:
first, to provide a thorough review of the notions of symplectic fermions that arise in the analysis of this scaling-limit convergence; and second, to present these ideas in a way accessible to a broader audience, including readers with limited familiarity with conformal field theory.

The fDGFF naturally arises in tree and forest models —see, e.g.,
[
CJS+04
]
or the more recent
[
BCHS21
]
—, which can be exploited to compute correlation functions in general domains (
[
CCRR23
]
).
Spanning trees have been classically linked to (log)CFT at
c=-2
;
see, e.g.,
[
MaRu01
]
where the connection goes via the abelian sandpile model or
[
Sal92
]
in the context of dense polymers.
In more modern contributions like
[
LPW21
]
, the uniform spanning tree has been linked with the symplectic fermions CFT in a random geometry setting.

The dimer model provides another key example.
It is widely regarded as a free fermionic theory that exhibits characteristic features of a conformally covariant theory with central charge
-2
—see
[
IPRH05
]
and references therein—.
Furthermore, recent progress in
[
Ada23
]
has shown that certain observables in this model carry the algebraic structure of the symplectic fermions.

The abelian sandpile model is a particularly interesting case.
It has been argued to be described by a logarithmic CFT at
c=-2
that is
not
equivalent to the symplectic fermions —see
[
Rue21
]
and references therein—.
However, the two theories share some common fields, which has been exploited in
[
CCRR23
]
to compute (the scaling limit of) the correlation functions of the height-one field in general domains of the complex plane.

Dense polymers (or spanning forests) provide yet another classical example of a critical model displaying features of a conformally covariant system with central charge
-2
(
[
Dup86
]
) that has been linked to the symplectic fermions CFT
(
[
Sal92
,
Iva99
]
).
A closely related model are the Hamiltonian walks, which exhibits too characteristics of a CFT at
c=-2
(
[
DuDa88
]
).

2The space of fields

In this section, we construct the space of fields of the symplectic fermions CFT.
In (bulk) two-dimensional conformal field theory, the space of fields is a representation of two commuting Virasoro algebras.
Naturally, this is also the case for the symplectic fermions CFT.

However, we start our discussion of the space of fields by constructing a chiral version of it, that is, a module of a single copy of the Virasoro algebra with a similar structure to the full non-chiral module.
Our motivation to do so is twofold.
Firstly, it is notationally lighter to work in the chiral setting, which allows us to discuss its logarithmic structure more transparently.
Secondly, by starting from the chiral module, we can appreciate a non-trivial difference with non-logarithmic CFT in the construction of the full non-chiral theory.
Gaberdiel and Kausch observed in
[
GaKa99
]
that,
the non-chiral module of a logCFT needs to satisfy a condition that is trivially satisfied by the modules that appear in non-logarithmic CFT.
This fact renders the construction of the non-chiral module of a logCFT notably more involved.

Section
2.1
contains the definition of the chiral logarithmic Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
of the symplectic fermions.
In Section
2.2
, we single out the vectors within the module that play an important role in the construction of the symplectic fermions CFT throughout the article.
In Section
2.3
, we outline how the logarithmic Fock space becomes a Virasoro representation through the Sugawara construction, and we highlight the logarithmic nature of it.
The space
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
can be proven to have, as a Virasoro subrepresentation, a staggered module—the proof of this fact and the necessary definitions are presented in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
.
Finally, the full non-chiral space of fields of the symplectic fermions CFT is defined in Section
2.6
as the (non-chiral) logarithmic Fock space of the symplectic fermions.
We conclude the section by giving some remarks in the direction of correlation functions in Section
2.7
.

2.3.Logarithmic Virasoro action

The chiral logarithmic Fock space can be rendered a Virasoro representation via a Sugawara construction,
i.e.,
one can define Virasoro modes as
an infinite sum quadratic on the current modes.
In the present case, the Virasoro action can be easily checked to be of the type that one expects in logarithmic CFT.

For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, define the linear operator
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
given by the formal sum

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,{\upchi}_{n-k}{\upeta}_{k}\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,\,,

(2.2)

where the normal ordering
\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,\cdots\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,{\upchi}_{k}{\upeta}_{\ell}\,\rotatebox[]{90.0}{\scalebox{.7}{$\ \bullet\,\bullet$}}\,\coloneqq\begin{cases}\phantom{-}{\upchi}_{k}{\upeta}_{\ell}&\mspace{10mu}\textnormal{ if }\;k\leq\ell\,,\\
\;-{\upeta}_{\ell}{\upchi}_{k}&\mspace{10mu}\textnormal{ if }\;k>\ell\,.\end{cases}

In order to argue the well-posedness of these infinite sums, we need, first, the following observation about
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
:
for any state
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
, there exists
K\in{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
such that, for all
k\geq K
, we have

\displaystyle{\upeta}_{k}\mathbf{v}\;=\;{\upchi}_{k}\mathbf{v}\;=\;0\,.

Well-definedness then follows since only finitely many terms produce non-zero vectors when acting on a given vector in
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
with any
{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
.

This definition leads to the following known result, referred to as the
Sugawara construction
, and justifies the name
Virasoro modes
for the operators
{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
[A,B]\coloneqq AB-BA
denote the usual commutator of linear operators.
The Virasoro modes (
2.2
) satisfy the Virasoro commutation relations with central charge
-2
.
That is, for all
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

\displaystyle[\,{\mathsf{L}}_{n},{\mathsf{L}}_{m}\,]\,=\;(n-m)\,{\mathsf{L}}_{n+m}+\frac{c}{12}(n^{3}-n)\,\delta_{n+m}\,\textnormal{id}_{\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}}

with
c=-2
.
See
[
Kac97
, Chapter 5.1]
for its discussion in the context of vertex algebras and
[
Ada23
, Section 6.2]
for a detailed computational proof.

\diamond

Our first observation regarding the Virasoro structure of
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
is the non-diagonalizability of the Virasoro mode
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
, a characteristic feature of the modules that appear in logarithmic CFT.
We record this fact in a remark for later reference.

Remark 2.2
.

The Virasoro mode
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
acts on the bosonic ground states as

\displaystyle\mathllap{{\mathsf{L}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,=\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\mspace{50mu}}\text{ and }\mathrlap{\mspace{50mu}{\mathsf{L}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\,=\,0\,.}

It is clear from these that
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
is non-diagonalizable in
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
.
The first relation is the reason to call
\boldsymbol{\omega}
the
logarithmic partner
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
.

\diamond

In spite of not being diagonalizable, the operator
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
provides a natural
{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
-grading
of
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
via its decomposition into generalized eigenspaces.
The generalized
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
-eigenvalues are called
conformal dimensions
.
The grading can be expressed simply in terms of the basis (
2.1
);
a straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\Big[\,{\mathsf{L}}_{0}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k},\bm{\ell}}\;\textnormal{id}_{\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}}\Big]^{2}\big({\upeta}_{-k_{r}}\cdots{\upeta}_{-k_{2}}{\upeta}_{-k_{1}}{\upchi}_{-\ell_{s}}\cdots{\upchi}_{-\ell_{2}}{\upchi}_{-\ell_{1}}\boldsymbol{\omega}\big)\,=\,0\,,

where
\Delta_{\mathbf{k},\bm{\ell}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}
is the conformal dimension of the corresponding basis state.
In particular, this implies that
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
has Jordan blocks of rank at most
2
.

Remark 2.3
.

By a straightforward computation using the expression of the Virasoro modes (
2.2
) one can check that we have

\displaystyle\mspace{170mu}{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\,=\,{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\,=\,{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,=\,{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,=\,0\mspace{90mu}\text{ for }n>0\,.

\diamond

2.4.Interlude: highest-weight representations at
c=-2

Remark
2.2
in the previous section suggests that the chiral Fock space contains, as a submodule, a staggered module in the sense of Kytölä and Ridout
[
KyRi09
]
.
That is the content of Proposition
2.1
below, but before defining staggered modules, we recall the definition of highest-weight modules and their submodule structure at
c=-2
.
To that end, we review here some well-known facts about highest-weight modules that date back to the works of Feigin and Fuchs
[
FeFu84
]
, who built on a conjecture by Kac
[
Kac79
]
that they had proved
[
FeFu82
]
.

The
Virasoro algebra
is the infinite dimensional Lie algebra

\displaystyle\textnormal{{Vir}}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbf{L}}_{n}\;\oplus\;{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{C}

equipped with the Lie bracket

\displaystyle\big[\,{\mathbf{L}}_{n},{\mathbf{L}}_{m}\,\big]\;=\;(n-m)\,{\mathbf{L}}_{n+m}\,+\,\frac{n^{3}-n}{12}\,\delta_{n+m}\,\mathbf{C}\mspace{50mu}\text{ and }\mspace{50mu}\big[\,{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\mathbf{C}\,\big]\;=\;0\,.

A Virasoro module
\mathcal{M}
is said to be a
highest-weight module
if
\mathbf{C}
acts as a constant multiple of the identity on
\mathcal{M}
, and it has a vector
{\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{M}}
that

(cyclic)

is cyclic, i.e., any other vector in
\mathcal{M}
can be obtained by the action of
Vir
on
\mathbf{h}
,

(
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
e.v.)

is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
, and

(primary)

is annihilated by all positive Virasoro modes, i.e

\displaystyle\mspace{20mu}{\mathbf{L}}_{n}\,\mathbf{h}=0\mspace{40mu}\text{ for }n>0\,.

The unique eigenvalue
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
of
\mathbf{C}
is called the
central charge
of
\mathcal{M}
and the
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
-eigenvalue
h\in{\mathbb{C}}
of
\mathbf{h}
is called the
highest weight
of
\mathcal{M}
.
Note that
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
acts diagonally on any highest-weight module; its eigenvalues are called
conformal dimensions
.

Any highest-weight module is (isomorphic to) a quotient of a
Verma module

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{h}^{(c)}\coloneqq\mathcal{U}\big(\textnormal{{Vir}}\big)/\big({\mathbf{L}}_{0}-h,{\mathbf{L}}_{1},{\mathbf{L}}_{2},\mathbf{C}-c\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{U}(\textnormal{{Vir}})
is the universal enveloping algebra of the Virasoro algebra,

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denotes the left ideal generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathcal{U}(\textnormal{{Vir}})
, and
h,c\in{\mathbb{C}}
are complex numbers.
Note that, since
{\mathbf{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{L}}_{2}
algebraically generate all the positive modes, we have
[\,{\mathbf{L}}_{n}\,]=0
in the quotient
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{(c)}
for all
n>0
.
By construction, since
[\,1\,]\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{(c)}
is a cyclic vector, all Verma modules are indecomposable.
However, they are not irreducible in general.

The relevant Verma module for the CFT at hand is
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{(-2)}
, which is, in fact, reducible.
Since the central charge is fixed to
-2
throughout the present work, we drop the superindex in the notation.
The submodule structure of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is commonly called of
chain type
, which we record in the form of a remark for later reference.

Remark 2.4
.

For the Verma module
\mathcal{V}_{0}
of highest weight
0
and central charge
c=-2
, we have the chain of submodules

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0}\supset\mathcal{V}_{1}\supset\mathcal{V}_{3}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{V}_{k\choose 2}\supset\mathcal{V}_{{k+1}\choose 2}\supset\cdots\,.

That is, for each integer
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
greater than
1
, the Verma module
\mathcal{V}_{0}
has a unique submodule isomorphic to
\mathcal{V}_{h_{k}}
that is cyclically generated by a vector with conformal
dimension
{h_{k}}\coloneqq{k\choose 2}
.
In turn, the submodule of highest weight
h_{k}
has a submodule isomorphic to
\mathcal{V}_{h_{k+1}}
that is cyclically generated by a vector with conformal dimension
h_{k+1}={{k+1}\choose 2}
.
Those are, moreover, all the proper submodules of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
.

\diamond

Starting from
\mathcal{V}_{0}
, we can define, for
h\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}\choose 2}
, the highest-weight representations

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}_{h}\;\coloneqq\;\mathcal{V}_{0}/\mathcal{V}_{h}

(2.3)

with highest weight
0
.
Note that only
\mathcal{H}^{0}_{1}
is irreducible.

For concreteness, we write down the cyclic vectors of the two largest submodules of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
in the following remark, which will be necessary in the proof of Proposition
2.1
below.

Remark 2.5
.

Up to a non-zero multiplicative constant, the cyclic vectors of the submodules
\mathcal{V}_{1}\subset\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{3}\subset\mathcal{V}_{0}
are, respectively,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{L}}_{-1}\,[\,1\,]\,,\mspace{50mu}\text{ and }\mspace{50mu}\Big(({\mathbf{L}}_{-1})^{2}-2{\mathbf{L}}_{-2}\Big)\,{\mathbf{L}}_{-1}\,[\,1\,]\,.

It takes a simple calculation to check that these two vectors are indeed annihilated by the action of the positive Virasoro modes in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
.
\diamond

2.5.Staggered (sub)module of
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}

In this subsection, we introduce staggered modules following the definition of Kytölä and Ridout.
We, moreover, prove the chiral logarithmic Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
to have a staggered module as a submodule.

A Virasoro module
\mathcal{S}
is a
staggered module
if
\mathbf{C}
acts as a constant multiple of the identity on
\mathcal{S}
, and

(indecom.)

it is indecomposable,

(
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
non-diag.)

the Virasoro mode
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
, as a linear operator
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
, has Jordan blocks of rank 2, and

(sh. ex. seq.)

it satisfies the short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\,\longrightarrow\,\mathcal{H}^{\text{L}}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathcal{S}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathcal{H}^{\text{R}}\,\longrightarrow\,0\,,

where
\mathcal{H}^{\text{L}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{R}}
are highest-weight modules.

The modules
\mathcal{H}^{\text{L}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{R}}
are called the
left module
and
right module
of
\mathcal{S}
, respectively.
The unique eigenvalue of
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
of
\mathbf{C}
is called
central charge
.
Note the central charges of
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{H}^{\text{L}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{R}}
must coincide.

Provided a left and right module, the existence or uniqueness of the corresponding staggered module is not, a priori, guaranteed.
The answer to such question is rather non-trivial and was fully elaborated in
[
KyRi09
]
.
Let us provide an example therefrom of a staggered module that does exist and whose short exact sequence implies its uniqueness.
We deliberately choose to give as an example the module in
[
KyRi09
, Example 2]
, which we then prove to be a submodule of the chiral logarithmic Fock space in Proposition
2.1
below.

Example 2.4

(
[
KyRi09
, Example 2]
)
.

There exists a staggered module
\mathcal{S}^{\,0}_{1,3}
with central charge
c=-2
and short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;\mathcal{H}^{0}_{1}\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;\mathcal{S}^{\,0}_{1,3}\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;\mathcal{H}^{0}_{3}\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;0\,,

where
\mathcal{H}^{0}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}^{0}_{3}
are as defined in (
2.3
).
The staggered module
\mathcal{S}^{\,0}_{1,3}
is, moreover, unique up to Virasoro module isomorphism.

\diamond

We now prove that
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
contains
\mathcal{S}^{\,0}_{1,3}
as a Virasoro subrepresentation.
In the following
—and
for the rest of the
text—
, let
{\mathcal{V}ir}\subset\textnormal{End}(\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}})
denote the associative algebra generated by the Virasoro modes
{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
.
Then, for any state
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
, we let
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\varphi}
denote the submodule of
\mathcal{F}^{\textnormal{(chi)}}
cyclically generated by the state
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
under the action of the Virasoro modes.

Proposition 2.1
.

We have the isomorphism

\displaystyle({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}\;\cong\;\mathcal{S}^{0}_{1,3}

as Virasoro representations.

Proof.

We argued in Remark
2.2
that
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
satisfies
(
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
non-diag.)
.
From the same remark, since we have
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\in({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
, we get that the module
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
generated by
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
is a submodule of
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
In particular, we have the short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;\;({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}\;\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{50mu}}\;({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}/({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\;\xrightarrow{\mspace{46mu}}\;0\,.

What is left in order to prove property
(sh. ex. seq.)
is that the left module
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
and the right module
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}/({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
are isomorphic to highest-weight modules. In particular, we prove that they are isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{0,1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0,3}
respectively.

In the left module, the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\in({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
satisfies
(cyclic)
by construction,
(
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
e.v.)
by

Remark
2.2
, and
(primary)
by Remark
2.3
.
The highest weight of
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
is
0
.
A straightforward computation using the expression of the Virasoro modes (
2.2
) leads to

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0\,.

From the submodule structure of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
—Remark
2.4
—, it follows that the left module
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{0,1}
, which is irreducible.

At this point we can easily see that
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
satisfies
(indecom.)
too: by the commutation relations of the Virasoro algebra, the generator
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
commutes —up to a multiplicative constant— with any other Virasoro generator.
Hence, we have
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}=({\mathcal{V}ir}){\mathsf{L}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\omega}=({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
.
It follows, then, that any submodule of
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
must contain the irreducible
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
; which implies that
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
is indecomposable.

As for the right module, we can argue that it is non-trivial since
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
is diagonal in
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
but not in
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}
.
In particular, we can see that
\boldsymbol{\omega}\notin({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
since the states
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
have the same conformal dimension.
It is also straightforward to check that the vector
\big[\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\big]\in({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}/({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
satisfies
(cyclic)
by construction,
(
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}
e.v.)
by Remark
2.2
, and
(primary)
by Remark
2.3
.
In view of Remark
2.5
, in order to prove that
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}/({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{0,3}
it remains to prove

\displaystyle\big[\,{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\big]\neq\;0\mspace{50mu}\text{ and }\mspace{50mu}\big[\,\big(({\mathsf{L}}_{-1})^{2}-2{\mathsf{L}}_{-2}\big)\,{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\big]=\;0

(2.4)

in the quotient
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}/({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
.
As for the first of the two, a simple computation leads to

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,=\,({\upeta}_{-1}{\upchi}_{0}-{\upchi}_{-1}{\upeta}_{0})\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\neq\,0\,,

which has conformal dimension
1
.
There is no vector in
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
with conformal dimension
1
—since we have
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0
—, so we can conclude that
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\notin({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
,
that is
[\,{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,]\neq 0
.
As for the second equation in (
2.4
), a small computation leads to

\displaystyle\big(({\mathsf{L}}_{-1})^{2}-2{\mathsf{L}}_{-2}\big){\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\;=\;-\,{\mathsf{L}}_{-3}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\,,

that is, in the right module we have
[\,(({\mathsf{L}}_{-1})^{2}-2{\mathsf{L}}_{-2})\,{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\,]=0
.
The submodule structure of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
—Remark
2.4
—, implies that the right module is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{0,3}
.

By Example
2.4
, the exact short sequence of
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{1,3}
uniquely determines its isomorphism equivalence class—we must have
({\mathcal{V}ir})\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\cong\,\mathcal{S}^{0}_{1,3}
.
∎

2.6.The full non-chiral representation

In bulk two-dimensional CFT, the space of fields is a module of two commuting copies of the Virasoro algebra.
In
[
GaKa99
]
, Gaberdiel and Kausch argued that the nilpotent part of the operator
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}-\mathrlap{\hskip-1.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.85}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{L}$}}}}\mathbf{L}_{0}

Note that this condition becomes relevant only in logarithmic CFT, where the Virasoro modes
{\mathbf{L}}_{0}

2.7.A glimpse of correlation functions

In Section
3
below, we will construct the correlation functions of the symplectic fermions CFT in domains of the complex plane.
In this section, we give some remarks that will guide us in such construction.

Remark 2.7
.

For each complex constant
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists an
\mathcal{SF}
-module automorphism, which
—by the
\mathcal{SF}
-cyclicity of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
in
\mathcal{F}
—
is fully determined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}\longmapsto\boldsymbol{\omega}+\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}\,,

that is clearly not trivial for
\alpha\neq 0
.
These maps automatically lift to
(\textnormal{{Vir}}\oplus\mathrlap{\hskip-0.6pt\overline{\scalebox{1.02}[1]{\phantom{$\textnormal{{Vir}}$}}}}\textnormal{{Vir}})
-module automorphisms.
Note that they act as the identity map when restricted to the submodules
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
.
These automorphisms can be related to rescalings of the coordinates within correlation functions — see Example
3.4
below.

Physically, this means that a state cannot be distinguished from its image under one of these automorphisms.
We should
not
expected, then, the correlation functions of a CFT built from the representation
\mathcal{F}
to be canonically unique.
Rather, to determine the correlation functions of the theory we need to fix one extra parameter that can take as many values as there are non-equivalent automorphisms of
\mathcal{F}
.

\diamond

Fix
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
and consider
n\in{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
fields
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}}
.
Their correlation function in a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued real-analytic multivariable function

\displaystyle(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\longmapsto\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}

of distinct points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\Omega
.
The collection of correlation functions of the theory is determined by the algebraic structure of the space
\mathcal{F}
.
In particular, a fundamental dictate of CFT says that the action of
{\mathbf{L}}_{-1}
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-1.0pt\overline{\scalebox{0.85}[1]{\phantom{$\mathbf{L}$}}}}\mathbf{L}_{-1}
translate, respectively, into holomorphic and antiholomorphic Wirtinger derivatives inside correlation functions. That is, we have

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big[{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}=\partial_{z}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\mspace{15mu}\text{ and }\mspace{25mu}\Big\langle\big[\mspace{1mu}\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}=\overline{\partial}_{z}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,,

for any field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Note, then, that the algebraic relations
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=0
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=0
translate into the correlations functions of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}
being antiholomorphic and holomorphic, respectively.

Remark 2.8
.

The ground state
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
satisfies both

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0\mspace{65mu}\text{ and }\mspace{65mu}\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0\,.

This implies that its correlation functions do not depend on the insertion point
z\in\Omega
.
In Example
3.1
, we will determine the precise action of
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
within correlation functions.

\diamond

Remark 2.9
.

The currents
\boldsymbol{\chi}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}\;=\;\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\;=\;0\,,

which justifies their name
holomorphic
currents.
Similarly, the
antiholomorphic
currents
\overline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
satisfy
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\overline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}={\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=0
.

\diamond

Lastly, the combined action of
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}
can be interpreted in terms of the laplacian operator
\Delta\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
, with
x=\textnormal{Re}(z)
and
y=\textnormal{Im}(z)
, as a consequence of its factorization
\Delta=4\partial\overline{\partial}=4\overline{\partial}\partial
in terms of the holomorphic and antiholomorphic Wirtinger derivatives.

Remark 2.10
.

The ground fermions satisfy

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\;=\;0\mspace{70mu}\text{ and }\mspace{70mu}{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}\;=\;0\,.

These algebraic identities translate to the correlation functions of the ground fermions being harmonic with respect to its insertion points.

\diamond

3Correlation functions

In this section, we define the correlation functions for the symplectic fermions CFT in domains of the complex plane.
In particular, we construct correlation functions on (non-empty) proper simply-connected open subsets of the complex plane.
For the remainder of the text, any domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is taken to be of such kind.

Correlation functions are the physically relevant quantities in a field theory.
In the context of statistical mechanics, one can think of CFT correlation functions as the scaling limit of the —appropriately renormalised— correlation functions of a lattice model.
However, this picture is not so simple in logarithmic CFT.
The presence of logarithmic fields —whose correlation functions feature logarithmic dependencies— render the correlation functions of the theory not exactly physical.
The origin of this can be seen more transparently in algebraic terms:
there exists a family of non-trivial Virasoro automorphisms of the space of fields.

In Remark
2.7
, we encountered a family of non-trivial automorphisms of
\mathcal{F}
indexed by a constant
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Essentially, this means that we cannot physically distinguish a field and its image under these automorphisms.
We also noted in Remark
2.7
, that these automorphisms act as the identity on the submodules
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
(\mathcal{SF})\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
.
We therefore expect the correlation functions of the fields in these submodules to be canonically defined.
However, to completely determine the correlation functions of the logarithmic fields —that is, those that are not invariant under the automorphisms of
\mathcal{F}
— we need to specify an extra piece of data; in particular, we need to specify a constant
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}
.

Fixing
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the
correlation functions
of the symplectic fermions are a collection, indexed by
n\in{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
, of linear maps

\displaystyle\big\langle\cdots\big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;\colon\;\mathcal{F}^{\,\otimes n}\longrightarrow C^{\,\omega}\big(\textnormal{Conf}_{n}(\Omega);{\mathbb{C}}\big)\,,

where
C^{\,\omega}\big(\textnormal{Conf}_{n}(\Omega);{\mathbb{C}}\big)
is the set of
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued real-analytic functions on the
configuration space

\displaystyle\textnormal{Conf}_{n}(\Omega)\coloneqq\Big\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\Omega^{n}\,\big|\,z_{i}\neq z_{j}\text{ for }i\neq j\Big\}\,

of the domain
\Omega
.
In other words, for
n\geq 1
fixed fields
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have a function

\displaystyle(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\longmapsto\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,

which is real-analytic on the variables
z_{i}\in\Omega
away from one another.

In Theorem
3.1
, we provide the characterization of the correlation functions of the symplectic fermions.
To do so, we follow two basic principles of CFT.
The first one, explored more in detail in Section
3.1
below, states what the action of the Virasoro modes
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}
translates into within correlation functions.
The second one involves the existence of expansions of the type

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}(z_{2})\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;=\;\sum_{i}f_{i}(z_{1}-z_{2})\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(z_{2})\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}

that are convergent for
|z_{1}-z_{2}|
small enough.
These are known as operator product expansions (OPEs); we describe them in more detail in Section
3.2
.

3.1.Conformal covariance

One of the axioms of CFT that connects the conformal symmetries of correlation functions with the algebraic structure of the fields is the following:
for any field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\phantom{\Bigg|}\Big\langle\big[{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;=\;\partial_{z}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,,\mspace{5mu}\text{ and }

(3.1)

\displaystyle\phantom{\bigg|}\Big\langle\big[\mspace{1mu}\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;=\;\overline{\partial}_{z}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,,

(3.2)

where
\partial
and
\overline{\partial}
are the holomorphic and antiholomorphic Wirtinger derivatives, respectively.

In Remark
2.10
, we anticipated that the identities

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\;=\;0\mspace{70mu}\text{ and }\mspace{70mu}{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\,\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}\;=\;0\,,

(3.3)

would guide our construction of the correlation functions of the theory.
Consider the
2
-point function

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w)\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}

(3.4)

of the ground fermions, and recall that the laplacian operator
\Delta\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
can be factorized as
{\Delta=4\partial\overline{\partial}=4\overline{\partial}\partial}
.
Then, from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we have that the identities (
3.3
) imply that the function

\displaystyle(z,w)\longmapsto\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}(z)\,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w)\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,,

is harmonic in each of its variables separately in
\textnormal{Conf}_{2}(\Omega)
.
Its singular behaviour when
|z-w|\to 0
is determined to be proportional to
\log|z-w|
by the conformal covariance of the theory (
[
Kau00
]
).
The exact value of the two-point function, however, depends on the boundary values we choose on the domain
\Omega
.
Consistently with this, in Theorem
3.1
, we fix it to be proportional to the Green’s function of the laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

For concreteness, the laplacian Green’s function with Dirichlet boundary conditions on a domain
\Omega
is the real-analytic function
\mathbf{G}_{\Omega}
on
\textnormal{Conf}_{2}(\Omega)
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mspace{8mu}\mathbf{G}_{\Omega}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{1}{|z-w|}+\mathbf{g}_{\Omega}(z,w)\,,\mspace{30mu}\text{ and }

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\partial\Omega}\mathbf{G}_{\Omega}(z,w)=0\,,\phantom{\Bigg|}

with
\mathbf{g}_{\Omega}\colon\Omega\times\Omega\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
a harmonic function on both variables separately.
In particular, the function
z\mapsto\mathbf{g}_{\Omega}(z,w)
on
\Omega
is the (unique) harmonic extention of the boundary values
x\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-w|
.
Note too that
\mathbf{G}_{\Omega}
is harmonic in both variables separately on its domain of definition
\textnormal{Conf}_{2}(\Omega)
.

Besides the harmonicity of the ground fermions, we also anticipated in Remark
2.8
the identities
{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}=0
.
Under the light of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), they imply that the correlation function

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}(z)\cdots\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}

does not depend on the insertion point
z\in\Omega
.
In Theorem
3.1
below, we will see that the field
\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}
acts as the identity field within correlation functions, i.e.
\big\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}(z)\cdots\big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}=\big\langle\cdots\big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}
.

3.2.Operator product expansions

In the bootstrap approach to field
theory
, one circumvents the lagrangian formalism and computes the correlation functions of the theory by exploiting certain properties thereof.
A celebrated feature of conformal field theories that make it possible to bootstrap them is the existence of an
operator product expansion
(
OPE
); which in the present case is defined as follows.
For any two fields
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}\in\mathcal{F}}
, there exists a countable collection
\{f_{i},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued functions
f_{i}
on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, and fields
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
, such that

for

\displaystyle\text{all domains\penalty\ $\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}$}\,,\text{ and }\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle\text{any fields\penalty\ }\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{3},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\,,\phantom{\Big|}

we have

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}(z_{2})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\phantom{\Bigg|}=\phantom{\Bigg|}\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}(z_{1}-z_{2})\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(z_{2})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}

whenever

\displaystyle 0<|z_{1}-z_{2}|<\min\big\{\!\textstyle\min_{j\geq 3}|z_{2}-z_{j}|\,,\,\mathsf{d}(z_{2},\partial\Omega)\big\}\,,\phantom{\bigg|}

where
\mathsf{d}(z_{2},\partial\Omega)
denotes the distance from the point
z_{2}
to the boundary of
\Omega
.

In such case, we then just write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}(z_{2})\,=\,\;\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}(z_{1}-z_{2})\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(z_{2})\,.

Naturally, since the correlation functions of the symplectic fermions are canonically defined only up to the field transformations in Remark
2.7
,
only fields modulo automorphism appear in the OPEs of the theory.
See the statement of Theorem
3.1
below for an example of this fact.

In the previous section, we stressed that, in algebraic terms,
the space of fields
\mathcal{F}
is said to be logarithmic because
the Virasoro modes
{\mathsf{L}}_{0}
and
\mathrlap{\hskip-0.3pt\overline{\scalebox{0.6}[1]{\phantom{$\mathsf{L}$}}}}\mathsf{L}_{0}
are non-diagonalizable thereon.
In terms of OPEs, the logarithmic nature of the theory can be seen more transparently.
Some of the expansions feature logarithms
—see Theorem
3.1
—,
in contrast with non-logarithmic CFTs, in which the OPE coefficients are of the type
(z_{1}-z_{2})^{\alpha}{(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})^{\beta}}
for
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Commonly in axiomatic descriptions of CFT, it is taken as an axiom that there exists a pair of fields —called the holomorphic and antiholomorphic stress–energy tensors— whose OPEs with other fields involve the action of the Virasoro modes.
However, in the CFT at hand, the Virasoro structure is a consequence of the symplectic fermions structure.
We, hence, formulate this second axiom in terms of the symmetry algebra.
In particular, in our characterization of the correlation functions
—Theorem
3.1
—,
it will be enough to require that the currents have the OPEs

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(z)\boldsymbol{\varphi}(w)\,=\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\frac{\,\big[{\upchi}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](w)\,}{\,(z-w)^{k+1}}\,,\mspace{130mu}\boldsymbol{\eta}(z)\boldsymbol{\varphi}(w)\,=\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\frac{\,\big[{\upeta}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](w)\,}{\,(z-w)^{k+1}}\,,

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(z)\boldsymbol{\varphi}(w)\,=\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\frac{\,\big[\mathrlap{\hskip-0.6pt\overline{\scalebox{1}[1]{\phantom{$\upchi$}}}}\upchi_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](w)\,}{\,(\overline{z}-\overline{w})^{k+1}}\,,\mspace{40mu}\text{ and }\mspace{50mu}\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(z)\boldsymbol{\varphi}(w)\,=\,\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,\frac{\,\big[\mathrlap{\hskip-0.6pt\overline{\scalebox{0.97}[1]{\phantom{$\upeta$}}}}\upeta_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big](w)\,}{\,(\overline{z}-\overline{w})^{k+1}}\,,

with any other field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{F}
.

It is sometimes more useful not to state all the terms in an OPE but rather the non-vanishing terms as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
.
For this reason, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\phantom{\bigg|}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}(z_{2})\,\mathrel{\overset{}{\scalebox{1.5}[1]{$\sim$}}}\,\;\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}^{(\alpha)}(z_{1}-z_{2})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(z_{2})

to indicate

\displaystyle\phantom{\Bigg|}\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}(z_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}(z_{2})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,=\,\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}^{(\alpha)}(z_{1}-z_{2})\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(z_{2})\cdots\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}(z_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;+\;o\big(|z_{1}-z_{2}|\big)

as
|z_{1}-z_{2}|\to 0
, for all fields
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{3},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
and any domain
\Omega
.

3.3.Characterization

In the following theorem, we provide a characterization of the correlation functions of the symplectic fermions CFT.

Theorem 3.1
.

For each complex number
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists a unique collection

\displaystyle\bigg\{\;\big\langle\cdots\big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\ \colon\;\mathcal{F}^{\,\otimes n}\longrightarrow C^{\,\omega}\big(\textnormal{Conf}_{n}(\Omega);{\mathbb{C}}\big)\;\bigg\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}}

of linear maps that satisfy the following properties:

(FER)

The correlation functions of the ground fermions are

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}(z_{1})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w_{1})\cdots\boldsymbol{\xi}(z_{n})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}(w_{n})\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\,=\,(4\pi)^{n}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\textnormal{sgn}(\sigma)\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{G}_{\Omega}\big(z_{i},w_{\sigma(i)}\big)

for
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},w_{1}\ldots,w_{n})\in\textnormal{Conf}_{2n}(\Omega)
,
they vanish when the number of

\boldsymbol{\xi}

\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big[{\mathsf{L}}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\big](z)\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}\;

\displaystyle=\;\Big\langle\big[{\upeta}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\big](z)\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}-\Big\langle\big[{\upchi}_{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}\big](z)\Big\rangle_{\Omega;\alpha}
