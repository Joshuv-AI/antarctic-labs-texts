Co-designing recommendations to improve adolescent and young adult healthcare in Queensland
Authors: Brianna McCoola, Clare Thomas, Rachael Beswick, Anja Christoffersen, Heidi Atkins, Lucy Holland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis research aimed to identify and co-design recommendations to optimise adolescent and young adult (AYA) healthcare through the development of a statewide strategy.MethodsAn exploratory mixed methods co-design constructionist epistemological framework was used to identify recommendations. Participants comprised young people (15–25 years of age) with experience accessing healthcare for acute or chronic concerns, carers and professionals. Focus groups and a statewide survey were conducted concurrently.ResultsEighty-one individuals varying in location and fields across Queensland participated in nine focus groups. Themes and subthemes were successfully identified through the thematic analysis of the focus groups. Within the context of barriers and enablers, participants identified gaps and proposed recommendations to optimise care. Across the nine focus groups, consistent themes emerged, demonstrating sufficient data collection and saturation of responses. Additionally, 107 responses were collected, scored and ranked through the statewide survey prioritising predetermined recommendations to optimise AYA care, based on scoping work completed in 2020. Of the 57 recommendations presented to survey participants, 41 (71.9%) of these were classified as ‘needed’ and ‘very important’ to optimise AYA healthcare. Collective thematic analysis resulted in a list of prioritised recommendations to improve healthcare services for AYAs.ConclusionThe research highlighted two central priorities: overcoming systemic challenges within AYA healthcare and establishing a clear, consistent definition and model of quality care in Queensland. The combined findings strongly support the urgent need to embed standardised principles, approaches and practices across the health system to ensure equitable and effective care for all young people.Keywords: Healthcare quality improvement, Patient-centred care, Adolescent, Focus Groups, Patient Advocacy

Introduction

The contemporary understanding of adolescence defines it as a developmental stage spanning from puberty to approximately 25 years of age.1 Adolescents represent a distinct cohort in healthcare due to their biopsychosocial development and unique healthcare needs. Accordingly, substantial evidence supports the need for developmentally tailored healthcare approaches that optimise acute and preventative interventions, enhance engagement and promote optimal health and developmental outcomes.2,11

However, the delivery of healthcare for this cohort remains complex. In contrast to the more structured models of paediatric care, healthcare for adolescents in Australia is fragmented, spanning multiple service settings—including tertiary, community and primary care—across metropolitan, regional and rural locations. This fragmentation is further compounded by the division of services into paediatric or adult care streams.37 12,14 Within Queensland, Australia’s largest state by area, public health services are delivered through 16 Hospitals and Health Services (HHS) encompassing 122 public hospitals and 109 private hospitals. Alongside numerous primary and community health services, these facilities collectively provide healthcare to approximately one million adolescents aged 10–25 years.15 Young people may also interact with many other intricate systems, including schools, the youth justice system, social services and other non-government organisations.

Within Australia, significant improvements in quality care have been made in some health fields. Young people 15–25 years with cancer now benefit from specialised youth cancer services.16 17 Youth mental health services have been developed in recognition of the specific risk to mental health that accompanies these years.7 18 19 In Queensland, the Mater Private Hospital opened the Mater Young Adult Health Centre in 2015 and provides specialist services for 16–25-year olds with chronic illness, perinatal care, emotional support and alcohol and other drug services.20 Primary care services have evolved to consider more youth-friendly practices, although these are far from consistent.21,24 Finally, specific transition programmes from paediatric to adult health services exist in certain fields.25,28 While these demonstrate significant progress in improving healthcare provision to this cohort, they are the exception rather than the rule, particularly within public health service delivery in Queensland. Largely, care for young people is delivered within either paediatric or adult services, neither designed to meet the cohorts specific developmental or psychosocial health needs.3 7 12 14

Accordingly, challenges for professionals in delivering quality care to young people are well reported.3 29 Young people and their families often report negative, disempowering experiences and feelings of alienation when receiving healthcare in both paediatric and adult settings.330,32 The net effect of poorly managed care includes significant impacts to physical and mental health, and quality of life outcomes; increased indigenous and vulnerable youth health inequality, disempowerment for healthcare professionals, young people and families alike; healthcare disengagement and the impacted development of health self-management capabilities; higher hospitalisation rates and increased health system burden and cost.2 3 33 Within Australia, the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the vulnerability of this cohort with increasing emergency department presentations for mental health concerns, increasing admissions for eating disorders and overwhelming demand for youth-specific services.34,36

The Lancet Commission (2016) clearly outlined the rationale and objectives for delivering quality adolescent care.3 Consistency and continuity in developmentally-informed approaches are essential. Health professionals and services have a responsibility to address young people’s health needs while supporting their developing self-management capabilities and achieve normative developmental milestones.3 Only then can both developmental and health outcomes be optimised.

Specific aims

In 2020, the Queensland Department of Health identified the need to recognise, enhance and optimise healthcare for young people as a health system priority. This cohort was formally defined as adolescents and young adults (AYA), encompassing young people 12–25 years of age.37 Despite the international and national evidence base in quality AYA healthcare,1238,42 there has been a paucity of research and health policy published in the context of the Queensland healthcare environment specific to improving the quality and safety of AYA healthcare. This research was undertaken to inform this endeavour. The aim was to identify and co-design recommendations to optimise AYA healthcare within Queensland, informed by consumer and clinician lived expertise through a process of co-design. This paper presents the methodology and results from phases 1 and 2 of a five-phase co-design process to develop an AYA strategy for Queensland Health (figure 1).

Methods

An exploratory mixed methods approach was used within a co-design constructionist epistemological framework43 to identify and prioritise recommendations to optimise AYA healthcare for Queensland. A co-design approach is recommended for the development of novel healthcare models, aligning with the Australian Safety and Quality Healthcare Standards that prioritise consumer partnership in healthcare design, delivery and evaluation.44,46

Context

The protocol for this study was developed by the authors, with a mix of clinical, consumer and academic experience in paediatric and AYA healthcare. The work was undertaken by the Queensland Child and Youth Clinical Network (QCYCN) as 1 of 23 clinical networks of the Department of Health that aim to improve the quality and efficiency of healthcare in Queensland. It was guided by the QCYCN AYA subnetwork, which was established in 2021 and is a statewide collaborative comprising multidisciplinary clinicians and consumers from various sectors and services that aim to improve AYA care. The work was funded by Clinical Excellence Queensland, Department of Health.

Patient and public involvement

Three patient consumer members of the QCYCN were involved in the design, conduct, reporting and dissemination of recommendations of this research project. Patient consumer AC was also a member of the research team, contributing as a coauthor on this manuscript.

Participants

Participants comprised patient and public young people, carers and health service professionals. AYA participants were 15–25 years of age who had lived experience of acute or chronic physical or mental health conditions and interfaced with health services across either tertiary, primary or community health sectors. Carer participants were those who were currently or had previously been primary carers for a young person aged 15–25 years with a chronic physical or mental health condition. Professional participants included healthcare professionals, health executives, academics and education professionals with expertise in AYA health from various disciplines and fields of expertise across services in Queensland. Exclusion criteria for all groups included an inability to give informed consent and understand English.

Interventions

This research was undertaken with the use of two research data collection methodologies conducted concurrently. The first explored current experiences of providing and experiencing healthcare, and the factors required to optimise care, through qualitative focus groups. Online focus groups were chosen as the data collection method to leverage the benefits of collective discussion while ensuring safe, statewide participation through an accessible digital platform during the COVID-19 pandemic. Concurrently, a statewide online survey sought to gain data from a broader stakeholder group.

Focus groups

Online focus groups were determined as the most appropriate methodology to capture a range of experiences from both consumer and professional perspectives and enable deep exploration of their shared experience, barriers to care, facilitators of care and recommendations to optimise AYA care.46,48 A request detailing the study’s purpose and requirements was emailed to potential participants via QCYCN contacts. Interested individuals contacted the research team and received written information and a consent form before a time was arranged for participation. In February 2022, each focus group was conducted in a conversational style, guided by a semistructured guide derived from preliminary scoping work undertaken by QCYCN in 2020 (online supplemental table 1). Online focus groups, where all participants attending were able to see and hear other participants, were facilitated by authors LH and BM. The consumer focus groups were additionally facilitated by author AC, who is an AYA consumer expert, experienced in group facilitation and co-design. All consumer participants were remunerated with a $30 AUD gift card for their time. Each focus group was audio-recorded and lasted three hours. On completion, all participants were offered the opportunity to complete a written evaluation to assess the focus groups’ efficacy and quality.

Survey

The online mixed-methods survey was administered statewide to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of both consumer and professional experiences, along with quantitative data that highlighted key factors essential for optimising AYA healthcare. Non-identifiable demographic and experience-based data encompassing gender, age, participant roles (consumer or professional experts), years of experience in the health system or AYA care, sector expertise and specialised fields within healthcare were collected. Fifty-seven recommendations needed to optimise healthcare were then examined across several domains, informed by scoping work undertaken by QCYCN and their AYA Subnetwork in 2020. The domains examined included: (1) the provision of AYA healthcare; (2) systemic factors and advocacy; (3) data; (4) funding; (5) resources and education; (6) consumer engagement; (7) collaboration and (8) research. The survey was administered via REDCap and took approximately 20 min to complete. An email detailing the study’s objectives and requirements was sent out via contacts of QCYCN. The survey was open for six weeks from mid-February 2022. All participants self-consented, with prompts for young people to confirm they were over the age of 15 years. Parental consent was required for those aged 15–18 years.

Data analysis

Focus groups

Audio-recorded qualitative data from focus groups was transcribed, de-identified and thematically analysed by LH, BM and AC. The final coding structure, themes and subthemes were determined through consensus among the project team. Quantitative survey data was imported into SPSS V.26.0 for organisation and analysis. All quantitative data was analysed descriptively. Qualitative and quantitative results evaluating the participant’s perception of the focus group efficacy and quality were analysed descriptively.

Survey

Survey participants were asked to determine if the recommendation listed ‘is needed to optimise AYA care’ and rank the importance of the recommendation using a 3-point Likert scale. Results from the surveys were scored, and the scores were summed for each item, with the average score for each item calculated. Summed item scores were then ranked to determine the priority factors required to optimise care. Survey questions scoring above 90% for the statement ‘this is needed to optimise AYA care’ and rated as ‘very important’ by more than 50% of participants were classified as the priority factors and recommendations needed to optimise AYA healthcare in Queensland.

Ethics

Project approval was granted by the Children’s Health Queensland Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee who deemed this quality improvement research project met the Low Risk requirements of the National Health and Medical Research Council’s National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007), and all subsequent updates, the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research (2007) and the CPMP/ICH Note for Guidance on Good Clinical Practice.

Results

Focus groups

A total of 81 individuals participated in nine focus groups held in February 2022. Consumer participants (n=13, 16%) comprised nine young people and four carers. Professional participants (n=68, 84%) comprised 58 multidisciplinary healthcare professionals and 10 healthcare executives. Professional participants were representative of both metropolitan (n=32) and regional or remote (n=30) HHS. They encompassed a range of professional fields, including acute health, mental health, community health, education and primary care services.

Within the context of barriers and enablers, focus group participants identified gaps and proposed recommendations to optimise care. Across nine focus groups, consistent themes emerged, demonstrating sufficient data collection and saturation of responses. Two major themes were successfully identified through the thematic analysis: recommendations to address systemic challenges and recommendations to promote quality AYA healthcare. Participant focus group evaluation

Thirty-three participants completed a written evaluation assessing the efficacy and quality of the co-design methodology used in the focus groups to achieve the primary aim. This included 13 young people or carers and 20 professionals. Thirty-two participants (97%) reported that the sessions were effective in identifying recommendations to optimise healthcare for AYAs in Queensland. All participants (n=33, 100%) indicated that the 3-hour duration was appropriate and that they felt well-engaged. Additional commentary further supported the efficacy and quality of the focus groups.

”I don't think anything was missing. I really liked the structure of the session and the prompts. I also appreciated how small the group was, so we all got many chances to speak.”

”Thank you so much for providing such a well-thought-out and supportive space for us to tell our stories; this is the first research I've ever participated in that I felt heard and hopeful for real change! Focus groups were such a great way to capture this info as we as participants kind of sparked ideas off from each other, and it ended up being a very honest and passionate discussion.”

”Thank you so much for doing this. I found this experience so incredibly validating, cathartic, and inspiring. I have never had anyone ask me those questions, and it meant everything to be asked and then listened to. It also gave me hope that I have never had before.”

Survey

Of the 107 participants who responded to the survey, 16 (15.5%) were young people aged 15–25 years, 7 (6.8%) were parents or carers, 76 (73.8%) were healthcare professionals, 3 (2.9%) were health services executives, 1 (1.0%) was an education professional. Four participants did not complete the survey and were excluded from the analysis. The professionals who completed the survey had experience across a range of fields of practice, and 60 (75%) worked with children (0–14 years) and 66 (82%) worked with adolescents (15–17 years), demonstrating strong participation from the paediatric sector. Most professionals worked with AYAs frequently reporting engagement every day (n=33, 40.7%), every week (n=32, 39.5%) or every month (n=9, 11.1%).

Of the 57 recommendations provided to participants under the eight domains, 41 recommendations (71.9%) were supported as ‘needed’ and ‘very important’ to optimise AYA healthcare in Queensland. These supported recommendations are provided in table 3, with the top 15 ranked factors overall highlighted bold in the table. Response rates throughout the survey ranged from 85% to 87.5%. Collective results support embedding standardised AYA healthcare principles, approaches and practices within our health system as a priority.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify recommendations to optimise AYA healthcare for Queensland. Using a mixed-methods approach—focus groups and a statewide online survey—we explored the lived experiences and insights of both consumers and healthcare professional.

Key findings and interpretation

The focus group identified significant barriers and enablers to AYA healthcare, highlighting gaps in care and their tangible impacts. Despite these challenges, participants identified several actionable recommendations to optimise care for this unique cohort. The discussions underscored the complexity of implementing change across diverse health environments and emphasised the importance of this work for young people, their families and the professionals that support them.

Interpretation

Thematic analysis revealed two major needs: addressing systemic challenges in AYA healthcare and defining, standardising and promoting quality care. These themes were strongly supported by the survey findings. Participants advocated for embedding consistent principles, approaches and practices into the health system to ensure equitable, high-quality care for all young people, regardless of how or where they access services.

Recommendations included the development of standardised models and practice frameworks for AYA healthcare, along with the creation of referral pathways and dedicated services within hospital. A statewide multidisciplinary consultation-liaison service was also proposed as well as enhancements to GP and school-based healthcare. There was a strong emphasis on providing targeted support for young people facing access inequities and building a competent workforce through education, training and supervision. Empowering young people and families through accessible health education and promotion was also seen as essential.

The survey reinforced urgency of these needs and highlighted the importance of system-level frameworks to support implementation. Additional priorities included improving data capture, funding structures, information sharing, cross-sector collaboration, consumer engagement in research and establishing clear definitions of AYA healthcare.

The mixed-methods design enriched the research by capturing diverse perspectives and enabling the prioritisation of recommendations. The saturation of qualitative data confirmed the robustness of the findings. This approach also facilitated the rapid translation of research into policy—particularly valuable for amplifying the consumer voice in health system reform.

Strengths

The study benefited from its timing, following the 2020 Queensland Department of Health meeting and the 2021 establishment of the AYA Subnetwork. This context allowed researchers to draw on clinical expertise and collaborate with committed partners to accelerate uptake and implementation.

A core strength of the study was its commitment to co-design and consumer engagement. Consumers were involved in co-planning, co-designing, co-facilitating and co-disseminating the research. However, engagement was uneven: consumers and carers represented only a small proportion of survey responses and focus group data. To address this imbalance, consumers’ responses were flagged during the analysis to ensure their visibility in the final recommendations. Encouragingly, alignment between professional and consumer perspectives helped mitigate concerns about bias.

Limitations

Several limitations were identified. Low consumer participants may have been influenced by COVID-19 pandemic disruptions and competing priorities. Although the study design was adapted to an online format to enable safe and accessible statewide participation, it is acknowledged that many young people and carers may have had many competing priorities during this period. Age restrictions excluded adolescents under 15 years of age, and recruitment through QCYCN contacts may have contributed to sampling bias, limiting geographic diversity. Time constraints, driven by funding, restricted the study to 1 year and prevented sequential data collection, such as conducting the survey after focus groups.

The findings informed the Optimising Adolescent and Young Adult Care in Queensland: A Statewide Strategy 2022–2027, which was endorsed by key Queensland Health leaders in 2023.13 The study’s recommendations align with national and international frameworks, including those from New South Wales Health, Victoria Health and the National Action Plan for Child and Youth Health. These frameworks advocate for comprehensive AYA services, empowerment of young people as active partners in their healthcare journey, the integration of consumer engagement, the addressing of safety concerns, investment in AYA research, advocating for accessible health services and education-focused empowerment initiatives.12 40 42 Globally, the need for empowerment through education; integration and partnership; appropriate AYA health services; competent AYA workforce; safe and accessible health services; equitable and non-discriminatory services; data; and participation and engagement are also well recognised.38 The alignment of themes identified in existing literature with the findings of this study is encouraging. This study advances the field by offering detailed, context-specific recommendations for Queensland and identifying priority areas for action.

Conclusion

This research prioritised co-design and consumer engagement, ensuring that both consumer and professional lived experiences were central to shaping recommendations for optimising AYA healthcare in Queensland. Thematic analysis of the focus groups’ revealed two overarching priorities: addressing systemic challenges in AYA healthcare and establishing a clear, standardised definition and model of care. These themes were echoed in the statewide survey, reinforcing the need to embed consistent principles, approaches and practices across the health system as a priority.

The use of a mixed-method approach significantly strengthened the study, enabling rich, diverse input from consumers and professionals. Collectively, these findings provide a clear roadmap to optimising AYA healthcare for all young people, identifying specific areas of priority for focus in the context of the Queensland health system.
