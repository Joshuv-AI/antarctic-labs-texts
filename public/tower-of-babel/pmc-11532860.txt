Effect of energy density on down surface characteristics of AlSi10Mg alloy fabricated via selective laser melting
Authors: Cheol Kang, Gun-Hee Kim, Won Rae Kim, Taeg Woo Lee, Hyun Soo Kang, Min Ji Ham, Seon-Jin Choi, Hye Yoon Choi, Ki-Seung Kim, Hyo Tae Kim, Hyung Giun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The correlation between surface roughness and energy density in the down surface area of AlSi10Mg alloy manufactured by selective laser melting was analyzed. This study investigated the relationship between the contour melt pool shape and surface roughness in the down surface area across an energy density range of 10–150 J/mm³. As the energy density increased, the contour melt pool in the down surface area became more stable, which significantly influenced surface roughness. Low energy density resulted in the unstable formation of the contour melt pool, leading to a deterioration in surface quality, whereas high energy density promoted the stable formation of the melt pool. Sufficient energy density is essential for the complete formation of the contour melt pool on the down surface, which plays a crucial role in reducing surface roughness. However, within the energy density range where the contour melt pool is fully formed, keyhole defects may occur, and it can be anticipated that these defects may worsen at energy densities exceeding the critical threshold.

Introduction

Aluminum alloys have high specific strengths, machinability, corrosion resistances, and electrical and thermal conductivities; hence, they are widely used in various applications, including aerospace, military engineering, and automobiles [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Currently, the market demands high-dimensional precision and complex components with diverse designs. However, the majority of Al alloy components are manufactured using traditional processes such as casting, forging, and extrusion. This results in high processing costs, prolonged production periods, and challenges in the fabrication of intricate shapes [6]. Selective laser melting (SLM) technology has attracted significant attention as a possible solution to this problem. SLM is a powder bed fusion (PBF) additive manufacturing (AM) process that utilizes a laser as a heat source to selectively melt metal powders, enabling precise layering required in AM. This technique is highly suitable for producing complex shapes with high precision [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. The SLM process is gaining recognition across various fields because of its ability to implement complex shapes through the selective melting of metal powders by adjusting the power and scan speed of the laser. It has attracted considerable attention for its ability to improve product's quality, leading to extensive studies in design, post-processing, and process optimization [[12], [13], [14]].

However, the SLM process suffers from the low dimensional precision and product surface quality. The interaction between the laser and metal powder results in laser beam scattering and localized differences in energy absorption rates, making it difficult to control surface roughness. High surface roughness concentrates stress and significantly affects the product lifetime, necessitating additional post-processing steps [[15], [16], [17]]. Mechanical post-processing methods, such as micro-blasting, are not always suitable for processing intricate internal features of complex shapes. In contrast, electrochemical post-processing can be performed for the entire product. However, this method involves the use of suitable chemical solutions depending on the type of the metal used. Additionally, the time required for the reaction between the product and solution along with the necessity of replacing the solution based on the reaction incur additional costs [[18], [19], [20]]. Therefore, various studies have been conducted to improve the surface quality of SLM. Among the product surfaces laminated via the SLM process, the down surface area is affected by laser beam scattering generating dross. The residual heat remaining after the metal powder is melted is transferred to the powder, promoting its surface attachment and increasing the surface roughness [[21], [22], [23]]. To solve this problem, the surface of the stacked area has been divided into the top, down, side, and upper parts. Each surface is classified based on the outline of its outermost region and filling of the inner region. Subsequently, different scanning strategies and energy density conditions are applied to each region. Different criteria and nomenclatures are used by different manufacturers to describe these areas [[24], [25], [26]]. However, predicting changes in the melt-pool formation and surface roughness based on the energy density owing to variations in the energy absorption rate caused by laser beam scattering in the contour area is a challenging task. Additionally, no previous studies on the correlation between the melt pool morphology and optical properties of the down surface area at different energy densities have been performed [27,28]. Therefore, it is essential to examine the surface roughness of each material as a function of the energy density and establish its correlation with the melt pool morphology in the contour area. In this study, we investigated the melt pool morphologies of the contour in the down surface area of the AlSi10Mg alloy at different energy densities using the laser power and scan speed as parameters. Its aim was to explore the relationship between the surface roughness and melt-pool morphologies of the contour.

Experimental

In this study, an M2 Series (GE) instrument and AlSi10Mg alloy powder produced via plasma atomization were used to study alloy properties at various energy densities. Basic powder characteristics were analyzed using a Mastersizer 3000 EV particle size analyzer (Malvern) and Quanta 250 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FEI). The down surface contour parameters for the SLM process of the AlSi10Mg alloy were designed based on the commercial energy density condition of 49.29 J/mm³ provided by GE. A 6x6 energy density matrix was designed for the experiment. As shown in Fig. 1(c), the power was set in the range of 125∼375W and scan speed was set in the range of 380∼1200 mm/s. The power and scan speed corresponding to each energy density are indicated by gray dots. The hatch distance and layer thickness were fixed at 75 μm and 40 μm, respectively. The specimen geometry was designed with dimensions of 15 × 15 × 20 mm and a 45° slope, as shown in Fig. 1(d), to measure changes in down surface roughness.

A roughness analysis of the down surfaces of AlSi10Mg alloy specimens was conducted by measuring their Ra (μm) values using an SV-3200 instrument (Mitutoyo).

The microstructural properties of the down surfaces of the AlSi10Mg alloy specimens were examined using an optical microscope VHX-5000 (Keyence). For this purpose, they were cut to observe planes parallel to the build direction. Mechanical grinding was performed using SiC paper with grits ranging from 200 to 2000. Subsequently, polishing was conducted using a 1-μm diamond suspension followed by polishing with an OPS solution.

Chemical etching was performed to observe the melt pool of the contour in the down surface area. The etching solution was prepared by mixing 2 mL HF, 3 mL HCl, and 5 mL HNO3 in 90 mL distilled water. Etching was conducted for approximately 20–30 s per specimen followed by rinsing with distilled water and drying.

Results and discussion

SLM is a PBF technique that selectively melts metal powders based on a designed model using a laser heat source, which results in a layered structure. In the internal area of the stacked specimen (known as an infill), the heat remaining from the formation of the melt pool is transferred to create a solid bulk from the previous layer. Fig. 2 shows that the underlying surface area is affected by the laser as it is contoured with only the powder present without a preexisting bulk. During this process, the formation of the molten pool becomes unstable owing to the energy absorption changes caused by multiple reflections of the powder. Additionally, because the thermal conductivity of the powder is lower than that of the bulk, the energy absorption is uneven, leading to the formation of dross. Note that residual heat is produced not only in the bulk but also in the powder, which promotes its surface attachment and increases the surface roughness [[29], [30], [31]].Fig. 2Schematic illustration of the melt pool formation and heat conduction in the infill and contour areas during SLM.Fig. 2

The roughness of the lower surfaces of the specimens produced during the SLM of the AlSi10Mg alloy was measured at various energy densities. As shown in Fig. 3, the surface roughness tends to decrease with increasing energy density forming specific energy density ranges. The surface roughness decreases in the energy density range from 0 to 25 J/mm3. In the energy density range of 25–70 J/mm3, the surface roughness fluctuates regardless of the energy density. The down surface area experiences fluctuations in the calculated absorption rate up to 20 % owing to laser beam scattering and absorption by the powder [33]. Therefore, it is essential to observe the melt pool morphology in the contour area with respect to energy density and to establish the correlation between surface roughness (Ra) and energy density [29].Fig. 4(a)–(d) show the melt pool morphologies observed in the contour area of the down surface at various energy densities through cross-sectional analysis. Representative optical microscopy (OM) images illustrate the correlation between the melt pool morphology and roughness (Ra) in the contour area established at various energy densities. In Fig. 4(a), the surface roughness measured at an energy density of 16.43 J/mm³ is equal to 32.1 (2.7) μm. The contour area on the down surface did not form a melt pool owing to the low energy density, at which the powder did not melt. However, dross was formed in the areas with high energy absorption rates due to laser beam scattering and absorption [34]. In Fig. 4(b), the surface roughness measured at an energy density of 49.29 J/mm³ is 27.8 (1.7) μm. In the contour area of the down surface, the powder melted; however, the energy density was insufficient for the stable formation of the melt pool. Owing to laser beam scattering and absorption, micro-sized grain structures formed in some areas, adversely affecting surface roughness. In Fig. 4(c) shows that the surface roughness measured at an energy density of 69.01 J/mm³ is 22.3 (2.2) μm. As the energy density increased, a melt pool formed in the contour area of the down surface; however, the melt pool shape and melt pool boundary were observed to be irregularly deposited. In Fig. 4(d), the surface roughness measured at an energy density of 108.44 J/mm³ amounts to 9.7 (1.3) μm. In the contour region of the down surface, melt pools formed regularly, and the distance between the melt-pool boundaries was constant. The stable formation of the melt pools decreased the surface roughness. Fig. 4(e)–(h) schematically illustrate the melt pool formation in the contour area at different energy densities. The morphology of a single melt pool based on the applied energy density is indicated by the red line. In Fig. 4(e), the morphology of a single melt pool at an energy density of 16.43 J/mm³ is depicted on the down surface. The low energy density resulted in a small melt-pool size, and a significant portion of the powder remained unmelted within the melt-pool boundary. In certain localized regions, uneven energy absorption occurred due to light scattering, leading to the formation of dross. Fig. 4(f) shows that at an energy density of 49.29 J/mm³, as the energy density increases, the area of the molten powder increases. However, it remains insufficient for the stable formation of the melt pool. In some areas with increased absorption rates, micro-sized grain structures grew, adversely affecting surface roughness. According to Fig. 4(g), at an energy density of 69.01 J/mm³, the melt pool is formed in the contour. However, the boundary between the powder and the contour melt pool contains unmelted powder, resulting in an irregular morphology. Therefore, in Fig. 4(h), at an energy density of 108.44 J/mm³, the contour melt pool is fully formed, and the melt pool boundaries are uniformly deposited, resulting in a substantial reduction in surface roughness. However, due to the high energy density, keyhole defects were observed under these conditions [35,36]. As a result, the mechanism governing surface roughness dependence on energy density in AlSi10Mg alloy has been identified. At sufficiently high energy densities, the contour melt pool is fully formed, leading to a reduction in surface roughness; however, this phenomenon is accompanied by the formation of keyhole defects. This suggests that there exists a critical energy density range for complete melt pool formation, which overlaps with the range where keyhole defects occur. Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the roughness of the down surface of the AlSi10Mg alloy fabricated via SLM by comparing the contour melt pool morphologies obtained at different energy densities.

The alloy exhibited a high surface roughness at a low energy density, and as the energy density increased, the surface roughness was variably distributed. The contour melt pool was fully formed, resulting in a reduction in surface roughness. Furthermore, as the energy density increased, the contour melt pool became more stable, which further contributed to the reduction of surface roughness. However, at higher energy densities, keyhole defects were observed.

This suggests that a critical energy density range exists for complete melt pool formation, which overlaps with the range where keyhole defects occur. Furthermore, if the energy density exceeds this critical range, keyhole defects may intensify, potentially leading to a degradation in product properties. Therefore, in SLM processes conducted at high energy densities, it is necessary to optimize the environment, and energy density to prevent the formation of keyhole defects.
