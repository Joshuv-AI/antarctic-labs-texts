T. Dex Bhadra, J. R. Lu, Natasha S. Abrams, Andrew Scharf, Edward Broadberry, Casey Lam, Macy J. Huston

Abstract
Gravitational microlensing is a powerful tool that can be used to find and measure the mass of isolated and dark compact objects. In many microlensing events, the lens, the source, or both may be a binary system. Therefore, in this study we present lensing equations for binary source and lens models in the Bayesian Analysis of Gravitational Lensing Events (BAGLE) Python microlensing package. The new binary source and lens models in BAGLE account for the complete Keplerian orbit. BAGLE also includes binary models that approximate the orbital motion as linear or accelerating motion of the secondary companion; these are useful when the orbit has a very low eccentricity or the orbital period is much longer than the microlensing timescale. The model parameterizations based on these binary lensing equations will enable joint fitting of photometric and astrometric data sets. Consequently, binary microlensing events with complex astrometric trajectories can be used to break several microlensing degeneracies that plague photometry-only microlensing modeling. These binary models will be used to fit microlensing event data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, the Nancy Grace Roman Telescope, and other surveys.

I
Introduction

Gravitational microlensing occurs in the Milky Way when a foreground object with mass (e.g., a star, black hole, or planet) passes in front of a background source star and the mass of the foreground lens temporarily magnifies and perturbs the observed position of the background source.
Microlensing is detectable even when the foreground lens is dark or too faint to observe, making it a powerful tool for probing cool, distant, and/or compact objects. It is one of the only methods for measuring the mass of isolated and dark black holes
(
C. Y. Lam et al. 2022
;
K. C. Sahu et al. 2022
;
P. Mróz et al. 2022
;
C. Y. Lam & J. R. Lu 2023
;
K. C. Sahu et al. 2025
)
, free-floating or widely separated low-mass exoplanets
(
B. S. Gaudi 2012
)
, and white dwarfs
(
K. C. Sahu et al. 2017
;
P. McGill et al. 2023
)
. Additionally, over 200 exoplanets orbiting their host stars have been detected with microlensing
(
P. Mróz & R. Poleski 2024
)
.

Microlensing is sensitive to both close and wide separation binaries, unlike radial velocity and transit probes, which are more sensitive to closely separated binaries or planet+star systems.
Recent simulations of Milky Way microlensing surveys show that 55% of observed microlensing events involve a binary star system
(
N. S. Abrams et al. 2025
)
, so robust binary microlensing models are even more important than previously thought.
Some microlensing events have timescales greater than the orbital period of the involved binary system. In these cases, photometric lightcurves and astrometric trajectories can only be properly modeled by accounting for the system’s orbital dynamics.

A detailed comparison of these packages for point-source, point-lens models is presented in

II
Binary Lens Geometries

BAGLE v1.0.1 and later support binary lens and binary source geometries as shown in different columns of Figure
1

The simplest case of a binary model in BAGLE involves the binary system moving with a fixed angular separation between the primary and secondary objects. This works under the assumption that the period of the orbits is much larger than the duration of the microlensing. For longer-duration events, the secondary companion moves with respect to the primary.

BAGLE provides models for the secondary companion’s motion that is linear, accelerating, or orbiting along a Keplerian trajectory (either circular or elliptical) as shown in the different rows of Figure

III
Binary Orbital Motion in BAGLE

Figure 2:
Trajectory of a binary orbit at
\textit{i}=0\degree
(binary disk is face-on) simulated using BAGLE for stationary proper motion. The orbit has an eccentricity of 0.8. The primary and secondary objects at the time of periastron passage have been highlighted.

BAGLE is capable of modeling microlensing events using physical parameters such as the mass of the lens, the distance to the lens, flux, sky position, and proper motion of the lens and the source star. In order to support binary companions to the lens, the source, or both, we introduce many new parameters to describe the mass ratios, flux ratios, and orbital parameters for each binary system.

To model the primary and secondary motion around the center of mass, the following Keplerian orbital parameters are used:

•

\omega_{pri}
: The argument of periastron of the primary object’s orbit in degrees. The secondary companion is placed
180\degree
across the primary’s argument of periastron.

•

i
: Inclination angle of the system in degrees. The primary and secondary objects share the same inclination angle. An inclination of
0\degree
means that the system is face-on.

•

\Omega_{sec}
: The longitude of the ascending node of the secondary companion’s orbit in degrees.

•

e
: Eccentricity of the Keplerian orbit. For circular orbits, this is fixed to 0.

•

P
: The orbital period of the binary system in days.

•

t_{p}
: The time of the periastron of the system in days.

•

\aleph_{pri}
: The projected semi-major axis of the primary object in mas.

•

\aleph_{sec}
: The projected semi-major axis of the secondary object in mas.

Note that not all of the Keplerian elements are required as inputs to BAGLE models. Depending on the nature of the binary object (i.e., whether it is a binary source or a lens) and the parameterization used, the input microlensing parameters vary.
The reference direction is the North direction in the plane-of-sky. Hence,
\Omega_{sec}
is recorded Eastward of North, and
\omega_{pri}
is Eastward of
\Omega_{sec}+180\degree
.

In BAGLE, the eight Keplerian elements presented above are used to estimate the Thiele-Innes constants as described in
S. C. Koren et al. 2016
,
T. N. Thiele 1883
and presented, in detail, in Appendix
A
. Using these constants, the positions of the primary and the secondary companion over time are computed. Let
\boldsymbol{X(t)}_{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{com},\sun}
be the primary object’s projected trajectory (on the sky plane) around the center of mass, with
{X(t)}_{{p,{com},E},\sun}
and
{X(t)}_{{p,{com},N},\sun}
being the East and North components, respectively. Let
\boldsymbol{X(t)}_{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{com},\sun}
be the secondary companion’s trajectory around the center of mass with
{X(t)}_{{s,{com},E},\sun}
and
{X(t)}_{{s,{com},N},\sun}
representing the East and North components, respectively. Then, the following set of equations holds:

\displaystyle{X(t)}_{{p,{com},E},\sun}

\displaystyle=X(t)B_{pri}+Y(t)G_{pri}

\displaystyle{X(t)}_{{p,{com},N},\sun}

\displaystyle=X(t)A_{pri}+Y(t)F_{pri}

\displaystyle{X(t)}_{{s,{com},E},\sun}

\displaystyle=X(t)B_{sec}+Y(t)G_{sec}

\displaystyle{X(t)}_{{s,{com},N},\sun}

\displaystyle=X(t)A_{sec}+Y(t)F_{sec}

(1)

where
X(t)
and
Y(t)
are the rectangular coordinates of the binary system;
A_{pri}
,
A_{sec}
,
B_{pri}
,
B_{sec}
,
G_{pri}
,
G_{sec}
,
F_{pri}
and
F_{sec}
are the Thiele-Innes constants.

Using Eqn
III
, BAGLE can model circular and elliptical orbital trajectories around the center of mass. An instance of a Keplerian elliptical orbital trajectory (eccentricity of 0.8) with stationary proper motion is shown in Figure
2
. Note that the equations in this section describe the motion around the center of mass. The proper motion of the center of mass in the observer’s frame of reference is accounted for separately.

IV.1
Static Approximation

In binary-source models, the components of the binary (i.e., the primary source and the secondary source companion) are initially at rest relative to each other. On the plane of the sky, in the Solar-System Barycenter (SSB) frame, at
t_{0,\sun}
, the binary source system initially has a fixed angular separation of:

\boldsymbol{s_{S}}(t_{0,\sun})=\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{s_{S}}(t_{0,\sun})
is the separation vector at
t_{0,\sun}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})
is the initial secondary source position on the sky at
t_{0,\sun}
, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})
is the initial primary source position on the sky at
t_{0,\sun}
. Note, we use the Sun symbol,
\odot
, to indicate SSB coordinates. In the binary-source models,
t_{0,\sun}=t_{prim,0,\sun}
, i.e.,
t_{0,\sun}
is the time of closest approach between the primary source and the lens (
t_{prim,0,\sun}
). The angular separation between the primary and the secondary companion is fixed at all times. In other words, the proper motion of the secondary companion is assumed to be the same as the proper motion of the primary source.

IV.2
Linear and Accelerated Orbital Approximations

In contrast to the static approximation, in both the linear and accelerated orbit models, the primary and the secondary companion move with different proper motions.

For linear approximations, BAGLE inputs a new parameter
\boldsymbol{\Delta\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}
, which is the proper motion of the secondary source relative to the primary source. With time, due to the proper motion of the primary and secondary sources, the separation vector
\boldsymbol{s(t)}
between the sources changes. The secondary source moves linearly relative to the primary source. The positions of the primary and secondary sources are given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p}},\sun}(t)=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p},0},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S},\sun}(t-t_{0,\sun})

\displaystyle+\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t)=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s},0},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t-t_{0,\sun})

\displaystyle+\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S},\sun}
is the proper motion of the primary source, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}
is the proper motion of the secondary source calculated as
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\Delta\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}
. Furthermore, we account for the parallactic motion at time
t
in the direction of the source-lens system
(\alpha,\delta)
, given by the difference of the Earth and Sun’s position, normalized by 1 AU. The parallactic motion is
\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)
where
\pi_{S}
is the
maximum parallax amplitude
1/d_{S}
and
\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)
is the actual parallax direction and fractional amplitude on the sky.

For accelerated approximations, along with
\boldsymbol{\Delta\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}
, BAGLE also inputs
\mathbb{a}_{\boldsymbol{S\mathrm{rel}},\sun}
, which is the acceleration of the secondary source relative to the lens. In the accelerated orbit model, the secondary source moves with constant acceleration relative to the primary source. The positions of the primary and secondary sources are given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p}},\sun}(t)=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p},0},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S},\sun}(t-t_{prim,0,\sun})

\displaystyle+\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t)=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s},0},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}(t-t_{prim,0,\sun})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{a}_{\boldsymbol{S\mathrm{rel}},\sun}[t-t_{prim,0,\sun}]^{2}+\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)

(6)

IV.4
Lensing a binary source

The equations of relative separation between each source and lens, in units of Einstein radii, are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\boldsymbol{0},\sun}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{p}},\sun}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\sun}}{\theta_{E}}

(9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\boldsymbol{0},\sun}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S_{s}},\sun}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\sun}}{\theta_{E}}

(10)

in the heliocentric frame.

If the lensing event could be fully resolved, we would expect to see four lensed images, two for each source.

The amplifications for the images are

\displaystyle A_{p,\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\sun}^{2}+2}{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\sun}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\boldsymbol{0},\sun}^{2}+4}}\pm 1\right)

(12)

\displaystyle A_{s,\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}^{2}+2}{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}^{2}+4}}\pm 1\right)

(13)

where the two images per source are labeled
+
for the major image and
-
for the minor image.

Each source’s intrinsic flux is magnified by its specific amplification factors. The total amplification for each source is

\displaystyle A_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\sun}^{2}+2}{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\sun}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{p,\sun}^{2}+4}}

(14)

\displaystyle A_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}^{2}+2}{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{s,\sun}^{2}+4}}

(15)

We can define a total amplification for the system using

\displaystyle A=\frac{A_{p}F_{p}+A_{s}F_{s}}{F_{p}+F_{s}}

(16)

where
F_{p}
and
F_{s}
are the intrinsic flux of the primary and secondary sources.

The observed flux for the lensed system is then

\displaystyle f_{obs}=(A_{p}F_{p}+A_{s}F_{s})\left(1+\frac{1-b_{sff}}{b_{sff}}\right).

(17)

where
b_{sff}
is the ratio of the source flux to the total flux of the source, neighbors, and the lens.

The lensed astrometry, or the image centroid, is then simply a flux-weighted combination of the lensed astrometry from the two sources.

V.1
Static Approximation

Like binary source models in BAGLE, the simplest implementation of a PSBL model in BAGLE fixes the angular separation between the primary and secondary lens at all times. The two lenses are separated by an initial separation given in Equation
18
.

\boldsymbol{s_{L}}(t_{0,\sun})=\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L_{s}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L_{p}},\sun}(t_{0,\sun})

(18)

For binary lenses,
t_{0,\sun}=t_{geom,0,\sun}
in static, linear and accelerated orbital approximations.
t_{geom,0,\sun}
represents the time of closest approach between the source and the geometric midpoint of the binary lenses. In the static approximation only, the angular separation between the primary and secondary lens is fixed at all times; both lenses move with the same proper motion vector.

V.4
Binary Lens Equation

The binary lens equation
(
P. Schneider & A. Weiss 1986
)
is a mapping of the source position in the “source plane” to image positions in the “lens plane”, or equivalently “image plane”. The equation is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{S}=\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}-m_{1}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{x}_{L1}}{|\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{x}_{L1}|^{2}}-m_{2}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{x}_{L2}}{|\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{x}_{L2}|^{2}}

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{S}
is the angular position of the source (in the source plane),
\boldsymbol{x}_{L1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{L2}
are the angular positions of the lenses (in the lens plane),
\boldsymbol{x}_{obs}
is the observed angular position of the lensed images (in the lens plane), and
m_{i}=\theta_{E,i}^{2}=\frac{4GM_{i}}{c^{2}}(\frac{1}{d_{L}}-\frac{1}{d_{S}})
, where
M_{i}
is the lens mass.

We recast the lens equation in the complex form

\displaystyle w=z-m_{1}\frac{1}{\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{1}}-m_{2}\frac{1}{\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{2}}

(27)

where

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=x_{S,E}+ix_{S,N}

(28)

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{L1,E}+ix_{L1,N}

(29)

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{L2,E}+ix_{L2,N}

(30)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=x_{obs,E}+ix_{obs,N}

(31)

Figure
7
shows how these vectors are projected onto the sky.

Figure 7:

Binary lens geometry projected onto the sky in the complex form.
The binary lens is at
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{2}
. The source star
is at
\boldsymbol{w}
and the direction of the relative proper motion is shown as
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}
.The angle
\alpha
is defined as the angle between North and the binary axis.
\alpha
increments eastwards of North.
\phi_{\pi_{E}}
is the angle East of North of
\hat{\mu}_{rel}
and also
\hat{\pi}_{E}
.

The complex conjugate of Equation
27
is:

\bar{w}=\bar{z}-m_{1}\frac{1}{z-z_{1}}-m_{2}\frac{1}{z-z_{2}}.

(32)

The Jacobian, which describes the transformation from source (
w,\bar{w}
) to lens (
z,\bar{z}
) plane is given by

J=\begin{bmatrix}\partial w/\partial z&\partial w/\partial\bar{z}\\
\partial\bar{w}/\partial z&\partial\bar{w}/\partial\bar{z}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

Differentiating Eqns.
27
and
32
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial\bar{z}}=1

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}

\displaystyle=m_{1}\frac{1}{(z-z_{1})^{2}}+m_{2}\frac{1}{(z-z_{2})^{2}}

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=m_{1}\frac{1}{(\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{1})^{2}}+m_{2}\frac{1}{(\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{2})^{2}}=\overline{\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}}

(36)

which means the determinant of the Jacobian is

\displaystyle|J|

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial\bar{z}}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial\bar{z}}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}

(37)

\displaystyle=1-\left|\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}\right|^{2}.

(38)

We can then find the amplification of the source by the lens as:

A=\frac{1}{|J|}.

(39)

There are places in which
|J|\rightarrow 0
. This corresponds to infinite amplification (if the source were a point). The curves in the lens plane where this is true are “critical curves” and the corresponding curves in the source plane are called “caustics.” In the maps of magnification below (see §
11
), we show the source plane in which we can see caustics. We also show the caustics in Figures
13
and
14
. When the source passes over a caustic, this is known as a “caustic crossing” and changes the number of lensed images of that source from 3 (when the source is outside of the caustic) to 5 (when it is inside the caustic).

To plot the critical curves and caustics, we first solve the equation
0=|J|=1-\left|\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}\right|^{2}
in the lens plane by noting that the solutions correspond exactly to complex numbers
z
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial z}=m_{1}\frac{1}{(z-z_{1})^{2}}+m_{2}\frac{1}{(z-z_{2})^{2}}=e^{i\theta}

(40)

for some
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. Clearing the denominators produces a quartic polynomial in
z
with coefficients depending on
z_{1},z_{2},m_{1},m_{2},
and
\theta
. Points along the critical curve are calculated by solving the four roots of this polynomial for a range of
\theta
values, and the corresponding caustic curve can then be plotted by using the lens equation to map points from the lens plane to the source plane. Examples of critical and caustic curves are shown in Figure
13
and Figure
14
for a range of different binary lens separations and mass ratios.

In the microlensing models added to BAGLE, we parameterize our models such that we know where the source is in the source plane (
w
), where the binary lenses are in the lens plane (
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
), the masses of the two lenses, and the distance to all objects. Therefore, we are solving for the observed position of the source in the lens plane (
z
).

In order to avoid working with an equation with a mix of complex numbers and their conjugates, it is standard to plug Eqn.
32
into Eqn.
27
and simplify
(
H. J. Witt 1990
;
H. J. Witt & S. Mao 1995
, see)
. This yields a fifth-order polynomial known as the “lens polynomial.” This typically yields the same solutions as the “lens equations.”

The pre-instantiated runtime is applicable to running many different sets of model parameters for an event at the same sky location (i.e. model fitting). On the other hand, the full runtime is applicable to large-scale simulations of multiple events located at different sky coordinates. For pre-instantiated runtimes, we find that all packages are quick for BSPL events. BAGLE is the quickest for BSPL model generations when calculating the full runtime. However, BAGLE is the slowest for PSBL model generation (pre-instantiated and full). This is due to BAGLE’s reliance on a polynomial root solver to calculate the images and amplification during a PSBL event. The polynomial root solver is used to find the roots of a fifth-order complex polynomial for each source position and epoch in BAGLE. The root solver enforces higher numerical precision and ensures more stable solutions, including near caustics. However, it is more computationally intensive than the contour-based or grid-based ray-shooting methods used by other codes. Future work involves implementing JAX to improve the efficiency of the root solver.

Lastly, a summary of binary modeling capabilities between different packages is provided in Table
3
. pyLIMA is included in this summary. All four packages support static binary lenses and sources. In terms of orbital motion of binary lenses, all packages can simulate full Keplerian motion or circular “approximations” that are close to the actual solutions. BAGLE can model linear and accelerated approximations, which are computationally resourceful in microlensing events with very long orbital periods. MulensModel can also support simpler approximations with the inputs
\frac{ds}{dt}
(rate of change of binary lens separation) and
\frac{d\alpha}{dt}
(rate of change of the angle between the binary axis and the proper motion vector). In terms of the magnification algorithm used for binary lenses, BAGLE uses a polynomial root solver; VBMicrolensing provides multiple methods that include the polynomial root sovler, the Newton-Raphson method or contour integration; MulensModel provides an adaptive contouring method
(
M. Dominik 2007

In terms of orbital motion for binary sources (often referred to as “xallarap”), all packages support circular orbital motion. MulensModel can handle elliptical orbital motion and BAGLE provides a linear and accelerated approximation too along with full elliptical orbital motion. It is important to re-iterate that pyLIMA and MulensModel implement a photometry-only fitting using their binary lens and source models. RTModel (developed to use VBMicrolensing for fitting events) can support joint photometric and astrometric fitting for all binary models except BSBL. BAGLE can support joint photometric and astrometric fitting of all binary models (i.e., PSPL, PSBL, BSPL, BSBL).

By solving the lens equation, the magnification of the source at any (projected) position relative to the position of the lens can be calculated. The magnification map is a visualization reflecting the calculated magnification at a given point. Magnification maps are generated using the inverse ray shooting method
(

)
. The inverse ray shooting method is a simple way to invert the lens equation by shooting
rays backwards from the observer to the lens.

•

Create a sample grid of image positions in the lens frame of reference.

•

Calculate information about the lens, i.e., the lens positions at the time of closest approach (between the source and the lens system’s center of mass) and the lens mass ratio.

•

Use the lens mass ratio, lens positions, the image grid, and the source position to get the lensed source positions.

•

By mapping the image grid to the source plane and then binning the resulting source positions, the magnification map indicates how many rays fall into each pixel in the source plane grid. More rays imply a higher magnification. The magnification map we present in Figure
11
is purely statistical.

The magnification map for a BSBL event at
t_{p}
is presented in Figure
11
. The large boundaries of amplification in the magnification map are caustics. Caustics are characteristic features of the photometric curves of binary lenses. Since the sources have a finite size, their amplification does not become infinitely high. However, it still presents itself in the form of sharp peaks in our lightcurves.

A magnification map can be generated using a call to the
get_magnification_map
function in
plot_models.py
. The function performs the inverse-ray-shooting method and plots a magnification map at
t_{p}
by default. An arbitrary time can be provided as an argument when calling the function.

Like the magnification map, a forward ray shooting method can be used to shoot from the source plane to the image plane, creating a color map of the absolute maximum centroid shift. This forward ray shooting method works in the following way:

•

Create a sample grid of source positions.

•

Solve the lens equation to get all possible image positions using the sample grid of source positions. Find the flux-weighted centroids of these possible image positions.

•

Bin the flux-weighted centroids to create a color map of how many rays fall into each pixel in the image plane grid. Normalize it by using the flux-weighted centroids on the colorbar.

In this section, we demonstrate the need to account for the orbital motion of binary systems by fitting a mock dataset to various models with orbital motion in BAGLE.

The mock dataset generated is intentionally designed to replicate a BSPL event with a complex lightcurve structure with apparent Keplerian motion. It is generated using BSPL_PhotAstrom_noPar_EllOrbs_Param1 and the parameters presented in Table

In our fitting process, BSPL models with linear, circular, and elliptical orbital motion are utilized to demonstrate how incorporating orbital motion enhances the quality of the fitting process. We present the photometric fitting results in Figure

IX
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce binary models in BAGLE. These binary models account for binary sources, binary lenses or both (with and without orbital motion). Binary models with orbital motion in BAGLE can be divided into four categories: linear, accelerated, circular and elliptical. Models with circular and elliptical motion depend on eight crucial Keplerian elements (
\omega_{pri}
,
\Omega_{sec}
,
i
,
e
,
P
,
t_{p}
,
\aleph_{pri}
, and
\aleph_{sec}
), and are better-suited for microlensing events where
\textit{P}\ll t_{E,\sun}
. On the other hand, models with linear and accelerated motion use fewer free parameters, making them computationally inexpensive and well-suited approximations for microlensing events where
\textit{P}\gg t_{E,\sun}
.

From our fitting procedure using a mock dataset that replicates a binary-source, point-lens event, we conclude that the inclusion of orbital motion in binary microlensing events helps model complex photometric light curves. In these simulations, the accuracy of our binary fits based on
\bar{\chi^{2}}
values improves with orbital motion.

BAGLE’s capabilities for handling point-source, point-lens events are presented in
J. Lu et al. submitted
, where BAGLE was compared with other microlensing packages like VBMicrolensing, pyLIMA, and MulensModel in detail. This paper includes a brief comparison between the different microlensing packages for point-source, binary-lens and binary-source, point-lens events. For point-source, binary-lens events, the residual difference in amplification between VBMicrolensing and BAGLE ranged around
10^{-2}
, as opposed to MulensModel. For binary-source, point-lens events, the residual difference in amplification ranged around
10^{-2}
between all three packages.

BAGLE’s model-fitting runtimes are comparable to VBMicrolensing and MulensModel for pre-instantiated binary-source, point-lens events. A large-scale simulation of multiple events is fastest with BAGLE for binary-source, point-lens events. BAGLE’s dependence on a polynomial root solver affects its runtime (full and pre-instantiated) for point-source, binary-lens calculations. Our future work involves exploring ways to improve the efficiency of the root solver through the use of JAX. We also aim to develop a similar lightcurve comparison for binary-source, binary-lens models, and compare events with orbital motion.

In conclusion, the wide array of models and parameterizations available in BAGLE make it suitable for a joint photometric and astrometric fitting of binary events, including binary-source, binary-lens (BSBL) events. BAGLE’s new binary models will be used to work with data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, the Nancy Grace Roman Telescope, and other surveys. It will be used to better characterize measured microlensing signals of black hole astrometric candidates. These new models, which accurately capture the orbital dynamics of binary systems, will enhance our search for dark lenses, such as black holes, exoplanets, free-floating planets, and other intriguing candidates.

Appendix A
Finding Thiele-Innes Constants

We begin by calculating the mean, eccentric, and true anomalies using the Keplerian orbital parameters. This method is adopted from
S. C. Koren et al. 2016
. The mean anomaly as a function of time
M(t)
is:

\displaystyle\textit{M(t)}=\frac{2\pi}{\textit{P}}(t-t_{p})

(A1)

For circular orbits, the mean anomaly is the same as the true anomaly.

The eccentric anomaly (
E(t)
) can be found using the mean anomaly and the eccentricity of the orbit as follows:

\displaystyle\textit{E(t)}-\textit{e}\sin\textit{E(t)}=\textit{M(t)}

(A2)

The true anomaly
\eta
is found using
M(t)
and
E(t)
.

\eta=2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+\textit{e}}{1-\textit{e}}}\tan{\frac{\textit{E(t)}}{2}}\right)

(A3)

The mean, eccentric, and true anomalies help us define the elliptical rectangular coordinates of a binary system’s orbit:

\textit{X(t)}=\cos\textit{E(t)}-\textit{e}

(A4)

\textit{Y(t)}=\sqrt{1-\textit{e}^{2}}\sin\textit{E(t)}

(A5)

Next, we find the Thiele-Innes Constants for the Keplerian orbits. These constants are solely used to transform the Keplerian orbital parameters into a partially linear basis, making it easier to find the binary system’s trajectory.

The Thiele-Innes Constants for the primary object are:

\displaystyle A_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(\cos\omega_{pri}\cos\Omega_{sec}-\sin\omega_{pri}\sin\Omega_{sec}\right)

\displaystyle B_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(\cos\omega_{pri}\sin\Omega_{sec}+\sin\omega_{pri}\cos\Omega_{sec}\right)

\displaystyle C_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(\sin\omega_{pri}\sin\textit{i}\right)

\displaystyle F_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(-\sin\omega_{pri}\cos\Omega_{sec}-\cos\omega_{pri}\sin\Omega_{sec}\cos\textit{i}\right)

\displaystyle G_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(-\sin\omega_{pri}\sin\Omega_{sec}+\cos\omega_{pri}\cos\Omega_{sec}\cos\textit{i}\right)

\displaystyle H_{pri}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{pri}\left(\cos\omega_{pri}\sin\textit{i}\right)

(A6)

In how we define our orbital parameterization, the only things we vary between the primary and secondary celestial objects are the length of the semi-major axis (
\aleph_{pri}
and
\aleph_{sec}
) and the argument of periastron (

\displaystyle B_{sec}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{sec}\left(\cos\omega_{sec}\sin\Omega_{sec}+\sin\omega_{sec}\cos\Omega_{sec}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\aleph_{sec}\left(\sin\omega_{sec}\sin\textit{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,N}+d\sin\theta_{\mu}\cos\phi-d\sin\theta_{s}

(B43)

\displaystyle u_{0,N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,N}+d(\sin\theta_{\mu}\cos\phi-(\cos\phi\sin\theta_{\mu}+\cos\theta_{\mu}\sin\phi))

(B44)

\displaystyle u_{0,N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,N}-d\cos\theta_{\mu}\sin\phi.

(B45)

We can use Eq.
B21
:

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin(\theta_{u}+90^{\circ}C)=C\cos\theta_{u}=C\hat{u}_{0,E}

(B46)

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\theta_{u}+90^{\circ}C)=-C\sin\theta_{u}=-C\hat{u}_{0,N}

(B47)

Hence:

\displaystyle u_{0,E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,E}+Cd\sin\phi\hat{u}_{0,E}

(B48)

\displaystyle u_{0,N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,N}+Cd\sin\phi\hat{u}_{0,N}

(B49)

Putting those together:

\boxed{\boldsymbol{u_{0}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{u_{0}}+Cd\sin\phi\hat{u}_{0}}.

(B50)

We may also want to go the opposite direction. To do so we can define an equivalent of Eq.
B20
for
\vec{u}_{0}^{\prime}
:

C^{\prime}\equiv(\hat{\mu}_{rel}\times\hat{u}_{0}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{z}

(B51)

So Eqs.
B46
and
B47
become

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin(\theta_{u^{\prime}}+90^{\circ}C^{\prime})=C^{\prime}\cos\theta_{u^{\prime}}=C^{\prime}\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0,E}

(B52)

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\theta_{u^{\prime}}+90^{\circ}C^{\prime})=-C^{\prime}\sin\theta_{u^{\prime}}=-C^{\prime}\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0,N}

(B53)

Hence

\displaystyle u_{0,E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,E}+C^{\prime}d\sin\phi\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0,E}

(B54)

\displaystyle u_{0,N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0,N}+C^{\prime}d\sin\phi\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0,N}

(B55)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+C^{\prime}d\sin\phi\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0}

(B56)

So Eq.
B50
becomes

\boxed{\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{\prime}-C^{\prime}d\sin\phi\hat{u}^{\prime}_{0}}.

(B57)

Following the derivation in
C. Y. Lam 2023

\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{s}\frac{1-q}{2(1+q)}\equiv\boldsymbol{s}q^{\prime}.

B50

(B62)

If the secondary is more massive, then the center of mass is closer to the secondary than the primary, so the two equations will swtich.
