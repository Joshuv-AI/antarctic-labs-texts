Enhanced detection of bacterial etiologies, antibiotic resistance profile, and treatment outcomes of meningitis patients in Ethiopia: a prospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Derso Wale Mesele, Alem Abrha Kalayu, Seifegebriel Teshome, Jemal Aman, Ashenafi Alemu, Lema Ayele, Aschalew Gelaw, Adane Mihret, Andargachew Mulu, Dawit Hailu Alemayehu, Getachew Tesfaye Beyene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial meningitis remains a significant global health concern, particularly in resource-limited settings where patients often present late and have co-existing conditions. This study aimed to identify the common bacterial pathogens causing meningitis, assess their antibiotic resistance profiles, and evaluate treatment outcomes in suspected meningitis patients.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cross-sectional study involving 195 patients suspected of meningitis at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Cerebrospinal fluid samples were collected and analyzed using both culture and PCR for bacterial etiology. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined via the disc diffusion method. Treatment outcomes were evaluated using the Glasgow Outcome Scale. We used descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariable analysis to identify factors associated with unfavorable patient outcomes, with statistical significance set at a p-value < 0.05 at a 95% confidence level.ResultsThe prevalence of bacterial meningitis, confirmed by either culture or PCR, was 28.2% (55/195). Among the 55 detected bacterial etiologies, E. coli was the most common (45.5%, n = 25), followed by H. influenzae (16.4%, n = 9), S. pneumoniae (10.9%, n = 6), N. meningitidis (7.3%, n = 4), S. aureus (5.5%, n = 3), S. agalactiae (5.5%, n = 3), K. pneumoniae (1.7%, n = 1), L. monocytogenes (1.7%, n = 1), and mixed infections of E. coli and K. pneumoniae (5.5%, n = 3). Notably, only 7.2% (14/195) of bacteria were recovered by culture, with S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae (4 each) being the most frequently cultured etiologies, followed by E. coli and S. aureus (2 each). Among K. pneumoniae isolates, 75% (3/4) were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and 50% (2/4) to meropenem, cefotaxime, and ciprofloxacin. One of four (25%) S. pneumoniae isolates showed resistance to erythromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, and rifampin. Overall, 13.8% (27/195) of study participants experienced unfavorable outcomes. Factors independently associated with unfavorable outcomes included a low Glasgow Coma Scale (AOR = 0.668, 95% CI = 0.529–0.845), comorbidity (AOR = 9.221, 95% CI = 1.580–53.815), and delayed presentation (AOR = 1.684, 95% CI = 1.206–2.352).ConclusionsPCR significantly outperformed culture in detecting bacterial pathogens. E. coli was the most common causative agent of bacterial meningitis, and we observed high rates of antibiotic resistance among Gram-negative bacteria. While most patients had favorable outcomes, low Glasgow Coma Scale scores, comorbidities, and delayed presentation to healthcare facilities were independently associated with unfavorable outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04569-1.Keywords: Bacterial meningitis, PCR, Antibiotic resistance, Treatment outcome, Ethiopia

Background

Bacterial meningitis (BM) is a life-threatening disease with substantial mortality and significant sequelae in survivors [1, 2]. Annually, it affects 1.2 million people and causes more than 30,000 deaths worldwide [3], with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa, commonly known as the “meningitis belt”. In this region, mortality exceeds 50% and about 1 in 5 develop severe complications [4].

Previously, Neisseria meningitidis (N. meningitidis), Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), and Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae) accounted for about 75–80% of BM cases worldwide [5]. However, Escherichia coli (E. coli) and others increased in importance after the introduction of vaccines and caused significant morbidity in specific age groups and underlying conditions [6, 7].

For the successful management of patients, rapid and accurate diagnosis is mandatory [8, 9]. This could be mainly achieved through cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis [8]. CSF microscopy and biochemistry remain the backbone of diagnosis, while culture is the gold standard. However, molecular techniques are increasingly becoming useful [10] as they are more sensitive, rapid, and not affected by viability [11].

Management of bacterial meningitis has been increasingly difficult due to the rise of antibiotic resistance, especially in developing countries like Ethiopia, and is complicated by the lack of well-equipped laboratories and antibiotic misuse [12–14]. The rise of antibiotic resistance limits treatment options and contributes to poor patient outcomes [15].

The inflammatory response within the subarachnoid space is associated with unfavorable outcomes. Administration of adjunctive corticosteroid has been shown to attenuate the inflammatory processes. Evidence suggests that adjunctive corticosteroid therapy in combination with antibiotics is more effective than antibiotic therapy alone in reducing the risk of unfavorable patient outcomes [16, 17]. However, this is still controversial in resource-limited settings [18, 19].

In Ethiopia, the treatment of meningitis is mostly empirical. Therefore, to manage meningitis patients rapidly and properly, data on the types of microorganisms prevalent in the local community and their up-to-date antimicrobial resistance profile against different antibiotics is an urgent need. The fact that the local routine diagnostics practice employs bacterial culture, which has a very low sensitivity, the bacterial etiologic profile, and antibiotic resistance of the pathogens causing meningitis in Ethiopia has not been well addressed. Furthermore, whether adjunctive anti-inflammatory therapies like dexamethasone improve patient outcomes or not remains controversial in our settings. Therefore, the study aimed to investigate the prevalence of bacterial etiologies using culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the antibiotic resistance profile of the isolates. Furthermore, the study explores the role of adjunctive corticosteroid treatment outcomes and other associated factors in presumptive meningitis patients.

Methods

Study design

A prospective cross-sectional study was performed at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH) from August 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023. The hospital is one of the biggest tertiary-level referral and teaching hospitals in the Amhara region, serving an estimated 7 million people from Gondar city and surrounding zones.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size (n = 195) was calculated using a single-proportion formula, considering a 13.2% BM prevalence [20], 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and a 10% nonresponse rate. One hundred ninety-five meningitis suspected patients greater than 28 days of age were included in the study. The study was run as an integrated part of routine practice, and patients were recruited by the attending physician continuously.

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire specifically developed for this study [Supplementary File 1], following a pre-test conducted on 5% of the study population at ALERT Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Before the actual data collection, an orientation was given to all data collectors. Socio-demographic variables, clinical variables at admission, and variables related to treatment outcome at the last date of hospital stay were recorded through face-to-face interviews and physical examinations by the attending physician and from patient medical records. Microbiological analysis and results were documented by the assigned medical microbiologist.

After collecting demographic and clinical data, the CSF samples were collected aseptically following standard operating procedures by the attending physician. Data on treatment received, and whether it was with adjunctive corticosteroid or not, length of hospital stays, and death or discharge outcomes using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) or left against medical advice, were collected.

Laboratory analyses of CSF

CSF glucose and protein levels were analyzed using a standard biochemistry analyzer (Mendery200), and CSF cell count was done manually by improved Neubauer chamber.

Culture identifications were done following the standard microbiological procedures. Briefly, the sediments of centrifuged CSF were inoculated on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar plates (Oxoid Ltd, Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK). The chocolate and blood agar plates were incubated in a candle jar with 5–10% CO2 concentration to create a microaerophilic condition, and MacConkey agar plates were incubated aerobically. All the agar plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Pure isolates were identified by their colony characteristics on their respective media, Gram-reaction, and confirmed by biochemical tests. Briefly, Gram-negative rods were identified by indole production, hydrogen sulfide (H2S) production, citrate utilization, motility test, urease test, oxidase, and triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, while Gram-positive cocci were identified by catalase, coagulase, bacitracin, and optochin susceptibility tests [13]. ATCC control starains were used to validate the biochemical tests.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

Antibiotic susceptibility test (AST) was performed for all bacterial isolates using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method according to the criteria set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2022). The antibiotic discs, their acronyms, with their concentration used for AST were as follows: Ampicillin (AMP) (10 µg), Ceftriaxone (CRO) (30 µg), Ciprofloxacin (CI P) (5 µg), Chloramphenicol (C) (30 µg), Erythromycin (E) (15 µg), Trimethoprim-Sulphamethoxazole (SXT) (1.25/23.75 µg), Gentamycin (GM) (10 µg) Rifampicin (RA) (5 µg), Meropenem (MEM) (10 µg), Imipenem (IMP) (10 µg), Vancomycin (VA) (30 µg), Cefepime (FEP) (30 µg), Ceftazidime (CAZ) (30 µg), Cefotaxime (CTX) (30 µg), and Tetracycline (TE) (30 µg).

For AST testing, the standard microbiology procedure was followed. Briefly the bacteria inoculated plates were incubated aerobically or in 5% CO2 environment as necessary at 37 °C for 24–48 h Then, the diameters of the zone of inhibition around the disc were measured using a digital caliper, and interpreted as sensitive, intermediate, and resistant according to the standardized table supplied by the CLSI 2022.

Molecular identification

Nucleic acid extraction and multiplex PCR assay

Extraction of nucleic acid from CSF was performed according to the protocol provided with the Bioer NPA-32P automated Nucleic Acid Purification method (Hangzhou Bioer Technology Co., Ltd, China). Conventional multiplex PCR was utilized for the detection of bacterial DNA. Target bacteria were multiplexed into two groups based on the characteristics of the primers [Table 1]. The first group comprises N. meningitides (ctrA), S. pneumoniae (lytA), H. influenzae (Hpd), and S. agalactiae (Sag). The second group of target bacteria consisted of E. coli (Eco), K. pneumoniae (Kpn), S. aureus (Sau), and L. monocytogenes (Lm).

For amplifying the target, platinum Taq PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to prepare the master mix. The final volume of each PCR reaction was 25 µL of [2.5 µL of 10X buffer, 1.2 µL of 50 Mm MgCl2, 1 µL dNTP of the master mix, 1µL of each forward and reverse primer, 0.2 µL Taq polymerase, 5 µL of target DNA, and 13.1 µL nuclease-free water]. PCR was performed using the Bio-Rad T100 thermal cycler. Amplification involved an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation (94 °C for 10 s), annealing (60 °C for 30 s), and extension (72 °C for 30 s), with a final extension step (72 °C for 5 min). The PCR products were visualized using a 2.5% agarose gel with ethidium bromide under UV light in a CS-DARKROOM, Cleaver Scientific.

Data management and statistical analysis

The data were first coded, cleaned, and entered into Epi-Info v 3.1, then transformed and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 26 statistical software. The results of the analysis were presented in text, figures, or tables where appropriate. Frequency and percentage were used to summarize categorical variables. Skewed data were presented as a median with an interquartile range. The association between variables was assessed using binary logistic regression and multivariable regression analysis. All independent variables with p < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were entered for multivariable analysis. Forward logistic regression analysis was done to identify the best-fit model. In all the cases, a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Operational definitions

The case definition for bacterial meningitis was based on the 2018 World Health Organization (WHO) recommended standard for surveillance of meningitis preparedness and response to epidemics in Africa.

Suspected meningitis was considered when a patient came up with a sudden onset of fever (> 38.5 °C rectal or 38.0 °C axillary), and neck stiffness or other meningeal signs.

Probable meningitis case any suspected case with macroscopic aspect of cerebrospinal fluid turbid, cloudy, or purulent; or with a CSF leukocyte count greater than 10 cells/µL, or with bacteria identified by Gram stain in CSF.

Confirmed meningitis case: any suspected or probable case that is laboratory confirmed by culture or PCR in the CSF.

The GOS was categorized as (1- death; 2- vegetative state; 3- severe disability; 4- moderate disability; 5- good recovery) at discharge. Later, the scores were dichotomized into favorable outcome (GOS = 5) and unfavorable outcome (GOS = 1 to 4) [19]. To ensure a shared understanding of the scale criteria and reduce interpersonal variability, we provided a 6-hour orientation to all assessors. Any discrepancies in scoring were addressed through consensus discussion among the assessors.

We consider patients with comorbidity if they have confirmed diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or HIV.

Laboratory findings of cerebrospinal fluid examination

Physical appearance and cell count

Out of the total 195 CSF samples, 152 (77.9%) appeared visually clear, 35 (17.9%) were turbid, and 8 (4.2%) were blood-stained. Among the 35 turbid CSF samples, 25 (71.4%) were from patients with confirmed BM.

The WBC counts of the CSF samples ranged from no cells to dense clusters of cells. The majority, 149 (76.4%), had WBC counts of less than 10 cells/µL. Of the 55 confirmed BM patients, 16 (29.1%) have a CSF cell count of greater than 100 cells/µL. Additionally, 31 (56.6%) confirmed BM patients have a glucose level of less than 40 mg/dL.

Culture findings

A total of 14 (7.2%) CSF samples were found positive for bacterial growth. One sample that demonstrated Gram-negative coccobacilli on Gram staining did not show growth in culture; however, it was positive for H. influenzae in PCR. Among the culture-positive samples, Gram-negative bacteria were 8 (57.1%). S. pneumoniae and K. pneumoniae were the most frequent culture-isolated pathogens, each accounting for 4 (28.6%), followed by E. coli and S. aureus with two isolates each (14.3%), and H. influenzae and N. meningitidis with one isolate each 1 (7.1%).

Antibiotic resistance profile of the bacterial isolates

Among the Gram-negative bacterial isolates, three-fourths (75%) of K. pneumoniae showed resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, while half (50%) of the isolates were resistant to meropenem, cefotaxime, and ciprofloxacin. Half of the 50% of E. coli isolates exhibited resistance to ceftazidime, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and ampicillin. Both isolates, N. meningitidis and H. influenzae, showed intermediate resistance to ciprofloxacin and chloramphenicol, respectively, while no resistance was detected to the rest of the tested drugs (Table 4). S. pneumoniae exhibited 1/4 (25%) resistance to erythromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, and rifampin. The S. aureus isolates were 2/2 (100%) resistant to chloramphenicol and rifampin, while 1/2 (50%) were resistant to tetracycline (Table 5).

Molecular detection of pathogens from CSF

Among the 195 samples from patients with presumptive meningitis, bacteria were detected in 55 (28.2%) samples. All the samples that were positive by the conventional methods (Gram stain and/or culture) were also positive by PCR. Three of the four K. pneumoniae culture-positive CSF samples were found to be positive for both K. pneumoniae and E. coli using PCR.

E. coli was the most common bacterium detected by PCR, accounting for 25 (45.5%), followed by H. influenziae (Fig. 1).

Proportion of bacterial etiologies detected by CSF PCR

Treatment outcome of the patient

The outcome of 195 meningitis suspected patients was assessed using GOS, and 27 (13.8%) had unfavorable outcomes GOS (1–4). The mortality rate was 8 (4.1%). Three patients left the hospital against medical advice (Fig. 2).

Treatment outcome of meningitis suspected patient

The majority of the patients, 177 (90.8%) were treated with ceftriaxone, and 175 (89.7%) of them received more than one antibiotic treatment. Of those 54 (27.7%) received a combination of ceftriaxone and vancomycin. Dexamethasone was given to 79 (40.5%) of the patients before or at the time of antibiotic administration.

In bivariate logistic regression analysis, neck stiffness, patient level of consciousness (GCS), duration of disease onset to hospital visit, comorbidity, history of antibiotics intake before hospital visit, CSF WBC, CSF protein, treatment with adjunctive dexamethasone, and infection with S. pneumoniae were found to be associated with unfavorable outcomes (Table 6).

However, on multivariate analysis, the effect of most of these variables disappeared, yet low GCS (AOR = 0.668, 95% CI = 0.529–0.845), comorbidity (AOR = 9.221, 95% CI = 1.580–53.815), and duration of disease onset to hospital visit (AOR = 1.684, 95% CI = 1.206–2.352) were independently associated with unfavorable outcome (Table 6).

Discussion

This study underscores the transformative impact of molecular diagnostics, specifically PCR, in the accurate identification of BM etiologies. A key finding was the universal PCR positivity among all CSF samples that yielded positive bacterial cultures. In stark contrast, conventional microbiological methods, including Gram staining and bacterial culture, identified only 14 meningitis etiologies, despite 55 PCR-positive CSF samples indicating the presence of potentially culturable bacteria. This discrepancy highlights the superior sensitivity of molecular approaches, a finding consistent with previous research from Ethiopia [25, 26] and China [27]. The diminished recovery rate of bacterial isolates in culture can largely be attributed to factors such as prior antibiotic treatment, which was reported in approximately 19.5% of the patients, effectively suppressing bacterial growth in vitro but leaving detectable nucleic acids for molecular methods.

The prevalence of bacterial meningitis among suspected patients, as determined by PCR, was 28.2% (55 cases). This figure aligns closely with molecular-based studies from Ethiopia (33.1%) [28] and China (32%) [5]. While our PCR-derived prevalence was lower than reported in studies from Lithuania (66.3%) [29] and Pakistan (88.9%) [30] - differences potentially influenced by predisposing factors such as intensive care unit admission and immunocompromising conditions like pediatric age - it notably surpassed prevalence rates reported in numerous culture-dependent studies from Ethiopia (ranging from 1.28% to 13.2%) [12–14, 25, 31–33], Iran (7.6%) [34], and Kenya (11.2%) [35]. This significant difference strongly suggests that reliance solely on culture-based methods, owing to their inherent lower sensitivity, substantially underestimates the true prevalence of bacterial meningitis. Molecular diagnostics thus provide a more accurate epidemiological picture, crucial for public health interventions.

The etiology of BM is dynamic, influenced by vaccine introduction and evolving antimicrobial resistance patterns [27]. Our molecular findings identified E. coli as the predominant bacterial pathogen, accounting for 21.9% (25) of the detected etiologies. This finding is in agreement with molecular epidemiological data from China [5, 36, 37] and Korea [38]. Conversely, some studies from Ethiopia and other regions, often relying on culture, reported S. pneumoniae as the primary pathogen [31, 32, 39]. The lower prevalence of S. pneumoniae in our study could be attributed to the high vaccination coverage among our participants, with approximately 60% being under five years of age and having received S. pneumoniae vaccination. Molecular methods, by offering a more comprehensive and unbiased detection, are vital for tracking these shifts in pathogen dominance.

The persistent rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria poses a significant challenge to global healthcare [12, 13, 31]. While molecular diagnostics primarily identify pathogens, their rapid and accurate identification is crucial for guiding timely and appropriate empirical antibiotic therapy, especially in the context of emerging resistance. Among Gram-negative isolates, E. coli exhibited substantial resistance (50%) to multiple antibiotics, including ceftazidime, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and ampicillin. These resistance patterns are consistent with findings from other Ethiopian studies [12, 14, 31, 33], though lower resistance levels have been reported elsewhere [33, 40]. Similarly, K. pneumoniae isolates demonstrated 50% resistance to meropenem, cefotaxime, and ciprofloxacin, aligning with reports from Pakistan [40] and Debre Markos [13]. The observed high resistance rates to β-lactam antibiotics in K. pneumoniae and E. coli strongly suggest the presence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing strains, a phenomenon linked to the bacteria’s ability to produce enzymes that degrade carbapenem and β-lactam ring structures [41]. This underscores the critical need for molecular surveillance of resistance genes to inform treatment strategies, such as the potential utility of carbapenems (e.g., imipenem) for infections caused by these highly resistant strains. Among Gram-positive organisms, S. pneumoniae isolates showed 25% resistance to erythromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, and rifampin, while remaining 100% sensitive to vancomycin, consistent with local studies [14]. The 100% resistance of S. aureus to chloramphenicol mirrors previous reports from Ethiopia [13, 14], likely due to widespread antibiotic exposure, self-medication, and incomplete dosages, driving the development of resistance mechanisms [41].

Regarding patient outcomes, 13.8% of meningitis-suspected patients experienced unfavorable outcomes (GOS = 1 to 4) upon discharge, which is comparable to a study in Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital, Ethiopia (15%) [42]. However, higher rates of unfavorable outcomes were reported from Jimma, Ethiopia (36.7%) [18, 19] and Bedele General Hospital, Ethiopia (32.6%) [43], possibly linked to the prevalence of etiologic agents like S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis, which are often associated with poorer prognoses. While molecular diagnostics do not directly influence patient outcomes, their ability to provide rapid and accurate pathogen identification can facilitate earlier, more targeted treatment, potentially mitigating some of the adverse effects.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified low GCS (AOR = 0.668, 95% CI = 0.529–0.845) as an independent factor associated with unfavorable outcomes. This indicates that for each unit increase in GCS score, the odds of an unfavorable outcome decrease by approximately 33.2%, aligning with findings from Jimma, Ethiopia [19] and the Netherlands [44]. Prolonged altered consciousness can lead to neurological impairments, and low GCS at admission may reflect complications from delayed presentation. Similarly, delayed presentation to the hospital from disease onset (AOR = 1.684, 95% CI = 1.206–2.352) was independently associated with unfavorable outcomes, meaning a one-day delay increased the likelihood of an unfavorable outcome by 1.6 times. This is consistent with studies from Ethiopia [45], Denmark [46], and England [47]. Such delays, often due to poor health-seeking behavior, limited access, or misdiagnosis, can complicate case management and contribute to poor outcomes [45, 48]. Comorbidity (AOR = 9.221, 95% CI = 1.580–53.815) was also strongly associated with unfavorable outcomes, with individuals having comorbidities being approximately 9.2 times more likely to experience adverse results, consistent with other studies [43, 49, 50]. The wide confidence interval, however, suggests variability in the exact magnitude of this effect.

Dexamethasone administration was observed in 40.5% of patients. While it showed an association with unfavorable outcomes in bivariate analysis (AOR = 2.424, 95% CI = 1.058–5.553), this effect dissipated in multivariate analysis. This suggests that in our cohort, adjunctive dexamethasone treatment might not confer significant benefit or harm, possibly due to delayed patient presentation where the inflammatory process in the central nervous system had already progressed. This finding contrasts with some Ethiopian studies associating dexamethasone with poorer outcomes [18, 19, 42] but aligns with others suggesting favorable outcomes [43, 51], reflecting the complex role of corticosteroids in modulating host inflammatory responses during meningitis.

Despite providing valuable insights, this study has limitations, including a small sample size and a single-center setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the absence of follow-up beyond hospital discharge restricts a comprehensive understanding of long-term patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study unequivocally demonstrates the superior detection capability of PCR over conventional bacterial culture for identifying meningitis-causing pathogens. Our molecular analysis revealed E. coli as the most frequent causative agent of bacterial meningitis, providing a precise etiological understanding. High rates of antibiotic resistance, particularly among Gram-negative bacteria, were observed, underscoring the urgency for molecular surveillance of resistance genes to guide appropriate empirical therapy. While most patients experienced favorable outcomes, unfavorable outcomes were significantly associated with low GCS at presentation, the presence of comorbidities, and delayed access to healthcare facilities. Based on these findings, we strongly recommend the integration of PCR into routine laboratory procedures to enhance the detection and accurate identification of meningitis-causing pathogens, thereby facilitating earlier and more effective management. Special attention should be given to patients presenting with low GCS and comorbidities to improve their outcomes. Future large-scale, multicenter studies incorporating molecular methods are warranted to further evaluate the association of adjunctive dexamethasone therapy with outcomes in presumptive meningitis patients.
