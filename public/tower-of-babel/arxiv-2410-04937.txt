Riemannian-geometric generalizations of quantum fidelities and Bures-Wasserstein distance
A. Afham, Chris Ferrie

Abstract
We introduce a family of fidelities, termed generalized fidelity, which are based on the Riemannian geometry of the Bures-Wasserstein manifold. We show that this family of fidelities generalizes standard quantum fidelities such as Uhlmann-, Holevo-, and Matsumoto-fidelity and demonstrate that it satisfies analogous celebrated properties. The generalized fidelity naturally arises from a generalized Bures distance, the natural distance obtained by linearizing the Bures-Wasserstein manifold. We prove various invariance and covariance properties of generalized fidelity as the point of linearization moves along geodesic-related paths. We also provide a Block-matrix characterization and prove an Uhlmann-like theorem, as well as provide further extensions to the multivariate setting and to quantum Rényi divergences, generalizing Petz-, Sandwich-, Reverse sandwich-, and Geometric-Rényi divergences of order α \alpha .

Introduction

Fidelity is a fundamental figure of merit in quantum sciences used to measure the similarity of two quantum states
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. It enjoys many valuable properties, such as achieving its maximum value of 1 if the states are equal and the minimum value of 0 if they have orthogonal support. Fidelity—specifically Uhlmann fidelity—is one of the various non-commutative generalizations of the Bhattacharyya coefficient
[
6
]
from statistics, which is a comparator for entrywise-positive vectors. It also features prominently in the definition of the Bures distance (a.k.a. Bures-Wasserstein distance), which is the natural distance on the Riemannian manifold of positive definite matrices once it is endowed with the Bures metric (a.k.a. Bures-Wasserstein metric)
[
7
,
8
]
. This manifold is ubiquitous not only in quantum information
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
but also in the machine learning world via its relation to the 2-Wasserstein distance on the space of probability measures
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

The Bures-Wasserstein (BW) distance is one of the non-commutative generalizations of Hellinger distance between two positive vectors. The (squared) Hellinger distance between two positive vectors
p,q\in[0,\infty)^{d}
, is defined as

\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q):=\left\|\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}+q_{i}-2\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}},

(1)

where the square root is taken element-wise. One sees that the Hellinger distance can be interpreted as the difference between the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean of the two vectors:

\mathrm{A}(p,q):=\frac{p+q}{2},\quad\mathrm{G}(p,q):=\sqrt{pq}\quad\implies\quad\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\mathrm{A}(p,q)-\mathrm{G}(p,q)\right]_{i},

(2)

where the product and square root of vectors are taken element-wise.
While generalizing to the set of positive semidefinite matrices, (the sum of) the arithmetic mean generalizes straightforwardly. However, there is no unique way of generalizing (the sum of) the geometric mean
\sum_{i=1}^{d}[\mathrm{G}(p,q)]_{i}
part, which is the
Bhattacharyya coefficient
or
classical fidelity
between
p
and
q
.

\operatorname{F}_{\text{cl}}(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}.

(3)

The non-commutative (quantum) generalization of this inner-product term is usually called a
quantum fidelity
. Various versions of quantum fidelities are central to quantum information, and some prominent ones are defined as follows.

1.

Uhlmann fidelity
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
:
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}QP^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]
.

2.

Holevo fidelity
[
16
,
17
]
:
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]
.

3.

Matsumoto fidelity
[
18
,
19
]
:
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P\#Q\right]
.

4.

Log-Euclidean fidelity:
\operatorname{F}^{\operatorname{LE}}(P,Q):=\text{Tr}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\ln P+\ln Q}{2}\right)\right].

5.

z
-fidelity:
\operatorname{F}^{z}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(P^{\frac{1}{2z}}Q^{\frac{1}{2z}}\right)^{z}\right]
for
z\geq 0
.

The
z
-fidelities are obtained by setting
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
in (the fidelity part of) the family of
\alpha\hyph z
Rényi relative entropies
[
20
,
21
]
and satisfies the data processing inequality for
z\geq 1/2
. The limiting case
z\to\infty
yields the Log-Euclidean fidelity. We remark that Uhlmann
[
2
]
initially defined the square of
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)
as
transition probability
.

For each of the above fidelities, one can define the associated (squared)
Hellinger quantity
as

\operatorname{B}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\operatorname{F}(P,Q).

(4)

Choosing the Uhlmann fidelity yields the Bures-Wasserstein distance:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)=\min_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{d}}\left\|P^{\frac{1}{2}}-Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U\right\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

which is the natural distance of the (Riemannian) Bures-Wasserstein manifold. Choosing the Holevo fidelity yields the (matrix) Hellinger distance:

d_{\mathrm{H}}^{2}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P+Q-2P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q)=\left\|P^{\frac{1}{2}}-Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

For Uhlmann fidelity and Holevo fidelity, the corresponding Hellinger quantities define valid distances. However, the corresponding Hellinger quantities are not distances for Matsumoto fidelity and Log-Euclidean fidelity. Instead, their squared versions are divergences
[
22
]
. Various information-theoretic properties of these quantum fidelities are also subjects of intense study
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we define generalizations of certain fidelities and Bures-Wasserstein distance motivated by the Riemannian geometry of the Bures-Wasserstein manifold. This approach is based on the linearization of the manifold about an arbitrary
base
point. Such a linearization has been previously studied in the context of the 2-Wasserstein distance on the space of probability measures
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be a triple of positive definite matrices. The
generalized fidelity between
P
and
Q
at
R
is defined as

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}}R^{-1}\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right].

(7)

See Figure
1
for an illustration of generalized fidelity between fixed rebit states
P
and
Q
with the base
R
varying over the rebit disk. As shown, the generalized fidelity between positive matrices can take negative values depending on the base. Other properties illustrated in the figure include the fact that even if
P
and
Q
commute, the generalized fidelity can differ from the classical fidelity. Additionally, it is shown that the generalized fidelity achieves its maximum value when the base is along the Bures-Wasserstein geodesic between
P
and
Q
. Along this geodesic, the generalized fidelity
\operatorname{F}_{R}\!\left(P,Q\right)
equals the Uhlmann fidelity
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,Q\right)
.

The corresponding squared
generalized Bures distance
between
P
and
Q
at
R
is defined as

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q),

(8)

where
\real z
denotes the real part of a complex number
z
.

The matrix
R
is referred to as the
base
of the generalized fidelity ( generalized Bures distance) and, as we later show, is the point such that linearizing the BW manifold at this point yields the generalized Bures distance as the natural distance (induced by the inner-product on the tangent space).

The generalized fidelity exhibits remarkable properties as a function of its base. For example, the Uhlmann fidelity between
P
and
Q
can be interpreted as the generalized fidelity when one chooses the base as
P
or
Q
(among other points). The Holevo fidelity corresponds to the generalized fidelity when the base is chosen to be the identity matrix
\mathbb{I}
, and Matsumoto fidelity is the generalized fidelity when one linearizes the manifold at
P^{-1}
or
Q^{-1}
(among other points). Additionally, various invariance and covariance properties emerge as the base moves along geodesic-related paths on the Riemannian manifold of positive definite matrices. These results are proven in Section
4
. See Figure
2
for an illustrative summary of the geometric results.

This article is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly review the necessary concepts from Riemannian geometry. In Section
3
, we formally define generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance, and study their basic properties. In Section
4
, we explore the invariance and covariance properties of the neralized fidelity between two fixed matrices as the base moves along various geodesic-related paths, corresponding to different metrics: Bures-Wasserstein, Affine-invariant, and Euclidean.

The rest of the paper is devoted to studying further properties of generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance. In Section
5
, we introduce a different family of fidelities called the
x
-
Polar fidelities
, which is a one-parameter family of fidelities that recovers Uhlmann-, Holevo-, and Matsumoto-fidelity. We also discuss certain results that relate generalized fidelity to the special unitary group
\mathrm{SU}(d)
. This leads to the realization that generalized fidelities can be seen to be (the elements of) the
extreme points
of a larger class of fidelities, which is obtained by taking convex combinations of generalized fidelity over different bases.

In Section
6
, we derive a block-matrix representation of generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance. In Section
7
, we establish results connecting the recently-introduced multivariate fidelity
[
21
]
, average fidelity maximizer (normalized Bures-Wasserstein barycenter)
[
12
,
28
]
, and generalized fidelity. In Section
8
we introduce an Uhlmann-like theorem for generalized fidelity, which characterizes generalized fidelity between states based on the inner-product of their purifications.

Finally, in Section
9
, we introduce an analogous base-dependent formulation of quantum Rényi divergences and show that it recovers various
\alpha
-
z
divergences
[
20
]
and
\alpha
-geometric Renyi divergence as we vary the base point. We end the article with a few open problems in Section
10
and discussions in Section
11
. Code to generate plots and further numerical simulations is available at GitHub

3
Generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance

We now formally introduce generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance. We begin with the definition and some basic properties of generalized fidelity. We then do the same for generalized Bures distance and finally conclude the section with the Riemannian-geometric motivation for the definition.

3.1
Generalized fidelity

Let us now define generalized fidelity.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be positive definite matrices. The
generalized fidelity between

P
and
Q
at
R
is defined as

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}}R^{-1}\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]

(18)

Here and henceforth,
R
is referred to as the
base
of the generalized fidelity between
P
and
Q
. Throughout the analysis, we require the base
R
to be positive definite while
P
and
Q
can be rank-deficient. However, for most of our analysis, we consider
P
and
Q
to be invertible as well. The first thing to notice is that the matrix inside the trace is not Hermitian in general. Thus, generalized fidelity is complex-valued in general. We now list some basic properties of generalized fidelity. Proofs are provided in Appendix
B
.

1.

Quantization of classical fidelity.
The generalized fidelity
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
is a quantization of classical fidelity for any triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. That is, if
P,Q,
and
R
mutually commute, the generalized fidelity reduces to the classical fidelity (Bhattacharyya coefficient) between
P
and
Q
.

2.

Conjugate symmetry.
Generalized fidelity is generally complex-valued and conjugate symmetric:

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)\in\mathbb{C}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}_{R}\!\left(Q,P\right)^{*}.

(19)

Moreover,
\operatorname{F}_{R}\!\left(P,P\right)=\operatorname{Tr}[P]
for any
P,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. It follows that the generalized fidelity of a state with itself is 1 at any base.

3.

Equivalent forms.
The generalized fidelity has the following equivalent forms.

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q):

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}}R^{-1}\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[(R^{-1}\#Q)R(R^{-1}\#P)\right],

where
U_{P}:=\text{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
,
U_{Q}:=\text{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
.

4.

Pure state simplification.
If both
P=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
and
Q=\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}
are pure states, then

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}_{R}\!\left(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right)=\frac{\langle\psi,\phi\rangle\langle\phi,R\psi\rangle}{\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right)}.

(21)

5.

Commutation with base implies reality.
If the base
R
commutes with
P
or
Q
, then
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)\in\mathbb{R}
.

6.

Multiplicativity.
Let
P_{1},Q_{1},R_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{d_{1}}
and
P_{2},Q_{2},R_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{d_{2}}
. Then

\operatorname{F}_{R_{1}\otimes R_{2}}\!\left(P_{1}\otimes P_{2},Q_{1}\otimes Q_{2}\right)=\operatorname{F}_{R_{1}}\!\left(P_{1},Q_{1}\right)\cdot\operatorname{F}_{R_{2}}\!\left(P_{2},Q_{2}\right).

(22)

7.

Additivity.
Let
P_{1},Q_{1},R_{1}\in\mathbb{P}_{d_{1}}
and
P_{2},Q_{2},R_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{d_{2}}
. Then

\operatorname{F}_{R_{1}\oplus R_{2}}\!\left(P_{1}\oplus P_{2},Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}\right)=\operatorname{F}_{R_{1}}\!\left(P_{1},Q_{1}\right)+\operatorname{F}_{R_{2}}\!\left(P_{2},Q_{2}\right).

(23)

8.

Unitary invariance.
For any triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and any unitary
U\in\mathbb{U}_{d}
,

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}_{URU^{*}}\!\left(UPU^{*},UQU^{*}\right).

(24)

9.

Unitary contravariance.
For any triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and any unitary
U\in\mathbb{U}_{d}
,

\operatorname{F}_{URU^{*}}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}_{R}\!\left(U^{*}PU,U^{*}QU\right).

(25)

10.

Scaling.
For positive scalars
p,q,r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\operatorname{F}_{rR}\!\left(pP,qQ\right)=\sqrt{pq}\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q).

(26)

Since the generalized fidelity is invariant with respect to any scaling of the base
R
, one can always choose the base to be a density matrix without loss of generality.

11.

Absolute value is bounded above by Uhlmann fidelity.
For any triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
,

|\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)|\leq\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,Q\right).

(27)

12.

Orthogonal support implies 0.
If any two matrices from
P,Q,R
have orthogonal support, then
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=0.

13.

Reduction to other fidelities.
For specific choices of the base
R
, one can recover various named fidelities from generalized fidelity.

•

Uhlmann fidelity:
If (but not only if)
R=P
or
R=Q
,

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}QP^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right].

(28)

•

Holevo fidelity:
If
R=\mathbb{I}
,

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

(29)

•

Matsumoto fidelity:
If (but not only if)
R=P^{-1}
or
R=Q^{-1}
,

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{M}}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}[P\#Q]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{P^{-\frac{1}{2}}QP^{-\frac{1}{2}}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

(30)

We note that these are not the only cases where generalized fidelity reduces to the above-mentioned fidelities. Other scenarios where this happens are discussed in Section
4
.

Next, we define the generalized Bures distance and discuss some basic properties and the geometric motivation behind the definition. We also see how generalized fidelity naturally appears in this geometric motivation.

3.2
Generalized Bures distance

Having defined generalized fidelity, we are in a position to define the
generalized Bures distance
. After defining it, we will show how it can be naturally derived from the geometry of the Bures-Wasserstein manifold.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be positive definite matrices. The squared
generalized Bures(-Wasserstein) distance between

P
and
Q
at
R
is defined as

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q):=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q).

(31)

We use capital
\mathrm{B}
to denote squared distance (and certain divergences) and
small

\mathrm{b}
to denote distances. For example, we will henceforth use

\operatorname{b}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)\equiv\sqrt{\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)}

(32)

to denote the Bures-Wasserstein distance. The superscript indicates the specific type of fidelity used. Similarly, we denote the generalized Bures distance as

\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)\equiv\sqrt{\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)}.

(33)

Essentially, the relation between generalized Bures distance and generalized fidelity is analogous to the relation between Bures distance and Uhlmann fidelity.

We now show that this definition is geometrically motivated. As mentioned before, the generalized Bures distance between
P
and
Q
at
R
is the distance between
P
and
Q
if we
flatten the
Bures-Wasserstein manifold at
R
. This is formalized in the next section.

3.2.1
Geometric motivation for generalized Bures distance

We now provide a (Riemannian-)geometrically motivated derivation of the generalized Bures distance. Since the generalized fidelity features in the definition of the generalized Bures distance, this also provides a geometric interpretation for generalized fidelity.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{g})
be a Riemannian manifold. It is a defining property of Riemannian manifolds
[
48
]
that for any
x\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
such that for any
y\in\mathcal{N}_{x}
, we have the natural distance between the points to be

\mathrm{d}^{2}(x,y)=||\mathrm{Log}_{x}[y]||_{x}^{2},

(34)

where
\mathrm{Log}_{x}[y]\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and where the norm is taken with respect to the inner product
\mathfrak{g}_{x}
on the tangent spce
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. It turns out that for the Bures-Wasserstein manifold,
\mathcal{N}_{P}=\mathbb{P}_{d}
for any
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Thus, we can write

\mathrm{b}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q)=\|\mathrm{Log}_{P}[Q]\|_{P}=\|\mathrm{Log}_{Q}[P]\|_{Q},

(35)

for any
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
where the norm is taken with respect to the Bures-Wasserstein metric tensor in the corresponding tangent space.

One can visualize this process as follows. We linearize the
curved
Bures manifold
\mathbb{P}_{d}

about
the point
P
. This is equivalent to replacing every point
S\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
with its image under the Riemannian log map
\mathrm{Log}_{P}[S]\in T_{P}\mathbb{P}_{d}
. The tangent space is an inner-product space, and thus, we can define the induced distance between two tangent vectors
X,Y\in T_{P}\mathbb{P}_{d}
as
\|X-Y\|_{P}^{2}
. Therefore the squared distance between the tangent vectors
\mathrm{Log}_{P}[P]
and
\mathrm{Log}_{P}[Q]
is given by

\|\mathrm{Log}_{P}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{P}[Q]\|_{P}^{2}=\|\mathrm{Log}_{P}[Q]\|_{P}^{2}=\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q),

(36)

where we note that
\mathrm{Log}_{S}[S]=0
for any
S\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
.

To obtain generalized Bures distance and thereby generalized fidelity, we flatten our manifold not at
P
or
Q
, but at an arbitrary third point
R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. That is, we map the points
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
to the tangent space
T_{R}\mathbb{P}_{d}
via the
\mathrm{Log}_{R}
map. We then compute the natural distance between these tangent vectors. It turns out that this is exactly the generalized Bures distance between
P
and
Q
at
R
. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be chosen arbitrarily. Then

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)=\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]\|_{R}^{2}.

(37)

Proof.

We will show that the RHS evaluates to the definition of the squared generalized Bures distance. For brevity, we will use the shorthand
P_{R}\equiv\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]
and
Q_{R}\equiv\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]
. Expanding the RHS, we have

\displaystyle\|P_{R}-Q_{R}\|_{R}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle P_{R}-Q_{R},P_{R}-Q_{R}\rangle_{R}

(38)

\displaystyle=\langle P_{R},P_{R}\rangle_{R}+\langle Q_{R},Q_{R}\rangle_{R}-\langle P_{R},Q_{R}\rangle_{R}-\langle Q_{R},P_{R}\rangle_{R},

where we used the bilinearity of the inner product to get the second line. Let us now analyze a single term, namely
\langle P_{R},Q_{R}\rangle_{R}
, of the second line. By the definition of the Bures-Wasserstein metric, we have

\displaystyle\langle P_{R},Q_{R}\rangle_{R}:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{R}(P_{R})\cdot R\cdot\mathcal{L}_{R}(Q_{R})\right]=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathcal{L}_{R}(\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P])\cdot R\cdot\mathcal{L}_{R}(\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q])].

(39)

Now recall that the definition of the BW
\mathrm{Log}_{R}
map is
\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]:=\mathcal{L}_{R}^{-1}(P^{-1}\#Q-\mathbb{I})
, and thus we have

\displaystyle\langle P_{R},Q_{R}\rangle_{R}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathcal{L}_{R}(\mathcal{L}_{R}^{-1}(R^{-1}\#P-\mathbb{I}))\cdot R\cdot\mathcal{L}_{R}(\mathcal{L}_{R}^{-1}(R^{-1}\#Q-\mathbb{I}))\right]

(40)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(R^{-1}\#P-\mathbb{I})R(R^{-1}\#Q-\mathbb{I})]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(R^{-1}\#P)R(R^{-1}\#Q)]-\operatorname{Tr}[(R^{-1}\#P)R]-\operatorname{Tr}[(R^{-1}\#Q)R]+\operatorname{Tr}[R]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{F}_{R}(Q,P)-\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)-\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right)+\operatorname{Tr}[R],

where we used the fact that
\text{Tr}[A(A^{-1}\#B)]=\text{Tr}\left[\sqrt{A^{\frac{1}{2}}BA^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(A,B)
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\|P_{R}\|_{R}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}[P+R]-2\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,R)=\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,R),

(41)

\displaystyle\|Q_{R}\|_{R}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}[Q+R]-2\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q,R)=\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q,R).

Substituting all of these in
Equation

38
, we get

\displaystyle\|P_{R}-Q_{R}\|_{R}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,R)+\operatorname{B}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q,R)-2\left(\operatorname{Tr}[R]-\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,R)-\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q,R)+\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=:\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Thus, the geometric interpretation of generalized Bures distance is clear. It is the distance between the points
P
and
Q
if we
linearize
the manifold at
R
. Analogously, the generalized fidelity is the
fidelity part
of the generalized Bures distance in this scenario. Thus, the various named fidelities have the following geometric interpretations:

•

Uhlmann fidelity is the generalized fidelity if we linearize the manifold at
P
or
Q
(among other points),

•

Holevo fidelity is the generalized fidelity if we linearize the manifold at
\mathbb{I}
, and

•

Matsumoto fidelity is the generalized fidelity if we linearize the manifold at
P^{-1}
or
Q^{-1}
(among other points).

The
other points
are discussed in Section
4
. We now discuss some other properties of the generalized Bures distance.

3.2.2
Further properties of generalized Bures distance

We begin with an alternative form of generalized Bures distance.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Then, the squared generalized Bures distance has the form

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\|U^{*}_{P}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U^{*}_{Q}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(43)

where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
,
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
, and
\|A\|_{2}:=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}[A^{*}A]}
denotes the Frobenius norm of a matrix
A
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left\|U^{*}_{P}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[(U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*}(U_{P}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}Q^{\frac{1}{2}})\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[P+Q-P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=:\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark.
By the adjoint invariance of Frobenius norm, we also have

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\|P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}-Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(45)

We now show that generalized Bures distance is a bona fide distance. A distance function
\operatorname{d}
must satisfy the following threey properties.

1.

Symmetry:
\operatorname{d}(x,y)=\text{d}(y,x)
.

2.

Nonnegativity:
\text{d}(x,y)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
x=y
.

3.

Triangle inequality:
\text{d}(x,y)\geq\text{d}(x,z)+\text{d}(z,y)
for any
z
.

We show that the generalized Bures distance satisfies the above three properties in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

The generalized Bures distance
\operatorname{b}_{R}(\cdot,\cdot)
at any
R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
satisfies

1.

Symmetry:
\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{b}_{R}(Q,P)
,

2.

Nonnegativity:
\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
P=Q
.

3.

Triangle inequality: For any triple
P,Q,S\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we have

\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)\leq\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,S)+\operatorname{b}_{R}(S,Q).

(46)

Proof.

The symmetry part follows directly from the definition. For the nonnegativity part, either observe that the generalized Bures distance is a norm in the tangent space
T_{R}\mathbb{P}_{d}
or observe that by Proposition
3.4
, we have

\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\|U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}\geq 0,

(47)

where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. We now show that equality is achieved if and only if
P=Q
. The direction
P=Q\implies\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=0
follows trivially. In the other direction, we want to prove

\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=0\implies P=Q.

(48)

To this end, observe that

\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\|U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}=0\quad\implies\quad U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}=U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(49)

Right multiply both sides by
R^{\frac{1}{2}}
to obtain

U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}=U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(50)

By Lemma
C.1
, we have that the above equality is the same as

\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\sqrt{R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(51)

Squaring both sides and right-left multiplying by
R^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, we get
P=Q
.

To prove that the generalized Bures distance satisfies the triangle inequality, choose arbitrary
P,Q,R,S\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. We then have,

\displaystyle\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,Q)=

\displaystyle\left\|U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{S}^{*}S^{\frac{1}{2}}+U_{S}^{*}S^{\frac{1}{2}}+U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{S}^{*}S^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|U_{S}^{*}S^{\frac{1}{2}}-U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{b}_{R}(P,S)+\operatorname{b}_{R}(S,Q).

Here
U_{S}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}S^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and we have used triangle inequality for the Frobenius norm.
∎

We now mention a different way of showing triangle inequality and the saturation of the nonnegativity by invoking the tangent space
T_{R}\mathbb{P}_{d}
. By Theorem
3.3
, we have

\text{b}_{R}(P,Q)=\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]\|_{R}=0\quad\implies\quad\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]=\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q].

(53)

Now recall that the map
\mathrm{Log}_{R}
is a diffeomorphism from
\cal M
to its image and, therefore, is invertible, which necessarily means
P=Q
. For the triangle inequality we have that for any quadruple
P,Q,R,S\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{b}_{R}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]\right\|_{R}

(54)

\displaystyle=\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[S]+\mathrm{Log}_{R}[S]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]\|_{R}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[P]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[S]\|_{R}+\|\mathrm{Log}_{R}[S]-\mathrm{Log}_{R}[Q]\|_{R}

\displaystyle=\text{b}_{R}(P,S)+\text{b}_{R}(Q,S),

where the inequality comes from the fact that the tangent space is an inner-product space, and therefore, triangle inequality holds.

Bures-Wasserstein (red) paths

We first discuss the paths that are related to the BW metric, which is colored red in Figure

. We consider six paths related to this metric. One defining property of the base
R
as it travels along these paths is that either
R
or
R^{-1}

Moreover, being an element of the BW geodesic (at time
t
) is equivalent to being the BW barycenter of a 2-point distribution with weights
(t,1-t)
. As such, it uniquely satisfies the following fixed-point equation.

C=(1-t)\sqrt{C^{\frac{1}{2}}AC^{\frac{1}{2}}}+t\sqrt{B^{\frac{1}{2}}AB^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(57)

R=\gamma^{\mathrm{BW}}_{PQ}(t)

Here the base
R
moves along the BW geodesic path between
P
and
Q
:

where

. As shown in the following theorem,
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
is constant and equal to Uhlmann fidelity along this curve.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be fixed. Let the base
R=\gamma^{\mathrm{BW}}_{PQ}(t)
for any
t\in[0,1]
. Then

Proof.

Paths 5 and 6:
R_{1}\gamma^{\mathrm{AI}}_{PP^{-1}}(t)
and

.

These are the geodesic paths from
P
to
P^{-1}
and
Q
to

with respect to the AI metric.

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mathrm{AI}}_{PP^{-1}}(t)=P^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(P^{-\frac{1}{2}}P^{-1}P^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{t}P^{\frac{1}{2}}=P^{1-2t}\equiv P^{x},

(70)

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mathrm{AI}}_{QQ^{-1}}(t)=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-1}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{t}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}=Q^{1-2t}\equiv Q^{x},

where we have denoted
x\equiv 1-2t
for convenience. As
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
move along these geodesics, the generalized fidelity takes the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{R_{1}}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}PP^{\frac{x}{2}}}P^{-x}\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}\right]

(71)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\#P^{\frac{1+x}{4}}QP^{\frac{1+x}{4}}\right].

A similar calculation shows

\operatorname{F}_{R_{2}}(P,Q)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\sqrt{Q^{\frac{x}{2}}PQ^{\frac{x}{2}}}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\#Q^{\frac{1+x}{4}}PQ^{\frac{1+x}{4}}\right].

(72)

These two paths are special because we recover all the three
named fidelities
along these paths:

\displaystyle t=0

\displaystyle\iff x=1\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q),

(73)

\displaystyle t={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\iff x=0\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q),

\displaystyle t=1

\displaystyle\iff x=-1\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{M}}(P,Q).

Moreover, these are paths along which the generalized fidelity varies while being real-valued throughout. Thus, we have a
one parameter
family of fidelities that recover all the three named fidelities. The one-parameter nature becomes even more apparent with the alternative form of generalized fidelity along these paths, as described in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{x}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}V_{x}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(74)

where

\displaystyle U_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{x}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad V_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{x}{2}}\right).

(75)

Proof.

Let
U_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{x}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
, which implies
U_{x}^{*}=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{x}{2}}\right)
. Thus we have

Q^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{x}{2}}=U_{x}^{*}\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}\iff U_{x}=\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}P^{-\frac{x}{2}}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(76)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{x}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}P^{-\frac{x}{2}}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

(77)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\sqrt{P^{\frac{x}{2}}QP^{\frac{x}{2}}}\right]=\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right),

which proves the first claim. Now let
V_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{x}{2}}\right)
, which implies
V_{x}^{*}=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{x}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. Using a similar calculation as above, we have

V_{x}=P^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-\frac{x}{2}}\sqrt{Q^{\frac{x}{2}}PQ^{\frac{x}{2}}},

(78)

which implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}V_{x}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-\frac{x}{2}}\sqrt{Q^{\frac{x}{2}}PQ^{\frac{x}{2}}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

(79)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{Q^{\frac{x}{2}}PQ^{\frac{x}{2}}}Q^{\frac{1-x}{2}}\right]=\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right),

as claimed. This completes the proof.
∎

Note that
\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)\neq\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)
for general values of
x
, except when
x\in{1,0,-1}
, where they are equal. In terms of the geodesic paths
\gamma^{\text{AI}}_{PP^{-1}}(t)
and

and

and

\overline{\operatorname{F}}_{x}(P,Q):=\frac{\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)+\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)}{2}.

(80)

We call this parametrized family of fidelities the

-Polar fidelities
and remark that it is a one-parameter family of fidelities that recover Uhlmann-, Holevo-, and Matsumoto-fidelity at

\mathrm{SU}(d)
, are discussed in Section
5

5
Polar fidelities and interior fidelities

In this section, we introduce a new family of fidelities and show how generalized fidelities can be thought of as elements of the extreme points of this family of fidelities. We begin with recalling one of the equivalent characterizations of generalized fidelity. For any
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we have

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(87)

where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. We call the unitary
U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}
the
unitary factor
of the generalized fidelity
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
.

We first show that the unitary factor of any generalized fidelity has
+1
determinant.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Then the unitary factor
U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}
of
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
has determinant +1. Or equivalently,
U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
.

Proof.

The proof will be done in two steps. We first show that the polar factor of any matrix that is the product of two positive definite matrices is a special unitary matrix. To this end, let
A,B\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. We aim to show that
U:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(AB\right)\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
.
By definition, we have

AB=U|AB|=U\sqrt{BA^{2}B}.

(88)

Now take determinant across to get

\det(A B)=\det(U |A B|)=\det(U)\det(|A B| ).

(89)

The LHS equals
\det(A)\det(B)
. By properties of determinant, we have

\det(|AB|)=\det(\sqrt{A B^2 A})=\sqrt{\det(A B^2 A)}=\sqrt{\det(A)^{2}\det(B)^{2}}=\det(A)\det(B).

(90)

Thus we have
\det(U)=+1
, or
\operatorname{Pol}\left(AB\right)\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
for any
A,B\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Thus we have
U_{P},U_{Q}\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
, and by the group structure of special unitaries, we have
U_{P}U_{Q}^{*}\in\mathrm{SU}(d).

∎

Thus, the unitary factor of
any
generalized fidelity is a special unitary. Let us now recall the definition of
x
-Polar fidelity. For
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we defined

\overline{\operatorname{F}}_{x}(P,Q):=\frac{\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)+\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)}{2}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{U_{x}+V_{x}}{2}\right)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(91)

where
U_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{x}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
V_{x}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{x}{2}}\right)
.
Observe that Polar fidelities are
not
generalized fidelities (except in the cases of
x\in\{1,0,-1\}
). In particular, convex combinations, over bases, of generalized fidelities are not, in general, generalized fidelities. We call such convex combinations of generalized fidelities over a distribution of bases as
Interior fidelities
. We first formally define it and then show why the name is appropriate.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
be a probability vector,
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{P}_{d}
be a
collection of bases
, and let
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Define the
interior fidelity
between
P
and
Q
over
(\mathcal{R},\mu)
as

\operatorname{F}_{(\mathcal{R},\mu)}\!\left(P,Q\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\operatorname{F}_{R_{i}}\!\left(P,Q\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}V_{i}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{V}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(92)

where
V_{i}
is the unitary factor of
\operatorname{F}_{R_{i}}(P,Q)
and
\overline{V}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}V_{i}
is the mean of the unitary factors.

One can also construct the corresponding
interior Bures distance
, and geometrically this would correspond to linearizing the manifold at different points and then taking the average distance over these different linearizations. We also note that since any generalized fidelity reduces to classical fidelity in the commuting case, so do interior fidelities. Thus, interior fidelities are valid quantizations of classical fidelity.

We now note a different kind of geometric property of generalized fidelities and interior fidelities. Specifically, generalized fidelities can be seen to be the
extreme points
of a convex family of fidelities, whose interior points are constituted by interior fidelities.

This correspondence is easier to see once we recall that the set of all unitaries from extreme points of the convex and compact
spectral-norm unit ball

[
5
, Theorem 1.10]
:

\mathcal{B}_{\infty}:=\{K:\|K\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}.

(93)

It then follows from the definition of interior fidelities that the ‘non-commutative part’ is an interior point of
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
. However, we note that the correspondence is not bijective as
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
also contains unitaries that are not special unitaries, whereas the unitary factor of any generalized fidelity is necessarily a special unitary.

Thus, we see that the
x
-Polar fidelity is actually an interior fidelity. We showed that the unitary factor of any generalized fidelity is a special unitary. An open question we pose is whether the converse is true. That is, for any
U\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
, does there exist a triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
such that
U=U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}
? In
[
53
, Remark 5.2]
, it was shown that
\mathrm{SU}(d)
can be generated by the set of polar factors of the product of two positive definite matrices. However, for our open question to be answered affirmatively, we would require that any special unitary can be written by the product of at most two polar factors of the above kind. It is unclear whether this is true. If the question can be answered affirmatively, then it would shed light on a new collection between polar decomposition, the Bures-Wasserstein manifold of positive definite matrices, and the Lie group
\mathrm{SU}(d)
.

6
Block matrix characterization of generalized fidelity

We now show that the generalized fidelity, and thereby the generalized Bures distance, has a Block-matrix representation. This representation is intimately related to the semidefinite program (SDP) for Uhlmann fidelity
[
5
]
and its generalization to SDP for optimal average fidelity
[
12
]
. We will first discuss the SDP for average fidelity (which, in this context, is actually an SDP for
total fidelity
) and then show how one can recover generalized fidelity and generalized Bures distance from it.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
be arbitrarily chosen. Define the matrices
A,B
as

A:=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\mathbb{I}\\
0&0&\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{I}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}_{3d},\quad B:=\begin{pmatrix}P&0&0\\
0&Q&0\\
0&0&R\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{P}_{3d},

(94)

and
\Phi:\mathbb{H}_{3d}\to\mathbb{H}_{3d}
to be the Hermitian preserving map which acts as

\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&M_{22}&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&M_{33}\end{pmatrix}\xmapsto{\Phi}\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}&0&0\\
0&M_{22}&0\\
0&0&M_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

(95)

Consider the SDP
(\Phi,A,B)
whose primal problem is

\displaystyle\rm{maximize}:

\displaystyle\langle A,\mathrm{X}\rangle,

(96)

\displaystyle\rm{subject}\,\,\rm{to}:

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}\geq 0,\quad\Phi(\mathrm{X})=B.

Then the optimal value of this SDP is
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right)
, and the unique optimal feasible point that attains this value is of the form

\mathrm{X}_{\star}:=\begin{pmatrix}P&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&Q&Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&R\end{pmatrix}

(97)

where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward application of results in
[
5
]
and
[
12
]
. The fact that the value of the objective function cannot exceed
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right)
is shown in
[
12
, Lemma 4 (Equation 37)]
. That is, for any feasible point
\mathrm{X}
,

\langle A,\mathrm{X}\rangle\leq\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right).

(98)

To show that the inequality can be saturated, we construct a feasible point that attains the optimal value
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right)
. Later, we will show that it is also the unique optimal feasible point. For
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
, define the
3d\times d
block matrix
T
as follows.

T:=\begin{pmatrix}P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}\\
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}

(99)

Now consider
\mathrm{X}_{\star}:=TT^{*}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}_{\star}=TT^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}\\
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}

(100)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}P&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&Q&Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&R\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}P&Z&Y_{P}\\
Z^{*}&Q&Y_{Q}\\
Y_{P}^{*}&Y_{Q}^{*}&R\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

In the last equality we renamed some quantities for brevity. We then see that

\langle A,\mathrm{X}_{\star}\rangle=\real\operatorname{Tr}[Y_{P}]+\real\operatorname{Tr}[Y_{Q}]=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right),

(101)

which implies that the SDP achieves optimality.

We now prove the uniqueness of the optimal feasible point. We first note that the positivity of
\mathrm{X}_{\star}
necessarily implies the positivity of the principle sub-block matrices

M_{P}:=\begin{pmatrix}P&Y_{P}\\
Y_{P}^{*}&R\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad M_{Q}:=\begin{pmatrix}Q&Y_{Q}\\
Y_{Q}^{*}&R\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

(102)

By
[
5
, Lemma 3.18]
, we have
M_{P}\geq 0
if and only if
Y_{P}=P^{\frac{1}{2}}KR^{\frac{1}{2}}
for some contraction
K:\|K\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Note that
\real\operatorname{Tr}[Y_{P}]
attains the maximum necessarily for some unitary
K
, which is an extreme point of the set of contractions. To see this, observe that

\real\operatorname{Tr}[Y_{P}]=\real\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}KR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]=\real\operatorname{Tr}\left[KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\leq\left\|KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{1},

(103)

where the last equality comes from the variational characterization of trace norm. Now note that for any contraction
K
we have
K^{*}K\leq\mathbb{I}
and thus

P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{*}KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq P^{\frac{1}{2}}RP^{\frac{1}{2}},

(104)

with equality if and only if
K
is a unitary. Since the operator square root function over positive semidefinite matrices is monotonic, we have

\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{*}KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}RP^{\frac{1}{2}}},

(105)

and taking trace across, we have

\left\|KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{*}KR^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]\leq\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{P^{\frac{1}{2}}RP^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,R).

(106)

Thus we see that the inequality is saturated at some unitary. Due to the linearity of the objective function
\operatorname{Tr}[Y_{P}]
, this also necessarily means that the optimum is attained at a unique unitary. This is because if there are two distinct optimal unitaries, by linearity of the objective function, any convex combination of these unitaries would also attain the optimum. However, such a (non-trivial) convex combination will not be a unitary, which would contradict the previous claim. Thus the optimal unitary is unique.

Indeed,
U_{P}
is the unique optimal feasible point that achieves this value for
M_{P}
, and similar reasoning shows that
U_{Q}
is the unique optimal unitary for
M_{Q}
. Thus,
\mathrm{X}_{\star}
is the unique optimal feasible point of the SDP
(\Phi,A,B)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Given the above SDP for a triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
one can
extract
the generalized fidelity
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
and the squared generalized Bures distance
\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)
from the
optimal primal feasible
of SDP. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.2
.

Define the SDP
(\Phi,A,B)
as above for an arbitrary triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Let
\mathrm{X}_{\star}
be the optimal primal feasible:

\langle\mathrm{X_{\star}},A\rangle=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,R\right)+\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(Q,R\right).

(107)

Then,

1.

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\langle K,\mathrm{X}_{\star}\rangle,

2.

\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\langle\frac{K+K^{*}}{2},\mathrm{X}_{\star}\right\rangle

3.

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)=\langle J,\mathrm{X}_{\star}\rangle,

where

K:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad J:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&-\mathbb{I}&0\\
-\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(108)

Proof.

From Proposition
6.1
, we have that the optimal feasible points
\mathrm{X}_{\star}
is of the form

\mathrm{X}_{\star}=\begin{pmatrix}P&Z&Y_{P}\\
Z^{*}&Q&Y_{Q}\\
Y_{P}^{*}&Y_{Q}^{*}&R\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&P^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&Q&Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}&R^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}^{*}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}&R\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

(109)

Taking trace-inner product of
\langle K,X_{\star}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[K^{*}\mathrm{X}_{\star}]
then gives

\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]=\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q).

(110)

A similar calculation yields

\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\langle\frac{K+K^{*}}{2},\mathrm{X}_{\star}\right\rangle.

(111)

Finally, we have

\langle J,\mathrm{X}_{\star}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[J\mathrm{X}_{\star}]=\operatorname{Tr}[P+Q]-2\real\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=:\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q).

(112)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Thus, we have shown a block-matrix characterization of generalized fidelity and squared generalized Bures distance. The SDP used in the characterization is closely related to the SDP for
optimal average fidelity

[
12
]
, which finds a problem that is equivalent to finding the Bures-Wasserstein barycenter of a finite collection of states. Further implications of this relation are explored in the next section.

7
Generalized multivariate fidelities and relation to optimal average fidelity

Let us now consider a slightly different scenario. Let
{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}}
be a collection of quantum states. In
[
21
]
, the authors introduced and studied various notions of multivariate fidelities—fidelities between a collection of
n\geq 2
states. One particular version of multivariate fidelity they define and study is the normalized sum of distinct pairwise fidelities:

\operatorname{F}(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j=1;i\neq j}^{n}\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right).

(113)

For a tuple of states
(\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n})
and a base
\sigma
, one can then define the analogous generalized multivariate fidelity at a state
\sigma\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
as

\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j=1;i\neq j}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right).

(114)

Now consider the problem of finding the state that maximizes the average fidelity over a fixed ensemble, as studied in
[
12
]
. It is shown there that the
fidelity maximzier
, defined as the state that maximizes the total fidelity
f(\rho):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho\right)
, uniquely satisfies the fixed-point equation:

\sigma=\frac{1}{f(\sigma)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{i}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(115)

We note that due to the relation between Uhlmann fidelity and BW barycenter, finding the average fidelity maximize is equivalent to finding the BW barycenter over the same distribution (up to a trace-normalization). One can relate the total fidelity of this maximizer and the generalized multivariate fidelity as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{D}_{d}
be a collection of states and
\sigma:=\argmax_{\rho\in\mathbb{D}_{d}}f(\rho)
be the barycenter of the collection. Then

f(\sigma)^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right).

(116)

Moreover, the generalized multivariate fidelity is related to the average fidelity of the maximizer as follows:

\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n})=\frac{f(\sigma)^{2}-n}{n^{2}-n}.

(117)

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma\sigma^{-1}\sigma

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{f(\sigma)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{i}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\sigma^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{f(\sigma)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(\sigma)^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{i}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}},

where the second equality is by
Equation

115
. Take trace across and rearrange to obtain

\displaystyle f(\sigma)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{i}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right),

(119)

which proves the first claim. Note that
n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{i}\right)
and subtract this quantity from both sides (respectively) to obtain

f(\sigma)^{2}-n=\sum_{i,j=1;i\neq j}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right).

(120)

Divide throughout by
n(n-1)
to obtain

\frac{f(\sigma)^{2}-n}{n^{2}-n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j=1;i\neq j}^{n}\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}\!\left(\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\right)=:\operatorname{F}_{\sigma}(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}),

(121)

which concludes the proof.
∎

8
Uhlmann-(like) theorem for generalized fidelity

We first recall the definition of a purification of a state.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. The
purifications
of
P
is the set of vectors

\emph{Pur}(P):=\left\{|v\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}_{B}:\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\left[|v\rangle\langle v|\right]=P\right\}.

(122)

This set is identical to

\emph{Pur}(P)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}|p_{i}\rangle\otimes|u_{i}\rangle:\{|u_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}\text{ form an orthonormal set}\right\},

(123)

where
P=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}|p_{i}\rangle\langle p_{i}|
is the eigendecomposition of
P
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. The
canonical purification
of
P
is given by

|P\rangle=(P^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\mathbb{I})|\Omega\rangle,

(124)

where
|\Omega\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i,i\rangle
is the unnormalized canonical Bell state. Since there is a unitary degree of freedom in the second space, we may write an arbitrary purification as

|P_{U}\rangle\equiv\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes U\right)|\Omega\rangle,

(125)

for an arbitrary
U\in\mathbb{U}_{d}
. An arbitrary purification of
Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
can similarly be written as

|Q_{V}\rangle\equiv\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes V\right)|\Omega\rangle,

(126)

for an arbitrary
V\in\mathbb{U}_{d}
. Their overlap is given by

\langle Q_{V},P_{U}\rangle=\left\langle\Omega,\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes V^{*}U\right)\Omega\right\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}(V^{*}U)^{\top}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}U^{\top}\overline{V}\right],

(127)

where
\overline{V}
denotes the complex conjugate of
V
. Uhlmann’s theorem states that the maximum (absolute-valued) overlap is achieved when
U^{\top}\overline{V}=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. This result also allows us to state an
Uhlmann’s theorem
for generalized fidelity.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
. Consider the purifications of
P
and
Q
defined as

|P_{U_{P}^{\top}}\rangle:=\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes U_{P}^{\top}\right)|\Omega\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad|Q_{U_{Q}^{\top}}\rangle:=\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes U_{Q}^{\top}\right)|\Omega\rangle,

(128)

where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. Then

\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\left\langle P_{U_{P}^{\top}},Q_{U_{Q}^{\top}}\right\rangle.

(129)

Proof.

Let
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. Consider the purifications

|P_{U_{P}^{\top}}\rangle=\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes U_{P}^{\top}\right)|\Omega\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad|Q_{U_{Q}^{\top}}\rangle=\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes U_{Q}^{\top}\right)|\Omega\rangle.

(130)

Now consider the overlap

\displaystyle\langle P_{U_{P}^{\top}},Q_{U_{Q}^{\top}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Omega,\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\overline{U}_{P}U_{Q}^{\top}\right)\Omega\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\left(\overline{U}_{P}U_{Q}^{\top}\right)^{\top}\right]

(131)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]=\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q).

This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, the generalized fidelity
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)
can be seen as the overlap of a pair of specific purifications of
P
and
Q
, with the choice of purification depending on
R
.

9
An analogous generalization of some Rényi divergences

We now briefly extend the formalism of generalized fidelities to quantum Rényi divergences. We refer to
[
20
,
54
,
55
]
for a detailed treatment. We restrict our treatment to normalized states (probability vectors and density matrices), keeping in mind that generalization to non-normalized states is straightforward and can be seen in the previously mentioned references.

Quantum Rényi divergences are
quantizations
of
classical
Rényi divergences
[
56
,
57
]
, which, for probability vectors
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, are defined as

D_{\alpha}(p\|q):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{\alpha}q_{i}^{1-\alpha}&\text{ if }p\ll q\text{ or }\alpha<1,\\
\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(132)

for
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
. In subsequent discussions, we also restrict our attention to the case
\text{supp }P\subset\text{supp }Q
(and thus conveniently ignore the cases where the divergence diverges to
\infty
).

As with classical fidelity, there is no unique way of generalizing this to positive semidefinite matrices. For an axiomatic approach see
[
20
,
54
,
55
]
. Here, we will mention the definitions of various well-studied quantum Rényi divergences and show how some of them can be unified by defining a quantity inspired by generalized fidelity. Every quantum Rényi divergence we study is of the form

D_{\alpha}(P\|Q):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log f_{\alpha}(P,Q),

(133)

where
f_{\alpha}(\rho,\sigma)
is a trace functional can be thought of as the (asymmetric)
fidelity part
of the divergence. Since the relation between
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)
and
f_{\alpha}(P,Q)
follows directly, we will restrict our attention to the trace functional
f_{\alpha}(P,Q)
.

The first family of divergences we discuss is the family of
\alpha
-
z
divergences
[
20
]
, which is defined as

D_{\alpha,z}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2z}}Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{z}}P^{\frac{\alpha}{2z}}\right)^{z}\right],

(134)

for
P\ll Q
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{1\}
(with the limit being taken for
\alpha\to 1
) and
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
(with limit being taken for
z\to 0
). This family unifies various various quantum Rényi divergences. In particular, set
\alpha=z
to obtain the
sandwich relative entropy
of order
\alpha
:

D_{\alpha,\alpha}(P\|Q)=D_{\alpha}^{\text{S}}:=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}PQ^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right].

(135)

Setting
z=1
recovers the
Petz-Rényi divergence of order
\alpha
:

D_{\alpha,1}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{PR}}_{\alpha}(P\|Q):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}[P^{\alpha}Q^{1-\alpha}].

(136)

Setting
z=1-\alpha
recovers the
reverse sandwich relative entropy
of order
\alpha
:

D_{\alpha,1-\alpha}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{RS}}_{\alpha}(P\|Q):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}QP^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}\right)\right].

(137)

From the sandwiched relative entropy, one can recover other divergences such as the
min-relative entropy
,
Umegaki quantum relative entropy
, and the
max-relative entropy
:

\displaystyle D_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{min}}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle:=-2\log\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}\!\left(P,Q\right),

(138)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 1}D_{\alpha,\alpha}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{Um}}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P\left(\log P-\log Q\right)\right],

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}D_{\alpha,\alpha}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{max}}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{\lambda:P\leq 2^{\lambda}Q\}.

Another quantum generalization of Rényi divergence is based on the AI geodesic between
P
and
Q
. Specifically, the
\alpha
-geometric Rényi divergence

[
58
,
59
,
60
]
between
P
and
Q
is defined as

D_{\alpha}^{\text{G}}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}[Q\#_{\alpha}P]=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}PQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

(139)

Thus, the quantity was first introduced in
[
58
]
and is the largest quantum Rényi divergence that satisfies data processing inequality. It is also known
[
58
,
54
,
60
]
that for
P\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}

\lim_{\alpha\to 1}D_{\alpha}^{\text{G}}(P\|Q)=D^{\text{BS}}(P\|Q):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P\log\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-1}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right],

(140)

where
D^{\text{BS}}(P\|Q)
is the
Belavkin-Staszewski
relative entropy
[
61
]
.

We will now define a quantity inspired by the definition of generalized fidelity, which can recover many of the above-mentioned divergences. In particular, the quantity we define will recover the following divergences

D^{\text{S}}_{\alpha},D^{\text{PR}}_{\alpha},D^{\text{RS}}_{\alpha},\text{ and }D^{\text{G}}_{\alpha}.

(141)

Since the sandwich relative entropy, in turn, can recover the min-relative entropy
D^{\text{min}}
, Umegaki relative entropy
D^{\text{Um}}
, and max-relative entropy
D^{\text{max}}
and the geometric Rényi divergence has the Belavkin-Staszewski
D^{\text{BS}}
as its
\alpha\to 1
limit, the quantity we define would recover these divergences too. We note that this section is meant for introductory and illustrative purposes alone, and further information-theoretic properties are deferred to a future article.

The key idea is to define a
base-dependent
quantity that generalizes the trace functional term. Similar to generalized fidelity, this term is complex in general, and therefore, we will only use its real part.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
. Define

\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\real\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}(P,Q),

(142)

where the trace functional
F_{R}^{\alpha}
is defined as

\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}R^{-1}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right].

(143)

We firstly note that in the
classical scenario
, where
P,Q
and
R
mutually commute, the above quantity reduces to the (classical) Rényi divergence.

We now show how the above quantity recovers the previously mentioned divergences. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 9.2
.

For
P\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, define
\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)
as above. Then we have

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{PR}}(P\|Q)\quad

(Petz-Rényi)

(144)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{S}}(P\|Q)\quad

(Sandwich)

(145)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=P^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{RS}}(P\|Q)\quad

(Reverse sandwich)

(146)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=Q^{-1}\quad

\displaystyle\implies\quad\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=D_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{G}}(P\|Q)\quad

(Geometric)

(147)

Proof.

We only need to work with the trace functional
\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right)
as its relation to
\hat{D}_{\alpha,R}
follows directly. Recall the form of the trace functional:

\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}R^{-1}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right].

(148)

Choosing
R=\mathbb{I}
, we easily see that

\operatorname{F}_{\mathbb{I}}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right)=\operatorname{Tr}[P^{\alpha}Q^{1-\alpha}],

(149)

which leads to the Petz-Rényi divergence.
For the Sandwich Rényi relative entropy, we choose
R=Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}
. Substituting in the trace functional, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right):

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}R^{-1}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

(150)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}PQ^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}Q^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\left(Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}QQ^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}PQ^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}Q^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}PQ^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\right],

which corresponds to the sandwich Rényi relative entropy. For the reverse sandwich Rényi relative entropy, set
R=P^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{R}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right):

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}PR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}R^{-1}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}QR^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

(151)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}PP^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}\right)^{\alpha}P^{\frac{-\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}QP^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}P^{\frac{-\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}QP^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(P^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}QP^{\frac{\alpha}{2(1-\alpha)}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right],

which is the trace functional that defines the reverse sandwich Rényi relative entropy. Finally, choose
R=Q^{-1}
to obtain the
\alpha
-geometric Rényi divergence:

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{Q^{-1}}^{\alpha}\left(P,Q\right):

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}PQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}Q\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}QQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\right]

(152)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}PQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}Q\cdot\mathbb{I}^{1-\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}PQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\alpha}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[Q\#_{\alpha}P\right],

which is the trace functional in the definition of
\alpha
-geometric Rényi divergence. This concludes the proof.
∎

10
Open problems

We now discuss some related open problems.

1.

Data Processing Inequality.
For a given pair
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, for what values of the base
R
does the squared generalized Bures distance satisfy the data processing inequality (DPI)? Equivalently, for what values of
R
does the following inequality hold for any quantum channel
\Phi
?

\operatorname{B}_{R}(P,Q)\overset{?}{\geq}\operatorname{B}_{\Phi(R)}\left((\Phi(P),\Phi(Q)\right).

(153)

Preliminary numerical experiments have not identified any instances that violate DPI.

2.

Convexity.
A related open question is regarding (joint) convexity of generalized Bures distance (and/or its squared version) in
P,Q
, and
R
. These appear to be difficult problems, and perhaps it would be easier to tackle these questions for
x
-Polar fidelities (for
x\in[-1,1]
).

3.

Recovering
z
-fidelities from generalized fidelities.
Can the
z
-fidelities and/or the Log-Euclidean fidelity be written as the generalized fidelity (or interior fidelity) for some choice of base (or a distribution over bases)? An affirmative answer to the above problem might also lead to the analogous generalization for more members of the
\alpha
-
z
divergences.

4.

Monotonicity of polar fidelities.
In the discussion of Paths 5 and 6, we remarked on the numerical observation that the generalized fidelity was monotonic along these curves. Does this observation always hold? That is, for any
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
and any pair
x,y\in[-1,1]
, does the following statement hold true?

x\geq y\implies\begin{cases}\operatorname{F}_{P^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)\geq\operatorname{F}_{P^{y}}\!\left(P,Q\right),\\
\operatorname{F}_{Q^{x}}\!\left(P,Q\right)\geq\operatorname{F}_{Q^{y}}\!\left(P,Q\right).\\
\end{cases}

(154)

An affirmative answer would also imply that the
x
-Polar fidelity is monotonic in
x
in the range
[-1,1]
. This numerically observed monotonic is also in line with the known relation regarding the named fidelities:

\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{M}}(P,Q)\leq\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q)\leq\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}(P,Q),

(155)

for any
P,Q\geq 0
. Moreover, it is also known
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{M}}
is the smallest and
\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{U}}
is the largest quantization of classical fidelity that satisfies DPI
[
18
]
. Thus, the family of
x
-Polar fidelities (in the range
x\in[-1,1]
) might be helpful in studying fidelities that satisfy the data processing inequality.

5.

Other bases for Holevo fidelity.

Another open problem we pose is whether other non-trivial bases exist where generalized fidelity recovers Holevo fidelity. Recall that we showed that
R=\mathbb{I}
implies
\operatorname{F}_{R}(P,Q)=\operatorname{F}^{\mathrm{H}}(P,Q)
. In fact, this is the only choice of
R
where we showed this equality for a general (non-commuting) pair of matrices
P,Q
. Are there other bases on which the generalized fidelity reduces to Holevo fidelity? To find such a non-trivial base, it would suffice to find
R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
such that

\operatorname{Pol}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}P^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\operatorname{Pol}\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(156)

We believe that results from
[
53
]
would be helpful in this endeavor.

6.

SDP representation.
The next question we ask is whether (the real part of) generalized fidelity or squared generalized Bures distance has a true semidefinite program representation. Though we have presented a block-matrix characterization of generalized fidelity, it is not an SDP. An SDP formulation could be vastly beneficial in optimization problems involving generalized Bures distance, such as in a potential formulation of metric learning
[
62
,
63
]
.

7.

Unitary factor of generalized fidelity and
\mathrm{SU}(d)
.
The final open problem we pose is asking whether the set of unitary factors of generalized fidelity and the set of special unitaries are the same. We have shown that the set of unitary factors forms a subset of
\mathrm{SU}(d)
, but it is unclear whether the reverse inclusion holds. To prove this, one would have to show that any special unitary
U\in\mathrm{SU}_{d}
has the form

U=U_{Q}U_{P}^{*}

(157)

for a triple
P,Q,R\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, where
U_{P}:=\operatorname{Pol}\left(P^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and
U_{Q}=\operatorname{Pol}\left(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
.
An affirmative answer would illuminate a connection between the Bures-Wasserstein manifold, generalized fidelity, and the Lie group
\mathrm{SU}(d)
.

11
Conclusion and discussions

In this paper, we introduce a family of fidelities between positive definite matrices that generalize and unify various existing quantum fidelities. The definition is motivated by the Riemannian geometry of the Bures-Wasserstein manifold, and it endows the existing fidelities with novel geometric interpretations. We also define and study the generalization of the closely related Bures-Wasserstein distance.

After studying the basic properties of these objects, we proved several remarkable geometric properties of generalized fidelity including invariance and covariance properties of generalized fidelity along geodesic-related paths related to the Bures-Wasserstein, Affine-invariant, and Euclideain Riemannian metrics on the manifold of positive definite matrices.

We then showed how convex combinations of generalized fidelity define a new family of fidelities. One such family, the
Polar fidelity
, is shown to be a family of fidelities parametrized by a single real number that recovers Uhlmann-, Holevo-, and Matsumoto fidelity. We then derived a block-matrix characterization of generalized fidelity and squared generalized Bures-Wasserstein distance and showed an interesting relation between generalized fidelity, multivariate fidelities, and Bures-Wasserstein barycenters.

We also discussed an
Uhlmann-like
theorem for generalized fidelity, and finally, we demonstrated how our formalism can also be extended to generalized certain quantum Rényi divergences. We then discussed various open problems.

We conclude this section by discussing potential applications of generalized Bures distance in quantum and classical machine learning. Distances are crucial in machine learning as we often embed data points in high-dimensional metric spaces, and often, we find that different distance metrics (and divergences) are suitable for different tasks. In particular, consider the problem of
Metric learning

[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
]
. The problem can be succinctly stated as follows: find the distance metric that is
best
suited to represent the data. For example, one could consider the case where we are given pairwise distances between many data points (represented by vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and we are interested in finding the positive definite matrix that defines the Mahalanobis distance which would be most consistent with the given distances
[
63
]
. Metric learning has applications in various machine learning fields, including classification algorithms like the K Nearest Neighbours algorithm
[
66
]
and dimensionality reduction problems
[
67
,
68
]
.

Generalized Bures distance could extend the problem to the setting where the data points are positive definite matrices or quantum states. Specifically, given a collection of states and associated data (such as pairwise distances or labels for each state), find a suitable base for the given task. For example, this could be a classification problem where the states are given labels indicating membership in disjoint classes. Then, the task would be to find a base such that the generalized Bures distance at this base increases
interclass
distance while decreasing
intraclass
distance. Thus, metric learning based on generalized Bures distance could benefit various classical ML problems involving the BW manifold or even quantum variants of the aforementioned (classical) ML problems
[
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
.
