Community organization capacity and barriers to mental health promotion efforts: a configurational analysis
Authors: Nathaniel Woodard, Maisha R Huq, Edward J Miech, Deborah L Bors, Amna Hussain, Cheryl L Knott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health concerns have been on the rise in recent years, especially in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Minoritized racial and ethnic groups are especially at risk for untreated mental health concerns. Mental health promotion via culturally relevant approaches in community settings serving minoritized groups can help to address mental health concerns among these populations. However, mental health promotion activities require resources reflective of organizational capacity. The current study investigates the role of organizational capacity and characteristics in barriers to mental health promotion among community organizations.MethodsWe surveyed 21 leaders of distinct community organizations (e.g., mosques, housing communities, and public libraries) in Maryland to assess their organizational capacity, characteristics, and barriers to mental health promotion. Data were analyzed using configurational analysis to identify characteristics that distinguished organizations with low, medium, and high levels of barriers to mental health promotion.ResultsWhile no single variable explained the level of barriers to mental health promotion, characteristics differentiating the level of barriers experienced by organizations in the current sample included county in which the organization was located, leader interest in health promotion, number of members in the organization, organization type (e.g., mosque or housing community), presence of a health team, and budget for health promotion. Background

Mental health, as defined by the World Health Organization, is mental well-being enabling individuals to cope with stress, function well in life, and contribute to society [1]. Despite overall gains made in many health outcomes, mental health outcomes have declined in recent decades, especially among young adults and adolescents [2]. Some research suggests that this decline in mental health may have been further exacerbated in recent years in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic [3]. Despite rising mental health concerns, there is an underutilization of mental health care in the United States, especially among minoritized racial and ethnic groups [4]. This is especially concerning given research that suggests the additional need for mental health care among racially and ethnically minoritized populations due to experiences of racism [5]. Beyond physical barriers to accessing mental health care (e.g., lack of available/accessible mental health care), examples of social or cultural barriers believed to discourage the utilization of mental health care include constructs like social stigma, lack of diversity and/or cultural humility among providers, and lack of trust earned by healthcare systems [6–8]. Conducting programming to improve mental health using community-engaged and culturally-relevant methods can help to overcome these barriers and address mental health concerns among those with the greatest need in underserved communities [9].

Health promotion, or the process of empowering people to improve their health, is increasingly common among community organizations serving traditionally minoritized populations often affected most by disparities [10–12]. This is because health promotion within community settings has implications for improving health outcomes among the same minoritized populations that are frequently missed in other approaches [10–12]. Health promotion in community settings frequently includes efforts like health education (e.g., sharing educational pamphlets or health education classes), providing access to care (e.g., on site health screening events, clinicians, or health counselors), and opportunities to engage in healthy behaviors (e.g., physical activity groups) [10, 12]. Interventions through community and organizational settings have been designed to tackle a number of health issues in a variety of populations [13–16]. Many community organizations have also engaged in health promotion efforts without a reliance on external interventionists [17–21], including for mental health promotion, or the process of empowering people to improve their mental health [17]. Beyond making mental health care more physically or geographically accessible, through such trusted organizations already embedded within communities, mental health promotion can help to circumvent social and cultural barriers community members face when accessing mental health care by helping to reduce social stigma within the community and having established trust that healthcare systems may not have earned [4, 5, 22]. However, just as barriers discourage or prevent individuals from accessing mental health care, similar barriers may prevent community organizations from including mental health promotion in their array of activities.

To successfully incorporate health promotion into their existing activities, an organization requires the ability to implement those health promotion efforts [23, 24]. Organizational capacity includes the structures and/or resources (e.g., funding and staffing) of an organization that affect its ability to execute a goal or action [23, 24]. The absence of these resources, or competing need for these resources between various organization-wide efforts, can serve as barriers to mental health promotion activities in these settings. Research summarizing work from over 80 studies has concluded that greater organizational capacity (e.g., financial resources and staffing) can positively affect program implementation [25], such as adoption [26] and fidelity [27], while reduced organizational capacity (e.g., staffing turnover) contributes to poorer implementation, including poorer fidelity and penetration outcomes [28]. Organizational capacity is especially critical in the context of community organizations implementing health activities, as they regularly operate with limited resources. These limited resources may often be allocated first and foremost to a primary mission (e.g., social, religious, educational) that does not explicitly include health promotion [29, 30]. Robust organizational capacity could allow organizations to overcome and/or eliminate barriers to integrating mental health promotion into their regular activities, yet this remains an understudied area.

The present study

The current study sought to assess the role of organizational characteristics and capacity for health promotion in organization-level barriers to mental health promotion among 21 community organizations in the state of Maryland. We expected that organizations presenting more favorable organizational capacity characteristics (e.g., sufficient financial resources, an existing health team, more members) would report fewer barriers to mental health promotion. We employed Coincidence Analysis (CNA), a recent and dynamic addition to the larger family of configurational comparative methods, to conduct this study [31]. Results from this research can inform future health promotion research and intervention in community settings.

Methods

Survey methods

Data used in the current analysis were collected as part of a project to understand the future of health promotion in community organizations from the perspectives of community organization constituents within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study enrolled a convenience sample of 22 community organizations. Brief (~ 10 min) questionnaires about health promotion culture, capacity, and activity were administered to 21 leaders from these organizations in the summer of 2022; one organization did not supply a response to the survey after three contact attempts. Eligible organization leaders were at least 18 years of age, willing and able to understand and respond to survey items presented in English, and self-identified leaders of a mosque, public library, low-income housing community, or university-affiliated fraternity/sorority that had at least 15 members, held an organization-wide meeting or event at least once per month, and were located in Maryland [30]. Mosques, public libraries, low-income housing communities, and university-affiliated fraternities/sororities were identified as community organizations for inclusion in the current study due to their intimate ties with community members and prior utilization in research as settings for community-based health promotion [17, 19, 20, 32].

Study recruitment occurred via email, phone, and word of mouth. Researcher relationships with community organizations were leveraged first to identify potentially eligible and interested organizations, after which outreach via publicly available contact information (e.g., posted online) and purposive snowball sampling were used to recruit additional community organizations to participate. The response rate among organizations approached to be enrolled in the study was 22/64 or 34.4%, while the percentage of organizations that participated by providing a questionnaire response was 32.8% (or 21/64). Organization leaders were provided with a $10 electronic gift card incentive for their completion of the questionnaire.

Measures

The current survey instrument was informed by existing research [24, 33], reviewed by community health experts, pilot tested with community partners, and revised based on their feedback before data collection began. An organization-level demographics module including organization size, county, and type was derived from prior work in faith-based settings and included as part of the survey [33]. A full list of the demographic and other survey items included throughout this analysis is available in Appendix A.

Organizational capacity

Organizational capacity for health promotion was assessed using items derived from the Faith-Based Organization Capacity Inventory (FBO-CI) [24] and supplemented by researcher- and community partner-generated items based on a review of the literature (e.g., “Is there regular internet access in the building?”). The FBO-CI was designed to evaluate organizational capacity in faith-based settings and includes three subscales: staffing and space (e.g., number of full-time staff, number of part-time staff; α = 0.79), health promotion experience (e.g., presence of health ministry; α = 0.83), and external collaboration (e.g., collaborations with outside organizations, receipt of technical assistance from outside partners; α = 0.67) [24]. Given that the current application was intended to span various types of community organizations, the FBO-CI items were reviewed and edited for wider applicability (e.g., presence of a health ministry was replaced with presence of a health/wellness team). All revised and new capacity items were reviewed by experts in community health promotion before use. Individual capacity indicators were assigned categorical values (e.g., yes/no, present/absent, low/intermediate/high, etc.) based on item response scales and data distributions to make them suitable for configurational analysis.

Barriers to mental health promotion within the community organization

Barriers to mental health promotion within the community organizations were assessed using a checklist of 10 researcher- and community partner-generated items, with an opportunity to write in another response option not captured in the list. Checklist items were developed in the absence of a readily available scale assessing barriers to mental health promotion within community organizations. Item development was informed by a review of the literature and community organization leader responses from qualitative interviews about health promotion culture, capacity, and activity in their organization [30]. Generated items were reviewed and revised by experts in community health promotion. Items were constructed in plain language to promote reader understanding and ability to respond appropriately. Phrasing for the parent item was, “Which of the following would you consider barriers to providing mental health programming at your organization? Mental health programming can include information/events on wellness, depression, anxiety, and suicide, and other mental health topics. Mark all that apply.” Checklist items included, “Our leadership does not see a need for it,” “Members do not see a need for it,” “Our leadership does not always support mental health programming,” “Members do not always support mental health programming,” “We do not know where to find programming customized for our community’s specific needs,” “We do not have access to experts to lead the programming,” “Members most in need do not attend our events,” “Members may be uncomfortable (for example, feel shame) attending an event on mental health in this setting,” “Attending an event on mental health in this setting could lead other members to treat you differently,” “Members feel spiritual information is good enough for mental health, and mental health programming is not needed,” and “Other, please specify ____.” “Other” responses, reported by only two respondents, were reviewed by the research team to determine whether the responses fell into one of the remaining 10 checklist options or whether new checklist response options were warranted. The team concluded that additional response options were not warranted based on participant responses for “other” barriers, and responses were collapsed into existing checklist options (e.g., “stigma” was collapsed into the “Attending an event on mental health in this setting could lead other members to treat you differently” code). A sum score of barriers (minimum of 0, maximum of 10) was derived from participant responses and scores were broken into three categories using a data-driven approach to make them suitable for configurational analysis: 0–1 barriers, 2–3 barriers, and 4 or more barriers.

Analysis

Coincidence analysis (CNA) was used to identify key organizational conditions linked to barriers to mental health promotion in the included community settings. CNA is a mathematical, case-based approach that applies Boolean algebra, logic, and set theory to identify the difference-making conditions that uniquely distinguish one group from another. CNA searches for minimally necessary and sufficient conditions for an outcome to appear and models both Boolean conjunctivity (when several conditions must appear together for the outcome to appear) and disjunctivity (when multiple paths lead to an identical outcome) [34–36]. CNA can be used across samples of various sizes, including small-n studies. CNA has an established and rapidly growing literature in health services research, with over 40 peer-reviewed articles appearing in the health services research literature alone since 2020 [29, 31, 37–44].

Factor selection

The exploratory phase of the analysis began with 43 factors. Following the same approach used in prior CNA studies [37–39, 41, 42], data was reduced using a configurational approach. The “minimally sufficient conditions” (msc) function within the R “cna” package was applied to scan the full dataset in order to identify configurations of conditions with strong connections to the outcomes (i.e., barriers to mental health promotion in the community organization). This process exhaustively considered all one-, two-, and three-condition configurations present in the dataset, evaluated each configuration against a prespecified threshold for consistency, and then generated a “condition table” that listed and organized all configurations that satisfied this criterion.

The msc function was run at 5 different consistency levels in the current study (75%, 80,%, 85%, 90%, 95%) in order to compare output at different thresholds and ensure that results were not artifacts of any one particular consistency setting [42, 44, 45]. When reviewing this mathematical output, we focused on configurations with the highest coverage scores among their peers (i.e., configurations with the same number of conditions) that were also consistent with logic, theory, and prior knowledge. We then used that condition-level information to guide selection of a smaller subset of factors to carry forward into the next phase of model development.

Model development

In the next step of the analysis, we iteratively developed models using the “cna” software package in R. CNA models have two main parameters of fit: consistency and coverage. Consistency indicates how reliably a model yields an outcome and represents how often cases with the outcome of interest are correctly identified by the model. Coverage refers to the explanatory breadth of a model and represents how many cases with the outcome of interest are accounted for by the model.

We selected final models that met al.l of the following criteria: ≥ 80% scores for both consistency and coverage; the same set of factors (taking on different values) explained both the presence and the absence of the outcome; and no model ambiguity [45–47]. The Coincidence Analysis package (“cna”) in R version 3.5.0 [48] and R Studio version 1.1.383 were used to support the analyses.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All study methods and procedures were approved by the University of Maryland Institutional Review Board. All participants were treated in accord with Declaration of Helsinki and provided their informed consent to participate in the study.

Results

Table 1 depicts descriptive statistics describing characteristics and organizational capacity indicators of participating organizations. Of the 21 organizations represented, five were low-income housing community sites, seven were public libraries, five were mosques, and four were university-affiliated fraternities/sororities. Organizations were dispersed throughout several Maryland counties, with leaders most often reporting organization localization in Baltimore City or Prince George’s County, MD. All participating organizations had conducted some kind of prior health promotion efforts. Most organizations owned the building that they operated in (61.9%) and had an active health/wellness team (57.1%). Most (71.4%) reported a sufficient budget for health promotion efforts, and nearly all (90.5%) reported that they had access to qualified experts that could lead health programming in their organization. Organization size was distributed evenly across three categories with seven organizations each: 0 to 99 members, 100 to 399 members, and 400 or more members (Median = 300, IQR = [50, 1250]). Eight organizations (38.1%) reported zero or one barrier to mental health promotion, nine (42.9%) reported two or three barriers, and four (19%) reported four or more barriers.

CNA analyses

Table 2 shows a visualization of the CNA model for three levels of barriers (low/intermediate/high) to mental health promotion reported by participating community organizations. For each of the three levels, more than one pathway was linked with the outcome (i.e., more than one pattern of organizational characteristics was observed relating to the outcome). Organizations that experienced the lowest level of barriers to mental health promotion (i.e., zero or one barrier) were from Baltimore City and saw leader interest in health promotion rise amidst the COVID-19 pandemic or were mosques with 100–399 members; this model had 100% consistency (i.e., cases with the outcome of interest were correctly identified by the model in all instances) and coverage (i.e., all of the cases could be explained by the model). Organizations that experienced the intermediate level of barriers to mental health promotion (i.e., two or three barriers) were located in Prince George’s County or had a health team and the greatest number of active members (i.e., 400+); this model had 100% consistency and 88.9% coverage (i.e., 88.9% of cases could be explained by the current model; 11.1%, or one case, could not be explained by the current model). Finally, organizations with the highest level of barriers (i.e., four or more barriers) were from Baltimore City and had reductions in leader interest in health promotion amidst the COVID-19 pandemic or did not have a health/wellness team, had the greatest number of active members (i.e., 400+), and reported a sufficient budget to support health promotion; this model had 100% consistency and coverage. The overall CNA model used only five explanatory factors total, all of which retained their original categorical values (i.e., no further data transformations were required).

Discussion

The current study identified organizational characteristics and indicators of capacity that can help explain barriers to mental health promotion among various community settings (i.e., mosques, public libraries, low-income housing communities, and university-affiliated fraternities/sororities). As most evidence on organizational factors is in the context of clinical or quasi-clinical settings (e.g., community mental health centers, primary care), our findings in non-clinical community organizations –though exploratory— provide insight and may inform future research. There was no single variable explaining differences between the low, medium, and high barrier groups. However, critical organization characteristics related to barriers to mental health promotion in the current sample included county in which the organization was located, leader interest in health promotion, organization size (i.e., number of members), organization type (e.g., mosque or housing community), presence of a health team, and budget for health promotion.

The significance of location (i.e., county) in the current analysis may suggest the influence of location, or other location-derived organizational characteristics, in determining organization barriers to mental health promotion. For instance, the resources, environment, socio-cultural context, and health priorities may differ from one location to the next. The lack of resources, unsuitable environment, cultural opposition, and competing priorities promoted within a county context could all serve as barriers to implementing mental health promotion in community settings. The intersection of location and leader interest in the current analysis is especially striking.

The current findings support prior research that suggests the importance of leadership buy-in to ensure successful health, including mental health, promotion efforts [26, 49, 50]. Increased leader interest in health promotion due to the COVID-19 pandemic was one of the factors linked to fewer barriers to mental health promotion in Baltimore; conversely, reductions in leader interest were linked with reporting the greatest number of barriers to mental health promotion among organizations in Baltimore. This finding may suggest that while the context of location may be influential in determining barriers to mental health promotion among community organizations, location does not supersede leadership commitment to health promotion. Following this finding, future research should explore how to cultivate leadership buy-in for mental health promotion efforts in non-clinical community organizations. Additionally, although trials have demonstrated the role of leadership buy-in on implementation outcomes, potential downstream impacts on mental health outcomes have rarely been examined [51]. Future research examining the potential links between leadership buy-in and downstream mental health outcomes is warranted.

Organization size has frequently been identified as a critical correlate of health promotion success in prior research [18, 21, 29, 52, 53]. The current study demonstrated that a large membership size does not necessarily translate to fewer barriers to mental health promotion. While number of volunteers, driven in part by membership size, is a frequent source of person power for programming in community organizations [21], larger membership was linked with greater barriers to mental health promotion in the current analysis. However, medium size was indicative of very few barriers to mental health promotion among mosques in this study. It may be that larger organizations are particularly at risk of having competing priorities, a higher proportion of members who may be less actively engaged in the organization and its activities, or communication about organization-wide efforts is more difficult given the larger membership size. These findings are corroborated by recent work in African American churches, suggesting that cohesion and prioritization may be easier to establish within smaller churches that may also have fewer competing priorities on their calendars of events [29]. Future research identifying the mechanisms (e.g., communication challenges, competing priorities, weaker cohesion) by which larger membership contributes to greater barriers to mental health promotion is warranted. This research could inform tailored mental health promotion efforts at larger organizations.

While one might expect that a sufficient budget for health promotion would be linked to fewer barriers to mental health promotion, a stronger budget did not guarantee fewer barriers in the current analysis. Organizations with sufficient budgets but the greatest number of members (i.e., 400+) and no health/wellness team reported the greatest number of barriers. Further, this finding suggests that large membership size may not always be indicative of greater capacity. The lack of a health/wellness team among these organizations may again suggest their competing priorities or shortage of available and committed members to successfully engage in health promotion. This finding may also suggest the importance of establishing a health or wellness team in community organizations to reduce their barriers to mental health promotion. Current findings reflecting the importance of leader interest and health team presence may also suggest the value of having an organizational representative to champion a health effort, which has been highlighted in prior research [54, 55].

Community organizations must navigate various obstacles to successfully engage in mental health promotion, some organizations more than others. Some organizations may be well-prepared for swift engagement in mental health promotion efforts, while others may require additional time or support. For those requiring additional support, some of their organizational characteristics linked to barriers to mental health promotion may be malleable. For example, interventions providing resources and/or support to a community organization can aid in the development of a health team for health promotion, which was tied to fewer barriers to mental health promotion in the current study. Interest in health promotion among organizational leadership (another characteristic linked to fewer barriers to mental health promotion) may be moldable if their awareness and perceived ability to address the health issue are raise, or if their membership calls for it. Efforts to address these malleable characteristics linked to fewer barriers may increase mental health promotion efforts in the organizational setting. In many instances, however, it may not be feasible (or desirable) to alter the characteristics of a community organization (e.g., to relocate the organization geographically or reduce membership size). It is imperative to understand how to engage these organizations in mental health promotion when their key characteristics are not malleable, presenting opportunities for further investigation within the current line of research.

Strengths/Limitations

Study strengths include an emphasis on community health promotion approaches to mental health care. Historical maltreatment of many communities in society, healthcare systems, and research contexts, has generated mistrust and low utilization of traditional sources of care [56–59]. As such, it is critical to explore other pathways of health promotion and health care delivery, such as through community settings [10–12]. The current study assessed community organization capacity for health promotion and implications for barriers to mental health promotion among trusted community organizations, using configurational analysis to identify characteristics that distinguished organizations with low, medium, and high levels of barriers to mental health promotion. Among other findings, the current findings point to organizational conditions that could reduce organizational barriers to mental health promotion. Though it would have been desirable to include more study sites to increase statistical power and generalizability, this was not feasible in the current study context due to funding limitations. We were, however, able to examine a variety of indicators of organizational characteristics and capacity to identify those that were co-present with fewer barriers to mental health promotion using CNA analysis, which is appropriate for small-n studies.

Regarding limitations, these same organizational capacity indicators may have served as proxies for other factors not included in the evaluation. Qualitative inquiry may help elucidate the mechanisms of the relationships observed in the current study and whether the indicators of organizational capacity involved in those relationships could be a proxy for additional factors to examine in future research. This study also relied on data self-reported by one leader from each community organization, which is subject to self-report bias, their understanding of the constructs included in the questionnaire, human error, and any limitations in the organizational knowledge of the leader. While leaders are often very knowledgeable about their organizations and are frequently identified to provide organization-level data [60], a single organizational constituent (including an identified leader) is not likely to have intimate knowledge of every aspect of an organization [61]. Response rate in the current study was 34.4% and data were not collected from those organizations not participating in the study, precluding comparisons of those that did, and did not, participate in the current study. Additionally, geographic restriction of the current study to Maryland and the absence of demographic data of the populations served by the organizations represented further limit the study. As such, the generalizability of the current study to other organizational contexts is limited, as there could be critical unidentified differences between organizations reflected in the current sample, and those not reflected. Finally, we characterize the indicators of organizational capacity as Boolean difference-makers that could be viewed as “possible causes” or “candidate causes,” pending additional confirmation via independent replication in other studies: we cannot ascertain the direction and magnitude of causal relationships from the present analysis. It is possible that other factors not included in the analysis could have contributed to between-organization variation in barriers to mental health promotion. While this study yields new insights into the role of community organization capacity in mental health promotion efforts, future research could build on this work by expanding upon the number of community organizations under study, achieving higher participation rates, and providing more detailed information about the local contexts served by those community organizations.

Conclusions

This study offers new insights into the role of organizational capacity in mental health promotion, including geographic context, leadership interested in health promotion, organization size, presence of a health team, budget for health promotion efforts, and type of community organization (e.g., mosque, library, or housing community). These results can inform future theory, research, and practice in the implementation of evidence-based mental health promotion interventions delivered in community settings. Building capacity for health promotion within community organizations may support mental health programming in these community-based settings. Such community-based mental health programming could help to address rising mental health concerns, especially among populations that may not otherwise access mental health care and support.
