Interval maps mimicking circle rotations
Jakub Bielawski 1, Thiparat Chotibut 2, Fryderyk Falniowski 3, Michał Misiurewicz 4, Georgios Piliouras 5

Abstract
. We investigate the dynamics of maps of the real line whose behavior on an invariant interval is close to a rational rotation on the circle. We concentrate on a specific two-parameter family, describing the dynamics arising from models in game theory, mathematical biology and machine learning. If one parameter is a rational number, k / n k/n , with k , n k,n coprime, and the second one is large enough, we prove that there is a periodic orbit of period n n . It behaves like an orbit of the circle rotation by an angle 2 ​ π ​ k / n 2\pi k/n and attracts trajectories of Lebesgue almost all starting points. We also discover numerically other interesting phenomena. While we do not give rigorous proofs for them, we provide convincing explanations.



1.
Introduction

This paper is mainly focused on the study of a remarkable family of maps

F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

F(x)=x+b-\frac{1}{e^{-ax}+1},

(1)

parametrized by two parameters
a>0
and
b\in(0,1)
. This family,
with different parametrization,
arises from the update rule in mathematical biology (two predator-one-prey
model), see
[
2
,
3
]
, as well as in machine learning and game theory,
see
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Since apparently
the paper
[
2
]
by
Eirola, Osipov and Söderbacka is the first one where it appeared, we
will call maps of this family
EOS maps
.

For important range of parameters, those maps have an invariant
interval that attracts all trajectories. And on this interval, if we
identify its endpoints (to get a circle) it looks almost like a circle
rotation. For a rational rotation on a circle, every point is periodic
of the same period. If our interval map is (mostly) very close to this
rotation, we may expect that at least one periodic orbit of this
period and with the same permutation will be present. We prove that
for appropriate range of parameters this is the case, and additionally
this surviving orbit is attracting.

From purely mathematical point of view, the problem we tackle is as
follows. We start with a rotation on the circle. Then we cut the
circle at one point, making it an interval. Our map develops a (big)
jump discontinuity. In order to make it continuous, we modify it in a
small neighborhood of the point of discontinuity. Will the resulting
continuous interval map still be in some sense similar to the circle
rotation? The answer is yes, if we do it the right way, although we
add a lot of dynamics we do not know much about.

We also investigate the family of EOS maps numerically and discover
some interesting phenomena. For example, if the rotation of the circle
is by
k/n
(that is, by the angle
2\pi k/n
), the bifurcation diagrams
for the same
n
, but different
k
, look very similar to each other.
We explain this phenomenon, although we do not provide a rigorous
proof of it.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we
introduce notation and state conditions that guarantee the results we
want. In Section
3
we prove our main theorem
(Theorem
3.3
). In Section
4
we show that for a
rational
b
and sufficiently large
a
the corresponding EOS map
satisfies the assumptions of this theorem. In Section
5
we
describe and explain other interesting properties of the family of the
EOS maps.

2.
Notation and assumptions

Let the map
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

F(x)=x+b-g(x),

(2)

where
b\in(0,1)
is a parameter and
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
is a
C^{3}
map. We will assume that its Schwarzian derivative, that is

SF=\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}\right)

is negative.
We make the following assumptions on the map
g
:

(A)

there exist
y_{-},y_{+}
, with
b-1<y_{-}<0<y_{+}<b
such that

A1.

0<g^{\prime}(x)<1
on
(-\infty,y_{-})\cup(y_{+},\infty)
,

A2.

g^{\prime}(y_{-})=g^{\prime}(y_{+})=1
,

A3.

g^{\prime}(x)>1
on
(y_{-},y_{+})
,

(B)

g(b-1)<b<g(b)
.

In particular,
g
is an increasing diffeomorphism.

Condition (
A
) implies that
F
is increasing on
(-\infty,y_{-})
and
(y_{+},\infty)
, and
decreasing on
(y_{-},y_{+})
, and condition (
B
) guarantees that

F(b-1)>b-1\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ F(b)<b.

(3)

Moreover, by the definition of
g
we have
F(y_{+})>y_{+}+b-1>b-1
, and
F(y_{-})<b
, so

F([b-1,b])\subset[b-1,b].

Because
F(x)=x
if and only if
g(x)=b
, thus as
F
is increasing outside of
[b-1,b]
and, by (
B
),
F(x)>x
to the left of
b-1
,
F(x)<x
to the right of
b
, the trajectory of every point outside of
[b-1,b]
eventually falls into it. Thus, conditions (
A
) and (
B
) guarantee that
[b-1,b]
is a globally attracting invariant interval for
F
.

Finally, we assume that

(C)

there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

C1.

g(x)<\varepsilon
for any
x<-\frac{1}{2n}
,

C2.

g(x)>1-\varepsilon
for any
x>\frac{1}{2n}
,

C3.

(n-1)\varepsilon<1-g(y_{+})
and

(n-1)\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2n}-\big(1-g(y_{+})+y_{+}\big)
,

C4.

(n-1)\varepsilon<g(y_{-})
and

(n-1)\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2n}-\big(g(y_{-})-y_{-}\big)
.

We denote the set of maps
F
given by (
2
) and satisfying all of the above assumptions by
\mathfrak{F}
. We give an example of such map in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.

F(x)=x+b-\frac{1}{e^{-90x}+1}

G
(

) for
b=3/11
. The map

belongs to
\mathfrak{F}
. The yellow region is the set
[b-1,b]^{2}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Observe that if we assume additionally symmetry

g(-x)+g(x)=1,

then

•

y_{-}=-y_{+}
,

•

(C1) and (C2) are equivalent,

•

(C3) and (C4) are equivalent.

Let us also define a map
G\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

G(x)=\begin{cases}x+b&\textrm{if}\ \ x<0,\\
x+b-1&\textrm{if}\ \ x>0,\end{cases}

(4)

(we do not have to choose the value of
G
at 0). In particular, we have

G(x)-F(x)=\begin{cases}g(x)&\textrm{if}\ \ x<0,\\
g(x)-1&\textrm{if}\ \ x>0.\end{cases}

(5)

Remark 2.2
.

For
G
the interval
[b-1,b]
is invariant, and if we glue together
the endpoints of this interval,
G
will be the rotation of the
resulting circle by
b
(that is, the angle
2\pi b
). Thus, if

b=k/n
,
k,n
coprime, then for any
x\in[b-1,b]

|G^{i}(x)-x|\geq\frac{1}{n}

(6)

for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, and
G^{n}(x)=x
.

Throughout the paper we set
b=k/n
, where
k,n
are coprime natural numbers and
k<n
.

3.
Rotation-like behavior

Under assumptions of the preceding section, we will show the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
F\in\mathfrak{F}
there exists an attracting periodic orbit of
F
of period
n
, that
lies in first and third lap. Both critical points of
F
are in the
immediate basin of attraction of this periodic orbit.

Proof.

Call a subinterval of
[b-1,b]

basic
if it is of the form

[i/n,(i+1)/n]
for an integer
i
. Clearly,
G
maps a basic interval
to a basic interval.

We will show that in the basic interval

[0,1/n]
there is a lap
[u,v]
of
F^{n}
, in which there is an
attracting fixed point of
F^{n}
. Moreover, the images of this lap
under
F^{i}
(
i=0,1,\dots,n-1
) are contained in basic intervals and

F
is increasing on them. Remember that we know the dynamics of
G

and by (C1) and (C2),
F
is very close to
G
except in a small
neighborhood of
0
. We will be thinking of

F-G
as a “correction” that we add to
G
(which is simple) to get

F
. The correction closer to the critical points (but on the
increasing laps) is taken care by (C3) and (C4).

Set
u=y_{+}
. By (C3),
u\in(0,\frac{1}{2n})
. We have

F(y_{+})-(b-1)=1-g(y_{+})+y_{+}
so
F(u)
is in the basic interval

[b-1,b-1+1/n]
, and its distance from the left endpoint of this
interval is
1-g(y_{+})+y_{+}
. For several next iterates of
F
the point

F^{i}(u)
is in some basic interval and, by conditions (C), its distance from the left
endpoint of that interval is still less than
\frac{1}{2n}
.

This clearly continues until
F^{i}(u)
gets to the basic interval

[-1/n,0]
. However, we can continue it further, since
F^{i}(u)
is
close to the left endpoint of this interval, and (C2) works for

|x|\geq\frac{1}{2n}
. Nevertheless, this moment is special, since in

[-1/n,0]
there is the critical point
y_{-}
of
F
. In our
construction we should have
y_{-}=F^{i}(v)
. Thus, we should work
backward in time to recover
v
.

This is possible, because locally
G
is of the form
G(z)-z+\alpha

for some
\alpha
. Therefore,

G^{-1}(x)-F^{-1}(x)=x-\alpha-F^{-1}(x)=F(F^{-1}(x))-G(F^{-1}(x)),

and we can use (C1) and (C2). Clearly, we can also go forward from
F^{i}(v)
. By
the same arguments as before, and using (C3) and (C4), we see that the
sets
F^{j}([u,v])
,
j=0,1,\dots,n
, are all contained in basic
intervals, and in the first and third lap of
F
.

Since
G^{n}(x)=x
for every
x
, we have
F^{n}(u)-u=F^{n}(u)-G^{n}(u)
, and
this number is equal to the sum of “corrections” along the
trajectory of
u
of length
n
. One of those corrections is

1-g(y_{+})
, and the rest of them are less than
\varepsilon
in absolute
value. Therefore, by (C3),
F^{n}(u)-u>0
. Similarly, when we estimate

F^{n}(v)-v
, we get
F^{n}(v)-v<0
.

Thus, there is a fixed point
z
of
F^{n}
in
(u,v)
. We know that

F^{\prime}<1
, so the orbit of
z
is attracting and both
u
and
v
(and
therefore of both critical points of
F
) are in the immediate basin
of attraction of the orbit of
z
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of the preceding lemma, the attracting periodic
orbit attracts trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points from
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
z
be the attracting periodic orbit of
F
of period
n
from Lemma
3.1
.
The map
F
has negative Schwarzian derivative by the assumption.
Moreover, we can compactify
\mathbb{R}
by adding points at plus and minus
infinity, and those points are repelling fixed points. It turns out
that from this and from what we already proved it follows that the
trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points of
\mathbb{R}
are attracted to
the orbit of
z
.

The proof of this fact is almost identical to the proof of Theorem 4.1
of
[
8
]
(whose main part is taken from the proof of Theorem 1.3
of
[
9
]
). The reader can find the details there. Here we will
only sketch the main idea of the proof.

Since the proofs we mentioned are for maps of a bounded interval into
itself, we will work with the map
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, smoothly
conjugate to
F
. Then the basic properties of
F
and
f
are the same. Moreover, the points
0

and
1
are repelling fixed points of
f
. We will denote by
Z
the
periodic orbit of
f
corresponding to the orbit of
z
for
F
.

Let
U
be the union of the immediate basin of attraction of
Z
and a
small neighborhood of
\{0,1\}
. Then we define
W
as a small open
neighborhood of
[0,1]\setminus U
, so that its closure is disjoint
from
Z
and does not contain the critical points of
f
.

An interval
J\subset[0,1]
is called a
homterval
if for every

n>0

f^{n}
restricted to
J
is a homeomorphism onto its image. Then
we can use Theorem 1.2 of
[
9
]
to deduce that for every homterval

J
there exists
m\geq 0
such that the closure of
f^{m}(J)
is
contained in
U
. Now we can use Theorem 1.3 of
[
9
]
. Note that
the assumption that
f
has no sinks really means that there are no
sinks in
W
, and the assumption on the negative Schwarzian derivative
can be replaced by the assumption that
f
is smoothly conjugate to a
map with negative Schwarzian derivative (the Lebesgue measure

\lambda
can be replaced by the measure
\mu
equivalent to the
Lebesgue measure, transported via the conjugacy).

Thus, we see that on
W
some iterate of
f
is expanding (perhaps for

\mu
, not
\lambda
). Now, the standard argument, as in the proof of
Theorem 4.1 of
[
8
]
shows that the trajectory of
\mu
-almost
every point (and therefore of
\lambda
-almost every point) sooner or
later enters
U
. Then such trajectory converges to
Z
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
b=k/n
, where
k,n\in\mathbf{N}
are coprime, then for every
F\in\mathfrak{F}
, there exists an attracting periodic orbit of
F
of period
n
, which attracts trajectories of Lebesgue almost all points from
\mathbb{R}
. This attracting periodic orbit lies in first and third lap, in the invariant interval
[b-1,b]
, and it behaves like an orbit of
G
. That is, if we glue
b-1
with
b
to get a circle, the order of the points on the orbit is the same as for the rotation by
b
.

4.
EOS maps

Now we will focus on the concrete family of maps from
\mathfrak{F}
.
Let us consider the family of EOS maps
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

F(x)=x+b-\frac{1}{e^{-ax}+1},

(7)

parametrized by two parameters
a>0
and
b\in(0,1)
. A family
of maps equivalent to (
7
) was first observed in
[
2
]
.
This family describes population dynamics in mathematical biology:
in
[
3
]
a one-dimensional version of two-predator-one-prey model is introduced by a family of maps
f_{PP}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

f_{PP}(x)=x+B-\frac{k}{1+e^{x}}.

By taking
x=kz
we get

f_{PP}(kz)=k\left(z+\frac{B}{k}-\frac{1}{1+e^{kz}}\right)=k\left(z+b-\frac{1}{e^{-az}+1}\right)=kF(z)

for
a=-k
and
b=\frac{B}{k}
, so
f_{PP}
is conjugate to
F
.

Moreover, it is also conjugate to the map from machine learning and game theory. That is, the evolution in a simple population game where agents are using multiplicative weights algorithm, established algorithm from machine learning and economics
[
7
]
, is described by the family of maps

f_{MW}\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
, given by

f_{MW}(y)=\frac{y}{y+(1-y)\exp(a(y-b))},

(8)

where
a>0
,
b\in(0,1)
(for a thorough discussion, see
[
4
,
6
]
).
By taking
x=\frac{1}{a}\log\frac{y}{1-y}
and

F(x)=\frac{1}{a}\log\frac{f_{MW}(y)}{1-f_{MW}(y)}

we see that the family
F
is conjugate to
f_{MW}
on
(0,1)
.

For the family of EOS maps we have
g(x)=\frac{1}{e^{-ax}+1}
. Observe that

g(-x)+g(x)=\frac{e^{-ax}}{e^{-ax}+1}+\frac{1}{e^{-ax}+1}=1,

so, by Remark
2.1
, the simplifications we mentioned apply.

Let us fix
b
as in the preceding section. We will show for
a
sufficiently large,
F
satisfies our
assumptions.

For
x\neq 0
we have by (
5
)

F(x)-G(x)=\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(x)}{e^{a|x|}+1},

(9)

where
\mathrm{sgn}(x)
is the sign of
x
. Therefore,

\textrm{if}\ \ \ |x|\geq\delta,\ \ \ \textrm{then}\ \ \ |F(x)-G(x)|\leq e^{-a\delta},

(10)

which is exponentially small in
a
. Thus, we get (C1) for sufficiently
large
a
.

As
a
goes to infinity,
g(b-1)
goes to 0, and
g(b)
goes to 1.
Therefore, (
B
) holds for sufficiently large
a
.

Let us make some calculations. We have

g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{at}{(t+1)^{2}},\ \ \textrm{where}\ \ t=e^{-ax}.

Therefore,
y_{+}
and
y_{-}
are given by
\xi(t)=0
, where

\xi(t)=(t+1)^{2}-at
. Denote the corresponding values of
t
by

t_{-},t_{+}
. They exist if
a>4
. We have
\xi(a-2)=1>0
and
\xi(a-3)=4-a<0
. Therefore,
a-3<t_{-}<a-2
.
Since
y_{-}=-\frac{1}{a}\log t_{-}
and
y_{+}=-y_{-}
, we get

\frac{1}{a}\log(a-3)<y_{+}<\frac{1}{a}\log(a-2).

(11)

Therefore, for sufficiently large
a
,
y_{+}\in(0,b)
and we get (
A
).

From (
5
) and (
11
) we
get

\frac{1}{a-1}<F(y_{+})-G(y_{+})<\frac{1}{a-2}.

(12)

Therefore, if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small and
a
is sufficiently
large, we get (C3).

Finally, the fact that the Schwarzian derivative of
F
is negative,
was proved in
[
5
]
.
Thus, the family of EOS maps defined by (
7
) belongs to
\mathfrak{F}
for sufficiently large
a
. Therefore, Theorem
3.3

applies.
