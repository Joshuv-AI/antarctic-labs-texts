The effectiveness of two doses of Hecolin vaccine in response to outbreaks in resource-limited settings
Authors: Fan Zhang, Jianbo Xia, Ling Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The use of Hecolin vaccine with a 2-dose schedule can replace the 3-dose schedule during campaigns in HEV outbreak settings.•Vaccination against hepatitis E during pregnancy may be feasible.•A single dose of the vaccine may not provide full protection.

Hepatitis E virus (HEV) represents a significant global cause of acute viral hepatitis. Annually, it is estimated to cause approximately 20.1 million infections worldwide, 3.3 million of which result in symptomatic illness. In developing regions, genotypes HEV-1 and HEV-2 are the primary strains responsible for widespread outbreaks and epidemics of acute jaundice (icteric hepatitis), transmitted predominantly through the fecal-oral route via contaminated water and food (Devarbhavi et al., 2023). Compared to the general population, pregnant women infected with HEV face a substantially higher risk of mortality, which can range up to 20%–30%. Additionally, HEV infection in this group can lead to adverse neonatal outcomes, including miscarriage, stillbirth, and vertical transmission (Zhang et al., 2022). An important goal of the HEV vaccine is to prevent HEV in some exceptional cases such as outbreaks and pregnant women (Peron et al., 2023). However, the routine hepatitis E vaccination schedule (0, 1 and 6 months) might not provide adequate protection for them in time. In this context, a study by Nesbitt et al., published in the Lancet Infectious Diseases, provides critical insights into the effectiveness of a two-dose regimen of the Hecolin vaccine during a HEV outbreak in Bentiu, South Sudan (Nesbitt et al., 2025). This study is particularly significant as it represents the first real-world application of the Hecolin vaccine in an outbreak setting, offering evidence that supports the use of a two-dose schedule for outbreak control. The findings have important implications for public health policy, especially in resource-limited settings where HEV outbreaks are prevalent.

The study was conducted in the context of an HEV outbreak in the Bentiu internally displaced persons (IDP) camp, a setting characterized by poor sanitation, limited access to clean water, and high population mobility (Nesbitt et al., 2025). These conditions are typical of many humanitarian crises, making the findings broadly applicable to similar contexts. The researchers employed a case-control design to estimate the effectiveness of two doses of Hecolin vaccine, comparing vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals. The primary analysis revealed an adjusted two-dose vaccine effectiveness of 84.0% (−208.5 to 99.2), while a secondary test-negative design analysis estimated effectiveness at 89.4% (56.4–98.0), which was similar to the results of our previous research on rabbits (Zhang et al., 2025). These results are encouraging, particularly given the logistical challenges of administering a three-dose vaccine in such settings.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has already suggested considering two-dose regimens in fragile, conflict-affected and vulnerable (FCV) settings with documented HEV circulation (WHO, 2024), and this study provides empirical support for that recommendation. The ability to use a two-dose schedule (0, and 1 months) could significantly enhance the feasibility of reactive vaccination campaigns, particularly in settings with high population mobility and limited healthcare infrastructure. The study’s findings suggest that two doses of Hecolin vaccine can provide substantial protection against HEV genotype 1, which is crucial for controlling outbreaks in vulnerable populations. If the outbreak is urgent, two doses of an accelerated vaccination schedule (0 and 7 days) might also be considered. Our previous study showed that two doses of the accelerated vaccination schedule could provide protective antibodies in a shorter time (Zhang et al., 2025). The accelerated schedule should be further recommended as a backup option during a hepatitis E outbreak.

Another important aspect of the study is its exploration of one-dose vaccine effectiveness. Although the primary focus was on two doses, the researchers also examined the protection conferred by a single dose. The results suggest that one dose may offer some level of protection, but the estimates were less precise and varied across different analytical methods. In our previous animal experiments, we found that a single dose of vaccine could not offer protection for rabbits completely (Zhang et al., 2015). Our study aimed to develop an immunization strategy for rabbits to prevent HEV infection and zoonotic transmission. We assessed the protective efficacy of an HEV vaccine in rabbits challenged with HEV strains of varying titers and genotypes, using different vaccine dosages and immunization schedules. Compared to placebo, a two-dose regimen effectively prevented HEV infection. In contrast, a single dose showed only 43% efficacy, which was not statistically different from the placebo group (P > 0.05).

Hecolin vaccine is currently licensed for individuals aged 16 years and older (Zhu et al., 2010). However, in the study, 70.6% of PCR confirmed cases were younger than 16 years and 26.7% were aged five years or younger. Research on the hepatitis E vaccine is still blank under 16 years old. In the Highlights from the Meeting of the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) on Immunization, 11–13 March 2024, the WHO encouraged additional research on hepatitis E vaccination in individuals under 16 years of age (WHO, 2024). Furthermore, the WHO also encouraged additional research on hepatitis E vaccination during pregnancy (WHO, 2024). Moreover, during the mass reactive vaccination campaign in Bentiu IDP camp, researchers found no evidence of an increased risk of fetal loss following vaccination during or before pregnancy (Nesbitt et al., 2024). In our previous study (Zhang et al., 2023), our findings demonstrate that a two-dose regimen rapidly induced high titers of protective anti-HEV antibodies in pregnant rabbits. This conferred protection against HEV infection and associated adverse pregnancy outcomes in the mothers. Furthermore, these vaccinated rabbits transferred maternal antibodies to their offspring via the placenta and breast milk, effectively shielding the neonates from HEV infection. These results indicate that, in addition to vaccinating nonpregnant individuals, Hecolin vaccine warrants consideration for maternal immunization strategies during HEV outbreaks.

The study’s methodological rigor is another strength. The use of multiple analytical approaches, including matched case-control and test-negative designs, strengthens the robustness of the findings. The researchers also conducted a bias indicator analysis to assess potential biases related to healthcare-seeking behavior, which is a common challenge in observational studies. This comprehensive approach enhances the credibility of the results and provides a model for future research in similar settings.

In conclusion, the study provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of a two-dose Hecolin regimen in controlling HEV outbreaks. It supports the WHO’s updated recommendations and highlights the potential for more widespread use of the vaccine in outbreak settings. Continued research and investment in hepatitis E vaccination are essential to further validate these findings and optimize outbreak response strategies. The study also underscores the importance of integrated approaches that combine vaccination with improvements in sanitation and hygiene to achieve sustainable reductions in HEV transmission. Overall, it represents a significant step forward in the fight against hepatitis E, particularly in the challenging contexts of humanitarian crises and resource-limited settings.
