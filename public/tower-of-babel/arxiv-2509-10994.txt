Direct reconstruction of anisotropic self-adjoint inclusions in the Calderón problem
Henrik Garde, David Johansson, Thanasis Zacharopoulos

Abstract
. We extend the monotonicity method for direct exact reconstruction of inclusions in the partial data Calderón problem, to the case of anisotropic conductivities in any spatial dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Specifically, from a local Neumann-to-Dirichlet map, we give reconstruction methods of inclusions based on unknown anisotropic self-adjoint perturbations to a known anisotropic conductivity coefficient. A main assumption is a definiteness condition for the perturbations near the outer inclusion boundaries. This additionally provides new insights into the non-uniqueness issues of the anisotropic Calderón problem.



Keywords: Anisotropic Calderón problem, inclusion detection, monotonicity method.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35R30, 35R05, 47H05.

1.
Introduction

The anisotropic Calderón problem, the inverse problem of determining an elliptic matrix-valued conductivity coefficient
A
from boundary measurements of the conductivity equation

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }\Omega,

is of major interest in the inverse problems community. Unlike the isotropic (scalar-valued) case, the anisotropic Calderón problem suffers from significant non-uniqueness issues. Indeed if boundary measurements are taken on
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
, for any
H^{1}
-diffeomorphism
\Phi\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\overline{\Omega}
for which
\Phi|_{\Gamma}
is the identity, the conductivity coefficient defined via the push-forward

\widetilde{A}=\frac{\mathop{\textup{D}\Phi}\nolimits A\mathop{\textup{D}\Phi}\nolimits^{\textup{T}}}{\lvert\det\mathop{\textup{D}\Phi}\nolimits\rvert}\circ\Phi^{-1}

(1.1)

produces the same local Neumann-to-Dirichlet (ND) map as the coefficient
A
. For
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
and with spatial dimension
d=2
, the obstructions to uniqueness are characterized by (
1.1
) for real-valued symmetric
L^{\infty}
-conductivities
[
4
,
37
]
. In spatial dimension
d\geq 3
, the full extent of the non-uniqueness issues (beyond real-analytic coefficients) remains one of the most important unanswered questions in the inverse problems community
[
32
,
39
]
. In a fractional Calderón problem, using the stronger non-local properties, the obstructions to uniqueness are characterized for
d\geq 2

[
9
]
. Despite knowledge on the possible obstructions, the non-uniqueness has prevented development of practically useful reconstruction methods. Unique recovery is only guaranteed with very strong restrictions on the considered class of coefficients
[
2
,
3
,
10
]
.

In this paper we study the exact reconstruction of anisotropic inclusions in any spatial dimension
d\geq 2
. We reconstruct the outer shape
D^{\bullet}
of

D=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A_{D}-A_{0}),

from knowledge of
A_{0}
and the local ND map
\Lambda(A_{D})
associated with the unknown conductivity
A_{D}
. Here
D^{\bullet}
is the smallest closed set with connected complement such that
D\subseteq D^{\bullet}
. We give direct reconstruction methods (Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
) based on the monotonicity of ND maps. The main assumptions are self-adjointness of the coefficients, that
A_{0}
satisfies a unique continuation principle, and that
A_{D}-A_{0}
satisfies a local uniform definiteness condition inside
D
near
\partial D^{\bullet}
; see Assumption
3.4
for the precise statement. In particular, inside
D
we do not assume more regularity than
L^{\infty}
for
A_{D}
, and
A_{D}-A_{0}
can even be indefinite at a positive distance away from
\partial D^{\bullet}
.

As an indirect consequence: if such a perturbation
A_{D}-A_{0}
, to a given “background conductivity”
A_{0}
, is known to satisfy the definiteness condition near
\partial D^{\bullet}
, this is enough to ensure that all coefficient-transformations (
1.1
), compatible with this condition, satisfy

\mathop{\textup{supp}}(\widetilde{A}_{D}-A_{0})^{\bullet}=D^{\bullet}.

Moreover, the inclusion detection problem is also unaffected by any other potential forms of non-uniqueness that could arise from the anisotropic Calderón problem when
d\geq 3
. Hence, for inclusion detection, practical reconstruction is sensible in a very high generality compared to reconstructing an anisotropic conductivity.

To give some intuition on why this is the case, let

\widetilde{D}=\Phi(D)

be the transformed inclusion under
\Phi
. If
\widetilde{D}^{\bullet}\neq D^{\bullet}
then
\Phi
will fail to be the identity in an open set intersecting
\partial D^{\bullet}
(the part where it is deformed by
\Phi
), which also changes the background conductivity in
\widetilde{A}_{D}
in that part of the domain via (
1.1
). From our results we conclude in this situation, that for

\widehat{D}=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(\widetilde{A}_{D}-A_{0}),

if
\widehat{D}^{\bullet}\neq D^{\bullet}
, the definiteness condition cannot be satisfied inside
\widehat{D}
near
\partial\widehat{D}^{\bullet}
. In particular, it may fail in part of the domain where the background conductivity in
\widetilde{A}_{D}
is modified.

In the context of Helmholtz scattering, see also the uniqueness results in
[
21
,
6
]
for inclusion detection with some anisotropic coefficients.

1.1.
The monotonicity method and related results

The reconstruction methods we present are based on the monotonicity of ND maps via the following operator inequalities (Proposition
5.5
):

\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle\leq\int_{\Omega}A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda(A_{j})
and
u_{2}
is the electric potential for conductivity
A_{2}
and Neumann condition
f
. The definiteness properties of
A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})
turn out to be the same as for
A_{2}-A_{1}
(Proposition
5.6
). The main idea in the
outer approach
of the monotonicity method, given in our main results Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
, is to construct test-operators that use the monotonicity to check if a given test-set
C
fully contains
D^{\bullet}
or not. This leads to an
if and only if
statement related to operator inequalities with the test-operators and the ND map for the unknown perturbed conductivity. In Theorem
3.7
the test-operators are linearized, which is beneficial for numerical implementation.

The proofs rely on the theory of localized potentials
[
20
]
, for the existence of certain localizing solutions to the conductivity equation, based on a unique continuation principle. We generalize the result on localized potentials to anisotropic (even non-self-adjoint) coefficients in Theorem
6.3
.

Under much stronger assumptions, we also give an
inner approach
of the monotonicity method in Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
, which checks if a given open set
B
is contained in
D^{\bullet}
.

The monotonicity method is well-established for
isotropic
conductivities, and for other similar inverse coefficient problems. We mention some of the results here, with a focus on the Calderón problem. In
[
38
]
they use the monotonicity to give bounds on inclusions, and later
[
26
,
29
]
prove that it is an exact reconstruction method for finite perturbations to the background conductivity, using the theory of localized potentials. It also includes the case with both positive and negative perturbations, if lower and upper bounds are known for the perturbed conductivity. This is expanded upon in
[
7
]
, and now the perturbed conductivity can simultaneously have parts with finite positive and negative perturbations, as well as extreme parts that are perfectly insulating and perfectly conducting. The need for lower and upper bounds is also removed. Finally, in
[
18
]
the method is generalized to reconstruct collections of Lipschitz cracks.

The method has also been used in other specialized directions: degenerate and singular perturbations based on
A_{2}
-Muckenhoupt weights
[
15
]
, inclusions in a fractional Calderón problem
[
27
,
28
]
, inclusions in a
p
-Laplace equation
[
5
]
, for nonlinear materials
[
36
]
, and for local uniqueness in an inverse coefficient problem for a non-resonant Schrödinger equation
[
25
]
. There are also rigorous connections to practically relevant electrode models
[
16
,
17
,
22
,
30
]
.

Certain reconstruction methods for the partial data isotropic Calderón problem also rely on the same type of methodology. For piecewise constant layered conductivities, there is an exact reconstruction method for both conductivity coefficient and the piecewise constant partition
[
11
,
12
]
. In a finite-dimensional settting (where the partition is known), a general piecewise constant conductivity is reconstructed in
[
13
]
, and likewise via a reformulation to a convex semidefinite optimization problem in
[
23
]
.

1.2.
Remarks on notation

All considered vector spaces are complex; this includes the classes of conductivity coefficients. At all occurrences of using support/supremum/infimum, we make use of the
essential
support/supremum/infimum.

We use the convention that inner products on complex Hilbert spaces are linear in the first entry and anti-linear in the second. We denote the Euclidean inner product as
z_{1}\cdot\overline{z_{2}}
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, in particular the “dot” is bilinear. The Euclidean norm is denoted
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert
.

For self-adjoint operators
A,B\in\mathscr{L}(H)
for a Hilbert space
H
, we write
A\geq B
when
A-B
is positive semidefinite; this is the Loewner ordering of such operators. Moreover, for
A
being a positive definite operator,
A^{1/2}
denotes its unique positive definite square root.

2.
The forward problem

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, with connected complement. For a bounded matrix-valued function
A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
, we use the norm

\lVert A\rVert_{*}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\lVert A(x)\rVert_{2},

where
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{2}
is the usual spectral norm for matrices, i.e. the operator norm on Euclidean space. Hence, we have

\lvert A(x)\xi\rvert\leq\lVert A\rVert_{*}\lvert\xi\rvert

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and a.e.
x\in\Omega
.

We use the notation

A^{\textup{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(A+A^{*}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\textup{I}}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\bigl(A-A^{*}\bigr),

such that

A=A^{\textup{R}}+\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}},

where we note that
A^{\textup{R}}
and
A^{\textup{I}}
are self-adjoint as matrices, i.e. Hermitian at every point in
\Omega
. This gives rise to the following general class of anisotropic coefficients:

\mathcal{H}(\Omega)=\{\,A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}\mid\exists c>0\colon A^{\textup{R}}\geq cI\text{ in the Loewner order in $\Omega$}\,\}.

The condition on
A
, the uniform positive definiteness of
A^{\textup{R}}
, can also explicitly be written:

\inf_{x\in\Omega}\mathop{\textup{Re}}\bigl(A(x)\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\bigr)=\inf_{x\in\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}(x)\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\geq c\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
; there are other classes of strongly elliptic coefficients (when restricting to
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) that we do not consider here, see e.g.
[
34
, chapter 4]
. We also have the subset where
A
is self-adjoint, i.e. when
A=A^{\textup{R}}
:

\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)=\{\,A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\mid A^{\textup{I}}=0\,\}.

Consider the partial data anisotropic conductivity problem for
A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
:

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\nu\cdot(A\nabla u)=\begin{cases}f&\text{on }\Gamma,\\
0&\text{on }\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Here
\nu
is the outer unit normal to
\Omega
,
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
is a non-empty open boundary piece, and the mean-free current density
f
belongs to

L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)=\{\,f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\mid\langle f,1\rangle=0\,\}.

We denote by
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
the standard inner product on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
is the associated norm. We likewise denote

H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)=\{\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mid\langle u|_{\Gamma},1\rangle=0\,\}.

A Poincaré inequality, related to the mean-free condition in
H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
, enables that

\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)}=\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/2}

becomes a norm on
H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
, equivalent with the usual
H^{1}
-norm. Moreover, the following continuity and coercivity estimates hold for all
w,v\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\int_{\Omega}A\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert A\rVert_{*}\lVert w\rVert_{H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)}\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\int_{\Omega}A\nabla v\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A\nabla v\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq c\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)}^{2}.

By the Lax–Milgram lemma, there exists a unique solution
u=u_{f}^{A}\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
to the corresponding weak problem associated to (
2.1
):

\int_{\Omega}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle f,v\rangle

(2.2)

for all
v\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
. We let
\Lambda(A)\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma))
denote the associated local ND map,

\Lambda(A)f=u_{f}^{A}|_{\Gamma},

and the forward problem is the nonlinear mapping
\Lambda\colon\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma))
. From (
2.2
) it holds that for any
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and
f,g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
, we have

\langle f,\Lambda(A_{1})g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{f}^{A_{2}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{g}^{A_{1}}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.3)

Thus the adjoint operator satisfies

\langle f,\Lambda(A_{1})^{*}g\rangle=\overline{\langle g,\Lambda(A_{1})f\rangle}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{A_{2}\nabla u_{g}^{A_{2}}}\cdot\nabla u_{f}^{A_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Of particular interest are the quadratic forms

\langle f,\Lambda(A)f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad\langle f,\Lambda(A)^{*}f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A^{*}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Specifically,

\Lambda(A)^{*}=\Lambda(A^{*}),

meaning that
\Lambda(A)
is self-adjoint if
A\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
. We also write

\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\Lambda(A)+\Lambda(A)^{*}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\bigl(\Lambda(A)-\Lambda(A)^{*}\bigr),

so for the forward problem we have

\Lambda=\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}+\mathrm{i}\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}},

where
\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)
and
\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)
are self-adjoint operators. In terms of the quadratic forms we thus have

\displaystyle\langle f,\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)f\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\langle f,\Lambda(A)f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\lvert(A^{\textup{R}})^{1/2}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\langle f,\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\textup{Im}}\langle f,\Lambda(A)f\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.5)

As one would expect, when multiplying with a complex scalar
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
, we get

(\kappa\Lambda)^{\textup{R}}=\kappa^{\textup{R}}\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}-\kappa^{\textup{I}}\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\kappa\Lambda)^{\textup{I}}=\kappa^{\textup{I}}\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}+\kappa^{\textup{R}}\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}.

3.
Outer reconstruction of general anisotropic inclusions

In the following we will assume that a so-called “background conductivity”
A_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
is known. For an unknown
A_{D}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
, we call

D=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A_{D}-A_{0})

the
inclusions
, noting that
D
may have several connected components. Our reconstruction methods will relate to the so-called outer shape of
D
.

Definition 3.1
.

The outer shape
D^{\bullet}
of
D
, is the smallest closed set with connected complement such that
D\subseteq D^{\bullet}
.

One of the key assumptions we will need, required for the existence of certain localized solutions, is that the background conductivity
A_{0}
satisfies a unique continuation principle.

Definition 3.2
.

Suppose
U\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
is a relatively open and connected set that intersects
\Gamma
. We say that
A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
satisfies the weak
unique continuation principle
(UCP) in
U
for the conductivity equation, provided that only the trivial solution of

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }U^{\circ}

can be identically zero in a non-empty open subset of
U
, and only the trivial solution has vanishing Cauchy data,
u
and
\nu\cdot(A\nabla u)
, on a non-empty open part of
\partial U\cap\Gamma
. If
U=\overline{\Omega}
we just say that
A
satisfies the UCP.

Remark 3.3
.

In spatial dimension
d=2
, the UCP holds for general real-valued
A\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
(and even in some non-self-adjoint cases), see
[
1
]
.

In spatial dimension
d\geq 3
, the UCP holds for real-valued Lipschitz continuous coefficients
A\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
, see
[
19
]
, and with counterexamples to the UCP for
\alpha
-Hölder continuous coefficients with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, see
[
35
,
33
]
.

We note that there also exist UCP results related to classes of complex coefficients, e.g. with
principally normal symbol

[
31
, chapter 28]
.

We define the class of admissible test-inclusions, used for the reconstruction methods, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\{\,C\Subset\Omega\mid C\text{ is the closure of an open set and has connected complement}\,\}.

Assumption 3.4
.

(i)

Assume there are
known
bounds on
A_{D}
, i.e. scalars
0<\alpha<\beta
such that

\alpha I\leq A_{D}\leq\beta I

in the Loewner order in
\Omega
.

(ii)

Assume that
D\Subset\Omega
and is the closure of an open set.

(iii)

Assume for every open neighborhood
W
of
x\in\partial D^{\bullet}
, there exists a relatively open connected set
V\subset D^{\bullet}\cap W
that intersects
\partial D^{\bullet}
, satisfying either of two options:

(a)

A_{D}-A_{0}
is positive semidefinite in
V
, and there exists an open ball
B\subset V
on which
A_{D}-A_{0}
is uniformly positive definite.

(b)

A_{D}-A_{0}
is negative semidefinite in
V
, and there exists an open ball
B\subset V
on which
A_{D}-A_{0}
is uniformly negative definite.

(iv)

Assume that
A_{0}
satisfies the UCP.

We give a few remarks on these assumptions.

Remark 3.5

(Remarks on Assumption
3.4
)
.

(a)

In case
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
, then
C\Subset\Omega
in the definition of
\mathcal{A}
can be replaced by
C\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
, and
D\Subset\Omega
in Assumption
3.4
(ii) can be replaced by
D\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
. Also, here “connected complement” is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(b)

Note that Assumption
3.4
(iii) only relates to what happens close to
\partial D^{\bullet}
, so further into
D
we may have that
A_{D}-A_{0}
is e.g. indefinite. Moreover, the definiteness condition is not exactly at
\partial D^{\bullet}
but just close to
\partial D^{\bullet}
, meaning that there can be a continuous deviation from
A_{0}
rather than a jump condition; in the isotropic case this mimics the condition of a strict local increase/decrease from the background conductivity when entering
D
from the outside
[
15
]
.

For a measurable set
C
, we define the test-coefficients

A^{-}_{C}=\begin{cases}\alpha I&\text{in }C\\
A_{0}&\text{in }\Omega\setminus C\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{+}_{C}=\begin{cases}\beta I&\text{in }C\\
A_{0}&\text{in }\Omega\setminus C,\end{cases}

and the corresponding test-operators

\Lambda^{-}_{C}=\Lambda(A^{-}_{C})\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{+}_{C}=\Lambda(A^{+}_{C}).

Theorem 3.6
.

Under Assumption
3.4
(i), for any measurable
C\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
we have

D\subseteq C\quad\text{implies}\quad\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}.

Under all of Assumption
3.4
, for any
C\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}\quad\text{implies}\quad D\subseteq C.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
7
.
∎

We also introduce linearized versions of the test-operators, which are more suitable for numerical computations. Note that for
A\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
and
B\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
, the Frechét derivative of
\Lambda
evaluated at
A
and in direction
B
is given by (c.f.
[
14
]
):

\langle f,\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda(A;B)f\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}B^{*}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

To shorten the notation in the following result, we introduce the test-operators

\displaystyle\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}

\displaystyle=\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda\bigl(A_{0};(\beta I-A_{0})\chi_{C}\bigr),

\displaystyle\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}

\displaystyle=\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda\bigl(A_{0};(A_{0}-\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha}I)\chi_{C}\bigr),

where
\chi_{C}
is a characteristic function on the set
C
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Pick
0<\alpha<\beta
such that Assumption
3.4
(i) holds for both
A_{D}
and
A_{0}
. For any measurable
C\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
we have

D\subseteq C\quad\text{implies}\quad\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})-\Lambda(A_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}.

Under all of Assumption
3.4
, for any
C\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})-\Lambda(A_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}\quad\text{implies}\quad D\subseteq C.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
8
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Under the given assumptions, Theorem
3.6
gives

D^{\bullet}=\cap\,\{\,C\in\mathcal{A}\mid\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}\,\},

while Theorem
3.7
gives

D^{\bullet}=\cap\,\{\,C\in\mathcal{A}\mid\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})-\Lambda(A_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}\,\}.

Moreover, from the proofs we also conclude:

•

If in Assumption
3.4
(iii) there is only positive definiteness near
\partial D^{\bullet}
, we only need to check the inequalities
\Lambda(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}
or
\Lambda(A_{D})-\Lambda(A_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}
.

•

If in Assumption
3.4
(iii) there is only negative definiteness near
\partial D^{\bullet}
, we only need to check the inequalities
\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})
or
\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda(A_{D})-\Lambda(A_{0})
.

4.
Inner reconstruction of uniformly definite anisotropic inclusions

Under much stronger definiteness assumptions (not just near
\partial D^{\bullet}
as before), we can also use the inner approach of the monotonicity method. We focus on the linearized version, so that the UCP requirement is only related to the background conductivity
A_{0}
.

Assumption 4.1
.

(i)

Assume that we have either:

(a)

Positive definite inclusions
,
A_{D}-A_{0}\geq cI
in the Loewner order in
D
, for known
c>0
.

(b)

Negative definite inclusions
,
A_{D}-A_{0}\leq-cI
in the Loewner order in
D
, for known
c>0
.

(ii)

Assume that
D\Subset\Omega
and is the closure of an open set.

(iii)

Assume that
A_{0}
satisfies the UCP.

Again, to shorten the notation in the following results, we introduce the test-operators

\displaystyle\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\widehat{\Lambda}^{+}_{B}

\displaystyle=\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda\bigl(A_{0};c(\tfrac{\alpha}{\beta})^{2}\chi_{B}I\bigr),

\displaystyle\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\widehat{\Lambda}^{-}_{B}

\displaystyle=\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda\bigl(A_{0};-c\chi_{B}I\bigr),

where
\chi_{B}
is a characteristic function on the set
B
; note the differences compared to the previous section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that

5.
Operator inequalities for Neumann-to-Dirichlet maps

Although we will only consider coefficients from
\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
in the reconstruction methods, we still prove the following more general inequalities based on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
for possible future reference. See also
[
24
, Lemma 2.1]
for the isotropic case with complex conductivity.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
we denote
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda(A_{j})
and
u_{j}=u_{f}^{A_{j}}
. Assume
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
is such that
\kappa A_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. We also define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{I}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{I}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{I}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{I}}+\mathrm{i}\bigl[(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{I}}-(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{I}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}\bigr].

Then

\displaystyle\langle f,[\overline{\kappa}(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})]^{\textup{R}}f\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[[\kappa(A_{2}-A_{1})]^{\textup{R}}-\mathcal{B}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\langle f,[\overline{\kappa}(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})]^{\textup{R}}f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}[\kappa(A_{2}-A_{1})]^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.2)

Proof.

We start by proving (
5.1
). A computation reveals that

(\kappa A_{1})[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{1})^{*}=(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{B}_{\kappa},

which is used below, combined with (
2.3
) and (
2.4
):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\bigl\lvert[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{1/2}\nabla u_{1}-[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1/2}(\kappa A_{1})^{*}\nabla u_{2}\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\kappa\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)-2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\kappa\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle\phantom{={}}+\int_{\Omega}\bigl[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{B}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\langle f,[\overline{\kappa}(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})]^{\textup{R}}f\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\bigl[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}-(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{B}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Next we prove (
5.2
). A computation reveals that

\displaystyle(\kappa A_{2})^{*}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{2})

\displaystyle=(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}+(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}[\kappa(A_{2}-A_{1})]^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{\kappa},

which is used below, combined with (
2.3
) and (
2.4
):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\bigl\lvert[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{1/2}\nabla u_{1}-[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1/2}(\kappa A_{2})\nabla u_{2}\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\kappa\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)-2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\kappa\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle\phantom{={}}+\int_{\Omega}\bigl[(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}+(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}[\kappa(A_{2}-A_{1})]^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\langle f,[\overline{\kappa}(\Lambda_{2}-\Lambda_{1})]^{\textup{R}}f\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\bigl[(\kappa A_{2})^{\textup{R}}[(\kappa A_{1})^{\textup{R}}]^{-1}[\kappa(A_{2}-A_{1})]^{\textup{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.\qed

Remark 5.2
.

Note that in the isotropic case, the large square bracket in
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}
vanishes (after the imaginary unit). Also, if
\kappa A_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
then
\mathcal{B}_{\kappa}=0
and if
\kappa A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
then
\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}=0
.

We get some corollaries of Theorem
5.1
, based on the choice of
\kappa
.

Corollary 5.3
.

In the setting of Theorem
5.1
with
\kappa=1
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1}-\mathcal{B}_{1}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})+\mathcal{C}_{1}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Corollary 5.4
.

In the setting of Theorem
5.1
with
\kappa=-\mathrm{i}
, provided that
A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}_{2}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}_{1})f\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[A^{\textup{I}}_{2}-A^{\textup{I}}_{1}-\mathcal{B}_{-\mathrm{i}}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}_{2}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}_{1})f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\bigl[A^{\textup{I}}_{2}(A^{\textup{I}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{I}}_{2}-A^{\textup{I}}_{1})+\mathcal{C}_{-\mathrm{i}}\bigr]\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

What we will need for the monotonicity method is the following, corresponding to the special case of Theorem
5.1
where
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
and
\kappa=1
. We also provide a slightly different proof that is arguably easier to read.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
we denote
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda(A_{j})
and
u_{j}=u_{f}^{A_{j}}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.4)

Proof.

We start by proving (
5.3
), which is by the computation below combined with (
2.3
),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\leq\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\lvert A_{1}^{1/2}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt=\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt=\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle.

Next we prove (
5.4
). Using that

A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})=A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}A_{2}-A_{2}

is self-adjoint, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt\geq\int_{\Omega}A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\lvert A_{1}^{1/2}\nabla u_{1}-A_{1}^{-1/2}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt=-\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt=\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle.\qed

Note that the first inequality in Proposition
5.5
implies monotonicity of the ND maps, since

\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\langle f,(\Lambda_{1}-\Lambda_{2})f\rangle\leq\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.5)

It is not immediately clear how one would make use of the second inequality in Proposition
5.5
, due to the matrix product in the front. What we need to consider is when
A_{2}-A_{1}
is positive/negative (semi)definite, if we can also infer that
A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})
has the same properties. This is the result of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
c\geq 0
and
V\subseteq\Omega
. Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
with
A_{1}\leq\beta I
and
A_{2}\geq\alpha I
in
V
for
\alpha,\beta>0
.

(i)

If
A_{2}-A_{1}\geq cI
in
V
, then
A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\geq cI
in
V
.

(ii)

If
A_{2}-A_{1}\leq-cI
in
V
, then
A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\leq-c(\tfrac{\alpha}{\beta})^{2}I
in
V
.

Proof.

Let
A_{2}-A_{1}\geq cI
in the Loewner order in
V
for some
c\geq 0
. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and note that
A_{1}^{-1}
is positive definite. Now we put the pieces together:

\displaystyle A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}

\displaystyle=(A_{2}-A_{1})A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}+(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}

\displaystyle=A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi\cdot\overline{(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi}+(A_{2}-A_{1})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}

\displaystyle\geq c\lvert\xi\rvert^{2},

which concludes the proof of (i).

For (ii), we first prove the assertion that if

A_{1}-A_{2}\geq cI

(5.6)

for some
c\geq 0
, then

A_{2}^{-1}-A_{1}^{-1}\geq c\beta^{-2}I.

(5.7)

The semidefinite case (
c=0
) of (
5.7
) is a simple consequence of
t\mapsto-t^{-1}
being an operator monotone function, c.f.
[
8
, chapter 7]
. To prove (
5.7
), first note that we have

\displaystyle A_{2}^{-1}-A_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=A_{1}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{2}^{-1}

\displaystyle=A_{1}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{1}^{-1}+A_{1}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})(A_{2}^{-1}-A_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle=A_{1}^{-1}\bigl[A_{1}-A_{2}+(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{2}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})\bigr]A_{1}^{-1}.

Note that
A_{1}^{-1}\geq\beta^{-1}I
and likewise that
A_{2}^{-1}
is positive definite. Hence, we have

\displaystyle(A_{2}^{-1}-A_{1}^{-1})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}

\displaystyle=(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{1}^{-1}\xi\cdot\overline{A_{1}^{-1}\xi}+A_{2}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{1}^{-1}\xi\cdot\overline{(A_{1}-A_{2})A_{1}^{-1}\xi}

\displaystyle\geq c\lvert A_{1}^{-1}\xi\rvert^{2}\geq c\beta^{-2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}.

Now assume that in
V
we have (
5.6
) for some
c\geq 0
. Then (
5.7
) implies

A_{2}A_{1}^{-1}(A_{1}-A_{2})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}=(A_{2}^{-1}-A_{1}^{-1})A_{2}\xi\cdot\overline{A_{2}\xi}\geq c\beta^{-2}\lvert A_{2}\xi\rvert^{2}\geq c(\tfrac{\alpha}{\beta})^{2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2},

which concludes the proof of (ii).
∎

6.
Localized potentials for anisotropic coefficients

We extend localization results from
[
20
]
to anisotropic coefficients in
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. For a measurable set
V\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
and
F\in L^{2}(V)^{d}
, we define
w=w_{F}^{A}
as the unique solution in
H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
to the variational problem

\int_{\Omega}A^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{V}F\cdot\overline{\nabla v}

(6.1)

for all
v\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega).
We define the
virtual measurement operator

L_{V}(A)\colon L^{2}(V)^{d}\to L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
as

L_{V}(A)F=w_{F}^{A}|_{\Gamma}.

In the following,
R
is used to denote the range of an operator.

Proposition 6.1
.

For
A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and measurable sets
V,V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
, there are the following properties of the virtual measurement operator:

(i)

R(L_{V}(A))
is independent of the function values
A|_{V}
,

(ii)

V_{1}\subseteq V_{2}
implies
R(L_{V_{1}}(A))\subseteq R(L_{V_{2}}(A))
,

(iii)

The adjoint
L_{V}(A)^{*}\colon L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(V)^{d}
is given by
L_{V}(A)^{*}f=\nabla u_{f}^{A}|_{V}
.

Proof.

(i): Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
such that
A_{1}=A_{2}
outside
V
. We will prove that
R(L_{V}(A_{1}))=R(L_{V}(A_{2}))
. Let
\varphi\in R(L_{V}(A_{1}))
, i.e.
\varphi=w|_{\Gamma}
with
w=w_{F_{1}}^{A_{1}}
for some
F_{1}\in L^{2}(V)^{d}
. Now define
F_{2}=(A_{2}-A_{1})^{*}\nabla w|_{V}\in L^{2}(V)^{d}
. Then (
6.1
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{2}^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}A_{1}^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{V}(A_{2}-A_{1})^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{V}F_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{V}(F_{1}+F_{2})\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x

for all
v\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
. Hence, we have that
\varphi=L_{V}(A_{2})(F_{1}+F_{2})
so indeed
\varphi\in R(L_{V}(A_{2}))
. Repeating the above proof with
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
interchanged results in
R(L_{V}(A_{1}))=R(L_{V}(A_{2}))
as desired.

(ii): Assume
V_{1}\subseteq V_{2}
, and let
\varphi=L_{V_{1}}(A)F=w|_{\Gamma}
for some
F\in L^{2}(V_{1})^{d}
. Let
\widetilde{F}
denote the extension by zero of
F
on the set
V_{2}
. Then

\int_{\Omega}A^{*}\nabla w\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{V_{1}}F\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{V_{2}}\widetilde{F}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x

for all
v\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
. Hence, we also have
\varphi=L_{V_{2}}(A)\widetilde{F}
, i.e.
R(L_{V_{1}}(A))\subseteq R(L_{V_{2}}(A))
.

(iii): Let
F\in L^{2}(V)^{d}
and
f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
, then from the variational forms (
6.1
) and (
2.2
), for
w=w_{F}^{A}
and
u=u_{f}^{A}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle L_{V}(A)^{*}f,F\rangle_{L^{2}(V)^{d}}

\displaystyle=\langle f,L_{V}(A)F\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\langle f,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla w}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{V}\overline{F}\cdot\nabla u\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle\nabla u|_{V},F\rangle_{L^{2}(V)^{d}}.

Since this holds for all
F\in L^{2}(V)^{d}
, we conclude that
L_{V}(A)^{*}f=\nabla u|_{V}
.
∎

We note that range inclusions imply norm inequalities for the adjoint operators.

Lemma 6.2

(Lemma 2.5 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let
H
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
be Hilbert spaces and let
T_{j}\in\mathscr{L}(K_{j},H)
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then

R(T_{1})\subseteq R(T_{2})\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists C>0,\,\forall x\in H\colon\lVert T_{1}^{*}x\rVert_{K_{1}}\leq C\lVert T_{2}^{*}x\rVert_{K_{2}}.

Finally, we are able to prove the existence of localized potentials.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
U\subset\overline{\Omega}
be a relatively open connected set that intersects
\Gamma
and let
B\subset U
be a non-empty open set. Assume
A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
where both
A
and
A^{*}
satisfy the UCP in
U
. Then there are sequences
(f_{j})
in
L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
and
(u_{j})
in
H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
, with
u_{j}=u_{f_{j}}^{A}
, such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B}\lvert\nabla u_{j}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega\setminus U}\lvert\nabla u_{j}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Proof.

We let
V_{1}\subseteq B\subset V\subseteq U
where
V_{1}
and
V
are relatively open non-empty connected sets, and define
V_{2}=\Omega\setminus\overline{V}
. These sets are chosen such that
V
intersects
\Gamma
,
\mathop{\textup{dist}}(V_{1},V_{2})>0
, and that
\partial(\Omega\setminus\overline{V_{j}})
is Lipschitz continuous.

We start by proving that
R(L_{V_{1}}(A))
and
R(L_{V_{2}}(A))
have a trivial intersection. Hence, let
\varphi\in R(L_{V_{1}}(A))\cap R(L_{V_{2}}(A))
, i.e.
\varphi=w_{j}|_{\Gamma}
with
w_{j}=w_{F_{j}}^{A}
for some
F_{j}\in L^{2}(V_{j})^{d}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. From (
6.1
) we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}A^{*}\nabla w_{j}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=0\text{ for all }v\in H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{V_{j}}).

From the weak form of the conductivity problem (
2.2
), with conductivity
A^{*}
, we thus have

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(A^{*}\nabla w_{j})

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{V_{j}}

\displaystyle\nu\cdot(A^{*}\nabla w_{j})

\displaystyle=0\text{ on }\partial(\Omega\setminus\overline{V_{j}}),

in particular
\nu\cdot(A^{*}\nabla w_{j})\equiv 0
on
\partial V\cap\Gamma
, i.e. the Cauchy data of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
coincide on
\partial V\cap\Gamma
. Unique continuation entails that
w_{1}=w_{2}
in
V\setminus\overline{V_{1}}
. We may now “glue”
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
together, using that they agree in
V\setminus\overline{V_{1}}
:

u=\begin{cases}w_{1}&\text{in }\Omega\setminus\overline{V_{1}},\\
w_{2}&\text{in }V_{1}.\end{cases}

Now we have
-\nabla\cdot(A^{*}\nabla u)=0
in
\Omega
and
\nu\cdot(A^{*}\nabla u)=\nu\cdot(A^{*}\nabla w_{1})\equiv 0
on
\partial\Omega
. By uniqueness of such a solution in
H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
, it follows that
u\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, so in particular
\varphi=w_{1}|_{\Gamma}=u|_{\Gamma}\equiv 0
, i.e.

R(L_{V_{1}}(A))\cap R(L_{V_{2}}(A))=\{0\}.

Next we prove that
R(L_{V_{1}}(A))
is non-trivial. Suppose that
0\equiv L_{V_{1}}(A)^{*}f=\nabla u_{f}^{A}|_{V_{1}}
, then
u_{f}^{A}
is a constant function in
V_{1}
, and also in
V
by unique continuation. In particular,
f=\nu\cdot(A\nabla u_{f}^{A})|_{\Gamma}\equiv 0
on
\partial V\cap\Gamma
. Thus,
L_{V_{1}}(A)^{*}
is injective on

W=\{\,f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)\mid\mathop{\textup{supp}}f\subseteq\partial V\cap\Gamma\,\}.

Since
\overline{R(L_{V_{1}}(A))}=N(L_{V_{1}}(A)^{*})^{\perp}
then
R(L_{V_{1}}(A))
contains a dense subset of
