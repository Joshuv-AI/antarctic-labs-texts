Existence of Invariant Probability Measures for Stochastic Differential Equations with Finite Time Delay
M. van den Bosch, O. W. van Gaans, S. M. Verduyn Lunel

Abstract
We provide sufficient conditions for the existence of invariant probability measures for generic stochastic differential equations with finite time delay. This is achieved by means of the Krylov–Bogoliubov method. Furthermore, we focus on stochastic delay equations whose deterministic coefficient satisfies a one-sided bound, which enables us to show that boundedness in probability of a solution X ⁡ ( t ) X(t) entails boundedness in probability of its solution segment X t X_{t} . This implies that for a large set of systems, we can infer that an invariant measure exists if only there is at least one solution that is bounded in probability. Applications include, but are not limited to, the stochastic Mackey–Glass equations [ 7 ] and the stochastic Wright’s equation [ 8 ] . The noise driving the dynamical system is allowed to be an integrable Lévy process.

Introduction

The main objective of this paper is to establish sufficient conditions guaranteeing the existence of an invariant measure, defined on the space of càdlàg functions
D[-\tau,0],
and thus that of a stationary solution, for generic autonomous initial value problems of the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathrm{d}X(t)&=&a(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(X_{t-})\,\mathrm{d}M(t),&\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0,\\[1.42271pt]
X(u)&=&\Phi(u),&\quad\text{ for }u\in[-\tau,0].\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

In here, the process
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a semimartingale (with restrictions specified later), and
a,b:D[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
are locally Lipschitz functionals with respect to the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and are, in addition, assumed
proper
for well-posedness; see §
2
. Throughout we write
D[0,\infty)
,
D[-\tau,\infty),
and
D[-\tau,0]
for the spaces of càdlàg functions defined on
[0,\infty)
,
[-\tau,\infty)
, and
[-\tau,0]
, respectively, along with
C[0,\infty)
,
C[-\tau,\infty),
and
C[-\tau,0]
being the spaces of continuous functions.

We obtain the existence of an invariant measure via a procedure thanks to Krylov and Bogoliubov
[
24
]
; one can find the existence theorem(s) for our delayed setting in Appendix
C
.
Thanks to the latter, it all comes down to finding sufficient conditions for which we have partial tightness of one set of solution segments; see Appendix
B
for general tightness results. The main steps are illustrated in Figure
1
.

A schematic on how we prove the existence of an invariant measure for system (

Since we focus on systems with finite time delays only, it is possible to derive general results with regard to boundedness in probability of solutions and their segments, see §
3
, as well as tightness of solution segments, see §
4
. Tightness of the segment process
(X_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
of a solution
(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}
to (
1.1
) is ensured when, in addition to global existence of all solutions and boundedness in probability of
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
, the following two sufficient conditions are satisfied:

(i)

the noise coefficient
b
is bounded, i.e., there is a constant
\beta\geq 0
such that

|b(\varphi)|\leq\beta,\quad\varphi\in D[-\tau,0];

(1.2)

(ii)

the process
\big(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}|a(X_{u})|\big)_{t\geq\tau}
is bounded in probability, i.e.,

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq\tau}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}|a(X_{u})|>R\right)=0.

(1.3)

Moreover, we prove that (i) and (ii) can be replaced by the following condition:

(iii)

the process
\big(\|X_{t}\|_{\infty}\big)_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability, i.e.,

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\|X_{t}\|_{\infty}>R\right)=0.

(1.4)

We provide three separate proofs demonstrating that these conditions imply partial tightness: one where
M
is a Brownian motion (§
4.1
); one where
M
is a square-integrable Lévy process (§
4.2
); and one inspired by
[
33
]
using semimartingale characteristics (see Appendix
B.5
), which allows
M
to be an integrable Lévy process (§
4.2
). In particular, the full segment process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in the continuous case and (iii) is in fact sufficient and necessary; see Proposition
B.18
. Finally, we claim that most if not all results mentioned above can be generalised to systems of delay equations.

The main results of this article are Theorem
4.3
and Theorems
4.7
–
4.8
. Furthermore, in §
4.3
we show that—for delay equations with stochastic negative feedback—conditions (ii) and (iii) are automatically satisfied when the solution
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability. In particular, this conclusion can be generalised to stochastic delay equations whose deterministic coefficient
a
in (
1.1
) has a one-sided bound. This result is due to Proposition
3.5
and summarised in Corollary
4.5
for a Brownian motion. Finally, let us point out that these general results are exploited in
[
7
,

]
.

Fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t<\infty}

\mathbb{F}

(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}

The space
\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0]
consists of all càdlàg processes
(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t\leq 0}

A process

is an

(2.1)

Note that the segment process can be regarded as an

is a

Throughout this section, we let

(2.2)

subject to an initial process

. In §

we consider the non-autonomous case.
All results throughout this section naturally extend to higher dimensional systems.
The framework we develop is in the spirit of
[
27
, Ch. 5]
, which provides existence and uniqueness results for (strong) solutions to stochastic

Existence and uniqueness results with more general delay terms can be found in, e.g.,

]
.

2.1
Autonomous equations

We write
\varphi(s-)=\lim_{t\nearrow s}\varphi(s)
and
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=\sup_{-\tau\leq s\leq 0}|\varphi(s)|
for any càdlàg function
\varphi
. Observe that (
2.2
) is shorthand for the following integral equation:

X(t)=\Phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}(X)(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{B}_{\Phi}(X)(s-)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(2.3)

where

1.

the maps
\mathbf{A}_{\Psi},\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}:\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)

\Psi\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0]

\mathbf{A}_{\Psi}(Y)(s,\omega)=a\left(Y^{\Psi}_{s}(\omega)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}(Y)(s,\omega)=b\left(Y^{\Psi}_{s}(\omega)\right),\quad s\geq 0;

(2.4)

2.

the process
(Y^{\Psi}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in (
2.4
) is the segment process of
Y^{\Psi}\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
, where

Y^{\Psi}(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Psi(s),&-\tau\leq s<0\\
Y(s),&s\geq 0;\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

3.

the functionals
a:D[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
and
b:D[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
in (
2.4
) are locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and

(see Definition

).

The first integral in (
2.3
) is to be understood as a Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral. The second integral is a stochastic integral as in

]
. For further information regarding the functionals or integrals, see the discussion succeeding the proof of Proposition

Let us clarify what is meant by a (strong) solution of initial value problem (
2.2
). For this we need the following notion. We denote by

X^{T}(t,\omega)=X({t\wedge T(\omega)},\omega)=X(t,\omega)\mathbf{1}_{\{t<T(\omega)\}}+X(T(\omega),\omega)\mathbf{1}_{\{t\geq T(\omega)\}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X=(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}
be a stochastic process and
T_{\infty}
a stopping time. Then
(X,T_{\infty})
, often abbreviated by
X

local (strong)

solution
of (
2.2
) on the interval
[-\tau,T_{\infty})
, whenever
X_{0}=\Phi
holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
X^{T_{k}}\in\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
, and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. we have

X(t\wedge T_{k})=\Phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{k}}{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}(X^{T_{k}})(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{\Phi}(X^{T_{k}})(s-)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(2.6)

for all integers
k\geq 1
, where
(T_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is any non-decreasing sequence of finite stopping times such that
T_{k}\nearrow T_{\infty}
holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., as

Further, if
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}|X(t\wedge T_{k})(\omega)|=\infty

, for almost all

then

is a

with

we have

(2.7)

for all integers

. Finally, a local solution

is a

of (

) if

).

Clearly, if

In addition, when we talk about a solution, we may as well always consider a version with càdlàg paths everywhere, because of the fact that these two versions are indistinguishable

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
a,b:D[-\tau,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are proper locally Lipschitz functionals. Then for every initial process
\Phi\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0]
, there exists a stopping time
T_{\infty}
and a stochastic process
X
such that
(X,T_{\infty})
is a local solution to initial value problem (
2.2
).
The stopping time
T_{\infty}
can be chosen such that
(X,T_{\infty})
is a maximal solution of (
2.2
). This maximal solution is unique.

If, in addition, the functionals
a,b
satisfy the linear growth condition, then (
2.2
) admits a unique global solution. This global solution is in fact a semimartingale.

Proof.

For general semimartingales
M
, we refer to
[
20
,
31
]
. Now let
M
be of class (HDol); see Appendix
A
. We shall proceed by dividing the proof into three steps. In Step 1, we show existence and uniqueness of global solutions assuming that the initial condition is square integrable, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}<\infty
, and that the functionals
a,b
are globally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. In Step 2, we extend the results to the case where
a
and
b
are locally Lipschitz.
In Step 3, we show that square integrability of the initial condition is a superfluous condition to pose.

Step 1.

First, suppose
X
is a global solution. Then the linear growth condition combined with an application of Hölder’s inequality, Doob’s maximal inequality
[
21
, Thm. 1.3.8]
, and Grönwall’s inequality
[
27
, Thm. 1.8.1]
, yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq t\leq T}|X(t)|^{2}\right]\leq(1+4\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2})e^{3K_{\rm lin}T(T+4\lambda)}-1,

(2.8)

for any
T\geq 0.
For more details, we refer to the proof of
[
27
, Lem. 2.3.2]
for the continuous case or the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
Growth estimate (
2.8
) together with global Lipschitz continuity yields uniqueness. Indeed, suppose
X
and
Y
are two global solutions, then for any
0\leq t\leq T
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq s\leq t}|X(s)-Y(s)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2K_{\rm lip}(T+4\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\|X_{s}-Y_{s}\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq 2K_{\rm lip}(T+4\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq r\leq s}|X(r)-Y(r)|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Since
T\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq s\leq T}|X(s)-Y(s)|^{2}\right]
is integrable thanks to (
2.8
), we obtain that, in fact,
the latter equals zero after invoking Grönwall’s inequality.

Existence of a global solution
X
is obtained via a Picard’s iteration. Define for any
n\geq 0
the process

X^{n+1}(t)=\Phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}(X^{n})(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{B}_{\Phi}(X^{n})(s-)\,\mathrm{d}M(s),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.10)

With similar techniques as before, one can show by induction that, for any
n\geq 0,
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|X^{n+1}(t)-X^{n}(t)|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C[LT]^{n}}{n!},

(2.11)

where
L=2K_{\rm lip}(T+4\lambda)
and
C=2K_{\rm lin}(T+4\lambda)(1+\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2})T.
Consequently, we can show with the help of Borel-Cantelli that there exists a process
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
such that
X\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
, for all
T\geq 0,
and for which we have
X^{n}\to X
uniformly in
t
with probability 1. Moreover, we clearly have convergence
X^{n}(t)\to X(t)
, for any
t\geq 0,

in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as well, thanks to (
2.11
), which allows us to show that
X
is a solution to
(
2.3
).
See
[
27
, Thm. 2.3.1]
for further details.

Step 2.

We shall follow a standard truncation procedure and it is completely in line with
[
27
, Thm. 2.3.4]
. For each integer
n\geq 1
, introduce the truncated functionals

a_{n}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}a(\varphi)&\text{ if }\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq n,\\
a(n\varphi/\|\varphi\|_{\infty})&\text{ if }\|\varphi\|_{\infty}>n,\end{cases}

(2.12)

and
b_{n}(\varphi)
similarly. Then
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are globally Lipschitz continuous, for any
n\geq 1.
Step 1 gives us a unique solution
X^{n}
to the equation

X^{n}(t)=\Phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}a_{n}\left(X^{n}_{s}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{t}b_{n}\left(X^{n}_{s-}\right)\mathrm{d}M(s),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.13)

Define the increasing sequence of stopping times
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by

T_{n}=n\wedge\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left|X^{n}(t)\right|\geq n\right\}.

(2.14)

If we assume, in addition, that
a
and
b
satisfy the linear growth condition, then one can show that
T_{n}\to\infty
holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., as
n\to\infty,
and that
X(t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}X^{n}(t)
is well-defined and solves (
2.3
). Indeed, we have
X^{n}(t)=X^{n+1}(t)
, for
0\leq t\leq T_{n}
, and
X(t\wedge T_{n})=X^{n}(t),
for any
n\geq 1.

However, whenever a functional no longer satisfies linear growth, an explosion may occur in finite time, hence only guaranteeing a maximal local solution.
Uniqueness can be proved by means of a stopping
time argument as well.

Step 3.
Instead of using a truncation argument as in
[
22
, p. 134]
, we proceed as follows. Suppose now
\Phi\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0]
is merely
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable and introduce the measurable map

m:\Omega\to(0,1],\quad\omega\mapsto e^{-\|\Phi(\omega)\|_{\infty}^{2}}.

(2.15)

Note that
\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\big]>0
because
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}<\infty
holds (
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.), since
\Phi
has càdlàg sample paths.
This allows us to define a new probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
as follows:

\mathbb{Q}(A)=\frac{\int_{A}m(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)}{\int_{\Omega}m(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)}=\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\big]\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{A}e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\big].

(2.16)

Clearly
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are equivalent measures, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\ll\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}.

Note that
\Phi
is square integrable with respect to the new measure

. Indeed, we have

\int_{\Omega}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}=\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\big]\right)^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}}\big]\right)^{-1}e^{-1}<\infty.

(2.17)

From Steps 1 and 2, we conclude that there exists a (local) solution to initial value problem (
2.2
), where equality holds

-a.s., and with the stochastic integral being computed under the law of this new probability measure. In particular, we have
X^{T_{k}}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathbb{Q}\times\mathrm{d}s)
, for all
T\geq 0,
for any appropriate sequence of stopping times

as in Definition

.
Since
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P},
it follows from
[

that all the stochastic integrals are
\mathbb{Q}
-indistinguishable from their corresponding versions computed under the law of
\mathbb{P}
. Under this law, all the stochastic integrals clearly exist in the ucp-sense, which is sufficient, but not necessarily with

-convergence. The solution we found satisfies initial value problem (

-a.s.. This completes the proof.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is of class
(HDol)
or
(HSqL)
, let
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a global solution of problem (
2.2
), and assume
a,b
satisfy the linear growth condition. For
p\geq 2,
if
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{-\tau\leq t\leq 0}|\Phi(t)|^{p}]<\infty
holds, then
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{-\tau\leq t\leq T}|X(s)|^{p}]<\infty
for all
T\geq 0.

Proof.

Fix
T>0
. As before, it suffices to consider the class (HDol) only. Introduce the increasing sequence of stopping times

T_{n}=T\wedge\inf\left\{t\in\left[0,T\right]:\left|X(t)\right|\geq n\right\},\quad n\geq 1.

(2.18)

Then the elementary estimate
|a+b+c|^{p}\leq 3^{p-1}(|a|^{p}+|b|^{p}+|c|^{p})
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq s\leq t}|X(s\wedge T_{n})|^{p}\right]\leq 3^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{p}

\displaystyle+3^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}|a(X(s))|\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}

(2.19)

\displaystyle+3^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\left|\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\int_{0}^{s\wedge T_{n}}b(X_{r-})\,\mathrm{d}M(r)\right|^{p},

for any
0\leq t\leq T.

Applying the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality

[
14
, Ch. VII]

results into

\mathbb{E}\left|\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\int_{0}^{s\wedge T_{n}}b(X_{r-})\,\mathrm{d}M(r)\right|^{p}\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}b(X_{s-})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s)\right|^{p/2},

(2.20)

for some
C_{p}>0
depending solely on
p\geq 2.

We like to point out that working with the predictable quadratic variation would be slightly unfavourable now, since this would result into an additional term
[
18
, Lem. VII.3.34]
, as opposed to (
2.20
). We refer to
[
25
]
for a similar discussion; see
[
25
, Thm. 4.19]
and
[
25
, Thm. 4.21]
in particular.

Since
([M](t))_{t\geq 0}
is a non-decreasing process, we can invoke Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}b(X_{s-})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s)\right|^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\big([M](T)\big)^{(p-2)/2}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}|b(X_{s-})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s)

(2.21)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\big(1+[M](T)\big)\big([M](T)\big)^{k_{p}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}|b(X_{s-})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s),

where
k_{p}=\lfloor(p-2)/2\rfloor\geq 0
is the greatest integer smaller or equal to the value
(p-2)/2
. If
M
is continuous, then it is a Brownian motion with drift and
[M](t)=\sigma^{2}t
is deterministic, making the final step in (
2.21
) and the part what will follow unnecessarily cumbersome.
For any integer
k\geq 1
and non-negative
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
-measurable function
A
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[[M](T)^{k}A\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[[M](T)^{k-1}A[M](T)\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[[M](T)^{k-1}A\int_{0}^{T}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times[0,T]}[M](T)^{k-1}A\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{M}\leq\lambda T\mathbb{E}\big[[M](T)^{k-1}A\big]\leq...\leq(\lambda T)^{k}\mathbb{E}[A].

(2.22)

Consequently, set
K_{p}=C_{p}(1+\lambda T)(\lambda T)^{k_{p}}
and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\int_{0}^{s\wedge T_{n}}b(X_{s-})\,\mathrm{d}M(s)\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq K_{p}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}|b(X_{s-})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}[M](s)

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leq K_{p}\lambda\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}|b(X_{s})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}K_{p}\lambda\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{n}}\left[1+\sup_{s-\tau\leq r\leq s}|X(r)|^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}K_{p}\lambda\int_{0}^{t}1+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq r\leq s}|X(r\wedge T_{n})|^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}s,

where we have used Fubini’s theorem and that
a,b
satisfy the linear growth condition.

A similar estimate can be obtained for the deterministic part. Grönwall’s lemma now implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{-\tau\leq s\leq t}|X(s\wedge T_{n})|^{p}\right]\leq(1+3^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{\infty})e^{KT}-1,\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(2.24)

for some
K>0.
Taking
n\to\infty
proves the assertion.
∎

About the functionals

Let us write
E
, e.g.,
E[0,\infty)
, where
E
can be either
C
or
D
. Of course, one could include the space of càglàd functions, but we will not need it here.

Given two normed spaces
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
and
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
, a map
f:X\to Y
is said to be locally Lipschitz continuous if for all integers
n\geq 1
there exists a constant
K_{n}>0
such that, for
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
satisfying
\|x_{1}\|_{X}\wedge\|x_{2}\|_{X}\leq n
, we have

\|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\|_{Y}\leq K_{n}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{X}.

(2.25)

The map
f
is (globally) Lipschitz continuous if
K_{n}=K_{\rm lip}
for all
n\geq 1,
for some
K_{\rm lip}>0.
The following definition is a special case, where
Y=\mathbb{R}
and
X=E[0,\infty)
is endowed with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}.
For completeness, we also add the definition of the linear growth condition.

Definition 2.4
.

A functional
f:E[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is called
(globally)  Lipschitz

continuous
when it satisfies:

\displaystyle\exists K_{\rm lip}>0\text{ such that }\forall\varphi,\psi\in E[-\tau,0]

\displaystyle\text{ we have }|f(\varphi)-f(\psi)|\leq K_{\rm lip}\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(s)-\psi(s)|.

(2.26)

A functional
f:E[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is called
locally  Lipschitz  continuous
when it satisfies:

\displaystyle\text{
$\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\,\exists K_{n}>0$ such that $\forall\varphi,\psi\in E[-\tau,0]$ we have}

(2.27)

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(s)|\vee|\psi(s)|\leq n\implies|f(\varphi)-f(\psi)|\leq K_{n}\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(s)-\psi(s)|.

A functional
f:E[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is said to satisfy the
linear  growth  condition
when it satisfies:

\text{
$\exists K_{\rm lin}>0$ such that $\forall\varphi\in E[-\tau,0]$ we have }|f(\varphi)|\leq K_{\rm lin}\left(1+\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(s)|\right).

(2.28)

Recall that globally Lipschitz
implies local Lipschitz continuity together with the linear growth condition. On the contrary, a locally Lipschitz functional satisfying the linear growth condition is not necessarily globally Lipschitz. An additional note, linear growth not only implies the existence and uniqueness of global solutions, but also allows for a variation of constants formula to hold
[
38
]
.

Example 2.5
.

Suppose

and consider

to be (locally) Lipschitz continuous. Then any

are finite signed Borel measures on

[

, p. 257]
and
[

, p. 1413]
. See also Example

Definition 2.6
.

A (locally) Lipschitz continuous functional

is said to be a

the mapping

(2.30)

where

for

, is càdlàg on
[0,\infty)
.

Fix

. If

\mathfrak{F}_{f,\psi}

Indeed,
let

(2.31)

This

Furthermore, observe that if we restrict our scope to the continuous setting, then being proper—that is, for all

such that

|\mathfrak{F}_{f,\psi}(t)-\mathfrak{F}_{f,\psi}(s)|\leq K_{n}\sup_{u\in[-\tau,0]}|\psi(t+u)-\psi(s+u)|\leq\varepsilon,

(2.32)

which holds for all time

with

Definition

seems a bit out of the ordinary at first glance, but is necessary to make sure that the maps

F:D[-\tau,\infty)\to D[0,\infty),\,\varphi\mapsto[t\mapsto f(\varphi_{t})],

(2.33)

and satisfies

F(\varphi)(t)=f(\varphi_{t}),\quad t\geq 0.

(2.34)

Observe that

is (lo)lidet; see the definition below (from
[

]
). Furthermore, note that lidet maps are the deterministic counterpart of the (random) Lipschitz functionals considered in

.

Definition 2.7
.

A map

deterministic

\displaystyle\text{

Ultimately, while a proper (locally) Lipschitz continuous functional gives rise to a (lo)lidet map, see identification (

Definition 2.8
.

A map
F:E[-\tau,\infty)\rightarrow E[0,\infty)

Proposition 2.9
.

For any autonomous
(

)

(

)

About the integrals

Let us recall that the first integral in (
2.3
) is a Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral. In here, the
s
may be replaced by
s-
, because the stochastic processes

\int_{0}^{\cdot}{Y(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s
and
\int_{0}^{\cdot}{Y(s-)}\,\mathrm{d}s

for any
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}\in\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
are indistinguishable. Indeed, we have
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely

\left|\int_{0}^{t}{Y(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}{Y(s-)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}{|\Delta Y(s)|}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{[0,t]\cap\{\Delta Y\neq 0\}}{|\Delta Y(s)|}\,\mathrm{d}s=0,

(2.41)

where
\Delta Y(s)=Y(s)-Y(s-),

since
t\mapsto\Delta Y(t)(\omega)
is equal to zero except for at most countably many times
t
, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
. See
[
2
,
10
,
19
,
39
]
for more on Lebesgue–Stieltjes integration.

The second integral in (
2.3
) is a stochastic integral, which is again a semimartingale and takes values in
\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
. A stochastic integral is defined by taking limits of integrated simple predictable processes, where convergence is in the ucp-sense. We thus follow the construction as in
[
31
]
. Note that the integrand of the second integral is an adapted càglàd process, provided that
X\in\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
, making (
2.3
) well-defined.
The first integral (after changing
s
into
s-
) can, in fact, be interpreted as a stochastic integral with respect to a (deterministic) semimartingale as well; the two interpretations coincide.
In the case of
M=(M(t))_{\geq 0}
being a Brownian motion, or more generally a continuous square integrable martingale, one is able to construct stochastic integrals with convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The advantage is that integrands do not need to be predictably measurable. It suffices, for instance, to assume progressive measurablity together with the integrabilty condition
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
,
T\geq 0
; this implies that the stochastic integral is again a square integrable martingale. One can extend this definition to a larger class of integrands by weakening the integrability condition slightly, resulting into an integral which is then merely a local martingale
[
19
,
21
,
27
,
34
]
. This allows for more general maps
\mathbf{A}_{\Psi}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}
, in the continuous setting. In practice it suffices to have
\mathbf{A}_{\Psi}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}
going from
\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
to itself.
It is worth pointing out that the stochastic integral can as well be extended to the class of progressively measurable integrands for suitable non-continuous (local) martingales
[
11
,
18
,
36
]
, e.g., square integrable martingales in (HDol).
However, one now needs to be cautious. For instance, the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral—if it exists—may no longer coincide with the stochastic integral if the integrand is not predictable; see
[
7
, Sec. 4.3]
for more details.

Non-autonomous equations

In this section, we briefly discuss stochastic differential equations with finite time delays where the coefficients

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathrm{d}X(t)&=&a(X_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+b(X_{t-},t-)\,\mathrm{d}M(t),&\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0,\\[1.42271pt]

(2.42)

System (

(2.43)

where

1.

the maps
\mathbf{A}_{\Psi},\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}:\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
, for some fixed

by

\mathbf{A}_{\Psi}(Y)(s,\omega):=a\left(Y^{\Psi}_{s}(\omega),s\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{B}_{\Psi}(Y)(s,\omega):=b\left(Y^{\Psi}_{s}(\omega),s\right),\quad s\geq 0;

(2.44)

2.

the process
Y^{\Psi}=(Y^{\Psi}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in (
2.44
) is the segment process of
Y^{\Psi}\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
, where
Y^{\Psi}(s)=\Psi(s)
for

and
Y^{\Psi}(s)=Y(s)
for
s\geq 0
;

3.

the functionals
a:D[-\tau,0]\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and

in (
2.44
) are assumed to be
time-proper
(

)
Lipschitz
(see Definition
2.10
).

Definition 2.10
.

A map

is called

(i)

, the first component

;

(ii)

for all

, the mapping

\mathfrak{G}_{f,\psi}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\,t\mapsto f(\psi_{t},t)

(2.45)

is also càdlàg.

A (local, maximal, global) solution to problem (

Example 2.12
.

Let

be locally Lipschitz. Define

as

(2.46)

where

are assumed to be bounded functions.
Exploiting the fact that both

If, in addition, we assume

3
Boundedness in probability of solution segments

In this section, we study the non-autonomous system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathrm{d}X(t)&=&a(X_{t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+b(X_{t-},t-)\,\mathrm{d}M(t),&\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0,\\[1.42271pt]
X(u)&=&\Phi(u)&\quad\text{ for }u\in[-\tau,0].\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

Without further explicit mention, we assume
\Phi\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0)
and that
a
and
b
are time-proper locally Lipschitz. Recall that for any
\varphi\in D[-\tau,0]
we have
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(s)|<\infty,
because
[-\tau,0]
is compact. In §
3.1
we provide the relevant definitions and a few additional preliminary notions.
In §
3.2
we study the impact of one-sided constraints imposed on the nonlinearity
a
and how this results into solution segments being bounded in probability. Finally, in §
3.3
we discuss how boundedness in probability of solutions and their segments relate to finite time blowups and global existence.

3.1
Preliminaries

Let
I
be some index set, e.g., take
I=\mathbb{N}
or
I=[0,\infty)
. A family

(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
of real-valued random variables is
bounded  above
(resp.,
below
)
in  probability
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a real number
M_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\eta\in I
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\eta}>M_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\big(\text{resp., }\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\eta}<M_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon\big).\quad

(3.2)

Differently put, we have
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is bounded above (resp., below) in probability if and only if

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\eta}\geq R\right)=0\quad\quad\quad\left(\text{resp., }\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\eta}\leq-R\right)=0\right).

(3.3)

A real-valued family
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is said to be
bounded  in  probability
if it is both bounded above and below in probability. That is, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a real number
M_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\eta\in I
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\eta}|>M_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon,

or, in short,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{\eta}|\geq R\right)=0.
We can generalise this concept.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space. An
X
-valued family
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is
bounded  in probability
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
M_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\eta\in I
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|Z_{\eta}\|>M_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon.

(3.4)

In other words,
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is bounded in probability when

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(\|Z_{\eta}\|\geq R\right)=0
holds.

Any
X
-valued random variable, and even any finite sequence of
X
-valued random variables, is bounded in probability. This is an immediate consequence of the continuity of the measure
\mathbb{P}
.
The following lemma provides another sufficient condition for a family to be bounded in probability.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is a family of
X
-valued random variables with

\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{E}\|Z_{\eta}\|^{2}<\infty.

(3.5)

Then
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is bounded in probability.

Proof.

An application of Markov’s inequality, for each
\eta\in I
, yields

\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(\|Z_{\eta}\|\geq R\right)=\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{P}\left(\|Z_{\eta}\|^{2}>R^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\sup_{\eta\in I}\mathbb{E}\|Z_{\eta}\|^{2}.

(3.6)

Taking
R\to\infty
shows that
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is bounded in probability.
∎

If we have two families of real-valued random variables
(Y_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
and
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
such that

Y_{\eta}\leq Z_{\eta},\quad\forall\eta\in I\quad\quad\quad\big(\textnormal{resp., }Y_{\eta}\geq Z_{\eta},\quad\forall\eta\in I\big),

(3.7)

and
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is bounded above (resp., below) in probability, then
(Y_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is so, too.
Another useful observation is that if two families
(Y_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
and
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
are bounded, bounded above or bounded below in probability, then so is its sum
(Y_{\eta}+Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
. The latter follows from
\mathbb{P}(Y_{\eta}+Z_{\eta}>R)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{\eta}>R/2)+\mathbb{P}(Z_{\eta}>R/2)

and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{\eta}+Z_{\eta}<R)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{\eta}<R/2)+\mathbb{P}(Z_{\eta}<R/2).

Remark 3.3
.

Throughout this work, we will often encounter that
(\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
needs to be bounded in probability (in
\mathbb{R}
). One could reformulate this as

(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
being bounded in probability in the Banach space
(D[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty}).
A reason to be cautious is because we know by Corollary
B.5
that segments
Y_{t}
are no
(D[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
-random variables; measurability cannot be with respect to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by the uniform topology.
We could speak of bounded in probability in
(D[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
while simultaneously we consider the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by the Skorokhod topology, but this deviates from the conventions in Appendix
B
and may lead to confusion. There is of course nothing to worry about when we restrict to the continuous setting
(C[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty}).

3.3
Additional comments on finite time blowups

In this section, we show how boundedness in probability of solutions relate to finite time blowups and global existence.
Let
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a (local) solution to (
3.1
) with explosion time
T_{\infty}
. By convention, we set
X=0
on
[T_{\infty},\infty)
.
If we now suppose that
X
is uniformly bounded
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. on
[-\tau,T_{\infty})
, then this implies
T_{\infty}=\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s..
On the other hand, if
X
is only known to be bounded in probability, then
\mathbb{P}(T_{\infty}=t)=0
holds, for all
t\geq 0,
but this does not exclude the possibility of
\mathbb{P}(T_{\infty}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}(\cup_{t\geq 0}\{T_{\infty}=t\})>0
being strictly positive because of the uncountable union.
Boundedness in probability gives us in particular that
\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathbb{P}(|X(s)|>R)=0
holds, for all
t\geq 0
, while the value of
\mathbb{P}(T_{\infty}\leq t)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|X(s)|>R)
remains inconclusive due to the fact that the supremum is inside the probability now.

More control is required to guarantee global existence. For instance,
if the segments are bounded in probability, i.e., if
(\|X_{t}\|_{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{\infty}<\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{T_{\infty}\in[(n-1)\tau,n\tau)\}\right)

(3.31)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{(n-1)\tau\leq t\leq n\tau}|X(t)|>R\right)=0,

by continuity of the measure
\mathbb{P}.
Likewise, if the process
(\sup_{\theta\in[-\tau,0]}X(t+\theta))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability, then a finite time blowup towards
+\infty
does not occur
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{s\to T_{\infty}}X(s)=+\infty,\,T_{\infty}<\infty\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{(n-1)\tau\leq t\leq n\tau}X(t)>R\right)=0.

(3.32)

Similarly, when
(\inf_{\theta\in[-\tau,0]}X(t+\theta))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded below in probability, then a finite time blowup towards
-\infty
will not happen.

Relaxing the global existence assumption in Proposition
3.5
still allows us to deduce that the stochastic process
(\sup_{\theta\in[-\tau,0]}X(t+\theta))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above in probability whenever
X
is bounded above in probability and
a\leq\alpha_{\rm max}
. This is due to fact that the bound in (
3.20
) expresses
X(t+\theta)
in terms of its history only, while (
3.23
) contains the term

on the right hand side. In particular, we can conclude from (
3.32
) that a finite time blowup towards
+\infty
will not occur
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., which is in line with Proposition
3.4
. Nevertheless, as mentioned previously, only assuming either
a\leq\alpha_{\rm max}
or

is insufficient to deduce that
(\|X_{t}\|_{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability; we merely have that the process
(\|X_{t}\|_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{t<T_{\infty}})_{t\geq 0}
is bounded in probability. It is important to note that the latter does not imply the former, as the following example illustrates.

Example 3.7
.

Let

be a non-negative random variable with probability distribution

absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Consider the initial value problem

Suppose

In order to prove Theorem
4.3
, we need the following tightness result. The proposition below is obtained by exploiting Kolmogorov’s tightness criterion (Theorem
B.24
). It is worth noting that the following proposition requires no additional assumptions on the noise coefficient
b.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
X=(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}
is a global solution to (
4.3
). If the stochastic process
(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}{\|X_{u}\|_{\infty}})_{t\geq\tau}
is bounded in probability, then the segment process
({X_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in
C[-\tau,0]
.

Proof.

Let
R>0
be arbitrary and define the truncated segment process

X_{t}^{R}(\theta):=X(t+\theta)\mathbf{1}_{\{\|X_{r}\|_{\infty}\leq R\text{ for all }r\in[t-\tau,t]\}},\quad\mbox{for all }\theta\in[-\tau,0]\mbox{ and }t\geq 0.

(4.8)

Fix now
t\geq\tau
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[-\tau,0]
with
\theta_{2}>\theta_{1}
. Introduce the set

{{A_{R;t}}}:=\bigl\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\,\|X_{r}(\omega)\|_{\infty}\leq R\mbox{ for all }r\in[t-\tau,t]\,\bigr\}.

(4.9)

Then
X_{t}^{R}=X_{t}
on
A_{R;t}
, and

\displaystyle\bigl|X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{2})-X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{1})\bigr|

\displaystyle=|X(t+\theta_{2})-X(t+\theta_{1})|\mathbf{1}_{{A_{R;t}}}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}|a(X_{s})|\mathbf{1}_{{A_{R;t}}}\,\mathrm{d}s+\left|\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|\mathbf{1}_{{A_{R;t}}}.

On this set
{A_{R;t}}
, observe that
b(X_{s})
and
b(X_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{\|X_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq R\}}
are equal. Thus, by the local character of stochastic integrals
[
14
, Thm. VIII.23]
, see also
[
31
, Thm. II.18]
, we obtain

\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W(s)=\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})1_{\{\|X_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq R\}}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\quad\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.s.\ on }{A_{R;t}}.

(4.11)

This yields

|X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{2})-X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{1})|\leq\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}|a(X_{s})|\mathbf{1}_{{A_{R;t}}}\,\mathrm{d}s+\left|\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{\|X_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq R\}}\mathrm{d}W(s)\right|.

(4.12)

Let
B_{R}:=\{\varphi\in C[-\tau,0]\colon\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq R\,\}
. Since the functionals
a
and
b
are locally Lipschitz, they are bounded on
B_{R}
,
i.e., there are some
\alpha_{R},\beta_{R}>0
such that

|a(\varphi)|\leq\alpha_{R}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad|b(\varphi)|\leq\beta_{R},\quad\hbox{for all }\varphi\in B_{R}.

(4.13)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl|X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{2})-X_{t}^{R}(\theta_{1})\bigr|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 8\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}|a(X_{s})|\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{R;t}}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{4}

(4.14)

\displaystyle+8\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})\mathbf{1}_{\{\|X_{s}\|\leq R\}}\,\mathrm{d}W(s)\right)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 8|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|^{4}\alpha^{4}_{R}+8C_{4}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t+\theta_{1}}^{t+\theta_{2}}b(X_{s})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|^{2},

with
\tilde{C}=8\alpha^{4}_{R}\tau^{2}+8C_{4}\beta^{4}_{R},
and where
C_{4}>0
is the constant from the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality
[
34
, Ch. IV.4]
.
Since
(X_{t}(-\tau))_{t\geq 0}
is tight, we conclude that
(X_{t}^{R})_{t\geq 0}
, for any
R>0,
is tight in
C[-\tau,0]
thanks to Theorem
B.24
.

From the latter, we infer that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrarily given. Since the process
(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}\|X_{u}\|_{\infty})_{t\geq\tau}
is bounded in probability by assumption, there is an
R_{\varepsilon}>0
where

\textstyle\mathbb{P}(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}{\|X_{u}\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\varepsilon}})>1-\varepsilon/2,\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq\tau.

(4.15)

Since
(X_{t}^{R_{\varepsilon}})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in
C[-\tau,0]
, there is a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}\subset C[-\tau,0]
such that

\mathbb{P}(X^{R_{\varepsilon}}_{t}\in K_{\varepsilon})>1-\varepsilon/2,\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0.

(4.16)

From this, as
X_{t}^{R}=X_{t}
on
A_{R;t}
for any
R>0
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\not\in K_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\not\in K_{\varepsilon},A_{R_{\varepsilon};t})+\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\not\in K_{\varepsilon},A_{R_{\varepsilon};t}^{c})

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\not\in K_{\varepsilon})+\mathbb{P}(A_{R_{\varepsilon};t}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\not\in K_{\varepsilon})\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\mathbb{P}(\exists u\in[t-\tau,t]:\|X_{u}\|_{\infty}>R_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{R_{\varepsilon}}\not\in K_{\varepsilon})\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\mathbb{P}(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}\|X_{u}\|_{\infty}>R_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon,

for all
t\geq\tau.
This shows that the partial segment process
(X_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is tight. Tightness of the finite time horizon process
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq\tau}
follows from Proposition
B.16
;
the family
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is stochastically continuous in
C[-\tau,0]
, since
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a (stochastically) continuous process
[
33
, Lem. 2.3]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3

The result readily follows from Proposition
4.6
and the Krylov–Bogoliubov existence theorem in Appendix

.∎

Theorem 4.8

Assume

The goal is to investigate when

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose
X=(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
is a stochastic process which is bounded in probability. Then the partial segment process
(X_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
, from time
\tau
and onwards, is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
whenever the family

\big(X(t+s)-X(t-\tau),s\in[-\tau,0]\big)_{t\geq\tau}

(4.21)

is tight in
D[-\tau,0].

Proof.

Define
Z_{t}(s):=X(t-\tau)
for all
s\in[-\tau,0],t\geq 0.
We subsequently obtain that the family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of constant processes on
[-\tau,0]
is tight in
C[-\tau,0]
, since
X
is bounded in probability for all time
(i.e., on
[-\tau,\infty)
). As a result of Corollary
B.21
, the family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
C
-tight.

We proceed by assuming the family in (
4.21
) is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
. In other words, we have that
(X_{t}-Z_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
. The sum
(\left(X_{t}-Z_{t}\right)+Z_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is also tight in
D[-\tau,0]
, which is due to Lemma
B.22
. Therefore
(X_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
.
∎

Our new objective is thus to show that the family (
4.21
) is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
. In the remainder of this section, we return to the notation in §
2
to improve readability. Let
X
be a global solution to equation (
4.1
), then
X
is in
\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
and satisfies

X(t)=\Phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{A}(X)(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{B}(X)(s-)}\,\mathrm{d}M(s),\quad t\geq 0,

(4.22)

where we write
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
and

; we drop the dependence of the initial condition.

We provide two different proofs of the proposition below. The semimartingale
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is assumed to be of class (HSqL) in one of those proofs. Showing this result under the less restrictive hypothesis (HIntL) is rather advanced; it makes use of semimartingale characteristics (see Appendix
B.5
) and the proof is to a large extend in line with the proof of
[

, Prop. 4.3]

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is of class
(HIntL)
and suppose
X=(X(t))_{-\tau\leq t<\infty}
is a global solution to (
4.1
) and bounded in probability. Then the family

\big(X(t+s)-X(t-\tau),s\in[-\tau,0]\big)_{t\geq\tau}

(4.23)

is tight in
D[-\tau,0]
if
\mathbf{B}
is bounded and

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq\tau}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)|>R)=0

holds.

Proof of Proposition
4.10
assuming
(HSqL)
.

It suffices to assume
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale, as the predictable finite variation part of a process in (HSqL) is directly propertional to
t.
In fact, the only thing we will need now is that the predictable quadratic variation process
\langle M\rangle=(\langle M\rangle(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfies
\langle M\rangle(t)=\lambda t,
for some
\lambda>0
.
We are led to consider the family
(Y_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
, where

\displaystyle Y_{t}(s)

\displaystyle=X(t+s)-X(t-\tau)

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\mathbf{A}(X)(u)\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{(t-\tau,t+s]}\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)\,\mathrm{d}M(u),

for
s\in[-\tau,0].
Since
X\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
, we obtain that the
Y_{t}
are
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables; see Remark
B.5
. Let us recall
X
is a semimartingale with respect to the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
with
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{-\tau\leq s<\infty}
. Introduce now for every
t\geq\tau
the filtration

\mathbb{F}_{t}=(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\in[t-\tau,t]}
and observe that a segment
Y_{t}
can be seen as semimartingale on
[-\tau,0]
adapted to
\mathbb{F}_{t}
.

Thanks to Lemma
B.22
, it suffices to show that the families
(J_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
and
(I_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
, defined by

J_{t}(s)=\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\mathbf{A}(X)(u)\mathrm{d}u\quad\text{and}\quad I_{t}(s)=\int_{(t-\tau,t+s]}\textbf{B}(X)(u-)\,\mathrm{d}M(u),\quad s\in[-\tau,0],

(4.25)

are tight families, as all the
J_{t}
are
C[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables; see Corollary
B.21
.
We obtain (
C
-)tightness of
(J_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
in case the following holds:

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq\tau}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)|>R\right)=0,

(4.26)

which is immediate from Lemma
B.25
.

Consequently, we are left with showing that
(I_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is a tight family.
We have that
I_{t}
is a square integrable martingale on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}_{t},\mathbb{P})
. The predictable quadratic variation of
(I_{t}(s))_{s\in[-\tau,0]}
reads

\langle I_{t}\rangle(s)=\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}|\textbf{B}(X)(u-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\langle M\rangle(s),\quad s\in[-\tau,0].

(4.27)

In accordance with Theorem
B.31
, we are expected to prove that the family of predictable quadratic variations
(\langle I_{t}\rangle)_{t\geq\tau}
is
C
-tight. Since the functional
\mathbf{B}
is bounded, say by
\beta\geq 0
, we find

\displaystyle\langle I_{t}\rangle(s)\leq\beta^{2}[\langle M\rangle(t+s)-\langle M\rangle(t-\tau)]=\lambda\beta^{2}(s+\tau).

(4.28)

Each process
\langle I_{t}\rangle
gets strongly majorised by the same continuous process
s\mapsto\lambda\beta^{2}(s+\tau)
. According to Lemma
B.23
, it now suffices to prove
C
-tightness of the continuous-time family
(Q_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
, where each

equals the

. The

will follow from tightness, due to Corollary

, and tightness is trivial due to the fact that a family consisting of a single measure only is tight in a complete separable metric space; see Appendix
B.3
.
∎

.

As in the previous proof, we consider the family
(Y_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
, where

\displaystyle Y_{t}(s)

\displaystyle=X(t+s)-X(t-\tau)

(4.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\mathbf{A}(X)(u)\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{(t-\tau,t+s]}\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)\,\mathrm{d}M(u),

(4.34)

(a_{Y_{t}})_{t\geq\tau}

For any

\displaystyle A_{Y_{t}}(s)=

\displaystyle\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\left|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)\right|\mathrm{d}u+(s+\tau)\left((|b|\beta+c)+\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big((\beta^{2}x^{2})\wedge 1\big)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right),

with

The claim basically follows from straightforward estimation. We demonstrate the most insightful estimate below:

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}\left(B_{Y_{t}}\right)(s)=

\displaystyle\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\bigg|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)+b\,\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)

(4.38)

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)|\leq 1\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right|\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)|+|b|\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}|\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)|\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle+\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|x\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)\right|\left|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x\mathbf{B}(X)(u-)|\leq 1\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\right|\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\int_{t-\tau}^{t+s}\left|\mathbf{A}(X)(u)\right|\mathrm{d}u+(s+\tau)(|b|\beta+c).

Observe that

is a continuous process, for all

B.21
. The second term of (

4.3
Delay equations with stochastic negative feedback

In this section we demonstrate how to apply Theorem

. A general approach would be to search for a real-valued family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
that is bounded in probability and such that

\sup_{u\in[t-\tau,t]}|a(X_{u})|\leq Z_{t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq\tau.

(4.43)

We perform this method on delay equations of the form (
4.44
), i.e., delay equations with stochastic negative feedback, as preparation for
[
7
]
. Of course, invoking Theorem
4.8
would also have sufficed.

Corollary 4.11
.

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz continuous, positive on
(0,\infty)
, and bounded from above. In addition, assume
\gamma,r>0
. Consider the stochastic delay differential equation

\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\left[-\gamma+re^{-Y(t)}f\big(e^{Y(t-1)}\big)\right]\mathrm{d}t+a\left(Y_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+b\left(Y_{t-}\right)\mathrm{d}L(t),

(4.44)

where
a,b
are time-independent and proper locally Lipschitz. Further, assume
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
is of class
(HIntL).
Let
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
be non-negative constants such that

|a(\varphi)|\leq\alpha\quad\text{ and }\quad b(\varphi)^{2}\leq\beta^{2},\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in D[-1,0].

(4.45)

Finally, assume that for every initial process
\Phi

the corresponding solution exists globally. If one of these solutions is bounded in probability,
then there exists an invariant measure and hence a stationary solution.

Proof.

According to Theorem
4.7
, it suffices to show

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{u\in[t-1,t]}|\mathbf{A}(Y)(u)|>R\right)=0,

(4.46)

for some solution
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
bounded in probability, where

\mathbf{A}(Z)(t)=-\gamma+re^{-Z(t)}f\big(e^{Z(t-1)}\big)+a(Z_{t}),\quad t\geq 0.

(4.47)

Let
M
be an upper bound for the nonlinearity
f
. A straightforward computation shows

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[t-1,t]}|\mathbf{A}(Y)(u)|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\gamma+r\sup_{u\in[t-1,t]}e^{-Y(u)}|f(e^{Y(u-1)})|

(4.48)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\gamma+rM\sup_{u\in[t-1,t]}e^{-Y(u)}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\gamma+rM\exp{\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}}.

Hence, due to the fact that the exponential is invertible, condition (

In the text below we clarify what is meant by each of these hypotheses and provide examples and additional information accordingly.

(HSpec)

The process
Z=(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
starts at zero, i.e.,
Z(0)=0
, and is a special semimartingale, i.e., a semimartigale which admits a unique decomposition

(M(t))_{t\geq 0}

(A(t))_{t\geq 0}
a finite variation process that is predictably measurable.

(HIntL)

The process
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}

Lévy processes are semimartingales, as a consequence of the Lévy–Itô decomposition
[
3
,
26
,
31
,
35
]

(HDol)

The process
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}

\mu_{M}\ll\mathrm{d}s\times\mathbb{P}

\lambda>0

The Doléans measure

36
]

\sigma

by

\mu_{M}(A)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(s,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}[M](s,\omega)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathrm{d}[M],\quad A\in\mathcal{P},

(A.2)

where

[M](t)=M(t)^{2}-2\int_{0}^{t}M(s-)\,\mathrm{d}M(s),

where

\nu_{M}(A)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(s,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\langle M\rangle(s,\omega)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathrm{d}\langle M\rangle,\quad A\in\mathcal{P}.

Surprisingly,

The importance of the bounded Radon–Nikodym derivative assumption in (HDol) is due to the fact that this results into the following inequality:

\int_{[0,t]\times\Omega}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{M}=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}[M](s)=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}\langle M\rangle(s)\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(HSqL)

The process

(HSqLM)

The process

Let

L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}

(HJudi)

The process

Note that

An extensive study of both the uniform topology and Skorokhod topology on the space of càdlàg functions can be found in, e.g.,

We can equip

(C[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\quad\text{and}\quad(D[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty}),

(C[-\tau,0],\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

(D[-\tau,0],

{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}})
fails to be separable
[
18
, p. 325]
.
Recall that separability is a topological property,
which leads us to the Skorokhod topology.

Definition B.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b.
The space
D[a,b]
of càdlàg functions
\varphi:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
can be endowed with the

d_{S}(\varphi,\psi):=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda[a,b]}\big(\|\varphi\circ\lambda-\psi\|_{\infty}+\|\mathrm{Id}-\lambda\|_{\infty}\big),\quad\varphi,\psi\in D[a,b],

(B.2)

where
\Lambda[a,b]:=\{\lambda:[a,b]\rightarrow[a,b]:\lambda
is an increasing homeomorphism
\}.
The metric

induces a topology called the
Skorokhod  topology
. Any
\lambda\in\Lambda[a,b]
is called a
change  of  time
.

Clearly, we have
d_{S}(\varphi,\psi)\leq\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty}
—choose
\lambda=\text{Id}
as homeomorphism—which yields that the Skorokhod topology is weaker than the uniform topology. A slightly less trivial fact is that the subspace topology of the Skorokhod topology on the space of continuous functions
C[a,b]
coincides with the uniform topology
[
18
, Prop. 1.17]
.
In the uniform topology, two functions
\varphi
and
\psi
are near one another if the graph of
\varphi
can be carried onto the graph of the function
\psi

by a uniformly small perturbation of the ordinates, i.e., a uniform deformation in the vertical axis, while the abscissas is
kept fixed, i.e., no deformations are made on the horizontal axis. On the other hand, we also allow a uniformly small change of the
time scale in the Skorokhod topology. While doing this, however, we need to take a penalisation factor into account. We illustrate the effect of a change of time in Figure

The metrics

Proof.

We refer to either
[

and

[

[

]
. In
[

as the Skorokhod metric and excludes the intermediate step of defining

[
38
]

Remark B.3
.

\sigma
-algebra of
D[-\tau,0]
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}(D[-\tau,0])
, coincides with the smallest
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
D[-\tau,0]
such that the maps
\pi^{t}:x\rightarrow x(t)
are measurable for all
t\in[-\tau,0].

Proof.

We refer to
[
29
, Thm. 2.1]
and
[
29
, Thm. 7.1]
.
∎

This means the Skorokhod topology is really the right topology if one wants to interpret segments of stochastic processes as random variables; see the corollary below and
[
4
, p. 135]
for additional information.

Corollary B.5

(Measurability of segment processes)
.

Only under the Skorokhod topology, we have that
X
is a
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variable if and only if, for any
t\in[-\tau,0]
,
X(\omega,t)=\pi^{t}(X(\omega))
defines a random variable on
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for any
Y\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty),
the segment process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted
D[-\tau,0]
-valued stochastic process.

Indeed, this statement is no longer valid when
D[-\tau,0]
is equipped with the uniform norm, due to the strict inclusion in (
B.5
). Regardless of the topology on
D[-\tau,0]
, we have for
Y\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
that, for each
t\geq 0
,
\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{s\in[-\tau,0]}|Y_{t}(s)|
is a real-valued random variable.

B.2
Arzelà–Ascoli theorems: compactness and modulus of continuity

In this section, our goal is to characterise compact subsets of
C[-\tau,0]
and
D[-\tau,0].
But first, we will state a general Arzelà–Ascoli result; see Theorem
B.7
. Throughout this section, we will assume that
X
is a compact Hausdorff space and we denote by
C(X)
the space of real-valued continuous functions on
X
, endowed with the topology induced by the supremum norm.

Definition B.6
.

A subset
A\subset C(X)
is said to be
equicontinuous
if for every
\varepsilon>0,
and every
x\in X
, there is a neighbourhood
U_{x}
about
x

such that for all
y\in U_{x}
we have
|f(y)-f(x)|<\varepsilon
, for every
f\in A
. Further, a subset
A\subset C(X)
is called
pointwise bounded
if for every
x\in X
we have

\sup_{f\in A}|f(x)|<\infty
.

We see that a family of functions
A
is equicontinuous when all functions are continuous, hence uniformly continuous, and when all functions variate controllably over an appropriately given neighbourhood about any point in space.
We like to point out that the Arzelà–Ascoli
theorem completely characterises relative compactness in
C(X)
; a set
A
is called
relatively compact
if the closure of
A
is compact. The following result is analogous, in some sense, to the Heine–Borel theorem.

Theorem B.7

(Arzelà–Ascoli)
.

Suppose
X
is a compact Hausdorff space. Then any
A\subset C(X)
is relatively compact if and only if it is equicontinuous and pointwise bounded.

Proof.

A proof can be found in
[
15
, Thm. 7]
.
∎

One can characterise compactness even more concretely by introducing a modulus of continuity. We restrict ourselves to
X=[-\tau,0]
together with the usual Euclidean topology.

Definition B.8
.

For any real-valued function
\varphi
with domain
[-\tau,0]
, and
T\subset[-\tau,0]
, we introduce

\omega(\varphi,T):=\sup_{s,t\in T}|\varphi(s)-\varphi(t)|.

(B.6)

The
modulus  of  continuity
for any
\varphi:[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\omega(\varphi,\delta):=\sup_{-\tau\leq t\leq-\delta}\omega(\varphi,[t,t+\delta])=\sup_{|s-t|\leq\delta}|\varphi(s)-\varphi(t)|,\quad 0<\delta\leq\tau.

(B.7)

Clearly, a necessary and sufficient condition for
\varphi
to be (uniformly) continuous over
[-\tau,0]
, i.e.,
\varphi\in C[-\tau,0]
, is

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\omega(\varphi,\delta)=0.

(B.8)

Theorem B.9

(Arzelà–Ascoli for
C[-\tau,0]
)
.

A necessary and sufficient condition for a subset
A\subset C[-\tau,0]
to be relatively compact in the uniform topology is to require

\sup_{\varphi\in A}|\varphi(s)|<\infty,\quad\text{for some $s\in[-\tau,0]$},

(B.9)

and

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\varphi\in A}\omega(\varphi,\delta)=0.

(B.10)

Proof.

Conditions (
B.9
) and (
B.10
) combined is equivalent to being pointwise bounded and equicontinuous (see further discussion), hence the statement is an immediate consequence of the general Arzelà-Ascoli theorem in Theorem
B.7
.

On the other hand, a much more direct proof for this statement can be found, for instance, in
[
4
, Thm. 7.2]
. Note there is a small difference in (
B.9
) present; we only assume
\sup_{\varphi\in A}|\varphi(s)|<\infty,
for some
s\in[-\tau,0]
, instead of
s
necessarily being the starting point
s=-\tau
. We can do this because condition (
B.10
), which is (uniform) equicontinuity over
[-\tau,0]
, together with (
B.9
) yields

\sup_{t\in[-\tau,0]}\sup_{\varphi\in A}|\varphi(t)|<\infty.

(B.11)

This uniform bound implies pointwise boundedness and allows us to let
s
in (
B.9
) be arbitrary.
∎

The idea would now be to introduce a modulus “of continuity” for the space of càdlàg functions. Differently put, we would like to have a mapping
\varpi
which ensures us that
\varphi\in D[-\tau,0]
holds true whenever a similar condition as in (
B.8
) is satisfied.

Proposition B.10
.

A function
\varphi:[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
is in
D[-\tau,0]
if and only if

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\varpi(\varphi,\delta)=0,

(B.12)

where

\varpi(\varphi,\delta):=\inf\left\{\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\omega(\varphi,[t_{i-1},t_{i})):k\in\mathbb{N},\,\{t_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\in\Xi\right\},\quad 0<\delta\leq\tau,

(B.13)

with
\Xi
the set of all finite sequences
\{t_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with
-\tau=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{k-1}<t_{k}=0
and
\min_{1\leq i\leq k}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|>\delta
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
[
4
, Lem. 1]
and the discussion thereafter.
∎

Notice that
\varpi(\varphi,\delta)
is unaffected if the value of
\varphi(0)
changes, which is a relevant property. Let us now compare the moduli
\omega
and
\varpi
for functions in
D[-\tau,0]
. Observe that the interval
[-\tau,0)
can be divided into subintervals
\left[t_{i-1},t_{i}\right)
satisfying
\delta<t_{i}-t_{i-1}\leq 2\delta<\tau,
hence

\varpi(\varphi,\delta)\leq\omega(\varphi,2\delta),

(B.14)

in case
\delta<\tau/2
holds. Obviously,
there cannot be such an inequality in the other direction, because the condition in (
B.8
) does not hold for discontinuous functions
\varphi
. Nonetheless, we do have

\omega(\varphi,\delta)\leq 2\varpi(\varphi,\delta)+\Delta_{\text{sup}}(\varphi),

(B.15)

for all
0<\delta\leq\tau
and
\varphi\in D[-\tau,0]
, where

\Delta_{\text{sup}}(\varphi)=\sup_{t\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(t)-\varphi(t-)|,\quad\varphi\in D[-\tau,0].

(B.16)

Any càdlàg function defined on a compact space only allows finitely many jumps to exceed a given positive number
[
4
, p. 122]
. Therefore, the overall maximum absolute jump
\Delta_{\text{sup}}(\varphi)
of any càdlàg function
\varphi
is finite and attained.
In conclusion, for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, we find

\varpi(\varphi,\delta/2)\leq\omega(\varphi,\delta)\leq 2\varpi(\varphi,\delta)+\Delta_{\text{sup}}(\varphi),\quad\varphi\in D[-\tau,0].

(B.17)

It is interesting to explicitly mention the fact

\varpi(\varphi,\delta/2)\leq\omega(\varphi,\delta)\leq 2\varpi(\varphi,\delta),\quad\varphi\in C[-\tau,0],

(B.18)

and thus the moduli
\omega
and
\varpi
are essentially equivalent for continuous functions. This observation is extremely useful in Corollary
B.21
, for example.

It turns out that the modulus
\varpi
“of continuity” allows us to state an analogue of Theorem
B.9
and characterise compact sets in the Skorokhod space
D[-\tau,0]
.

Theorem B.11

(Arzelà–Ascoli for
D[-\tau,0]
)
.

A necessary and sufficient condition for a set
A\subset D[-\tau,0]
to be relatively compact in the Skorokhod topology is to require

\sup_{\varphi\in A}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}<\infty,

(B.19)

and

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\varphi\in A}\varpi(\varphi,\delta)=0.

(B.20)

Proof.

We refer to the proof of
[
4
, Thm. 12.3]
. In the setting of càdlàg functions with their domain being the half line
[0,\infty)
, see the slightly more involved proof in
[
18
, Thm. 1.14]
.
∎

Obviously, the theorem above cannot be a corollary of Theorem
B.7
, because we are simply no longer in the continuous setting. Due to its high resemblance with Theorem
B.9
, we will still call it an Arzelà–Ascoli theorem.
It is interesting to note that (
B.11
) and (
B.19
) coincide, since we may interchange the order of the suprema, i.e.,

\sup_{t\in[-\tau,0]}\sup_{\varphi\in A}|\varphi(t)|\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\varphi\in A}\sup_{t\in[-\tau,0]}|\varphi(t)|

(B.21)

are equal. The main difference with the Skorokhod space is that now no longer a single
s
satisfying
\sup_{\varphi\in A}|\varphi(s)|<\infty
together with condition (
B.20
) implies (
B.19
). In order to see this, we construct a counterexample: for
A=\{n\mathbf{1}_{[-\tau/2,0]}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, we have
\varpi(\varphi,\delta)=0
for all
\varphi\in A
if
\delta<\tau/2
holds, but at the same time we have
\sup_{\varphi\in A}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=\infty
.

Ultimately, the important part of Theorem
B.11
is in fact the sufficiency, which will be used to prove tightness, as we will particularly see in Proposition
B.18
.

B.3
Tightness

Suppose
X
is a Hausdorff space. Denote by
\mathscr{M}(X)
the set of Borel signed measures
\mu
on
X
. We write
\mathscr{M}^{+}(X)\subset\mathscr{M}(X)
for the subset of all Borel measures.

Definition B.12
.

A collection
M\subset\mathscr{M}^{+}(X)
is said to be
tight
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}\subset X
with

\mu(X\backslash K_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon,\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in M.

(B.22)

Limiting ourselves to probability distributions, or in other words random variables, results into the following definition of tightness.

Definition B.13
.

A family of
X
-valued random variables
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is called
tight
in
X

if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}\subset X
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\eta}\notin K_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon,\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in I.

(B.23)

For
X
a finite dimensional normed space, we have that
(Z_{\eta})_{\eta\in I}
is tight if and only if it is bounded in probability. On the other hand, if the normed space is infinite dimensional, e.g.,
C[-\tau,0]
, then tightness is no longer equivalent with boundedness in probability. This is due to the fact closed balls in a normed space
X
are compact if and only if the dimension of
X
is finite.
Moreover, if
X=(X,d)
is a complete metric space and separable, then this results into any
X
-valued random variable, or any finite sequence of
X
-valued random variables, to be tight. This is because separability together with completeness implies that Borel measures are also Radon
[
5
]
.

Now assume
X=(X,d)
is a separable metric space and write
\mathscr{P}(X)
for the space of all Borel probability measures
\mu
on
X
. Thus, we have
\mu(X)=1
for all
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
. We endow
\mathscr{P}(X)
with the
weak topology
. This topology is, for example, induced by the metric

{\@index{\@indexphrase{$d_{0}$}}}d_{0}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\rm Lip_{\infty}}\left|\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right|,\quad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(X),

(B.24)

where
\rm Lip_{\infty}
is the space of functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}

with Lipschitz constant at most 1 and
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Separability ensures that
\mathscr{P}(X)
is metrisable, where
d_{0}
is an appropriate metric
[
5
, p. 193]
, and
\mathscr{P}(X)
itself becomes separable within the weak topology
[
5
, p. 213]
. Furthermore, if the metric space
X
is in addition assumed to be complete, then
(\mathscr{P}(X),d_{0})
is complete too
[
5
, p. 232]
.

Theorem B.14

(Prokhorov)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete separable metric space and suppose
\Gamma
is a subset of
\mathscr{P}(X)
. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

\Gamma
is relatively compact in the weak topology;

(ii)

\Gamma
is tight.

Proof.

A proof can be found in many textbooks; see, e.g.,
[
4
, Ch. 5]
and
[
5
, Thm. 8.6.2]
.
∎

When sufficient regularity of a continuous-time family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is presumed, we find with the help of Prokhorov’s theorem that any finite time horizon process
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
,
T\geq 0
, is tight. Sufficient regularity turns out to be continuity in probability in metric spaces.

Definition B.15
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a metric space. An
(X,d)
-valued process
Z=(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is said to be
continuous  in  probability
, or
stochastically  continuous
, if for any time
t_{0}\geq 0
fixed, we have

\mathbb{P}(d(Z_{t},Z_{t_{0}})>\varepsilon)\to 0,\quad\text{as }t\to t_{0},

(B.25)

for all
\varepsilon>0.

The next result can be interpreted as a generalisation of tightness of finite sequences of measures.

Proposition B.16
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete separable metric space and consider
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to be acontinuous-time family of
X
-valued random variables. If
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is stochastically continuous in
X
, then any finite time horizon process
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
,
T\geq 0
, is tight in
X
.

Proof.

Let
d_{E}
denote the Euclidean metric on the non-negative reals
[0,\infty)
, and introduce the map

P:([0,\infty),d_{E})\to(\mathscr{P}(X),d_{0}),\,t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(Z_{t}),

(B.26)

where
\mathcal{L}(Z_{t})
denotes the law of
Z_{t}
. We claim that
P
is continuous, hence the image

P([0,T])=\{\mathcal{L}(Z_{t}):t\in[0,T]\},

(B.27)

for any
T\geq 0
, is compact in the weak topology. Theorem
B.14
concludes that
\{\mathcal{L}(Z_{t}):t\in[0,T]\}
is tight. In other words, the finite time horizon process
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is tight in
X
.

We now prove that
P
is indeed continuous.
Let
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence such that
t_{n}\to t_{0}
, as
n\to\infty
, with
t_{0}\geq 0
arbitrary yet fixed. Define

\Omega_{n,\varepsilon}:=\big\{\omega\in\Omega:d\big(Z_{t_{n}}(\omega),Z_{t_{0}}(\omega)\big)>\varepsilon\big\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(B.28)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Since
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is stochastically continuous in
(X,d)
, we have for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
that there exists some natural number
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{n,\varepsilon})<\varepsilon^{\prime}
holds for all
n\geq n_{0}.

Let
f\in\rm Lip_{\infty}
be arbitrarily given—recall the notation in (
B.24
)—and observe

\displaystyle\left|\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(Z_{t})-\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(Z_{t_{0}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}f(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int_{\Omega}f(Z_{t_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\big|f(Z_{t})-f(Z_{t_{0}})\big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{n,\varepsilon}}\big|f(Z_{t})-f(Z_{t_{0}})\big|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}+\int_{\Omega_{n,\varepsilon}}\Big(\big|f(Z_{t})\big|+\big|f(Z_{t_{0}})\big|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\text{Lip}(f)\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{n,\varepsilon}}d(Z_{t},Z_{t_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}+2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{n,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Lip}(f)\,\varepsilon\,\mathbb{P}(\Omega\backslash\Omega_{n,\varepsilon})+2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{n,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+2\varepsilon^{\prime},

(B.29)

for any
n\geq n_{0}
, where

\text{Lip}(u)=\sup_{x,y\in X,\,x\neq y}{|u(x)-u(y)|}/{d(x,y)}
is the Lipschitz constant of a function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
. Since we may take
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
, we obtain

d_{0}\big(P(t_{n}),P(t_{0})\big)=d_{0}\big(\mathcal{L}(Z_{t_{n}}),\mathcal{L}(Z_{t_{0}})\big)\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(B.30)

Because this holds for any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
t_{n}\to t_{0}
, for every instant
t_{0}\geq 0
, we deduce the (sequential) continuity of the map
P
.
∎

Remark B.17
.

If
Y\in\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
is stochastically continuous, e.g., a Lévy process, then the segment process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
is a family of
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables which is stochastically continuous
[
33
, Lem. 2.3]
.

Remember
D[-\tau,0]
is endowed with the Skorokhod topology, recall Remark
B.3
, and that this is a separable completely metrisable space; see the previous section. Due to the fact Prokhorov’s theorem expresses tightness in terms of compactness, the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem is often encountered in combination with Prokhorov’s theorem. In the continuous setting, tightness can be characterised in terms of the modulus of continuity; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
34
]
. Similar results hold for the right-continuous setting; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
18
]
, but observe that in these references they restrict to sequences.

Proposition B.18
.

A continuous-time family of
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
we have

(i)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|Z_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq R\right)=0;

(ii)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(\varpi\left(Z_{t},\delta)\geq\varepsilon\right)=0
.

Proof.

In essence, the result directly follows from Arzelà–Ascoli in
D[-\tau,0]
; see Theorem
B.11
. The proof is entirely conform to the proof of
[
4
, Thm. 13.2]
or
[
18
, Prop. VI.3.26]
. For continuous-time families we are required to replace the
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}
-part in
[
4
,
18
]
with the slightly stronger
\sup_{t\geq 0}
-part, since we can no longer use the fact that we have tightness for a finite horizon of random variables.
∎

The following is a direct consequence of Proposition
B.16
and Proposition
B.18
.
It is worthwhile to point out that, under sufficient regularity of the family, we are only interested in what happens at infinity like in the case of sequences.

Corollary B.19
.

A stochastically continuous process
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
D[-\tau,0]
is tight if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
we have

(i)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|Z_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq R\right)=0;

(ii)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\varpi\left(Z_{t},\delta)\geq\varepsilon\right)=0
.

Conform to
[
18
]
, we will introduce the concept of
C
-tightness for continuous-time families.

Definition B.20
.

A continuous-time family of
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is said to be
C
-tight
if for every
\varepsilon>0
we have

(i)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|Z_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq R\right)=0
;

(ii)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\omega(Z_{t},\delta)\geq\varepsilon\right)=0
.

Every
C
-tight family is obviously tight and follows from (
B.17
).
Introducing
C
-tightness is done in a more abstract way in
[
18
]
by defining it as a tight sequence or family whose laws converge to the laws of continuous processes (where the convergence is in the sense of subsequences, and thus “limit points” is indeed plural).
The equivalence of these two approaches is due to
[
18
, Prop. VI.3.26]
.
As a result of the inequalities in equation (
B.18
), we obtain the following handy corollary for families of continuous processes.

Corollary B.21
.

A tight family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
C[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables is
C
-tight.

We end this section by stating (and proving) two useful lemmas. All results in
[
18
]
are actually for sequences, i.e., with index set
I=\mathbb{N},
only. Nonetheless, those results that we implement in our work easily extend to families with index set
I=[0,\infty).
In particular, we see that the proof of the result below is no different and simply requires Proposition
B.18
and our definition of
C
-tightness.

Lemma B.22

(Corollary 3.33 of
[
18
]
)
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
C
-tight family. In addition, assume

(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a tight
(
resp.,
C
-tight
)
family of
D[-\tau,0]
-valued random variables. Then the sum
\left(Y_{t}+Z_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is also tight
(
resp.,
C
-tight
)
.

We say
Z=(Z(s))_{s\in[-\tau,0]}
is a
non-decreasing  process
when
Z
is càdlàg in the pathwise sense, non-negative, non-decreasing, and
Z(-\tau)=0.
A non-decreasing process
Y
is said to
stronglymajorise
another non-decreasing process
Z
whenever
Y-Z
is also a non-decreasing process.

Lemma B.23

(Proposition 3.35 of
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose, for any
t\geq 0
fixed, that
Y_{t}
is a non-decreasing process that strongly majorises the non-decreasing process
Z_{t}.
If the family
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight, then so is the family
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. The same result holds when exchanging tight for
C
-tight.

Indeed, this statement easily extends to continuous-time families as well, since the result immediately follows from Proposition
B.18
and Definition
B.20
, once one notices that
\left|Z_{t}(s)\right|\leq\left|Y_{t}(s)\right|
,
s\in[-\tau,0]
,
\varpi\left(Z_{t},\delta\right)\leq\varpi\left(Y_{t},\delta\right)
, and
\omega\left(Z_{t},\delta\right)\leq\omega\left(Y_{t},\delta\right)
holds.

B.4
Direct applications

In this section, we provide two intermediate results that are useful for showing tightness of solution segments; see §
4
. The following tightness result is applied in §
4.1
, where we consider the stochastic integrator of the SDDE to be continuous.

Theorem B.24

The second tightness result plays a crucial role in §

Lemma B.25
.

Let
(f(s))_{-\tau\leq s<\infty}
be a stochastic process in
\mathbb{D}[-\tau,\infty)
. Define, for every

, the continuous-time family

of

-valued random variables by

Let us write

, for each

. If

is bounded in probability, then the family
(F_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
C
-tight.

Proof.

Define for all
M\geq 0
the sets

C_{M}:=\{F\in C[-\tau,0]:\|F\|_{\infty}\leq\tau M,\quad\text{Lip}(F)\leq M\},

(B.33)

where

\text{Lip}(g)=\sup_{x,y\in[-\tau,0],\,x\neq y}{|g(x)-g(y)|}/{|x-y|}
denotes the Lipschitz constant of a function
g:[-\tau,0]\to\mathbb{R}
. Any set
C_{M}\subset C[-\tau,0]
is pointwise bounded and equicontinuous, which directly follows from the uniform bounds
\|F\|_{\infty}\leq\tau M
and
\text{Lip}(F)\leq M
, respectively. Theorem
B.7
allows us to deduce that

is relatively compact in
C[-\tau,0].

Now, let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrarily given. By the boundedness in probability assumption, there exists a constant
M_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
such that, for all
t\geq 0
fixed, there is some
\Omega_{t}\subset\Omega
satisfying
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{t})\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
\sup_{s\in[t-\tau,t]}|f(s,\omega)|\leq M_{\varepsilon}
for every
\omega\in\Omega_{t}
. Hence, for any

, this results into the pointwise estimates

|F_{t}(u)-F_{t}(v)|=\left|\int_{t+v}^{t+u}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{t+v}^{t+u}|f(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{t+v}^{t+u}M_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}s=M_{\varepsilon}(u-v),\quad\text{on }\,\Omega_{t},

(B.34)

(B.35)

Consequently, for any time

, we have

for all

Recall that

is relatively compact and let us denote its closure by

We obtain that the family

Appendix C
Krylov–Bogoliubov existence theorem

In this appendix, we state and prove the Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem in the setting of SDDEs with càdlàg noise. Although the proof is standard (for the non-delay case), we present a detailed outline with references to the literature to guide the interested reader.

To this end, let
M=(M(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a semimartingale,
M(0)=0
, and consider the autonomous initial value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathrm{d}X(t)&=&a(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(X_{t-})\,\mathrm{d}M(t),&\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0,\\[1.42271pt]
X(u)&=&\Phi(u),&\quad\text{ for }u\in[-\tau,0],\end{array}\right.

(C.1)

with
\Phi\in\mathbb{D}[-\tau,0]
.
Recall that well-posedness of (
C.1
) is ensured when we assume that the functionals

are proper locally Lipschitz; see §
2
. As explained below, we require
M
to have independent and stationary increments, i.e., we need
M
to be a Lévy process.
Then, since we restrict ourselves to time-independent coefficients, we are not able to tell the time while perceiving the system. These conditions result into translation invariance properties, which are relevant for proving the theorem below.

Theorem C.1

(Krylov–Bogoliubov: càdlàg case)

Consider
