Impact of neutrino decays on the Cosmic Neutrino Background anisotropies
Shin’ichiro Ando

Abstract
The anisotropies of the Cosmic Neutrino Background (C ν \nu B) offer an ideal tool to test non-standard neutrino interactions, since they directly trace the perturbations in the neutrino distribution function. Here, we study how invisible neutrino decays impact the C ν \nu B anisotropies, in a framework where neutrinos decay non-relativistically to dark radiation and lighter neutrinos in a manner consistent with the measured mass splittings. For this purpose, we perform the first implementation of such a late-time neutrino decay scenario within a linear Einstein-Boltzmann solver, and compute the C ν \nu B angular power spectra from the Boltzmann hierarchy solutions for a range of lifetimes and decay channels. We find that neutrino decays leave very strong signatures on the C ν \nu B angular spectra, about two orders of magnitude larger than on the CMB angular spectra, particularly for lifetimes comparable to the age of the Universe. We show that a future polarized tritium target run of the PTOLEMY experiment, with sufficient counting statistics to measure just the first ∼ 15 \sim 15 multipoles of the neutrino sky map, could test neutrino decay models that remain undetectable with CMB data. 2
Theoretical framework

2.1
The physical system

In this work, we consider the 2-body decay of a massive active neutrino
\nu_{H}
into a lighter active neutrino
\nu_{l}
and a massless scalar particle
\phi
.
Following Ref.
[
18
]
, we assume that this non-standard interaction is described by the effective Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}=\mathfrak{g}_{ij}\bar{\nu}_{i}\nu_{j}\phi.

(2.1)

Here
i
and
j
label mass eigenstates, and
\mathfrak{g}_{ij}=\mathfrak{g}
is a universal coupling constant. The scalar
\phi
population is assumed to be produced at late times only through neutrino decay. We concentrate on the regime of small coupling strengths
\mathfrak{g}
, for which
2\leftrightarrow 2
scattering processes are always irrelevant and the decay process
\nu_{H}\rightarrow\nu_{l}+\phi
dominates
[
18
]
. The corresponding rest-frame decay rate is given by
[
26
]

\Gamma=\frac{\left(m_{\nu H}-m_{\nu l}\right)\left(m_{\nu H}+m_{\nu l}\right)^{3}}{4\pi m_{\nu H}^{3}}\mathfrak{g}^{2},

(2.2)

where
m_{\nu H}
and
m_{\nu l}
are the masses of the heavier and lighter neutrino, respectively, and for concreteness it is assumed that neutrinos are Majorana particles.
3
3

3

For Dirac particles, the decay rate can simply be obtained as
\Gamma^{\text{Dirac}}=\frac{1}{4}\Gamma^{\text{Majorana}}
.

Within the small-
\mathfrak{g}
regime, we focus on ‘late-time’ decays, meaning that neutrinos are assumed to decay after becoming non-relativistic. Hence, the contribution from inverse decay processes is kinematically suppressed and can be safely neglected.
The region of non-relativistic decays is set by the threshold condition on the rest-frame neutrino lifetime
\tau_{\nu}>H^{-1}(a_{\rm{nr}})
, where
a_{\rm{nr}}
is the approximate scale factor at which neutrinos transition to a non-relativistic regime, and is defined as
3T_{\nu}\left(a_{\rm{nr}}\right)=m_{\nu H}
. The Hubble scale at
a_{\rm{nr}}
is given by
[
27
]

\displaystyle H\left(a_{\rm{nr}}\right)

\displaystyle=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{m}}\left(\frac{m_{\nu H}}{3T_{\nu 0}}\right)^{3/2}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\simeq 765\mathrm{~Gyr}^{-1}\left(\frac{H_{0}}{68\mathrm{~km}/\mathrm{s}/\mathrm{Mpc}}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega_{m}}{0.3}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{3m_{\nu H}}{1\mathrm{eV}}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{1.5\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{eV}}{T_{\nu 0}}\right)^{3/2},

where
T_{\nu 0}
is the present-day neutrino temperature. To ensure that even neutrinos in the high-energy tail of the distribution are non-relativistic during the decay, we have chosen values of
\tau_{\nu}
well within the bounds set by
Eq. 2.3
.

Previous works have considered the limit of massless daughter neutrinos,
m_{\nu l}\rightarrow 0
, meaning that neutrinos decay entirely into a
dark radiation
(DR) fluid. This scenario predicts a late-time transfer of
energy from the matter to radiation sector, reducing the impact of neutrinos on the expansion rate and structure growth. This in turn allows to significantly relax the very tight cosmological bounds on neutrino masses
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. However, it presents two crucial limitations:

1.

It is strictly not compatible with the mass splittings determined by oscillation experiments
[
14
]
, unless
m_{\nu H}\lesssim\sqrt{|\Delta m^{2}_{32}|}\simeq 0.05\ \rm{eV}
(where
\Delta m^{2}_{32}
denotes the atmospheric mass gap), or
\nu_{l}
is taken to be a very light sterile neutrino instead of an active neutrino
[
26
]
.

2.

The strong relaxation of neutrino mass bounds is hardly compatible with a potential direct detection of the C
\nu
B. Indeed, to relax mass bounds appreciably, neutrinos need to decay to DR with lifetimes shorter than the age of the universe
\tau_{\nu}\lesssim 0.1t_{\mathrm{U}}
, in which case the C
\nu
B would have decayed away at present
[
23
]
. Conversely, if neutrinos decay to DR with a lifetime
\tau_{\nu}\sim t_{\mathrm{U}}
—such that a sizable population of relic neutrinos still remains today—the impact on the C
\nu
B anisotropies is very small (as we explicitly checked).

Therefore, in this work we reinstate a finite mass for the lighter daughter neutrino, since such decays can account for the experimentally determined mass splittings and, as we will show, leave strong signatures on the C
\nu
B temperature anisotropies of the daughter neutrino.
4
4

4

The late-time effect of
\nu_{H}\rightarrow\nu_{l}+\phi
on CMB and LSS observables has not yet been studied in the literature. However, this scenario is expected to only slightly alleviate the neutrino mass bounds, since the decay products always contain an active massive neutrino, and therefore the impact on the expansion rate and structure growth is not significantly reduced. We will explore this in a forthcoming publication.

2.2
Boltzmann equations

We now introduce the Boltzmann equations to track the cosmological evolution of the
\{\nu_{H},\nu_{l},\phi\}
system at both the homogeneous (background) and the perturbed (first-order) level. We follow the same conventions as
[
53
]
. We choose to work in the synchronous gauge, whose line element is given by

ds^{2}=a(\tau)^{2}[-d\tau^{2}+(\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}(\bm{x},\tau))dx^{i}dx^{j}],

(2.4)

where
a(\tau)
is the scale factor and
h_{ij}(\bm{x},\tau)
are the scalar perturbations to the metric. We split the phase space distribution (PSD) of the
i
th particle into a homogeneous and isotropic background contribution plus a linear perturbation

f_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{q}_{i},\tau)=\bar{f}_{i}(q_{i},\tau)\left[1+\Psi_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{q}_{i},\tau)\right],

(2.5)

where
\bm{x}
are the spatial coordinates,
\bm{q}_{i}
is the comoving momentum (with
q_{i}\equiv|\bm{q}_{i}|
), and
\tau
denotes conformal time. The evolution of the PSD is dictated by the Boltzmann equation, given in relativistic notation by

P^{\mu}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x^{\mu}}-\Gamma^{\nu}_{\rho\sigma}P^{\rho}P^{\sigma}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial P^{\nu}}=\frac{\epsilon_{i}}{a^{2}}\left(\frac{df_{i}}{d\tau}\right)_{C},

(2.6)

where
P^{\mu}
is the 4-momentum,
\epsilon_{i}\equiv\left(q_{i}^{2}+a^{2}m_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
is the comoving energy of species
i
, and
\Gamma^{\nu}_{\rho\sigma}
are the Christoffel symbols that capture all gravitational physics. On the r.h.s. is the collision term, which was derived at zeroth- and first-order for the neutrino decay and inverse processes
\nu_{H}\leftrightarrow\nu_{l}+\phi
in Ref.
[
18
]
. In the following, we present these Boltzmann equations in the non-relativistic limit, i.e., excluding inverse decay and quantum statistics terms.

Background equations

The zeroth-order Boltzmann equations for the parent neutrino
\nu_{H}

\displaystyle q_{1\pm}^{(\nu l)}

In our case, the daughter neutrino
\nu_{l}

Perturbation equations

Following the usual procedure, we decompose the Fourier transform of the first-order perturbed part of the PSD,

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(k,q_{i},\hat{k}\cdot\hat{q}_{i},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{\ell}(2\ell+1)\Psi_{i,\ell}\left(k,q_{i},\tau\right)P_{\ell}(\hat{k}\cdot\hat{q}_{i}).

(2.17)

By combining

(see Eq. 4.18 in
[

]

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}_{i,0}\left(q_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{q_{i}k}{\epsilon_{i}}\Psi_{i,1}\left(q_{i}\right)+\frac{\dot{h}}{6}\frac{\partial\ln\bar{f_{i}}}{\partial\ln q_{i}}+\mathcal{C}_{0}^{(1)}\left[\Psi_{i}\left(q_{i}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}_{i,1}\left(q_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{i}k}{\epsilon_{i}}\left(-\frac{2}{3}\Psi_{i,2}\left(q_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{3}\Psi_{i,0}\left(q_{i}\right)\right)+\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}\left[\Psi_{i}\left(q_{i}\right)\right],

(2.18)

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}_{i,2}\left(q_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{i}k}{\epsilon_{i}}\left(-\frac{3}{5}\Psi_{i,3}\left(q_{i}\right)+\frac{2}{5}\Psi_{i,1}\left(q_{i}\right)\right)-\frac{\partial\ln\bar{f}_{i}}{\partial\ln q_{i}}\left(\frac{2}{5}\dot{\eta}+\frac{1}{15}\dot{h}\right)+\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}\left[\Psi_{i}\left(q_{i}\right)\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{2\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}-a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}+m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2q_{1}q_{2}},

\displaystyle=\frac{2\epsilon_{1}q_{3}-a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2q_{1}q_{3}}.

F_{\mathrm{dr},\ell}
reads

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\mathrm{dr},0}=-kF_{\mathrm{dr},1}-\frac{2}{3}r_{\mathrm{dr}}\dot{h}+\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,0}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\mathrm{dr},1}=\frac{k}{3}F_{\mathrm{dr},0}-\frac{2k}{3}F_{\mathrm{dr},2}+\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,1}^{(1)},

(2.25)

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\mathrm{dr},2}=\frac{2k}{5}F_{\mathrm{dr},1}-\frac{3k}{5}F_{\mathrm{dr},3}+\frac{4}{15}r_{\mathrm{dr}}(\dot{h}+6\dot{\eta})+\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,2}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\mathrm{dr},\ell\geq 3}=\frac{k}{2\ell+1}\left(\ell F_{\mathrm{dr},\ell-1}-(\ell+1)F_{\mathrm{dr},\ell+1}\right)+\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}.

To obtain the DR first-order collision term
(dF_{\mathrm{dr}}/d\tau)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}
from
Eq. 2.21
, we just reproduce the calculations performed in App. C of Ref.
[
54
]
, but using
\Psi_{{\rm{dr}}}=\Psi_{\phi}
. Our complete derivation is presented in App.
B
. We arrive at the expression

\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}=\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}(q_{1})\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\right)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})},

(2.26)

where

\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(x\right)\equiv\int_{-1}^{+1}\frac{P_{\ell}(u)}{(1-xu)^{3}}du,

(2.27)

and
\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}
is given by

\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}=\varepsilon\frac{r_{\mathrm{dr}}a\Gamma m_{\nu H}n_{\nu H}}{\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}}.

(2.28)

The collision term in
Eq. 2.26
is identical to the one found in
[
54
]
, except for the extra factor of
\varepsilon
, which is again accounting for the repartition of energy between the two decay products.

2.3
C
\nu
B anisotropies

To model the C
\nu
B temperature anisotropies, we use the formalism presented in
[
56
]
, which provides a way to compute the C
\nu
B angular power spectrum given the solutions of the neutrino Boltzmann hierarchy in linear theory. In this work, we consider only the anisotropies of the daughter neutrino
i=\nu_{l}
, since the parent neutrino
\nu_{H}
has fully decayed at present for sufficiently short lifetimes. For notational simplicity, we thus omit the index
i
in what follows.

The starting point is to parameterize the perturbed PSD
f(\bm{x},\bm{q},\tau)
in terms of a variable
\Delta
, such that
f=(e^{\frac{q}{T_{\nu 0}(1+\Delta)}}+1)^{-1}
. By expanding the PSD to linear order around a Fermi-Dirac distribution, and comparing to
Eq. 2.5
, one arrives at the following relation between
\Delta
and
\Psi

\Delta=-\left(\frac{d\ln\bar{f}}{d\ln q}\right)^{-1}\Psi.

(2.29)

We emphasize that, despite its resemblance to a temperature perturbation,
\Delta
should strictly not be interpreted as one. Indeed, from the previous relation, one sees that
\Delta
depends not only on spacetime but also on momenta,
\Delta=\Delta(\bm{x},\bm{q},\tau)
, which leads to departures from a thermal distribution at the perturbed level (this is generally true for massive neutrinos
[
57
]
). Furthermore, to derive
Eq. 2.29
, it is assumed that the background PSD
\bar{f}
follows a Fermi-Dirac distribution at all times, which is a valid assumption for free-streaming neutrinos, but not for neutrinos participating in the decay. For this reason, we regard
\Delta
merely as a convenient variable that can be defined through
Eq. 2.29
, which allows us to make connection with previous studies of C
\nu
B anisotropies
[
58
,
56
]
.

After decomposing the Fourier transforms of
\Delta
and
\Psi
into Legendre polynomials, one can use
Eq. 2.29
to relate the temperature multipoles
\Delta_{\ell}(k,q,\tau)
to the solutions of the neutrino Boltzmann hierarchy,
\Psi_{\ell}(k,q,\tau)
.
7
7

7

Ref.
[
56
]
developed a line-of-sight approximation that allows to compute
\Delta_{\ell}(k,q,\tau_{0})
up to a very large multipole
\ell_{\rm max}
without having to solve a Boltzmann hierarchy of
(\ell_{\rm max}+1)
equations. While this alternative method is considerably faster, it cannot be easily extended to the decaying neutrino case, and doing so is beyond the scope of our work.
With this, the angular power spectrum for a given neutrino comoving momentum
q
can be obtained as follows
[
56
]

C_{\ell}(q)=4\pi A_{s}T_{\nu 0}^{2}\int d\ln k\left(\frac{k}{k_{\star}}\right)^{n_{s}-1}\Delta_{\ell}^{2}(k,q,\tau_{0}),

(2.30)

where
A_{s}
is the amplitude of the primordial spectrum,
n_{s}
is the spectral index, and the reference wavenumber is fixed to
k_{\star}=0.05\ \rm{Mpc}^{-1}
. We perform the integral in
Eq. 2.30
for a range of wavenumbers
10^{-4}\leq k/\rm{Mpc}^{-1}\leq 10^{-1}
, which are hence in the linear regime of present-day perturbations. The power spectra are computed up to
\ell_{\text{max}}=17
, which is the multipole at which we truncate the neutrino Boltzmann hierarchy. This
\ell_{\text{max}}
value is chosen due to computational limitations, but we note that multipoles
\ell\leq 15
are also the ones that receive contributions mainly from linear scales for neutrino masses
m_{\nu}\leq 0.05\ \mathrm{eV}

[
56
]
, making our approach self-consistent. Moreover, the initially poor angular resolution of a future directional C
\nu
B experiment like PTOLEMY implies that it will be sensitive only to large-scale variations.

The rates for relic neutrino capture are not strongly sensitive to the incoming momentum of the captured neutrinos
[
50
]
.
Therefore, we eliminate the
q
-dependence from
C_{\ell}(q)
using the momentum averaging introduced in Ref.
[
58
]

C_{\ell}=\left[\frac{\int dq~q^{2}\epsilon(q)\bar{f}_{\nu l}(q)\sqrt{C_{\ell}(q)}}{\int dq~q^{2}\epsilon(q)\bar{f}_{\nu l}(q)}\right]^{2}.

(2.31)

Other choices of average angular power spectra are of course possible, and the appropriate definition ultimately depends on how measurements are performed in a neutrino capture experiment. For instance, Ref.
[
56
]
proposes a similar
q
-bin averaging, but the
q
-dependence is integrated out directly at the level of the temperature multipoles
\Delta_{\ell}
. Here, we have adopted the
q
-averaging of
Eq. 2.31
as it allows for a more straightforward interpretation of the results. Let us also note that the average involves the present-day background PSD of the daughter neutrino
\bar{f}_{\nu l}(q)
, which generally departs from a Fermi-Dirac distribution due to the neutrino decay process (see
Sec. 3
). This directly impacts the weights given to the different
q
-dependent angular power spectra
C_{\ell}(q)
in
Eq. 2.31
, as well as the maximum momentum
q_{\rm max}
up to which we carry out the momentum integrals.

Results

In this section, we study the impact of invisible neutrino decays of the type
\nu_{i}\rightarrow\nu_{j}+\phi
on the CMB and C
\nu
B anisotropies, and compare the sensitivities of these two probes to such non-standard neutrino interactions. To this end, we use our modified
CLASS++
version (described in
Sec. 3
) to compute the lensed CMB temperature (TT) power spectrum as well as the C
\nu
B power spectrum (via
Eq. 2.31
) for various neutrino decay models. To isolate the signatures of the decay, we always present residual differences in these power spectra with respect to the equivalent stable scenario, i.e., the one with the same neutrino mass spectrum and
\Gamma=0
. All other cosmological parameters are fixed to
\{H_{0}=67.37~\mathrm{km}/\mathrm{s}/\mathrm{Mpc},~\omega_{b}=0.02233,~\omega_{\mathrm{cdm}}=0.1198,~n_{s}=0.9652,~\ln(10^{10}A_{s})=3.043,~\tau_{\mathrm{reio}}=0.0540\}
, which correspond to the
\Lambda
CDM best-fit parameters from
Planck
2018
[
7
]
.

In order to assess which neutrino decay models may be measurable by the CMB or C
\nu
B, we also show the fractional uncertainties associated to each probe. For the CMB, we take the measurement errors from
Planck
2018. For the C
\nu
B, we assume that the dominant contributions arise from cosmic variance and the PTOLEMY counting statistics for the total number of neutrino capture events. The 1
\sigma
uncertainty on
C_{\ell}
from cosmic variance is given by
[
62
]

\frac{\Delta C_{\ell}}{C_{\ell}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{2\ell+1}},

(4.1)

and is larger than 20

for multipoles
\ell\leq 17
. To compute the fractional uncertainty on
C_{\ell}
from the PTOLEMY counting statistics, we follow the same procedure as Ref.
[
52
]
. Namely, we generate a temperature map for the stable limit of each decay scenario, and interpret it as a distribution of neutrino capture events, where each pixel records the number of captures for a total of
N
neutrino events. The expected count per pixel is then smeared using Poisson statistics, and the map is inverted to find the corresponding power spectrum
C_{\ell}

\ell

1/\sqrt{N}
, but in our plots we take

In general, we expect the C

\nu_{i}\rightarrow\nu_{j}+\phi

5
Conclusions and outlook

The angular distribution of neutrino anisotropies provides a valuable probe of LSS and fundamental neutrino properties. In this paper, we have investigated the impact of neutrino decays on the C
\nu
B anisotropies, within a framework where neutrinos decay non-relativistically into lighter neutrinos and a massless scalar. For this purpose, we have performed the first implementation of late-time decays
\nu_{H}\rightarrow\nu_{l}+\phi
in a linear Einstein-Boltzmann solver, and computed the C
\nu
B angular power spectrum of the daughter neutrino from the Boltzmann hierarchy solutions, exploring a range of neutrino lifetimes and decay channels.

We have found that invisible neutrino decays leave considerable imprints on the C
\nu
B power spectrum—about two orders of magnitude larger than on the CMB TT power spectrum—particularly for lifetimes comparable to the age of the Universe. As a consequence, neutrino decay models yielding per-mille-level (and thus undetectable) effects in CMB observables still produce signatures at the 40-80% level in the C
\nu
B anisotropies, exceeding cosmic variance even for multipoles
\ell\leq 17
. A future polarized tritium target run of the PTOLEMY experiment, with sufficient counting statistics to measure
C_{\ell}
up to
\ell\sim 15
, could therefore test these neutrino decays.
We also found that the decay mass gap dictates the shape of the
C_{\ell}
residuals: single-decay channels with the solar gap produce a step-like suppression, whereas two-decay channels with one near-common atmospheric gap yield a low-
\ell
suppression and a high-
\ell
enhancement. Hence, a C
\nu
B anisotropy measurement could allow to disentangle between different decay scenarios.

Our work demonstrates the great potential of the C
\nu
B anisotropies to test non-standard neutrino interactions, specially if these produce significant distortions in the neutrino PSD (to which the CMB is largely insensitive
[
63
]
). We hope that this gives further motivation to develop experimental techniques that could measure the multipoles in the relic neutrino sky map, like the one proposed in Ref.
[
52
]
. Additionally, our new
CLASS
version will allow to confront this decaying neutrino model against the most up-to-date cosmological data, and to possibly test different models of warm dark matter decay with massless and massive decay products.

For this proof-of-principle, we have made several simplifying assumptions, which should be refined as we move closer to the first direct C
\nu
B detection. In particular, to better connect with what PTOLEMY will observe, the temperature maps should be converted into capture rate maps, which for a given mass eigenstate depend on the fraction of electron-flavor as well as the Dirac or Majorana nature of the neutrino
[
56
]
. Beyond this particle-physics dependence, the translation between temperature and capture-rate maps is further complicated by astrophysical effects. Namely, Earth’s peculiar motion introduces a modulation of the signal
[
51
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
, while the local gravitational environment—including the Milky Way halo and the nearby LSS—can significantly distort the local neutrino PSD
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
]
. The latter is especially relevant for neutrino masses

[

Appendix A
Momentum integration of the background collision term

In this appendix, we derive
Eq. 2.12
by
carrying out the momentum integration of the background collision term of the DR. As we mentioned in section
Sec. 2.2.1
, we follow the steps outlined in App. A of Ref.
[
54
]
, but considering a finite
m_{\nu l}
and using
\bar{f}_{{\rm{dr}}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\bar{f}_{\phi}
. The starting point is the zeroth-order Boltzmann equation for
\bar{f}_{{\rm{dr}}}(q_{3})
,

\frac{\partial\bar{f}_{{\rm{dr}}}\left(q_{3}\right)}{\partial\tau}=\frac{a^{2}m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)q_{3}^{2}}\int_{q_{1-}^{\phi}}^{\infty}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right),

(A.1)

where
q_{1-}^{\phi}
is given in
Eq. 2.11
. We integrate
Eq. A.1
over
4\pi a^{-4}dq_{3}q_{3}^{3}
, which gives
\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{dr}}+4aH\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}
on the left hand side. On the right hand side, we have

\frac{4\pi m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{3}q_{3}\int_{q_{1-}^{(\phi)}}^{q_{1+}^{(\phi)}}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right),

(A.2)

where
q_{1+}^{(\phi)}=\infty
. Due to the
q_{3}
dependence of
q_{1-}^{(\phi)}
, we cannot evaluate the
dq_{3}
integral directly. Nevertheless, we can relax the lower bound to zero by introducing the step functions,

\frac{4\pi m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{3}q_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}\Theta\left(q_{1}-q_{1-}^{(\phi)}\right)\Theta\left(q_{1+}^{(\phi)}-q_{1}\right)\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1}).

(A.3)

The aim is to turn the step functions that bound the
q_{1}
integral into bounds on the
q_{3}
integral, which we can easily evaluate. To do so, we will use equation (A.27) of Ref.
[
18
]

\Theta\left(1-\cos^{2}\beta^{*}\right)=\Theta\left(q_{1}-q_{1-}^{(\phi)}\right)\Theta\left(q_{1+}^{(\phi)}-q_{1}\right),

(A.4)

where
\cos\beta^{*}
is given by

\cos\beta^{*}=\frac{2\epsilon_{1}q_{3}-a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2q_{1}q_{3}}.

(A.5)

By taking the
q_{3}
-roots of
1-\cos^{2}\beta^{*}
,

q_{3\pm}^{(\nu H)}=\frac{m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}}{2m_{\nu H}^{2}}\left(\epsilon_{1}\pm q_{1}\right),

(A.6)

we arrive at the useful identity

\Theta\left(q_{1}-q_{1-}^{(\phi)}\right)\Theta\left(q_{1+}^{(\phi)}-q_{1}\right)=\Theta\left(1-\cos^{2}\beta^{*}\right)=\Theta\left(q_{3}-q_{3-}^{(\nu H)}\right)\Theta\left(q_{3+}^{(\nu H)}-q_{3}\right),

(A.7)

which allows us to swap the integration bounds. With this, the double integral becomes

\frac{4\pi m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{3}q_{3}\Theta\left(q_{3}-q_{3-}^{(\nu H)}\right)\Theta\left(q_{3+}^{(\nu H)}-q_{3}\right),

(A.8)

where we inverted the order of integration as
q_{3\pm}^{(\nu H)}
depend on
q_{1}
. The step functions turn into bounds on the
dq_{3}
integral, which we can readily evaluate

\int_{q_{3-}^{(\nu H)}}^{q_{3+}^{(\nu H)}}dq_{3}q_{3}=\frac{\left(q_{3+}^{(\nu H)}\right)^{2}-\left(q_{3-}^{(\nu H)}\right)^{2}}{2}=\frac{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)^{2}}{2m_{\nu H}^{4}}\epsilon_{1}q_{1}.

(A.9)

Substituting this result in
Eq. A.8
gives for the right hand side

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})\int_{q_{3-}^{(\nu H)}}^{q_{3+}^{(\nu H)}}dq_{3}q_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\Gamma\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{a^{2}m_{\nu H}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\Gamma\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{a^{2}m_{\nu H}}\frac{a^{3}}{4\pi}n_{\nu H}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon a\Gamma m_{\nu H}n_{\nu H}

(A.10)

where we have defined
\varepsilon\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(1-m_{\nu l}^{2}/m_{\nu H}^{2}\right)
. Putting the two sides back together, we obtain the background equation of motion for the DR species,

\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{dr}}+4aH\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}=\varepsilon a\Gamma m_{\nu H}n_{\nu H}.

(A.11)

Appendix B
Momentum integration of the perturbed collision term

In this appendix, we derive
Eq. 2.26
by taking the momentum averaged perturbation (
Eq. 2.24
) of the first-order collision term of the DR (
Eq. 2.21
). As mentioned in section
Sec. 2.2.2
, we follow along the steps in App. C of Ref.
[
54
]
, but considering a finite
m_{\nu l}
and using
\bar{\Psi}_{{\rm{dr}}}\equiv\bar{\Psi}_{\phi}
. We note that the momentum integral of the first-order collision term contains a term with
-\Psi_{\phi,\ell}(q_{3})
which exactly cancels one of the terms in
\dot{F}_{{\rm{dr}},\ell}
(see Ref.
[
54
]
for details). Hence, we are left with the integral

\displaystyle\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}\equiv\frac{r_{\mathrm{dr}}}{\left(\frac{a^{4}\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}}{4\pi}\right)}\int dq_{3}q_{2}^{3}\bar{f}_{\mathrm{dr}}\left(q_{3}\right)\left(\frac{a^{2}m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)q_{3}^{2}\bar{f}_{\mathrm{dr}}\left(q_{3}\right)}\int_{q_{1-}^{(\phi)}}^{\infty}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\beta^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi a^{2}m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{3}\int_{q_{1-}^{(\phi)}}^{\infty}dq_{1}\frac{q_{1}q_{3}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\beta^{*}\right)=\frac{4\pi a^{2}m_{\nu H}^{3}\Gamma}{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}\mathcal{I},

(B.1)

where we have used

\frac{r_{\mathrm{dr}}}{\int dq_{3}q_{3}^{3}\bar{f}_{\mathrm{dr}}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{dr}}}{\left(\frac{a^{4}\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}}{4\pi}\right)}=\frac{4\pi}{\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}.

(B.2)

We now focus on the integral
\mathcal{I}
. Using
Eq. A.4
, and remembering that
q_{1+}^{(\phi)}=+\infty
, we can relax the lower bound to zero by introducing the step function

\mathcal{I}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}\Theta\left(1-\cos^{2}\beta^{*}\right)\frac{q_{1}q_{3}}{\epsilon_{1}}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\beta^{*}\right).

(B.3)

We can perform a change of variables by defining
u\equiv\cos\beta^{*}
, which gives

q_{3}=\frac{a^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2\epsilon_{1}-2q_{1}u},\quad\quad dq_{3}=\frac{2a^{2}q_{1}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{\left(2\epsilon_{1}-2q_{1}u\right)^{2}}du,

(B.4)

and thus the integral becomes

\mathcal{I}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}\int_{u_{-}}^{u_{+}}du\Theta\left(1-u^{2}\right)\frac{a^{4}q_{1}^{2}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)^{2}}{4\epsilon_{1}^{4}}\frac{\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)P_{\ell}\left(u\right)}{\left(1-\frac{q_{1}u}{\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{3}}.

(B.5)

The bounds are

u_{-}=\lim_{q_{3}\rightarrow 0}u\left(q_{3}\right)=-\infty<-1,\quad\quad u_{+}=\lim_{q_{3}\rightarrow\infty}u\left(q_{3}\right)=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{q_{1}}>+1,

(B.6)

which means that the integration limits are actually enforced by
\Theta\left(1-u^{2}\right)
at
\pm 1
. By introducing the scattering kernel

\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\left(1-x^{2}\right)^{2}}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}du\frac{P_{\ell}(u)}{(1-xu)^{3}},

(B.7)

we can rewrite the integral as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)\frac{a^{4}\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)^{2}}{2\epsilon_{1}^{4}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{q_{1}^{2}}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)^{2}}{2m_{\nu H}^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\right),

(B.8)

where in the second equality we have simplified the kinematical factor

2\epsilon_{1}^{4}\left(1-\frac{q_{1}^{2}}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)^{2}=2\left(\epsilon_{1}^{2}-q_{1}^{2}\right)=2a^{4}m_{\nu H}^{4}.

(B.9)

With this, the collision term reads

\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}=\frac{4\pi a^{2}\Gamma\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2m_{\nu H}\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}\left(q_{1}\right)\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{l}}\right).

(B.10)

Using

\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{m_{\nu l}^{2}}{m_{\nu H}^{2}}\right)\frac{r_{\mathrm{dr}}a\Gamma m_{\nu H}n_{\nu H}}{\rho_{\mathrm{dr}}}=\frac{\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2m_{\nu H}^{2}}\frac{a^{5}\Gamma m_{\nu H}n_{\nu H}}{\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}},

(B.11)

we can rewrite the prefactor as

\frac{4\pi a^{2}\Gamma\left(m_{\nu H}^{2}-m_{\nu l}^{2}\right)}{2m_{\nu H}\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}=\frac{\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}\left(q_{1}\right)},

(B.12)

which yields the following expression for the integrated collision term

\left(\frac{dF_{\mathrm{dr}}}{d\tau}\right)_{C,\ell}^{(1)}=\dot{r}_{\mathrm{dr}}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})\Psi_{\nu H,\ell}(q_{1})\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}}\right)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{1}q_{1}^{2}\bar{f}_{\nu H}(q_{1})}.

(B.13)
