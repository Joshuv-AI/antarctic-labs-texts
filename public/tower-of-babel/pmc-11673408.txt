Magnetically Responsive Enzyme and Hydrogen‐Bonded Organic Framework Biocomposites for Biosensing
Authors: Francesco Carraro, Margherita Aghito, Simone Dal Zilio, Heimo Wolinski, Christian J Doonan, Bernd Nidetzky, Paolo Falcaro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe one‐pot synthesis of multicomponent hydrogen‐bonded organic framework (HOF) biocomposites is reported. The co‐immoblization of enzymes and magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) into the HOF crystals yielded biocatalysts (MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1) with dynamic localization properties. Using a permanent magnet, it is possible to separate the MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 particles from a solution. Catalase (CAT) and glucose oxidase (GOx) show increased retention of their activity when coimmobilized with MNPs. MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 biocomposites are used to prepare a proof‐of‐concept glucose microfluidic biosensor, where a magnet allow to position and keep in place the biocomposite inside a microfluidic chip. The magnetic response of these biocatalysts can pave the way for new applications for the emerging HOF biocomposites.Keywords: biocatalysis, biocomposite, biosensor, enzyme, HOF

Introduction

Enzymes are a class of proteins functioning as biological catalysts for a variety of chemical reactions essential to life processes. Their application in man‐made catalytic processes is attractive because the use of enzymes can improve the efficiency and environmental sustainability of a number of chemical syntheses.[ 1 ] Furthermore, their selectivity can be used for the fabrication of precise biosensors.[ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] However, their utilization outside native environments is typically limited by the stability of the biocatalyst.[ 1 ] Such shortcomings are often alleviated by immobilization of the enzyme on solid support, furthermore, this strategy also facilitates the reuse of the enzyme.[ 1 , 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] In the field of supported enzymes, the inclusion of magnetically responsive nanoparticles (MNPs) in the biocomposite was investigated to improve recyclability and biocatalyst‐products separation: this strategy enables the dynamic localization of the biocatalytst by using an external magnetic field.[ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] We note that, on a laboratory scale, the combination of enzymes and magnetic responsiveness has been successfully applied to the synthesis of fine chemicals, and to the fabrication of biosensors, and lab‐on‐a‐chip devices.[ 10 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] As a result, enzyme immobilization in magnetically active porous materials is a fervent research field.[ 2 , 19 , 20 ]

Recently, hydrogen‐bonded organic frameworks (HOFs)[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ] have emerged as a new class of porous materials for the immobilization of enzymes. HOFs are porous crystalline materials assembled primarily through intermolecular hydrogen bonds of organic building blocks.[ 23 ] HOF‐based biocomposites are typically prepared by using a one‐pot approach under biocompatible synthesis conditions (e.g., room‐temperature and water).[ 21 , 26 ] For example, the first protocol reporting the preparation of an enzyme@HOF biocomposite employed water soluble tetra–amidinium and tetra–carboxylate building blocks (i.e., BioHOF‐1), and framework synthesis was carried out at room‐temperature.[ 21 ] Subsequent studies further highlighted the promising performances in terms of protein loading capacity[ 27 ], stability over a broad pH range,[ 21 , 28 ] and protection against chemical/physical stressors.[ 21 , 28 ] These findings have sparked increasing interest in the investigation and design of enzyme@HOF systems for biocatalysis and biosensing applications.[ 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] However, in this emerging research area, the opportunity to impart extrinsic functional properties (e.g., magnetic responsiveness) to enzyme@HOF biocomposites remains unexplored.

Here, we report the one‐pot synthesis (Figure 1 ), characterization, and catalytic activity of a magnetically responsive HOF biocomposite: MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1. We investigated the co‐immobilization of nanoparticles (NPs), namely citrate‐capped Fe3O4 NPs (hereafter, MNPs) and two enzymes into BioHOF‐1 via a one‐pot synthesis. Citrate‐capped MNPs were selected because they are widely studied magnetically responsive NPs (i.e., we can collect them from a solution by using a permanent magnet) and they were previously successfully integrated in enzyme‐based composite materials.[ 15 , 31 ] Catalase (CAT), an iron‐heme enzyme that catalyzes the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen, was selected as a model enzyme.[ 21 , 28 , 32 ] In addition, glucose oxidase (GOx) was explored as it is an oxidoreductase enzyme that is widely used for biotechnological applications including biosensors for biomedical and food processing applications.[ 33 , 34 ] We show that the MNPs and the enzyme were co‐immobilized in the BioHOF‐1 crystals and that the final HOF composite is catalytically active and magnetically responsive. Furthermore, the magnetically responsive composites show enhanced enzymatic activity compared to the one prepared without MNPs. Finally, we examined the catalytic and dynamic localization capabilities of MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 within proof‐of‐concept microfluidic biosensors. Specifically, we explored: 1) the biocomposite's efficacy in catalyzing the oxidation of glucose in a continuous flow system; 2) the ability of MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 to respond to magnetic fields, enabling the positioning and retention within the biosensor by using a permanent magnet. Our results highlight the biocomposite's potential for targeted catalysis in specified microfluidic environments.

Results and Discussion

MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 is synthesized by dispersing the MNPs in an aqueous solution containing the enzyme and compound 1 (1 = methanetetrayltetrakis(benzene‐4,1‐diyl)tetrakis(aminomethaniminium)). This mixture is then combined with an aqueous solution of compound 2 (2 = 4,4′,4″,4‴‐methanetetrayltetrabenzoate), as illustrated in Figure 1. To explore how the MNP concentration influences the formation of MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1, we screened different concentrations of MNPs (0.5–0.05 mg mL−1 range), while maintaining the BioHOF‐1 precursor concentrations at 2 and 1.5 mg mL−1, and CAT at 0.5 mg mL−1. Upon mixing of all components, the solution became turbid, suggesting that the rapid nucleation of the HOF material occurred. The reaction mixture was left to react at room‐temperature and static conditions for 30 min. The solid product was recovered via low‐speed centrifugation followed by the use of a magnet to assist in the separation of the composite from the supernatant (Figure 1; Figure S1, Supporting Information). The biocomposite was washed three times with deionized water employing the magnetically assisted separation procedure (see Supporting Information for details). During the exploration of the composite components, we identified that a concentration of MNPs of 0.5 mg mL−1 was the threshold concentration needed to allow the complete magnetic collection of the solid product. The distinct color difference between the magnetically‐responsive particles (i.e., orange micrometric needle‐like crystals) and the magnetically unresponsive ones (i.e., white micrometric needle‐like crystals, as the bare HOF) visually confirms the immobilization of MNPs in the magnetically‐responsive particles. To minimize the gravimetric contribution to the composite of the MNP while ensuring an effective magnetically responsive behavior, the concentration of MNPs = 0.5 mg mL−1 was selected as for subsequent experiments. The crystallinity of the biocomposite was analyzed via X‐ray Diffraction (XRD, Figure 2a). Close analysis of the diffraction pattern confirmed that BioHOF‐1 is obtained and that the crystallinity of the HOF was not significantly affected by the presence or absence of CAT and MNPs. We note that the Fe3O4 reflections cannot be observed since they overlap those of BioHOF‐1 (i.e., main Fe3O4 diffraction peak (311) is at 35.7° and overlaps with high‐order reflections of the HOF).[ 13 , 35 ] The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of CAT was evaluated by quantifying the amount of residual protein in the supernatant and it was calculated to be 88 ± 3%. Accordingly, the protein loading (i.e., weight of enzyme per weight of final dry material) was calculated to be 8.7 ± 0.6%. The chemical composition of the solid product was investigated via IR spectroscopy (Figure 2b) that showed the presence of the typical bands of BioHOF‐1,[ 36 ] CAT (e.g., Amide I, 1700–1600 cm−1)[ 37 ] and Fe3O4 (e.g., Fe‐O stretching, 600–500 cm−1).[ 15 ] This set of vibrational modes confirms the formation of a BioHOF‐1 with immobilized enzymes and MNPs (MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1). The morphology of the MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 crystals and MNP particle distribution on BioHOF‐1 carrier were investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). SEM micrographs of MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 show a typical BioHOF‐1 micrometric needle‐like particle shape[ 21 ] with a length of ≈50 µm and a squared section of ≈1 × 1 µm (see Figure 2c). Compared to the SEM micrographs of CAT@BioHOF‐1 and BioHOF‐1 synthesized following the same protocol (Figure S2, Supporting Information), we notice that the morphology of the crystals is unchanged; however, a closer inspection of the MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 surface shows layer of fine particles (Figure 2c; Figure S3, Supporting Information). Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the presence of Fe3O4 in the MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 biocomposite (Figure S4, Supporting Information). STEM micrographs show that the MNPs are mainly located on the surface of the BioHOF‐1 crystals (Figure 2d), suggesting that the rough surface observed via SEM could be associated with the presence of MNPs on the crystals’ surface.

a) XRD patterns of MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1, CAT@BioHOF‐1, and BioHOF‐1. b) FTIR spectra of MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1, CAT@BioHOF‐1, BioHOF‐1, MNPs, and CAT. c) SEM, and d) STEM micrographs of MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1.

We then evaluated the enzymatic activity of CAT in the multicomponent BioHOF‐1 biocomposite. Compared to free CAT, the results of the enzymatic assays showed that the activity of CAT in MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 is largely preserved (i.e., effectiveness factor6 62 ± 5%, Figure 3a; Table S1, Supporting Information). To assess the contribution of the different components, the enzymatic activity of BioHOF‐1, MNPs@BioHOF‐1, and CAT@BioHOF‐1 were tested. The data show that BioHOF‐1 and MNPs@BioHOF‐1 have negligible catalytic activity toward the decomposition of H2O2, while CAT@BioHOF‐1 had an effectiveness factor of 31 ± 4% (see Figure 3c; Table S1, Supporting Information). To distinguish between the surface exposed and fully encapsulated CAT, we used tryptic digestion to inactivate the enzyme.[ 27 ] After exposure to trypsin, the residual activity of MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 is 30 ± 8% (Figure S17, Supporting Information). This suggests that ca 70% of the enzyme is accessible to protease, thus at least partially protruding from the HOF surface to the liquid phase and in the proximity of MNPs.[ 27 ] Comparable residual activity was measured for CAT@BioHOF‐1 (i.e., 19 ± 8%, control sample), again indicating that a large proportion of enzymes (≈80%) are immobilized but not fully encapsulated and protected by the HOF material. This comparable reduced activity in the control sample suggests that the spatial localization of the enzyme is not significantly affected by the presence of the MNPs. Notably, these data demonstrated that the one‐pot co‐immobilization of the enzyme and the MNPs in the multicomponent biocomposite improves the performances of the immobilized CAT. To further investigate the localization of the enzyme in the biocomposites we prepared a set of samples using fluorescein Isothiocyanate tagged CAT (FCAT) and we characterized them via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).We synthesized and analyzed the magnetically responsive MNPs‐FCAT@BioHOF‐1 and, for comparison, FCAT encapsulated in BioHOF‐1 (FCAT@BioHOF‐1), FCAT adsorbed on pre‐synthesized BioHOF‐1 crystals (FCAT‐on‐@BioHOF‐1), FCAT and the MNPs adsorbed on pre‐synthesized BioHOF‐1 crystals (MNPs‐FCAT‐on‐@BioHOF‐1) and control samples without fluorescein (BioHOF‐1 and MNPs@BioHOF‐1). The CLSM images (Figure S18, Supporting Information) show a difference between the samples with FCAT immobilized in the crystals via one‐pot syntheses (MNPs‐FCAT@BioHOF‐1 and FCAT@BioHOF‐1, Figure S18a–f, Supporting Information) and with FCAT adsorbed on pre‐synthesized crystals (FCAT‐on‐@BioHOF‐1 and MNPs‐FCAT‐on‐@BioHOF‐1, Figure S18g–n, Supporting Information). In both cases, the fluorescent signal is mostly localized from a region close to the surface of the HOF needles. However, the florescent signal originated from the one‐pot synthesized samples is emitted by a region that extends to the core of the HOF crystal while FCAT is adsorbed on HOF crystals show a clear localized emission from the crystal surfaces. For both the one‐pot syntheses and the surface adsorbed samples, the FCAT spatial localization is independent by the presence of the MNPs. These results are in line with the trypsin digestion results and confirm that the one‐pot synthesis used in this work lead to the localization of CAT close to the surface of the BioHOF‐1 crystals. Next, we investigated whether the synthetic protocol used for the preparation of for MNPs‐CAT@BioHOF‐1 could be applied to an enzyme employed in several commercial applications: we targeted glucose oxidase (GOx) for the co‐immobilization with MNPs in BIOHOF‐1. We examined the EE of GOx in MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 and in GOx@BioHOF‐1 (control sample). For these distinct samples, the encapsulation efficiency was measured to be 53% and 52% respectively (Figure 3d; Table S2, Supporting Information), indicating that the amount of GOx immobilized in BIOHOF‐1 is comparatively less efficient for CAT XRD analysis shows that the crystal structure of BioHOF‐1 was retained (Figure S5, Supporting Information) when the HOF is formed in presence of GOx and MNPs. IR spectroscopy (see Figure S6, Supporting Information) confirmed the presence of MNPs and proteins in the HOF biocomposite (MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1). Next, we investigated the enzymatic activity of immobilized GOx toward the oxidation of D‐glucose to D‐glucono ‐1,5 – lactone and H2O2. Compared to free GOx, the results of the enzymatic assays showed that the activity of MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 is highly preserved (i.e., effectiveness factor 86 ± 5%, Figure 3b,d; Figure S7 and Table S2, Supporting Information) and higher than GOx@BioHOF‐1 (67 ± 1%, Figure 3b,d; Figure S7 and Table S2, Supporting Information). Following the same strategy used for FCAT, the localization of GOx in the biocomposites was investigated using CLSM and fluorescein Isothiocyanate tagged GOx (FGOx). Similarly to the results obtained with FCAT, the CLSM images (Figure S19, Supporting Information) suggest that the majority of the enzyme is localized in the proximity of the surface of the HOF crystals. The enhancement of the catalytic activity of both GOx and CAT immobilized in presence of MNPs could be ascribed to a combination of different contributions: enhanced mass transfer induced by the presence of defects, an active catalytic role of the MNPs, and a different spatial localization of the enzymes in the biocomposite. For the first contribution, we note that the literature confirms how the presence of NPs at the surface of the porous crystals could be associated to the presence of defects that facilitate the substrate diffusion through the porous particles.[ 38 , 39 ] To examine the structural effect of nanoparticles on the HOF, we prepared carboxyl‐functionalized SiO2 NPs of identical size to the MNPs (i.e., average diameter 37 ± 10 nm, Figure S7, Supporting Information) and prepared SiO2‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 biocomposites via the same one‐pot approach (Figures S7–S11, Supporting Information). Compared with enzyme@BioHOF‐1 activities (enzyme = GOx or CAT), only a minor increase of the average effectiveness factor was measured in presence of chemically inert SiO2 (i.e., <15% in the presence of SiO2 NPs, while it is >30% in presence of MNPs). We concluded that the presence of defects generically induced by NPs on the surface of the crystals is not sufficient to explain the improvement of the catalytic performances of the enzymes immobilized in BioHOF‐1 in the presence of MNPs. For the second contribution, the activity increase could be associated with the affinity of MNPs towards H2O2 and to the documented peroxidase‐like activity.[ 40 ] Given this, we examined the catalytic activity of free MNPs in solution. The collected data indicate the Fe3O4 nanoparticles are themselves catalytically inactive in the assays’ tested in this work (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Next, we explored a potential synergic effect between the enzyme and the particle, by preparing a solution of free enzymes (both GOx and CAT) and free MNPs (absence of HOF material or related building blocks). In this experiment, the same concentrations of enzymes and MNPs used for the preparation of the biocomposites were used. The catalytic activity was compared to the one of the free enzyme and showed the same enzymatic activities (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Thus, the contribution of Fe3O4 particles or the synergic effect with enzymes on the catalytic activity in absence of the HOF material cannot explain the enhanced activity of the composite. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the co‐presence of Fe3O4 particles and enzymes within HOF is the only configuration that afford enhanced catalytic performance.

Finally, we tested the dynamic localization properties imparted by the MNPs on the MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 biocomposite in a model biosensor device. Given GOx's established role in processing glucose for biomedical glucose sensors,[ 3 , 34 , 41 ] we focused our attention on GOx‐based biocomposites. We hypothesized that the dynamic localization properties of MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 would allow for the magnetic positioning and retention of the active biocatalysts in flow biosensors and the subsequent recollection.

When examining potential applications, we note that current glucose biosensors are based on amperometry and have a linear dynamic range between 1.6 and 22.2 mm, which covers the normal glucose concentration in the blood (Figure S15, Supporting Information).[ 3 ] However, fervent research in glucose sensing targets lower glucose levels as these can be found in different biological fluids (e.g., sweat, tears, saliva Figure S15, Supporting Information).[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ] The detection of such low amounts of sugar is challenging and usually necessitates customized sensors.[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ]

To examine the potential use of our system as a proof‐of‐concept (PoC) in‐flow glucose biosensor, we connected the inlet of a microfluidic device based on a commercial microfluidic chip with a reaction chamber (30 µL) designed for magnetic beads‐based assays to two syringe pumps to dispense glucose solution at controlled flows (for details, see Figure 4a–e; Figure S13, Supporting Information). The outlet of the chip was then connected to an oxygen optical sensor: this allowed to monitoring in continuous the concentration of oxygen in the solution. Initially, the microfluidic circuit was loaded with buffer and the MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 particles were loaded in the circuit with the help of a micropipette. Then, by using a magnet, we transferred the MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 in a central microfluidic chamber close to the chip's outlet (Figure 4a,b). The magnet was then fixed to keep in place the magnetically responsive biocomposite during the flow administration of the glucose solution. Preliminary experiments showed that by modifying the flow rate of the analyte and the quantity of biocomposite within the chip, glucose could be detected in different concentrations. Loading 40 µL of a 4 mg mL−1 solution of MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 (that corresponds to 0.01 mg of encapsulated GOx), and flowing glucose solutions with low concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 2 mm at 0.1 mL min−1 (residence time in reaction chamber: 18 s), we obtained a linear glucose detection range (R2 = 0.99, Figure 4c,f). For higher concentrations between 2 and 61 mm, the calibration curve exhibited a hyperbolic relationship between the oxygen consumed (ΔO2) and glucose concentration (Figure S14, Supporting Information). Then, by adjusting the flowrate of glucose solutions to the amount of biocomposite in the chip, we found the conditions to measure a linear response for higher glucose concentrations. For example, decreasing the volume of MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 injected (20 µL, equivalent to 0.05 mg of encapsulated GOx) while increasing the analyte flow rate to 0.5 mL min−1 (residence time: 3.6 s), we afford a linear response with R2 = 0.99 in a 6–20 mm range (Figure 4g).

a–d) Schematic view of the different working steps of the proof‐of‐concept glucose flow sensor. e) Recyclability of the biosensor reported as cycle number (note: 1 cycle = time requested to process a flow of the analyte equal to the volume of the reaction cavity). The trend line (black) is qualitatively representative of the decrease in performance during the exposure to continuous glucose flow. f) Biosensor calibration curve in the glucose concentration range 0.2–2 mm (linear fit: y=(11.7 ± 0.5)+(27.1 ± 0.5)*x, R2=0.997). g) Biosensor calibration curve in the glucose concentration range 6–20 mm (linear fit: y=(24 ± 2)+(6.7 ± 0.2)*x, R2=0.997). Typically, after reaching the steady state, the oxygen concentration was recorded over 10 min: the final value of ΔO2 was averaged on the steady state reached.

These results demonstrate that by employing MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 in a microfluidic system, we could monitor glucose in a wide range of concentrations with a linear response.

Next, we tested the robustness of our PoC glucose sensor (i.e., activity retention of the immobilized GOx) over several hours of glucose exposure at constant flowrate. The oxygen conversion was retained at 80% over 600 cycles. After 1833 cycles (corresponding to 8 h of continuous exposure to glucose) the biocomposite maintained a 37% oxygen conversion (Figure 4e). These cyclability results outperformed previously reported HOF biocomposites based on GOx[ 29 ] or other enzymes[ 47 ] that were tested for sensing applications. Our data, and the comparison with other HOF‐systems from the literature, highlight the potential of MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 for the development of robust flow‐based sensors.

After the cycling tests, we switched the flow from the analyte solution to deionized water to wash the chip and the biocomposite and, after the removal of the magnet, the particles were released from the chip chamber and collected at the outlet of the chip (Figure 4d). XRD and SEM analysis of the collected HOF biocomposites confirmed that the crystal structure and morphology of the particles were preserved (Figures S10,S11 and S16, Supporting Information). These proof‐of‐concept experiments demonstrated that magnetically responsive and biocatalytically active BioHOF‐1 biocomposites can be positioned in microfluidic devices, held in a selected area during the sensing activity, released on demand, and subsequently magnetically re‐collected.

Conclusion

We have developed a one‐pot synthesis method to prepare magnetically responsive enzyme@BioHOF‐1 biocomposites. Our findings show that incorporating magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) into the biocomposites enhances the enzymatic activities of catalase (CAT) and glucose oxidase (GOx) based HOF biocomposites and impart dynamic localization capabilities. We designed a microfluidic glucose biosensor prototype based on MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1, focusing on the catalytic oxidation by GOx and monitoring the related oxygen variation in the same solution. By using a permanent magnet, we successfully localized MNPs‐GOx@BioHOF‐1 particles within a microfluidic chip, held them in position during the in‐flow process, released them on demand, and recovered the biocomposites from the chip. Demonstrating high stability (retaining 100% activity after 70 cycles, 80% after 700 cycles, and 37% after 1200 cycles) and a linear response over a large glucose concentration interval, we show the potential of magnetically responsive HOF biocomposites for biosensing.

We foresee that the properties of MNPs‐based HOF biocomposites could be employed for biotechnological applications where the magnetic responsiveness of the biocomposites could favor efficient catalyst positioning and recovery.

Experimental Section

Materials

Unless stated, chemicals and reagents were used as received from Sigma‐Aldrich, TCI chemicals, or BLD Pharmatech Ltd.

Synthesis of Citrate Capped Fe3O4 Magnetic Nanoparticles (MNPs)

The synthetic protocol reported by Schejn et al. was followed.[ 48 ] Briefly, In a 50 mL, single‐neck, round bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stir bar, FeCl3·6(H2O) (0.65 g) and FeSO4·7(H2O) (0.56 g) were dissolved in 16 mL of water. Aqueous ammonia solution (2 mL, 25% w/w) was added in 0.5 mL steps under vigorous stirring. The mixture was then heated at 90 °C, then trisodium citrate (1.8 g, powder) was added to the hot solution. After 30 min of stirring at 90 °C, the solution was cooled to room‐temperature. Then, a strong ND45 magnet (fitted into a glass tube) was immersed in the solution to collect the MNPs. The supernatant was discharged and replaced with fresh deionized (DI) water, and the magnet was extracted to re‐suspend the particles. This washing process was repeated with water (3 × 500 mL), ethanol (3 × 20 mL), and water (3 × 50 mL).

Synthesis of the Biocomposites

The synthetic protocol of MNPs‐enzyme@BioHOF‐1 is based on the dispersion of the MNPs (1–0.1 mg mL−1 range) in a 1 mL aqueous solution of CAT (1 mg mL−1, Catalase from bovine liver C9322 Sigma–Aldrich) or GOx (1 mg mL−1, Glucose oxidase from Aspergillus niger G0050 TCI) and of 1 (1 = methanetetrayltetrakis(benzene‐4,1‐diyl))tetrakis(aminomethaniminium), CAS RN. 313484‐93‐4, BLD Pharmatech Ltd.), 4 mg mL−1). Then, this solution was added to 1 mL of a 3 mg mL−1 aqueous solution (containing 50 µL of 1% NH3(aq) to ensure the solubility of the ligand) of 2 (2 = Tetrakis(4‐carboxyphenyl)methane, CAS RN. 160248‐28‐2, TCI Chemicals). Immediately, the solution turned cloudy, suggesting the instantaneous formation of HOF crystals. The precursors were left to react at room‐temperature and static conditions for 30 min and then collected and washed. enzyme@BioHOF‐1 and of BioHOF‐1 were prepared following the same protocol, but in the absence of MNPs and of MNPs and enzyme, respectively.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

SEM micrographs were collected using Tescan VEGA 3 SEM (TESCAN ORSAY HOLDING, a.s., Brno, Czech Republik) with tungsten source filament (20 kV). Prior to the analysis, the powder samples were dropcasted on a piece of Si (100), dried overnight at room‐temperature, and finally sputter‐coated with gold.

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM)

STEM analysis was performed with a Zeiss Supra 40 microscope equipped with a high‐ angle annular dark‐field (HAADF) detector. Images were collected at an energy between 20–30 KeV and WD of 5 mm, line noise reduction acquisition mode.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FT‐IR spectra were recorded on a Bruker ALPHA FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) using the ATR accessory with a diamond window in the range of ν̃ 400–4000 cm−1, 256 scans, resolution 4 cm−1.

Powder X‐Ray Diffraction (PXRD)

XRD patterns were acquired using a Rigaku SmartLab II (Rigaku Europe SE, Neu‐Isenburg, Germany) equipped with a Cu anode (9 kW, λ = 1.5406Å). Prior to the analysis the powder samples were dropcasted on a piece of Si (100) and dried overnight at room‐temperature.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM)

Images were captured using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with spectral detection (Leica Microsystems, Inc.) and a Leica HC PL APO 63× NA 1.2 W CORR CS2 objective. Prior to imaging, coverslip correction was performed following the manufacturer's instructions to optimize the fluorescence signal. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was excited at 488 nm, emission detected in the 500–550 nm. The samples were measured using a photomultiplier tube, with a scan speed of 700 Hz, a pinhole size of AU 1, and sampling at 70 × 70 × 120 nm (x/y/z). Fluorescence and transmission images were acquired simultaneously. FITC‐tagged enzymes (FCAT, FGOx) were prepared following the protocol reported in reference 21. The synthesis of FCAT and FGOx containing samples was performed following the same protocol developed for the CAT and GOx materials (see “Synthesis of the biocomposites”).

Enzymatic Activity Assays

The enzymatic assay of catalase was performed following the protocol reported by Sigma–Aldrich (Enzymatic Assay of Catalase (EC 1.11.1.6), https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/AT/en/technical‐documents/protocol/protein‐biology/enzyme‐activity‐assays/enzymatic‐assay‐of‐catalase). Briefly, the continuous spectrophotometric rate of H2O2 reduction was determined by measuring the absorbance at 240 nm of a 11.5 mm H2O2 solution in phosphate buffer (50 mm, pH 7). In a typical measurement, 2.9 mL of a 11.5 mm H2O2 solution in phosphate buffer (50 mm, pH 7) was loaded in a quartz cuvette. Then, 0.1 mL of the enzyme or biocomposite solution was added to the cuvette, and the collection of UV–vis spectra (1 spectrum every 2 s; Spectrophotometer: NanoDrop One UV–vis by Thermo Fisher) started. Phosphate buffer (50 mm, pH 7) was measured as background.

The enzymatic assay for glucose oxidase‐free and immobilized was performed using an oxygen sensor to monitor the initial rate of oxygen consumption during the oxidation of glucose. Initially, an equilibration time of ≈20 s was observed and the linear regression analysis was done within the first minute of the reaction. A fiber‐optic oxygen meter consisting of a FireStingO2 (FSO2‐4) control unit with an OXROB10 probe (PyroScience GmbH, Aachen, Germany) was used to monitor the oxygen level. The measurement was done under stirring with a magnetic stirrer (6 × 3 mm, 350 rpm) at 35 °C. 100 µL of enzyme solution, previously diluted (2 mg mL−1 free enzyme, 1:100; 0.2 mg mL−1 of encapsulated enzyme, 1:10) were added to 1.7 mL of buffer (sodium acetate buffer 50 mm, pH 5.0). Then 100 µL of glucose solution 61.2 mm was added to the vial. The specific activity in these conditions was 23 ± 2 U mg−1.

Protocol for Trypsin Treatment

A trypsin stock solution was prepared in HCl 1 mm (10‐30 mg mL−1). The trypsin concentration during incubation with the biocomposite was 5 mg mL−1. A phosphate buffer 50 mm, pH 8.6, was used to dilute the solution to 500 µL. The amount of biocomposite treated was approximately 0.08 mg. The trypsin was left in incubation with the biocomposite for 60 min at RT. A sample of biocomposite was diluted in the same amount in the phosphate buffer without trypsin and its activity tested in parallel. After the test the supernatant of the biocomposite in buffer was analyzed to see if enzyme was released from the surface of BioHOF‐1. Each test was negative, meaning the buffer could not wash the enzyme from the surface. Free catalase was also tested in the same buffer.
