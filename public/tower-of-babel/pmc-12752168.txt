Quantification of the IgG antibody response half-life for hybrid immunity to SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Felicia Bongiovanni, Eamon Conway, Lauren Smith, Jacob Cumming, Ramin Mazhari, Nicholas Kiernan-Walker, Emily M Eriksson, Jodie McVernon, Ivo Mueller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA firm understanding of SARS-CoV-2 hybrid immunity is crucial for our ongoing efforts to protect people from severe and fatal disease and assess population vulnerability to emerging novel variants. As many components of the immune response are unobserved and complex to investigate, some ambiguities and unanswered questions remain about hybrid immunity to COVID-19, such as the duration of the antibody response in individuals.MethodsTo address this, we evaluated longitudinal data that spanned up to 21 months from 52 SARS-CoV-2 naive individuals and 88 SARS-CoV-2 recovered individuals. We further separated individuals according to whether they received an mRNA or non-mRNA vaccine for their primary two-dose vaccinations. A hierarchical Bayesian framework was used to fit the parameters of mono-phasic exponential decay and bi-phasic exponential decay models to the observed data to estimate the magnitude and half-life of the spike-specific and receptor-binding domain (RBD)-specific IgG responses.ResultsResults from both our mono-phasic and bi-phasic exponential decay models estimate that the median half-life of the spike-specific and RBD-specific IgG response in individuals with hybrid immunity is almost double that of naive individuals who were only vaccinated. Recovered mRNA recipients were estimated through the mono-phasic decay model to have a median IgG response half-life of 448 days (95% CrI: [375.08, 547.54]) to spike antigen, compared to an estimated 222 days (95% CrI: [179.90, 286.62]) for naive mRNA recipients. Posterior estimates from the bi-phasic exponential decay model show that recovered individuals who received mRNA vaccinations had a median IgG response half-life of 810 days (95% CrI: [650.91, 1031.63]) to spike antigen, compared to 451 days (95% CrI: [336.81, 618.14]) for naive mRNA recipients. A similar pattern is seen in non-mRNA vaccine recipients, with IgG response half-lives differing slightly. Our results show that, across different model assumptions, individuals with hybrid immunity have an IgG response half-life that is considerably greater than that of individuals with only infection- or vaccine-induced immunity.ConclusionsOur work provides important insight into the longevity of the IgG response to SARS-CoV-2 in individuals with hybrid immunity and can guide effective immunisation approaches to maintain and improve population-level protection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04490-4.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Antibody kinetics, IgG, Antibody decay, Hybrid immunity, Vaccine, Immunology

Background

Since the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 in December 2019, extensive research has been conducted worldwide to understand the immune response to the virus. Despite this, our knowledge of the development and persistence of immunity to COVID-19 remains incomplete. Vaccines are primarily used to maintain or enhance immunity against pathogens, including SARS-CoV-2. However, vaccination is known to not always produce immune responses comparable to those elicited by natural infections [1–4]. In particular, numerous studies report that vaccination-acquired immunity is less durable and protective compared to infection-associated immunity [1, 5–8]. However, other studies suggest that there are no significant differences in protection against severe disease and the risk of reinfection in fully vaccinated individuals compared to those who have previously been infected [9–13]. Furthermore, additional studies demonstrate that hybrid immunity (achieved through a combination of natural infection and vaccination) provides more robust immunity against other variants of SARS-CoV-2 and more durable immunity than natural infection or vaccination alone [7, 12, 14–16]. These contrasting findings underscore that much remains to be understood about immunity to SARS-CoV-2 acquired by vaccination and natural infection.

The magnitude of the antibody response to SARS-CoV-2 depends on factors such as the type of vaccine received [17, 18], whether the exposure is due to vaccination or natural infection, and an individual’s exposure history. A significant gap in our knowledge is the duration of protection against SARS-CoV-2 mediated by antigen-specific antibodies [19]. As antibodies, especially the IgG isotype, are well known to be correlated with protection against disease [20–23], a significant area of interest is to determine the duration of the antibody response. Long-term humoral immunity against SARS-CoV-2 is maintained by long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs) that continuously secrete antibodies for months to years and memory B cells that maintain immunological memory to mount rapid and robust responses upon re-exposure. Cells such as LLPCs that are critical to protective immunity remain understudied due to the challenges in investigating this cell population without invasive procedures given their residence in the bone marrow [24]. Additionally, given that hybrid immunity has become the dominant immune status in the population, it is increasingly complex to ascertain an individual’s exposure history. Thus, a comprehensive assessment of the duration and strength of the antibody response elicited by natural exposure and vaccination to SARS-CoV-2 is challenging to determine using data from the current population.

In contrast to LLPCs, antibody titres are readily available in the blood and can be measured to provide insight into the underlying dynamics of humoral kinetics through mathematical modelling [25–27]. Many previous studies modelling the antibody response to SARS-CoV-2 antigens explain kinetics through mono-phasic exponential decay models and linear regression [28–34]. These are limiting to our understanding of immunity to COVID-19 as they overlook essential components of antibody maintenance and do not account for differences in short- and long-lived immune dynamics. Other studies account for bi-phasic antibody decay through power-law models [35], gamma models [36], and bi-phasic exponential decay models [37–41], accounting for the contribution of SLPCs and LLPCs to antibody dynamics. Of these studies, none report on the interface between mRNA and non-mRNA vaccine-acquired immunity, naturally acquired immunity, and how this translates to hybrid immunity. This information is crucial to inform long-term recommendations on global vaccine uptake, scheduling vaccine administration, and better protecting and preparing the population against future pandemics caused by novel viruses.

In this study, we aimed to quantify the magnitude and duration of the IgG antibody response to the SARS-CoV-2 spike and receptor-binding domain antigens using longitudinal serological data from Victoria, Australia, and an antibody kinetics model that accounts for bi-phasic exponential decay. The data from Australia presented a unique resource for investigating immunity to COVID-19 in a controlled, real-world setting, given the strict lockdown procedures implemented and the limitations on international travel within the country. These factors created a low-transmission early population cohort that could be studied without a significant risk of frequent or undetected SARS-CoV-2 infections influencing the results. Additionally, we compared the bi-phasic exponential decay model to a mono-phasic exponential decay model to highlight the importance of model assumptions and their impact on evaluating immunity. We considered the differences between mRNA and non-mRNA vaccine types, as well as the infection history of individuals, to report on the effect of vaccine-acquired immunity and naturally acquired immunity in achieving hybrid immunity.

Methods

Study population

The SARS-CoV-2 study population comprises data collected from individuals recruited as part of the COVID PROFILE study [42], which was launched in Melbourne, Victoria, in October 2020. The recruitment period for the study was from October 7, 2020, to July 5, 2022. Most participants were enrolled between October 2020 and June 2021, and the study sample collection and follow-up concluded in May 2023. Adult community members were recruited through advertising campaigns in primary care networks, tertiary teaching hospitals, and online social media platforms. A total of 178 individuals were enrolled in the study, of which seven participants were excluded from the study as they failed to provide a baseline sample, as required by the study schedule. The study inclusion criteria considered only non-pregnant individuals over 18 years of age.

Individuals who were SARS-CoV-2 naive (n=73) and those who had recovered from SARS-CoV-2 infection (n=98) were enrolled, with all participants providing written informed consent as per the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute (Projects 20/08) and the Melbourne Health Human Research Committees (RMH69108). Most participants received at least two doses of a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. The majority of individuals in both the SARS-CoV-2-naive and SARS-CoV-2-recovered groups received either the Pfizer–BioNTech mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2) or the AstraZeneca adenoviral vector vaccine (ChAdOx1-S), with a minor subset of participants who received either the Moderna mRNA vaccine (mRNA-1273) or the Novavax virus-like particle vaccine (NVX-CoV2373). All vaccines contained the spike antigen of the ancestral variant. A subset of vaccinated individuals also received up to five doses, most of which were booster vaccinations with BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273. Clinical and experimental data were available from each participant, including their date of diagnosis and vaccination, symptoms, and longitudinal antibody data.

Serology data

Antibody levels were measured in plasma collected from individuals at their enrolment date and, in most cases, with multiple follow up sample collections until either the conclusion of the study or at the individual end date. Samples were collected at enrolment, two weeks after any vaccination or natural infection, and then every three months after vaccination or infection. IgG antibodies that recognise and bind to the full-length spike and receptor-binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2 were measured in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) using a Luminex MAGPIX multiplex assay [43]. All antigens were based on the ancestral variant sequence. Antibody levels for each participant were reported in adjusted MFI, which was calculated using a five-parameter logistic regression model algorithm based on standard curves and quality control samples, to account for plate-to-plate variation. Positive controls were plasma or saliva from COVID-19 diagnosed convalescent individuals and negative controls were naive control plasma collected from individuals before the start of the pandemic or from those who were SARS-CoV-2 negative.

Inference

Posterior samples were drawn using a No-U-Turn sampler (an extension of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) implemented with rstan (version 2.21.8) [56].

Four chains with 10,000 iterations each were run for the mono-phasic exponential decay model, discarding 4000 iterations total for warm-up. This provided 36,000 posterior samples for estimated parameters. All sampler parameters were left as the Stan default.

For the bi-phasic exponential decay model, four chains were run with 20,000 iterations each, of those 5000 were discarded as warm-up. This produced 60,000 posterior samples for each model parameter at the population-level and individual-level. Some of the Stan parameters that control the sampler were changed from the default to improve estimations. We set adapt_delta = 0.999 and max_treedepth = 12, while all other sampler parameters were left as the default values.

Traceplots and the effective sample size, R^, were evaluated using the rstan package to ensure that chains had converged and were well mixed for both models. R^ values are provided in Additional File 1: Table S1 and Additional File 1: Table S2.

Statistical analysis

To test whether the difference in geometric means between the cohort and vaccination groups was significant, we conducted a t-test using the R stats package (version 4.4.0) [57] on the log-transformed adjusted MFI of the spike-specific and receptor-binding domain-specific IgG titres.

A Tukey’s test was conducted after an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to measure the differences in age between the cohort and vaccination groups to determine whether the mean age between the groups differed significantly. Both the Tukey’s test and ANOVA were performed using the R stats package.

Results

Summary of observed data

The COVID PROFILE data consisted of 171 individuals. Before conducting our modelling, we cleaned the data and removed 17 individuals with fewer than two antibody measurements. Additionally, we excluded any samples from individuals where an antibody measurement was not accompanied by a date of collection. We also removed four individuals who did not receive a vaccination and did not experience an infection, as they would have no information on the antibody response to exposure. One individual was removed that only received one vaccination and had only provided one sample after said vaccination. Another individual experienced a natural infection before completing their primary vaccination schedule, so they were removed. Lastly, we removed four individuals who were vaccinated prior to enrolment in the study. These changes reduced the total sample size to 142 individuals, separated as 52 SARS-CoV-2-naive individuals and 88 SARS-CoV-2-recovered individuals. We separated both the naive and recovered cohorts based on the type of vaccine individuals received for their initial two doses. Those who received either BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273 were grouped into the mRNA vaccine group, and anyone who received ChAdOx1-S or NVX-CoV2373 was considered to be in the non-mRNA vaccine group. A summary of the groupings of individuals by cohort and initial vaccine received is presented in Table 2.

Grouping of COVID PROFILE individuals

A small number of individuals had asymptomatic infections at enrolment into the COVID PROFILE cohort and these were all, except for one, detected by qPCR. The remaining individual who was enrolled in the beginning of the pandemic was observed to have very high titres of antibodies directed against SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein and spike-associated proteins using the Luminex serology assay. Confirmed natural infections that occurred during the study were self-reported based on PCR results or rapid antigen tests. We also analysed the serological data for the IgG response to nucleocapsid, spike, and RBD to try capture asymptomatic infections that occurred during follow-up that were not identified. To do so, we first calculated the mean nucleocapsid-specific IgG titres from the initial samples taken after a confirmed natural infection for the recovered cohort and the naive cohort, separately. We chose to calculate the mean separately for the cohorts as antibody levels in the recovered cohort may reach higher titres given it is a second natural infection as opposed to the naive cohort where individuals are experiencing a natural infection for the first time. We then identified individuals that experienced an increase in IgG titres specific for nucleocapsid, spike, and RBD more than three months after receiving a vaccination. We only considered increases in IgG titres three months post-vaccination given evidence of increases in nucleocapsid-associated IgG levels after spike-based vaccination given the potential induction of cross-reactive antibodies against nucleocapsid [41, 60, 61]. If this criteria was met, and the nucleocapsid antibody levels reached the mean post-infection titre calculated for their respective cohort, the individual was considered to have experienced an asymptomatic infection. For any individuals naturally infected with SARS-CoV-2 during the study, we removed any observations after the exposure.

The group with the highest number of individuals was the recovered cohort that received a primary two-dose schedule of mRNA vaccinations, comprising a total of 49 people. Similarly, in the naive cohort, more people received mRNA vaccinations (n=32) than non-mRNA vaccinations (n=20). Table 3 also shows that there were more female participants in most groups than male participants and mRNA recipients were, on average, younger than non-mRNA recipients. Results from a Tukey’s test show that the mean age differs significantly between the recovered non-mRNA and naive mRNA groups and the recovered non-mRNA and recovered mRNA groups (p-value < 0.01, Additional File 1: Table S3). Significant differences in age were not observed for individuals across the other comparison groups (Additional File 1: Table S3).

Comparing the observed IgG antibody response across cohorts

We observe the distribution and geometric mean of spike-specific and RBD-specific IgG antibodies produced in response to first vaccination, second vaccination, and booster vaccination in Fig. 1. Anti-spike IgG measured 2–4 weeks after both the first and second vaccination in the recovered cohort had a higher geometric mean for both the mRNA and non-mRNA recipients than their respective counterparts in the naive cohort (p-value < 0.01, Additional File 1: Table S4). We obtained similar results for anti-RBD IgG in recovered non-mRNA recipients. However, we no longer observed a significant difference between the naive and recovered cohorts after individuals received a booster vaccination (p-value > 0.05, Additional File 1: Table S4). This indicates that it took a complete two-dose primary vaccination schedule and a booster vaccination for individuals in the naive cohort to reach IgG antibody titres similar to those in the recovered cohort. Individuals who received mRNA vaccinations in both the naive and recovered cohorts were observed to have higher IgG titres toward spike antigen after two vaccinations than non-mRNA recipients (p-value < 0.01, Additional File 1: Table S5). We see a similar difference in response to RBD protein (p-value < 0.05, Additional File 1: Table S5). Therefore, the results in Fig. 1 suggest that a higher level of IgG titres is achieved through two doses of mRNA vaccination (being predominantly BNT162b2) than non-mRNA vaccination (predominantly ChAdOx1-S).

Antibody increase from vaccination alone

We then compared this to naive individuals who received mRNA and non-mRNA vaccinations. Those who received mRNA vaccinations (top row of Fig. 2) were estimated to have a median increase in adjusted MFI of 1006.35 (95% CrI: [704.13, 1546.94]) for anti-RBD IgG, with a 18.45% probability of being higher than the estimates for those who experienced a natural infection alone. This was associated with a 1.31-fold increase (95% CrI: [1.09, 1.54]) in antibody increase for each exposure after the first vaccination. In contrast, anti-spike IgG antibodies were estimated to have a median increase of 1727.13 adjusted MFI (95% CrI: [1348.96, 2266.94]) in naive mRNA recipients, representing a 80.06% probability that the estimates were higher than those obtained for natural infection alone. We also observed a 0.51-fold reduction (95% CrI: [0.37, 0.66]) in the increase in anti-spike IgG following subsequent exposures after the first vaccination. These results indicate that, upon initial vaccination, the increase in anti-RBD antibodies is similar to that observed in natural infection alone; however, this increase becomes larger with subsequent vaccinations. Conversely, our results indicate that the production of anti-spike IgG is greater than that observed for natural infection alone; however, this production decreases after the first vaccination. The increase in anti-RBD IgG for naive individuals receiving non-mRNA vaccinations (second row of Fig. 2) was estimated to be similar to that of naive mRNA recipients, with a median of 1080.43 (95% CrI: [653.22, 1886.94]) adjusted MFI. The antibody response to spike increased at a median of 996.08 (95% CrI: [629.36, 1685.66]) adjusted MFI, with only a 19.54% probability that the estimates were greater than those obtained for natural infection alone. The naive non-mRNA vaccination recipients had a 1.45-fold (95% CrI: [1.08, 1.84]) and 1.56-fold (95% CrI: [1.10, 2.07]) increase in antibody production to the spike and RBD proteins, respectively, following the first vaccination. These results, similar to those for naive mRNA recipients, suggest that initial vaccination with a non-mRNA vaccine results in a similar increase in IgG titres as natural infection alone, which gets larger after the first vaccination.

Antibody increase from natural infection and vaccination

Lastly, in Fig. 2, we assess the increase in IgG for recovered individuals who also received vaccinations. Those who received mRNA vaccinations (third row of Fig. 2) had a median increase of 2289.56 (95% CrI: [1736.13, 3077.83]) in adjusted MFI in response to spike protein, a 92.36% probability that the estimates were higher than those of their naive counterparts. Anti-RBD antibodies were estimated to increase with a median of 1901.34 (95% CrI: [1428.64, 2581.44]) adjusted MFI, indicating a probability of 99.18% that the estimates would be higher than those of naive individuals receiving mRNA vaccinations. Both anti-spike and anti-RBD IgG production were associated with an equal median fold-reduction of 0.35 (95% CrI: [0.22, 0.52] for spike, 95% CrI: [0.22, 0.52] for RBD) for further exposures, indicating a decrease in antibody production with vaccination following natural infection. Similarly, recovered individuals who received non-mRNA vaccinations (fourth row of Fig. 2) had a 0.45-fold reduction (95% CrI: [0.27, 0.69]) and a 0.46-fold reduction (95% CrI: [0.27, 0.71]) in antibody production to spike and RBD antigens, respectively, across exposures. These were also associated with a greater production of IgG upon exposure than naive counterparts in response to spike (median of 2023.41, 95% CrI: [1420.53, 2932.02]) and RBD (median of 1733.21, 95% CrI: [1202.72, 2251.30]). Similar to naive mRNA recipients, we observe the estimates for antibody increase in mRNA and non-mRNA recovered recipients to be higher for spike-specific IgG than for RBD-specific IgG. Given we observe this across multiple groups, these results could be reflective of the immune response to RBD being of a lower magnitude compared to spike, or it could be indicative a more comprehensive antibody response for spike-specific IgG than for RBD due to the spike antigen being composed of multiple antigen domains, including RBD. Full details on posterior population-level parameter estimates are provided in Additional File 1: Table S8 and Additional File 1: Table S9.

Half-life of IgG antibody responses to spike and RBD protein

Results assuming a mono-phasic decay

Insight from the single-phase exponential decay model reveals distinct differences from the bi-phasic exponential decay model in the half-life of the IgG response between the naive and recovered cohorts, as shown in Fig. 3. When estimated through the mono-phasic decay model, the median antibody response half-life for a natural infection alone was estimated as 324 days (95% CrI: [273.18, 394.46]) in response to spike and 345 days (95% CrI: [286.087, 428.258]) to RBD, shown by the blue bars in the bottom panel of Fig. 3. Estimates of IgG half-life for the spike antigen had a 99.11% probability of being higher for natural infection than those of the naive cohort who received mRNA vaccination (median half-life 222 days, 95% CrI: [179.90, 286.62]) and a 99.31% probability of being higher than the estimates for naive non-mRNA recipients (median half-life 208 days, 95% CrI: [164.86, 279.85]). Similar results are observed in response to RBD in Fig. 3. These results suggest that the IgG response has a longer half-life after a single natural exposure to SARS-CoV-2 than after at least two doses of either an mRNA or non-mRNA vaccine. Recovered individuals who received mRNA vaccinations were estimated by the mono-phasic decay model to have a median half-life of 448 days (95% CrI: [375.09, 547.54]) in response to spike, having a 100% probability that the estimates were higher than their naive counterparts, and a 99.14% probability of being higher than for that estimated for natural infection alone. Identical patterns are seen for recovered individuals receiving non-mRNA vaccinations and for the response to RBD protein (Additional File 1: Table S7). This indicates that there was a substantial increase in the longevity of the IgG response in individuals who experienced a natural infection and received either an mRNA or non-mRNA vaccine schedule.

Results assuming a bi-phasic exponential decay

Our interpretation of the half-life of the IgG response through the bi-phasic decay model is performed by estimating the half-life of the LLPCs (μdL). As seen in Fig. 3, assuming antibodies decay in two phases leads to different results than the mono-phasic decay model estimations. The IgG response to spike protein for natural infection alone was estimated to have a median half-life of 494.79 days (95% CrI: [382.41, 661.49]) and 436 days (95% CrI: [337.19, 584.73]) in response to RBD, illustrated by the red bars in the bottom panel of Fig. 3. The probability that estimates for the naive mRNA recipients were higher than for natural infection alone for spike protein was 32.73% (median half-life 451 days, 95% CrI: [336.81, 618.14]) and a 47.66% probability (median half-life 431 days, 95% CrI: [306.18, 615.40]) for RBD. Conversely, for naive non-mRNA recipients, there was a 15.69% probability (median half-life 395 days, 95% CrI: [282.27, 564.89]) of estimates being higher than the estimated longevity of the IgG response through natural infection alone to spike and a 25.88% probability (median half-life 376 days, 95% CrI: [268.44, 543.82]) for estimates for the response to RBD. These results suggest that the longevity of the IgG response elicited by mRNA vaccination has a higher probability of leading to a greater half-life compared to natural infection alone than when receiving non-mRNA vaccination.

A substantial difference is seen in Fig. 3 for the recovered individuals who were vaccinated. The median half-life of the antibody response to spike protein was 810 days (95% CrI: [650.91, 1031.63]) for recovered mRNA recipients and 815 days (95% CrI: [606.65, 1084.85]) for non-mRNA recipients. These estimates have a 99.85% and 99.90% probability of being higher than those obtained for their mRNA and non-mRNA naive counterparts, respectively. Additionally, the longevity of the IgG response to spike antigen had a probability of being 99.59% higher in recovered mRNA recipients compared to estimates from natural infection alone and 99.11% for recovered non-mRNA recipients. The longevity of the anti-RBD IgG response demonstrates similar increases, with recovered mRNA recipients having a median half-life of 764 days (95% CrI: [612.84, 970.69]) and non-mRNA recipients having a median half-life of 752 days (95% CrI: [550.27, 1013.70]). Similar to the results from the single-phase decay model, these estimates indicate a substantial increase in the longevity of the IgG response resulting from hybrid immunity in individuals who have experienced both a natural infection and vaccination.

Although the results are consistent across the models regarding which cohorts and vaccination types are estimated to have more extended IgG decay periods, the estimates between the two model types differ greatly. Both naive and recovered individuals receiving either mRNA or non-mRNA vaccination are estimated to have a median IgG response half-life, as determined by the bi-phasic decay model, almost twice that estimated by the mono-phasic decay model (Additional File 1: Tables S7–S9). Interestingly, the single-phase exponential decay model estimated a longer antibody response half-life to RBD than spike for the recovered mRNA group and natural infection alone (Additional File 1: Table S7). Conversely, the bi-phasic decay model estimated a higher median half-life for the IgG response to spike than RBD across all cohorts and vaccine groups (Additional File 1: Tables S8–S9). This suggests that the varying assumptions of both models can lead to different interpretations of the antibody response to the same antigen, resulting in conflicting outcomes.

Analysis of the impact of age on the antibody response

As age is a known factor to impact the immune response in individuals, we assessed the median posterior parameter estimates for the long-lived plasma cell (LLPC) half-life (Fig. 6A) and the median estimates of the antibody increase per exposure (Fig. 6B) for all individuals to test for correlation. The median estimate for each individual is separated based on shape for the vaccine they received for their primary vaccination schedule, and by colour for the cohort they belonged to. We examine the posterior parameter estimates of the bi-phasic decay model in Fig. 6, given there is no estimation of antibody boost for the mono-phasic decay model. For both median LLPC half-life estimate and median antibody increase estimate, there is no clear correlation between the age of an individual and the durability of their antibody response or magnitude of their antibody increase. We do observe a strong association in Fig. 6A for the cohort of an individual and their median LLPC half-life estimate. Recovered individuals (blue dots and triangles), across all ages and vaccine types, have higher median LLPC half-life estimate than that of the naive cohort (red dots and triangles). This association is not reflected in Fig. 6B, as the median increase in antibodies per exposure for individuals does not appear to differ greatly between cohorts, although the largest estimates are obtained by recovered individuals. Therefore, these results suggest that whether an individual experienced a natural infection prior to vaccination has a greater impact on the longevity of the antibody response than the age of the individuals in our study. The increase of antibodies upon exposure also has no clear association with the age of our study individuals or infection history, indicating that there may be many different factors that influence this.

Discussion

Despite the extensive number of studies published on humoral immunity to SARS-CoV-2, there remains ambiguity regarding the duration of the antibody response following infection. Given this, we investigated the antibody kinetics across individuals exposed to SARS-CoV-2 through natural infection and vaccination to quantify the IgG response half-life and identify the differences between hybrid immunity and the humoral immunity conferred by either vaccination or infection alone. We estimated the IgG response half-life using a mono-phasic exponential model, assuming that antibody decay is constant, and a bi-phasic exponential decay model, which assumes a short- and long-lived component of IgG waning and allows us to estimate the strength of the IgG response upon exposure. This was done not only to illustrate the varying results that different model assumptions can yield but also to determine if patterns across exposure groups (e.g. a specific type of vaccine eliciting a stronger response) were consistent across different assumptions. The results in this work demonstrate that after a full primary-vaccination schedule (two doses), recipients of an mRNA vaccination had significantly higher IgG levels than recipients of a non-mRNA vaccination. This is in line with previous studies that have found that the BNT162b2 vaccine is more effective in generating a greater antibody response than the ChAdOx1-S vaccine [17, 18, 38, 62]. We also found that there was no significant change in the anti-spike or anti-RBD IgG levels of the recovered cohort between the first and second vaccination, regardless of the type of vaccination received, while there was in the naive cohort. This has been identified in previously infected individuals who received the BNT162b2 vaccine [18, 63, 64], but our results demonstrate that it also applies to those receiving other non-mRNA vaccines.

Further confirming our results, studies have shown that only one vaccination is required for recovered individuals to achieve a high antibody level [18, 64, 65], which may explain why there is no further increase in the maximum IgG titre after the first vaccination for the recovered cohort. Interestingly, the most significant increase in both the spike-specific and RBD-specific IgG titres observed in the COVID PROFILE data was in naive non-mRNA recipients between their second dose and their booster vaccination. This could be the result of heterologous vaccination, as all participants in the COVID PROFILE study who received ChAdOx1-S for their first two doses subsequently received an mRNA vaccination (the majority received BNT162b2) for their booster. These results are in line with studies that have found a greater increase in SARS-CoV-2 specific IgG antibodies after heterologous vaccination with an initial two-dose vaccine schedule of a non-mRNA vaccine, followed by a booster mRNA vaccine [17, 65–68].

Although individuals from the recovered cohort demonstrated consistently high spike-specific and RBD-specific antibody titres, examination of the underlying IgG kinetics shows a reduction in the increase of antibodies produced with additional exposure. This is in line with results from other studies, which show a reduced IgG response in previously infected individuals after vaccination and is suggested to be due to the presence of a higher level of antibodies pre-exposure compared to naive individuals [18, 39, 54, 69, 70]. Our results indicate that the reason that recovered individuals maintain higher IgG titres than naive individuals is related to a longer antibody response half-life observed with hybrid immunity. This could also explain why naive individuals exhibit a larger increase in anti-spike and anti-RBD IgG production after exposure. It is not necessarily reflective of a stronger antibody response but rather a lower baseline of IgG levels before vaccination, given that the estimated half-life of the IgG response in the naive cohort is half the duration of those with hybrid immunity. As these results are consistent across bi-phasic decay modelling and mono-phasic decay modelling of the IgG response to both spike and RBD, we suggest that they reflect changes in immunity that enhance the long-term maintenance of the humoral response elicited only through hybrid immunity, which is not obtained through natural infection or vaccination alone. This supports similar results that others have found for hybrid immunity [7, 14–16, 71–73].

Our posterior parameter estimates from both models demonstrate the impact that different model assumptions can have on results, as we found drastically different results depending on our assumptions about antibody decay. This has been observed in other studies modelling the antibody response to spike, RBD, and nucleocapsid [35, 36]. Grandjean et al. [36] observed that the difference in assuming mono-phasic decay or bi-phasic decay through different gamma models did not produce different results for the short-lived IgG response to nucleocapsid or RBD. However, a significant change was observed in the estimation of the antibody response half-life to spike protein. Contrastingly, Cohen et al. [35] found that a power law model, accounting for bi-phasic decay, was better suited to their data and led to greater half-life estimates for the IgG response to nucleocapsid, RBD, and spike than that of mono-phasic decay. This has important implications for our conclusions regarding long-term immunity to SARS-CoV-2. Given that a mono-phasic decay model neglects differences in short- and long-lived dynamics, it can underestimate the longevity of the antibody response and impact decisions on vaccine schedules and cost-effectiveness analysis, which are based on information on antibody waning [74]. Underestimating the half-life of the antibody response can result in vaccine schedules with shorter intervals, which can produce ineffective antibody responses [75]. This has important implications not only for SARS-CoV-2 but also for any other existing or novel pathogens that may arise in the future, for which we need appropriate vaccination schedules.

Additionally, the type of antibody kinetics model we use can assess the impact and longevity of the antibody response to vaccination differently and have consequences for the type and number of vaccines recommended for different cohorts of people. These all have downstream consequences for public health decisions and the health of the population. Thus, we highlight through our analyses using different models, the importance of checking model assumptions and how they align with biological mechanisms we know to be true in response to infectious diseases. Given that we understand the antibody response to SARS-CoV-2 to have a bi-phasic wane [76–78], we find the results from our bi-phasic exponential decay model, rather than the mono-phasic decay, to be of importance. A consideration for future analysis would be to assess if the number of samples and the frequency at which they are taken in the data have an impact on results for different models, and if one type of model is more appropriate for certain distributions of samples.

There are several limitations to this analysis. Although the bi-phasic exponential decay model is better suited to the data than the mono-phasic exponential decay model, it still has its own limiting assumptions. The model does not consider any delay in the initiation of the humoral immune response after primary exposure to SARS-CoV-2 and does not account for changes in the proportion of short-lived plasma cell population over time, which we know to occur during infection [79–82]. Additionally, due to identifiability issues with model parameters, we are unable to estimate the short-lived and long-lived plasma cell populations, as well as the antibody secretion rate. Based on this, we cannot determine how much variation in the antibody response is attributable to each of these parameters. The model also does not account for changes in the half-life of long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs) over time for an individual, instead assuming that it remains constant. Given our data and the short interval between vaccinations, it would be challenging to determine if there is a change in LLPC half-life throughout individual follow-up. As our model does not account for changes to the LLPC half-life over time or changes to the amount they contribute, this may lead to an overestimation in the LLPC half-life. To our knowledge, there are no studies that account for a fluctuation in how much the LLPC population contributes to the immune response over time and how the half-life of these cells would change over a total follow-up period. It may be challenging to estimate this information, even by relaxing our model assumptions, as it may not be clearly identifiable from serological data, given that identifiability issues already exist in separately estimating short-lived plasma cell and LLPC populations. This may require further data for examination of the plasma cell population changes over the course of the antibody response, which can then be used to inform model parameter estimation.

This leads to a further limitation of our data. For our groups of individuals with hybrid immunity (recovered individuals who have received either mRNA or non-mRNA vaccination), we do not know how many vaccinations are required to significantly increase the IgG response half-life compared to the naive cohort or natural infection alone. A study involving individuals who have previously been infected and only received one, two, or three vaccinations that have long follow-up times could provide greater insight into the impact of the number of vaccines received on the longevity of the antibody response in recovered individuals. Additionally, this information could provide a way to measure changes in the proportion of SLPCs and the half-life of LLPCs across multiple exposures. The time between natural infection and the first blood sample collection for the recovered cohort, which for many is their antibody production phase and initial antibody decay, is unobserved in our data and may have led to parameter estimates relying more heavily on prior information due to the limited number of samples available to inform them. This can bias not only posterior estimates for the antibody production phase, but also for the LLPC half-life estimates, given the initial reliance on prior distributions for information. This may lead to an underestimation or overestimation in our estimation of the longevity of the IgG response. A future study that collects blood samples in the initial weeks after natural infection could provide this data and lead to more informed estimates that more accurately capture the complete antibody response. Additionally, an extension to this work in future could be to conduct an analysis to simulate early-phase data for natural infection to assess to what extent missing data or the inclusion of predicted data influences posterior parameter estimates. More information can also be gained by learning more about the early immune response to natural infection to determine whether the magnitude and durability of the antibody response changes depending on the amount of time between a natural infection and receiving a vaccination. This can help to inform appropriate vaccination scheduling for recovered individuals to maximise the benefit of strengthening their immunity with a vaccination and achieving hybrid immunity.

In assessing our results, it is also important to consider the age of individuals and the effect that might have on the magnitude and longevity of the spike-specific and RBD-specific IgG response. Age is a notable factor to impact immunity to SARS-CoV-2, with evidence of reduced immune responses in young children and the older adults [83–85]. Although the complete age range for the individuals in our study was 19–84, only 7.64% of participants were over the age of 65, a prominent age of immune decline [86–88]. Therefore, we do not expect significant impacts on immunity for the majority of participants in our study due to age. However, it is worth noting that non-mRNA recipients in both the naive and recovered groups do have a median age of almost ten years more than their respective mRNA counterparts, which may limit our comparisons between the groups. This may have some effect on the results between the mRNA and non-mRNA groups. However, our results do not indicate a strong correlation between the estimated longevity in the antibody response or increase in antibodies upon exposure and age for the majority of our participants. Additionally, the mean age was found to differ significantly only between the recovered non-mRNA and naive non-mRNA groups, as well as between the recovered mRNA and recovered non-mRNA groups. Among the few individuals over the age of 65, there appear to be lower estimates for the magnitude of the antibody response; however, some individuals maintain high estimates for the LLPC half-life. This could be due to these individuals being in the recovered cohort and the impact of hybrid immunity. Due to limitations in sample size, we could not perform modelling with data stratified by age group, but Stocks et al. [38] and Deichmann et al. [40] carried out such analyses and found associations between age and reduced antibody responses.

The study design behind serological data used can have an influence on the posterior parameter estimates obtained. The demographics of the study participants, the transmission setting of where the data is collected, and the sampling frequency and number of samples collected all contribute to how well informed our model estimation can be. In addition, the timing of follow-up and the length of follow-up can impact results. The longer a follow-up period for a study, the longer we observe the decline in antibodies over time, which can better inform parameters such as LLPC half-life. Additionally, the number of exposures (whether through natural infection or vaccination) an individual has will contribute to the magnitude and longevity of their immune response, and these factors become more challenging to determine as we move further away from the emergence of SARS-CoV-2, as people continue to be vaccinated or are unknowingly infected. These factors can lead to different estimates for the half-life and magnitude of the IgG response, given that they can change how informative our data is. Given that our data was collected early in the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and the strict Australian lockdowns created a low-transmission setting, it provides a unique dataset that may be difficult to find elsewhere, which may lead to parameter estimates that may be difficult to obtain elsewhere. We do see some evidence of similarity in our LLPC half-life estimates through the bi-phasic model for infection and vaccination alone [89] and vaccination alone and hybrid immunity [39] in other studies, suggesting that, even in different populations, there are underlying biological mechanisms that are common with exposure to SARS-CoV-2 that our model can capture.

Another limitation of our data is that serology was based on ancestral antigens of SARS-CoV-2. Throughout the time frame of the COVID PROFILE study, circulating viruses and vaccines changed from the ancestral strain. To account for infection with different viral strains, we removed any observations for individuals who were naturally infected during the study. However, some individuals enrolled in the study continued to receive vaccinations until April 4, 2023, and could have received one of the bivalent vaccines provisionally approved in late 2022 and early 2023 by the Therapeutic Goods Administration of Australia, which contained ancestral and Omicron strains of SARS-CoV-2 [90]. We suggest that the bivalent vaccines would have a similar impact on the boosting and maintenance of IgG antibodies against the ancestral spike and the RBD antigen as the monovalent ancestral vaccines. Firstly, the ancestral spike antigen was included in the Moderna and Pfizer bivalent vaccines, which would stimulate immune recall in individuals previously infected and/or vaccinated with the monovalent vaccine and lead to more antibody production [34, 91, 92]. Second, there is strong evidence in the literature for the presence of immune imprinting for SARS-CoV-2 [71, 93–98], suggesting that individuals primed by the ancestral strain would be back-boosted by exposure to variant antigens. We find no substantial evidence in the literature to suggest a significantly increased or decreased ancestral spike-specific and RBD-specific IgG response resulting from vaccination with the bivalent ancestral and Omicron strain vaccines compared to the antibody response from the first monovalent SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines. This could have important implications for extending the longevity of the IgG response to ancestral antigens of SARS-CoV-2, particularly for individuals primed through natural infection. Continued back-boosting of the IgG response to ancestral antigens through vaccination or infection with different SARS-CoV-2 strains may result in ongoing maintenance of immune memory and antibody secretion.

A notable consideration is that the COVID PROFILE data does not include serology of neutralising antibodies, which are recognised as a proxy correlate of immune protection. Our analysis was focused only on total IgG binding antibodies. Neutralising antibodies have important relevance for protection against SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern, with a high titre of said antibodies being associated with a decreased risk of reinfection and severe disease [69, 99–101]. A strong correlation has been noted between spike-specific binding IgG antibody titres and neutralising antibody levels [35, 102–107], indicating that measuring binding IgG antibodies can be informative in understanding relationships between immune protection and antibody titres. There may also be scenarios to note where a high level of binding IgG antibodies may not correlate with high neutralising antibody levels, such as with immune escape variants. Several studies have identified a low level of neutralising antibodies to new SARS-CoV-2 variants in individuals that also retain high binding IgG titres [108–110]. This has important implications for the interpretation of binding IgG titres and their translation to neutralising antibody titres. Given that our data is specific to the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain, we reason that our work is important when considering protective immunity in the absence of immune escape variants. There is no established protective threshold that can be used with serological testing to define protection against reinfection [101]. However, it is well understood that high titres of neutralising antibodies could result in greater protection. Thus, a high level of binding IgG can also indicate greater protection, excluding scenarios of immune escape variants. This may be demonstrated in our results, where we have shown that a single vaccination is sufficient for SARS-CoV-2 recovered individuals to reach a high IgG antibody titre, and other studies have shown greater protection against variants of concern for those with hybrid immunity [7, 12, 14–16]. As we have also shown that hybrid immunity results in a longer half-life of the IgG response, this may suggest that immune protection also lasts longer for recovered individuals after vaccination. The WHO has recommended a single dose of an mRNA vaccine every 12 months for previously infected individuals to maintain population-level protective immunity, given their high antibody titres and sustained response after vaccination [75], in line with our results from the bi-phasic exponential decay model. Thus, we demonstrate that our results from antibody kinetics modelling on binding IgG antibodies can provide important information on the maintenance of protective immunity, as do studies on neutralising antibodies. This can be a helpful alternative given that assays to measure binding antibodies have more reproducible results, lower costs, and are less complex than neutralisation assays [107].

Additionally, this has important implications for informing public health decisions on modifying vaccine regimes to suit populations with specific immune profiles. This application extends beyond the case study of SARS-CoV-2. We emphasise that modelling of antibody production and decay is of great use to better understand immunity at both an individual and population level, to determine appropriate vaccine scheduling [26, 27, 111, 112], and for identification of clinical and subclinical cases of disease [113] for a range of different pathogens.
