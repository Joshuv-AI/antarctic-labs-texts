18F-Fluciclovine PET enables accurate and safe resection of malignant brain tumors: illustrative cases
Authors: Nozomi Otsuka, Taijun Hana, Chiaki Murakami, Yuki Kawaguchi, Kazunori Todoroki, Morihiro Higashi, Shunya Hanakita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIn malignant brain tumor surgery, maximal safe resection is essential to lower recurrence while minimizing complications. However, accurate preoperative assessment of tumor infiltration remains difficult and an unmet need. 18F-Fluciclovine (18F-FACBC) PET, an innovative molecular imaging technique, has recently emerged as a preoperative tool. The authors present cases highlighting its clinical utility.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report on the cases of 3 brain tumor patients: a 54-year-old female with glioblastoma, a 17-year-old male with recurrent teratoma, and a 24-year-old female with inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor. In all cases, preoperative 18F-FACBC PET was performed, gross-total resection was achieved, and no patient developed permanent neurological deficits. In a representative case, 18F-FACBC identified a tumor-infiltrated region not visualized on contrast-enhanced MRI and indistinguishable from normal brain during surgery; this region was resected and later histopathologically confirmed as tumor tissue. Without PET, it would likely have remained unresected. Surprisingly, molecular analysis suggested that 18F-FACBC accumulated mainly not in tumor cells but in infiltrating immune cells, contributing to the imaging signal.LESSONS18F-FACBC PET can aid in the accurate resection of malignant brain tumors and improve outcomes. Yet, the mechanisms of uptake and visualization remain unclear. Larger cohort studies are needed to confirm the long-term efficacy of this promising imaging tool. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25707

Accurate assessment of tumor infiltration in malignant brain tumors is crucial for both maximal resection and preservation of neurological function. Gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted (Gd-T1W) and FLAIR MRI are commonly used to define the resection area, but some highly malignant regions lack enhancement, posing a risk of incomplete resection if not identified.1,2 Concerns about the limitations of preoperative imaging in delineating tumor infiltration may prompt surgeons to perform more extensive resections. Yet, without precise knowledge of the true extent of infiltration, this can lead to excessive and unnecessary removal of functional brain tissue, increasing the risk of postoperative complications.3

To visualize tumor infiltration more accurately, supplementary preoperative imaging modalities, such as PET with tracers like 11C-methionine and 18F-FDG, as well as cerebral perfusion imaging, have been investigated. However, due to logistical issues such as rapid tracer delivery and concerns regarding diagnostic precision, no universally accepted imaging method has yet been established.4,5 Accurate visualization of high-grade tumor infiltration to ensure both complete and safe resection remains an unmet need in neurosurgical management. 18F-Fluciclovine (18F-FACBC) PET, approved in May 2016 in the United States (Axumin, Blue Earth Diagnostics Ltd.), is mainly used in urology for prostate cancer assessment, but it also applied in neurosurgery for malignant brain tumors. Although evidence is limited to small-scale studies and its clinical use is still in the early stages,6 18F-FACBC PET has been reported to delineate glioma extent more accurately than Gd-T1W MRI, without notable safety concerns regarding the radiotracer.7

We report cases in which 18F-FACBC PET was used for preoperative evaluation of a malignant brain tumor, allowing for improved tumor delineation beyond that of Gd-T1W MRI, which contributed to a higher resection rate and enhanced surgical safety.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

Clinical History and Preoperative Evaluation

A 54-year-old woman presented with a 2- to 3-week history of cognitive impairment. Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a 4-cm cystic mass in the right frontal lobe (Fig. 1A and B). 18F-FACBC PET showed focal uptake adjacent to the supplementary motor area (region X), approximately 1 cm in diameter (Fig. 1C). This region was not clearly delineated on Gd-T1W or FLAIR MRI and was undetectable on other imaging modalities, including contrast-enhanced CT imaging. Surgery

Under general anesthesia, a right frontal craniotomy was performed with neuronavigation. The patient received oral 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) preoperatively, and intraoperative motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring was used throughout. The main tumor region was clearly distinguishable from normal brain by visual inspection and 5-ALA fluorescence. Resection proceeded with real-time navigation and fluorescence guidance (Fig. 1D and E). Region X was indistinguishable from normal brain intraoperatively but was completely resected with guidance from preoperative PET imaging (Fig. 1F and G). Since it was not visualized on preoperative MRI, without 18F-FACBC PET it might have been left unresected. Region Y, defined as the area between the main tumor and region X, showed little tracer uptake on preoperative PET imaging and was submitted separately for pathological examination as an independent tumor specimen (Fig. 1H). Carmustine wafers (Gliadel, Eisai Inc.) were placed in the cavity before closure. Postoperatively, the patient developed transient left hemiparesis from supplementary motor area manipulation, which resolved without permanent deficit.

Postoperative Course and Subsequent Management

Postoperative MRI confirmed complete resection of the tumor, including region X (Fig. 1I). Histopathological examination revealed the diagnosis of glioblastoma (GBM) (Fig. 1J). The tumor’s Ki-67 labeling index (LI) varied depending on the region. The Ki-67 LI exceeded 50% in both the main tumor mass and region X, indicating high malignancy, whereas it was less than 1% in region Y (Fig. 1K– M). These findings suggest a correlation between the degree of 18F-FACBC uptake and the histological malignancy of the tumor in each corresponding region. The patient received the Stupp regimen postoperatively (temozolomide 75 mg/m2 with radiotherapy of 60 Gy in 30 fractions). She was discharged home with a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score of 100. Currently, she is receiving maintenance chemotherapy along with tumor-treating fields (TTFields, Novocure Inc.) on an outpatient basis.

Case 2

Clinical History and Preoperative Evaluation

A 17-year-old male had undergone two craniotomies for resection of a pineal region tumor before the age of 1 year. Although a residual mass remained postoperatively, it was diagnosed as a mature teratoma and the patient was asymptomatic, so he was managed conservatively. The residual tumor demonstrated no significant changes over the subsequent 17 years. However, at the age of 17 years, routine follow-up contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a newly developed enhancing lesion in the right parietal lobe (Fig. 2A). Multiple residual lesions persisted in the pineal region, but none demonstrated contrast enhancement (Fig. 2B and C). The right parietal lesion was suspected to represent an abruptly developed malignant tumor arising after years of quiescence, and resection was promptly planned. However, given the presence of multiple residual lesions that had remained stable for many years, it was essential to noninvasively determine whether these lesions had also undergone progressive changes prior to surgery. For this purpose, preoperative evaluation with 18F-FACBC PET was performed. The imaging confirmed an absence of tracer uptake in all residual lesions except for the right parietal lesion (Fig. 2D and E). This information enabled us to proceed with resection limited to the newly emerged lesion in the right parietal lobe. FIG. 2.Radiological and pathological findings of case 2. Surgery

Under general anesthesia, tumor resection was performed using a neuronavigation system in conjunction with MEP monitoring. The tumor was soft, easily aspirated, and showed no fluorescence with 5-ALA. A very thin layer of tumor tissue adherent to the primary motor cortex carried a high risk of permanent motor deficit if resected. Because this area demonstrated no significant 18F-FACBC uptake, it was intentionally left in place. The remainder of the lesion was completely removed, resulting in a near-total resection exceeding 99.9% of the total tumor volume (Fig. 2F). As preoperative 18F-FACBC PET imaging had suggested that the other residual lesions lacked aggressive biological activity, a highly invasive and extensive resection involving deep brain structures was avoided. Instead, surgical intervention was limited to resection of the newly developed lesion in the right parietal lobe.

Postoperative Course and Subsequent Management

No new postoperative neurological deficits were observed. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of teratoma with somatic malignancy (Fig. 2G). The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 14 with a KPS score of 100. He was subsequently transferred to a specialized neuro-oncology center for adjuvant therapy. During the course of treatment, no enlargement has been observed in the other intracranial tumor lesions apart from the right parietal lesion.

Case 3

A 24-year-old woman presented with sensory disturbances in the left upper and lower extremities. MRI revealed a tumor in the right frontotemporal region (Fig. 3A). Subsequent 18F-FACBC PET demonstrated intense tracer uptake within the lesion (Fig. 3B), suggesting a high-grade malignancy. The lesion underwent gross-total resection and was diagnosed histopathologically as an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor rather than a high-grade malignancy (Fig. 3C–E). The Ki-67 LI was low (2%), indicating limited proliferative activity. Notably, the tumor exhibited marked immune cell infiltration, indicating a pronounced inflammatory response within the lesion. This case highlights an important consideration: intense 18F-FACBC uptake on PET imaging does not necessarily correlate with high malignancy. In this instance, tracer accumulation appeared to reflect an inflammatory microenvironment rather than intrinsic tumor aggressiveness. FIG. 3.Radiological and pathological findings of case 3. Molecularly Informed Analysis of 18F-FACBC PET–Visualized Cells

The experiences from cases 1 through 3 raised an important question regarding 18F-FACBC PET imaging: does intense tracer uptake correlate with the histopathological grade of malignancy within the corresponding region? 18F-FACBC PET is a molecular imaging modality using a radiolabeled synthetic amino acid that is primarily transported into cells via amino acid transporters, most notably ASCT2 and LAT1.8 Accordingly, gliomas with higher expression levels of ASCT2 and LAT1 are expected to demonstrate more extensive 18F-FACBC accumulation on PET imaging. On the hypothetical premise that highly aggressive tumor regions exhibit greater 18F-FACBC uptake, tumors showing widespread accumulation would be presumed to harbor higher overall malignancy and, consequently, poorer prognosis. In this context, tumors with elevated expression of ASCT2 and LAT1—key mediators of 18F-FACBC uptake—might be associated with worse clinical outcomes.

To examine this hypothesis, we performed an exploratory analysis using publicly available gene expression data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) GBM cohort, focusing on the association between ASCT2 and LAT1 expression levels and patient survival outcomes.9 No statistically significant correlation was observed between overall survival and expression of SLC1A5, the gene encoding ASCT2 (Fig. 4A). In contrast, high expression of SLC7A5, which encodes LAT1, was unexpectedly associated with better prognosis compared with the low-expression group (Fig. 4B). In light of this paradoxical result, we further investigated the underlying mechanisms by conducting single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)–based analysis. We utilized a publicly available scRNA-seq resource of GBM to assess which cell types within the tumor microenvironment express SLC1A5 and SLC7A5. 10 Both genes were expressed at relatively low levels in neoplastic tumor cells, whereas strong expression was predominantly observed in immune cells (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that although 18F-FACBC accumulates in malignant brain tumors, it is not necessarily taken up by tumor cells themselves. Rather, the tracer appears to accumulate primarily in nonneoplastic cells, particularly immune cells. Consequently, uptake patterns observed on 18F-FACBC PET may reflect the distribution of infiltrating immune cells rather than the tumor mass itself.

Discussion

Observations

We presented 3 cases in which 18F-FACBC PET appeared to be clinically valuable for the resection of malignant brain tumors. In case 1, the area designated as region X (Fig. 1C and H) would likely have remained unresected without the PET information, which might have led to a poorer clinical outcome. In contrast, in regions with relatively low 18F-FACBC uptake, histopathological evaluation revealed lower tumor malignancy. These findings suggest that the degree of 18F-FACBC accumulation correlates with the histological aggressiveness of tumor tissue. In case 2, PET imaging was effective in distinguishing stable or inactive tumor that did not require surgical intervention. This imaging finding enabled us to appropriately limit resection to the newly developed lesion in the right parietal lobe. In case 3, PET imaging provided information consistent with at least moderate malignancy, which prompted timely resection without delay.

In clinical practice, the greatest utility of this tracer can be considered to be in guiding the extent of resection for malignant brain tumors. For example, when FLAIR hyperintensity extends beyond the contrast-enhancing area on MRI, 18F-FACBC PET can help define surgical margins and, as in case 1, may identify occult regions harboring high-grade disease. Gliomas are therefore the tumors in which this tracer is expected to be most beneficial, although our series suggests potential value in other malignant brain tumors as well. Looking ahead, 18F-FACBC PET may also serve as a noninvasive tool for assessing tumor aggressiveness in glioma cases with ambiguous MRI findings, in which the indication for aggressive resection is uncertain. Nevertheless, such applications will require further validation in larger cohorts and more detailed clinical studies.

Previous studies have demonstrated that, compared with 18F-FDG PET, which shows high levels of physiological uptake in normal brain tissue, 18F-FACBC PET exhibits superior sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of high-grade gliomas.11–13 In addition, 18F-FACBC PET requires substantially less stringent dietary restrictions before scanning compared with 18F-FDG PET, further enhancing its clinical practicality.14 11C-methionine PET is also useful for the preoperative evaluation of gliomas; however, because 11C has a short half-life of approximately 20 minutes compared with the 110-minute half-life of 18F, its use is limited to specialized facilities equipped with an on-site cyclotron.15 In contrast, the longer half-life of 18F-FACBC allows centralized production and delivery to hospitals.16 Taken together, these data indicate that, compared with other PET tracers, 18F-FACBC PET offers distinct advantages and is highly useful for the evaluation of malignant brain tumors.

On the other hand, molecularly informed analysis suggested that although 18F-FACBC accumulates within malignant brain tumor tissue, it may not predominantly localize within neoplastic cells. Rather, the radiotracer appears to accumulate primarily in infiltrating and resident nontumor cells, particularly immune cells. This implies that the regions of high 18F-FACBC uptake visualized on PET may reflect areas of intense immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment, rather than directly representing clusters of highly malignant tumor cells. However, it is important to note that regions infiltrated by high-grade tumor cells are often associated with pronounced inflammatory responses and marked immune cell infiltration.17,18 Therefore, even if the 18F-FACBC PET signal does not directly reflect neoplastic cells, the areas of high radiotracer uptake may nonetheless correspond to high-grade tumor tissue as a result of their inflammatory microenvironment. Case 3 involved a tumor of relatively low histological malignancy, yet exhibited areas of intense 18F-FACBC PET uptake. This observation is consistent with our earlier molecular analyses, suggesting that regions with substantial inflammatory cell infiltration—even in the absence of high-grade neoplastic features—can demonstrate marked 18F-FACBC accumulation.

Lessons

18F-FACBC PET appears to be effective for the preoperative evaluation of malignant brain tumors and can facilitate accurate delineation of tumor-infiltrated regions. However, the underlying mechanisms of tracer accumulation and the specific cellular targets responsible for 18F-FACBC uptake remain incompletely understood. Further validation in larger patient cohorts, as well as detailed biological studies, is warranted.
