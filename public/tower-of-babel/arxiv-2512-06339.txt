The influence of heavy quark potential on quarkonium production in quark-gluon plasma
Taesoo Song, Jiaxing Zhao

Abstract
Remler formalism is the Wigner projection of a two particle state to bound states, which is carried out when the bound state begins to exist or when one of the two particles scatters in medium. This method has been successfully applied to quarkonium production in box simulations and in heavy-ion collisions. In this study this method is extended to strongly bound states with considerable binding energies by taking into account the potential energy between heavy quark pairs in the quark-gluon plasma (QGP). We find that an attractive heavy-quark potential enhances quarkonium production and the results are consistent with those from the statistical model in box simulations, if a proper spatial cutoff is introduced to the potential to mimic quantum effects.

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r^{\prime}e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{r}^{\prime}}\psi\bigg(\vec{r}+\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}}{2}\bigg)\psi^{*}\bigg(\vec{r}-\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}}{2}\bigg),

III
heavy quark pair

The partition function for two-body system in canonical ensemble is given by

\displaystyle Z(T,V,2)=\int\frac{dr_{1}^{3}dp_{1}^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{dr_{2}^{3}dp_{2}^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-\beta H(r_{1},p_{1};r_{2},p_{2})},

(18)

\displaystyle N(T,V,\mu)=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mu_{c}}

\displaystyle\vec{P}

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\int\frac{dp^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-p^{2}/(2\mu T)-V(r)/T}

\displaystyle g_{c}d_{1}d_{2}\bigg(\frac{\mu T}{2\pi}\bigg)^{3/2}e^{-(M+P^{2}/2M)/T}

IV
quarkonium

In order to study the number of a bound state in the Remler formalism, the corresponding Wigner function is needed.
For example, simply assuming the wavefunction from the potential of simple harmonic oscillator, the Wigner function for 1S quarkonium state is given by

\displaystyle W_{1S}=8\frac{D}{d_{1}d_{2}}e^{-r^{2}/\sigma^{2}-p^{2}\sigma^{2}}

where

Inserting Eq. (

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}\frac{dN^{1S}}{d^{3}Rd^{3}P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle De^{-(M+P^{2}/2M)/T}

(28)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int\frac{dp^{3}}{\pi^{3}}e^{-p^{2}/(2\mu T)-p^{2}\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int d^{3}re^{-V(r)/T-r^{2}/\sigma^{2}}.

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}\frac{dN^{1S}}{d^{3}Rd^{3}P}\approx De^{-(M+P^{2}/2M)/T}.

This is why the coalescence model is successful for a loosely bound system such as deutron whose mass is almost same as the sum of two nucleon mass (

However, if the binding energy is not small, that is,

M_{\Phi}
being the mass of bound state, Eq. (

\Phi

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}\frac{dN^{1S}}{d^{3}Rd^{3}P}\approx De^{-(M_{\Phi}+P^{2}/2M)/T}e^{-\varepsilon_{0}/T}.

where
\varepsilon_{0}
is the positive binding energy of quarkonium (

We note that

Additionally,

These two suppression may be compensated by the attractive potential through

In fact, the Wigner function in Eq. (

Now we study the effects of the potential on quarkonium coalescence by considering the heavy quark potential energy from lattice QCD

Since the potential energy does not converge to 0 in the limit of

V
1-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator

The potential for simple harmonic oscillator is
V(x)=(K/2)x^{2}
with
K
being the string tension and the particle distribution at temperature
T
is given by

\displaystyle f(x,p)=A\exp\bigg[-\frac{p^{2}/(2m)+(K/2)x^{2}}{T}\bigg],

(37)

where
A
is the normalization factor such that

\displaystyle 1=\int\frac{dxdp}{2\pi}f(x,p)=AT\sqrt{\frac{m}{K}}.

(38)

That is,

\displaystyle f(x,p)=\sqrt{\frac{K}{mT^{2}}}\exp\bigg[-\frac{p^{2}+mKx^{2}}{2mT}\bigg].

(39)

On the other hand, the Wigner function for 1-dimensional harmonic oscillator is given by

\displaystyle W_{n}(x,p)=\int dx^{\prime}e^{ipx^{\prime}}\psi_{n}\bigg(x+\frac{x^{\prime}}{2}\bigg)\psi_{n}^{*}\bigg(x-\frac{x^{\prime}}{2}\bigg),

(40)

where
\psi_{n}(x)
is the wavefunction with
n
’th excited state:

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x)=\bigg(\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{1/4}\frac{H_{n}(x/\sigma)}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}e^{-x^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}

(41)

with
H_{n}
being the Hermite polynomial and
\sigma^{2}=1/\sqrt{mK}
.
For example, the ground state

\displaystyle W_{0}(x,p)=2e^{-x^{2}/\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{2}p^{2}}.

(42)

Applying the Remler formalism, the probability for the ground state is given by

\displaystyle P_{0}=\int\frac{dxdp}{2\pi}f(x,p)W_{0}(x,p)=\frac{2}{1+2T/\omega},

(43)

where
\omega=\sqrt{K/m}
.
However, Eq. (
43
) is different from the probability in quantum statistics
14
:

\displaystyle P_{0}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\omega/(2T)}=1-e^{-\omega/T},

(44)

where
Z=[2\sinh(\omega/(2T))]^{-1}
is the partition function.

Figure 5:
The probability for the ground state in 1-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator as a function of scaled temperature in quantum statistics and from the Wigner projection

Fig.

shows the probability for the ground state in 1-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator in quantum mechanics and from the Wigner projection.
In the low temperature limit (
T\rightarrow 0

P_{0}=2
).
It is understandable from Eq. (
42

x,p\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow 0

On the other hand,
P_{0}
from the Wigner projection is consistent with that from quantum statistics as temperature increases, and both Eq. (
43

44
) converge to
\omega/T
at high temperature.

In quantum mechanics position and momentum cannot be specified simultaneously due to the uncertainty principle.
For example, the off-diagonal component of the density operator of free gas in volume
V
is given by
14

\displaystyle\langle\vec{r^{\prime}}|\rho|\vec{r}\rangle=\frac{1}{V}\exp\bigg[-\frac{\pi}{\lambda^{2}}(\vec{r^{\prime}}-\vec{r})^{2}\bigg],

(45)

where the thermal wavelength
\lambda=\sqrt{2\pi/mT}
which is infinitely large at
T=0
but negligible at high temperature.
Furthermore, the density distribution of an oscillator in the ground state at
T=0
is not a delta function
\delta(\vec{r})
but is given by
14

\displaystyle\langle\vec{r}|\rho|\vec{r}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}\exp\bigg[-\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg].

(46)

VI
the screened Coulomnbic potential

The binding energy in Coulomnbic potential is given by

\displaystyle E_{n}=-\frac{R}{n^{2}},

(47)

where the Rydberg constant
R=\hbar^{2}/(2\mu a_{0}^{2})
with
\mu
being the reduced mass and
a_{0}
being the Bohr radius (=
\hbar^{2}/\mu e^{2}
).
Considering the degeneracy factor for
n
’ th excited
state is
n^{2}
, the partition function from the bound states

\displaystyle Z_{bound}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}\exp\bigg(\frac{R}{n^{2}T}\bigg)

(48)

and from unbound states

\displaystyle Z_{unbound}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\exp(-E_{k}/T)

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}p\exp\bigg(-\frac{p^{2}}{2\mu T}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\bigg(\frac{\mu T}{2\pi}\bigg)^{3/2},

where
V
is a volume.
Since the wavefunction size is infinitely large for
n\rightarrow\infty
in Eq. (
48
),
V
in Eq. (
49
) diverges
8
.
Therefore, the probability for the ground state vanishes unless
T\rightarrow 0
:

\displaystyle P_{0}=\frac{e^{R/T}}{Z_{bound}+Z_{unbound}}\rightarrow 0,~~~{\rm for}~T\neq 0.

(50)

However, as temperature increases, more and more electrons are set free, forming a QED plasma in which the Coulomb potential is screened
6
:

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{r}\rightarrow-\frac{4\pi\alpha}{r}e^{-m_{D}r},

(51)

with the Debye screening mass
m_{D}=eT/\sqrt{3}
.
Then the number of bound states becomes finite and
V
in Eq. (
49
) is limited due to the screening effect.
In other words, both
Z_{bound}
and
Z_{unbound}
become finite and
P_{0}
will not vanish.
Since there is no analytic solution of wavefunction and eigenenergy for the screened potential, one should rely on numerical calculations.

Now we turn to the Remler formalism. Assuming that only one pair of particles exists in volume
V
, from Eq. (
23
)

\displaystyle P_{0}=\frac{4\pi}{A}\int_{1/\mu}^{r_{D}}drr^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-(p^{2}/2\mu+V(r))/T}W_{0}(r,p),

(52)

where
A
is the normalization factor

\displaystyle A=4\pi\int_{1/\mu}^{r_{D}}drr^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-(p^{2}/2\mu+V(r))/T}

(53)

with
r_{D}=(3V/4\pi)^{1/3}
.
We note that the lower limit of
r
integration (
1/\mu
) is necessary to prevent the divergence at
r=0
.

In the limit of high temperature (
V(r)/T\rightarrow 0
), one can find the normalization factor of Eq. (
53

49

Furthermore, Eq. (
52

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{unbound}}\int d^{3}r\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-p^{2}/(2\mu T)}W_{0}(r,p)

(54)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{unbound}}\int d^{3}r\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}W_{0}(r,p)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{unbound}}.

The last approximation is valid if the expectation value of the bound state radius squared is large enough
\langle r^{2}\rangle\gg 1/\mu T

27

Considering that the binding energy of the ground state,

VII
quantum effects on spatial distribution

As mentioned in Sec.
V
, particle position and momentum cannot simultaneously be specified in quantum mechanics.
Based on the uncertainty principle, once particle position is specified, particle momentum is unknown, and vice versa.
One can include the quantum effects simply by smearing the position and momentum of heavy (anti)quark
17
.
In this study we focus on spatial smearing.
The smearing will be most effective near
r\sim 0
where the potential rapidly changes.
So we simply assume that if the radial distance is smaller than
r_{min}
, the potential energy
is saturated:
V(r)=V(r_{min})
.
In other words, the potential is blurred in space with the resolution of
r_{min}
.
Now we return to Sec.
IV
where quarkonium is formed by the free-energy potential.

VIII
summary

In our previous studies we have developed the Remler formalism for quarkonium production in thermal box, because the number of quarkonium must follow the thermal distribution in the box.
But if the binding energy of quarkonium is large, the Remler formalism or Wigner projection underestimates quarkonium production.

This underestimation can be cured by considering the potential between heavy quark pair, which attracts them to each other.
In this case the distance between heavy quark pair in color singlet decreases and Wigner projection is enhanced.

But we have found that the effect of heavy quark potential is too strong at low temperature, and the similar behavior is observed not only in the quarkonium production but also in the simple harmonic oscillator and the screened Coulomb potential.
The reason for the overestimation is the quantum effects which are more visible at low temperature.
By definition Wigner projection at
T=0
, where
r,p\rightarrow 0
, turns to
2^{N}
with
N
being the spatial dimension.
However, position and momentum cannot be specified simultaneously in quantum mechanics due to the uncertainty principle.

In order to incorporate the quantum effect, the heavy quark potential is simply smeared by introducing the minimum radius which can physically be interpreted as the spatial resolution.
We have found that
J/\psi
needs larger smearing and
\Upsilon(1S)
less smearing, and the smearing lengths are expressed in an unified form which is proportional to the thermal wavelength of heavy quark in QGP.
