Introduction

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Whenever a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be mentioned below, it will be assumed tacitly that it is also a convex body, namely, it is compact and has nonempty interior. The isoperimetric quotient
1
1

1

[

\mathrm{iq}(K)=\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(\partial K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}},

By the classical isoperimetric theorem, the isoperimetric quotient of any convex body

\mathrm{iq}(K)\geqslant\mathrm{iq}(B_{\ell^{n}_{\!\!2}})=\frac{n\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}}\asymp\sqrt{n}.

(2)

\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

B^{n}=B_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}

The following theorem shows how we may improve the asymptotic estimate

Theorem 1
.

Fix
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant n+1
. Every convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upphi

\mathrm{iq}(K)\gtrsim\max\Bigg\{\frac{n}{\sqrt{1+\log\frac{\upphi}{n}}},\sqrt{n}\Bigg\}.

(3)

Furthermore, there exists a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upphi
facets whose isoperimetric quotient is at most a universal constant multiple of the right hand side of (
3
).

By considering Cartesian products of cross-polytopes, it is not hard to convince oneself that for many values of
n
and
\upphi
the lower bound on
\mathrm{iq}(K)
in (
3

K
. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the cross-polytope
B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{m}}=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|x_{1}|+\ldots+|x_{m}|\leqslant 1\}
satisfies
\mathrm{iq}(B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{m}})\asymp\sqrt{m}
. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-fold Cartesian product
(B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{m}})^{k}
is an
n
-dimensional convex polytope for
n=km
that has
\upphi=k2^{m}
facets and whose isoperimetric quotient equals
k\mathrm{iq}(B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{m}})\asymp k\sqrt{m}=n/\sqrt{m}\asymp n/\sqrt{1+\log(\upphi/n)}
.

So, the main content of Theorem
1
is to demonstrate that the above explicit and elementary computation for Cartesian products of cross-polytopes produces the worst-possible behavior (up to universal constant factors) among all
n
-dimensional convex polytope that have
\upphi

3

2
below.

Remark 2
.

One could also wonder about improving the classical estimate
\mathrm{iq}(K)\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
in (
2
) when
K
is restricted to be a convex polytope that has
\upbeta
vertices for some integer
\upbeta\geqslant n+1
. If
\upbeta\geqslant 2n
, then this is impossible since
B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{n}}
has
2n

\mathrm{iq}(B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{n}})\asymp\sqrt{n}
, so by considering the convex hull of
B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{n}}
with additional
\upbeta-2n

\mathbb{R}^{n}\smallsetminus B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{n}}
that are in general position and arbitrarily close to
\partial B_{\ell_{\!\!1}^{n}}
, one sees that there is a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upbeta
vertices and whose isoperimetric quotient is of order
\sqrt{n}
. If
\upphi=n+1
, then a stronger isoperimetric lower bound holds since a convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
n+1

\mathrm{iq}(K)\geqslant\mathrm{iq}(\triangle_{n})\asymp n
by
[

[

\triangle_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the regular simplex. It remains open to determine how to interpolate between the aforementioned asymptotic lower bounds on
\mathrm{iq}(K)
when
K
is a convex polytope that has
\upbeta
vertices and
n+1<\upbeta<2n
.

More generally, one could ask how the estimate
\mathrm{iq}(K)\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
in (

K
is a convex polytope that has a fixed number of faces of a given intermediate dimension, or even while fixing its numbers of faces whose dimensions belong to given subset of
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}

The reverse isoperimetric theorem
[
5
]
states that every convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has an affine image whose isoperimetric quotient is at most the isoperimetric quotient of the regular simplex
\triangle_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus:

\partial_{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min_{\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\leqslant\mathrm{iq}(\triangle_{n})\asymp n,

(4)

where
\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
denotes (as usual) the group of linear transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose determinant is
1
, and we adopt in (
4
) the notation that was introduced in
[
20
]
for the affinely invariant quantity
\partial_{K}
.

For every
n,\upbeta\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upbeta\geqslant n+1
there is a convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upbeta
vertices which satisfies
\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\gtrsim n
for every
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Similarly, for every
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant n+1
there is a convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upphi
facets which satisfies
\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\gtrsim n
for every
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Both of these statements follow from a compactness argument by considering suitable perturbations of the regular simplex
\triangle_{n}
, which satisfies
\partial_{\triangle_{n}}\asymp n
; the details appear in Section
3
below.

It is thus impossible to obtain an improved reverse isoperimetric theorem in the spirit of Theorem
1
for convex polytopes which either have a given number of vertices or a given number of facets. Nevertheless, a markedly different reverse isoperimetric phenomenon holds if one considers origin-symmetric convex polytopes
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(or translates thereof), namely, those for which
-K=K
.

By
[
5
]
, every origin-symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\partial_{K}\leqslant\partial_{[0,1]^{n}}=2n
, improving (
4

\partial_{\triangle_{n}}=(e-o(1))n
. This might lead one to expect that the difference between the general case and the origin-symmetric case remains of this lower-order nature even when one restricts to polytopes that have either a given number of vertices or a given number of facets. This indeed holds for polytopes with restricted number of facets, i.e., for every
\upphi\in 2\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant 2n
there is an origin-symmetric convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upphi
facets yet
\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\gtrsim n
for every
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, as seen by considering perturbations of the hypercube
[-1,1]^{n}
(details are provided in Section
3
). However, for origin-symmetric convex polytopes with a given number of vertices, we have the following sharp asymptotic improvement over the upper bound
\partial_{K}\lesssim n
in (
4

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
n\in\mathbb{N}
and that
\upbeta\in 2\mathbb{N}
satisfies
\upbeta\geqslant 2n
. Then, for every origin-symmetric convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upbeta
vertices there exists
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that:

\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\lesssim\min\bigg\{{\textstyle\sqrt{n\log\frac{\upbeta}{n}}},n\bigg\}.

(5)

Furthermore, there exists an origin-symmetric convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upbeta
vertices such that
\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)
is at least a universal constant multiple of the right hand side of (
5
) for every
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

As for Theorem
1
, our proof of (
5
) is a quick concatenation of (substantial) results that are available in the literature; its details appear in Section
3
below.

A conjectural
[
33
]
reverse isoperimetric phenomenon asserts that if in addition to judiciously choosing an affine image one is permitted to pass to a certain
O(1)
-perturbation of a given convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it is always possible to arrive at a convex body whose isoperimetric quotient is
O(\sqrt{n})
, i.e., after both a prudent choice of basis and a bounded correction,
every

n
-dimensional convex body behaves (in terms of isoperimetry) up to positive universal constant factors like the Euclidean ball. A precise statement is:

Conjecture 4

(weak isomorphic reverse isoperimetry)
.

For every origin-symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exist
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and an origin-symmetric convex body
L\subseteq\mathsf{A}K
that satisfies:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(L)^{\frac{1}{n}}\gtrsim\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{iq}(L)\lesssim\sqrt{n}.

The term “weak” is used in
[
33
]
to name Conjecture
4
because
[
33
]
also formulates a stronger conjectural phenomenon; we do not need to recall that stronger conjecture herein since the present article does not address it, and furthermore the above weaker version suffices for certain applications in nonlinear functional analysis and theoretical computer science; see
[
33
]
for details.

The partial results towards Conjecture
4
that are currently known can be found in
[
33
,
25
]
. Here, we will prove the following statement:

Theorem 5
.

Fix
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant n+1
. For every convex polytope that has
\upphi
facets there are a vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and an origin-symmetric convex body
L\subseteq z+\mathsf{A}K
that satisfies:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(L)^{\frac{1}{n}}\gtrsim\frac{n}{\upphi}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{iq}(L)\lesssim\frac{\upphi}{\sqrt{n}}.

(6)

Theorem
5
shows that Conjecture
4
holds when
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex polytope that has
\upphi=O(n)
facets. Indeed, if
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an origin-symmetric convex body, then the body
L
that Theorem
5
provides is, in fact, contained in
\mathsf{A}K
(not only in its translate
z+\mathsf{A}K
) by the following straightforward argument:

L=\frac{1}{2}(L+L)=\frac{1}{2}(L-L)\subseteq\frac{1}{2}\big((z+\mathsf{A}K)-(z+\mathsf{A}K)\big)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{A}K-\mathsf{A}K)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{A}K+\mathsf{A}K)=\mathsf{A}K,

where we used the convexity of
L
and
\mathsf{A}K
in, respectively, the first and last steps, and we used the fact that
L
and
\mathsf{A}K
are origin-symmetric in, respectively, the second and penultimate steps. While this is modest evidence for Conjecture
4
, it was previously unknown, and its justification is not entirely trivial. The proof of Theorem
5
appears in Section
4
below. It proceeds through a reformulation of Conjecture
4
from
[
33
, Section 1.6.1]
in terms of the spectrum of the Laplacian on
K
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, and it yields a spectral bound that improves asymptotically over the best-known bound
[
33
]
if
\upphi=o(n\log n)
.

1.1.
Historical comments

It is quite curious that the statement of Theorem
1
has not been previously obtained in the literature. One possible explanation is that while such questions have been studied for a very long time, this was done with the aim to determine the exact isoperimetric minimizers (constrained to have a fixed number of facets), which turns out to be an extremely difficult (perhaps even hopeless) goal. Aiming to understand the phenomenon up to universal constant factors opens the door to a powerful toolkit that has been developed over the past decades in the local theory of Banach spaces; modulo such known results, our proof of Theorem
1
is short. In one of our motivations for examining the question that Theorem
1
answers (see below), universal constant factors do not matter.

The extremal property of balls with respect to the isoperimetric problem was known to the ancient Greeks; for example, Zenodorus suggested an argument first proving that regular polygons are optimal in the plane, and even claimed that Euclidean balls are optimal in three dimensions; see
[
9
]
for the history.
In higher dimensions, the isoperimetric inequality for convex bodies was proved by the works of Steiner, Schwarz, Weierstrass and Minkowski (the history is covered in
[
24
]
. Briefly, Steiner famously provided a symmetrization method showing that given the volume, only Euclidean balls can be the minimizers of the surface area. However, Steiner did not prove the existence of a minimizer, which was subsequently verified by Weierstrass and Schwarz). Concerning convex polytopes, Zenodorus already suggested that among planar convex polygons that have a given number of sides, the regular ones have the minimal perimeter, but this was only proved rigorously by Weierstrass; see
[
43
]
. In higher dimensions,
Steiner using his symmetrization method also proved that among simplices of given volume, the regular one has minimal surface area; see
[
26
]
(this also follows from
[
36
]
). The literature contains very few other types of convex polytopes for which the problem has been understood. In
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
it was proved
that for
\upphi=6,12
, among
3
-dimensional convex polytopes of given volume and having at most
\upphi
facets, the ones with minimal surface area are the cube and dodecahedron, respectively. For convex polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that have
n+2
vertices, the ones which minimize the isoperimetric quotient were determined in
[
10
]
. For origin-symmetric polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that have
2n
facets (parallelepipeds),
[
36
]
implies that hypercubes have minimal isoperimetric quotients. To the best of our knowledge, no other convex polytopes are known to be exact minimizers of the isoperimetric quotient while fixing their number of facets.

We were partially motivated to examine the question that Theorem
1
answers by the recent work
[
34
]
, which constructs for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
a convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that its integer translates
\{z+K\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
tile
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, yet
\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(\partial K)=n^{1/2+o(1)}
as
n\to\infty
; any measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\{z+E\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
tile
\mathbb{R}^{n}
must satisfy
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)=1
, so the aforementioned statement from
[
34
]
is equivalent to
\mathrm{iq}(\partial K)=n^{1/2+o(1)}
. Beyond their intrinsic geometric interest, the search for such tiling bodies
is motivated by issues in computer science
[
15
,
29
,
30
]
. The potential utility in this regard of the construction of
[
34
]
would necessitate the tiling body
K
to have an “efficient” description, e.g. a constant-factor polynomial time optimization oracle (see
[
23
]
for background) would be of use here (but, that is not all that would be needed for possible algorithmic implications). By Theorem
1
, the number of facets of
K
must grow as
n\to\infty
faster than any power of
n
, i.e.,
K
cannot have a short description as the intersection of
n^{O(1)}
half-spaces. This rules out one possible approach to the aforementioned question, and it remains open to understand whether other routes towards obtaining an efficient version of a tiling body as in
[
34
]
are possible.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

In the context of Theorem
1
, we introduce the following notation for every
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant n+1
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\upphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{\mathrm{iq}(K):\ K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \mathrm{is\ a\ convex\ polytope\ that\ has\ }\upphi\ \mathrm{facets}\big\}.

(7)

As discussed in Section
1.1
,
\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\upphi)
has been computed for only a few values of
n,\upphi
. The purpose of the present section is to prove Theorem
1
, which evaluates
\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\upphi)
up to universal constant factors. Writing
\upphi=\varrho n
, using the notation (
7
) Theorem
1
can be stated as follows:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\varrho n)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}n&\mathrm{if}\ 1+\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\varrho\leqslant 2,\\
\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log\varrho}}&\mathrm{if}\ 2\leqslant\varrho\leqslant 2^{n},\\
\sqrt{n}&\mathrm{if}\ \varrho\geqslant 2^{n}.\end{array}\right.

Remark
6
below records for ease of later references a standard observation that allows one to pass from isoperimetric statements about convex polytopes with at most a given number of facets or vertices to the corresponding statements for convex polytopes whose number of facets or vertices exactly equals a given larger value; it implies in particular that
\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\upphi)\geqslant\mathsf{Isoperim}_{n}(\Phi)
for
n,\upphi,\Phi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\Phi\geqslant\upphi\geqslant n+1
.

Remark 6
.

We will use the following very simple fact multiple times. Given
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
, if
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex polytope with
\upphi
facets (so, necessarily
\upphi\geqslant n+1
), then for every integer
\Phi\geqslant\upphi
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a convex polytope
L\subseteq K
that has
\Phi
facets and
\mathrm{iq}(K)-\varepsilon\leqslant\mathrm{iq}(L)\leqslant\mathrm{iq}(K)+\varepsilon
. One of multiple possible ways to justify this is to fix any vertex
v
of
K
, by Hahn–Banach take
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\langle x^{*},v\rangle=\alpha
and
K\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \langle x^{*},y\rangle\leqslant\alpha\}
, and for every
\delta>0
slice away from
K
a small neighborhood of
v
by considering the polytope
L(1,\delta)=K\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \langle x,v\rangle\leqslant\alpha-\delta\}
. Then,
\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{iq}(L(1,\delta))=\mathrm{iq}(K)
and for small enough
\delta
the number of facets of
K
equals
\upphi+1
. By iterating this procedure
\Phi-\upphi
times we arrive at the desired polytope
L
. When
K
is furthermore origin-symmetric,
L
can be taken to be origin-symmetric by repeating the above with the approximant
K\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ |\langle x,v\rangle|\leqslant\alpha-\delta\}
. Also, if
K
has
\upbeta\in\mathbb{N}
vertices, then for every integer
\mathrm{B}\geqslant\upbeta
and every
\varepsilon>0
there is a convex polytope
L^{\prime}\supseteq K
that has
\mathrm{B}
vertices and
\mathrm{iq}(K)-\varepsilon\leqslant\mathrm{iq}(L^{\prime})\leqslant\mathrm{iq}(K)+\varepsilon
, as seen by taking any
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\mathrm{B}-\upbeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\smallsetminus K
that are in general position and sufficiently close to
K
, and considering
L^{\prime}=\mathrm{conv}(K\cup\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{\mathrm{B}-\upbeta}\})
. If
K
is origin-symmetric and
\mathrm{B}
is even, then
L^{\prime}
can be taken to be origin-symmetric, as seen by considering
\mathrm{conv}(K\cup\{\pm u_{1},\ldots,\pm u_{\mathrm{B}-\upbeta}\})
.

The following simple lemma records basic properties of the isoperimetric quotient:

Lemma 7
.

Fix
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
and
h>0
. Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex polytope whose facets are
F_{1},\ldots,F_{\upphi}
. Suppose that
K\supseteq hB^{n}
and
F_{1}\cap hS^{n-1},\ldots,F_{\upphi}\cap hS^{n-1}\neq\varnothing
. Then

\mathrm{iq}(K)=\frac{n}{h}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

(8)

Furthermore, every convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
K\supseteq hB^{n}
satisfies:

\mathrm{iq}(K)\leqslant\frac{n}{h}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

(9)

Proof.

The identity (
8
) is a consequence of the following obvious computation. By assumption, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,\upphi\}
there is
u_{i}\in hS^{n-1}
such that
F_{i}\cap hS^{n-1}=\{u_{i}\}
. As
K\supseteq hB^{n}
, we necessarily have
F_{i}\subseteq u_{i}+u_{i}^{\perp}
. Hence,
\mathrm{conv}(\{0\}\cup F_{i})
is a cone whose cusp is at the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and whose height equals
\|u_{i}\|_{\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}=h
. The volume of
\mathrm{conv}(\{0\}\cup F_{i})
therefore equals
h\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(F_{i})/n
. As
\mathrm{conv}(\{0\}\cup F_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{conv}(\{0\}\cup F_{\upphi})
have pairwise disjoint interiors and their union equals
K
, and also any two of the facets
F_{1},\ldots,F_{\upphi}
intersect in a set of codimension at least
2
and their union equals
\partial K
, the desired identity (
8
) is justified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)=\mathrm{vol}_{n}\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\upphi}

i\in\{1,\ldots,a\}
and
r,s\in\{1,\ldots,b_{i}\}
the
(r,s)
-entry of the covariance matrix of
\sigma_{K}
equals the following quantity:

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{a}-1}(\partial K)h^{2}}{\upphi_{1}\cdots\upphi_{a}h_{i}^{2}}\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{a})\in\{1,\ldots,\upphi_{1}\}\times\ldots\times\{1,\ldots,\upphi_{a}\}}u_{ij_{i}r}u_{ij_{i}s}\\
=\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{a}-1}(\partial K)h^{2}}{\upphi_{i}h_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\upphi_{i}}u_{ijr}u_{ijs}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bi isotropic}}}{{=}}h^{2}\mathrm{vol}_{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{a}-1}(\partial K)\frac{1}{b_{i}h_{i}^{2}}\delta_{rs}.

Consequently, if in addition to the above assumptions
h_{i}=1/\sqrt{b_{i}}
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,a\}
, then the covariance matrix of
\sigma_{K}
is a multiple of the identity matrix on
\mathbb{R}^{b_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{R}^{b_{a}}
, so by
[
36
]
we have
\mathrm{iq}(K)=\partial_{K}
, i.e., the first equality in (
27
) holds. The second equality follows by substituting (
25
) and (
26
) into the definition of
\mathrm{iq}(K)
while using the assumption that
h_{i}=1/\sqrt{b_{i}}
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,a\}
.
The final asymptotic equivalence in (
27
) is a straightforward consequence of Stirling’s formula.
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem

, it remains to show that (

) holds for every convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upbeta
vertices and some
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., our goal is to demonstrate that
\partial_{K}\lesssim\max\{\sqrt{n\log(\upbeta/n)},n\}
.

Proof of (

).

The following volumetric estimate was proved in
[
8
]
for every
n,\upbeta\in\mathbb{N}
:

\forall v_{1},\ldots,v_{\upbeta}\in B^{n},\qquad\mathrm{vol}_{n}\big(\mathrm{conv}(\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\upbeta}\})\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}{\textstyle\sqrt{1+\log\frac{\upbeta}{n}}}.

(32)

By John’s theorem
[
27
]
every origin-symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
r>0
such that
rB^{n}\subseteq\mathsf{A}K\subseteq\sqrt{n}rB^{n}
. If
K
is furthermore a convex polytope with
\upbeta
vertices for
\upbeta\geqslant 2n
, then by (

) we have
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K)^{1/n}\lesssim r\sqrt{\log(\upbeta/n)}/\sqrt{n}
. At the same time,
\mathrm{iq}(\mathsf{A}K)\leqslant n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K)^{1/n}/r
by the second part (
9
) of Lemma

. When
\upbeta<2^{n}
, the desired estimate (

) follows by concatenating these two bounds. For
\upphi\geqslant 2^{n}
, the desired estimate (

) is a special case of the reverse isoperimetric theorem
[
5
]
.
∎

4.
proof of Theorem
5

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\lambda(K)
be the smallest
\lambda>0
such that there is
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that vanishes on
\partial K
and is smooth and satisfies
-\Delta\varphi=\lambda\varphi
on the interior of
K
, where
\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{2}/(\partial x_{i}^{2})
is the standard Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The (classical and rudimentary) background on spectral properties of the Dirichlet Laplacian that is relevant to the ensuing reasoning can be found in
[
37
,
14
]
; in particular, it has discrete pure point spectrum on the compact domain
K
, so the definition of
\lambda(K)
makes sense, and:

\forall s\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{0\},\qquad\lambda(sK)=\frac{\lambda(K)}{s^{2}}.

(33)

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 13
.

Fix
n,\upphi\in\mathbb{N}
with
\upphi\geqslant n+1
. Suppose that
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex polytope that has
\upphi
facets. Then, there exists a positive definite invertible matrix
\mathsf{B}\in\mathsf{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{B}K)^{\frac{1}{n}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\upphi}{n}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\lambda(\mathsf{B}K)\lesssim\upphi.

(34)

Prior to proving Theorem
13
, we will next assume its validity and explain how it implies Theorem
5
:

Deduction of Theorem
5
from Theorem
13
.

Note first that the following quick consequence of Fubini holds for every compact subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)>0
:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{vol}_{n}\big(E\cap(x-E)\big)\geqslant\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)^{2}}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E+E)}.

(35)

Indeed,
E\cap(x-E)\neq\varnothing
if and only if
x\in E+E
, whence:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E+E)\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{vol}_{n}\big(E\cap(x-E)\big)\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{vol}_{n}\big(E\cap(x-E)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{E}(y)\mathbf{1}_{E}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)^{2}.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body. Then,
\sqrt[n]{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K\cap(x-K))}\geqslant\sqrt[n]{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}/2
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by an application of (
35
) with
E=K
and the fact that the convexity of
K
can be restated as
K+K=2K
. Denote:

K^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{2}x+K\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad K^{\prime\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}K^{\prime}\cap(-K^{\prime}).

(36)

Then
K^{\prime\prime}
is an origin-symmetric convex body that satisfies:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K^{\prime\prime})^{\frac{1}{n}}\gtrsim\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

(37)

If furthermore
K
is a convex polytope that has
\upphi
facets (per the setting of Theorem
5
), then
K^{\prime\prime}
is an origin-symmetric convex polytope that has at most
2\upphi
facets.

Apply Theorem
13
to
K^{\prime\prime}
, thus obtaining a positive definite matrix
\mathsf{B}\in\mathsf{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfies:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{B}K^{\prime\prime})^{\frac{1}{n}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\upphi}{n}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\lambda(\mathsf{B}K^{\prime\prime})\lesssim\upphi.

(38)

As
\mathsf{B}
is positive definite and invertible, we may consider the matrix
\mathsf{A}=(\mathrm{det}\mathsf{B})^{-1/n}\mathsf{B}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Now:

\lambda(\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})^{\frac{2}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:hpomogeneity of lambda}}}{{=}}\lambda(\mathsf{B}K^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{B}K^{\prime\prime})^{\frac{2}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:spectal two parts''}}}{{\lesssim}}\frac{\upphi^{2}}{n}.

(39)

Following
[
33
]
, let
\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
denote the Cheeger body of
\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
, namely, it is the unique measurable subset of
\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
that satisfies
\mathrm{Per}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})/\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})\leqslant\mathrm{Per}(E)/\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)
for every measurable
E\subseteq\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
with
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(E)>0
, where
\mathrm{Per}(\cdot)
denotes perimeter in the sense of Caccioppoli and de Giorgi (a thorough treatment of this notion of perimeter can be found in e.g.
[
2
]
). By
[
1
]
, such a minimizer exists and it is indeed unique, and furthermore it is a convex subset of
\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
. The aforementioned (substantial) theorem of
[
1
]
that this minimizer is unique implies in particular that since
\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
is origin-symmetric, its Cheeger body
\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
is also origin-symmetric. By substituting (
39
) into equation (1.62) of
[
33
]
, we see that:

\frac{\mathrm{iq}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\lesssim\frac{\upphi}{n}.

(40)

By the isoperimetric theorem (
2
) applied to
\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
and the inclusion
\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{iq}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\sqrt{n}}&\left(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\gtrsim\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{iq}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\sqrt{n}},\left(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}\\
&=\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{iq}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\sqrt{n}},\left(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K^{\prime\prime})}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:vol K''}}}{{\gtrsim}}\max\left\{\frac{\mathrm{iq}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}{\sqrt{n}},\left(\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\right\},\end{split}

(41)

where the second step of (
41
) holds because
\mathsf{A}
is volume-preserving.

Finally, if we choose
z=-(1/2)\mathsf{A}x
and
L=\mathrm{Ch}\mathsf{A}K^{\prime\prime}
, then
L
is an origin-symmetric convex body, thanks to the definitions (
36
) we know that
L
is a subset of
z+(\mathsf{A}K)\cap(\mathsf{A}x-\mathsf{A}K)\subseteq z+\mathsf{A}K
, and by combining (
40
) with (
41
) we conclude that the desired requirements (
6
) of Theorem
5
indeed hold.
∎

Remark 14
.

The above proof of Theorem
5
demonstrates that for every convex polytope
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\upphi
facets there exists
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\lambda(\mathsf{A}K)\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{2/n}\lesssim\upphi^{2}/n
. The previously best-known bound here
[
33
, page 51]
(for any convex body
K
) is
\lambda(\mathsf{A}K)\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{2/n}\lesssim n(\log n)^{2}
. Thus, we obtain an asymptotic improvement whenever
\upphi=o(n\log n)
and not only when
\upphi=O(n)
.

Proof of Theorem
13
.

Because
K
is origin-symmetric and bounded, its number of facets
\upphi\geqslant 2n
is even, so we may write
\upphi=2m
for some integer
m\geqslant n
. Thus, we can fix
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\smallsetminus\{0\}
such that

K=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}|\langle x,y_{i}\rangle|\leqslant 1\big\}.

(42)

As
K
has nonempty interior,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so if we let
\mathsf{Y}\in\mathsf{M}_{n\times m}(\mathbb{R})
be the
n
-by-
m
matrix whose columns are
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
, then the rank of
\mathsf{Y}
equals
n
. Consequently,
n
-by-
n
matrix
\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{Y}^{*}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is positive semidefinite and invertible, so we can define
\mathsf{C}=(\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{Y}^{*})^{-1/2}\mathsf{Y}\in\mathsf{M}_{n\times m}(\mathbb{R})
. It is straightforward to verify that
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C^{*}}
is the
n
-by-
n
identity matrix
\mathsf{I}_{n}
. In other words, the rows
\uprho_{1},\ldots,\uprho_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
\mathsf{C}
are orthonormal, so there are
\uprho_{n+1},\ldots,\uprho_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\uprho_{1},\ldots,\uprho_{m}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
\mathsf{V}\in\mathsf{O}_{m}
denote the orthogonal matrix whose rows are
\uprho_{1},\ldots,\uprho_{m}
. Let
\upgamma_{1},\ldots,\upgamma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the columns of
\mathsf{V}
, which are also an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Letting
\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the restriction operator from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
for every
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, observe that:

\mathsf{B}K=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}|\langle x,\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\upgamma_{i}\rangle|\leqslant 1\big\}\qquad\mathrm{where}\qquad\mathsf{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{Y}^{*}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(43)

Indeed,
\mathsf{B}K=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}|\langle z,\mathsf{B}^{-1}y_{i}\rangle|\leqslant 1\}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}|\langle z,\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{B}^{-1}y_{i}\rangle|\leqslant 1\}
by (
42
), and
\mathsf{B}^{-1}y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the
i
’th column of
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{B}^{-1}\mathsf{Y}
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, which equals by definition
\upgamma_{i}
.

As
\mathsf{B}K
has
2m
facets, it follows from (
43
) that
\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\upgamma_{i}\neq 0
. We can therefore denote

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,m\},\qquad c_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\|\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\upgamma_{i}\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad u_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\upgamma_{i}\in S^{n-1}.

(44)

Using this notation, we can rewrite (
43
) as follows:

\mathsf{B}K=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}c_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle^{2}\leqslant 1\big\}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\leqslant\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\bigg\}.

(45)

Furthermore, because
\upgamma_{1},\ldots,\upgamma_{m}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def ciui}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x,\upgamma_{i}\rangle^{2}=\|\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{m}}^{2}=\|x\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}^{2}.

In other words, the following matrix identity holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}u_{i}\otimes u_{i}=\mathsf{I}_{n}.

(46)

Thanks to (
46
), according the geometric form of the
Brascamp–Lieb inequality
[
11
]
that was first formulated in
[
3
]

(see also e.g. Theorem 2 in
[
7
]
), if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
are measurable, then:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(\langle x,u_{i}\rangle)^{c_{i}}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{c_{i}}.

(47)

Consequently,

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{B}K)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:write AK using ui}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\big[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\big]}(\langle x,u_{i}\rangle)^{c_{i}}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:quote BL}}}{{\leqslant}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\right)^{c_{i}}.

(48)

By taking the trace of (
46
) we get
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=n
. The maximum of the right hand side of (
48
) over all possible
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}>0
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=n
is attained when
c_{1}=\ldots=c_{m}=n/m
. Hence (
48
) implies that:

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathsf{B}K)^{\frac{1}{n}}\leqslant 2\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\upphi}{n}},

thus proving the first part of (
34
).

To prove the second part of (
34
), thus concluding the proof of Theorem
13
, define
\varphi:K\to\mathbb{R}
by setting:

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-c_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle^{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def ciui}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\langle x,\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\upgamma_{i}\rangle^{2}\right).

(49)

Then,
\varphi
is smooth (it is a polynomial), and thanks to (
45
) it vanishes on the boundary of
K
. The standard (see e.g.
[
14
]
) Rayleigh quotient
characterization of the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian
-\Delta
on
K
with Dirichlet boundary conditions therefore gives:

\lambda(\mathsf{B}K)\leqslant\frac{\int_{\mathsf{B}K}\|\nabla\varphi(x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mathsf{B}K}\varphi(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}.

(50)

To treat the numerator in (
50
), define
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting:

\forall z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\Phi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-z_{i}^{2}\big).

(51)

Recalling that
\upgamma_{1},\ldots,\upgamma_{m}
are the columns of the orthogonal matrix
\mathsf{V}\in\mathsf{O}_{m}
, i.e.,
\upgamma_{i}=\mathsf{V}e_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
is the standard coordinate basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have:

\varphi(x)=\Phi\big(\langle x,\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}e_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle x,\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}e_{m}\rangle\big)=\Phi\big(\langle\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x,e_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x,e_{m}\rangle\big)=\Phi(\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x).

Hence,
\nabla\varphi(x)=\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}\nabla\Phi(\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}x)=\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}\nabla\Phi(\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x)
by the chain rule. Consequently,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\!\!\!\|\nabla\varphi(x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}^{2}=\|\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}\mathsf{V}\nabla\Phi(\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}^{2}\leqslant\|\mathsf{V}\nabla\Phi(\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{m}}^{2}=\|\nabla\Phi(\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{m}}^{2}\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def Phi}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}4\langle\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x,e_{i}\rangle^{2}\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\smallsetminus\{i\}}\big(1-\langle\mathsf{V}^{*}\mathsf{R}_{m\to n}^{*}x,e_{j}\rangle^{2}\big)^{2}=4\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle^{2}\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\smallsetminus\{i\}}\big(1-c_{j}\langle x,u_{j}\rangle^{2}\big)^{2}.\end{split}

(52)

Consequently, if for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
we define

\forall t\in\Big[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\Big],\qquad g_{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{(tu_{i}+u_{i}^{\bot})\cap\mathsf{B}K}\bigg(\prod_{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\smallsetminus\{i\}}\big(1-c_{j}\langle x,u_{j}\rangle^{2}\big)^{2}\bigg)dx,

(53)

then by Fubini we have

\int_{\mathsf{B}K}\|\nabla\varphi(x)\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:nabla bound}\wedge\eqref{eq:def gi}\wedge\eqref{eq:write AK using ui}}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}4c_{i}\int_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}}t^{2}g_{i}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(54)

To estimate the right hand side of (
54
), observe that for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
the function

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad h_{i}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sum_{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\smallsetminus\{i\}}\log\big(1-c_{j}\langle x,u_{j}\rangle^{2}\big)&\mathrm{if\ }x\in\mathsf{B}K,\\
0&\mathrm{if\ }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\smallsetminus\mathsf{B}K,\end{array}\right.

is well-defined by (
45
), and concave on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
since
\mathsf{B}K
is convex and
(t\in[-1,1])\mapsto\log(1-t^{2})
is concave (its second derivative equals
-2(1+t^{2})/(1-t^{2})^{2}
). The function
g_{i}
that is defined in (
53
) is thus the marginal of the log-concave function
e^{h_{i}}
in the direction of
u_{i}
, whence
g_{i}
is log-concave on
[-1/\sqrt{c_{i}},1/\sqrt{c_{i}}]
by
[
38
]
. Also,
g_{i}
is nonnegative and even, because
\mathsf{B}K
is origin-symmetric, so it is nonincreasing on
[0,1/\sqrt{c_{i}}]
(if
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
, then write
s=\lambda t+(1-\lambda)(-t)
for
\lambda=(s+t)/(2t)\in[0,1]
and estimate
g(s)\geqslant g(t)^{\lambda}g(-t)^{1-\lambda}=g(t)
by log-concavity). For fixed
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we can therefore bound the
i
’th summand in (
54
