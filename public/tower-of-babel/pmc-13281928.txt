The long journey of a retained guidewire, from vein to aorta leading to cardiac tamponade: a case report
Authors: Hazal Ünlügenç, Bekir Serhat Yıldız, Emre Demir, Osman Nuri Tuncer, Oğuz Yavuzgil, Jamal Nasir Khan, Georgios Benetos, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplete loss of a guidewire during central venous catheterization is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication and is considered a preventable ‘never event.’ Retained guidewires mostly recognized during or shortly after the procedure, some may be stay in vascular system for a long time without detection and are associated with wide range of complications including vascular and cardiac chamber perforation. The decision to remove the guidewire through surgical or percutaneous technique should follow an individualized approach based on its location, associated complications, and the patient's clinical condition.Case summaryA 61-year-old male presented with sharp chest and epigastric pain, initially raising suspicion of acute coronary syndrome. Chest X-ray and initial echocardiogram were unremarkable. Electrocardiogram findings and serial troponin measurements were inconclusive. Abdominal ultrasound showed no acute intra-abdominal pathology. Due to persistent fever and worsening abdominal symptoms, a computed tomography scan was obtained revealing a linear radiopaque object extending from the right femoral vein, perforating right atrium and ascending aorta with associated haemorrhagic pericardial effusion. Emergent surgery was performed, and guidewire was successfully removed. Retrospective imaging showed its unnoticed presence for almost nine years.DiscussionAlthough guidewire retention is well-documented, delayed diagnosis with major vascular injury is exceedingly rare. Introduction

Central venous catheterization (CVC) is a common procedure in critical care and perioperative settings, used for haemodynamic monitoring, drug administration, dialysis, and transvenous pacemaker device placement. Although Seldinger is the most commonly used1 and considered the safest technique for catheter placement, some complications such as arterial puncture, infections, haematoma, catheter-related thrombosis, haemothorax, pneumothorax, and guidewire loss or retention are still significant risks of the technique.2,3 Reported complication rates vary from 3% to 15%, depending on procedural techniques, patient characteristics, and definitions used.2–4.

Among these, complete intravascular guidewire loss is an uncommon but serious complication. In this case report, we present a rare complication of retained guidewire which remained undetected within the venous system for nearly a decade and ultimately presented with cardiac tamponade secondary to atrial and aortic perforation, mimicking acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a complaint of progressively worsening chest and epigastric pain over the past few days. He described a sharp pain that started in the epigastric area and radiated to the chest and back intermittently. The patient had no known history of chronic medical conditions, including cardiovascular disease, and was not taking any routine medications. However, he had a significant smoking history spanning approximately 30 years. Surgical history included an internal fixation of the left femur and tibia following a motor vehicle accident in 2015.

On initial evaluation, the patient was haemodynamically stable, with palpable bilateral peripheral pulses. Physical examination was unremarkable.

An initial 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1A) demonstrated sinus rhythm with no ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities suggestive of myocardial ischaemia. Serial troponin T measurements (40–33 ng/L; reference range <13 ng/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP) (19 mg/L; reference range 0–5 mg/L) levels were mildly elevated while all other laboratory data were within normal limits. Chest radiograph (Figure 2) revealed no acute intrathoracic pathology. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed normal biventricular ejection fractions with no regional wall motion abnormality or evidence of ascending aortic dissection. A mild pericardial effusion was identified, predominantly localized around the right cardiac chambers.

( A ) Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrates sinus rhythm without ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities suggestive of myocardial ischaemia. (B) Follow-up ECG remains unchanged, showing no ischaemic findings.

Chest X-ray on admission demonstrated a normal cardiothoracic ratio with no evidence of pleural effusion, pneumonia, or other acute pulmonary pathology.

Due to elevated troponin levels and progressively worsening chest and epigastric pain, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit with a preliminary diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), despite atypical ECG findings. Empirical treatment with aspirin (100 mg/day) was initiated for presumed ACS. However, the clinical picture was atypical and serial electrocardiograms (Figure 1B) failed to demonstrate dynamic ischaemic changes. Thereafter, troponin levels normalized rapidly. The emergence of fever and the atypical nature of the chest pain further reduced the likelihood of ACS. Considering these findings, and in the absence of compelling electrocardiographic or echocardiographic evidence of ischaemia, urgent coronary angiography was deferred.

During follow-up, the patient developed persistent fever and new-onset right upper quadrant abdominal tenderness. Laboratory investigations revealed a marked rise in inflammatory markers, with CRP peaking at 119 mg/L. Blood and urine cultures were obtained to evaluate for systemic infection. Abdominal ultrasonography showed no evidence of hepatobiliary or intra-abdominal pathology.

To further assess for occult infection or alternative causes of inflammation, a contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) scan was performed. The CT scan revealed a linear, radiopaque foreign body consistent with a retained guidewire. The guidewire extended from the right femoral vein into the right atrium, traversed the wall of the ascending aorta, and terminated in the anterior mediastinum (Figures 3A–C; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). Additionally, imaging identified a pericardial effusion with characteristics suggestive of haemorrhagic content (Figure 3D).

Repeat TTE demonstrated a rapid progression of the pericardial effusion, measuring approximately 1 cm circumferentially around both the right and left cardiac chambers, indicating a potential progression towards cardiac tamponade.

As the patient’s condition deteriorated, presenting with tachycardia and hypotension, an urgent assessment was undertaken by cardiovascular surgery and interventional radiology teams. Given the imaging findings and high suspicion for cardiac tamponade, the decision was made to proceed with emergent surgical intervention for guidewire retrieval.

Intraoperatively, haemopericardium was confirmed upon opening the pericardium. The retained guidewire was visualized traversing the right atrial wall and perforating the ascending aorta (Figure 4) and successfully extracted without complication.

In the intraoperative view, the black arrow indicates the retained guidewire passing through the right atrium and perforating the ascending aorta.

The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. He was discharged on postoperative day 10 in stable condition. At one-year follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with no sequelae related to intravascular retained guidewire or the surgical intervention.

Discussion

CVC, while routinely performed in various clinical settings, carries a known risk of complications. Among these, complete intravascular loss of a guidewire is an uncommon yet potentially life-threatening event.5 Contributing factors include anatomical variation, operator inexperience, distraction during procedures, excessive clinical workload, and inadequate supervision.6 In fact, the literature identifies lack of supervision and procedural distraction as the most frequently cited risk factors for guidewire retention3,7.

Retained guidewires are most frequently identified within large central veins (e.g. brachiocephalic vein, superior or inferior vena cava), the pulmonary arteries, the right atrium or ventricle.1 While most retained guidewires are detected intraoperatively or soon after the procedure, some, as in this case, can remain asymptomatic and unrecognized for a long time.3,6

Although retained guidewires may remain entirely asymptomatic, they also carry the risk of serious complications, such as cardiac arrhythmia, thrombosis, embolism, major vascular injury, and cardiac tamponade secondary to chamber perforation.4,6,7 In our case, the guidewire traversed the right atrium and penetrated both walls of the ascending aorta, terminating in the anterior mediastinum, resulting in haemopericardium.

What makes this case especially noteworthy is the guidewire's silent persistence in the vascular system for an estimated nine years or more. Upon inquiry of imaging performed for unrelated orthopaedic indications, pelvic radiograph revealed a radiopaque linear structure consistent with a retained guidewire as early as 2017 (Figure 5A). Subsequent spinal imaging in 2022 demonstrated further upward migration, reaching the level of the diaphragm (Figures 5B and C).The inferior orientation of the soft J-tip suggests that the CVC was likely placed via the subclavian or internal jugular vein (Figure 5A).

( A ) Pelvic radiograph from 2017; the black arrow indicates a linear structure along the course of the right femoral vein, and the red circle marks the soft J-tip directed inferiorly. (B and C) Spinal radiographs from 2022; the black arrow shows the retained guidewire, while the white asterisk highlights the rigid, flat proximal segment extending to the level of the diaphragm.

The patient’s recent onset of symptoms is presumed to have resulted from gradual guidewire migration and delayed perforation of both the right atrium and the aorta. The clinical presentation, closely mimicking ACS, illustrates the diagnostic complexity.

The guidewire was not visualized on initial TTE, likely due to its trajectory being outside standard echocardiographic imaging planes or being overlooked.

Another important clinical consideration is the rapid progression of pericardial effusion following the initiation of aspirin therapy. Although the patient initially exhibited only a mild pericardial effusion, the subsequent increase is presumed to reflect haemorrhagic effusion resulting from ongoing bleeding at the sites of atrial and aortic perforation.

The retrieval of retained guidewires can be performed either percutaneously, surgically or hybrid approaches.5,8 Among these, the percutaneous method is generally considered the first-line strategy due to its high success rate and low associated morbidity.6,8 However, in the present case, surgical retrieval was considered the safest and most appropriate strategy due to several critical factors. The first is the risk of exacerbating haemopericardium. The guidewire had already perforated the ascending aorta, and further intravascular manipulation could have worsened bleeding. The second is the need to avoid anticoagulation. Percutaneous retrieval often requires systemic heparinization to prevent thrombotic complications.9,10 In a patient with ongoing bleeding and evidence of cardiac tamponade, initiating anticoagulation carries a significant haemodynamic risk. The third is the chronicity of retention. Long-standing guidewires are more likely to become endothelialized or fibrotically adherent, increasing the risk of vascular injury during percutaneous extraction.5,8

Conclusion

This case underscores the potentially life-threatening consequences of retained intravascular guidewires, which, though rare, are entirely preventable. The guidewire in this case remained clinically silent for nearly a decade before presenting with signs mimicking ACS and progressing to haemopericardium due to atrial and aortic perforation. Adherence to well-defined safety protocols including the use of checklists, continuous supervision, and verification following the procedure, is imperative to mitigate the likelihood of guidewire retention and uphold patient safety standards.
