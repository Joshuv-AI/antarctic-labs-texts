Integrating Brain Morphological Features and Ionized Serum Magnesium to Identify Mild Tic Comorbidity in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder
Authors: Xi Chen, Xiang Zhou, Bo-Ya Yin, Feng-Yun Zou, Shuang-Shuang Zhong, Ya‑Yin Deng, Jia-Yuan Zhao, Yu-Xuan Ni, Wen-Ying Zhou, Ruo-Mi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) frequently co-occurs with tic disorders, yet clinical differentiation remains challenging. This study developed and validated a predictive model combining brain morphological imaging and serum trace elements to distinguish ASD alone from ASD with comorbid mild tic disorders.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional diagnostic study included 104 children aged 4–15 years (90 boys and 14 girls): 53 with ASD alone and 51 with ASD and mild tic disorders. Participants were randomly divided into training and internal validation cohorts at a 7:3 ratio. Candidate predictors were screened in the training cohort with correction for multiple comparisons and further selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression. These features were incorporated into a multivariable regression equation and a nomogram. Model performance and internal validation were assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsIndependent predictors included asymmetry indices of the caudate nucleus, nucleus accumbens, and paratenial thalamic nucleus; cortical curvatures of the left anterior cingulate cortex and right lateral occipital gyrus; and ionized serum magnesium levels (all p < 0.05). The model achieved the areas under the ROC curves (AUROCs) of 0.904 (95% CI: 0.834–0.975) in the training cohort and 0.826 (95% CI: 0.664–0.988) in the internal validation cohort, outperforming individual predictors. Calibration was acceptable, and DCA suggested potential clinical utility within this cohort.ConclusionThe nomogram prediction model accurately distinguishes between ASD and ASD-mT, showing strong discriminative power and clinical value. It may aid clinicians in early comorbidity detection and guide treatment decisions.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, tic disorders, nomogram, blood elements

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) with onset in early childhood. Core symptoms comprise impaired social interaction, communication deficits, and stereotyped repetitive behaviors,1 significantly impacting children’s social functioning and learning. Moreover, 75–85% of children with ASD have comorbid NDDs such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, depression, and tic disorders.2 Tic disorders are brief, repetitive, nonrhythmic motor or vocal phenomena, but their distinction from stereotyped behaviors in children with ASD can be clinically challenging because both may present as repetitive movements and may co-occur in the same patient.3 Despite their clinical relevance, the neurobiological characteristics of tic comorbidity in ASD remain incompletely understood.

The cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical (CSTC) circuit regulates cortical-subcortical excitation–inhibition balance and has been implicated in the pathophysiology of tic disorders and is also relevant to neurodevelopmental alterations in ASD.4 Neuroimaging studies of tic disorders have reported alterations in frontal cortical morphology and in the volumes of the caudate nucleus, putamen, pallidum, and thalamus.5–7 ASD has also been associated with morphological abnormalities in cortical and subcortical regions within the CSTC circuit.8–10 However, the direction and regional distribution of these findings have been inconsistent. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in age, medication exposure, symptom severity, sample size, imaging protocols, segmentation methods, and statistical approaches. These overlapping but heterogeneous findings indicate that isolated regional measures may be insufficient to distinguish ASD with tic comorbidity from ASD alone.

Trace elements are essential for central nervous system structure and function, and are closely linked to growth and cognitive development in NDD. Studies report decreased serum levels of zinc, iron (Fe), and ionized serum magnesium (iMg), along with elevated lead and copper, in children with ASD.11,12 These imbalances are associated with neural impairment and biological dysfunction. Similar alterations—reduced zinc and iron, elevated lead—are seen in tic disorders, aligning with ASD trends. However, changes in copper and iMg remain controversial,13,14 likely due to limited sample sizes and geographic variations. Given these inconsistencies, and considering the potential relevance of iMg to neuronal excitability and neurodevelopment, iMg was included as a candidate predictor in the present study.

Although machine-learning and nomogram-based models have been explored for ASD diagnosis or tic-related clinical problems, models specifically designed to distinguish children with ASD alone from those with ASD and mild tic symptoms remain limited. We focused on mild tic symptoms because they are more likely to be overlooked in routine ASD assessment and may be difficult to differentiate from stereotyped behaviors. However, because the present study was restricted to children with mild tic symptoms, the findings should not be directly generalized to patients with moderate or severe tic disorders without further validation.

Moreover, few studies have integrated whole-brain morphological features with serum trace-element measures for this clinically challenging distinction. Therefore, this retrospective diagnostic study aimed to develop and internally validate a multimodal nomogram for identifying mild tic comorbidity in children with ASD.

Methods and Materials

Study Objects

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (II2023-003-01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all guardians. From June 2020 to August 2024, 67 children with ASD and 60 with ASD-mT were recruited from our hospital’s Behavior and Development Center. ASD diagnosis was confirmed by two child psychiatrists according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) criteria, supported by the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS), the Autism Behavior Checklist (ABC), and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R).15,16 For the ASD-mT group, tic symptoms were verified by two movement-disorder experts through video-based assessment. Tic severity was assessed using the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) Global Severity Score, for which scores of 10–25 correspond to mild tic-related global severity.17,18 Therefore, ASD-mT was defined as ASD with clinically observable tic symptoms and a YGTSS Global Severity Score of 10–25. Exclusion criteria included: 1) schizophrenia or bipolar disorder per “Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children-Present and Lifetime Version” (Kiddie-SADS-PL); 2) history of head trauma, metal implants, congenital brain abnormalities, or neurological disorders such as epilepsy; and 3) use of antipsychotic medication within the past three months. Participants with excessive motion or incomplete MRI scans were also excluded during quality control.

MRI Data Acquisition and Morphological Data Processing

All MRI data were acquired using a 3.0 T scanner (SIGNA Architect, GE Healthcare, USA) with a 48-channel head coil. All MRI scans were performed during natural sleep in the supine position, and no sedative or sleep-inducing medication was administered to any participant. Besides routine sequences, high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted images (3D-T1WI) were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 7.7 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.1 ms, inversion time (TI) = 450 ms, flip angle = 12°, field of view (FOV) = 256×256 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, acceleration factor = 2, number of excitations (NEX) = 1, total time = 4 min 18s. All structural MRI data were reviewed for image quality, including motion-related artifacts, incomplete anatomical coverage, geometric distortion, and unsuccessful segmentation. Datasets that did not meet the predefined quality criteria were removed from subsequent analyses. Since the study was based on three-dimensional T1-weighted structural images rather than time-series functional data, temporal motion correction was not performed. Potential motion effects were reduced by acquiring images during natural sleep and excluding scans of insufficient quality.

All images in this study were processed using the uAI Research Portal (United Imaging Intelligence). The preprocessing pipeline included skull stripping, bias field correction, and resampling to an isotropic resolution of 1 mm. Subsequently, T1-weighted images were parcellated into 109 regions of interest (ROIs) according to the Desikan–Killiany (DK) atlas using a pre-trained cascaded VB-Net model. Cortical surface reconstruction was performed within the CorticalFlow framework, whereby cortical vertices were iteratively deformed to delineate the cortical boundaries.19 Additionally, the hippocampus and amygdala were segmented into their respective subfields.20,21 Thalamic nuclei were further segmented, generating bilateral thalamic subfield volumes and proportional volumes. Bilateral asymmetry indices were calculated for each paired structure. The segmentation model employed a two-stage architecture combining coarse anatomical localization with refined segmentation, demonstrating high robustness and accuracy in medical image segmentation tasks.22

Trace Element Acquisition

All children fasted for ≥8 h before morning venous blood collection between 08:00 and 10:00 a.m. Samples were obtained using trace-element certified, metal-free blood collection tubes (IMPROVACUTER): EDTA-K2 (cat. 722030202), additive-free (cat. 602030202), and sodium-heparin (cat. 662030202). Skin was disinfected with alcohol, followed by metal-free saline. Tubes were inverted gently after collection and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min within 30 min. Serum and whole blood were aliquoted into pre-screened vials; hemolyzed samples (n = 2) were excluded. Aliquots were stored at −20 °C and thawed no more than two times. To accommodate ASD-related behavioral characteristics, sampling was conducted early in the morning using distraction techniques to minimize distress. Blood lead was measured in whole blood, whereas iron, iMg, and ceruloplasmin were measured in serum. Analytical methods, instruments, and quality-control procedures are summarized in Table S1. Detection limits were: lead (Pb), 0.2 μg/L; Fe, 0.05 μmol/L; iMg, 0.02 mmol/L; ceruloplasmin, 0.02 g/L.

Model Construction and Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software, version 4.1.3. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Welch’s t-test; non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. MRI analysts, laboratory technicians, and statisticians were blinded to group assignment during image processing, laboratory testing, and statistical analysis, respectively.

To avoid information leakage, participants were first randomly assigned to a training cohort and an internal validation cohort in a 7:3 ratio. All feature screening and model development procedures were conducted exclusively within the training cohort. Univariable screening was performed in the training cohort only, and false-discovery-rate correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons across the candidate variables. Variables surviving the prespecified FDR threshold were then entered into LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation for further feature selection.

Continuous predictors were standardized using the mean and standard deviation of the training cohort; the same scaling parameters were then applied to the validation cohort. The final prediction model was refitted in the training cohort using multivariable logistic regression restricted to the predictors selected by LASSO. A post hoc sample-size justification was performed based on the final model complexity and the number of outcome events in the training cohort. A nomogram was constructed from the final logistic regression coefficients. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analysis and the area under the curve with 95% confidence intervals. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and Brier scores. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis. The internal validation cohort was used only for independent performance assessment.

Results

Demographic Data

Of the 67 children initially enrolled with ASD, 9 were excluded due to comorbidities and 5 for psychotropic medication use (n = 1), abnormal MRI (n = 2), or intellectual disability (n = 2), resulting in 53 children in the ASD group. Of the 60 initially recruited ASD-mT children, 9 were excluded for intellectual disability (n = 1), congenital brain abnormalities (n = 3), or psychotropic medication use (n = 5), leaving 51 in the ASD-mT group. The screening process is summarized in Figure 1. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The ASD-mT group had significantly higher IQ (96.76 ± 16.93 vs 86.17 ± 19.82, p = 0.02) and lower ADI-R social interaction (12.56 ± 4.43 vs 15.09 ± 5.39, p = 0.01) and language communication scores (9.82 ± 3.50 vs 11.36 ± 4.29, p = 0.05) compared with the ASD group. No significant differences were observed in age, sex, handedness, or other scale scores between groups. The mean YGTSS Global Severity Score in the ASD-mT group was 15.65 ± 3.21, and all ASD-mT participants met the pre-specified mild Global Severity Score range of 10–25. Regarding verbal status, 40 children in the ASD group and 43 children in the ASD-mT group were verbal, whereas 13 and 8, respectively, were nonverbal. In the ASD-mT group, motor tic disorders were observed in 34 children, vocal tic disorders in 17, simple tic disorders in 42, and complex tic disorders in 9. These categories were not mutually exclusive.

Whole-Brain Morphological Data

Intergroup comparisons of brain morphological measures are detailed in Tables S2–S7. Relative to the ASD-mT group, the ASD group exhibited a smaller left nucleus accumbens volume (0.81 ± 0.14 cm3 vs 0.88 ± 0.17 cm3, p = 0.04) and reduced right frontal cortical surface area [median (IQR): 5.08 (4.50–5.41) cm2 vs 5.25 (4.89–5.79) cm2, p < 0.01]. Curvature was lower in the left anterior cingulate cortex (0.19 ± 0.04 vs 0.21 ± 0.05, p = 0.03) and right transverse temporal gyrus [0.099 (0.090–0.112)% vs 0.104 (0.097–0.127) %, p = 0.04], but higher in the right posterior cingulate gyrus [0.25 (0.23–0.27) vs 0.24 (0.22–0.26), p = 0.05] and right lateral occipital cortex [0.255 (0.239–0.265) vs 0.240 (0.232–0.261), p = 0.01]. Significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05) were also found in asymmetry indices of global white matter, cerebral hemispheres, caudate nucleus, anterior middle frontal gyrus, nucleus accumbens, and ventral diencephalon. Although whole-thalamus volume did not differ significantly between groups, thalamic subfield analysis revealed differences in the left paratenial nucleus and several thalamic asymmetry indices (P = 0.02, 0.05, and 0.02, respectively). The ASD group had a smaller percentage area of the right ventrolateral posterior nucleus [5.74 (5.60–5.96)% vs 5.95 (5.73–6.12)%, p = 0.02], and significantly different asymmetry indices in the ventral lateral posterior nucleus [0.08 (0.04–0.14) vs 0.04 (0.00–0.09), p < 0.01], ventral posterolateral nucleus [0.09 (0.03–0.15) vs 0.06 (−0.01–0.11), p = 0.04], and lateral pulvinar nucleus [0.22 (0.09–0.30) vs 0.11 (0.03–0.23), p < 0.01] (Table 2).

The serum levels of trace elements and ceruloplasmin are shown in Table S8. Among the measured biomarkers, iMg differed significantly between groups and was lower in the ASD-mT group than in the ASD group (0.77 ± 0.06 vs 0.83 ± 0.05 mmol/L, p < 0.001). Model Development

The distribution of brain morphological and trace-element indicators was similar between the training and validation sets (Table 3). After stratified random allocation, all feature screening and model construction were performed in the training cohort. A total of 21 candidate predictors were initially considered, including 20 brain morphological indices and 1 serum trace-element measure. These variables were screened exclusively in the training cohort. Following FDR-corrected univariable screening and LASSO logistic regression, six predictors were retained for final model construction. For post hoc sample-size justification, the final model included six selected predictors. Based on the 7:3 training-validation split, the training cohort included 36 ASD-mT events, corresponding to 6.0 outcome events per selected predictor. These predictors were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model refitted in the training cohort: caudate nucleus asymmetry index, nucleus accumbens asymmetry index, left anterior cingulate cortex curvature, right lateral occipital gyrus curvature, paratenial thalamic nucleus asymmetry index, and ionized serum magnesium level (Figure 2). In an IQ-adjusted sensitivity analysis of the validation cohort, the AUC changed from 0.826 (95% CI: 0.664–0.988) to 0.832 (95% CI: 0.675–0.989). Performance Evaluation of the Nomogram Model

ROC analysis (Figure 3B and C) showed AUROCs of 0.904 (training) and 0.826 (validation) for the model, which were higher than those of individual predictors (caudate nucleus asymmetry index: 0.592; nucleus accumbens asymmetry index: 0.652; left anterior cingulate cortex curvature: 0.642; right lateral occipital gyrus curvature: 0.644; paratenial thalamic nucleus asymmetry index: 0.683; ionized serum magnesium: 0.800). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded p-values of 0.918 (training) and 0.137 (validation) (both P > 0.05), indicating no evidence of poor calibration (Figure 4A and B). Decision curve analysis suggested potential net benefit across both sets. Brier scores were 0.12 in the training cohort and 0.18 in the validation cohort (Figure 4C and D).

Discussion

Movement disorders are common in children with ASD, presenting as uncoordinated gross and fine motor skills, repetitive stereotyped behaviors, and abnormal postures. Mild tic symptoms are challenging to distinguish because they overlap with these behavioral features, assessment tools vary, and standardized screening is limited. Evidence indicates that targeted pharmacological and behavioral interventions can alleviate tic symptoms and may indirectly improve ASD-related functioning.23,24 Although not statistically significant, the ASD-mT group showed a numerically higher ABA score than the ASD-alone group. This pattern may suggest an association between tic comorbidity and greater behavioral difficulties in children with ASD, but it should be interpreted cautiously because it was exploratory and not statistically significant.25 This study developed an internally validated nomogram based on brain regional curvature, asymmetry indices, and serum trace-element measures associated with ASD-mT. By integrating multimodal objective indicators, the model showed promising discriminative performance in this single-center cohort. Its potential clinical use remains preliminary and requires external validation.

In contrast to prior neuroimaging studies comparing ASD or tic disorders to healthy controls, this study found that conventional measures like whole-brain volume, cortical thickness, and surface area did not enhance the diagnosis of comorbid tic disorders. Cortical curvature has been proposed as a geometric biomarker reflecting aberrant neurodevelopment in ASD. Previous research has established a pathway from “local overfolding—network dysfunction—genetic enrichment—behavioral phenotype”.26 We confirmed increased curvature in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) in children with ASD,27 which was further elevated in those with comorbid tic disorders, potentially due to synergistic mechanisms. Animal studies suggest that ASD-related Shank3 mutations reduce glutamatergic synaptic function in the ACC, while tic disorders involve deficient GABAergic inhibition.28 This compounded excitation–inhibition imbalance may promote local oscillatory activity and cortical overfolding. ACC structural abnormalities are linked to social-emotional dysfunction in ASD29 and may also regulate premonitory tic urges. Persistent hyperactivation could stimulate microvascular proliferation and surface expansion, increasing curvature—though exact mechanisms remain unclear. Postmortem studies report disrupted minicolumn organization and increased cortical curvature in the right lateral occipital cortex (rLOC) in ASD.30 Interestingly, our study found reduced rLOC curvature in the ASD-mT group, aligning with findings by Fahim et al Delayed folding in the parieto-occipital cortex, consistent in tic disorders, may mask curvature overgrowth typically seen in ASD.31

Large-sample studies indicate that children with ASD exhibit reduced asymmetry in cortical thickness and surface area, but increased putaminal asymmetry, supporting “atypical lateralization” as a neural phenotype.32 Our diagnostic model incorporated asymmetry indices of the caudate nucleus, nucleus accumbens, and paratenial thalamic nucleus. The nucleus accumbens asymmetry was −0.05 ± 0.12 in ASD and 0.03 ± 0.11 in ASD-mT, consistent with ENIGMA-ASD reports of left-side reduction. Previous studies have reported lateralized dopamine D2 receptor distribution in the nucleus accumbens; altered dopaminergic signaling may therefore be one possible explanation for the observed difference in nucleus accumbens asymmetry.33 In the present cohort, caudate asymmetry differed between groups (−0.03 ± 0.04 in ASD-mT vs −0.01 ± 0.04 in ASD; P = 0.01), but the biological interpretation of this directional difference remains uncertain. Group differences in paratenial thalamic nucleus asymmetry may further indicate altered thalamic involvement in tic-related regulation, although this interpretation requires functional validation.34,35

Trace-element imbalance may be relevant to neurodevelopment and mental health. We observed lower iMg in children with ASD-mT than in children with ASD alone, consistent with prior reports of altered magnesium-related measures in neurodevelopmental conditions.36,37 Genetic variation in magnesium transport pathways may contribute to altered magnesium homeostasis, although this possibility was not examined in the present study. Magnesium is involved in NMDA receptor-mediated glutamatergic neurotransmission, and altered magnesium homeostasis may be associated with neuronal excitability in ASD and tic disorders.38,39 However, NMDA receptor activity, D2 receptor function, NR2B phosphorylation, genotype, and intracellular magnesium concentration were not assessed in the present study; therefore, the involvement of these pathways remains hypothetical.

A key strength of this study is the development of a multimodal model for distinguishing ASD-mT from ASD alone using noninvasive imaging and laboratory markers. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, although tic symptoms were assessed by two movement-disorder experts using video-based evaluation and YGTSS Global Severity Scores, mild tic symptoms may still overlap clinically with stereotyped behaviors in some children with ASD; therefore, potential misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Third, the modest single-center sample size and 70/30 internal split may increase the risk of model optimism and overfitting. Fourth, the model was internally validated only, and its performance in independent, multicenter, and more clinically heterogeneous cohorts remains unknown. Finally, the present study was restricted to mild tic symptoms; the findings should not be generalized to moderate or severe tic disorders without further validation.

Conclusion

In summary, a nomogram was developed incorporating brain morphological and laboratory markers, specifically, asymmetry indices of the caudate nucleus, nucleus accumbens, and paratenial thalamic nucleus; curvatures of the left anterior cingulate cortex and right lateral occipital gyrus; and ionized serum magnesium level, to predict mild tic comorbidity in ASD. The model demonstrates discriminative performance in the present cohort and provides a preliminary predictive equation for identifying ASD with mild tic comorbidity. Future studies with larger, multicenter, and multi-population datasets are warranted to further evaluate and enhance its generalizability and scalability.
