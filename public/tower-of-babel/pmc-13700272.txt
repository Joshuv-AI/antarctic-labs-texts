Second victim experiences, organisational responses and intention to leave among operating room nurses in China
Authors: Lingai Ren, Junxiang Cheng, Guangmei Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety events can have substantial emotional and professional consequences for clinicians involved in them. This study examined second victim experiences among operating room nurses in China and how organisational support, just culture and career resilience were associated with burnout and intention to leave.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was used. Between March and July 2025, a cross-sectional survey was administered to operating room nurses in 10 tertiary general hospitals in Shanxi and Henan provinces; 540 of 820 eligible nurses provided analysable data. Measures included the Chinese Second Victim Experience and Support Tool and scales for organisational support, just culture, career resilience, emotional exhaustion and intention to leave. Hierarchical linear and logistic regressions examined associated factors. Psychological distress scored highest (mean 3.64/5), and the second victim impact index1 was moderate (mean 3.20/5). Introduction

Patient safety incidents remain an inevitable feature of complex health-care systems and can cause harm not only to patients but also to the clinicians involved. Since Wu first described clinicians as “second victims” of medical error in 2000, a substantial body of research has highlighted the emotional, physical and professional consequences that follow involvement in adverse events [1, 2]. Health professionals who experience second victim phenomena commonly report guilt, shame, anxiety, intrusive memories, sleep disturbance and reduced confidence in their clinical abilities, and these reactions can persist for months or longer if insufficient support is available [3, 4]. Although the terminology has been debated, with some authors arguing that the phrase “second victim” risks obscuring patient harm, the construct remains widely used in research and in measurement tools such as the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST) [5]. The construct remains widely used in research and in tools such as the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST) [5], although its terminology continues to be debated.

Nurses are especially exposed because they are continuously present during care and are frequently involved in both technical and relational aspects of patient safety [6, 7]. Cross-sectional studies suggest that between one-third and four-fifths of nurses report second victim experiences, with psychological symptoms often more prominent than physical ones [6, 7]. Such distress has been linked with burnout, lower job satisfaction and stronger turnover intentions [4, 8]. In China, punitive responses and inadequate support have been associated with more severe distress after safety events [9, 10], but most evidence comes from general wards rather than perioperative settings [7].

The operating room (OR) is a high-risk environment characterised by time pressure, complex technology and interdependent teamwork. Perioperative nurses are directly involved in invasive procedures, medication administration, surgical counts and acute deterioration events. OR culture may emphasise perfectionism, hierarchy and blame, which can discourage help-seeking after incidents [3]. A recent Chinese OR nurse study linked responsibility attribution, organisational support and career resilience to second victim distress [11], but multi-centre mixed-methods evidence remains limited.

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [12, 13]. In JD-R theory, job demands are physical, psychological or organisational aspects of work that require sustained effort and may impair well-being when they exceed available resources, whereas job resources are functional in reducing demands, facilitating goal achievement or stimulating growth [12]. Involvement in perioperative adverse events was therefore conceptualised as a salient job demand, indexed by event frequency, perceived responsibility and the second victim impact index derived from C-SVEST symptom and confidence domains. Work resources included perceived organisational support (POS), which reflects whether the hospital values staff contributions and cares about their welfare [14], and just-culture perceptions, which capture whether incident responses are fair, consistent and learning-oriented rather than punitive [15]. Personal resources were represented by career resilience, defined as the capacity to adapt to setbacks and persist in nursing despite professional difficulty [11, 16]. Emotional exhaustion and intention to leave were treated as downstream strain and withdrawal-related outcomes within the same framework [8, 17].

Just culture and POS were both included because they address related but distinct facets of the organisational environment: POS reflects general institutional care for staff, whereas just culture focuses specifically on fairness after safety events. Prior work suggests that organisational support can buffer second victim distress and turnover intentions [8, 17], while just culture provides the normative context that determines whether staff trust formal support pathways [15]. Career resilience was included because personal adaptive capacity may influence how the same adverse event is experienced, particularly when organisational resources are limited [16]. These variables were not selected for novelty; together they map directly onto the demand–resource–outcome structure of the JD-R model in perioperative second victim research1.

Study aims and research questions

China faces ongoing challenges in retaining experienced perioperative nurses [17]. To address gaps in multi-centre evidence, this sequential explanatory mixed-methods study examined second victim experiences among OR nurses in ten tertiary hospitals in Shanxi and Henan provinces. Three research questions guided the quantitative component:RQ1. What is the prevalence and profile of second victim experiences among OR nurses, including symptom severity, support received and event characteristics?RQ2. Which event-related, organisational and personal factors are associated with the second victim impact index after adjustment for age and OR experience?RQ3. How are second victim impact, emotional exhaustion, organisational support and career resilience associated with high intention to leave perioperative nursing?

The qualitative component asked how OR nurses experienced adverse events, how they perceived organisational and peer responses, and how these experiences related to professional confidence and career intentions. Integrating both strands aimed to inform OR-specific, non-punitive support strategies for nurse well-being and retention.

Methods

Design

This study used a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design comprising a cross-sectional survey followed by semi-structured interviews. The quantitative component was undertaken first to estimate the prevalence and severity of second victim experiences and to examine associations between second victim impact, organisational conditions, career resilience and turnover-related outcomes. The subsequent qualitative component was then used to explain and contextualise the survey findings, particularly the ways in which organisational responses and peer support shaped nurses’ experiences after perioperative adverse events. The survey component was reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [18], and the interview component with reference to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ). Integration was planned at the design, sampling, data-collection and interpretation stages of the study.

Setting

The study was conducted across 10 large tertiary general hospitals located in Shanxi and Henan provinces in north-central China. These provinces represent a significant population centre in China, and the selected hospitals function as major regional referral centres. Each participating hospital had a bed capacity ranging from 700 to 2,000 beds and managed a high volume of surgical cases, including a mix of elective lists and emergency trauma or acute care surgeries.

The operating departments within these facilities typically comprised between 8 and 20 operating theatres. The nursing staff establishment in these departments ranged

from 40 to 120 registered nurses, who fulfilled roles including scrub nurse, circulating nurse, and OR-based anaesthesia or recovery room nurse. In terms of technological context, none of the participating hospitals had established formalised robotic surgery programmes or high-technology “hybrid” theatres at the time of the study, ensuring a degree of homogeneity in the clinical environment. However, all hospitals utilised standard electronic incident reporting systems and adhered to hospital-wide patient safety management protocols. Crucially, at the time of data collection, no hospital had implemented a dedicated second-victim support programme for OR staff, although general employee assistance programmes or psychological counselling services were theoretically available to all staff.

Participants and sampling

The target population was registered nurses currently employed in the ORs of the 10 participating hospitals.

Inclusion criteria: (1) holding a current practising certificate; (2) working primarily in the OR as a scrub, circulating, or anaesthesia/recovery nurse; (3) having at least six months of OR clinical experience; and (4) being directly involved in perioperative patient care.

Exclusion criteria: (1) nurses in purely managerial positions without direct clinical OR duties; (2) nursing students or interns; (3) agency or temporary staff not on the hospital’s regular OR roster; and (4) nurses on prolonged leave (e.g., maternity leave or long-term sick leave) who were unavailable during the data-collection period.

For the quantitative survey, a census of all eligible OR nurses was conducted using current personnel rosters at each hospital. This approach involves complete enumeration of a finite, definable workforce rather than probability sampling from a larger population [19]. Sample size was determined before data collection. Based on an anticipated 10–12 predictors in the final multivariable models and a rule of at least 10–15 observations per predictor, a minimum of 100–180 participants was required [20]. With 820 eligible nurses across 10 hospitals, the study had adequate power for planned regression analyses.

For the qualitative phase, purposive maximum-variation sampling was used within the survey sample. At the end of the survey, respondents indicated whether they were willing to be contacted for interview. From those who agreed, a sampling matrix was applied to ensure variation across: (1) second victim impact index (low ≤ 2.5, moderate 2.5–3.8, high ≥ 3.8); (2) POS (low ≤ 2.5, moderate 2.5–3.5, high ≥ 3.5); (3) just-culture score (low, moderate, high using the same tertile cut-points); (4) years of OR experience (<3, 3–10, >10 years); (5) hospital site (at least one participant from each of the 10 hospitals where possible); and (6) primary OR role (scrub, circulating, rotating). Recruitment proceeded iteratively: after each interview, the matrix was reviewed and the next participant was selected to fill under-represented cells. Interviewing continued until thematic saturation was reached (no substantively new issues in the final four interviews). The final sample comprised 24 nurses (11 scrub, 9 circulating, 4 rotating). Possible self-selection among volunteers was acknowledged in interpretation.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected between March and July 2025 using a structured self-administered questionnaire. At each hospital, the research team liaised with the OR nursing manager to obtain the eligible staff roster. Questionnaires were distributed in two modes according to local preference and infection-control requirements: (1) paper-based forms completed anonymously in private during breaks or after shifts, with sealed collection boxes placed in OR staff areas; and (2) identical electronic forms accessed via a secure QR code link for nurses who preferred online completion. Both modes used the same item order and instructions. Research assistants were available on site during distribution periods to explain the voluntary nature of participation and answer procedural questions, but did not assist with item completion. Completed paper questionnaires were entered into the database by two research assistants using double-entry verification; electronic responses were exported directly. The survey required approximately 20–25 minutes to complete.

Qualitative data were collected between August and October 2025 through semi-structured interviews with purposively sampled survey respondents. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private hospital room or by secure telephone according to participant preference, lasted 40–60 minutes, and were audio-recorded with written consent.

Measures

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics

The survey captured sociodemographic and employment characteristics including age, gender, highest educational qualification, marital status, total years of nursing experience, years of OR experience, primary OR role, employment status and usual shift pattern. Age, total nursing experience and years of OR experience were recorded as continuous variables and also categorised for descriptive reporting. Highest educational qualification was coded as diploma/associate, bachelor or master and above; primary OR role as scrub, circulating or rotating; and shift pattern as day only, rotating including nights or mainly nights. These variables were used primarily for sample description, and age and years of OR experience were included as covariates in the multivariable models because of their theoretical relevance to professional exposure and adaptation. Exposure to adverse events was assessed by asking whether participants had ever been involved in a perioperative patient safety event or near miss that caused or could have caused patient harm. Participants also reported the approximate number of such events across their career and during the previous 12 months. The number of events in the previous 12 months was used as an event-exposure predictor in regression models. For the most memorable event, participants identified the event type and their perceived level of personal responsibility (none, shared or primary); perceived primary responsibility was coded as a binary variable for regression analysis, with none or shared responsibility as the reference category.

Second victim experiences were measured using the Chinese version of the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (C-SVEST) [21]. The C-SVEST contains 31 items organised into nine subscales validated in Chinese nursing samples: psychological distress (4 items), physical distress (3 items), professional self-efficacy (4 items), colleague support (4 items), supervisor support (4 items), organisational support (3 items), non-work support (3 items), absenteeism (3 items) and turnover intention (3 items) [5, 21]. Organisational context was assessed using an 8-item just-culture scale derived from validated measures of non-punitive response to error and fairness in incident investigation [9]. The items assessed whether staff felt treated fairly after safety events, whether incident reviews emphasised learning rather than blame and whether management responses were applied consistently. Higher scores indicated more positive perceptions of a just culture. Perceived organisational support

Two complementary indicators of organisational support were measured. The 3-item C-SVEST organisational support subscale assesses support perceived specifically after adverse events (e.g., whether the organisation checked on staff wellbeing after an incident). The 8-item POS scale, adapted from a short form of the Survey of Perceived Organizational Support [14], assesses broader hospital-wide perceptions that the organisation values staff contributions and cares about employee welfare. These constructs overlap but are not identical: in this sample, the correlation between C-SVEST organisational support and POS was r = 0.65, whereas POS and just culture correlated at r = 0.52. Only POS was entered into multivariable models because it aligns with the JD-R conceptualisation of general work resources and avoids duplicating the event-specific support items embedded within the C-SVEST distress/support measure used to derive the impact index. The C-SVEST organisational support subscale was retained for descriptive reporting alongside other C-SVEST dimensions.

Career resilience

Career resilience was measured using a six-item scale validated for Chinese nurses [11]. The scale assesses the nurse’s capacity to adapt to changing work demands, recover from setbacks and persist in the profession despite professional difficulty. Higher scores indicate greater career resilience. Translation and pilot testing

Any instruments not already available in Chinese underwent forward-backward translation before use. Two bilingual researchers translated the items into Chinese, a professional translator back-translated them into English, and discrepancies were reconciled by the research team. Content relevance and clarity were reviewed by a panel of five senior perioperative nurses. A pilot study with 30 OR nurses from two participating hospitals, who were not included in the analytic sample, confirmed the feasibility and comprehensibility of the survey package.

Interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was initially developed from theoretical models of second victim recovery and prior qualitative work on nurses’ experiences after safety events [10]. After preliminary survey findings were reviewed, the guide was refined to include targeted probes on low perceived organisational support, perceived responsibility and the functions of peer support as a coping resource. The guide began with brief contextual questions about the participant’s OR role and working environment and then moved to an open invitation to describe the most memorable perioperative adverse event or near miss in which the participant had been directly involved. It was organised around eight thematic domains: recounting the event; immediate emotional and physical responses; responsibility, blame and meaning-making; responses from colleagues, leaders and the organisation; organisational culture and just culture; support received, support needed and barriers to help-seeking; ongoing impact on professional confidence, clinical practice and career intentions; and recommendations for an ideal support pathway. Core questions were asked of all participants, while follow-up prompts were used flexibly to explore issues raised in each account. The detailed guide is provided in Supplementary Text S1.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Survey data were analysed in three stages. First, descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics, exposure to adverse events and scale scores. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations or, where appropriate, medians and interquartile ranges; categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Internal consistency of multi-item scales was examined using Cronbach’s α .

Second, bivariate relationships among the second victim impact index, organisational variables, career resilience, emotional exhaustion, job satisfaction and intention to leave were examined using Pearson correlation. Third, hierarchical linear regression was used to identify variables associated with the second victim impact index. Based on the study aims and prior literature, age and years of OR experience were entered in Step 1, event-related variables (number of second victim events in the past 12 months and perceived primary responsibility for the index event) in Step 2, and organisational support, just culture and career resilience in Step 3. Binary logistic regression was then used to examine factors associated with high intention to leave perioperative nursing. The logistic model included the second victim impact index, emotional exhaustion, perceived organisational support, career resilience and age as predictors. Additional sensitivity analyses examined cluster-robust standard errors by hospital for the linear regression model and a stricter definition of high intention to leave based on a mean intention-to-leave score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \ge {\rm{4}}$$\end{document} across the three items.

Questionnaires with more than 20% missing on key scales were excluded before analysis. All analyses were conducted on the resulting analytic sample. Given that only 10 hospitals were included, clustered standard errors were used as a sensitivity check for the linear regression model rather than fitting more complex multilevel models. For multivariable models, standardised regression coefficients or odds ratios with corresponding uncertainty estimates were reported. Model assumptions were checked through inspection of residual distributions, assessment of linearity for continuous predictors and multicollinearity diagnostics. Because perceived organisational support and just culture were conceptually related, variance inflation factors were examined in the final linear model; all VIF values were below 2.0, suggesting that multicollinearity was not severe. For the logistic regression, model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. All statistical tests were two-sided, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p{\rm{ < 0}}{\rm{.05}}$$\end{document} considered statistically significant.

Qualitative analysis

Interview data were analysed using an inductive thematic analysis approach informed by Braun and Clarke’s [22] procedures rather than a formal inter-rater reliability design. Analysis proceeded iteratively alongside data collection so that emerging issues could inform subsequent interviews. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in Chinese and checked against the audio files for accuracy. The analysis involved repeated reading of transcripts, inductive coding of meaning units, grouping of related codes, development of candidate themes, and iterative refinement of themes against the full dataset. Two researchers (LAR and JXC) independently coded an initial subset of transcripts to refine the coding framework. Coding agreement was not quantified with a statistic such as kappa; instead, differences in coding and interpretation were discussed through consensus meetings, with attention to divergent cases and the fit between candidate themes and the underlying data. The refined coding framework was then applied and updated across the full dataset as new insights emerged.

To enhance analytic credibility, preliminary interpretations were discussed with five interview participants to assess whether the interpretations resonated with their experiences and to clarify wording where needed. This procedure was used as a credibility check and clarification process rather than as formal validation of final themes. Recruitment ceased when additional interviews were no longer contributing substantively new issues to the analysis. The lead researcher (LAR), a perioperative nurse with clinical and research experience in patient safety, kept a reflexive journal throughout interviewing and analysis to document assumptions, decisions and evolving interpretations.

Integration

Integration occurred at three points in the sequential explanatory design. First, survey responses informed purposive selection of interview participants using the maximum-variation matrix described above. Second, early quantitative findings (e.g., low POS and high impact scores) informed targeted interview probes. Third, quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated during interpretation using side-by-side comparison and a joint display (Table 9), following established mixed-methods integration logic [23]. This allowed interview data to explain statistical patterns, including subgroup gradients and areas where organisational responses appeared to amplify or buffer distress.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital Ethics Committee, Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (Reference: SPPH-2025–01). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Return of the survey questionnaire was treated as implied consent, and written informed consent was obtained from all interview participants. Given the sensitivity of discussing adverse events and possible errors, interviewers were prepared to pause interviews if participants became distressed and to provide information about available psychological support services where required. Confidentiality was maintained throughout, and all datasets were anonymised before analysis.

Results

Participant flow and characteristics

Across the 10 participating tertiary general hospitals in Shanxi and Henan provinces, 820 operating room nurses met the inclusion criteria and were invited to participate in the survey. A total of 564 nurses returned a questionnaire, yielding a questionnaire return rate of 68.8%. Twenty-four questionnaires (4.3% of those returned) were excluded because more than 20% of items on key scales were missing, leaving 540 questionnaires for analysis (analytic sample 65.9% of those invited). Prevalence and profile of second victim experiences

Descriptive statistics for the C-SVEST subscales are shown in Table 2. Because all respondents completed the C-SVEST items with reference to perioperative adverse events or near misses, these scores are presented for the full analytic sample. On the 1–5 scale, psychological distress was the highest-scoring dimension (mean 3.64, SD 0.78), followed by physical distress (mean 3.13, SD 0.87), whereas professional self-efficacy was lower (mean 2.82, SD 0.76), indicating reduced confidence after adverse events for many respondents. The second victim impact index had a mean of 3.20 (SD 0.66), indicating a moderate overall level of adverse impact when symptoms and reduced professional self-efficacy were summarised together. Colleague support (mean 3.73, SD 0.73) and non-work support (mean 3.80, SD 0.73) were comparatively high, whereas organisational support was lower (mean 2.85, SD 0.85). Immediate and short-term reactions were also common. Guilt or self-blame was reported by 351 nurses (78.0%), intrusive thoughts or rumination by 308 (68.4%), and fear of disciplinary action or litigation by 294 (65.3%). Difficulty sleeping was reported by 240 nurses (53.3%), 195 (43.3%) reported avoiding discussion of the event at work, and 177 (39.3%) said they had seriously considered leaving their job or profession as a result.

The first theme, “Carrying the event alone,” described how many nurses internalised blame and tried to cope in silence. Participants commonly described replaying the event repeatedly and questioning their competence, especially when they perceived themselves as primarily responsible. Several nurses expressed difficulty talking about the event, fearing they would be seen as incompetent or weak. One nurse explained that after a serious count discrepancy, she “kept thinking about that case every night, but I did not dare to tell anyone how scared I was.” This theme echoed the quantitative finding that distress was higher when nurses perceived primary responsibility and when organisational support was low.

The second theme, “Support depends on who is in charge,” highlighted the variability in managerial and organisational responses. In some cases, head nurses and surgeons were described as supportive, focusing on understanding system factors and emphasising learning and improvement. In other cases, nurses described being singled out in meetings, with language that felt accusatory or shaming. A number of participants contrasted “good” and “bad” incident reviews: in supportive reviews, leaders acknowledged the emotional impact and framed the event as a shared system problem; in unsupportive reviews, the emphasis was on identifying the individual at fault. Nurses in the latter situation reported more intense and prolonged distress and a stronger desire to leave the OR.

The third theme, “Peer solidarity as a lifeline,” underscored the central role of colleagues in helping nurses cope with second victim experiences. Participants described informal debriefings with trusted peers after difficult cases, sharing feelings of guilt or fear and receiving reassurance that others had experienced similar situations. These peer interactions were often described as more helpful than formal channels, which some perceived as bureaucratic. Nurses emphasised the importance of simple gestures such as a colleague staying behind after a late case to talk or a senior nurse quietly telling them that “you are not alone in this.” This theme paralleled the relatively high scores on colleague and non-work support subscales in the survey.

The fourth theme, “Wanting a structured but non-punitive response,” captured nurses’ ideas for an ideal support system. Participants articulated a desire for clear procedures

after adverse events that would include immediate emotional first aid, confidential opportunities to talk to trained peers or counsellors, and follow-up to assess ongoing impact. Crucially, they stressed that any formal support programme must be embedded in a just, learning-oriented culture. Several nurses suggested brief, regular debriefings at the end of operating lists, not only after major events, to normalise discussion of difficulties. Others proposed that OR managers receive training in how to respond to second victims in ways that are empathetic and constructive rather than purely disciplinary.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study provides multi-centre evidence on second victim experiences among OR nurses in tertiary hospitals in Shanxi and Henan provinces. Quantitatively, more than four-fifths of participants reported at least one second victim experience, and nearly two-thirds reported such an event within the previous year. Psychological distress was the highest-scoring symptom domain, and the second victim impact index was moderate overall, with around one fifth of nurses falling into a high-impact category. More frequent recent events and perceived primary responsibility were associated with higher impact scores, whereas greater OR experience, perceived organisational support, just-culture perceptions and career resilience were associated with lower impact scores. The impact index and emotional exhaustion were, in turn, strongly associated with high intention to leave, whereas organisational support and career resilience were associated with lower odds of this outcome. The qualitative findings remained closely aligned with these results: nurses often described carrying the event alone in blame-oriented environments, while peer solidarity and constructive leadership responses were described as important resources.

Comparison with previous literature

The prevalence of second victim experiences in this study was high and broadly consistent with the upper range reported in prior nursing literature. A scoping review suggested that approximately 30–40% of nurses report recent second victim phenomena in many settings [6], whereas higher prevalences have also been described in more acute or higher-risk environments [24]. Our findings support the view that OR nurses are a particularly exposed group, likely because of the technical intensity, time pressure and interdependence of perioperative care [25]. The pattern of symptoms was also consistent with earlier C-SVEST-based work, with psychological distress scoring higher than physical distress. The qualitative theme of “carrying the event alone” adds contextual depth to this pattern, suggesting that fear of blame and judgement may help explain why distress remains highly salient for many nurses after such events [10, 15].

Our regression models identified perceived responsibility and recent event frequency as the strongest event-related correlates of higher second victim impact. This is consistent with the limited literature focused specifically on OR nurses in China, which has similarly linked responsibility and support to second victim symptom severity [11], and with wider evidence from mixed clinician samples [26]. In addition, greater OR experience was modestly associated with lower impact scores after adjustment for other factors, suggesting that clinical exposure and adaptation to the perioperative environment may be related to more favourable responses to adverse events, even if they do not eliminate vulnerability.

We also found a strong relationship between second victim distress, emotional exhaustion and intention to leave. This is in line with growing evidence that second victim-related distress is closely tied to workforce instability and burnout-related outcomes among nurses [16, 27]. In our sample, the prevalence of high intention to leave was 40.0% under the primary, more sensitive definition and 16.3% under the stricter mean-score definition; importantly, the direction of associations was unchanged across these definitions. This strengthens confidence that the observed relationships are not simply an artefact of a single operationalisation of turnover intention, while also showing that the absolute prevalence of “high intention to leave” depends on how this outcome is defined.

The observed associations for POS and just culture were consistent with prior research linking safety culture and fair incident management with lower staff distress [25, 28]. POS showed the largest standardised association with impact (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta {\rm{ = - 0}}{\rm{.29}}$$\end{document}), followed by just culture (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta {\rm{ = - 0}}{\rm{.24}}$$\end{document}). Qualitative accounts indicated that these associations were experienced through leader behaviour after incidents rather than through policy documents alone. Importantly, because the design was cross-sectional, these findings indicate co-occurrence rather than directional effects; for example, distressed nurses may perceive organisational responses as less supportive.

Interpretation within the JD-R framework

The regression results and descriptive patterns can be read as a perioperative second-victim pathway within JD-R theory [12, 13]. Job demands included exposure to adverse events (frequency and responsibility attribution) and the resulting second victim impact index (psychological and physical symptoms together with reduced professional confidence). Work resources comprised POS (general organisational care) and just-culture perceptions (fairness after incidents). Personal resources were represented by career resilience. Outcomes included emotional exhaustion (strain) and intention to leave (withdrawal-related behaviour).

In this cross-sectional sample, demands and resources showed expected associations: event frequency and responsibility were positively associated with impact scores, whereas POS, just culture and career resilience were inversely associated. Impact and exhaustion were, in turn, associated with higher intention to leave, while POS and resilience were associated with lower intention to leave. This pattern is consistent with JD-R propositions that limited resources in the face of high demands coincide with strain and withdrawal cognitions [8, 12], but causal or mediational pathways cannot be confirmed without longitudinal data.

Two conceptual overlaps warrant explicit discussion. First, POS and just culture correlated moderately (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r{\rm{ = 0}}{\rm{.52}}$$\end{document}) because fair incident management is one way hospitals demonstrate that staff are valued; yet just culture also captures blame norms and consistency of investigation that POS items do not fully measure. Their independent associations with impact scores suggest that general organisational care and event-specific fairness are related but not redundant. Second, C-SVEST organisational support and POS correlated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r{\rm{ = 0}}{\rm{.65}}$$\end{document}, reflecting overlap between event-targeted and hospital-wide support; only POS was modelled to avoid statistical duplication with C-SVEST-based impact scores and to align with JD-R work-resource definitions [14].

Career resilience was moderately high (mean 3.34) while impact scores were also moderate (mean 3.20). Qualitative data clarified this tension: resilience appeared to buffer distress when organisational resources were present, but was insufficient when nurses felt blamed or exposed (Themes 1 and 2). Peer support, although not entered into regression models, emerged as a critical informal resource (Theme 3) that hospitals could build on when designing formal pathways.

Implications for practice and policy

The close association between second victim impact and turnover intention suggests that staff support could be considered part of workforce sustainability and patient-safety strategy rather than a peripheral wellness initiative [17]. Hospitals may benefit from evaluating OR-specific support pathways, drawing on frameworks such as the AORN Second Victim Tool Kit [29], that include immediate emotional first aid, confidential peer support and clear routes to professional counselling. Beyond formal protocols, the broader organisational context also warrants attention. In particular, leaders may benefit from training in empathic communication and just-culture principles so that incident reviews are experienced as fair and learning-oriented rather than punitive [15]. Routine debriefing after operating lists, not only after major events, has been suggested as a way to normalise discussion of difficulty and reduce silence after adverse events.

Furthermore, given the importance of colleague support in both the survey and interview data, formalising peer-support roles may be a practical way to build on support that nurses already perceive as meaningful. Training experienced OR nurses to provide basic psychological first aid and structured follow-up could operationalise this support while maintaining professional boundaries [30]. At an individual level, orientation and continuing education programmes for perioperative nurses could more explicitly address the emotional realities of adverse events and provide coping strategies that have been associated with greater career resilience early in professional development.

Implications for research

Future research should move beyond cross-sectional designs to longitudinal studies tracking how distress, exhaustion and turnover intention evolve over time. Intervention studies are needed to evaluate whether peer-support programmes, leadership training and just-culture-informed response pathways improve staff outcomes in the Chinese perioperative context [9]. Comparative work across hospitals and regions could also clarify how organisational culture shapes second victim experiences and recovery trajectories.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the multi-centre design across ten hospitals, the relatively high survey return rate, and the mixed-methods approach that integrated statistical modelling with lived experience. The quantitative component was strengthened by the use of validated instruments, transparent reporting of model assumptions, and sensitivity analyses showing that the main inferences were robust to hospital-clustered standard errors and to an alternative definition of high intention to leave. The qualitative phase enhanced trustworthiness through iterative analysis, participant feedback and reflexive journaling.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the survey prevents causal inference; for example, emotional exhaustion may influence reports of second victim impact just as second victim impact may co-occur with exhaustion. Perceived responsibility should also be interpreted cautiously because nurses with greater ongoing distress may retrospectively assign more responsibility to themselves. Second, the study relied on self-report measures, which are susceptible to recall bias, social desirability bias and common method variance. Asking participants to describe their most memorable event may have amplified recall of emotionally salient or unresolved events, and reported reactions may have been influenced by subsequent recovery or later organisational responses. Third, although the second victim impact index summarised psychological distress, physical distress and reduced professional self-efficacy, professional self-efficacy is conceptually distinct from symptom distress and is closer to a personal resource in JD-R terms. The index should therefore be interpreted as a pragmatic, exploratory indicator rather than as a validated C-SVEST total score; exploratory one-factor analysis supported unidimensionality only partially (47.6% variance explained), and confirmatory factor analysis was not pre-specified. Sensitivity analyses excluding nurses who denied second victim status (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n{\rm{ = 450}}$$\end{document}) and separate reporting of the three subscales support cautious interpretation (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Fourth, while the qualitative component added explanatory depth, interviews were conducted at a single time point, translated quotations may involve minor semantic loss and participation depended on respondents voluntarily agreeing to be contacted. Nurses who agreed to be interviewed may have differed from those who did not in distress level, coping style or attitudes toward organisational support, although maximum-variation sampling was used to include a range of experiences among those eligible. Fifth, the sample was restricted to two provinces in north-central China, which may limit transferability to settings with different organisational resources, surgical case mix or institutional cultures. Sixth, POS, just culture and C-SVEST organisational support showed moderate inter-correlations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r{\rm{ = 0}}{\rm{.52}}$$\end{document}); although VIF diagnostics did not suggest severe multicollinearity and constructs were distinguished theoretically, some conceptual overlap is likely. Seventh, because only 10 hospitals were included, more complex multilevel modelling was not pursued; although clustered sensitivity analyses suggested that the linear-model inferences were stable and between-hospital variance in the impact index was low, residual clustering in other outcomes cannot be ruled out. Finally, the primary definition of high intention to leave was intentionally sensitive and combined intentions to leave the OR, the hospital or the nursing profession, which are substantively different outcomes. The stricter mean-score sensitivity analysis produced a lower prevalence estimate but similar directions of association, so the findings should be interpreted as evidence about turnover-related concern rather than a precise estimate of any single form of intended leaving.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study shows that second victim experiences are common among OR nurses in tertiary general hospitals in Shanxi and Henan and that these experiences are characterised particularly by psychological symptoms, physical reactions and reduced professional self-efficacy. More frequent recent events and perceiving primary responsibility were associated with higher second victim impact scores, while organisational support, just-culture perceptions, career resilience and, to a lesser extent, greater OR experience were associated with lower impact scores. The impact index and emotional exhaustion were also strongly associated with intention to leave perioperative nursing.

The qualitative findings indicate that many OR nurses cope with adverse events in silence, fearing blame and questioning their competence, while also highlighting the value of peer solidarity and the need for structured, non-punitive support pathways. Together, these results suggest that second victim experiences among OR nurses in China are closely connected to staff well-being, workforce stability and the quality of perioperative organisational culture. Developing and evaluating just-culture-aligned, perioperative-specific support systems that combine organisational commitment, trained peer support and resilience-building strategies may be a useful direction for future practice and research.
