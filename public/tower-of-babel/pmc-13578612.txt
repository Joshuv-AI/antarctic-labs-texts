Effectiveness of Nursing and Non‐Pharmacological Interventions in Reducing Intradialytic Hypotension Among Haemodialysis Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Mi‐Kyoung Cho, Yoon Hee Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of non‐pharmacological interventions in reducing intradialytic hypotension among adult patients undergoing haemodialysis and to examine their implications for nursing practice.DesignSystematic review and meta‐analysis.Data SourcesPubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, SCOPUS, DBpia and RISS were searched for studies published between January 2000 and July 2025.Review MethodsRandomized controlled trials and quasi‐experimental studies evaluating non‐pharmacological interventions were included. Methodological quality was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Random‐effects meta‐analysis and subgroup analyses were conducted and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE.ResultsSixteen studies were included in the meta‐analysis. Non‐pharmacological interventions were associated with modest improvements in systolic blood pressure during haemodialysis, although substantial heterogeneity was observed. Dialysis technology‐based interventions showed a significant improvement in systolic blood pressure, whereas nurse‐led interventions showed a numerically similar effect but did not reach statistical significance. The overall certainty of evidence was very low.ConclusionNon‐pharmacological interventions may support haemodynamic stability during haemodialysis; however, improvement in systolic blood pressure represents a surrogate haemodynamic outcome and does not necessarily indicate a reduced incidence of intradialytic hypotension. Further well‐designed studies using clinically meaningful and patient‐centred outcomes are needed.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareNurses can contribute to intradialytic hypotension prevention through assessment, monitoring, early recognition of haemodynamic instability, timely bedside interventions and patient education.ImpactThe findings highlight the potential complementary roles of nurse‐led care and dialysis technology‐based approaches in the individualized management of adults undergoing haemodialysis.Reporting MethodPRISMA 2020.No Patient or Public ContributionPatients or members of the public were not involved because this study was a systematic review and meta‐analysis of previously published studies.Trial and Protocol RegistrationPROSPERO (CRD420251150743)

Introduction

Intradialytic hypotension (IDH) is one of the most common complications among haemodialysis patients, occurring in approximately 15%–50% of all dialysis sessions (Shoji et al. 2004; Flythe et al. 2015). Beyond causing transient discomfort, IDH can result in serious clinical consequences, including the accumulation of metabolic toxins due to inadequate dialysis, a decline in residual renal function, myocardial ischemia and neurological injury caused by cerebral hypoperfusion (Shoji et al. 2004; McIntyre 2010; Burton et al. 2009). Recurrent episodes of hypotension further increase patients' fatigue and anxiety, often necessitating early termination of dialysis sessions or adjustment of ultrafiltration volume, thereby reducing overall treatment efficiency (Shoji et al. 2004; Flythe et al. 2015; Lindsay et al. 2006; Kanbay et al. 2020). Over time, these complications contribute to cardiovascular morbidity, higher hospitalization rates and increased mortality (Shoji et al. 2004). Consequently, IDH has been recognized as a major determinant of both prognosis and health‐related quality of life in haemodialysis patients (Lindsay et al. 2006; Kanbay et al. 2020; Wang, Yao, et al. 2023).

Although systolic blood pressure (SBP) is the most commonly reported hemodynamic outcome in studies evaluating interventions for IDH, it represents a surrogate marker of hemodynamic stability rather than a direct measure of clinically important outcomes, such as the incidence of IDH episodes, treatment interruption or patient quality of life (Kanbay et al. 2020; Wang, Yao, et al. 2023). Nevertheless, SBP remains the most consistently reported hemodynamic outcome in intervention studies of IDH and therefore provides a practical and comparable measure for quantitative synthesis in meta‐analysis (Kanbay et al. 2020).

The pathophysiology of IDH is multifactorial (Habas et al. 2025). Excessive fluid removal during haemodialysis leads to a reduction in plasma volume, while inadequate cardiovascular compensatory responses further contribute to the development of hypotension. In addition, various physiological and environmental factors—including impaired autonomic function, underlying cardiovascular disease, postprandial state, vasodilation and high dialysate sodium concentration—interact to increase susceptibility to IDH (Habas et al. 2025). The condition occurs more frequently among older adults and patients with diabetic nephropathy, heart failure or autonomic neuropathy, owing to decreased vascular compliance and blunted sympathetic responses (Hryciw et al. 2021). Therefore, IDH cannot be explained by a single mechanism, and prevention of its occurrence should include both pharmacological and non‐pharmacological strategies.

Over the years, various strategies have been implemented to prevent IDH in clinical settings. Pharmacological approaches such as vasopressors (e.g., midodrine) and plasma expanders (e.g., albumin, mannitol) have been used (Kanbay et al. 2020; Hryciw et al. 2021), but their potential side effects and cost have hindered their widespread use (Kanbay et al. 2020; Costa‐Pinto et al. 2023). In contrast, non‐pharmacological interventions have been studied as safer and more sustainable approaches from both nursing and technical perspectives. Several systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have examined specific non‐pharmacological strategies for IDH, such as cold dialysate (Subhash et al. 2025), sodium profiling (Dunne 2017) or biofeedback‐assisted dialysis (Mambelli et al. 2024). However, these reviews have typically focused on single technical or device‐based approaches and have not comprehensively synthesized the full spectrum of non‐pharmacological interventions. Furthermore, existing evidence syntheses have not explicitly distinguished interventions initiated, delivered or managed by nurses during routine haemodialysis care.

Despite these diverse approaches, comprehensive reviews of the effectiveness of non‐pharmacological interventions for preventing IDH remain inadequate (Kanbay et al. 2020; Hamrahian et al. 2023). Most existing studies have been conducted at single centres with small sample sizes, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Moreover, heterogeneity in intervention methods, intensity, timing and outcome measures has led to inconsistent results across studies (Mc Causland and Charytan 2024). Many of these studies were also quasi‐experimental in design, lacking methodological rigour such as randomization and blinding, which further limits the strength of the evidence (Hamrahian et al. 2023; Peyronel et al. 2020).

From a nursing perspective, IDH is not merely a physiological phenomenon. The occurrence of hypotension during haemodialysis is closely related to nurses' clinical judgement, intervention skills and monitoring ability, and its prevention constitutes a core component of nursing practice (Ozen and Cepken 2020; McIntyre et al. 2021). Therefore, it is essential to comprehensively examine IDH prevention strategies from a nursing perspective and to establish a foundation for evidence‐based practice (Peyronel et al. 2020).

Against this background, the present study aims to systematically analyse the effectiveness of interventions for preventing hypotension in haemodialysis patients by synthesizing findings from randomized and quasi‐experimental studies. Furthermore, it seeks to examine the effects according to intervention type and study design, thereby providing clinically applicable evidence for future practice. In particular, this study is significant in that it comprehensively compares and analyzes various non‐pharmacological interventions, offering a scientific foundation for integrated nursing and dialysis care aimed at improving patient safety and treatment efficiency.

In this review, nonpharmacological interventions refer to all strategies that prevent or alleviate IDH without the use of medications, including dialysis regimen modifications, device‐based approaches and behavioural or physical interventions. For the purposes of this review, nurse‐led interventions were defined as nonpharmacological interventions that nurses independently initiate, directly deliver or continuously manage during haemodialysis sessions. These include patient assessment and monitoring, positioning, application of compression devices, patient education and symptom‐triggered responses based on clinical judgement. These interventions highlight the role of nursing practice within the broader category of nonpharmacological strategies.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

The population was restricted to adults (≥ 18 years) receiving maintenance haemodialysis for at least 3 months. Eligible interventions comprised non‐pharmacological strategies intended to reduce the occurrence or severity of IDH. The Comparison groups included participants receiving standard care, alternative interventions not classified as non‐pharmacological or sham procedures. The primary outcome was SBP, while secondary outcomes included intradialytic reduction in SBP, mean arterial pressure (MAP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), intradialytic weight gain (IDWG) and symptom scores related to IDH. Given the heterogeneity in the definitions of IDH across the included studies, this review adopted author‐defined criteria for IDH, typically involving symptoms and/or SBP thresholds (e.g., ≥ 20 mmHg drops or nadir SBP < 90 mmHg), without applying a uniform operational definition. For studies with more than one intervention arm (study IDs: 3, 10, 12, 13 and 16), each intervention group was analysed independently to compute individual effect sizes. Both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi‐experimental studies were eligible because non‐pharmacological interventions for IDH have been evaluated using both designs in clinical haemodialysis settings. Quantitative synthesis was limited to studies providing sufficient information to derive an effect size, including sample sizes, means, standard deviations or other relevant statistics. Ineligible studies were those involving: (1) participants younger than 18 years; (2) patients with acute renal failure or those receiving peritoneal dialysis; (3) interventions involving pharmacological treatment (e.g., midodrine or sertraline) or studies without blood pressure outcomes; (4) an effect estimate could not be derived from the reported data; (5) full text was unavailable, the report was an unpublished manuscript or conference abstract or the publication language was neither English nor Korean; or (6) only the incidence or frequency of IDH was reported, without pre‐ and post‐intervention blood pressure measurements.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Six electronic databases—PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, SCOPUS, DBpia and RISS—were searched for relevant studies published from January 1, 2000, through July 31, 2025. The search was restricted to publications in English or Korean. The database search was carried out from September 19 to October 2, 2025. The search strategy was designed to identify experimental studies evaluating the effects of non‐pharmacological interventions on blood pressure among patients receiving haemodialysis. For PubMed, MeSH terms and free‐text keywords were combined with the Boolean operators AND and OR. Search concepts were organized around the PICO‐SD components: (1) Population: “renal dialysis”[MeSH Terms] OR “haemodialysis”[Other Term] OR “dialysis”[Other Term]; Intervention (I): “Nursing Care”[MeSH Terms] OR “Patient Care Planning”[MeSH Terms] OR “non‐pharmacological”[Other Term] OR “non‐drug”[Other Term] OR “cool dialysate”[Other Term] OR “dialysate temperature”[Other Term] OR “ultrafiltration profiling”[Other Term] OR “UF profiling”[Other Term] OR “sodium modelling”[Other Term] OR “positioning”[Other Term] OR “biofeedback”[Other Term] OR “exercise”[Other Term] OR “stretching”[Other Term] OR “aromatherapy”[Other Term]; Outcome (O): “hypotension”[MeSH Terms] OR “intradialytic hypotension”[Other Term] OR “dialysis hypotension”[Other Term] OR “IDH”[Other Term]; Study Design (SD): “randomized controlled trial”[MeSH Terms] OR “Non‐Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic”[MeSH Terms] OR “clinical trial”[MeSH Terms] OR “Controlled Clinical Trial”[MeSH Terms]. The search was limited to human studies involving adults and older adults, published in English or Korean between January 1, 2000 and July 31, 2025. Search terms were tailored for each database to ensure compatibility with its indexing schema and retrieval functions. Database‐specific search details are presented in Table S1. Both authors contributed to the development and implementation of the searches. The review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251150743), where the protocol record is publicly available: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251150743.

Selection Process

Study selection followed PRISMA 2020 (Page et al. 2021). Titles and abstracts of records retrieved from the databases were independently reviewed by two reviewers for potential eligibility. Differences in screening decisions were settled through discussion, with a third reviewer involved when agreement could not be achieved. Screening and selection were managed manually in Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The software was used to manage records, remove duplicates and organize citations for screening. Records retained after title and abstract screening were subsequently reviewed in full text to determine their eligibility. Reference lists of pertinent systematic reviews were also examined for additional studies not retrieved through the database searches. Study selection was performed without automated or machine‐learning–based screening tools. Figure 1 summarizes the study selection pathway in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data Collection Process and Data Items

Extracted information was organized in a structured Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). One reviewer entered the data into the extraction form, and a second reviewer independently checked the entries for accuracy. Data collection covered four categories: study characteristics (publication year, country, centre, study design, IRB approval and funding), sample characteristics (total N and group allocation), intervention characteristics (type, duration, number of sessions, outcome measurement timing and control group intervention) and outcome variables. For meta‐analysis, post‐intervention means, SDs and group‐specific Ns were collected for SBP (primary) and for secondary outcomes including intradialytic reduction in SBP, MAP, DBP, IDWG and symptom scores. SBP was designated as the primary outcome because it provided sufficient continuous data across studies for quantitative evaluation of the hemodynamic response to non‐pharmacological interventions using standardized mean differences.

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was appraised with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists specific to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (Barker et al. 2023) and quasi‐experimental studies (Quasi‐E) (Tanriver‐Ayder et al. 2021). Prior to formal appraisal, two reviewers reviewed the guidance notes and reached consensus on the interpretation of each item. To ensure consistency in scoring, one RCT and one Quasi‐E study were independently evaluated as pilot cases. The two reviewers subsequently completed separate risk‐of‐bias appraisals. The JBI checklists contain 13 items for RCTs and nine for Quasi‐E studies, each evaluated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable.” For scoring purposes, a “Yes” response was assigned one point, whereas all other responses received zero points. Item scores were then summed to derive an overall quality score for each study.

Effect Measures

Hedges' g was selected as the effect measure, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported for each estimate (Barker et al. 2024; Tanriver‐Ayder et al. 2021). Hedges' g was selected due to its bias correction, which was particularly advantageous when synthesizing results from trials with relatively small sample sizes (Murad et al. 2019). For each comparison, the effect estimate was derived from post‐intervention data.

Synthesis Methods

Pooled estimates were obtained under a random‐effects framework. Between‐study variance (τ 2) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) (Murad et al. 2019). Primary analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Hedges' g was used as the standardized mean difference (SMD), and each estimate was accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (CI) (Lin and Aloe 2021). Hedges' g was chosen to reduce small‐sample bias (Lin and Aloe 2021; Taylor and Alanazi 2023). For multi‐arm studies in which intervention arms shared a common control group, each arm was analysed as a separate comparison. To account for the statistical dependency introduced by shared control participants, the variance (vi) for comparisons originating from the same study was adjusted in accordance with recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook. This adjustment was implemented using the metafor package (version 4.4–0, Maastricht University, Netherlands) in R, which was used exclusively for variance correction in multi‐arm studies and for calculating 95% confidence intervals for heterogeneity indices. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (Flechner and Tseng 2011). Heterogeneity was characterized using I 2 and Cochran's Q (Ruppar 2020), with the magnitude of I 2 interpreted according to the reference values proposed by Higgins et al. (Ruppar 2020). The 95% confidence intervals for I 2 were estimated using the meta package in R (version 4.5.1). Subgroup analyses and meta‐regression were conducted to identify potential sources of the observed heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to prespecified study characteristics, including publication year, region, intervention type, study design and methodological quality. Study design was included to examine the contribution of differences between RCTs and Quasi‐E studies to heterogeneity. Additionally, to explore whether intervention delivery characteristics influenced treatment effects, interventions were further classified into nurse‐led interventions and dialysis technology‐based interventions. Nurse‐led interventions were defined as interventions that could be initiated and delivered by dialysis nurses without modification of dialysis prescriptions or dialysis machine settings. Dialysis technology‐based interventions were defined as interventions requiring modification of dialysis machine settings, dialysis prescriptions or physician‐prescribed dialysis treatment. The same REML random‐effects approach was retained for subgroup estimates. Sensitivity analysis comprised three approaches: (1) sequential omission of individual studies (leave‐one‐out analysis); (2) reanalysis after removal of outliers detected with the Galbraith plot; and (3) comparison of the primary REML estimate with estimates from the DerSimonian–Laird random‐effects and fixed‐effects models.

Reporting Bias Assessment

Assessment of publication bias was restricted to the primary outcome (SBP), for which more than 10 studies were available. Funnel plot asymmetry was examined graphically and with Egger's regression test. Duval and Tweedie's trim‐and‐fill procedure was additionally used to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies on the pooled effect (Duval and Tweedie 2000).

Certainty of Evidence

Evidence certainty was determined for each outcome according to the GRADE framework (Guyatt et al. 2008). Ratings were informed by five domains—risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias—and assigned to one of four levels: high, moderate, low or very low. Two reviewers completed the GRADE evaluation independently, with differences in judgement settled by consensus.

Results

Study Selection

Screening was therefore undertaken for 582 records, of which 547 did not proceed beyond title and abstract review. The remaining 35 records underwent full‐text review, and 19 were removed because the reported data did not permit calculation of an effect size. The quantitative synthesis consequently comprised 16 articles. Five articles (study IDs 3, 10, 12, 13 and 16) contributed more than one eligible intervention arm; these arms were analysed separately, resulting in 26 effect‐size comparisons. Figure 1 summarizes the selection process in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Study Characteristics

The meta‐analysis comprised 16 studies published from 2000 to 2025; nine appeared after 2016 and ten originated from Asian countries. Twelve studies recruited participants from a single centre, while 15 reported Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval. Twelve studies disclosed receiving external funding. Study designs comprised four quasi‐experimental crossover studies, six crossover RCTs and six parallel‐group RCTs. Eight studies included sample sizes of 50 or more participants. The types of interventions varied: three studies applied dialysate cooling, six utilized sodium profiling and seven investigated other non‐pharmacological strategies such as adjusting dialysis frequency, UF profiling, acupoint or exercise‐based interventions. The methodological quality scores ranged from 6 to 11 (Table 1).

Risk of Bias in Studies

Of the 16 studies, 12 were RCTs, with a mean JBI quality score of 9.33 on the 13‐item appraisal tool. The highest score observed was 11 and the lowest was 7. The most frequently satisfied items were Item 11 (intention‐to‐treat analysis) and Item 12 (appropriate statistical analysis), both of which were reported in 11 of the 12 RCTs. In contrast, Item 5, concerning blinding of intervention deliverers, was unclear across all RCTs. Only four RCTs met the criterion for allocation concealment (Item 2) and three for participant blinding (Item 4). Four studies were quasi‐experimental designs, with a mean quality score of 7.25 out of 9 (range: 6–8). Most of the items were generally well addressed, except for Item 8 (reliable outcome measurement), which was not reported in any of the quasi‐experimental studies (Table 2).

Results of Syntheses

Effect of Non‐Pharmacological Interventions on SBP

The primary analysis of SBP comprised 11 studies, providing 17 comparisons of non‐pharmacological interventions for IDH prevention during haemodialysis. SBP was significantly higher following non‐pharmacological intervention than in the control condition (Hedges' g = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.56; p = 0.015). Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 95.29, df = 16, p < 0.001; I 2 = 83.0%, 95% CI: 74.1 to 88.9) (Figure 2). Across studies, adverse effects directly attributable to the interventions were infrequently reported. When noted, they were generally mild (e.g., thirst, cold sensation, cramping) and transient, suggesting that the interventions were overall well tolerated.

Subgroup Analysis of Non‐Pharmacological Interventions on SBP According to Study Characteristics

Given the substantial heterogeneity, subgroup analyses examined variation in effect estimates by publication year, region, number of study centres, IRB approval, funding status, study design, sample size, intervention type, intervention duration, number of sessions, timing of outcome measurement, comparator type and methodological quality.

The pooled estimates favoured the intervention in studies published after 2016 (Hedges' g = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.34 to 0.85), studies conducted in Asia (Hedges' g = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33 to 0.72), and single‐centre studies (Hedges' g = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.65). Estimates were also significant for studies with IRB approval (Hedges' g = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.62; p = 0.024) and those with no funding or with funding status not reported (Hedges' g = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.73; p = 0.003). Across study‐design categories, statistical significance was reached only for quasi‐experimental crossover studies (Hedges' g = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.62; p = 0.008).

For nurse‐led interventions, Hedges' g was 0.30, with a 95% CI from −0.07 to 0.66 and no observed heterogeneity (I 2 = 0.0%). For dialysis technology‐based interventions, Hedges' g was 0.34, with a 95% CI from 0.01 to 0.68; heterogeneity was high (I 2 = 86.9%).

In terms of intervention types, significant effects were found in studies evaluating increased dialysis frequency, intermittent haemodiafiltration (IHDF) and UF profiling, although a single study represented each. In contrast, cooling dialysate, sodium profiling and stimulation‐based interventions (e.g., exercise, acupoint stimulation) did not yield statistically significant pooled effects.

For interventions lasting less than 2 months, Hedges' g was 0.39 (95% CI: 0.13 to 0.66). The estimate was 0.45 for interventions delivered in fewer than 12 sessions (95% CI: 0.23 to 0.67) and 0.28 for outcomes assessed immediately after intervention (95% CI: 0.08 to 0.48). For studies using another non‐pharmacological intervention as the comparator, Hedges' g was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.29 to 0.88) (Table 3).

To evaluate the robustness of the results, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed, including the leave‐one‐out method, exclusion of outliers identified through the Galbraith plot and the use of alternative meta‐analytic models (Table 5). Sequential omission of individual studies produced no material change in the pooled estimate (Table 5). Five comparisons (study IDs 1, 3b, 3c, 6 and 10a) fell outside the 95% confidence limits on the Galbraith plot (Figure 3). Reanalysis without these comparisons yielded a slightly smaller effect, which remained statistically significant and retained the same direction. Reporting Biases

For SBP, the funnel plot showed no marked asymmetry (Figure 4). Egger's regression yielded an intercept of −0.43 (t = −1.40, p = 0.183), providing no statistical indication of funnel plot asymmetry (Table 7). The trim‐and‐fill analysis estimated no missing studies; consequently, the adjusted Hedges' g remained 0.31 (95% CI: 0.06 to 0.56), identical to the original estimate. Thus, the analyses provided no indication of publication bias for the pooled SBP estimate. Publication bias was not examined for the secondary outcomes because fewer than 10 studies contributed to these analyses. Discussion

This meta‐analysis systematically synthesized evidence from randomized and quasi‐experimental studies to evaluate the effectiveness of non‐pharmacological interventions in preventing IDH among haemodialysis patients. The interventions examined in this review included cool dialysate, sodium and UF profiling, biofeedback dialysis and nurse‐led approaches. The findings indicated that non‐pharmacological interventions were associated with a statistically significant improvement in SBP during haemodialysis (Hedges' g = 0.31, 95% CI 0.06–0.56, p = 0.015). The use of SBP as the primary outcome was based on its consistent reporting across the included studies, which enabled quantitative comparison of intervention effects. However, SBP represents a surrogate hemodynamic outcome and its improvement does not necessarily indicate a reduced incidence of IDH. Although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I 2 = 83.0%), the overall pooled effect favoured the intervention groups. Nevertheless, this heterogeneity reduces confidence in the pooled effect estimates and suggests that the overall findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

According to previous studies, cooling the dialysate induces peripheral vasoconstriction and helps maintain blood pressure during haemodialysis. Subhash et al. (Subhash et al. 2025) also demonstrated in their meta‐analysis that cool dialysate reduced the incidence of IDH and improved SBP stability, which aligns with the present findings. However, some studies also reported adverse reactions, such as shivering and discomfort among elderly patients (Subhash et al. 2025). These adverse effects may be attributed to variations in target dialysate temperature, thermoregulatory capacity and intervention duration. These findings suggest that individualized dialysate temperature adjustment based on patient tolerance is warranted.

UF and sodium profiling were also found to have beneficial effects on blood pressure control, consistent with the results of Ertuglu et al. (Ertuglu et al. 2021) and Kanbay et al. (Kanbay et al. 2020). These strategies alleviate rapid fluid shifts and osmotic disequilibrium, contributing to circulatory stability. Furthermore, biofeedback dialysis—which continuously monitors changes in blood volume—was associated with improved cardiac output and reduced IDH incidence, corroborating the findings of Mambelli et al. (Mambelli et al. 2024).

Subgroup analyses revealed that IHDF and UF profiling were particularly effective, whereas interventions lasting more than 12 sessions did not show significant improvements. These findings imply that the duration and patient adaptation to interventions may influence treatment efficacy. In addition, subgroup analysis according to intervention delivery characteristics distinguished dialysis technology‐based interventions from nurse‐led interventions. Dialysis technology‐based interventions demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in SBP, whereas nurse‐led interventions showed a comparable effect size but did not reach statistical significance. Because only four studies with a relatively small sample size evaluated nurse‐led interventions, this finding may reflect limited precision resulting from the small number of studies and participants; therefore, the effectiveness of nurse‐led interventions remains uncertain. Nevertheless, nurse‐led interventions may complement dialysis technology‐based approaches through individualized assessment, continuous monitoring and timely bedside interventions during haemodialysis.

Differences in effect sizes were also observed across study characteristics. Recent studies and those conducted in Asian populations demonstrated relatively greater intervention effects. The enhanced effects in recent studies are likely attributable to advances in dialysis technology and improvements in clinical monitoring systems (Mitra et al. 2025). The adoption of automated sodium control systems, hemodynamic biofeedback devices and real‐time monitoring technologies may have increased the precision and adaptability of these interventions (Mitra et al. 2025).

In contrast, the greater effects observed in Asian studies may have resulted from a combination of demographic and physiological factors. First, demographic or physiological characteristics may differ between Asian and non‐Asian populations, potentially affecting relative hemodynamic responses to the same absolute UF rate (Assimon et al. 2016). Second, vascular reactivity and autonomic function may vary across populations due to genetic and environmental influences, which could contribute to differences in responsiveness to interventions (Yim et al. 2012). Future research should therefore explore how population‐specific and physiological factors influence the outcomes of non‐pharmacological strategies.

The findings of this study reaffirm that the pathophysiology of IDH is multifactorial (Kanbay et al. 2020; Habas et al. 2025). Excessive ultrafiltration rates that exceed plasma refill capacity result in intravascular volume reduction, a relationship supported by studies examining plasma refill rate and ultrafiltration dynamics during haemodialysis (Wang, Negoianu, et al. 2023; Sohn et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2024). Meanwhile, in elderly or diabetic patients, diminished compensatory vasoconstriction—likely due to impaired autonomic responsiveness and reduced vascular compliance—further compromises hemodynamic stability (Seravalle et al. 2021). These mechanisms highlight the importance of non‐pharmacological approaches as complementary strategies to pharmacologic treatment in addressing the hemodynamic causes of IDH.

Clinically, maintaining haemodynamic stability during dialysis is crucial for patient comfort and for reducing the risk of recurrent ischemic stress to the myocardium and brain. Burton et al. (Burton et al. 2009) and McIntyre (McIntyre 2010) demonstrated that repetitive IDH episodes induce subclinical ischemic damage, leading to progressive cardiac dysfunction and cognitive impairment. Thus, the moderate improvements in blood pressure stability observed in this meta‐analysis may support haemodynamic stability rather than direct clinical protection (Shoji et al. 2004; McIntyre 2010; Burton et al. 2009).

This systematic review and meta‐analysis adds new knowledge to the existing literature in several important ways. First, it synthesizes evidence across a broad range of non‐pharmacological interventions, explicitly incorporating interventions that are directly implementable by nurses during routine haemodialysis care. Second, by comparing effects across intervention types, study characteristics and implementation features, this review identifies conditions under which non‐pharmacological strategies are more likely to stabilize SBP. Third, the findings highlight the practical relevance of short‐term, targeted interventions that can be integrated into everyday nursing workflows. However, the clinical significance of SBP improvement should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should prioritize patient‐centred outcomes, including the frequency of IDH episodes, symptom severity and dialysis tolerance.

This study has several limitations. First, although both randomized and quasi‐experimental studies were included to ensure comprehensiveness, variability in study design and the lack of a standardized definition of intradialytic hypotension across studies may have contributed to the substantial heterogeneity observed in this meta‐analysis. In addition, quality appraisal indicated that several included studies had methodological limitations, particularly inadequate randomization, allocation concealment and blinding, which may have introduced bias and influenced the pooled effect estimates. Future research should adopt standardized diagnostic criteria and outcome measures for IDH and conduct well‐designed multicentre randomized controlled trials to improve methodological rigour and comparability across studies. Second, the diversity of intervention types and patient characteristics limited subgroup comparisons. Standardized reporting of key parameters such as dialysate temperature, sodium concentration and UF targets is necessary to enhance comparability across studies. Third, most included studies were conducted in single centres with relatively small sample sizes, which limits the generalizability of the findings across different dialysis settings and patient populations. Future studies should include large multicentre randomized controlled trials to improve the external validity and generalizability of the evidence. Moreover, details of nurse‐led interventions were often insufficiently described. Future research should incorporate specific nursing parameters, including timing, frequency and decision‐making processes. Fourth, most studies focused on short‐term hemodynamic outcomes, whereas evidence on long‐term endpoints—such as cardiovascular events, residual renal function and quality of life—remains scarce. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the sustained effects of non‐pharmacological interventions. Finally, although publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger's regression test and the trim‐and‐fill method, the relatively small number of included studies and substantial heterogeneity may have reduced the reliability of these methods. Therefore, the findings regarding publication bias should be interpreted with caution. Overall, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE approach, primarily because of methodological limitations, inconsistency and imprecision across the included studies.

Implication for Nursing Practice

The prevention of IDH is an important component of routine haemodialysis nursing care. Although dialysis technology‐based interventions demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in SBP in this review, their implementation generally depends on dialysis prescriptions and equipment settings. In contrast, nurse‐led interventions, including positional adjustments, compression stockings, relaxation therapy and herbal acupoint therapy, can be implemented directly at the bedside as part of routine nursing care (Marinovich et al. 2020; Bennett et al. 2015; Tsai et al. 2018). Nurses play a central role in preventing IDH through ongoing patient assessment, early recognition of hemodynamic instability, continuous monitoring during dialysis, timely bedside interventions and patient education (McIntyre et al. 2021; Kesik and Özdemir 2020). Integrating these nursing activities with dialysis technology‐based strategies may support individualized clinical decision‐making, patient management and safe haemodialysis care. Nursing education should strengthen competencies in early recognition of hemodynamic instability, patient assessment and timely intervention, while future research should further evaluate standardized nurse‐led interventions and their effects on clinically meaningful and patient‐centred outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta‐analysis found that non‐pharmacological interventions were associated with modest improvements in SBP during haemodialysis. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as improvements in blood pressure represent a surrogate hemodynamic outcome and do not necessarily indicate a reduced incidence of IDH. While selected short‐term and targeted interventions may support hemodynamic stability during dialysis sessions, further well‐designed studies are required to determine whether these strategies translate into clinically meaningful reductions in IDH episodes, symptom burden and patient‐centred outcomes. From a nursing perspective, these results highlight the potential value of integrating carefully selected non‐pharmacological strategies into routine dialysis care, accompanied by standardized protocols and ongoing evaluation of clinical effectiveness.
