Impact of breastfeeding education with WhatsApp follow-up on infant feeding, growth, and health outcomes: a quasi-experimental study in Malaysia
Authors: Halimah Jalil, Mei-Chan Chong, Muhammad Yazid Jalaludin, Li Ping Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExclusive breastfeeding (EBF) promotes optimal growth and health in infants. Despite national and global initiatives, EBF rates in Malaysia remain below the 70% target set for 2030. This study evaluated the impact of structured breastfeeding education supplemented with WhatsApp follow-up on infant feeding practices, growth, and health outcomes.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted at six public health clinics in Gombak District, Selangor. Mothers of healthy-term infants were assigned to either an intervention group (structured education with WhatsApp follow-up) or a comparison group (routine care). A total of 500 mother–infant pairs were enrolled. Data on feeding practices, anthropometric indicators, length-for-age (LAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-length z-scores (WLZ) and common illnesses, including upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), gastroenteritis (GE), contact dermatitis (CD), bowel disorders (BD), and other illnesses (OI), were collected at Months 1, 3, and 6. Analyses included descriptive statistics, linear mixed-effects models, and generalised logistic mixed models.ResultsOf the 602 mothers screened, 500 were enrolled across six purposively selected clinics, and 460 (220 in the comparison group and 240 in the intervention group) were included in the final analysis. Baseline maternal and infant characteristics were comparable between groups. EBF prevalence was initially higher in the comparison group but increased more significantly in the intervention group by Month 6. Although some baseline z-scores were negative, descriptive trends showed modest, non-significant improvements in LAZ, WAZ, and WLZ over time, indicating overall upward growth trajectories. Linear mixed-effects models confirmed significant time effects, but no group-by-time interactions were observed. Illness prevalence decreased with age, with significant reductions observed for GE and OI and a borderline improvement for URTI in the intervention group. Overall, the intervention improved EBF practices and selected morbidity outcomes but did not significantly alter growth trajectories.ConclusionsStructured breastfeeding education, combined with WhatsApp follow-up, effectively enhanced exclusive breastfeeding practices and reduced certain infant illnesses, but showed no significant short-term effects on growth. Longer-term follow-up is warranted to assess the sustained effects on growth and health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01796-w.

Background

Proper nutrition during infancy is crucial for optimal growth, health, and development [1, 2]. Infant feeding practices are essential determinants of early-life nutrition, significantly impacting both short-term and long-term health outcomes [3–6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) as feeding infants only breast milk directly or expressed, without any additional food or drink (not even water), except for oral rehydration solutions or drops/syrups of vitamins, minerals, or medicine during the first six months of life [7]. EBF provides substantial benefits, including optimal nutrition, stronger immunity, and improved developmental outcomes such as cognitive and motor skills in early life [8–10]. It also fosters stronger maternal–infant bonding by enhancing maternal responsiveness and interaction [11]. In contrast, partial or non-breastfeeding is associated with higher risks of diarrhoea, respiratory infections, and infant mortality [12, 13]. Beyond infant health, breastfeeding also protects mothers by lowering their risk of breast and ovarian cancers [14, 15].

Global initiatives, such as the Baby-Friendly Clinic Initiative (BFCI), aim to promote EBF by fostering supportive environments in community healthcare settings. However, in Malaysia, including districts such as Gombak, EBF rates remain suboptimal. As of 2016, only 47.1% of mothers reported practising EBF, falling short of the national target of 70% by 2030, in line with the WHO’s global nutrition goals [16]. Recent studies in Malaysia have identified several contributing factors, including unsupportive workplace environments, early maternal return to work, perceived insufficient milk supply, sociocultural beliefs supporting early supplementation, and inadequate community-level support [17, 18]. Additional barriers include inconsistent implementation of BFCI steps at the primary care level and limited awareness of the International Code of Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes [19, 20]. These challenges highlight the need for targeted, community-based interventions to strengthen breastfeeding support beyond hospitals.

The Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) has demonstrated positive impacts on breastfeeding outcomes. Nevertheless, some studies have reported short-term increases in 30-day infant readmissions following implementation, mainly due to early breastfeeding-related issues such as dehydration or hyperbilirubinaemia. These effects were transient; one-year readmission rates did not increase, and emergency department visits declined over the first year of life [21].

To complement facility-based strategies, structured health education has emerged as a promising intervention to promote and sustain EBF. Evidence indicates programmes delivered during antenatal and postnatal care enhance maternal knowledge, self-efficacy, and breastfeeding skills. A systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that theory-driven educational interventions significantly improved EBF practices up to six months postpartum [22]. Similarly, a randomised trial in Ethiopia showed that targeted maternal education led to higher rates of EBF compared to standard care [23]. In Malaysia, where primary healthcare clinics are central providers of maternal and child services, integrating structured health education into routine care may help close the EBF gap and improve infant outcomes at the population level.

Despite the well-documented benefits of EBF, some mothers practise non-exclusive breastfeeding (NEBF) due to medical conditions, personal choice, or social barriers. Public breastfeeding campaigns have also faced criticism for creating pressure and inducing guilt among women who cannot or choose not to breastfeed [24]. These concerns highlight the need for balanced interventions that promote optimal infant nutrition while respecting maternal autonomy [25].

Considering these factors, this study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured health education programme in improving infant feeding practices, growth outcomes, and overall health status in a community health setting.

Primary hypothesis

H₀ (Null Hypothesis)

Structured health education has no significant effect on infant feeding practices, growth indicators on length-for-age z-score (LAZ), weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), and weight-for-length z-score (WLZ), and health status over time between the comparison group and intervention group.

H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis)

Structured health education has a significant impact on improving infant feeding practices, growth indicators (LAZ, WAZ, and WLZ), and health status over time in the intervention group compared to the comparison group.

Methods

Design and setting

This quasi-experimental study was conducted to evaluate the effect of structured health education on infant feeding practices, growth, and health status in urban and suburban community health settings. A quasi-experimental design was selected because random assignment was not feasible within the real-world clinical context [26]. To minimise logistical challenges and ensure consistent delivery of the intervention, entire clinics were assigned to either the comparison or intervention group. This approach enhanced implementation efficiency and helped maintain separation between study arms. Participant blinding was not possible in this design, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Infants were followed from one to six months of age, with data collected at three time points: baseline (Month 1, pre-test), mid-point (Month 3, follow-up), and endpoint (Month 6, post-test).

The study was conducted at six government health clinics in the Gombak District of Selangor, Malaysia. This district was selected for its diverse population, encompassing both urban and suburban communities, and representing a range of socioeconomic backgrounds. Additionally, the district has a high volume of mothers seeking breastfeeding advice at public health clinics, making it a suitable setting for evaluating the effects of structured health education on infant feeding practices. Clinics were assigned to either the comparison group (Selayang, Gombak Setia, AU2) or the intervention group (Taman Ehsan, Sungai Buloh, Rawang) based on practical considerations, including clinic size, service load, and logistical feasibility. Within each clinic, eligible mother–infant pairs were recruited through purposive sampling during routine postnatal visits.

Mothers were eligible to participate if they had delivered healthy, term (≥ 37 weeks of gestation) singleton infants and were willing to complete follow-up assessments over six months. Exclusion criteria included plans to relocate or transfer to another clinic within six months, maternal comorbidities likely to impact study outcomes, and infants with significant congenital anomalies.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size for this study was calculated using the OpenEpi web-based tool (version 3.01) [27], based on parameters reported in previous studies. The calculation assumed a 95% confidence level, 80% power, an odds ratio of 2.6, and an expected 7% difference in outcome prevalence between groups, with continuity correction applied. This resulted in an estimated required sample size of 500 mother–infant pairs. To allow for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 20%, the initial target screening number was set at 602 mother–infant pairs. Recruitment was planned across six purposively selected clinics, with each expected to screen approximately 100 participants, depending on the clinic’s size, service load, and feasibility.

Health education intervention

The structured health education materials were developed following a comprehensive review of the published literature [28–30] and adapted from national guidelines, including the Breastfeeding Guidebook 2012 and the Safe Handling and Feeding of Milk in Childcare Centres and Nurseries 2012, both published by the Ministry of Health Malaysia (MOH). The programme was aligned with current breastfeeding recommendations and was reviewed by subject matter experts to ensure content and face validity. Prior to implementation, the materials were pilot-tested with a small group of mothers, and refinements were made based on their feedback.

The health education was considered structured because it followed a standardised delivery protocol that included predefined topics, validated materials (e.g., PowerPoint slides, pamphlets), and consistent delivery by the researcher, a trained community health nurse, thereby ensuring uniformity and reliability across all intervention sites.

Participant recruitment

At each selected clinic, eligible mothers were identified during their first postnatal follow-up at the maternal and child health (MCH) clinics. The principal researcher approached the mothers in the waiting area, provided a brief explanation of the study, and assessed eligibility using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Mothers who met the requirements and agreed to participate were given an information sheet and a consent form. Following written informed consent, participants were enrolled in the study.

Ten clinics were considered, of which four were excluded (two newly upgraded, one small in size, and one with low client volume). From the remaining six clinics, 602 mothers were assessed for eligibility; 102 were excluded (42 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 60 declined). A total of 500 mothers were enrolled, with recruitment yielding approximately 83–84 participants per clinic, resulting in nearly equal numbers across the intervention and comparison arms.

The researcher, a trained community health nurse, delivered a 40-minute structured face-to-face health education session, conducted in small groups (4–6 mothers) at the participating clinics. The session included a PowerPoint presentation, interactive discussion, and the distribution of educational pamphlets in either Bahasa Malaysia or English.

To reinforce the session content, a one-time WhatsApp message was sent to each mother in the intervention group when the infant reached 3 months old. This timing coincided with the midpoint of the recommended 6-month EBF period, a phase often marked by challenges such as returning to work, concerns about milk supply, or declining motivation. The mobile health (mHealth) message provided a brief and encouraging reminder that reiterated key points from the session, including the benefits of breastfeeding, latching techniques, proper milk expression and storage, and signs of adequate feeding. While primarily one-way, the platform allowed limited participant interaction. This low-frequency contact was designed to enhance engagement without overburdening participants or disrupting routine care. Additionally, the message served as an informal check-in to help identify potential dropouts before the final post-test at the end of Month 6.

The comparison group did not receive this follow-up, consistent with standard care practices. However, to ensure ethical parity, they were provided with the same educational pamphlet after completing the Month 6 data collection.

The educational content delivered through both the PowerPoint session and the accompanying pamphlets covered a comprehensive range of breastfeeding topics. These included the benefits of breastfeeding for both infants and mothers, as well as the definition and importance of EBF. The materials emphasised the significance of colostrum and highlighted the risks associated with pre-lacteal feeds. Instruction was also provided on correct latching and positioning techniques, supported by visual aids. Additional topics addressed the adverse effects of bottle feeding, signs of adequate milk intake in infants, and guidance on the safe expression, storage, and feeding of expressed breast milk, particularly tailored for working mothers.

Questionnaire

The study questionnaire was adapted from a previously established tool and used with the owner’s permission [31]. It comprised four sections: Section A included 14 items that captured demographic and socioeconomic information on the mother–infant pairs. Section B contained 33 items related to infant feeding practices, covering breastfeeding, expressed breast milk, mixed feeding, formula, and other types of milk. Participants reported the types of feeding provided and the methods used for preparation. For formula feeding, mothers selected one of four responses: no advice received, did not follow advice, followed some advice, or followed all advice. Additional items in this section recorded the age of the infant at the initiation of feeding and the frequency of daily feeding.

Section C comprised 19 items assessing maternal perceptions of infant growth and feeding. This section was divided into two parts: the Mothers’ Views on Infant Growth and Feeding (MVIGF), measured using an 8-item scale rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to agree strongly; and the Mothers’ Views on Feeding Recommendations (MVFFR), which included 11 items assessed using the same scale. Section D contained 18 items. These included anthropometric measurements of infant growth (head circumference, length, and weight) and 15 health conditions documented in the child’s health record or computerised system, each recorded as either ‘yes’ or ‘no’.

A pilot study was conducted between May and September 2015 to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Malay version of the instrument. Eighty mother–infant pairs, who were excluded from the main study, participated in the pilot. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.65 for MVIGF and 0.83 for MVFFR. An expert panel confirmed content validity, and no revisions to the questionnaire were required after the pilot.

Data collection

Data were collected from February 2015 to January 2019 using a structured, researcher-assisted self-administered questionnaire (RA-SAQ), adapted from a validated tool. The questionnaires were completed in person during clinic visits, and each session lasted approximately 30–40 min. The same questionnaire was administered at Month 1 postpartum (pre-test) and Month 6 postpartum (post-test). Mothers were recruited at the end of Month 1 to ensure that some initial feeding experience had occurred and that infants had reached one month for baseline measurements. At Month 3, interim data on infant feeding practices, such as breastfeeding cessation or the introduction of formula, were collected for the intervention group via WhatsApp messages or phone calls. This follow-up primarily served to reinforce the educational content provided at baseline and also allowed the researchers to monitor changes in infant feeding behaviours over time. Data collection continued through Month 6 to align with WHO recommendations and to capture longitudinal changes in feeding and growth.

To support participant retention across both groups, a neutral reminder card was provided to all mothers during the recruitment process. The card outlined the study follow-up points at Months 3 and 6, which aligned with the national immunisation schedule for infants. It contained no educational or motivational content and served purely as a logistical tool to help mothers anticipate upcoming study-related contacts during routine clinic visits.

Anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardised and calibrated equipment across all study sites. Infant length and weight were measured by trained healthcare professionals using length boards and weighing scales, respectively, and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg. Each measurement was taken twice to ensure accuracy, with a third taken if discrepancies arose. Anthropometric indices for length-for-age z-score (LAZ), weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), and weight-for-length z-score (WLZ) were generated using the WHO Anthro software version 3.2.2, based on the 2006 WHO Child Growth Standards [32]. The prespecified primary outcome was WLZ, chosen for its sensitivity to short-term changes in feeding practices. LAZ and WAZ were analysed as secondary exploratory outcomes. Head circumference for-age z-score (HCZ) was calculated but not included in subsequent analyses.

In addition, infant morbidity data were collected from the child’s health records or from the clinic’s computerised system. Diagnoses recorded by health staff during routine visits or illness consultations were reviewed at Months 1, 3, and 6. The common illnesses recorded were upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), gastroenteritis (GE), contact dermatitis (CD), and bowel disorder (BD). Illnesses not classified under these categories were grouped as Other Illnesses (OI), which encompassed less common conditions such as hernia, skin infections, otitis media, and unspecified fever episodes.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Baseline differences between groups were assessed using independent-sample t tests or χ² tests, as appropriate.

Longitudinal changes in infant growth outcomes were analysed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) for LAZ, WAZ, and WLZ. LMMs were selected because they account for the correlation of repeated measures within individuals, accommodate unbalanced data (i.e., missing observations at some time points), and incorporate both fixed and random effects. Group (comparison vs. intervention), time (Months 1, 3, and 6), and the group-by-time interaction were specified as fixed effects. Infant feeding practice (EBF vs. NEBF) was included as a covariate. However, common illnesses were not included in the growth models because morbidity data were collected as secondary exploratory outcomes and were not uniformly available across all participants and time points. Infant identity (ID) was modelled as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation. A variance components covariance structure was applied. Model assumptions were checked by examining the normality and homoscedasticity of residuals, as well as identifying influential outliers; no serious violations were detected. Results are presented as parameter estimates with standard errors (SEs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were generated to plot growth trajectories by Group and Time, with error bars representing ± 1 SE.

For illness outcomes, generalised logistic mixed models (GLMM) were applied using complete-case data to estimate associations between group allocation and infant feeding practice (EBF vs. NEBF) with the occurrence of URTI, GE, CD, BD, and OI at Months 1, 3, and 6. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and p values were reported. Assumptions of logistic regression, including the absence of multicollinearity and the linearity of continuous predictors with the logit, were evaluated and met. Statistical significance was set at p <.05 (two-tailed).

Results

A total of 602 mothers were assessed for eligibility; 102 were excluded (42 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 60 declined). The remaining 500 were enrolled across six purposively selected clinics, with nearly equal numbers in the intervention and comparison arms. At follow-up, nine participants were lost to follow-up at 3 months, and 31 were excluded from analysis at 6 months, leaving 460 mothers (220 in the comparison group and 240 in the intervention group) for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Although this gap narrowed at Month 3 (66.3% vs. 55.6%, χ² = 5.80, p =.016), EBF remained higher in the comparison group. Baseline anthropometric measures also revealed some group differences. Infants in the intervention group were significantly longer and heavier than those in the comparison group, with higher length-for-age z-scores (LAZ) (t(498) = − 3.05, p =.002) and a higher mean weight (t(498) = − 2.09, p =.037). Weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ) (t(498) = − 1.80, p =.073) and weight-for-length z-scores (WLZ) (comparison: − 0.20 ± 1.44; intervention: − 0.39 ± 1.27; t(498) = 1.63, p =.105) did not differ significantly. Full baseline comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (Additional file 1).

Over the follow-up period, descriptive trends in growth indicated that both groups experienced steady increases in mean length and weight, but with more variable changes in standardised growth indices (Supplementary Table S2, Additional file 2). For LAZ, the comparison group improved modestly (–0.30 ± 1.09 to 0.17 ± 1.09), while the intervention group showed a greater increase (–0.01 ± 1.19 to 1.04 ± 0.79). For WAZ, the comparison group showed a slight increase at Month 3 but declined by Month 6 (0.35 ± 1.05 to − 0.22 ± 0.82), whereas the intervention group improved steadily (–0.36 ± 0.98 at baseline to 0.52 ± 0.77 at Month 6). For WLZ, both groups showed minimal positive changes, with values becoming slightly less negative from baseline to Month 6 (comparison group: − 0.25 ± 1.13 to − 0.19 ± 1.02; intervention group: − 0.36 ± 1.09 to − 0.13 ± 1.09). These descriptive trends suggest modest, non-significant improvements in LAZ and WAZ among the intervention group. At the same time, WLZ remained relatively stable in both groups, reflecting proportionate weight gain relative to length.

To formally evaluate these patterns, LMMs were fitted using an autoregressive [AR(1)] covariance structure. The intra-infant correlation across repeated measures was moderate (ρ = 0.41), and clinic-level variance was minimal (0.014, SE = 0.017). Table 3 presents the fixed effects from the LMMs. LAZ mean z-scores showed significant temporal changes at Month 3 (β = − 0.60, 95% CI − 0.92 to − 0.27, p <.001) and Month 6 (β = − 0.52, 95% CI − 0.77 to − 0.28, p <.001) relative to baseline, indicating significant changes over time but no significant group or interaction effects. Similar time-related changes were observed for WAZ (Month 3 β = − 0.64, p <.001; Month 6 β = − 0.61, p <.001) and for WLZ (Month 3 β = − 0.43, p =.062; Month 6 β = − 0.42, p =.029), again without group or interaction effects.

In summary, exclusive breastfeeding initially favoured the comparison group, but by Month 6, it was higher in the intervention group. Although some baseline z-score values were negative, the overall growth outcomes improved over time in both groups, as reflected by increases in LAZ, WAZ, and WLZ values. These upward trends indicate better growth trajectories, even though the between-group differences were not statistically significant. Illness prevalence also decreased with age, with significant reductions observed for GE and other illnesses, and a borderline improvement for URTI in the intervention group.

Discussion

Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) is recognised globally as a cornerstone of infant health during the first six months of life [33, 34]. Evidence from Malaysia [35], Indonesia [36], Iran [37], Greece [38], and Denmark [39] shows that structured education can improve breastfeeding knowledge and practices. In Malaysia, the Baby-Friendly Clinic Initiative (BFCI) has institutionalised community-based support; yet, national surveys continue to report high non-exclusive breastfeeding (NEBF) rates. In this study, mothers were enrolled at the end of the first postpartum month. By that time, some had already introduced formula or discontinued EBF, possibly contributing to the higher EBF rates observed in the comparison group at Month 1. The reversal of this trend by Month 6 suggests that the intervention group may have benefited from the initial face-to-face education provided at enrolment, which was later reinforced with a one-time WhatsApp reminder at Month 3. Although the digital component was minimal, such a timely follow-up may have supported sustained breastfeeding practices [40–42].

Despite improved feeding practices, growth trajectories did not differ significantly between groups. Linear mixed models (LMMs) revealed significant time effects for length-for-age (LAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-length (WLZ), but no significant group-level or interaction effects were observed. Although model coefficients were negative, descriptive analyses indicated upward (less negative) shifts in z-scores, particularly among the intervention group, suggesting modest but non-significant improvements in growth status.

Although the intervention group showed modestly greater improvements in LAZ and WAZ by Month 6, these differences were not statistically significant. Importantly, baseline anthropometry was collected at one month of age, after early postnatal growth trajectories had already begun. Thus, any observed improvements reflect post–Month 1 growth rather than classic catch-up from birth. Nonetheless, the intervention group’s more favourable anthropometric trends may indicate early signs of improved growth stability. The parallel WLZ trajectories in both groups suggest proportionate gains in weight relative to length, with no evidence of wasting. The slightly lower WLZ in the comparison group may reflect a subtle tendency toward thinness, consistent with typical growth patterns in early infancy.

These findings are consistent with studies from China and Ethiopia, where short-term breastfeeding interventions successfully improved feeding practices but rarely translated into measurable anthropometric gains within the first six months of life [43, 44]. Evidence indicates that linear growth (LAZ) typically requires sustained nutritional inputs and longer follow-up to demonstrate change, whereas weight-related indicators may respond more rapidly [9]. Systematic reviews also show that breastfeeding interventions more consistently improve feeding behaviours and morbidity outcomes than short-term growth measures. The overall pattern supports the view that early growth faltering is common during infancy [45, 46], and that observable anthropometric improvements generally require longer-term, multi-component interventions addressing both feeding and broader determinants of nutrition [4].

This study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that structured education, supported by a WhatsApp reminder, improves EBF beyond levels previously reported in Malaysia [47]. While growth and morbidity outcomes showed favourable but non-significant trends, the findings underscore the potential of integrating mobile health (mHealth) into community-based breastfeeding initiatives. Global evidence from the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) [48–50] and Malaysia’s Baby-Friendly Clinic Initiative (BFCI) [51] supports the value of structured support, and our results suggest digital tools may enhance these strategies. Group differences in growth may also have been reduced by higher EBF levels in the comparison group at Month 3, although the intervention group maintained more stable adherence by Month 6 [47, 52, 53].

Regarding morbidity, the GLMM analysis showed no consistent group-by-time effects across illness types. Gastroenteritis declined in both groups, and URTI showed a borderline reduction favouring the intervention, while contact dermatitis (CD) and bowel disorder (BD) remained uncommon. Other illnesses (OI) were significantly lower in the intervention group at Month 3, with a borderline effect at Month 6. These conditions included hernia, skin infections, otitis media, and unspecified fever episodes. The decline in OI may reflect natural age-related improvements in immunity, better caregiver recognition and reporting of symptoms, or indirect benefits from enhanced health awareness following the intervention. The adjusted GLMM results thus provide a more conservative and contextually appropriate interpretation of the intervention’s effects.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Although the calculated minimum required sample size was 500, the final analytic sample consisted of 460 participants due to attrition. This modest shortfall may have slightly reduced the statistical power to detect more minor effects; however, the sample remained sufficient to support the primary analyses and conclusions regarding the intervention’s impact [54]. The brief duration of the intervention, consisting of a single structured education session with one WhatsApp reinforcement, also limited the likelihood of detecting significant anthropometric effects, particularly for length gain, which typically requires more extended follow-up periods.

Additionally, as participants were enrolled at one month postpartum, very early feeding behaviours, particularly those occurring in the neonatal period, were not captured. This limits the interpretation of early breastfeeding initiation and immediate postpartum feeding decisions, which may have influenced later EBF trajectories.

Although feeding practices were included as covariates in the growth models, adjustment for morbidity was not possible, as illness data were secondary outcomes and not consistently available across participants and time points. This limitation may have introduced residual confounding, particularly considering the potential influence of recurrent illnesses on infant growth.

There were some reductions in common illnesses, particularly for OI and a borderline reduction for URTI. These findings cannot be entirely attributed to the education programme. The sessions mainly focused on promoting breastfeeding, with limited emphasis on hygienic and responsive feeding practices and no explicit content on infection prevention. Furthermore, although illness data (URTI, GE, CD, BD, OI) were collected, these variables were not included as covariates in the growth models, which might have led to residual confounding, as illness episodes can influence growth trajectories.

The quasi-experimental design without randomisation further limits the ability to draw causal inferences between the intervention and the outcomes. As the study was conducted in a single urban–suburban district, the findings may not be generalisable to rural populations or regions with different sociodemographic profiles and healthcare infrastructures. Data collection relied on a mix of self-administered and face-to-face interviews, which may have introduced response bias and increased the time and resource burden for participants and researchers. Finally, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, particularly psychosocial factors such as maternal stress, breastfeeding confidence, and social support. These domains, known to influence feeding practices, were not analysed here but were captured in Section C of the questionnaire and will be explored in future secondary analyses [55, 56].

Public health implications

The findings suggest that a combined approach of structured in-person health education with mHealth reinforcement has potential for integration into routine MCH services. Leveraging widely used platforms such as WhatsApp requires minimal additional resources and may help bridge follow-up gaps, sustain EBF, and modestly improve selected health outcomes. However, given the limited duration and intensity of the present intervention, broader implementation should be accompanied by further research to evaluate feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and scalability across diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion and practice implications

The structured health education programme improved exclusive breastfeeding and showed modest, non-significant improvements in growth and selected morbidity outcomes. Longer-term or more intensive interventions may be required to achieve measurable biological effects.
