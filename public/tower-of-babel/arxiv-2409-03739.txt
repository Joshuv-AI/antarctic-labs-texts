Better bounds on finite-order Grothendieck constants
Sébastien Designolle, Tamás Vértesi, Sebastian Pokutta

Abstract
Grothendieck constants K G ​ ( d ) K_{G}\left({d}{}\right) bound the advantage of d d -dimensional strategies over 1 1 -dimensional ones in a specific optimisation task. They have applications ranging from approximation algorithms to quantum nonlocality. However, apart from d = 2 d=2 , their values are unknown. Here, we exploit a recent Frank-Wolfe approach to provide good candidates for lower bounding some of these constants. The complete proof relies on solving difficult binary quadratic optimisation problems. For d ∈ { 3 , 4 , 5 } d\in\{3,4,5\} , we construct specific rectangular instances that we can solve to certify better bounds than those previously known; by monotonicity, our lower bounds improve on the state of the art for 3 ⩽ d ⩽ 9 3\leqslant d\leqslant 9 . For d ∈ { 4 , 7 , 8 } d\in\{4,7,8\} , we exploit elegant structures to build highly symmetric instances achieving even greater bounds; however, we can only solve them heuristically. We also recall the standard relation with violations of Bell inequalities and elaborate on it to interpret generalised Grothendieck constants K G ​ ( d ↦ 2 ) K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right) as the advantage of complex d d -dimensional quantum mechanics over real qubit quantum mechanics. Motivated by this connection, we also improve the bounds on K G ​ ( d ↦ 2 ) K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right) .



I
Introduction

Published in French in a Brazilian journal, Grothendieck’s pioneering work on Banach spaces from 1953
[
1
]
, now informally known as his
Résumé
, long remained unnoticed.
In 1968, Lindenstrauss and Pełczyński
[
2
]
discovered it and rephrased the main result, the Grothendieck inequality, which proves a relationship between three fundamental tensor norms through the so-called Grothendieck constant, denoted
K_{G}
.
Since then, this far-reaching theorem has found numerous applications
[
3
]
, in particular in combinatorial optimisation where it is at the heart of an algorithm to approximate the cut-norm of a matrix
[
4
]
.

Quantum information is another field where this result is influential: following early observations by Tsirelson
[
5
]
, an explicit connection has been established with the noise robustness in Bell experiments
[
6
,
7
]
.
These experiments aim at exhibiting a fascinating property of quantum mechanics in correlation scenarios: nonlocality
[
8
]
.
The link with Grothendieck’s theorem has led to a surge of interest in the value of
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
, the Grothendieck constant of order three.
Many works have thus demonstrated increasingly precise lower bounds
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
and upper bounds
[
15
,
16
,
14
]
on its value.
More recently, a numerical method has also been developed to come up with an even more precise (but not provable) estimate of
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)

[
17
]
.

For Grothendieck constants of higher orders, the link with quantum nonlocality remains
[
7
,
18
]
.
However, the bounds on their values have been less studied and are less tight
[
15
,
19
,
10
,
20
,
11
,
13
]
.
There are quite a few difficulties that explain this relative scarcity of results, many of them being manifestations of the curse of dimensionality.
Finding suitable high-dimensional ansätze indeed becomes increasingly hard and the resulting instances involve sizes that rapidly become intractable.

In this article, we combine the recent projection technique from
[
14
]
with the powerful solver developed in
[
13
]
to obtain better lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
for
3\leqslant d\leqslant 9
.
Following
[
19
]
, we also consider symmetric structures in high dimensions emerging from highly symmetric line packings
[
21
]
to suggest even better bounds on
K_{G}\left({4}{}\right)
,
K_{G}\left({7}{}\right)
, and
K_{G}\left({8}{}\right)
, that we unfortunately cannot prove as they involve optimisation problems that we only solve heuristically.
We also consider the generalised Grothendieck constants
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
and interpret them as the advantage of
d
-dimensional quantum mechanics over real qubit quantum mechanics, a fact that was already studied in
[
18
,
22
]
.
Our bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
are analytical, while those on
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
strongly rely on numerical methods: we post-process the upper bound on
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)
to convert it into an exact result, but the lower bounds remain inaccurate.

We first formally define the constants we want to bound in
Section

II
before presenting in
Section

III
our method and main results on lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
, summarised in
Tables

1
and
2
.
Then we turn to generalised constants in
Section

IV
, where we review existing bounds before deriving ours, which are particularly tight on the value of
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)
.
Finally, we recall the connection with quantum mechanics in
Section

IV.4
and conclude in
Section

V
.

II
Preliminaries

Given a real matrix
M
of size
m_{1}\times m_{2}
, we define

\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)=\max\left\{\sum_{x=1}^{m_{1}}\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}\left\langle{a_{x}},{b_{y}}\right\rangle\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ \forall x\in[m_{1}],\penalty\ a_{x}\in S^{d-1},\penalty\ \forall y\in[m_{2}],\penalty\ b_{y}\in S^{d-1}\right\},

(1)

where
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and where
S^{d-1}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which reduces to
S^{0}=\{-1,1\}
for
d=1
.
By introducing the set

\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}=\conv\left\{X\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ X_{xy}=\left\langle{a_{x}},{b_{y}}\right\rangle,\penalty\ \forall x\in[m_{1}],\penalty\ a_{x}\in S^{d-1},\penalty\ \forall y\in[m_{2}],\penalty\ b_{y}\in S^{d-1}\right\},

(2)

we get the equivalent definition

\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)=\max\left\{\left\langle{M},{X}\right\rangle\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ X\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}\right\}.

(3)

The reason for this name comes from the interpretation of the Grothendieck constant that we are about to define in the context of rank-constrained semidefinite programming
[
23
]
.
In the following, when
m_{1}=m_{2}
, we simply write
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m,m}=\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m}
; also, when the size
m_{1},m_{2}
is either clear from the context or irrelevant, we will use the shorthand notation
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}=\mathrm{SDP}_{d}
.

In essence, the Grothendieck inequality states that there exists a (finite) constant independent of the size of the matrix
M
that bounds the ratio between the quantities
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)
for various
d
.
More formally, given
n\leqslant d
, for all real matrices
M
, we have
[
1
]

\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)\leqslant K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)\mathrm{SDP}_{n}\left({M}\right),

(4)

so that the exact definition of these
generalised Grothendieck constants
is

K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)=\sup\left\{\frac{\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)}{\mathrm{SDP}_{n}\left({M}\right)}\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ M\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times m_{2}},\quad m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

(5)

The standard Grothendieck constant of order
d
is obtained when
n=1
, in which case we use the shorthand notation
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{1}\right)=K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
.
The infinite-order Grothendieck constant
K_{G}
initially studied in
[
1
]
corresponds to the limit
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
, but it is out of the scope of our work, although we briefly mention it in
Section

V
.
We refer the reader interested in general aspects and further generalisations of these constants to
[
24
]
.

III
Lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)

Given the definition of
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
in
Eq.

5
, any matrix
M
automatically provides a valid lower bound.
However, there are two main difficulties when looking for
good
lower bounds.

Problem 1
.

Given
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, how to find a matrix
M
such that the inequality in
Eq.

4
is as tight as possible?

Problem 2
.

Given such a matrix
M
, how to compute the resulting
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)
or at least a close upper bound?

Problem

2
is the most limiting one, as the computation of
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)
is equivalent to MaxCut and therefore NP-hard, so that the sizes of the matrices to consider are limited by the methods and resources available to compute this number.
In general, solving
Problem

1
exactly is out of reach, but good candidates can be found and this suffices to derive bounds.

In the rest of this section, we first recall the method from
[
14
]
for addressing both problems above, in particular
Problem

1
.
To illustrate the idea of the method, we give up on solving
Problem

2
for the sake of the elegance of the solution to
Problem

1
, providing instances of remarkable symmetry for which future works may solve
Problem

2
to improve on the bounds on
K_{G}\left({4}{}\right)
,
K_{G}\left({7}{}\right)
, and
K_{G}\left({8}{}\right)
.
We then focus on
Problem

2
and consider rectangular matrices allowing us to obtain certified bounds on
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
,
K_{G}\left({4}{}\right)
, and
K_{G}\left({5}{}\right)
that beat the literature up to
d=9
, by monotonicity.

III.1
Obtaining facets of the symmetrised correlation polytope

When
n=1
, the set
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}
is a polytope, called the correlation polytope
[
25
]
.
In
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
the method used to solve
Problem

1
is to start from a point
P\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}
and to derive
M
as a hyperplane separating
P
from
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}
.
This hyperplane is obtained by solving the projection of
P
onto the correlation polytope
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}
via Frank-Wolfe algorithms.
We refer to
[
26
]
for a gentle introduction to FW algorithms, to
[
27
]
for a complete review, and to
[
14
, Appendix C]
for the details of our implementation.
Note that some accelerations developed in
[
14
,
28
]
and used in this work are now part of the FrankWolfe.jl package
[
29
]
.

In particular, the symmetrisation described in
[
28
]
is crucial, but as it depends on the underlying group and its action on the matrices we consider, exposing the full structure of the
symmetrised correlation polytope
for each group would be tedious.
Instead, we give a brief general definition of this polytope and refer to
[
28
]
for a detailed example.
Given a group
G
acting on
[m_{1}]
and
[m_{2}]
(by means of signed permutations), the symmetrised correlation polytope
G(\mathrm{SDP}_{1}^{m_{1},m_{2}})
is the convex hull of the averages of all orbits of the vertices of
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}^{m_{1},m_{2}}
under the action of
G
.
Note that this polytope lives in a subspace of the space invariant under the action of
G
on matrices of size
m_{1}\times m_{2}
.

In
Fig.

1
we illustrate the various geometrical cases that we encounter in this work.
Their understanding justifies the procedure that we describe in
Algorithm

to derive facets of the symmetrised correlation polytope. Importantly, for symmetric instances, the dimension of the ambient space is strictly smaller than
m_{1}m_{2}
.
In the following, we consistently denote these facets by
A
, while
M
will indicate separating hyperplanes without this extra property.
Putative facets will also be denoted by
A
, that is, in cases where we rely on heuristic methods to compute
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({A}\right)
.

These heuristic methods play an important role throughout the FW algorithm as they quickly give a good direction to make primal progress.
Here we present them in a generalised framework that will prove useful when generalising our algorithm to
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
in
Section

IV
.
At a given step of the FW algorithm minimising the squared distance to the set
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}
, finding the best direction with respect to the current negative gradient
M
is exactly the problem in
Eq.

3
.
This subroutine is called the Linear Minimisation Oracle (LMO) and, in the course of the algorithm, we usually use an alternating minimisation to obtain heuristic solutions that are enough to make progress
[
14
, Appendix B.1]
.
This procedure is known as a
seesaw
optimisation in the quantum community
[
30
]
.

More formally, for all
x\in[m_{1}]
, we pick a random
a^{h}_{x}\in S^{n-1}
and we compute, for all
y\in[m_{2}]
,

b^{h}_{y}=\argmax_{b_{y}\in S^{n-1}}\left\langle{b_{y}},{\sum_{x=1}^{m_{1}}M_{xy}a^{h}_{x}}\right\rangle,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad b^{h}_{y}=\frac{\sum_{\vphantom{y}x=1}^{m_{1}}M_{xy}a^{h}_{x}}{\left\|\sum_{\vphantom{y}x=1}^{m_{1}}M_{xy}a^{h}_{x}\right\|}.

(6)

In the unlikely case where the denominator happens to be zero, we set
b^{h}_{y}
to be a predetermined vector, for instance,
(1,0,\ldots,0)\in S^{n-1}
.
Similarly, we then use these
b^{h}_{y}
to compute

a^{h}_{x}=\argmax_{a_{x}\in S^{n-1}}\left\langle{a_{x}},{\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}b^{h}_{y}}\right\rangle,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad a^{h}_{x}=\frac{\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}b^{h}_{y}}{\left\|\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}b^{h}_{y}\right\|},.

(7)

and we repeat
Eqs.

6
and
7
until the objective value in
Eq.

1
stops increasing, up to numerical precision when
n>1
.
Depending on the initial choice of
a^{h}_{x}
, the value attained will vary.
Therefore we restart the procedure a large number of times: from a few hundred or thousand within the FW algorithm, to
10^{5}
in
Table

and

in

III.2
Heuristic results with highly symmetric line packings

Starting from a good point
P\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}
in the procedure described above is key to obtaining good bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
.
Following
[
13
]
, we observed that spanning the
d
-dimensional projective unit sphere in the most uniform way seems to be favourable, which served as our guiding principle to come up with structures in higher dimensions.
In this
Section

III.2
, we use the same distribution of points on both sides in
Eq.

2
, that is,
m_{1}=m_{2}=m
and
a_{1}=b_{1},\ldots a_{m}=b_{m}
; the matrix
P
is therefore a Gram matrix.

The intuitive but vague notion of uniform spreading on the sphere can be formally seen as the problem of finding good line packings
[
32
,
21
]
.
In
Table

1
, for various symmetric
d
-dimensional line packings, we give the values of
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({P}\right)/\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({P}\right)
and
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({A}\right)/\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({A}\right)
.
Since the Gram matrix
P
is positive semidefinite, the ratio
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({P}\right)/\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({P}\right)
is upper bounded by
K_{G}^{\succeq}(d)
, the positive semidefinite Grothendieck constant of order
d

[
33
]
, whose value is known to be
\gamma(d)\pi/2

[
24
, Theorem 4.1.3]
, where
\gamma(d)
is defined in
Eq.

10
below.
Note that, for
d\geqslant 9
, this bound is the previously best known lower bound on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
which is naturally greater than
K_{G}^{\succeq}(d)
.

Among the structures that we present in
Table

1
, the
d
-dimensional configurations reaching the best known kissing number in dimension
d
are of particular interest.
Recall that it corresponds to the greatest number of non-overlapping unit spheres that can be placed such that they each touch (“kiss”) a common unit sphere.
In dimension
d=3
, different realisations of the kissing number give rise to different values of
\mathrm{SDP}_{3}\left({A}\right)/\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({A}\right)
.
As the icosahedron is an optimal line packing, this indicates that giving good bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
does not, in general, boil down to finding good line packings.
Note that the unicity of the configuration in dimension
d=4
was recently proven
[
34
]
.

Upon inspection of
Table

it is apparent that, in the small dimensions that we consider in this work (
d\leqslant 8
), root systems play an important role in obtaining good kissing configurations.
These structures are inherently symmetric and play an important role in the theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras.
We refer to
[
35

for definitions and elementary properties.
The family
D_{d}
has been studied in
[
19
]

Moreover, for

is derived for

; in other words,
[

is giving the best lower bound on

.
Here we go a bit further by extending this result to

, a fact that was suspected in
[
19
]
, but we also observe that the corresponding matrices

are actually facets of the symmetrised polytope

III.3
Exact results in asymmetric scenarios

Now, we discuss how to address
Problem

2
, namely, the computation of

\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)=\max_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a_x=\pm 1}{b_y=\pm 1}}\sum_{x=1}^{m_{1}}\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}{a_{x}}{b_{y}}.

(8)

In
[
14
]
this problem is reformulated into a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation (QUBO) instance, which is then given to the solver QuBowl from
[
39
]
.
The instance solved there involves
m_{1}=m_{2}=97
and is bigger than the ones from
[
13
]
(of maximal size
m_{1}=m_{2}=92
).
However, it is worth noticing that the solution of the exact instance solved in
[
14
]
stands out in the landscape of all binary variables.
More precisely, the exact solution is likely to be unique and its value is far above the other feasible points, so that the solver could efficiently exclude vast portions of the search space.
This empirical observation is supported by two facts: instances from later stages of convergence of the Frank-Wolfe method cannot be solved by QuBowl (even within ten times as much time), and the symmetric instance
A
of size
63\times 63
obtained from the
E_{7}
root system (see
Table

1
) cannot be solved by QuBowl either, although it is way smaller.

Given these difficulties, we consider other formulations exploiting the specificities of the problem.
In particular, the branch-and-bound algorithm from
[
13
]
works by breaking the symmetry between the binary variables
a_{x}
and
b_{y}
in
Eq.

8
, fixing the latter to obtain:

\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)=\max_{a_{x}=\pm 1}\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}\left|\sum_{x=1}^{m_{1}}M_{xy}{a_{x}}\right|,

(9)

which has half as many variables.
Importantly, the parameter
m_{2}
plays a less critical role in the complexity of the resulting algorithm, which is indeed still exponential in
m_{1}
, but now linear in
m_{2}
.
This suggests using rectangular matrices
M
in our procedure, with a large
m_{2}
to increase the achievable lower bound on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
and a relatively small
m_{1}
to maintain the possibility to solve
Problem

2
, that is, to compute
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)
.

Such rectangular matrices can be obtained by following the procedure described in
Section

starting from rectangular matrices

Good starting points are still obtained by using well-spread distributions on the sphere, but these distributions

and

are now different, so that the matrix

with entries

IV
Bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)

We now turn to the generalised Grothendieck constants
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
, which have not received a lot of attention so far.
Motivated by the quantum interpretation of these constants (see
Section

IV.4
), we extend the techniques presented above and in previous works to refine the bounds known on their values.

IV.1
Bounds arising from the literature

There is an explicit lower bound on
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)
for any
d>n
that uses a particular one-parameter family of linear functionals of infinite size.
This bound, initially proven in
[
22
,
20
]
(see also
[
24
, Theorem 3.3.1]
for the proof), reads

K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)\geqslant\frac{\gamma(d)}{\gamma(n)},\quad\text{with}\quad\gamma(d)=\frac{2}{d}\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\right)^{2},

(10)

where
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t
is the gamma function.
When
n=2
,
\gamma(n)=\pi/4
and
Eq.

10
can be rewritten:

K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)\geqslant\frac{d\binom{d-1}{k}^{2}}{2^{2d-3}}\quad\text{when}\quad d=2k\qquad\text{and}\qquad K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)\geqslant\frac{2^{2d+1}}{d\binom{d-1}{k}^{2}\pi^{2}}\quad\text{when}\quad d=2k+1.

(11)

The bounds in
Eq.

11
are better than indirect bounds derived from natural combinations of bounds on the Grothendieck constants
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
, even when considering the improved bounds proven in this article.
Starting with the Grothendieck inequality (
4
), dividing both sides by
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}\left({M}\right)
and lower and upper bounding the left and right sides, we indeed obtain the following relation

K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)\leqslant K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)K_{G}\left({n}{}\right).

(12)

Then, inserting
K_{G}\left({2}{}\right)=\sqrt{2}
from
[
15
]
and using our lower bounds presented in
Table

2b
yields
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)\geqslant 1.0159
,
K_{G}\left({4}\mapsto{2}\right)\geqslant 1.0506
, and
K_{G}\left({5}\mapsto{2}\right)\geqslant 1.0560
, which does not improve on the values from
Eq.

11
.

As can be seen in
Table

3
,
Eq.

11
also outperforms all known bounds obtained with finite matrices.
Therefore, to the best of our knowledge,
Eq.

11
represents the state of the art.

\vdots\\
b_{y,d}\end{pmatrix}}\right\rangle\penalty\ \Bigg|\penalty\ \forall x\in[m_{1}],\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{x,i}^{2}=1,\penalty\ \forall y\in[m_{2}],\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{y,i}^{2}=1\right\}.

IV.3
Exact upper bound on
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)

Here we reformulate the procedure first introduced in
[
45
,
46
]
and later used to obtain better upper bounds on
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
in
[
16
,
14
]
in a way that makes the generalisation to other Grothendieck constants more transparent.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{p_{1},p_{2}}
with underlying vectors
a_{1}^{P},\ldots,a_{p_{1}}^{P},b_{1}^{P},\ldots,b_{p_{2}}^{P}\in S^{d-1}
such that

•

there exist
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in(0,1)
for which
\eta_{1}S^{d-1}\subset\conv\{a_{i}^{P}\}_{i=1}^{p_{1}}
and
\eta_{2}S^{d-1}\subset\conv\{b_{j}^{P}\}_{j=1}^{p_{2}}
,

•

there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that
\alpha P\in\mathrm{SDP}_{n}^{p_{1},p_{2}}
.

Then we have the following bound:

K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}.

(14)

Proof.

Consider a matrix
M
of size
m_{1}\times m_{2}
.
Since

\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)=\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\max_{N\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}}\left\langle{M},{N}\right\rangle=\max_{N\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}}\left\langle{M},{\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,N}\right\rangle,

(15)

we focus on
\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,N
for
N\in\mathrm{SDP}_{d}^{m_{1},m_{2}}
with underlying vectors
a_{1}^{N},\ldots,a_{m_{1}}^{N},b_{1}^{N},\ldots,b_{m_{2}}^{N}\in S^{d-1}
.

By the first assumption on
\{a_{i}^{P}\}_{i=1}^{p_{1}}
and
\{b_{j}^{P}\}_{j=1}^{p_{2}}
, we know that we can write, for
x\in[m_{1}]
and
y\in[m_{2}]
,

\eta_{1}a_{x}^{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{p_{1}}v_{i}^{(x)}a_{i}^{P}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{2}b_{y}^{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{2}}w_{j}^{(y)}b_{j}^{P},\quad\text{so that}\quad\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,N_{xy}=\alpha\sum_{i,j}v_{i}^{(x)}w_{j}^{(y)}\left\langle{a_{i}^{P}},{b_{j}^{P}}\right\rangle

(16)

where
v_{i}^{(x)}\geqslant 0
,
w_{j}^{(y)}\geqslant 0
,
\sum_{i}v_{i}^{(x)}=1
, and
\sum_{j}w_{j}^{(y)}=1
.

Next we use the second assumption, which can be written as follows: there exist positive weights
\lambda_{k}
summing up to one and vectors
\tilde{a}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{p_{1}}^{(k)},\tilde{b}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\tilde{b}_{p_{2}}^{(k)}\in S^{n-1}
indexed by
k
(in a finite set) such that
\alpha\langle{a_{i}^{P}},{b_{j}^{P}}\rangle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\langle{\tilde{a}_{i}^{(k)}},{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(k)}}\rangle
holds for all
i\in[p_{1}]
and
j\in[p_{2}]
.
This gives, for
x\in[m_{1}]
and
y\in[m_{2}]
,

\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,N_{xy}=\sum_{i,j}v_{i}^{(x)}w_{j}^{(y)}\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\left\langle{\tilde{a}_{i}^{(k)}},{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(k)}}\right\rangle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\left\langle{\sum_{i}v_{i}^{(x)}\tilde{a}_{i}^{(k)}},{\sum_{j}w_{j}^{(y)}\tilde{b}_{j}^{(k)}}\right\rangle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\left\langle{\tilde{a}_{x}^{(k)}},{\tilde{b}_{y}^{(k)}}\right\rangle,

(17)

where we have defined the vectors
\tilde{a}_{x}^{(k)}=\sum_{i}v_{i}^{(x)}\tilde{a}_{i}^{(k)}
and
\tilde{b}_{y}^{(k)}=\sum_{j}w_{j}^{(y)}\tilde{a}_{j}^{(k)}
in the
n
-dimensional unit ball
B^{n}
.

Combining
Eq.

15
with
Eq.

17
and using the definition of
\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)
in
Eq.

1
, we obtain

\alpha\,\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,\mathrm{SDP}_{d}\left({M}\right)\leqslant\max\left\{\sum_{x=1}^{m_{1}}\sum_{y=1}^{m_{2}}M_{xy}\left\langle{\tilde{a}_{x}},{\tilde{b}_{y}}\right\rangle\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ \forall x\in[m_{1}],\penalty\ \tilde{a}_{x}\in B^{n},\penalty\ \forall y\in[m_{2}],\penalty\ \tilde{b}_{y}\in B^{n}\right\}\leqslant\mathrm{SDP}_{n}\left({M}\right),

(18)

so that
Eq.

14
follows from the definition of
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{n}\right)
.
∎

To obtain our bound on
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)
, we start from the Gram matrix
P
obtained from a centrosymmetric polyhedron with 812 vertices, that is, 406 lines.
Note that this polyhedron is different from the one used in
[
14
]
and features a slightly higher shrinking factor through the following construction: starting from an icosahedron, apply four times the transformation adding the normalised centres of each (triangular) facet.
Taking
v_{0}=0.8962
and running our Frank-Wolfe algorithm, we obtain a decomposition using 7886 extreme points of
\mathrm{SDP}_{2}^{406}
and recovering
v_{0}P
up to a Euclidean distance of
\varepsilon\approx 2.7\times 10^{-4}
.
This algorithm is strictly identical to the one in
[
14
]
except for the LMO, which corresponds to the case described in
Section

III.1
when
n=2
.
Following
[
14
, Appendix D]
we can make this decomposition purely analytical by noting that the unit ball for the Euclidean norm is contained in
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}
and therefore in
\mathrm{SDP}_{2}
.
Note that, contrary to
[
14
]
where the extremal points of
\mathrm{SDP}_{1}
were automatically analytical as they only have
\pm 1
elements, an extra step is needed here to make numerical extremal points of
\mathrm{SDP}_{2}
analytical.
Here we follow a rationalisation procedure very similar to the one described in
[
14
, Appendix A]
and we obtain a value
\alpha=v_{0}/(1+\varepsilon)
that can be used in
Eq.

14
to derive the bound

K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)\leqslant 1.1233\ldots

(19)

whose analytical expression is provided in the supplementary files
[
40
]
.
This upper bound is indeed entirely rigorous, contrary to the lower bounds presented above in
Section

IV.2
, which rely on numerical methods that we did not convert into exact results.

IV.4
Implications for Bell nonlocality

The connection between
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
and Bell nonlocality was first established by Tsirelson
[
5
]
and was later investigated more thoroughly in
[
7
]
.
We refer to the appendix of
[
18
]
for a detailed construction of the quantum realisation of the Bell inequalities constructed in our work.
Note that the new lower bound mentioned in
Section

III.3
slightly improves on the upper bound on
v_{c}^{\mathrm{Wer}}=1/K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
, namely, it reduces it from about
0.69606
to
0.69604
; see also
[
14
]
for the definition of this number and its interpretation as a noise robustness.

Works anticipating this connection with
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
go back to 2008
[
47
,
18
]
, when Vértesi and Pál investigated the realisation of Bell inequalities in real quantum mechanics and showed that
d
-dimensional complex systems can outperform real ones.
This line of research was further developed by Briët et al.
[
20
]
.

We refer to
[
18
]
for the explicit connection; for this, the examination of
Table

3
should be of some help as it gives the bridge between their notations and ours.

V
Discussion

We generalised the method used in
[
14
]
to obtain lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
to Grothendieck constants of higher order
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
.
We obtained certified lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({3}{}\right)
,
K_{G}\left({4}{}\right)
, and
K_{G}\left({5}{}\right)
beating previous ones, and setting, by monotonicity, the best known lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}{}\right)
for
3\leqslant d\leqslant 9
.
For these constants of higher orders, we also showed how our Frank-Wolfe algorithm can be used to derive putative facets, but solving the corresponding quadratic binary problems remains open.
The instances given in this article would allow immediate improvement on
K_{G}\left({4}{}\right)
,
K_{G}\left({7}{}\right)
, and
K_{G}\left({8}{}\right)
.
We expect future work to exploit their symmetry to make the computation possible.

Beyond the perhaps anecdotal improvement brought to their respective constants, developing such tools could unlock access to higher-dimensional structures with a size way larger than anything that numerical methods could ever consider solving.
This could in turn give rise to lower bounds on finite-order Grothendieck constants strong enough to compete with the best known lower bound on
the
(infinite-order) Grothendieck constant, namely, the one presented by Davie in 1984
[
48
]
and independently rediscovered by Reeds in 1991
[
49
]
, both works being unpublished.
This bound reads

K_{G}\geqslant\sup_{0<\lambda<1}\frac{1-\rho(\lambda)}{\max\{\rho(\lambda),F_{\rho(\lambda)}(\lambda)\}}\approx 1.676956674\ldots,\quad\text{attained for}\quad\lambda\approx 0.255730213\ldots

(20)

where

\rho(\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\hphantom{{}^{2}\!\!}\lambda^{2}\!\!}{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{\rho}(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{2}}+\rho\left(1-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{\lambda}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\hphantom{{}^{2}\!\!}x^{2}\!\!}{2}}\mathrm{d}x\right).

(21)

We also extended the procedure of
[
14
]
to derive bounds on the generalised constant
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
.
These constants appear quite naturally when considering the advantage of quantum mechanics over real quantum mechanics.
In particular, our analytical upper bound on
K_{G}\left({3}\mapsto{2}\right)
delivers some quantitative insight on this question, while our numerical lower bounds on
K_{G}\left({d}\mapsto{2}\right)
could prove useful when looking for estimates of the benefit of considering higher-dimensional quantum systems.

We also note that our techniques naturally apply in the complex case, for which similar Grothendieck constants have also been defined.
However, since the complex finite-order Grothendieck constants do not have known interpretations, we refrained from deriving bounds on them, although our code is already capable of dealing with this case.
