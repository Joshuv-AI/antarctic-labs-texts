Dimethyl fumarate improves nuclear factor erythroid-related factor 2-mediated antioxidant response to ameliorate functional and molecular deficits in experimental diabetic neuropathy
Authors: Amrutha Jindam, Anil Kumar Kalvala, Vijay Kumar Arruri, Sourav Das, Ashutosh Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:In the present study, we tested the functional/pharmacological significance of dimethyl fumarate (DMF) in streptozotocin-induced diabetic neuropathy (DN) in rats and high glucose-exposed Neuro2a (N2a) cells.MATERIALS AND METHODS:To evaluate the pharmacological effects of DMF on diabetic neuropathy, we assessed behavioral and functional parameters of peripheral neuropathy, oxidative stress markers, and target protein expression using immunohistochemistry/immunocytochemistry, and Western blotting in diabetic rats and hyperglycemic N2a cells.RESULTS:Diabetic rats exhibited hyperalgesia, allodynia, and compromised sensory and motor nerve conduction velocities in comparison to normal rats. Dorsal root ganglias of diabetic rats showed decreased antioxidant levels and increased pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as nuclear factor erythroid-related factor 2 and nuclear factor-kappa B, alongside reduced expression of the heat shock protein (HSP) 90. Administering DMF to diabetic rats for 2 weeks reversed these effects in a dose-dependent manner. We observed significant compromise in mitochondrial function, indicated by reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, increased free radical levels, and compromised mitochondrial complex activities in N2a cells exposed to elevated glucose levels. Conversely, DMF treatment restored mitochondrial function and augmented mitochondrial biogenesis through the upregulation of PGC-1α and improved chaperone activity by increasing the expression of HSP 60 and HSP 70.CONCLUSIONS:Overall, DMF alleviated neurobehavioral deficits in DN rats and enhanced mitochondrial function and chaperone activity under hyperglycemic conditions in both diabetic rats and N2a cells.

Introduction

The global socioeconomic burden of diabetes continues to increase over the years due to a surge in the incidence of new cases and the lack of disease-modifying strategies.[1] Notably, the relative rise in new diabetes cases is anticipated to be highest in middle-income countries (21.1%) compared to 11.9% in low-income countries and 12.2% in high-income countries.[2] Diabetic neuropathy (DN) is one of the most frequent and severe complications of diabetes, with no available therapeutic choice to adequately control the pain and its refractory nature. Hence, developing effective strategies to circumvent DN is necessary to improve the patient’s overall quality of life.

Accumulating evidence highlights the role of oxidative stress and perturbed expression of molecular chaperones termed heat shock proteins (HSPs) in the pathophysiology of DN.[3] High glucose (HG)-induced excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) from mitochondria is considered a single upstream pathological event that drives different malicious signaling pathways, including polyol pathway, increased advanced glycation end products formation, activation of protein kinase C isoforms, and hexosamine pathway that underlies the metabolic abnormalities associated with diabetes.[4] The nuclear factor erythroid 2 -related factor 2 (Nrf-2) belongs to the family of basic region leucine zipper transcription factors, whose activation is tightly regulated by the redox status of the cell.[5] Nrf-2-mediated antioxidant response is compromised in diabetic conditions and its activation has been shown to mitigate various sensory, functional, and biochemical abnormalities associated with DN.[6] HSPs are constitutively expressed molecular chaperones essential for the normal growth of the cells and are also induced in response to various physiological and pathological stimuli to regulate various biological processes including protein folding, intracellular transport of proteins, and the translocation of cellular organelles across the membrane.[78] Substantial evidence indicates the therapeutic potential of modulating HSPs to manage neuropathy in different rodent models.[910]

Dimethyl fumarate (DMF), a methyl ester of fumaric acid, has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration as an orally bioavailable option for treating relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis in 2013 and has been used in Europe for the treatment of psoriasis.[11] Moreover, DMF is a well-known Nrf-2 activator and its therapeutic potential has been demonstrated in several neuronal diseases.[12] In the context of neuropathic pain, although a recent study reported that DMF oral treatment alleviated pain behavior in rats following spared nerve injury, its effect on diabetic neuropathy has not yet been studied.[13] In the present study, we tested the pharmacological efficacy of oral DMF treatment to mitigate diabetes-induced neuropathic behavior in rats.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and kits

Streptozotocin (STZ) and the majority of the chemicals utilized were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (USA). DMF was sourced from TCI Chemicals, India. Immunohistochemistry detection kit and isoflurane were obtained from Raman and Weil Pvt. Ltd. (Mumbai, India), respectively. All the chemicals and solvents used were of analytical grade.

Neuronal cell culture

The mouse neuroblastoma (Neuro2a [N2A]) cell lines, acquired from the National Centre for Cell Sciences in Pune, India, were grown in Minimum Essential Medium enhanced with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% streptomycin/penicillin, and 2% glutamine. The cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. Hyperglycemia was induced by exposing cells to HG at a final concentration of 30 mM, and based on the results of the MTT assay, the doses of DMF (5 and 10 μM) were selected to assess its neuroprotective potential.

Experimental animals and induction of diabetes

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (250–300 g), aged 12 weeks, were housed in cages (temperature 24°C ± 1°C, humidity 55 ± 5%) with 12 h of light and dark cycles, fed on a standard diet and water ad libitum. All the experiments performed were as per the guidelines prescribed by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals and Institutional Animal Ethics Committee, NIPER-Hyderabad (NIP/8/2017/PC/249). A single intraperitoneal dose of STZ (55 mg/kg) in ice-cold citrate buffer (pH 4.5) as a vehicle was used to induce experimental diabetes in animals. GOD-POD kit (Accurex, India) was used for plasma glucose level estimation after 48 h of STZ injection. Diabetic animals (blood glucose levels >250 mg/dL) were divided into three different groups (8 animals each), namely, diabetic control (STZ-D), DMF-treated low dose (STZD + DMF1), and DMF-treated high dose (STZD + DMF2). Healthy control animals were randomized into two groups (8 animals each): normal control (NC) and treatment control (DMF-2) administered orally with DMF (50 mg/kg). The treatment started at 6 weeks after diabetes induction for 2 weeks (6th–8th week). The treatment groups received two oral doses of DMF; 25 mg/kg (STZD + DMF1) and 50 mg/kg (STZD + DMF2) by suspending it in 0.5% CMC. Twenty-four hours after the administration of the final dose, the behavioral (nociceptive) and functional parameters were measured (end of 8th week). Sciatic nerves and dorsal root ganglias (DRGs) from each animal were used for biochemical evaluations.[6]

Behavioral parameters

Thermal hyperalgesia was evaluated by tail immersion method (hot immersion test at 55 ± 2°C temperature; cutoff time 15 s), hot plate test (cutoff time 20 s),[14] and tail flick response test (cutoff time 15 s) as described previously.[15] Mechanical hypersensitivity to noxious stimuli was assessed using the Randall–Selitto apparatus (IITC Life Sciences, USA). To evaluate mechanical allodynia, Von Frey fibers (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 15 g; Samitek, USA) were applied gently to the plantar surface of the rat paw, gradually moving from lower to higher pressure, with paw withdrawal being counted as a positive endpoint.[16]

Functional parameters

Motor and sensory nerve conduction velocities

Motor nerve conduction velocity (MNCV) and sensory nerve conduction velocity (SNCV) were assessed using the PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Australia). After anesthesia, needle electrodes were used to perform sciatic nerve stimulation at the sciatic notch or Achilles tendon, at a stimulus of 3V. To record the stimulation, bipolar surface electrodes were inserted into the plantar surface of the foot muscles, and the M-wave and H-reflex latencies were estimated through LabChart software. MNCV and SNCV (m/s) were obtained from the physical distances between stimulation and recording points and latencies of wave appearance.[17]

Nerve blood flow

Nerve blood flow (NBF) measurements were conducted using a laser Doppler flow system (Moor Instruments, UK). After anesthetizing the animals, the left sciatic nerve was exposed, and a laser probe was applied gently in contact with the sciatic trunk, ensuring minimal interference from epineural or perineural blood vessels. Flux measurements were obtained, with the NBF being reported as arbitrary perfusion units.[15]

Biochemical evaluation

Plasma glucose levels estimation

Blood samples obtained from the tail vein were collected into microcentrifuge tubes containing 5000 IU of heparin. Plasma blood glucose levels (mg/dL) were determined using a GOD-POD kit (Accurex, India) following the manufacturer’s protocol.[18]

Measurement of reduced glutathione

Reduced glutathione (GSH) levels in sciatic nerve homogenates were determined using Ellman’s method. Five percent trichloroacetic acid was used to precipitate the proteins in sciatic nerve homogenate. Subsequently, 100 μL of DTNB solution was added to the resulting supernatant and the mixture was incubated for 10 min at 37°C. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm and the results are expressed in terms of μM/mg protein (GSH).[14]

Measurement of lipid peroxidation

To measure the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method was used. Briefly, 1.5 mL of 20% glacial acetic acid solution (pH 3.4), 0.2 mL of 8.1% SDS, and 1.5 mL of 0.8% thiobarbituric acid were mixed with 0.2 mL of sciatic nerve protein homogenate. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 95°C for 60 min, centrifuged and the absorbance measured at 532 nm. The concentration is reported as TBARS in μM/mg.[18]

Measurement of nitrite levels

Griess test was used to assess the nitrite levels in sciatic nerve homogenates. To 150 μL of tissue homogenate, 20 μL of Griess reagent was added along with 130 μL of deionized water, followed by incubation for 30 min at 37°C. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 548 nm. The nitrite concentration is expressed as μM/mg of protein.[19]

Mitochondrial complex enzymes activity assay

Mitochondrial complex activities of Complex I (ab287847), II (ab109908), and III (ab287844) were evaluated in mitochondria isolated from L4-L6 DRGs as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Values are expressed as mOD per minute per milligram of protein after protein normalization.[15]

Measurement of mitochondrial potential (Δψm) by JC-1 dye

The JC-1 staining method was employed to measure mitochondrial membrane potential, following previously described protocols. Following plating in a 6-well plate, N2A cells were exposed to the specified concentrations of DMF, for 6 h. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).[6]

Immunocytochemistry

The cells were cultured on appropriate glass coverslips placed in 6-well plates and subjected to various specified treatment conditions. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, followed by a 10-min treatment with 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature to increase permeability. Following blocking with 3% BSA, the cells were incubated with PGC-1α primary antibody (CST, USA) at a 1:400 dilution, followed by a secondary anti-rat FITC-conjugated antibody. The coverslips were subsequently mounted onto glass slides with DAPI (Fluoroshield™) (Sigma) and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8 Laser Scanning Spectral Confocal, Germany).[19]

Immunohistochemistry

The L4-L6 DRG microsections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using a series of gradient alcohols. Following blocking with BSA, the sections were incubated with SOD-2 antibody (CST, USA), followed by secondary antibody. Chromogenic staining using the VECTASTAIN® Elite ABC Reagent kit (Vector Labs, CA, USA) was performed as per the manufacturer’s protocol, to achieve the desired brown intensity. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, the sections were mounted with DPX and observed under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan).[2021]

Western blotting analysis

Proteins extracted from cells and the DRGs of animals were separated by SDS-PAGE. Following the transfer, the nitrocellulose membranes were blocked with 3% BSA. Next, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies targeting ATP synthase C (Abcam, USA), SOD2, NRF-1, Nrf-2, HSP70 and β-actin (CST, USA) (1:1000) for cells, and Nrf-2, SOD2, NQO-1, NF-kB, CLPX1, and β-actin (CST, USA) (1:1000) for tissues overnight at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, USA) at a dilution of 1:20,000 for 2 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescence signals were visualized using a Fusion-FX chemiluminescence imager (Vilber Lourmat, Germany). The relative band densities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.48, NIH, USA).[16]

Statistical analysis

The data collected in this study were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Intergroup variations were assessed using one-way analysis of variance with GraphPad Prism software Version 5.0, (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test was used for post hoc analysis, and statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

Dimethyl fumarate treatment attenuates neurobehavioral and functional deficits in diabetic rats

Two of the most notable neuropathic features seen in DN phenotypes are hyperalgesia and allodynia. Diabetic rats displayed a significant decrease (P < 0.001) in both tail withdrawal latency and paw withdrawal latency during the hot water immersion test and hot plate test, respectively [Figure 1a and b]. In addition, during the mechanical allodynia assessment using Vonfrey filaments, diabetic rats showed a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in paw withdrawal threshold as compared to normoglycemic rats [Figure 1c]. A similar trend was observed (P < 0.001) in diabetic rats during the Randal–Selitto test while assessing mechanical hyperalgesia [Figure 1d]. However, DMF treatment for 14 days dose-dependently reversed this amplified pain-responsive behavior compared to diabetic rats. It is apparent that excess blood glucose in diabetes severely compromises NBF and its electrochemical impulse propagation abilities. A significant reduction (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001) in motor and sensory nerve conduction velocities in the sciatic-tibial system of diabetic rats was seen, accompanied by decreased (P < 0.001) sciatic NBF compared to normal rats. However, 14 days of DMF administration significantly improved MNCV (P < 0.05; DMF 25 mg/kg and P < 0.001; DMF 50 mg/kg, p.o.) [Figure 1e], as well as SNCV and sciatic NBF (P < 0.01; DMF 25 mg/kg and P < 0.001; DMF 50 mg/kg, p.o.) in diabetic rats [Figures 1f and 1g].

Dimethyl fumarate treatment curtails oxidative stress markers in diabetic rats

Metabolic imbalance provoked by HG-induced oxidative stress during diabetes is considered one of the key pathomechanisms of diabetic neuropathy. Diabetic rats showed increased (P < 0.001) sciatic levels of nitrite, lipid peroxidation, and reduced GSH in comparison to NC rats. However, the nitrite and MDA levels were significantly reduced (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001; DMF 25 mg/kg, p. o and P < 0.001; DMF 50 mg/kg, p. o) after 14 days of treatment with DMF [Figure 2a and b], which was accompanied by elevated levels (P < 0.01; DMF 25 mg/kg, p. o and P < 0.001; DMF 50 mg/kg, p. o) of GSH [Figure 2c].

Dimethyl fumarate treatment improves antioxidant defense and mitochondrial function in diabetic rats

We further analyzed the antioxidant markers and mitochondrial parameters in the DRG homogenates. The protein expression of Nrf-2 and its target gene NQO-1 in the DRGs of diabetic rats were markedly reduced (P < 0.001) compared to normoglycemic rats. SOD2, HSP90, and Clpx1 protein levels were also markedly decreased (P < 0.001) in DRGs of diabetic rats whereas expression of nuclear factor-kappa B was significantly high (P < 0.001). However, a significant reversal of these changes was observed when treated with DMF for 14 days (P < 0.001; DMF 50 mg/kg, p. o) [Figure 3]. There was also a significant decrease in (P < 0.001) in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, complex II, and complex III activities in the DRGs of diabetic animals in comparison to normal animals. DMF administration (50 mg/kg, p. o.) significantly restored the activity of these complex enzymes (P < 0.001) [Figure 4a-c]. In addition, the immunopositivity of SOD2 [Figure 4d] and HSP90 [Figure 4e] was found to be markedly decreased in the DRG microsections of diabetic rats as compared to normal rats. However, DMF treatment markedly restored the expression of these proteins.

Dimethyl fumarate treatment restores mitochondrial membrane potential in high glucose-challenged neuro2a cells

HG triggers mitochondrial dysfunction through excess ROS production and is the pathological hallmark of HG-induced neurotoxicity. We studied the effect of DMF on ROS production and mitochondrial membrane potential in HG-challenged N2a cells. DMF doses 5 μM and 10 μM were selected based on the MTT assay [Figure 5a]. There was a significant reduction in the ratio of J-aggregate (red) to J-monomer (green) in the N2a cells challenged with HG indicating the compromised mitochondrial membrane potential [Figure 5b-f]. However, treatment with DMF significantly decreased the ROS production (P < 0.001; DMF 10 μM) and restored (P < 0.01) the mitochondrial membrane potential.

Dimethyl fumarate treatment augments antioxidant markers and mitochondrial function in high glucose-challenged neuro2a cells

Immunocytochemistry results showed a decreased immunopositivity of PGC-1α in N2a cells exposed to HG in comparison to control cells, whereas DFM (10 μM) treated cells showed increased PGC-1α immunoreactivity indicating the beneficial effects of DMF in augmenting mitochondrial biogenesis [Figure 5g]. The protein levels of antioxidant markers and heat shock response such as Nrf-2, SOD2, HSP90, and HSP60 were significantly reduced (P < 0.001) in the N2a cells exposed to HG. Similarly, the expression of NRF-1 and ATP synthase C were markedly decreased (P < 0.001) in HG-challenged cells indicating a compromise in mitochondrial function under excess glucose conditions. DMF treatment significantly restored these protein levels (P < 0.001 for DMF 10 µM) [Figure 6].

Discussion

Chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes triggers ROS production which contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction that potentiates pathological amplification of pain signaling, ultimately leading to the initiation and progression of DN.[15] Accumulating evidence also suggests the dysregulation of HSPs in the development of neuropathic pain under diabetic conditions.[22] Here, we studied the effects of DMF administration on oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and HSPs to alleviate the functional and molecular deficits associated with STZ-induced DN in rats.

Diabetic rats exhibited increased pain responses, with decreased paw withdrawal thresholds indicating hyperalgesia and allodynia, key features of neuropathic pain. Two weeks of DMF treatment controlled these effects, potentially improving NBF and sensory and MNCVs. In hyperglycemic conditions, oxidative stress often reduces the activity of Nrf-2, a key redox-regulated transcription factor. We and others previously showed that Nrf-2 activation is beneficial in the attenuation of neuropathic features under diabetic conditions.[2324] Substantial evidence indicates that DMF not only activates Nrf-2 but is also responsible for its stabilization which induces its target gene expression in a sustained manner.[25] In line with our previous findings, DRG homogenates of diabetic animals showed a marked reduction in Nrf-2 levels, which is correlated with the increased oxidative stress parameters as evidenced by enhanced lipid peroxidation and nitrite levels. In addition, the levels of GSH were decreased in diabetic rats manifesting the imbalance between pro- and antioxidant capacities under hyperglycemic conditions. These effects are reversed by DMF treatment. The increased antioxidant defense after DMF treatment is also manifested by a significant increase in the expression of Nrf-2 downstream target NQO-1 and a mitochondrial-free radicle scavenger enzyme SOD2. Antioxidant activity of SOD2 plays an important role in neuronal oxidative defense under HG conditions, which is supported by the finding that SOD2 haploinsufficiency led to the development of neuropathy in diabetic mice.[26] We consider the protective effects of DMF to attenuate neuropathic behavior in rats partly due to its profound antioxidant effects.

HG exposure-induced mitochondrial dysfunction severely compromises the metabolic indices of sensory neurons that contribute to altered conduction of pain signaling seen in DN. Mitochondrial membrane potential generated by respiratory chain complexes is essential to drive ATP synthesis through oxidative phosphorylation by harnessing the transmembrane potential of the hydrogen ions.[27] Loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and excessive production of mitochondrial superoxides in HG-exposed N2a cells suggest compromised mitochondrial function, which is significantly attenuated by DMF treatment. PGC-1 alpha is a transcription coactivator that regulates a wide spectrum of physiological responses in cells,[28] and chronic hyperglycemia results in decreased expression of PGC-1 alpha which is associated with altered mitochondrial homeostasis leading to defective energy metabolism seen in DN.[14] Moreover, diabetic mice lacking PGC-1 alpha showed a severe form of neuropathy with increased mitochondrial dysfunction as compared to control diabetic mice, suggesting the neuroprotective role of PGC-1 alpha in limiting DN progression.[29] On the other hand, transcription factor NRF-1 induces nuclear genes responsible for mitochondrial respiration and maintenance of mitochondrial DNA integrity.[30] Decreased levels of NRF-1 are correlated with neuropathic behavior under hyperglycemia and various published reports indicate the importance of restoration of NRF-1 to combat DN.[14] Consistent with these findings, we observed a significant reduction in PGC-1 alpha and NRF-1 in N2a cells exposed to HG conditions suggesting aberrant mitochondrial homeostasis which is reversed by DMF treatment. Recent evidence suggest that the altered HSP70 expression underlies the pathogenesis of diabetes and its associated complications.[3] Herein, we observed a reduced expression of HSP70 and HSP90 in DRGs of diabetic rats, which might underlie the compromised chaperone activity in diabetic rats which is reversed by DMF treatment.

Conclusion

Overall, DMF strengthened antioxidant defense and improved mitochondrial function and molecular chaperones to alleviate abnormalities in nerve conduction and neuropathic behavior in diabetic rats. Further studies are warranted to identify the intricate molecular mechanism for the neuroprotective effects of DMF and can be further pursued for futuristic management of diabetic neuropathy and neuropathic pain.
