Association of baseline eosinophil counts with clinical prognosis in cancer patients undergoing immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy
Authors: Wanrong Jiang, Tinghui Mo, Chen Chen, Jiarui Feng, Weixing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBaseline eosinophil indices can capture host immune tone during immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. We synthesized evidence from 30 studies (4,385 patients) to quantify how baseline absolute (AEC) and relative eosinophil count (REC) relate to outcomes. Compared with lower AEC, high AEC was associated with longer overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), higher disease control, and objective response, and increased immune-related adverse events. High REC likewise predicted improved OS and PFS. These results position routine eosinophil measures as low-cost biomarkers for prognostic stratification and for balancing expected benefit with toxicity risk in cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Over the past few years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including inhibitors targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, have demonstrated unprecedented effectiveness in managing various advanced cancers, such as melanoma,1 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC),2 and renal cell carcinoma,3,4 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).5,6,7,8 Notably, the phase III NADINA trial (NCT04949113), published in NEJM, showed that neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab significantly extended event-free survival in resectable stage III melanoma.9 At a median follow-up of 9.9 months, the 12-month event-free survival was 83.7% (99.9% CI, 73.8–94.8).9 The 5-year follow-up of the CheckMate 040 trial demonstrated durable responses and sustained survival in patients with advanced HCC treated with nivolumab plus ipilimumab after sorafenib.10 These findings underscore the evolving role of ICIs in routine oncological practice and further support their inclusion in treatment guidelines for cancers. As detailed in the Cancer Research Institute’s 2025 Cancer Immunotherapy Insights + Impact Report, the total number of FDA-approved immunotherapeutic agents surpassed 150 by late 2024, including 17 additions within that year alone—of which ICIs accounted for 81%.11,12 Since 2011, their clinical implementation has grown by more than 20-fold. Market forecasts valued the global ICI industry at approximately 47 billion in 2023, with projections indicating an increase to over 230 billion by 2033–2034, reflecting an estimated compound annual growth rate of nearly 17%.13 Unlike platinum-based chemotherapy, ICIs have been associated with notable improvements in survival outcomes while presenting a comparatively lower toxicity profile. Despite their substantial therapeutic impact, ICIs benefit only some patients across most cancer types. The modest response rates, combined with high treatment costs and the potential for serious immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies, myocarditis, and neurologic syndromes, limit their broad applicability. Current biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden, offer only limited predictive value and lack consistency across tumor types. Moreover, resistance mechanisms remain poorly defined, and the underrepresentation of certain populations in clinical trials raises concerns about generalizability. These limitations underscore the need to identify reliable markers of response and toxicity to better guide patients in the selection of ICI therapy.

Currently, IHC testing for PD-L1 is a biologic predictor of prognosis for cancer patients after treatment with ICIs, but minimally invasive predictors are more acceptable to patients.14 Emerging evidence has highlighted the strong correlation between hematological indices and the ICI effectiveness. Of particular interest is the eosinophil count, an accessible biomarker that has recently garnered widespread attention.15 Eosinophils contribute to the tumor microenvironment in multifaceted ways, including directly inducing tumor cell cytotoxicity through the release of toxic granules and modulating anti-tumor immunity by engaging with T cells.16 Notably, eosinophils exhibit unique immunologic properties that distinguish them from other leukocyte subsets. Through rapid degranulation and interactions with dendritic cells, macrophages, and cytotoxic lymphocytes, eosinophils act as key orchestrators of both innate and adaptive immune responses. Their involvement in vascular remodeling and immune cell recruitment further enhances their relevance in shaping the tumor milieu.17 These distinct biological roles position eosinophils as promising biomarkers and potential immunologic modulators in the context of immune checkpoint blockade.

Findings from several clinical studies suggest that eosinophil counts may serve as distinct and reliable predictors when compared to other inflammatory markers. For instance, pre-treatment eosinophil levels have been identified as independent prognostic indicators in patients undergoing immunotherapies such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, or a combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab. High baseline eosinophil levels have been positively linked to prolonged overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in NSCLC patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors.18

In this context, we focused on two commonly reported eosinophil indices: Absolute eosinophil count (AEC), defined as the absolute number of eosinophils per unit volume of peripheral blood (×109/L), and relative eosinophil count (REC), defined as the proportion of eosinophils among circulating leukocytes (%). Although AEC and REC are correlated, they capture different aspects of the host immune and inflammatory status—AEC reflects the absolute eosinophil burden, whereas REC reflects the eosinophil fraction within the white blood cell compartment, which may diverge in the setting of leukocytosis or lymphopenia. We therefore analyzed AEC and REC separately to clarify their respective prognostic utilities. The present study aimed to determine whether baseline AEC or REC, measured before the initiation of ICI therapy, is associated with clinical outcomes in patients with advanced cancers, thereby contributing to precision medicine and supporting evidence-based treatment decision-making in this population.

Baseline AEC and OS、PFS

Among the 30 studies included in our study, 16 reported the association between baseline AEC and OS, contributing a total of 2,232 cancer patients treated with ICIs. Due to substantial inter-study variability (I2 = 51.6%, p = 0.007), statistical processing employed random-effects modeling. Results revealed that high pre-therapy AEC significantly correlated with superior survival outcomes (HR: 0.655, 95% CI: 0.542–0.791, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). To further explore sources of between-study heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses by geography and tumor type. The data demonstrated that subjects exhibiting higher pre-treatment eosinophil enumeration experienced significantly reduced progression risk (HR:0.633, 95%CI: 0.500–0.801, p < 0.001) (Figure 4A). Despite considerable heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 56.0%, p = 0.009) necessitating random-effects modeling, the principal finding remained statistically significant. To further explore sources of heterogeneity in the association between baseline AEC and PFS, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by tumor type (HCC, melanoma, NSCLC, RCC, and other malignancies). High baseline AEC was consistently associated with longer PFS, with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (HCC, NSCLC, RCC). In contrast, the melanoma and “other” subgroups displayed substantial residual heterogeneity (I2 = 86.6% and 60.4%), likely reflecting differences in study design, patient characteristics, and treatment regimens. Overall, these findings suggest that tumor type is an important contributor to the between-study heterogeneity observed in the pooled PFS analysis. (Figure S2).

Baseline AEC and DCR、ORR

Next, we examined whether associations exist between baseline AEC and therapeutic outcomes following ICIs in malignancy cases. Due to minimal heterogeneity detected in the analytical outcomes depicted in Figure 6, the application of a fixed effect statistical model was deemed appropriate. Across six studies reporting DCR and including 858 patients, high baseline AEC was associated with a significantly higher probability of disease control (OR: 1.474, 95% CI: 1.083–2.006, p = 0.014; Figure 6A). Similarly, five studies reporting ORR and comprising 764 patients showed a consistent trend toward improved responses in the high-AEC group (OR: 1.982, 95% CI: 1.131–3.473, p = 0.017; Figure 6B). For ORR, a notable degree of heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 55.6%, p = 0.061), which is likely related to the diversity and complexity of the tumor types included across the studies.Figure 6Forest plots of the association between baseline AEC(A) Disease control rate.(B) Objective response rate.OR, odds ratio; CI, confidence interval.

Baseline AEC and irAEs

ICIs are highly likely to be accompanied by irAEs. To determine whether high baseline AEC can predict irAEs, we adopted a random effects model. Interestingly, patients with high baseline AEC had a significantly elevated risk of irAEs compared to those with low baseline AEC (4 investigations reported, 541 participants, OR: 2.433, 95% CI: 1.255–4.714) (Figure 7). The analysis of irAEs also showed a certain degree of heterogeneity. This is likely related to the clinical heterogeneity of the included malignancies and treatment regimens, as well as differences in the definition, grading, and reporting of irAEs across studies. Given the limited number of studies contributing to this endpoint, more refined subgroup or meta-regression analyses were not feasible, and these results should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.Figure 7Forest plot of the relationship between baseline AEC and immune-related adverse eventsOR, odds ratio; CI, confidence interval.

Baseline REC and OS、PFS

Among all eligible studies, our investigation encompassed data from seven independent studies with a total of 1357 participants to evaluate potential correlations between initial REC and OS in malignancy patients treated with ICIs. Statistical analysis revealed minimal heterogeneity across these investigations (I2 = 22.1%, p = 0.267), prompting the application of a fixed-effects model for calculating the combined HR. Results indicated that high baseline REC correlated significantly with extended OS duration (HR: 0.452, 95% CI: 0.376–0.543, p < 0.001) (Figure S3A).

For PFS assessment, we evaluated four studies focused on REC measurements, comprising 406 participants collectively. Aggregated data analysis demonstrated that higher initial REC exhibited a significant association with improved PFS outcomes (HR: 0.627, 95% CI: 0.486–0.810, p = 0.005) (Figure S3B).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the reliability of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically excluding individual studies to assess the effects on the aggregated outcomes. The analysis revealed that omitting any single study did not substantially alter the pooled HR for OS or PFS in relation to baseline AEC thresholds. Regardless of which study was excluded, the combined results for the remaining studies retained statistical significance, as illustrated in Figure S4. Similarly, the sensitivity test for baseline REC showed that removing individual studies had no major influence on the overall analysis concerning OS and PFS outcomes (Figure S5). However, the substantial influence of individual studies on the pooled DCR estimates for AEC. As demonstrated in Figure S6A, the exclusion of the Caliman et al. (2022) trial resulted in a notable alteration of the effect magnitude, yielding an adjusted OR of 1.191 (95% CI: 0.840–1.689) that crossed the null threshold.23 The findings indicate that the literature is sensitive to the combined effects, and the results are not robust.

The exclusion of individual studies substantially affected the pooled estimates (Figure S6B). Omitting Bai et al. (2021) resulted in an OR of 2.246 (95% CI: 0.969–5.205); the exclusion of Ikoma et al. (2024) produced the same OR with a wider CI (0.867–5.817). Removal of Ma et al. (2022) yielded an OR of 2.147 (95% CI, 0.969–4.760), with the CI crossing the null.31,47,48 These findings indicate that the overall estimate is sensitive to single studies and may lack robustness.

In leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, after excluding Chu et al. (2020), the pooled estimate for the association between baseline AEC and ORR was OR = 2.221 (95% CI: 0.931–5.302), suggesting that this study influenced the overall effect size; the association remained in the same direction, but the confidence interval crossed unity (Figure S7).

Publication bias

The analysis revealed no indication of significant publication bias for OS with AEC (Egger’s test: p = 0.155; Begg’s test: p = 0.303), nor for PFS with AEC (Egger’s test: p = 0.382; Begg’s test: p = 0.537). Similarly, no evidence of publication bias was observed for OS with REC (Egger’s test: p = 0.073; Begg’s test: p = 0.133), PFS with REC (Egger’s test: p = 0.222; Begg’s test: p = 0.308), DCR for AEC (Egger’s test: p = 0.959; Begg’s test: p = 1.000), ORR for AEC (Egger’s test: p = 0.416; Begg’s test: p = 0.462), or irAEs for AEC (Egger’s test: p = 0.692; Begg’s test: p = 1.000). However, several endpoints were based on relatively few studies/datasets (DCR for AEC, n = 6; ORR for AEC, n = 5; irAEs for AEC, n = 4; OS with REC, n = 7; PFS with REC, n = 4), and Egger’s/Begg’s tests are underpowered under these conditions. Therefore, the non-significant findings should be interpreted as no clear evidence of publication bias rather than definitive proof that publication bias is absent.

Discussion

Across 30 included studies, we found that both high baseline AEC and REC were significantly associated with prolonged OS and PFS in patients treated with ICIs. Additionally, high baseline AEC was linked to improved DCR and ORR. These findings support the role of eosinophils as practical, accessible, and cost-effective biomarkers for predicting clinical outcomes in cancer immunotherapy.

The tumor microenvironment plays a central role in immunotherapy for cancer, where eosinophils demonstrate a multifaceted function. These cells contribute to anti-tumor immune responses by releasing cytotoxic proteins, including eosinophil cationic protein and eosinophil peroxidase, and major basic protein,49 as well as cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-12 and IL-10, which are known to inhibit tumor cell motility.50 Additionally, eosinophils are capable of directly eliminating methylcholanthrene-induced fibrosarcoma cells.51 Beyond their direct cytotoxicity, eosinophils influence the tumor microenvironment by facilitating T cell activity—particularly through the recruitment and activation of CD8+ T cells. Moreover, these cells modulate anti-tumor responses by expressing natural killer cell-associated activation receptors, such as LY49, NKG2D, and 2B4, thereby driving IFN-γ release to optimize immune defenses against tumors.52

However, this immunostimulatory role may also underlie the development of irAEs. Our meta-analysis showed that higher baseline AEC was significantly associated with increased irAE incidence, including severe toxicities such as ICI-related pneumonitis.53 This suggests that eosinophils may serve as a dual-purpose biomarker—predictive of both therapeutic benefit and toxicity risk.

Limitations of the study

Although our meta-analysis provides important insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the majority of the included studies were retrospective, which may introduce potential biases in data extraction and interpretation. Second, heterogeneity was present across studies, likely driven by differences in eosinophil cut-off thresholds, cancer types and stages, treatment settings, and laboratory methods, which may affect the comparability of results. Importantly, exploring heterogeneity is a key function of meta-analysis. Accordingly, we performed extensive subgroup analyses by cut-off values, geographic region, tumor type, and other relevant factors. Across these strata, the direction of association was generally consistent, and heterogeneity was attenuated in several subgroups, suggesting that the observed variability more likely reflects expected differences in case mix and analytical approaches rather than the absence of an underlying prognostic signal. Nevertheless, some residual heterogeneity remained, which may be attributable to unmeasured or inconsistently reported differences in study design, patient populations, and laboratory procedures.

Third, eosinophil levels may be influenced by other factors such as allergic conditions, parasitic infections, or prior drug use—variables that were not adequately addressed in many of the studies included in the analysis. Finally, due to the relatively small number of studies and the limited data on specific tumor types and treatment lines, large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to further elucidate the mechanisms linking eosinophil-driven immunity to clinical outcomes and irAEs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Not applicable. This study is based on previously published clinical studies and does not involve any new experimental models or study participants.

Method details

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD420251249992). From database inception to June 2, 2025, a systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library using the terms “immune checkpoint inhibitors” [MeSH], “cancer,” and “eosinophils” to identify relevant studies. Detailed strategies are shown in Table S1 (Supplementary material). Gray literature was identified via Google Scholar. Finally, the identified references were manually screened. The studies that met the inclusion criteria were conducted between 2010 and 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Population: patients with malignant tumors treated with ICIs, including PD-1, PD-L1, and/or CTLA-4 inhibitors, administered as monotherapy or in combination; (2) Prognostic factor: baseline AEC and/or REC measured before ICI initiation; (3) Comparator: outcomes reported according to high versus low baseline eosinophil levels using a prespecified or study-defined cut-off; and (4) Outcomes: reporting at least one of the following endpoints— OS, PFS, or disease control rate (DCR), irAE, objective response rate (ORR). DCR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease, and ORR was defined as the proportion achieving CR or PR, according to the response criteria used in each original study. We included randomized controlled trials and observational studies (prospective or retrospective cohorts, including registry-based studies) that provided extractable effect estimates or sufficient data to compute them. We excluded conference abstracts without full text, editorials, letters, commentaries, narrative reviews, case reports, and small case series. Studies were also excluded if eosinophils were categorized into three or more groups or if two cut-offs were used to create three or more categories. Takeuchi et al. (2023) categorized eosinophils into three groups and was therefore excluded from the present analysis.7 In addition, studies focusing primarily on eosinophil changes during/after treatment, or reporting eosinophil values only after ICI initiation without baseline data, were excluded. For overlapping cohorts, when multiple publications analyzed the same or partially overlapping populations, we included the study with the largest sample size and/or the most complete outcome reporting and the most robust methodology.

To ensure methodological consistency and comparability across endpoints, each outcome-specific meta-analysis included only those studies or databases that reported (or allowed derivation of) an effect estimate for that endpoint. For time-to-event outcomes (OS and PFS), we synthesized hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between baseline AEC/REC and the relevant survival endpoint. For binary outcomes (DCR, ORR, and irAEs), we used odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Studies that did not provide sufficient data for a given endpoint were excluded from that specific meta-analysis but could still contribute to other outcome- or biomarker-specific analyses if relevant effect estimates were available.

Data extraction and quality assessment

In Table 1, we set out the information we searched for from the articles, including author name, study period, study area, retrospective or prospective study, baseline eosinophil parameters, and other key information. We preferentially obtain data from multivariate HR analysis. Studies with a score of 6 or more on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses the methodological quality of articles, are classified as high-quality studies.54 These procedures were conducted independently by the two authors under the guidance of the corresponding author, and the data of each other was checked.

Baseline eosinophil parameters were extracted as either AEC (×109/L or cells/μL) or REC (defined as the percentage of eosinophils among total leukocytes). When both measures were reported, AEC and REC were recorded separately and treated as distinct exposure variables to allow comparison of their prognostic performance.

Definition of irAEs and inclusion criteria

IrAEs were defined as adverse events with a presumed immune-mediated mechanism that occurred during treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors and were considered treatment-related by the original investigators. In the included studies, irAEs were generally assessed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, versions 3.0–5.0).

IrAEs can involve multiple organs, including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, endocrine system, lungs, kidneys, and cardiovascular system. In CTCAE grading, grade 1 events are mild and do not significantly affect daily activities; grade 2 events are moderate, may limit daily activities, and often require symptomatic treatment or temporary treatment interruption; grade 3 events are severe, markedly impair function, and usually require hospitalization or intensive management; grade 4 events are life-threatening and require urgent intervention; and grade 5 events are deaths related to toxicity.

In our analysis, we only included studies that explicitly reported the association between baseline absolute eosinophil count (AEC) and the overall incidence of irAEs. Studies that examined baseline eosinophils only in relation to a single, organ-specific irAE (such as rash or pruritus alone) without reporting overall irAE incidence were not pooled in the quantitative synthesis.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata 18.0. AEC-based and REC-based outcomes were analyzed separately to clarify the prognostic value of each eosinophil metric. HR was used to look at survival outcomes.55 OR was used to look at drug reactions. Heterogeneity was assessed for the chi-square test. We used a fixed-effects model when the p-value was greater than 0.1 or the I2 statistic was less than 50% and a random effects model otherwise.56 We utilized Egger’s and Begg’s tests to evaluate potential publication bias in our meta-analysis. Whenever bias was identified, a “trim and fill” methodology was implemented to quantify its effect on aggregated outcome measures. The robustness of our findings was verified through systematic sensitivity analyses. Results were deemed statistically significant when the p-value fell below the predetermined threshold of 0.05.
