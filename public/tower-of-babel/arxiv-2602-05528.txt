Strong Normalisation for Asynchronous Effects
Danel Ahman, Ilja Sobolev

Abstract
Asynchronous effects of Ahman and Pretnar complement the conventional synchronous treatment of algebraic computational effects with asynchrony based on decoupling the execution of algebraic operation calls into signalling that an operation’s implementation needs to be executed, and into interrupting a running computation with the operation’s result, to which the computation can react by installing matching interrupt handlers. Beyond providing asynchrony for algebraic effects, the resulting core calculus also naturally models examples such as pre-emptive multi-threading, (cancellable) remote function calls, multi-party applications, and even a parallel variant of runners of algebraic effects. In this paper, we study the normalisation properties of this calculus. We prove that if one removes general recursion from the original calculus, then the remaining calculus is strongly normalising, including both its sequential and parallel parts. However, this only guarantees termination for very simple asynchronous examples. To improve on this result, we also prove that the sequential fragment of the calculus remains strongly normalising when a controlled amount of interrupt-driven recursive behaviour is reintroduced. Our strong normalisation proofs are structured compositionally as a natural extension of Lindley and Stark’s ⊤ ⁣ ⊤ \top\top -lifting based approach for proving strong normalisation of effectful languages. All our results are also formalised in Agda.

Introduction

When studying computer programs, it is interesting, and often even
crucial, to know when certain classes of programs are guaranteed to terminate
and not enter an infinite loop.
In this paper, we study the normalisation properties of a core

\lambda
-calculus for asynchronous programming with algebraic effects proposed
by Ahman and Pretnar
[
2
,
3
]
, called
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
. This
calculus was originally designed to address the synchrony in the operational
treatment of algebraic
effects
[
5
,
8
,
16
]
,
in which a program whose execution encounters an operation call
\mathsf{op}\;(V,y.M)
is blocked until some implementation of
\mathsf{op}
finishes executing and unblocks

M
.
Importantly however, Ahman and Pretnar also showed that
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
naturally models a number of
other examples, such as pre-emptive multi-threading, (cancellable) remote
function calls, multi-party applications, and even a parallel
variant of runners of algebraic effects
[
1
]
.

The
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus of Ahman and Pretnar addresses the forced synchrony by
decoupling the different phases involved in executing operation calls into
separate programming abstractions, giving the programmer fine-grain control over
when the execution should block, and when blocking is not needed. First,
programmers can issue
outgoing signals

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{signal})
,
with which to indicate that some implementation of
\mathsf{op}
needs to be executed.
Next, from the perspective of
\mathsf{op}
’s implementations, these signals become

incoming interrupts

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{interrupt})
, to which these
implementations can react by installing
interrupt handlers

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N_{handler}
, where the handler code
M_{\mathsf{op}}
is
executed only when a corresponding interrupt is intercepted by the interrupt
handler. Finally, the variable
p
bound in the continuation
N_{handler}
of an interrupt
handler carries a distinguished
promise type

\langle X\rangle
,
which allows its value to be explicitly
awaited
with

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}await}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}until}\;\langle x\rangle\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N_{await}
, blocking the execution of the program until the
promise is fulfilled with a value. Asynchrony is achieved by the
continuations
M_{signal}
,
M_{interrupt}
, and
N_{handler}
of signals,
interrupts, and interrupt handlers having a non-blocking semantics, while the
continuation
N_{await}
of awaiting has a blocking semantics.
In order to model both an operation’s call site and its
implementation, the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus organises individual sequential
computations into parallel processes that communicate by issuing signals and
reacting to received interrupts.

The advantage of the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus is that the same programming
abstractions can be used both for expressing algebraic operation calls

\mathsf{op}\;(V,y.M)
, by first issuing a signal to request the execution of

\mathsf{op}
’s implementation and then awaiting for an interrupt with
\mathsf{op}
’s result to
arrive and be handled, and for defining the implementation(s) of
\mathsf{op}
, by first
awaiting for an interrupt with the request to execute
\mathsf{op}
’s implementation and
then issuing a signal with the implementation’s result back to the operation’s
call site. Concretely, this takes the form

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}~~~\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}\;\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op\text{-}req}\,(V,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op\text{-}resp}\;y\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,\langle y\rangle)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}await}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}until}\;\langle y\rangle\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;M))\big)\\
\mathbin{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}}}\\
~~~\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}\;\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;\big(\mathsf{op\text{-}req}\;x\mapsto\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;y=M_{\mathsf{op}}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op\text{-}resp}\,(y,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,\langle()\rangle)\big)\big)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,p\big)\end{array}

The calculus is however flexible enough that not all signals have to have a
corresponding interrupt, and not all interrupts have to be responses to some
signals, allowing programmers to not only naturally model asynchronous algebraic
operation calls, but also model server-like and remote code execution examples,
and moreover, also model spontaneous behaviour, such as a user clicking a
button, a sensor sending data, or the environment pre-empting a thread.

The goal of this paper is to study the normalisation properties of

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
(reviewed in
\cref
sec:aeff), to give termination guarantees for
asynchronous programs written in or modelled using it.
First
, we prove
that when one removes general recursion from the original formulation of

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
, then its sequential fragment, which models the code being executed
by individual asynchronous computations, becomes strongly normalising
(
\cref
sec:sn-seq).
Second
, we show that if one reintroduces a
controlled amount of recursive behaviour into the programs via (a variant of)
the concept of reinstallable interrupt handlers (originally proposed by Ahman
and Pretnar to be able to model interesting asynchronous examples in the absence
of general recursion), then the sequential part of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
remains strongly
normalising (
\cref
sec:sn-reinstallable).
Third
, we establish that
without adding reinstallable interrupt handlers, also the parallel part of the
general recursion-free
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus is strongly normalising, confirming
the intuition that reinstallable interrupt handlers indeed add expressive and
computational power (
\cref
sec:sn-parallel).

As
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
is higher-order and effectful, our proofs are naturally
structured as an extension of Lindley and Stark’s
\top\top
-lifting based
approach
[
11
]
, which itself is an extension of the
Girard-Tait style type-directed reducibility approach to proving strong
normalisation of higher-order languages
[
7
,
19
]
. We demonstrate that
the reducibility and
\top\top
-lifting based approaches naturally extend to the

challenging features
of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
: it is effectful, it contains sequential and
parallel parts, it is highly non-deterministic and even
non-confluent
[
2
,
3
]
, it includes programming abstractions
based both on algebraic
effects
[
14
,
15
]
(signals,
interrupt handlers, awaiting) and effect
handling
[
5
,
8
,
16
]

(interrupts), it includes scoped algebraic
operations
[
13
]
(interrupt handlers), it includes
reduction rules corresponding to the commutativity of algebraic operations
(between signals and interrupt handlers), and it includes a programming abstraction
with non-blocking continuation that binds variables (interrupt handlers).

All the results presented in this paper are also formalised in Agda. The
formalisation is available at

https://github.com/IljaSobolev/aeff-agda-normalisation/.

The work in this paper is based on and extends one of the authors’ Bachelor’s thesis
[
18
]
.

3
Strong Normalisation for Sequential Computations

In this section, we prove that the sequential part of the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus
that we reviewed in
\cref
sec:aeff:seq is strongly normalising, i.e., we
show that there are no infinite reduction sequences starting from well-typed
computations. As noted in
\cref
sec:intro, our normalisation proof is based on
the compositional Girard-Tait style type-directed reducibility approach to
proving strong normalisation of higher-order languages
[
7
,
19
]
, and
its extension to effectful programming languages via the
\top\top
-lifting
technique proposed by Lindley and Stark
[
11
]
.

3.1
Reducibility and
\top\top
-Lifting Based Strong Normalisation Proofs

We first recall how the Girard-Tait style type-directed reducibility approach to
proving strong normalisation is structured at a high level. The approach is
originally due to Tait
[
19
]
, and later improved and extended by
Girard
[
7
]
. We follow the style of the latter presentation.

First, we remind the reader that a term
M
is
strongly normalising
when
there does not exist an infinite reduction sequence
M\leadsto M^{\prime}\leadsto\ldots
starting from
M
. A calculus is strongly normalising when all its
well-typed terms are. We denote the set of all strongly normalising terms of
type
X
with
\mathcal{SN}_{\!X}
, often omitting the
X
.
For
M\in\mathcal{SN}
, we write
max(M)
for the length of the longest reduction
sequence starting from
M
.
In proofs, it is convenient to give
\mathcal{SN}
an
equivalent
inductive characterisation
:
M\in\mathcal{SN}
if
\forall N.\,M\leadsto N\implies N\in\mathcal{SN}
.

Proving strong normalisation of higher-order languages directly by induction on
the syntax (or typing derivations) does not work well. Not only can the number
of redexes grow after some steps,
knowing that a term of a certain type is strongly normalising usually does not
immediately imply that its subterms are also strongly normalising (e.g., knowing
that a function abstraction is strongly normalising does not directly imply that
its body is as well).

The Girard-Tait style approach addresses this situation by defining
a notion of
reducibility
at every type, a form of a unary logical
relation
[
17
]
. Strong normalisation is then proved
in two steps: one proves that (i) every reducible term is strongly normalising,
and that (ii) every well-typed term is reducible at its type. The
earlier problems are solved by the reducibility relation being
amenable to induction on the structure of types, allowing one to abstractly
specify that subterms of reducible (strongly normalising) terms have the same
property.

The
\top\top
-lifting technique proposed by Lindley and
Stark
[
11
]
extends this approach to effectful languages.
As the types of computations typically do not characterise the structure of the
computation, only that of the return values, then one cannot define reducibility
for computations simply over the structure of types as one does for values.
Lindley and Stark’s proposal is to define the notion of reducibility for
computations itself in two steps: (i) a computation is reducible when it is
strongly normalising in any reducible term context (called continuations
in
[
11
]
), and (ii) such a continuation is reducible when
it is strongly normalising when filled with any reducible value at the
corresponding type. The continuations model the environment the computation is
executing in, and this definition of reducibility then naturally extends
reducibility of (return) values to computations and their environments.

In this section, we show how these ideas apply to the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus. We
note that the work of Lindley and Stark is presented in the context of Moggi’s
computational metalanguage
[
12
]
, while the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus
is based on FGCBV
[
10
]
. The difference in these calculi is however
only superficial and the
\top\top
-lifting based approach straightforwardly
extends to the latter
[
18
, §2]
.

Below we consider a
skeletal version
of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
, in which we omit
effect annotations. The
skeletal types
are defined as

X,Y\mathrel{\;{:}{:}{=}\ }\mathsf{b}\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}X\to Y\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}\langle X\rangle.

This is to emphasise that this normalisation result does not depend on
effect-typing. Typing rules are similar to before.

3.2
Continuations

Following Lindley and Stark
[
11
]
, we first
define a notion of
continuations

K
, i.e., term contexts that model the
environments in which computations execute, given by the grammar:

K\mathrel{\;{:}{:}{=}\ }\textit{Id}\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}K\circ(x)N\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}K\circ\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)

Their intuitive meaning is best understood by how they are
applied to
computations
:

\begin{array}[]{r c l}\textit{Id}\mathbin{@}M&\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}&M,\\
(K\circ(x)N)\mathbin{@}M&\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}&K\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;x=M\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N),\\
(K\circ\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V))\mathbin{@}M&\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}&K\mathbin{@}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M).\end{array}

The first two cases are already present in Lindley and Stark’s work, and
they express that after executing
M
there is either nothing more to be done,
or that
N
is sequentially executed after
M
returns. The third case is new
for
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
: it expresses that the environment has an interrupt ready to
propagate into
M
. This allows us to prove that computations are strongly
normalising for any finite number of interrupts propagated to them by the
environment.

The continuations
K
do not include any of the other new computations of

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
(signals, interrupt handlers, awaiting). This is
because they express actions by computations, and not by the
environment. Semantically speaking, the difference is that signals, interrupt
handlers, and awaiting behave like algebraic operations (i.e., constructors of
computations), while sequencing and interrupts behave like effect
handling (i.e., eliminators for computations).

We characterise
well-typed continuations
with the judgement
\Gamma\vdash K:X\multimap Y
, defined by

\frac{\phantom{...}}{\Gamma\vdash\textit{Id}:X\multimap X}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}\frac{\Gamma,x\mathinner{:}X\vdash N:Y\quad\Gamma\vdash K:Y\multimap Z}{\Gamma\vdash K\circ(x)N:X\multimap Z}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}\frac{\mathsf{op}\in\Sigma\quad\Gamma\vdash V:A_{\mathsf{op}}\quad\Gamma\vdash K:X\multimap Y}{\Gamma\vdash K\circ\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V):X\multimap Y}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}

It is easy to see that the application
K\mathbin{@}M
of continuation
\Gamma\vdash K:X\multimap Y
to computation
\Gamma\vdash M:X
preserves typing.
We also define the
length
|K|
of a continuation
, as the number of
compositions
\circ
in
K
. This is a measure of the size of the environment of
a computation.

3.3
Reducibility

We now turn to defining the notion of
reducibility
, as type- and
context-indexed predicates
\mathbf{VRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
,
\mathbf{CRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
, and

\mathbf{KRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
over well-typed values
\Gamma\vdash V:X
, computations

\Gamma\vdash M:X
, and continuations
\Gamma\vdash K:X\multimap Y
,
respectively.
When a well-typed value, computation, or continuation satisfies the respective reducibility
predicate, we say that it is
reducible
.

Compared to Lindley and Stark, we do not define these predicates
pointwise for contexts
\Gamma
, but instead in the style of Kripke’s possible
world semantics
[
9
]
.
This is needed to reason about interrupt handlers
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N
in

\cref
prop:sn-seq:interrupt-handlers-sn,prop:sn-seq:interrupt-handlers-reducible,
because we need to weaken a reducible continuation
K\in\mathbf{KRed}_{Y}^{\Gamma}
with
p
to apply it to

N
.
1
1

1

The Bachelor’s thesis
[
18

of one of the authors
demonstrates how a non-Kripke-style definition of reducibility can be used
instead, with the subtle caveat that then one has to extend
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-values
with new constants

that intuitively model promises that
have not yet been fulfilled at a given time.
To give such a Kripke-style

\begin{array}[]{l c l}V\in\mathbf{VRed}_{\mathsf{b}}^{\Gamma}&\iff&\top,\\[2.15277pt]

Observe that
\mathbf{VRed}_{\langle X\rangle}^{\Gamma}
uses an auxiliary notion of

\forall\,\Gamma^{\prime}.\,\forall\,\rho\in\mathsf{Ren}\;\Gamma\;\Gamma^{\prime}.\,\forall\,V\in\mathbf{VRed}_{X}^{\Gamma^{\prime}}.\,(K^{\langle\rangle}[\rho])\mathbin{@}\langle V\rangle\in\mathcal{SN}

these await continuations model environments which are awaiting a promise to be
fulfilled.

Interestingly, despite the additional features of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
, the overall structure of
these predicates is identical to Lindley and Stark’s work, apart from the
Kripke-style indexing.
In particular, while in
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
the interrupt handlers

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N

techniques such as step-indexing

to ensure

not because of the handler code
M_{\mathsf{op}}
, but instead because of additional
variables being bound in the non-blocking continuation
N
.

next result.

Proposition 1
.

Given
V\in\mathbf{VRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
and
\rho\in\mathsf{Ren}\;\Gamma\;\Gamma^{\prime}
, then we
have

, and analogously for the other
reducibility predicates for computations and continuations.

Furthermore, we can prove that application of continuations preserves
reducibility.

Proposition 2
.

If
{\Gamma\vdash\!K:X\!\multimap\!Y}
,
{K\!\in\!\mathbf{KRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}}
, and

{M\!\in\!\mathbf{CRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}}
, then
{K\!\mathbin{@}\!M\!\in\!\mathbf{CRed}_{Y}^{\Gamma}}
.

3.4
Strong Normalisation

As is standard in Girard-Tait style normalisation proofs, as a first step we
prove that strong normalisation follows (straightforwardly) from a computation
being reducible.

Theorem 3
.

Given
\Gamma\vdash M:X
, such that
M\in\mathbf{CRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
, then
M\in\mathcal{SN}_{\!X}
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lindley and Stark. By the definition of

\mathbf{CRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
, we have
\textit{Id}\mathbin{@}M=M\in\mathcal{SN}_{\!X}
if we can show

\textit{Id}\in\mathbf{KRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
. But by the definition of
\mathbf{KRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
, it
suffices to show that
\textit{Id}\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,V)\in\mathcal{SN}_{\!X}
, for all
V\in\mathbf{VRed}_{X}^{\Gamma}
, which is trivially true.
∎

The rest of the normalisation proof amounts to showing that all well-typed
computations are reducible. It is customary to split the proof of this result
(
\cref
thm:sn-seq:sn) into propositions for the typing rules of
computations. We only discuss the cases involving
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-specific
computations—details of other cases can be found in our Agda formalisation, or in

[
11
,
18
]
.

First, as standard, we prove that
variables
are reducible, by induction
on their types.

Proposition 4

4
Strong Normalisation for Reinstallable Interrupt Handlers

While strongly normalising, we cannot write very interesting asynchronous
examples using the
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-computations from
\cref
sec:aeff:seq. For
instance, in a client-server scenario, there is no way for the server process to
react to an undetermined number of requests from clients, nor can a thread be
stopped and started an undetermined number of times by the environment. Any
such iterative behaviour has a statically fixed bound given by installed interrupt
handlers.

To address this shortcoming, Ahman and Pretnar
[
3
]
extended

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
with a capability to reinstall interrupt handlers. They conjectured
that the sequential part of the resulting calculus is strongly normalising. In
this paper, we first demonstrate that their conjecture does not hold
(
\cref
sec:sn-reinstallable:non-sn), and then propose a variant that is
strongly normalising (
\cref
sec:sn-reinstallable:sn).

4.1
Failure of Strong Normalisation for Reinstallable Interrupt Handlers

To reintroduce a controlled amount of recursive behaviour into

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-computations, Ahman and Pretnar
[
3
]
extended interrupt
handlers to a form
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\,r\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N
, where the handler code

M_{\mathsf{op}}
can contain an additional (function-typed) variable
r
, whose
application results in the same interrupt handler being

reinstalled
, as expressed by the reduction rule

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~} l}&\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\,r\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N)\\[4.30554pt]
\leadsto&\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;p=M_{\mathsf{op}}\big[V/x,{({\mathop{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}fun}}}\;()\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\,r\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;q\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q)/r}\big]\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N).\end{array}

Observe that when such interrupt handler is triggered, the variable
r
in
the handler code
M_{\mathsf{op}}
is bound to a function, which upon application
installs the same interrupt handler again.

For example, with such interrupt handlers one can write recursive server processes as

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{request}\;x\,r\mapsto(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;y=M_{\text{process}}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{response}\,(y,r\,())))\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\ldots,

where an interrupt handler is used to wait for
\mathsf{request}
s from clients, which
are processed by
M_{\text{process}}
, the result is sent back to the
client in a
\mathsf{response}
signal, and the application
r\,()
is
used to reinstall the same interrupt handler, to wait for further

\mathsf{request}
s from clients.

As the recursive reinstalling of interrupt handlers is only triggered by
incoming interrupts, and there is ever only a finite number of them surrounding
any given interrupt handler in a sequential computation, then Ahman and Pretnar
conjectured that the sequential part of this extension of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
ought to be
strongly normalising. Unfortunately, this is not the case.

For one concrete counterexample, consider the computation

M_{1}\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\,r\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(x,r\,()))\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,p),

where in the handler code an interrupt is wrapped around the reinstalling
application
r\,()
. One can show that
M_{1}
is well-typed for an effect
annotation
(\emptyset,\iota)
, where
\iota
is given infinitely recursively as

\iota=\{\mathsf{op}\mapsto(\emptyset,\{\mathsf{op}\mapsto(\emptyset,\dotsc)\})\}
, using the
coinductive definition of
I
. On the other hand, when we look at how this
computation executes, then we see that the subterm
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,r\,())

leads to a same interrupt handler being triggered and reinstalled infinitely often.

For another counterexample, consider the computation

M_{2}\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\,r\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,\langle{\mathop{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}fun}}}\;()\mapsto N\rangle)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}await}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}until}\;\langle y\rangle\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;y\,())),

where
N\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;p=r\,()\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}await}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}until}\;\langle y\rangle\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;y\,())
. This
computation is well-typed, but also exhibits infinite reduction behaviour.
This time the culprit is the leaking of the reinstall variable
r
in the body
of the function
\langle{\mathop{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}fun}}}\;()\mapsto N\rangle
into the continuation of the
interrupt handler, where the reinstalled interrupt handler is triggered again
by the original interrupt.

4.2
Strongly Normalising Variant of Reinstallable Interrupt Handlers

If we look at the counterexamples listed above, we see that the failure of strong
normalisation is caused by the freedom that the reinstallable interrupt handlers
proposed by Ahman and Pretnar give to the programmer for where and how the reinstall
variable
r
can be used.
In particular, they allow
r
to appear under interrupts in
M_{\mathsf{op}}
, and to be
leaked into
N
.
Based on these observations, we propose a restricted variant of
reinstallable
interrupt handlers
:

\frac{({o^{\prime}},{\iota^{\prime}})\sqsubseteq_{O\times I}\iota\,(\mathsf{op})\quad\Gamma,x\mathinner{:}A_{\mathsf{op}}\vdash M_{\mathsf{op}}:\langle X\rangle+\mathsf{unit}\and(o^{\prime},\iota^{\prime})\quad\Gamma,p\mathinner{:}\langle X\rangle\vdash N:Y\and(o,\iota)}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N:Y\and(o,\iota)}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}

Instead of modelling the reinstalling capability with a first-class variable, we
use sum types to encode it in the return type of the handler code
M_{\mathsf{op}}
.
Returning in the left injection of the sum type models the interrupt
handling finishing without reinstalling, and returning in the right injection
models the interrupt handler getting reinstalled. This behaviour is summarised by the following
reduction rule for triggering reinstallable interrupt handlers:

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~} l}&\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N)\\[4.30554pt]
\leadsto&\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;p=\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;y=M[V/x]\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;y\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z{}\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,w{}\mapsto R\}{}\big)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N),\end{array}

where
R=\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;q\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q

reinstalls
the
interrupt handler when
M_{\mathsf{op}}
returns
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,()
. Observe that this
way it is both impossible to wrap interrupts around reinstalled interrupt
handlers in
M_{\mathsf{op}}
, and to leak the reinstalling capability into
N
.

The upside of this definition is that we can still express all the interesting
recursive examples of Ahman and Pretnar
[
3
]
, while the sequential
part of the calculus remains strongly normalising.
For instance, we can write the server process example in this extension as

\quad\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{go}\;y\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}finish}\;\langle()\rangle)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\\
\quad\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}await}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}until}\;\langle z\rangle\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}reinstall}\\

where we write

5
Strong Normalisation for Parallel Processes

We conclude our normalisation results by showing that without adding
reinstallable interrupt handlers, the
entire

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-calculus presented in

\cref
sec:aeff is in fact strongly normalising.

5.1
Parallel Processes and Reinstallable Interrupt Handlers

We first recall from the work of Ahman and Pretnar
[
3
]
that in the
extension of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
with reinstallable interrupt handlers, the parallel
processes are not strongly normalising. The same is also true for our variant of
reinstallable interrupt handlers. Consider the processes

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}~~~\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}\;\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{ping}\,\big((),\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;\big(\mathsf{pong}\;x\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{ping}\,((),\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}reinstall})\big)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,p\big)\big)\\
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}\\
~~~\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}\;\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{pong}\,\big((),\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;\big(\mathsf{ping}\;x\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{pong}\,((),\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}reinstall})\big)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,p\big)\big).\end{array}

These two parallel processes send each other an infinite number of ping-pong
signals. This means that by proving below that in
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
without
reinstallable interrupt handlers (and without general recursion) the parallel
processes are strongly normalising, we demonstrate that the addition of
reinstallable interrupt handlers adds real expressive and computational power.

5.2
Parallel Processes Without Reinstallable Interrupt Handlers

As illustrated by the previous example, if we want to prove the strong
normalisation of parallel processes, it is not enough if all we know is that the
corresponding sequential computations are strongly normalising. While
computations including reinstallable interrupt handlers are strongly
normalising, the parallel processes containing them are not. So we need some
additional information about computations to prove normalisation for parallel
processes.

A convenient distinguishing feature between computations for which parallel
processes are strongly normalising, and for which they are not, turns out to be
effect-typing. Namely, the normalisation proof below relies on considering
computations effect-typed using
finite
effect annotations
\iota\in I
,
given by defining
I
inductively, as the least fixed point of
\Phi(X)\mathrel{\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}}\Sigma\Rightarrow(O\times X)_{\bot}
from
\cref
sec:aeff:seq. This rules out
examples involving reinstallable interrupt handlers because to type such
examples we would need a coinductive definition of
I
(see
[
3
]
).

In addition to making use of effect-typing, this section differs from the
previous sections in that we do not need to employ a Girard-Tait style
reducibility argument. This is largely because
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
does not contain
first-order processes. Instead, the normalisation proof can proceed by direct
induction on the lexicographic order of four measures that we define next.

Size of effect annotations:

For a finite
\iota
, denote with
\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
the
size
of

\iota
: the number of internal nodes when
\iota
is considered as a tree. We then
define
\lvert(o,\iota)\rvert_{i}
as
\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
. In fact, only the size of
\iota
s counts below, and the
o
-annotations could be omitted altogether.
We write
\lvert P\rvert_{i}
for the sum of the sizes
\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
over all
individual computations in the
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}
-leaves of a process
P
.

Proposition 22
.

If
\mathsf{op}\not\in\iota
, then
\lvert{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota)\rvert_{i}=\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
, and if
\mathsf{op}\in\iota
, then
\lvert{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota)\rvert_{i}<\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
.

Maximum number of outgoing signals:
By
\cref
thm:sn-seq:sn, every
computation is strongly normalising. Since a
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
-computation has only finitely many
one-step reducts, then every computation has finitely many normal forms
(see
[
3
]
for
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}

for the maximum number of top-level signals in a normal form of

, i.e.,

max\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,(M)
is the maximum number of signals

can emit while it reduces.
We write

for the sum of

over all individual

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}

.

Note that if a computation does not have a handler for an interrupt, then acting with the
corresponding interrupt can not reveal any new signals in this computation:

Proposition 23
.

If
\Gamma\vdash M:X\and(o,\iota)
and
\mathsf{op}\not\in\iota
, then

.

Parallel shapes:
We define a notion of
parallel shapes

to model the parallel part of a process that is independent of the individual computations in the
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}
-leaves:

S,T\mathrel{\;{:}{:}{=}\ }\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}S\mathbin{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}}}T\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,S\mathrel{\,\;\big|\ \ \!}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,S

We can define a reduction relation

for parallel shapes by
restricting
\cref

.
It is easily shown that this reduction relation is strongly normalising. For a
shape

, we write

for the length of longest reduction sequence
starting from
S
.
We also note that every parallel process
P
has a straightforwardly determined

shape(P)
.

Maximum number of
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}
-reduction steps:
For the last induction measure, we define
max_{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}}(P)
as the sum of

for all individual computations

in the
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}

We can now prove that also the parallel part of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
from

Theorem 24
.

Every well-typed parallel process
\Gamma\vdash P:C
is strongly normalising.

Proof.

By induction on the lexicographic order of
\lvert P\rvert_{i}
,

max\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,(P)
,
max_{sh}(shape(P))
, and
max_{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}run}}(P)

), (

Our Agda formalisation also defines and proves normalisation for a variant of

6
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the Girard-Tait style reducibility approach to proving
strong normalisation, and its
\top\top
-lifting extension to effectful
languages by Lindley and Stark, is well-suited for proving normalisation
properties of
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
, a core calculus for asynchronous programming with
algebraic effects
[
2
,
3
]
. For one, studying the
normalisation properties of this calculus is desirable because it allows one to
model many compelling and natural examples, such as pre-emptive multi-threading,
(cancellable) remote function calls, multi-party applications, and a parallel
variant of runners of algebraic effects. On the other hand, studying the
normalisation properties of this calculus is also interesting because it
contains a number of advanced and challenging features. Our results are also
formalised in Agda.

In future, we plan on extending our work to the other extensions Ahman and
Pretnar proposed for
\lambda_{\text{\ae}}

[
3
]
: modal types that allow
signals and interrupts to carry higher-order data, a stateful variant of
reinstallable interrupt handlers, and the dynamic spawning of new processes.
Moreover, observe that as the use of the sum type in the reinstallable interrupt
handlers of
\cref
sec:sn-reinstallable:sn somewhat resembles Elgot
iteration
[
6
]
, it would be interesting to study
a more formal connection there. In a related but different direction, we also
want to investigate how to present our strongly normalising reinstallable
interrupt handlers without making (explicit) use of sum types, more in the
original abstract style of Ahman and Pretnar.

K\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,W)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,x\mapsto M,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,y\mapsto N\}{})\in\mathcal{SN}
.

Proof.

We observe that
K\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,V)\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,x\mapsto M,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,y{}\mapsto N\}{})
reduces to only
K\mathbin{@}M[V/x]
, which is strongly
normalising by assumption, and analogously to for the other statement about
pattern-matching on a right injection.
∎

Proof of

The proof proceeds by induction on the lexicographic order of three measures:

|K|_{\downarrow}
,
|K|+max(K\mathbin{@}N)
, and the structure of
N
, where

|K|_{\downarrow}
denotes the number of interrupts
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)
in
K
, and we
show that all reducts of the computation
K\mathbin{@}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N
are strongly normalising.
For better readability, we omit explicit actions of (weakening) renamings and
context indices from the reducibility predicates.

Below we discuss the case for the most interesting reduction rule (
r9
) in detail:

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~} l}&K\mathbin{@}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;p\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;N\\[2.15277pt]
\overset{\eqref{r9}}{\leadsto}&K^{\prime}\mathbin{@}\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;p=(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;y=M[V/x]\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;y\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z{}\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,\star{}\mapsto R\}{})\\[2.15277pt]
&\hskip 25.6073pt\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N)\big)\end{array}

where
K=K^{\prime}\circ\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)
and
p\not\in fv(V)
, and
R=\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;q\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q
.

We first notice that

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~} l}&K^{\prime}\mathbin{@}\big(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;p=(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}let}\;y=M[V/x]\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;y\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z{}\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,w{}\mapsto R\}{})\\[2.15277pt]
&\hskip 25.6073pt\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N)\big)\\[4.30554pt]
=&\big(K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\circ(y)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;y\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,w{}\mapsto R\})\big)\mathbin{@}M[V/x]\end{array}

Next, by assumption, we have that
M[V/x]\in\mathbf{CRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
, so it suffices to show

K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\circ(y)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;y\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,w\mapsto R\}{})\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}.

Now, by the definition of
\mathbf{KRed}
and
\cref
prop:let-return, it suffices that for all
V^{\prime}\in\mathbf{VRed}_{\langle X\rangle+\mathsf{unit}}
,

\big(K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\big)\mathbin{@}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}match}\;V^{\prime}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}with}\;\{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inl}\,z\mapsto\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z,\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}inr}\,w\mapsto R\}{}\in\mathcal{SN},

which by the definition of
\mathbf{SRed}
is equivalent to having to prove that

K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\circ((z)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,z),(w)R)\in\mathbf{SRed}_{\langle X\rangle,\mathsf{unit}}.

Next, we observe that by the definition of
\mathbf{SRed}
and
\cref
prop:match-inject,
this is equivalent to having to prove the following two statements:

•

(K^{\prime}\circ(p)\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,W)\in\mathcal{SN},\text{for all }W\in\mathbf{VRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
, and

•

(K^{\prime}\circ(p)\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}promise}\;(\mathsf{op}\;x\mapsto M_{\mathsf{op}})\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}as}\;q\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}in}\;\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q)\in\mathcal{SN}
.

Note that the first of these statements is exactly the definition of
K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
, which holds by
\cref
prop:sn-seq:interrupt-handlers-auxiliary-results and the hypothesis that
K\circ(p)N\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
.

For proving the second of the above statements, we apply the induction hypothesis.
For this, we begin by observing that the first induction measure has decreased:

|K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))|_{\downarrow}=|K^{\prime}|_{\downarrow}<|K^{\prime}\circ\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)|_{\downarrow}=|K|_{\downarrow}.

Secondly, we observe that
K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\circ(q)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q)\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
, because it is equivalent to the statement
K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
, which holds by the above.
Finally, we note that
\big(K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\big)\mathbin{@}(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}return}\,q)\in\mathcal{SN}
, again because
K^{\prime}\circ(p)(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,N))\in\mathbf{KRed}_{\langle X\rangle}
.
Therefore, all the conditions to be able to apply the induction hypothesis are
satisfied.
This concludes the proof for the reduction rule (
r9
).
∎

Appendix C
Proof of
\cref
prop:sn-par:act-size-decr

appendix]appendix:prop:sn-par:act-size-decr

We can view a finite effect annotation
\iota\in I
as a finite set

paths(\iota)
of paths from the root to the internal nodes of
\iota
: a
path
is a tuple
(\mathsf{op_{1}},\dotsc,\mathsf{op_{n}})
of operations.
We write
paths(o,\iota)
for
paths(\iota)
.
Next, we note that
\lvert paths(\iota)\rvert=\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
, and that

\mathsf{op}
acts on an
\iota
path-by-path, as

paths({\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota))=\{{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,p\mid p\in paths(\iota)\},

where the action
{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,p
of an interrupt
\mathsf{op}
on a path
p
is
defined as follows:

{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,p=\begin{cases}p^{\prime}&\mbox{if }p=(\mathsf{op},p^{\prime})\\
p&\mbox{otherwise, i.e., if $p$ is empty or its first signal is not $\mathsf{op}$}\end{cases}

Now, to prove the proposition, we consider the following two cases based on
whether
\mathsf{op}\in\iota
.

If
\mathsf{op}\not\in\iota
, then there is no path in
paths(\iota)
which starts
with
\mathsf{op}
, so in that case
{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota)=(o,\iota)
, and therefore

\lvert{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota)\rvert_{i}=\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}
.

If
\mathsf{op}\in\iota
, then it holds that the length-one path
(\mathsf{op})\in paths(\iota)
and that the empty path
()\in paths(\iota)
(the latter holds if
and only if
i\neq\emptyset
). Acting with
\mathsf{op}
sends both
(\mathsf{op})
and
()

to the same element
()
. From this, the desired strict inequality follows. More
formally,

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~} l}&\lvert{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota)\rvert_{i}\\[2.15277pt]
=&\lvert paths({\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,(o,\iota))\rvert\\[2.15277pt]
=&\lvert\{{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,p\mid p\in paths(\iota)\}\rvert\\[2.15277pt]
=&\lvert\{{\mathsf{op}}\,{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}}\,p\mid p\in paths(\iota)\setminus\{()\}\}\rvert\\[2.15277pt]
\leq&\lvert paths(\iota)\setminus\{()\}\rvert\\[2.15277pt]
<&\lvert paths(\iota)\rvert\\[2.15277pt]
=&\lvert\iota\rvert_{i}.\end{array}

∎

Appendix D
Flat Model of Parallel Processes

appendix]appendix:flat-processes

We summarise an alternative model for the parallel part of

\lambda_{\text{\ae}}
where processes are represented by non-empty lists of individual
computations, written as
P,Q\mathrel{\;{:}{:}{=}\ }M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}
.

We write
\Gamma\vdash P
for the (skeletal) typing of such processes,
with the typing rules requiring that each
of the individual computations
M_{i}
in
P
is well-typed at some computation type.

We define the small-step operational semantics of such processes using two relations:

\frac{M_{i}\leadsto M_{i}^{\prime}}{M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}\leadsto M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{i}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}\frac{P\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}Q}{P\leadsto Q}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}

and

\frac{\phantom{...}}{(\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{1}))\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{2})\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{n})}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}\frac{M_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}M^{\prime}_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M^{\prime}_{n}}{M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{1})\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M^{\prime}_{2}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M^{\prime}_{n}}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}

The first reduction relation
P\leadsto Q
expresses how processes reduce at
the top-level: either one of the individual computations makes a step, or one of
the individual computations issues a signal
\mathsf{op}
with payload
V
that gets
propagated to other processes as an interrupt.

The second reduction relation
P\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}Q
then
formalises how these signals are propagated to other processes as interrupts: if
the signal is issued in the left-most process, then the first rule propagates it
immediately to all the processes to the right of it, while nested repeated
applications of the second rule propagate the signal also to the processes left
of it. The second rule is due to the formal inductive definition of non-empty lists of processes. In a more informal presentation, we can replace the two rules with a single rule of the form:

\frac{\phantom{...}}{M_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{i})\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{n}\overset{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V)}{\leadsto}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{1})\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}M_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\ldots\mathrel{\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}||}}\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\downarrow}\,\mathsf{op}\,(V,M_{n})}\quad\textsc{Lab={\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}}}

Similarly to the normalisation of the tree-shaped model of parallel processes in

\cref
sec:sn-parallel, in the absence of general recursion and without adding
reinstallable interrupt handlers, and when the individual computations
M_{i}
are
effect-typed using finite effect annotations
\iota
(as in

\cref
sec:sn-parallel), we can prove that this variant of parallel processes is
also strongly normalising.

Theorem 28
.

Every (skeletally) well-typed parallel process
\Gamma\vdash P
is strongly
normalising.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
\cref
thm:sn-par:sn. Here we proceed by
induction on the lexicographic order of three measures: the sum
\lvert P\rvert_{i}
of
the sizes of the effect annotations of the individual computations in
P
, the
sum
max\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\uparrow}\,(P)
of the maximum number of outgoing signals in the individual
computations in
P
, and the sum
max(P)
of the maximum number
of reduction steps of the individual computations in
P
, and we show that all the reducts of
P
are strongly normalising. Further details can be found in our Agda formalisation.
∎

We leave formally relating this flat list-based model of parallel processes to
the original tree-shaped model of parallel processes discussed in

\cref
sec:aeff:par for future work.
