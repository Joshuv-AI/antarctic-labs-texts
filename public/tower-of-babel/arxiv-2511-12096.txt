The Calderón Problem for Quasilinear Conductivities of Conformally Transversally Anisotropic Media
Xi Chen, Ziyun Jin

Abstract
. This paper investigates Calderón’s problem on a conformally transversally anisotropic manifold ( M , g ) (M,g) of dimension n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , where the conductivity a ⁡ ( s , x , p ) a(s,x,p) might depend on both the electric potential and the electric field. We establish that for all ( t , x ) ∈ ℝ × M (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times M and β ∈ ℕ 1 + n \beta\in\mathbb{N}^{1+n} the derivatives ∂ ( s , p ) β a ⁡ ( s , x , p ) | ( s , p ) = ( t , 0 ) \partial_{(s,p)}^{\beta}a(s,x,p)|_{(s,p)=(t,0)} are uniquely determined by the boundary voltage-current measurements. If a ⁡ ( s , x , p ) a(s,x,p) is analytic in p p , then a ⁡ ( s , x , p ) a(s,x,p) can be uniquely recovered.



1.
Introduction

The
Electrical Impedance Tomography
(EIT) problem, also known as
Calderón’s problem
, is to recover the interior electrical conductivity of a medium by making current and voltage measurements at its boundary.

Calderón
[
4
]
formulated the prototype of the inverse boundary value problem models for the EIT problem. Generally, we let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, and consider the following Dirichlet problem of divergence-type quasilinear elliptic equations:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{i}(\gamma^{ij}(u,x,\nabla u)\partial_{j}u)&=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega,\\
u&=f\quad\text{on}~\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where the coefficient

(1.2)

\gamma:=(\gamma^{ij}(s,x,p))\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\text{\rm Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))

is symmetric and uniformly elliptic, in the sense

(1.3)

\gamma^{ij}(\lambda,x,0)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq C(\lambda)\delta^{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}>0,\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},~(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}.

The matrix
\gamma
models the conductivity of the underlying medium, which is anisotropic and possibly depends on the electrical potential and electrical field.

The voltage-current measurement is represented by the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
. For any
0<\alpha<1
, we denote the ball in
C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
centered at
f_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
with radius
\delta_{0}>0
by

(1.4)

B^{2,\alpha}_{\partial\Omega}(f_{0},\delta_{0}):=\{f\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega):\|f-f_{0}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\delta_{0}\}.

A standard argument of implicit function theorem proves that there exists a map
\delta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for any
f\in B^{2,\alpha}_{\partial\Omega}(\lambda,\delta(\lambda))
, (
1.1
) admits a unique solution
u^{f}
near
\lambda
. See for example Lemma
1
. Then, the corresponding
DN map
(short for Dirichlet-to-Neumann map) is defined as follows:

\displaystyle N_{\gamma}:\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}B^{2,\alpha}_{\partial\Omega}(\lambda,\delta(\lambda))

\displaystyle\longrightarrow C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\otimes\Omega^{n-1}(\partial\Omega),

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto\gamma^{ij}\left(u^{f}(x),x,\nabla u^{f}(x)\right)\partial_{j}u^{f}(x)\nu_{i}.

Here
\nu_{i}=\nu_{i}(x)
denotes the differential form
\iota^{*}(\partial_{i}\llcorner dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n})
, where
\iota:\partial\Omega\to\overline{\Omega}
is the standard embedding. In local coordinates,
\nu_{i}=n_{i}d\mbox{Area}_{\partial\Omega}
, where
n_{i}
is the
i
-th component of the unit outer normal vector on
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, Calderón’s problem reduces to an inverse problem of (
1.1
): is it possible to recover the anisotropic conductivity
\gamma=(\gamma^{ij})
uniquely from the information of
N_{\gamma}
?

It is well-known that the DN map is coordinate-invariant, and whence the best one can achieve is the unique determination of
\gamma
up to the following gauge transform induced by coordinate changing.

Definition 1

(Gauge transform)
.

For a
C^{3,\alpha}
-diffeomorphism
\Phi\in\text{\rm Diff}(\overline{\Omega})
, the
gauge transform

H_{\Phi}
on
C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\text{\rm Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))
is defined by

(1.5)

H_{\Phi}:K(s,x,p)\longmapsto\frac{D\Phi(\cdot)^{T}K(s,\cdot,D\Phi(\cdot)p)D\Phi(\cdot)}{|\det D\Phi(\cdot)|}\circ\Phi^{-1}(x).

The DN map is invariant under this gauge transform, that is,
N_{H_{\Phi}\gamma}=N_{\gamma}
. See Lemma
2
for details.

If
\gamma
is linear and isotropic, i.e.,
\gamma^{ij}(s,x,p)=\gamma(x)\delta^{ij}
, Calderón’s problem is well-understood. The uniqueness of inversion is due to
[
33
]
for
n\geq 3
, and
[
27
]
for
n=2
. See for example
[
34
,
1
,
2
]
for further development.

If
\gamma
is linear and anisotropic, i.e.,
\gamma(s,x,p)=\gamma(x)
has distinct eigenvalues, the EIT problem is far more involved. A key observation made in
[
24
]
linked anisotropic Calderón’s problems with inverse coefficient problems of elliptic equations on a closed Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with boundary
\partial M
, and of dimension
n\geq 3
. Explicitly, the metric
g
is given by

(g_{ij})=(\det\gamma)^{\frac{1}{n-2}}(\gamma^{ij})^{-1},~\text{\rm or equivalently}~(\gamma^{ij})=\sqrt{\det g}(g_{ij})^{-1}.

When
g
is analytic, the unique retrieval of
\gamma
was proved in
[
23
,
22
,
21
]
. When
g
is
conformally transversally anisotropic
(see Definition
2
) in some given conformal class,
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
reconstructed the conformal factor of
g
on certain conditions. In general, the anisotropic Calderón problem remains an open problem.

The conductivities of a medium might vary as electrical potentials and electric fields change. This corresponds to nonlinear conductivities
\gamma(s,x,p)
and nonlinear elliptic equations. For semilinear equations

(1.6)

-\Delta u+a(u,x)=0,

[
14
]
developed the first-order linearization method to identify
a(s,x)
with some constraints on
a
.
[
20
,
13
,
19
]
recovered
a(s,x)
for
a
analytic in
s
, by applying the higher-order linearization scheme originated in
[
18
]
for nonlinear hyperbolic equations. Furthermore,
[
26
,
7
]
extended this framework to inverse problems of quasilinear equation (
1.1
) with
\gamma^{ij}(s,x,p)=a(s,x,p)\delta^{ij}
, and
[
25
]
applied this to the general quasilinear anisotropic case in dimension
n=2
. See
[
30
,
17
]
for recent progress on partial data problems.

For practical applications, it is more meaningful but more challenging to determine anisotropic coefficients in nonlinear equations. For analytic coefficients
\gamma^{ij}(s,x,p)=\gamma^{ij}(s,x)
,
[
32
]
solved inverse problems of (
1.1
) via the second-order linearization. When the electric field is independent of the electric potential, i.e.,
J(s,x,p)=J(x,p)
,
[
15
,
9
]
reconstructed the coefficients of the Taylor expansion in
p
under various a priori conditions. For more recent results, see
[
31
,
6
,
29
,
16
,
8
]
.

In this article, we are interested in inverse problems on
conformally transversally anisotropic
(CTA for short) manifolds, introduced by Dos Santos Ferreira, Kenig, Salo, and Uhlmann in
[
10
]
.

Definition 2

(CTA manifold)
.

A Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
with boundary is called a conformally transversally anisotropic manifold, if there exists a Riemannian manifold
(M_{0}^{n-1},g_{0})
such that
(\overline{M},g)\subset(\mathbb{R}\times M_{0},c\cdot(e\oplus g_{0}))
, where
(\mathbb{R},e)
is the real space with canonical metric, and
c(x)>0
is the conformal factor. Moreover, if
c(x)=1
, then the metric is said to be
transversally anisotropic
(TA for short). If in addition,
(M_{0},g_{0})
is a simple manifold, which means that
\partial M_{0}
is strictly convex and that
exp_{p}
is a diffeomorphism onto
\overline{M_{0}}
for all
p\in M_{0}
, then we call
M
a
simple CTA manifold
.

Now suppose
(M,g)\subset(\mathbb{R}\times M_{0},c\cdot(e\oplus g_{0}))
is a compact CTA manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M
. The quasilinear anisotropic conductivity
\gamma
, associated with
g
, takes the form

(1.7)

\gamma(s,x,p)=a(s,x,p)A(x),

where
a\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}\times TM)
is strictly positive on
\overline{M}
, and that

(1.8)

\displaystyle A=\sqrt{\det g}\cdot g^{-1}.

We may rewrite the anisotropic conductivity
\gamma(s,x,p)
as

\gamma(s,x,p)=\frac{a(s,x,p)}{c(x)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}\left(c(x)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}A(x)\right).

With
A
in (
1.7
) replaced by
c^{\frac{n-2}{2}}A
and
a(s,x,p)
by
c(x)^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}a(s,x,p)
, the equation (
1.1
) remains unchanged. Meanwhile, the corresponding Riemannian metric to
c^{\frac{n-2}{2}}A
is exactly
e\oplus g_{0}
. In this sense, we may assume that
c\equiv 1
without loss of generality.

The analogue of (
1.1
) on
M
reads

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \nabla^{*}(a(u,x,\nabla u)\nabla u)&=0\quad\text{in}~M,\\
u&=f\quad\text{on}~\partial M,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\nabla^{*}
and
\nabla
denote the divergence and gradient associated with
g
respectively. Lemma
1
yields a well-defined DN map
N_{\gamma}
for each
\gamma
in (
1.7
) on
\partial M
. The inverse problem of concern is to retrieve the information of
\gamma
from the knowledge of
N_{\gamma}
. Our main result in this article is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(M,\partial M,g)
be a compact simple CTA manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M
. Write
\gamma_{m}(s,x,p):=a_{m}(s,x,p)A(x)
, for
m=1,2
as in (
1.7
) and (
1.8
). If
N_{\gamma_{1}}=N_{\gamma_{2}}
, then for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times M
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{1+n}
, it holds that

(1.10)

\partial_{(s,p)}^{\beta}a_{1}(s,x,p)\Big|_{(s,p)=(t,0)}=\partial_{(s,p)}^{\beta}a_{2}(s,x,p)\Big|_{(s,p)=(t,0)}.

If in addition,
a_{m}(s,x,p)
is analytic in
p
, then
a_{1}=a_{2}
.

Remark 1
.

We use Einstein’s summation convention throughout this article. Unless otherwise specified, indices in
[1,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
are denoted by Roman letters, while those in
[0,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
are denoted by Greek letters.

For isotropic media, the classical framework for linear conductivity equations, introduced in
[
33
]
, converts Calderón’s problem to an inverse potential problem for Schrödinger equations. The latter is achieved by establishing the injectivity of a certain integral transform of the potential through
complex geometric optics
(CGO) solutions of the Schrödinger equations. See also
[
28
]
for inverse potential problems of Schrödinger equations. For nonlinear equations, the approach entails higher-order linearization, which reduces inverse conductivity problems for nonlinear equations to inverse potential problems for linearized equations (see, for example,
[
13
,
20
,
19
]
). Generally, when the nonlinearity depends also on the electric fields, the relevant integral transforms involve multiple products of the gradient of harmonic functions, as shown in
[
7
,
5
,
25
]
.

Anisotropic media are more involved. On CTA manifolds,
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
utilized Gaussian beam quasi-modes to construct the principal terms of CGO solutions and applied Carleman estimates to analyze the remainder terms. This approach determines the conformal factor of the conductivity. Existing work on nonlinear equations in anisotropic media
[
32
,
13
,
6
]
required various additional assumptions on the conductivity. In general, the CTA property required in Theorem
1
does not satisfy the conditions therein.

Our strategy to tackle Theorem
1
consists of two components. First, we follow the framework of
[
7
]
to linearize the problem, and to convert the problem of unique inversion of coefficients to the injectivity of some integral transforms.

Then, to prove (
1.10
) for each
\beta
, we use the following methods to treat the relevant integral transforms.

•

For
|\beta|=0
, it is directly reduced to the inverse problem for linearized equations on CTA manifolds, solved in
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
.

•

For
|\beta|=1
, we first recover
\partial_{(s,p)}^{\beta}a(s,x,p)|_{(s,p)=(t,0)}
at the boundary, and then extend it to the interior. The proof at the boundary, inspired by
[
32
]
, applies singular solutions constructed in
[
3
]
to the integral transforms. The dependence of
a(s,x,p)
on
p
produces the gradients of harmonic functions in the integral transforms. We utilize Stokes’ theorem and the boundary determination results to convert the integral transform involving gradients of harmonic functions to that involving only harmonic functions.

•

For
|\beta|\geq 2
, we use the CGO solutions constructed in
[
12
,
13
]
with the Gaussian beams concentrated near some non-tangential geodesics (see Definition
3
). Inspired by
[
19
]
, we plug two pairs of such CGO solutions into relevant integral transforms. This reduces the integral transforms to a linear system, the coefficients of which are the tangent vectors of the geodesics at a point. The choice of tangent vectors ensures the uniqueness and existence of the solution to this system.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Forward problems

First, we need to show that the DN map for (
1.1
) is well-defined. In fact, one can prove that the forward problem is well-posed near constant solutions, by adapting the proof of
[
17
, Theorem B.1]
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the boundary value problem (
1.1
) with
(\gamma^{ij})
satisfying the elliptic conditions (
1.3
). Then for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a positive number
\delta(\lambda)
such that
for any boundary value
f\in B^{2,\alpha}_{\partial M}(\lambda,\delta(\lambda))
the problem (
1.1
) admits a unique solution
u^{f}
near the constant solution
\lambda
.

Then one can verify the gauge-invariance of the DN map under the gauge transform
H_{\Phi}
in (
1.5
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
\gamma
be a conductivity in (
1.1
).
If
\Phi\in\text{\rm Diff}(\overline{M})
is a
C^{3,\alpha}
-diffeomorphism satisfying
\Phi_{\partial M}=\mathrm{id}_{\partial M}
, then
N_{H_{\Phi}\gamma}=N_{\gamma}.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Let
u^{f}\in B^{2,\alpha}_{\partial M}(\lambda,\delta(\lambda))
be some solution of (
1.1
), and let
\tilde{u}=u^{f}\circ(\Phi^{-1})
with
\Phi\in\text{\rm Diff}(\overline{M})
a
C^{3,\alpha}
diffeomorphism satisfying
\Phi_{\partial M}=id_{\partial M}
. The function
\tilde{u}
will thus be the solution of the following equation:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{i}(\gamma^{ij}(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi,x,\nabla(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi))\partial_{j}(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi))&=0\quad\text{in}~M,\\
\tilde{u}&=f\quad\text{on}~\partial M.\end{aligned}\right.

This means that for any
\psi=\varphi\circ\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
, it holds that

\int_{M}\gamma^{ij}(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi(x),x,\nabla(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi)(x))\partial_{j}(\tilde{u}\circ\Phi)(x)\partial_{i}(\varphi\circ\Phi)(x)dx=0.

Let
x=\Phi^{-1}(y)
, the integral above becomes

\int_{M}\frac{(D\Phi_{\Phi^{-1}(y)}\nabla\varphi)_{i}(y)\gamma^{ij}(\tilde{u}(y),\Phi^{-1}(y),(D\Phi_{\Phi^{-1}(y)}\nabla\tilde{u})(y))(D\Phi_{\Phi^{-1}(y)}\nabla\tilde{u})_{j}(y)}{|\det D\Phi|_{\Phi^{-1}(y)}}dy=0,

which can be exactly rewritten as the following:

\int_{M}\partial_{k}\varphi(y)(H_{\phi}\gamma)^{kl}(\tilde{u}(y),y,\nabla\tilde{u}(y))\partial_{l}\tilde{u}(y)dy=0,\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M).

Therefore, we know that
\tilde{u}=S^{H_{\Phi}\gamma}(f)
, and that

N_{H_{\Phi}\gamma}(f)(y)=(H_{\phi}\gamma)^{kl}(\tilde{u}(y),y,\nabla\tilde{u}(y))\partial_{y^{k}}\tilde{u}(y)\iota^{*}(\partial_{y^{l}}\llcorner dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^{n})\\
{\mathrel{\to}^{\text{\rm Let }y=\Phi(x)}_{u^{f}=\tilde{u}\circ\Phi}}\gamma^{ij}(u(x),x,\nabla u(x))\partial_{x^{i}}u(x)\iota^{*}(\partial_{x^{j}}\llcorner dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n})=N_{\gamma}(f)(x)\\
{\mathrel{\to}^{\Phi_{\partial M}=id_{\partial M}}}N_{\gamma}(f)(y),\quad\forall y\in\partial M,

which completes the proof.
∎

In fact, we may view the conductivity
(\gamma^{ij}(s,\cdot,p))
at fixed
(s,p)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a Riemannian metric
(g_{ij}(\cdot))
. The gauge transform of the conductivity is indeed the pullback of the metric
(g_{ij})
. This is clear as we have the following relationship between the equations:

\partial_{i}(\gamma^{ij}\partial_{j}u)=(\det g)\Delta_{g}(u),~\text{namely}~\partial_{i}(\gamma^{ij}\partial_{j}u)=0\Leftrightarrow\Delta_{g}(u)=0,

and that between the corresponding DN maps:

N_{g}u:=\iota^{*}(\text{\rm grad}^{g}u\llcorner d\text{\rm Vol}_{g})=\iota^{*}(\partial_{x^{i}}u\gamma^{ij}\partial_{x^{j}}\llcorner dx^{1}\wedge dx^{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n})=N_{\gamma}u.

2.2.
The Calderón problem for linear conductivities

We list some results on inversion of linear conductivities, which are useful for nonlinear conductivity problems.

First,
[
10
,
12
]
showed that the conformal factor of a linear CTA conductivity is uniquely determined by the DN map.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(M,\partial M,g)
and
A
be as in Theorem
1
and (
1.8
). Consider the linear conductivities
\gamma_{m}:=c_{m}A
with positive factors
c_{m}\in C^{\infty}(M)
for
m=1,2
. If
N_{\gamma_{1}}=N_{\gamma_{2}}
holds, then we have
c_{1}(x)=c_{2}(x)
for any
x\in M
.

The core of recovering conformal factors in Proposition
1
is to show the products of harmonic functions are dense in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
, which was proved in
[
10
, Subsection 6.1]
:

Lemma 3
.

Let
(M,g)
be as in Theorem
1
and
f\in L^{\infty}(M)
. Denote by
\mathcal{H}_{g}(M)
the collection of harmonic functions on
(M,g)
. If there holds

\int_{M}fu_{1}u_{2}=0,\quad\forall(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{1}g}(M)\times\mathcal{H}_{c_{2}g}(M),

then
f\equiv 0
.

Retrieving the second derivative requires special solutions with prescribed singularity only at a single point, which was constructed in
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain,
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
(A^{ij})\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega,\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))
. Suppose that
x_{0}=0
and
A^{ij}(x_{0})=\delta^{ij}
. There exists a solution of
\partial_{i}(A^{ij}\partial_{j}w)=0
with prescribed singularity at the point
x_{0}=0
. Specifically, such a solution takes the form

(2.2)

w(x)=H_{n}(x)+\omega_{n}(x),

where
H_{n}=|x|^{2-n}
is the Green function,
and
\omega_{n}\in C^{3,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies the order estimate

(2.3)

|\omega_{n}(x)|+|x||\nabla\omega_{n}(x)|\leq C_{\beta,\overline{\Omega},(A^{ij})}|x|^{2-n+\beta},

with
\beta\in(0,1)
and
C_{\beta,\overline{\Omega},(A^{ij})}<+\infty
.

The reconstruction of higher-order derivatives involves non-tangential geodesics on
(M_{0},g_{0})
introduced in
[
11
, Definition 1.2]
.

Definition 3

(Non-tangential geodesics)
.

A geodesic
\gamma:[-T,T]\to M_{0}
is called non-tangential, if there holds that

•

the interior of
\gamma
lies in the interior of
M_{0}
, i.e.,
\{\gamma(x):x\in(-T,T)\}\subset M_{0}^{int}
;

•

the endpoints of
\gamma
are at the boundary of
M_{0}
, i.e.,
\gamma(\pm T)\in\partial M
;

•

\gamma
is not tangent to the boundary of
M_{0}
, i.e.,
\dot{\gamma}(\pm T)\notin T_{\gamma(\pm T)}(\partial M_{0})
.

In particular, we need to estimate some products of the gradients of harmonic functions, where CGO solutions are effective. In this circumstance, a key component of the suitable CGO solutions is the following Gaussian beam quasi-modes from
[
13
, Section 4.1]
and
[
19
, Proposition 5.2]
.

Proposition 3

(Gaussian beam quasi-modes)
.

Suppose
(M_{0},g_{0})
is a compact Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M_{0}
of dimension
n-1
. Let
\gamma:[-T,T]\to M_{0}
be a non-tangential geodesic. For any
k,K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
, there is a family of functions

\left\{v_{s}\in C^{\infty}(M_{0}):s=\tau+i\lambda,\tau\geq 1,\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|<1\right\}

satisfying the estimates

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left\|(-\Delta_{g_{0}}-(\tau+i\lambda)^{2})v_{\tau+i\lambda}\right\|_{H^{k}(M_{0})}&=O(\tau^{-K})\\
\left\|v_{\tau+i\lambda}\right\|_{L^{4}(M_{0})}&=O(\tau^{-\frac{n-2}{8}})\\
\left\|v_{\tau+i\lambda}\right\|_{L^{4}(\partial M_{0})}&=O(\tau^{-\frac{n-2}{8}})\end{aligned}\right.\quad\mbox{ as $\tau\to\infty$.}

Specifically, each
v_{s}
reads

(2.4)

v_{s}(x^{\prime})=e^{i(\tau+i\lambda)\psi(x^{\prime})}b_{\tau+i\lambda}(x^{\prime}),

where

•

the phase function
\psi(x^{\prime})
satisfies

\psi(\gamma(t))=t,\quad\nabla\psi(\gamma(t))=\dot{\gamma}(t),\quad\text{\rm Im}((\psi\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t))\geq 0,\quad\text{\rm Im}(\nabla^{2}\psi(\gamma(t)))|_{\dot{\gamma}(t)^{\bot}}>0,

for
t
close to
t_{0}
;

•

the amplitude
b_{\tau+i\lambda}(x^{\prime})
is supported within a
\delta
-tubular neighborhood of the geodesic
\gamma
, and admits an expansion along
\gamma
,

b_{\tau+i\lambda}(\gamma(t))=\tau^{\frac{n-2}{4}}(b_{(0)}(\gamma(t))+O(\tau^{-1})),

with
b_{(0)}(\gamma(t))
non-vanishing.

The desired CGO solutions, constructed in
[
13
, (44)]
and
[
19
, Proposition 5.3]
, are the following.

Proposition 4

(CGO solutions)
.

Let
(M,g)
be as in Theorem
1
and
\gamma:[-T,T]\to M_{0}
a non-tangential geodesic. Then for any
\tau\geq 1,~\delta>0,~\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|<1
and

, the equation
\Delta_{cg}u=0
admits a CGO solution of the form:

u_{\tau+i\lambda}=e^{-(\tau+i\lambda)x^{1}}(v_{\tau+i\lambda}(x^{\prime})+r_{\tau+i\lambda})

where
v_{\tau+i\lambda}(x^{\prime})=e^{i(\tau+i\lambda)\psi(x^{\prime})}b_{\tau+i\lambda}(x^{\prime})
is the Gaussian beam quasi-mode given as in Proposition
3
.
More precisely, for any
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
one can find

(2.5)

b_{\tau+i\lambda}=\tau^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}(\tau+i\lambda)^{-k}b_{(-k)}

such that the remainder term
r_{\tau+i\lambda}
obeys

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|r_{\tau+i\lambda}\|_{H^{k}(M)}

\displaystyle=O(\tau^{-K}),\quad\mbox{ as $|\tau|\to\infty$ }.

2.3.
Linearization of Nonlinear Equations

We perform
L
-fold linearization on (

3.1.

When

(4.16)

Q_{\alpha_{1}}Q_{\alpha_{2}}a_{1}(t,x,0)=Q_{\alpha_{1}}Q_{\alpha_{2}}a_{2}(t,x,0).

If we insert this into (
4.1
), we will get that
w_{0}^{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}}=0
. In other words, we can define

(4.17)

w^{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}}:=w_{1}^{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}}=w_{2}^{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}}.

4.2.
Higher-Order Linearization: Proof by Induction on Orders

In this subsection, we prove the following proposition for recovery of higher-order derivatives.

Proposition 8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, equation (
1.10
) holds for
|\beta|\geq 3
.

We take the symmetric tensor fields
T_{m}^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}
and the operator
T_{m}^{J}
to be

\displaystyle T_{m}^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}(x)

\displaystyle:=Q_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots Q_{\alpha_{J}}a_{m}(t,x,0)

\displaystyle T_{m}^{J}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{J})(x)

\displaystyle:=T_{m}^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}(x)P_{\alpha_{1}}u_{1}(x)P_{\alpha_{2}}u_{2}(x)\cdots P_{\alpha_{J}}u_{J}(x).

We consider
T_{0}^{J}:=T_{1}^{J}-T_{2}^{J}
. Then Proposition
8
reduces to
T_{0}^{L}\equiv 0
for all
L\geq 3
.

We consider the boundary values in the form of
f_{\epsilon}=t+\sum_{k=1}^{L+1}\epsilon^{k}h_{k}
and repeat the linearization as in (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) for
m=1,2
. The linearized equation reads

(4.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \nabla^{*}(a(t,x,0)\nabla w_{m}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}})&=-\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}\nabla^{*}\big(\phi_{m}^{L}(h_{\sigma(1)},\cdots,h_{\sigma(L+1)})&\\
&\qquad+T_{m}^{L}(w^{h_{\sigma(1)}},\cdots,w^{h_{\sigma(L)}})\nabla w^{h_{\sigma(L+1)}}\big)&\quad\text{\rm in}~M,\\
w_{m}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}}&=0&\quad\text{\rm on}~\partial M,\end{aligned}\right.

where
a(t,x,0):=a_{1}(t,x,0)=a_{2}(t,x,0)
by Proposition
1
, while the corresponding DN map is

(4.19)

\begin{split}D^{L+1}N_{\gamma_{m}}(h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1})=\nu\cdot\bigg[&a(t,x,0)\nabla w_{m}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{L+1}}\\
&+\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}\big(\phi_{m}^{L}(h_{\sigma(1)},\cdots,h_{\sigma(L+1)})\\
&+T_{m}^{L}(w^{h_{\sigma(1)}},\cdots,w^{h_{\sigma(L)}})\nabla w^{h_{\sigma(L+1)}}\big)\bigg]\quad\text{\rm on}~\partial M,\end{split}

where
\phi_{m}^{L}
, by definition in (
2.11
), only depends on
T_{m}^{J-1}
and
w_{m}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{J}}
for
J\leq L
.

We prove Proposition
8
by induction on
L\geq 3
, while the case
L=3
has been proved in Proposition
7
. The inductive hypothesis is that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &T_{0}^{J-1}\equiv 0\\
&w_{0}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{J}}:=w_{1}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{J}}-w_{2}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{J}}\equiv 0\end{aligned}\right.\quad\mbox{for $J\leq L$}.

This implies
\phi_{1}^{L}=\phi_{2}^{L}
. Consequently, taking the difference of (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) for
m=1,2
gives that

(4.20)

\begin{cases}\nabla^{*}\left(a(t,x,0)\nabla w_{0}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}}\right)=-\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}\nabla^{*}\left(T_{0}^{L}(w^{h_{\sigma(1)}},\cdots,w^{h_{\sigma(L)}})\nabla w^{h_{\sigma(L+1)}}\right)&\text{\rm in}~M,\\
w_{0}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}}=0&\text{\rm on}~\partial M,\end{cases}

and that

(4.21)

0=\nu\cdot\left(a(t,x,0)\nabla w_{0}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}}+\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}T^{L}(w^{h_{\sigma(1)}},\cdots,w^{h_{\sigma(L)}})\nabla w^{h_{\sigma(L+1)}}\right)~\text{\rm on}~\partial M.

In analogy with (
3.5
), we can get that for any
h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+2}\in C^{\infty}(\partial M)
,

(4.22)

\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}\int_{M}T^{L}(w^{h_{\sigma(1)}},\cdots,w^{h_{\sigma(L)}})\langle\nabla w^{h_{\sigma(L+1)}},\nabla w^{h_{L+2}}\rangle=0.

It suffices to prove the following proposition:

Proposition 9
.

Let
J\geq 2
. If the functions
S^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}(x)
are symmetric in indices
\{\alpha_{K}\}_{K=1}^{J}
, and the equation

(4.23)

\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(J+1)}\int_{M}S^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}P_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\sigma(1)}P_{\alpha_{2}}u_{\sigma(2)}\cdots P_{\alpha_{J}}u_{\sigma(J)}\langle\nabla u_{\sigma(J+1)},\nabla u_{J+2}\rangle=0

holds for any

u_{l}\in\{u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M}):\nabla^{*}(a(t,x,0)\nabla u)=0\},\quad l=1,\cdots,J+2,

then every
S^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}(x)
vanishes on
M
.

Proof.

We prove this proposition by induction on
J\geq 2
. The statement is true for
J=2
by adapting the proof of Proposition
7
. Now we want to prove that the statement holds for
J=L
if it is true for
J=L-1,~L\geq 3
.

First we show that
S^{0,\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{L}}\equiv 0
. Taking
u_{1}=1
in (
4.23
) for
J=L
gives

L\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(L)}\int_{M}S^{0,\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{L}}P_{\alpha_{2}}u_{\sigma(1)+1}\cdots P_{\alpha_{L}}u_{\sigma(L-1)+1}\langle\nabla u_{\sigma(L)+1},\nabla u_{L+2}\rangle=0,

as
\nabla u_{1}=0
. By induction hypothesis, we know that
S^{0,\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{L}}\equiv 0
.

By symmetry of indices we know that
S^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{L}}\equiv 0
if
\prod_{J=1}^{L}\alpha_{J}=0
. Putting this back to (
4.23
), we obtain

(4.24)

\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(L+1)}\int_{M}S^{l_{1},\cdots,l_{L}}\partial_{l_{1}}u_{\sigma(1)}\partial_{l_{2}}u_{\sigma(2)}\cdots\partial_{l_{L}}u_{\sigma(L)}\langle\nabla u_{\sigma(L+1)},\nabla u_{L+2}\rangle=0.

It remains to prove
S^{l_{1},\cdots,l_{L}}\equiv 0
for
1\leq l_{1},\cdots,l_{L}\leq n
. For simplification, we let
S^{L}
denote the
(0,L)
-tensor

S^{L}(X^{(1)},\cdots,X^{(L)}):=S^{l_{1},\cdots,l_{L}}X_{l_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots X_{l_{L}}^{(L)}.

We can still choose the following four harmonic functions in
(M,g)
:

u_{L-1}:=w^{1},~u_{L}:=w^{2},~u_{L+1}:=w^{3},~u_{L+2}:=w^{4},

where
w^{k},~k=1,2,3,4
is given in (
4.5
). Inserting the four functions above into the equation (
4.24
), we get that

(4.25)

0=\tau^{4}\int_{M}\left|b_{(0)}^{(1)}\right|^{2}\left|b_{(0)}^{(2)}\right|^{2}e^{2\text{\rm Im}\lambda x^{1}-2\text{\rm Re}\lambda\psi^{(1)}(x^{\prime})}U(\nabla\psi^{(1)},\nabla\psi^{(2)},\nabla u_{{1}},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})+O(\tau^{3}),

where
U(\nabla\psi^{(1)},\nabla\psi^{(2)},\nabla u_{{1}},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{U(\cdots)}{L!}:=

\displaystyle S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\nabla u_{1},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(2)},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(2)},\nabla u_{1},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+S^{L}(\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(2)},\nabla u_{1},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{L-2}\big[S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\nabla\psi^{(2)},\nabla u_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\nabla u_{{j}}},\cdots,\nabla u_{{L-2}})

\displaystyle\cdot\langle\nabla u_{j},\partial_{1}-i\nabla\psi^{(2)}\rangle\big].

By Lemma
5
,
U=0
at any
x=(x^{1},y_{0})\in M
.

Now it becomes a problem of linear algebra to prove
S^{L}=0
. We denote by
\xi^{(m)}
the tangent vector
\nabla\psi^{(m)}(y_{0})=\dot{\Gamma}^{(m)}|_{\Gamma^{(m)}=y_{0}}
, and by
\eta_{k}
the tangent vector
\nabla u_{k}
. At any
x=(x^{1},y_{0})\in M
,
U=0
amounts to

(4.26)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(1)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(2)},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(2)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+S^{L}(\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(2)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})\langle\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{L-2}\big[S^{L}(\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(1)},\partial_{1}+i\xi^{(2)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,\widehat{{\eta_{j}}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})\langle{\eta_{j}},\partial_{1}-i\xi^{(2)}\rangle\big].

Notice that
T_{(x^{1},y_{0})}M=T_{x^{1}}\mathbb{R}+T_{y_{0}}M_{0}
results in

\left(T_{(x^{1},y_{0})}M\right)^{L}=(T_{x^{1}}\mathbb{R})^{L}+(T_{y_{0}}M_{0})^{L}+\sum_{\mathrm{sym}}T_{x^{1}}\mathbb{R}\times T_{y_{0}}M_{0}\times\left(T_{(x^{1},y_{0})}M\right)^{L-2}.

So we divide the proof of
S^{L}=0
into the corresponding three parts:

(i)

S^{L}(\partial_{1},\xi,{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})=0
for any
\xi\in T_{y_{0}}M_{0},\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M
.

(ii)

S^{L}(\partial_{1},\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{1})=0.

(iii)

S^{L}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\cdots,\eta_{L})=0
for any
\eta_{k}\in T_{y_{0}}M_{0}
.

Firstly we prove Case (i). Since
\mathrm{span}V_{y_{0}}=T_{y_{0}}M_{0}
, it suffices to prove (i) for
\xi\in V_{y_{0}}
. Substitute
-\xi^{(1)}
for
\xi^{(1)}
in (
4.26
) and take the difference. We get that

S^{L}(\partial_{1},\xi^{(2)}-\langle\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)}\rangle\xi^{(1)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})=0,\quad\forall\xi^{(m)}\in V_{y_{0}},\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M.

By similar methods for (
4.12
), we obtain that

(4.27)

S^{L}(\partial_{1},\xi^{(1)},{\eta_{1}},\cdots,{\eta_{L-2}})=0,\quad\forall\xi^{(1)}\in V_{y_{0}},\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M.

For Case (ii), taking
\eta_{1}=\cdots=\eta_{L-2}=\partial_{1}
in (
4.26
) and applying (
4.27
) gives

(4.28)

(L+2)S^{L}(\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{1})=0.

Finally, it remains to prove Case (iii). Since
V_{y_{0}}=\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
, we can find
\{\xi_{l}^{(1)}\}_{l=1}^{n-1}\subset V_{y_{0}}
such that

T_{y_{0}}M_{0}=\text{\rm span}\{\xi_{l}^{(1)}\}_{l=1}^{n-1}.

Let
\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)}\in V_{y_{0}}
be parallel to
\xi_{l}^{(1)}+\xi_{l^{\prime}}^{(1)}
, and choose
\xi^{(2)}\in V_{y_{0}}-\{\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)}\}_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{n-1}
. Taking
\eta_{L-2}=\partial_{1},\xi^{(1)}=\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)}
in (
4.26
) gives

S^{L}(\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)},\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)},\xi^{(2)},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{L-3})=0,\quad\forall\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M.

Since
\{\xi_{l}^{(1)}\}_{l=1}^{n-1}
span
T_{y_{0}}M_{0}
and
S^{L}
is symmetric, we obtain

(4.29)

S^{L}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\xi^{(2)},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{L-3})=0,\quad\forall\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in T_{y_{0}}M_{0},~\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M.

Taking
\xi^{(1)}=\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)}
in (
4.26
) and applying (
4.29
) leads to

2S^{L}(\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)},\xi_{l,l^{\prime}}^{(1)},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{L-2})=0,\quad\forall\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M.

Again by using the fact that
T_{y_{0}}M_{0}=\text{\rm span}\{\xi_{l}^{(1)}\}_{l=1}^{n-1}
we know

(4.30)

2S^{L}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{L-2})=0,\quad\forall\zeta_{l}\in T_{y_{0}}M_{0},\eta_{k}\in T_{x}M,

which completes the proof of
S^{L}=0
.

To conclude, the statement of Proposition
9
holds for
J=L,~L\geq 3
if it holds for
J=L-1
. By induction on

, we finish the proof for all
J\geq 2
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
8
.

Assume
T_{0}^{J-1}\equiv 0
and
w_{0}^{h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{J}}\equiv 0
for
J\leq L
. By (
4.22
), the coefficients
T_{0}^{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}}
of the operator
T_{0}^{J}
satisfy the conditions in Proposition

, and hence

(4.31)

T_{0}^{L}\equiv 0.

Hence, the right-hand side of the elliptic equation (
4.20

(4.32)

w_{0}^{h_{1},\cdots,h_{L+1}}=0.

Therefore, we have proved the result for

).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

If
a_{m}
is analytic in
p
, the Taylor series at
p=0
converges. As we have shown in Proposition
1
, Proposition
6
, Proposition
7
and Proposition
8
, all derivatives of
a_{m}
at
p=0
are equal, and the choice of
t
does not influence this equality. By analytic continuation, equality of all derivatives at

and any
(s,x)
implies
