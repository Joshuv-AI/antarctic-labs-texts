Knowledge, attitude, practice and symptoms of computer vision syndrome: a comparative study among university students and support staff
Authors: Tadpong Tantipanjaporn, Soranun Surapad, Kwanjira Yatong, Kiattisak Satamunai, Pattraporn Chomchey, Sudawadee Yasaka, Pantip Hinhumpatch, Kanpitcha Kiatkitroj, Sutthisaranyupong Chanyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the high prevalence of computer vision syndrome (CVS) in academic settings, there is a lack of comparative data regarding how different university cohorts manage these risks. This study evaluated and compared CVS knowledge, attitudes, preventive practices, and symptoms between undergraduate students and supporting staff at a university in Thailand.MethodsThe study included 485 participants (269 students, 216 staff). A questionnaire assessed demographics, computer usage, CVS knowledge, attitudes, practices, and 16 CVS symptoms. Symptoms were evaluated on a 4-point frequency and 2-point intensity scale. The total CVS score was calculated by summing the products of frequency and intensity for each symptom; a score ≥ 6 indicated CVS presence. Statistical analyses included Chi-square tests (comparing prevalence between participant groups), independent t-tests (comparing mean CVS scores), Pearson correlation (relationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices), and multiple linear regression (determining factors associated with CVS).ResultsMost participants demonstrated moderate CVS knowledge (51.1%), attitudes (79.4%), and practices (76.1%). Staff had significantly higher knowledge and attitude scores, whereas students showed significantly better preventive practices. Overall, preventive practices were inversely correlated with knowledge (r = -0.246, p < 0.001) and attitude (r = -0.138, p = 0.002); however, stratified analysis revealed that this “knowledge-practice decoupling” was highly pronounced among support staff (knowledge: r = -0.389, p < 0.001; attitude: r = -0.169, p = 0.013) but non-significant among undergraduate students. Overall CVS prevalence was 70.3%, being higher in students (74.0%) than staff (65.7%). Students also reported significantly higher CVS scores (14.13 ± 10.35) than staff (11.81 ± 9.59, p = 0.011). In the pooled sample, the regression model revealed that CVS preventive practice was the only significant factor associated with lower CVS scores (B = -0.25, p = 0.022). However, stratified analysis indicated this effect was significant exclusively among support staff.ConclusionDespite staff demonstrating better knowledge and attitudes regarding CVS, students showed higher preventive practices. However, CVS remains highly prevalent overall, especially among students. Since preventive practice was the only significant variable associated with CVS, targeted interventions should prioritize practical behavioural modifications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28701-6.Keywords: Digital eye strain, Health behaviour, Occupational health, Screen time, Higher education

Background

In the age of technology, the widespread incorporation of video display terminals (VDTs), including computers, tablets, and smartphones, has become the norm of educational and occupational settings [1]. While digital technologies has greatly reshaped job responsibilities and increased productivity, they have also introduced significant occupational and public health risk factors [2]. The most prevalent effect of the prolonged use of digital electronic devices is Computer Vision Syndrome (CVS), also commonly known as Digital Eye Strain (DES) [3, 4]. CVS encompasses a complex of ocular and extraocular symptoms, resulting from prolonged digital screen use, including symptoms such as eye strain, headaches, blurred vision, dryness and musculoskeletal disorders like the neck and shoulders pain [5–8]. Symptoms worsen due to the specific visual challenges of digital screens, including unclear imprecise letter clarity and reduced contrast [5]. The global prevalence of CVS is notably high. A recently conducted systematic review reported that 69.0% of participants across studies experienced CVS, with prevalence ranging from 12.1% to 97.3% [9], making it one of the most important occupational problems of the 21st century.

A digital learning ecosystem is used to develop the digital competencies of university students [10]. An, Qian [11] reported that undergraduate students face elevated academic pressures that often require extensively engagement in digital tasks, including e-learning, research activities, and virtual interactions, resulting in increased digital fatigue and stress. Recent global cross-sectional studies underscore the widespread severity of this problem among university students with prevalence rate ranging from 73.7% to 89.7% [12–15]. Across theses contexts, high prevalence of CVS is associated with intensive screen use in academic tasks, a lack of adequate rest between screen watching periods and failure to observe the 20-20-20 rule. In the context of Thailand, the burden posed by CVS is similarly significant. During the COVID-19 Pandemic, Wangsan, Upaphong [16] studied CVS among students at Chiang Mai University. They found an 81% prevalence rate of CVS, which was influenced by a viewing distance of less than 20 cm, screen glare, low screen brightness, inadequate break time between classes, and increased screen time during online learning. Additionally, Seresirikachorn, Thiamthat [17] also reported 70.1% of students suffering from CVS, while its severity was related to online learning duration and digital device usage.

University support and administrative staff encounter a distinct set of occupational stressors. Supporting staff routines usually involve prolonged, continuous exposure to desktop monitors for standard 6–8-hour workdays [18, 19], in contrast to students. A study among computer office workers in Sri Lanka reveals that a 1-year prevalence of CVS was 67.4%, and the majority of the workstations (88.4%) were non-compliant with standard ergonomic compliance, increasing the risk of CVS [20]. A study in Ethiopia also reported high prevalence of CVS university academic staff (78.8%), with main risk factors such as working with visual display terminals, being exposed to improper illumination levels, and not taking rest breaks [21]. Comparable patterns are found in Thailand. Tingsa, Chaikittiporn and Pongpan [22] reported that prolonged, continuous computer work for over five hours per day without adequate breaks was a primary factor related to severe CVS among the university support staff in Lampang Province, with 37.5% of participants experiencing severe CVS. Although both groups share the same campus environment, their risk characteristics vary significantly. Supporting staff encounter continuous operational demands at fixed workstations [23], while students also are at risk of developing CVS due to their irregular use of digital devices, extended mobile device usage, and often poorly optimized study environments [24].

The high rate of CVS in both groups highlights the importance of understanding the behavioural factors contributing to the symptoms. A Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) model is a vital framework for collecting information on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of specific groups, understanding their relationships, and serving as a reference for evaluating related programmes or interventions [25, 26]. The KAP was originally introduced by Hochbaum during the middle of the 19th century [27]. Existing literature suggests a considerable disconnect between knowledge and practice among users. For instance, the majority of the university students (89.9%) in Malaysia reported at least one CVS symptoms, and this study assessed both their knowledge and practices related to computer use [28]. Additionally, KAP studies among both medical and non-medical university populations have highlighted that students generally possess moderate knowledge and attitudes toward CVS prevention; however, this awareness often does not translate into consistent preventive practices, in spite of the high prevalence of CVS symptoms [29, 30].

Based on distinct behavioural and environmental characteristics between the two groups, this study developed an a priori epidemiological hypothesis that the participants’ specific exposure profiles would moderate the KAP-to-symptom pathways. Specifically, it is hypothesized that the rigid and continuous workstation demands experienced by supporting staff would limit their ability to translate knowledge and positive attitudes into preventive practice. On the other hand, students, whose screen exposure is generally more flexible were expected to result in different pattern of associations between KAP factors and CVS symptoms. This hypothesis is further supported by a notable gap in the existing literature. Although studies on CVS in higher education have been increasing, most studies have investigated either the student population or occupational staff separately, rather than comparing both undergraduate students and support staff together. Consequently, a critical research gap remains in concurrently investigating both groups under a standardized framework for assessing KAP. To date, there is a notable lack of comparative data within the Thai institutional ecosystem examining how the distinct environmental, psychosocial, and working demands of students and support staff influence their KAP pathways and subsequent CVS symptom severity. Moreover, understanding the key determinants in a Thai university setting is crucial, as cultural work practices and institutional resources can strongly influence the effectiveness of health interventions. A direct comparison these two distinct groups, universities can introduced targeted strategies, including ergonomic improvement for staff workstations and digital wellness and health-promoting campaigns for students. Furthermore, the rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in academic and administrative activities has intensified screen exposure [31], potentially heightening visual strain and the risk of CVS. To address these critical research gaps in the literature, this study aims to: (1) evaluate and compare the knowledge, attitudes, preventative practices (KAP), and symptoms of CVS between undergraduate students and supporting staff at a university in Thailand, (2) determine the relationships between the knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding CVS among the participants, and (3) determine factors associated with CVS severity within these two distinct university populations. Operationally, this study focused exclusively on desktop and laptop computer users. To ensure a standardized and rigorous evaluation, CVS was assessed based on 16 different symptoms using the validated questionnaire developed by Seguí Mdel, Cabrero-García [7], which evaluates both symptom frequency and intensity.

Methods

Study design, participants, and setting

This comparative cross-sectional study conducted at a university located in Phitsanulok Province, Thailand. The minimum required sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1) [32], to ensure adequate statistical power for comparisons between two independent groups. Based on a two-tailed independent-samples t-test, with an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an anticipated small-to-medium effect size (d = 0.3), the required sample size was 176 participants per group. To allow for potential incomplete responses, the target sample size was increased accordingly. Data were collected among 269 undergraduate students and 216 supporting staff members, resulting in a total of 485.

A multi-stage sampling technique was employed for participant selection. For undergraduate students, a combination of stratified and cluster sampling was used to randomly select three faculties, representing one from each major discipline: Humanities and Social Sciences, Health Sciences, and Science and Technology. Subsequently, quota and simple random sampling methods were applied to recruit individual participants. For supporting staff, five faculties or administrative units were randomly selected from a total of 26, followed by the use of quota and random sampling techniques to identify the final participants. Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) current undergraduate students or supporting staff members with a minimum of one hour of daily computer usage, specifically restricted to desktop or laptop computers; (2) aged 20 years or older; (3) at least three months of experience in computer operation; (4) the absence of pre-existing eye diseases, such as pterygium, cataracts, or glaucoma; and (5) proficiency in the Thai language (reading, writing, and speaking). Exclusion criteria included individuals who were in the process of leaving the university (e.g., resignation or graduation) or those who declined to participate or provide uncompleted data.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of four parts: demographic characteristics, computer usage patterns, CVS-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices, and the occurrence of 16 CVS symptoms. Knowledge of CVS was assessed using 10 dichotomous (Yes/No) questions. Each correct answer was awarded 1 point, yielding a total possible score of 0–10. Participants were then categorised into three levels based on their total scores: high (≥ 80%; 8–10 points), moderate (50–79%; 5–7 points), and low (< 50%; 0–4 points), according to the criteria originally developed by Bloom, et al., as cited by Alzahrani [33]. There were each 10 questions for attitude toward CVS with negative and positive questions. The participants were measured using a 5-point Likert scale. For positively worded items, scores ranged from 5 (“strongly agree”) to 1 (“strongly disagree”). For negatively worded items, reverse scoring was applied, with scores ranging from 1 (“strongly agree”) to 5 (“strongly disagree”). The interpretation of scores was categorised into three levels: high, moderate, and low, using the class interval method proposed by Best and Kahn [34]. The interval width was calculated as (maximum mean score − minimum mean score) divided by the number of levels. Based on this approach, mean scores of 3.68-5.00 were interpreted as high, 2.34–3.67 as moderate, and 1.00-2.33 as low. Participants were asked 10 questions regarding CVS preventive practices. Responses were rated on a 4-point scale: “very high” (4 points), indicating regular and consistent practice (approximately 5–7 days per week); “high” (3 points), indicating regular but inconsistent practice (approximately 3–4 days per week); “low” (2 points), indicating occasional practice (approximately 1–2 days per week); and “very low” (1 point), indicating rare or no practice. Behavioural levels were classified into three categories which were low, moderate, and high, using the method proposed by Best and Kahn [34]. Mean scores ranging from 1.00 to 2.00 were interpreted as low-level behaviour, 2.01-3.00 as moderate-level behaviour, and 3.01-4.00 as high-level behaviour.

To assess CVS symptoms, this study used a reliable and valid CVS questionnaire [7]. There were 16 symptoms: burning, itching, feeling of a foreign body, tearing, excessive blinking, eye redness, eye pain, heavy eyelids, dryness, blurred vision, double vision, difficulty focusing for near vision, increased sensitivity to light, coloured halos around objects, feeling that sight is worsening, and headache. The 16 symptoms were analysed separately with each of the two rating scales (frequency and intensity). The frequency of symptom occurrence was assessed using a 4-point scale (0–3), where 0 = never, 1 = occasionally (sporadic episodes or about once per week), 2 = often (two to three times per week), and 3 = very often or always (almost every day). Symptom intensity was evaluated on a 2-point scale, with 1 = moderate and 2 = intense. For analysis, symptoms reported as “never” were assigned a value of 0 (none) for intensity. The total questionnaire score was calculated by summing the products of frequency and intensity for each symptom across all 16 items (i.e., total score = Σ [frequencyi × intensityi], for i = 1 to 16). CVS presence was defined as a total symptom score threshold of ≥ 6. This specific cut-off point is empirically justified by the original questionnaire developers [7], as the optimal threshold for distinguishing individuals with CVS from those without the condition. The original questionnaire achieves both sensitivity and specificity over 70%, alongside good test-retest repeatability for the continuous scores (ICC = 0.802; 95% CI: 0.673, 0.884) and CVS classification (K = 0.612; 95% CI: 0.384, 0.839) [7].

As the study participants were Thai, the questionnaire was developed in the Thai language, with the exception of the CVS section. The CVS portion underwent a translation and validation process, including forward translation into Thai, synthesis of the translated versions, back-translation into English, and a comparative review of the original and back-translated versions. Content validity was assessed by three experts using the Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) index, with all items achieving an average score above 0.5. A pilot study was conducted with 30 individuals (15 undergraduate students and 15 supporting staff) who were not part of the main study population. Reliability testing was performed on the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) sections, yielding coefficients of 0.65 for knowledge, 0.65 for attitude, and 0.79 for practices.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.2.0 [35], was applied to analyse data. Descriptive statistics were used to characterise the data in terms of frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation (S.D.), and minimum and maximum values. A Chi-square test and an independent samples t-test were performed to examine the differences in demographic characteristics, computer usage patterns, and CVS-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices between students and supporting staff. A Chi-square test was also used to identify the relationship between academic status and the prevalence of CVS. To further evaluate the severity of symptoms, an independent samples t-test was conducted to compare the mean CVS scores between the two groups. A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the core theoretical variables: knowledge of CVS, attitude toward CVS prevention, and CVS preventive practices. Prior to conducting the independent t-test and Pearson correlation, visual inspections of histograms and Q-Q plots confirmed that the data were normally distributed. A univariable linear regression analysis was initially conducted to examine the association between each independent variable and the total score of CVS. Subsequently, a pooled multiple linear regression analysis was conducted using the enter method to identify variables associated with the CVS total score. This model included variables from the core theoretical framework (Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice) alongside demographic and occupational factors including age, gender, academic status, computer use duration, frequency of eye breaks, and type of computer used. To ensure the robust integrity of the regression, collinearity diagnostics were performed using Tolerance and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A stratified multivariable linear regression analysis, separating undergraduate students and support staff, was subsequently conducted. Reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20) formula for the knowledge section and Cronbach’s alpha for the attitude and practice sections.

Results

Study participant profile

This study included 485 participants, comprising undergraduate students (55.5%) and supporting staff (44.5%). Both groups were predominantly female. The mean age was 40 years for the staff group and 21 years for students. Regarding type of computer used, laptops were primary used for students (89.2%), while the majority of the staff used desktop computers (87.0%). Daily computer usage was significantly higher among staff (6.8 ± 2.0 h), compared to student (5.0 ± 2.8 h). While staff reported significantly higher scores of CVS knowledge and attitude toward CVS, students showed significantly better CVS preventive practices (Table 1).

Among the total participants, 341 (70.3%) had CVS (defined as a score ≥ 6). There was a significant association between academic status and the prevalence of CVS (p-value = 0.048). Correlations among KAP variables

Table 5 presents the correlation matrix for the total sample, as well as stratified by academic status for students and staff. In the total sample, there was a moderate positive correlation between knowledge of CVS and attitude toward CVS prevention (r = 0.528, p-value < 0.001). However, CVS preventive practices demonstrated a significant negative correlation with both knowledge of CVS (r = -0.246, p-value < 0.001) and attitude toward CVS prevention (r = -0.138, p-value = 0.002). Distinct patterns were identified across the subgroups. Among students, while knowledge and attitude remained positively correlated (r = 0.355, p-value < 0.001), neither variable showed a significant correlation with CVS preventive practices. Conversely, among staff members, there was a significant, moderate positive correlation between knowledge of CVS and attitude toward CVS prevention (r = 0.482, p-value < 0.001). Higher levels of knowledge (r = -0.389, p-value < 0.001) and more positive attitudes (r = -0.169, p-value = 0.013) were significantly associated with poorer CVS preventive practices.

Factors associated with CVS

As presented in Table 6, statistically significant univariable factors included CVS preventive practices (p-value = 0.029), age (p-value = 0.015), academic status (p-value = 0.011), and laptop usage (p-value = 0.040) and combined desktop/laptop usage (p-value = 0.020). A multiple linear regression analysis (Table 6) revealed that the overall framework was statistically significant (p-value = 0.009, Adjusted R2 = 0.028). CVS preventive practices emerged as the only independent factor of lower CVS scores (B = -0.25). However, collinearity diagnostics flagged a critical statistical issue. Academic status showed a high Variance Inflation Factor (VIF = 8.698), alongside elevated VIF values for age (4.627) and laptop computer type (4.225). This indicated severe multicollinearity due to structural demographic confounding. A stratified multivariable regression analysis was performed separately for undergraduate students (Table 7). The stratification successfully eliminated collinearity, reducing all VIF values below 1.6 across both sub-groups. For the student model, the overall multivariable model was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.303, Adjusted R2 = 0.006). Conversely, the support staff model showed statistical significance (p-value = 0.003, Adjusted R2 = 0.073). Among support staff, CVS preventive practices was a significant factor of CVS severity (B = -0.51). Additionally, a higher frequency of eye breaks was associated with a reduction in CVS scores (B = -14.85) within this work environment.

Discussion

This study examined and compared knowledge, attitude, practices and symptoms of CVS in students and supporting staff and also identify factors associated with CVS in this educational setting. The study revealed a high overall prevalence CVS among participants, alongside generally moderate levels of CVS-related knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices. A notable difference was observed between demographic groups. While staff showed significantly better knowledge and attitudes, students showed higher preventive practices. Students reported a higher prevalence and severity of CVS. Importantly, preventive practice was identified as the significant factors for reducing CVS symptoms in the pooled sample.

The findings of this study consistently demonstrate that students not only have a higher likelihood of experiencing CVS (prevalence = 74.0%, compared to 65.7% for staff) but also experience more intense symptoms (CVS score = 14.13 ± 10.35, compared to 11.81 ± 9.59 for staff) than support staff (Tables 3 and 4). The 74.0% prevalence of CVS observed in our student population among students closely aligns with previous studies conducted internationally (73.7% in Oman [13]; 80.2% in Iran [12]; 84.8% in Egypt [36]; 83.1% in the U.S [15]. ; 89.7% in Saudi Arabia [14]) and within Thailand (70.1% [17] and 81.0% [16]). Similarly, the prevalence of CVS among staff in this study (65.7%) is comparable to previous international and domestic studies, which reported rates of 53.3% among supporting staff in Thailand [37]; 67.4% in Sri Lanka [20]; 78.8% in Ethiopia [21]. It is important to note that this study applied the CVS questionnaire developed by Seguí Mdel, Cabrero-García [7] and defined CVS as a total score of ≥ 6 based on the combined frequency and intensity of symptoms. Several studies have used the same questionnaire and criteria [12, 15–17, 37]. In contrast, Almuqrashi, Al-Noumani [13] and Iqbal, Elzembely [36] used the CVS Survey Form 3 (CVS-F3) with different diagnostic criteria. Iqbal et al. applied a five-symptom cut-off, whereas Almuqrashi et al. used broader criteria based on the presence or recurrence of ocular or extraocular symptoms.

When considering the severity of individual CVS symptoms, the highest-ranked symptoms among the total population were the feeling of a foreign body, followed by eye pain and blurred vision (Table 2). When stratified by group, eye pain was the most severe symptom for both students and staff. For students, this was followed by the feeling of a foreign body and itching. In contrast, the second and third most severe symptoms for staff were blurred vision and the feeling of a foreign body, respectively (Table S1, Supplementary Information). Other studies showed top 3 or 4 symptoms were quite similar. For example, a study in Thai university students reported that eye pain was the most common symptom, followed by burning sensation and headache; however, the most severe symptoms were worsening eyesight, then headache, and eye pain [16]. A study among college students in Pakistan reported the top 4 symptoms being headache, eye pain, itching, and feeling of worsening eyesight [38]. Among the university staff in Ethiopia, the most common reported symptoms were headache, eye redness, and blurred vision [21].

The higher prevalence of CVS among students compared to staff reported in the currents study is supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis of over 66,000 participants by Ccami-Bernal, Soriano-Moreno [9]. This review showed a higher prevalence of CVS in university students (76.1%, 95%CI 70.7–81.2), compared to the general populations (67.9, 56.7–78.3)) and worker groups (69.2, 64.7–73.6). Furthermore, a comparative study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that college students exhibited significantly higher rates of blurred distant vision and excessive tearing than employees [39]. This difference may be attributed to ergonomic disparities, as the majority of students (approximately 90%) in this study used laptops, often under non-ergonomic conditions [40, 41], compared to the standardised workstations typically provided to support staff. This may result in a significantly increased risk of CVS. While this study strictly focused desktop and laptop computer usage, external literature assessing broader digital screen time (including smartphones and tablets) provides essential lifestyle context. Students frequently use multiple devices, including smartphones and tablets, for both academic and recreational purposes [40]. Their screen time is often associated with mixed postures (e.g., both lying and sitting) [39, 41], which differs from the standard seated posture of employees and may further contribute to the risk of CVS. Excessive digital screen time defined as daily usage over 8 h for smartphones, 6 h for tablets, or 5 h for computers was also found among university students [42]. It should be noted that this study did not assess these specific contributing factors of CVS. Further investigation of these factors is warranted, especially particularly as AI tools become increasingly integrated into academic and staff workflows.

Although staff showed higher CVS scores of knowledges and attitude toward CVS, their preventive practices were lower than students (Table 1). It might be because the staff encounter several barriers, including inflexible schedules and supervisors’ limited awareness of eye strain and its potential impacts. As a result, staff may not be encouraged to take adequate rest breaks during prolonged periods of continuous computer use [43, 44]. Additionally, this “knowledge-practice decoupling” can be explained through the health belief model, specifically the conflict between perceived benefits and structural barriers. Despite having superior knowledge, support staff face immutable structural workplace bounds, such as fixed desktop monitors, rigid seating, and continuous occupational tasks. Over time, these constraints override preventive intentions, forcing staff to develop an exposure tolerance where they physically endure ocular discomfort to meet operational demands. Therefore, the contribution of executives and relevant stakeholders is vital, and further research is needed to develop and implement effective interventions aimed at reducing CVS. Regarding KAP relationships (Table 5), a moderate positive correlation between knowledge and attitude toward CVS prevention was found. This was similar with a study among adolescence in school of Dehradun, India, reporting a positive correlation between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.519) [45]. Interestingly, higher knowledge and positive attitudes did not translate into better practices; rather, an inverse relationship was observed. This finding is well-supported by Tafese, Nega [46] who studied among data processing workers in University of Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia. They reported that although the workers had good knowledge and positive attitude towards computer ergonomics, their practices were below the mean score. Similarly, a study among medical students reported that 71.0% of them understood that the meaning of CVS, but only 25.5% of them were practicing the ideal viewing distance at the level below the eye [47]. To address the knowledge-attitude-practice gap in educational setting, university policies should develop beyond simple awareness campaigns. University staff should be encouraged to implement mandatory micro-breaks and supported ergonomic workstations to decrease occupational barriers to positive practice. Improving the physical study environment among students, including encouraging the use of laptop stands, should be focused. A mixed method with quantitative and qualitative studies could be done to identify barriers of practices among educational population. Additionally, interventional designs to reduce eye strain should be conducted.

The pooled multiple regression model indicated that only CVS preventive practice was the significant variable associated with the total CVS score, emphasising higher preventive practices significantly reduces CVS severity (Table 6). This is aligned with a multiple logistic regression model of CVS predictors showing that preventive practices, including always taking regular breaks, were related to decreased CVS prevalence (adjusted OR = 0.11; 95% CI 0.02–0.80) [48]. Also, poor ergonomic practices were significantly linked with CVS [18]. The multiple regression model in this study provided the low explanatory power (adjusted R2 = 0.028), reflecting the complex, multifactorial origins of visual fatigue, encompassing unmeasured environmental and biological variables. Therefore, KAP variables alone may not be sufficient to fully describe the risk characteristics of CVS. Future research should investigate additional risk factors to develop a more comprehensive predictive model.

Although the pooled model (Table 6) suggested only CVS preventive practices as significant, the stratified analysis (Table 7) revealed a more detailed understanding these associations. The stratification showed that the protective effect of preventive practices was significant only among supporting staff, with no effect observed in students. Contrasting work environments may reflect this difference. Support staff normally work at fixed workstations for prolonged periods, making ergonomic practices and regular eye breaks more applicable in decreasing CVS symptoms (e.g., frequency of eye breaks: B = -14.85). On the other hand, students use portable devices in more flexible environments, where frequent posture and environmental changes may decrease the additional protective effect of formal preventive practices.

This study provides several remarkable strengths in this study. First, a comparative design between two groups within an educational setting which rarely employed in previous studies, especially in Thailand, was unutilized. Future studies, however, would include other academic populations like researchers and lecturers, for broader comparison. Methodologically, the KAP questionnaire was pilot-tested prior to administration, yielding acceptable reliability, while CVS was assessed using a standardized and widely adopted questionnaire developed by Seguí Mdel, Cabrero-García [7]. Although a formal validation study assessing the diagnostic performance of CVS instrument in a Thai population has not yet been conducted, the translated version underwent rigorous translation, back-translation, and content validity assessment procedures. Additionally, a multistage probability sampling technique confirmed that the samples were highly representative of the target population. Certain limitations must be acknowledged. This study did not investigate several potential covariates, for example ophthalmic factors (e.g. pre-exiting eye disease, contact lens use, and uncorrected refractive errors), environmental factors (e.g. light intensity, glare, and room humidity), and devices and workstations (e.g. ergonomic station, screen size, and other accessories). Nevertheless, this aligns with the study’s primary aim, which focused strictly on KAP regarding CVS. Another limitation is potential confounding by presbyopia in older support staff. Overlapping symptoms between presbyopia and CVS may have increased symptom scores, and the absence of objective eye examinations prevented adjustment for this factor. Future studies should include optometric measurements. As CVS status was determined using self-reported symptoms, the findings may be subject to recall and reporting bias. Additionally, as this study was conducted at a single university, the generalizability of the findings to other academic or occupational settings may be limited. The age difference between support staff and students may also have contributed to differences in symptom perception and reporting. Consequently, the prevalence estimates reported in this study represent subjective symptom experiences rather than clinically diagnosed ocular issues. Finally, the nature of cross-sectional design prevents the ability to establish causal effect analysis, causing the risk of reverse causality. Specifically, individuals experiencing severe ocular strain may have adopted preventive practices as a coping strategy. Prospective longitudinal research is necessary to confirm temporal relationships.

Regarding practical implications, the stratified analysis showed CVS preventive practices and the frequency of eye breaks as significant independent variables of symptom severity in supporting staff. These findings highlight the necessary of complementing educational campaigns with structural workplace interventions. Organisations should encourage regular eye breaks and facilitate the implementation of preventive behaviour through ergonomic workstation adjustments. Among undergraduate students, health intervention strategies should focus on improving postural ergonomics during prolonged digital device use in diverse learning environments.

Conclusion

This study highlights a high overall prevalence of CVS (70.3%) within educational population, with students experiencing significantly higher rates, compared to support staff. Although staff members showed greater knowledge and more positive attitude regarding CVS, students performed better preventive practice. Notably, while knowledge positively correlated with attitude, preventive practices negatively correlated with both, identifying a distinct knowledge-practice gap within this educational population. Eventually, preventive practices emerged as the sole significant variable associated with CVS, emphasising the essential need for interventions focused on practical behavioural changes rather than just gaining knowledge.
