Towards a Theoretical Understanding to the Generalization of RLHF
Zhaochun Li, Mingyang Yi, Yue Wang, Shisheng Cui, Yong Liu

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and its variants have emerged as the dominant approaches for aligning Large Language Models with human intent. While empirically effective, the theoretical generalization properties of these methods in high-dimensional settings remain to be explored. To this end, we build the generalization theory on RLHF of LLMs under the linear reward model, through the framework of algorithmic stability. In contrast to the existing works built upon the consistency of maximum likelihood estimations on reward model, our analysis is presented under an end-to-end learning framework, which is consistent with practice. Concretely, we prove that under a key feature coverage condition, the empirical optima of policy model have a generalization bound of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 1 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}) . Moreover, the results can be extrapolated to parameters obtained by gradient-based learning algorithms, i.e., Gradient Ascent (GA) and Stochastic Gradient Ascent (SGA). Thus, we argue that our results provide new theoretical evidence for the empirically observed generalization of LLMs after RLHF.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

We situate our work within the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) paradigm as formulated in
(
53
)
, given a dataset of prompts
\boldsymbol{S}=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
and a fixed
reference policy
\pi_{\rm{ref}}

r(x,a)
is learned from preferences, we operate in a setting where ground-truth rewards on prompts
x\in\mathcal{X}
and response

For a given
x

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r(x,a)\right]-\lambda\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_{\rm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)).

(1)

The goal of RLHF is to learn a policy
\pi
to maximize the objective
J(\pi)
, where:

\displaystyle J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}[f_{\pi}(x)],

(2)

i.e., we want to maximize the expected reward without deviating too much from the reference policy. W.l.o.g., to simplify following analysis, we set
\lambda=1
below. In the case that
\lambda\neq 1
, we divide both sides of (
1
) by
\lambda
, and treat the term
\frac{r(x,a)}{\lambda}
as a new reward function. This rescaling does not affect the solutions of the original objective function.

Before introducing the parametric model, we first characterize the optimal solution to (
2
) over the space of all valid probability distributions.

Proposition 1

(
32
)
.

For any reward function
r(x,a)
, the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
that maximizes (
2

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(a|x)=\frac{\pi_{\rm{ref}}(a|x)\exp(r(x,a))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi_{\rm{ref}}(a|x)\exp(r(x,a))}.

(3)

Motivated by this optimal form, we adopt a parametric approach to approximate the reward and the policy. We consider the widely used linear reward assumption
(
51
;
41
)
and the corresponding posterior Boltzmann policy class.

Definition 1

(linear reward function class)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed feature mapping. We consider the class of linear reward functions defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{r_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|r_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,a)=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\right\rangle\right\}.

Correspondingly, motivated by the optimal solution form in (
3
), we consider the following policy class:

Definition 2

(Posterior Boltzmann policy function class)
.

\displaystyle\Pi=\left\{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a|x)=\frac{\pi_{\rm{ref}}(a|x)\exp\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\right\rangle\right)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{\rm{ref}}(a^{\prime}|x)\exp\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\right\rangle\right)}\right\}.

To facilitate theoretical analysis, we make the following standard regularity assumptions regarding the boundedness of features and the realizability of the true reward.

Assumption 1
.

For all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, the feature norm is bounded such that
\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\|\leq C
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a ground-truth parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the true reward function is linear, i.e.
r(x,a)=\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)\rangle

\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\right\|\leq D

Under Assumption
2

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
, denoted as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(4)

However, since the context space

is vast, directly maximizing the expected objective in (

we approximate the expectation using a finite prompt dataset
\boldsymbol{S}=\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x_{i}),

(5)

We use

to denote the parameters obtained on

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}\in\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta_{R}|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=0\}\triangleq\Theta_{\boldsymbol{S}}.

(6)

\displaystyle\underbrace{|J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})-J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}})|}_{\mathrm{Concentration\ error}}+\underbrace{|J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}})-J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})|}_{\mathrm{Optimization\ error}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{|J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})-J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})|}_{\mathrm{Generalization\ error}},

Generalization error.

The generalization error measures the discrepancy between the true expected performance of the learned policy and its empirical performance. This gap arises because the policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}}
is optimized based on the training set
\boldsymbol{S}
, rendering the empirical objective a potentially biased estimate of the true objective.

To rigorously bound this error, we adopt the framework of
algorithmic stability
. The central premise is that if a learning algorithm is insensitive to small perturbations in the dataset, it prevents overfitting and ensures that the empirical objective is a reliable proxy for the expected objective.

For algorithm
\mathcal{H}:\boldsymbol{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we first formally define the generalization error and the notion of uniform stability used in our analysis.

Definition 4

(Generalization Error)
.

For learned parameters
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{S})=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}
, the generalization error is the gap between its empirical/population risks:

\small\left|J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})-J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})\right|.

(9)

Definition 5

(Uniform Stability
(
7
)
)
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{H}
is
\mathcal{E}_{\rm{stab}}
-uniformly stable if for any two datasets
\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
that differ by at most one example, the uniform stability is:

\small\mathcal{E}_{\rm{stab}}(\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{S}))=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime}}\left|f_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{S})}(x)-f_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime})}(x)\right|.

(10)

It has been proven that uniform stability implies generalization, which is the theoretical foundation for our subsequent analysis in Sections 4 and 5.

Proposition 2

(

\displaystyle\left|J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})\!-\!J_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}})\right|\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\rm{stab}}\log(n)\log\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\!+\!6RC\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(1/\rho\right)}{n}}.

Suboptimality Gap of Empirical Stationary Points

In this section, we first analyze the performance of the empirical stationary points, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}\in\Theta_{\boldsymbol{S}}

5.1
RL with Sufficient Prompts

Standard machine learning theory typically relies on the assumption that the training data provides sufficient coverage of feature patterns, thereby ensuring that the learned model can accurately extrapolate to the entire feature space. In linear settings, this requirement is formally characterized by the positive definiteness of the feature covariance matrix.

Assumption 3
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
x
and some
\sigma

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathsf{Var}_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a\mid x)}[\boldsymbol{\phi}(x,a)]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\boldsymbol{V}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\succeq\sigma\boldsymbol{I}
.

As can be seen, this Assumption implies the expected covariance matrix is positively-definite, meaning its column space covers the whole Euclidean space, thereby covering the feature space as well. Within this coverage assumption to the feature space, we have the following theorem, which states that with sufficient prompts, the empirical stationary point is exactly the ground-truth parameters with high probability.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
3
, when

\small n>\frac{128C^{4}}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\log{\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)}+d\log{\left(\frac{432RC^{5}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right),

(12)

it holds that with probability at least
1-\rho
, the ground truth parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
is the unique empirical stationary point with the region
\Theta_{R}
.

Our result indicates that with sufficient data coverage, the empirical stationary point achieves exact identification of the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
with high probability.

Notably, this result relies on the assumption that feature vectors span the entire Euclidean space. However, in practice, due to limited prompt diversity, feature vectors typically reside on a low-dimensional manifold. This motivates our analysis of the insufficient coverage regime in the following.

\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{V}_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}))}{\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\boldsymbol{V}_{\boldsymbol{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}))}
and the magnitude of the residual term
\boldsymbol{r}_{\boldsymbol{S}}
.

The estimation of the algorithmic stability coefficient hinges on Lemma
6
, which explicitly bridges the pointwise objective difference with the empirical gradient norms. The core intuition is that both empirical stationary points,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{S}}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime}}
, exhibit negligible gradient norms on the empirical objective
J_{\boldsymbol{S}}
. Consequently, this smallness in gradient norms directly translates into a tight stability coefficient via Lemma
6

Building upon the generalization error bound for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}
established in Theorem
2
, we can now proceed to bound the suboptimality gap with respect to the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumption
1
,
2
,
5
, for any
\rho\in(0,1)
, the following bound holds with probability at least
1-\rho
over the randomness of
\boldsymbol{S}
,

\displaystyle|J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}}^{*}})-J(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})|

(15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{n}+2RC\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{n}}\right)\log(n)\log\left(\frac{2}{\rho}\right)+6RC\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\rho)}{n}}

\displaystyle+3RC\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\rho)}{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

This result demonstrates that under the posterior Boltzmann policy model structure, we achieve a dimension free suboptimality gap of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, even when the training data provides limited coverage of the feature space. The constant term
\Gamma_{1}
dependence is adopted from the analysis to generalization error.

The intuitive explanation for this dimension-independent bound is that the feature patterns learned by the model are determined by the linear subspace spanned by the empirical covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{V}_{\boldsymbol{S}}
. Under the feature coverage assumption, as the sample size
n
increases, this subspace progressively expands. Consequently, the feature patterns of unseen data are effectively covered by the patterns already captured in the training set, implying that the dimensions orthogonal to the covered subspace make negligible contribution to the generalization error, thereby decoupling the suboptimality gap from the dimension

8
Concluding Discussion

In this paper, we have established an end-to-end theoretical framework for RLHF based on algorithmic stability. Departing from existing paradigms grounded in the consistency of reward parameter estimation, we prove that under a key
feature coverage
condition, the empirical optima of the policy model have a suboptimality gap of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to gradient-based algorithms, specifically Gradient Ascent and Stochastic Gradient Ascent , deriving explicit suboptimality bounds for the policies obtained by these algorithms. These results theoretically confirm that RLHF processes can learn generalizable policies solely from limited feedback, providing new theoretical evidence for the empirically observed success of RLHF.

Impact Statement

This work establishes a theoretical framework for the generalization of RLHF. Positively, our derivation of dimension-independent bounds validates that alignment can be achieved with high sample efficiency under sufficient feature coverage. This theoretical insight supports the development of more data-efficient training pipelines, potentially reducing the computational footprint of large-scale model development. However, practitioners must be cognizant of the limitations inherent in our analysis. The reliance on the linear reward assumption implies that our bounds may not fully capture the risks in scenarios involving complex, non-linear human preferences. Applying these theoretical guaranties to safety-critical domains without accounting for model misspecification could lead to an underestimation of alignment errors. Furthermore, our results on algorithmic stability suggest that the learned policy will faithfully capture the distributional properties of the covered features. Consequently, if the training data contains societal biases, the stability of the algorithm implies that these biases will be propagated to the deployed model, necessitating rigorous data auditing alongside algorithmic improvements.

Remark 6
.

Crucially, while both the optimization and generalization bounds are probabilistic, they are not independent events. The optimization bound depends on the randomness of the single parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S},T}^{\rm{SGA}}
, while the generalization bound depends on the randomness of both
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S},T}^{\rm{SGA}}
and the perturbed parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime},T}^{\rm{SGA}}
. Consequently, the event where the generalization bound holds strictly implies the satisfaction of the optimization bound.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
be any two subspaces of the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, the orthogonal complement of their intersection,
(\mathbb{U}\cap\mathbb{V})^{\perp}
, is equal to the sum of their individual orthogonal complements,
\mathbb{U}^{\perp}
and
\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
. That is:

\small\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}=(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}.

Proof.

We prove the equality by showing both inclusions. First, we show that
(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
.
Clearly we have
\mathbb{U}\subseteq\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V}
, it is straightforward to show that

(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{U}^{\perp},

similarly, we also have

(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{V}^{\perp},

combining these two conditions, we have

(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}.

On the other hand, we will show that
\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\subseteq(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}
.
Let
x
be an arbitrary vector in
\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, which means that for any
y_{1}\in\mathbb{U}
and
y_{2}\in\mathbb{V}
, we have

\langle x,y_{1}\rangle=\langle x,y_{2}\rangle=\langle x,y_{1}+y_{2}\rangle=0.

Since
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are chosen arbitrarily, we have
x\in\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V}
. Therefore, we can obtain that

\mathbb{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\subseteq(\mathbb{U}+\mathbb{V})^{\perp}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

∎

Lemma 21
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two positive semi-definite matrices, then it holds that

\displaystyle\text{N}(A+B)=\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B).

(97)

Proof.

We prove the equality by showing both inclusions. First, we show that
\text{N}(A+B)\subseteq\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B)
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}
be an arbitrary vector in
\text{N}(A+B)
, which means that
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfies

(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}=0,

then we have

\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}=0,

moreover, given that
A
and
B
are both positive semi-definite, it follows that

\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B\boldsymbol{x}=0,

it is straightforward to know that
\boldsymbol{x}
belonging to both
\text{N}(A)
and
\text{N}(B)
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B)
. Therefore, we conclude that

\text{N}(A+B)\subseteq\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B).

On the other hand, we will show that
\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B)\subseteq\text{N}(A+B)
. Similarly, for an arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B)
, we have

\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}A\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}B\boldsymbol{x}=Ax=B\boldsymbol{x}=0.

Therefore, it holds that
(A+B)\boldsymbol{x}=0
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{x}\in\text{N}(A+B)
. Therefore, we conclude that

\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B)\subseteq\text{N}(A+B).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two positive semi-definite matrices, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}(A+B)=\mathrm{C}(A)+\mathrm{C}(B).

(98)

Proof.

For the positive semi-definite matrix
A+B
, we have

\small\mathrm{C}(A+B)=\mathrm{C}((A+B)^{\top})=\text{N}(A+B)^{\perp},

where the first equality holds because the matrix

and lemma

, it holds that

\small\mathrm{C}(A+B)=\text{N}(A+B)^{\perp}=(\text{N}(A)\cap\text{N}(B))^{\perp}=\text{N}(A)^{\perp}+\text{N}(B)^{\perp}=\mathrm{C}(A^{\top})+\mathrm{C}(B^{\top})=\mathrm{C}(A)+\mathrm{C}(B),

which proves our conclusion.

.

Given a positive vector
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1

C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
can be demonstrated as

\mathrm{C}(C_{\boldsymbol{p}})=\{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}=0,\boldsymbol{v}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\}.

Proof.

Let vector
\boldsymbol{v}
be any vector falls in the null space of matrix
C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
, then we have

\small 0=C_{\boldsymbol{p}}\boldsymbol{v}=\left(\mathsf{diag}\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}-\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{v}=\mathsf{diag}\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{p},

then for any
i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, it holds that

\small 0=p_{i}v_{i}-(\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})p_{i}=p_{j}v_{j}-(\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})p_{j},

therefore, it holds that

\small v_{i}=(\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})=v_{j},

for vector
\boldsymbol{p}
is a positive vector.
It follows that the null space of matrix
C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
is
\text{N}(C_{\boldsymbol{p}})=\{\boldsymbol{v}|\boldsymbol{v}=k(1,\cdots,1)^{\top},k\in\mathbb{R}\}
. According to the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, we can further express the row space of
C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
as

\mathrm{C}(C_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\top})=\{\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}=0,\boldsymbol{v}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\}.

Furthermore, since
C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
is a symmetric matrix, its row space and column space are identical, which proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a matrix with full column rank and
C_{\boldsymbol{p}}
be a matrix satisfying Lemma
23
, where
m>n
. Then, the column spaces of matrices
AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top}
and
AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}
are identical, and both can be expressed in the form of
\{A\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}=0,\boldsymbol{v}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\}
, i.e.

\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top})=\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})=\{A\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}=0,\boldsymbol{v}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\}.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top})
is contained in
\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})
. For any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top})
, there exists a
\boldsymbol{u}
such that

\boldsymbol{y}=AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot(A^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}),

thus it is clear that we also have
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})
.

On the other hand, for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})
, we can find a
\boldsymbol{v}
such that

\boldsymbol{y}=AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}.

Since matrix
A
has full column rank, it follows that there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{u}
such that

\small A^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}.

We have thus far proven that
\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}}A^{\top})=\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})
. Furthermore, using Lemma
23
, it is straightforward to show that

\mathrm{C}(AC_{\boldsymbol{p}})=\{A\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}=0,\boldsymbol{v}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\},

which proves our conclusion.
∎
