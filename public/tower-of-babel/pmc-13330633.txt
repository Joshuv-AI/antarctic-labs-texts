From pixels to length: Body length estimation of aquatic macroinvertebrates from digital images for ecological applications☆
Authors: Anita Szloboda, Zsolt Kovács, José Barquín, Gabriel Singer, Zoltán Csabai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate measurement of macroinvertebrate body length is a key component of ecological research, providing essential data for estimating biomass, evaluating growth rates, and understanding trophic interactions in aquatic ecosystems. Yet, comprehensive datasets remain scarce, largely due to the labor-intensive and error-prone nature of traditional manual measurements – especially when processing bulk, mixed-species samples, where body sizes of individual specimens may range over orders of magnitude. This study presents a streamlined, non-destructive approach using Fiji, a free open-source image processing software, in combination with the Labkit plugin to extract body length information from digital images. Its novelty lies in enabling high-density bulk sample processing, interactive segmentation to overcome computational limitations, and taxon-specific area-length regression within a reproducible and transparent end-to-end protocol. In this workflow, body length is manually measured for a representative subset of specimens, while body area is extracted from segmented images for all. A linear regression of body length (Y) on image-derived area (X) allows reliable length estimation for specimens not measured manually. This approach improves throughput, offering an accessible and scalable solution for large-scale ecological assessment.• Semi-automated workflow using Fiji and Labkit.• Regression-based estimation of body length from image-derived area.• Fast, reproducible alternative to traditional manual measurements.

Background

Large-scale ecological monitoring projects often generate extensive collections of macroinvertebrate specimens across numerous sampling sites and events. These bulk samples are usually taxonomically diverse with various species and developmental stages covering a wide range of body sizes, which is technically understood as a linear body dimension, e.g., body length or width of a sclerotized head capsule. Accurate measurement of body size, particularly body length, is essential for estimating key ecological parameters such as biomass and secondary production. Traditionally, body length measurements were performed manually on preserved specimens using rulers, calipers, dissecting microscopes, or ocular micrometers. While these approaches are practical and well suited for small-scale studies or foundational work, they have clear limitations when applied to large-scale monitoring involving large numbers of individuals. Manual measurements are time-consuming and labor-intensive, particularly when processing large numbers of specimens [[3], [4], [5]]. In addition, extensive handling can damage fragile specimens, limiting their further use for additional analyses or long-term archiving. Furthermore, they are not easily repeatable or verifiable due to the lack of permanent digital records. To overcome the limitations and meet the increasing demand for large-scale and reproducible ecological data, more recent developments have shifted toward non-invasive, image-based, and semi-automated methods, which can help improve efficiency and consistency [3]. While different image-based and machine learning approaches exist, fully standardized workflows for bulk macroinvertebrate samples remain scarce, and many require large annotated datasets or high computational resources, limiting accessibility in routine ecological monitoring. In addition, many deep learning approaches require iterative model tuning, validation, and technical expertise, which may limit both their accessibility and reproducibility across studies and users.

In our study, we adapted a previously developed image analysis protocol based on Fiji, a distribution of ImageJ [6] to fit our specific needs. The plugin, the original workflow relied on, struggled to handle the high density of individuals, taxonomic diversity, and variable image quality typical of our bulk macroinvertebrate samples. We often faced computer crashes, memory overload issues, or inaccurate object detection, mostly due to large sample sizes - with some images containing up to 800 individual specimens in a single frame. As a solution, we integrated the Labkit plugin, as it performs segmentation (the process of distinguishing objects of interest from the background) in real-time and in an interactive way, so segmentation errors can be corrected while training without having to restart the whole process. This drastically reduced computational load, making it suitable to process complex images without memory issues on a standard laptop (8 GB RAM is typically sufficient). The ability to make manual adjustments immediately improved segmentation accuracy and significantly reduced the time spent on this workflow step. The key output of segmentation was a binary image with clearly separated and labeled individuals from which surface area could be extracted automatically. After estimating individual surface area, we manually measured body lengths for a subset of individuals to establish taxon-specific linear regressions between the measured lengths and the extracted area. This enabled us to obtain body length data automatically for many more specimens in the same or other samples.

This semi-automated workflow provides a robust basis for further ecological studies requiring large-scale body length data, as it significantly reduces processing time and effort while preserving specimen integrity. Rather than introducing a new algorithm, this study presents a fully reproducible, step-by-step workflow integrating Fiji and the Labkit plugin, specifically adapted for high density, taxonomically diverse bulk samples. The combination of interactive segmentation and taxon-specific regression-based length estimation provides a practical and scalable solution that, to our knowledge, has not been described in detail before. Advanced segmentation approaches (e.g., deep learning) exist, but they often require substantial annotated datasets and computational expertise, limiting their accessibility for routine monitoring workflows. In practice, this workflow enables body length estimation for the majority of individuals (∼80–95%) based on manual measurements of only a small subset (∼5–20% per taxon), substantially reducing overall measurement effort in large datasets. Although formal benchmarking was not performed, the workflow substantially reduces manual measurement effort and enables efficient processing of high-density samples in practice.

Digital image analysis is widely used in ecological studies, for example, to estimate plant cover [7] or to assess marine biomass [8]. However, standardized and detailed protocols for measuring the body length of macroinvertebrates - particularly from bulk samples using digital images - remain limited. Images were acquired using a standard digital camera under consistent lighting conditions and with a uniform, high-contrast background to facilitate segmentation. A reference scale was included in each image and used for spatial calibration in Fiji prior to analysis.

Image resolution should be sufficient to clearly resolve specimen boundaries; in practice, resolutions of at least ∼3000–5000 pixels along the longest image axis were found to be adequate for reliable segmentation of bulk samples. In general, each specimen should be represented by a sufficient number of pixels (typically, >50–100 pixels per individual) to ensure accurate boundary detection.

Importantly, the workflow does not require specialized imaging equipment or high-performance computing resources and can be implemented using standard laboratory setups and commonly available personal computers. The method is suitable for processing large sample sizes and includes detailed step-by-step instructions to ensure reproducibility. Although the workflow does not yet incorporate automated specimen recognition, it lays the groundwork for future approaches in taxonomically resolved, image-based community biomass estimation. Photographing

Photographing is a crucial step in this protocol, as the accuracy of the final body length dataset, as well as the entire image processing workflow is directly influenced by the quality and consistency of the captured images. Minor factors - such as background color or specimen positioning - can have a significant impact on segmentation efficiency.

This section outlines a streamlined imaging setup rethought through extensive trial and error, incorporating lessons learnt from mistakes (e.g., poor contrast, overlapping specimens). Following the steps described here can substantially reduce the need for manual corrections during image analysis and improve reproducibility across studies or sampling events.

Image segmentation and morphometric analysis using Fiji and the Labkit plugin

Accurate image analysis - and thereby precise segmentation - is fundamental for extracting reliable key morphological metrics from macroinvertebrate specimens. Although standardized, high-quality, high-contrast images are ideal for efficient processing, such optimal conditions are not always achievable in practice. In such contexts, high throughput becomes critical. This protocol combines manual and automated techniques using Fiji, and to further enhance efficiency, we integrated the Labkit plugin, which introduces intuitive, real-time segmentation. Labkit allows interactive correction on the fly - saving time on restarting the process after adjustments, decreasing computational time even for suboptimal or artifact-prone images, and performs well even on dense images. The workflow was refined to address common challenges such as overlapping specimens, low contrast, and reflections. By following the calibration, cropping, and segmentation steps described here, researchers can obtain reproducible, high-throughput results suitable for both individual-level measurements and large-scale ecological datasets.

Image Processing Workflow overview

The following steps summarize the core procedure for processing macroinvertebrate images in Fiji and extracting morphometric measurements and are intended to aid understanding of the full workflow.

Step 1: Image preparation: Calibration serves to ensure accurate area and length measurements, the pixel-to-length ratio of each image must be calibrated based on a known distance. This step is essential for converting image data into real-world units (e.g., mm or cm). Cropping removes unnecessary areas from the image (e.g., labels, unused corners) and focuses the analysis on the region of interest. During this process, ensure that relevant metadata (e.g., country, site code, date, taxon name) is included in the file name to maintain traceability.

Step 2: Image conversion and segmentation: After the region of interest has been cropped, the image must be thresholded, converted into a binary (black and white) format for analysis, which serves as the basis for segmentation - the process of isolating individual specimens. It is essential that the image does not contain specimens touching the edges or overlapping with each other, as this may result in inaccurate measurements or exclusion during processing. Such issues should be corrected prior to analysis. Depending on the specimen characteristics and image quality, we present two different segmentation methods in this section: Thresholding, which is suitable for images containing a small number of uniformly colored specimens that exhibit high contrast against the background. Segmentation (using Labkit plugin), which is recommended for more complex images, containing diverse - with multiple genera, varying colors, sizes - or abundant specimens, where manual thresholding is unreliable.

Step 3: Extracting measurements in Fiji: Once the segmented image has been exported from Labkit, and the necessary corrections are done, all necessary visual information is available to analyze particles and extract the morphometric measurements. This step generates key size and shape descriptors (e.g., area, perimeter, body length). Proper configuration of the measurement settings ensures consistency across specimens and facilitates reproducible and standardized data.

Step 4: Data export: The previous step generates three key outputs: a labeled image displaying numbered specimens, a Results table with the extracted measurements, and the ROI Manager containing all segmented outlines. All three need to be exported from Fiji.

Keep the original image file name for each row to enable easy matching with sample metadata during analysis.

Manual measurement of body length in fiji (on a subset of individuals)

While Fiji’s Analyze Particles tool provides automated estimates of length-related parameters, note that this measurement does not always reflect the true length of macroinvertebrates. For this reason, manual body length measurement remains a critical step for many groups. This section details how to accurately perform manual length measurements in Fiji, ensuring that each taxon is measured consistently based on morphological landmarks. This final step outlines how to estimate body length for specimens that were not measured manually, using taxon-specific regression models. How to use this method may depend on the research objectives and the precision required. In some studies - such as those using composite bulk samples from a single site - measuring 50–100 individuals per taxon may be sufficient to estimate average body lengths. This range was found to provide stable regression relationships (consistently high R2 values) across taxa in our empirical tests. For broader-scale studies involving multiple sampling sites, dates or countries, the same number of measurements per taxon per location / per date can provide representative estimates.

To facilitate this process even more, a full R script has been developed (see Supplementary material 2), which builds linear regression equation per taxa and calculates the body length values for the unmeasured specimens using the derived regression models - provided the models meet a minimum reliability criterion of R2 ≥ 0.75.

In general, taxon-specific regressions demonstrated strong fits (R2 ≥ 0.85 for most taxa). Some taxa of Coleoptera and Diptera exhibited slightly lower accuracy, likely due to e.g., morphological variability, but these deviations remained within acceptable limits, confirming the model’s overall reliability. Accordingly, taxa with higher morphological variability may require increased manual validation of segmentation and regression outputs compared to more morphologically uniform groups. The combination of high R2 values (typically ≥ 0.85) and agreement patterns observed in Bland-Altman analysis indicates that the regression-based estimates provide sufficient accuracy for ecological applications. The estimation errors were small relative to specimen size, with no evidence of major systematic bias, supporting the method’s suitability for reproducible, high-throughput morphometric analysis. In this context, finer taxonomic resolution may further improve model performance by reducing within-group morphological variability.

Limitations

Although the method described here was designed to enhance throughput, improve reproducibility, and expedite image analysis, it has certain limitations that need to be mentioned.

First, the lack of high-contrast image or having mixed taxa with suboptimal background, can slow down the image processing workflow and can also affect the image segmentation accuracy. These factors also limit the transferability of trained classifiers in Labkit, as classifiers may not perform consistently across images taken under varying conditions.

In addition, the regression-based estimation of body length relies on the relationship between two-dimensional surface area and one-dimensional body length. This relationship may vary depending on body shape, posture, or life stage of the specimens. Specimens that are curved or not fully flattened may introduce minor bias into length estimates. The use of taxon-specific regressions helps mitigate this issue, as regression equations reflect typical shapes and size ranges for each taxon, and specimens that could introduce bias due to posture were excluded from regression modeling. Validation was performed on the most abundant taxa, which represent a wide range of body sizes and morphologies typical of freshwater macroinvertebrate assemblages; however, performance for rare or highly specialized taxa may vary.

Second, we faced taxonomic limitations. For example, Oligochaeta were excluded from analysis due to their fragmented appearance in images, which made it impossible to extract valid area or length measurements. More generally, certain taxa, such as soft-bodied organisms, coiled larvae, or fragmented specimens, may not be reliably measured using automated image-based approaches. When necessary for dataset completeness (e.g., rare taxa), these specimens can still be measured manually from the original images.

Third, while several steps are semi-automated, manual intervention remains necessary - for example, during segmentation correction or manual length measurement. This may introduce some operator dependence but can be minimized through clearly defined criteria and consistent workflow application. In particular, overlapping, or clustered specimens may reduce segmentation accuracy, and careful manual correction is recommended to ensure reliable measurements. Although such corrections may be required in high-density samples, they are typically localized to clustered individuals and affect only a small proportion of specimens, remaining manageable without substantially limiting overall workflow efficiency.

Finally, in our research the goal was to get genus-level body length dataset, but the images were taken based on broader taxonomic groups. So, we had to include an additional and time-consuming step, the re-identification (for details see Supplementary material 3) prior to manual length measurements. To avoid such workflow inefficiencies, we recommend that, depending on the taxonomic resolution required by the study, only individuals belonging to the relevant taxonomic group be included within a single image frame during specimen photography.

These limitations should be considered when applying the workflow, particularly in relation to sample composition, imaging conditions, and taxonomic variability. With careful imaging practices and consistent protocols, the method remains robust and widely applicable.
