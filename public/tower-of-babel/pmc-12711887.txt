Damage mechanism investigation on fiber reinforced recycled cement aggregate concrete in a cold region
Authors: Wei Li, Zheng Li, Wenyuan Xu, Yongcheng Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe microscopic, mechanical, and freeze–thaw cycle experimental programs were conducted to investigate the internal damage mechanism of carbon fiber-reinforced recycled concrete. Three categories of recycled concrete specimens with different recycled concrete admixtures and reinforcement modes were designed in this study. Axial compression and freeze–thaw tests, combined with SEM and fracture surface observation and analysis, were used to study the effects of the ratio of carbon fiber reinforcement and the admixture of recycled aggregates. Research results indicate that carbon fiber reinforcement can improve recycled concrete’s mechanical performance and frost resistance. The reinforcement ratio is positively related to mechanical strength and frost resistance. Compared with the unreinforced concrete, the axial strength of semi-reinforced and fully reinforced specimens was enhanced by 25 and 42.5%, respectively. The recycled substitution rate with fully reinforced specimens has compressive strengths similar to unreinforced natural aggregate concrete, and the effect of the various recycled substitution rates was discussed. Furthermore, the damage mechanism of strength loss in recycled concrete was investigated through microstructural analysis. A uniaxial compression numerical model of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete was presented, and a modified CFRP-reinforced cement-recycled concrete was proposed to accurately determine the ontological relationship for different reinforcement ratios and recycled aggregate admixtures. A BP neural network model was developed by MATLAB to effectively predict the concrete compressive strength with different mix ratios subjected to various freeze–thaw cycles.Keywords: Recycled concrete, CFRP reinforcement, Mechanical properties, Microscopic observation, Freeze–thaw cycleSubject terms: Civil engineering, Structural materials

Introduction

With the accelerated pace of urban construction, construction and demolition waste continues to increase. The solid waste generated after construction and demolition has a very negative impact on the environment and wastes a large amount of resources. Therefore, it has become an inevitable trend to solve the environmental pollution and resource waste caused by solid waste. Concrete consists of coarse aggregate, cement, and other components, and solid waste from demolition construction does not affect the properties of concrete aggregate itself. As a result, solid waste can be recycled by replacing natural coarse aggregate in the construction of solid waste1,2.

Recycled concrete is made from recycled aggregates instead of natural aggregates by crushing, screening, and removing old construction solid wastes. Preparing recycled concrete is applicable to protect the environment and conserve resources, but it has disadvantages. Research has shown that the mechanical properties of concrete prepared with recycled aggregates decrease by about 10–25% compared to natural aggregates due to high water absorption caused by a considerable void fraction3,4. Some studies have also shown that when medium- to high-strength recycled coarse aggregates are used as raw materials, the reduction in mechanical properties is significantly reduced, the effect of recycled aggregates on the strength of concrete is small and within controllable limits compared to the mix ratio, and the strength of recycled concrete can be increased to the compressive strength of standard concrete by adjusting the water-cement ratio5–7.

Due to the poor mechanical properties of recycled concrete, external constraints are usually added to concrete to improve its strength and durability8. Carbon fiber cloth has the advantages of being light, having high tensile strength, having good mechanical properties, having high corrosion resistance, etc., and is widely used in engineering9. Studies have shown that CFRP reinforcement can improve concrete’s ductility, bearing capacity, and compressive strength, and some researchers have established a stress–strain model for fiber cloth-reinforced concrete10–13. It can be observed that the carbon fiber material can significantly improve the strength of concrete through microscopic observation. The degree of improvement is affected by the carbon fiber material’s properties, and the carbon fiber material can inhibit the generation of large cracks14,15. Some scholars have also analyzed carbon fiber-reinforced concrete’s compressive strength and damage pattern with finite element simulation16,17.

Freeze resistance of concrete in cold regions is crucial. Due to the high internal porosity of recycled aggregates, although their compressive strength is lower than that of natural aggregate concrete, they eliminate the stresses caused by water freezing and expansion and, therefore, have superior performance against freeze–thaw cycles18,19. Some scholars have also attempted to improve the frost resistance of concrete by adding fiber reinforcement and internal addition of fibers, by which concrete has been made to obtain better frost resistance and salt freezing resistance20–22.

In summary, research on the compressive strength of recycled and carbon fiber-reinforced concrete has been relatively complete. However, most of the current research has focused on incorporating different types of fibers into concrete to improve its mechanical properties and frost resistance. However, this approach is only applicable to new structures. In contrast, CFRP reinforcement systems apply to new structures and the maintenance of old ones. At the same time, more research is required to correlate recycled concrete before and after reinforcement. Different recycled aggregate replacement rates and aggregate types significantly affect the properties of the concrete specimens themselves and influence the bond pattern between the CFRP reinforcement system and the concrete, leading to changes in the ontological relationships of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete. The stress–strain relationships and microstructure of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete structures must be investigated in depth. Therefore, concrete specimens with substitution rates of 0, 50, and 100% were meticulously selected to investigate the mechanical properties of different types of recycled concrete after CFRP reinforcement in this study. The specimens, each with different admixtures, were reinforced using three types of reinforcement: unreinforced, semi-reinforced, and fully reinforced. Rigorous tests, including axial compression tests, microscopic observation analysis, and freeze–thaw cycle tests, were conducted on concrete specimens with two recycled aggregates. The results were then meticulously analyzed, focusing on the different specimens’ axial compressive strength, modulus of elasticity, stress–strain curves, damage patterns, and frost resistance. The culmination of this thorough research process is a modified constitutive model, which serves as a robust research basis for the mechanical properties and engineering applications of different types of CFRP recycled concrete.

Test methods

Mechanical experimental loading and measurement program

In order to discuss the intrinsic relationship of CFRP recycled concrete specimens, the axial compression test was conducted on the axial compression specimens according to the Standard for Test Methods of Physical and Mechanical Properties of Concrete (GBT50081-2019)25, the axial compression test consists of mechanical control and data acquisition systems. The former is responsible for loading the specimen by controlling the YAM-5000F electro-hydraulic servo-hydraulic pressure tester. The latter collects data through strain and displacement gauges, and the test equipment is shown in Fig. 2a. In order to study the axial strain and transverse strain of the axial compression specimen, longitudinal strain gauges and transverse strain gauges were pasted at 1/2 height of the axial compression specimen, and the pasting position is shown in Fig. 2b.

Microstructure observation

To investigate the mechanical properties and microstructure of two different recycled aggregate concrete specimens, their side surfaces, and internal damage surfaces were observed using a digital microscope with a magnification of 100 times, as shown in Fig. 3. The damage mechanism of axial compression specimens was studied. Firstly, the fragments of the specimens damaged by the axial compression test were selected. The surface of the specimens was rinsed and dried, and finally, the damaged surface of the axial compression specimens was observed.

Micro-observation equipment for damage section of axial compression specimen.

SEM observation

For the microscopic observation of recycled cement concrete, a scanning electron microscope (SEM), JSM-7500F, produced in Japan, was used. After 28 years of curing, the axial compression test fragments of two different types of recycled concrete specimens were carefully polished and processed to meet the specification requirements of the length of the width < 10 mm, thickness < 5 mm samples. The surface was then treated with gold for observation and analysis, as shown in Fig. 4.

JSM-7500F scanning electron microscope.

Freeze–thaw test setup

Adhering to the “GB/T 50082–2009” test specifications, we conducted the freeze–thaw cycle test using the rapid freeze–thaw method. This test, which is crucial for understanding the durability of the concrete, involved several meticulous steps. After 28 days of water maintenance, the specimen was directly subjected to the freeze–thaw test. The specimen's initial mass and the transverse fundamental frequency value were measured before the freeze–thaw. The specimen was then placed into the freeze–thaw box with water, ensuring the water level was more than 5 mm above the specimen. Each freeze–thaw cycle was controlled within 2–4 h, and the temperature was set in the interval of − 18 and 4 ℃ to ensure that the center temperature of the specimen was not higher than 7 ℃ and not lower than − 20 ℃ at any time. The specimen’s fundamental frequency and mass were measured every 25 times to ensure the accuracy of the test.

Measurement of dynamic modulus of elasticity and mass loss

The dynamic elastic modulus meter was used to measure the fundamental frequency and mass of specimens with different numbers of freeze–thaw cycles, and the dynamic elastic modulus and mass loss rate were calculated according to the formulas provided in the specification “GB/T 50082–2009”.

With the axial compression test, the surface of the specimen began to appear with a small number of small cracks. With the continuous loading, the number of small cracks continued to increase, and the length of the cracks continued to increase. On the other hand, for the semi-reinforced recycled concrete axial compression specimen, there is no apparent phenomenon in the specimen at the beginning of loading; as the loading proceeds, small cracks appear in the unreinforced part of the specimen, and the development of cracks is relatively slow. Because CFRP has a restraining effect on the recycled concrete along the length direction of the radial force, which inhibits the cracks from occurring and developing, and when the loading reaches 70% of the ultimate bearing capacity, the specimen emits continuous and slight cracks. When the load reaches 70% of the ultimate load capacity of the specimen, the specimen makes a continuous and slight “popping” sound, and part of the epoxy resin cracks. Figure 6 shows that different cement recycled aggregate substitution rates affect the compressive strength of concrete. The overall trend is that the strength of recycled concrete specimens decreases as the ratio of recycled aggregate dosage increases. The strengths of specimens with 50% dosage and 100% dosage decreased by 6 and 8.3%, respectively, compared with the concrete without cement recycled aggregate. It is mainly because the recycled cement aggregate has mortar attached to its surface, resulting in incomplete bonding of the recycled aggregate with the cement. This leads to lower strength of the specimens containing recycled aggregate. On the other hand, the overall compressive strength of the CFRP-reinforced specimens was higher than that of the unreinforced specimens. Taking 50% cement recycled aggregate as an example, compared with the unreinforced specimens, the strength of the specimens after half-reinforced treatment and full-reinforced treatment increased by 25 and 42.5%, respectively, which showed a non-linear overall increasing trend. Even for specimens with a 100% regeneration substitution rate, the compressive strength can be similar to plain concrete after complete reinforcement treatment. Therefore, the compressive strength of concrete can be significantly increased by wrapping carbon fiber cloth to offset the negative effect of adding recycled aggregate.

Stress–strain curve

The stress–strain curve is an essential index in the axial compression test of concrete. In order to comprehensively analyze the mechanical properties of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete specimens, this study investigates the stress–strain curves of different reinforcement ratios and different concrete substitution rates by axial compression test. The stress–strain relationship of the axial compression test of recycled aggregate with different replacement rates is illustrated in Fig. 7. The overall shapes of the specimen curves with different replacement rates are similar, consisting of ascending and descending segments. However, there are significant differences in peak stress and peak strain. The maximum stress decreased with the increase in recycled aggregate content, the corresponding peak strain increased, and the slope of the upward curve gradually decreased. Taking the 100% recycled aggregate substitution rate specimen as an example, the compressive strength of the semi-reinforced specimen was 23% higher than that of the unreinforced specimen, and the compressive strength of the fully reinforced specimen was 14% higher than that of the semi-reinforced specimen. The results revealed that the slope of the recycled concrete decreases with the increase in aggregate replacement rate; the decline curve becomes flatter with the increase in aggregate replacement rate, indicating that different specimens have different ductility. The main reason is that recycled aggregate’s hardness is lower than natural aggregate’s. It can be observed that the higher the reinforcement ratio compared to the recycled concrete specimens with different reinforcement ratios, the more significant the strength difference between ordinary concrete and recycled concrete. Therefore, CFRP reinforcement can increase the ultimate strain and peak strain of concrete specimens, and the larger the bond area of CFRP, the more pronounced the effect of increasing the strain and compressive strength.

Modulus of elasticity

The modulus of elasticity was calculated by taking the point where 0.4 times the ultimate compressive strength is located in the rising section of the stress–strain curve, and the cut line modulus at that point was selected as the modulus of elasticity of the recycled concrete26. Figure 8 shows that recycled concrete’s modulus of elasticity is lower than that of ordinary concrete. For unreinforced concrete, the modulus of elasticity decreases by 7.9% when the replacement rate of recycled coarse aggregate is 50% and decreases to 25.8% when the replacement rate of recycled aggregate becomes 100%. The main reason for the reduced modulus of elasticity is that the recycled coarse aggregate is poorly bonded to the cement, resulting in a lower modulus of elasticity. For unreinforced concrete, the modulus of elasticity decreased with the increase in cement recycled aggregate substitution rate in the semi-reinforced specimens, but it was not linear. In the semi-reinforced specimens, the modulus of elasticity of the concrete with 50 and 100% cement recycled aggregate was reduced by 9.3 and 27.2%, respectively, compared to that of the concrete with no recycled aggregate. In the fully reinforced specimen, the modulus of elasticity decreased to 25.8% compared to that of the concrete with natural aggregates. In the fully reinforced specimens, the modulus of elasticity of concrete with 50 and 100% recycled cement aggregates decreased by 11.2 and 25.6%, respectively, compared to the concrete with natural aggregates. It can be noted that although the modulus of elasticity decreases nonlinearly, as the reinforcement ratio increases, the rate of decrease of the modulus of elasticity increases. For the same material, the greater the strength of the concrete and the regeneration of the mixing amount of the modulus of elasticity of the concrete, the more significant the impact. When the amount of recycled cement aggregate is 0, the modulus of elasticity of CFRP semi-reinforced and fully reinforced specimens compared with ordinary concrete increased by 17.6 and 23.3%, respectively; the modulus of elasticity of concrete mixed with 50% recycled cement aggregate increased by 15.7 and 18% for the recycled concrete with 50 and 100% reinforcement ratios compared with unreinforced specimens; the modulus of elasticity of the recycled concrete specimens with all the recycled aggregate replaced by recycled concrete increased by 15.7 and 18% compared with unreinforced specimens; the modulus of elasticity of the recycled concrete specimens with all the recycled aggregate replaced by recycled concrete increased by 15.7% compared with unreinforced specimens.

It can be realized that although the elastic modulus tends to decrease with the increase of recycled aggregate content, the elastic modulus of CFRP-reinforced cemented recycled concrete specimens has a significant improvement effect. In the fully-reinforced specimens, the elastic modulus of recycled concrete, even with a 50% substitution ratio, is more vital than that of the unreinforced ordinary recycled concrete. However, it is not a linear increase; the reinforced reinforced concrete modulus is stronger than the unreinforced recycled concrete specimens. However, the reinforced concrete modulus is stronger than the unreinforced recycled concrete specimens. The overall modulus of elasticity of the reinforced recycled concrete is higher than that of the unreinforced recycled concrete, which indicates that the effect of CFRP-reinforced concrete is significant. It can offset the adverse effects of low natural hardness of cement recycled aggregate and low modulus of elasticity to a certain extent so that the strength of the CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete specimens can be ensured.

Microstructural analysis

Micro-observation of damage surfaces

In order to realize further research on the damage of recycled cement aggregate and carbon fiber cloth at the time of damage, the damage mechanism of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete was realized by observing the specimens and their falling fragments after uniaxial compression damage using a digital microscope. The observation of the concrete damage cross section and the carbon fiber cloth damage was conducted by a digital microscope with a magnification of 100 times. It can be observed that the axial compression damage of concrete containing cemented recycled aggregate produces cracks through the aggregate (Fig. 9a), thus leading to the destruction of the recycled aggregate itself. Moreover, the sealing and bonding of the mortar to the recycled aggregate in the transition zone of the concrete interface deteriorated, and cracks were generated through the transition zone of the recycled aggregate and the mortar (Fig. 9b). By observing the recycled concrete after CFRP reinforcement, it can be found that the carbon fiber cloth used for reinforcement is broken along the length direction (Fig. 9c), which indicates that the CFRP reinforcement system gives full play to the mechanical properties of the carbon fiber material itself and effectively improves the axial compressive strength of recycled concrete. On the other hand, it was observed that the epoxy resin adhesive bonding CFRP and recycled concrete on the side was pulled off during axial compression. The epoxy resin coating on the upper and lower surfaces of the recycled concrete specimens also cracked in the compression process (Fig. 9d), meaning that the epoxy resin adhesive was subjected to the load during axial compression. It was only destroyed once the material’s compressive strength was fully utilized. At the same time, the recycled concrete specimen cement base appeared to have cracks (Fig. 9e). Because the recycled aggregate itself adheres to the old mortar, and the new cement base is not sufficiently combined. The recycled aggregate cannot effectively share the stress generated in the destruction process, and the new cement base has poor strength and cannot effectively bear the stress, which produces penetration cracks. The strength of CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete is improved compared with that of recycled concrete. However, the difference between the center damage zone and the damage of ordinary concrete is that recycled concrete usually occurs first in the recycled aggregate itself and then develops to the transition zone of the mortar interface.

Constitutive model analysis

The equation of the concrete stress–strain curve under compression is the principal relationship. In order to express the stress–strain principal relationship of concrete, the commonly used Guo Zhenhai’s model is used to dimensionlessize the stress–strain curve of carbon fiber cloth-reinforced recycled aggregate concrete in axial compression by taking ε/ε0 (ε0 is the peak strain) as x and σ/f′c (stress to peak stress ratio) as y. In this study, the method of principal equation proposed by Guo Zhenhai et al. was selected to provide theoretical support. As mentioned in the study, considering the poor homogeneity of concrete, the dispersion of curves in the same group of specimens is large, so it is not practical to select too many curves. Therefore, in this study, the average values of a and b of four specimens in each group were selected as the parameters of the rising and falling sections of the group. The a and b are the parameters of the rising and falling sections of the different stress–strain curves, respectively. The curves vary according to the material properties controlled by the two parameters. Therefore, by varying these two parameters, the stress–strain curves of different concretes can be expressed more accurately. Meanwhile, to investigate the effect of recycled aggregate admixture and CFRP reinforcement ratio on the constitutive relationship, different aggregate substitutions r and θ and fiber cloth reinforcement ratios θ are proposed based on the parameters of the rising and falling sections (a and b). The parameters a and b of the rising and falling segments were determined by the aggregate replacement ratio r (0 ≤ r ≤ 1) and the carbon fiber reinforcement ratio θ (0 ≤ θ ≤ 1). These two parameters control the shape of the stress–strain curves in different segments, respectively. Through nonlinear fitting, it was found that the parameters a and b varied with the replacement ratio r of recycled aggregate and carbon fiber reinforcement ratio θ in the form of nonlinear surfaces, as shown in Fig. 11. Therefore, the parameters a and b in the aggregate replacement rate r and carbon fiber reinforcement ratio θ under the joint influence were analyzed using cubic function fitting. Through statistical regression analysis, the modified constitutive model of carbon fiber-reinforced recycled cement-aggregate concrete was obtained, in which the replacement rate of recycled aggregate was r and the carbon fiber cloth reinforcement ratio was θ, and R2 were close to 1, with a better fitting result. Therefore, the constitutive model proposed in this paper could accurately calculate the stress–strain data of carbon fiber cloth-reinforced cement-recycled concrete with specific types of recycled aggregates under actual stresses in different mixing ranges.

The parameters a (1.36 ≤ a ≤ 1.77) and b (3.67 ≤ b ≤ 4.5) of the ascending curve and the descending curve, respectively, control the shapes of the stress–strain curves under the simultaneous action of different cement regeneration aggregate dosages and CFRP reinforcement ratios. The slope decreases with the increase of the regeneration aggregate dosage, which is because the regeneration aggregate is not as suitable as the combination of the natural aggregate with the cement base, and there is a larger compressed space in the damage, and the strain is significant. With the increase of the regeneration aggregate dosage, the curve of the descending section b has a rising trend, and with the corresponding increase in recycled aggregate dosage, the descending curve b tends to increase. In contrast, the corresponding descending stress–strain curve value is smaller. In the same interval, the curve with more recycled aggregate dosage decreases more gently. The higher the dosage of recycled aggregate, the lower the stress of the concrete under the same normalized strain, and the slope of the decline is higher than that of the natural aggregate concrete, which is related to the low strength of recycled cement aggregate itself.

The effect of the fiber cloth reinforcement ratio on stress–strain was also observed, and specimens with the same recycled aggregate substitution rate were compared. It can be appreciated by fitting the equation that the effect of reinforcement ratio θ on the shape of the curve of the rising section is less pronounced than that of the other variable as the CFRP reinforcement ratio is increased. It can be observed that the difference between the shapes of the rising sections of the concrete specimens at the same 50% admixture is insignificant, and only the curve of the fully reinforced specimen is slightly flattened for a small section up to the peak strain. Correspondingly, the falling segment parameter b has the same pattern. In the case of the same amount of recycled aggregate, the falling section parameter b increases as the reinforcement ratio increases. However, the overall stress value of the falling section of the constitutive curve becomes lower, and the curve becomes smooth. However, the overall effect is less dramatic than the recycled aggregate substitution rate (r). Therefore, compared with the reinforcement ratio θ, the recycled aggregate substitution rate r has more influence on the stress–strain curve. The upward and downward curves are gentler, with the recycled aggregate substitution rate increasing under the same reinforcement ratio.

Quality loss rate analysis

The mass loss rates for three different reinforcement ratios at 0–100 freeze–thaw cycles are shown in Fig. 12. The mass loss rate increases gradually but not linearly as the number of freeze–thaw cycles increases. It can also be observed that the mass loss rate under the same number of freeze–thaw cycles increases gradually with the increase in recycled aggregate dosage. At 25 freeze–thaw cycles, the spalled mass of the specimen is less than the mass of the specimen absorbing water, so the mass increases with the increase of recycled aggregate. However, the increase in mass is not apparent due to the less erosion of the fully reinforced specimen. The rate of mass loss was especially pronounced after 50 freeze–thaw cycles. After 100 freeze–thaw cycles, the mass loss rates for unreinforced specimens with the three recycled aggregate substitution rates were 0.6, 0.9, and 1.4%, which were close to a linearly increasing law. Compared with recycled aggregate, the reinforcement ratio has a decisive effect on the mass loss of specimens in freeze–thaw cycles. The mass loss rates of semi-reinforced specimens under 100 freeze–thaw cycles are 0.5, 0.8, and 1%, respectively, which suggests that the semi-reinforcement has a specific protective effect on freeze–thaw cycles and can effectively prevent part of the concrete from spalling. Meanwhile, it can be observed that the mass loss rate of the specimens, even with a 100% recycled aggregate substitution rate, does not exceed 0.9% under fully reinforced conditions. With the increase in freeze–thaw cycles, the surface of the concrete specimens spalled continuously, and even the unreinforced specimens would expose the coarse aggregate. However, the appearance of the fully reinforced specimens did not change significantly; the concrete itself was well preserved, and the carbon fiber cloth was not damaged. All specimens have less than 5% loss of quality, which aligns with the specification requirements for frost resistance.

It was observed that there was an inverse relationship between the recycled aggregate replacement rate and the dynamic elastic modulus. After 100 freeze–thaw cycles, the dynamic elastic modulus of the specimens without recycled aggregate decreased by 15%, and the specimens with a 50% recycled aggregate replacement rate decreased by 7%. The specimens with a 100% recycled aggregate replacement rate decreased by 4% compared to the natural aggregate concrete. Through microscopic observation, there are a small number of closed pores inside the recycled aggregate, which play a role similar to that of an air-entraining agent in the freeze–thaw cycle process. The presence of a small amount of air can reduce the stress caused by the concrete in the process of freezing and expanding, which can slow down the expansion of cracks, reduce the damage caused by freezing and thawing, and reduce the reduction of the dynamic elasticity modulus. The dynamic modulus of elasticity of all the above specimens is more than 60%, which aligns with the specification requirements for frost resistance.

Modeling of BP neural network structure

Determination of the number of hidden layers

The BP neural network consists of three parts: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. The hidden layer, as an essential link connecting the input and output layers, directly affects the change in neural network performance. Although the increase of hidden layers can increase the computational accuracy, it also costs more network training time, complicates the structure, and even causes the model overfitting phenomenon, ultimately leading to a reduction in the scope of application of the model. It has been shown that when the input and output layers use linear functions, and the hidden layer uses the Sigmod function, the neural network with one hidden layer can arbitrarily approximate the rational function and adopt a hidden layer structure.

Determination of the number of neurons in the hidden layer

The number of neurons in the hidden layer is a critical factor that significantly influences neural network prediction results. If this number is tiny, it can lead to an extensive training error and imprecise calculations. Conversely, many neurons can reduce the error. However, it also leads to longer training times, overfitting, and the risk of getting stuck in a local optimum, ultimately preventing the network from reaching the optimal solution.

Different network models are established by changing the number of neurons in the hidden layer with other parameters unchanged, and the training samples are brought into the neural network for training by taking the mean square error, training set, and test set variance as the indexes. The number of neurons in the hidden layer is finally determined to be 4, and the neural network topology is finally determined to be 4-4-1. The mean square error is 0.014, and the structure of the network and the mean square error are shown in Fig. 15.

BP neural network structure.

Other parameter determinations

The learning rate is a parameter used to control the learning speed in adjusting the weights and thresholds, i.e., the amount of change in the weights and thresholds produced by each training cycle. The learning rate is too large to make the network converge too quickly at the beginning, but fluctuations in the near-optimal solution, which may lead to a non-convergence state; the learning rate is too small to lead to too long a time from the calculation to the convergence process, but it can ensure that the network error value tends to be minimized. The learning rate interval is empirical, generally at [0.01, 0.1]. It found that the learning rate of 0.01 when the training results are optimal, and finally determined, the transfer function is set to the Sigmod function, the number of iterations (epochs) is 1000, and the error threshold (goal) is 1 × e−6. The learning rate (lr) is 0.01.

BP neural network training and result analysis

Training data is disrupted and re-divided into two groups, of which 70% are training samples and 30% are test samples. The two groups are independent of each other without overlapping data, using training samples to determine the nonlinear relationship between the input and output values of the network and test samples to verify the network's ability to generalize the application. The training is terminated when the test sample error does not appear to decrease while the number of training increases, utilizing a strategy similar to early termination to prevent the network from overfitting.

The number of input nodes in this study was determined to be 4, i.e., recycled concrete aggregate type (1 and 2 represent two different recycled aggregate types), recycled aggregate admixture, reinforcement ratio, and several freeze–thaw cycles, and the output layer was one node, i.e., concrete compressive strength. The number of neurons in the hidden layer is finalized as 4 through training and comparison.

The training set of data R2 is 0.9833, and the test set of data R2 is 0.94385; the network through the linear fitting of the fitted straight line presents a 45° angle, the fitting effect is good, which indicates that this paper through the BP neural network training to obtain the value and the actual value is generally consistent with the training effect is good. Figures 17, 18 show that the prediction results significantly correlate with the actual values measured by the test. The error of the prediction results is independent of the input variables; the average absolute error value is 0.8 MPa, and the average relative error is 2.6%. The results show that the error is small, which indicates that this model can effectively predict the mechanical properties of concrete after freeze–thaw cycles.

Conclusion

In order to study the mechanical properties and frost resistance of CFRP-reinforced cement recycled concrete, this paper adopts a variety of means, such as mechanical experiments, microscopic observation, and freeze–thaw tests, to analyze CFRP-reinforced recycled concrete and study the axial compressive strength, modulus of elasticity, and intrinsic relationship of the recycled concrete, as well as the analysis of the mechanical properties of the recycled concrete after freezing and thawing, in addition to microscopic observation of the recycled concrete. The conclusions were obtained as follows:

(1) Compared with ordinary concrete, the axial compressive strength of recycled concrete specimens with 50 and 100% substitution decreased by 6 and 8.3%; compared with unreinforced concrete, the axial strength of semi-reinforced and fully-reinforced specimens increased by 25 and 42.5%, and the specimens with 100% regeneration substitution had compressive strengths similar to the strengths of unreinforced concrete with natural aggregates after the full-reinforcement treatment.
