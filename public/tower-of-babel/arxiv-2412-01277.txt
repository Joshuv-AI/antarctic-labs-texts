Streamlining the Action Dependency Graph Framework: Two Key Enhancements
Joachim Dunkel

Abstract
. Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) is critical for coordinating multiple robots in shared environments, yet robust execution of generated plans remains challenging due to operational uncertainties. The Action Dependency Graph (ADG) framework offers a way to ensure correct action execution by establishing precedence-based dependencies between wait and move actions retrieved from a MAPF planning result. The original construction algorithm is not only inefficient, with a quadratic worst-case time complexity it also results in a network with many redundant dependencies between actions. This paper introduces two key improvements to the ADG framework. First, we prove that wait actions are generally redundant and show that removing them can lead to faster overall plan execution on real robot systems. Second, we propose an optimized ADG construction algorithm, termed Sparse Candidate Partitioning (SCP) , which skips unnecessary dependencies and lowers the time complexity to quasi-linear, thereby significantly improving construction speed.



1.
Introduction

MAPF
is a well-known problem in artificial intelligence, where the goal is to find collision-free paths for multiple agents, typically robots, operating in shared environments
(
Stern et al. [n.d.]a
)
. In real-world applications such as warehouse automation, the complexity of
MAPF
increases due to dynamic factors like varying robot speeds, unforeseen obstacles, and continuous task generation. While state-of-the-art
MAPF
solvers can generate feasible plans for large robot fleets in relatively short times, robust execution on physical robots—subject to kinematic and dynamic constraints—remains challenging due to operational uncertainties.

In recent years, three key approaches have been developed to address these challenges: (1) Integrate operation uncertainty into the planning process:
This includes methods like
(
Zhang et al. [n.d.]
)
,
(
Atzmon et al. [n.d.]
)
or
(
Chen et al. [n.d.]
)
, where the goal is to find
k
-robust plans (where each agents operational delay will not cause conflicts for
k
time steps). (2) Predictive control of agents during execution: In this category fall works such as
(
Gregoire et al. [n.d.]
)
and
(
Wen et al. [n.d.]
)
. (3) Post-process a plan into a time independent execution policy: These include works such as
(
Hoenig et al. [n.d.]b
)
and the
Action Dependency Graph (ADG)
framework, proposed by Hönig et. al
(
Hoenig et al. [n.d.]a
)
. Due to its simplicity and low communication overhead the
ADG
-framework has become a commonly used approach, that has since been adopted in different works such as
(
Varambally et al. [n.d.]
)
,
(
Berndt et al. [n.d.]a
)
,
(
Berndt et al. [n.d.]b
)
and
(
Liu et al. [n.d.]
)
.

The
ADG
ensures robust execution of
MAPF
-plans by establishing dependencies between actions based on their precedence relations. However, the original
ADG
construction algorithm exhaustively checks all actions for potential conflicts, leading to a worst-case time complexity of
O(n^{2})
, where
n
is the number of actions.
Furthermore, this process results in a graph with many redundant dependencies between actions, leading to unnecessary communication and memory overhead.

This paper introduces two key improvements to the
ADG
framework. First, we prove that wait actions are redundant and can be removed without affecting execution correctness. Furthermore, we show that removing them can lead to faster overall plan execution (makespan) in scenarios where consecutive motion is expected to be faster than stop-and-go behavior.
Second, we propose an optimized
ADG
construction algorithm termed
Sparse Candidate Partitioning (SCP)
that efficiently skips unnecessary dependencies resulting in a significantly faster construction process and a sparser graph without any redundant dependencies.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has specifically focused on improving the runtime of
ADG
construction itself. While in
(
Berndt et al. [n.d.]b
)
, the authors discuss the conversion of an
ADG
into a
Spatially Exclusive Action Dependency Graph (SE-ADG)
that does not contain wait actions, they do not directly drop them nor prove that they are unnecessary.

Although solving the
MAPF
-problem optimally is NP-complete
(
Yu and LaValle [n.d.]
)
, making it the primary computational bottleneck in
MAPF
-based systems, our improved
ADG
construction process still provides meaningful performance gains, especially in systems utilizing fast planning schemes such as
Prioritized Planning (PP)

(
Erdmann and Lozano-Pérez [n.d.]
)
. In lifelong
MAPF
, where tasks arrive continuously,
ADG
construction and execution must be performed repeatedly. As a result, the performance improvements we introduce become even more significant.

2.
Problem Description

In this section, we describe the
ADG
framework and introduce key concepts and definitions used throughout this paper. Further we will discuss the original
ADG
construction algorithm and its limitations.

The construction of an
Action Dependency Graph (ADG)
– further referred to as
G_{ADG}
– begins once a valid
MAPF
solution
\Pi
is found. A solution
\Pi
is a set of
k
single-agent plans
\pi_{i}
for each agent (robot)
R_{i}
. A single agent plan
pi_{i}
represents a sequence of actions
(a_{0},...,a_{n})
that lead
R_{i}
from its start to its goal vertex.

Definition 0.

Given a valid
MAPF
-Solution
\Pi
, let
A
be the set of all actions
\{a_{0},...,a_{n}\}
. An action
a\in A
is a four-tuple
\langle s,g,t,R\rangle
with:

•

s
: the start vertex of action
a
,

•

g
: the goal vertex of action
a
,

•

t
: the time step at which action
a
occurs,

•

R
: the agent (robot) performing action
a
.

Note:
While an
ADG
consists of action vertices with additional status information {completed, enqueued, pending}, this is not relevant for the purpose of this paper. For simplicity, we will use the terms "action vertex" and "action" interchangeably.

Definition 0.

If
\Pi
is a valid
MAPF
-Solution , there are no two actions
a
and
b
that start or end at the same vertex
v
and time step
t
. More formally: for all
a,b\in A,\;\text{if }a.t=b.t:\{a.s\neq b.s\lor a.g\neq b.g\}
must hold. (Otherwise,
\Pi
would contain a collision.)

Given a valid
MAPF
-Solution
\Pi
, all its actions
a_{i}\in A
are then used to construct the
ADG
.

2.1.
Action Dependency Graph Construction

The graph
G_{\text{ADG}}
is a directed acyclic graph representing the temporal dependencies between actions in a
MAPF
-Solution
\Pi
. Actions
a\in A
represent the vertices of the graph, and dependencies between actions are represented as directed edges.
If e.g. there is an edge from action
a
to
b
, this means that action
b
depends on action
a
and can only be executed after
a
has been completed.

Note
that a direct dependency between two actions
a
and
b
is denoted as
a\to b
, meaning
b
depends on
a
. An indirect dependency, such as
a\to c\to b
, is denoted as
a\rightsquigarrow b
. Since
(a\to b)
implies
(a\rightsquigarrow b)
we will consider a direct dependency
(a\to b)
as a special case of an indirect dependency
(a\rightsquigarrow b)
.

Definition 0.

Every consecutive action pair
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
for the same agent
R_{a}
is connected by a type1 dependency. Formally: for all
a,b\in A
, if
a.R=b.R\land a.t=b.t-1
then
a\to b
.

The original ADG construction algorithm works as follows:

(1)

Connect all consecutive actions of the same shuttle with
type1
dependencies.

(2)

Create all
type2
dependencies by exhaustively checking for potential conflicts between actions of different agents. This process is depicted in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm

exhaustively checks all actions against each other and therefore has a time complexity of

, where

Note
that Algorithm

is a simplified version of the original algorithm adapted to our notation. Specifically, the original algorithm discussed in
(

)
assumes actions are sorted per agent, and thus avoids iterating over other actions of the same agent, saving some iterations. However, the original algorithm still has a quadratic time-complexity, and therefore performs similarly to our simplified version.

3.
Approach

The contribution of this paper is two-fold: First, we prove that wait actions are redundant and can be removed from
G_{\text{ADG}}
. Second, we provide a more efficient algorithm for Action Dependency Construction and prove its correctness.

3.1.
Wait Actions are redundant

In this section we will prove that wait actions (these are actions where start and goal vertex are the same) are redundant, because they do not contribute any unique dependency to
G_{\text{ADG}}
and can safely be ignored during the construction process.

Proposition 0.

For every action
b\in A
, if
b.t>1
then there exists a
previous
action
a\in A
with
b.s=a.g\land b.t=a.t+1\land b.R=a.R

Definition 0.

For two given actions
a
and
b
,
b
is considered
reachable
from
a
if there exists a direct dependency
a\to b
or an indirect dependency
a\rightsquigarrow b
.

Definition 0.

A dependency
a\to b
is called
redundant
if removing it does not change the action precedence order. More formally
a\to b
is
redundant
if
b
still remains
reachable
from
a
after dependency
a\to b
was removed from
G_{\text{ADG}}
.

To prove that wait actions are generally
redundant
we will show that they do not contribute to any unique dependency in
G_{\text{ADG}}
. First we will show that any outgoing dependency from a wait action is
redundant
.

Theorem 4.

Suppose agent
R
has a wait action
w
, and another agent
R^{\prime}
has an action
a^{\prime}
that depends on
w
(thus
a^{\prime}.t\geq w.t\land a^{\prime}.g=w.s
) then
a^{\prime}
also depends on the next move action
m
after
w
.

Proof.

•

Since
a^{\prime}
depends on
w
,
a^{\prime}.t>w.t
must hold because
a^{\prime}.t=w.t
would imply that
a^{\prime}
and
w
have the same goal and time step, which contradicts Definition
2
.

•

Since wait action
w
and its subsequent next action
a
have the same start vertex,
a^{\prime}
must also depend on
a
because
a^{\prime}.t\geq a.t\land a^{\prime}.g=a.s
.

•

Since
a^{\prime}
depends on both
w
and
a
, and
w
precedes
a
via a
type1
dependency (Definition
3
), the dependency
w\rightsquigarrow a^{\prime}
exists implicitly through
w\to a\to a^{\prime}
.

•

If the next action
a
after
w
is a wait action this argument can be repeated until the next move action
m
is reached.

•

Hence, the direct dependency from
w
to
a^{\prime}
is
redundant
.

∎

Further we will show that any dependency from another action to a wait action is also
redundant
.

Theorem 5.

Suppose agent
R
has a wait action
w
, and agent
R^{\prime}
has an action
a^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}\to w.t
, then
w
’s
previous
action
a
for agent
R
also depends on
a^{\prime}
.

Proof.

•

Since
w
depends on
a^{\prime}
, it follows that
a^{\prime}.t<w.t
, otherwise
a^{\prime}
and
w
would have the same goal and time step, which contradicts with Definition
2
.

•

Since
w.g=a.g
and
w
depends on
a^{\prime}
then
a
must also depend on
a^{\prime}
because
a^{\prime}.t\leq a.t
and
a^{\prime}.s=a.g
.

•

Since
a
depends on
a^{\prime}
and
a
precedes
w
via a
type1
dependency (Definition
3
), the dependency
a^{\prime}\to w
exists implicitly through
a^{\prime}\to a\to w
.

•

Hence, the direct dependency from
a^{\prime}
to
w
is redundant.

∎

Wait actions are redundant in the ADG framework

Given Theorem
4
, any
type2
dependency from a wait action
w
to another agent action
b
is redundant and given Theorem
5
, any
type2
dependency from another agent action
b
to a wait action
w
is redundant.
Thus, wait actions do not add any unique dependencies and can be safely removed from
G_{\text{ADG}}
without altering the overall action execution flow. Not only are wait actions redundant, removing them improves the
ADG
framework in the following ways:

(1)

Improved construction efficiency
: Fewer actions and dependencies need to be added, accelerating the overall process.

(2)

Sparser graph
: This reduces communication overhead during execution, making the system more efficient.

(3)

Increased system throughput
: If robot
R_{a}
is waiting for robot
R_{b}
to finish its action,
R_{a}
can begin its next task immediately once
R_{b}
completes, rather than waiting for the duration of a scheduled wait action. This is particularly beneficial when
R_{b}
finishes earlier than planned, allowing
R_{a}
to start its next action sooner than if wait actions were used.

3.2.
Improved ADG-Construction via Candidate Partitioning

After proving that wait actions are redundant and can be safely ignored in the
ADG
construction process, we now turn our focus to further optimizing the construction algorithm itself. Instead of exhaustively checking all actions against each other, for each action
a_{i}\in A
, only looking at other actions
a_{j}
where
a_{j}.s=a_{i}.g
is sufficient.
These actions are further denoted as candidate actions
C
.

Definition 0.

For any action
a\in A
its candidate list
C[c_{0},...,c_{n}]
represents all actions where a type2 dependency
c_{i}\to a
is possible.

Based on this insight, it is easy to create an improved
ADG
-construction algorithm. This works as follows:

(1)

Remove all wait actions from
A
.

(2)

Connect all consecutive actions of the same shuttle with
type1
dependencies.

(3)

Create a
candidate action lookup

(S)
:

S
is a mapping from a vertex
v
to a list of candidate actions
C
that have
v
as their start vertex.
Note
that any efficient key-value lookup data structure can be used for this purpose.

(4)

For each action
a_{i}\in A
only consider the candidate actions
c_{i}
from
S[a_{i}.g]
and add a dependency
c_{i}\to a_{i}
if
c_{i}.R\neq a_{i}.R\land c_{i}.t<a_{i}.t
holds.

This process is depicted in Algorithm
2
.

Partitioning actions based on their start vertices significantly reduces the number of comparisons needed to create
type2
dependencies in practice. While the worst-case asymptotic complexity remains quadratic at
O(n^{2})
, the average runtime is reduced to
O(n^{2}/x)
, where
x
is the number of distinct start vertices in the graph. This reduction occurs because all actions in a
MAPF
solution are typically evenly spread across the grid, resulting in a smaller number of candidate actions for each action.

3.3.
Skipping Redundant Dependencies with Sparse Candidate Parititioning (SCP)

Having established an improved
ADG
construction algorithm based on candidate partitioning, we now turn to further optimizing the process by skipping redundant dependencies.
SCP
leverages the insight that many dependencies between an action
a
and its candidate actions
c_{i}\in C
are redundant. Specifically, it is sufficient to only add a dependency from the latest (different robot) candidate action, as defined in Theorem
7
.

Theorem 7.

For a given action
a\in A
and its candidate list
C[c_{0},..,c_{n}]
, only adding a dependency
c_{k}\to a
, with
c_{k}
being the latest action in
C
with
c_{k}.t\leq a_{i}.t\land c_{k}.R\neq a_{i}.R
, still maintains an indirect dependency
c_{i}\rightsquigarrow a
for all
c_{i}\in C
, and thus is sufficient in maintaining execution correctness in the
ADG
framework.

Based on Theorem

.

In (1-3) a candidate action lookup

is sorted by time in ascending order. In (6-13)

3.3.2.
Proving Theorem
7

Corollary 0.

As defined in Definition
2
, Theorem
7
implies that for each action
a_{i}\in A
,
a_{i}
is
reachable
from all of its candidate actions
c_{i}\in C
with
{c_{i}.t\leq a_{i}.t}
.

Therefore, to prove Theorem
7
we will show that every candidate action
c_{i}\in C
is
reachable
from
a
. In other words: we will prove that for every
c_{i}\in C
there is an indirect dependency
c_{i}\rightsquigarrow a
. To prove this it is sufficient to show that there is a dependency between every two consecutive candidate actions
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}\in C
.

Proof.

•

Given by Definition
2
, it is obvious that no two consecutive candidate actions
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}
can have the same time step and thus:
c_{i}.t<c_{i+1}.t

•

Given by Proposition
1
, for
c_{i+1}
there is a
previous
action
b_{i+1}
with
b_{i+1}.t=c_{i+1}.t-1
and thus:
c_{i}.t\leq b_{i+1}.t
.

•

To show that
c_{i}\rightsquigarrow c_{i+1}
it is sufficient to show that
c_{i}\to b_{i+1}
holds.

•

When
SCP
eventually iterates over
b_{i+1}
and a dependency is added between
b_{i+1}
and its last candidate action
c_{k}
, it is therefore necessary that
c_{k}=c_{i}
to ensure that
c_{i}\to b_{i+1}
holds.

•

Since
b_{i+1}.g=a.g
they also share the same candidate set containing
c_{i}
.

•

Since no two actions in
A
can share the same start and time step, it is guaranteed that
c_{i}
is the latest candidate action in
C
with
c_{i}.t\leq b_{i+1}.t
. Therefore, applying the algorithm to action
b_{i+1}
creates a dependency
c_{i}\to b_{i+1}
.

•

Thus, applying the algorithm to every action in
A
will create dependencies between every two consecutive candidate actions
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}\in C
.

∎

3.3.3.
Runtime Analysis of
SCP

The runtime complexity of the
SCP
algorithm is derived from two key operations:

(1)

Sorting of Candidate Lists: First a candidate list
C
is constructed for every distinct action start vertex
x
. In practice the number of actions in each candidate list is limited. Asymptotically this step has a runtime of
O(n\log(n))
where
n
is the total number of actions.

(2)

Binary Search and Dependency Creation: For each action
a_{i}
, our algorithm performs a binary search on the sorted candidate list to find the latest relevant candidate action. This search operation is
O(\log(n))
for each action. Given that this is done for every action in
A
, the overall complexity for this step is
O(n\log(n))
.

Overall, the total runtime complexity of the
SCP
algorithm is
O(n\log(n))
, making it significantly more efficient than the original exhaustive construction algorithm which has a complexity of
O(n^{2})
.

4.
Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we evaluate two key aspects of our contributions: first, we compare the proposed
ADG
construction algorithms, and second, the impact of removing wait actions on overall execution performance. The following experiments compare the efficiency of our newly developed
ADG
construction algorithms and demonstrate how omitting wait actions reduces the system’s makespan by better taking advantage of faster-then planned action execution.

To evaluate our proposed improvements, we selected the
MAPF
benchmark introduced by Tan et al., which includes six representative maps from the official
MAPF
benchmark suite
(
Stern et al. [n.d.]b
)
. Each map contains 125 unique scenarios, equally distributed across five different agent counts. Specifically, we utilize the authors’ pre-computed
MAPF
solutions. For further details we refer the reader to
(
Tan et al. [n.d.]
)
.

The evaluation and experimental code used in this study is publicly available at
(
Dunkel 2024
)
.

4.2.
Influence of Wait Actions on Execution Performance

In Section
3.1
, we proved that wait actions are redundant and can be removed from the
ADG
without altering the overall action execution flow. In addition to a more streamlined graph, a slightly faster construction process, and reduced agent communication overhead, the removal of wait actions also influences overall execution performance.
To demonstrate this impact, we conducted a series of experiments. Given our chosen benchmark, we selected two maps (Paris, warehouse) to evaluate the
ADG
execution duration on all their given pre-computed
MAPF
-solutions. These solutions were used to construct
G_{\text{ADG}}
.
Note
that some pre-computed solutions from the benchmark led to cycles within the
ADG
due to the planner not accounting for
ADG
constraints and where thus excluded. Then during execution, each agent communicates its actions within the
ADG
framework until they eventually run out of actions.

In real-world scenarios, the time required for an agent (robot) to execute an action (e.g. moving between two adjacent cells) is not constant but depends on the robot’s internal kinematic constraints. One key advantage of the
ADG
framework is that it allows robots to perform continuous motion across multiple consecutive move actions, thereby avoiding unnecessary stop-and-go behavior. When a robot can move directly through multiple locations, it reaches its goal more quickly.
To model plan execution within the
ADG
framework, we developed a discrete-event simulation where agents perform actions and, upon completion, advance the simulation time. If the action was a wait action or a single move action, the time advances by 1 second. However, if there is another move action immediately following in the shuttle’s queue, the current move action will only take 0.8 seconds, reflecting the faster consecutive movement compared to the typical stop-and-go behavior.

Figure
4
shows the results of these experiments. The makespan when wait actions are included (blue) is generally higher than when they are omitted (green). This difference in makespan likely results from situations where agents complete their actions faster than planned due to consecutive motion. In such cases, subsequent agents can begin their actions sooner, leading to a cascading effect that reduces the overall makespan. In contrast, when wait actions are used, agents are restricted to their predetermined timing, even when a preceding agent finishes early, which limits these potential optimizations.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced two key improvements to the
Action Dependency Graph (ADG)
framework: the removal of redundant wait actions and an optimized construction algorithm. Our experiments demonstrate that omitting wait actions consistently improves overall makespan by taking advantage of faster-than-planned consecutive motion.
Further, the proposed
Sparse Candidate Partitioning (SCP)
algorithm significantly reduces the runtime of the
ADG
construction process, with a practical and theoretical complexity of
O(n\log(n))
.

SCP
also generates a graph with at most one
type2
dependency per action, resulting in reduced memory overhead and minimized communication requirements. We believe these improvements not only enhance the scalability of the
Action Dependency Graph (ADG)
framework but also make it a more practical solution for adoption in systems handling larger agent sets in the future.
