Quality of life after diet liberalization in individuals with phenylketonuria treated with Pegvaliase
Authors: Chiara Cazzorla, Giacomo Gaiga, Jessica Carretta, Vincenza Gragnaniello, Christian Loro, Daniela Gueraldi, Andrea Dorigato, Alessandro P. Burlina, Alberto B. Burlina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the impact of pegvaliase treatment on quality of life and individuals' perception of and satisfaction with therapy in Italian individuals with classical phenylketonuria (PKU) after diet liberalization.MethodsThis longitudinal observational study included individuals with classical PKU and persistently poor dietary adherence who initiated treatment with pegvaliase and achieved diet liberalization. Blood Phe levels were measured at baseline and throughout treatment for up to 12 months following diet liberalization. Quality of life was assessed using the Phenylketonuria–Quality of Life (PKU-QOL) questionnaire at three time points (before treatment, and 6 and 12 months after diet liberalization); a qualitative satisfaction survey was also administered.ResultsSeven participants (1 male, 6 females; mean age 27.7 years) achieved diet liberalization within a mean of 61 weeks after maintenance period, with a mean reduction in blood Phe levels of 79.7%. At 6 and 12 months after diet liberalization, PKU-QOL scores showed a consistent decrease, indicating improved quality of life, particularly in the “PKU in general” subscale, assessing the emotional, practical, and social impact of PKU. Patient-reported outcomes highlighted improvements in energy levels, sleep quality, emotional well-being, and social functioning. Common challenges included injection-site discomfort and initial treatment-related anxiety.ConclusionsPegvaliase treatment was effective in reducing blood Phe levels and achieving diet liberalization, thereby improving quality of life in individuals with PKU who historically had poor dietary adherence. Despite initial treatment challenges, the therapy enabled substantial improvements in quality of life and dietary freedom.

Introduction

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a genetic metabolic disorder caused by impaired functioning of the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) enzyme, resulting in the impairment of the conversion of phenylalanine (Phe) to tyrosine (Tyr) [1]. Classical PKU generally refers to the severe phenotype of PAH deficiency and has historically been defined by pretreatment blood Phe concentrations >1200 μmol/L [2]. Newborn screening for PKU has been implemented in Italy since 1980, and the incidence of the classical PKU has been reported to be approximately 1 in 15,000 live births [3], [4].

Treatment options include dietary restriction of the Phe intake with supplementation of other amino acids, sapropterin dihydrochloride, a synthetic form of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), and pegvaliase, a bacterial phenylalanine ammonia lyase enzyme.

Lifelong treatment is required to control blood Phe levels for most patients with PKU [5], [6]. United States guidelines recommend blood Phe levels ≤360 μmol/L at all ages, whereas European guidelines suggest a target range of 120–360 μmol/L in children up to 12 years of age and during pregnancy, allowing a broader range (120–600 μmol/L) from age 12 onward. Both U.S. and European guidelines recommend weekly blood Phe monitoring in infancy and monthly monitoring thereafter [2], [6], [7].

Over time, the gold-standard therapy has consisted of a protein-restricted diet adjusted according to individual Phe tolerance, supplemented with Phe-free amino acid formulas providing all other required amino acids, minerals, and vitamins, and ensuring adequate protein and energy intake needed for growth and optimal nutrition [8]. However, the diet can be unpalatable, leading to social difficulties [9], [10] and poor adherence [11]. Moreover, individuals with PKU may experience neurocognitive difficulties, social disengagement, and low self-esteem [12], [13].

The long-term management of PKU can significantly affect quality of life, both in childhood and adulthood [14], [15], [16], [17]. Therefore, it is important to investigate the quality of life in people with PKU by highlighting symptoms and the aspects that pose the greatest difficulties in daily life, in order to enable a clinical focus on factors that are relevant for supporting individuals in disease management and improving therapy adherence.

For this reason, in 2015 a tailored instrument for the assessment of quality of life in individuals with PKU was developed [18]. The Phenylketonuria–Quality of Life (PKU-QOL) questionnaire is the only instrument created and validated for assessing quality of life and for evaluating disease-specific aspects and their impact on individuals with PKU across different life stages, from childhood to adulthood, as well as on their caregivers. The first study [19] that used the PKU-QOL questionnaire in several European countries showed a substantial impact of the disease on quality of life, describing a strong emotional impact and anxiety related to blood Phe levels. Numerous studies [10], [14], [16], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24] investigated the quality of life of individuals with PKU in different countries using the PKU-QOL questionnaire, highlighting difficulties related to dietary management and the intake of Phe-free amino acid supplements, and bringing to light a strong sense of guilt associated with poor therapeutic adherence and reduced food enjoyment.

To address these difficulties related to adherence to dietary therapy and the intake of Phe-free amino acid supplements, new therapies have been developed that may lower blood Phe levels and allow liberalization of diet restrictions, with the aim of improving quality of life in individuals with PKU. One therapy that has been investigated is sapropterin dihydrochloride. However, not all individuals with PKU respond to sapropterin, and the degree of response may vary among responders, often allowing only partial liberalization of dietary restrictions [6]. Furthermore, there are few studies in the literature that have evaluated improvements in quality of life associated with this therapy using instruments specific to individuals with PKU. One study [19], using the PKU-QOL questionnaire in groups of individuals treated with sapropterin, reporting a lower practical and emotional impact of the diet and Phe-free amino acid supplement intake. Another study [25] developed a different disease-specific instrument and reported that sapropterin-responsive individuals experienced an improvement in their quality of life during treatment.

In 2020, pegvaliase (PALYNZIQ™, BioMarin Pharmaceutical Inc.), a PEGylated recombinant phenylalanine ammonia lyase that converts Phe into ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid [26], was approved in Italy for use in individuals with PKU aged ≥16 years, who have inadequate blood Phe control (>600 μmol/L), low diet adherence, and who are non-responsive to sapropterin therapy. Pegvaliase treatment requires a gradual dose-escalation period, including induction and titration phases, until an individualized maintenance dose is reached; this period may be lengthy and may be associated with treatment-related adverse events, particularly during the early phases of therapy, including injection site reactions, arthralgia, and hypersensitivity reactions, which may affect tolerability and quality of life during treatment initiation [27]. This treatment has been demonstrated to facilitate diet liberalization and reduce or eliminate the need for Phe-free amino acid supplementation [28], [29], [30], [31], thereby positively influencing daily functioning and potentially improving quality of life.

Only limited evidence is currently available regarding quality of life in individuals with PKU treated with pegvaliase. The phase 4 OPAL study [32], presented at the 2025 ACMG Annual Clinical Genetics Meeting, reported improved quality of life in individuals with PKU receiving this therapy. In Italy, an expert panel also identified pegvaliase as a therapeutic option with the potential to both reduce blood Phe levels and enhance quality of life in individuals with PKU [33].

The aim of our study is to report the experience of the Padua Center and assess the impact of pegvaliase treatment on the quality of life and on the perception of, and satisfaction with, treatment in individuals with classical PKU after reaching the maintenance period of therapy and consequent diet liberalization.

Materials and methods

Study design

This longitudinal observational study recruited individuals with classical PKU followed at the Reference Centre for Expanded Newborn Screening, Division of Inherited Metabolic Diseases, University Hospital of Padua (Italy). With the exception of one individual born in an Eastern European country where neonatal screening (NBS) was not available, all participants were diagnosed following NBS and treated since birth.

Individuals eligible for pegvaliase therapy were identified among individuals with classical PKU showing persistently elevated blood Phe levels above the adult safety threshold (>600 μmol/L), as recommended by European guidelines [6], poor adherence to strict dietary therapy (Phe intake <400 mg/day), and documented lack of responsiveness to sapropterin treatment.

All individuals undergoing pegvaliase treatment who achieved complete dietary liberalization after the maintenance therapy period were included in this study. Exclusion criteria for the assessment of quality of life and therapy satisfaction evaluation included severe cognitive impairment (IQ <70).

Participants were enrolled between March 2022 and November 2025, and the longitudinal observation covered a total period of 45 months.

The dose of pegvaliase was gradually titrated based on individual tolerability until each participant reached an effective maintenance dose. To minimize potential treatment-related adverse effects, we adopted a slower dosing escalation; consequently, the titration period was longer than recommended in the Summary of Product Characteristics (SPC) of the drug and was individualized according to each participant's clinical needs.

Blood Phe levels were calculated based on the average concentration over the last 2 years before starting pegvaliase treatment (pre-treatment blood Phe level), during treatment, and over the 12 months after reaching the maintenance period and diet liberalization (post-treatment blood Phe level).

During clinic visits, we assessed quality of life before treatment (T0), and at 6 months (T1) and 12 months (T2) after achieving full diet liberalization. Furthermore, at T2 a qualitative questionnaire on participants' satisfaction and experience with pegvaliase was administered.

All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study adhered to the principles of Good Clinical Practice (GCP), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as amended) and the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH)/GCP guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (Ethics Committee Approval No. 5637/AO/23, University Hospital of Padua).

Instruments

Quality of life assessment

The quality of life of the participants included in the study was assessed using the adolescent or adult version of the PKU-QOL questionnaire, a reliable instrument designed to assess the impact of PKU and its dietary treatment on quality of life in individuals with PKU [18], [19]. The questionnaire was administered at T0, T1, and T2 during routine clinic visits.

Qualitative analysis of patients' perception and satisfaction

An ad hoc questionnaire was developed to evaluate participants' perceptions and satisfaction with pegvaliase treatment. At T2, participants were asked to answer 7 questions. The first part of the survey included four closed-ended questions concerning participants' satisfaction with the improvements reported in the latest cognitive and neuropsychological tests and in the most recent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan performed. A preliminary report describing neuropsychological testing and brain MRI findings in three individuals from this cohort has been published previously [34]. Qualitative responses to questions #1 and #2 were given on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “very satisfied” to “not satisfied at all”. Quantitative responses to questions #3 and #4 were given on a Likert scale from 0 to 5, where higher scores indicated greater satisfaction. Participants were also asked to answer three open-ended questions relating to improvements noted since the beginning of therapy and any challenges encountered during treatment. The questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Statistical analyses

The total scores of the PKU-QOL and its four subscales were reported for all participants along with the median value and interquartile range for the sample.

Additionally, differences in PKU-QOL subscale and total scores between T0, T1 and T2 were investigated. Given the non-normal distribution of the data, non-parametric statistical tests were applied. For each outcome variable, a Kruskal–Wallis test was first conducted to assess overall differences across the three assessment time points (T0, T1, T2). Subsequently, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired samples, comparing T0–T1, T1–T2, and T0–T2. P-values for the Kruskal-Wallis test and for Wilcoxon pairwise comparisons were reported in Table 3. The significance threshold was set at 0.05 for the analyses.

The results of the qualitative analysis of participants' perceptions and satisfaction with pegvaliase were reported descriptively (Table 4 and Supplementary material 1).

Results

Study population

Among 70 individuals with classical PKU, 20 individuals showed persistent poor metabolic control despite long-term dietary management, blood Phe levels >600 μmol/L, and lack of responsiveness to sapropterin therapy.

Of these 20 individuals invited to initiate enzyme substitution therapy with pegvaliase, 10 agreed to start the treatment. Therapy with pegvaliase was initiated for various reasons, including feelings of guilt related to inadequate blood Phe level control, frustration with dietary management, a desire for diet liberalization, and a desire for greater freedom in social interactions involving food. Subsequently, 3 participant who had initiated pegvaliase treatment were excluded from the final study cohort and from the QoL and satisfaction analyses: 2 participants did not achieve complete dietary liberalization, and 1 participant was excluded due to severe cognitive impairment (IQ <70) related to late diagnosis of the disease. Consequently, 7 participants who achieved complete dietary liberalization while receiving pegvaliase treatment were included in the final study cohort. Characteristics of pegvaliase treatment

Prior to treatment, all participants had blood Phe levels >600 μmol/L (mean 962.1 μmol/L, standard deviation [SD] 190.9 μmol/L) and reported significant difficulties in adherence to dietary management. All participants achieved a significant decrease in blood Phe levels after the maintenance period (mean time to achieve therapy maintenance = 61 weeks) and therefore fully transitioned to a liberalized diet, no longer requiring Phe-free amino acid formulas. The mean post-treatment blood Phe level was 215.6 μmol/L (SD 193.6 μmol/L), with a mean decrease of 79.7%. All participants achieved blood Phe levels <600 μmol/L, and 5/7 participants had values <360 μmol/L.

To standardize the assessment intervals, we report the results at 6 and 12 months after diet liberalization; however, we note that two participants in the cohort have already reached 36 months of follow-up.

PKU-QOL scores decreased overall in all participants over time, indicating a significant improvement in perceived quality of life. A significant difference was observed in the “PKU in general” subscale and in the total score.

Participants showed a significant progressive reduction in scores over time, with a marked decrease at 6 months after diet liberalization and a further reduction at 12 months.

However, results differed in the “Symptoms” subscale: no significant differences were found in the overall comparison across time points, and three participants showed an increase in scores at T1 or T2.

Finally, the “Administration of Phe-free protein supplements” and “Dietary protein restrictions” subscales showed a high impact at T0, demonstrating the burden of diet and Phe-free amino acid supplementation on quality of life in individuals with PKU. At T1 and T2, these subscales were not assessed, as dietary treatment and Phe-free supplements were no longer required (scores for participant #5 at T0 are not reported because the participant was not following an unrestricted diet and was not taking amino acid mixtures, contrary to our center's dietary prescription).

Discussion

Classical PKU management requires adherence to a low-protein dietary regimen combined with the use of Phe-free amino acid mixtures. Dietary therapy is effective in controlling blood Phe levels and, consequently, in preventing cognitive impairment [12]. However, maintaining a restrictive diet is particularly challenging during adolescence and adulthood. The literature reports a significant impact on quality of life and perceived well-being in affected individuals [20], [35], with consequences for social relationships and the development of adequate self-esteem [36].

The ATTITUDE study, conducted in the Italian population over a 12-month period from August 2016 to August 2017 [11], focused on dietary habits and psychological factors influencing disease perception and dietary adherence in adults with PKU treated with a Phe-restricted diet and/or the synthetic form of BH4 (sapropterin). This research showed overall unsatisfactory dietary adherence, with high consumption of natural protein sources and reduced daily use of amino acid supplements due to poor palatability and difficulties eating in social situations.

In light of this evidence, it is important to consider therapeutic alternatives for individuals with PKU and poor dietary adherence, to support them in achieving adequate quality of life. Several studies [25], [37], [38], [39], [40] have shown that individuals treated with sapropterin who were able to follow an unrestricted or liberalized diet and maintain appropriate blood Phe levels reported better quality of life and psychosocial functioning. However, these studies used different quality-of-life instruments rather than consistently applying the PKU-QOL questionnaire, and other studies have reported mixed findings [41], [42]. Nevertheless, data on the impact of pegvaliase on psychological well-being assessed using PKU-specific instruments remain limited. A single contribution, presented at the 2025 ACMG Annual Clinical Genetics Meeting, reported interim results from the phase 4 OPAL study, describing improvements in quality of life scores among 51 individuals with PKU treated with pegvaliase who had at least 24 weeks of follow-up [32].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of pegvaliase treatment on quality of life and subjectively perceived well-being in Italian individuals with PKU after achieving complete diet liberalization, using PKU-specific instruments and a long-term follow-up.

Some participants from the ATTITUDE cohort were subsequently included in the present study and initiated pegvaliase treatment. Our results indicate that treatment with pegvaliase led to a significant reduction in blood Phe levels (mean decrease 79.7% from baseline) and enabled complete diet liberalization in all participants. However, it should be noted that, in this cohort, the maintenance period was longer than that reported in the drug SPC. This reflected a deliberately slower dose escalation aimed at minimizing treatment-related adverse effects and was individualized according to participants' characteristics. Notably, no participant discontinued therapy or required hospitalization for anaphylactic reactions.

These findings are in line with previous clinical trials and real-world evidence studies [28], [29], [30], [33], [34], [43], [44].

Moreover, our study showed a significant improvement in quality of life in a longer period of time, at 6 and 12 months after diet liberalization. These findings support the clinical value of pegvaliase in individuals with PKU and poor dietary adherence, as it allows diet liberalization and a consequent improvement in perceived well-being and quality of life. Reduction in blood Phe levels, diet liberalization, and discontinuation of amino acid mixtures appear to be associated with improved quality of life. Consistent with our results, Douglas et al. [25] reported that diet liberalization may represent an important factor for improved psychological well-being in individuals treated with sapropterin. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the psychological burden of dietary management in PKU [19], [23], [45].

In line with the available evidence, PKU-QOL scores in our cohort confirmed the substantial impact of adherence to dietary restriction and supplemental amino acid mixtures on quality of life, with high impact reported in the “Administration of Phe-free protein supplements” and “Dietary protein restriction” subscales. Interestingly, the “Symptoms” subscale, which assesses self-rated health status and physical, cognitive, and behavioral symptoms, such as tiredness, lack of concentration, slow thinking, irritability, and anxiety, did not improve in all participants. This finding may be explained by early pegvaliase treatment-related adverse events, including injection-related discomfort and hypersensitivity reactions [28], [44]. However, as reported in the qualitative analysis, the impact of adverse events decreased over time and did not substantially affect participants' overall perceived quality of life, suggesting the need for an initial adaptation period when starting therapy.

Finally, we explored participants' subjective perceptions of enzyme substitution therapy with pegvaliase. Participants reported satisfaction with the improvements in well-being perceived after diet liberalization. They described relevant benefits, including increased energy, improved sleep, better concentration, and reduced emotional distress. The most frequently cited positive change was the ability to eat more freely and without stress, further supporting the significant psychosocial impact of diet liberalization.

This study has some limitations. The primary limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 7), representing approximately 10% of the individuals with classic PKU followed at our Centre. This reflects the stringent eligibility criteria required for pegvaliase treatment in Italy — specifically, documented poor dietary adherence and non-responsiveness to sapropterin — and is consistent with the real-world prevalence of this treatment indication. As pegvaliase has only recently become available in Italy following its approval in 2020, the number of individuals who have achieved stable diet liberalization and completed the full follow-up assessment is necessarily limited at this stage. In addition, a potential self-selection bias should be considered, as the participants included in the QoL analysis were those who accepted pegvaliase treatment, tolerated therapy sufficiently to reach complete diet liberalization, and were able to complete the QoL questionnaires at the planned assessment time points. Severe cognitive impairment was an exclusion criterion, as participants were required to understand and complete the questionnaires. Therefore, individuals who did not achieve complete diet liberalization or who could not complete the assessments are not represented, and the favorable QoL outcomes observed in our cohort may overestimate the benefit expected in the broader population of individuals initiating pegvaliase therapy. This interpretation is consistent with preliminary data from the phase 4 OPAL study [32], which included a larger and less stringently selected cohort and reported QoL improvements, although more modest than those observed in our single-center real-life cohort. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as applicable primarily to individuals who achieve successful diet liberalization with pegvaliase, and may not be generalizable to all individuals starting treatment. The primary QoL time points reported here were 6 and 12 months after diet liberalization; however, two participants have already reached 36 months of clinical follow-up without loss of efficacy or perceived quality of life benefit, supporting the durability of the findings. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these results in a more representative cohort.

Second, the final study cohort was predominantly female and entirely Caucasian, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and should be considered when interpreting the quality-of-life outcomes. Further studies in larger and more demographically diverse cohorts are needed to confirm these findings. Finally, the PKU-QOL questionnaire used to assess participants' quality of life was developed when dietary treatment was the most widely available therapeutic option. Consequently, the instrument largely focuses on difficulties related to adherence to dietary restrictions and amino acid supplementation; therefore, it is not specifically designed to capture improvements in other dimensions of quality of life, and may not fully capture participants' subjective perceptions of pharmacological treatments that allow diet liberalization. For this reason, we developed a qualitative instrument to assess participants' perceptions of and satisfaction with pegvaliase treatment.

Conclusion

This study showed that, in individuals with classical PKU treated with pegvaliase, significant improvements in quality of life and subjectively perceived well-being were observed after achieving diet liberalization.
