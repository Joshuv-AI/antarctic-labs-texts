Evaluation of the efficacy of sliding mechanisms on round versus rectangular archwires in canine retraction after first premolar extraction in class II malocclusion: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Mohammad N Kheshfeh, Mohammad Y Hajeer, Mohammad Khursheed Alam, Mhd Firas Al Hinnawi, Hussein Abou Hamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective(s)To compare the efficacy of sliding mechanics on a 0.020-inch round posterior segment incorporated into a bi-dimensional archwire (BA) versus conventional rectangular-section archwire (CONV) for maxillary canine retraction, rotation, angulation, anchorage loss, overbite change, and external apical root resorption (EARR).DesignRandomized, parallel-group, two-arm clinical trial with 1:1 allocation.SettingDepartment of Orthodontics, University of Damascus; recruitment and treatment July–December 2023.Participants and interventionsPatients aged 18–25 years with mild–moderate skeletal Class II (ANB + 5° to + 7°) and 5–10 mm overjet scheduled for camouflage treatment with extraction of maxillary first premolars were randomized (N = 38) to: (a) BA—bi-dimensional archwire (0.021 × 0.025″ rectangular in the incisor segment; 0.020″ round in canine/premolar/molar slots) or (b) CONV—conventional rectangular-section retraction mechanics.Main outcome measure(s)Primary outcome was monthly maxillary canine retraction rate measured on digitally scanned casts (3D analysis). Introduction

In adult Class II camouflage treatment, extraction of the first premolars is a routine approach [1], followed by a two-phase retraction protocol in which the canines are retracted first, followed by the incisors [2]. However, conventional techniques may extend treatment duration to as long as 36 months, presenting considerable clinical challenges [3]. Prolonged orthodontic therapy increases the risk of complications, such as dental caries, root resorption, and periodontal problems, underscoring the need for more efficient, time-saving treatment modalities [4, 5].

To accelerate orthodontic tooth movement, both surgical and non-surgical strategies have been extensively explored [6–8]. Over the past two decades, surgical interventions—such as corticotomy [9], piezocision [10], flapless cortico-alveolar perforations [9], and periodontal accelerated osteogenic orthodontics [11], have shown promising outcomes. Nevertheless, due to their invasive nature, these approaches have prompted increasing interest in non-surgical alternatives, which are generally more acceptable to both clinicians and patients [12]. Such alternatives include physical modalities (e.g., low-level laser therapy [13], and electrical stimulation [14, 15]), pharmacological interventions (e.g., platelet-rich plasma injections [16]), and mechanical techniques (e.g., self-ligating brackets [17]).

Despite these advancements, canine retraction remains biomechanically challenging, primarily due to a relatively slow rate of tooth movement and difficulties in controlling undesired rotation and angulation [18]. Both frictional and frictionless mechanics have been investigated to overcome these obstacles [19, 20]. The conventional friction-based approach involves sliding teeth along a rigid rectangular archwire that fits snugly within the bracket slot, thereby ensuring torque control during retraction [20]. However, this tight fit increases friction at the bracket–wire interface, impeding efficient movement [21]. Although rectangular wires provide excellent torque control, their greater surface contact leads to significant frictional resistance, which may delay canine retraction [21].

Alternatively, round-section archwires have gained attention for their potential to reduce friction while preserving adequate control [21]. These wires are designed to maintain proper vertical dimension and reduce bracket-wall contact, thereby enabling smoother sliding mechanics and improved control over canine angulation during distalization [20, 21]. The reduced frictional resistance may also minimize stress on anchorage units, further enhancing treatment efficiency [22].

Although the concept has been preliminarily studied, most notably in a single clinical investigation by Hamid [23], that study has been criticized for several methodological shortcomings, including a short observation period of only four weeks and the absence of data collection beyond early canine movement stages (i.e., before achieving full Class I canine relationship).

To our knowledge, no randomized clinical trial has evaluated a welded bidimensional hybrid archwire combining a 0.021 × 0.025″ rigid anterior segment with a 0.020″ round posterior segment while reporting comprehensive clinical outcomes (3-D cast-based retraction rates, cephalometric angulation, anchorage loss and panoramic EARR) across the full canine retraction phase. Prior clinical reports have been limited to short pilot series (e.g., Kheshfeh et al., 2025 [24]) and most prior work has been bench or biomechanical in design; this trial therefore addresses a gap between biomechanical evidence and randomized clinical outcomes by testing a clinically reproducible hybrid configuration under randomized conditions.

Several clinical and biomechanical studies have described bidimensional or two-dimensional approaches and their theoretical advantages for combining anterior torque control with reduced posterior friction (Tepedino et al., 2020; finite-element and bench studies 2024) [25, 26]. These works document how slot/wire dimensional variability and torsional play influence torque expression and sliding resistance, and they provide the biomechanical rationale for testing a hybrid round-posterior/rectangular-anterior configuration in vivo.

Specific objectives or hypotheses

This randomized clinical trial aimed to compare sliding mechanics using a 0.020‑inch round posterior segment incorporated into a bi‑dimensional archwire (BA) versus conventional rectangular‑section mechanics (CONV). The prespecified objectives were to evaluate: (1) monthly canine retraction rate, (2) rotational control, (3) anchorage preservation, (4) canine angulation change, (5) overbite change, and (6) external apical root resorption (EARR). We hypothesized that the BA configuration would increase retraction rate and improve angulation control without clinically meaningful increases in rotation, anchorage loss, or root resorption.

Methods

Trial design and any changes after trial commencement

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted as a single-center, single-blinded, two-arm parallel study. The trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07243509; registration date: insert actual date as listed on ClinicalTrials.gov). Retrospective registration occurred because the administrative approval process and initiation of participant recruitment progressed before the research team became aware of the mandatory prospective registration requirement for interventional trials. However, a written protocol containing the study design, eligibility criteria, intervention details, and pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes was finalized before the enrollment of the first participant, and no changes were made to the specified outcomes after registration. Ethical approval was obtained prior to study initiation from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at the University of Damascus (Approval No: DN-30082022-9). The study was carried out at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, University of Damascus, with institutional funding (Ref No: 501100020595). All participants received detailed written information and provided signed informed consent prior to enrollment.

No important changes to the prespecified primary or secondary outcomes or to the trial design were made after trial commencement. Any minor administrative clarifications are documented in the trial log.

Participants, eligibility criteria, and setting

The single-site trial was conducted at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, University of Damascus, with institutional funding (Ref. No. 501100020595) and ethical approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. DN-30082022-9). Patients attending the department between July and December 2023 were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were adults aged 18–25 years with mild–moderate skeletal Class II (ANB 5°–7°), a normal to slightly increased vertical growth pattern (Björk’s sum > 390° and < 406°), an overjet of 5–10 mm, and a planned camouflage treatment involving extraction of the maxillary first premolars. Exclusion criteria included systemic conditions affecting tooth movement, active periodontal disease, missing permanent maxillary teeth (excluding third molars), crowding ≥ 4 mm, or inadequate oral hygiene. Of 276 records screened, 71 individuals underwent eligibility assessment; 28 were excluded per prespecified criteria and three eligible individuals declined participation, leaving 40 in the sampling frame. To achieve the prespecified sample size of 38 (19 per group), two eligible individuals were removed from the sampling frame prior to enrollment by computer-generated random selection; this procedure was performed before consent or allocation and is documented in the trial log. All enrolled participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed study information.

Interventions

Anchorage was reinforced by soldering a transpalatal arch (TPA) to the maxillary first molar bands to ensure adequate anchorage control during the retraction phase (Fig. 1). In the experimental (bi-dimensional archwire) group, leveling and alignment followed the study’s standardized archwire sequence and continued until a 0.021 × 0.025-inch nickel–titanium (NiTi) archwire was placed (JISCOP Co., Ltd., Gunpo-si, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Bilateral extraction of the maxillary first premolars was performed prior to insertion of the 0.017 × 0.025-inch NiTi archwire during the leveling/alignment phase. Canine retraction used a custom-fabricated bi-dimensional stainless steel archwire consisting of a rigid anterior segment (0.021 × 0.025-inch stainless steel) spanning the four maxillary incisor brackets, welded by Nd: YAG laser (BK-JW150X, Wuhan Baike Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) to 0.020-inch round stainless steel posterior segments (Fig. 2). This configuration provided increased anterior stiffness and torque control while reducing posterior friction, optimizing sliding mechanics during maxillary canine distalization. All bi-dimensional hybrid archwires were produced by a single experienced laboratory technician following a standardized fabrication protocol; Nd: YAG welding was performed with predefined parameters (pulse energy 9–12 J; pulse duration 2–4 ms; repetition frequency 8–10 Hz) established during pilot runs to achieve reproducible fusion with minimal thermal distortion. Each welded joint underwent macroscopic and microscopic inspection (×20 magnification) to verify continuity, alignment, and absence of visible porosity or cracks, and joints were lightly polished to remove surface irregularities without altering cross-sectional geometry. Representative samples from the clinical batch underwent simple mechanical verification (manual bend/flex tests) to confirm handling integrity for intraoral use; no clinical wire failures, fractures, or functional distortions occurred during the study. Formal tensile and cyclic-fatigue testing were not performed for this series, but the welding approach, parameters, and post-weld handling followed established orthodontic laser-welding recommendations. Treating clinicians received standardized training in engagement, ligation, and clinical handling of the hybrid archwire to minimize operator variability. The design is not commercially prefabricated but is reproducible using conventional 0.021 × 0.025-inch and 0.020-inch stainless-steel segments and standard Nd: YAG laser welding systems. A calibrated bilateral retraction force of 150 g was applied using closed-coil NiTi springs (NT3, American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, Wisconsin, USA) attached from the canine brackets to hooks on the maxillary first molars; force magnitude was verified every two weeks with an intraoral force gauge (Model 040-711-00; Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany). Springs were reactivated or replaced if force fell below 150 g and adjusted if above the target, and springs were inspected routinely for deformation or fatigue.

Welded Connection of Metal Components

Rationale for the welded bidimensional hybrid wire the primary clinical objective of this trial was to evaluate canine retraction performed on a 0.020″ round posterior segment. To provide a low-friction posterior sliding surface while preserving anterior control, we used a welded bidimensional hybrid archwire consisting of a 0.021 × 0.025″ rectangular anterior segment welded to 0.020″ round posterior segments. This configuration increases anterior sectional stiffness and torque expression compared with a uniformly small-section wire, while providing a discrete round posterior surface for sliding. Compared with an alternative strategy of using mixed bracket slot sizes (e.g., 0.018″ anterior / 0.022″ posterior) with a single continuous wire, the welded hybrid offers practical advantages: it avoids the need for mixed bracket inventories or bracket replacement, simplifies chairside logistics (single fabricated wire per arch), and produces a reproducible geometry through standardized laser welding. These pragmatic and biomechanical considerations motivated our choice for a study focused specifically on 0.020″ posterior-segment retraction.

We did not perform quantitative bench testing of slot–wire play angle or friction coefficients for the clinical batch of welded hybrid archwires. Fabrication quality was verified by macroscopic and microscopic inspection and simple manual handling checks; no clinical wire failures occurred. Quantitative bench friction and play‑angle measurements were beyond the scope of this clinical trial.

In the control (conventional) group, leveling and alignment were completed according to the same standardized archwire sequence, with leveling proceeding until a 0.019 × 0.025-inch NiTi archwire was placed. Bilateral extraction of the maxillary first premolars was performed with identical timing and protocol to the experimental group (i.e., prior to insertion of the 0.017 × 0.025-inch NiTi archwire). Maxillary canine distalization was carried out using sliding mechanics on a 0.019 × 0.025-inch stainless steel archwire. The same calibrated 150 g bilateral retraction force protocol (NT3, American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, Wisconsin, USA) and biweekly force verification with the same intraoral gauge were applied; springs were reactivated, replaced, or adjusted as required and inspected for material fatigue.

Outcomes

Measurement error and reliability were assessed for 3D casts, lateral cephalograms, and panoramic radiographs; paired tests showed no systematic error and ICCs indicated excellent reliability.

Primary outcome measure: rate of maxillary canine retraction

The primary outcome was the rate of maxillary canine retraction, quantified from 3D digital models. Alginate impressions (Caves imperssional, Cavex, Haarlem, Netherlands) were taken at five standardized intervals: T0: (baseline, immediately after leveling and alignment, before canine retraction), T1 (One month after retraction onset), T2 (Two months after retraction onset), T3 (Three months after retraction onset), TF (Final, Upon complete canine retraction and establishment of a Class I canine relationship). Models were scanned with a high-precision 3D scanner (AutoScan DS-EX Pro; SHINING 3D, China) and analyzed in Blender4.4 (Blender Foundation, Netherlands). Landmarks and plane construction: the canine apex was defined as the most apical point of the maxillary canine root on the 3D cast, and the medial end of the third palatal ruga was defined as the most medial (mesial) point of the third ruga on the palatal surface. The mid-palatal plane was constructed in Blender by fitting a plane through three reproducible palatal landmarks: the medial ends of the right and left third palatal rugae and the incisive papilla. All landmarks were placed on the digital models using the Blender 3D cursor and landmark tool (Fig. 3A). Measurement procedure and automation: landmark placement was performed manually by a trained examiner, after calibration. Subsequent projection of the canine apex and the medial end of the third palatal ruga onto the mid-palatal plane and the calculation of linear distances were performed using a semi-automated workflow in Blender (manual landmarking followed by an automated script to project points and compute distances). The linear distance between the projected points was recorded at each time point and monthly retraction rates were calculated by dividing the change in displacement by the corresponding interval in months. For each subject, mean monthly rates for right and left canines were determined, and the cohort retraction rate was expressed as the average across all patients.

Secondary outcome measures

Change in canine rotation: Using the same 3D datasets and time points (T0–TF), canine rotation was defined as the angle between the mid-palatal plane and the line connecting the mesial and distal ridges of the canine crown (Fig. 3B). Angular measurements were derived from the manually placed crown-edge landmarks and calculated automatically by the same Blender script; the angular shift between consecutive intervals was divided by the interval duration to yield monthly rotation rates. Per-subject averages for right and left canines were computed and then averaged across participants.

Anchorage loss (molar drift): Anchorage loss was assessed by measuring first-molar movement relative to the medial end of the third palatal ruga. Linear distances between the maxillary first molar fossa and the medial end of the third palatal ruga, both projected onto the mid-palatal plane (Fig. 3C), were recorded at T0–TF. Monthly drift rates were calculated similarly to canine retraction, averaged per side, and then across the cohort.

Anchorage loss was quantified as linear molar displacement projected onto the mid‑palatal plane; this method captures translational drift but does not explicitly measure molar rotation. Rotational components of anchorage loss may therefore be underestimated by the present metric.

Change in canine angulation: Canine angulation change was evaluated at T0 and TF via standardized lateral cephalograms (Cranex D Model PP1, Finland). To enhance measurement accuracy, stainless-steel reference wires (0.021 × 0.025 mm; 12 mm apical extension) were affixed to each canine: circular-ended on the right, triangular-ended on the left. Radiographs were analyzed in ImageJ (NIH, USA). Angulation was defined as the angle between each wire and the Sella–Nasion plane (Fig. 4). This validated, two-dimensional method controlled for head positioning, magnification, and anatomical overlap, consistent with protocols from Sueri and Turk [27].

Angulation measurement performed using ImageJ software, defined as the angle between the reference wire and the Sella–Nasion plane

Change IN overbite: Overbite was measured at two time points: before the start of canine retraction (T0) and at the end of the retraction phase (TF). At each time point, the vertical overlap of the maxillary incisors over the mandibular incisors was recorded in millimeters using standardized digital measurements. Overbite change (T0 → TF) was calculated as the difference between the TF and T0 values, with positive values indicating an increase in overbite and negative values indicating a reduction. This measure was used to quantify the total change in vertical incisor overlap during the retraction phase.

Canine root resorption: External apical root resorption (EARR) was assessed by comparing maxillary canine root lengths on panoramic radiographs taken at T0 and TF. Baseline root length was recorded at T0; at TF, the reduction in length was measured, and the percentage of resorption calculated according to the Linge and Linge method [28]. All radiographic measurements were performed in ImageJ, consistent with prior studies (e.g., Jacobs et al. [29]).

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome, monthly maxillary canine retraction rate, using parameters entered into MinitabVersion 19 (Minitab Inc., State College, Pennsylvania, USA). The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) was defined a priori as 35% of the average retraction rate typically achieved with conventional mechanics. Based on an expected conventional rate of 0.95 mm/month reported in the literature, the expected rate in the intervention arm under the MCID assumption was 0.62 mm/month (0.95 × 0.65), yielding an absolute MCID ≈ 0.33 mm/month. A standard deviation of 0.31 mm from a previous study was used. Assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and 85% power for an independent-samples t-test, the required sample size was calculated to be 17 participants per group. To account for possible attrition, 19 participants were planned for enrollment in each arm.

Interim analyses and stopping guidelines

No interim analyses were conducted and no stopping rules or early-termination guidelines were specified or applied; there were no protocol deviations that warranted stopping. Missingness in the primary outcome was minimal, so complete-case analyses are reported; no imputation or interim analyses were performed.

Randomization

A computer-generated random allocation sequence was created in Minitab (v17) using variable block randomization (block sizes 4 and 6) to ensure balanced group sizes and reduce predictability; the sequence was produced by an independent statistician who had no role in participant recruitment, treatment, or outcome assessment, and allocation assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, tamper-evident envelopes prepared and stored securely by that statistician, with each envelope opened only after participant eligibility and baseline measurements were confirmed; the independent statistician generated the sequence and prepared the envelopes while the principal investigator enrolled participants and assigned interventions by opening the next envelope in sequence at the time of allocation, and every envelope opening was recorded in a randomization log (date, time, and name of the person opening the envelope) to preserve transparency and traceability.

Blinding

Blinding of participants and treating clinicians was not feasible due to the nature of the interventions. To minimise detection bias, outcome assessors (3D cast measurer; cephalometric and panoramic measurer) and the study statistician were blinded to group allocation. Assessors underwent calibration training prior to measurements. All radiographs and digital models were anonymised and coded before measurement, and the allocation key was held by an independent faculty member and released only after the primary outcome analyses were finalised and locked. Standardised treatment protocols and operator training were used to reduce performance variability. The limitation of unblinded operators is acknowledged and is discussed in the Limitations section.

Statistical analysis

All analyses followed the prespecified statistical analysis plan. Data management and inferential statistics were performed in SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were screened for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test; normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± SD and non‑normal variables as median (IQR). Primary inferential analyses for the monthly canine retraction rate were conducted using linear mixed‑effects models (LMMs) with fixed effects for group and time, adjusted for baseline overjet, and a random intercept for subject to account for within‑patient correlation [30–32]. For repeated‑measures summaries of other longitudinal outcomes we report F statistics and partial eta squared (η²) as measures of effect size. Between‑group comparisons at single time points or for non‑longitudinal outcomes used independent‑samples t tests when assumptions were met and Mann–Whitney U tests when they were not. Non‑parametric effect sizes are reported as Hodges–Lehmann median differences with 95% confidence intervals computed by bootstrap (2,000 replications). Interaction terms (Group×Time) were examined using Wald tests; because these interactions were non‑significant (p > 0.05), they were removed from the tables presented for clarity while group and time main effects were retained in the fitted models. Ninety‑5% confidence intervals for medians and Hodges–Lehmann estimates were obtained by bootstrap when analytic CIs were not available. Exact p values are reported throughout. To control family‑wise Type I error we predefined logical families of related hypotheses and applied Bonferroni correction within each family as specified a priori: canine retraction family (five comparisons: T0–T1, T1–T2, T2–T3, T3–TF, and overall T0–TF) → adjusted α = 0.01; canine angulation family (three outcomes: total amount, rate, degrees/mm) → adjusted α ≈ 0.017; root resorption family (two outcomes: absolute EARR and percent) → adjusted α = 0.025. Other secondary outcomes were grouped and adjusted similarly (table footnotes). Model diagnostics included inspection of residuals, QQ plots, and influence statistics; no major departures from model assumptions were observed. Sensitivity analyses were performed by (a) additionally adjusting the primary LMM for age and sex and (b) refitting the model excluding baseline overjet; these checks produced consistent point estimates and did not change inference.

longitudinal analyses

Thirty-eight participants (19 per arm) completed the retraction phase and were included in the longitudinal analyses. Linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for subject and adjustment for baseline overjet were fitted for each outcome (Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimation; denominator degrees of freedom approximated by the Kenward–Roger method). Type III Wald tests were used to evaluate overall fixed effects; model diagnostics showed no major departures from assumptions. For the primary outcome (monthly canine retraction rate), the model demonstrated a significant overall effect of treatment group. The estimated group difference (BA vs. CONV) was β = 0.554 mm/month (SE = 0.236; 95% CI 0.09 to 1.02; p = 0.022) (Table 2), and the overall Wald test for Group was significant (F = 29.594; df ≈ 56.95; p < 0.001). Time and the Group×Time interaction were not significant (Time: F = 0.068, p = 0.977; Group×Time: F = 0.755, p = 0.525), indicating similar temporal trajectories between arms; therefore, the observed group difference reflects an overall advantage for the BA configuration across the observation period. Descriptively, median retraction rates were 1.17 mm/month (BA) versus 0.82 mm/month (CONV). Individual participant trajectories and group mean trends are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 (spaghetti plot). For canine rotation, there was a significant effect of time (F = 6.650; p = 0.001), indicating changes in rotation across follow-up irrespective of group. The overall Group effect did not reach significance by the Wald test (F = 3.138; p = 0.080), although the group coefficient in the fixed-effects table was positive (β = 2.482∘ per interval; SE = 1.216; 95% CI 0.13 to 4.83; p = 0.039) (Table 2). The Group×Time interaction was non-significant (F = 1.332; p = 0.274). Rotation trajectories are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. For anchorage loss, both Group (F = 5.158; p = 0.025) and Time (F = 23.805; p < 0.001) showed significant overall effects. The estimated group difference at the reference time was β = 0.079 mm/month (SE = 0.042; 95% CI − 0.00 to 0.16; p = 0.057) (Table 2), indicating a tendency toward greater anchorage loss in the BA group that reached significance when tested across all time points but did not reach conventional significance at the reference time point. The Group×Time interaction was non-significant (F = 1.840; p = 0.147). Spaghetti plots for anchorage loss are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3. When expressed relative to retraction distance, the BA protocol showed less tipping per millimeter (1.38 ± 0.47°/mm vs. 2.29 ± 0.96°/mm; mean difference 0.91°/mm; 95% CI − 1.41 to − 0.41; p = 0.001). Monthly angulation change rate did not differ significantly between groups (1.63°/month vs. 1.92°/month; mean difference 0.30°/month; 95% CI − 0.78 to 0.19; p = 0.223). Collectively, these findings indicate superior root angulation control with the BA protocol despite a comparable monthly pace of tipping. Harms

No serious adverse events were recorded. Minor soft-tissue complaints were reported intermittently in both groups and were managed conservatively. No clinical wire failures, fractures, or functional distortions occurred during the study.

Discussion

Main findings in the context of the existing evidence, interpretation

To our knowledge, this randomized clinical trial is the first controlled study to evaluate the clinical efficacy of sliding on a 0.020-inch round posterior segment integrated within a custom hybrid archwire for accelerating maxillary canine retraction while quantifying associated dentoalveolar changes. Our randomized findings extend prior pilot clinical observations (Kheshfeh et al., 2025) and complement bench studies that have examined slot/wire dimensional variability and sliding resistance [24]. By providing model-based longitudinal estimates and non-parametric effect sizes with bootstrap confidence intervals, the present trial strengthens the clinical evidence base for a welded bidimensional approach and helps bridge the gap between in-vitro mechanics and patient-centred outcomes. Our findings are consistent with prior biomechanical and clinical reports suggesting that reducing posterior wire–bracket contact can lower sliding resistance while preserving anterior torque (Tepedino et al., 2020; recent finite-element analyses 2024) [25, 26]. However, earlier clinical reports were limited by short follow-up, small samples, or non-randomized designs; the present randomized trial extends these observations by providing model-based longitudinal estimates and non-parametric effect sizes with bootstrap confidence intervals.

Comparison with mixed-slot strategies Using different bracket slot sizes anteriorly and posteriorly (for example 0.018″ anterior / 0.022″ posterior) with a continuous wire is a valid alternative to achieve anterior torque control and reduced posterior friction. However, because our trial specifically evaluated canine retraction on a 0.020″ posterior sliding surface, we selected a welded bidimensional hybrid that combines a larger rectangular anterior section with a discrete 0.020″ round posterior segment. This design secures higher anterior sectional stiffness while providing the intended low-friction posterior surface—without changing bracket types. Direct head-to-head bench testing (play angle, static/dynamic friction) and randomized clinical comparisons would be valuable to determine relative mechanical performance and practical advantages.

Using longitudinal linear mixed-effects models (fixed effects: group, time, group × time; adjusted for baseline overjet; random intercept for subject; reported values are model-based estimated marginal means unless otherwise stated), the hybrid (BA) protocol produced a statistically significant between-subjects advantage in retraction magnitude (Wald F = 29.594; p < 0.001; partial η² = 0.471). Our modelling strategy follows recent orthodontic longitudinal studies that used LMMs to characterise tooth-movement trajectories and root [30–32], supporting the appropriateness of LMMs for repeated dental measurements.

The treatment × time interaction was not significant (Wald F = 1.134; p = 0.311; partial η² = 0.034), and reduced models without the interaction yielded consistent inference. Descriptive median retraction rates over T0–TF were 1.17 mm/month (IQR 0.20; bootstrap 95% CI 1.08–1.24) for BA versus 0.82 mm/month (IQR 0.13; 95% CI 0.76–0.90) for CONV, an absolute median difference that—if sustained—could meaningfully shorten the active space-closure phase in routine clinical practice.

Beyond speed, the BA protocol improved the quality of movement. Total canine angulation change and tipping per millimeter of retraction were significantly smaller with BA (total angulation mean difference 2.90°; 95% CI − 4.97 to − 0.82; p = 0.007; tipping per mm mean difference 0.91°/mm; 95% CI − 1.41 to − 0.41; p = 0.001) (Table 3). Interval-specific tipping rates (°/month) did not differ significantly between groups, indicating a comparable monthly pace of tipping despite lower cumulative tipping with the hybrid approach. These findings indicate superior root angulation control with BA while the temporal rate of tipping remained similar.

Secondary outcomes (rotation, anchorage loss, overbite change, and external apical root resorption) showed modest between-group differences in some analyses; absolute magnitudes were small (typically < 0.2–0.3 mm or ~ 1–2°) and many comparisons did not meet prespecified family-wise adjusted thresholds (Tables 2 and 4). Median EARR and percent root shortening trended lower with BA but did not reach the prespecified Bonferroni-adjusted significance for the root-resorption family (adjusted α = 0.025); these are therefore reported as non-significant trends. Because root resorption was assessed on panoramic radiographs, which have known limitations in absolute root length estimation and spatial resolution compared with cone-beam CT, the study may be underpowered to detect subtle three-dimensional differences; definitive conclusions about small differences in EARR would benefit from CBCT in a subset of patients.

EARR and overbite comparisons were reported as Hodges–Lehmann median differences with bootstrap 95% CIs; although absolute and percent EARR showed trends favoring the conventional group, these differences did not reach the prespecified Bonferroni-adjusted significance level (α = 0.025) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The hybrid archwire was selected to reconcile two clinical priorities: preserve incisor torque and anterior arch form achieved during leveling, and exploit the lower sliding resistance of a round posterior segment to accelerate canine translation. To avoid loss of extraction space during alignment and to minimize transient biologic acceleration after extraction, premolar extractions were deferred until a 0.017 × 0.025-inch stainless steel archwire was reached and performed at least two months before initiating canine retraction [33, 34]. A preceding clinical pilot demonstrated feasibility, clinically relevant retraction rates, minimal mesiodistal angulation change, and no clinically significant root resorption at retraction end—supporting progression to the present randomized trial [24].

Hybrid wires were fabricated by Nd: YAG laser welding of round and rectangular segments. Nd: YAG welding produces a narrow heat-affected zone, requires no filler, and yields a homogeneous, high-strength joint with minimal alteration of the parent wire’s mechanical properties; careful finishing avoids abrupt geometric transitions that could irritate soft tissues or act as stress concentrators [35]. These fabrication details are important for reproducibility and are reported in the Methods.

Mechanistically, the clinical pattern observed, accelerated translation with reduced tipping per millimeter, aligns with established biomechanical principles. A round posterior segment reduces bracket–wire contact area and therefore static and kinetic sliding resistance, diminishing binding, notching and stick–slip phenomena that slow translation when rectangular wires fully engage the slot [36, 37]. Lower sliding resistance allows a greater proportion of the applied distal force to act nearer the tooth’s center of resistance, promoting bodily movement rather than uncontrolled tipping. Maintaining a rectangular anterior segment preserves incisor torque and anterior arch form, enabling clinicians to combine low-friction posterior sliding with controlled anterior mechanics; the resulting moment-to-force balance limits excessive distal tipping. These mechanistic inferences are consistent with bench friction studies, although friction was not measured in vivo in this trial and therefore the mechanistic account remains inferential.

From a pragmatic clinical perspective, the hybrid archwire approach offers two principal advantages. First, efficiency: the BA protocol produced a clinically meaningful increase in monthly canine retraction speed that can shorten the active space-closure phase and potentially reduce total treatment time when applied appropriately. Second, quality of movement: reduced tipping per millimeter implies less need for subsequent uprighting mechanics and fewer finishing interventions to correct root angulation. Secondary differences (anchorage loss, rotation, and EARR) were small in absolute terms and are unlikely, by themselves, to require routine changes in clinical mechanics; nevertheless, clinicians should continue to plan anchorage reinforcement where indicated (for example, transpalatal arch or temporary anchorage devices) and to monitor canine angulation and rotation during retraction, because the hybrid approach reduces but does not eliminate the occasional need for uprighting or rotational control. Our anchorage assessment did not quantify molar rotation; future studies using 3‑D orientation measures (e.g., CBCT or 3‑D model registration with orientation metrics) would better characterize combined translational and rotational anchorage changes.

Our results extend prior experimental and clinical observations by demonstrating that the frictional advantage of round wires can translate into faster clinical tooth movement when combined with an anterior rectangular segment that preserves torque. Earlier clinical reports of pure round-wire sliding sometimes documented increased tipping; by contrast, the hybrid design here appears to reconcile efficiency with control. For context, some prior reports using different wire diameters and protocols reported larger tipping magnitudes—for example, Hayashi et al. reported ~ 7.94° tipping over two months with a 0.018-inch wire (≈ 3.97°/month) [38]. In our trial mean monthly tipping was 1.62° ± 0.54°, representing a markedly lower tipping rate and suggesting that the hybrid approach can substantially reduce distal tipping compared with some pure round-wire techniques. Direct cross-study comparisons are limited by differences in measurement methods, anchorage strategies, bracket systems and patient characteristics, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Regarding rotation and adjunctive modalities, our rotation findings are broadly consistent with studies that evaluated non-mechanical adjuncts (e.g., low-level laser therapy) which did not demonstrate clinically important differences in tipping or rotation [39]. The present data therefore situate the hybrid mechanical approach within the broader literature on strategies to accelerate or modulate tooth movement.

Limitations

The trial was powered for the primary outcome (canine retraction rate), so several secondary endpoints (notably rotation and external apical root resorption) may have been underpowered to detect small but potentially meaningful differences and non-significant findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Root resorption was assessed using panoramic radiographs rather than cone-beam CT; panoramic imaging can underestimate absolute root shortening and lacks three-dimensional resolution, and although CBCT would offer greater accuracy it was not ethically justified for all participants solely for research imaging, therefore we mitigated this limitation by analyzing within-subject relative changes and percentage reductions as sensitivity checks. Radiographic angulation was measured only at baseline and at TF (end of retraction) to minimize radiation exposure, so the temporal resolution is limited and more frequent imaging would be required to fully characterize the biomechanics of angulation change. The study was conducted at a single centre under standardized protocols with a limited number of operators, which reduced procedural variability but may limit generalizability to other clinical settings, bracket systems, or operator skill levels. Our mechanistic interpretation relies on established frictional principles and bench data because direct in-vivo measurement of sliding resistance was not performed; such measurements would strengthen causal inference. Although Nd: YAG welding produced robust joints in this trial, subtle differences in fabrication or weld geometry could influence clinical performance, so reproducibility across different laboratories and clinicians should be confirmed. Finally, analyses were restricted to aggregate data and we did not perform subgroup analyses (for example by sex or age strata) because the sample size limited statistical power and would risk producing unreliable inferences. We did not measure slot–wire play angles or friction coefficients in vitro for the welded hybrid wires used in this trial. Consequently, we cannot provide direct mechanical estimates of play or friction; rather, our clinical outcomes reflect the net in‑vivo effect of the configuration. Future work should include standardized bench testing (play angle, static/dynamic friction) and finite‑element or in‑vitro friction assays to link mechanical parameters with clinical performance.

Generalizability

The findings of this single-centre randomized trial are most directly applicable to young adults (18–25 years) with mild–moderate skeletal Class II (ANB 5°–7°) and 5–10 mm overjet undergoing camouflage treatment with bilateral maxillary first-premolar extraction and standardized anchorage reinforcement. External validity is limited for other age groups, more severe malocclusions, non-extraction plans, or vertical growth patterns outside the study range. Generalisability is further constrained by the single-site setting and the use of a bespoke, laser-welded bi-dimensional archwire fabricated by a single technician; outcomes may differ with alternative fabrication methods, commercially produced wires, clinician experience, or different force-delivery systems. Conversely, the use of common clinical mechanics (TPA anchorage, 150 g closed-coil springs, and conventional stainless-steel archwires) supports applicability to similar orthodontic practices. Confirmation in larger, multicenter studies with broader age ranges, diverse clinical settings, and standardized commercial wire systems would strengthen confidence in the observed effects on canine retraction and angulation control.

Conclusions

Sliding retraction of the maxillary canine on a 0.020-inch round posterior segment integrated within a hybrid archwire produced a clinically meaningful acceleration of canine movement, approximately a 43% relative increase in retraction rate compared with conventional rectangular-wire mechanics—and this advantage was supported by longitudinal mixed-effects analysis (F = 28.528; p < 0.001) while the treatment × time interaction was not significant (F = 1.134; p = 0.311), indicating similar temporal trajectories but consistently higher retraction values with the hybrid approach; moreover, the technique provided superior control of mesiodistal canine angulation (significantly lower total tipping and tipping per millimeter, p = 0.007 and p = 0.001, respectively) without producing clinically important changes in canine rotation, anchorage loss, or overbite depth, and without confirmed increases in root resorption after correction for multiple testing, therefore, the hybrid (rectangular anterior + 0.020″ round posterior) strategy offers a pragmatic balance of biomechanical efficiency and angular control during space closure, while routine anchorage planning and clinical monitoring remain advisable.
