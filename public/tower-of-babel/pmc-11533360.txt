Streptococcus canis transcriptomic modifications in host cell entry environments of human keratinocytes
Authors: Haruno Yoshida, Mieko Goto, Yuzo Tsuyuki, Jae-Seok Kim, Takashi Takahashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStreptococcus canis is a commensal bacterium in companion animals. This microorganism can infect humans who have been in deep contact with or bitten by pet dogs, suggesting that the skin/soft tissue is one of infection entry sites. To understand pathological process in human cells, we aimed to determine S. canis transcriptomic changes in invasive environments of human keratinocytes.MethodsWe selected one isolate from candidates with whole-genome sequences, based on re-obtained cell invasion ability (CIA) data into human keratinocytes along with bacterial cytotoxicity. RNA-sequencing was conducted for the samples at baselines and 2 h/5 hr post-inoculation using NovaSeq 6000. Global/differential gene expression analyses [principal component analysis (PCA)/k-means clustering analysis/differentially expressed gene (DEG) analyses] were performed. We classified DEGs into their functional categories. To validate transcriptomic results, we did quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays.ResultsFU1 isolate was selected from seven candidates, based on re-obtained CIA data with less cytotoxicity. Total read bases of 6.17–9.02 Gbp were obtained by RNA-sequencing. PCA and k-means clustering analysis indicated clustering according to their inoculation times. Volcano plots and Venn diagrams revealed that S. canis invasion into keratinocytes produced altered distributions of many genes. Gene ontology enrichment analysis showed most of the gene expressions were downregulated. DEG functional analysis showed the downregulated DEGs belonging to energy production and conversion/carbohydrate transport and metabolism/amino acid transport and metabolism/nucleotide transport and metabolism, with the upregulated DEGs belonging to transcription. qRT-PCR assays for downregulated/upregulated expressions of four genes (pgk–slo/opuAA–kdpB) validated transcriptomic results.ConclusionOur observations suggest that S. canis can downregulate its metabolism-associated gene expressions in human keratinocyte environments. The observed gene expression changes can imply the latent infection in human cells. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms for the latent infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10974-z.Keywords: Streptococcus canis, Transcriptome, Human keratinocyte, Invasion, Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

Introduction

Devriese et al. [1] designated a species of carbohydrate antigenicity group G streptococci from animals as Streptococcus canis in 1986. This microorganism can form large, smooth, gray/white-colored colonies with β-hemolysis on sheep blood agar plates. S. canis constitutes part of the resident microflora of the oropharynx, skin, urogenital tract, and anus in healthy dogs [2]. This bacterium is an emerging pathogen causing self-limiting dermatitis among companion animals (dogs or cats). However, S. canis infection can occasionally result in severe diseases in dogs/cats, including arthritis, toxic shock-like syndrome, septicemia, arthritis, necrotizing fasciitis, and pneumonia [3, 4]. Furthermore, S. canis can infect humans who have been in close contact with companion animals and cause either local or systemic diseases [5]. This microorganism was recovered from two Japanese patients with bacteremia who were in deep contact with or bitten by pet dogs [6, 7]. Moreover, periprosthetic joint infection with S. canis has been described in a man undergoing elective primary total hip arthroplasty [8]; a pet dog had frequently licked his legs, suggesting the skin and soft tissue as one of main infection entry sites. Many Japanese individuals keep dogs or cats in their homes. Additionally, medical institutes and nursing homes are introducing animal-assisted therapy as a mental health service for hospital patients and elderly individuals. It is important for clinicians to be aware of the possibility of S. canis-related infections being underdiagnosed.

Pathogenesis of bacterial infections is initiated with the adhesion of bacteria to host cells. Subsequently, some microorganisms (including Streptococcus pyogenes) have evolved a specialized strategy to survive and evade the immune system (especially antibodies) and β-lactam-class antimicrobial exposures by entering the cells (“intracellular invasion”) [9]. These invasive pathogens are localized to membrane-bound phagosomes. Within a few hours after destroying the phagosome membrane, some microorganisms can invade the cytoplasm, whereas others remain entrapped in compartments [9]. In the former conditions, the pathogenic bacteria can intracellularly survive, multiply, and eventually spread to the adjacent cells. In the latter situations, phagosome fusion with vacuoles can produce intracellular degradation environments, leading to lysis of the microorganisms [9]. Cell invasion ability (CIA) of animal commensals of Streptococcus agalactiae [10], Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis [11], and S. canis [12] has been evaluated using human colon cancer epithelium [Caco-2] and human keratinocyte [HaCaT] cell lines. These results of HaCaT entries by the five high-frequency CIA isolates and four low-frequency CIA isolates revealed the data similar to those of Caco-2 entries [12].

S. canis comparative genomic features based on pan-genome orthologous group analysis have been conducted according to sequence type 9 [13]. The bacterial gene expressions should be clarified under the pathological situations using in vitro/in vivo experiments. Transcriptome analysis [known as RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis] is a comprehensive gene expression method. Hirose et al. [14] has described S. pyogenes transcriptome modifications in the inflammatory environment of necrotizing fasciitis using a mouse model. Additionally, this approach can show upregulation of arginine catabolism to exert S. pyogenes pathogenesis on mouse skin surface [15].

We searched for related manuscripts by entering the following keywords “streptococcus canis, transcriptome” and “streptococcus canis, rna-sequencing” into PubMed database (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). However, there appear to be no adequate hits in related articles (as of 1-Jul-2024). S. canis is one of companion animal commensals. The skin/soft tissue infections are one of main human diseases. To understand the pathological process in human cells, we determined S. canis transcriptomic changes in invasive environments using human keratinocytes.

Materials and methods

Selection of one isolate from candidates with whole-genome sequences (WGSs)

Based on the previous data CIA into human keratinocytes [12] and the presence of WGSs [13] because of WGS necessity to perform RNA-seq, we decided seven isolates of FU1–FU6–FU29–FU53–FU93–FU149–TA4 with WGSs (Nucleotide accession numbers BLIS00000000.1–BLIT00000000.1–BLKN00000000.1–BLKO00000000.1–BLKP00000000.1–BLRR00000000.1–BEWZ00000000.1) as candidates. FU1–FU6–FU29–FU53–FU93–FU149–TA4 were from the cat pus–cat pus–dog vagina–cat nasal cavity–dog pus–dog blood–human blood. Of seven, one isolate was selected based on the re-obtained results of CIA, which were performed for this study over again. National Collection of Type Cultures (NCTC) 12,191(T) with WGS from the bovine mastitis was included as a quality control.

CIA measurement

Supplementary Fig. 1 shows experimental schema to assess CIA from companion animals and humans into human-lineage keratinocytes [HaCaT]. We selected the HaCaT because the human skin is one of main S. canis infection entry sites. CIA measurement was re-performed as previously described [11, 12]. There were the following minor revisions: multiplicity of infection (MOI) used was 10 for mid-log growth phase S. canis based on the bacterial count; isolates resuspended in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) was added onto semi-confluent HaCaT (2–5 × 105 cells/well) and grown in a 12-well culture plate with DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. After 1 h and 4 h of incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline to remove the bacteria that had attached the cell surfaces and had not reached the intracellular invasion. The monolayers were maintained in media containing penicillin (10 U/mL) and gentamicin (100 µg/mL) for 1 h. Whole cells were trypsinized using 0.25% trypsin (containing 1mM EDTA), vortexed, and disrupted with sterile distilled water to obtain the cell extracts. The cell lysates were serially diluted with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline without calcium and magnesium to appropriate concentrations and plated on agar plates containing Todd-Hewitt broth supplemented with yeast extract, to determine the internalized bacterial count 2 h/5 hr after S. canis inoculation. We measured internalized bacterial colony-forming units (CFU)/100 HaCaT cells in 4 wells of every CIA assay, to determine the CIA. Data are expressed as means ± standard deviations CFU/100 HaCaT cells in 4 wells.

Additionally, we conducted the trypan blue staining 2 h after the inoculation to confirm the HaCaT cytotoxicity due to S. canis.

Extracted RNA samples and their quality check

Biological replicates for RNA-seq were performed in duplicate because this is the first challenging study. Supplementary Fig. 2 indicates experimental procedure to extract bacterial RNA samples as previously reported [14]. Keratinocytes, which were maintained in a 100-mm culture dish, were inoculated with MOI 10 of S. canis. The harvested cells were immediately placed in an RNAlater (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). These cells were placed in lysing Matrix D microtubes containing 1.4 mm silica beads (Qbiogene, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with an RLT lysis buffer (RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and homogenized at 6,500 rpm for 45 s using a MagNA lyser (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The lysate was centrifuged, and the obtained pellet was resuspended in lysing Matrix B microtubes containing 0.1 mm silica beads (Qbiogene) with the RLT lysis buffer and homogenized at 6,500 rpm for 60 s using the MagNA lyser. The final lysate was centrifuged, and bacterial RNA was extracted from the collected supernatant with an RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit. We prepared the three-stage extracted RNA samples (baselines, 2 h and 5 h post-inoculation). We evaluated RNA integrity number by using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) as previously documented [14].

Acquisition of RNA-seq data and their global/differential analyses

Bacterial total RNA was treated to remove rRNA using an Ribo-Zero Plus rRNA Depletion Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA). RNA-seq libraries were generated with an Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 100-bp paired-end reads (Macrogen, Daejeon, South Korea) as previously described [14]. Dataset was created in the fastq format. Based on phred-type quality scores (Q20/Q30), the reads were trimmed using quality trimming tool in a Qiagen CLC Genomics Workbench version 8 (Aarhus, Denmark) with default parameters (quality limit/maximum number of ambiguous bases). RNA-seq reads were mapped against the isolate WGS and S. canis NCTC 12,191(T) WGS (accession no. LR134293.1) using the CLC Genomics Workbench. We conducted the normalization using the reads per kb per million reads (RPKM) values.

To assess the global gene expression profiles of the sample as pre-analyses, we conducted principal component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering analysis (k = 4) with RPKM value of each sample using an iDEP 2.0 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu./idep/) as previously reported [14, 16]. Differentially expressed gene (DEG; absolute log2 fold change of > 1, adjusted P value of < 0.1) analyses [14, 17] was performed with the iDEP and RPKM values as differential analyses. Data were visualized using volcano plots (iDEP) and Venn diagrams (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment pathway analysis was conducted with all DEGs. We divided the DEGs top 50 into their functional categories based on a National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) clusters of orthologous gene (COG) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/research/cog) [18]. Of whole DEGs in isolate, we extracted the potential virulence factor-associated DEGs with downregulation/upregulation using a Virulence Factor DataBase (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) [19] and a Victor [20].

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays for validity of DEG analysis data

We performed qRT-PCR assays as previously reported [14]. The remaining RNAs were applied after finishing the RNA-seq data analysis, and cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). qRT-PCR assays were conducted using a Thermal Cycler Dice® Real Time System III (TP800) (Takara Bio Inc.) with a TB Green®Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio Inc.).

Statistical analysis

Continuous CIA variables between isolate and NCTC 12,191(T) and between 2 h and 5 h post-inoculation in the same strain were compared using Student’s/Welch’s t-test following F-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Selection of FU1 isolate based on CIA data

We selected FU1 isolate from seven candidates, based on the re-obtained data regarding CIA into human keratinocytes. FU1 revealed 18.70 ± 2.73 and 9.95 ± 1.80 CFU/100 HaCaT cells (as CIA data) at 2 h and 5 h post-inoculation, respectively. In contrast, NCTC 12,191(T) showed 7.52 ± 2.40 and 2.55 ± 0.85 CFU/100 cells at 2 h and 5 h post-inoculation, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). We found significant differences in the CIA data between FU1 and NCTC 12,191(T) at 2 h/5 hr post-inoculation (Supplementary Fig. 3). There were significant decreases in the CIA data between 2 h and 5 h post-inoculation in FU1/NCTC 12,191(T).

Supplementary Fig. 4 shows the findings of trypan blue staining 2 h after the inoculation and there were no positive HaCaT cells, indicating the less cytotoxicity due to FU1 and NCTC 12,191(T).

Extracted RNA samples and their quality check

Supplementary Table 1 shows extracted RNA samples with their quality check data. We obtained the 12 RNAs consisting of FU1-m-rep1/FU1-m-rep2 (replication samples at baselines), FU1-2 h-rep1/FU1-2 h-rep2 (replication samples at 2 h post-inoculation), FU1-5 h-rep1/FU1-5 h-rep2 (replication samples at 5 h post-inoculation), NCTC-m-rep1/NCTC-m-rep2 (replication samples at baselines), NCTC-2 h-rep1/NCTC-2 h-rep2 (replication samples at 2 h post-inoculation), and NCTC-5 h-rep1/NCTC-5 h-rep2 (replication samples at 5 h post-inoculation). Supplementary Fig. 5 indicates bioanalyzer profiles of total RNA samples extracted from FU1 at baseline and 2 h/5 hr post-inoculation for the RNA quality control. Using 2100 Bioanalyzer, we observed the presence of human-origin 18 S/28S rRNAs with that of Streptococcus-origin 16 S/23S rRNAs.

Obtained RNA-seq data and their global/differential analyses

Supplementary Table 2 summarizes RNA-seq reads data from FU1 and NCTC 12,191(T). The total read bases from 6.17 Gbp to 9.02 Gbp were obtained by RNA-seq. The base quality of each RNA sample reached Q20 and Q30, which accounted for 90% or more, making these RNA-seq results reliable. Using scatter plots for the replication samples, we obtained the adequate R values between FU1-m-rep1 and FU1-m-rep2 (R = 0.995), between FU1-2 h-rep1 and FU1-2 h-rep2 (R = 0.994), between FU1-5 h-rep1 and FU1-5 h-rep2 (R = 0.988), between NCTC-m-rep1 and NCTC-m-rep2 (R = 0.960), between NCTC-2 h-rep1 and NCTC-2 h-rep2 (R = 0.988), and between NCTC-5 h-rep1 and NCTC-5 h-rep2 (R = 0.984).

Discussion

Our observations appear to provide the challenging and attractive RNA-seq data, suggesting S. canis DEG profiles in host cell entry environments of human keratinocytes. Interestingly, there were the upregulated DEGs belonging to transcription at the 2 h–baseline or 5 h–baseline comparisons in FU1 (Fig. 5). In fact, we found the several transcriptional elements of sigma subunit of DNA-directed RNA polymerase [COG0568], transcriptional antiterminator [COG3711], DNA-binding transcriptional regulator of MarR family [COG1846], predicted transcriptional regulator [COG1395], DNA-binding transcriptional regulators containing XRE-family HTH domain [COG1476], transcriptional regulator spxA [NCBI conserved protein domain family PRK13344], and putative transcriptional activator regulator protein. In contrast, S. canis downregulated its metabolism-associated genes during the invasion. Of the downregulated DEGs, seven are shown to encode the pyrimidine salvage PyrR [COG2065], pyrimidine biosynthesis PyrB [COG0540], pyrimidine degradation UraA [COG2233], orotate phosphoribosyltransferase PyrE [COG0461], arginine deiminase ArcA [COG2235], arginine biosynthesis ArgF [COG0078], and carbamate kinase ArcC [COG0549], suggesting significant modifications of arginine metabolic pathway [14, 15].

In an S. pyogenes mouse model of necrotizing fasciitis [14], the upregulated DEGs included molecules associated with virulence [sagA (streptolysin S precursor)–speB (cysteine protease)–spd (secreted DNase)], carbohydrate utilization, amino acid metabolism, trace-metal transport, and vacuolar ATPase transport system. On the other hand, in our S. canis study by cell entry experiment into human keratinocytes, there were the downregulated DEGs belonging to the energy production and conversion, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, amino acid transport and metabolism, nucleotide transport and metabolism, and virulence [hly III (hemolysin III)–slo (streptolysin O)–hlyA1 (hemolysin)]. These findings suggest that the mouse model can reflect active-stage infection and that the in vitro experiment can reflect latent infection. We confirmed the trypan blue staining results 2 h after the inoculation: there were no positive keratinocytes, showing the less possibilities of cytotoxicity by FU1 and NCTC 12,191(T). Therefore, we will also need to establish in vivo S. canis study and to specify transcriptomic modifications (upregulated/downregulated gene expressions) through in vivo investigation referring to the descriptions [14, 15].

Hertzén et al. [21] reported intracellular S. pyogenes in human macrophages that displayed an altered gene expression profile using microarrays and qRT-PCR. To assess early-phase infection involving adaptation to intracellular compartment, the samples were collected 2 h/6 hr post-infection. The majority of differentially regulated genes were associated with metabolic and energy-dependent processes. Crucial upregulated genes in early-phase intracellular S. pyogenes were ihk and irr that encode a two-component gene regulatory system (TCS). Comparison of gene expression of selected genes at 2 h/6 h post-infection revealed a dramatic shift in the regulators over time with downregulation of ihk/irr genes and upregulation of covR/S TCS. We further should focus on overall expression changes of network between regulator gene and regulated gene by RNA-seq in S. canis.

Identifying genetic determinants of S. pyogenes-host interactions has been described in a murine skin epithelium infection model [22]. Dual RNA-seq was performed to assess both bacterial and host gene expression changes at 5 h/24 hr post-infection. The characteristic gene expression changes were associated with metabolic pathways and activation of Rgg2/Rgg3 quorum-sensing pathway. The gene expression changes in murine skin epithelium were related to inflammatory response. Using dual RNA-seq, we may conduct global/differential analyses of host and bacterial gene expression alterations in human keratinocytes and S. canis, to understand host-pathogen interactions.

There are several limitations. We applied trypan blue staining to confirm the HaCaT cytotoxicity. Tunnel staining or Cell Counting-Kit 8 also can be used. Instead of CIA, we should perform transmission electron microscopy to assess the intracellular bacteria. Biological replicates for RNA-seq further need to be performed in triplicate. Sequential RNA-seq approaches to monitor differences in another isolate should be conducted among the same populations to comprehend the pathogenesis of animal bacterium invading human cells, because of the use of a single isolate (FU1). We cannot avoid the potential impact of genetic variability on the observed gene expression changes. FU1 WGS assembly was scaffold, while NCTC 12,191(T) assembly was complete. We could not ideally compare RNA-seq data and their analytical results in FU1 with those in NCTC 12,191(T). Additionally, S. canis has not been registered into a GO Resource (https://geneontology.org). The biological process–cellular component–molecular function, which have been provided by the Resource, are unclear in S. canis. Lastly, we analyzed three-stage RNAs (baselines and 2 h/5 hr post-inoculation). However, the 5 h–2 h comparison produced the poor alteration distributions (small DEG numbers) in FU1/NCTC 12,191(T). The RNA samples at long-stage (24 h post-inoculation) should be investigated in future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our observations support S. canis transcriptomic changes (mainly downregulated gene expressions) in host cell entry environments of human keratinocytes. These observed changes in gene expressions can imply the latent infection in host cells. In our study, S. canis downregulated a variety of genes, especially metabolic pathway-associated genes, while the genes related to transcription were upregulated. However, the evidence supporting S. canis latent infection is limited. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. We should carry out the functional studies to validate the identified DEG roles and investigation of host cell responses to S. canis infection in future.
