Early low-dose dexamethasone is associated with shorter acute symptom duration in Chikungunya virus infection: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wiwat Chancharoenthana, Thansita Bhunyakarnjanarat, Kanyarat Udompornpitak, Wassawon Ariyanon, Asada Leelahavanichkul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChikungunya virus (CHIKV) causes acute febrile illness with severe arthralgia; management is largely supportive and corticosteroid use remains controversial. We evaluated whether early, short-course, low-dose dexamethasone was associated with shorter acute symptom duration during the 2019 Bangkok outbreak.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 187 adults with confirmed CHIKV and severe arthralgia. Thirty-nine received dexamethasone 4 mg/day for 3 days within ≤ 48 h of symptom onset plus standard care; 148 received standard care alone. Primary outcomes were duration of the acute febrile phase and duration of rheumatic manifestations abstracted from records and patient reports. Secondary outcomes included C-reactive protein (CRP) and adverse events. Follow-up laboratory tests occurred at clinician discretion and were summarized in early (days 3–5) and late (days 6–8) windows.ResultsThe dexamethasone group had shorter acute febrile phase (median 3 vs. 5 days, p < 0.001) and rheumatic manifestations (7 vs. 10 days, p < 0.001). At late follow-up, median CRP was lower (20 vs. 35 mg/L, p < 0.001). Adverse events were uncommon and similar (gastrointestinal discomfort 5% vs. 3%, p = 0.45).ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort, early, short-course, low-dose dexamethasone was associated with shorter acute symptom duration and lower inflammatory markers without an observed increase in adverse events. Randomized trials are warranted to determine efficacy, safety, and generalizability.Clinical trialThis study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20201022004) on 22 October 2020.Keywords: Chikungunya virus, Dexamethasone, Observational study, Symptom duration, Arthralgia, C-reactive protein

Introduction

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is an arbovirus transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, causing an acute febrile illness characterized by severe arthralgia, rash, and myalgia. First identified in Tanzania in 1952 [1], CHIKV has since caused outbreaks across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with notable epidemics on Reunion Island in 2005–2006 [2] and in the Americas starting in 2013 [3]. While the acute phase typically resolves within 7–10 days, up to 50% of patients develop chronic arthralgia, significantly impacting quality of life [4].

No specific antiviral treatment exists for CHIKV, leaving management dependent on supportive care with analgesics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). However, these measures often fail to alleviate severe symptoms, prompting consideration of corticosteroids to mitigate the intense inflammatory response in the early phase of infection. Their use remains controversial due to potential risks, such as immunosuppression or enhanced viral replication. A systematic review of 28 clinical management guidelines (CMGs) revealed significant heterogeneity in recommendations, with only four deemed high-quality [5].

Proponents of early corticosteroid use, cited in 39% of CMGs (11/28) [6–16], advocate for a short course in cases refractory to analgesia, including severe joint pain (80% of these guidelines) [6–8, 10–13, 15], highly inflammatory forms like bursitis or synovitis (30%) [6, 15, 16], disabling arthritis/arthralgia (40%) [6, 8, 10, 16], or when NSAIDs are contraindicated (10%) [15]. Prednisolone is frequently recommended, with dosing varying from 10 to 20 mg/day [6, 11, 15] to 0.5 mg/kg/day for severe cases [6, 8, 15, 16], and duration ranging from 5 days [6, 8, 15] to tapering over 1–2 months [6, 16]. Conversely, 43% of CMGs (12/28) [17–28] advise against corticosteroid use in the acute phase, citing lack of evidence [19], no benefit irrespective of administration form [26], or risk of rebound symptoms [28]. This discordance underscores an evidence gap in CHIKV management. Given variation across clinical management guidelines, we selected dexamethasone for this evaluation because it was on formulary at the study sites during the 2019 Bangkok outbreak, allows once-daily dosing due to its longer half-life, and provides potent anti-inflammatory effects with low mineralocorticoid activity. The chosen dose (4 mg/day for 3 days) is roughly equivalent to ~ 26.7 mg/day of prednisolone, facilitating comparison to prednisolone-based recommendations while reflecting local practice.

This study assessed whether early, short-term, low-dose dexamethasone was associated with shorter duration of acute symptoms in CHIKV patients with severe arthralgia during the 2019 Bangkok outbreak, aiming to inform clinical practice amid these conflicting recommendations.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University (MUTM 2020-040-01) and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20201022004). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective study was conducted from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2019. Patients were identified through electronic medical records using the following inclusion criteria: [1] confirmed CHIKV infection, defined as a positive quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) for CHIKV RNA or serological detection of anti-CHIKV IgM/IgG antibodies [2], severe arthralgia requiring hospitalization or significantly impairing daily activities, and [3] age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: [1] dengue virus co-infection by qRT-PCR or rapid diagnostic test [2], atypical or severe acute CHIKV (e.g., multi-organ failure, meningoencephalitis, hemorrhage) [3], immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, active chemotherapy or malignancy-related immunosuppression, prior solid-organ or stem-cell transplantation, chronic systemic corticosteroids, or biologic immunomodulators) [4], pregnancy, and [5] incomplete records precluding assessment of primary outcomes. Notably, a total of 187 patients met the inclusion criteria: 39 received dexamethasone in addition to standard care, and 148 received standard care alone.

Intervention

The dexamethasone group received dexamethasone 4 mg once daily for 3 consecutive days, initiated within ≤ 48 h of symptom onset, in addition to standard care (analgesics such as paracetamol, NSAIDs when not contraindicated, hydration, rest). Dexamethasone was selected based on site formularies, clinician familiarity, once-daily dosing, and anti-inflammatory potency (4 mg dexamethasone ≈ 26.7 mg prednisolone). The control group received standard care alone. Treatment allocation reflected routine clinical care. Early dexamethasone was generally considered when patients presented within ≤ 48 h, had severe pain refractory to initial analgesia, or had NSAID contraindications; ultimately, the decision rested with the treating physician.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes comprised of the duration of the acute febrile phase (time from fever onset [temperature ≥ 38 °C] to resolution [< 38 °C for ≥ 24 h]) and duration of rheumatic manifestations (time from arthralgia onset to complete resolution or significant improvement, per patient self-report and clinical evaluation). ‘Severe arthralgia’ was defined as arthralgia requiring hospitalization or significantly impairing activities of daily living at presentation. ‘Rheumatic manifestations’ encompassed patient-reported arthralgia with or without stiffness, swelling, or tenosynovitis documented in the clinical record. Duration of the febrile phase and rheumatic manifestations was measured from symptom onset to complete resolution or patient-reported ‘much improved,’ documented by clinical evaluation when available. Meanwhile, the secondary outcomes were incidence of adverse events (e.g., gastrointestinal discomfort, hyperglycemia, secondary infections) associated with dexamethasone as well as changes in C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and hematological parameters (white blood cell and platelet counts).

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized chart abstraction form. Collected variables included demographics (age, gender), clinical symptoms (fever, arthralgia, rash, conjunctivitis, myalgia, headache), laboratory results (qRT-PCR for CHIKV, anti-CHIKV IgM/IgG, white blood cell count, platelet count, CRP levels), treatment details (medications, dosage, duration), and outcome measures (symptom onset/resolution dates, adverse events). In addition to electronic medical records, we contacted next of kin/caregivers to verify key dates or outcomes for 28/187 patients (15.0%). Variables not documented were considered absent.”

Case definition and laboratory testing

Specimens

Serum or EDTA plasma collected as part of routine clinical care at presentation/admission and at subsequent in-hospital follow-up—ordered at the attending physician’s discretion—were used for molecular and serologic testing. Because sampling times varied in routine practice, follow-up specimens were grouped into prespecified analysis windows for reporting: early (calendar days 3–5 from presentation) and late (days 6–8).

RNA extraction

Viral RNA was extracted from 140 µL of serum/plasma using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

CHIKV qRT-PCR

Detection of chikungunya virus RNA used the RealStar® Chikungunya RT-PCR Kit 2.0 (altona Diagnostics, Hamburg, Germany) on a LightCycler® 480 Instrument II (Roche Diagnostics). Thermal cycling followed the kit IFU: reverse transcription 55 °C for 20:00, initial denaturation 95 °C for 2:00, then 45 cycles of 95 °C for 0:15, 55 °C for 0:45 (fluorescence acquisition), and 72 °C for 0:15; run volume 30 µL (master mix 20 µL + sample 10 µL).

DENV qRT-PCR (co-infection exclusion)

Dengue RNA was tested with the CDC DENV-1–4 Real-Time RT-PCR Assay (RUO) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Dx/7500 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Instrument settings followed the CDC package insert: Stage 1 (RT) 50 °C for 30 min; Stage 2 95 °C for 2 min; Stage 3 (×45 cycles) 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min with acquisition at 60 °C; run mode “Standard 7500,” 25 µL reaction volume.

Rapid dengue testing

Point-of-care testing used the SD BIOLINE Dengue Duo (NS1 Ag + IgM/IgG) immunochromatographic assay (Abbott Rapid Diagnostics; formerly Standard Diagnostics, Suwon, Republic of Korea) on serum/plasma per the instructions for use.

CHIKV serology

CHIKV IgM/IgG were measured by EUROIMMUN Anti-Chikungunya Virus ELISA (EUROIMMUN Medizinische Labordiagnostika AG, Lübeck, Germany). Results were interpreted per manufacturer: positive index > 1.1 (borderline 0.8–1.1).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics: means ± standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. The Mann-Whitney U test compared primary outcomes between groups due to non-normal distributions. Adverse event incidence was analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Changes in CRP levels and hematological parameters were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

This retrospective cohort study assessed 187 patients with confirmed Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) infection from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2019. Of these, 39 patients received dexamethasone in addition to standard care, while 148 received standard care alone.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were similar between groups (Table 1). The mean age was 45 ± 10 years in the dexamethasone group and 47 ± 11 years in the control group (p = 0.34). Sex distribution (55% male vs. 52% male, p = 0.72) and comorbidities (20% in both, p = 1.00) showed no differences. Clinical symptoms, including fever (77% in both), rash (60% vs. 65%, p = 0.55), myalgia (46% in both), headache (15% vs. 16%, p = 0.88), and conjunctivitis (10% vs. 12%, p = 0.75), were comparable. Severe arthralgia was present in 100% of patients in both groups.

Primary outcomes

The dexamethasone group exhibited significantly shorter symptom durations (Table 2). The median acute febrile phase lasted 3 days (IQR 2–4) in the dexamethasone group versus 5 days (IQR 4–6) in the control group (p < 0.001). Rheumatic manifestations resolved in a median of 7 days (IQR 5–9) with dexamethasone compared to 10 days (IQR 8–12) with standard care (p < 0.001).

Secondary outcomes

No hyperglycemia meeting predefined threshold (glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL) and no secondary infections were documented during acute hospitalization in either group; mild gastrointestinal discomfort occurred in 5% vs. 3% (p = 0.45).

CRP was available at presentation and at clinician-directed follow-up; for analysis we summarized measurements obtained during the early (days 3–5) and late (days 6–8) windows. Availability was baseline 33/39 (84.6%) vs. 124/148 (83.8%); early 27/39 (69.2%) vs. 100/148 (67.6%); late 33/39 (84.6%) vs. 124/148 (83.8%). Hematology

Complete blood counts (CBCs) were obtained as part of routine clinical care at presentation and at clinician-directed follow-up. Because timing varied, follow-up measurements were summarized within an early analysis window (calendar days 3–5 from presentation). CBC testing beyond day 5 was uncommon; therefore, a late (days 6–8) window is not reported. Availability within the early window was 84/187 (44.9%) on day 3, 41/187 (21.9%) on day 4, and 62/187 (33.2%) on day 5; some patients had > 1 measurement within the window. When multiple results were available, the earliest value in the window was used for any window-level summaries; day-specific medians are shown below.

At presentation, median WBC was 5,100 cells/µL (IQR 3,650–5,850) in the dexamethasone group versus 5,500 cells/µL (3,100–6,800) in controls (p = 0.45). Median platelets were 220 × 10³/µL (150–240) versus 220 × 10³/µL (145–270) (p = 0.80).

Overall, these data suggest a trend toward faster hematologic recovery with early dexamethasone that warrants confirmation in prospective studies.

Safety of corticosteroid use

Dexamethasone use was well-tolerated, with no progression to severe CHIKV cases or meningitis observed in either group. Adverse events were minimal, limited to mild gastrointestinal discomfort (5% dexamethasone vs. 3% control, p = 0.45), indicating a favorable safety profile for corticosteroid use in this cohort.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, we observed an association between early, short, low-dose dexamethasone (4 mg/day × 3 days) and shorter duration of fever and rheumatic manifestations, compared with standard care alone. We also observed lower CRP at late follow-up and no excess in adverse events, suggesting that corticosteroids may effectively attenuate the systemic inflammatory response during the acute phase of CHIKV infection.

Our findings are directionally consistent with a South Indian randomized parallel-group trial in acute chikungunya arthritis, where adding prednisolone (10 mg/day) to aceclofenac improved pain and function versus aceclofenac alone; however, its 6-week regimen differs from our 3-day, low-dose dexamethasone approach, limiting direct comparability [29]. In contrast, Caldas C, 2021 [30] reported no efficacy of high-dose corticosteroids in the subacute phase, highlighting the critical role of timing and dosage in corticosteroid therapy for CHIKV. Our results suggest that early, low-dose intervention may offer a therapeutic advantage over later, higher-dose regimens, potentially by mitigating the cytokine storm associated with acute CHIKV infection. This is further corroborated by the significant reduction in CRP levels at late follow-up (days 6–8) in the dexamethasone group (median 20 mg/L vs. 35 mg/L, p < 0.001), indicating a dampened inflammatory response [31].

A notable trend toward faster hematological recovery was observed in the dexamethasone group, with higher median white blood cell (WBC) counts (e.g., 7,000 vs. 6,500 cells/µL by day 4, p = 0.15) and platelet counts (e.g., 270 vs. 250 × 10³/µL by day 4, p = 0.10), though these differences did not reach statistical significance. This aligns with the known immunomodulatory effects of corticosteroids, which may accelerate normalization of hematological parameters [32]. However, the small sample size of the dexamethasone group (n = 39) and the retrospective design limit definitive conclusions about these secondary outcomes.

The safety profile of dexamethasone was favorable, with adverse events limited to mild gastrointestinal discomfort in 5% of the dexamethasone group versus 3% in the control group (p = 0.45), and no progression to severe CHIKV manifestations or meningitis. This addresses concerns about immunosuppression or exacerbation of viral replication, which have historically restricted corticosteroid use in acute viral infections [33]. Our findings suggest that a short, low-dose regimen may minimize these risks in CHIKV infection, though larger studies are needed for confirmation.

Heterogeneity in corticosteroid recommendations for CHIKV management is well-documented. A systematic review of 28 clinical management guidelines (CMGs) found that only 19% supported short-course corticosteroids for severe joint pain refractory to analgesia [5]. The Brazilian Society of Rheumatology and the Pan-American League of Associations of Rheumatology – Central American, Caribbean and Andean Rheumatology Association (ACCAR) advocate corticosteroid use for severe arthralgia in the subacute phase [10, 26]. Our study extends this discourse by proposing a role for corticosteroids in the acute phase, particularly for patients with severe arthralgia, challenging the 43% of CMGs that advise against their use in this period due to insufficient evidence or potential rebound effects [5].

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Our retrospective design limits causal inference, and unmeasured confounding may persist despite similar baseline characteristics. We did not collect long-term outcomes, so we could not assess post-acute rebound or chronic arthralgia, outcomes that are clinically relevant in CHIKV. Short-course corticosteroids could theoretically precipitate rebound in some contexts; prospective follow-up with standardized patient-reported outcomes is needed.

Future directions

Prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trials are needed to determine whether early, short-course, low-dose dexamethasone provides net clinical benefit in acute CHIKV. Trials should enroll patients early (≤ 48 h), compare pragmatic regimens (e.g., dexamethasone 4 mg once daily for 3 days vs. placebo, with optional prednisolone-equivalent arms), standardize background analgesia, and use blinded outcome assessment. Primary endpoints should be time to sustain defervescence and time to clinically meaningful improvement in joint pain/function; secondary endpoints should include functional recovery, days of activity loss, analgesic use, inflammatory markers and safety, including hyperglycemia, secondary infection, and potential symptom rebound. To test whether early inflammatory attenuation reduces persistent arthralgia, follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months with embedded biomarker sub-studies is essential [34].

Given the heterogeneity of current clinical management guidelines, harmonized core outcome sets and standardized treatment algorithms would facilitate evidence synthesis and implementation across settings [5]. In parallel, pragmatic implementation studies—addressing feasibility during outbreaks, clinician decision support, and cost-effectiveness—and ethically inclusive recruitment (e.g., older adults; atypical or severe presentations; pregnant or immunocompromised individuals) will improve generalizability and speed translation into practice.

Conclusion

Early, short-term, low-dose dexamethasone may serve as a beneficial adjunctive therapy in managing acute CHIKV infection with severe arthralgia, attenuate symptom duration and inflammation without increasing adverse events. These findings provide a foundation for future RCTs to refine clinical management strategies for this debilitating disease.
