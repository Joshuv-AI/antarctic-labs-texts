Time domain electromagnetic survey in deep mine galleries for mineral exploration
Authors: Mamadou Cherif Diallo, Lizhen Cheng, Chong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Time-domain ElectroMagnetic (TDEM) methods are central to mineral exploration, yet the detectability of targets at depths of several kilometers remains limited or in most cases impossible for surface deployments. We evaluate a deep-gallery approach that uses a small transmitter loop in these infrastructures to enhance excitation of deep conductors. Through 3D modeling, a 250 m by 5 m gallery loop was designed and compared its primary field to that of a large 2000 m by 2000 m surface loop. We further investigated the secondary magnetic field generated by nearby conductors to assess detectability and response characteristics in whole-space conditions typical of underground settings. Field tests were conducted at the LaRonde mine (Abitibi, Québec) at ~ 3 km depth using a gallery transmitter (Tx) and a borehole receiver (Rx) configuration. Modeling indicates that, at depth, the small gallery loop can produce a stronger effective primary field than a large surface loop; survey design for this gallery loop should also prioritize increasing the number of turns over extending loop length. The secondary magnetic field modeling shows a polarity change in the horizontal components whether the conductor is located above, below, or on both sides of the loop, providing a diagnostic tool for interpretation. The measurements at LaRonde mine validate two known lenses and reveal an in-hole anomaly at 700 m, corroborating the feasibility and interpretive value of deep-gallery TDEM for exploring deep mineral deposits.

Introduction

The global need for mineral resources has increased significantly in recent decades, from 26.7 to 100 billion tons between 1970 and 2017, and could reach 185 billion tons by 20501. The mining industry is facing a major challenge related to the depletion of shallow resources, as exploration efforts have been mainly directed toward known mineralization zones and shallow resources2. This requires increased mineral exploration to discover new mineral deposits that may lie at greater depths. The industry is obliged to innovate, develop new measurement systems, adapt existing ones, and establish new methodologies to explore deep deposits. One of the challenges related to the exploration of deep deposits is the existence over the majority of the Abitibi Province of a thin variable Quaternary overburden, and furthermore in certain geological contexts a Proterozoic cover overlying the Archean formations in Abitibi3,4, cover which can reach 700 m thickness in its western part. It may also be geographic or climatic contexts, such as exploration beneath sands in the desert5, or the increased interest in exploration for critical minerals localized beneath the seafloor6,7, where the water layer plays the a masking effect as some conductive sediments (i.e. clay and till in Canada, weathered and saline regolith in Australia) in continental environments. Among the plethora of geophysical methods used in mineral exploration, for example VMS (Volcanogenic Massive Sulfide) deposits exploration, time domain electromagnetic (TDEM) method is widely used. This technique offers the possibility of doing the measurements from the sky - airborne surveys8–12, from the ground surface13–15 or in boreholes16–18. Considerable effort has been expended in the processing and interpretation of TDEM data over the past decades, including the development of numerical simulation algorithms for forward modeling or data inversion19–26. Although these studies are important, they do not address the limitation regarding the depth of investigation, nor do they sufficiently mitigate the masking effect of thick and/or conductive sedimentary cover when present.

To address these two issues, developments have been made in the field, and new measurement systems have been implemented. The InfiniTEM27,28, uses multiple rectangular loops in series and offers the possibility of having the receivers on the ground surface or in a borehole. This configuration allows the generation of a strong primary field sufficient to reduce the masking effect of conductive overburden, but also to sufficiently excite the conductors in the subsurface29–31. Malo-Lalande and Milkereit32 present a case study where this technique successfully identified a deposit that could not be detected by an Airborne TDEM survey performed in the same area. Guoqiang et al.33 propose a short-offset time-domain (SOTEM) approach for deep exploration. This approach was used by Chen et al.34 to identify embedded zones in a deep coal deposit (1000 to 1200 m), and in another study, they used it to detect an iron deposit under a conductive overburden35.

These innovations are very important and make it possible to improve exploration methods. But they remain limited when the targeted depth of investigation is several kilometers. Some active or abandoned mines have depths greater than or equal to 3 km, such as the LaRonde mine in Abitibi36–38. In these situations, the TDEM method with a surface configuration will be impotent. To meet this need for deep exploration, Cheng et al.39 propose to make the measurements from deep galleries. Few studies have been conducted based on this approach, at least none for the previously mentioned depth (3 km). Nevertheless, there are applications of the concept in a potash mine to identify salt deposits using horizontal layer models40. Li et al.41 also used numerical modeling to study the effectiveness of the approach to detect karst cavities in a tunnel construction area. It is also used during tunneling operations for detecting water-bearing geological units in the tunnel front and the side walls. Some numerical simulations42,43 show how TDEM can assess water flow in tunnel vicinities, while44,45 highlight effective applications of the method to detect underground water close to tunnel construction site and used a system that combine Magnetic Resonance Sounding and TDEM to investigate the same issues46. A combination of modelling (pre-design of field measurement) and field application in water-rich coal mining areas to ensure human and operational safety47,48. Xue et al.49 present a review of electrical and electromagnetic survey design developed for the same purpose. Tang et al.50 introduced the concept of a small multi-turn coils system used to detect underground water and coal ore bodies at the vicinity of the mining galleries, which allowed the detection of targets 80 m around the galleries. This approach naturally solves the conductive overburden problem, while allowing to explore deep structures. One of the problems that could arise is the interference of facilities or mining infrastructures, Dai et al.51 address this issue by performing measurements before surveys to characterizing the ambient electromagnetic noise and propose a denoising methodology. On the other hand, TDEM measurements in galleries mean a small loop because the width of the galleries is usually about 5 m, which implies a less intense primary field compared to the case of a large loop at the ground surface. Hence this study aims to define the parameters of such small loop through numerical modeling to design an underground survey, and ultimately to establish a procedure for interpreting underground measurements.

Method and theoretical background

The time domain electromagnetic method (TDEM) is based on the generation of a primary magnetic field from a transmitter loop with an alternative current. The variation over time of this primary field will excite the conductive bodies in the subsurface and create eddy currents within conductors (Fig. 1b). Then a secondary magnetic field is created from this process and measured by a receiver52. The measurement system can be arranged in several ways (Fig. 1a), in the air (Tx1-Line 1) known as Airborne TEM, on the ground surface (Tx2) with a receiver on the surface (Line 2), or in a borehole (BH1). Ultimately, the measurement system can be set in deep mining galleries, with a transmitting loop in the gallery (Tx3) and the receiver in the gallery (Line3) or in a borehole (BH2). Airborne and ground TEM systems have been extensively studied and their applications in mineral exploration are widespread. Therefore, the focus of this study is exclusively limited to the development of a TDEM exploration strategy from deep mining galleries.

B-field analysis for loop in the gallery

Conceptual model setting

The secondary magnetic field (B) induced by the underground conductors, following their excitation by a primary field (H), can be calculated by forward modeling. To do this, it is necessary to define the geological model and to associate configuration parameters representative of a given transmitter-receiver measurement system with it. In this study, the geological model shown in Fig. 7a was defined. It consists of a homogeneous layer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\rho\:}_{b}$$\end{document}) of electrical resistivity of 105 Ωm representing unaltered basement rocks, a gallery 2000 m long at the depth of 1000 m, with a transversal section XZ of 5 m by 10 m (Fig. 7b), a conductive body with dimensions of 500 m by 250 m and a thickness of 50 m (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\rho\:}_{c}$$\end{document}) having an electrical resistivity of 2 Ωm which can be associated with mineralization such as massive sulfides.

Conceptual model setting for the secondary magnetic field modeling, (a) 3D model showing the location of four conductors and Line 3 in the gallery, (b) visualization of the gallery and a small loop (Tx3) configured in it, (c) section view of five conductors locations and three designed boreholes.

This model was generated with the software Maxwell considering a hexahedral discretization, which was refined in the center of the model to insert the gallery (Fig. 7c). The conductive body (C1) is located perpendicularly to the main axis of the gallery, which corresponds to the zone defined as the ideal localization of the area to investigate compared to the loop axis (Fig. 5b). For this forward modeling, the primary field transmission parameters were set up by considering the previous modeling results. Specifically, the shortest loop length (250 m) with 5 turns (Hg1, Table 1), in which a current of 30 A is transmitted. These parameters represent the worst case in the different scenarios studied (Table 1). As for the receiver, it is defined by the place where the measurements are taken. They can be made in a gallery (Line 3, Fig. 7a, b) or in boreholes (Fig. 7c). The geological model and the setup parameters of the measurement system are the inputs of Loki algorithm (3D simulation code) based on finite element discretization24, which has been parallelized54 to reduce the calculation time.

Effect of receiver positions

As mentioned previously, the measurements can be made in galleries or boreholes, so in the first case of the simulation of the B-field, these two situations are assessed. The focus is on the depth of investigation depending on whether the receiver is in a gallery or in a borehole. To do this, the conductor in position C1, which is located at a distance of 100 m laterally and vertically from the gallery, then are moved to positions C2, C3, and C4, which are respectively 250 m, 500 m, and 750 m from the gallery (Fig. 7c). With a receiver in the gallery (Line 3), the results of the vertical component of the B-field are presented in Fig. 8.

First, a model without conductors was simulated to serve as a reference and to identify the effect of the conductors (Fig. 8a). It can be seen that the conductors at positions C1 (Fig. 8b) and C2 (Fig. 8c) produce strong B-field response, compared to the response of the initial model (Fig. 8a), while for the conductor at position C3 (Fig. 8c), the measured intensity of B-field is close and similar to the response of the initial model. So, for this configuration, a conductor can be detected if only located in a cylinder of 250 m radius around the loop. When measurements are made in the boreholes considering the different positions (BH1, BH2, and BH3) in Fig. 7c, several observations were made (Fig. 9). Firstly, comparing the measurements made from the gallery with the measurements made in the borehole, and more specifically in BH2 (Fig. 9a, b and c, and Fig. 9d), which presents a situation where the borehole crosses the conductors, the four responses highlight strong B-field record and different shapes of the anomalies due to size of the conductor located above (Fig. 9a) or below (Fig. 9b) the borehole.

Whole-space modeling

TDEM measurements in underground galleries imply a situation with geological units on both sides of the measurement system (Tx and Rx). In this case, a half-space geological model no longer corresponds to the problem, but rather a whole-space model. The conductor can be above or below the measurement system, or on both sides simultaneously. These three scenarios are addressed by considering the conductor C1 (below) and C5 (above) separately and simultaneously, but also the measurement Line 3 in the gallery. The three components of the B-field for the different cases are shown in Fig. 10. On the vertical components, the only difference is in the intensity of the B-field, the maximum is around 0.1 nT (Fig. 10a1) for the single above conductor (C5), 0.15 nT (Fig. 10b1) for the below (C1) conductor, and 0.3 nT (Fig. 10c1) for the two conductors above and below which is double of the highest value mentioned previously.

On the radial and tangential components, sign changes are observed depending on the position of the conductor compared to the gallery. The sign of the radial component changes from negative to positive for the above (Fig. 10a2) conductor and inversely for the conductor below (Fig. 10b2), while on the tangential component, the only difference is that the anomaly is positive for the above conductor (Fig. 10a3) and negative for the below conductor (Fig. 10b3). Measurements in deep mine galleries

The effectiveness of the approach recommended in this study for the exploration of deep deposits is subject to a process of field testing. TDEM measurements were conducted at the LaRonde mine between 10 and 14 June 2019. This mine is located about 50 km from the City of Rouyn-Noranda (Canada), it is one of the deepest mines in the world still in operation, with an exploitation depth of more than 3 km. These measurements were carried out in a 300 m long gallery at a depth of 3 km, which coincides with a zone where two main mineralized structures Zone 6 and Zone 19–20 are known (Fig. 11a and b). The transmission system consists of 6 turns of a loop having 260 m by 4.5 m as dimensions and connected to a Geonics TM57 transmitter. Through this system, a current of 15.3 A is transmitted in the loop, which gives a total of 91.8 A with the 6 turns of the loop. The waveform of this current is given in Fig. 11c, a trapezoidal shape was used with a turn-on and turn-off time of 1.2 ms and 0.8 ms respectively, for a total transmission time (On-time) of 16.667 ms, and an off-time of 16.667 ms (Fig. 11c). The actual measurements were made in the two holes (BH93 and BH97) that intersected the mineralized zone (Zone 19–20), using the Geonics BH43-3 borehole probe and the SMARTem24 receiver, which is synchronized with the transmitter.

The collected data in the two boreholes allow the detection of both mineralized zones. In BH93 the measures are performed only over 400 m because the hole was blocked at that location. Nevertheless, the effect of Zone 6 is strongly predominant over the first 200 m (Fig. 12c). The proximity of Zone 6 to the measurement system (Tx & Rx) produced a masking effect as would have occurred with surface measurements and conductive overburden. This effect generated strong response of the time derivative of the B-field (dB/dt) over 200 m (Fig. 12b and c), but this did not prevent the detection of the mineralized Zone 19–20 intersected between 700 and 800 m depth in borehole BH97 (Fig. 12b).

Conclusions

Traditionally TDEM measurements are made using transmitters on the surface, transporting this Tx several km underground opens a new phase of deep exploration. To date, no study demonstrates the use of this approach at a depth of approximately 3 km and oriented toward mining exploration. Hence the proposal presented in this study is for the use of a small TEM loop in a deep gallery to explore deep mineral resources.

A series of simulations of the primary field based on the use of a small loop in a deep gallery was carried out, with a loop dimension of 5 m in width and a variable length (250 m, 500 m, and 1000 m). These models showed the possibility of having a primary field superior to that of a large loop (2000 m by 2000 m) on the surface, over at least 740 m of the gallery with a 250 m long loop. In the design of the loop, the number of turns should be prioritized over the length of the loop.

Simulations of the secondary field allow us to study certain characteristics of the response of conductors according to different situations. Among others, the change in polarity of the signal according to the position of the conductors in relation to the transmitter-receiver pair. These simulations also confirmed that the use of a receiver in a borehole offered a greater depth of investigation compared to measurements in a gallery.

Finally, the strategy of starting with a design and validation phase using numerical modeling, followed by measurements carried out in a very deep gallery, ensures success. These measurements confirmed two massive sulfide lenses located in the study area with an off-hole anomaly at the beginning of the profiles and another in-hole anomaly more than 700 m deep. Another off-hole anomaly unknown prior to this study has been identified. This study validated the feasibility of using the TDEM method in deep galleries.
