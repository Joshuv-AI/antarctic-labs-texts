Balanced Dynamics in Strongly Coupled Networks Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 24, 2026.
Cristobal Quiñinao, Jonathan Touboul

Abstract
Many mathematical models of interacting agents assume that individual interactions scale down in proportion to the network size, ensuring that the combined input received from the network does not diverge. In theoretical neuroscience, Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk proposed in 1996 that, should these scalings be violated (and under appropriate conditions), the system may not diverge but rather approach a balanced state where the inputs to each neuron compensate each other (in neuroscience, where inhibitory currents compensate the excitatory ones). We come back to this observation and formulate here a mathematical conjecture for the occurrence of such behaviors in general stochastic systems of interacting agents. From a mathematical viewpoint, this conjecture can be viewed as a double-limit problem in the space of probability measures, which we discuss in detail, as it provides several possible mathematical avenues for proving this result. We provide some numerical and theoretical explorations of the conjecture in classical models of neuronal networks. Moreover, we provide a complete proof of an asymptotic result consistent with one of the double-limit problems in a one-dimensional model with separable coupling inspired by models of chemically-coupled neurons. This proof relies on asymptotic methods, and particularly desingularization techniques used in some PDEs, that we apply here to the mean-field limit of the network as the coupling is made to diverge. From the applications viewpoint, this theory provides an alternative, minimalistic explanation for the widely observed balance of excitation and inhibition in the cerebral cortex not requiring the assumption of the existence of complex regulatory mechanisms.

Introduction

A number of classical models in physics and biology rely on the description of macroscopic behaviors emerging from the coordinated activity of a large number of interacting agents, often marked by randomness. Boltzmann’s kinetic theory of thermodynamics, which relates the stochastic movements of each atom composing the gas to thermodynamic quantities such as temperature and pressure, constitutes an emblematic example

In these systems, randomness arises in the occurrence and timing of various ecological events that control population sizes, including birth, death, encounters between prey and predators, precipitation, and fires. These phenomena combine to reveal the emergence of specific ecosystems, and some of the core modern theoretical questions studied are the robustness of ecosystems, critical transitions and early warning signals

From the theoretical viewpoint, the question of finding macroscopic, effective equations to describe large-scale statistical systems has attracted a lot of interest from mathematicians and statistical physicists alike. A variety of techniques have been proposed for deriving macroscopic equations describing the collective behavior of a large system of interacting, stochastic agents. Most have in common the identification of averaging effects resulting from the combination of a large number of random contributions, a
mean-field
of interaction, which is usually a deterministic term that describes the combined impact of the whole system on a given agent. For these approaches to be valid, all require the assumption that individual interactions scale with the network size. In detail, those techniques assume that the amplitude of pairwise interactions becomes smaller as the network size increases. Under these assumptions, in the asymptotic regime where the network size diverges, each neuron interacts with the network through the superposition of a large number of small contributions, which converges (or is approximated) through a classical limit theorem (law of large numbers, or central limit theorem). These assumptions are often justified by the necessity of avoiding divergence of the interaction term: in the absence of scaling, the interaction term will be on the order of the network size and would diverge in the large network limit. The most common choice for the scaling of the interaction term consists in considering that synaptic connections are inversely proportional to the network size, which results in more-or-less classical versions of McKean-Vlasov theory and limiting behaviors

About 30 years ago, a celebrated and pioneering paper by Haim Sompolinsky and Carl van Vreeswijk

In detail, Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk’s paper investigated the dynamics of a neural network system composed of two neural populations: excitatory cells, which, when they activate, tend to activate other cells, and inhibitory neurons that, when they activate, tend to decrease the activity of the cells they are connected to. In such a system, Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk suggested that, in the absence of mean-field rescaling, the network activity would quickly evolve to reach a state where large excitatory inputs to any given neuron would be perfectly counterbalanced by an equally large inhibitory input. This regime where excitation and inhibition balance each other is referred to as the
balanced regime

From the applications viewpoint, identifying which scaling is more consistent with the systems considered would be essential to assess the relevance of the Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk regime. Typically, these scalings are introduced formally, and experimental studies estimating the amplitude of the interactions as a function of the number of interacting agents are scarce. A notable exception is the work of Barral and Reyes
[
10
]
that directly estimated in neural networks the relationship between the strength of interaction and the number of inputs received by neurons. This experimental

was made possible thanks to their ability to observe dissociated cells generate networks in vitro. In their experimental preparation, they observed the spontaneous development of axons and dendrites from initially dissociated cells, spontaneously yielding a network. From these preparations, the authors were able to measure, for each neuron, the number
n

J

[

). This observation further highlights the importance of understanding the dynamics of statistical systems in this regime.

Mathematically, this is certainly an interesting problem to investigate. Indeed, in addition to possible averaging effects arising in more classical asymptotic problems in large-scale networks, one also needs to handle a possible rapid shift of the network activity to a balanced state. Mathematical results in this direction are scarce. In a somewhat related work, we showed that mean-field limits of neuronal networks with diverging coupling in the context of neural networks with electrical synapses lead to clamping of the solutions onto a Dirac mass where the interaction term vanished
[
60
]
. While not described as such in that manuscript, this result provided a mathematical proof for a particular type of convergence to a balanced regime. However, the particular form of coupling used in that paper, diffusive coupling, did not differentiate between excitation and inhibition, and instead forced neurons to agree on a common voltage, leading to the total input effectively canceling out. In this sense, the network achieves a form of balance in the input each neuron receives. From a mathematical point of view, this particular choice of coupling was also instrumental in deriving analytical results, and generalizing the approach to different types of coupling, with excitatory and inhibitory neural populations, is a highly nontrivial task. In
[
60
]
, the proof of convergence to a balanced regime focused on the analysis of a mean-field equation where the coupling coefficient was made to diverge. The sequence of probability measures was then studied using desingularization techniques (Hopf-Cole methods) under the assumption that initial conditions showed a Gaussian concentration to a Dirac mass. This blow-up method allowed showing that the sequence of measures collapsed to a Dirac mass, and that the blow-up profile was Gaussian. Following this work, an alternative approach was proposed, using Wasserstein distances, and used to lift the assumption on initial conditions
[
15
]
or achieve a stronger notion of convergence
[
14
]
, where interactions were extended to include a dependence in space. All methods used in these papers strongly rely on the particular choice of connectivity made, and all relied on the study of sequences of measures describing a mean-field limit of the system. Here, we go beyond the particular case and formulate this problem in mathematical terms by proposing that this observation is one facet of a general mathematical conjecture on asymptotic regimes of strongly connected networks.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces a general setting for the study, and formulates our conjecture. Section
3
provides two applications of the conjecture to neurosciences, and provides numerical simulations of these regimes in a few different settings. Section
4
discusses different possible angles to approach the conjecture theoretically, by introducing it as a double-limit problem. This yields two distinct approaches. The first limit consists of considering what happens at very short timescales (typically, at timescales inversely proportional to the rate of divergence of the interaction term). We suggest that a non-stochastic dynamical system governs the dynamics at these timescales, with all variables fixed except those affected by the divergence of the interaction. From this perspective, the balanced regime may naturally appear as a dynamical equilibrium of the system on short timescales. The other limit involves first considering that the network size diverges (under appropriate scaling assumptions on the coupling coefficients) and then taking the interaction term to infinity, thereby prioritizing the accounting for collective effects before considering the emergence of balance. The latter framework is formalized in section
5
, which provides a complete proof supporting the result stated in the conjecture. The proof relies on functional analysis techniques and addresses a particular system given by a one-dimensional equation with separable coupling. The details of the proof are provided in the Appendix. We conclude by reviewing the main challenges and the relevance of this regime, particularly its applications to neurosciences.

2
Mathematical framework and conjecture

In the original work of Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk
[
83
]
, the authors considered a system composed of two populations: excitatory neurons that, when they activate, tend to increase the activity of other neurons, and inhibitory neurons that, when they activate, reduce the activity of other cells. Each of these populations consists of a large number of neurons, and these neurons interact through a random, sparse network. Making the problem slightly more general, we define here an abstract model of
N
stochastic interacting agents, distributed into
P
different populations labeled
\alpha\in\{1\cdots,P\}
. The type of interactions between neurons depends on the population to which they belong (these generalize the excitatory/inhibitory populations described in the original work of Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk
[
83
]
). Each agent’s activity is defined by a state variable,
(x^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, which is a stochastic process taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This multi-dimensional vector describes the dynamics of each agent, and generalizes the typical variables that describe a neuron’s activity. Typically, in neuroscience, the quantities used to describe the state of a neuron are its firing rate or, in more biophysically realistic descriptions, a vector that includes the electrical membrane potential, ionic currents, and recovery variables. These systems are described by a general stochastic network equation of the type:

dx^{i}_{t}=\left(f_{p_{i}}(x^{i}_{t})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}J^{N}_{ij}\,b_{p_{i},p_{j}}(x^{i},x^{j})\right)\,dt+\sigma_{p_{i}}dW^{i}_{t}.

(1)

where
p_{i}
denotes the population of agent
i
,
(x^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the state of agent
i
in the network,
f_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the intrinsic dynamics of agents in population
p
, and
b_{p,q}(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the impact of an agent in population
q
and in state
y
on an agent in population
p
and state
x
. The stochastic processes
(W^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are independent
K

K

\sigma_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
encode the contributions to each noise source onto each dimension of the state variable. In these equations,
J_{ij}^{N}
is an
N\times N
matrix whose coefficients are the coupling intensities between neurons
i
and
j
, whose scaling is assumed to depend on the network size.

We are interested here in the behavior of the system in the large
N
limit. For specificity and simplicity of notation, we assume in the rest of the manuscript that the populations contain the same number of neurons, noted
n
(so
N=Pn
)

, and the coupling coefficients
J_{ij}^{N}
(or their statistics, when they are random) only depend on the populations of neurons

N

When
J_{ij}^{N}=\frac{1}{n}g_{p,q}

d\bar{x}_{t}^{p}=\left(f_{p}(\bar{x}_{t}^{p})+\sum_{q=1}^{P}g^{p,q}\,\int_{y}b_{p,q}(\bar{x}_{t}^{p},y)\mu_{t}^{q}(dy)\right)\,dt+\sigma_{p}dW^{p}_{t}

(2)

where
\mu_{t}^{q}
is the implicitly defined probability distribution of
\bar{x}^{q}(t)
.
Another situation studied in the literature previously relates to randomly connected networks, where the
J_{ij}^{N}
are iid, centered coefficients scaling as
1/\sqrt{n}
. In this situation, the network dynamics converge to a complex, non-Markovian equation, similar to the original system, but where the interaction term is replaced by a Gaussian process whose covariance depends on the solution of the equation. This result is evocative of a functional Central Limit Theorem, but the proof is vastly different and requires somewhat more sophisticated stochastic analysis tools, including, for instance, large-deviation theory
[
69
,
2
,
3
,
23

In both of these cases, the sum of all coefficients is (almost surely) bounded, and therefore no divergence of the coupling term happens. These dynamics agree, heuristically, with Boltzmann’s original molecular chaos hypothesis, also known as stoßzahlansatz (often referred to mathematically as the propagation of chaos property). In the kinetic theory of gases, the stoßzahlansatz posits that the velocities of colliding gas particles are uncorrelated, and independent of their positions. In the more general context of this paper, it consists in considering that the states of the agents are independent, identically distributed, and thus allow applying one of the classical limit theorems of probabilities (law of large numbers or central limit theorem) to provide accurate predictions of the asymptotic behavior of the system.

Here, we consider cases where the sum of the coefficients diverges. Beyond its mathematical interest, it is, as reviewed above, relevant in computational neuroscience, the context of Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk’s claim in
[
83
]
, and to account for the coupling regime identified by Barral and Reyes in
[
10
]
. In that case, Boltzmann’s stochastic number is not effective in providing predictions for the system’s dynamics in the large
n
limit, since, under this assumption, the interaction term has a positive probability of diverging. To simplify our presentation further, let us assume that the divergence rate is uniform for any pair of populations, denoted
\gamma(n)
for some function
\gamma
that tends to infinity as
n\to\infty
. We note
J_{ij}^{N}=\gamma(n)\frac{g_{p,q}}{n}
, so the equation for neuron

dx^{i}_{t}=\left(f_{p}(x^{i}_{t})+\frac{\gamma(n)}{n}\sum_{q=1}^{P}g_{p,q}\sum_{j;p_{j}=q}\,b_{p,q}(x^{i},x^{j})\right)\,dt+\sigma_{p}dW^{i}_{t}.

(3)

Building upon Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk’s intuition, we propose that instead of a convergence of the system to an unconstrained equation, the divergence of the interaction term will govern the behavior of the system at short timescales, and will lead to two possible behaviors: the dynamics may run away and variables may quickly increase or decrease to reach large positive or negative values, or, if a balanced regime exists and is attractive, the system will quickly collapse onto the set of balanced dynamics. This balanced regime is defined as the set of voltage distributions that correspond to a zero net input. In detail, we define the
balanced regime
as the set of measures such that, for any point on the support

\mathcal{B}=\{(\mu^{1},\cdots\mu^{P})\in\mathcal{M}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{P},\,\,\forall x_{p}\in Supp(\mu^{p}),\sum_{q=1}^{P}g_{p,q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b_{p,q}(x_{p},y)\mu^{q}({dy})=0\},

(4)

where the distribution of the random variable is such that the net input received is zero. This regime may be composed of a continuum of solutions on which the system may pursue its dynamics while remaining confined to the manifold.

We note that, for any possible measure
\mu
in the balanced regime,
\mathcal{B}
is invariant under the differential equations:

\frac{dx_{p}}{dt}=\sum_{q=1}^{P}g_{p,q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b_{p,q}(x_{p},y)\mu_{q}({dy}),

(5)

in the sense that if
(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{P})\in\mathcal{B}
, then for any
x_{p}
in the support of
\mu^{p}
,
x_{p}
is a fixed point of (
5

In detail, we propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Under the current formalism, let
X^{n}=(x^{n}_{i_{1}},\cdots,x^{n}_{i_{p}})
be a vector composed of one representative neuron in each of the
P
populations.
If the set
\mathcal{B}
is not empty, then we conjecture that there exist asymptotic solutions that are confined to the balanced regime for all times.

Moreover, if the balance manifold
\mathcal{B}
given by (
4
) is
attractive
for the dynamical system (
5
), in the sense that for initial conditions
x_{p}^{0}
near the support of
\mu_{p}

\mu_{p}
, then even for initial conditions outside of
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{B}
at a timescale inversely proportional to

(that is, for any

While broader and more abstract, this conjecture, however, reflects precisely the balanced regime predicted by Sompolinsky and van Vreeswijk in
[

\mathcal{B}
is composed of distributions associated with regimes where the input received by each neuron from the rest of the network combines to a zero net input. Such regimes do not always exist. For instance, in a one-dimensional system and in the case where

\sum_{q=1}^{P}g_{p,q}b_{p,q}(x_{p},x_{q}){=0},\qquad p\in\{1\cdots P\},

Before we proceed to exploring the mathematical underpinnings of this conjecture, we first examine its predictions for various neural network models.

3
Asymptotic behaviors of electrically or chemically coupled networks of FitzHugh-Nagumo neurons

The FitzHugh-Nagumo model is a phenomenological mathematical neuron model introduced in the middle of the past century
[
35
,
54
]
. It is particularly popular in computational neuroscience for its relative simplicity and its ability to capture critical characteristic features of neuronal dynamics, including, in particular, neural excitability
[
26
]
. The FitzHugh-Nagumo model describes the coupled dynamics of a voltage variable
x
and a recovery (or adaptation) variable
y
, subject to the differential equation:

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=f(x)-y+I\\
\dot{y}=a(b\,x-y)\\
\end{cases}

where
f
is a cubic polynomial with negative leading coefficient (typically,
f(v)=v(1-v)(v-\lambda)
),
I
represents the input to the neuron,
a
is the (typically small) timescale ratio between the recovery variable and the voltage, and
b
is a positive parameter controlling the response of the recovery variable (its ‘adaptation’) to changes in the voltage. The network models described below build upon the FitzHugh-Nagumo equation and explore the dynamics of large networks with two choices of coupling.

3.2
Example 2: FitzHugh-Nagumo model with chemical synapses and excitatory and inhibitory populations

In this section, we consider the dynamics of a two-population neural network with

n

This type of synapse, the most ubiquitous in the cerebral cortex, mediates the communication between cells through the release of vesicles filled with neurotransmitter molecules that bind to specific receptors on the membrane of the post-synaptic cell. This binding modifies the neuron’s conductances, leading to changes in ion exchange across the membrane and generating a current that alters the cell’s voltage. These vesicles are released when the neuron fires an action potential (or spike), which corresponds to a substantial increase in the cell membrane’s voltage. The subsequent modification of the membrane’s conductance features a rapid rise and a slower decay, which was modeled in
[
33
]
as a first-order differential equation nonlinearly coupled to the voltage (through a sigmoidal function of the voltage
\alpha(x)

4
A few mathematical viewpoints on the problem

There are many ways to approach the conjecture formulated in this paper mathematically. Here, we discuss a possible approach that relies on reformulating the conjecture as a double-limit problem. In detail, the system considered here is a particular asymptotic regime of the equation:

dx^{i}_{t}=\left(f_{p_{i}}(x^{i}_{t})+\gamma\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{{g}_{p_{i},p}}{n}\,b_{p_{i},p}(x^{i},x^{j})\right)\,dt+\sigma_{p_{i}}dW^{i}_{t}.

(11)

where

and
\gamma
simultaneously go to infinity. Decoupling the divergence of
\gamma
and
n
in that fashion is not a mere artificial reformulation of the system: it opens the way to treat the conjecture as a double-limit problem (see diagram in Figure
3
). It heuristically sheds light on the phenomena that combine into the conjectured convergence to a balanced regime. In this diagram, there are at least two ways to approach the limit at hand: a first method (Method M1) consists in taking first the limit
n\to\infty
with
\gamma
fixed (going left to right in the diagram of Figure
3
, or step M1.a), and then taking the limit
\gamma\to\infty
(M1.b). The second consists of taking the limit
\gamma\to\infty
first, and then
n\to\infty
(Method M2). We discuss what each of these limits will tell us about the system, as well as possible promising mathematical approaches to handle these questions.

M1.a

Taking the limit

Under mild conditions on the system’s parameters, the empirical measure of the collection of neurons in a given population, as well as the probability distribution of the state of each agent, converges to McKean-Vlasov processes, described by stochastic differential equations whose coefficients depend on the distribution of the solution itself. This thus yields a family of McKean-Vlasov processes indexed by
\gamma
.

M1.b

The second step in Method M1 consists of characterizing the adherent points and convergence properties of the family of processes as
\gamma

[
15

14

13

\gamma
, and studying adherent points and convergence of the sequence of functions as
\gamma\to\infty
in an appropriate functional space.

M2.a

The second view on the problem consists in taking the limit
\gamma\to\infty
first, emphasizing balance phenomena before considering how large-scale interactions maintain or alter these dynamics. Here, we expect that a change of time, proportional to
\gamma^{-1}
will desingularize the network equation. It will make each term of the interactions of order
1/n

\gamma

On this system, we expect the theory of regular perturbations of stochastic differential equations to yield convergence to a deterministic network equation, given by an ordinary differential equation, which emphasizes the emergence of balance. This approach is illustrated in section
4.2
in the case of the two-population FitzHugh-Nagumo network with chemical synapses.

M2.b

Asymptotic regimes of these network equations as
n\to\infty
and convergence properties now need to be identified. Asymptotic limits of deterministic systems are also well developed and should be available for the system.

Of note, one difficulty in method M2 consists in understanding what happens at intermediate times. Indeed, after taking the limit
\gamma\to\infty
, the description obtained after step M2a is valid on an infinitesimal time interval, and the differential equations obtained no longer depend on variables or dynamical terms that were not part of the diverging coupling. As soon as the balance has been ensured in these systems, the subsequent dynamics are no longer described by the same equations and will need a specific treatment. In that view, this double-limit is not a classical problem of commutation of limits, but rather two complementary views on the stochastic equation, where the limit
\gamma\to\infty
informs us of the rapid collapses onto a balanced manifold, when it happens, yielding the system to be confined in the vicinity of the balanced regime where one can then explore the dynamics and averaging effects under the balance constraints.

The two subsections below discuss, in practice and formally, what both of the techniques outlined above entail. Due to the difficulties outlined regarding the dynamics at intermediate times in method M2, we focused our attention on method M1 in this paper. The asymptotic results we obtain for the sequence of McKean-Vlasov processes rely on functional analysis, more precisely on a desingularization technique that allows us to define viscosity solutions for the sequence of McKean-Vlasov processes, to prove that the sequence of probability densities associated are relatively compact, thus converging along subsequences, and to characterize some properties of the adherent points of the sequence (see section

Discussion and Conclusion

The mathematical theory of large-scale systems of interacting agents has been largely focused on regimes where interactions between neurons become infinitesimal in the large
n
limit, favoring averaging effects
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
23
,
24
,
65
]
, and preventing any possible divergence of the interaction term. Recently, Ramanan and collaborators dropped the hypothesis of infinitesimal interactions, and investigated the behavior of stochastic particle systems on sparse graphs such that each neuron is expected to receive a finite input even in the asymptotic regime
[
62
,
63
,
40
,
44
,
61
]
. These novel techniques rely on local limits of random graphs, Markov random fields, and stochastic analysis. While interactions are kept non-vanishing, the expected interaction term remains finite again because of the dilution of the graph, with a connection probability between any two agents decaying as the network size increases.

The regime we explored in the present manuscript is different from these works in that we consider that neither the scaling of the synaptic weights nor the scaling of the connection probability ensures boundedness of the interaction term. In our case, under appropriate assumptions, the entire dynamics must conspire to maintain finite interactions for the equation not to diverge. In this regime, conjectured by Van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky in
[
84
]
, those scalings are indeed too slow (in terms of decay according to the network size) to ensure boundedness of the interaction term. Phenomenologically, Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk instead proposed that in this case, the dynamics will first quickly evolve to cancel these interactions (that is,
balance
excitation and inhibition), when possible and when the dynamics allow, leading the system to collapse rapidly on a statistical manifold where the interactions are kept in check, and where non trivial (in the original paper, chaotic) dynamics can emerge. It is this regime that we studied in the present paper. From a mathematical viewpoint, the classical
mean-field
techniques reviewed above, which apply to systems with vanishing interaction terms or vanishing connection probabilities and reveal averaging effects, are inoperant in the present setting. This question raises several new mathematical problems that are not addressed by any existing theory, to the best of our knowledge. This is why we initially presented the problem in its full generality and formulated a relatively general mathematical conjecture. Proving this conjecture, or some aspects of it, constitutes an interesting challenge that shall combine stochastic analysis techniques with dynamical systems techniques and analysis. Although we had not yet identified the generality of the problem, we first analyzed problems related to these regimes of activity in
[
60
]
in a particular neural network. In that paper, we proved a concentration of the distribution of mean-field solutions when coupling diverged, and to that purpose used asymptotic desingularization techniques for PDEs that had been introduced in the context of mathematical ecology
[
9
,
8
,
51
,
17
,
49
]
. These results were then proven using other stochastic analysis techniques by Blaustein and Filbet, including the control of the convergence in the Wasserstein space (allowing them to lift some assumption on initial conditions
[
15
]
) or achieve a stronger notion of convergence
[
14
]
, where interactions were extended to include a dependence in space. All of these works relied substantially on the straightforward nature of the interaction term and are not trivial to generalize to other types of couplings.

In terms of proofs, while we were able in the present manuscript to generalize desingularization techniques to a one-dimensional system with separable coupling, we believe the desingularization approach based on Hopf-Cole transforms (or similar) is bound to be limited to a narrow set of models. We proposed here two theoretical points of view on the conjecture, by formulating it as a double-limit problem in the space of probability measures on continuous functions (or, in the space of random processes). This yields two distinct approaches. The first limit consists of considering what happens at very short timescales (on the order of the typical divergence of the interaction term). As timescales vanish with the network size, one obtains a typically simple, non-stochastic dynamical system where all variables are fixed except those affected by the divergence of the interaction term. The balanced regime may appear, from this lens, as a dynamical equilibrium of the system at short timescales. The other limit considers first that the network size diverges, and then takes the interaction coefficient to infinity. This provides two avenues to approach the conjecture. This lifts part of the veil on a new problem and a new phenomenology that are well worth studying mathematically and computationally.

From the application’s point of view, in neuroscience, the importance of this phenomenon and of its consequences is hard to overstate. After its first formulation by Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk, it gave rise to a vast literature that explored balanced networks in rhythm generation
[
22
,
21
]
, cluster formation with plasticity
[
47
,
46
]
, pattern formation
[
66
]
, and computational capacities of learning networks
[
45
,
43
,
72
]
. Biologically, this theory of a dynamical balance can account for many of the observations made in the maintenance of balance in networks subject to perturbations
[
29
,
27
]
, but does not provide an answer as to which of the many possible mechanisms are at play in this maintenance
[
82
,
79
,
80
,
81
]
, and how, at the end of the day, indivual neurons adjust to reach the balanced manifold.

While not explicitly stated as balanced regimes, several recent works on particle systems tend to reflect the emergence of a collapse to a balance manifold, similar to the result we formulate here. In particular, Bruna, Burger, and De Wit
[
20
]
recently proposed a mathematical model of a foraging colony of ants, where each ant is modeled as a Brownian particle and interactions are modeled through chemotaxis, wherein ants align their orientations upwards towards the gradient of pheromone they release. In addition to some more classical behaviors of typical Keller-Segel type equations
[
4
]
, they revealed the emergence of singular distributions for larger interaction strengths, including the formation of lanes and Dirac distributions, which are evocative of the balanced manifold predicted in this manuscript. Similarly, polarization phenomena have recently been linked to the stronger type of interactions that may arise in the modern world
[
70
]
, where stronger interactions are reported to lead to more segregated components and fewer bridges between them. In ecology, such balances have garnered more interest in recent years
[
58
,
55
,
50
,
19
]
, and theoretically seem to provide a foundation for addressing possible questions related to balancing cooperation and competition in ecosystems subject to severe external environments. Because the nature of the limiting object and its dynamics are drastically different in balanced systems compared to mean-field regimes, this paper argues for a reevaluation of the choice of models and for exploring which model better describes the collective behavior of large systems in applications, as well as for theoretical statistical models. Among choice models in mathematics, the study of spin glasses has been an active area of research, providing a number of techniques and results to the broader community working on statistical models. Incidentally, just less than a week ago, at the time of this writing, Bates and Sohn introduced a balanced multi-species spin glass model
[
11
]
, the study of which may provide an ideal framework for analyzing these new dynamics.

\displaystyle-\frac{I_{1}-I_{2}}{\varepsilon}\int\alpha v_{2}\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot{m_{\kappa}}-\frac{I_{1}-I_{2}}{\varepsilon}\int\alpha v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\partial_{x}{m_{\kappa}}

Hölder’s inequality yields:

|I_{1}-I_{2}|\leq\left(\int\frac{\beta^{2}(x)}{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\left(\int(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2},

because
\beta
has at most polynomial growth at
\pm\infty
. Now we take advantage of
\kappa
to get the conclusion. More precisely, we have that

\displaystyle\int\alpha(x)v_{2}\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot{m_{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\alpha^{2}v_{2}^{2}m_{\kappa}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int|\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int v_{2}^{2}\cdot m_{1}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int|\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq Ca_{1}\left(\int|\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2},

similarly, since
\partial_{x}m_{\kappa}=2\kappa xm_{\kappa}
, then

\displaystyle\int\alpha(x)v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot\partial_{x}{m_{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\int 2\kappa x\alpha(x)v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot{m_{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int 4\kappa^{2}x^{2}\alpha^{2}v_{2}^{2}m_{\kappa}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq Ca_{1}\left(\int(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}\cdot{m_{\kappa}}\right)^{1/2}.

Summarizing, there exists a constant
C
depending on
\varepsilon
such that

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{-1}(I_{1}-I_{2})\int\alpha v_{2}\partial_{x}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot{m_{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq Ca_{1}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}\|\partial(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4a_{1}^{2}C^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\|\partial(v_{1}-v_{2})\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2},

and also

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{-1}(I_{1}-I_{2})\int\alpha v_{2}(v_{1}-v_{2})\cdot\partial_{x}{m_{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leq Ca_{1}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}a_{1}^{2}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2},

thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|^{2}_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+C^{2}a_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}+1\right)\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}}{2}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{2}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}^{2},

for well chosen positive constants
C_{1}(\varepsilon)
and
C_{2}
. By Gronwall’s lemma, it follows that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}(e^{C_{1}T}-1)}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L_{m_{\kappa}}^{2}}

and finally, for
T
small enough such that
\sqrt{\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}\left(e^{C_{1}T}-1\right)}<1
,
\Phi
is a contraction. Therefore,
\Phi
has a unique fixed point and there exists
\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\in A
a solution to the following equation

\partial_{t}\mu(t,x)=-\partial_{x}\left[\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)\int\beta(y)\mu(t,y)\right)\mu(t,x)\right]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}\mu(t,x),\,t\in(0,T),

with initial condition
\mu_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\mu_{0,\varepsilon}(x)
. Iterating in time, we find a global solution to equation (
17
).

To finish this section, we show that the notion of convergence of the sequence of functions
I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. This will provide us with a specific concentration profile for the interaction function, which will be helpful in proving a limit for the sequence of solutions
\mu_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that the hypotheses of Theorem
1
hold, and that the functions
f,\alpha
and
\beta
are such that there exists
C>0
and a non-negative polynomial
p
such that:

C^{-1}\leq\beta^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y),\qquad\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)\alpha^{2}(y)+\beta^{\prime}(y)\alpha^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y)\geq 0,\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R},

(23)

and

|f^{\prime\prime}(y)|\leq C(1+|y|),\qquad|\beta^{(iv)}(y)|\leq C(1+p(y)),\qquad|\alpha^{\prime\prime}(y)|\leq C(1+p(y)).

(24)

Then, we have the local uniform BV bound

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\leq C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime}T,

where
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
are positive constants. Consequently, after extraction of a subsequence,
I_{\varepsilon}(t)
converges a.e. to a function
I(t)
, as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.

Before we proceed to proving this result, we recall that for a general function
g(y)
, a simple application of the mean value theorem implies that

\text{if }(\exists C>0,k\in\mathbb{N})\text{ such that }|g^{\prime}(y)|\leq C(1+|y|^{k})\text{ then }|g(y)|\leq C^{\prime}(1+|y|^{k+1}),

for a possibly larger constant
C^{\prime}
. This means that under (
24
) functions
f,\alpha
along with their first and second derivatives are upper-bounded by polynomial functions. The same remark holds for
\beta
and its first four derivatives.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\beta^{\prime}(y)\Big(f(y)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\Big)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\underbrace{\Big(\beta^{\prime}(y)f(y)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)\Big)}_{g_{1}(y)}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)-\frac{I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)}{\varepsilon}\int\underbrace{\beta^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y)}_{g_{2}(y)}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int g_{1}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)\;\;\;\leq\;\;\;\int C^{\prime}(1+q(y))\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy),

where
q
is some polynomial in
y
, using the assumption that
\beta^{(iv)}
and
f
are sub-polynomial. This expression provides a uniform bound to the left-hand side, owing to the bounds of Gaussian moments of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
proven in Theorem
1
.

Let us now establish the lower-bound inequality. We rewrite the term
J_{\varepsilon}(t):=\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}J_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int g_{1}^{\prime}(y)\Big(f(y)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\Big)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int g_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)}{\varepsilon}\int g_{2}^{\prime}(y)\Big(f(y)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\Big)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)}{\varepsilon}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)-\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon}\int g_{2}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy).

We notice that the term proportional to
\varepsilon^{-2}
,
I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\int g_{2}^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)
, is non-negative (and can thus be ignored for establishing a lower-bound), since:

\int g_{2}^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)=\int\left(\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)\alpha^{2}(y)+\beta^{\prime}(y)\alpha^{\prime}(y)\alpha(y)\right)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)\geq 0

under assumption (
23
).

The rest of the proof relies on the assumed sub-polynomial growth of the functions
f,\alpha
and
\beta
, along with Lemma
3
and Gaussian moments of the solutions. All terms follow the same approach, which we make explicit for the same term for clarity.

\int g_{1}^{\prime}(y)f(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)=\int\left(\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)f(y)+\beta^{\prime}(y)f^{\prime}(y)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)\right)f(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)

and there is a constant positive
C^{\prime}
and a nonnegative polynomial
q(y)
such that

\left|\beta^{\prime\prime}(y)f(y)+\beta^{\prime}(y)f^{\prime}(y)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\beta^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)\right|\leq C^{\prime}(1+q(y)),

readily implying

\int g_{1}^{\prime}(y)f(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)\geq-C^{\prime}\int(1+q(y))\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)\geq-C^{\prime\prime}

thanks to the uniform bounds on the Gaussian moment of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
. Other terms are readily treated using a similar approach,
ensuring the following bound:

\frac{d}{dt}J_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq-\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{J_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon}\int g_{2}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy),

for a possibly larger constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
. Finally, take
(J_{\varepsilon}(t))_{-}=\max(0,-J_{\varepsilon}(t))
, we show that for
J_{\varepsilon}(t)<0
:

(J_{\varepsilon}(t))^{\prime}_{-}=-J_{\varepsilon}(t)^{\prime}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{-J_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\varepsilon}\int g_{2}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy)=\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{(J_{\varepsilon}(t))_{-}}{\varepsilon}\int g_{2}(y)\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,dy),

and from the hypothesis (
23
), it follows that

(J_{\varepsilon}(t))^{\prime}_{-}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{C\varepsilon}(J_{\varepsilon}(t))_{-}.

The previous inequality is, in fact, trivial for
J_{\varepsilon}(t)>0
, since in that case the derivative is zero. From this inequality, it is standard to deduce that

-J_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\max(0,-J_{\varepsilon}(t))=(J_{\varepsilon}(t))_{-}\leq C^{\prime\prime}C+(J_{\varepsilon}(0))_{-}e^{-t/C\varepsilon},

from we get that
J_{\varepsilon}(t)
is uniformly lower-bounded.
We finish the proof by noticing that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\,dt+2\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)_{-}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq I_{\varepsilon}(T)-I_{\varepsilon}(0)+2\left(C^{\prime\prime}CT+(J_{\varepsilon}(0))_{-}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-t/C\varepsilon}\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=I_{\varepsilon}(T)-I_{\varepsilon}(0)+2\left(C^{\prime\prime}CT+(J_{\varepsilon}(0))_{-}C\varepsilon(1-e^{-T/C\varepsilon})\right),

which implies that there are some positive constants
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{d}{dt}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\,dt\leq C^{\prime}T+C^{\prime\prime}.

Appendix B
Hopf-Cole transformation analysis

We have established so far that, under the hypotheses of Theorem
1
, we have that for fixed
\kappa\in(0,1)
, for each
\varepsilon>0
there is a probability density
\mu_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T],L^{2}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}))
that is a weak solution of equation (
17
). As a consequence, at least formally, we can take the Hopf-Cole transformation
\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\varepsilon\ln\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
and observe that

\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\partial_{t}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\quad\partial_{x}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x),

and

\partial^{2}_{xx}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\partial^{2}_{xx}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x).

therefore
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is a solution of the equation:

\partial_{t}\phi_{\varepsilon}=-\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)-\frac{\varepsilon f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon}|\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi_{\varepsilon}.

(26)

The function
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is thus in the kernel of the following operator acting on maps continuously differentiable with respect to time and twice continuously differentiable with respect to space
C^{1}([0,\infty);C^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
:

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\phi:=\partial_{t}\phi+\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\\
+\left(\varepsilon f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}\phi-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon}|\partial_{x}\phi|^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi.

For simplicity we write
I_{\varepsilon}=I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}
, and
\Lambda(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\alpha(s)ds
.

Proposition 5
.

Assume that initial conditions
\phi_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon,0}(x)
satisfy the conditions

\sup_{0<\varepsilon<1}\varepsilon\log\mu_{0,\varepsilon}(x)\leq-Ax^{2}+B,

for some constants
A,B
with
A
positive. Then there is another set of positive constants
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},D^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
such that

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq-A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\Lambda(x)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+D^{\prime}t+E^{\prime},

at all positive times.

Proof.

Consider the map

\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=-A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\Lambda(x)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+D^{\prime}t+E^{\prime},

for some
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},D^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
positive constants to be specified later. Applying the map
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
to
\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
we find that

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}=D^{\prime}-A^{\prime}I^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\Lambda(x)+\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\\
-\left(\varepsilon f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\varepsilon B^{\prime}x+A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\alpha(x)\Big)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\Big(\varepsilon B^{\prime}x+A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\alpha(x)\Big)^{2}\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big(\varepsilon B^{\prime}+A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\alpha^{\prime}(x)\Big)=:D^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{-1}h_{1}(t,x)+h_{2}(t,x)+\varepsilon h_{3}(t,x),

with

\displaystyle h_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}\alpha(x)^{2}I_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\left(1-\frac{A^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle h_{2}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-A^{\prime}I^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(t)\Lambda(x)+\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\varepsilon}(t)\left(\frac{A^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle+B^{\prime}x\alpha(x)I_{\varepsilon}(t)(1-\sigma^{2}A^{\prime})-f(x)A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\alpha(x),

\displaystyle h_{3}(t,x)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(x)-B^{\prime}xf(x)-B^{\prime 2}x^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}+B^{\prime}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}.

First, we work with the terms of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
, to find that for any
A^{\prime}<\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}
it holds that

h_{1}(t,x)=A\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}A}{2}\right)I_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\alpha(x)^{2}\geq 0.

For the other terms, we set
M>0
large and notice that
h_{2}(t,\cdot)
and
h_{3}(t,\cdot)
are continuous functions, and then there is a positive constant
C_{M}
such that they are larger than
-C_{M}
for all
x\in[-M,M]
. Secondly, from (
21
) it follows that

\displaystyle h_{3}(t,x)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(x)-B^{\prime}xf(x)-(B^{\prime})^{2}x^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}+B^{\prime}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq-C^{\prime}(1+x^{2})-B^{\prime}\left(xf(0)+C_{0}x^{2}-C_{0}\frac{x^{4}}{3}\right)\geq-C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime}x^{4},

with
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
two positive generic constants,
C^{\prime\prime}
small enough. Finally, for
h_{2}(t,x)
, we use the fact that both
I_{\varepsilon}
and
I^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded. Again,
h_{2}
is continuous and bounded in the interval
[-M,M]
. Outside this region, have that
\Lambda(x)=O(1+x^{2})
,
\alpha^{\prime}(x)=O(1)
. Moreover
x\alpha(x)=O(1+x^{2})
and
f(x)\alpha(x)=(xf(x))\frac{\alpha(x)}{x}=O(1+x^{2})
, then it follows that

h_{2}(t,x)=O(1+x^{2})\geq-C^{\prime}(1+x^{2})

for some
C^{\prime}>0
.
Altogether, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=D^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{-1}h_{1}(t,x)+h_{2}(t,x)+\varepsilon h_{3}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq D^{\prime}-C^{\prime}(1+x^{2})-C^{\prime}\varepsilon+C^{\prime\prime}x^{4}\varepsilon

which is nonnegative for
D^{\prime}=D^{\prime}(\varepsilon)
large enough. To finish the proof, notice that from the uniform initial conditions bound, we have that

\sup_{0<\varepsilon<1}\phi_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<1}\varepsilon\log\mu_{0,\varepsilon}(x)\leq-Ax^{2}+B,

but since
I_{\varepsilon}(t)
is uniformly bounded by some
C_{I}
it follows that

\psi_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=-A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(0)\Lambda(x)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+E^{\prime}\geq-A^{\prime}C_{I}|\Lambda(x)|-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+E^{\prime},

and again
\Lambda(x)=O(1+x^{2})
outside of the compact
[-M,M]
and bounded by below inside
[-M,M]
. As a consequence, there is a constant
C>0
such that

\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\geq-C(1+x^{2})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+E^{\prime}\geq-Ax^{2}+B.

This proves that the inequality claimed in the proposition is true for the initial condition, for a proper choice of constants.

B.1
Regularity of the solutions of the problem

We now prove that the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
has a regularizing effect such that solutions are uniformly Lipschitz in space at any positive time, independently of the regularity of the initial conditions. To that aim, we will study the equation associated to with the space derivative of
\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
. In particular, we have the following:

Proposition 6
.

Assume that
\varepsilon\leq 1
and let
F=\sqrt{E^{\prime}+D^{\prime}T}
for
E^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
the same positive constants from Proposition
5
, and let
T>0
be an arbitrary time horizon. Then the map
w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\sqrt{2F^{2}-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)}
is well defined and there exists a constant
\theta(T)
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

\left|\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T).

Proof.

First, notice that proposition
5
implies in particular that

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq D^{\prime}T+E^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 2F^{2}-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\geq D^{\prime}T+E^{\prime}>0,

and thus ensures that
w_{\varepsilon}
is well-defined. Our proof of an upper bound relies on a well-chosen change of variable. In detail, let
\gamma
be a smooth invertible map, with nonvanishing derivative, and define
\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\gamma(w_{\varepsilon}(t,x))
. Elementary calculus yields:

\partial_{t}\phi_{\varepsilon}=\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon},\qquad\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}=\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon},

and

\partial^{2}_{xx}\phi_{\varepsilon}=\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})|\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\partial^{2}_{xx}w_{\varepsilon},

implying that
w_{\varepsilon}
is a solution to

\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)|\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}w_{\varepsilon}.

(27)

Define
p_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}
, and take an extra spatial partial derivative on (
27
) to get

\partial_{t}p_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}p_{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}+\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{|\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\,p_{\varepsilon}\\
-\left(f^{\prime}(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)p_{\varepsilon}-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}p_{\varepsilon}\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right)p_{\varepsilon}^{3}\\
+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}p_{\varepsilon},

and multiplying by
p_{\varepsilon}
the equation becomes

\partial_{t}\frac{|p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}||^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|p_{\varepsilon}|\partial^{2}_{xx}|p_{\varepsilon}|+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\\
-\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\,p_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{|\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\,|p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\\
-\left(f^{\prime}(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\,|p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right)|p_{\varepsilon}|^{4}\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|^{2}.

We find that:

\partial_{t}|p_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}|p_{\varepsilon}|\leq\\
-\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\,\frac{p_{\varepsilon}}{|p_{\varepsilon}|}+\frac{\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)}{|\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})|^{2}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})\,|p_{\varepsilon}|\\
-\left(f^{\prime}(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)|p_{\varepsilon}|-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|\\
+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right)|p_{\varepsilon}|^{3}\\
+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|.

Let us now specify the map
\gamma:x\mapsto-x^{2}+2F(T)^{2}
. Recall that, by definition, we have
F(T)\leq w_{\varepsilon}
, and, moreover

\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})^{2}}\leq-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{w_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\leq-2\varepsilon^{-1}.

We also recall that
f
and
\alpha
have linear growth at infinity, thus all their derivatives are bounded. Altogether, we have that:

\partial_{t}|p_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}|p_{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{C_{M}(1+\varepsilon)}{2F(T)}+\frac{C_{M}(1+\varepsilon)}{2F(T)^{2}}|p_{\varepsilon}|+C_{M}(1+\varepsilon^{-1})|p_{\varepsilon}|-\sigma^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}|p_{\varepsilon}|^{3}\\
-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|.

Assuming that
\varepsilon<1
(consistent with the large connectivity regime we are interested in), there exists a constant
C_{M}(T)
such that:

\partial_{t}|p_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}|p_{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{M}(T)(1+|p_{\varepsilon}|)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|p_{\varepsilon}|^{3}+\varepsilon^{-1}\left(C_{M}|p_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|p_{\varepsilon}|^{3}\right)\\
-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|.

Noting that the expression on the first line is a third-degree polynomial in
|p_{\varepsilon}|
, with coefficients either of order 1 or
\varepsilon^{-1}
and depending on
T
, there exists a large enough positive constant
\theta(T)
such that

\partial_{t}|p_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}|p_{\varepsilon}|\leq-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(|p_{\varepsilon}|-\theta(T)\right)^{3}(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\\
-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)p_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{x}|p_{\varepsilon}|.

Noticing that the spatially homogeneous function
y_{\varepsilon}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^{2}(1+\varepsilon)t}}+\theta(T),
which is such that
y(0)=\infty
, is a solution of the equation:

\partial_{t}y(t,x)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}y(t,x)=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(y(t,x)-\theta(T)\right)^{3}(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\\
-\left(f(x)-\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}y(t,x)+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}{\gamma^{\prime}(w_{\varepsilon})}\right)y(t,x)\cdot\partial_{x}y(t,x),

we conclude that

|p_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^{2}(1+\varepsilon)t}}+\theta(T),\qquad 0<t\leq T.

Corollary 7
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
6
, the family of Hopf-Cole transformations
\{\phi_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\leq 1}
is uniformly bounded in compact subsets of
(0,\infty)

\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Proposition
5
implies that

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq-A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\Lambda(x)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}B^{\prime}x^{2}+D^{\prime}t+E^{\prime}\leq-A^{\prime}I_{\varepsilon}(t)\Lambda(x)+C(T),

with
C(T)=D^{\prime}T+E^{\prime}
. Under the current assumptions, for
x>M
for some
M>1
, we have:

Cx-\delta\leq\alpha(x)\leq Cx+\delta

and thus

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\alpha(s)ds+\int_{M}^{x}\alpha(s)ds\geq C(M)+\frac{Cx^{2}}{2}-\delta x

\displaystyle\geq C(M)+\frac{Cx^{2}}{2}-\delta x^{2}=:C(M)+C(\delta)x^{2},

with
C(\delta)
positive for
M
large enough; an analogous bound exists for
x\in(-\infty,-M]
. Since
I_{\varepsilon}(t)
is always positive, we have that

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq-AI_{\varepsilon}(t)(C(M)+C(\delta)x^{2})+C(T),

and using that
I_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq K^{-1}
we finally have the desired quadratic bound:

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq-AK^{-1}C(\delta)x^{2}+C(M,T)=:-C_{1}x^{2}+C_{2},

which is only valid for
|x|>M
. Take now
R>M
it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]}\mu_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]}e^{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)/\varepsilon}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]}e^{-(C_{1}x^{2}-C_{2})/\varepsilon}\,dx,

which, for
R
large enough and
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for some
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough, is certainly upperbounded by
\frac{1}{2}
.
Mass conservation thus necessarily implies that
\int_{-R}^{R}e^{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)/\varepsilon}\,dx\geq\frac{1}{2}
. We deduce that for each
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there is some
x_{\varepsilon}\in[-R,R]
such that
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})>-1
, meaning that:

w_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{\varepsilon})<\sqrt{2F^{2}+1}.

In Proposition
6
we showed that the space derivative of
w_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded for each point in
[t,T]\times[-R,R]
and then

|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x+h)-w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right)h,

and using
x_{\varepsilon}
and this previous inequality, we have that

w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq w_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{\varepsilon})+|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-w_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{\varepsilon})|\leq\sqrt{2F^{2}+1}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right)R.

By definition of
w_{\varepsilon}
, we finally get the desired lower bound

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t^{\prime},x)=2F^{2}-w_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t,x)\geq 2F^{2}-\left(\sqrt{2F^{2}+1}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right)R\right)^{2}

for all
(t^{\prime},x)\in[t,T]\times[-R,R]
.

Remark 8
.

The solution to the equation (
26
) is actually, uniformly continuous in space on
[t_{0},T]\times[-R,R]
for any
t_{0}>0
and
R>0
fixed. Indeed, notice that

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=2F^{2}-w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)^{2},

therefore

\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=-2w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\partial_{x}w_{\varepsilon}(t,x).

From Proposition
6
we have that the space derivative of
w_{\varepsilon}
is bounded, then we have

|\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq 2|w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right),

where
\theta(T)
does not depend on
\varepsilon
. From Corollary
7
we also have that
w_{\varepsilon}
is also bounded, thus

|\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq 2\left(\sqrt{2F^{2}+1}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right)R\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{t\sigma^{2}}}+\theta(T)\right):=M(t_{0},T,\sigma),

with
F
not depending on
\varepsilon
. As a consequence, for
t\in[t_{0},T]
and
x\in(-R,R)
and
h
small, we have

|\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x+h)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq M(t_{0},T,\sigma)|h|.

The last auxiliary result we need to construct the proof of convergence of the families of Hopf-Cole transformations
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is the regularity of the solutions in time. The proof of the following result is an adaptation of the method used in
[
8
, Lemma 9.1]
(see also
[
57
,
9
]
for other applications of these methods) and that we have also previously adapted to FitzHugh-Nagumo models with diffusive coupling in
[

]
.

Lemma 9
.

For all
\eta>0
,
R>0
and
t_{0}>0
, there exists a positive constant
\Theta
such that for all
\varepsilon<1
and
(t,s,x)\in[t_{0},T]\times[t_{0},T]\times(-R/2,R/2)
such that
0<t-s<\Theta
we have

|\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)|\leq 2\eta.

Proof.

The idea of the proof consists in finding two constants
A,B
such that for any
x\in(-R/2,R/2)
,
s\in[t_{0},T]
and for every
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
:

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)\leq\eta+A(e^{\varepsilon(x-y)^{2}}-1)+B(t-s),

and

-\eta-A(e^{\varepsilon(x-y)^{2}}-1)-B(t-s)\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x).

We present the proof of the first inequality; the second inequality is proven following the same arguments, and is thus omitted here. Fix some
s\in[t_{0},T]
and some
-\frac{R}{2}<x<\frac{R}{2}
, and define

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,y)=\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)+\eta+A(e^{\varepsilon(x-y)^{2}}-1)+B(t-s),

with
K
and
C
constants that we will specify later on. The function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is clearly well defined for
t\in[s,T)
and
y\in(-R,R)
. In Corollary
7
we showed that the function
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in compact sets of
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, then

M:=\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},T]\times[-R,R])}<+\infty.

In particular, we can find
A
such that

0<\frac{2}{e^{\frac{R^{2}\varepsilon}{4}}-1}\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},T]\times[-R,R])}<A,

and then taking
y\in\{-R,R\}
we have the inequality

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,\pm R)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)+\eta+A(e^{\varepsilon(x\mp R)^{2}}-1)+C(t-s)

\displaystyle\geq\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,\pm R)-2\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\eta+A(e^{\frac{R^{2}\varepsilon}{4}}-1)

\displaystyle\geq\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,\pm R)+\eta>\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,\pm R),

because
(x\mp R)^{2}\geq\frac{R^{2}}{4}
. This inequality extends to the whole interval
(-R,R)
. Indeed, if that were not the case, then there would exist
\eta>0
such that for all constants
A
we have some
y_{A,\varepsilon}\in(-R,R)
such that

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(s,y_{A,\varepsilon})=\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)+\eta+A(e^{(x-y_{A,\varepsilon})^{2}}-1)\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,y_{A,\varepsilon})

in particular

\eta+A(e^{(x-y_{A,\varepsilon})^{2}}-1)\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,y_{A,\varepsilon})-\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)\quad\Rightarrow\quad e^{(x-y_{A,\varepsilon})^{2}}-1\leq 2MA^{-1},

taking
A\rightarrow\infty
, we get that necessarily
y_{A,\varepsilon}\rightarrow x
. Moreover, from remark
8
we know that
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly continuous in space on
[t_{0},T]\times[-R,R]
, then for
A
large enough

|\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{A,\varepsilon})-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq\frac{\eta}{2},

which is a contradiction. Finally, notice that

\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,y)=B,\quad\partial_{y}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,y)=2A\varepsilon(y-x)e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}},

and

\partial_{yy}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,y)=2A\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}}+4A\varepsilon^{2}(y-x)^{2}e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}},

thus

\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}+\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(y)-\alpha^{\prime}(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)+\left(\varepsilon f(y)-\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{y}\varphi-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon}|\partial_{y}\varphi|^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{yy}^{2}\varphi\\
=B+\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)-\left(\varepsilon f(y)-\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)2A(y-x)e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}}\\
-\frac{\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}{2}\left(2A(y-x)e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(2A\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}}+4A\varepsilon^{2}(y-x)^{2}e^{\varepsilon(y-x)^{2}}\right),

all functions are bounded below, independently of
\varepsilon
as soon as it is smaller than 1, thus there are some constants
B,A
such that

\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}+\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(y)-\alpha^{\prime}(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\\
+\left(\varepsilon f(y)-\alpha(y)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{y}\varphi-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\varepsilon}|\partial_{y}\varphi|^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{yy}^{2}\varphi\\
B-C(C_{f},C_{\alpha},C_{I})\,\geq 0,

whenever
B
is large enough. From here, we deduce that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a super-solution of the equation solved by
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and then

\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,y)\leq\varphi(t,y)=\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)+\eta+A(e^{\varepsilon(x-y)^{2}}-1)+B(t-s),

the other inequality follows the same arguments. Finally, by taking
y=x
we have

|\phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\phi_{\varepsilon}(s,x)|\leq\eta+B(t-s)\leq\eta+B\Theta=2\eta,

when
\Theta=\eta/B
.

Appendix C
The large coupling limit

Equipped with all previous results, we can now take the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in the sequence
\phi_{\varepsilon}
. This further allows us to characterize any adherence point of the set of Hopf-Cole transformations. Again, the technique adapts the methodology of
[
9
]
and references therein. In the following, we work on the compact subset
C_{R}=[t_{0},T]\times[-R,R]
.

•

Step 0:
From Lemma
4
we have that the integral part of the equation
I_{\varepsilon}(t)
, after extraction of a subsequence, converges a.e. to a function
I(t)
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.

•

Step 1:
so far we have that the sequence
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly continuous on
C_{R}
. The Arzela-Ascoli theorem implies that we can extract a subsequence
\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t,x)
that converges locally uniformly to a continuous function
\phi(t,x)
.

•

Step 2:
The limit
\phi(t,x)
is everywhere non-positive. Indeed, if there was a point
(t^{*},x^{*})\in[t_{0},T]\times[-R,R]
with
\phi(t^{*},x^{*})>0
, then there would be a small spatial interval
S
such that for any
x\in S
we would have that
\phi(t^{*},x)>\delta
, for some
\delta>0
. Thanks to the positivity and mass conservation principle:

1\geq\int_{S}e^{\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t,y)/\varepsilon_{n}}dy\geq|S|e^{\delta/\varepsilon_{n}},

which is not possible for small values of
\varepsilon_{n}
.

Moreover, the maximum of
\phi(t,x)
is exactly
0
. Indeed, the quadratic upperbound we proved on
\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
for
R
large enough,

\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t,x)\leq-AK^{-1}C(\delta)x^{2}+C(M,T)=:C_{1}x^{2}+C_{2},

implies that
\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
is strictly negative for
R>0
large enough. This implies that there is a value
R
such that the limit
\phi(t,x)
is strictly negative on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]
. We thus deduce that

1=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t,x)/\varepsilon_{n}}\,dx,

which implies that the maximum value of
\phi(t,x)
cannot be strictly negative. As a result, the maximum value of
\phi(t,x)
is necessarily zero.

•

Step 3:
Fix some
t\in(0,1)
and assume that for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
the limit
\phi(t,x)=-a<0
. From uniform continuity in compacts of
C_{R}
, we find
\delta>0
such that if
n
is large

\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\leq-\frac{a}{2}<0\text{ for all }(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in(t-\delta,t+\delta)\times(x-\delta,x+\delta),

so

\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}=\int_{x-\delta}^{x+\delta}e^{\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})/\varepsilon_{n}}\,dx^{\prime}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}0,

therefore along converging subsequence the support of the limit
\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t,x)
reduces to the set of
x
such that
\phi(t,x)
is zero, i.e.

\text{sup}\,\mu(t,\cdot)\subset\{\phi(t,\cdot)=0\},\quad\text{for almost every $t$.}

•

Step 4:
For any
n
we rearrange the terms of the equation (
26
) to find that
\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
is a solution to

\varepsilon_{n}\partial_{t}\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}=-\varepsilon_{n}\left(\varepsilon_{n}f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)\right)\\
-\left(\varepsilon_{n}f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}|^{2}+\varepsilon_{n}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}.

Define
H_{\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
by

\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon}(x,p)=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\left(\varepsilon f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)-\left(\varepsilon f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(t)\right)p+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}p^{2},

which as
\varepsilon
vanishes along the sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})
, converges locally uniformly toward

H(x,p)=\alpha(x)I(t)\,p+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}p^{2},

since
I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)
converges uniformly toward
I(t)
on any interval
[0,T]
with
T>0
. At the same time,
\phi_{\varepsilon_{n}}
converges locally uniformly on
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
toward the continuous function
\phi(t,x)
(i.e. in compact subsets).

Let
\varphi\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
be a regular test function with
\varphi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\phi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
for some
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
, such that
\phi(t,x)\leq\varphi(t,x)
at a small neighborhood of
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. Similarly, for each
\varepsilon_{n}
, take a test function
\varphi_{n}
, a time

and a point

satisfying the same properties. It holds that

\varepsilon_{n}\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon_{n}}+\left(\varepsilon_{n}f(x)-\alpha(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)\right)\partial_{x}\varphi_{\varepsilon_{n}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}\varphi_{\varepsilon_{n}}|^{2}-\varepsilon_{n}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}^{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon_{n}}\\
\leq-\varepsilon_{n}\left(\varepsilon_{n}f^{\prime}(x)-\alpha^{\prime}(x)I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)\right),

but
I_{\mu_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(t)\rightarrow I(t)
a.e.
t\geq 0
as

. Using the local uniform convergence, it follows that

-\alpha(x^{\prime})I(t^{\prime})\partial_{x}\varphi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}\varphi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})|^{2}\leq 0,

therefore
\phi(t,x)
is a subsolution to the equation

-\alpha(x)I(t)\,\partial_{x}\phi(t,x)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}\phi(t,x)|^{2}=0.

(28)
