A four-year retrospective study on epidemiological updates of dermatophytosis in Kuwait
Authors: Faten Al-Wathiqi, Afnan Alharby, Mohammad Asadzadeh, Khaled Alobaid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDermatophytosis is a common superficial fungal infection caused by dermatophytes that infect keratinized tissues, including the skin, scalp, and nails. Dermatophytes are categorized into three groups: anthropophilic, zoophilic, and geophilic. In this study, we aimed to explore the recent epidemiology of dermatophytosis across Kuwait from January 2021 to December 2024. Data were collected from the Reference Mycology Laboratory in Kuwait, and a statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 10. A total of 214 dermatophyte isolates from 211 patients were obtained during the study period. The predominant dermatophytes isolated were zoophilic species, accounting for 127 (59%) cases, followed by 57 cases involving anthropophilic species (26.5%), 8 cases involving geophilic dermatophytes (4%), and 22 cases involving unidentified species (10.5%). The most frequently isolated dermatophyte was Trichophyton spp., with 132 cases identified (61.5%), followed by 73 cases involving Microsporum spp. (34%) and 8 cases involving Nannizzia spp. (4%). Epidermophyton spp. were rarely isolated, with only one case being found (0.5%). Seven cases of Trichophyton indotineae (3.3%) were also detected among all the Trichophyton spp. The most frequently affected age group was children under 10 years of age. Dermatophytosis was slightly more prevalent in males than in females. The abundance of zoophilic dermatophytes was greater than that of anthropophilic species. Furthermore, the appearance of T. indotineae cases in Kuwait highlights the need for antifungal stewardship in dermatology clinics, active surveillance of refractory recurrent cases, and optimized mycology diagnostic services in dermatology clinics.Keywords: Dermatophytes, Zoophilic, Microsporum, Epidermophyton, T. indotineae

Introduction

Dermatophytes are a group of fungi that are widely distributed in nature and cause infection in keratinized tissues, including the skin, hair, and nails of humans and animals. Dermatophytes can infect tissues by producing keratinase enzymes that penetrate and breakdown keratin, leading to the development of dermatophytosis [1]. Dermatophytes are categorized into three groups, anthropophilic, zoophilic, and geophilic, on the basis of the habitats they occupy in the environment [2]. Anthropophilic dermatophytes cause mycosis primarily in humans; in comparison, geophilic dermatophytes are typically found in the wider environment and can be spread to both humans and animals. Zoophilic species are known to frequently affect animals and can, in some cases, also affect humans [2]. More than 40 species are present within the major genera, namely, Trichophyton, Microsporum, and Epidermophyton [3]. In comparison, Arthroderma, Ctenomyces, Guarromyces, Lophophyton, Nannizzia, and Paraphyton are primarily geophilic species [4]. Although dermatophytes are prevalent worldwide, they are more commonly found in tropical countries because of their high levels of humidity, overpopulation, and poor hygiene standards [2].

Dermatophytosis, also known as ‘tinea infection’, can be classified according to the site of infection into the following categories: Tinea capitis, an infection of the scalp; tinea faciei, an infection of the face; tinea unguium, an infection of the nail; tinea pedis, an infection of the feet; tinea corporis (or tinea circinate), an infection of the arms and legs; tinea manuum, an infection of the hand; and tinea cruris, an infection of the groin area [5]. The most common symptoms of dermatophytosis include circular, erythematous, and itchy spots, which are also referred to as ‘ringworm’ [5]. The patient’s immune status, the dermatophyte species, and the infection site determine the infection severity [6].

Dermatophytes infect keratinized layers of skin, hair, and nails, and their prevalence increases under warm, humid conditions with an optimum temperature of 25–28 °C [7]. Specific factors are known to increase the risk of dermatophytosis, including contact with animals, high temperature and humidity, the use of closed shoes for prolonged periods, the use of tight clothes, prolonged antibiotic therapy, and the sharing of towels, socks, or shoes [8].

Dermatophytes exhibit distinct clinical patterns, and their prevalence and species distribution vary by region, which is influenced by socioeconomic factors, modern life, the intensification of travel, and the migration of populations from the southern hemisphere to the northern hemisphere [2]. In a five-year study conducted in the Kuwait capital region from 2000 to 2005, researchers identified Trichophyton mentagrophytes as the most common dermatophyte among 2730 patients, followed by Microsporum canis and Epidermophyton floccosum [9]. Another domestic study conducted in southern Kuwait in 2007 reported M. canis as the most frequently isolated species, followed by Trichophyton violaceum [10]. The authors of a recent study conducted in Kuwait also reported that M. canis was the most commonly isolated species [11]. In Europe, the increase in temperature has led to an increased prevalence of anthropophilic dermatophytes, such as Trichophyton rubrum, resulting in tinea capitis infections among children [12]. Studies conducted in European countries such as Slovakia and Sweden have highlighted T. rubrum as the predominant dermatophyte species [13, 14]. Moreover, T. mentagrophytes is predominant in coastal areas, such as Odisha in eastern India [15]. In contrast, M. canis remains the most common agent in warmer climates in America, causing tinea capitis infections, followed by Trichophyton tonsurans [16].

Kuwait is a small country in the Middle East that covers a surface area of approximately 17,818 square kilometers. On the basis of the latest population estimates of Kuwait Nationality in December 2024, the approximate number of Kuwaitis stands at 1,567,983, and the number of non-Kuwaitis stands at 3,419,843. Dermatophyte infections are considered to be relatively common in Kuwait. However, few studies have explored the epidemiological and clinical features of dermatophyte-related infections across limited population areas within the country. Given the prevalence of dermatophyte infections and the potential variation in species distribution and affected age groups over time, we conducted this study to highlight the epidemiological patterns of dermatophytosis in Kuwait and to analyze the variations in and distribution of dermatophyte species across diverse age groups and seasonality for the most common dermatophyte species.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The database used in this study was supplied by the Reference Mycology Laboratory (RML) at Mubarak Al-Kabeer Hospital. This is the sole clinical diagnostic laboratory offering specialized medical mycology services to a large cohort of patients from all government hospitals in Kuwait. The data included all dermatophyte isolates collected from January 2021 to December 2024, which were categorized on the basis of variables such as gender, age, sample type, sampling site, collection date, and genus/species identification. Each isolate was defined as the first positive culture of a dermatophyte obtained from a patient. Duplicate isolates, including dermatophytes from the same patient, dermatophytes of the same species, and dermatophytes from the same site within a three-month period, were excluded. In cases of mixed infections where the patient had different dermatophyte isolates, each species was considered separately. Isolates showing poor growth, contamination, or only nonspecific morphological features were reported at the genus level only based on colony morphology and microscopic features following the guidelines of the Atlas of Clinical Fungi by de Hoog et al. (2020) and Identification of Pathogenic Fungi by Campbell et al. (2013) [17, 18]. The seasonal classification for each year was formulated as follows: Winter (November–February), spring (March–May), summer (June–September), and autumn (October). Patient symptoms that initially suggested dermatophytosis included erythematous, scaly lesions with itching; thickened and discolored nails; and/or hair loss.

Dermatophyte identification used at the RML involved several steps, with direct microscopy performed on all clinical samples. First, 10–20% KOH was applied to the skin, nail, or hair samples and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature before they were analyzed under a microscope. Second, Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA; Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltman; USA) was used for fungal isolation. For culture, the samples were inoculated onto SDA and incubated at 25–30°C for 1–4 weeks. Afterward, both colony morphology and microscopic features were examined. Only 9 out of 214 dermatophyte isolates were identified using PCR and DNA sequencing upon the physician’s request, particularly in refractory infections. Molecular methods identified seven isolates as T. indotineae, two as T. rubrum, and one as T. tonsurans. The latter two species were consistent with prior phenotypic identification methods and were confirmed by sequencing. To perform DNA sequencing, a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen; Hilden; Germany) was used for DNA extraction; and the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was subsequently amplified using the following primers: ITS1 (5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’) and ITS4 (5’-TCTTTTCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’). Following purification using a PCR purification kit (Qiagen; Hilden; Germany), both strands were sequenced using a 3400 Series Genetic Analyzer—Biomed Global (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltman; USA) with the following primers: ITS1FS (5’-ACCTGCGGAAGGATCATT-3’), ITS2 (5’-TCGCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’), ITS3 (5’-TCGCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3’), and ITS4RS (5’-GATATGCTTAAGTTCAGCG-3’). To perform this procedure, a previously described method was followed [19, 20]. The DNA sequence data were assembled, and the final species identification was compared to reference sequences in the GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The DNA sequencing data reported in this study have been submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PX423816 to PX423821, PX487856 to PX487858 and OP926975.

Statistical analysis

All the data were assigned numerical codes and labels for each variable using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office version 16.98, USA). All duplicate isolates for which multiple samples from the same patient produced the same findings were excluded. The prevalence of dermatophytes was determined annually, and patients were categorized by gender. Patients were also grouped by age (< 10, 10–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and > 60 years old) to determine the frequency of dermatophytosis sites. The age groups were then subdivided into the following groups: Neonates (newborn to < 1 month), infants (> 1 month to < 1 year), toddlers (> 1 year to < 3 years), children (> 3 years to < 12 years), and adolescents (> 12 years to 18 years). The number of isolated dermatophytes was calculated according to the site of infection. A chi-square test was employed to analyze the association between clinical specimens and age group in relation to dermatophytosis. In addition, a chi-square test for independence was also employed to determine the frequency of cases among the four seasons throughout the years. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 10.5.0, USA), with a P value < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 214 fungal cultures from 211 patients (three patients presented with mixed dermatophyte infections; each species was considered separately) were collected during the study period (January 2021-December 2024). The mean age of the patients (± SD) was 23.6 ± 18.6 years. The skin scale (61.5%) was the predominant specimen, followed by the scalp (32%) and then the nails (6.5%) (Table 1) (Χ2 = 46.14, df = 9, P < 0.001). Males were more frequently affected (55%) than females were (45%; Table 2; Fig. 1). The majority of dermatophytes were found among children (37%; < 10 years old; Table 2) (Χ2 = 123.7, df = 18, P < 0.0001). The pediatric group was further divided into neonates, infants, toddlers, children, and adolescents (Table 3). Children were the most frequently affected group, with 66 cases (72.5%), followed by toddlers, with 19 cases (21%).

Discussion

Approximately 20–25% of global fungal infections are caused by dermatophytes. Over the past decade, the prevalence of cutaneous infections has increased, particularly in relation to skin and nail infections, driven by a number of factors, including travel and migration, a growing number of immunocompromised individuals, climate change, and close interactions with domestic animals [22]. In this study, M. canis was the most frequently isolated dermatophyte, accounting for 61 cases (28.5%), the majority of which involved tinea capitis infections (22%). The second most common isolated dermatophyte was the T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex, representing 27.5% of skin infection cases, primarily tinea corporis (20%; Table 4). Skin-related infections were the most prevalent superficial infections throughout the year, mainly causing tinea corporis (43.5%), followed by tinea cruris (8%), tinea faciei (5%), tinea manuum (2%), and tinea pedis (2%) (Fig. 1; Table 4). Nail infections were exclusively associated with Trichophyton spp.

Our results demonstrated variation in dermatophytosis among the different age groups, with children (< 10 years old; 37%; Table 2) representing the most frequently affected age group. Within this age group, Microsporum spp. were the most common etiologic agents, and most reported cases were associated with tinea capitis (51%; Fig. 2). The recorded incidence of dermatophytosis was primarily caused by three agents: M. canis, with 61 cases (28.5%); T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex, with 60 cases (27.5%); and T. tonsurans, with 25 cases (11.5%). It appears that the epidemiology of dermatophytosis has changed over time, with shifts between genera and/or species observed across the years. Both Adel et al. (2007) and Al-Aryan et al. (2022) reported that M. canis was the most common species isolated from children under 10 years old, with a higher prevalence in males than in females [10, 11]. However, neither study represents the broader epidemiology of Kuwait, as Adel et al. focused only on cases of tinea capitis in the southern region of Kuwait, and Al-Aryan et al. conducted research on a small population over a short period (only one year). Yehia et al. (2010) provided wider data from the capital of Kuwait, but their study was limited to a single hospital and revealed that the most common species was T. mentagrophytes, followed by M. canis, both of which were common in children and adults, with a mean age of 28.5 years [9]. Other studies conducted in the same region, specifically in Saudi Arabia, reported that the prevalence of tinea capitis infection among patients was the highest (22.3%); in comparison, tinea barbae infection was the least common. In this study M. canis was also the most common species identified [23]. In Egypt, M. canis is a common dermatophyte that is primarily responsible for tinea capitis infection in children [24].

Trichophyton spp., particularly the T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex, predominantly affect both young and middle-aged adults, with the majority of cases being tinea corporis infections (see Fig. 2; Table 4). Zoophilic dermatophytes, such as T. mentagrophytes and M. canis, are frequently found in their natural hosts, including cats, dogs, and rabbits, and are known to cause zoophilic infections in humans when infected arthrospores are transmitted from the haircoat of infected animals [25, 26]. When these dermatophytes are transmitted to humans, they primarily affect the exposed skin and scalp, causing conditions such as tinea corporis and tinea capitis. These observations could be attributed to factors such as the adoption of asymptomatic infected animals as pets, increased human exposure to infected animals, and limited access to veterinary services [27]. The greater occurrence of these zoophilic dermatophytes than anthropophilic dermatophytes in Kuwait, especially among children, may be due to several factors, including poor hygiene, contact with stray animals, and pet ownership. However, data on animal contact or environmental exposures were not collected, which limits our ability to understand why zoophilic species were more common in our study.

One of the T. indotineae isolates detected in our study was previously identified as T. mentagrophytes on the basis of its colonial and microscopic features. This isolate was documented in a case of generalized tinea infection involving the face and body, where the patient did not respond to multiple courses of antifungal medications and was found to be resistant to terbinafine [28]. The patient improved only after the isolate was accurately identified using molecular methods, prompting a change in treatment regimen [29]. However, the extent of this issue in Kuwait remains unknown, as most clinical isolates in mycology laboratories are not identified using molecular methods.

Our findings demonstrated that males constituted a slightly greater number of cases than females did, particularly regarding infections with Microsporum spp. The results of a study involving 356 patients clinically suspected of having dermatophytosis in India demonstrated that nearly all forms of tinea occurred primarily in men (223 cases; 62.6%) compared to females (133 cases; 37.4%) [29]. Similarly, the results of another study conducted in Korea involving 4,532,655 subjects with dermatophytosis revealed that it was predominant in males [30]. In contrast, the authors of a study conducted in Ethiopia involving 305 patients reported that females predominated in the majority of dermatophytosis cases [2]. These findings highlight the variation in gender predominance, suggesting that dermatophytosis can affect individuals regardless of gender, depending on numerous factors, such as socioeconomic conditions, hygiene, outdoor activities, and immune status.

The frequency of dermatophytosis based on seasonality from 2021 to 2024 (Fig. 3) demonstrated that occurrences were uniformly distributed throughout the year, with slight increases during the summer season. The differences between seasons were statistically significant (P value = 0.0087), indicating that the frequency of dermatophytosis is not evenly distributed across the seasons in Kuwait. In addition, spikes in spring 2021 and 2024 may have been influenced by high and/or warm temperatures and humidity, which can lead to excess moisture, making the summer and spring seasons ideal for the development of various fungal infections, including dermatophytosis [28, 31]. The spike in the number of cases in the winter of 2024 could have been due to people wearing layered clothing and spending more time indoors, which may contribute to increasing moisture, body heat, and close contact, which can increase fungal growth and transmission [32]. In addition, cooler temperatures may also reduce the natural defenses of the skin, making individuals more susceptible to infections [33].

Accurate species-level identification of dermatophytes is essential; however, accurate identification remains a common challenge in most diagnostic microbiology laboratories in Kuwait. Additionally, a significant proportion of cases involved unidentified species, accounting for 22 (10.5%), which were reported as genus-level based on morphological features observed following guidelines of the Atlas of Clinical Fungi and Identification of Pathogenic Fungi [17, 18]. This limited the ability to fully characterize the range of dermatophytes present and highlights the need for broader implementation of molecular identification methods in future studies. Another limitation was that the proportion of zoophilic isolates in this study may have been overestimated, as the isolates of the T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex were classified as zoophilic, although T. interdigitale is anthropophilic. Since molecular testing was not performed, which was not routinely available in our setting, accurate species-level differentiation between these very closely related species was not possible. Additionally, data on animal contact or environmental exposures were not available, which limited our ability to contextualize the predominance of zoophilic species. Only 7 isolates were identified as T. indotineae using PCR and DNA sequencing, as these tests are requested selectively for cases that are recurrent or refractory to therapy, based on the treating physician’s judgment. These clinical features have increasingly been associated with T. indotineae infections and were reported in a recent case study by Dashti et al. [28]. Introduction of molecular testing/MALDI TOF has become mandatory to detect rare or emerging species such as T. indotineae. Moreover, antifungal susceptibility testing was not available at the time of the study, as it is not routinely performed and is carried out only upon the physician’s request. Furthermore, private hospitals and clinics were not included. However, the RML serves a large population, including high-, middle-, and low-income individuals, from all governmental hospitals and dermatology clinics accessible to the entire population of Kuwait. It is also the only laboratory specializing in diagnostic mycology tests in Kuwait. Therefore, these surveillance data largely reflect the dermatophyte profile of the Kuwaiti population.

Conclusions

In this study, zoophilic species emerged as the predominant dermatophyte, accounting for 127 cases. These findings highlight the significant role of animal-to-human transmission and reveal that potential exposure to animals is a key risk factor for dermatophytosis in Kuwait. M. canis was the most frequently isolated dermatophyte and was involved mainly in tinea capitis infections, followed by the T. metagrophytes/interdigitale complex, which primarily caused tinea corporis infections. Among all age groups, children under 10 years of age were the most frequently affected, particularly those with M. canis. We also identified several cases of T. indotineae. However, the actual burden and resistance profile of T. indotineae in Kuwait still need to be determined. Although E. floccosum is common, it was rarely detected in our study. Monitoring the epidemiology of fungal infections, particularly dermatophytosis, is essential. These data will aid in identifying causative agents to develop effective treatment and prevention strategies, particularly given the limited information on the distribution of dermatophyte species in Kuwait. Access to diagnostic mycology services and improved local surveillance of dermatophytes will enhance our understanding of the disease and lead to better clinical outcomes, particularly when antifungal treatments are selected on the basis of the species involved. Moreover, this approach could aid in pinpointing the infection source, which is crucial for preventing recurrence and managing outbreaks.
