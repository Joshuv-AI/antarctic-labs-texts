Demonstration of sequential processors with quantum advantage and analysis of classical performance limits Preprint: APS/123-QED
Shota Tateishi 1, Wenhao Wang 2, Baptiste Chevalier 2, Takafumi Ono 1, Masahiro Takeoka 2,3, Wojciech Roga 2

Abstract
In this paper, we theoretically and experimentally analyze sequential processors with limited communication between parts. We compare the expressivity of sequential quantum and classical processors under the same constraints. They consist of three or four modules each of which processes local data. The modules of the quantum processor are linked through one qubit or one qutrit communication, while those of the classical processor communicate through one bit or one trit. For the classical processor, we prove bounds on its performance in terms of inequalities on correlations of the output with a target function. We show that the quantum processor violates these inequalities. We show this violation experimentally on a silicon photonics setup. We describe how to find the classical bound on correlations with arbitrary target function by reducing the problem to minimization of an Ising-type spin-glass Hamiltonian. Our theory is applicable in general problems, such as the low-rank binary matrix approximation.



I
Background and overview

Studying and comparing simple structure classical and quantum processors is motivated by the urge to understand the fundamental differences between them. This interest is driven by progress in quantum technology in general and quantum computing in particular. In addition, quick progress in such applications as machine learning raised new questions about the benefits of quantum processing units.

In
Peres
, the authors studied a simple one-qubit processor as an effective classifier. The classical data points
X_{i}
were encoded in classically tuned parameters of the circuit gates; see Fig.
1
. Free parameters of the circuit allowed for training the processor so that the finally measured probability of a chosen state could be used as an indicator of a classification label.

Figure 1:
Single qubit and single bit sequential processors of similar structure.

The authors proved the universality of such a classifier, that is, an analogue of the universal approximation theory in a single-layer neural network. This proved that even a single-qubit system can compute complicated functions of parameters of the circuit if it is long enough and is appropriately trained. These ideas were experimentally demonstrated
Dutta
;
Ono
;
Abe2025
;
Tolstobrov2024
.

Universality holds for both a quantum and a classical processors if they are sufficiently extended. In such a situation, a natural question is if there is an advantage of a quantum single-qubit processor over a fairly constructed classical counterpart with fixed finite size.
The authors in
Peres
discussed the possible advantage from entanglement and in
Roga
from parallelization. In the context of communication complexity
Trojek2005
;
Brukner2004
, the authors formulated a similar problem showing a quantum advantage in sending information in qubit states over the analogous scheme with classical bits. Similar conclusions appeared in the context of temporal entanglement and temporal Bell inequalities
Leggett
;
Brukner2009
. In another paper
Zukowski2012
, the author formulated the problem in the context of macro-realism and showed a quantum advantage in speeding-up the computation of certain functions. In
Trojek2005
an experiment was performed on bulk optics showing that a quantum multiparty communication line with a single qubit of communication (analogous to the sequential processor in Fig.
1
) can output a binary target function of local parameters
\{X_{i}\}
which is not achievable in a single-bit communication classical setup. Also, in
Bravyi
;
Maslov2021
the authors showed that certain deterministic outputs from quantum processors cannot be generated by classical circuits in limited space.
In this sense, one can speak of a larger expressive power of the quantum processor as in
Gao2022
;
Anschuetz2023
.

To show these results, it was necessary to prove rigorous limits on how well some functions could be approximated by the output of classical fixed-structure processors. The functions were shown for which the classical limit was known or established by the authors. However, for arbitrary functions this classical limit is unknown, and a complete understanding of the advantage of the quantum processor over the classical one even in limited space is still a subject of research.

Here, we study some aspects of this problem posing the following questions:

1.

Is there an advantage of using a fixed length quantum sequential processor with a single-qubit (or single qutrit) communication between processing modules, with respect to the same structure classical processor processing a single bit (or trit)?

2.

Can we quantify it rigorously?

3.

Can we demonstrate it experimentally on the silicon photonic platform?

Our original results are summarized as follows.

•

We report an experimental demonstration of the qubit and qutrit sequential processors on silicon photonics chip and demonstrate the achievable advantage of quantum processors in realizing certain functions of the input parameters. The qubit processor demonstration is analogous to the earlier bulk optics experiment
Trojek2005
, however, different technology–silicon photonics, and different degrees of freedom–a single photon in two modes are involved.

•

To face specific conditions of the design of the silicon photonics chip with four stages and qutrit communication (two photons in two modes) we needed to develop the theory showing the smallest Hamming distance between the deterministic part of the ideal quantum processor output and the optimal output of the corresponding classical processor. We established this bound based on the properties of the quantum processor output and optimization in the space of binary functions.

•

We showed that the problem of minimal distance between an arbitrary binary target function and the output of a classical sequential processor with limited communication can be reduced to optimization in the Ising model.

•

We successfully solved the Ising problems from the previous point for the classical processors with some target functions. Our models involved up to 48 spins with multipartite interaction. The models were solved on a simulated annealing machine Fixstars Amplify.

•

We indicate consequences of mapping the problem of the smallest distance between the output of a classical sequential processor and a given target for such problems as binary low-rank matrix completion and low-rank binary tensor approximation.

IV.1
Bound for one-bit processor

Proposition 1
.

The number of errors between the output of the three-stage processor with one-bit communication and the nonzero part of the target function (
5
) cannot be less than 8.

Proof.

In the proof, we explicitly use the properties of the target function (
5
). The rows of this matrix correspond to the processor action depending on the first-stage output. As the first module can have only two different symbols, the target reshuffled as a
4\times 16
matrix needs to be rank 2. So, two lines need to be identical. Let these lines be 1st and 3rd (the same can be repeated for all other choices). We add the two rows, obtaining a binary vector of length 16.

We reshape this vector into a matrix
4\times 4
. It needs to be rank 2, as the rows describe the processor’s actions depending on the output from the second stage, which can output at most two symbols. However, we observe that each row is different and that the minimum number of different bits between each pair of rows (minimum Hamming distance) is 2. We need to correct two bits in two rows. In this way, we necessarily introduce 4 errors.

Finally, we have the remaining lines 2 and 4 of the target reshaped into the matrix
4\times 16
. When we reshape each of these lines into
4\times 4
matrices, for the same reason as before, we need to correct 4 additional bits.

This justifies that the number of errors that we need to introduce to the target function (
5

In this section, we considered the problem of how well a classical sequential processor with a single bit or single trit communication can approximate a given target function of distributed input data. The target functions considered by now were specific ones generated by the outputs of quantum processors for the same data. By showing the minimum number of errors in the approximation, we show the gap in expressivity of the classical processor with respect to the quantum processor for specific tasks. The proofs of this section are based on explicit properties of the target functions.

In the next section, we consider a more general problem. Given an arbitrary binary target function, what is the best approximation by an output of a sequential processor? In general, this problem belongs to the high-complexity class. Solving it efficiently would imply an efficient algorithm for, for example, a low-rank binary matrix approximation, which is known to be NP-hard
Valiant
;
Lokam
;
Dan
. In the following section, we reduce the problem to an Ising problem, which can be approachable by nowadays and future Ising solvers based on simulated or quantum annealing, or other techniques.

Consider an
N
stage sequential processor with one-bit communication between modules and two-bit inputs in each module. The local inputs are not known to other modules. The performance of the processor can be optimized on the strategies of the modules.

We formulate two tasks that are addressed in the subsequent sections:

1.

Decide if a given binary target function
T(\{X_{i}\})
can be achieved as an output from the processor.

2.

Determine the smallest Hamming distance between the output of the classical processor
O(\{X_{i}\})

and the target function
T(\{X_{i}\})
.

V.1
Task 1: Existence criterion

For simplicity, assume that the number of processing modules is
N=3
. We can answer the first challenge by checking the number of different blocks of size 4, and 16 in the target function. There are 16 blocks of size 4. They are indexed by sequences of bits
(X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}X_{4})
and defined as

t^{[4]}(X_{1}...X_{4},X_{5}X_{6})=T(X_{1},...,X_{6}).

There are 4 blocks of size 16. They are indexed by sequences of bits
(X_{1}X_{2})
defined as

t^{[16]}(X_{1}X_{2},X_{3}...X_{6})=T(X_{1},...,X_{6})

Theorem 1.

The processor defined above can output functions
O(X_{1},...,X_{6})
with at most two different rows in the matrix
O^{[4]}(X_{1}X_{2},X_{3}...X_{6})
and at most two different rows in the reshuffled matrix
O^{[16]}(X_{1}...X_{4},X_{5}X_{6})
.

Proof.

There are four possible outputs (for each
X_{1}X_{2}
, one) from the first processor but only two binary symbols
0
or
1
. For each symbol there is one and only one succession (the row of the matrix
O^{[4]}(X_{1}X_{2},X_{3}...X_{6})
), as the following processor cannot distinguish the same symbols output for different inputs of the first processor. So, there can be only two different rows in matrix
O^{[4]}
. Similarly, there are 16 outputs (for each sequence
(X_{1}...X_{4})
, one) from the second processor, but only two binary symbols. For each symbol, there is one succession. The row of the matrix
O^{[16]}(X_{1}...X_{4},X_{5}X_{6})
for a chosen value of
(X_{1}...X_{4})
is equal to one of these successions. If we recognize that the target function consists of only two distinct rows
O^{[16]}
and only two distinct rows
O^{[4]}
we can associate symbols
0
or
1
as the output of the first processor in the positions corresponding to different distinct rows. Similarly, we associate
0
or
1
as the outputs of the second processor in the positions corresponding to distinct rows of
O^{[16]}
.
∎

The argument can be easily extended to processors with more modules and different limits on communication between the modules.

Therefore, we can formulate the exisence criterion as the following theorem.

Theorem 2. [Existence criterion]

For a binary function of several variables, one can check if it can be an output from a sequential processor with a single bit communication and a given distribution of variables between the modules by checking if each reshuffling of this function into a matrix with rows indexed by the local variables of earlier modules, and columns indexed by the remaining variables is row-rank at most 2.

For sequential processors with a single trit communication, the row-rank should be 3, etc.

VI
Extensions and Outlook

In this paper, we have studied theoretically and experimentally the separation between performance of quantum and classical processors of the same structure tackling the problem of approximation of certain functions of input variables when the variables are available only locally to different modules and the communication between the modules is restricted. In particular, binary functions can be achieved by the quantum sequential processors with qubit or qutrit communication, which can only be approximated by the output of the corresponding classical processors. We showed that existing silicon optics technology allows us to demonstrate this separation.

To prove the separation in the cases tailored to our experiment, we needed to develop a general theoretical framework which allows us to put strict boundaries on the performance of classical processors. We did it two ways. From the properties of specific target binary functions related to the output of our experiment, and from the reduction of the optimization in the set of binary function which some properties to the Ising problem for arbitrary binary targets.

If the target function is an arbitrary binary function, the Ising optimization, or alternatively the optimization of the corresponding correlation function gives the solution to the problem of the best approximation of one arbitrary binary function by another which satisfies some restrictions. In our case, the restriction is to be the output of a sequential processor with communication between the modules restricted to a link with given dimensionality. The restriction has a clear interpretation when we reshape the function in the form of a matrix, the rows of which correspond to the processor response to given outputs of one module. As the number of symbols in the output is restricted, the number of different rows is restricted. The number of different rows in a binary matrix corresponds to the rank. So, the problem of finding the optimal output of a two-module processor with limited communication to an arbitrary binary function directly corresponds to the problem of approximation of an arbitrary binary matrix by a binary matrix of specific rank. The optimality is defined here by the Hamming distance.

Unlike in low rank approximation of real or complex matrices, which can be done by reducing rank in the singular value decomposition, the low rank approximation of binary matrices is known as the hard problem. A side result of our work is the reduction of this problem to the generalized Ising problem, which is also known to be hard in general, but dedicated approximate solvers exist and are still improving.

Another restriction on approximation of the general binary target function of a number of variables can be the output of a multi-module processor. In this case, we approximate a general tensor (indexed by the variables) in terms of a concatenation of tensors of specific ranks. Finding an optimal approximation in terms of the Hamming distance is again a difficult task, which we explicitly reduce to a generalized Ising problem and can attack with existing Ising solvers.

The third related application of the techniques we developed in this paper is the binary matrix completion. We could ask, what is a good fixed rank binary matrix completion of a binary matrix with restricted number of known entries. Again we tacle this problem by creating the binary matrix with highest possible correlation function with the existing part of the reference matrix and with required values of the rank or ranks in different matrix reshapings.

Finally, the direct application of our techniques is the logic and sequential logic design. In fact, given an arbitrary binary target function of a number of variables can be interpreted as the truth table for some logical formulas of the corresponding Boolean variables. The optimal construction of the logical circuits or circuits satisfying specific conditions such as being distributed or sequential is a highly desirable and unsolved problem. Although approximations exist and are widely used, new techniques, such as the one proposed in this paper, can contribute to the design improvement.
