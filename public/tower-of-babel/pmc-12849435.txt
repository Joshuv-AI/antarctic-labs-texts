Methodological development for core outcome sets in population health research: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Ameeta Retzer, Peymane Adab, Melanie Calvert, Niyah Campbell, Justin Varney, Paul Fisher, Dyna Arhin-Tenkorang, Joseph Merriman, Isobel M Harris, Foram Khatsuria, Pamela Nayyar, Chloe Gay, Kate Jolly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCore outcome sets (COS) aid evidence synthesis and promote relevance of and reduce waste from trials. COS are commonly used in biomedical research settings, but their application in population health research is less established. There is growing interest in using COS in population health trials, but population health interventions are often complex and involve a wide range of stakeholders, differing from biomedical research. Current guidance for COS development was originally intended for effectiveness trials in biomedical research, and COS implementation in trials is limited. This exploratory research formulates COS methodology for use in population health research trials, identifies priority topics for COS development, and considers how to promote COS use once developed.MethodsThree mixed-method work packages (WP) are as follows: (1) Public health evidence review to identify outcome heterogeneity and reporting of indicators of inequality and disadvantage, (2) stakeholder data review of COS on the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials Initiative database and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) public health guidance, and (3) semi-structured focus group discussions via an expert workshop exploring how routinely collected data can be considered in population health core outcome sets, differences in population health research that COS methodology would need to accommodate, barriers and facilitators to COS uptake applicable to population health research, and areas of population health research that would benefit from COS development.ResultsIn WP1, 264 population health Cochrane reviews were identified and analysed. Eighty reviews recommended outcome standardisation, including 13 specifically recommending COS development. In WP2, 50 non-population health COS, 22 population health COS, and 61 NICE Public Health guidance documents used an average of 7, 4.7, and 6.1 stakeholder groups, respectively. The WP3 workshop involved 41 participants. Six themes (COS development, routine data collection, population health research, stakeholders, methodology, COS uptake) were identified. Five methodological recommendations were formulated.ConclusionsThis study proposes how COS should be developed for use in population health research, identifies areas for COS development, recommends COS implementation strategies for population health research trials, and explores novel applications of COS methodology such as to standardise routinely collected health and non-health data often used in population health trials. The study findings complement existing COS guidance, promoting a consistent and rigorous approach to COS development for use in population health trials.Study registrationThis study was approved by the University of Birmingham Research Ethics Committee (ERN 21-1821) and registered at Research Registry (researchregistry7693, 28.02.2022).Keywords: Core outcome set, Methodology, Public health, Trials

Background

In trials, outcomes are chosen to assess the impact of interventions on health conditions or risk factors [1]. Clearly defined outcomes allow for replication in other trials and facilitate comparison, data synthesis, and meta-analyses. Choosing appropriate outcomes can be complex, and decisions are based on several factors including stakeholders’ views, responsiveness to the interventions in question, associated financial cost, and acceptability to participants [2]. Core outcome sets (COS) are developed to improve comparability of similar trials, reduce outcome reporting bias, and uphold the relevance of trial findings and systematic reviews [3]. COS represent the minimum outcomes to be collected and reported in all trials in a specific health research area and are developed through consensus-driven ratification [4]. There is comprehensive methodological guidance for COS development available that identifies and engages with key stakeholders and uses systematic processes and consensus methods to agree and finalise COS for use in specific applications. COS can address research waste through minimising duplication [5–8]; involving key stakeholders; promoting transparent trial design, outcome standardisation and use of patient-centered outcomes [9–11]; and promoting complete outcome reporting from trials—an issue across health research [12, 13] and public health research specifically [14].

However, trial outcome conceptualisation differs depending on setting, and current COS methodology does not accommodate the differences between population health and biomedical research. COS to date have been developed for use in effectiveness trials in biomedical research, but in population health research, intervention development and outcome use differ, and a complex systems perspective may be required [15–17]. Population health can be defined as “the health outcomes of a group of individuals, as well as the distribution of such outcomes within the group” [18]. For example, in 2022, the overall life expectancy in the UK was 80.9 years; however, depending on gender, life expectancy varies—for males, this being 79.0 and 82.9 for females [19], a pattern observed internationally [20]. Similarly, other indicators of inequality and disadvantage, such as age, ethnicity, race, and socio-economic status, can be used to understand and explore the distribution of outcomes. Inequalities in health are significant and widening, reflecting social and economic disparities, a trend observed globally [21–24]. In England, people living in the most deprived areas develop multiple long-term conditions 10 years earlier than those in the least deprived areas [25]. As such, population health interventions aim to improve health outcomes across the population while considering the differential impacts of interventions and how outcomes are distributed between population subgroups. As a result, indicators of inequality need to be considered alongside COS when designing trials with a population health focus.

Additionally, within the paradigm of the “social determinants of health”, patterns of health and disease in society are determined by how a particular society organises itself and delivers well-being to its population [26], placing the focus of study on populations or communities as a whole. Population health interventions are often complex, draw on a range of data sources, and result in a wide range of outcomes, some of which are beyond health or may be attitudinal, behavioural, or a risk factor associated with an adverse health outcome [27]. A population approach addresses the distribution of risk exposure in a population on the basis of its contextual conditions. Within this, population health interventions address contextual conditions of risk to alter the distribution of health risk in populations, such as smoking bans in public places to reduce smoking, water fluoridation to reduce caries, and mandatory folate fortification policies to reduce the prevalence of neural tube defects [28]. Further, non-population health actors, including those responsible for the built environment, transport, education, and employers, often implement interventions impacting on population health [29] but value non-health outcomes as these can impact upon funding allocation [30].

COS are important tools for trialists, but how current COS methodology accommodates population health research is not yet known. Consensus on appropriate methodology is needed before multiple approaches are used in population health COS development, resulting in incomparable, rapidly outdated outputs [31] with variable potential for use and to identify priority areas to maximise utility. We aimed to generate guidance to underpin the development of COS for use in population health research trials relating to the wider determinants of health and identify areas of population health research that would benefit from COS development and novel application of COS methodology.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) was provided by public contributors supporting the National Institute for Health and Care Research Applied Research Collaboration West Midlands (NIHR ARC WM) public health theme. Through regular involvement in project management and dedicated PPIE meetings throughout the course of the research, they contributed to the development of the original research proposal and application, advised on the conduct and delivery of the research, particularly the inclusion of public participants in the expert workshop and formulation of key messages for dissemination. Involvement was overseen by the NIHR ARC WM PPIE lead.

A three-stage, sequential mixed-method process was used to develop methodological recommendations and identify priority areas for COS’ development.

Search strategy and study selection

A strategy was formulated using available methodological guidance [32, 33]. The review identified evidence syntheses from the Cochrane Database describing population health research trials relating to the wider determinants of health. The Cochrane Database was downloaded with a 10-year restriction and screened to identify evidence syntheses describing population health research relating to the wider determinants of health. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were generated with the study management group, with particular input from public health professionals and research colleagues. The group refined the criteria by reviewing a random sample of evidence syntheses. Evidence syntheses were included if they related to the wider determinants of health as defined by Dahlgren and Whitehead [34] and were population-level interventions (those intended to be delivered to groups as a whole rather than on an individual basis [28]). Those relating to COVID-19 and rapid reviews were included if they related to the wider determinants of health. Reviews on the uptake, reach, and delivery of screening programmes were included. Reviews focusing on low- and middle- income country settings only, specific clinical populations, and screening tool sensitivity were excluded. Investigators were applied health researchers experienced in evidence synthesis and systematic evaluation. Calibration exercises were conducted prior to the review [35] until 90% agreement was reached. A second investigator independently assessed a 10% sample, and agreement was monitored. Disagreements were reconciled through regular discussion with a third investigator.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a pre-designed checklist composed of items including review author rationale for outcome selection, reported use of a COS or a standardised set of outcomes, any reported prioritisation of the outcomes selected, the review author’s explicit recommendation for outcome standardisation (including a COS), the review author’s explicit specific recommendation for COS development (rather than outcome standardisation generally), planned subgroup analyses of indicators of inequality and disadvantage (age, gender, ethnicity, and socio-economic status), and whether these occurred. Data were extracted by one investigator, and a second investigator extracted data from a 10% sample following calibration exercises involving the wider team. Disagreements were reconciled through discussion with a third investigator, and agreement was monitored.

Two investigators independently assigned eligible reviews to a wider determinant of health [34] category. Where more than one category within the wider determinant of health model seemed applicable, this was recorded. Disagreements were reconciled through discussion with a third investigator.

Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using MS Excel on the number of items from the checklist present in the reviews.

Data collection

COS on the COMET Database are registered with stakeholder involvement data, and individuals who self-identify as committee members are listed on NICE guidance consultations [36]. WP2 used three datasets: (i) population health COS, (ii) COS unrelated to population health (non-PH COS) indexed on the COMET COS database, and (iii) NICE Public Health reviews addressing the wider determinants of health.

Data

All COMET Database COS were downloaded, and two investigators independently hand-searched for population health COS according to criteria used in WP1, following calibration exercises involving the wider team. Stakeholder data were available on the COMET website registry entry and were collected separately for each entry. A data extraction form was developed using the COMET Database stakeholder categories. Two investigators independently extracted COS stakeholder data. Documents for all NICE public health guidelines were reviewed to identify committee membership lists for each guidance document. Stakeholder data from the NICE public health guidance were categorised using the COMET stakeholder categories. Stakeholders were categorised iteratively by two independent investigators. Disagreements were reconciled through discussion with a third investigator. New categories were developed for NICE public health guidance stakeholders that could not be categorised in the original COMET categories. These categories were finalised through review by the research team, including members with experience of public health practice who checked categories for relevance.

A convenience sample of 50 non-population health (non-PH) COS was identified from the COMET COS database by downloading the COMET COS database a second time and selecting the first 50 entries.

Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to present frequencies of stakeholder involvement and the mean, median, and minimum and maximum range of stakeholder groups per data source.

Study population and recruitment

Expert workshop participants were based in the UK and were population health academics, outcome methodologists, health economists, PPIE advisors, public members, public health practitioners, and local authority personnel. Participants were recruited through the research team’s networks (including via NIHR School for Public Health Research, NIHR Applied Research Collaboration (ARC) National Consortia, Birmingham City Council, Sandwell Metropolitan Borough Council) and snowball sampling. Recruitment was monitored as it progressed, and sampling became increasingly purposeful to achieve maximum variation. Participants were sampled in accordance with predetermined categories, namely population health academics, outcome methodologists, health economists, PPIE professionals, public members, and public health practitioners, aiming for < 8 per group. The NIHR INCLUDE [37] guidance was used, and outreach recruitment was planned, including travel to community spaces to undertake in-person recruitment, but this was limited due to COVID-19. Sample size was derived based on the number of topics to be discussed, having sufficient group sizes for the breakout sessions, and past experience of methodological workshops.

Potential participants were contacted by research team members by email with an invitation to join the workshop, and an information sheet was shared.

Participant preparation

To minimise participant burden, prereading documentation was limited, and alternative forms of media and in-person interaction were used, including a bespoke animation [38], to accessibly convey the key concepts used in the study. Public members were invited to a preparation session and offered honoraria of £25 per hour to recognise their time and contribution.

Workshop format

The workshop consisted of presentations and facilitated discussions to draw upon the expertise of the group, using videoconferencing software (Zoom), planned in collaboration with the PPIE panel. Findings from WP1 and WP2 were presented. Discussions were led by topic experts from within the research team. Participants were split into four groups in breakout rooms with even representation of each participant type. Following PPIE advice to address potential power dynamics, two public participants and one PPIE advisor were assigned to each group. Discussions were recorded using encrypted digital recorders, and detailed notes were taken by research team members experienced in qualitative methods.

Discussions were semi-structured, allowing exploration of new topics as they arose. The topic guide explored the following: how routinely collected data can be considered in population health COS, differences in population health research that COS methodology would need to accommodate, barriers and facilitators to COS uptake applicable to population health research, and areas of population health research that would benefit from COS development. On the advice of the PPIE panel, all groups discussed all topics. After the breakout group discussions, the wider group reconvened, and discussions were relayed for further comment.

Discussion summaries

Discussion summaries were generated using the workshop notes and recordings. Statements relating to COS development in population health research were identified from those relating to general COS methodology by a team member with experience of COS development (e.g. those referring to the types of data collected in population health research, study designs used, stakeholders involved, and the aims of public health research insofar as they differed from biomedical research). The statements were formulated into recommendations, reviewed by the wider research team, and reviewed by workshop participants and those unable to attend. Participants were invited to check the interpretation of their contributions.

Data synthesis

Data from all work packages were integrated during interpretation and reporting of findings [39], and conclusions were drawn relating to priority areas for COS development and key methodological considerations. The evidence syntheses identified in WP1 and the population health COS identified in WP2 were considered and matched for potential use.

Results

Cochrane Library review

The Cochrane Library was searched on March 1, 2022. In total, 6189 Cochrane reviews were screened against our inclusion criteria, identifying 264 eligible reviews. These were mapped to the wider determinants of health model, resulting in the majority (n = 222) being mapped to one category and 42 mapped to more than one. Most of the reviews either mapped to healthcare services (n = 88) or individual lifestyle factors (n = 85) as their primary category. Thirty-two percent (n = 83) were placed in seven of the remaining health determinant categories as their primary category. No reviews were placed in “living and working conditions” or “unemployment” as their primary category, though these were used as secondary categories for two reviews.

Eighty Cochrane reviews recommended outcome standardisation, 13 of these specifically recommending COS development (Table 1). The majority of these reviews related to either healthcare services (n = 27) or individual lifestyle factors (n = 25).

Expert workshop

The expert workshop was held via Zoom on May 11, 2022, involving 41 participants including 6 population health academics, 2 outcome methodologists, 4 health economists, 4 PPIE professionals, 10 public members, and 15 public health practitioners. The workshop lasted for approximately 4 h including breaks. Further comments were received until August 4, 2022. Six themes were identified: areas for COS development, routine data collection, population health research methods, buy-in and consensus among population health stakeholders, promoting public involvement, and implementation of population health COS:

Areas for COS development

Areas for COS development

Workshop participants discussed the scope of COS required for population health research and how, due to its broad, interdisciplinary nature, subject-specific COS focusing only on research may be less helpful than sets for broad themes. Specific suggestions for these themes were not posited, though participants considered the potential usefulness of mental wellbeing as a core outcome across population health research. If a core set applicable across population health was viable, using subject-specific add-ons may be a solution. Participants described how specific outcome sets give detail concerning a narrower range of outcomes, and broad outcome sets allow comparisons across a wider geographic area or range of activities. For health economist participants, the value of standardising outcome collection in population health research was the potential for the resulting COS to help decision-makers prioritise interventions based on their effectiveness. Health economist participants described how the COS would need to be broad enough to make comparisons of effectiveness across interventions throughout population health research to inform choice between interventions. Health economist participants advised how, for economic evaluation, population health uses generic, allocative efficiency questions, whereas technical efficiency is used in a disease-specific context. As a result, participants described how, where intervention-specific sets are developed, they should be used in conjunction with a broader set so comparison can still take place.

Participants suggested the use of existing population health frameworks, such as the Public Health Outcomes Framework (PHOF) [40] focus areas (wider determinants, health improvement, health protection, healthcare, and premature mortality) or to consider the PHOF in terms of short, medium, and long-term impacts, and the wider determinants of health. Place-based outcomes, digital inclusion, service provision and access, communities (including healthy environments, energy used on estates, housing, poverty, domestic violence), and health inequalities were all proposed. Participants suggested that a horizon scanning approach could allow focus on areas with insufficient data or where this may be the case in the future.

Participants described challenges relating to sensitive and stigmatising topics or those experienced differently by various socio-economic groups and how this interacted with barriers to research participation experienced by particular groups, limiting research generalisability. Public health researchers described how for COS relating to research reliant on purchasing data (for example alcohol, foods/beverages, and tobacco) the usefulness and uptake of the resulting COS would be minimised by the challenges encountered when securing these data.

2.Routine data collection

Routine data collection

Participants described the use of routine data in population health research, and that COS could help define this data, particularly those collected by local authorities, addressing the issue of COS prescribing the collection of a specific outcome without it being feasible in practice. Participants described several gaps in routine data collection and challenges of using this data in population health trials, including data availability (due to timeliness, expense, issues relating to data linkage), data granularity, and data of interest not being routinely collected. Outcome methodology participants described how using a replacement policy may allow for routinely collected data that is not being analyzed to be replaced, with the acknowledgment that routinely collected data is done so for administrative rather than research purposes.

To enable interpretation of differential experiences of intervention outcomes and inequality of uptake, workshop participants supported collecting data relating to the structural drivers of inequality in addition to sociodemographic data. Participants described how these indicators are not consistently collected in routine data or research, and there is variation in which data are collected, the categories and cut-off points used, and the levels at which these data are aggregated. Participants described how consensus-generating activities are required to standardise practice, and without this, analyses to explore inequality of outcomes based on these indicators are not possible.

Workshop participants expressed how public engagement efforts are needed to ensure acceptable data collection in terms of trust, burden, and consent related to use of and access to data. Participants expressed how, while important for all additional routine data collection, efforts to collect data relating to inequality and disadvantage must be sensitive to and informed by historical and ongoing mistrust of research that may be felt by the communities and individuals this would involve. Participants described how this can be best achieved through meaningful collaboration and engagement with communities throughout the research cycle.

3.Population health research methods

Population health research methods

Population and public health researchers described how study designs used in population health trials differ from those used in biomedical research and include natural experiments and complex interventions, impacting upon outcome selection. This meant for natural experiments reliant on before and after data, outcome selection would need to be dictated by available datasets, whereas in prospective studies and randomised controlled trials, there is more control over outcome choice as researchers can use bespoke surveys or data collection and aspirational outcomes can be more feasibly built into study design. As a result, participants described how outcomes may be conceptualised differently in population health research whereby natural experiment exposures result in diffuse outcomes measured differently compared to a controlled research environment, taking place over longer periods of time and different conceptions of effectiveness (particularly in the case of natural experiments), and resulting in different outcomes depending on a range of factors including those relating to inequity. Participants stated how this requires consideration of the theory of change for the intervention in question, including the role of causal factors associated with place of residence, education, historical antecedents, the unintended impacts of local or national policies, and understanding of policy priority and objectives for those using research findings as this will affect whether an intervention is implemented. Participants advised how process outcomes may have greater emphasis, such as those relating to access, uptake, and engagement, and how these might interact with indicators of disadvantage and inequality. As a result, participants described how the collection of a diverse range of outcomes requires the use of a wide range of data collection methods and recognition that some outcomes will not be known in advance. Workshop participants warned that prespecifying outcomes in a COS may mean that those unknown outcomes remain unidentified, though this is mediated as COS specify minimum requirements and triallists can choose additional outcomes.

4.Buy-in and consensus among population health stakeholders

Buy-in and consensus among population health stakeholders

Population and public health researchers described how population health interventions are multicomponent in nature and have a wide range of goals. Further, participants stated that population health evaluations of social interventions may use non-health primary outcomes, such as those in education or the judicial sector. Health economist participants described how interventions may be funded by stakeholders who are not the primary beneficiaries of the intervention, resulting in nonpublic health actors delivering interventions with a public health benefit and vice versa. They advised how this means that from an economic evaluation perspective, there is a shift from the use of primary outcomes to a cost-consequences model whereby a range of outcomes are presented to allow stakeholders to use as required in their individual decision-making contexts. They advised how capturing a diverse range of outcomes experienced by a range of stakeholders, including those in non-health settings, allows for an incentivising mechanism that facilitates further funding of population health interventions. Outcome methodology participants advised how this can occur by using the consensus-generating mechanisms within COS development to accommodate differences in stakeholder priorities. Public health and health economic participants discussed how decision-making for population health intervention funding often involves non-health actors and stakeholders so their inclusion in COS development promotes their investment in and impact upon the outcome standardisation process so the information they require for funding decision-making is collected. A participant described how, for example, research funders may prioritise health outcomes and the use of primary outcomes compared to a flexible model that enables the collection of a wide range of health and non-health outcomes; by definition, the interests of those communities underserved by research may not be represented, and priorities held by members of the public may be more intangible or difficult to demonstrate the cost-effectiveness. They shared that as a result, the priorities of policymakers and those responsible for budgets must be considered when deciding outcomes.

Participants proposed solutions including exploring how community and population values may be integrated within outcomes used in cost evaluations; using PPIE to help conceptualise the boundaries of COS and learn about the anticipated outcomes of interest from their perspective using qualitative methods; including stakeholders, particularly policymakers, during outcome identification processes during COS development to ensure buy-in; conveying the distinction between lower-level complaints and genuine high-level concern when prioritising outcomes; and investing in consensus-generating processes that are accessible and constructive for all stakeholders.

Participants emphasised how identifying and including all relevant stakeholders enable their outcomes of interest to be included in the prioritisation process, and that this may include members of the public and policymakers.

5.Promoting public involvement

Promoting public involvement

Participants familiar with COS methodology described how COS development in population health requires engagement with the general public rather than patients with specific lived experiences. They described how this challenges assumptions of prior knowledge of subject areas that may be expected from those with experience as patients and means efforts are needed to convey necessary background information. They stated that the centrality of disadvantage and inequality to population health would require further efforts to promote engagement and accessibility in COS development with those under-represented in research. 6.Implementation of population health COS

Implementation of population health COS

The participants discussed the use of population health COS and advised that an implementation strategy configured in advance can promote eventual use of COS in trials and beyond, which may include dissemination of COS by those involved in its development; considering stakeholder buy-in during development and its eventual use; considering the context, e.g. in population health and integrated care systems; and ensuring participants in development are aware that the COS represents the minimum requirement.

Participants discussed how, due to the use of routinely collected data in population health research, drawing on these during COS development rather than research literature (which may be more aspirational and expensive to collect) may enable ready implementation. However, they emphasised that some outcomes specified in COS are aspirational and represent absolute gaps, identified as being crucial but without tools for current use, so serve as a prompt for future research. In these cases, participants affirmed that COS should not be limited by available data but should serve to prospectively establish a standard, and that items not routinely collected or without an established or standardised metric can be acknowledged as a limitation.

Population health researchers and public health practitioners described how, while COS methodology could be used to standardise data collected in local authorities, there is variation between local authorities in terms of capacity to undertake routine data collection. They stated that the development of COS for this purpose would require implementation planning because additional data collection requires strategic oversight, and the process is labour-intensive. Participants described how those collecting data are wary of workload and perceived burden, reluctance from members of the public, and incomplete data. They also shared how issues of trust, burden, and consent must be interrogated, and historical and contemporary causes of mistrust and fear must be addressed to do this effectively. They advised how compared to in biomedical research adding items to routinely collected data requires public engagement using clear language, minimal acronyms, careful attention to how wording may be interpreted, and ensuring linguistic and functional accessibility. Participants shared how this input can inform volume, content, and frequency of data collection to minimise participant attrition and missing data. PPIE managers shared examples of how the public can be integrated into oversight processes, and data trusts made up of public members were cited as a useful way to reassure the public contributing their data and clarify the rationale for data collected, its intended use, and planned access.

Discussion

This research aimed to (1) describe outcome heterogeneity and reporting of indicators of inequality and disadvantage in population health evidence syntheses, (2) identify areas of population health research that may require outcome standardisation, (3) characterise key stakeholders involved in the development of population health COS and NICE public health reviews, and (4) explore methodological considerations of COS development in population health research and formulate methodological recommendations to support this.

Several areas for COS development have been identified. Eighty Cochrane reviews recommended outcome standardisation due to heterogeneity in the sample of trials. Thirteen reviews specifically called for COS development. Five of the population health COS identified have been suggested for potential use in cases where outcome standardisation was recommended. This is less than what has been observed outside of population health research [41]. Using existing outcomes frameworks as a basis for COS development may ensure alignment with current routine data collection and enable implementation of COS, a challenge consistently identified in research [42]. While no consensus was reached relating to the ideal scope of population health COS, there was interest in a COS that encompassed the breadth of population health rather than focusing on specific subject areas.

Population health COS used fewer stakeholders than non-PH COS. Ten new stakeholder groups have been proposed for use and should be chosen based upon COS scope and feasibility considerations [4]. The new categories may inform the development of population health COS and, if adopted, could promote transparent reporting of stakeholder involvement. In the workshop, participants discussed the challenges of unifying the diverse stakeholders involved in population health, stressing the importance of early involvement, trusting in the consensus-generating process, and prioritising the generation of data that is required for policymaking and funding allocation decisions, particularly for non-health stakeholders who may be beneficiaries of population health interventions. COS developed for use in population health trials may require broader conceptualisation of outcomes that allows for non-health outcomes, reduced emphasis on primary outcomes, and flexible outcome use due to unanticipated impacts and longer time frames in complex population health interventions using different study designs. Additionally, COS development would need to accommodate new sources in the outcome identification process such as outcome frameworks and routinely collected data. Promoting the uptake of population health COS in trials and beyond requires involvement of intended COS users in the development process to promote buy-in, relevance, and practical consideration for the context of eventual use. This could be formulated into a strategy in advance and include deliberate communication of the purpose of the COS to development participants.

We found that indicators of inequality and disadvantage were inconsistently collected and reported in publications, which undermined the meta-analysis in the included Cochrane reviews, echoing the existing literature [43]. The potential usefulness of COS in population health trials could be hindered by inconsistent collection and reporting of these data alongside core outcomes, inhibiting interpretation of the differential distribution of outcomes related to these variables. Research funders are increasingly aware of the need to ensure research samples are representative of target populations across a range of characteristics [44]. There is a taxonomy for characteristics that stratify health opportunities and outcomes [45], though guidance over the precise characteristics to be collected and reported in all public health research to promote equity-centred meta-analyses is currently unavailable. How to promote the consistent collection of indicators of inequality and disadvantage, and whether standardisation of these to enable comparison and meta-analyses would be useful or feasible, is yet to be explored. Standardisation implies the existence of common domains, while indicators of inequality and disadvantage are context-specific and highly variable. However, the acceptability of any additional collection of indicators of inequality and disadvantage must be undertaken in partnership with the public [46]. Feasibility will also need to be considered due to the limited capacity of local authorities and to manage burden.

In population health research, recruiting and engaging the public, including those underserved by research, has inherent challenges due to the likelihood that individuals may have limited prior knowledge of a particular topic (unlike those with lived experience involved in biomedical research), COS concepts being difficult to convey, and the use of processes in COS development that can be inaccessible, such as Delphi survey [47]. Unlike in non-PH COS that can use clinical pathways to recruit those with lived experience, alternative recruitment pathways are needed to involve members of the public in population health COS development.

There are strengths and limitations to consider. A broad range of data sources has been used in this work. While this strengthens the study, a potential limitation is that only the COS focuses on outcome standardisation, while the other data sources were not intended to be considered in this way. In WP1, it was not possible to use commonly used evidence synthesis frameworks (e.g. populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes) due to the broad research area without particular search terms likely to consistently identify relevant records. WP1 was limited by the narrow focus of Cochrane reviews, restricting outcome standardisation conclusions to that field of inquiry, minimising the generalisability to the wider research area. A strength of the study is that we characterised the reviews by mapping on the Whitehead and Dahlgren model, but we found that the majority of reviews related to health care services and individual lifestyle factors, underrepresenting categories such as “unemployment” and “living and working conditions”.

In WP2, the COMET Database stakeholder review is limited by the accuracy of COS developer registration and availability of relevant categories, and it is not known how the original stakeholder categories were developed. Due to the narrow scope of the research aims, exploration of the characteristics of the COS identified in WP2 in terms of their scope and final content was not possible. This WP was reliant on the information provided during registration on the COMET Database, and while the COMET Initiative reviews and updates on the availability of publications, the final COS are not included in the database record. However, future research on this topic should involve a wider mapping exercise to follow up the resulting COS and explore the topic areas and the final outcomes. Due to the rapid and exploratory nature of this work, convenience sampling was used to identify non-PH COS rather than rigorous approaches using stratification or randomisation. Because of this, our findings are based on descriptive statistics, so the data may not have been fully utilised.

As COS research can be inaccessible, this was mediated in the WP3 expert workshop using a bespoke informational video and providing preparation sessions for public members. Despite this, participants shared their lack of understanding and confidence with the topic, limiting meaningful participation. This may be addressed in future methodologically focused workshops through further consultation with public advisors during planning. We chose a workshop format rather than one-to-one interviews or focus group discussions to add to the appeal of the event and to capitalise on participant interaction [48]. However, topics may not have been explored in as much depth as would have been possible using one-to-one or smaller group methods, which may have also addressed challenges to accessibility of the topic for participants.

Conclusions

These findings can inform future population health COS development and enable methodological consistency to aid evidence synthesis and promote relevance of and reduce waste from population health trials and beyond. Further research is required to develop a broad COS applicable across population health and methods to uphold collection and reporting of indicators of inequality and disadvantage. Our identification of additional population health stakeholders may inform COS development processes and promote transparent reporting. We recommend further research to develop methodology for integrating routine data in core outcome set development.

We have developed methodological guidance to underpin the development of COS in population health research relating to the wider determinants of health, identified areas in which population health trials would benefit from COS development, and explored novel applications of COS methodology that would support population health trials of various designs. This complements existing guidance available for COS development [2, 3].
