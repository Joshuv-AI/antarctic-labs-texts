Exploring the functional role of XIST in breast cancer from mechanisms to clinical implications
Authors: Nada I. Zyada, Mahmoud M. Kamel, Emad M. Elzayat, Abdel Hady A. Abdel Wahab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as pivotal regulators in cancer biology, with the X-inactive specific transcript (XIST) playing a particularly significant role in breast cancer development and progression. This review summarizes current insights into XIST’s mechanistic roles, highlighting its involvement in intricate regulatory networks through interactions with microRNAs, transcription factors, and key signaling pathways. Furthermore, we explore its significant influence on critical aspects of tumor progression, including chemotherapy resistance, modulation of the tumor microenvironment, and regulation of cancer stem cells. Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that XIST functions are not uniform across breast cancer subtypes; rather, its expression patterns and biological effects appear to be context-dependent, varying between luminal and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) categories. While XIST is frequently associated with tumor-promoting activities, certain subtype-specific contexts suggest a more complex regulatory role, underscoring the need for stratified interpretation. By bridging mechanistic insights with clinical applications, we demonstrate XIST’s considerable promise as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker and as a novel therapeutic target. In this review, we demonstrate that targeting XIST may offer a novel and effective therapeutic avenue, contributing to personalized treatment strategies and improved outcomes in breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide and remains a major public health concern. Worldwide, over 2.3 million new cases of cancer were reported in 2020, along with an estimated 685,000 deaths. This accounted for about 16% of all cancer-related deaths in women [1]. The uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal cells characterizes this disease and is heterogeneous, originating in the breast tissue. These cancer cells can spread to other parts of the body and form metastases, which can affect organs such as the brain, liver, and bones. Complex interactions between genetic, hormonal, environmental, and behavioral variables impact the pathophysiology of breast cancer, which is multi-stage and multi-factorial [2]. There has been a significant decrease in mortality despite the growing prevalence due to improvements in screening, diagnosis, and treatment. There was a 44% decrease in breast cancer mortality from 1989 to 2022, saving an estimated 517,900 deaths [3]. Improving patient outcomes is a top priority, and this progress highlights the importance of early identification, public awareness, and timely access to effective medications. To reduce breast cancer mortality, early detection and prompt treatment intervention are still crucial. More and more research in recent years has focused on the molecular factors that cause breast cancer to develop and progress. The role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) has been highlighted as crucial in breast cancer. These RNAs impact cell processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, metastasis, and resistance to therapy. By understanding their role, we can gain new insights into breast cancer biology and identify potential therapeutic targets.

LncRNAs have emerged as key regulators in cancer biology, influencing gene expression through chromatin remodeling, transcriptional control, and post-transcriptional modifications. Once considered transcriptional noise, they are now recognized for their roles in tumor progression and metastasis [4]. LncRNAs also play a critical role in maintaining cancer stem cells (CSCs), a subpopulation responsible for tumor recurrence and drug resistance. The clinical potential of lncRNAs extends to diagnostics and therapeutics. Tissue-specific expression patterns make them promising biomarkers; for example, PCA3 is FDA-approved for prostate cancer detection [5], and UCA1 is elevated in bladder cancer [6]. In contrast, HULC shows marked up-regulation in liver cancer [7]. These findings highlight the broader potential of lncRNAs as biomarkers across malignancies.

Long non-coding RNA X-inactive specific transcript (XIST) was first identified in the early 1990s during investigations into the molecular mechanisms governing X-chromosome inactivation (XCI). Structurally, XIST is located on the X chromosome and produces a 17-kilobase RNA transcript comprising at least eight exons [8], although alternative splicing variants have also been reported. A key breakthrough in understanding the genetic and epigenetic regulation of XCI came with the discovery that XIST is exclusively transcribed from the inactive X chromosome (Xi) in female mammals [9]. Unlike protein-coding RNAs, XIST does not translate into proteins; rather, it functions as a molecular scaffold, recruiting protein complexes essential for chromatin remodeling [10]. During cis-propagation from the X chromosome selected for inactivation (Xi), XIST facilitates progressive gene silencing by altering chromatin structure, condensing the chromosome, and forming RNA-protein complexes [11]. The regulation of XIST transcription and expression is governed by a highly sophisticated interplay of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms. These include regulatory factors and promoter elements within XIC, a crucial locus that controls the initiation and maintenance of XCI. Importantly, repeated sequences within the XIST transcript interact with specific proteins, reinforcing its role in chromatin remodeling and the establishment of inactivation [12]. While broader reviews on XIST in carcinogenesis exist [13], providing a pan-cancer perspective on its diverse functions, this article focuses specifically on breast cancer. We highlight the intricate molecular mechanisms of XIST—such as chromatin remodeling, RNA–protein interactions, and microRNA sponging—and connect these mechanisms to their clinical implications. This review provides a disease-oriented overview that integrates current evidence on XIST in breast cancer, delineating unique translational opportunities and highlighting unresolved challenges that may guide future therapeutic innovations.

Biological function of LncRNA – XIST

XIST/Xist (XIST in humans, Xist in mice) is a pivotal lncRNA orchestrating XCI in female placental mammals. This process is both necessary and sufficient for establishing stable and efficient silencing of one X chromosome in female somatic cells during early development [14]. Xist is known to act as a scaffold for protein recruitment and an organizer of Xi in 3D space [15], ensuring balanced gene expression between sexes [16]. Discovered in the early 1990s [17], XIST remains a cornerstone of dosage compensation, silencing one X chromosome during early embryogenesis to prevent lethal overexpression of X-linked genes [18]. Beyond development, dysregulation of XIST has been implicated in sex-biased diseases, including autoimmune disorders and cancers, underscoring its dual role in physiological and pathological contexts [19]. Despite its significance, the molecular mechanisms underlying XIST’s function remain partially obscured, largely due to incomplete knowledge of its RNA-binding protein (RBP) partners [12].

The role of XIST in chromosome inactivation and gene dose compensation

XIST is very important for sex determination and dosage compensation in mammals. In most mammals, sex is determined by the X and Y chromosomes: females are XX and males are XY. As a consequence, in the absence of dosage compensation, the extra X chromosome in females can lead to unbalanced X-linked gene expression. XCI produces dosage compensation by randomly inactivating one of the X chromosomes in females [20, 21]. The long noncoding transcript XIST (19 kb in humans and 17 kb in mice) has been suggested to play vital roles in random XCI. Random XCI has three phases: initiation, establishment, and maintenance, which have been extensively investigated in mice [22]. The XIST/Xist gene is located in XIC [8] and can be transcribed from the X chromosome to be inactivated (Xi), which functions in cis to coat Xi and nucleate dynamic protein complexes. Xist RNA-containing complexes gradually expand, allowing Xist to spread across Xi [11]. Meanwhile, these complexes alter chromatin architecture, thereby compacting the chromosome and leading to progressive gene silencing along the Xi, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [10, 23].

XIST-mediated gene silencing and epigenetic regulation

Dysregulation of XIST’s epigenetic regulatory mechanisms contributes to inappropriate recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes and consequent aberrant gene silencing. The molecular interactions between Xist RNA and proteins are largely mediated by its structured RNA motifs and conserved tandem repeats [12, 24]. In mice, Xist RNA contains six evolutionarily conserved tandem repeat regions (A–F), which are critical for its silencing function [25]. Although their copy numbers vary significantly, these repeats are conserved across mammalian species. Notably, the A-repeat domain is the most conserved region, retaining both sequence homology and repeat number between mice and humans (Fig. 2). This domain is essential for recruiting silencing complexes during the early phases of X-chromosome inactivation [26]. Conservation outside the repeats is relatively poor [27]. XIST RNA, which spans approximately 15,000–17,000 nucleotides, contains several conserved repeat regions (notably the A-repeat) that are essential for initiating the silencing process by recruiting epigenetic modifiers to establish a repressive chromatin state [27, 28], ultimately leading to the formation of a condensed structure known as the Barr body [29].

XIST and its interaction with other non-coding RNAs and proteins

Previous genetic and biochemical studies have revealed a complex XIST interactome comprising hundreds of potential interactions [12, 30], and subsequent work has predicted that over 30 RNA-binding proteins directly interact with XIST [31]. Among these, SAF-A mediates XIST anchoring to the nuclear matrix [20], while RBM15/RBM15b is implicated in the m6A pathway responsible for XIST post-transcriptional modification [21]. Additionally, hnRNPK is required for Polycomb recruitment, and the subsequent deposition of repressive histone modifications such as H3K27me3 and H2AK119ub [12, 32], and Spen (also known as SHARP/MINT) is essential for establishing primary gene silencing. In contrast, LBR is necessary to anchor Xi to the nuclear periphery [26]. One key interactor is SHARP, which directly binds to XIST and contributes to transcriptional silencing by activating HDAC3. This histone deacetylase removes acetyl groups from histones, resulting in chromatin compaction and the exclusion of RNA polymerase II from the inactive X chromosome [33]. Moreover, XIST interacts with RBM15 and RBM15B to recruit the m6A methyltransferase complex that adds methyl groups to adenosine residues on XIST, thereby enhancing its stability and silencing function [21]. In addition, the antisense transcript Tsix—produced from the same genomic region but in the opposite direction—acts as a negative regulator by repressing XIST expression on the active X chromosome, ensuring that only one X is inactivated [34].

Beyond these direct protein interactions, XIST also functions as a molecular scaffold that recruits chromatin-modifying complexes to establish a repressive chromatin state. A key interactor in this process is the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2). XIST recruits PRC2, facilitating the deposition of the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 across the X chromosome, which is essential for maintaining gene silencing [12]. In the context of breast cancer, aberrant recruitment of PRC2 by XIST may contribute to the dysregulated expression of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, thereby influencing tumor progression [65].

In addition to its epigenetic functions, XIST participates in competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks, forming regulatory loops with various microRNAs. For example, in addition to the miRNA interactions detailed in subsequent sections, XIST has been shown to sponge let-7 family members. This sponging activity derepresses downstream targets, such as the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, which, in turn, activates signaling pathways, such as STAT3, associated with cancer stem cell maintenance and tumor aggressiveness [65]. Collectively, these findings underscore that XIST-mediated gene regulation operates through a combination of direct epigenetic chromatin modifications via PRC2 and post-transcriptional modulation via miRNA-sponging mechanisms.

Furthermore, other non-coding RNAs within XIC also modulate XIST expression; for example, Jpx functions as an RNA activator by titrating out the chromatin insulator CTCF from the XIST promoter region [35], and Ftx contributes to a permissive chromatin environment that enhances XIST transcription [36]. Together, Jpx and Ftx act as positive regulators that fine-tune XIST expression during the early stages of X chromosome inactivation (Fig. 3).

XIST in sex-based cancers

Sex-based differences in cancer incidence and progression are well established, with numerous malignancies displaying significant gender-associated variations. For instance, males display higher rates of bladder, lung, colorectal, and liver cancers, whereas breast and thyroid cancers are more prevalent in females [37]. A key molecular contributor to these differences is the long non-coding RNA XIST. Emerging evidence indicates that XIST plays divergent roles in tumorigenesis depending on sex and cancer type.

In male-biased cancers, the ability of some tumor suppressor genes in non-pseudoautosomal regions of the X chromosome, such as ATRX, DDX3X, KDM5C, and KDM6A/UTX, to evade XCI reduces cancer risk in females. These genes, known as EXITS (Escape from X-Inactivation Tumor Suppressors), show a higher frequency of loss-of-function mutations in males [38]. They are considered tumor suppressor genes because cancer often involves mutations in these genes, which might cause female-biased expression and allow them to evade X-inactivation [39. 40]. KDM6A, for example, exhibits higher expression in female bladder cancer patients and correlates with improved survival [41]. While XIST is ordinarily lacking in male somatic tissues, it is aberrantly expressed in several male-biased malignancies, including bladder, colorectal, and lung cancers. Tumor aggressiveness is associated with increased XIST levels, which in turn correlate with reduced apoptosis and improved proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [42, 43]. Mechanistically, XIST acts as a molecular sponge for tumor-suppressive miRNAs. In bladder cancer, upregulated XIST can bind miR-124 and promote the expression of Androgen Receptor (AR), a key driver of carcinogenesis in males [44]. Beyond miRNA sponging, XIST directly binds proteins to modulate gene expression. It suppresses p53 by inhibiting the DNA demethylase TET1 [45] and represses KLF2 by recruiting EZH2 [46].

In female-biased cancers, the role of XIST is more context-dependent. In breast cancer, XIST is typically downregulated, and its reduction is linked to increased metastatic potential, particularly to the brain [47]. Interestingly, the tumor suppressor role of KDM6A observed in male-biased cancers appears to reverse in breast cancer, where it promotes oncogenic gene expression in conjunction with MLL4 [48]. In gynecologic malignancies such as ovarian and cervical cancers, XIST’s role varies. XIST is usually downregulated in ovarian cancer, particularly when it is advanced, and is linked to a better prognosis. XIST overexpression inhibits proliferation by suppressing miR-214-3p and miR-106a [49, 50]. Conversely, in cervical cancer, XIST is overexpressed and contributes to tumor progression by sponging multiple tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-200a, miR-140-5p, and miR-889-3p) [51–53]. Thyroid cancer demonstrates a pronounced female predominance, particularly during the reproductive years, where the incidence can be up to three times higher in women than in men. This gender disparity has been largely attributed to the modulatory effects of estrogen and other reproductive hormones on thyroid cell proliferation, differentiation, and tumor growth [54]. Molecular mechanisms and clinical impact of lncRNA-XIST in breast cancer

LNCRNA-XIST as a diagnostic marker

Previous studies have proposed XIST as a potential biomarker for detecting breast cancer. Given the significant variation in XIST expression between normal and malignant breast tissues, it may hold diagnostic value. Specifically, the downregulation of XIST in breast cancer tissues is associated with more aggressive tumor phenotypes and poorer patient outcomes, contrasting with its higher expression in healthy breast tissue [55, 56]. XIST’s diagnostic potential is further supported by its involvement in key biological pathways, such as the miR-155/CDX1 axis and miR-101-3p/KLF6/C/EBPα axis, all of which impact critical cancer processes like cell migration, invasion, and proliferation [55, 57]. Moreover, its specificity is demonstrated by the varying levels of XIST expression across different breast cancer subtypes, particularly in aggressive forms like triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Notably, XIST upregulation in TNBC has been linked to decreased tumor growth and enhanced sensitivity to chemotherapy, showing promise for its role in subtype-specific diagnosis and treatment strategies [58].

LNCRNA-XIST as a prognostic marker

XIST expression levels serve as a crucial prognostic indicator in breast cancer, with its low expression correlating negatively with the development of brain metastases. Patients with low XIST expression tend to have worse outcomes, as this is linked to more aggressive tumors, increased EMT rates, and heightened stemness characteristics. Furthermore, the absence of XIST facilitates immunosuppressive mechanisms by influencing microglial polarization and suppressing T-cell activity, creating a more conducive environment for tumor progression. These findings underscore XIST’s potential in predicting breast cancer metastasis, particularly to the brain, and assisting in therapeutic decision-making [47]. Additionally, reduced XIST expression has been associated with heightened sensitivity to cisplatin in various cancers, suggesting its role as a predictive biomarker for chemotherapy response. This association is particularly evident in HER2-negative, stage III breast cancer patients, where low XIST levels predict a better recurrence-free survival rate following aggressive platinum-based treatments [59]. Moreover, the abnormal expression of XIST may offer further insight into patient survival outcomes in breast cancer, with its potential interactions with tumor-associated genetic alterations influencing immune cell infiltration and responses to immunotherapy in BRCA patients [60].

Despite the accumulating evidence supporting XIST as a prognostic biomarker, a critical appraisal of the literature reveals noteworthy contradictions that warrant careful consideration. A meta-analysis encompassing multiple cancer types concluded that elevated XIST expression correlates significantly with poorer overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS), and is positively associated with aggressive clinicopathological features such as larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and advanced TNM stage [81]. This aligns with findings in specific breast cancer cohorts, such as the study, where high XIST expression, in conjunction with low 53BP1, predicted worse outcomes in BRCA1-like breast cancer patients following intensive chemotherapy [62].

In stark contrast, other studies have reported an inverse relationship, in which loss of XIST is linked to more aggressive disease. For instance, another Research demonstrated that low XIST expression in breast cancer patients is significantly associated with an increased risk of brain metastasis [47]. Mechanistically, this study revealed that XIST loss drives metastatic progression by activating the c-Met pathway via moesin (MSN)-mediated protein stabilization, which, in turn, promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enhances stemness in tumor cells [47]. The complexity of XIST’s role is further underscored by functional in vivo studies where XIST knockout in mouse mammary glands accelerated both primary tumor growth and brain metastasis formation, demonstrating that the loss of XIST can be sufficient to drive aggressive disease [47].

These conflicting reports highlight a critical complexity in interpreting XIST’s prognostic significance. The divergent outcomes likely reflect the highly context-dependent nature of XIST function, which may vary not only by cancer type and subtype but also by the specific molecular pathways engaged and the stage of disease. This duality suggests that XIST cannot be simply categorized as a universal oncogene or tumor suppressor; rather, its prognostic value must be interpreted within a specific biological and clinical context, a theme that warrants further investigation in well-defined patient cohorts. Despite these complexities, large-scale analyses have reinforced the overall clinical relevance of XIST as a prognostic biomarker. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Research systematically evaluated XIST’s prognostic value across multiple cancer types. By pooling data from PubMed, Web of Science, and EMBASE, and subsequently validating their findings with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, the study concluded that XIST holds promise as a biomarker for early diagnosis and outcome prediction. These findings not only support its clinical utility but also offer novel insights for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies [87].

LNCRNA-XIST as a predictive marker

A reduction in the expression of the tumor suppressor protein BRCA1 significantly increases the likelihood of breast cancer development [61]. Notably, XIST is highly expressed in BRCA1-like breast cancers, positioning it as a potential predictive biomarker for these patients [62, 63]. The expression of XIST appears to be closely tied to BRCA1 levels, suggesting its involvement in breast cancer pathogenesis. Additionally, elevated serum exosomal XIST levels have been associated with recurrence in TNBC patients, indicating that XIST could serve as a non-invasive biomarker for monitoring disease progression [64].

The potential for non-invasive disease monitoring has been significantly advanced by studies on liquid biopsies. Other Research identified serum exosomal XIST (exo-XIST) as a potential non-invasive biomarker in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). In their study, elevated levels of circulating exo-XIST were observed in patients with recurrent TNBC and were significantly associated with poorer overall survival. Notably, exo-XIST levels decreased after tumor resection and increased upon recurrence, suggesting its utility in monitoring tumor burden and disease progression [64]. These findings provide direct, patient-derived evidence supporting XIST’s clinical relevance as a non-invasive biomarker for disease monitoring in TNBC.

XIST exerts a substantial influence on breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) through proinflammatory signaling pathways, thereby fostering tumor growth and metastasis [65]. Its role in regulating critical oncogenic pathways, including interactions with microRNAs like miR-125b-5p, further underscores its association with malignant breast cancer [66]. When predicting brain metastases in breast cancer, XIST has shown significant predictive power. Higher XIST expression correlates with an increased risk of brain metastasis, whereas lower expression levels are associated with a greater likelihood of metastasis to other sites, such as bone. Monitoring XIST levels may thus enable early detection of brain metastases and facilitate timely, targeted therapy. Furthermore, XIST’s role in altering the tumor microenvironment, which drives immune evasion and metastasis, makes it a potential predictive biomarker for brain metastasis risk [47].

XIST is also gaining recognition in the context of treatment response, particularly as a biomarker for predicting the efficacy of histone deacetylase inhibitors like abexinostat. Breast cancer cell lines have been categorized based on XIST expression: one group demonstrated sensitivity to low doses of abexinostat, leading to the differentiation of CSCs, while another group, with consistent CSC populations, responded to high doses [67]. Additionally, XIST expression has been linked to chemotherapy susceptibility, particularly in response to high-dose alkylating agents such as cisplatin [59, 62].

LNCRNA-XIST as a therapeutic marker

XIST plays a pivotal role in tumor dynamics by indirectly influencing macrophage polarization, thereby subtly but significantly affecting the immune landscape. Elevated levels of XIST are observed in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, suggesting its role in maintaining their aggressive phenotype. However, when XIST expression is silenced, a striking shift occurs, with M1 macrophages transforming into their anti-inflammatory M2 counterparts. This shift is driven by the downregulation of key transcription factors, C/EBPα and KLF6, creating an environment conducive to tumor cell growth and movement. This highlights the potential therapeutic implications of targeting XIST, which extends beyond macrophage polarization to modify the tumor microenvironment [57] actively. By targeting XIST, researchers may find a way to reprogram macrophages to halt tumor progression, providing a new avenue for cancer treatment. The growing understanding of XIST’s role in immune modulation suggests it could revolutionize cancer treatment strategies.

Various studies have also revealed the importance of epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation and histone deacetylation, in the progression and metastasis of breast cancer [68, 69]. XIST is a critical regulator of epigenetic stability in breast cancer cells and is frequently linked to major epigenetic changes that disrupt cellular processes such as differentiation and proliferation. Dysregulation of XIST expression in breast tumors may significantly contribute to carcinogenesis. A deeper understanding of how XIST regulates these epigenetic alterations could reveal novel therapeutic targets to curb tumor growth and metastasis [70, 71]. TNBC, which constitutes 10–20% of all breast cancer cases, poses a significant challenge due to its aggressive nature and lack of expression of key receptors such as estrogen, progesterone, and ERBB2 [72]. The absence of these receptor targets further exacerbates its resistance to conventional therapies. Recent studies suggest that XIST is crucial for TNBC, as it inhibits tumor growth by interacting with miR-454. This interaction highlights XIST as a novel regulatory component in the development of this difficult-to-treat subtype, pointing toward potential therapeutic strategies for TNBC [73]. By modulating the miR-92b/Slug/ESA signaling pathway and sequestering these molecules, XIST inhibits tumor formation and influences critical cellular processes linked to cancer progression [74]. Additionally, XIST has been shown to upregulate PHLPP1 expression by displacing HDAC3 from its promoter, thereby attenuating AKT phosphorylation and limiting breast cancer cell viability [56]. These findings underscore XIST’s multifaceted role in regulating key signaling pathways, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for TNBC. Further investigation into XIST overexpression in breast cancer, using both in vitro and in vivo models, has shown that it regulates the miR-362-5p/UBAP1 axis, inhibiting breast cancer cell progression and suggesting that XIST may be a valuable therapeutic agent for breast cancer [75].

New research shows that XIST expression varies among breast cancer subtypes and cell lines, indicating that its role is more context-dependent than consistently carcinogenic. For instance, one study demonstrated that in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where XIST is typically found at low levels, its experimental upregulation acts as a molecular sponge for oncogenic miR-454. This interaction leads to the inhibition of cell proliferation, reversal of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and induction of apoptosis, thereby revealing a tumor-suppressive function [58]. Conversely, another study showed that in both luminal and TNBC cells, elevated XIST can promote cancer stem cell characteristics and activate pro-inflammatory IL-6/STAT3 signaling via sponging let-7a-2-3p, thereby supporting an oncogenic role [65]. These contrasting findings emphasize that XIST may function as either a tumor suppressor or an oncogene depending on the specific cellular and molecular context. This duality, which is critical for evaluating XIST-based therapeutic strategies, is further elaborated by the latter study [65], which provides a detailed mechanistic foundation for XIST’s oncogenic role by demonstrating how it acts as a nuclear sponge for let-7a-2-3p to derepress IL-6 production from bulk tumor cells, subsequently activating paracrine STAT3 signaling in cancer stem cells to promote self-renewal. Collectively, these studies underscore that the net biological outcome of XIST expression is dictated by the available miRNA milieu and the specific downstream pathways engaged in a given subtype or cellular context.

Moreover, miR-155 has been shown to directly target CDX1, with this axis playing a critical role in counteracting XIST-mediated effects in breast cancer cells, thereby inhibiting their migration, invasion, and proliferation [55]. Dephosphorylation of AKT depends on HDAC3 recruitment to the PHLPP1 promoter, a process enhanced when either XIST or its co-factor SPEN/SHARP is silenced. This mechanism plays a crucial role in tumor development by decreasing PHLPP1 expression and promoting AKT phosphorylation. These insights reinforce XIST’s complex role in controlling cancer-related signaling pathways and its potential as a therapeutic target to restore regulatory balance in breast cancer [56].

XIST has also been shown to regulate the PD-L1 signaling pathway in TNBC by inhibiting MALAT1’s oncogenic activity. In contrast to the upregulation of miR-182-5p, PD-L1, and MALAT1, XIST expression was downregulated in TNBC cells and tissues. These findings suggest that XIST may act as a potential therapeutic target in TNBC by modulating immune checkpoint pathways, as demonstrated by co-transfection experiments with miR-182-5p antagomirs [76]. Additionally, oleuropein, a naturally occurring molecule, has shown promise in modulating the miR-194/XIST/PD-L1 signaling axis in breast cancer. The results indicated that Oleuropein treatment increased XIST expression while reducing PD-L1 and miR-194 levels, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for triple-negative breast cancer and its relevance in immune oncology [77]. Finally, the inhibition of miR-125b-5p’s targeting of NLRC5 by XIST has been shown to enhance migration, invasion, and proliferation of cancer cells. Suppressing XIST significantly reduced these cancer-promoting activities, indicating that XIST targeting could be a promising therapeutic strategy for breast cancer [66].

XIST and resistance to chemotherapy

In the complex interplay between cancer immunity and brain metastases, XIST emerges as a pivotal mediator of immune evasion in TNBC. The XIST gene enhances PD-L1 production, a key factor in immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is activated when crucial stem cell markers, such as OCT4 and NANOG, are triggered, driving this process. By modulating these pathways, XIST makes the tumor more resistant to immune system attack and increases the likelihood of metastasis, particularly to the brain. In TNBC patients with brain metastases, XIST plays a multifaceted role in immune evasion, contributing to treatment resistance and unfavorable clinical outcomes [78].

Another study explores how XIST impacts the chemoresistance of breast cancer cells to doxorubicin. XIST functions as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA), regulating miR-200c-3p and the genes it targets [79]. Inhibition of cell proliferation and migration was observed upon ANLN knockdown, a potential biomarker for early breast cancer diagnosis [80]. New evidence indicates that XIST plays a crucial role in tumor growth and in regulating BCSCs. XIST regulates cytokine interactions by decreasing the levels of IL-6 and IL-8, two pro-inflammatory cytokines. It also acts as a microRNA sponge, increasing IL-6 production, which, in turn, promotes CSC self-renewal via STAT3 activation and the overexpression of key CSC components, such as c-MYC, KLF4, and SOX9. Consequently, cancer stem cells are notoriously difficult to treat, but targeting XIST or its downstream signaling pathways—particularly the IL-6/STAT3 axis—could improve therapeutic outcomes [65].

In patients undergoing high-dose chemotherapy, particularly those with BRCA1-like breast cancer, poor prognosis is associated with elevated XIST levels and reduced 53BP1 expression. A study involving 28 BRCA1-like patients revealed that individuals with higher XIST expression or lower 53BP1 expression responded poorly to treatment. However, patients with lower XIST and higher 53BP1 expression showed significant benefits from high-dose chemotherapy in terms of overall survival, disease-free survival, and recurrence-free survival. These findings suggest that XIST and 53BP1 could serve as biomarkers to predict chemotherapy resistance and patient outcomes in BRCA1-like breast cancers [62].

Several tumors have demonstrated increased sensitivity to cisplatin when XIST expression is downregulated. Additionally, HER2-negative stage III breast cancer patients who underwent intensive platinum-based chemotherapy exhibited longer recurrence-free survival when their XIST levels were lower, indicating that XIST plays a significant role in chemotherapy resistance [59]. Clinically, altered XIST levels are associated with brain metastasis, immune suppression, poor prognosis (including BRCA1-like tumors), subtype-specific behavior, recurrence risk, and response to platinum-based chemotherapy. In TNBC, XIST modulates EMT, apoptosis, immune checkpoint signaling, and AKT-related pathways. It also contributes to chemoresistance via PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation, IL-6/STAT3 signaling, ceRNA mechanisms, and interaction with 53BP1. Clinical translation challenges of XIST-targeted therapies in breast cancer

Despite the potential therapeutic benefits of targeting XIST in breast cancer, several biological and technical challenges must be addressed before it is used in clinical trials. Targeting malignancies with RNA-based therapies, especially long non-coding RNAs such as XIST, remains very difficult. Targeting XIST for therapeutic purposes is more difficult than targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) due to its large size (about 17 kb), complex secondary structure, and predominant nuclear localization. Lipid nanoparticles, polymeric carriers, and viral vectors represent contemporary delivery techniques; however, they exhibit limitations in tissue selectivity, cellular uptake, endosomal release, and circulatory stability [82, 83]. Targeting extrahepatic tissues, such as breast tumors, continues to be highly problematic, despite significant advancements in drug delivery to the liver [84]. Current advancements in drug delivery methods encompass ligand-conjugated nanoparticles, virus-like particles, and exosomes; yet, comprehensive animal testing is necessary for each method before human use [85].

There are good reasons to be careful since XIST interacts with so many different things that it might have unexpected, bad consequences. XIST interacts with several chromatin modifiers, including various microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins such as hnRNPK, SHARP, and RBM15, and the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) [12, 65]. Consequently, therapeutic modifications to XIST may inadvertently interfere with normal epigenetic processes. The possible breakdown of dosage compensation and proper tissue homeostasis caused by the failure of X-chromosome inactivation in normal female cells is a major worry [9, 14]. Recent CRISPR-based lncRNA screens have shown that not all growth-inhibitory effects from lncRNA disruption come from lncRNA activity directly. Instead, they may result from accidentally targeting important protein-coding genes adjacent to the lncRNA in the genome [86]. This underscores the need to meticulously examine off-target signals and validate targeted specificity.

Also, cytoplasmic RNA sensors and Toll-like receptors (TLRs) function as pattern recognition receptors that RNA-based treatments may activate, hence activating innate immune responses. Many clinical investigations using RNA therapies indicate that these responses may elicit inflammatory signals and toxicities, limiting the dose.

Therapeutic targeting is complicated by the variability of XIST’s activities, which are contingent on the environment and subtype-specific, as elucidated in Sect. 4. The role of XIST as a tumor suppressor or oncogene depends on the specific cell type in which it resides [58, 65]. As a result, several breast cancer subtypes and unique genetic profiles necessitate highly customized treatment approaches. Given this duality, it is imperative to classify patients based on their XIST expression levels and functional state to facilitate effective therapy, since a uniform approach to targeting XIST would be insufficient.

Thorough preclinical validation and optimization of delivery systems are needed to ensure specificity, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. Concentrating on XIST presents a viable approach for breast cancer treatment. Future research should emphasize the following priorities: (i) the creation of breast tumor-specific delivery systems that improve nuclear targeting and cellular uptake; (ii) thorough off-target profiling utilizing objective methodologies; (iii) a detailed investigation of the potential effects on normal X-chromosome inactivation and tissue homeostasis; and (iv) subtype-specific preclinical studies to determine patient populations most likely to benefit from XIST-targeted therapies.

Summary and future directions

- This review highlights that lncRNA XIST plays a multifaceted role in breast cancer biology, with evidence pointing to both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive functions depending on the molecular and clinical context. Across the studies synthesized, several consistent findings emerged. First, XIST expression is altered in a subtype-specific manner: it is frequently downregulated in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and associated with increased metastatic potential, particularly to the brain. At the same time, it shows high expression in BRCA1-like tumors and correlates with poor response to platinum-based chemotherapy. Second, the detection of exosomal XIST in serum samples underscores its promise as a non-invasive biomarker for diagnosis and disease monitoring. Third, functional studies demonstrate that XIST contributes to chemotherapy resistance (e.g., to cisplatin and doxorubicin) and immune evasion by regulating PD-L1; however, in some contexts, its overexpression can inhibit proliferation and invasion via microRNA sponging.- Despite these promising insights, our synthesis also revealed important limitations. Most evidence comes from small patient cohorts or pre-clinical models, limiting the generalizability of findings. Furthermore, there is no standardized, clinically validated assay for XIST quantification in tissue or biofluids, which poses a major barrier to its translation into clinical practice. The dualistic nature of XIST’s functions—oncogenic in some settings and tumor-suppressive in others—also raises concerns for therapeutic targeting, as interventions may produce unintended consequences if the molecular context is not carefully defined.- Looking ahead, several key measures are necessary. Large-scale, multi-center validation studies are urgently needed to confirm XIST’s utility as a diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarker. Mechanistic studies should continue to dissect the XIST interactome, focusing on its interactions with microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins, and epigenetic regulators, to explain its context-dependent effects. Standardized protocols for detecting XIST in blood and tissue must be developed to enable its integration into routine clinical workflows. Finally, therapeutic exploration should proceed with caution, prioritizing strategies such as antisense oligonucleotides or CRISPR-based modulation only after a clearer understanding of when XIST functions as a driver versus a suppressor.- In conclusion, our review establishes that XIST is not a passive bystander but an active regulator of breast cancer pathogenesis, with significant implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy. By addressing current methodological and mechanistic gaps, future research can determine how best to leverage XIST for personalized breast cancer management.
