A multimodal evaluation of immunogenicity and field performance of infectious bursal disease vaccines in Ethiopian broilers
Authors: Debebe Ashenafi Bekele, Nanoshe Taye Jima, Abebe Garoma, Bedaso Mammo Edao, Jan Paeshuyse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfectious bursal disease (IBD) continues to undermine poultry health and productivity in Ethiopia. Despite the availability of commercial vaccines, there are raising concerns about vaccine performance, particularly in broiler production systems. This study employed a multi-modal evaluation to compare the immunogenicity and field performance of commercially available IBD vaccines in Ethiopia. The research integrated a controlled in vivo trial assessing vaccine-induced antibody responses and a field-level serological survey of broiler flocks under typical management conditions. Additionally, a structured questionnaire was administered to poultry producers to gather data on vaccination practices, biosecurity measures, and general flock management. A total of 100 one-day-old broiler chicks were assigned into four groups. Group 1 received the CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine at day one; Group 2 received the CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD vaccine at day 14; and Group 3 received the IBD VIRUS LC-75 vaccine at day 14 and booster dose at day 21; Group 4 served as an unvaccinated control. Blood samples were collected at multiple time points up to day 42 post-vaccination, and antibody titers were measured using ELISA. Additionally, blood samples were also collected at the same time points from commercial broiler chickens vaccinated with CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine. In parallel, a field survey involving 93 poultry sector participants was implemented to assess vaccine usage and management practices. In the controlled experimental setting, groups vaccinated with the CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine showed higher antibody titers at day 7 post-vaccination (p < 0.05), while the ones vaccinated with CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD and IBD VIRUS LC-75 vaccines demonstrated robust responses from day 14 post-vaccination onward. Similarly, chickens vaccinated with the CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine at the field level showed relatively higher antibody titers at day 7 post vaccination, but lower when compared with the one under controlled experimental conditions. Despite those differences, each vaccine elicited a measurable humoral immune response, though the timing of seroconversion and peak antibody levels varied depending on the specific vaccine platform used. Survey results indicated that improved vaccines and optimized vaccination schedules were crucial for effective disease control. Additionally, 63% of respondents emphasized the importance of awareness and training to enhance IBD management. Hence, our findings highlighted the need for improved vaccines, optimized vaccination schedules, veterinarians, and farmer training to improve Gumboro disease management in Ethiopia.Keywords: Broilers, ELISA, Immunity, IBD, Vaccines

Introduction

Poultry production, as a vital segment of livestock farming, plays a significant role in the agricultural activities of many developing countries, particularly in Africa. This sector is expanding rapidly due to its unique potential to enhance the local socio-economic landscape (FAO, 2019). The primary driver of this growth is the quick return on investment provided by poultry, which requires minimal capital, has a short reproduction cycle, occupies little land, and experiences rapid growth compared to most other livestock species (Korver, 2023). Poultry farming serves as an important means of providing food and improving food security. It contributes to the reduction of poverty and malnutrition in countries like Ethiopia (Tirfie, 2021). Although Ethiopia is home to approximately 57.01 million poultry (CSA, 2021), the economic returns from poultry production remain low. This is attributed to various challenges, including poor management practices, limited genetic potential in local breeds, and the prevalence of diseases such as infectious bursal disease (Terefe, 2018).

Infectious bursal disease (IBD), commonly known as Gumboro disease, is an immunosuppressive disease that primarily affects young chicks between 3 and 6 weeks of age. It can lead to either clinical or subclinical infections (Muller et al., 2012). Its etiological agent, named infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), is a member of the Birnaviridae family, genus Avibirnavirus, with a double-stranded RNA genome made of two segments (segment A and segment B). Two serotypes, Serotype 1 and Serotype 2, have been identified through virus neutralization studies. While both serotypes can naturally infect a variety of poultry and birds, including chickens, turkeys, ducks, guinea fowls, and ostriches, only Serotype 1 is considered pathogenic to chickens (Mahgoub, 2012; Dey et al., 2019). Moreover, the disease has been classified into four phenotypes based on its pathogenicity and antigenic characteristics: classic, variant, very virulent, and attenuated (van den Berg et al., 2004). Variant and virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) strains have been isolated from disease outbreaks despite the presence of high levels of maternal antibody to classic strains of IBDV (Jackwood and Saif, 1987).

To prevent the disease, the most used conventional vaccines globally include killed vaccines and live attenuated vaccines derived from classic or variant strains (Sze et al., 2016). Technological advancements have led to the development of next-generation vaccines, which are designed to overcome the challenge posed by maternally derived antibodies (MDAs). These include commercially available options such as the IBD vector vaccine, which utilizes turkey herpes virus (HVT) as a vector for the viral protein 2 (VP2) gene of IBDV (Bublot et al., 2007), and the immune-complex vaccine, comprising a combination of the intermediate plus strain and antibodies. This formulation is absorbed by macrophages and remains inactive until MDAs decrease (Prandini et al., 2016).

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) continues to pose a major challenge to Ethiopia’s poultry sector, despite the introduction of various control measures. Since its first confirmed outbreak in 2002 on a commercial poultry farm in Bishoftu (Zeleke et al., 2003), the disease has remained endemic, with repeated occurrences reported across breeding centers, multiplication units, and village poultry systems (Mazengia, 2012; Jenberie et al., 2013; Zeleke et al., 2005). Mortality rates in affected flocks, particularly in exotic and crossbred chickens, have been reported between 25% and 75%, highlighting the severity of the disease’s impact (Zeleke et al., 2005; Woldemariam and Wossene, 2007). Furthermore, IBD-related losses are not confined to commercial production; frequent outbreaks among village chickens, particularly in those under six weeks old, also contribute to economic instability in rural communities (Shegu et al., 2020; Mekuriaw et al., 2017).

Despite routine vaccination efforts and improved biosecurity in commercial farms (Shegu et al., 2020), persistent IBD outbreaks indicate potential vaccine failure or antigenic mismatch, especially in the face of emerging variants and very virulent IBDV strains (Jenberie et al., 2014; Bari, 2021). These outbreaks have occurred even in the presence of maternal antibodies, suggesting limitations in current vaccine protocols. Considering Ethiopia’s national poultry development program under the “Bounty to Basket” (Ye Lemat Tirufat) initiative (Dessie et al., 2023), mitigating such persistent viral threats is vital. Hatcheries rely on subcutaneous injection or in ovo delivery (Isihak et al., 2021), and utilization of both imported and locally produced vaccines. Hence, a performance evaluation of both vaccines under both real-world and experimental conditions is urgently needed.

In vivo studies offer controlled environments for evaluating immune responses to different vaccine types. On the other hand, field-based surveys provide complementary insights into farmers’ vaccination practices, awareness, and practical challenges that may affect outcomes. However, studies in Ethiopia have often focused on controlled experimental studies where they assessed humoral immune responses to different live-attenuated vaccines of IBD such as D78, LC - 75, IBD EXTREM, and CEVAC® IBDL IBD (Mekuriaw et al., 2017; Kassa et al., 2019; Legese et al., 2022). Furthermore, there is an increasing use of Transmune (immune-complex) vaccines in commercial hatcheries in Ethiopian poultry systems. But, no comparative studies exist assessing their efficacy alongside traditional live-attenuated vaccines at both the controlled experimental and field levels.

To bridge this knowledge gap, an integrated approach that combines in vivo vaccine immunogenicity testing with field-based antibody titer evaluation and a farmer survey is essential. This multimodal strategy enables assessment of humoral immune responses under controlled and real-world conditions. It also captures contextual factors such as vaccine handling, administration practices, and farmer knowledge that influence vaccine performance. Such integration strengthens the robustness of vaccine evaluation and supports evidence-based recommendations for tailored immunization programs against IBD. Hence, this study aims to: (i) Compare antibody responses of broiler chickens vaccinated with immune-complex and live-attenuated IBD vaccines under controlled experimental conditions; (ii) Assess field level antibody titers in commercial broiler flocks vaccinated with immune-complex vaccine; (iii) Conduct a structured survey to evaluate poultry farmers’ knowledge, perceptions, and practices related to IBD vaccination and management; (iv) Identify gaps in vaccine handling and administration that may affect immunogenicity; and (v) Provide evidence-based recommendations for optimizing vaccination strategies in Ethiopian poultry systems.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is Bishoftu Town, Central Ethiopia, an area of major poultry production and frequent reports of IBD outbreaks (Fig. 1). Bishoftu is located 45 km southeast of Addis Ababa. The area is located at 9°N latitude and 40°E longitude at a maximum altitude of 1850 m above sea level. The town has an annual rainfall of 866 mm, of which 84% is in the long rainy season (June to September) (NMSA, 2010). There have been frequent IBD outbreaks affecting millions of chickens in the area. This town is characterized by the presence of several commercial farms of different sizes; hatcheries; and breeding centers, from which pullets of various ages are distributed to different parts of the country (ADARDO, 2007; CSA, 2021).

Map of the study area (developed using ArcGIS). This map displays the geographic location of poultry farms included in the survey study and Bishoftu town where the field and experimental studies conducted.

Study population

Cobb 500 chickens, a broiler breed known for its rapid growth and exceptional feed efficiency, were used both for the experimental and field-based IBD vaccine immunogenicity tests. Their genetic uniformity minimizes variability, ensuring consistent and reliable research outcomes. This makes them particularly suitable for evaluating vaccine immunogenicity. For the questionnaire survey, the study included small- and medium-scale commercial poultry farms. These farms were operated by private owners, micro and small enterprises, and cooperatives raising exotic chicken breeds. The farms were selected regardless of their production type; both broilers and layers were included in the study.

Study design

Both controlled experimental and field-based studies were conducted to evaluate the immunogenicity of infectious bursal disease (IBD) vaccines. Additionally, a semi-structured survey was carried out among chicken farm owners and attendants in Bishoftu town to gain a comprehensive understanding of infectious bursal disease and the practices related to vaccine usage for its prevention.

Study methodology

Questionnaire survey

A semi-structured questionnaire survey was conducted to comprehensively assess poultry farmers’ knowledge and practices regarding the use of vaccines for IBD and its management. The survey instrument was structured into six sections (A–F), each containing between five and fifteen targeted questions (Supplementary Material). Section A captured demographic information of the respondents, while Section B focused on flock history and management practices. Section C assessed respondents’ general knowledge and experience related to IBD outbreaks and their perceived impacts. Section D explored the progression and characteristics of disease outbreaks in relation to vaccine use. Section E addressed IBD vaccine utilization, handling, and on‑farm management practices. Finally, Section F examined the criteria respondents use when selecting IBD vaccines, their approaches to evaluating vaccine performance, and their suggestions for improving disease control.

To ensure the robustness of the data, these questionnaires underwent a two-stage validation process. First, content validity was established through a review by experts working in the field to ensure that all indicators of IBD vaccine immunogenicity, including clinical manifestations, immune response measurements, and field-level realities, were accurately represented. Second, a pre-test (pilot study) was conducted with a representative sample of 10 participants. This served as a reliability check to identify potential ambiguities in technical terminology and to ensure the logical flow of the survey. Feedback from the pre-test led to the refinement of respondents' perceptions of disease status (awareness) and vaccine performances.

Data collection involved face-to-face interviews with poultry farm owners, focusing on those willing to participate after providing informed consent. Farm owners or operators who declined to participate were excluded from the study.

In vivo vaccine efficacy

Chickens, housing, and feeding conditions

For this experimental study, 100 day-old COBB-500 broiler chicks were sourced from Alema Farms P.L.C. Upon arrival, the chickens were housed in the AAU-CVMA poultry facility in floor pens lined with straw. A brooder was provided to maintain warmth, ensuring optimal comfort and welfare. The chickens had unrestricted access to food and water (ad libitum). Lighting and temperature conditions within the pens were carefully regulated according to the breeder's guidelines to meet the specific needs of broiler chickens. The chickens were randomly divided into four groups, each consisted of 25 chicks (Table 1).

Experimental design (vaccines, immunization regimes, and routes of administration)

Two live-attenuated and one immune-complex IBD vaccines were used for this study. The first vaccine was CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD, an immune-complex IBD vaccine that contains the Winterfield 2512 strain of live infectious bursal disease (IBD). The virus is complexed with IBD immunoglobulins (VPI: Virus-Protecting Immunoglobulins) in freeze-dried form. The second vaccine is CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD, a live-attenuated vaccine that contains the LIBDV intermediate strain of live IBD virus in freeze-dried form. The third one is the IBD VIRUS LC-75 vaccine, a freeze-dried vaccine containing an intermediate standard live IBD virus strain stabilized with suitable agents. A fourth group, consisting of unvaccinated controls, was included in the study.

Vaccines were administered according to the manufacturers’ instructions. CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD and IBD VIRUS LC‑75 were given orally (drinking water), whereas CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD was administered subcutaneously at one day old in the hatchery. Although booster doses are recommended for live‑attenuated vaccines, only chicks receiving IBD VIRUS LC‑75 were given a booster. Booster doses were not provided for CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD (due to unavailability) or CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD (which is designed for single administration at the hatchery). These differences in vaccine type, timing, route, and booster use may influence the resulting immune responses.

Field-level immune response evaluation

For optimal evaluation of field level performance of IBD vaccines on similar production systems (broilers), we assessed the immune response of chicks for the CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine. For this, blood samples were collected on 7th, 14th, 21st, 28th, and 35th days post-vaccinations.

Laboratory analysis of samples

Blood samples (2 mL per chick) were collected on various days post-vaccination (7th, 14th, 21st, 28th, 35th, and 42nd days (Table 1)) using a 3 mL sterile disposable syringe from the wing vein. Additionally, the same volume of blood was obtained from day-old chicks to assess the maternal antibody (MDA). Each sample was clearly labeled with the corresponding chicken identification number. The collected blood was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes to separate sera, which were then transferred into labeled cryovials. The cryovials containing the sera were stored at −20 °C until further laboratory analyses were performed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The collected serum samples were tested for antibody response against each vaccine using a commercial indirect ELISA kit (IDvet, Grabels, France) at serology laboratory of the Animal Health Institute (AHI), Ethiopia. In the pre-dilution plate, wells A1, B1, C1, and D1 were designated as controls. To each well, 5 μL of the sample to be tested and 245 μL of Dilution Buffer 14 were added. In the ELISA microplate, 100 μL of Negative Control (A1 and B1), 100 μL of Positive Control (C1 and D1), and 90 μL of Dilution Buffer 14 were added to the corresponding sample wells. The plate was then covered and incubated at 21 °C for 30 min.

After incubation, the plates were emptied, and the wells were washed three times with 300 μL of Wash Solution 1X. Next, 100 μL of Conjugate 1X was added to each well, and the plate was covered and incubated at 21 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, the wells were washed three more times with 300 μL of Wash Solution 1X. Substrate solution was then added to each well, and the plate was covered and incubated in the dark at 21 °C for 15 min. After incubation, 100 μL of stop solution was added to each well. The results were read at 450 nm using an ELISA microplate reader. Ethical clearance

The present study underwent a thorough review by the Animal Welfare and Research Ethical Review Committee of the College of Veterinary Medicine and Agriculture at Addis Ababa University, with consideration of all pertinent ethical and animal welfare considerations. The approval was endorsed, and accompanied by a certificate reference number VM/ERC/04/29/16/2024. To ensure the ethical integrity of the study, due diligence was exercised in obtaining the necessary ethical clearances and consents from all relevant participants, as well as adherence to relevant principles in public health and animal welfare ethics. The humane sacrifice of severely ill/experimental chickens was carried out in accordance with established animal welfare protocols (Terefe and Cherinet, 2020).

Data management and statistical analysis

The results from the indirect ELISA and the questionnaire survey responses were recorded in Microsoft Excel version 16.0 for data organization and initial processing. Survey responses were systematically coded using a numerical system and analyzed to determine the frequency distribution of responses for each question in the survey. Repeated-measures data from the indirect ELISA results were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects modeling approach in R (version 4.5.1). The outcome variable (antibody titer) was modeled as a function of time, treated as a fixed effect, while the subject was included as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation arising from repeated observations. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Humoral immune responses (MDA, controlled experiment and field levels)

Upon arrival, the maternal antibody (MDA) titer was recorded at 3097.21 EU/mL. This initial concentration represents a robust baseline of passive humoral immunity. It establishes the immunological threshold against which the subsequent immunogenicity of the administered vaccines was measured (antibody titer >875 EU/mL) (IDvet, Grabels, France). The in vivo evaluation of humoral immune responses to the different IBD vaccines induced measurable antibody levels. But, variations were observed in the magnitude and timing of peak responses. At day 7 post-vaccination, antibody titers of chicks vaccinated with CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine were higher than those of chicks vaccinated with other vaccines. But, the titer decreased significantly until day 28 DPV, which was delayed but strong in the later stages of the experiment (p < 0.001; Table 2). On the other hand, chicks vaccinated with CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD and IBD VIRUS LC-75 vaccines produced a significant antibody titer from day 14 DPV onwards. Specifically, the IBD VIRUS LC-75 vaccine produced consistently high antibody titers throughout the experimental period, indicating robust and sustained immune stimulation (Table 2).

Questionnaire survey results

Demographic and general information on the respondents and farms

Among the participants in the current survey, 28% were female, and 72% were male. In terms of experience in poultry farming, 3% had less than one year, 65% had between one and five years, and 32% had more than five years of experience. Regarding educational background, 28% were able to read and write, while 32% attended higher education (Table 4).

Information on flock history and management

Of the farms surveyed, 66% were engaged in layer farming, 26% in broiler production, and 9% practiced dual-purpose farming. In terms of farm size, 31% of the respondents had fewer than 1000 chickens, 42% had between 1000 and 2500 chickens, and 27% had more than 2500 chickens (Fig. 2).

Overall knowledge and experience on Gumboro disease outbreaks and its impact

With regards to knowledge related to IBD, half of the respondents (51%) indicated they were very familiar with the disease, while 43% reported being somewhat familiar with it. The rest had no awareness of it. Additionally, 91% of respondents believed that IBD significantly impacts the poultry industry. In line with this, 37% of respondents reported having experienced an IBD outbreak. It was also observed that the economic impact of IBD outbreaks was felt by respondents, either through increased mortality (8%), profit losses (15%), or both (54%) (Fig. 3).

Progress of Gumboro disease outbreak

Participants were asked whether the use of IBD vaccines had led to a reduction in the disease outbreaks in the past. The results showed a significant decrease in outbreaks for 39% of respondents, a moderate decrease for 43%, and 14% reported no reduction in outbreaks. The first reason for the significant decrease in IBD was the availability of better vaccines than before (32%). On top of that, improved biosecurity measures (23%), enhanced vaccination programs (16%), better disease monitoring and diagnostics (10%), and a combination of these factors (19%) (Fig. 4).

Survey results on IBD vaccine utilization and management

The survey revealed that 66% of poultry farmers utilize vaccines manufactured locally. A vast majority (96%) indicated awareness of the appropriate storage conditions for IBD vaccines. Key challenges identified in vaccine administration included limited vaccine availability (32%), inadequate training for proper application (30%), and issues related to storage (2%). When asked about the sources of information for IBD status and vaccine updates, respondents ranked veterinarians/animal health assistants as the primary source (58%). Training programs (9%), scientific journals (4%), industry publications (4%), and peers in the industry (3%) were the other sources. Most respondents followed manufacturer protocols for IBD vaccination (66%), while the rest relied on advice from veterinarians or animal health practitioners (29%) (Fig. 5).

Efficacy of IBD Vaccines: information and insights

For IBD vaccine selection, respondents took immunogenicity (45%), availability (19%), cost (18%), manufacturer reputation (11%), ease of administration (4%), and a combination of cost and immunogenicity (2%). Of all the respondents, 37% conducted follow-up activities to evaluate the long-term performance of IBD vaccines, while 63% did not. To assess vaccine immunogenicity, respondents used clinical sign observations (18%) and laboratory testing (1%). On the contrary, 56% and 25% of them never measured vaccine immunogenicity or hadn’t thought about it (Fig. 6).

Overall, the participants in this survey demonstrated knowledge and awareness of IBD vaccines and management. They also provided several recommendations to improve the performance of IBD vaccines for better control and prevention of outbreaks. These suggestions included enhanced training programs (16%), and improved vaccine storage facilities (2%). Besides, better monitoring systems (30%), vaccine matching and immunogenicity testing (34%), and strengthened biosecurity measures (16%) were also recommended (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) remains a significant threat to the poultry industry in various countries. In addition to direct losses, particularly from vvIBDV strains, a decrease in immunocompetence, often subclinical, can undermine control measures for other diseases. This reduction in immune function can lower animal resistance and vaccine effectiveness, leading to indirect losses, poor animal welfare, reduced performance, and increased use of treatments like antimicrobials (Ingrao et al., 2013). In this study, a controlled experimental and field-based study (to evaluate the humoral immune responses of chickens to commonly used IBD vaccines) coupled with a questionnaire survey (to assess poultry farmers' overall knowledge of IBD vaccines and their management at the field level) was conducted. The in vivo results confirmed the immunogenic potential of currently used IBD vaccines, while the survey findings exposed management-related gaps that may hinder their immunogenicity in the field.

In the poultry industry, protection against IBD is achieved using commercially available live, inactivated, or genetically engineered IBD vaccines. The survey in the current study revealed that the most used IBD vaccines are live-attenuated, immune-complex, and vector-based types. Based on availability and usage frequency, one immune-complex and two live-attenuated vaccines were selected for the current in vivo efficacy test as well. The type of vaccine used is essential in determining and controlling the effectiveness of vaccination against IBD (Müller et al., 2012). In the current study, it was seen that 61% of the respondents use a locally produced live-attenuated vaccine. The growing trend of hatchery vaccination is beneficial, driven by the expanding capacity of poultry production and the focus on improving vaccination management through automated techniques, including subcutaneous injections on the first day of life or in ovo vaccination (Jacquinet and Gardin, 2011).

Vaccination of the parent flock is a common practice, where newly hatched chicks receive immunological protection through maternal-derived antibodies (MDAs) passed from the parents via the yolk sac (Abdalla, 2005). These maternal antibodies are vital in determining the ideal time for administering the Gumboro vaccine to chickens. All vaccines in the current study were administered according to the manufacturers' instructions and in function of the level of MDAs. At Day 1, chickens exhibited a mean maternal antibody titer of 3097.21 EU/mL. This high level of pre-existing circulating antibodies indicates a significant baseline immunogenic status. This is critical for evaluating the timing of seroconversion and the potential for maternal antibody interference. The control group’s mean antibody titers were recorded as 145.62 EU/mL, 32.77 EU/mL, 17.14 EU/mL, and 2.29 EU/mL on the 7th, 14th, 21st, and 28th days post-vaccination, respectively, indicating a rapid decline in MDA protection levels (Table 2). Studies also indicate that maternal antibody levels drop rapidly, reaching negligible levels by around day 20 after hatching (Mymensing, 2002). Skeeles et al. (1979) also observed a decrease in MDA from 7 to 28 days of age. This reduction aligns with the known half-life of MDA, which is estimated to range from 3 to 5 days (Zorman et al., 2011).

In the current study, CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD and CEVAC® GUMBO L vaccines produced higher antibody titers later in the experimental period, while the IBD VIRUS LC – 75 vaccines consistently showed high responses. Research shows that maternal antibody levels significantly decline within the first few weeks, affecting the efficacy of live vaccines against infectious bursal disease. For example, on the 7th day post-vaccination, the mean antibody titers of both live-attenuated vaccines (CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD and IBD VIRUS LC – 75) were lower. A low antibody titer at this stage is typical of immune response progression. The delay before reaching peak antibody levels is due to the time it takes for virus replication, antigen presentation, and subsequent antibody production (El-Mahdy et al., 2013). CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine was the only vaccine showing significant antibody levels this early at both the experimental and field levels. This is expected as it is administered at the hatchery (Day 1) and also due to the protective effect of specific antibodies in the immune-complex vaccine from MDA.

By the 14th day post-vaccination, CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD vaccine demonstrated a higher antibody response than the other two vaccines. On day 21 post-vaccination, CEVAC® GUMBO L IBD and IBD VIRUS LC – 75 vaccines peaked and were significantly higher than both the unvaccinated control and CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine. At this point, the CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine was at its lowest (the ‘immunity gap’) response both at the controlled experimental and field levels (Table 2, Table 3). This finding aligns with results reported by Sedeik et al. (2019). Besides, the differences between the vaccine responses could be due to the presence of a virus-neutralizing factor (VNF) still attached to the virus in the vaccine, potentially reducing its immunization effectiveness (Bose et al., 2003). Such variations in vaccination responses may impact the immune reaction triggered by the vaccines. Booster doses employed for IBD VIRUS LC – 75 vaccinated chicks may have provided the immune system with more opportunities to recognize the antigens, resulting in stronger and longer-lasting immunity compared to the immune-complex vaccine (Kajal et al., 2023). This was also supported by findings in a field survey where 81% of the respondents applied a booster dose for IBD.

All three vaccines were significantly higher than the unvaccinated control at day 28 post-vaccination. CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine surged back to become the highest-ranking group significantly (p < 0.001; Table 2). For CEVAC® TRANSMUNE IBD vaccine, it was observed that antibody titers in the field were lower than those obtained under controlled experimental conditions, although the overall response pattern remained consistent. The reduced antibody titers observed at the farm level may not be a result of diminished vaccine potency or reduced chickens' performance. Instead, they likely reflect the heterogeneity of field management conditions, including variability in vaccine handling, administration accuracy, and access to the recommended dose. These findings underscore the influence of management‑related factors on serological outcomes and highlight the importance of standardized vaccination practices to achieve uniform immune responses in commercial settings. By day 42 post-vaccination, there were no statistically significant differences between any of the vaccines by the end of the trial. All reached successful immunogenic levels (p > 0.05; Table 2).

Regarding questionnaire survey, 65% of the respondents had experience in poultry farming between 1 and 5 years, whereas 32% of them had more than 5 years of involvement in the sector. This experience in the industry plays a crucial role in disease control by improving knowledge, enhancing management practices, and facilitating early detection (Dashe et al., 2009). It is commendable that the majority were aware of the current prevalence of IBD in their area, as early detection and prompt response are vital for managing outbreaks. However, the proportion of participants who were unaware of the situation suggests there may be gaps in information dissemination or monitoring systems.

Study participants reported experiencing IBD outbreaks at various times and faced the disease's negative impacts, including increased mortality and profit losses (53.8%). These impacts emphasize the need for effective prevention and control measures to reduce the impact of disease on poultry populations and related industries. Understanding the factors that contribute to outbreaks can help develop targeted intervention strategies (Alkie and Rautenschlein, 2016). While a few respondents noted exceptions, 97.8% of them indicated they adhere to the cold chain requirements for vaccine storage. Properly storing vaccines in the cold chain preserves their effectiveness, prevents damage, and ensures they remain potent enough to protect poultry from IBD (Sharif and Ahmad, 2018).

Regarding challenges in administering IBD vaccines, respondents identified a lack of proper training (30%) as the main obstacle. Providing educational training on vaccination protocols positively impacts flock health, productivity, cost-effectiveness, and the overall reputation of the poultry industry (Okata and Al-Hassan, 2023). In line with vaccine administration, 65.6% and 29.5% of the respondents used vaccination protocols recommended by vaccine manufacturers or veterinarians/animal health practitioners, respectively, highlighting the importance of expert guidance in setting vaccination protocols. Only 3% of them set their protocols independently, which may occur in the absence of veterinary or health practitioner support. This indicates that the majority of respondents value expert advice and manufacturers’ guidelines when making vaccination decisions. The importance of following established protocols is crucial to minimize risks associated with improper vaccine administration and to ensure effective protection against IBD (Adino and Bayu, 2022). According to the survey, 70% of the respondents adhered to the instructions provided, with only a few stating they did not follow the guidelines. Some partially followed the instructions. By adhering to a structured vaccination plan, producers can improve disease prevention, enhance flock health, and safeguard public health (Habte et al., 2017).

Regarding factors influencing the selection of IBD vaccines, immunogenicity appeared as the most important factor (45%), followed by availability and cost. The reputation of the vaccine manufacturer was also a key factor in decision-making. Poultry producers tend to favor vaccines from manufacturers with a proven track record of delivering high-quality, effective products, a trend that is consistent with preferences for other poultry vaccines (Islam et al., 2023). Effective information dissemination is essential for implementing control measures to mitigate the impact of disease outbreaks on farms (Sabitu et al., 2022). Understanding the sources from which poultry farmers obtain vaccine information is vital for improving dissemination strategies and ensuring that accurate, up-to-date details reach the target stakeholders. In this regard, 58% of the respondents rely on veterinarians or animal health assistants for such information, highlighting the central role these professionals play in sharing knowledge within the industry. A smaller percentage of respondents rely on scientific journals, industry publications, and training programs, emphasizing the importance of staying informed about the latest developments in veterinary medicine. A few participants noted that they obtain information from colleagues in similar fields, but informal networks may not always provide reliable or verified data.

Respondents’ perception of IBD vaccine performance showed significant variation, with only 26% characterizing the vaccines as highly effective. The fact that the majority (57%) reported only moderate effectiveness, while 16% found them ineffective, underscores a potential disconnect between the high immunogenicity observed in laboratory settings and the perceived seroconversion in the field. This disparity may be reflective of the management inconsistencies and environmental stressors previously noted as factors influencing field level antibody titers. It is important to emphasize that the survey findings do not provide a definitive assessment of the actual performance of IBD vaccines. To get more complete information, participants were asked how they evaluate vaccine performance. About 18% relied on observing clinical signs, while 56% had never considered assessing vaccine efficacy. Notably, none of the respondents reported using laboratory testing for this purpose (Fig. 6). This highlights the critical need for in vivo efficacy testing, as conducted in the present study, to evaluate the performance of available IBD vaccines.

To enhance vaccine performance, respondents recommended several strategies, including implementing regular vaccine efficacy testing and vaccine matching studies (35.5%), strengthening monitoring systems (30%), and improving training programs and biosecurity measures (16% each). Enhancing biosecurity practices such as restricting access to poultry farms, enforcing strict hygiene practices, and preventing cross-contamination between farm areas can significantly reduce the risk of IBDV introduction and spread (Huber et al., 2022).

Understanding respondents' perspectives and experiences regarding the relationship between IBD vaccine use and IBD outbreaks is essential to assess the true-world performance of the vaccine. Accordingly, 39% of the respondents reported a significant reduction in IBD outbreaks after vaccination, supporting the idea that vaccination plays a key role in controlling the disease (Kurukulasuriya, 2017). On the other hand, 14% of them reported no reduction in IBD outbreaks following vaccination. This raises questions about potential factors contributing to this lack of immunogenicity or whether other variables are affecting disease dynamics. Additionally, 4% of the respondents were unaware of the vaccination impact, which underscores the need for further investigation and clarification on the relationship between vaccination and IBD outbreaks.

One key factor identified as helping to control the incidence of IBD outbreaks was the availability of safer vaccines. Advances in vaccine technology have led to the development of more effective and safe vaccines against infectious bursal disease (Li et al., 2020). Another important factor highlighted in the survey responses was the adoption of enhanced biosecurity measures. Strict biosecurity protocols, such as controlling access, implementing disinfection procedures, and maintaining hygiene, help minimize the risk of IBD outbreaks (Huber et al., 2022). Effective vaccination strategies, including proper timing, dosage, and administration techniques, are crucial to ensuring optimal immune responses in poultry flocks. Regular vaccination schedules, tailored to specific flock needs, significantly contribute to reducing Gumboro-related morbidity and mortality. Additionally, respondents emphasized the importance of improved disease monitoring and diagnostics in managing IBD outbreaks. Combining multiple strategies, such as vaccination, biosecurity practices, monitoring programs, and diagnostic tools, strengthens the overall resilience of poultry operations in facing IBD challenges (Swai et al., 2011).

According to the field surveys, 63% of respondents believed that enhancing awareness and education about Gumboro outbreaks and vaccination practices would improve disease management on poultry farms. Awareness and education are essential for implementing effective vaccination practices. A solid understanding of disease transmission dynamics, vaccination schedules, and potential side effects enables farmers to make informed decisions about their vaccination programs. Additionally, regularly monitoring flocks for signs of illness and promptly reporting any issues to veterinary services can help with early detection and intervention, ultimately reducing disease spread and minimizing economic losses, as suggested by Manabe et al. (2011).

Study limitations

One key limitation of this study is the lack of a viral challenge, which would have allowed for a more realistic evaluation of the vaccines' effectiveness in actual field conditions. Additionally, the study was constrained by the unavailability of other vaccines during the experimental period.

Conclusions

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) poses a major economic challenge to poultry farming, making effective vaccination strategies essential. This study compared humoral immune responses from different IBD vaccines under experimental and field conditions, where all vaccines showed immunogenicity to varying degrees. It was observed that antibody titers in the field were lower than those obtained under controlled experimental conditions. This might likely reflect the heterogeneity of field management conditions, including variability in vaccine handling, administration accuracy, and access to the recommended dose. These findings underscore the influence of management‑related factors on serological outcomes and highlight the importance of standardized vaccination practices to achieve uniform immune responses in commercial settings. Field surveys highlighted gaps in vaccine awareness and management practices. While antibody monitoring remains critical, future studies should include challenge trials and assess cellular immunity to identify the most effective and practical vaccines. On top that, continuous surveillance of vaccine use and performance is key to improving disease control.
