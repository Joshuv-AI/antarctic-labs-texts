Small-cell transformation of advanced non-small-cell lung cancer harboring driver mutations following tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy: a multicenter real-world study
Authors: Yuhai Lu, Xiaoliang Kang, Qiang Xu, Yu Zhou, Changbo Li, Hongxia Hu, Mao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are a mainstay of therapy for advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harboring actionable driver mutations. However, histologic transformation from NSCLC to small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) represents a particularly challenging mechanism of treatment resistance. This study aimed to characterize the incidence, timing, and clinical outcomes of SCLC transformation in this population in routine clinical practice.MethodsThis multicenter real-world cohort study included consecutive patients with stage III–IV NSCLC treated at three hospitals in Sichuan Province, China, between January 2018 and December 2021. Eligible patients had pathologically confirmed NSCLC, driver mutations, baseline whole-body imaging, complete clinical data, and received TKIs. Re-biopsy was performed to evaluate histologic changes. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.ResultsAmong 387 enrolled patients, driver mutations included EGFR (73.4%), ALK (12.6%), ROS1 (8.3%), and HER2 (5.7%). First-line treatment was TKI monotherapy in 75.2% and TKI-based combinations in 24.8%. Of the 251 patients (64.9%) who underwent re-biopsy, SCLC transformation was identified in 36 patients (14.3%). Transformation occurred predominantly after first-line TKI therapy (27/36), with additional cases after second-line therapy (9/36). OS and PFS were significantly shorter in patients with transformed SCLC than in non-transformed patients.ConclusionsIn this multicenter real-world cohort study of driver-mutated advanced NSCLC treated with TKIs, SCLC transformation was not rare among re-biopsied patients and occurred mainly after first-line therapy. These findings support a low threshold for repeat tissue sampling at disease progression to detect SCLC transformation and guide timely transition to SCLC-directed treatment.

Key findings

What is known and what is new?

• SCLC transformation is a recognized but challenging mechanism of acquired resistance to TKIs in driver-mutated NSCLC.

• This multicenter real-world study provides evidence that SCLC transformation is not rare among re-biopsied patients and may occur beyond EGFR-mutant NSCLC, including ALK- and ROS-mutated tumors.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for repeat tissue biopsy when patients develop rapid clinical deterioration, bulky progression, atypical resistance patterns, or increased neuroendocrine markers.

• Early identification of SCLC transformation may support timely transition from TKI-based treatment to SCLC-directed systemic therapy, particularly platinum-based chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a major global health burden. It is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with approximately 2.48 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022 (1). Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80–85% of all lung cancers (2,3). Despite advances in screening and systemic therapy, many patients still present with advanced-stage disease, and outcomes remain poor in the metastatic setting. A major advance in NSCLC management has been the identification of actionable oncogenic driver alterations, enabling molecularly stratified treatment (4,5). EGFR mutations are particularly prevalent in Asian patients with lung adenocarcinoma (>50%), whereas ALK rearrangements occur in approximately 3–5% and ROS1 fusions in 1–2% of cases (6).

Management of NSCLC is increasingly stage- and biomarker-driven, integrating local and systemic modalities. For early-stage disease, surgery remains the cornerstone when feasible, with neoadjuvant or adjuvant systemic therapy and radiotherapy tailored according to stage and recurrence risk (7,8). For advanced or metastatic NSCLC without actionable driver alterations, platinum-based chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and selected combination regimens are chosen based on histology, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, comorbidities, and disease burden (9,10). In contrast, for oncogene-addicted tumors, comprehensive molecular profiling is recommended to identify targetable alterations (e.g., EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, MET exon 14 skipping, RET, NTRK, KRAS G12C, and ERBB2/HER2), because genotype-directed therapy can improve response rates and disease control (11,12).

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are therefore a cornerstone of treatment for advanced NSCLC harboring actionable driver mutations (13,14). However, acquired resistance is inevitable, and histologic transformation from NSCLC to small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) represents a particularly challenging mechanism of resistance (15-17). This phenomenon has been reported in approximately 3–14% of epidermal growth factor receptor-TKI (EGFR-TKI)-resistant cases and may be underrecognized because repeat biopsy at disease progression is not routinely performed (18,19). Transformed tumors often retain the original driver alteration but lose dependence on the targeted pathway, resulting in diminished sensitivity to TKIs (19-21). Clinically, small-cell transformation typically necessitates a change in therapeutic strategy and is associated with unfavorable outcomes. Nonetheless, multicenter real-world evidence regarding its incidence, timing, genomic characteristics, and post-transformation outcomes remains limited. Accordingly, we conducted a multicenter real-world study to characterize small-cell transformation in advanced NSCLC harboring driver mutations following TKIs therapy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0489/rc).

Methods

Patients

This research prospectively involved individuals diagnosed with NSCLC who underwent treatment between January 2018 and December 2021 at three medical institutions in Sichuan Province, China: The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College, Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College, and The Second People’s Hospital of Neijiang. Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (I) a confirmed pathological diagnosis of NSCLC at stage III or IV, deemed unsuitable for surgical resection according to the 8th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC); (II) detection of driver gene mutations through next-generation sequencing (NGS); (III) completion of full-body imaging including brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT) scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis prior to the initiation of anti-tumor therapy; (IV) availability of complete baseline clinical data and laboratory test results; and (V) commencement of first-line treatment using TKIs, either alone or in combination with chemotherapy. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (I) lacked comprehensive clinical or follow-up data; (II) received only palliative care without systemic anti-cancer therapy; (III) were chose chemotherapy as the initial therapeutic approach; (IV) were lost to follow-up. A total of 1,706 patients were diagnosed with NSCLC during the study period at the three medical institutions. Ultimately, 387 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included in the study. Informed written consent was obtained from the study participants before the commencement of the study, and the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by ethics committee review boards of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College (approval No. 10248CD204), Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (approval No. 3007CB009), and The Second People’s Hospital of Neijiang (approval No. 10324NJ1001).

Data collection

Clinical data were collected for all participants, including gender, age, and smoking status, height, weight, driver gene mutations, TNM staging, metastatic locations, imaging findings, therapeutic responses, and treatment strategies. In addition, laboratory parameters including complete blood counts, liver and kidney function tests, and tumor biomarkers were also documented. Data extraction was performed by trained investigators at each center and cross-checked to address potential sources of bias.

Clinical indications for re-biopsy

Based on published reports, clinical indications for re-biopsy to evaluate possible SCLC transformation include rapid clinical deterioration, unusually aggressive or bulky progression, tumor behavior atypical for conventional TKI resistance, and rising neuroendocrine markers such as nerve-specific enolase (NSE) or pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP). Pathologic assessment of SCLC transformation in re-biopsy specimens was performed by experienced pathologists at each of the three participating tertiary hospitals.

Patient follow-up

The follow-up was conducted by scheduled outpatient visits or structured telephone interviews and ended on June 30, 2025. Patients were followed up for prognostic outcomes, which encompassed overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), occurrence of complications, and quality of life metrics. OS was defined as the period from the start of treatment until death from any cause, while PFS referred to the duration between the initiation of therapy and either disease progression or death, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan-Meier method was used to generate survival curves, and differences in survival outcomes were evaluated using the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. All tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Results were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Regarding missing data, we carefully reviewed the clinical records from each participating center and excluded patients with incomplete variables required for the primary analyses. Multivariable models were used to adjust for potential confounding factors that might influence outcomes.

Results

The flow chart of the patient enrollment was described in Figure 1. A total of 387 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included in this study. Treatment strategies for NSCLC

In this study, 291 (75.2%) patients received TKI monotherapy as their first-line treatment, whereas 96 (24.8%) individuals underwent a combination of TKIs with additional modalities such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiotherapy as their first-line treatment (Table 2). Specifically, 48 (12.4%) patients were treated with TKIs alongside chemotherapy agents, including pemetrexed or a combination of pemetrexed and cisplatin, 28 (7.2%) patients received radiotherapy in conjunction with TKIs, and 16 (4.1%) patients underwent concurrent chemoradiotherapy in conjunction with TKIs. Targeted therapy was administered to only 4 patients, and three patients received a regimen that included bevacizumab and paclitaxel, while one patient was treated with bevacizumab alone (Table 2).

Small-cell transformation

Of the 387 included patients, 251 (64.9%) underwent re-biopsy when there were clinical indications to evaluate possible SCLC transformation (Table 3). Among these, 158 re-biopsies were conducted following first-line treatment and 93 after second-line treatment. In addition, 188 re-biopsies were obtained from patients with EGFR mutations, 40 biopsies were from patients with ALK mutation, and 23 biopsies were from patients with ROS mutation. SCLC transformation was identified in 36 of the 251 (14.3%) patients; 28 patients carried EGFR mutations; 6 patients carried ALK mutations; and 2 patients carried ROS mutations (Table 3). Specifically, 27 patients experienced SCLC transformation after receiving first-line TKI treatment, while 9 cases occurred after second-line TKI therapy (Table 3).

Survival outcomes of patients with transformed SCLC and non-transformed patients

Patients in the study were followed up to assess survival outcomes. June 30, 2025, was designated as the final follow-up date for survival analysis. Based on these data, OS and PFS curves were generated, as shown in Figure 2. Patients with transformed SCLC had a median OS of 12.13 months, whereas non-transformed patients had a median OS of 30.56 months. OS was significantly shorter in patients with transformed SCLC than in non-transformed patients [hazard ratio (HR) =2.75; 95% CI: 1.34–4.82; P=0.001] (Figure 2A). Patients with transformed SCLC also had a shorter median PFS than non-transformed patients (7.38 vs. 17.42 months). PFS was significantly shorter in patients with transformed SCLC than in non-transformed patients (HR =3.42; 95% CI: 2.13–5.65; P=0.003) (Figure 2B).

Discussion

In this multicenter real-world cohort of 387 patients with advanced NSCLC harboring actionable driver alterations and treated with TKIs, re-biopsy at disease progression was performed in 251 (64.9%) patients. Histologic transformation to SCLC was identified in 36 (14.3%) of these re-biopsied patients, including 28 cases with EGFR mutations, 6 cases with ALK alterations, and 2 cases with ROS mutations. Most transformations were detected after first-line TKI therapy (27/36), with the remainder identified after second-line therapy (9/36). This transformation proportion is at the upper end of the commonly reported range for EGFR-TKI resistance (approximately 3–14%), supporting the notion that transformation is often underrecognized when repeat tissue sampling is infrequent.

The present study revealed the relatively high rate of post-progression biopsy, which likely improved ascertainment of SCLC transformation. In routine practice, transformation may be overlooked because progression is often managed using plasma genotyping alone, empiric treatment changes, or radiographic assessment without histologic confirmation (22,23). Our observation that transformation occurred predominantly after first-line TKI exposure is consistent with the concept that lineage plasticity can emerge early under selective pressure; however, the interval from diagnosis to transformation varies substantially across studies, with median times of approximately 18–24 months in several published cohorts (24,25).

Mechanistically, SCLC transformation represents a prototypical manifestation of treatment-induced lineage plasticity. Prior studies indicate that transformed tumors often retain the original oncogenic driver alteration but become less dependent on that pathway (23,26). This shift is accompanied by neuroendocrine differentiation, high proliferative activity, and characteristic genomic events—most notably functional loss of RB1 and TP53. In EGFR-mutant lung cancers, concurrent RB1/TP53 alterations in the baseline adenocarcinoma have been associated with an increased risk of subsequent transformation, supporting a model in which a permissive genomic background enables phenotypic switching under therapeutic pressure (27). Beyond RB1 and TP53, emerging evidence implicates additional regulators of neuroendocrine lineage programs, and experimental and clinical sequencing studies have proposed candidate genes that may cooperate with TP53/RB1 loss (28). Collectively, these findings suggest that “transformed SCLC” is unlikely to represent a single biological entity, and that intertumoral heterogeneity may contribute to variable clinical trajectories.

From a clinical perspective, early suspicion of transformation is critical because it typically necessitates a shift in treatment strategy. Practical indications for re-biopsy include rapid clinical deterioration, new bulky nodal or visceral progression, evolution toward SCLC-like radiographic behavior, or increases in neuroendocrine biomarkers. Several reports have noted that serum NSE and/or ProGRP may rise around the time of transformation and decline with SCLC-directed therapy, suggesting potential utility as adjunctive surveillance tools, although specificity is limited and prospective validation remains insufficient (29,30). In this context, our real-world re-biopsy rate and detection of transformation across multiple lines of therapy underscore the importance of maintaining a low threshold for tissue sampling at progression, particularly when therapeutic decisions depend on distinguishing on-target resistance mechanisms from histologic shift.

The prognosis for patients with transformed SCLC was very poor, with a median OS of only 12 months. OS and PFS were significantly shorter in patients with transformed SCLC than in non-transformed patients. These findings support a low threshold for repeat tissue sampling at disease progression to detect SCLC transformation and guide timely transition to SCLC-directed treatment. Therapeutically, available evidence supports the use of standard SCLC regimens, most commonly a combination of platinum and etoposide plus PD-L1 inhibitors (21). Our study also supports that the combination of platinum-based chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors had more OS benefits than chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy plus an EGFR-TKI for patients with transformed SCLC.

Notably, we also observed SCLC transformation in ALK-positive disease (4 cases). Although less common than EGFR-associated transformation, ALK-related histologic transformation is increasingly recognized as a mechanism of resistance, supported primarily by case reports and small series (31,32). This finding broadens the clinical relevance of transformation beyond EGFR-mutant adenocarcinoma and reinforces that repeat biopsy should be considered in any oncogene-addicted NSCLC with atypical resistance patterns, regardless of driver genotype. The relatively high proportion of transformation among re-biopsied patients and poor prognosis of transformed patients suggest that transformation may contribute meaningfully to late mortality in real-world TKI-treated populations.

Overall, this study provides multicenter real-world evidence regarding the frequency of biopsy-confirmed SCLC transformation in NSCLC harboring driver mutations treated with TKIs, demonstrating that re-biopsy at progression is feasible in routine practice and transformation is not rare, occurring in 14.3% of re-biopsied patients, predominantly in EGFR-mutant disease but also in ALK-altered tumors. These findings support a practical clinical message: when resistance to TKIs emerges, particularly in the setting of aggressive clinical features, tissue re-evaluation should be prioritized to identify transformation and guide subsequent therapy.

There were also limitations that should be acknowledged. First, potential selection bias may exist because SCLC transformation could only be confirmed in patients who underwent re-biopsy when clinically indicated; the reported 14.3% reflects the biopsy-confirmed transformation rate among re-biopsied patients rather than the incidence in the entire cohort; therefore, the true overall incidence of transformation could not be determined in this real-world study. Second, we didn’t assess genomic data from the transformed tumors, including TP53 and RB1 status in this observational real-world study, which limited our ability to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying SCLC transformation. Third, the lack of centralized pathology review may have introduced inter-institutional variability in histopathologic interpretation.

Conclusions

In this multicenter real-world cohort of driver-mutated advanced NSCLC treated with TKIs, SCLC transformation was identified in 36 of 251 patients who underwent re-biopsy after progression (14.3%), occurring mainly in EGFR-mutant tumors but also in ALK-altered disease. Practical indications for re-biopsy include rapid clinical deterioration, new bulky nodal or visceral progression, evolution toward SCLC-like radiographic behavior, or increases in neuroendocrine biomarkers. These findings reinforce the clinical importance of repeat tissue sampling to detect histologic transformation and to enable timely transition to SCLC-directed treatment strategies.
