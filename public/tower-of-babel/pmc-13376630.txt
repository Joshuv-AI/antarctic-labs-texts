Xyloglucan xylosyltransferase stem region mediates heterodimer formation
Authors: Jordan D. Julian, Ning Zhang, Reyna J. Winders, Charles E. Stewart, Alan T. Culbertson, Olga A. Zabotina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Xyloglucans are plant cell wall hemicelluloses composed of a glucan backbone substituted with xylosyl residues with diverse branching patterns. Xyloglucan xylosyltransferases (XXT) 2 and XXT5 are both required to fully xylosylate the glucan backbone and physically interact, yet the mechanism of this interaction has remained undefined. Here, we present crystal structures of the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer, organized as an obligatory heterodimer and bound to both substrates. These structures confirm the presence of a secondary binding pocket in XXT5 that likely accommodates a pre-xylosylated glucan chain. A previously uncharacterized interaction is also revealed between XXT2’s stem region and XXT5, which is absent in the XXT1 homodimer structure. Truncated and chimeric protein experiments support that this stem polypeptide controls oligomerization, a mechanism likely shared by other glycosyltransferase complexes. Together, these findings provide the structural basis for understanding how XXTs coordinate xyloglucan xylosylation and offer insight into the mechanism of their dimerization.

Introduction

Xyloglucans (XyGs) are abundant hemicellulosic polysaccharides found within the primary cell wall of dicots and nongraminaceous monocots1. XyGs are critical to the plant cell’s structural integrity, plant growth2,3, and the diet of many mammalians4, while also being utilized in industry and medicine as a thickening agent5. XyG is composed of a β−1,4-glucan chain that is decorated with α−1,6-linked xylose monosaccharides in specific patterns, most of which can be represented as XXXG or XXGG according to established nomenclature (Fig. 1A)6. Accordingly, G denotes an unbranched glucose residue, while X denotes a xylosylated glucose residue (Fig. 1A)6. Xylosylation of the β−1,4-glucan backbone is carried out by glycosyltransferases (GTs) named Xyloglucan Xylosyltransferases (XXTs), which catalyze the transfer of xylose from uridine diphosphate (UDP) xylose to the glucan chain. XXTs are type 2 membrane proteins composed of four typical regions: the N-terminal cytoplasmic domain, a single transmembrane domain (TMD) embedded within the Golgi, a disordered stem region, and a C-terminal catalytic domain in the Golgi lumen (Fig. S1A)7. The TMD anchors the XXTs to the membrane, possibly aiding in their oligomerization8, while the stem region links the catalytic domain to the TMD, and the catalytic domain catalyzes sugar transfer to the acceptor molecules.

Five XXTs, designated XXT1-5, have been identified in Arabidopsis and are classified into two distinct clades within GT family 349. Clade 1 includes XXT1 and XXT2, which sequentially di-xylosylate the glucan backbone in the XyG subunit, producing an XXGG product10. Clade 2 includes XXT3, XXT4, and XXT5, which are responsible for the xylosylation of the third glucosyl residue in the subunit producing XXXG (Fig. 1B)11–13. Further, protein-protein interaction studies suggest these enzymes, along with other XyG biosynthetic enzymes, form higher-order complexes. In particular, XXT2 and XXT5 have been shown to physically interact, whereas XXT1 shows limited interactions with either XXT2 or XXT514–16. This suggested that XXT2 and XXT5 form a potentially stable heterodimer within a larger complex1,7,8,11,14–17.

In addition to differential preferences for protein–protein interactions, the XXTs also appear to exhibit different substrate specificities. In vitro activity assays using cellohexaose, a linear β−1,4-linked glucan oligosaccharide composed of six glucose residues, have been widely used to characterize XXT activity10,12,18–20. However, in vivo, these enzymes act on a nascent elongated β−1,4-glucan chain during xyloglucan biosynthesis rather than on short free oligosaccharides. Thus, cellohexaose represents a simplified pseudo-substrate that may not fully capture the native biosynthetic process. These in vitro assays demonstrated that XXT1 and XXT2 alone can add two xyloses to cellohexaose to the 3rd followed by the 2nd glucose from the non-reducing end10,12,19,20. In contrast, XXT5 has limited ability to add a single xylose to cellohexaose, which is a long-standing question due to its critical role in in vivo experiments11,13,18,20. Cell walls of Arabidopsis mutants lacking clade 2 XXTs lack tri-xylosylated XyG13. Further, the crystal structure of XXT1 and homology models of XXT2 suggested that these enzymes are unable to bind to di-xylosylated glucan oligosaccharide due to steric hindrance from I35112. This is proposed to impair tri-xylosylation of cellohexaose on the glucose residue in the fourth position from the non-reducing end by XXT1 or XXT2, preventing completion of the XXXG structure12. In contrast, XXT3-5 have a glycine at the corresponding position rather than isoleucine, likely creating a more accommodating binding pocket for di-xylosylated acceptors, enabling the transfer of the third xylose12,13. However, structural studies of XXT2 or XXT5 alone are limited by their poor solubility18.

In this study, co-expression of the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer yielded a highly stable protein complex capable of efficiently producing tri-xylosylated cellohexaose, a function not achieved by individual homodimers. Structural characterization of the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer revealed key interface residues that determine their heterodimerization specificity, including a previously unobserved interaction in which the stem region of XXT2 engages with the catalytic domain of XXT5. This unusual protein-protein interaction was also validated in vitro using pulldown assays and mass photometry analysis, as well as in vivo using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), supporting the hypothesis that this interaction is critical for stable heterodimer formation. This crystal structure represents the only multiprotein structure among plant polysaccharide-synthesizing GTs to date. This study reveals how plant GTs assemble into functional heterodimers to control polysaccharide structure, providing a model for the formation of glycosyltransferase complexes.

Results

Expression and confirmation of XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer formation

His-tagged truncated variants of both XXT2 (amino acids 42-461; Uniprot ID# O22775) and XXT5 (amino acids 71-457; Uniprot ID# Q9CA75) from Arabidopsis (Fig. S1B) were expressed in HEK293F cells and purified from the media using Ni-NTA resin. While expression of XXT2 alone primarily resulted in a dimer, expression of XXT5 alone resulted in very low levels of total protein in either aggregated or monomeric form (Fig. 1C). Co-expression of both XXT2 and XXT5 resulted in an increase in total protein, reduced aggregation and the prevalence of dimers, in which XXT5 is likely stabilized by XXT2 (Fig. 1C). LC-MS/MS analysis after trypsin digestion was used to confirm XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer formation, due to the close molecular weights of the proteins preventing clear separation using SDS-PAGE gel. XXT2 and XXT5 share 67% sequence identity, the differences of which can be detected and sorted using LC-MS/MS software. The ratio of XXT2 to XXT5 was determined to be ~0.975:1, consistent with heterodimerization (Fig. 1D).

Activity assays were preformed, followed by product analysis using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization—time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and high-performance anion exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD, Fig. S2). Product analysis of six-hour reactions with equal total protein revealed that the XXT2 homodimer was fully di-xylosylated and slightly tri-xylosylated cellohexaose (Fig. 2A, B)10,18,20, whereas the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer produced a significantly higher amount of tri-xylosylated product in comparison with XXT2 (Fig. 2A, C). Finally, the XXT5 homodimer had substantially lower activity and produced only mono-xylosylated cellohexaose (Fig. 2A, D). Since an equal amount of total protein was used in all activity assays and considering the lower activity of XXT5 alone, it was expected that the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer would exhibit roughly half the activity of the XXT2 homodimer due to containing a single XXT2 protomer per heterodimer. To further investigate the effect of the heterodimer formation on stability and activity of the XXTs, mutations to the Mn2+ binding motif, DxD, were prepared for each XXT: XXT2AXA and XXT5AXD. Products from assays using co-expressed proteins, XXT2-XXT5AXD, XXT2AXA-XXT5, and XXT2AXA-XXT5AXD were also analyzed after six-hour reactions. With half of each functional XXT present and bound with another mutated, similar results to the respective homodimers were observed, while the double mutant assay displayed very minimal activity (Fig. 2A, F–H). Interestingly, the XXT2AXA-XXT5 mutant exhibited slightly more activity than the XXT5 homodimer, producing a small amount of di-xylosylated product, with only half the number of protomers active, indicating successful stabilization of XXT5.

To examine the role of XXT5 in the heterodimer, time-course assays were performed using the XXT2 homodimer, XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer and XXT2-XXT5AXD heterodimer. The products formed at each time point were collected and analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS and HPAEC-PAD (Fig. 2I–L). Within the first hour, mono-xylosylation of cellohexaose was completed by all three reactions, with approximately 30% mono- and 70% di-xylosylated products. However, the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer had already begun producing a larger amount of tri-xylosylated product compared to the XXT2 homodimer and the XXT2-XXT5AXD heterodimer. After two hours, tri-xylosylation in the XXT2 and XXT2-XXT5AXD reactions was slightly detectable by MALDI-TOF MS, whereas the heterodimer had tri-xylosylated ~25% of cellohexaose by that time (Fig. 2I-L and S3B). After three hours, the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer contained about 40% of tri-xylosylated cellohexaose, whereas the XXT2 homodimer and XXT2-XXT5AXD heterodimer produced only 10% tri-xylosylated cellohexaose. In summary, across all four time points, the three constructs showed similar patterns for the mono- and di-xylosylated products; however, the XXT2–XXT5 heterodimer consistently produced a significantly larger amount of tri-xylosylated cellohexaose.

To directly analyze XXT5’s tri-xylosylation, xylosylated-cellohexaose containing either primarily mono- or di-xylosylated cellohexaose was prepared by performing xylosylation reactions on cellohexaose with only XXT2 for one or eight hours, respectively (Fig. S4B, G). Next, six-hour activity assays containing either substrate were carried out using XXT2, XXT2-XXT5 or XXT2AXA -XXT5. Product analysis revealed that XXT2 and XXT2-XXT5 reaction products resembled those of the previous assays using cellohexaose (Fig. S4A–D, F–I). In contrast, XXT2AXA -XXT5 reaction products were significantly different from those obtained with the cellohexaose substrate. With mono-xylosylated acceptor, XXT2AXA -XXT5 slowly di-xylosylated cellohexaose, but failed to yield significant levels of tri-xylosylation (Fig. S4A, E). Identical reactions using a di-xylosylated substrate produced significantly more tri-xylosylated product than XXT2 alone, close to the amount produced by the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer. Additionally, XXT2AXA -XXT5 failed to utilize all the remaining mono-xylosylated cellohexaose, unlike either of the other two protein combinations (Fig. S4E, J). This suggests that XXT5 utilizes the di-xylosylated product more efficiently than XXT2 and that the two enzymes differ in their substrate specificity. This data further suggests that the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer is required for the complete synthesis of XyG.

To characterize the pattern of in vitro xylosylation by XXT2-XXT5, the products from six-hour reactions of XXT2 and XXT2-XXT5, resulting in di- and tri-xylosylated cellohexaose, were digested using xyloglucan-specific endo-β-1,4-glucanase (XEG) and β-glucosidase (β-Gluc). XEG cleaves between an unsubstituted glucose and a xylosylated glucose on the reducing end of the former (e.g., producing XXG from GGXXG). β-Gluc cleaves all non-xylosylated glucoses on the non-reducing end. XEG digestion of the XXT2 homodimer and XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer products resulted in a mixture of products consistent with previous reports for XXT2 (Fig. S5)10,18. These XEG digestions of XXT2 and XXT2-XXT5 products yielded similar results (Fig. S5B and E), suggesting these reactions generate comparable xylosylation patterns. Further analysis using β-Gluc to digest the XXT2-XXT5 product confirmed tri-xylosylated cellohexaose beginning on the second glucose (Fig. S5C). It also confirms a mixture of di-xylosylated products, GXXGGG and GGXXGG, as previously reported for the XXT2 homodimer10.

3D structure of XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer

The XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer was crystallized using PEG 3350 and lithium sulfate, yielding thin rhomboid crystals approximately 25 microns in size. Data from two apo crystals of the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer were merged to generate the apo dataset used for refinement and model building, which indexed to the P1 space group and the final structure was refined at a resolution of 3.2 Å. Molecular replacement was performed using an AlphaFold-Multimer model, which predicted an XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer model that had high structural similarity to the XXT1 homodimer described previously12. This revealed four heterodimers within the asymmetric unit (ASU; Fig. S6). Further refinement confirmed the presence of XXT2 and XXT5 in the heterodimer, consistent with the initial LC-MS/MS assessment. For the assessment of the heterodimer, all interactions reported below were confirmed in three to four of the heterodimers. Like XXT1, both XXT2 and XXT5 adopt a GT-A fold, with a core β-sheet flanked by α-helices (Fig. 3A, B). Similar to XXT1, the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer is stabilized by a pseudosymmetric interface formed between α-helix 2 and β-strand 2 of each XXT chain (Fig. 3C)12. This is largely composed of a hydrophobic core containing L172, L173, I176, I180 and Y192 of each XXT (Fig. S7A-D). Shared electrostatic interactions between each XXT flanking the core include hydrogen bonds between: D169 to H170; the backbone amide of Y192 to K177; Y415 to H218; XXT5-E189 to XXT2-K452; and XXT5-E220 to XXT2-H448 (Fig. S7A-D). In addition, XXT5’s C-terminus forms a unique α-helix, which contributes to the dimer interface via a hydrophobic pocket containing V435, P436, and L137 that interacts with XXT2’s P122, I124 and W126 (Fig. 3E). Additional electrostatic interactions are found between XXT2-W134 and XXT5-D442, and XXT2-E220 and XXT5-R447 (Fig. 3E, F). Both XXT2 and XXT5 contain a Glu at position 433, compared to an Asn in XXT1, which eliminates interaction with the backbone of L120. Instead, XXT5-E433 hydrogen bonds to XXT2-K213 (Fig. 3F). The inverse interaction is not present as XXT5 encodes R213, which hydrogen bonds to XXT5-D198, leaving XXT2-E433 unpaired (Fig. 3G).

To determine the substrate-bound structure, crystals were soaked with the solution containing 10 mM MnCl2, 8 mM UDP, and 4 mM cellohexaose. These attempts resulted in a 3.2 Å resolution UDP-bound structure with additional density for cellohexaose bound to a single subunit of XXT2 (Fig. 3H, I, Fig. S8). In apo and substrate-bound structures, the Mn2+ ion is coordinated through the DXD motif, D228 and D230, as well as H378. Overall, the UDP-binding site is conserved among the XXTs. However, XXT5 contains a His at position 196 that hydrogen bonds to the carbonyl group of UDP, whereas XXT1 and XXT2 encode L196, which does not interact with the uracil moiety of UDP (Fig. 3I). Similar to XXT1, K393 in both XXT2 and XXT5 interacts with UDP and stabilizes its phosphate group. The XXT2-bound cellohexaose aligns closely with that of the cellohexaose-bound XXT1 structure (Fig. S9). The cellohexaose is bound to only a single protomer of XXT2, while all others in the ASU are partially blocked by crystal contacts, likely impairing the binding of cellohexaose to these subunits. The acceptor binding cleft of XXT2 resembles that of XXT1, with little room for xylosylated residues at the N site due to the steric hindrance from I352 described before7,12. However, XXT5 contains a glycine at the position corresponding to I352, which allows for a pocket to form to accommodate a xylose at the N position and transfer the xylose to N + 2 glucose, two glucose residues towards the non-reducing end, confirming previous predictions from XXT5 homology models (Fig. 3J-K)12. This pocket accommodates space for pre-xylosylated XXGG substrate, allowing XXT5 to accommodate and complete the XXXG subunit.

XXT2 stem region is a key determinant for dimerization with XXT5

Similar to XXT1, the stem region of XXT5 (residues 72-120) was unresolved in the density map12. However, an unassigned density on the catalytic domain of XXT5 shows polypeptide features consistent with residues 89-109 of the XXT2 stem region (Fig. 4A, B). The XXT2 stem region is bound to the XXT5 catalytic domain through mostly non-polar interactions, with some electrostatic interactions. The interface between the XXT2 stem and the XXT5 catalytic interface is ~830 Å2, which is roughly 30% of the 2860 Å2 interface between catalytic domains (PDBe PISA; www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/)21. Thus, this large interface of XXT2 stem region to the XXT5 catalytic domain suggests that it makes a substantial contribution to the formation and stability of the heterodimer. Moreover, all four heterodimers in the asymmetric unit adopt nearly identical stem-region conformations, supported by well-defined electron density for the large side chains (Fig. S10), indicating that this interface mediates a stable interaction. The primary interaction between the XXT2 stem region and XXT5 involves XXT2-Y94, which inserts into a deep hydrophobic pocket in XXT5 (Fig. 4C, D). This interaction is stabilized by a hydrogen bond between the hydroxyl group of XXT2-Y94 and the backbone carbonyl of XXT5-C382 (Fig. 4C). Further non-polar contacts between the aromatic ring of XXT2-Y94 and the hydrophobic residues within the XXT5 pocket include I167, L387, A389, and V393 (Fig. 4C, D). In addition, electrostatic interactions between the backbones of each protomer include XXT2-S91 carbonyl bonded to the amide group of XXT5-V393, and the XXT2-V104 amide and XXT5-L423 carbonyl groups (Fig. S7E-F). Aside from these few electrostatic interactions, the rest of the stem region is predominantly stabilized through hydrophobic interactions including XXT2’s F97, I99, I102, F103, and P162 with XXT5’s I167, I397, L423, and P425 (Fig. 4E). Sequence alignment of this interacting stem region across other species shows either high conservation, or a complete absence of the motif (Fig. 4F). To further investigate protein-protein interactions in the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer in vivo, we conducted a series of domain-swap experiments using chimeric proteins and analyzed their interactions using BiFC. Four regions of each XXT were investigated for their role in heterodimerization: cytoplasmic N-terminus (N), TMD (T), stem region (S), and catalytic domain (C). Two Golgi-localized proteins unrelated to XXT function that do not interact with either XXT were chosen for these experiments: GAUT10, a pectin-synthesizing GT, and MNS1, a hydrolase involved in N-glycosylation (Fig. S11)14,15. In the initial experiments, the N-terminal cytoplasmic tail and TMD (NT) of XXT2 or XXT5 were fused to the stem region and catalytic domain (SC) of GAUT10, and reciprocally, the NT of GAUT10 was fused to the SC of XXT2 or XXT5. These constructs were co-expressed in Col-0 plants with non-chimeric XXTs (XXTWT) with all constructs fused to either the N- or C-portion of YFP on the protein’s N-terminus (Fig. S12 and S13). Upon co-expression, neither XXT2NT-GAUT10SC paired with XXT5WT nor XXT5NT-GAUT10SC paired with XXT2WT produced any YFP signal, indicating the proteins did not interact. However, co-expression of GAUT10NT-XXT2SC with XXT5WT and GAUT10NT-XXT5SC with XXT2WT both produced a YFP signal, suggesting that the N-terminal cytoplasmic and TMDs of XXT2 and XXT5 are not directly involved in heterodimerization (Fig. 5C–F).

To address the potential role of the stem regions in heterodimerization, another set of chimeric constructs was prepared, where the cytoplasmic, TMD, and stem regions (NTS) from XXT2 or XXT5 were fused to the GAUT10 catalytic domain (C) and vice versa. When co-expressed with their XXTWT counterpart (XXT2NTS-GAUT10C with XXT5WT, XXT5NTS-GAUT10C with XXT2WT, GAUT10NTS-XXT2C with XXT5WT, and GAUT10NTS-XXT5C with XXT2WT), YFP was detected in each pairing (Fig. 5G–J).

To further isolate interactions between stem-to-stem, stem-to-catalytic, or catalytic-to-catalytic domains, MNSI was used14,15 in addition to GAUT10. Co-expressions of both the XXT stem regions together (XXT5NTS-GAUT10C with XXT2NTS-MNS1C or XXT5NTS-MSN1C with XXT2NTS-GAUT10C) restored the YFP signal (Fig. 5K). YFP was also detected when either XXT stem region was co-expressed with the corresponding XXT’s catalytic region (XXT2NTS-MNS1C with GAUT10NTS-XXT5C, and XXT5NTS-MNS1C with GAUT10NTS-XXT2C), suggesting the stem to catalytic interactions, as seen in the crystal structure (Fig. 5L-M). However, co-expression of both chimeric proteins with XXT catalytic domains only (GAUT10NTS-XXT2C with MNS1NTS-XXT5C or MNS1NTS-XXT2C with GAUT10NTS-XXT5C) did not produce fluorescence (Fig. 5N). In summary, YFP fluorescence was not detected in the absence of any XXT stem domain, reconfirming the critical role of the stem regions of XXTs in heterodimer formation.

Discussion

XyG biosynthesis serves as an excellent model for complex polysaccharide synthesis due to its well-defined chemical structure, the use of enzymes with diverse nucleotide sugar donors and acceptors, and the high fidelity of these enzymes to produce highly branched polymers with specific patterns. Enzyme specificity for distinct donor and acceptor substrates for GTs involved in plant cell wall biosynthesis remains largely unclear. However, structural studies of XXT1 have provided insights into acceptor specificity, showing how differences in the acceptor binding pockets between XXT1/2 versus XXT5 likely determine their distinct substrate preferences, altering the XyG branching patterns12. The earlier proposed N + 2 model describes the sequence of xylosylation events catalyzed by XXTs and defines their substrate specificity12. According to this model, XXTs xylosylate a glucose (N + 2), which lies two monomers after the glucose at position N when counting from the reducing end of cellohexaose. When the N glucose is already xylosylated, the attached xylose fits into a binding crevice in XXT5 that accommodates a glucan acceptor with the pre-linked xylose, while XXT1 and XXT2 lack this binding crevice (Fig. 3J–K)12. This N + 2 rule for steric limitation has recently been extended to galactosyltransferases involved in glucomannan branching, where structural comparisons to XXT1, using homology modeling, helped explain their ability to add galactose residues at distinct positions along the mannan backbone23.

Up to this point, this N + 2 model had been proposed solely based on theoretical grounds and homology modeling, with no experimental confirmation. The XXT2–XXT5 crystal structure now confirms the presence of the XXT5 binding crevice that likely accommodates a xylosylated glucose in the acceptor at the N position. This structural feature most likely enables XXT5 to recognize and extend already pre-xylosylated chains and add an additional xylose, whereas this site is sterically blocked in XXT1 and XXT2, rendering them unable to add a third xylose in vivo in the plant’s Golgi, as confirmed via a reverse-genetic study13. This is further supported by activity assays, producing more tri-xylosylation of cellohexaose by the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer, in comparison to XXT2 (Fig. 2A). Additionally, when pre-xylosylated substrates generated by XXT2 were used as acceptors in the assay, XXT5 exhibited limited xylosylation activity with the mono-xylosylated acceptor. In contrast, XXT5 demonstrated a higher preference for the di-xylosylated cellohexaose, yielding more tri-xylosylated product in comparison to XXT2 used alone (Fig. S4). The tri-xylosylated product observed in in vitro reactions catalyzed by XXT2 homodimer is likely formed due to the ability of short glucan oligosaccharides, like cellohexaose, to freely rebind into the active site of XXT2 by shifting it along the binding pocket to avoid the N + 2 rule7,12. Further investigation is required to confirm the mode of binding of pre-xylosylated acceptor substrate by XXT5.

In addition to individual enzyme specificity, which plays a role in determining the distribution of XyG decoration patterns, higher-order protein interactions have also been proposed to influence polysaccharide architecture in plants1,7,11,14–16 and humans24–26. However, these suggestions have largely been based on cellular and biochemical protein-protein interaction studies, with limited knowledge about the structural organization of such heterocomplexes. Across all known GTs, only three heterodimeric structures have been reported, all from human cells, including heparin sulfate synthases EXT1 and EXT2, glycogen synthases GYS1 and GYG1, and the O-glycosylating OGT bound to hydrolase OGA27–30. Among these, EXT1 and EXT2 act as an obligatory heterodimer that is stable and functionally active only when both proteins are co-expressed28,29. Here, we determined the structure of the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer, the only structure among all plant GT complexes to date. Although XXT2 and XXT5 can be expressed and function individually in vitro, previous studies have noted their instability and the challenges in purifying them when expressed separately10,18,20. Our findings support the notion that these two XXTs likely form an obligate heterodimer. This is supported by the fact that co-expression of XXT2-XXT5 significantly enhances their solubility and stability, particularly for XXT5, and that XXT5 exhibits improved activity when paired with inactive XXT2AXA mutants compared to the XXT5 homodimer. This heterocomplex can be purified and concentrated to over 10 mg/ml, demonstrating high stability and preference for a heterodimeric assembly. Further, the formation of the XXT2–XXT5 heterodimer stabilized XXT5 for functional assays, providing experimental evidence that XXT5 utilizes the di-xylosylated product produced by XXT2 (Fig. 2), supporting the N + 2 model.

In addition to the core heterodimer interface between XXT2 and XXT5, which resembles that observed for the XXT1 homodimer (Fig. S7A–D)12, we observed additional electron density on the catalytic domain of XXT5 that represents part of the XXT2 stem region. It has long been presumed that the stem region functions as more than just a tether of the GT catalytic domain to its TMD. For example, control of protein oligomerization via the stem regions has been described for GTs involved in N-glycosylation31–35. Here, we present structural evidence supporting these proposals. XXT2 in our XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer structure demonstrates a unique structural ordering in the stem region, with 22 amino acids bound to XXT5. This structural evidence, along with pulldown assays and MP experiments, strongly supports that the XXT2 stem region, alongside the catalytic core, makes a substantial contribution to efficient heterodimer formation.

Pulldown and BiFC results further suggest that the XXT5 stem region also contributes to dimerization. However, the XXT5 stem region was not resolved in the crystal structure, most likely due to the disordered nature of stem regions, which was also observed for the XXT1 homodimer12. BiFC suggests an interaction between XXT5’s stem with XXT2’s catalytic domain (Fig. 5M), but there was no obvious interaction in the crystal structure of the heterodimer. It is possible that this interaction involves two highly dynamic regions of XXT2 and XXT5 and thus is disordered in the crystal structure, or that the interactions are transient and subsequently sterically blocked by crystal contacts. Further, BiFC experiments reflect protein behavior in vivo, where XXTs are attached to Golgi membranes and may have a different organization compared to crystallized recombinant proteins. They are also members of higher-order multiprotein complexes shown to function in the Golgi, potentially containing more than one XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer per complex14–16. Such arrangements may potentially facilitate additional interactions between the stem of XXT5 and the catalytic domain of XXT2, which is not readily formed in solution or in the crystal14–16. These findings raise questions that warrant further investigation.

The BiFC study also demonstrated that XXTs do not form heterodimers solely through their catalytic domains and seem to require the presence of at least one of the stem regions (Fig. 5K). This may also be attributed to the different behavior of membrane-bound proteins in the Golgi and recombinant soluble proteins in vitro. Thus, the GTs present in plant Golgi are found at low concentrations, while functioning in a highly crowded environment alongside other types of GTs. This lowers their chances of successful organization into the correct complexes for the synthesis of highly branched polysaccharide structures. We hypothesize that stem regions of XXTs are the initial driving force in their mutual recognition, which brings them into close proximity to form stable homo-, hetero-, and even multiprotein complexes. This data provides insights into how XXT5 may differentiate between XXT1 and XXT2 in vivo14. The dimer interface between the XXT1 homodimer and the XXT2-XXT5 heterodimer shares a central hydrophobic core but differs in several peripheral contacts. These include unique interactions between XXT2-K198 and XXT5-E433, XXT2-W134 and XXT5-D442 and XXT2-E220 with XXT5-R447. Thus, it is likely that these peripheral contacts between the catalytic domains contribute to the selectivity of XXT5 heterodimerization with XXT2 rather than XXT1. Further, although XXT1 and XXT2 contain a similar interacting stem sequence with no significant changes between the two paralogs (Fig. 4G), additional determinants of selectivity may reside in the unresolved upstream region of the stem (Fig. 6). Thus, it is likely that the primary determinants driving XXT2-specific heterodimerization with XXT5 are the peripheral region of the catalytic core and potentially other elements of the stem regions closer to the TMDs. Heterodimerization of any XXT with XXT5 likely requires the stem region, as the XXT2 stem sequence that interacts with XXT5 is conserved in plants producing XXXG-type XyG, which require XXT5, but is absent in those producing only XXGG-type XyG (Fig. 4F).

Understanding GT architecture and specificity is central to enabling precise synthesis of plant polysaccharides for industrial and biomedical use. GTs synthesize a large set of distinct complex polysaccharides, capable of overcoming regiospecific and stereospecific complexities that present time-consuming and expensive challenges for chemical synthesis36,37. However, without structural information, the mechanisms of substrate binding and specificity remain unclear, hence limiting efforts to biochemical engineering of GTs. For example, modifications to the sugar composition of xyloglucan alter its physical properties, such as viscosity, a principal component in its current industrial applications and medical research5,38,39. Further structural characterization and protein engineering will enable the development of large-scale, customizable XyG for specific applications.

Here, we present the XXT heterodimer, which represents the first structurally characterized heterodimeric GT complex in plants. This structure reveals a previously uncharacterized interaction within the stem region, providing structural evidence for stem-mediated dimerization of GTs. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the stem regions play a crucial role in mediating the assembly of higher-order complexes within the XyG biosynthetic machinery. The formation of such an obligatory heterocomplex enhances the efficiency and fidelity of xylosyl substitution of the glucan backbone, with XXT2 (and XXT1) initiating the process by adding the first two xylosyl residues, the product that is used by XXT5 as an acceptor substrate to form the final XXXG structure. Our study highlights the importance of ongoing structural characterization of large heterocomplexes, where computational modeling predictions, such as AlphaFold, may fail to capture critical nuances.

Methods

Cloning of XXT constructs

For structural and biochemical characterization, truncated XXTs (XXT2Δ42-461 and XXT5Δ71-457) were cloned into a pGeN2 vector containing an N-terminal 8xHis tag, followed by an AviTag, sfGFP, and Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease cut site using gene-specific primers12,18,40. For DxD mutants, XXT2AxA and XXT5AxD were cloned from previously sequenced DxD mutants in PET vectors using the same primers as wild-type18. For in vitro pulldown assays, a HA-tag (XXT2) or FLAG-tag (XXT5) was added following the TEV cut site of XXT2Δ42-461 and XXT5Δ71-457, as well as constructs lacking the stem region (XXT2Δ121-461 and XXT5Δ118-457) using overlap extension PCR for XXT5Δ118-457 (Table S1). For the BiFC assay and confocal microscopy study, all genes (XXT2, XXT5, GAUT10 and MNS1 were cloned with gene-specific primers from previous vectors14,15,41 and full-length complementary (cDNA). The PCR products were digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes and inserted into pSAT vectors42. For the BiFC assay, we constructed a fused protein with either the N- or C-terminal fragment of YFP (nYFP, cYFP) at the N-terminus14,15. All constructs used in this study were sequenced prior to expression.

Expression and purification of XXTs

XXTs were expressed and purified as mentioned before12. XXTs were expressed using HEK293F cells at a cell density of 2-2.5 million. Cells were transfected with a total of 1.5 μg of DNA per million cells (0.75 μg of each XXT per million cells for co-expression), and mixed, followed by 3 μg polyethylenimine per million cells. After 24 hours, media volume was then doubled, and valproic acid was added to a final concentration of 4 mM.

Cells were harvested 5 days post-transfection at 1000 xg for 10 min and the media was collected and mixed with NiNTA resin for one hour at 4 °C. Resin was then washed twice with wash buffer containing 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, and 40 mM imidazole, followed by elution using the 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole. Protein was then concentrated to 1 mL using 30 kDa cutoff centrifugal spin columns (Vivaspin), and imidazole was reduced by diluting with 4 mL of 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, and 150 mM NaCl (TBS) and reconcentrated down to less than 1 mL. XXTs were then incubated with excessive TEV protease to cleave GFP and the His-tag overnight at 4 °C with mixing. The following day XXTs were incubated with Ni-NTA resin and mixed for 1 hour. Flowthrough was collected and further purified using size exclusion chromatography with a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) and 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl running buffer. The corresponding dimer peak was then collected and concentrated to 10 mg/mL for crystallography screens. Excessive protein was flash frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80 °C.

Crystallization

The initial hit of 27% polyethylene glycol 3350 (PEG 3350), 0.2 M lithium sulfate, and 0.1 M bis-tris propane pH 7.0 was found using an LMB screen (Molecular Dimensions). Crystals were obtained after 5 days, using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method. Further optimization revealed that XXT crystallization occurred between 20 and 24% PEG 3350, 0.1 M lithium sulfate, and 0.05 M bis-tris propane or HEPES pH 7.5. Apo-XXT crystals were soaked in 20% glycerol and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Ligand-bound crystals were soaked 10 mM MnCl2, 8 mM UDP and 2 mM cellohexaose (Neogen) for 5 minutes, dipped in a solution of 20% glycerol, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Datasets were collected at the Advanced Light Source.

Datasets were processed using XDS and merged (Apo) using Pointless and further scaled using Aimless. The data was cut at a I/sigl > 1 in the highest resolution shell with a CC1/2 > 0.30. The initial XXT2/XXT5 dimer was solved using a truncated structure predicted by AlphaFold-Multimer (XXT2 and XXT5, amino acids 120-461; Uniprot IDs# O22775 and Q9CA75) that aligned well with the XXT1 homodimer (PDBID: 6SWB) for Phaser-MR (molecular replacement). Structural refinement was carried out using phenix.refine, with manual refinement in COOT. For the substrate-soaked crystal structure, substrates were fit to the density. A single cellohexaose was fitted into the density alongside chain E of XXT2 with a partial occupancy of 0.84, which was determined by phenix.refine. Density along chain C also resembles cellohexaose, the density was much weaker, and thus this was left unmodeled. UDP was fit in two separate conformations, described previously12. UDP was placed in bent conformation in chains 1 and 7, and in a straight conformation in the other six chains (Fig. S8, Table S2).

Activity assays

Initial assays (Fig. 2) were performed with 2.5 μM total protein, 2 mM UDP-xylose (Carbosource), 0.25 mM cellohexaose (Neogen), 2 mM MnCl2, 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, and 150 mM NaCl. Reaction mixtures of 100 μL (6 h) or 250 μL (Time trials) reaction mixture were shaken at room temperature in a 96-well plate and stopped by boiling the sample for 10 minutes. For hourly analysis, 50 μL were removed from the total volume and immediately boiled, while the remaining reaction continued in the well. Boiled protein was pelleted and removed by spinning at 20,000 xg for 10 min. Product was precipitated by mixing supernatant with 100% acetone at a final concentration of 90% and chilling at −20 °C overnight18,19. Product was then pelleted at 20,000 xg for 10 min, dried and resolubilized at their starting volume of 50 or 100 μL.

For enzyme kinetics, assays were coupled with pyruvate kinase lactate dehydrogenase (PKLD) detection assay at a total volume of 100 μL in a 96-well plate. 3 mM MnCl2, 1.2 mM NADH, 1 mM PEP and 3 uL of PKLD enzyme (Sigma Aldrich P0294) were mixed with 1 mM cellohexaose, 1.5 μM total XXT enzymes, and 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 or 4 mM UDP-Xylose. Samples were run in triplicate and measured every 10 s over 1 h on a 96-well plate reader at 340 nm. Sample concentrations were determined using a 0.25 to 2 mM NADH standard curve.

Pre-xylosylated substate was prepared by incubating 2.5 μM XXT2, 3 mM UDP-xylose, 1 mM cellohexaose, 2 mM MnCl2, 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, and 150 mM NaCl for 1 or 8 h to generate mono- and di-xylosylated products, respectively. Samples were boiled, and the inactivated protein was pelleted. Products were precipitated overnight, dried, and resuspended in 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl. Assays were performed over six hours as previously stated, with 25% (v/v) pre-xylosylated product instead of cellohexaose for all assays and 1 mM UDP-xylose for di-xylosylated assays.

MALDI-TOF MS and HPAE-PAD product analysis

For MALDI-TOF MS analysis, 1 μL 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid matrix was spotted onto the MALDI-TOF plate followed by 1-2 μL of resolubilized product and dried at 30 °C. Samples were analyzed using Shimadzu AXIMA Confidence MALDI-TOF Mass Spectrometer in reflectron mode at 100 mV with 100 profiles collected. IC6000 chromatography with a PAD detector was used to quantify the yield of xylosylated product in the same samples used for MALDI-TOF MS. 20 μL of each sample was diluted in 80 μL of water and injected onto a 2 mm CarboPac PA-20 column and eluted using a 100 mM to 350 mM NaOH gradient over 35 min. The column was re-equilibrated to the initial 100 mM NaOH over 15 min in between runs.

Enzymatic digestion

Following acetone precipitation, samples were resuspended in 100 μL of water. 15 μL of sample was diluted with 15 μL of 50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.3. 3 μL of 10 ng/μL XEG43 or 1 μL of 1:3 (enzyme: water) diluted β-Glucosidase (Megazyme) was added. Both samples were incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours. Samples were then boiled for 10 minutes, enzymes pelleted, and supernatant was acetone precipitated overnight.

LC-MS/MS quantification of proteins within a heterodimer

Three biological replicates of FPLC purified TEV-cleaved XXTs were submitted to the Iowa State University Protein Facility. XXTs were digested overnight using trypsin and Lys-C. The peptides were separated by HPLC and analyzed by MS/MS by fragmenting each peptide with a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer with an HCD fragmentation cell. The resulting intact and fragmentation patterns were compared to a theoretical fragmentation from both Mascot 3.0 and Byonic44, provided by the sequence of each XXT from Uniprot, to find peptides that can be used to identify the proteins. For semi-quantification, unique peptides with a charge of +1 to +5 were detected for each XXT were selected and quantified using Proteome Discoverer, which was based on MS1 intensities. Each XXT was quantified by the total MS1 intensity of all unique peptides assigned to each XXT.

In vitro pulldown assays

Pulldown assays were conducted by mixing 75 nmol of protein with 30 uL of anti-FLAG resin (Pierce A36801) in 200 μL TBS following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein with resin was incubated for one hour at 4 °C followed by 4 × 200 μL wash using TBS and 1 final 200 μL water wash. Protein was eluted using 0.1 mM glycine pH 2.8 and neutralized using 1 M Tris pH 8.5 in a 20:3 ratio for a total volume of 200 μL.

Western blot

Pulldown assays were analyzed by Western blot. Samples were separated by a 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PFVF membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with mouse primary antibodies diluted in TBST: Anti-FLAG (1:10,000; Rockland Immunochemicals, Item No. 200-301-383) or Anti-HA (1:10,000; Pierce Biotechnology, No. 26183) in 1% milk powder for one hour. Membranes were then incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:25,000; Invitrogen, Cat. # A21235). SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for the detection of proteins.

Mass photometry

Mass photometry experiments were carried out using TwoMP on an anti-vibration platform (Refeyn). 100 nM samples were added to a PBS-filled cassette diluting the protein 10-fold to a final protein concentration of 10 nM prior to measurements. Standards of β-Amylase (56, 112, and 224 kDa) and bovine thyroglobulin (670 kDa) were used for calibration. Experiments were carried out at RT over 59.9 seconds using AcquireMP and analyzed using DiscoverMP (Refeyn).

Transient expression in arabidopsis protoplasts

The recombinant plasmids (nYFP-XXT2WT, nYFP-GAUT10WT, cYFP-XXT5WT, cYFP-MNS1WT, nYFP-GAUT10NT-XXT2SC, nYFP-XXT2NT-GAUT10SC, nYFP-GAUT10NTS-XXT2C, nYFP-XXT2NTS-GAUT10C, nYFP-XXT2NTS-MNS1C, cYFP-GAUT10NT-XXT5SC, cYFP-XXT5NT-GAUT10SC, cYFP-GAUT10NTS -XXT5C, cYFP-XXT5NTS-GAUT10C, cYFP-XXT5NTS-MNS1C, cYFP-MNS1NTS-XXT5C. were purified from E. coli cells. Protoplasts from 20–30 leaves of 6-week-old Arabidopsis were prepared and transformed with the purified plasmids according to previously described protocols14. After 2-day incubation in the dark at RT, the protoplasts were imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica SP5 system)14,15.
