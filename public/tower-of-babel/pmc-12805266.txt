The first 50 VT ablations: A fellow’s guide to the learning curve
Authors: Robert N. Kerley, Usha B. Tedrow
Published: 2025

My first ventricular tachycardia (VT) ablation procedure lasted 9 hours. I was a cardiology fellow in Ireland, watching what seemed like controlled chaos—entrainment mapping, activation mapping, and substrate modification. I understood little, but I knew I wanted to master this discipline. That case eventually brought me to Boston to train at the Brigham, where volume, mentorship, and complexity promised immersion in VT ablation.

Fifty procedures later, I have realized that VT ablation is not learned by repetition alone—it is learned through layers of insight. Each stage has its own lessons, and it is those lessons I wish I had known earlier.

The electrocardiogram is your first map

Early on, I treated the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) as a checklist of morphologies. Gradually, it became a language. Localizing VT is like solving a vector equation: each lead gives direction and magnitude, guiding you toward the exit site. When the eventual activation map aligns with what you predicted on paper, it feels like the mathematics of electrophysiology made visible. For fellows, time spent sketching vectors before the case pays off inside the laboratory—it sharpens both your hypotheses and your confidence.

Anatomy and imaging are everything

No amount of mapping can compensate for poor preparation. Reviewing magnetic resonance imaging scans for scar location, computed tomography scans for coronary anatomy, and echocardiograms for chamber geometry and valvular pathology transforms the case from exploration to execution. Understanding where the catheter can—and cannot—go is as important as understanding where the VT originates.

Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) brings anatomy to life in real time. In Ireland, I rarely saw ICE used, and initially I underestimated its value. Learning to interpret ICE images and integrate them into 3-dimensional mapping systems during fellowship was transformative—turning structures from abstract surfaces into living, moving landmarks. Detailed ultrasound integration bridges imaging, anatomy, and spatial awareness. The best procedures begin long before the sheath is inserted, but they succeed when you can see what your map cannot.

The electrogram is king

If the ECG is the overture, electrograms are the score. Every spike, split, and fractionated potential tells a story about conduction, block, or vulnerability. Listening to electrograms is integral to successful VT ablation: it remains the foundation of understanding conduction and arrhythmia behavior.

One case made that lesson permanent: a young patient with recurrent ventricular fibrillation and no structural heart disease whose septum was alive with ventricular fibrillation–triggering Purkinje-like potentials. There was no scar to guide me—only the signals themselves. Mapping their timing and relationships taught me that understanding why tissue behaves abnormally is as important as knowing where it does.

That case became the foundation of my research into Purkinje de-networking and fascicular substrate modification. The Purkinje system occupies only a small percentage of myocardium, yet its physiology can dictate life or death. In an era of pulsed field ablation and high-power systems, we risk becoming structural electroanatomists rather than physiologists. The electrogram remains the anchor—it reminds us that every successful ablation starts with understanding, not energy delivery.

Success extends beyond the catheter

VT management is multidisciplinary, involving anesthesia, advanced heart failure, and critical care teams. Stellate ganglion block and mechanical circulatory support have become essential adjuncts, enabling mapping and ablation in unstable patients and restoring control when arrhythmia overwhelms physiology. Autonomic modulation, including stellate ganglion block and emerging neuromodulatory techniques, continues to expand as a bridge or complement to ablation. Stereotactic body radiotherapy is another option when ablation is either not appropriate or not enough. Recognizing when to integrate these strategies, and collaborating across specialties to deliver them, is as important as knowing how to perform ablation itself.

Competence means knowing your limits

My first independent success—a posteromedial papillary muscle VT ablation—was exhilarating, followed quickly by my first para-Hisian VT case, which caused transient heart block. It reminded me how fine the line is between precision and harm. Competence is not the absence of supervision; it is the judgment to know when you still need it.

Power and precision in the future

Modern catheters can deliver energy with remarkable breadth and depth—sometimes achieving true transmurality, particularly with pulsed field ablation. The goal should no longer be to ablate more tissue but to ablate smarter, targeting the critical circuit isthmus while preserving viable myocardium and conduction tissue. The best outcomes arise not from the size of the lesion but from the clarity of understanding that guided it.

The road ahead

I believe the future of VT ablation lies in integration, combining high-resolution imaging, real-time ultrasound, and computational modeling to anticipate arrhythmia rather than react to it. Yet even as technology advances, the fundamentals endure: the ECG, the anatomy, the electrogram, and the judgment to connect them.

For fellows beginning their own journey, I hope these reflections help you approach each case with curiosity and humility. The next case will always be your best teacher, and the learning curve never stops.
