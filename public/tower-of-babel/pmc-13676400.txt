Modifications of Lysine Residues 294 and 311 Within the Microtubule‐Binding Region Alter Tau Aggregation and Tubulin Binding Equilibria
Authors: Dominik P. Vogl, Mario Migotti, Robert Konrat, Anne C. Conibear, Christian F. W. Becker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDysfunction of the microtubule‐associated protein Tau is a central feature of Alzheimer's disease and related tauopathies, yet how site‐specific lysine modifications modulate the functional and pathological states of Tau remains poorly understood. Here, we combine protein semi‐synthesis with segmental isotope labelling and NMR spectroscopy of full‐length Tau, to dissect how lysine acetylation and carboxymethylation within the microtubule‐binding region of Tau regulate its interactions with tubulin, microtubules, and amyloid assembly. Site‐specific modification at lysine 294 delays Tau‐mediated tubulin polymerization and fibril formation, whereas acetylation at lysine 311 exerts more moderate effects, but alters fibril morphology. NMR spectroscopy of segmentally isotope‐labelled Tau variants indicates that single lysine acetylation does not measurably weaken Tau binding to pre‐formed microtubules; however, bivalent acetylation reduces microtubule binding, possibly by cumulative charge neutralization. Together, these results indicate that lysine acetylation redistributes Tau between functional states in a site‐ and valency‐dependent manner. Our study helps to establish a mechanistic framework linking combinatorial lysine modifications to Tau dysfunction and highlights the utility of region‐resolved structural approaches to decipher posttranslational modification–dependent equilibria in intrinsically disordered proteins.

Introduction

The microtubule‐associated Tau protein is an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) expressed in six isoforms in the human brain [1]. The longest isoform and focus of this study, Tau4 (2N4R, 441 amino acids), contains four highly conserved microtubule‐binding repeats (R1‐R4) within its microtubule‐binding region (MTBR, residues 244–368, Figure 1a), complemented by a weakly homologous pseudorepeat (Rʹ, residues 369–399) [2, 3]. These repeats harbor key aggregation‐prone motifs [PHF6 (306VQIVYK311) and PHF6* (275VQIINK280)], and lysine residues that mediate electrostatic interactions with the negatively‐charged microtubule surface [4, 5]. As an IDP, Tau exhibits conformational and functional plasticity. Originally identified in its role in associating with tubulin and regulating microtubule assembly, Tau also interacts with proteins in synaptic vesicles and heat shock proteins involved in neuronal function (Figure 1b) [6, 7]. Furthermore, Tau interacts with membrane components and synaptic proteins, and binds to phosphatases and other signaling molecules, amongst other roles [8]. The functional diversity of Tau also makes it sensitive to perturbations arising from mutations, competing interactions, and posttranslational modifications (PTMs), many of which are enriched within the MTBR and proline‐rich region [4, 9, 10, 11]. Dysregulation of Tau functions and interactions is a hallmark of tauopathies and implicated in several neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease [1, 12].

The inset shows the amino acid sequence between the two native cysteine residues (orange) as one‐letter code, with the PHF6 aggregation motif underlined. The two lysine residues (K294 and K311) for which modifications were investigated in this study are shown in pink. (b) Schematic of Tau functional equilibria. Under physiological conditions, Tau dynamically partitions between four interconnected states: (i) a pool of soluble Tau; (ii) productive engagement with soluble tubulin to promote microtubule polymerization; (iii) transient binding to the microtubule lattice mediated primarily by the repeat regions R1–R4 (and Rʹ); and (iv) self‐association to form soluble and insoluble aggregates. Tau is among the most posttranslationally modified proteins in the human brain, with up to 35% of its residues subject to modification [9]. While (hyper)phosphorylation has long been considered a central driver of Tau pathology, increasing evidence indicates that lysine PTMs—particularly acetylation and carboxymethylation—have critical and underexplored roles in modulating Tau functions [13, 14, 15]. These PTMs occur predominantly in the MTBR and have been linked to impaired microtubule stabilization, altered aggregation, and reduced Tau clearance [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Whereas acetylation neutralizes the positive charge of lysine side chains, carboxymethylation (a non‐enzymatically formed advanced glycation end product) introduces a secondary amine and a negatively‐charged carboxyl group [21, 22, 23]. Although phosphorylation of soluble proteins, for example, in signaling cascades, is often rapid and reversible, it is also known to be irreversible in Alzheimer's disease brains. Lysine PTMs can also be longer‐lived and might induce shifts in Tau equilibria, particularly when multiple lysine residues are modified or when deacetylation or ubiquitination pathways are compromised [24, 25]. Models of Tau pathology increasingly point toward disease progression arising from the combinatorial accumulation of PTMs, rather than individual PTM events [9].

The implications of Tau acetylation in multiple, and sometimes opposing, biological processes complicates efforts to assign a specific role to this PTM in disease and highlights a gap in our understanding of how site‐specific lysine acetylation regulates the balance between the physiological and pathological states of Tau at a molecular level [17, 19]. In studies by several groups, acetylation of Tau within the KXGS motifs inhibited its aggregation [19, 26], whereas in other cases, acetylation at K280 accelerated Tau aggregation, hindered its binding to microtubules and promoted development of oligomers and short fibrils [27]. Analysis of post‐mortem brain tissue from Alzheimer's patients has identified many additional acetylated lysine residues, including in the MTBR [28]. The role of acetylation in Tau degradation by proteolysis and autophagy has also been investigated, with conflicting outcomes that indicate the need for more detailed clarification at a molecular level [9, 26, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Furthermore, addressing these questions with high structural resolution is challenging due to the intrinsic disorder of Tau (resulting in spectral crowding in NMR studies) and the heterogeneity of naturally‐sourced or enzymatically‐modified Tau samples [33].

Here, we focus on disease‐relevant lysine residues K294 and K311 [15, 24, 26, 27] within the MTBR. These residues are located in two short motifs with different known functions, namely K311 as part of the crucial β‐sheet forming motif (306VQIVYK311) and K294 as part of the 290KXGSK294 motif that is key to binding microtubules. Building on previous findings in our group, specifically that K294 modification impacts tubulin polymerization, we examine how single and double acetylation events, as well as carboxymethylation at K294 [9, 34], regulate the functional equilibria of Tau. Focusing on full‐length Tau, we combine protein semi‐synthesis for site‐specific incorporation of PTMs [35] with segmental isotope labelling to overcome spectral crowding in NMR studies [33] and map region‐resolved effects on microtubule interactions. By integrating aggregation studies with tubulin polymerization assays and NMR spectroscopy, we dissect how lysine PTMs redistribute Tau functional equilibria and propose a mechanistic framework highlighting how multivalent PTM patterns might shape Tau pathology.

Results

Semi‐synthesis Provides Access to Tau Variants Bearing Site‐Specific Lysine Modifications

Taking advantage of the two native cysteine residues in Tau (Cys291 and Cys322, Figure 1a), and based on previous work on Tau phosphorylation and ubiquitination PTMs in our group [36, 37], we generated five full‐length Tau variants differing in their PTMs and nine full‐length Tau variants with different PTMs and isotope‐labelling patterns via a three‐segment native chemical ligation (NCL) strategy (Figure 2) [38, 39]. This strategy preserves the native Tau sequence and allows for site‐specific introduction of PTMs in the middle synthetic segment, which encompasses part of the most abundantly‐modified MTBR. As shown in Figure 2, recombinantly expressed segment Tau_322‐441 was ligated to the synthetic middle segment Tau_291‐321 bearing the respective PTM(s) [Tau(unmod.), Tau(AcK294), Tau(AcK311), Tau(AcK294_311), and Tau(K294CML)] and an Acm side‐chain protecting group on the N‐terminal Cys291 (Supporting Information Data S1). After ligation, the Acm group was removed and the second ligation was carried out with the N‐terminal segment Tau_2‐290, bearing a C‐terminal thioester generated by thiol‐mediated intein cleavage [39, 40].

Building on previous optimizations of the semi‐synthesis strategy [36, 37], the synthesis proceeded smoothly, with some minor adjustments. Yields of Tau_2‐290 were improved by site‐directed mutation of Thr3 of the Mxe intein to Cys, thereby suppressing premature intein cleavage by inactivation of the catalytic Cys1 of the intein through intramolecular disulfide formation (Supporting Information Data S2). Hydrazide‐to‐thioester conversion [41] and ligations were carried out in a one‐pot manner to minimize the number of HPLC purification steps. Minor hydrolysis side‐products were observed but could be separated by RP‐HPLC. Overall yields and analytical characterization data for the five semi‐synthetic variants and recombinantly expressed Tau [Tau(recomb.)] as a control are provided in (Supporting Information Data S3).

Lysine Acetylation Suppresses Tau Fibrillization and Alters Fibril Morphology

To investigate the effects of site‐specific lysine acetylation in the MTBR of Tau, we carried out in vitro fibrillization assays, monitoring the formation of Tau fibrils by the fluorescence intensity of Thioflavin T (ThT) upon induction of fibrillization with the surfactant octadecyl sulfate (ODS) [42, 43]. As shown in Figure 3, unmodified Tau aggregated rapidly, reaching a fluorescence plateau within ∼420 min. Acetylation at K311 [Tau(AcK311)] did not alter initial aggregation kinetics but reduced maximal ThT fluorescence by ∼50%, indicating altered fibril properties rather than impaired fibril formation. The doubly acetylated variant Tau(AcK294_311) had similar initial kinetics to Tau(AcK311), with a further slight reduction in maximal ThT fluorescence (∼40% relative to unmodified Tau). In contrast, acetylation at K294 [Tau(AcK294)] strongly delayed aggregation, resulting in a prolonged lag phase (∼700 min), absence of a detectable growth phase within the assay timeframe, and only ∼20% of the ThT fluorescence of the unmodified variant. The Tau variant with carboxymethyllysine modification [Tau(K294CML)] showed similar delayed aggregation to Tau(AcK294) (Supporting Information Data S4), suggesting that the position, rather than the specific nature of the PTM, or electrostatic effect has a larger effect on aggregation.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirmed the presence of fibrils for all Tau variants after the aggregation assay (Figure 3c). Tau(AcK294) formed a carpet of fibrils but decreased aggregation kinetics and showed a lower maximal ThT fluorescence (Figures 3b,c). Furthermore, residual soluble Tau(AcK294), indicates that less protein is trapped in fibrils (Figure 3d). Fibrils formed by Tau(AcK311) appeared thicker than those of Tau(recomb.) and formed larger aggregate clumps, which might allow less ThT binding in keeping with the lower maximal fluorescence observed. Tau(AcK294_311) fibrils were of low abundance and formed aggregate clumps like those of Tau(AcK311), suggesting an additive effect of the two acetylations. Fibrils of Tau(K294CML) were sparser than those of Tau(AcK294) (Supporting Information Data S4), in keeping with the low maximal ThT fluorescence. Here, we analyzed the aggregated Tau variants by SEM to confirm fibril formation and observe their general appearance. Studies by other groups have shown that PTMs can induce structural variations in Tau fibrils; however, only very large PTMs such as ubiquitination have, until now, shown significant structural differences [18].

Analysis of post‐aggregation supernatants by RP‐HPLC revealed some residual soluble monomeric Tau(AcK294) and Tau(K294CML) (Figure 3d and Figure S4, respectively), consistent with its delayed aggregation and reduced ThT fluorescence. Notably, a later‐eluting peak at ∼18 min was observed for Tau(AcK311) and Tau(K294CML) (Supporting Information Data S4), suggesting the formation of soluble oligomers, as has been reported by Lashuel and coworkers for a Tau variant acetylated at K280 [27]. Interestingly, a defined peak at ∼18 min was observed in the RP‐HPLC of the supernatants, although it is likely that a range of oligomer sizes is formed. For Tau(AcK311) no monomeric species were observed. For the doubly‐acetylated Tau(AcK294_311), only trace oligomeric species were detected, further supporting that reduced ThT fluorescence reflects altered ThT–aggregate interactions rather than impaired aggregation. Unmodified Tau(recomb.) showed no residual soluble species, indicating complete aggregation.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that site‐specific acetylation or carboxymethylation at K294 impairs Tau fibrillization and promotes soluble species, whereas acetylation at K311 alters fibril structure and/or ThT dye binding without having a large effect on the initial aggregation rate, possibly by promoting the formation of soluble oligomers as a precursor to fibrillization.

Lysine PTMs Delay the Onset of Tubulin Polymerization

Tubulin polymerization assays revealed that site‐specific lysine PTMs within the MTBR markedly delayed Tau‐induced tubulin polymerization (Figure 4), in agreement with previous studies [27, 36]. Whereas acetylation at K311 caused only a modest delay in polymerization efficiency compared to Tau(unmod.), acetylation at K294 or both K294 and K311 substantially delayed the onset of tubulin polymerization. Comparable tubulin polymerisation activity was observed for recombinant [Tau(recomb.)] and semi‐synthetic [Tau(unmod.)] Tau, confirming that semi‐synthetic Tau behaves as expressed Tau protein (Supporting Information Data S5). Carboxymethylation at K294 moderately reduced tubulin polymerization activity (Supporting Information Data S5), as previously observed [36]. Together, these results suggest that K294 acetylation dominates the regulatory effects on Tau‐mediated tubulin polymerization. Due to the intrinsically‐disordered nature of Tau, structures of Tau binding to tubulin monomers or oligomers have been mostly limited to segments of the repeat domains of Tau. Based on a cryo‐EM structure of the R2 repeat region with two β‐tubulin and one α‐tubulin monomers [44], we used AlphaFold3 [45] to predict the interaction between full‐length Tau and four tubulin monomers (two β‐tubulin and two α‐tubulin units). While AlphaFold predictions for IDPs are known to have limitations [46], and the majority of the Tau sequence was predicted with low confidence, the MTBR was ordered over the tubulin tetramer with medium confidence (90> plDDT > 70). As an indication of the relative sizes and possible orientations, the two modified lysine residues K294 and K311 investigated in this study sit at the interfaces between the α‐ and β‐tubulin units, in proximity to the tubulin C‐terminal tails (Figure 4c).

Segmental Isotope‐Labelling of Tau Variants Facilitates Region‐resolved Information on Interactions With Microtubules

To gain deeper insights into the interactions of site‐specifically acetylated Tau variants with microtubules, and the equilibrium between monomeric Tau, Tau oligomers and aggregates, tubulin polymerization and microtubule‐bound Tau, we took advantage of our semi‐synthesis strategy (Figure 2) to introduce segmental isotope labelling patterns into the Tau variants for NMR spectroscopy [33]. As Tau is an intrinsically disordered protein, 1H‐15N HSQC spectra show poor dispersion and high signal overlap in the backbone amide region, making it difficult to unambiguously assign changes in signal intensity upon binding interactions [47, 48]. Our semi‐synthesis strategy lends itself to uniform 15N‐labelling of either the recombinantly‐expressed N‐terminal segment Tau_2‐291 or the C‐terminal segment Tau_322‐441, or both, while site‐specific PTMs can be incorporated in the synthetic segment Tau_291‐321 (Figure 5a), as we have demonstrated with other intrinsically disordered proteins [49]. The resulting segmentally‐labelled and site‐specifically modified Tau variants show reduced spectral crowding in 1H‐15N HSQC spectra, enabling assignment of most signal‐intensity changes upon interaction with pre‐formed microtubules (Supporting Information Data S6).

Residue‐ and region‐specific mapping of Tau—microtubule interactions were first investigated using segmentally labelled Tau variants in which the N‐terminal segment Tau_2‐290 was 15N‐labelled (Figure 5b). Comparison of the signal intensities in the 1H‐15N HSQC spectra of Tau variants alone and in the presence of microtubules revealed spatially distinct attenuation patterns that report on interactions with microtubules. The large size and corresponding slow solution tumbling of microtubules causes Tau residues that interact with microtubules to have an increased correlation time and therefore reduced NMR signal intensities. Within 15N‐labelled residues 2–290, significant decreases in the signal intensities were observed for residues ∼200‐290, corresponding to microtubule‐binding repeats R1 and R2, with minimal perturbation outside these regions. Interaction of Tau with microtubules was confirmed by native PAGE analysis of the NMR samples (Supporting Information Data S7). Comparison of the segmentally‐labelled and modified Tau variants with the corresponding segmentally‐labelled unmodified variant Tau(unmod.) (Figure 5c) revealed small (< ± 0.2) differences in the intensity ratios over most of the labelled residues 2–290. Residues ∼130‐200 had more negative (± 0.2 – 0.4) intensity ratio differences, suggesting that acetylation at K294, K311, or both might increase Tau interaction with microtubules in this region, although the negative values are only from a few residues. Carboxymethylation of K294 gave similar results to those observed for acetylation of K294 (Supporting Information Data S6.5).

Using the same semi‐synthesis strategy, we then generated segmentally‐labelled Tau variants Tau(AcK294) and Tau(AcK294_311) in which the C‐terminal segment Tau_322‐441 was 15N‐labelled (Figure 5d), as these variants had the biggest effects in the Tau aggregation and tubulin polymerization assays (Figures 3 and 4). Comparison of the signal intensities in the 1H‐15N HSQC spectra of Tau variants alone and in the presence of microtubules revealed that, within the 15N‐labelled residues 322–441, decreases in the signal intensities were observed for residues ∼322‐400, corresponding to microtubule‐binding repeats R3 and R4. Compared to the unmodified variant Tau(unmod.) (Figure 5e), doubly‐acetylated Tau(AcK294_311) showed a smaller reduction in signal intensity in the region ∼322‐400, indicating weaker microtubule binding.

Overall, these results imply that acetylation of both K294 and K311 in the MTBR weakens microtubule binding within the C‐terminal region of Tau (including R4 and the pseudorepeat region Rʹ), whereas single lysine PTMs have only small effects on interactions of the MTBR with pre‐formed microtubules. Furthermore, the modular readout enabled by the segmental labelling strategy confirms that Tau engages microtubules primarily through its repeat regions R1‐R4 (and Rʹ), while distal N‐ and C‐terminal segments remain weakly interacting and disordered.

Discussion

Tau operates at the intersection of competing equilibria: binding to soluble tubulin to promote microtubule assembly; stable association with formed microtubules; and self‐assembly into oligomers and amyloid fibrils (Figure 6). Our unique access to site‐specifically modified and segmentally isotope‐labelled full‐length Tau variants allows us to combine biochemical and NMR data, demonstrating that lysine acetylation does not uniformly modulate Tau behavior but instead redistributes Tau between these states depending on the number, nature, and position of lysine PTMs. Our results identify acetylation at K294 as a key regulatory PTM, delaying fibril formation, delaying tubulin polymerization, and stabilizing soluble Tau monomers. In contrast, acetylation at K311 has smaller effects on initial aggregation kinetics and tubulin polymerization but appears to alter fibril‐associated ThT fluorescence and promote soluble oligomer formation. The effects of the combined acetylations at K294 and K311 are neither cancelling nor synergistic; whereas the effects on fibrillization are dominated by K311 acetylation, the delay in tubulin polymerization is dominated by K294 acetylation. Especially K294 PTMs appear to shift the Tau functional equilibria toward the soluble Tau population.

Comparison of the tubulin polymerization and microtubule binding data points toward a functional decoupling of these two properties of Tau. Whereas K294 acetylation and K294_311 acetylation strongly delay tubulin polymerization, the NMR data show that single acetylation at K294 does not significantly weaken Tau interactions with pre‐formed microtubules. Only double acetylation K294_311 produces a detectable decrease in interactions of the Tau R3/R4/Rʹ region with microtubules. A hypothesis that might fit these observations is that neutralization of key lysine residues (K294) disrupts electrostatic interactions that promote tubulin‐tubulin interactions, whereas binding to intact microtubules is more tolerant of single‐site charge neutralization and only becomes detectably weakened when multiple lysine residues in the MTBR are modified. Our aggregation assays indicate that lysine acetylation (and carboxymethylation) modulates aggregation pathways and equilibria, rather than completely blocking aggregation. These findings could support a model in which decreased interaction with tubulin increases the soluble pool of Tau, increasing its propensity to aggregate. A decrease in positive charge upon acetylation would be consistent with decreased binding to the negatively charged surface of microtubules, and to the formation of denser aggregate clumps, as observed for Tau(AcK294_311). Furthermore, if tubulin polymerization is slowed, fewer pre‐formed microtubules would be available for Tau binding, thereby further shifting the equilibrium toward the pool of soluble Tau.

Interpreted more broadly, we hypothesize based on the combined results that soluble oligomers of Tau take a central place in the functional equilibria by shifting the conformational equilibria of Tau toward a more linear population (within the MTBR) that is predisposed to either aggregation or binding to tubulin monomers and promoting tubulin polymerization (Figure 6). Neutralizing the positive charge on K294 might decrease the pre‐arranged oligomer population, favoring the disordered monomer population and thereby decreasing aggregation and slowing tubulin polymerization. On the other hand, neutralizing the positive charge on K311 or adding a negative charge by carboxymethylation of K294 might favor the formation of soluble oligomeric species predisposed to aggregation or tubulin polymerization. Further investigations into the dynamics and conformational populations of Tau and its modified variants would be needed to support this hypothesis, but could be facilitated by our semi‐synthesis strategy, for example, by installing spin‐labels or FRET probes. Furthermore, the semi‐synthesis strategy could also be used to investigate the potential hierarchy amongst different PTMs and crosstalk between them, for example, whether acetylation/carboxymethylation of lysine residues have the same effects when there is a nearby phosphorylation, or vice versa.

Taken together, the data suggest that lysine PTMs modulate the functions of Tau by electrostatic and multivalent effects, in agreement with several previous reports [15, 21, 50, 51]. Acetylation at K294 appears to be particularly potent in suppressing the physiologically important role of Tau in tubulin polymerization, but multiple acetylation events are needed to measurably weaken binding to pre‐formed microtubules, suggesting a progressive model in which Tau function deteriorates gradually as PTMs accumulate. From the perspective of Tau pathology in disease, our findings position lysine PTMs in the MTBR as regulatory modifications that redistribute the equilibrium between Tau functions, rather than simply inactivating it. Moreover, lysine acetylation also competes with degradation signals for Tau such as polyubiquitylation, thereby increasing protein half‐life. We note that other lysine residues nearby that were not investigated in this study can also be acetylated or ubiquitinated, such as K217 (in Alzheimer's disease) or K369 (in cortico‐basal degeneration). In a physiological context, it is unlikely that PTMs are present in stochiometric amounts; however access to the site‐specifically modified variants provides the opportunity to combine differently modified variants in defined ratios to begin to decipher such nuances. More broadly, this study demonstrates how the combination of site‐specific protein modifications and segmental isotope labelling enables us to zoom into the PTM‐mediated modulation of intrinsically disordered protein equilibria and localize functional consequences of PTMs. The strategy can be further applied to other PTMs and combinatorial studies of how PTMs redistribute the functional equilibria of Tau and may be extended to other intrinsically disordered proteins in neurodegeneration.
