Counterfactual explainability of black-box prediction models
Zijun Gao, Qingyuan Zhao

Abstract
It is crucial to be able to explain black-box prediction models to use them effectively and safely in practice. Most existing tools for model explanations are associational rather than causal, and we use two paradoxical examples to show that such explanations are generally inadequate. Motivated by the concept of genetic heritability in twin studies, we propose a new notion called counterfactual explainability for black-box prediction models. Counterfactual explainability has three key advantages: (1) it leverages counterfactual outcomes and extends methods for global sensitivity analysis (such as functional analysis of variance and Sobol’s indices) to a causal setting; (2) it is defined not only for the totality of a set of input factors but also for their interactions (indeed, it is a probability measure on a whole “explanation algebra”); (3) it also applies to dependent input factors whose causal relationship can be modeled by a directed acyclic graph, thus incorporating causal mechanisms into the explanation.



keywords

Causality, Directed acyclic graph, Explainable AI, Global sensitivity analysis.

Introduction

With the increasing complexity of machine learning prediction models and their wider applications, there is a rapidly growing need to explain these black-box models in practice. Although the term “explainable artificial intelligence” (XAI) is a fairly recent invention
(
Dwivedi et al. 2023
, for a recent survey, see)
, there is a long-standing and quickly expanding literature on variable importance and black-box model visualization, including Sobol’s indices in global sensitivity analysis
(
Sobol 1993
)
, functional analysis of variance
(
Hoeffding 1948
)
, Shapley’s value in game theory
(
Shapley 1953
)
, partial dependence plots
(
Friedman 2001
)
, accumulated local effect plots
(
Apley and Zhu 2020
)
, and various other variable importance measures
(
Breiman 2001
;
Hines et al. 2022
;
Williamson et al. 2023
)
.
A main drawback of these existing tools is that it is not easy to incorporate contextual information that is crucial for model explanation practice. These methods depend entirely on the joint distribution of the observed data and thus ultimately describe associational rather than causal relationships without further assumptions
(
Zhao and Hastie 2021
)
.

We next give two paradoxical examples to highlight why a lack of causal interpretation can be particularly problematic in the real world. Specifically,
Example

1
will show that it is insufficient to consider the joint distribution of model inputs in interpreting the model, and
Example

2
will show that knowledge of causal mechanism can completely change our interpretation of the model. For the rest of this paper, by model “inputs” we mean
K\geq 1
variables
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
and by “prediction model” or simply “model” we mean a deterministic function
Y
that maps
W=(W_{1},\dots,W_{K})
to a real value. (We will take the dualistic view and notation that
Y
is both a function of
W
and also a random variable itself.)

Example 1

(Inconsistency of explanations)
.

Suppose
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are two independent Rademacher random variables (so they take
-1
or
1
with probability a half), and the model is simply
Y(W_{1},W_{2})=W_{2}
.
Since
Y
is determined solely by
W_{2}
, and
W_{1}
is “independent” of
W_{2}
, it seems logical that
W_{1}
cannot explain
Y
at all.
However, if we know
W_{2}
is “generated” by
W_{2}=W_{1}W_{2}^{\prime}
, where
W_{2}^{\prime}
is another Rademacher random variable that is independent of
W_{1}
, then we can write the prediction model alternatively as
Y(W_{1},W_{2}^{\prime})=W_{1}W_{2}^{\prime}
. In this case,
W_{1}
clearly has something to do with
Y
. So the same random variable play different roles in two apparently equivalent models.

Example 2

(Copycat)
.

Suppose
W_{1}=W_{2}
in the observed data, so it may seem fair to divide explainability equally between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
. However, if we know that
W_{2}
is merely a “copycat” of
W_{1}
, then our intuition is that the full credit should be given to
W_{1}
instead of
W_{2}
when explaining the prediction model
Y
. To explain what we mean by “copycat”, think about one YouTube channel just uploading video contents that are copied from another channel without permission (
W_{1}
is the original video and
W_{2}
is the copycat video). If
Y
represents the predicted revenue from these videos, then all the credits should be attributed to the original content creator.

These two examples demonstrate the need to consider the causal relationship between model inputs in model explanation. Relatedly, a widely held view is that some considerations of the causal (and perhaps counterfactual) relationship between model inputs are necessary in formulating good notions of fairness of algorithmic decision making
(
Kusner et al. 2017
;
Imai and Jiang 2023
)
. In a recent survey, it is concluded that “tools potentially based on causal methods are needed to assist in the identification of protected variables and groups as well as their proxies” to make machine learning “fairer”
(
Caton and Haas 2024
)
. Counterfactual causality (contrasting potential outcomes in “different worlds”) is sometimes regarded as the foundation for explanation
(
Pearl 2009
;
VanderWeele 2015
)
; in view of Pearl’s association-intervention-counterfactuals Ladder of Causation, it is crucial to “climb up” to the counterfactual ladder for “imagining, retrospection, understanding”
(
Pearl and Mackenzie 2018
)
. Counterfactual reasoning can be found in causal mediation analysis and applications to law and epidemiology; some key concepts include natural direct/indirect effect
(
Robins and Greenland 1992
;
Pearl 2001
)
, probability of causation
(
Tian and Pearl 2000
;
Dawid et al. 2017
)
, and notions related to attributable fraction
(
Suzuki and Yamamoto 2023
)
. However, there is little work on counterfactual explainability of black-box prediction models to our knowledge.

The above examples and considerations motivate the following definition of counterfactual explainability of black-box prediction models. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
be a subset of indices for the model inputs. To begin with, when the model inputs
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
are “causally independent” in the sense that they are probabilistically independent and have no causal effects on each other (more rigorous definition using potential outcomes can be found in
Section

2
), we propose to define the counterfactual explainability of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
to
Y
as

\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}):=\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left(Y(W)-Y(W^{\prime}_{\mathcal{S}},W_{-\mathcal{S}})\right)}{2\mathsf{Var}(Y(W))},

(1)

where
W^{\prime}_{\mathcal{S}}
is an identically distributed copy of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
that is independent of
W
. More generally, when the causal relationship between
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
can be described by a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
(
Pearl 2009
;
Hernan and Robins 2023
, see e.g.)
, we propose to define the counterfactual explainability of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
to
Y
as

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}):=\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left(Y(W^{*})-Y(W^{*\prime}_{\mathcal{S}},W^{*}_{-\mathcal{S}})\right)}{2\mathsf{Var}(Y(W^{*}))},

(2)

where
W^{*}
is the “basic potential outcomes schedule” of
W
that will be precisely defined later in
Section

3
(
W^{*}
completely determines
W
and thus
Y
), and
W^{*\prime}_{\mathcal{S}}
is an identically distributed copy of
W^{*}_{\mathcal{S}}
that is independent of
W^{*}
. Using the causal DAG framework, the causal independence assumption can be described as a DAG with no edges between
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
, and it is not difficult to show that the more general definition (
2
) then reduces to (
1
). The normalization factor
2\mathsf{Var}(Y)
in (
1
) and (
2
) make sure that
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
is bounded between
0
and
1
.

Heuristically, (
1
) and (
2
) measures the “importance” (or in our terminology, counterfactual explainability) of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
by the average amount of change to
Y
in an intervention that replaces
W_{\mathcal{S}}
(or more precisely, the basic potential outcomes of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
) with an independent and identically distributed copy. This idea is directly motivated by (but has some subtle differences from) the definition of heritability in twin studies
(see e.g.
Hartl et al. 1997
, p. 434-441)
, although the genetics literature on heritability often does not use a causal language explicitly (but see
Kohler et al. 2011
for an exception). We will explore this connection in the example below.

Example 3

(Genetic heritability)
.

Let gene
G
and environment
E
be two causally independent factors for some trait
Y=Y(G,E)
, and let
G
denote the gene of person
A
. Now think of a hypothetical “fraternal twin” B of A that has an independent draw of genes denoted by
G^{\prime}
and goes through exactly the same environment
E
. The counterfactual explainability of
G
to
Y
,

\xi_{Y}(G)=\frac{\mathsf{Var}(Y(G,E)-Y(G^{\prime},E))}{2\mathsf{Var}(Y(G,E))},

measures how much
A
differs from
B
on average and can be regarded as a notion of genetic heritability in this context.
1

1

This should be regarded as a heuristic example instead of a precise definition of genetic heritability because “fraternal twins” actually share half the genes on average. Genetic heritability as a concept can be dated at least to Francis Galton’s famous “regression towards the mean” observation in his investigation of heredity in the 19th century, and interestingly there are perhaps just as many definitions of heritability in the genetics literature
(
Jacquard 1983
;
Hartl et al. 1997
, see e.g.)
as variable importance in the machine learning literature.

We end the Introduction with a brief overview of the rest of this article. When multiple input factors are involved, it is often desirable to distinguish between “main effects” and “interaction effects”. To this end, we propose to quantify interaction explainability using the inclusion-exclusion principle. For example, the explainability of the interaction between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
to
Y
is defined as
\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\wedge W_{2})=\xi_{Y}(W_{1})+\xi_{Y}(W_{2})-\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\vee W_{2})
. We will see that this can be alternatively expressed using the variance of an interaction contrast as follows (assuming there are only
K=2
causally independent input factors),

\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\wedge W_{2})=\frac{\mathsf{Var}(Y(W_{1}^{\prime},W_{2}^{\prime})-Y(W^{\prime}_{1},W_{2})-Y(W_{1},W^{\prime}_{2})+Y(W_{1},W_{2}))}{4\mathsf{Var}(Y(W_{1},W_{2})},

(3)

where
(W_{1},W_{2})
and
(W^{\prime}_{1},W^{\prime}_{2})
are independent and identically distributed. This will be further extended to the case with more than two input factors using the anchored decomposition of a multivariate function
(
Sobol 1969
;
Kuo et al. 2010
)
. In fact, we will see that (
1
) defines a
unique probability measure
on what we call the “explanation algebra” generated by
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
; see
Section

2
for detail. In
Section

3
, we show how the definition of counterfactual explainability can be extended, almost effortlessly, to causally dependent input factors described by a causal DAG by replacing a variable with its basic potential outcomes. In this case, counterfactual explainability is generally not point identified, but we show in
Section

4
that the partial identification region of counterfactual explainability collapses to a point under a comonotonicity assumption on the potential outcomes. We discuss some simple methods to estimate counterfactual explainability in
Section

4
and further demonstrate this concept using some numerical examples in
Section

5
. We conclude the article with a comparison of counterfactual explainability to other variable importance measures in the literature and some future research directions.

2
Causally independent input factors

Example

1
shows that probabilistic independence is not sufficient to define a consistent notion of explainability. In this Section, we use a stronger notion called “causal independence” to illustrate the definition of counterfactual explainability in (
1
) and how it may be extended to the whole “explanation algebra” to define interaction explainability as in (
3
).

Roughly speaking, causal independence means that changing the value of any input factor
W_{k}
in an intervention does not change any other input factors. We will not give a formal definition here but note that this can be regarded as a special case of the setting discussed in
Section

3
by using an “empty” causal DAG between
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
that has no directed edges. Causal independence implies probabilistic independence, but not vice versa.

To see how causal independence can resolve the paradox in
Example

1
, one simply needs to write down the causal model in the two cases considered there. In the first case where
Y(W_{1},W_{2})=W_{2}
, the conclusion that
W_{2}
offers no explanation to
Y
depends on the implicit assumption that
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are causally independent (so the potential outcome is
W_{1}(w_{2})=W_{1}
for all
w_{2}
). In the second case where
Y(W_{1},W_{2}^{\prime})=W_{1}W_{2}^{\prime}
, by saying
W_{2}
is “generated” by
W_{2}=W_{1}W_{2}^{\prime}
, we implicitly treat this as a structural equation (the potential outcome of
W_{2}
is given by
W_{2}(w_{1})=w_{1}W_{2}^{\prime}
), so
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are no longer causally independent. See
Pearl 2009
and
Example

9
below for a discussion on the meaning of structural equations and how they can be translated to potential outcomes.

The counterfactual explainability of a subset of input factors indexed by
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
is denoted as
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
and defined in (
1
) in the Introduction. This quantity should be viewed as the “total explainability” of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
to
Y
(instead of some kind of main effect) and is always bounded between
0
and
1
:
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=0
means that the inputs
W_{\mathcal{S}}
has no counterfactual explainability at all, and
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=1
means that
Y
is completely determined by
W_{\mathcal{S}}
. It can be viewed as the causal analogue to Sobol’s upper sensitivity index (see
Section

6
).

2.1
Counterfactual explainability of interaction between factors

Often, we are interested in more than just total explainability. For instance, in the genetic heritability example (
Example

3
), we may be interested in knowing the role of gene-environment interaction or gene-gene interaction (in the latter case, we need two genes
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
in that example). Interaction explainability can be defined in two equivalent ways. Here we introduce the definition using the anchored decomposition of
Y
that generalizes the definition in (
3
). In the next subsection we introduce an alternative definition using the inclusion-exclusion principle.

Let us first introduce the anchored decomposition. The interaction contrast with respect to a non-empty
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
anchored at
w
and evaluated at
w^{\prime}
is defined as

I_{\mathcal{S}}(w,w^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}|-|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|}Y(w^{\prime}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}},w_{-\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}).

By convention,
I_{\emptyset}(w,w^{\prime})=Y(w)
is the value of
Y
evaluated at the anchor
w
. The anchored decomposition then refers to the identity

Y(w^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]}I_{\mathcal{S}}(w,w^{\prime})
, which holds for any
w
and
w^{\prime}
. For more discussion, see
Kuo et al. 2010
and
Owen 2023
.

If
w
and
w^{\prime}
are close,
I_{\mathcal{S}}(w,w^{\prime})
is a finite difference approximation to the partial derivative of
Y
with respect to
W_{\mathcal{S}}
at
w
. When
K=2
, the anchored decomposition reduces to
Y(w^{\prime})=I_{\emptyset}(w,w^{\prime})+I_{1}(w,w^{\prime})+I_{2}(w,w^{\prime})+I_{\{1,2\}}(w,w^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}(w,w^{\prime})=Y(w_{1}^{\prime},w_{2})-Y(w_{1},w_{2}),~I_{2}(w,w^{\prime})=Y(w_{1},w_{2}^{\prime})-Y(w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle I_{\{1,2\}}(w,w^{\prime})=Y(w_{1},w_{2}^{\prime})-Y(w^{\prime}_{1},w_{2})-Y(w_{1},w^{\prime}_{2})+Y(w_{1},w_{2}).

The last quantity is commonly used to measure interaction effect in the causal inference literature
(
VanderWeele and Tchetgen 2014
)
, especially in factorial experiments
(
Dasgupta et al. 2015
)
.

Definition 4

(Interaction explainability)
.

Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
be causally independent variables. For
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
, the counterfactual explainability of the interaction between
W_{\mathcal{S}}
to
Y
is defined as

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}):=\frac{\mathsf{Var}[I_{\mathcal{S}}(W,W^{\prime})]}{2^{|\mathcal{S}|}\mathsf{Var}(Y(W))},

(4)

where
W
and
W^{\prime}
are independent and identically distributed.

In (
4
), interaction explainability is quantified by the average magnitude of the interaction contrast (note that
I_{\mathcal{S}}(W,W^{\prime})
has mean
0
by symmetry). It can be shown that (
4
) is always bounded between
0
and
1
(see
Theorem

7
below), and a larger value indicates greater explainability by the interaction between
W_{\mathcal{S}}
. To gain some intuitions in the
K=2
case, it can be shown that
\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\wedge W_{2})=0
if and only if
Y(W_{1},W_{2})=f_{1}(W_{1})+f_{2}(W_{2})
(i.e.
Y
is additive), and
\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\wedge W_{2})=1
if
Y(W_{1},W_{2})=W_{1}W_{2}
.
The next example further demonstrates this.

Example 5

(Multilinear form)
.

Suppose
Y(W)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}
, where the factors are causally independent and each
W_{k}
has a symmetric distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
. Then

\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset}\beta_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}^{2}}{\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq[K]}\beta_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}^{2}},\quad\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{S}}\beta_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}^{2}}{\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq[K]}\beta_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}^{2}}.

2.2
Counterfactual explainability on the explanation algebra

The definition of total explainability in (
1
) and interaction explainability in (
4

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|+1}\cdot\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}W_{k}),

(5)

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|+1}\cdot\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}W_{k}).

(6)

These identities motivated the notation

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\vee W_{2})+\xi_{Y}(W_{1}\wedge W_{2})=\xi_{Y}(W_{1})+\xi_{Y}(W_{2}).

It is remarked in previous sections that
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
and
\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
are always between
0
and

\xi_{Y}(\cdot)
behaves just like a probability measure. In fact, this is true and can be made precise by introducing what we call the explanation algebra.

Definition 6

(Explanation algebra)
.

The explanation algebra
\mathcal{E}(W)
generated by a collection of variables
W=(W_{1},\dots,W_{K})
is the Boolean algebra defined by the join operation
\vee
, meet operation
\wedge
, and negation
\neg
that is isomorphic to the set algebra for the power set of
\{0,1\}^{K}
(each element in this set algebra is a collection of binary vectors of length
K
) defined by set union
\cup
, set intersection
\cap
, and set complement under the map
W_{k}\mapsto\{w\in\{0,1\}^{K}:w_{k}=1\}
,
k\in[K]
.

To make this definition more concrete, when
K=2
we have the following representation for some elements in
\mathcal{E}(W)
:

\displaystyle W_{1}\mapsto\{(1,0),(1,1)\},~W_{2}\mapsto\{(0,1),(1,1)\},

\displaystyle\neg W_{1}\mapsto\{(0,0),(0,1)\},~\neg W_{2}\mapsto\{(0,0),(1,0)\},

\displaystyle W_{1}\vee W_{2}\mapsto\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},~W_{1}\wedge W_{2}\mapsto\{(1,1)\},

so
\xi_{Y}(\neg W_{1})
means the amount of counterfactual variability of
Y

not
explained by
W_{1}
and similarly for
\xi_{Y}(\neg W_{2})
. One can also consider other elements in
\mathcal{E}(W)
, for example
W_{1}\wedge(\neg W_{2})
(which maps to
\{(1,0)\}
), which can be interpreted as explanation “solely” due to
W_{1}
.

Theorem 7
.

If
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
are causally independent input factors, the total counterfactual explainability as defined in (
1
) can be uniquely extended to a probability measure on
\mathcal{E}(W)
.

We will also use
\xi_{Y}(\cdot)
to denote this unique probability measure, which is now defined on
\mathcal{E}(W)
instead of just for a subset or its interaction.
In
Section

6
, we show that the measure can be interpreted as the causal counterpart to Sobol’s upper sensitivity index (after normalization)
(
Sobol 1993
)
and its generalizations
(
Liu and Owen 2006
;
Hooker 2004
)
.
We will prove (
5
), (
6
), and
Theorem

7
in the Appendix.

Next are some useful properties of counterfactual explainability that follow from
Theorem

7
:

1.

\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=1
if
Y
is a just function of
W_{\mathcal{S}}
.

2.

\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}W_{k})\geq\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
and
\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}W_{k})\leq\xi_{Y}(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})
for any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq[K]
.

3.

\xi_{Y}(W_{1})+\dots\xi_{Y}(W_{K})\geq\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in[K]}W_{k})
.

The second property shows that if the interaction explainability of
\mathcal{S}
is zero, so is any of its superset (which represents higher-order interactions). This hierarchy may be useful when trying to construct tests for vanishing interactions. The third property is basically the Efron-Stein inequality
(
Efron and Stein 1981
)
and can be further tightened using the inclusion-exclusion principle (which also provides lower bounds of
\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in[K]}W_{k})
; the corresponding interlacing inequalities are named after Boole and Bonferroni in probability theory).

3
Causally dependent input factors

To define counterfactual explainability for causally dependent inputs, we require a causal graphical model for those input factors. Because the theory of DAG causal model is very well developed, we will only introduce some main concepts here and refer the reader to
Pearl 2009
;
Hernan and Robins 2023
for further discussion.

Consider a DAG
\mathcal{G}
with vertices being the variables
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
. We use
\pa(k)
to denote the set of vertices with a directed edge pointing to
W_{k}
(aka “parents” of
W_{k}
) in
\mathcal{G}
. We use
W_{k}(w_{\mathcal{S}})
to denote the potential outcome of
W_{k}
under an intervention that sets
W_{\mathcal{S}}
to
w_{\mathcal{S}}
, and define the potential outcomes schedule as the collection of all such variables:

\displaystyle W(\cdot)=\left(W_{k}(w_{\mathcal{S}}):k\in[K],\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K],w_{\mathcal{S}}~\text{in the support of}~W_{\mathcal{S}}\right),

The schedule of all basic potential outcomes of
W_{k}
is defined as

W_{k}^{*}=(W_{k}(w_{\pa(k)}):w_{\pa(k)}~\text{in the support of $W_{\pa(k)}$}).

For any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
, let
W_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot)
and
W_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
collects all the potential outcomes and basic potential outcomes for
W_{\mathcal{S}}
, respectively.

Definition 8

(Causal Markov model)
.

We say a probability distribution on
W(\cdot)
is causal Markov with respect to a DAG
\mathcal{G}
with vertex set
W
if the following are true:

1.

The next event holds with probability
1
:

The basic potential outcomes schedules

Because the basic potential outcomes

Therefore, the definition of counterfactual explainability of causally

Y
is a function of
W^{*}
, and second,
W_{1}^{*},\dots,W_{K}^{*}

W_{1},\dots,W_{K}

We next illustrate these rather abstract definitions with some examples.

Example 9

(NPSEM-IE)
.

The structural equations associated with a DAG
\mathcal{G}

W_{k}=f_{k}(W_{\text{pa}(k)},E_{k}),~k\in[K]

and independent errors
E_{1},\dots,E_{K}

W_{k}
are given by
f_{k}(w_{\text{pa}(k)},E_{k})
with
w_{\text{pa}(k)}
varying over its support. Because
E_{1},\dots,E_{K}
are independent, the basic potential outcomes
W_{1}^{*},\dots,W_{K}^{*}
are also independent and thus the causal Markov assumption is satisfied. By recursive substitution, we can rewrite the model as a function of error terms; for notational simplicity, we will still denote this function as

Rather than using basic potential outcomes to define counterfactual explainability, we can instead use the error terms and rewrite equation (

\displaystyle\xi_{Y}(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left(Y(E)-Y(E^{\prime}_{\mathcal{S}},E_{-\mathcal{S}})\right)}{2\mathsf{Var}(Y(E))},

where

is an independent and identically distributed copy of

.

In view of (

is essentially the explainability of its exogenous noise

. The reason we do not use the NPSEM-IE definition from the beginning is that two different sets of structural equations (e.g. obtained by rescaling the noise variables) may generate the same distribution of the potential outcomes schedule and thus may lead to ambiguous definitions. It is more desirable to directly define counterfactual explainability using the basic potential outcomes schedule, which collects all “intrinsic” noise variables.

4
Identification and estimation

For causally dependent inputs, the counterfactual explainability cannot be precisely determined using the distribution of
W
, the function
Y(W)
, and the DAG
\mathcal{G}
. We illustrate this with an example.

Example 10

(Identification)
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}
be two causally independent Rademacher random variables.
Consider two NPSEM-IE models associated with the DAG
W_{1}\to W_{2}
:

\displaystyle\text{Model 1}:\quad W_{1}(w)=E_{1},\quad W_{2}(w)=w_{1}+w_{1}E_{2},\quad Y(w)=w_{2}.

\displaystyle\text{Model 2}:\quad W_{1}(w)=E_{1},\quad W_{2}(w)=w_{1}+E_{2},\quad Y(w)=w_{2}.

It is not difficult to see that the observable random variables
W_{1},W_{2},Y
have the same distribution under these two models. However,
\xi_{Y}(W_{1})=1
in Model 1 and
\xi_{Y}(W_{1})=1/2
in Model 2 are not the same (these values can be directly computed from
Example

5
).

It is possible to bound counterfactual explainability using the causal DAG and distribution of
W
, but that is rather complex and we will take a simpler approach by assuming the basic potential outcomes are co-monotone (or rank-preserving in the terminology of
Hernan and Robins 2023
).

Definition 11

(Comonotonicity)
.

Fix an anchor
w
in the support of
W
. There exist non-decreasing functions
h_{k,w^{\prime}}
such that the event
W_{k}(w^{\prime})=h_{k,w^{\prime}}(W_{k}(w))
for all
k\in[K]
and
w^{\prime}
in the support of
W
holds with probability
1
.

This assumption essentially requires that any two basic potential outcomes are perfectly positively correlated given their marginal distributions. It is satisfied in the NPSEM-IE model if the noise is additive, i.e.
W_{k}=f_{k}(W_{\text{pa}(k)})+E_{k}
.

Comonotonicity allows us to determine the joint distribution of the basic potential outcomes using their marginal distributions, which can be obtained from the observable data distribution under a causal DAG model. Specifically, let
Q_{k}(e\mid w_{\pa(k)})
(
e\in[0,1]
) denote the quantile function of
W_{k}
given
W_{\pa(k)}=w_{\pa(k)}
. The observable data distribution can then be described by a sequence of conditional quantile transformations:

W_{k}=Q_{k}(E_{k}\mid W_{\pa(k)}),~E_{k}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\text{Unif}[0,1],~k=1,\dots,K.

(9)

Under comonotonicity, the joint distribution of the potential outcomes schedule is the same as that obtained by treating the above equations as structural, that is by assuming
W_{k}(w_{\pa(k)})=Q_{k}(E_{k}\mid w_{\pa(k)})
. Following
Example

9
, we can treat
E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{K})
as causally independent noise variables and
Y
as a function of
E
. This leads to the next identifiability result.

Proposition 12

(Identifiability under comonotonicity)
.

Suppose the input factors
W
follow a causal Markov model with respect to a DAG
\mathcal{G}
and the basic potential outcomes are comonotone. Then the explainability
\xi_{Y}\left(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}\right)
for any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
is identified by (
8
), and the entire explainability measure on the algebra
\mathcal{E}(W)
can subsequently be identified using probability calculus.

To actually compute explainability, the basic step is to evaluate the function
Y(E)
. This is simple: we can apply the conditional quantile transformations in (
9
) using a topological order of the DAG
\mathcal{G}
to compute
W=W(E)
and then evaluate
Y(W)
. From there, it is easy to approximate (
8
) using Monte Carlo by simulating many realizations of
E
and
E^{\prime}
. This is essentially the “pick-and-freeze” method in
Sobol 2001
; see also
Owen 2023
.

Thus far, we have assumed the conditional quantile functions in (
9
) are known. In practice, we can estimate them empirically using the data and then plug the estimates in (
8
). When
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
are known to be causally independent (so we are in the special case considered in
Section

2
), we can use the inverse of the empirical distribution function of each input factor. The resulting estimator of the explainability
\xi_{Y}\left(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k}\right)
(more precisely, its numerator
\mathsf{Var}\left(Y(W)-Y(W^{\prime}_{\mathcal{S}},W_{-\mathcal{S}})\right)
) is then a U-statistic
(
Hoeffding 1948
)
.

6
Discussion

Counterfactual explainability can be viewed as the extension of functional analysis of variance
(
Hoeffding 1948
)
and Sobol’s global sensitivity indices
(
Sobol 1993
)
to a causal setting. To see this, any function
f
of (probabilistically) independent variables
W_{1},\dots,W_{K}
can be decomposed as
f(W)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]}f_{\mathcal{S}}(W_{\mathcal{S}})
, where the terms can be obtained inductively via (let
f_{\emptyset}(w)=\mathbb{E}[f(W)]
)

f_{\mathcal{S}}(w_{\mathcal{S}})=\mathbb{E}\Big[f(W)-\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}}f_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(W)\mid W_{\mathcal{S}}=w_{\mathcal{S}}\Big],

and they are orthogonal in the sense that
\mathbb{E}[f_{\mathcal{S}}(W_{\mathcal{S}})f_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(W_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}})]=0
for all different
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq[K]
. Let the variance component of
\mathcal{S}
be defined as
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}:=\mathsf{Var}(f_{\mathcal{S}}(W_{\mathcal{S}}))
, so the total variance of
f(W)
can be decomposed as
\mathsf{Var}(f(W))=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]}\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
. This decomposition provides the basis for a variety of variable importance measures. For a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]
, Sobol’s lower and upper indices are defined, respectively, as

\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathsf{Var}(\mathbb{E}[f(W)\mid W_{\mathcal{S}}])=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}},\quad\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Var}(f(W)\mid W_{-\mathcal{S}})]=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset}\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}.

Furthermore, the superset importance is defined as
(
Hooker 2004
;
Liu and Owen 2006
)

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{S}}\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}.

Shapley’s value is an axiomatic way of attribution in cooperative games
(
Shapley 1953
)
. If we use Sobol’s lower index as the value of any subset
\mathcal{S}
, the Shapley value of
W_{k}
is given by
(
Owen 2014
)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K]\setminus\{k\}}\left(\binom{K-1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\right)^{-1}(\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}\cup\{k\}}-\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}})=\sum_{\{k\}\subseteq\cal S\subseteq[K]}\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}/|\mathcal{S}|.

The variance of any interaction term is distributed equally to the factors involved, and various extensions of Shapley’s value to incorporate non-zero interaction terms have been proposed
(
Grabisch and Roubens 1999
;
Rabitti and Borgonovo 2019
;
Sundararajan et al. 2020
)
.
One major limitation of these measures is that functional analysis of variance requires (probabilistically) independent input factors. Although there are extensions to the dependent case
(
Hooker 2007
;
Chastaing et al. 2012
;
Owen and Prieur 2017
, see e.g.)
, these variable importance measures do not behave as nicely as in the independent case. Moreover, our critique on associational variable importance measures from the Introduction still applies; recall that
Example

1
shows probabilistic independence of model inputs is not enough to give consistent explanations.

When the input factors are causally independent (so comonotonicity is trivially satisfied), our definition of total counterfactual explainability in (
1
) is point identified by Sobol’s upper index (after normalization), and our definition of interaction explanability in (
2
) and
Definition

4
is point identified by the superset importance (after normalization):

\xi(\vee_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=\frac{\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathsf{Var}(Y)},\quad\xi(\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})=\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathsf{Var}(Y)},~\text{for all}~\mathcal{S}\subseteq[K].

Moreover, counterfactual explanability of the irreducible elements of the explanation algebra is identified by the individual components in the functional analysis of variance:

\xi((\wedge_{k\in\mathcal{S}}W_{k})\wedge(\wedge_{k\notin\mathcal{S}}\neg W_{k}))=\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathsf{Var}(Y)}.

Thus, the explanation algebra help us unify several notions of variable importance. More importantly, our proposal can be seen as the extension of the classical approach by Sobol and others to a causal setting:

1.

We clarify that a successful application of functional analysis of variance to model explanation in the real world requires causal independence (rather than just probabilistic independence) of the inputs.

2.

We provide a simple recipe for explainability of multiple causally dependent inputs using the causal DAG theory.

These clarifications and extensions are made possible by using a counterfactual (rather than just a probabilistic or interventional) language for model explanation.

We briefly comment on some future research directions. As mentioned in
Section

4
, partial identification of counterfactual explainability naturally arises with causally dependent input factors. When
Y
is a real-world variable rather than a known function of the factors, this becomes an even more complex problem and may require tools from multi-marginal optimal transport
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
. It would also be useful to extend our approach from DAGs to graphs with bidirected edges
(
Pearl 2009
;
Richardson et al. 2023
)
to allow unmeasured confounders between the input factors.
