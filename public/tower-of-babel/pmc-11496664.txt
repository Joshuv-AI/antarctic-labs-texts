Source free domain adaptation for kidney and tumor image segmentation with wavelet style mining
Authors: Yuwei Yin, Zhixian Tang, Zheng Huang, Mingxuan Wang, Huachun Weng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKidney cancer is a serious malignant disease, and early diagnosis along with precise segmentation are crucial for effective treatment. However, due to the scarcity of labelled medical image data, the development of the intelligent diagnosis of kidney cancer is restricted. To address this challenge, we propose a novel unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) framework specifically designed for kidney and tumor CT image segmentation. Our framework consists of a generation phase and an adaptation phase. In the generation phase, we employ a wavelet-based style mining generator to create class-specific source-like images, facilitating domain alignment. In the adaptation phase, we introduce contrastive domain extraction and compact-aware domain consistency modules, enhancing feature-level and output-level adaptability through data augmentation techniques. Experimental results demonstrate that our method performs better in kidney and tumor segmentation tasks, exhibiting higher accuracy and generalization capability than state-of-the-art domain adaptation methods. This indicates that our approach has significant advantages in medical image segmentation for kidneys and tumors.Keywords: Deep learning, Unsupervised domain adaptation, Kidney diseaseSubject terms: Engineering, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

In recent years, the global incidence of kidney tumors has risen, with kidney cell carcinoma being the most common type, accounting for over 90% of all kidney cancer cases1. Early diagnosis and precise segmentation are crucial for improving treatment outcomes2. CT scans can provide detailed information about the size and location of the tumor, as well as any involvement of adjacent organs. It is a relatively reliable imaging method for diagnosing kidney cancer at present3. In addition, accurate segmentation of kidneys and tumors is crucial for surgical planning, computer-aided diagnosis, visual enhancement, and image-guided interventions4,5. However, challenges such as low tissue detail contrast in CT images and irregular shapes of the kidneys and tumors make accurate segmentation difficult6. Advanced artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms can effectively distinguish benign from malignant masses in CT scans, but these algorithms are often data-driven, especially by labelled data. Although transfer learning has solved the problem of the small amount of training data, the domain shift problem in kidney image segmentation still causes the model’s performance to remain poor7. Therefore, the domain shift problem should be solved when dealing with clinical issues like kidney tumor segmentation.

Domain shift is a pervasive and challenging issue in medical image analysis. This problem mainly arises from differences between data distributions, such as imaging equipment, scanning protocols, patient population diversity, and image noise and artifacts8. These differences will impact the generalization ability of the model on different data sets, thereby affecting its performance on unseen data. Researchers have proposed a variety of methods to deal with the above problems. Among them, Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) is the most classic type of solution8–10. Recently, some studies have explored source-free domain adaptation (SFDA) methods, which do not require access to source data but adapt pre-trained source models to the unlabeled target domain. These methods hold high practical value in medical image analysis.Therefore, we aim to achieve SFDA to bridge the distribution gap between different CT datasets, ensuring the segmentation model’s accuracy and generalization capability in new environments11.

The Fourier Visual Cueing (FVP) SFUDA method has been relatively successful in medical image processing. It works by incorporating low-frequency visual cues into the input target data, guiding the frozen pre-trained model to perform effectively in the target domain. However, FVP struggles with handling complex details and high-frequency information, leading to limited segmentation accuracy and stability. To address this issue, we propose a simple yet effective spectral transfer strategy based on stationary wavelet transform (SWT) for unsupervised domain adaptation in kidney CT image segmentation. The wavelet transform, with its superior time-frequency localization properties, is more effective in capturing local features and edge details in non-stationary signals. By decomposing and altering frequency components, we can stylize images without compromising semantic information, improving both image quality and adaptability. Additionally, we apply various image enhancement techniques, to further extract detailed features. These enhancements not only optimize the visual quality of the images but also improve the model’s ability to learn complex kidney image features, significantly boosting the accuracy and robustness of cross-domain analysis. We evaluate the proposed domain adaptation method on CT image segmentation tasks focues on kidneys and tumors, demonstrating superior performance compared to state-of-the-art SFDA methods.

Related work

Kidney image segmentation studies have mainly relied on supervised deep learning methods that require large amounts of labelled data and deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to achieve high-precision segmentation. For example, Sakib Mahmud12et al. proposed a novel Kidney Ultrasound Segmentation Network (KUSNet) for kidney segmentation from ultrasound images. They developed a Prenatal Hydrocephalus Classification Network (PHCNet) to classify the severity of hydrocephalus according to the Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) criteria. Patel13et al. proposed a 3D- Trans-Residual DenseUnet++ (3D-TR-DUnet++) network for segmenting normal and abnormal kidney regions. Chetana14et al. accurately segmented kidney structures and cysts in T2W MRI images by improving the deep learning models (especially UNet + + and UNet3+), which fulfils the accurate diagnosis of the urgent need of ADPKD. These improvements include combining residual staging, switch normalization, and cascade jump connectivity (CSC) to enhance the accuracy of feature fusion, extraction, and segmentation. However, applying supervised methods is limited by the costly and time-consuming acquisition of labelled data. For this reason, researchers have explored weakly supervised and unsupervised deep learning methods to achieve high-precision segmentation despite the scarcity of labelled data, reducing the cost and time investment.

Current research is gradually shifting towards the application of weakly supervised and unsupervised learning methods and unsupervised domain adaptation(UDA)techniques in kidney image segmentation. For example, Zeng15et al. proposed a reliable source approximation (RSA) method, which generates source-like images guided by different edges of the target image through a conditional diffusion model, predicts and refines the pseudo-labels using an uncertainty estimation module, and then updates the model to adapt to the domain bias. Jin16et al. proposed prior knowledge-driven domain adaptation and dual-domain augmented self-corrective learning schemes. They proposed a domain adaptation-based self-correcting model (DASC-Net) for COVID-19 infection segmentation of CT images. Xu17et al. proposed an enhanced information maximization (IM-DCL) approach, which learns the query set through a transduction mechanism and introduces distance-aware contrast learning (DCL) to optimize the decision boundaries for the target task. Fan18 et al. proposed a novel probabilistic-aware weakly supervised learning pipeline that integrates three innovative components: probabilistic-based pseudo-label generation technique for synthesizing dense segmentation masks from sparse annotations; probabilistic multi-head self-attention network for robust feature extraction in probabilistic converter networks; and probabilistic-informed segmentation loss function for enhanced training of annotation confidence. These components are used to segment 3D medical images Bhadra19et al. proposed a weakly supervised learning method using an intensity prior generated edema segmentation as a pseudo-label and pseudo-labels of muscle, subcutaneous, and visceral adipose tissues as a context for finer segmentation of abdominal CT images. Zhang and Yang20,21 et al. proposed a method for segmenting abdominal images based on a weakly supervised learning method validated in multiple real datasets validated on a weak-to-strong consistency framework for glomerular segmentation semi-supervised learning method and a method based on unsupervised colouring enhancement for segmentation of glomerular instances, respectively. Liu8et al. proposed an unsupervised domain adaptive method for abdominal organ segmentation by adapting the target and source image spectral components through spectral shifting strategy and histogram matching.

Although some domain adaptation techniques (Wen22et al., Kollias23et al., Wang24 et al.) have yielded significant results in other medical image domains, they have not yet been thoroughly investigated in kidney image segmentation, reflecting the fact that their potential for application in the field of medical image analysis still needs to be explored. This paper uses the domain adaptation method for kidney CT image segmentation. Different from the process that only uses the source model as a pre-trained model to generate pseudo-labels, we utilize the BatchNorm statistic in the source model to create source-like images, consider the denseness of lesion regions and organs, and introduce the shape denseness metric to re-weight the pseudo-labels.

Methodology

This paper proposes an unsupervised source-free domain adaptation (SFDA) framework for kidney image segmentation based on wavelet transform, which aims to enhance the adaptability of the source model to the target domain. The entire framework is divided into two main phases: generation for generating class source images and self-adaptation. The specific process is shown in Fig. 1.

The proposed SFDA framework for kidney and tumor segmentation. The first part of the generation phase consists of the following two parts: (a) Coarse generation by BN constraints (b) Fine generation by mutual wavelet transform. the second part of the adaptation phase consists of the following three parts: (c) Histogram match (d) contrast domain distillation (e) compact aware domain consistency.

In the generation stage, as shown in Fig. 1(a), the original target image and Gaussian noise image are transmitted to a fixed frozen source model for processing. This is a well-trained model from the source-domain image, featuring stylized characteristics of the source-domain data. Then, rough source-like images are generated by Batch Normalization (BN) constraints, and the input noisy images are optimized by source content loss and source style loss and the corresponding features are learned. Subsequently, the target image and the coarse-generation image are meticulously reconstructed by the inverse transform of the wavelet transform to obtain a more refined source-like image , as shown in Fig. 1(b). This process can capture the local features and edge information of the image more effectively, thus improving the quality of the image. The goal is to improve the generalization ability and robustness of the model in the new domain. CDD module reduces the difference between the class source and target domain images by pairing them with a “pull” mechanism through a contrast loss . Wavelet mining generation stage

In order to fully utilize the pre-trained models on the source domain, we introduce a wavelet transform-based mining feature generator that generates high-quality source-like images (WSMs) by means of BN constraints and the mutual wavelet transform (FWT). The generator consists of two steps: coarse generation based on BN constraints and fine generation based on mutual wavelet transform.

BN constraint-based image coarse generation stage

In the coarse generation stage, the style loss and content loss are used to optimize the noisy image , which generates the coarse source-like image . Wavelet transform-based image fine-grained generation stage

As shown in Fig. 1(a), although the classic BN constraint method can capture the style of the source image, it often leads to unnecessary blurring of the image texture information. The wavelet transform can effectively separate and analyze the multi-scale frequency components of an image, providing us with a new way of image refinement.

A wavelet transform is first applied to the generated rough image and the target image , a process that can be observed in the first and second rows of Fig. 1(b). In the second level of wavelet transform, is further decomposed into and the corresponding detail components , , . The detail components at each level capture the texture information in different directions in the original image, while the approximation components capture the overall structure of the image. Then, we define a binary mask with a value of 1 in the center region and 0 in the other regions, and control the scale of the exchange operation through it. The exchange range of the low-frequency components is controlled through the parameter β. The operation is performed by replacing the low-frequency amplitudes of the generated image with the corresponding components of the target image in the low-frequency region defined by the mask . Eventually, the adjusted amplitude components are combined with the phase components of the target image, and these components are reconstructed back into the RGB space by inverse wavelet transform. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the image generated by this method is rich in details, the quality is significantly improved, the source pattern is preserved and the texture information is clear, and the gap between the source and target domains is effectively reduced.

Adaptation stage

Using the high-quality class source images generated in the generation phase, we feed the paired class source images together with the target samples into the segmentation model to learn the domain invariant representation by distributional alignment. To further refine the distribution alignment process, we introduce a histogram matching technique to optimize the feature-level alignment. Histogram matching is responsible for adjusting the intensity distribution of to better match the distribution of , thus reducing the pixel-level differences between the source and target images. Specifically, in addition to the proposed CDD and CADC modules for feature-level and output-level alignment, we also apply histogram matching before these two modules to ensure that the class source images entering the segmentation model are closer to the target image in terms of statistical properties.

Histogram matching stage

The class source image and the target sample are first collected and then both are preprocessed by histogram matching technique. The flow of data for histogram matching begins with collecting luminance information for both image types, which in turn computes their histograms, which reflect the distribution of pixels at each luminance level. Subsequently, to match the two histograms, their cumulative distribution function (CDF) is computed, which provides a cumulative probability for each possible luminance value.

The combined application of these steps strengthens the model’s ability to generalize to the target domain and provides a new avenue for unsupervised cross-domain learning.

Compact-aware domain consistency(CADC)

Since the target image and the generated class source image share the same semantics but have different style information, they should produce similar segmentation results. Therefore, the CADC module is introduced to apply consistency learning to and to achieve output-level adaptation as shown in Fig. 1d.

First, the class source image is passed to the source model to generate pseudo-labels . These pseudo-labels represent segmentation predictions of the content of the class source image. Then, we use a consistency loss function to compare the segmentation predictions of the target and source-like images, forcing the model to produce similar segmentation results for images with the same structure but different styles. Doing so allows the segmentation model to learn domain-invariant features by focusing on the learning of image content and ignoring stylistic differences. However, considering that pseudo-labels may contain noise, we introduce a shape tightness metric to assess the reliability of pseudo-labels and reweight the consistency loss according to it to mitigate the effect of noisy pseudo-labels. Experiments and results

Datasets

We conducted comprehensive experiments in kidney and tumors to evaluate the proposed wavelet style mining-guided SFDA improvement framework, which covers cross-center domain scenarios. Specifically, cross-center SFDA experiments are performed on CT images for kidney and tumor segmentation.

Kidney and tumor segmentation

Cross-center adaptation was performed using the publicly available kidney screening CT datasets KiTS23 and KIPA22. The KiTS23 data included patients who underwent cryoablation, partial nephrectomy, or radical nephrectomy for suspected kidney malignancy at M Health Fairview Medical Center between 2010 and 2022. A retrospective review of these cases was performed to identify all patients with contrast-enhanced preoperative CT scans, including all kidneys. Each case’s most recent contrast-enhanced preoperative scan (at the corticomedullary or nephrogenic stage) was segmented for each instance of the following semantic categories. Kidney: includes all parenchyma and non-fatty tissue within the hilum. Tumor: a mass found in the kidney suspected of being malignant preoperatively.

The KIPA2022 raw images are unenhanced abdominal CT images from 130 patients. The images were first labeled by proven algorithms and then adjusted by 3 different experts. Each image was acquired on a Siemens dual-source 64-slice CT scanner with contrast agent injected during CT image acquisition.The X-ray tube current, convolution kernel, exposure time, and voltage were 480 mA, B25f, 500 ms, and 120 KV, respectively.The kidney tumor types included clear kidney cell carcinoma, papillary carcinoma, smoky cell carcinoma, vascular smooth muscle lipoma, and eosinophilic adenoma. Kidney sizes ranged from 73.73 ml to 263.02 ml, and tumor sizes ranged from 2.06 ml to 646.68 ml. We selected 30 CT images from each of the two data sets and used 12,782 axial slices from the KIPA22 dataset as the source domain and 15,533 axial slices from the KiTS23 dataset as the target domain, respectively, and randomly divided them into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Data enhancements such as random cropping, rotation and luminance were used for source domain training and target domain adaptation. For training and testing, we resized all images to 256 × 256.

Implementation details and evaluation metrics

The segmentation models for the KiTS23 and KIPA22 datasets are based on Deeplab-v225, and the ResNet-10126backbone is pretrained on ImageNet27. We conducted experiments on NVIDIA 3090 Ti GPUs using the PyTorch library. To obtain the source model, we first trained the segmentation network on 70 instances of data (29,500 slices) from the labeled source data KIPA22 dataset, with a batch size of 16 for 200 epochs.Adam was chosen for optimization with a learning rate of 1e-4. In the generation phase, we passed the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-2 Optimization is performed on the input noisy image for 100 epochs. In the adaptive phase, the segmentation model is trained on the source domain and target images with a batch size of 8 for 200 periods and optimized by SGD with a learning rate of 5e-4. Considering that the convolutional layer is not rotationally invariant and the segmentation model requires rotational invariance, we apply a random degree of rotation of the image as a data augmentation strategy in the CCD module and regularize the segmentation model to be rotationally consistent. The configuration of hyperparameters in this framework includesβ = 5e-3, = 1, = 0.5, n = 2, = 0.01 and = 0.1.

Conclusion

We propose a new wavelet mining-based SFDA framework that effectively handles the SFDA problem of kidney and kidney tumor image segmentation for the first time. First, we seamlessly transfer the knowledge from the source domain to the target domain via a pre-trained source model and design a WSM generator to generate source-like images from the source model. A histogram matching module is introduced to enhance the quality of the source-like images further before the feature and output-level distribution alignment is performed. Subsequently, distribution alignment was achieved through the CDD and CADC modules. We have conducted extensive experiments in cross-center and cross-device kidney and tumor image segmentation tasks, and the results show that this method significantly outperforms existing techniques.

Despite the promising segmentation results, our method remains challenging regarding the accurate segmentation of small and irregular shapes, and further improvements are needed to achieve finer segmentation. In addition, clinical feasibility has not been fully established, so we emphasize the importance of rigorous validation based on clinical criteria and collaboration with medical experts during tool development. Future research will aim to enhance the clinical reliability of the model and explore the potential application of this strategy in other medical image modalities and natural image domain adaptation.
