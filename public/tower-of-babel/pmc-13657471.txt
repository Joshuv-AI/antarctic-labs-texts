Sialadenectomy for management of a large submandibular sialolith using an extraoral approach: a case report
Authors: Adham Alemdar, Haqi Ismail Ibrahim, Yamen Nohily, Ziad Albash, Abdulkarim Khalil, Wajih Kashkash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Submandibular sialolithiasis is a common condition that can cause significant discomfort and complications if left untreated. Treatment strategies depend largely on the size and location of the sialolith, with various surgical and nonsurgical techniques available.Case presentation:This report describes the successful management of a large sialolith in the submandibular gland via sialadenectomy using an extraoral approach. The case highlights the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic strategy for managing large sialoliths and demonstrates the efficacy of sialadenectomy in carefully selected patients.Conclusion:The extraoral approach using a Risdon incision provided excellent exposure of the submandibular gland and enabled safe removal of the large sialolith. Further research is warranted to refine treatment algorithms for sialolithiasis and optimize patient outcomes.

Introduction

Sialolithiasis is the second most common disease of the salivary glands after mumps[1]. It affects approximately 12 per 1000 adults, with a higher prevalence in males[2]. Salivary calculi consist of an initial nidus surrounded by laminated layers of organic (18%) and inorganic (82%) substances[4]. They commonly measure between 1 and 10 mm, while giant calculi exceeding 15 mm are very rare[5].

Diagnosis is based on clinical symptoms and radiographic imaging. Typical symptoms include recurrent pain and swelling, particularly during meals (mealtime syndrome). Complete ductal obstruction may lead to persistent symptoms and secondary infection of the affected gland. Bimanual palpation of the floor of the mouth is a useful diagnostic maneuver[1].

Although most salivary stones are radiopaque, up to 20% may be radiolucent; therefore, plain radiography alone is insufficient. Additional imaging modalities include computed tomography (CT), sialography, and ultrasonography[1].

This paper presents a case involving the removal of a large sialolith via an extraoral approach, reported in compliance with the SCARE 2020 criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 55-year-old male patient presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at a university hospital with the chief complaints of swelling on the left side of the floor of the mouth and pain during meals for approximately 3 years. Approximately 1 year prior to presentation, the patient had undergone lithotripsy elsewhere, with no significant relief. He reported no breathing difficulties, fever, or other relevant medical history. His general health was good, with no systemic diseases, hereditary conditions, congenital anomalies, or syndromes, and he was not taking any regular medications. There was no family history of radicular cysts.

Clinical findings

Physical examination revealed a visible swelling in the anterior part of the left floor of the mouth. Bimanual palpation detected a large, hard mass along Wharton’s duct. The left submandibular gland was enlarged, tender, and painful upon palpation.

Diagnostic assessment

A CT scan demonstrated a radiopaque mass in the left floor of the mouth within the submandibular gland region (Fig. 1). The CT images showed the sialolith measuring 15.7 mm and defining its precise location. A diagnosis of left submandibular sialolithiasis was made. Timeline of the case

Three years before presentation: The onset of intermittent pain and swelling occurred during meals.

One year before presentation: Lithotripsy was performed elsewhere, with partial, temporary relief.

Day of the presentation: Clinical and CT diagnosis of a large left submandibular sialolith.

Day of surgery: Uneventful sialadenectomy via the Risdon approach.

Postoperative follow-up (6 weeks): There was complete resolution of symptoms, no recurrence, a good cosmetic outcome, and normal marginal mandibular nerve function.

Discussion

Sialolithiasis is a pathological condition resulting from obstruction of a salivary gland or its duct by a calculus, which forms through deposition of tricalcium phosphate salts around an initial nidus[7]. Submandibular sialolithiasis can lead to considerable discomfort and complications if untreated.

The primary goal of management is to preserve the gland and restore its function[8]. Treatment decisions depend largely on stone size. While conservative measures such as gland massage and sialagogues may be tried initially, large sialoliths often require more invasive interventions.

When conservative measures fail, surgical options such as sialadenectomy may be necessary[8]. In this case, the decision to perform sialadenectomy was based on the large stone size (15.7 mm), its location within the gland parenchyma, the patient’s persistent symptoms, and the risk of recurrent sialadenitis.

The extraoral approach via a Risdon incision provided excellent exposure of the submandibular gland and facilitated safe, complete removal of the large sialolith. This approach also allowed thorough exploration of the gland, reducing the risk of recurrence[9,10]. Furthermore, it provided good cosmetic outcomes and minimized the risk of injury to the marginal mandibular nerve[9,10].

It is important to acknowledge alternative treatment modalities for sialolithiasis. Nonsurgical and minimally invasive options include sialendoscopy with stone retrieval, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, and sialendoscopy-guided laser lithotripsy. These techniques aim to fragment or extract the stone without gland removal and are viable in selected cases[11]. However, for large, parenchymal, or recurrent stones, sialadenectomy remains a definitive and effective solution[8,11].

The choice of treatment should be individualized based on stone size and location, symptom severity, patient preference, and available institutional expertise. While less invasive approaches are preferable when feasible to preserve salivary function, sialadenectomy offers high success rates and durable symptom resolution for large or complex sialoliths not amenable to other techniques.

Limitations

As a single case report, the findings are not generalizable. Long-term follow-up and comparative studies are needed to further define optimal treatment algorithms.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates the successful management of a large submandibular sialolith using sialadenectomy via an extraoral approach. By considering available treatment options and tailoring the approach to the specific clinical scenario, clinicians can effectively manage sialolithiasis and provide optimal patient care. Further research and comparative studies are warranted to refine treatment algorithms based on individual patient and stone characteristics.
