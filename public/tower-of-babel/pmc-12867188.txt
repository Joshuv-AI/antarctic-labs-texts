A Comparative Analysis of Triceps-Splitting Versus Paratricipital Surgical Approaches in The Management of Extra-Articular Distal Humerus Fractures: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Mojtaba Baniasadi, Khatere Mokhtari, Hasan Rezaei, Pouria Tabrizian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Extra-articular distal humerus fractures (EDHFs) are common in both younger and older adults, presenting clinical challenges related to fracture complexity and patient comorbidities. This study compares the triceps-splitting and paratricipital surgical approaches to determine which method optimizes clinical outcomes over a 6-month follow-up period.Materials and Methods:In this prospective cohort study, 120 patients with radiographically confirmed EDHF were assigned to undergo either the triceps-splitting or paratricipital approach. Each patient was assessed at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively for surgical site infection, nerve injury, pain (using a 10-point visual analog scale), and range of motion (using a universal goniometer). Data were analyzed via Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal–Wallis test.Results:Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, and hospital stay, did not differ significantly between groups. At 6 months, patients who underwent the paratricipital approach reported significantly lower pain levels and demonstrated fewer limitations in extension compared to the Triceps-Splitting group. While infection and nerve injury rates were low and similar in both cohorts, the paratricipital approach showed a lower necessity for reoperation and reduced postoperative complications.Conclusions:Both the triceps-splitting and paratricipital approaches effectively manage EDHF. However, the paratricipital technique may confer advantages in terms of pain reduction, triceps function preservation, and lower reoperation rates. Surgeons should consider individual patient factors and fracture characteristics when selecting the optimal surgical approach.

INTRODUCTION

Extra-articular distal humerus fractures (EDHFs) represent a prevalent and complex category of fractures occurring in the elbow region.[1] These fractures occur due to various reasons, including direct causes such as occupational injuries,[2] elbow impacts from accidents,[3] and high-energy trauma.[4] Across various age groups, including indirect factors such as the transmission of force from the arm bone to the elbow joint resulting from falls and hand pulls, particularly in the elderly.[5] The EDHFs account for 2% of all fractures and almost one-third of all adult arm fractures.[6] Additionally, the annual incidence rate of these fractures is 7.5 per 100,000 individuals.[7] Nevertheless, it is anticipated that the incidence rate of these fractures will progressively increase due to the aging population and the rising use of transportation.[8]

Various classification systems, including SOFCOT, AO/OTA, and Dubberley, have been developed to categorize distal humerus fractures according to the affected arm segments and fracture patterns.[1] Among these, the AO/OTA classification system is the most prevalent and extensively utilized. It employs computed tomography (CT) imaging to provide a detailed description of fracture localization.[9] This classification system categorizes EDHF into three main groups: A, B, and C, with each group further subdivided into three distinct subgroups. Group A encompasses extra-articular fractures, Group B includes partial articular fractures, and Group C comprises complete articular fractures.[1011] Extra-articular arm fractures account for 16% of all arm fractures and 3% of arm fractures in adults.[12] The management of these fractures is particularly challenging due to factors such as the small size of the distal segment, comminution of the surrounding bones, and the potential presence of osteoporosis in elderly patients.[13]

Various conservative and nonconservative treatment methods have been introduced for these fractures.[14] Conservative treatment methods, such as BABP plaster casting, are more frequently utilized in younger individuals. However, this approach is associated with complications, including joint stiffness.[15] Surgical intervention is regarded as the primary and most effective treatment for these fractures, with conservative methods serving a supplementary role, especially in older adults.[16] Nonsurgical treatments appear to be effective primarily for nondisplaced fractures or as a provisional initial management in elderly individuals before arthroplasty.[17]

In recent years, a range of surgical approaches has been introduced for the management of EDHF. Each fracture type necessitates a specific approach that facilitates adequate exposure of the fracture site and, when necessary, provides access to the joint for comprehensive treatment.[18] Surgical approaches such as olecranon osteotomy, triceps-splitting, triceps-sparing, triceps-lifting, and posterior paratricipital techniques are among the most frequently employed methods for managing EDHF.[19] The choice of the optimal surgical approach is contingent upon factors including the mechanism and type of fracture, the specific fracture pattern, and the surgeon’s expertise.[20] Ultimately, the treatment outcomes for these fractures depend on the success rate in joint reconstruction, fixation stability, and postoperative recovery.[21] Therefore, the most important determinant of successful treatment is selecting the appropriate treatment method.

Given the importance of the EDHF and their increasing incidence due to demographic changes and high-energy traumas.[8] A comprehensive comparative study is necessary to determine the best treatment approach. Considering the various treatment approaches available for these types of fractures,[14] each has its own specific advantages and disadvantages. Choosing the appropriate surgical approach for EDHF is crucial, as it can significantly impact treatment outcomes and the incidence of complications. This study aims to identify the optimal method by comparing the efficacy of the triceps-splitting and paratricipital approaches in treating EDHF. By evaluating key outcomes such as surgical site infection, pain levels, nerve injury, and range of motion (ROM), this research seeks to determine which technique offers superior clinical benefits. Given the lack of comprehensive comparative studies in this area, our study addresses this gap and aims to provide valuable insights that will guide orthopedic surgeons in selecting the most appropriate and effective approach. The findings will serve as a reference for optimizing surgical strategies, ultimately improving patient care and recovery outcomes in this diverse age group.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This prospective cohort study was conducted at Kashani Hospital in 2023, involving 120 patients with distal humerus fractures. Demographic information (age, duration of hospitalization, and gender) was extracted from patient records. Patients meeting the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled and divided into two surgical approach groups: Triceps-Splitting or Paratricipital. The choice of surgical approach was guided by the attending surgeon’s assessment of fracture characteristics and clinical judgment. Surgical procedures were then performed according to the selected approach.

All patients were monitored and reassessed at 3 and 6 months postoperatively under the supervision of an orthopedic specialist. During each follow-up visit, a single orthopedic resident under senior supervision assessed surgical site infections, nerve injuries, ROM (using a universal goniometer), and pain (via a 10-point visual analog scale). Any signs of infection were confirmed by laboratory tests where appropriate, and nerve injuries were evaluated through both clinical examinations and electromyography if needed.

Inclusion criteria encompassed adult patients (≥18 years old) with radiographically confirmed EDHF who were medically stable and consented to participate. Exclusion criteria included intra-articular fractures, severe chronic conditions (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes or immunocompromising disorders), active infection, concurrent fractures in the ipsilateral upper limb, or fractures requiring surgical methods other than triceps-splitting or paratricipital as determined by the treating physician. Patients withdrawing from the study or failing to complete the 6-month follow-up were also excluded.

In consultation with a statistician, a power analysis was performed to determine the required sample size. Using a two-tailed significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% and assuming a minimum clinically significant difference of 15% in primary outcomes (e.g., pain and ROM) between the two surgical approaches, it was calculated that 60 patients per group would be necessary, for a total of 120 patients.

Surgical procedures

In the paratricipital surgical approach, the patient is positioned in the supine position and placed under general anesthesia. A sterile pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the upper arm following Esmarch exsanguination. A longitudinal incision is made along the midline, extending through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia, while carefully preserving the ulnar nerve from the incision site. The long head of the triceps muscle is then exteriorized from the intermuscular septum separating the muscle compartments. Dissection along this septum facilitates lateral movement of the triceps muscle without compromising the extensor mechanism. The lateral head of the triceps muscle is longitudinally separated along its fibers adjacent to the triceps tendon, ensuring preservation of the central extensor mechanism. Distally, the anconeus muscle origin from the lateral column is released, but the continuity between the lateral head of the triceps and the anconeus muscle is maintained to avoid nerve injury. If necessary, fractures are managed with osteosynthesis reduction, initially adjusted with a pin and subsequently stabilized with a plate and screws. Postoperatively, patients are instructed to use a sling for 15 days, during which only passive elbow movements are allowed. This is followed by a regimen of active and progressive shoulder and elbow exercises. Resistance exercises are then introduced based on individual patient needs and recovery progress.[22]

In the triceps-splitting surgical approach, the interval between the long and lateral heads of the triceps muscle is carefully dissected to separately expose the medial head. The medial head, originating from the distal groove and radial nerve, is then separated along a line parallel to its muscle fibers. This distal split is extended over the olecranon in a subperiosteal manner, preserving the full thickness of the internal and lateral fascial sleeves, thereby maintaining continuity with the flexor carpi ulnaris and anconeus muscles. Following the procedure, the patient’s elbow is immobilized using a bulky dressing and maintained at 90 degrees of flexion. Patients are scheduled for follow-up visits 2–3 weeks postoperatively for suture removal and wound assessment. Subsequent follow-ups include clinical examination and radiographic evaluation, with postoperative physiotherapy protocols determined by the treating surgeon.[23]

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using both descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, and frequency tables. Inferential statistics comprised the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal–Wallis test. Data normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, revealing non-normal distributions for age and length of hospitalization. Consequently, nonparametric tests were employed for these variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Among the 120 participants, 65% of the patients in the Triceps-Splitting group and 68.3% in the Paratricipital group were male. The average ages of patients in the Paratricipital and Triceps-Splitting groups were 24.17 ± 34.98 and 17.69 ± 37.57 years, respectively, while the average durations of hospitalization were 1.50 ± 3.40 and 2.10 ± 3.38 days, respectively. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the groups regarding age, duration of hospitalization, or gender (P > 0.05) [Table 1].

The predominant mechanism of injury varied between groups: in the Paratricipital group, 40% of injuries resulted from accidents and 38.3% from falls on the same level, whereas in the Triceps-Splitting group, 40% were due to falls from height and 31.7% from accidents. Reoperation was required for 5% of patients in the Paratricipital group and 13.3% in the Triceps-Splitting group. Specifically, in the Paratricipital group, the 5% reoperation rate reflected two cases of symptomatic hardware irritation and one case of fixation failure. In the Triceps-Splitting group, which had a 13.3% reoperation rate, three patients underwent surgery for hardware prominence, four for fixation instability, and one for postoperative infection requiring debridement. Additionally, infection rates were 6.7% in the Paratricipital group and 5% in the Triceps-Splitting group. In this study, infections were classified as superficial (involving only skin and subcutaneous tissue) or deep (extending beneath the fascia or involving bone). The one infection in the Triceps-Splitting group was deep, requiring surgical debridement and intravenous antibiotics, while the remaining infections in both groups were superficial and managed successfully with local wound care and short-course oral antibiotics. Chi-square tests indicated no significant differences between the groups in terms of injury mechanism, need for reoperation, or infection rates (P > 0.05). Pain levels were significantly higher in the Triceps-Splitting group, with 23.3% of patients reporting pain compared to 8.3% in the Paratricipital group (P < 0.05) [Table 2].

A comparative analysis of nerve damage and motion limitation between the paratricipital and triceps-splitting approaches revealed that over 90% of cases in the paratricipital approach exhibited normal outcomes (absence of nerve damage and motion limitations). The triceps-splitting approach also showed a similar percentage of normal cases but was associated with a lower incidence of nerve damage, particularly affecting the ulnar and radial nerves (approximately 5%). The triceps-splitting approach exhibited a higher level of extension motion limitation (around 10%) and a lower level of flexion motion limitation (<5%).

DISCUSSION

This study compared the triceps‐splitting and paratricipital surgical approaches in managing EDHFs, with a focus on postoperative pain, ROM deficits, nerve injuries, reoperation rates, and infection risks. Our results indicate that although both techniques provide effective fracture stabilization, there are significant differences in clinical outcomes that warrant consideration in surgical decision-making. The management of EDHF remains challenging despite recent therapeutic advances.[24]

One of the most significant findings was that postoperative pain was markedly higher in the Triceps-Splitting group compared to the Paratricipital group. The more invasive nature of the triceps-splitting approach—which involves direct incision through the triceps brachii—may explain the increased bleeding and hematoma formation, ultimately leading to greater postoperative discomfort.[23]

This observation is consistent with previous research indicating that triceps-splitting techniques can exacerbate local inflammation due to extensive soft tissue disruption.[25]

Another key outcome was the need for reoperation, which was 5% in the Paratricipital group and 13.3% in the Triceps-Splitting group. Specifically, in the Paratricipital group, reoperations were necessary for symptomatic hardware irritation (two cases) and fixation failure (one case), whereas in the Triceps-Splitting group, there were three cases of hardware prominence, four cases of fixation instability, and one deep infection requiring debridement. This discrepancy suggests that the preservation of soft tissues in the paratricipital approach may reduce postoperative complications, aligning with the findings of Patino et al.,[22] who reported minimal reoperations using a similar extensor mechanism–sparing technique.

ROM limitations also differed notably between the two approaches. Extension-type deficits predominated in the Triceps-Splitting group, while the Paratricipital group presented primarily with flexion restrictions. This outcome may be attributed to the disruption of the triceps mechanism in the triceps-splitting technique, leading to postoperative fibrosis and muscle weakness. Conversely, preserving triceps continuity in the paratricipital approach appears to safeguard elbow extension. Similar findings were reported by Metwally et al.,[27] who observed more pronounced extension deficits in patients undergoing triceps-splitting procedures.

Although nerve injuries did not significantly differ between groups, there was a slight tendency for the Paratricipital group to have more radial nerve injuries, while the Triceps-Splitting group exhibited a near-equal distribution of radial and ulnar involvement. Importantly, these nerve injuries did not substantially affect final outcomes, mirroring previous observations that, when carefully performed, both approaches can yield comparable nerve safety profiles.[28]

In contrast to these differences in pain, reoperation, and ROM, infection rates remained low and were not significantly different between groups. Consistent with other studies, superficial infections were managed with local wound care and oral antibiotics, while one deep infection in the Triceps-Splitting group required surgical debridement and intravenous antibiotics.[21]

Demographic characteristics—including age, gender, and length of hospital stay—were balanced between the two groups (as described in the Methods section), ensuring that these factors did not substantially bias the comparison of outcomes. Although the majority of participants were younger males (average age around 35 years), which is common in high-energy trauma, past investigations have noted that individual characteristics such as age and gender can influence fracture incidence and treatment outcomes.[2930] Nevertheless, in our study, these demographic factors did not significantly affect reoperation, infection, or nerve injury rates.

Despite these meaningful observations, our investigation is limited by a relatively small sample size and a 6-month follow-up period, which may not capture long-term functional recovery, including late-onset complications or strength deficits. Additionally, patients with chronic illnesses (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, immunocompromised states) were excluded, which may limit generalizability to more diverse populations. Future larger-scale cohort studies with extended follow-up would provide further insight into these techniques’ long-term efficacy and potential complications.

CONCLUSIONS

Both the triceps-splitting and paratricipital approaches proved effective for EDHF stabilization. However, higher postoperative pain, more extensive extension deficits, and increased reoperation rates were noted in the Triceps-Splitting group. Conversely, the paratricipital technique exhibited better triceps preservation, fewer complications, and may be preferable in most scenarios requiring soft-tissue conservation. Ultimately, fracture characteristics, surgeon expertise, and patient-specific factors should guide the choice of surgical approach, potentially optimizing functional outcomes in this challenging fracture pattern.
