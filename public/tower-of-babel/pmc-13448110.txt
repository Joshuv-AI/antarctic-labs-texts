Biological activity of Pleurotus eryngii based on antioxidant, anticholinesterase and antiproliferative effects using optimized extraction methods
Authors: Ayşenur Gürgen, Mustafa Sevindik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to evaluate the antioxidant potential, anticholinesterase activity, antiproliferative effects, and phenolic contents of optimized extracts derived from Pleurotus eryngii. Single-objective and multi-objective optimized extracts of P. eryngii were analyzed using antioxidant assays (TAS, TOS, DPPH, FRAP, and OSI), anticholinesterase activity (AChE and BChE inhibition), and antiproliferative activity against the A549 lung cancer cell line. Additionally, phenolic compositions were determined via LC-MS/MS analysis under optimized extraction conditions. The single-objective extract exhibited higher TAS values (7.212 ± 0.060 mmol/L) compared to the multi-extract (5.682 ± 0.054 mmol/L), while the multi-objective extract demonstrated higher TOS and OSI values. Both extracts showed significant anticholinesterase activities, with IC₅₀ values indicating stronger inhibition compared to previously reported studies. The single-objective extract displayed superior antiproliferative activity against A549 cells, and both extracts showed dose-dependent effects. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that phenolic compounds such as gallic acid, caffeic acid, and protocatechuic acid were more abundant in the single-objective extract. The optimized extracts of P. eryngii demonstrated substantial antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative activities, with the single-objective extract showing enhanced bioactivity compared to the multi-objective extract. While P. eryngii presents a promising natural source of antioxidants and enzyme inhibitors, its relatively high oxidant production potential suggests that further toxicological evaluation is required to assess its safe use. Further studies are recommended to enhance extraction methods and explore molecular mechanisms underlying its therapeutic properties.Keywords: Antioxidant, Anticancer, Phenolic content, Medicinal mushroom, OptimizationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Mushrooms are rich natural sources of biologically active compounds that have been widely used throughout human history for nutritional and medical purposes. In recent years, the antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and immunomodulatory effects of mushrooms attract the attention of the scientific world and industry, and increase studies aimed at understanding the potential of these organisms in the field of health1,2. Various natural metabolites such as phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, terpenoids and sterols underlie the biological activities of mushrooms. The discovery of these components offers new opportunities in the production of natural medicines and functional foods3. In order to fully evaluate these biological effects of mushrooms, optimizing the chemical content of their extracts is of great importance. Optimizing extraction methods ensures a high yield of bioactive compounds while improving their stability and bioavailability, thereby enhancing the potential applications of mushrooms4. Determining the correct extraction parameters both increases the effectiveness of antioxidant compounds such as phenolic compounds and allows a better understanding of the positive effects of these compounds on health5. In addition, optimized extraction processes make an important contribution not only to the evaluation of biological activities but also to the development of environmentally friendly and sustainable production methods. Today, optimization of mushroom extracts is a basis for the development of innovative products in the food, pharmaceutical, cosmetic and agricultural industries. In this context, the present study focuses on evaluating the biological activities of P. eryngii extracts in relation to optimized extraction conditions4,6. In this study, the biological activities of P. eryngii extracts and the properties of bioactive components affecting these activities were discussed, also, the effect of optimization of extraction processes on biological activity was investigated in detail. P. eryngii is a mushroom species also known by different names such as king trumpet mushroom, horn mushroom, eryngi, king oyster mushroom, king brown mushroom, steppe mushroom, trumpet royale and aliʻi oyster7. This mushroom, which is native to the Mediterranean region, the Middle East and North Africa, is also successfully cultivated in many regions of Asia. P. eryngii, which has a long shelf life, is a frequently produced commercial mushroom. Having a mild aroma in its raw form, this mushroom gains a distinct umami flavor and meaty texture when cooked. P. eryngii has gained an important place in the edible mushroom market in Korea, especially since its introduction in 1995, accounting for approximately 30% of the market. This mushroom is often used as a meat substitute in vegan recipes. It is also stated that it may contain immune-supporting compounds and may function as a cholesterol-lowering dietary substance8,9. In this context, the main hypothesis of the present study is that optimization of extraction parameters using artificial intelligence-based approaches (ANN and GA/NSGA-II) significantly enhances the biological activities of P. eryngii extracts compared to non-optimized or conventionally obtained extracts. Furthermore, the novelty of this study lies in the integrated application of both single-objective and multi-objective optimization strategies to simultaneously evaluate antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative activities, together with detailed phenolic profiling using LC-MS/MS. Unlike previous studies, this work provides a comprehensive and comparative framework linking optimization conditions with multiple biological activities, thereby offering a more holistic understanding of the bioactive potential of P. eryngii.

Materials and methods

Mushroom specimens used in this research were collected from Antalya province, Türkiye, in 2021. Following collection, the fungi were transported to the lab, thinly sliced and dried for approximately six hours at 40 ˚C using a Tribest-Sedona Supreme dryer. Once dry, the material was pulverized with a mechanical grinder to prepare it for extraction. Extraction for the AI-driven optimization phase was conducted using a Gerhardt SOX-414 system, following pre-set, computer-managed parameters.

Extraction procedure method

The investigation integrated both single and multi-objective optimization approaches, using experimental data to refine mushroom extraction protocols. Utilizing water as the solvent, the study examined temperature (40–70 °C), extraction time (3–9 h), and extract concentration (0.5-2 mg/mL) across four levels. This systematic approach resulted in 64 distinct trials performed via Soxhlet extraction. All extraction times are expressed in hours (h) throughout the study.

Modelling process

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) were the primary tool for modeling in this study. For single-objective optimization, input variables, temperature, time, and concentration, were used to determine TAS levels. The multi-objective model incorporated these same inputs but aimed to predict both TAS and TOS values simultaneously. The dataset was partitioned into training (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) segments. The training phase employed the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm, testing 20 distinct hidden neuron setups. Execution parameters featured learning and momentum coefficients of 0.5, with a 500-iteration limit and a 1 × 10⁻⁵ error threshold. The ANN structure was designed as a feedforward multilayer perceptron (MLP) consisting of an input layer with three neurons (temperature, time, and concentration), a single hidden layer, and an output layer. For the single-objective model, the output layer contained one neuron representing TAS, whereas for the multi-objective model, two neurons were used to simultaneously predict TAS and TOS values. Sigmoid activation functions were employed in the hidden layer, while a linear activation function was used in the output layer. The optimal network architecture was selected based on minimum MSE and maximum correlation coefficient (R) values.

To circumvent the potential for performance surfaces to get trapped in local minima during training, the networks were subjected to repeated iterations to approximate a global minimum. In this research, 1,000 separate training cycles were executed for each model, with selection based on established performance benchmarks. Optimization process

Optimization approaches in this study were structured under two main frameworks: single-objective and multi-objective optimization. For each mushroom species, both strategies were systematically designed and implemented. In the single-objective optimization phase, the primary aim was to enhance the total antioxidant status (TAS) of P. eryngii, which was defined as the objective function. In contrast, the multi-objective optimization framework simultaneously addressed multiple criteria, targeting not only the maximization of TAS but also the reduction of total oxidant status (TOS). To achieve these goals, a classical genetic algorithm (GA) was applied in the single-objective scenario, whereas the multi-objective optimization was conducted using the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm-II (NSGA-II), a widely adopted evolutionary algorithm capable of handling conflicting objectives and generating optimal trade-off solutions.

In single-objective optimization problems, solution comparison is relatively direct, as candidates can be ranked using conventional mathematical criteria, ultimately leading to a unique optimal result. In contrast, multi-objective optimization involves the simultaneous consideration of several, often conflicting, objectives, making such direct comparisons inadequate. For this reason, solution evaluation relies on the principle of Pareto optimality. Within this framework, no single solution is considered universally superior; instead, a set of non-dominated solutions, defined as the Pareto optimal set, is obtained. Each solution in this set represents a different trade-off among the objectives. To identify a single preferred solution from these alternatives, additional decision-making strategies are required. In the present study, the LINMAP (Linear Programming Technique for Multidimensional Analysis of Preference) method was employed to determine the most suitable solution from the Pareto optimal set generated through multi-objective optimization.

Extraction stage for biological activity tests

In this study, the extraction conditions optimized for mushroom-derived samples were applied with the aim of enhancing their biological activity profiles. For the single-objective optimization, the extraction parameters were identified as 48.144 °C, 5.561 h, and 1.876 mg/mL. In the multi-objective optimization approach, these parameters were refined to 50.620 °C, 6.540 h, and 1.580 mg/mL, respectively. Extraction procedures were conducted using the Gerhardt SOX-414 system, ensuring that experimental conditions closely matched the optimized values. Following extraction, solvents were removed with a Buchi R100 Rotary Evaporator, and the resulting crude extracts were collected for subsequent experimental analyses.

Antioxidant activity tests

DPPH free radical scavenging activity

Optimized mushroom-derived extracts were first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain stock solutions at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. From these stocks, 1 mL aliquots were mixed with 160 µL of a 0.267 mM DPPH solution, previously prepared in 4 mL of 0.004% methanol. The reaction mixtures were then incubated at room temperature in the absence of light for 30 min to allow the radical scavenging reaction to proceed. Following incubation, absorbance values were measured at 517 nm. The antioxidant capacity of the extracts was subsequently quantified and expressed as milligrams of Trolox equivalents per gram of extract (mg TE/g)10.

Total antioxidant and oxidant analysis

The total antioxidant capacity of the optimized mushroom extracts was quantified using a commercial Rel Assay diagnostic kit, and the results were expressed as mmol Trolox equivalent per liter. In parallel, total oxidant status (TOS) was measured with the corresponding Rel Assay kit, with values reported as µmol hydrogen peroxide equivalent per liter. All analyses were conducted in accordance with the protocols established by Erel11,12. The oxidative stress index (OSI) was subsequently derived by calculating the ratio of TOS to TAS and expressing this value as a percentage, as previously outlined13.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

Optimized mushroom extract solutions were initially prepared as stock solutions in a final volume of 100 µL. An aliquot of this stock was then combined with 2 mL of freshly prepared FRAP reagent. The reagent was formulated by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃·6 H₂O solution with 10 mM 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ) solution in a 10:1:1 ratio. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 min to allow the reduction process to occur. Subsequently, absorbance was recorded at 593 nm, and the ferric reducing antioxidant power of the extracts was expressed as mg Trolox equivalent per gram (mg TE/g) 10.

Anticholinesterase activity tests

Mushroom extracts obtained under optimized conditions were assessed for their anticholinesterase potential using the Ellman spectrophotometric assay14. In this context, galantamine was employed as the reference standard. Extract stock solutions were prepared within a concentration range of 200–3.125 µg/mL. For the assay procedure, 130 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) was first dispensed into each microplate well, followed by the addition of 10 µL of extract solution and 20 µL of either AChE or BChE enzyme solution. The reaction mixtures were incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 20 µL of DTNB (5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)) solution and 20 µL of the appropriate substrate (acetylthiocholine iodide or butyrylthiocholine iodide) were introduced. Absorbance readings were then recorded at 412 nm. Enzyme inhibition percentages were calculated based on the obtained absorbance values, and IC₅₀ values (µg/mL) were determined to quantify inhibitory potency. The results were subsequently evaluated to characterize the anticholinesterase activity of the extracts.

Cell line culture

The A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cell line (ATCC CCL-185) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine. The cells were maintained under standard culture conditions at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells reaching approximately 70–80% confluency were used for the antiproliferative activity assay.

Antiproliferative activity test

Optimized mushroom extracts were investigated for their antiproliferative effects on the A549 human lung adenocarcinoma cell line. For this purpose, extract stock solutions were prepared at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. Cells reaching approximately 70–80% confluency were detached using Trypsin–EDTA solution and subsequently seeded into appropriate culture plates. Following a 24-hour stabilization period, the prepared extract solutions were administered, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. Thereafter, the culture medium was replaced with an MTT solution (1 mg/mL), and incubation was continued at 37 °C until the formation of insoluble purple formazan crystals. These crystals were then solubilized using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The resulting absorbance values were recorded at 570 nm using an Epoch spectrophotometer, and the data were used to determine the antiproliferative activity of the extracts10.

Phenolic analysis

The phenolic composition of the optimized mushroom extracts was characterized using the LC-MS/MS technique. Within the scope of this analysis, a total of 24 reference compounds were screened in the samples. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a C18 Inertsil ODS-4 analytical column (3.0 × 100 mm, 2 μm particle size) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase system consisted of water supplemented with 0.1% formic acid (Phase A) and methanol containing 0.1% formic acid (Phase B). Throughout the analysis, the flow rate was fixed at 0.3 mL/min, while the injection volume was set to 2 µL to ensure precise and reproducible measurements.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis for all data in this study was carried out using the ‘SPSS 21.0 for Windows’ program. A One-Way ANOVA was employed to assess the differences between groups in the tests performed. To pinpoint the specific variations among the groups, the Duncan test was conducted at a significance level of (α = 0.05). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

This table summarizes the TAS and TOS values of P. eryngii extracts obtained under varying extraction conditions, including temperature (40–70 °C), time (4–10 h), and concentration (0.25-2 mg/mL). In general, increasing both extraction temperature and duration resulted in an increase in TAS values accompanied by a reduction in TOS levels. The most favorable results were observed at 50 °C and 6 h, where the highest TAS value (6.838 ± 0.092 mmol/L) and the lowest TOS value (19.837 ± 0.230) were recorded. Additionally, increasing extract concentration consistently contributed to enhanced TAS values. However, excessively high temperatures (e.g., 70 °C) and prolonged extraction periods (8–10 h) led to a noticeable decline in antioxidant capacity, most likely due to thermal degradation of bioactive constituents. The relatively low standard deviation values indicate a high level of experimental precision and reproducibility. Overall, these findings offer a clear basis for identifying optimal extraction parameters and improving the antioxidant potential of the extracts. Beyond the descriptive presentation of the data, these findings suggest that the observed increase in antioxidant capacity under moderate temperature and time conditions is likely associated with the enhanced extraction efficiency of thermally stable phenolic compounds. However, the decline in TAS values at higher temperatures and prolonged extraction durations indicates possible thermal degradation or structural alteration of sensitive bioactive molecules. This trend is consistent with previous studies reporting that excessive thermal exposure may negatively affect phenolic stability and antioxidant performance15,16. Moreover, the balance between TAS and TOS values highlights the dual nature of mushroom-derived extracts, where both antioxidant and pro-oxidant components may coexist depending on extraction conditions. Therefore, the optimization process should not only focus on maximizing antioxidant output but also consider the biochemical equilibrium between oxidant and antioxidant compounds. This interpretation provides a more comprehensive understanding of how extraction parameters influence the functional and biochemical properties of P. eryngii extracts.

Antioxidant activity

Mushrooms are natural resources known for their strong antioxidant properties and may have significant effects on human health. Mushroom extracts, which are rich in antioxidant substances such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, tocopherols, ascorbic acid and selenium, provide protection against oxidative stress caused by free radicals in the body17,18. Oxidative stress can damage cells and lead to many chronic diseases such as aging, cancer, heart diseases and neurological disorders. Antioxidants contained in mushrooms protect cell health by neutralizing these harmful free radicals and prevent cell damage19. However, beyond this general comparison, the higher antioxidant capacity observed in the present study may be attributed to the application of artificial intelligence-based optimization techniques, which enable precise control of extraction parameters. Unlike conventional extraction approaches reported in the literature21–23, the ANN/GA-based optimization strategy used in this study allows the identification of conditions that maximize the extraction efficiency of key phenolic compounds. In addition, variations between the present results and previous studies may also be influenced by differences in sample origin, extraction solvent systems, and experimental design. Therefore, the superior antioxidant performance observed in this study is likely a combined result of both optimized extraction conditions and methodological differences, highlighting the importance of advanced optimization techniques in enhancing the bioactivity of mushroom extracts. The TAS value is an indicator of all the antioxidant compounds found in the mushroom4. In our study, it was observed that the optimized extracts of P. eryngii, which had the highest biological activity, had high antioxidant potential. In addition, when the TAS values of the single and multi-objective optimized extracts used were compared with other mushroom species such as L. deliciosus, H. erinaceus, C. cibarius, L. cristata and C. candolleanus, it was revealed that P. eryngii is a potentially important antioxidant source. In addition, in our study, it was determined that the single-objective optimized extract of P. eryngii had higher TAS values compared to the multi-objective optimized extract.

The TOS value is an indicator of the totality of oxidant compounds found in the mushroom4. The OSI value shows how much the mushroom suppresses endogenous oxidant compounds with endogenous antioxidant compounds4. In our study, it was observed that the multi-objective optimized extracts of P. eryngii had higher TOS values compared to the single-objective optimized extracts. In our study, the TOS and OSI values of both the single and multi-objective optimized extracts of P. eryngii were determined to be higher than L. deliciosus, H. erinaceus, C. cibarius and C. candolleanus, and lower than L. cristata. In this context, it was observed that this mushroom has a high potential to produce oxidant compounds. The fact that a natural product has high oxidant compounds may pose negative situations in terms of consumption. In this context, although P. eryngii has high antioxidant levels, these findings indicate that further investigation is needed to evaluate the potential biological implications of its oxidant-producing capacity. However, it is thought that P. eryngii samples produced under the right conditions can be used as a natural oxidant source. Future studies should aim to maximize the potential health benefits of this mushroom by further developing extraction methods and optimization techniques.

Anticholinesterase activity

Alzheimer’s disease is a neurological disease characterized by decreased acetylcholine levels. Since the cholinesterase enzyme breaks down acetylcholine, inhibiting this enzyme can alleviate the symptoms of the disease. Cholinesterase inhibitors can improve cognitive functions by increasing acetylcholine levels29. Recent studies have shown that some natural compounds, especially mushroom and plant extracts, offer a potential alternative in the treatment of Alzheimer’s by inhibiting cholinesterase30. Anticholinesterase activities of different Pleurotus species have been reported in the literature. In our study, it was determined that optimized P. eryngii extracts exhibited higher acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase activities compared to these values. Optimization of extracts plays an important role in increasing the obtained biological activities4. In our study, extracts obtained from P. eryngii mushroom by optimizing them with different methods were evaluated. Anticholinesterase activities of optimized single and multiple extracts were examined. It was determined that single-objective optimized extract had higher acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase activities compared to multiple extracts. However, compared to galantamine, a standard inhibitor, P. eryngii extracts were found to have lower anticholinesterase activity. Inhibition of enzymes in the treatment of diseases is one of the fundamental approaches of modern medicine. Controlling the activities of enzymes allows the development of targeted and effective treatment strategies32. In this context, detailed examination of enzyme inhibition mechanisms can help increase the effectiveness of therapeutic drugs while minimizing their side effects33. Especially in the treatment of chronic and complex diseases, approaches based on enzyme inhibition can provide more successful treatment processes and an increase in the quality of life of patients34. In this context, extracts obtained from P. eryngii with optimized methods are promising in terms of biological activity and offer a new perspective for the evaluation of natural inhibitor sources. Investigation of further optimization processes to increase the effectiveness of extracts can contribute to the discovery of the pharmacological potential of natural products.

Antiproliferative activity

Antiproliferative mushrooms are mushrooms that contain compounds that inhibit or slow down the growth and proliferation of cancer cells. These mushroom species are being investigated as promising natural resources for cancer treatment. Mushrooms exhibit antiproliferative effects thanks to the polysaccharides, phenolic compounds, triterpenoids, and other bioactive compounds they contain35. These compounds are expected to target the growth pathways of cancer cells, stop the cell cycle, induce apoptosis (programmed cell death), and inhibit tumor vascularization36. In this study, the antiproliferative effects of optimized extracts obtained from P. eryngii on the A549 lung cancer cell line was investigated. The results are presented in Fig. 5.

In the present study, the antiproliferative activity results were presented as optical density (OD) values because OD is the direct absorbance output of the MTT assay and reflects the amount of formazan formed by metabolically active cells. Lower OD values indicate reduced mitochondrial metabolic activity and, therefore, decreased cell viability after extract treatment. In the literature, it has been reported that a protein isolated from P. eryngii exhibited activity against the A549 lung cancer cell line37. In addition, P. eryngii extracts have been shown to be effective against different cancer cell lines, including AGS human gastric carcinoma, HepG2 human hepatoblastoma, and MCF-7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells38. In the present study, the antiproliferative effect of optimized P. eryngii extracts was evaluated against the A549 lung cancer cell line. The results showed that the single-objective optimized extract exhibited stronger antiproliferative activity than the multi-objective optimized extract. Moreover, both extracts showed increased activity with increasing extract concentration, indicating a concentration-dependent suppressive effect on A549 cells. Since the MTT assay is based on the conversion of MTT into formazan crystals by metabolically active cells, the decrease in OD values observed after extract treatment indicates a reduction in cellular metabolic activity and viable cell population. Therefore, the lower OD values obtained at higher extract concentrations suggest that optimized P. eryngii extracts may inhibit the proliferation of A549 cells in a dose-dependent manner. The stronger effect of the single-objective optimized extract may be associated with its higher antioxidant capacity and richer phenolic profile, particularly its higher levels of gallic acid, caffeic acid, protocatechuic acid, naringenin, and vanillic acid. These compounds are known to contribute to biological activities through mechanisms such as oxidative stress modulation, induction of apoptosis, and interference with cell cycle progression39. Thus, the enhanced antiproliferative response observed in the single-objective optimized extract may be related not only to the optimized extraction conditions but also to the greater recovery of bioactive phenolic constituents. Overall, these findings support the antiproliferative potential of P. eryngii against A549 lung cancer cells and are consistent with previous studies reporting the anticancer properties of this mushroom37,38. However, since IC₅₀ values and molecular pathway analyses were not performed in the present study, further investigations are required to clarify the exact mechanisms underlying the antiproliferative effects of optimized P. eryngii extracts. Future studies should therefore focus on IC₅₀ determination, apoptosis-related markers, cell cycle analysis, and molecular signaling pathways to better define the anticancer potential of P. eryngii.

Phenolic contents

Mushrooms synthesize various bioactive compounds to protect against environmental stress. These compounds are part of the defense mechanisms that ensure the survival of fungi and may have different biological activities40. This feature reveals that mushrooms not only provide therapeutic benefits but also have significant potential to support healthy living41. In this study, phenolic compound contents of optimized extracts of P. eryngii were investigated by LC-MS/MS device and six different phenolic compounds were detected in both multi and single-objective optimized extract. These compounds were determined as gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, naringenin and vanillic acid. According to the study findings, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid was detected at higher concentration in multi-objective optimized extracts, whereas gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, caffeic acid, naringenin and vanillic acid were found at higher levels in single-objective optimized extract. While caffeic acid was the phenolic compound with the highest concentration in single-objective optimized extract, gallic acid undertook this role in multi-objective optimized extract. Phenolic compounds have been reported in the literature to exhibit cancer, antioxidant, antiproliferative and neuroprotective effects42–44. In the extract optimization process, critical parameters such as temperature, concentration and extraction times were optimized. In particular, different temperatures (40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C), concentrations (0.25, 0.50, 1, 2 mg/mL) and extraction times (4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 10 h) were tested to increase the extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds. As a result of the optimization process, key phenolic compounds such as gallic acid (3925.68 mg/kg) and caffeic acid (4157.40 mg/kg) were obtained at markedly higher concentrations in the single-objective optimized extract compared to the multi-objective extract. These compounds are well known for their strong antioxidant and anticancer properties45–47, and their elevated levels are consistent with the enhanced biological activities observed in this study. The antioxidant activity can be primarily attributed to the strong radical scavenging capacity of phenolic compounds, particularly gallic acid and caffeic acid, which effectively neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduce oxidative stress45–47. This is in agreement with the higher TAS values obtained for the single-objective optimized extract. In addition, the antiproliferative effects observed against A549 cells may be associated with the ability of these phenolic compounds to regulate apoptosis and cell cycle pathways48,49. Similarly, the anticholinesterase activity detected in the extracts can be mechanistically explained by the interaction of phenolic compounds with the active sites of acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase enzymes, thereby inhibiting neurotransmitter degradation50. The comparatively lower concentrations of these compounds in the multi-objective optimized extract correspond well with its relatively reduced biological activity profile. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the biological activities of P. eryngii extracts are not only dependent on extraction efficiency but are also strongly influenced by the qualitative and quantitative composition of phenolic compounds. The integration of optimization strategies with phenolic profiling provides a mechanistic basis for explaining the enhanced bioactivity observed in the single-objective optimized extract. The results also indicate that P. eryngii is a rich source of bioactive phenolic compounds, and that optimization of extraction conditions significantly improves their recovery. However, to better understand the biological relevance of these findings, further investigations involving detailed in vitro and in vivo studies are required. In addition, comparative studies with other mushroom species would be valuable to clarify the distinctive bioactive profile of P. eryngii. Therefore, the potential application of optimized P. eryngii extracts in pharmaceutical and functional food systems should be explored through advanced formulation and product development studies.

Conclusion

In this study, antioxidant, anticholinesterase, antiproliferative activities and phenolic compound contents of optimized extracts from P. eryngii mushroom were investigated. It was determined that single-objective optimized extract exhibited higher antioxidant, anticholinesterase and antiproliferative activities compared to multi-objective optimized extract. In addition, single-objective optimized extract was found to be richer in phenolic compounds such as gallic acid and caffeic acid. In conclusion, P. eryngii is a natural source that exhibits strong biological activities and is rich in phenolic compounds. However, in order to interpret these findings in a broader context, the bioactivity of phenolic compounds should be investigated in more detail in vitro and in vivo systems. In future studies, further development of extraction methods and optimization techniques should aim to maximize the potential health benefits of this mushroom.
