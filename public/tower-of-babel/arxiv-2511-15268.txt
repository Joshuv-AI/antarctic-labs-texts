Photoinduced topological phase transition in monolayer 1T ′ -MoS 2
Mohammad Mortezaei Nobahari ID

Abstract
We investigate the nonequilibrium topological phases of monolayer 1T ′ –MoS 2 under high-frequency circularly polarized driving using a low-energy k ⋅ p k\!\cdot\!p Hamiltonian combined with a van Vleck expansion. The off-resonant field generates spin- and valley-dependent mass corrections that reshape the Berry curvature profile and shift the conditions for band inversion. By evaluating the quasienergy bands, Berry curvatures, Hall conductivities, and spin- valley-resolved Chern numbers, we identify a sequence of light-controlled topological transitions marked by well-defined gap closings. Depending on the Floquet coupling strength and the electric-field parameter, the system evolves between the equilibrium quantum spin Hall (QSH) state and a set of driven phases including spin-polarized quantum Hall insulator (S-QHI), quantum valley Hall (QVH or BI) and photo-induced quantum Hall insulator (P-QHI) regimes. The results establish 1T ′ –MoS 2 as a tunable platform where circular driving selectively manipulates spin and valley degrees of freedom, enabling controlled access to non-equilibrium topological phases in transition-metal dichalcogenides.



I
Introduction

The discovery of topological phases of matter has profoundly reshaped modern condensed matter physics, uncovering quantum states that are characterized not merely by broken symmetries but by global topological invariants defined in momentum space. Among these, the quantum spin Hall (QSH) effect stands as a cornerstone of two-dimensional (2D) topological physics, representing a time-reversal-symmetric analogue of the quantum Hall effect, where counterpropagating spin-polarized edge states coexist at opposite boundaries of an insulating bulk
Kane and Mele 2005
;
Chang and Yang 2009
;
König et al. 2007
;
König et al. 2008
. These helical edge modes are protected by time-reversal symmetry (
\mathcal{T}
) and are immune to nonmagnetic backscattering, leading to dissipationless spin currents and quantized edge conductance of
2e^{2}/h

Sheng et al. 2006
;
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Qi and Zhang 2011
.

Theoretical proposals of the QSH effect in graphene
Kane and Mele 2005
demonstrated the essential role of spin–orbit coupling (SOC) in generating a nontrivial topological gap, though the small SOC in graphene limited experimental realization. This challenge was overcome by Bernevig, Hughes, and Zhang in HgTe/CdTe quantum wells, where a thickness-tuned band inversion produced a measurable QSH phase
Bernevig et al. 2006
. The experimental observation of the QSH effect in these wells
Konig et al. 2007
inaugurated the era of 2D topological insulators (TIs), motivating extensive efforts to identify new material platforms with large band gaps, chemical stability, and compatibility with devices
Liu et al. 2008
;
Ando 2013
.

In the ensuing decade, the QSH state has been realized or proposed in a wide variety of 2D systems, including InAs/GaSb quantum wells
Knez et al. 2011
;
Du et al. 2015
, silicene and germanene
Liu et al. 2011a
;
Ezawa 2012
, bismuth bilayers
Murakami 2006
;
Reis et al. 2017
, stanene
Xu et al. 2013
, and particularly transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) in their distorted 1T
′
phase
Qian et al. 2014
;
Tang et al. 2017
;
Fei et al. 2017
. The topological character of these materials is typically characterized through the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant or the spin Chern number
PhysRevLett.97.0368086
;
Fu and Kane 2007
, both of which relate to the Berry curvature distribution and band inversion near the Fermi level.

The bulk–edge correspondence implies that a nontrivial topological invariant guarantees the presence of gapless helical edge states traversing the bulk gap
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Qi and Zhang 2011
. These edge modes yield quantized spin Hall conductance and are robust against time-reversal-preserving perturbations, contrasting with the quantum anomalous Hall effect (QAHE), where
\mathcal{T}
symmetry is broken by magnetism
Yu et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Rachel 2018
. The interplay between SOC, crystal symmetry, and inversion-breaking perturbations determines the onset of QSH behavior, and recent work has emphasized the tunability of these properties via strain, electric fields, or light–matter coupling
Liu et al. 2011b
;
Lu et al. 2013
;
Oka and Aoki 2009
;
Wang et al. 2013
.

In parallel, the discovery of atomically thin van der Waals materials has provided an ideal playground for realizing 2D topological phases. Among them, transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) with formula MX
2
(M = Mo, W; X = S, Se, Te) have attracted enormous attention due to their rich polymorphism and strong SOC
Chhowalla et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2012
;
Manzeli et al. 2017
. The semiconducting 2H phase of MoS
2
is the most widely studied, exhibiting a direct band gap of about 1.8 eV in the monolayer limit and valley-selective circular dichroism
Mak et al. 2010
;
Xiao et al. 2012
. However, metastable metallic 1T and semimetallic 1T
′
phases of MoS
2
exhibit drastically different properties, including Peierls distortions, charge density wave instabilities, and topologically nontrivial band orderings
Heising and Kanatzidis 1999
.

The distorted 1T
′
phase, characterized by a zigzag chain of Mo atoms, has been theoretically predicted to host a robust QSH state with an energy gap on the order of 0.1–0.3 eV, suitable for room-temperature operation
Qian et al. 2014
. First-principles calculations revealed that strong SOC and lattice distortion drive band inversion between Mo-
d
and S-
p
orbitals near the
\Gamma

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topology
Qian et al. 2014
;
Lu et al. 2017
. Experimental signatures of this topological insulating phase have been reported in monolayer WTe
2

Fei et al. 2017
;
Wu et al. 2018
, and related observations in Mo-based TMDs suggest a shared underlying mechanism
Chen et al. 2019
;
Katsuragawa et al. 2020
. In 1T
′
-MoS
2
, the magnitude of the SOC and the presence of inversion asymmetry further enrich its spin and valley physics, making it a compelling platform for exploring QSH and valley Hall effects
Xu et al. 2023
.

The ability to manipulate the topological character of 1T
′
-MoS
2
using external fields or optical driving opens exciting prospects for nonequilibrium topological phenomena. Floquet engineering—the periodic modulation of a system by light—has emerged as a powerful tool to dynamically induce and tune topological states
Kitagawa et al. 2011
;
Mikami et al. 2016
. When a 1T
′
-MoS
2
monolayer is subjected to a circularly polarized field, the effective Hamiltonian acquires light-induced mass terms that modify the Berry curvature and band topology, leading to Floquet–Bloch bands with controllable Chern numbers
Dehghani et al. 2014
;
Claassen et al. 2016
;
Hübener et al. 2017
. Such light-driven transitions between trivial, QSH, and quantum anomalous Hall phases have been theoretically demonstrated in several 2D materials
Perez-Piskunow et al. 2014
;
Foa Torres et al. 2014
;
Usaj et al. 2014
, making 1T
′
-MoS
2
an ideal candidate for tunable topological spintronics.

Time-periodic driving is a versatile tool to create and control topological phases that do not exist in equilibrium. A canonical example is the generation of Haldane-like masses in Dirac materials by circularly polarized light (CPL), which breaks time-reversal symmetry and can convert a quantum spin Hall state into a quantum anomalous Hall state. Monolayer 1T
′
-MoS
2
exhibits strong spin–orbit coupling and a low-energy bandstructure amenable to an effective two-band description; this makes it an excellent platform for Floquet-driven topological transitions. Our results demonstrate that suitable tuning of the external fields enables the system to transition among the QSH, QVH (or BI), P-QHI, and S-QHI phases.

In this paper, we focus on the spin- and valley-resolved Hall conductivity of monolayer 1T
′
-MoS
2
under periodic driving. By introducing a tunable Floquet parameter
\xi
, we demonstrate how light–matter coupling can modify the band inversion, Berry curvature distribution, and Hall conductivity, revealing a controllable crossover between topological and trivial regimes. The obtained results provide theoretical guidance for future experimental efforts toward optically tunable topological devices based on 1T
′
-phase TMDs.

II.1
Coupling to a periodic drive

We consider spatially-uniform circularly polarized light in the dipole approximation. The vector potential is

\mathbf{A}(t)=A_{0}\big(\cos(\omega t),\,\eta\sin(\omega t)\big),

(18)

\eta=\pm 1\ \text{(right/left helicity)},

and minimal coupling is implemented via
\mathbf{k}\mapsto\mathbf{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{A}(t)
.

Retaining terms linear in
\mathbf{A}
(the paramagnetic coupling; the
A^{2}
diamagnetic scalar contributes only an overall Stark shift to leading order) the time-dependent Hamiltonian becomes

H_{\kappa s}(t)=H_{\kappa s}(k)+e\,\nu_{1}A_{x}(t)\,\sigma_{y}-e\,A_{y}(t)\,\Pi_{\kappa s},

(19)

where we defined

\Pi_{\kappa s}\equiv s\,\nu_{2}\,\sigma_{x}+\kappa\,\nu_{-}\,\sigma_{0}+\kappa\,\nu_{+}\,\sigma_{z}.

(20)

For a
T=2\pi/\omega
-periodic Hamiltonian the Schrödinger equation admits Floquet solutions
\Psi_{\alpha}(t)=e^{-i\epsilon_{\alpha}t/\hbar}\Phi_{\alpha}(t)
with
\Phi_{\alpha}(t+T)=\Phi_{\alpha}(t)
. In the off-resonant (high-frequency) regime,
\hbar\omega
is taken larger than the bandwidth and relevant interband energies, avoiding real photon absorption; then a controlled expansion (van Vleck / Floquet–Magnus) yields an effective static Floquet Hamiltonian

H_{F}=H^{(0)}+\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,[H_{-1},H_{+1}]+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{-2}),

(21)

H_{(\pm 1)}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,H(t)e^{\pm i\omega t},

(22)

where
H_{\pm 1}
are the
\pm\omega
Fourier components of
H(t)
.

Using

A_{x}(t)=\frac{A_{0}}{2}\big(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t}\big),\qquad A_{y}(t)=\frac{\eta A_{0}}{2i}\big(e^{i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega t}\big),

the Fourier components from Eq. (
22
) are

\displaystyle H_{+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{eA_{0}}{2}\Big(\nu_{1}\sigma_{y}+\frac{\eta}{i}\Pi_{\kappa s}\Big),

(23)

\displaystyle H_{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{eA_{0}}{2}\Big(\nu_{1}\sigma_{y}-\frac{\eta}{i}\Pi_{\kappa s}\Big).

(24)

A direct commutator evaluation (using
[\sigma_{y},\sigma_{x}]=-2i\sigma_{z}
and
[\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}]=2i\sigma_{x}
) gives

[H_{-1},H_{+1}]=e^{2}\nu_{1}\,\eta\,A_{0}^{2}\left(\kappa\,\nu_{+}\,\sigma_{x}-s\,\nu_{2}\,\sigma_{z}\right).

(25)

Substituting Eq. (
25

21

\delta H_{F}^{(\kappa s)}=\frac{e^{2}\nu_{1}A_{0}^{2}}{\hbar\omega}\,\eta\left(\kappa\nu_{+}\sigma_{x}-s\nu_{2}\sigma_{z}\right).

(26)

and total Hamiltonian is

\begin{split}H_{F}^{(\kappa s)}(\mathbf{k})\approx\;&\hbar k_{x}\,\nu_{1}\,\sigma_{y}-\hbar k_{y}\!\left(s\,\nu_{2}\,\sigma_{x}+\kappa\,\nu_{-}\,\sigma_{0}-\kappa\,\nu_{+}\,\sigma_{z}\right)\\
&+\Big[\Delta_{\mathrm{so}}(\alpha-s\kappa)+\xi\,\nu_{+}\,\eta\,\kappa\Big]\sigma_{x}\\
&-\xi\,\nu_{2}\eta\,s\,\sigma_{z}+V\sigma_{0}.\end{split}

(27)

where we defined
\xi\equiv\frac{e^{2}\nu_{1}}{\hbar\omega}\,A_{0}^{2}

It is convenient to write
H_{F}=\mathbf{d^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\bm{\sigma}+d_{0}(\mathbf{k})\sigma_{0}
with components

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{x}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-\hbar k_{y}\,s\nu_{2}+\Delta_{\mathrm{so}}(\alpha-s\kappa)+\xi\,\nu_{+}\eta\,\kappa,

(28)

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{y}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\hbar k_{x}\,\nu_{1},

(29)

\displaystyle d_{z}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-\hbar k_{y}\,\kappa\nu_{+}-\xi\,\nu_{2}\,\eta\,s,

(30)

\displaystyle d_{0}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=V-\hbar k_{y}\,\kappa\nu_{-}.

(31)

The quasienergies are
\epsilon^{\pm}(\mathbf{k})=d_{0}(\mathbf{k})\pm\sqrt{d_{x}^{\prime 2}+d_{y}^{\prime 2}+d_{z}^{\prime 2}}

At
\mathbf{k}=0

M_{x}\equiv d^{\prime}_{x}(0)=\Delta_{\mathrm{so}}(\alpha-s\kappa)+\xi\,\nu_{+}\eta\,\kappa,

(32)

M_{y}\equiv d^{\prime}_{y}(0)=0

(33)

M_{z}\equiv d^{\prime}_{z}(0)=-\xi\,\nu_{2}\,\eta\,s.

(34)

Therefore the quasieigenvalues at

\epsilon^{\pm}(0)=V\pm\sqrt{M_{x}^{2}+M_{z}^{2}}

The CPL-induced shift of the

\alpha_{c}^{(\kappa s)}=s\kappa-\frac{\xi\,\nu_{+}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{so}}}\,\kappa\eta.

(35)

Simultaneously,

II.3
Berry Curvature of the Floquet Hamiltonian

For a generic two-band Hamiltonian of the form

H(\mathbf{k})=d_{0}(\mathbf{k})\,\sigma_{0}+\mathbf{d}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\bm{\sigma},

(36)

with
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})=(d^{\prime}_{x},d^{\prime}_{y},d^{\prime}_{z})
, the Berry curvature of the upper (
+
) and lower (
-
) bands is given by

\Omega_{\pm}^{z}(\mathbf{k})\;=\;\pm\,\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\cdot\big(\partial_{k_{x}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\times\partial_{k_{y}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\big)}{|\mathbf{d}^{\prime}|^{3}},\qquad|\mathbf{d}^{\prime}|=\sqrt{d_{x}^{\prime 2}+d_{y}^{\prime 2}+d_{z}^{\prime 2}}.

(37)

The scalar term
d_{0}(\mathbf{k})
only shifts the energy and does not contribute.

For the Floquet effective Hamiltonian of monolayer 1T
′
-MoS
2
under circularly polarized light we obtained

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{x}

\displaystyle=-\hbar k_{y}\,s\nu_{2}\;+\;M_{x},

(38)

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{y}

\displaystyle=\hbar k_{x}\,\nu_{1},

(39)

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\hbar k_{y}\,\kappa\nu_{+}\;+\;M_{z},

(40)

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle=V-\hbar k_{y}\,\kappa\nu_{-},

(41)

Evaluating the derivatives,

\partial_{k_{x}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(0,\hbar\nu_{1},0),\qquad\partial_{k_{y}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=(-\hbar s\nu_{2},\,0,\,-\hbar\kappa\nu_{+}),

(42)

one finds

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\cdot\left(\partial_{k_{x}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\times\partial_{k_{y}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\right)=\hbar^{2}\nu_{1}\left(\kappa\nu_{+}M_{x}-s\nu_{2}M_{z}\right),

(43)

which is independent of
\mathbf{k}
.

The final Berry curvature of the lower band is therefore

{\;\Omega_{+}^{z}(\mathbf{k})\;=\;\frac{\hbar^{2}\nu_{1}}{2}\,\frac{\kappa\nu_{+}M_{x}-s\nu_{2}M_{z}}{|d^{\prime}|^{3}}\;}

(44)

where

|d^{\prime}|^{3}=\big[(M_{x}-\hbar k_{y}s\nu_{2})^{2}+(\hbar k_{x}\nu_{1})^{2}+(M_{z}-\hbar k_{y}\kappa\nu_{+})^{2}\big]^{3/2}

(45)

while the lower band has the opposite sign,

\Omega_{+}^{z}(\mathbf{k})=-\,\Omega_{-}^{z}(\mathbf{k}).

(46)

Note that the tilt velocity
\nu_{-}
and scalar potential
V
appear only in
d_{0}
and therefore
do not affect the Berry curvature. The sign and magnitude of the curvature are controlled by the
effective Floquet masses
M_{x}
and
M_{z}
, which depend explicitly on the light helicity
\eta

and amplitude
A_{0}
.

II.4
Hall conductivity

We begin from the Berry curvature of the upper Floquet band for a given valley
\kappa=\pm 1
and spin
s=\pm 1
,

\Omega_{+}^{z}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\hbar^{2}\nu_{1}}{2}\;\frac{BM_{x}-AM_{z}}{|d^{\prime}|^{3}},

(47)

where, for compactness, we defined

A\equiv s\,\nu_{2},\qquad B\equiv\kappa\,\nu_{+},

The zero-temperature intrinsic Hall conductivity (chemical potential inside the gap)
for sector
(\kappa,s)
is

\sigma_{xy}^{(\kappa s)}\;=\;\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)}\;\Omega^{z}(\mathbf{k})\;=\;\frac{e^{2}}{h}\,C_{\kappa s},

(48)

with the Chern number

C_{\kappa s}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}k\;\Omega^{z}(\mathbf{k}).

(49)

The Chern number formula can be written as (see Appendix
V
)

{\;C_{\kappa s}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{sgn}\!\big(\kappa\nu_{+}\,M_{x}-s\nu_{2}\,M_{z}\big)\;.}

(50)

Let
C_{\kappa s}
denote the Chern number of the occupied band manifold in valley
\kappa=\pm
and spin channel
s=\uparrow,\downarrow
.
The total Chern number is then

C_{\mathrm{tot}}(\xi)=\sum_{\kappa=\pm}\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}C_{\kappa s}=\sum_{s}C_{s}(\xi)=\sum_{\kappa}C_{\kappa}(\xi),

(51)

where

C_{s}(\xi)=\sum_{\kappa}C_{\kappa s}
and

C_{\kappa}(\xi)=\sum_{s}C_{\kappa s}

are the spin- and valley-resolved Chern numbers, respectively.
We further define

\Delta C_{\mathrm{valley}}=\frac{C_{+}-C_{-}}{2},\qquad\Delta C_{\mathrm{spin}}=\frac{C_{\uparrow}-C_{\downarrow}}{2}.

(52)

IV
Conclusions

The results of this work delineate how circular driving restructures the spin–valley landscape of 1T
′
–MoS
2
and organizes its nonequilibrium topological phases. The Floquet-renormalized spectrum exhibits a sequence of drive-controlled gap closings, each accompanied by a redistribution of Berry curvature among the spin and valley sectors. These critical points define boundaries separating quantum spin Hall, valley-polarized, spin-polarized, and Chern insulating regimes. The spin- and valley-resolved Hall conductivities reveal that the response of individual sectors can be tuned independently through the Floquet coupling strength and the electric-field. This tunability originates from distinct light-induced mass renormalizations acting differently on the two valleys and spin channels. Overall, the results identify 1T
′
–MoS
2
as a material whose topological transport properties can be reconfigured continuously and reversibly by high-frequency driving, highlighting its potential for light-programmable topological functionalities.
