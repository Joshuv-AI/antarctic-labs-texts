Quantum Circuits for the Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm
Baptiste Claudon, Pablo Rodenas-Ruiz, Jean-Philip Piquemal, Pierre Monmarché

Abstract




Abstract

Szegedy’s quantization of a reversible Markov chain provides a quantum walk whose mixing time is quadratically smaller than that of the classical walk. Quantum computers are therefore expected to provide a speedup of Metropolis-Hastings (MH) simulations. Existing generic methods to implement the quantum walk require coherently computing the acceptance probabilities of the underlying Markov kernel. However, reversible computing methods require a number of qubits that scales with the complexity of the computation. This overhead is undesirable in near-term fault-tolerant quantum computing, where few logical qubits are available. In this work, we present a quantum walk construction which follows the classical proposal-acceptance logic, does not require further reversible computing methods, and uses a constant-sized ancilla register. Since each step of the quantum walk uses a constant number of proposition and acceptance steps, we expect the end-to-end quadratic speedup to hold for MH simulations.

I
Introduction

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods constitute a set of algorithms based on Markov processes to draw samples from a probability distribution. They have found application in diverse areas, from molecular dynamics
Paquet and Viktor 2015
, with implications in drug design, to the training of neural networks in machine learning.
Kirkpatrick et al. 1983
. These algorithms also serve as a tool of choice in the study of lattice and spin systems in statistical physics
Landau and Binder 2005
or the estimation of the volume of convex bodies
Lovász and Vempala 2006
. The most widely used MCMC method is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
Hastings 1970
. In order to sample from a distribution
\pi
that is known only up to a multiplicative constant, the algorithm consists in simulating a Markov process with stationary distribution
\pi
. The Markov process is simulated through a succession of move proposals that are accepted with certain probabilities. These acceptance probabilities are chosen so that the Markov process is reversible with respect to the target
\pi
. The algorithm’s cost is described by the number of simulation steps required to approach the stationary distribution, known as the mixing time. When the process is reversible, the mixing time is of the order of the inverse spectral gap of the chain (i.e. the difference between the modulus of its two largest eigenvalues).

Recent efforts have focused on designing algorithms based on the principles of quantum mechanics. In 2004, Szegedy showed that every reversible Markov chain can be mapped to a quantum walk with quadratically larger spectral gap
Szegedy 2004
. In addition, the stationary distribution is invariant under the quantum walk dynamics. Combined with algorithms to reflect through eigenspaces
Kitaev 1995
;
Claudon 2025
and amplitude amplification
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Yoder et al. 2014
, Szegedy’s walk requires quadratically less steps to sample from the stationary distribution, compared to the classical mixing time.

Despite this reduction in the number of steps, the overall complexity of the walk is given by the number of steps times the cost of each step, which could make the quantum walk prohibitively expensive in practice. Generic implementation methods rely on the coherent computation of the transition probabilities of the underlying kernel (see
Chiang et al. 2009
for an overview of existing methods and an efficient implementation in the sparse case). In the presence of additional symmetries, more efficient methods have been developed. For instance, there exists methods without coherent arithmetic for certain simple random walks on groups
Loke and Wang 2012
;
Loke and Wang 2017
. Nonetheless, the MH algorithm does not require the computation of the transition probabilities, nor presents a group structure. Computing the diagonal elements of the Markov kernel, the probability of rejecting the proposed move, can involve evaluating a sum of many terms. Each step of the quantum walk may be much more costly than a classical step, which could negate the quantum speedup.

The quantum walk of the MH kernel should therefore not be implemented by computing the transition probabilities. Instead, it should rely on steps of the proposal kernel and the computation of the move acceptance probability matrix. As explained in the Appendix A of
Lemieux et al. 2020
, the difficult part of implementing this quantum walk lies in the rejection of certain samples. Indeed, the sample forgetting step is not invertible, thus nontrivial to simulate using unitary dynamics. Lemieux et al. address the problem by following a different quantization procedure from Szegedy’s
Lemieux et al. 2020
. The authors provide complete quantum circuits to study the Ising model. Childs et al. address the problem with reversible computing methods, closely following the classical move acceptance procedure
Childs et al. 2022
. Still, the qubit requirements of their technique are not fixed and depend on the acceptance probability matrix. Thus, the method might be discarded in the near term.

In this work, we construct general circuits based solely on proposal and acceptance steps. Moreover, we minimize the number of required state registers and ancilla qubits. Instead of quantizing the MH kernel, we quantize a so-called dual kernel. The dual is not a stochastic process on the vertices (states) of the state space, but on its edges (the pairs of states between which a transition is possible). The extended state space allows to keep track of the rejected samples. The proposal step consists in proposing a new head for the current edge. The acceptance step consists in flipping or not the current edge. Because there is no sample rejection step, the underlying dynamics is easier to implement using unitary operations. We show how to recover samples of
\pi
from the stationary measure of this dual kernel. We also show that the spectral gap of the dual kernel is as large as that of the MH kernel. As a consequence, the quadratic speedup is maintained. To the best of our knowledge, our construction is the only Szegedy quantization of MH kernels that does not rely on the coherent computation of their matrix elements or transition probabilities. As a consequence, it suggests that the MH quantum walks can always be implemented without prohibitive algorithmic overhead. To further support this claim, we explicitly compile quantum walk circuits. The significantly reduced qubit requirements of our method allow for their exact numerical simulations. Thus, we illustrate the performance of our construction for the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin algorithm, of wide interest in molecular dynamics and machine learning.

summarizes the key properties of our construction. In particular, it shows that a single quantum walk step is made from a constant number of proposal
O_{T}
and acceptance
O_{A}

II
Use case: Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm aims at obtaining samples from a probability distribution
\pi
. It proceeds in a succession of proposal and acceptance steps. When targeting a multi-dimensional continuous measure
\pi
, proposal are often obtained using a Langevin dynamics. Informally, the process is a gradient descent with random fluctuations. Let us define the process more precisely. Given an initial position
X_{0}=x_{0}
, the position at step
k+1
is chosen using the position at step
k
according to:

X_{k+1}=X_{k}+\tau\nabla\log(\pi(X_{k}))+\sqrt{2\tau}\xi_{k},

(1)

where
\tau>0
is a small time step and the
(\xi_{k})_{k}
are independent and identically distributed standard Gaussian variables. The process resulting from adding the acceptance step is called Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA).

In this work, we construct a quantum circuit acting on
4
state registers and using
3
ancillae to discretize the dynamics. Given a state space of size
n
, we only require
m=\lceil\log n\rceil
qubits to label all the states. Therefore, our circuit uses a total of
4m+3
qubits. To address the general context of molecular simulation, let us consider molecular dynamics of
N
-atom systems. The relevant state space is
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
, where each atom is described with three spatial coordinates. Thus, the qubit requirements of
12N
coordinate registers and
3
ancillae scale linearly with the number of atoms under study, offering a scalable path towards realistic systems. In addition to its low qubits requirements, our circuit does not make use of expensive quantum arithmetic operations. It only uses a constant number of quantum proposal and acceptance steps.

For concreteness, consider a well-known toy model where
U
is a one-dimensional two-well potential and
\pi
is the corresponding Boltzmann distribution
\pi\propto e^{-U}
. As shown on Figure
1
for a total of 27 qubits (with state registers of size
m=6
representing up to 64 classical states), the underlying unitary has a spectral gap that is quadratically larger than that of the classical process
\delta
. Indeed, its spectral gap is larger than the theoretical minimum gap of
\cos^{-1}\left(\sqrt{1-\delta/2}\right)\in\Omega\left(\sqrt{\delta}\right)
. This work therefore suggests the oracular quadratic speedup to hold for the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm in practical applications. It is supplemented by an open-source code
Rodenas Ruiz and Claudon
. The code contains additional examples and allows to apply our construction to any user-specified proposal and acceptance steps.

III.1
Ergodic Markov kernels and their mixing time

Let us define the central object of our study.

Definition 1
.

A Markov kernel is a function
P:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to[0,1]
such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{S}:\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}}P(x,y)=1.

(4)

We will only consider Markov chains on finite state spaces with a single non-degenerate eigenvalue on the unit circle. Moreover, we will assume that the Markov chains are irreducible in the sense of the following Definition
5
.

Definition 2
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel on state space
\mathbb{S}
. It is said to be irreducible if and only if

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:P^{t}(x,y)>0.

(5)

The following propositions ensure that the Markov kernels under study are such that
P^{t}
converges as
t\to\infty
to a strictly positive probability distribution on
\mathbb{S}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P
be a Markov chain with a single non-degenerate eigenvalue on the unit circle. Then, there exists a function
\pi:\mathbb{S}\to[0,1]
such that:

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:\lim_{t\to\infty}P^{t}(x,y)=\pi(y).

(6)

The above proposition suggests the following definition.

Definition 3
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel on a finite state space
\mathbb{S}
.
\pi
, defined as in Proposition
6
, is called the stationary distribution of
P
.

In this study, we are interested in chains such that
\pi
is strictly positive element-wise. This property holds for ergodic chains, defined as follows.

Definition 4
.

Let
P
be a Markov chain on a finite state space
\mathbb{S}
.
P
is said to be ergodic if it is irreducible and has a single non-degenerate eigenvalue on the unit circle.

The following proposition ensures strict positivity of the stationary distribution of an ergodic Markov chain.

Proposition 2
.

Let
P
be an ergodic Markov chain, then its stationary distribution
\pi
takes values in
]0,1[
.

If a kernel admits a strictly positive left eigenvector, it is possible to define a scalar product induced by this eigenvector.

Definition 5
.

Define the adjoint
P^{\star}
of
P
as its adjoint according to the scalar product induced by
\pi
. More precisely, for
f,g:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{C}
, define

\braket{f,g}_{\pi}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x).

(7)

Then
P^{\star}
is the unique operator such that:

\forall f,g:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{C}:\braket{f,Pg}_{\pi}=\braket{P^{\star}f,g}_{\pi}.

(8)

Let us now introduce a way to measure the distance between probability distributions.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{S}
. The total variation distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined to be:

d_{TV}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|.

(9)

Proposition
6
implies that for each probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}
,
d_{TV}(\mu P^{t},\pi)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. In particular, the following definition is well-posed.

Definition 7
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
. Denote the
\epsilon
-mixing time of a Markov chain
P
by:

\tau(\epsilon)=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{x\in\mathbb{S}}d_{TV}\left(P^{t}(x,\cdot),\pi\right)\leq\epsilon\right\}.

(10)

Definition 8
.

Define the spectral gap
\delta
of an ergodic Markov kernel
P
by:

\delta=1-\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)\backslash\{1\}}|\lambda|.

(11)

III.2
Reversible Markov chains

Definition 9
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel and
\pi
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}
. We say that
P
is reversible with respect to
\pi
if and only if:

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:\pi(x)P(x,y)=\pi(y)P(y,x).

(12)

Equivalently,
P=P^{\star}
.

Reversibility implies that
\pi
is the stationary distribution, but the converse does not hold in general.
For ergodic and reversible kernels, the relationship between the spectral gap and the mixing time is well-known
Levin et al. 2008
and given by Theorem
13
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P
be an ergodic reversible Markov chain with stationary measure
\pi
and
\epsilon\in]0,1[
. Define
\pi_{*}=\min_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)
. Then, the
\epsilon
-mixing time of
P
satisfies:

\left(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\right)\ln\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\leq\tau(\epsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\ln\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon\sqrt{\pi_{*}}}\right).

(13)

In particular, the mixing time is of the order of the inverse spectral gap.

III.3
Projected unitary encodings and qubitized walk operators

Let
\square:\mathbb{C}^{j}\to\mathbb{C}^{l}
, for
j,l\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that
\square
is a partial isometry if and only if
\square^{\dagger}\square
is the projection on the support of
\square
. In this section, we will consider partial isometries and unitary operators acting on finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Definition 10
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator and
\square_{L},\square_{R}
be partial isometries.
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
is said to be Projected Unitary Encoding (PUE) of
A=\square_{L}^{\dagger}U\square_{R}
. If
\square_{L}=\square_{R}=\square
and
U
is also symmetric,
(U,\square)
is called a Symmetric Projected Unitary Encoding (SPUE) of
A=\square^{\dagger}U\square
.

Let us now introduce qubitized walk operators.

Definition 11
.

Let
(U,\square)
be a SPUE. Denote its qubitized walk operator
\mathcal{W}
by the operator
\mathcal{W}=(2\square\square^{\dagger}-1)U
.

Definition
11
closely resembles that of the Szegedy walk operators of the form
2\square\square^{\dagger}-1
(see Definition
17
in the next subsection). These two kinds of walk operators differ only by multiplication by a symmetric
U
on the right-hand side. As stated by Theorem
2
, the added multiplication by
U
ensures that qubitized walk operators leave (up-to-)two-dimensional subspaces invariant. Their dynamics is ”qubitized”.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(U,\square)
be a SPUE of
A
. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the qubitized walk operator of
(U,\square)
. If
\lambda\in]-1,1[
is an eigenvalue of
A
with eigenvector
\ket{v}
, then
e^{\pm i\cos^{-1}(\lambda)}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{W}
with normalized eigenvectors:

\ket{\mu(\lambda)^{\pm}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin(\theta)}\left(e^{\pm i\theta}-U\right)\square\ket{v},

(14)

where
\theta=\cos^{-1}(\lambda)
. If
\pm 1
is an eigenvalue of
A
with eigenvector
\ket{v}
, then
\square\ket{v}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{W}
with the same eigenvalue. Moreover, it as a complete description of the spectrum of
\mathcal{W}
in the sense that:

\sigma\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\backslash\{\pm 1\}=\left\{e^{\pm i\cos^{-1}(\lambda)}:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\cap]-1,1[\right\}

(15)

with multiplicities.

Because of Theorem
2
, SPUEs play a central role in the design of quantum algorithms
Sünderhauf 2023
. Starting from a PUE, we will often work with its so-called hermitianization, its symmetric counterpart defined in the following Proposition
3
(see for example
Sünderhauf 2023
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\left(U,\square_{L},\square_{R}\right)
be a PUE of
A
. Define
\overline{U}=\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes U+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)\left(X\otimes 1\right)
and
\overline{\square}=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\square_{L}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\square_{R}
. Then,
\left(\overline{U},\overline{\square}\right)
is a SPUE of
\overline{A}=\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes A+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes A^{\dagger}
. In particular,
\overline{U}
can be constructed from a controlled-
U
gate, its adjoint and an
X
gate.
\left(\overline{U},\overline{\square}\right)
is called the hermitianization of
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
. Moreover, the eigenvalues of
\overline{A}
are plus or minus the singular values of
A
.

III.4
Szegedy quantization of Markov chains

Definition 12
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel. Define its quantum step operator
\square:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
by:

\square\ket{x}=\ket{x,P(x,\cdot)}=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}}\sqrt{P(x,y)}\ket{x,y}.

(16)

Furthermore, define the Szegedy walk operator
R:\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
of
P
as

R=2\square\square^{\dagger}-1.

(17)

We note that the Szegedy walk operator is the qubitized walk operator of
(1,\square)
. Indeed,

\begin{split}\square^{\dagger}\square&=\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\ket{x}\bra{x,P(x,\cdot)}\right)\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}}\ket{y,P(y,\cdot)}\bra{y}\right)\\
&=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\ket{x}\bra{x},\end{split}

(18)

and
\square
is a partial isometry. Moreover, if
(O,1,\ket{0})
is a PUE of the quantum step operator
\square
, then we can construct the unitary
R
using the equation:

R=2O\ket{0}\bra{0}O^{\dagger}-1=O\left(2\ket{0}\bra{0}-1\right)O^{\dagger}.

(19)

Since reflecting through the
\ket{0}
state is an easy task, it is sufficient to construct such PUEs of the quantum step operator to implement the Szegedy walk operator efficiently. Let
S:\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
be the SWAP operator
S\ket{x,y}=\ket{y,x}
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
P
be a reversible ergodic kernel with stationary measure
\pi
and spectral gap
\delta
. Then,
(S,\square)
is a SPUE of
D=\square^{\dagger}S\square
where the discriminant
D
of
P
is:

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:D(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(y)}}P(x,y).

(20)

In particular,
\square\ket{\pi}
is the only eigenvector of
\mathcal{W}
, the qubitized walk operator of
(S,\square)
, in the range of
\square
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{W}
has a quadratically amplified angular gap
\Delta
compared to
P
:

\begin{split}\Delta&=\min\left\{\theta\neq 0:e^{i\theta}\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})\right\}\\
&=\cos^{-1}\left(\max\{\sigma(P)\backslash\{1\}\}\right)\\
&\in\Omega\left(\sqrt{\delta}\right).\end{split}

(21)

In particular, the spectral gap of the qubitized walk operator is quadratically larger than that of the Markov process.

III.5
Metropolis-Hastings algorithm

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
Hastings 1970
aims at sampling from a probability distribution
\pi:\mathbb{S}\to]0,1[
while only being able to compute ratios of equilibrium probabilities
\pi(x)/\pi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
. It consists in designing a reversible ergodic Markov kernel
P
from a proposal kernel
T
and move acceptance probabilities
A
. Starting from a state
x\in\mathbb{S}
, propose a new state
y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}
according to the distribution
T(x,\cdot)
. Then, accept this new state with probability
A(x,y)
. Otherwise, stay in state
x
. Figure
2
illustrates the proposal-acceptance MH steps from a state with two neighbours.

Figure 2
:
Graphical representation of a MH step. Step 1. The process is in the state
x
, with neighbours
y,z
. Step 2. Select the outgoing edge
(x,y)
with probability
T(x,y)
and the outgoing edge
(x,z)
with probability
T(x,z)
. Step 3. Given the current edge
(a,b)
, let the process be in state
b
with probability
A(a,b)
and be in state
a
with probability
1-A(a,b)
.

Definition 13
.

Let
T
be a Markov kernel on
\mathbb{S}
. It is said to be a valid proposal kernel if
T(x,y)>0\implies T(y,x)>0
for every
x\neq y\in\mathbb{S}
and
T(x,x)=0
for each
x\in\mathbb{S}
. Let
A:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to]0,1]
be the so-called acceptance probability matrix. Define the Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
associated with
T
and
A
by:

P(x,y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &T(x,y)A(x,y)\text{ if $x\neq y$,}\\
&1-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}}T(x,z)A(x,z)\text{ if $x=y$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
.

The reversibility condition can be written:

\pi(x)T(x,y)A(x,y)=\pi(y)T(y,x)A(y,x),

(23)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
and can be satisfied by carefully choosing the matrix
A
.

Definition 14
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{S}\to]0,1[
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{S}
and
T
be a valid proposal kernel. If for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
such that
T(x,y)>0
we have

A(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\pi(x)T(x,y)}{\pi(y)T(y,x)}},

(24)

the Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
defined from
T
and
A
is called the Metropolis-Hastings kernel with Glauber choice.

Definition 15
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{S}\to]0,1[
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{S}
and
T
be a valid proposal kernel. If for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
such that
T(x,y)>0
we have

A(x,y)=\min\left(1,\frac{\pi(y)T(y,x)}{\pi(x)T(x,y)}\right),

(25)

the Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
defined from
T
and
A
is called the Metropolis-Hastings kernel with Metropolis choice.

If
P
is a Metropolis-Hastings kernel with Glauber or Metropolis choice, then Equation
23
is clearly satisfied.

Proposition 5
.

Let
P
be a Metropolis-Hastings kernel with Glauber or Metropolis choice. Then,
P
is reversible with respect to
\pi
.

IV
Results

IV.1
Problem statement

Let
P
be an ergodic and reversible Metropolis-Hastings kernel with stationary measure
\pi
. The classical simulation of
P
consists in sampling from a proposal kernel
T
and the computation of the matrix elements of an acceptance probability matrix
A:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to[0,1]
. Hence, we only want to rely on the simulation of the proposal kernel, using a PUE
(O_{T},1,\ket{0})
of
\square_{T}
defined by:

O_{T}\ket{0}=\square_{T}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\ket{x,T(x,\cdot)}\bra{x},

(26)

and a PUE
(O_{A},1,\ket{0})
such that:

\begin{split}&\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:O_{A}\ket{x,y}\ket{0}\\
&=\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,y}\ket{0}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y}\ket{1}.\end{split}

(27)

Using solely
O_{T}
and
O_{A}
, our goal is to construct the quantum update step of a reversible Markov kernel with stationary measure
\pi
and the same spectral gap as
P
. We will construct the quantum step operator of a Markov kernel on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with stationary measure
\pi(x)T(x,y)
. The first marginal of this distribution is precisely
\pi
. Moreover, this kernel has exactly the same mixing time as
P
(under the Glauber choice). Hence, its spectral gap is of the same order of magnitude as that of
P
and the quadratic speedup is maintained. Since it does not require performing arithmetic on qubits registers, our construction significantly reduces the resource requirements of the MH Szegedy quantization.

IV.2
Dual Metropolis-Hastings kernel

In this subsection, we introduce Markov processes on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the set of pairs of states. The enlarged state space allows to keep track of rejected samples, facilitating their implementation through invertible unitary operations. For every pair
(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we will refer to
x
as the tail and
y
as the head. Denote by
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
the set of edges of the proposal kernel’s graph:

\mathcal{S}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:T(x,y)>0\}.

(28)

For any probability measure
F
on the edges in
\mathcal{S}
, we will refer to the probability distribution of the tails as the first marginal
\mu
and the probability distribution of the heads given the tail as the conditional distribution
Q
. In particular,
F(x,y)=\mu(x)Q(x,y)
for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
,
\mu
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{S}
and
Q
a Markov kernel on
\mathbb{S}
.

IV.2.1
Dual proposal
\mathcal{T}
and acceptance
\mathcal{A}
kernels

Let us introduce the dual proposal kernel on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Given a pair of states in
\mathbb{S}
, the dual proposal kernel proposes a new head while keeping the same tail.

Definition 16
.

Define the Markov kernel
\mathcal{T}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by:

\forall(x,y),(z,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:\mathcal{T}((x,y),(z,t))=\delta_{x}(z)T(x,t).

(29)

Sampling a new edge from
\mathcal{T}
always results in an edge in
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore, our subsequent analysis can safely be reduced to
\mathcal{S}
. Given a probability distribution on
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{T}
returns a probability distribution with the same first marginal but with conditional distribution replaced by
T
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F:\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
be a probability measure with first marginal
\mu
and conditional
Q
. Then,
F\mathcal{T}
has first marginal
\mu
and conditional
T
.

Proof
.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. Then,

\begin{split}F\mathcal{T}(x,y)&=\sum_{(z,t)\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(z)Q(z,t)\delta_{x}(z)T(x,y)\\
&=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}}\mu(z)\delta_{x}(z)T(x,y)\\
&=\mu(x)T(x,y).\end{split}

(30)

∎

We will now introduce a key measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, whose first marginal is
\pi
and whose support is
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 17
.

Define the probability measure
\nu:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to[0,1]
by:

\nu(x,y)=\pi(x)T(x,y),

(31)

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
.

Whereas the proposal kernel
T
is not necessarily reversible with respect to any measure, its dual
\mathcal{T}
is reversible with respect to
\nu
. From now on, the
⋆
superscript will always denote the adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\nu}
.

Proposition 6
.

\mathcal{T}
is reversible with respect to
\nu
.

Proof
.

Let
(x,y),(z,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then,

\begin{split}\nu(x,y)\mathcal{T}((x,y),(z,t))&=\pi(x)T(x,y)\delta_{x}(z)T(x,t)\\
&=\pi(z)T(z,y)\delta_{z}(x)T(z,t)\\
&=\nu(z,t)\delta_{z}(x)T(z,y)\\
&=\nu(z,t)\mathcal{T}((z,t),(x,y)).\end{split}

(32)

∎

Let us now define the dual
\mathcal{A}
of the acceptance matrix.
\mathcal{A}
flips the input edge
(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
to
(y,x)
with probability given by the acceptance matrix
A
. Otherwise, the edge remains in state
(x,y)
. Compared to the MH algorithm, the rejected sample is not completely forgotten but stored in the head.

Definition 18
.

Define the Markov kernel
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by:

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{A}((x,y),(y,x))=A(x,y)\\
&\mathcal{A}((x,y),(x,y))=1-A(x,y).\end{aligned}\right.

(33)

The reversibility condition
23
implies that
\mathcal{A}
has to be reversible with respect to
\nu
.

Proposition 7
.

If
P
is a reversible Markov kernel, then
\mathcal{A}
is reversible with respect to
\nu
.

Proof
.

Let
x\neq y\in\mathbb{S}
. Then,

\begin{split}\nu(x,y)\mathcal{A}((x,y),(y,x))&=\pi(x)T(x,y)A(x,y)\\
&=\pi(x)P(x,y)\\
&=\pi(y)P(y,x)\\
&=\pi(y)T(y,x)A(y,x)\\
&=\nu(y,x)\mathcal{A}((y,x),(x,y)),\end{split}

(34)

using the reversibility condition of
P
, Equation
23
.
∎

We can now define the dual Metropolis-Hastings kernel
\mathcal{P}
of
P
.

Definition 19
.

Define the dual Metropolis-Hastings kernel
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by the equation
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{TA}
.

\mathcal{P}
acts on the first marginals as
P
, as stated precisely by Lemma
2
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
F:\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
be a probability measure with first marginal
\mu
. Then,
F\mathcal{P}
has first marginal
\mu P
.

Proof
.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. By Lemma
1
,
F\mathcal{T}(x,y)=\mu(x)T(x,y)
for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore,

F\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}(x,y)=\mu(x)T(x,y)(1-A(x,y))+\mu(y)T(y,x)A(y,x).

(35)

Computing the first marginal,

\begin{split}&\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}}F\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}(x,y)\\
&=\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(x)T(x,y)(1-A(x,y))+\mu(y)T(y,x)A(y,x)\\
&=\mu(x)P(x,x)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}\mu(y)P(y,x)\\
&=\mu P(x).\end{split}

(36)

∎

Since
\mathcal{P}
is the product of two reversible kernels,
\nu
is a stationary measure of
\mathcal{P}
. However,
\mathcal{P}
is not necessarily reversible with respect to
\nu
. Still, a step of
\mathcal{P}
followed by a step of
\mathcal{T}
results in a reversible process. Proposition
8
describes the action of
\mathcal{TAT}
on the marginal and condition distributions. Figure
3
illustrates how
\mathcal{TAT}
selects the next edge.

Proposition 8
.

For every probability distribution
F
on
\mathcal{S}
with first marginal
\mu
,

\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}:F\mathcal{TAT}(x,y)=(\mu P)(x)T(x,y).

(37)

In words,
F\mathcal{TAT}
has first marginal
\mu P
and conditional
T
.

Proof
.

By Lemma
2
,\mathcal{TA}=\mathcal{P}
applies
P
to the first marginal. By Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{T}
takes any conditional distribution to
T
and does not affect the first marginal.
∎

:
Graphical representation of a
\mathcal{TAT}
step. Step 1. The process is in the state
(x,y)
. Step 2. Select the new head
y
with probability

and the new head

Proposition
8
directly implies that
\mathcal{TAT}
is ergodic (on its essential states
\mathcal{S}
) and has the same mixing times as
P
. In particular, its spectral gap must be of the same order as that of
P
. As it turns out, we can deduce a lot more from Proposition
8
.

Corollary 1
.

Let us consider the restriction of the dual kernel to
\mathcal{S}
. The Markov kernel
\mathcal{TAT}
is reversible with respect to
\nu
. Moreover,
\mathcal{TAT}
and
P
have the same spectrum, up to trivial eigenvalues:

\sigma(P)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{TAT})\subset\sigma(P)\cup\{0\}.

(38)

In particular,
\mathcal{TAT}
is ergodic. Note that considering
\mathcal{TAT}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
only adds
0
eigenvalues.

Proof
.

The first claim follows from
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{\star}
(Proposition
6
) and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{\star}
(Proposition
7
). Let now
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{TAT})
. Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
be a real left eigenvector of
\mathcal{TAT}
. Decompose
f
as the sum of its positive and negative parts:
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
, where
f_{\pm}:\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
. Without loss of generality,
f
can be normalized such that
f_{+}+f_{-}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{S}
. Define, for every
x\in\mathbb{S}
,

\mu_{\pm}(x)=\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}}f_{\pm}(x,y).

(39)

Then,

f_{\pm}(x,y)=\mu_{\pm}(x)Q_{\pm}(x,y),

(40)

for some Markov kernels
Q_{\pm}
on
\mathbb{S}
. With these notations,

f(x,y)=\mu_{+}(x)Q_{+}(x,y)-\mu_{-}(x)Q_{-}(x,y).

(41)

Using Proposition
8
, the eigenvalue equation can be written as:

\lambda f(x,y)=((\mu_{+}P)(x)-(\mu_{-}P)(x))T(x,y),

(42)

for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. If
\mu_{+}=\mu_{-}
, then
\lambda f=0
and
\lambda=0
. Let us now assume that
\mu_{+}\neq\mu_{-}
. Summing over all
y\in\mathbb{S}
,

\lambda(\mu_{+}-\mu_{-})=(\mu_{+}-\mu_{-})P,

(43)

namely
\lambda\in\sigma(P)
. Therefore,
\sigma(\mathcal{TAT})\subset\sigma(P)\cup\{0\}
. Let now
\lambda\in\sigma(P)
. Define
f:\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
by:

f(x,y)=g(x)T(x,y),

(44)

where
g
is a left eigenvector of
P
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Decomposing
g=\mu_{+}-\mu-
with
\mu_{\pm}:\mathbb{S}\to[0,1]
. Using Proposition
8
,

\begin{split}f\mathcal{TAT}(x,y)&=((\mu_{+}P)(x)-(\mu_{-}P)(x))T(x,y)\\
&=\lambda f(x,y),\end{split}

(45)

for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore
\sigma(P)\subset\sigma(\mathcal{TAT})
.
∎

Any SPUE of the discriminant of
\mathcal{TAT}
would therefore have a qubitized walk operator of angular gap
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\delta}\right)
. We can easily construct such a SPUE from the step operators of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{A}
with
4
edge registers. In what follows, we provide a construction of the same efficiency with only
2
edge registers.

IV.3
Quantum circuits for the dual quantum walk

In this subsection, we describe the encoding of the quantum step operators of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\star}
. Let us start with a technical modification of
O_{A}
. Even though we are mainly interested in its action on states
\ket{x,y,x}
for
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
, we make sure that it preserves the
\ket{0}
state of the ancilla for every possible input state.

Lemma 3
.

We can implement a circuit
O_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
(O_{\mathcal{A}},\ket{0},\ket{0})
is a PUE of the transformation which does:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ket{x,y,x}&\to\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,x,y}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x},\\
\ket{x,x,z}&\to-\sqrt{A(x,z)}\ket{x,x,z}+\sqrt{1-A(x,z)}\ket{x,z,x},\\
\ket{x,y,z}&\to\ket{x,z,y},\end{aligned}\right.

(46)

for every
x,y,z\in\mathbb{S}
. Considering a generic input state
\ket{x,y,z}
, the first line corresponds to
x\neq y
, the second to
x\neq z
and the third to the other cases. In particular,

\begin{split}&\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}:\bra{0}(O_{\mathcal{A}}\ket{0})\ket{x,y,x}\\
&=\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,x,y}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x}.\end{split}

(47)

Proof
.

Impose that
A(x,x)=0
for every
x\in\mathbb{S}
. Distinguish the possible input states:

\ket{x,y,z}\ket{0}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\ket{x,y,x}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,z}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,y,z}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(48)

with
x,y,z\in\mathbb{S}
all pairwise different. Flip the ancilla if the first two registers are in the same state:

\to\left\{\begin{aligned} &\ket{x,y,x}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,z}\ket{1},\\
&\ket{x,y,z}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(49)

To perform this operation, the first register is copied to the second by a succession of bitwise CNOT gates. If
x=y
, the second register will be in the state
\ket{x\oplus x}=\ket{0}
. A multi-controlled Toffoli gate, with the second register being on state
\ket{0}
as control and the ancilla as target, achieves the desired result. Finally, we uncompute the copy operation with another succession of bitwise CNOT gates from the first register to the second.

If the ancilla is in state
\ket{1}
, swap the second and third registers,

\to\left\{\begin{aligned} &\ket{x,y,x}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,z,x}\ket{1},\\
&\ket{x,y,z}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(50)

Now apply a controlled-
O_{A}
gate, controlled by the third register being equal to the first, which leads to:

\to\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x}\ket{0}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x}\ket{1},\\
&-\sqrt{A(x,z)}\ket{x,z,x}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-A(x,z)}\ket{x,z,x}\ket{1},\\
&\ket{x,y,z}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(51)

Since
A(x,x)=0
, the gate has no effect on the state
\ket{x,x,x}\ket{1}
. If the ancilla is in state
\ket{0}
, swap the second and third registers:

\to\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,x,y}\ket{0}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x}\ket{1},\\
&-\sqrt{A(x,z)}\ket{x,x,z}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-A(x,z)}\ket{x,z,x}\ket{1},\\
&\ket{x,z,y}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(52)

If the first and third registers are equal, flip the ancilla:

\to\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{1-A(x,y)}\ket{x,x,y}\ket{0}+\sqrt{A(x,y)}\ket{x,y,x}\ket{0},\\
&-\sqrt{A(x,z)}\ket{x,x,z}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-A(x,z)}\ket{x,z,x}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,z,y}\ket{0},\\
&\ket{x,x,x}\ket{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(53)

∎

IV.4
Gap of the qubitized walk operator

The purpose of this subsection is to show that the qubitized walk operator has a quadratically amplified spectral gap. We prove this result by showing that

IV.4.1
The Glauber case

Corollary 2
.

Under the Glauber choice, the spectral gap
\delta_{\star}
of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
is equal to that of
P
:
\delta=\delta_{\star}
.

Proof
.

Under the Glauber choice,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{2}
. This follows from the fact that
A(x,y)+A(y,x)=1
, which implies:

\begin{split}&\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}((x,y),(x,y))&\mathcal{A}((x,y),(y,x))\\
\mathcal{A}((y,x),(x,y))&\mathcal{A}((y,x),(y,x))\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}1-A(x,y)&A(x,y)\\
A(y,x)&1-A(y,x)\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}1-A(x,y)&A(x,y)\\
1-A(x,y)&A(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}1-A(x,y)&A(x,y)\\
1-A(x,y)&A(x,y)\end{pmatrix}^{2}\end{split}

(63)

for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
.
\mathcal{A}
is a direct sum of perfectly mixed Markov kernels. Therefore,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}=\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}^{2}\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{TAT}
. Conclude with Corollary
1
.
∎

We now obtain an upper bound for the second largest eigenvalue of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
.

Corollary 3
.

The second largest eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
satisfies
\lambda=\sqrt{1-\delta_{\star}}
. In particular,

1-\lambda=1-\sqrt{1-\delta_{\star}}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}-\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{2}\right),

(64)

where
\delta
is the spectral gap of the Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
with Glauber choice.

Proof
.

The second largest eigenvalue of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is the second largest singular value of
\mathcal{P}
(with respect the scalar product
\braket{\cdot,\cdot}_{\nu}
). By definition, it is the square root of the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
. Thus,
\lambda=\sqrt{1-\delta_{\star}}
and the equality of Equation
64
holds true. The inequality follows from Corollary
2
.
∎

IV.4.2
General acceptance matrix

Under the Metropolis choice and when targeting the uniform distribution with a symmetric proposal kernel,
\mathcal{A}^{2}=1
. As a consequence,

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}=\mathcal{T}^{2}=\mathcal{T}.

(65)

In particular,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
is not ergodic. By replacing
A
by
A/2
, namely implementing the lazy Metropolis-Hastings kernel, we can ensure that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
is ergodic with a mixing time equal to that of
P
, up to a multiplicative constant. In this subsection,
\mathcal{P}
will always denote the dual kernel of
(1+P)/2
.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\delta
be the spectral gap of a reversible ergodic Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the dual kernel of
(1+P)/2
. Then, the spectral gap
\delta_{\star}
of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
satisfies:

\delta_{\star}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(66)

Proof
.

Since
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}}{2}\right)
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}&=\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}}{2}\right)^{2}\mathcal{T}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{T}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{T}.\end{split}

(67)

Recall from Corollary
1
that
\mathcal{TAT}
has a spectral gap
\delta
. Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
be such that
\langle f,f\rangle_{\nu}=1
and
\nu f=0
. The variational principle gives:

\left\langle f,\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{T}f\right\rangle_{\nu}\leq 1-\delta.

(68)

\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{T}
is also reversible and such that:

\left\langle f,\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{T}f\right\rangle_{\nu}\leq 1.

(69)

Then,

\begin{split}\left\langle f,\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}f\right\rangle_{\nu}&=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle f,\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{T}f\right\rangle_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle f,\mathcal{T}\left(\frac{1+\mathcal{A}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{T}f\right\rangle_{\nu}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\delta\right)+\frac{1}{2}\\
&=1-\frac{\delta}{2}.\end{split}

(70)

Maximizing over all possible
f
, the variational principle implies that the spectral gap
\delta_{\star}
of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}^{\star}
satisfies:

\delta_{\star}\geq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(71)

∎

We now obtain an upper bound for the second largest eigenvalue of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
.

Corollary 4
.

The second largest eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
satisfies
\lambda=\sqrt{1-\delta_{\star}}
. In particular,

1-\lambda=1-\sqrt{1-\delta_{\star}}\geq\frac{\delta}{4}-\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{2}\right),

(72)

where
\delta
is the spectral gap of the Metropolis-Hastings kernel
P
with Glauber choice.

Proof
.

Repeat the steps of the proof of Corollary
3

∎

IV.4.3
Main result

We found a lower bound for the second largest eigenvalue of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
for a general MH kernel. We will now turn this result into a lower bound for the angular gap of the qubitized walk operator
\mathcal{W}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
P
be an ergodic reversible Metropolis-Hastings kernel with proposal kernel
T
, acceptance matrix
A
, stationary distribution
\pi
and spectral gap
\delta
. Using a constant number of the uses of
O_{T}
and
O_{A}
, we can construct a unitary
\mathcal{W}
with unique
1
-eigenvector

\boxtimes\ket{+}\ket{\nu}\ket{0},

(73)

in the range of a partial isometry
\boxtimes
, the reflection through which can also be implemented with a constant number of the oracles
O_{T}
and
O_{A}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{W}
has an angular gap in
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\delta}\right)
.

Proof
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be the qubitized walk operator of the hermitianization
(\mathcal{U},\boxtimes)
of the PUE
(S,\square_{\star},\square)
. Recall that
\square
is the quantum update step operator of
\mathcal{P}
(defined in Proposition
9
) and
\square_{\star}
is the quantum step operator of
\mathcal{P}^{\star}
(defined in Proposition
10
). By definition (see Proposition
3
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &2\boxtimes\boxtimes^{\dagger}-1&=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\left(2\square_{\star}\square_{\star}^{\dagger}-1\right)\\
&&+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\left(2\square\square^{\dagger}-1\right),\\
&\mathcal{U}=X\otimes S.&\end{aligned}\right.

(74)

Propositions
9
,
10
and Equation
19
show how to construct
2\square_{\star}\square_{\star}^{\dagger}-1
and
2\square\square^{\dagger}-1
with a constant number of uses of
O_{T}
and
O_{A}
.
2\boxtimes\boxtimes^{\dagger}-1
is a product of their
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
controlled versions.
\mathcal{U}
is the tensor product of an
X
gate on the ancilla and a register SWAP operator. The qubitized walk operator
\mathcal{W}=\left(2\boxtimes\boxtimes^{\dagger}-1\right)\mathcal{U}
can therefore be implemented as shown on Figure
5
, from a constant number of
O_{A}
and
O_{T}
. That its only
1
-eigenvector in the range of
\boxtimes
is
\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}
is a consequence of Proposition
11
and Theorem
2
. Corollary
4
states that
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
(and therefore
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
) has a spectral gap in
\Omega(\delta)
. Theorem
2
finally ensures that the angular gap of
\mathcal{W}
is in
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\delta}\right)
.
∎

Proposition
13
shows that we can implement a unitary mapping
\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}
to
\ket{\pi}
with a constant number of
O_{T}
and
O_{A}

Proposition 13
.

The following equation holds true:

\begin{split}&\ket{+}\ket{\pi}\ket{0,0}\\
&=O_{T}^{\dagger}O^{\dagger}\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes S+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes 1\right)\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}.\end{split}

(75)

Here,
O_{T}^{\dagger}
means
1\otimes O_{T}^{\dagger}\otimes 1
and
O^{\dagger}
means
1\otimes O^{\dagger}
.

Proof
.

Since
\bra{\nu}\left(\square_{\star}^{\dagger}S\square\ket{\nu}\right)=\bra{\nu}(\mathcal{D}\ket{\nu})=1
, we have
\square_{\star}\ket{\nu}=S\square\ket{\nu}
and:

\begin{split}\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}\otimes\square_{\star}\ket{\nu}+\ket{1}\otimes\square\ket{\nu}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}\otimes S\square\ket{\nu}+\ket{1}\otimes\square\ket{\nu}\right)\\
&=\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes S+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes 1\right)\ket{+}\square\ket{\nu}.\end{split}

(76)

Thus,

\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes S+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes 1\right)\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}=\ket{+}\square\ket{\nu}.

(77)

Because
(O,1,\ket{0})
is a PUE of
\square
, we also have
\square\ket{\nu}=O\ket{\nu}\ket{0}
. In particular,

\ket{+}\ket{\nu}\ket{0}=O^{\dagger}\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes S+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes 1\right)\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}

(78)

(writing
O^{\dagger}
for
1\otimes O^{\dagger}
) and we can prepare
\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}
from
\ket{+,\nu}
. The first marginal of
\nu
is
\pi
, so preparing
\ket{\nu}

\pi

\ket{\pi}

\ket{\nu}=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{\pi(x)T(x,y)}\ket{x,y}=O_{T}\ket{\pi}\ket{0}.

(79)

Therefore, writing
O_{T}^{\dagger}
for
1\otimes O_{T}^{\dagger}\otimes 1

\begin{split}&\ket{+}\ket{\pi}\ket{0,0}\\
&=O_{T}^{\dagger}O^{\dagger}\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes S+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes 1\right)\boxtimes\ket{+,\nu}.\end{split}

(80)
