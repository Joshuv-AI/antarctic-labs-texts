Research frontiers in ambit stochastics: In memory of Ole E. Barndorff-Nielsen
Fred Espen Benth, Almut E. D. Veraart

Abstract
This article surveys key aspects of ambit stochastics and remembers Ole E. Barndorff-Nielsen’s important contributions to the foundation and advancement of this new research field over the last two decades. It also highlights some of the emerging trends in ambit stochastics.



Keywords:
Ambit fields, trawl processes, Lévy semistationary processes, stochastic volatility, limit theory, turbulence, stochastic growth model

1
Introduction

This article remembers and celebrates
Ole E. Barndorff-Nielsen’s contributions to ambit stochastics and points towards recent developments in this field.

Throughout his research, Ole was fascinated by developing stochastic models for turbulence that align with the
Statistical Theory of Turbulence
as described by Kolmogorov and Obukhov, see
Kolmogorov (1941)
;
Obukhov (1941)
and also
Frisch (1995)
.
This endeavour led to the introduction of a new field in probability, termed ambit stochastics.

Ambit fields and processes are powerful tools for modelling random phenomena in space-time, see the relevant survey papers

Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2015)
;
Podolskij (2015)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2016)
;
Schmiegel (2018)
. Also, the recent monograph

Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2018)
gives a comprehensive summary of some of the key results in the area of ambit stochastics that were developed until 2018.

Let us briefly review the definition of an ambit field.
Let
Y=(Y(\mathbf{x},t))
denote a random field taking values in
\mathbb{R}
.
Here,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
) and
t\in\mathbb{R}
are coordinates in space and time, respectively. For instance, we can think of
Y
describing the wind speed at time
t
in location
\mathbf{x}
.
Since general random fields are often difficult to deal with, we typically impose some structural or model assumptions to achieve mathematical tractability. At the same time, we want to ensure that the model class we consider is very flexible and can describe empirical data.
Ambit fields provide a flexible, yet analytically tractable class of random field models, which go far beyond the widely used Gaussian random fields.

The key component of an ambit field takes the form

Y(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{A(\mathbf{x},t)}g(\mathbf{x},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{\xi},s)L_{\mathcal{T}}(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds).

(1)

Here,
A(\mathbf{x},t)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
is referred to as the ambit set which can vary with the space-time coordinates
(\mathbf{x},t)
.
Moreover, the integrand consists of a product of a deterministic kernel function
g
and a stochastic volatility/intermittency field
\sigma
. Finally,
L
is a Lévy basis, that is, a particular random noise term which generalises (Gaussian) white noise and Lévy processes to space-time settings.
For some applications, it might be useful to consider a Lévy basis which is subordinated by a metatime
\mathcal{T}
, see
Barndorff-Nielsen & Pedersen (2012)
, in which case we write
L_{\mathcal{T}}
.

1.1
Stochastic volatility/intermittency

Ole was particularly interested in the concept of stochastic volatility (a term widely used in financial econometrics) and/or intermittency (a term more dominant in the turbulence literature). Volatility/intermittency are relative concepts and need to be understood as additional fluctuations besides those one would otherwise anticipate. Here the stochastic volatility/intermittency field/process
\sigma

introduces such additional variability via spatial scaling of the Lévy noise
L
.
What is more, Ole always stressed the importance of modelling stochastic volatility/intermittency via stochastic time change or, more generally, the concept of metatimes.

Ole contributed widely to the literature on stochastic time change for stochastic processes, see, amongst others,
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2001)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2001)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen & Pérez-Abreu (2008)
and wrote the influential book on
Change of Time and Change of Measure
, see
Barndorff-Nielsen & Shiryaev (2015)
.
In order to also cover spatial and spatio-temporal settings, Ole introduced the concept of metatimes in the paper
Barndorff-Nielsen & Pedersen (2012)
.

Early work in the area of ambit stochastics focusses predominantly on modelling questions. For instance, together with Jürgen Schmiegel, Ole had a long-standing collaboration on working on stochastic models for turbulence, with the first paper mentioning the ideas of ambit fields in this context being
Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2007)
.

1.2
The role of the ambit set and the kernel function

A key feature in the definition of an ambit field is the

ambit set

A(\mathbf{x},t)
.
The term
ambit
has been derived from the Latin word
ambitus
which means
border, boundary or sphere of influence
. Here, at each point in space-time
(\mathbf{x},t)
, the ambit set describes the relevant region over which random noise (described via the Lévy basis
L
) needs to be taken into account to model
Y(\mathbf{x},t)
.
Intuitive examples of ambit sets include the light or sound cone.

The reader might wonder, why ambit fields were defined as in (
1
)
rather than using the simpler expression

Y(\mathbf{x},t)=\int G(\mathbf{x},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)L_{\mathcal{T}}(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds),

where
G(\mathbf{x},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)=\mathbb{I}_{A(\mathbf{x},t)}(\boldsymbol{\xi},s)g(\mathbf{x},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{\xi},s)
.
The reason for including the ambit sets explicitly is that they give the modeller a means to directly include any boundaries for the sphere of influence of the random fluctuations in space-time.
Also, by separating the deterministic kernel function
g
from a stochastic volatility component
\sigma
, we will later be able to derive tailor-made inference approaches for various applications of interest.
That said, it will always be important to investigate the identifiability of the particular models used.

1.3
Outline

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. Section
2
surveys some of the key results on statistical inference for stochastic volatility in ambit fields and related processes.
Section
3
discusses the important relation between ambit fields and stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) and surveys some of the recent advances in volatility estimation for infinite-dimensional settings, directly extending results discussed in Section
2
.
Next, we showcase some of the key application areas of ambit fields in
Section
4
, and
Section
5
concludes and gives an outlook on some of the latest advances in ambit stochastics.

2
Statistical inference for stochastic volatility and intermittency

We have discussed the importance of including stochastic volatility in the modelling and will now study the question of how volatility can be estimated.
Crucially, the answer to this question will depend on the particular structure of the process and we will pick three stylised cases to explain the general theory; in all these cases we will focus on ambit processes driven by a Brownian motion and will give references to results in the Lévy case below.

We will begin our study with a purely temporal setting focussing on Brownian semistationary processes. Here we need to distinguish two cases: the semimartingale and the non-semimartingale case. Finally, we review relevant findings for ambit processes in space-time.

2.1
Volatility estimation for BSS processes

An important class of ambit fields is obtained in the null-spatial or purely temporal case, often restricted to a (semi)-stationary setting.

In
Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2009)
, the Brownian semistationary (BSS) process was introduced as follows. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}},\mathbb{P})
denote a probability space and

W
an
(\mathcal{F}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
. The deterministic (kernel) function

g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is assumed to satisfy
g(t)=0
for
t\leq 0
and
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and the
(\mathcal{F}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
-adapted càdlàg stochastic volatility process is denoted by

\sigma=(\sigma(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.
We shall assume that,
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}g^{2}(t-s)\sigma^{2}(s)ds<\infty,\quad\text{almost surely}.

The BSS process (without drift term) is defined as
(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
with

\displaystyle Y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}g(t-s)\sigma(s)dW(s),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

A BSS process becomes a Lévy semistationary process if the Brownian motion
W
is replaced by a general Lévy process
L
.

Following Ole’s fruitful work on volatility estimation in semimartingale settings, highlighted in Neil Shephard’s review, see
Shephard (2024)
, Ole was interested in extending the ideas of realised variance and more general realised power variations to BSS processes. Related to this endavour is the question of under what conditions BSS processes are semimartingales.

Sufficient conditions for a BSS process to be a semimartingale were established in
Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2009)
and we will state them briefly.
Suppose the following conditions hold:
(i) The function value
g(0+)
exists and is finite;
(ii) the kernel function
g
is absolutely continuous with square-integrable derivative
g^{\prime}
;
(iii) the process
(g^{\prime}(t-s)\sigma(s))_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is square integrable for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a semimartingale with representation

\displaystyle Y(t)=Y(0)+g(0+)\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW(s)+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)ds,\qquad\text{for }t\geq 0,

where, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

\displaystyle a(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{s}g^{\prime}(s-u)\sigma(u)dW(u).

We note that, in the semimartingale case, the BSS process can be expressed via a stochastic differential equation (SDE), as follows

\displaystyle dY(t)=g(0+)\sigma(t)dW(t)+a(t)dt.

2.1.1
The semimartingale case

Consider the BSS process defined in (
2
). In the special case when a BSS process is a semimartingale, then the classic theory of volatility estimation via realised variance and power variation applies.

Suppose we observe the BSS process
over a time interval
[0,T]
for
T>0
at the observation times

0,\Delta_{n},2\Delta_{n},\dots,\lfloor T/\Delta_{n}\rfloor\Delta_{n}
,
where
\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function.
The volatility estimation is carried out based on an

in-fill asymptotics
framework,
where

\Delta_{n}\to 0
and the length
of the observation interval
T
is fixed.
Let us write

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{n}Y:=Y(i\Delta_{n})-Y((i-1)\Delta_{n}),\quad\text{ for }i=1,\dots,\lfloor T/\Delta_{n}\rfloor,

for
the increments of the BSS process
Y
.

In the following, we will denote by

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{u.c.p.}}}{{\rightarrow}}
uniform convergence on compacts in probability, see e.g.
Protter (2005)
, and by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

the concept of stable convergence in law, see e.g.
Rényi (1963)
.

The quadratic variation of
Y
in
[0,T]
is given by

\displaystyle[Y](t)=g(0+)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds,\qquad\text{for}\;t\in[0,T].

The quadratic variation contains the accumulated squared volatility over the interval
[0,t]
. It can be estimated consistently, using
realised variance
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(\Delta_{i}^{n}Y)^{2},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

(3)

We will state a weak law of large numbers and a central limit theorem for realised variance. These were first derived in
Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2002)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2003)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard (2004)
and later generalised in
Jacod (2008, Theorems 2.4 and 2.9)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that the volatility process
\sigma
is càdlàg and adapted.

(i)

Law of large numbers: As
\Delta_{n}\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{u.c.p.}}}{{\rightarrow}}g^{2}(0+)\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds,\quad\text{ as }\Delta_{n}\to 0.

(ii)

Central limit theorem: As
\Delta_{n}\to 0
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)-g^{2}(0+)\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sqrt{2}g^{2}(0+)\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)dB(s),

on the Skorohod space
\mathcal{D}([0,T])
equipped with the uniform topology. Here,
B
denotes a Brownian motion defined on an extension of the original probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which is independent of
\mathcal{F}
.

Realised variance and extensions to other realised variation measures have been studied extensively in the last 25 years. For instance, jump-robust estimators have been developed by Ole and collaborators, see the work on realised multipower variation by
Barndorff-Nielsen, Graversen, Jacod, Podolskij & Shephard (2006)
and
Barndorff-Nielsen, Shephard & Winkel (2006)
and also the survey by
Shephard (2024)
. Around the same time, truncated realised variance was introduced, see
Mancini (2001)
,
Mancini (2004)
,
Mancini (2009)

and also
Jacod (2008)
.
Some of these developments were further summarised in
Jacod & Protter (2012)
and
Aït-Sahalia & Jacod (2014)
.

2.1.2
The non-semimartingale case

Ole’s work on ambit stochastics was greatly influenced by his interest in stochastic models for turbulence. Here, it turns out that the non-semimartingale case is of greater importance whereas the work on realised variation measures in a semimartingale setting was primarily driven by its potential for volatility estimation in finance.

As soon as we consider the non-semimartingale case, then the existence of a limit of realised variance is no longer guaranteed, and a new asymptotic theory is required. Ole and various collaborators embarked on the journey to establish this new asymptotic theory.

An important insight in these theoretical developments was that the realised variance for BSS processes outside the semimartingale framework typically needs to be scaled appropriately in order to achieve convergence. The scaling factor typically depends on the kernel function of the BSS process.
Let us make this more precise in the following.

We define the
Gaussian core
(GC) as a special case of a BSS process with
\sigma\equiv 1
given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{GC}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}g(t-s)\,dW(s),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with autocorrelation function denoted by

\displaystyle r(t)=\text{Cor}(\mathrm{GC}(u),\mathrm{GC}(u+t))=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)g(s+t)ds}{||g||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}},\quad t\geq 0,

where
||g||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{2}(s)ds
.
The variogram of the Gaussian core is given by

\displaystyle R(t):=\mathbb{E}[(\mathrm{GC}(t+u)-\mathrm{GC}(u))^{2}]=2||g||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(1-r(t)).

We define the scaling constant

\displaystyle\tau_{n}:=\tau(\Delta_{n}):=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\Delta_{i}^{n}\mathrm{GC})^{2}]}=\sqrt{R(\Delta_{n})}=\sqrt{2||g||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(1-r(\Delta_{n}))}.

We summarise the asymptotic theory presented in
Corcuera, Hedevang, Pakkanen & Podolskij (2013, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2)
, see also
Podolskij (2015)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2011)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2013)
.
In the following assumption, all functions
L_{f}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, indexed by a mapping
f
, are assumed to be continuous and slowly varying at 0. Also,
f^{(m)}
denotes the
m
th derivative of the function
f
.
The following assumption were formulated in
Corcuera, Hedevang, Pakkanen & Podolskij (2013, Assumptions A1, A2)
, who consider the case when
g
has a single singularity at
0
, the case of multiple singularities was considered in
Gärtner & Podolskij (2015)
.

Assumption 2.1
.

Suppose that
there exists an
\alpha\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)\cup(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that the following conditions hold :

(i)

g(x)=x^{\alpha}L_{g}(x)
.

(ii)

g^{\prime}(x)=g^{(1)}(x)=x^{\alpha-1}L_{g^{\prime}}(x)
and
g^{\prime}\in L^{2}((\epsilon,\infty))
, for any
\epsilon>0
. Also, there exists a constant
a>0
such that
g^{\prime}
is non-increasing on
(a,\infty)
.

(iii)

For any strictly positive
t
, we have that

\displaystyle F(t)=\int_{1}^{\infty}(g^{\prime}(s))^{2}\sigma^{2}(t-s)ds<\infty.

(iv)

The variogram of the Gaussian core satisfies
R(x)=x^{2\alpha+1}L_{R}(x)
.

(v)

R^{(2)}(x)=x^{2\alpha-1}f_{R^{(2)}}(x)
.

(vi)

There exists a constant
b\in(0,1)
such that

\limsup_{x\downarrow 0}\sup_{y\in[x,x^{b}]}\left|\frac{L_{R^{(2)}}(y)}{L_{R}(x)}\right|<\infty
.

We have the following results, cf.
Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2013, Theorems 1 and 2)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.

(i)

Law of large numbers: As
\Delta_{n}\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\tau_{n}^{-2}\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{u.c.p.}}}{{\rightarrow}}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds.

(ii)

Central limit theorem: Suppose that the volatility process
\sigma
is Hölder continuous of order
1/2<\gamma<1
and suppose that
\alpha\in(-1/2,0)
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(\Delta_{n}\tau_{n}^{-2}\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}K\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)dB(s),

on the Skorohod space
\mathcal{D}([0,T])
equipped with the uniform topology, where
B
denotes a Brownian motion defined on an extension of the original probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which is independent of
\mathcal{F}
. Also

\displaystyle K^{2}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta_{n}^{1-2H}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor 1/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(\Delta_{i}^{n}B^{H})^{2}\right),

where
B^{H}
denotes a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=\alpha+1/2
.

Although the above result is interesting from a mathematical point of view, it is infeasible since the scaling factor
\tau_{n}
is unknown in practice.
This problem can be avoided by considering suitable ratio statistics. Let us consider the setting of a BSS process with a
gamma kernel
, as studied in

Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2009)
.
The gamma kernel is proportional to

\displaystyle g(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-\lambda x},\quad\text{ for }\lambda>0,\ \alpha\in(-1/2,0)\cup(0,1/2),\;\mathrm{for}\;x>0,

(4)

and
g(x)=0
for
x\leq 0

and satisfies
Assumption
2.1
. Hence, the above asymptotic theory is applicable.
Moreover, we can consider the ratio of two power variations evaluated over different frequencies termed the
change-of-frequency statistics
, see
Corcuera, Hedevang, Pakkanen & Podolskij (2013, Section 4)
:

Corollary 2.1
.

For fixed
t>0
and
g
given by (
4
), we have

\displaystyle S_{n}(t):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Y(i\Delta_{n})-Y((i-2)\Delta_{n}))^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/(2\Delta_{n})\rfloor}(Y(i\Delta_{n})-Y((i-1)\Delta_{n}))^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}2^{2\alpha+1},\quad\text{ as }\Delta_{n}\to 0,

and, hence,

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log_{2}S_{n}(t)-1\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\alpha,\quad\text{ as }\Delta_{n}\to 0,

where
\log_{2}
denotes the logarithm to base 2 and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in probability.

Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2011)
;
Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2013)
;
Corcuera, Hedevang, Pakkanen & Podolskij (2013)
extended the above result further and also developed a suitable central limit theorem.

While estimating integrated volatility requires some knowledge of
\tau_{n}
(or a suitable estimator thereof),

relative volatility
can be estimated without further knowledge of the scaling factor, see
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2014)
.
Consider a fixed time
T>0
and define the
relative volatility
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{RelVol}(t):=\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds}{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(s)ds},\quad\text{ for }t\leq T.

Corollary 2.2
.

1.

Law of large numbers: Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{RelVol}^{n}(t)}:=\frac{\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)}{\mathrm{RV}^{n}(T)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathrm{RelVol}(t),\quad\text{ as }\Delta_{n}\to 0,

uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
.

2.

Central limit theorem:
Assume the same Hölder regularity of the volatility process
\sigma
as described in Theorem
2.2
(ii), then:

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(\widehat{\mathrm{RelVol}^{n}(t)}-\mathrm{RelVol}(t)\right)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{K}{\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(s)ds}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)dB(s)-\mathrm{RelVol}(t)\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(s)dB(s)\right),

on the Skorohod space
\mathcal{D}([0,T])
equipped with the uniform topology, where
B
denotes a Brownian motion defined on an extension of the original probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which is independent of
\mathcal{F}
.

We presented the results above for realised variance to simplify the exposition. However, similar results hold for general
p
-power variations as outlined in the articles mentioned throughout the section and also in
(
Barndorff-Nielsen et al., 2018
, Chapter 3)
. We also note that an additional drift term can often be included without impacting the presented asymptotic theory. We refer to
Barndorff-Nielsen, Corcuera & Podolskij (2011)
for a detailed study of this aspect.

While the results in the semimartingale and non-semimartingale
“look”
similar apart from the corresponding scaling terms in the realised variance, it is important to note that the method of the proofs varies significantly in the two cases. For the non-semimartingale case, the proofs frequently proceed as follows:
We typically focus on the Gaussian core first, i.e. a Brownian semistationary process without stochastic volatility and derive all results in the absence of stochastic volatility. Here, we need to find a suitable scaling factor for the realised variance.
We then extend all results to the general case with stochastic volatility using Bernstein’s blocking technique, where the volatility is
“frozen”
on a coarser time grid.
The weak law of large numbers can be proven using classical techniques for the convergence of measures.
For the central limit theorem, we typically invoke the powerful fourth-moment theorem by
Nualart & Peccati (2005)
.

2.2
Volatility estimation for ambit processes

The third scenario in volatility estimation we will discuss in this survey, based on
Barndorff-Nielsen & Graversen (2011)
, is within a spatio-temporal setting, where the literature is much less developed than in the temporal case.

Consider an ambit field in the 1-dimensional spatial case with Gaussian Lévy basis
W
, i.e.

Y(x,t)=\int_{A(x,t)}g(x-\xi,t-s)\sigma(\xi,s)W(d\xi,ds),

with an ambit set
satisfying
A(x,t)=A+(x,t)
, for some
A:=A(0,0)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the following, we will assume that
\sigma
is a continuous random field, independent of
W
, with
(\xi,s)\mapsto\mathbb{E}(\sigma^{2}(\xi,s))
being locally bounded.

Let

v(\theta)=(x(\theta),t(\theta))
denote a smooth curve in space-time, where
\theta\mapsto t(\theta)
is nondecreasing.
Define

X(\theta)=Y(x(\theta),t(\theta)),

and assume that
X=(X(\theta))_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
is a well-defined stochastic process, which we will call an
ambit process
.
Some natural questions we might ask are: When is the quadratic variation of
X
well defined?
Can we define a stochastic differential
dX(\theta)
?
In the situation where
X
is a semimartingale, the answer is
“yes”
to the two above questions. Outside the semimartingale framework, the situation is more complex, but some of the insights formulated in the purely temporal case carry over, in particular, when it comes to scaling the realised variance appropriately to obtain convergence.

We review the key results by

Barndorff-Nielsen & Graversen (2011)
in the following. They were particularly interested in the case of a closed set

\displaystyle A=\{(\xi,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|-M\leq s\leq 0,c_{1}(s)\leq\xi\leq c_{2}(s)\},

for a constant
M>0
that can be considered as decorrelation time and two smooth functions
c_{1}:[-M,0]\to(-\infty,0)
and
c_{2}:[-M,0]\to(0,\infty)
, where
c_{1}
is increasing and
c_{2}
decreasing.

As before, one can then define the realised variance as

\displaystyle\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(\Delta_{i}^{n}X)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(X(i\Delta_{n})-X((i-1)\Delta_{n}))^{2},\quad\text{for }t>0,

and the scaled realised variance as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SRV}^{n}(t)=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{c(\Delta_{n})}\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t),\quad\text{for }t>0,

for a positive scaling function
c
.

The key result derived in
Barndorff-Nielsen & Graversen (2011)
is the following: Under suitable technical conditions, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{SRV}^{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(x(s)-u,t(s)-u^{\prime})ds\pi(du,du^{\prime}),\qquad\mathrm{as}\;\Delta_{n}\to 0,

(5)

where the measure
\pi
is constructed below.

2.2.1
Technical conditions for the weak law of scaled realised variance

Let us briefly summarise the (additional) technical conditions used by
Barndorff-Nielsen & Graversen (2011)
to establish the weak law of scaled realised variance stated in (
5
).

The smooth curve
v
is assumed to be a straight line such that

\displaystyle\Delta v(\Delta_{n}):=(\Delta x(\Delta_{n}),\Delta t(\Delta_{n})):=(x(z+\Delta_{n})-x(z),t(z+\Delta_{n})-t(z)),

for
z,\Delta_{n}>0
.

In order to define the probability measure
\pi
above, we first introduce the function
\psi
as follows:

\displaystyle\psi_{\Delta_{n}}(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(g(\Delta x(\Delta_{n})+u,\Delta t(\Delta_{n})+u^{\prime})-g(u,u^{\prime}))^{2},&\mathrm{for}\;(u,u^{\prime})\in(-A)\cap(-A-\Delta v(\Delta_{n})),\\
g^{2}(u,u^{\prime}),&\mathrm{for}\;(u,u^{\prime})\in(-A)\setminus(-A-\Delta v(\Delta_{n})),\\
g^{2}(\Delta x(\Delta_{n})+u,\Delta t(\Delta_{n})+u^{\prime}),&\mathrm{for}\;(u,u^{\prime})\in(-A-\Delta v(\Delta_{n}))\setminus(-A).\end{array}\right.

From the above definition, we can see that
\psi_{\Delta_{n}}(u,u^{\prime})=0
for
(u,u^{\prime})\not\in(-A)\cup(-A-\Delta v(\Delta_{n}))
and that, under suitable smoothness conditions,
\psi_{\Delta_{n}}(u,u^{\prime})
typically has order
\Delta_{n}^{2}
for
(u,u^{\prime})\in(-A)\cap(-A-\Delta v(\Delta_{n}))
.
The scaling function
c
can now be defined as

\displaystyle c(\Delta_{n}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi_{\Delta_{n}}(u,u^{\prime})dudu^{\prime},

assuming
c(\Delta_{n})>0
, which will be the case under the additional assumptions given below.
One can then define the probability measure, for
\Delta_{n}>0
, as

\displaystyle\pi_{\Delta_{n}}(du,du^{\prime}):=\frac{\psi_{\Delta_{n}}(u,u^{\prime})}{c(\Delta_{n})}dudu^{\prime}.

Note that any weak limit point
\pi_{0}
of
\pi_{\Delta_{n}}
is a probability measure concentrated on
(-A)
. We then define
\pi
as the image measure of
\pi_{0}
under the transformation
(u,u^{\prime})\mapsto(-u,-u^{\prime})
and note that it is concentrated on
\partial A
.

We can now state the key convergence result, see
(
Barndorff-Nielsen & Graversen, 2011
, Theorem 1)
:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
A
be a bounded, closed, convex set with nonempty interior
{\kern 0.0ptA}^{\mathrm{o}}
and piecewise
C^{\infty}
boundary. Suppose that
v
is a straight line and suppose that, for some
-1/2<\alpha<1/2
, the kernel function
g
can be represented as
g({\bf z})=\phi({\bf z})h_{\alpha}({\bf z})
, where
\phi
is Lipschitz continuous and not identically 0 of the part of
\-\partial A
nonparallel to
v
, and

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}(-{\bf z})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-T({\bf z}-{\bf z}_{0}))^{\alpha},&\mathbf{z}\in A,\\
0,&\mathbf{z}\not\in A,\end{array}\right.

where the function
T
denotes the gauge function of the set
A-{\bf z}_{0}
for some
{\bf z}_{0}\in{\kern 0.0ptA}^{\mathrm{o}}
.
Then there exists a probability measure
\pi
concentrated on
\partial A
such that (
5
) holds.

Remark 2.1
.

Gauge functions are discussed in

Dunford & Schwartz (1958, Chapter 5)
: Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote a bounded, closed, convex set with
{\bf 0}\in{\kern 0.0ptC}^{\mathrm{o}}
. Then
T({\bf z}):=\inf\{t>0|{\bf z}\in tC\},
for
{\bf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called the gauge function of
C
.

3
Ambit fields and SPDEs

Ambit fields
Y(\mathbf{x},t)
in (
1
) are stochastic models of the evolution of physical phenomena in time and space. Usually, in science, dynamic systems are modelled by partial differential equations accounting for random fluctuations by including stochastic terms. Ambit fields provide a new perspective on such stochastic partial differential equations, allowing for more flexibility in the modelling of the probabilistic features of the phenomenon we want to describe. We discuss this in the following,
referring the interested reader to
Barndorff-Nielsen, Benth & Veraart (2011)
for a more in-depth analysis.

Following
Peszat & Zabczyk (2007)
, let
U
be a Lévy process taking values in the separable Hilbert space
H
with covariance operator
Q
being positive definite and trace class. Further, let
(\mathcal{S}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a
C_{0}
-semigroup with a densely defined generator
\mathcal{A}
on
H
, and consider the class of linear parabolic SPDEs given as

dZ(t)=\mathcal{A}Z(t)dt+\nu(t)dU(t),

(6)

with
Z(0)=Z_{0}\in H
given and
(\nu(t))_{t\geq 0}
being a predictable process taking values in the space of linear operators on
H
such that
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{T}\|\nu(s)Q^{1/2}\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}\,ds]<\infty
for
T<\infty
. A mild solution is a predictable
H
-valued process

(Z(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
such that

Z(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)\nu(s)dU(s).

(7)

Often,
H
is some space of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or a subset thereof). For example,
H
could be
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, some
Sobolev space on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or the Filipovic space on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(see
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2018)
). Furthermore, one can express the semigroup operator
\mathcal{S}(t)
as an integral operator in terms of a Green’s function
\mathcal{S}(t)f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\mathbf{x},t-s;\boldsymbol{\xi})f(\boldsymbol{\xi})d\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Moreover, the
H
-valued Lévy process
U
may be rephrased into a Lévy basis
L
. If
H
is a Banach algebra, as
is the case for the Filipovic space, or imposing additional assumptions on the process
\nu
, we may express
\nu(t)dU(t)
as a
pointwise multiplication operator
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\xi},t)L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},dt)
for
(\sigma(t))_{t\geq 0}
being a predictable
H
-valued process accounting for
\nu
and the covariance structure of
U
encoded in the
Q
-operator. After some reformulations based partly on the theory of
Walsh (1986)
, we can express the mild solution in (
7
) as

Z(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\mathbf{x},t-s;\boldsymbol{\xi})\sigma(\boldsymbol{\xi},s)L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds).

(8)

For simplicity, we let
Z_{0}=0
. Notice that
\mathbf{x}\mapsto Z(\mathbf{x},t)
is a an element in the chosen Hilbert space of functions. Indeed, we see that
Z(\mathbf{x},t)
is an ambit field
as defined in (
1
).

We remark in passing that ambit fields are Volterra processes in time, and can be lifted to be mild solutions of parabolic SPDEs where the generator is given by
\mathcal{A}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
. We refer to
Benth & Eyjolfsson (2017)
for further details.

Worth observing here is that the kernel function
g
appears as a Green’s function of the operator
\mathcal{A}
. In real-world
applications, the operator
\mathcal{A}
may not be precisely known. Also, it may be difficult to analytically express the Green’s function. In the perspective of ambit fields,
g
models the dependency structure in space and time, and offers a significant extension in modelling flexibility beyond Green’s functions, where it may even be an approximation based on probabilistic considerations. Furthermore,
g
can also be re-phrased accounting for an ambit set moving in time and space, further enhancing the flexibility in describing the time-space evolution of the phenomenon in question.
The connection with mild solutions of parabolic SPDEs opens, on the other hand, a way on dealing with realized variance for ambit fields.

3.1
Volatility estimation for SPDEs

In
Benth, Schroers & Veraart (2022)
;
Benth et al. (2024)
, a law of large numbers and
central limit theorem are presented for the volatility

\nu
in parabolic SPDEs (
6
) driven by an
H
-valued
Wiener
process
U
.

Having an equally spaced time grid
t_{i}=i\Delta_{n}
for
\Delta_{n}=1/n
and
i=1,2,\ldots,\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor
,
Benth, Schroers & Veraart (2022)
introduced the
semigroup-adjusted realized covariation
(SARCV) as

\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2},

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product in the Hilbert space
H
(note here that we are back to a general separable Hilbert space). The SARC defines a sequence of stochastic processes on
[0,T]
taking values in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt valued operators on
H
. In
Benth, Schroers & Veraart (2022)
, it is shown that the SARCV converges uniformly on compacts in probability (ucp) in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm to the integrated covariance process, i.e., for all
\epsilon>0
and every
T>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2}-\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s)ds\right\|_{\text{HS}}>\epsilon\right)=0

under rather weak conditions. Here,
\nu^{*}(s)
is the adjoint of
\nu(s)
.

Under a fourth moment integrability condition on the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the volatility process
\nu
one can show central limit theorems for the SARCV. To describe these, we introduce the operator-valued process
(\Gamma(t))_{t\geq 0}
taking values in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
H
into itself, denoted
\mathcal{H}:=L_{\text{HS}}(H,H)
: for
B\in\mathcal{H}
,

\Gamma(t)B=\int_{0}^{t}(\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s))(B+B^{*})(\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s))ds.

If the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
satisfies a certain regularity condition after acting on the volatility process, we have that the stochastic process

\left(\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2}-\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s)ds\right)\right)_{t\in[0,T]}

converges, when
n\rightarrow\infty
stably in law in the Skorohod space
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{H})

to a conditionally continuous Gaussian
\mathcal{H}
-valued independent increment process with mean zero and covariance operator process
\Gamma
. One could dispense on the regularity condition on the semigroup, and achieve a central limit theorem for finite-dimensional projections of the SARCV. Indeed, letting

B=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}h_{j}\otimes g_{j}
for
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
h_{j},g_{j}
belonging to a certain subspace of
H
, we have that

\left(\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2}-\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s)ds\right),B\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}

converges stably in law in the Skorohod space
\mathcal{D}([0,T],H)
to a conditionally continuous
Gaussian
H
-valued independent increment process with mean zero and covariance operator process
\langle\Gamma B,B\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. The elements
h_{j},g_{j}
belong to the so-called
1/2
-Favard space defined by the adjoint of the semigroup. In the finite-dimensional central limit theorem, we have moved the regularity from the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
to a class of finite-dimensional operators
B
on which we can project the SARCV.

In practice, we do not know the asymptotic covariance operator

\Gamma
and hence the central limit theorems are infeasible. In order to have feasible limit theorems, we introduce an estimator of
\Gamma
using semigroup-adjusted multipower variation (SAMPV). In particular, one uses the fourth-power and second-bipower variation processes, defined as

SAMPV^{n}(t;4)=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 4},

and

SAMPV^{n}(t;2,2)=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor-1}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2}\otimes(Z(t_{i+1})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i}))^{\otimes 2},

respectively. Define the estimator

\widehat{\Gamma}^{n}(t)=\Delta_{n}^{-1}\left(SAMPV^{n}(t;4)-SAMPV^{n}(t;2,2)\right).

This defines a consistent estimator of
\Gamma
, since

\widehat{\Gamma}^{n}\rightarrow\Gamma
when
n\rightarrow\infty
in
\mathcal{H}^{4}
uniformly on compacts in probability (ucp). Here,
\mathcal{H}^{4}=L_{\text{HS}}(H,L_{\text{HS}}(H,\mathcal{H}))
. If the volatility process
\nu
is cádlág in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators, we obtain a feasible central limit theorem: As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\langle\widehat{\Gamma}^{n}(t)B,B\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1/2}\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(Z(t_{i})-\mathcal{S}(\Delta_{n})Z(t_{i-1}))^{\otimes 2}-\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)Q\nu^{*}(s)ds\right),B\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable. As above,
B
is a finite-dimensional operator defined as tensors of elements in the
1/2
-Favard subspace of
H
. We refer to
Benth et al. (2024)
for details on the above discussion of central limit theorems.

3.2
Links to turbulence

Let us return to parabolic SPDEs.
Peszat & Zabczyk (2007)
study nonlinear parabolic SPDEs driven by Lévy noise defined on a Hilbert space
H
, of the form

dZ(t)=(\mathcal{A}+F(Z(t)))dt+G(Z(t))dU(t),

(9)

where
F
is a Lipschitz map from
H
into itself while
G
is a Lipschitz map from
H
into Hilbert-Schmidt operators, both of at most linear growth in appropriate norms (see
Peszat & Zabczyk (2007)
for precise conditions). A mild solution is defined as the process
Z
solving the nonlinear integral equation

Z(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)Z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)F(Z(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)G(Z(s))dU(s),

(10)

where
Z(0)=Z_{0}
is the initial condition (again we refer to
Peszat & Zabczyk (2007)
for additional regularity conditions).

Birnir (2013)
studies the stochastic Navier-Stokes equation for fully developed turbulence within this context. In his formulation, supposing that the fluid satisfies periodic boundary conditions on its domain, the motion
u=(u(\mathbf{x},t))_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{3},t\geq 0}
of the fluid follows the nonlinear parabolic SPDE dynamics

\displaystyle du(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=(\nu\Delta u(\mathbf{x},t)-u(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla\Delta^{-1}\text{tr}(\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t))^{2})dt+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}c_{\mathbf{k}}^{1/2}db^{\mathbf{k}}(t)e_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},\mathbf{k}\neq 0}d_{\mathbf{k}}\eta_{\mathbf{k}}dte_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})+u(\mathbf{x},t)\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},|\mathbf{k}|\leq m,\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\mathbf{k}}\overline{N}^{\mathbf{k}}(dt,dz),

where
u(\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
. Here,
e_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})
are the Fourier coefficients, and
b^{\mathbf{k}}
are independent Brownian motions. Furthermore, we have finitely many jump processes modelled by the compensated Poisson measures
\overline{N}^{\mathbf{k}}
,
|\mathbf{k}|\leq m
.

The equations for the invariant measures of both the one-point and two-point statistics of turbulence are computed. The two-point statistics describes the velocity differences
u(\mathbf{x},t)-u(\mathbf{y},t)
, and
Birnir (2013)
provides an explicit solution of the PDE describing the probability density function of the invariant measure. It turns out to be a normal inverse Gaussian distribution (NIG), corresponding to empirical turbulence data as evidenced in
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2004)
(see also
Birnir (2013)
).

Rather than studying integral equations such as the mild formulation (
10
) of the SPDE (
9
), one can define Volterra equations where the semigroup operator
\mathcal{S}
is substituted with some integral kernel. This is analogous to extending the mild solution of a linear parabolic SPDE to ambit fields (
8
).
Chong (2017)
provides the existence and uniqueness of solutions to such equations, being on the form

Z(\mathbf{x},t)=Z_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\mathbf{x},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)\sigma(Z(\boldsymbol{\xi},s))L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds).

Here, the stochastic volatility is state-dependent, and the deterministic function
\sigma
is supposed to be Lipschitz continuous. In the null-space case, similar
Volterra equations have been lifted to parabolic SPDEs and analysed in
Benth, Detering & Krühner (2022)
.

4.1
Turbulence

Throughout his research career, Ole focused extensively on
stochastic models and statistical inference techniques that
are applicable to physical turbulence; this area has been
at the heart of advances in ambit stochastics ever since.
It is important to ensure that such stochastic models for turbulence align with the
Statistical Theory of
Turbulence
founded by Kolmogorov and Obukhov, see
Frisch (1995)
;
Birnir (2013)
for surveys.

Based on the detailed summary in

Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2018, Chapter 9)
, we will provide a short overview of some of the key aspects at the interface of turbulence and ambit stochastics.

Turbulence research typically focusses on the dynamics of the three-dimensional velocity vector
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)=(v_{x}(\mathbf{x},t),v_{y}(\mathbf{x},t),v_{z}(\mathbf{x},t))
as a function of position

\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
and time
t
in a fluid flow.
One can then define the

energy dissipation
in an incompressible flow
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\mathbf{x},t):=\frac{\nu}{2}\sum_{i,j=x,y,z}\left(\partial_{i}v_{j}(\mathbf{x},t)+\partial_{j}v_{i}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)^{2},

(11)

which
represents the loss of kinetic energy due to friction
forces. The scaling factor
\nu
denotes the viscosity, measuring the resistance to gradual deformation by shear stress or tensile stress.

When carrying out high-resolution experiments, one typically obtains measurements of a time series of a single component of the velocity vector in the direction of the mean flow and at a single location
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Therefore, it is common to define the
temporal energy dissipation
for stationary, homogeneous and
isotropic flows as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0},t)\equiv\frac{15\nu}{\overline{v}^{2}}\left(\frac{dv(\mathbf{x}_{0},t)}{dt}\right)^{2}\,,

(12)

where the constant
\overline{v}
represents the mean velocity, in the direction of the
mean flow.

We would anticipate that some of the statistical properties of the true energy
dissipation defined in (
11
) can be estimated via the
temporal energy dissipation defined in (
12
).
In doing so, we note that one needs to transition from the spatial derivatives in (
11
) to the
temporal derivative in (
12
). This can be done under a suitable set of assumptions: Specifically, we assume that the flow is stationary, homogeneous and isotropic and that Taylor’s Frozen Flow Hypothesis (TFFH), see
Taylor (1938)
, holds.
Under TFFH, we can express
spatial increments along the direction of the mean flow (in direction

x
) in terms of temporal increments

\displaystyle v(x,y,z,t+s)-v(x,y,z,t)=v(x-\overline{v}s,y,z,t)-v(x,y,z,t).

In the following, we will describe two particularly relevant ambit-based models for turbulence in more detail.

4.1.1
Spatio-temporal models for turbulence

Let us consider a
trawl field
given by

Y(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{A(\mathbf{x},t)}L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds)=L(A(\mathbf{x},t)),

(13)

which is a special case of the ambit field mentioned in (
1
), where the kernel function
g
and the stochastic volatility fields
\sigma
are assumed to be identical to one and where there is no metatime
\mathcal{T}
incorporated.

Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2004)
pointed out that, when working with an

exponentiated trawl field
, which is obtained by setting

X(\mathbf{x},t)=\exp(Y(\mathbf{x},t))=\exp(L(A(\mathbf{x},t))),

one obtains a favourable scaling behaviour of the correlators which can be adapted to the requirements in turbulence. More precisely, we note that the
two point correlator of order
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
of
X

is defined by

c_{p,q}(t_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1};t_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})^{p}X(\mathbf{x}_{2},t_{2})^{q}]}{\mathbb{E}[X(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})^{p}]\mathbb{E}[X(\mathbf{x}_{2},t_{2})^{q}]}.

The correlator essentially extends the concept of the autocorrelation function used in time series to spatio-temporal processes.
One can show that, for exponentiated trawl fields defined above, the two point correlator is given by

c_{p,q}(t_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1};t_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\exp\left(c[p,q]Leb(A(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})\cap A(\mathbf{x}_{2},t_{2}))\right),

where

c[p,q]=\mathrm{k}(p+q)-\mathrm{k}(p)-\mathrm{k}(q)
,
and
\mathrm{k}(\theta):=\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\text{e}}^{\theta L^{\prime}}\right]\right)
denotes the kumulant function of the Lévy seed
L^{\prime}
.
We note that
\log(c_{p,q})
factorises into a part depending on the distribution of the Lévy seed and one depending on the size of the overlap between the ambit sets
A(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})
and
A(\mathbf{x}_{2},t_{2})
. This
leads to great flexibility in terms of stochastic modelling as described in

Schmiegel (2005)
,
Schmiegel et al. (2005)
and
Hedevang & Schmiegel (2013)
.
Note that the correlators exhibit the following
self-scaling property
:

c_{p,q}(t_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1};t_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2})=c_{1,1}(t_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1};t_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2})^{\bar{c}[p,q]}\,,

where
\bar{c}[p,q]=c[p,q]/c[1,1]
; such a self-scaling behaviour is widely observed in empirical studies of
homogeneous turbulent flows.

In the stationary setting, the results can be further simplified:
Let us assume that the ambit set satisfies
A(\mathbf{x},t)=A+(\mathbf{x},t)
, i.e. we have a fixed set
A=A(\mathbf{0},0)
, at location
{\bf 0}
and time 0, which is then translated in space and time and whose shape does not change as it moves around.
In that case, the trawl field defined in (
13
) becomes stationary and its joint cumulant function takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}C(\phi,\psi;Y(\mathbf{0},0),Y(\mathbf{x},t))&=\log(\exp(i\phi Y(\mathbf{0},0)+i\psi Y(\mathbf{x},t)))\\
&=Leb(A)\{(c(\phi)+c(\psi))\bar{r}(t,\mathbf{x}))+c(\phi+\psi)r(t,\mathbf{x})\}.\end{split}

(14)

Here,
c(\zeta)=C(\zeta;L^{\prime})=\log(\exp(i\zeta L^{\prime}))
denotes the cumulant function of the Lévy seed
L^{\prime}
and

r(t,\mathbf{x})=\frac{Leb(A\cap A(\mathbf{x},t))}{Leb(A)},

denotes the
autodependent function
of
Y({\bf x},t)

and
\bar{r}=1-r
.
An immediate implication of (
14
) is that

C(\zeta;Y(\mathbf{x},t)-Y(\mathbf{0},0))=Leb(A)(c(-\zeta)+c(\zeta))\bar{r}(t,\mathbf{x}).

Hence, if
L^{\prime}
has a symmetric law, then

C(\zeta;Y(\mathbf{x},t)-Y(\mathbf{0},0))=2Leb(A)c(\zeta)\bar{r}(t,\mathbf{x})
.

Example 4.1
.

For instance, if one works under the assumption that
L^{\prime}
has a
symmetric normal inverse Gaussian (NIG) distribution, then the resulting trawl field
Y(\mathbf{x},t)
and all increments of
Y
also follow an NIG distribution.

In the stationary case,
we consider the two-point correlator

c_{p,q}(t,\mathbf{x}):=c_{p,q}((0,\mathbf{0});(t,\mathbf{x}))\,,

and note that

\log(c_{p,q}(t,\mathbf{x}))=Leb(A)\left(\mathrm{k}(p+q)-\mathrm{k}(p)-\mathrm{k}(q)\right)r(t,\mathbf{x})
.
Hence,
the self-scaling exponent
\bar{c}(p,q)
satisfies

\bar{c}(p,q)=\frac{\mathrm{k}(p+q)-\mathrm{k}(p)-\mathrm{k}(q)}{\mathrm{k}(2)-2\mathrm{k}(1)}.

We might wonder how the ambit set
A
should be chosen to match a particular self-scaling property observed in empirical data.

Schmiegel et al. (2005)
and
Hedevang & Schmiegel (2013)
tackle this question and establish that, in the stationary setting, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log(c_{p,q}(t,\mathbf{x}))=\left(\mathrm{k}(p+q)-\mathrm{k}(p)-\mathrm{k}(q)\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}Leb(A\cap(A+(\mathbf{x},t)))\,.

Suppose for a moment that

\mathbf{x}\equiv x\in\mathbb{R}
, and

A=\{(\xi,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<s<M,|\xi|\leq w(s)\}-(0,M)\,,

(15)

where

w(t)
is a
nonnegative decreasing function on the interval
(0,M)
and

M
denotes a finite decorrelation time. We get that, for any
t\in(0,M)
,

Leb(A\cap(A+(0,t)))=2\int_{t}^{M}w(s)\,ds.

Differentiating both sides leads to

w(t)=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{k}(2)-2\mathrm{k}(1))^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\log(c_{1,1}(t,0)).

(16)

We note that the
derivative of
\log(c_{1,1}(t,0))
can be estimated
from data on the timewise evolution of
Y(x,t)
for given
x
.

Hedevang & Schmiegel (2013)
considered a particular example, where, for a decorrelation time
M=1
, the
function
w
in the
ambit set
A
defined as in (
15
) is given by

w(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^{\theta}}{1+(t/b)^{\theta}}\right)^{1/\theta}

for a constant
b\in(0,1)
.
Note that an arbitrary decorrelation time
M

can be obtained by using the function
w(t/M)
instead. Here,
\theta
denotes a tuning parameter and
b
represents the time scale below which the scaling behaviour has terminated.
Hedevang & Schmiegel (2013)
point out that the function
w
is in fact its own inverse, which is well related to TFFH.

We note that
Sauri & Veraart (2024)
tackled a closely related estimation problem to the one in (
16
) from a nonparametric perspective.
Such estimation methods enable the determination of shapes of ambit sets that are consistent with data and guarantee suitable scaling properties.

4.1.2
Temporal models for turbulence

A lot of research in turbulence also concentrates on the purely temporal case, where new mathematical methodology can often be developed more easily before it is further adapted to spatio-temporal settings.
Hence, let us focus on a relevant temporal model for the energy dissipation next.

Let
v
denote the temporal recording of the main component of the turbulent velocity vector at a fixed spatial position. Under the TFFH, the integrated energy dissipation is
proportional to

\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{dv(s)}{ds}\right)^{2}\,ds.

We change the notation from
v
to
Y
to indicate that we are working with a stochastic process.

However, we note that for many relevant models, such as when we assume that
Y
is a BSS process with gamma kernel, see (
4
),
where
\alpha\in(-1/2,0)
, then

Y
is not differentiable.
In such a case, one could try to define the integrated energy dissipation as a suitable limit of the realised variance. I.e. suppose we observe the process
Y
at times
i\Delta_{n}
, for
i=0,\ldots,\lfloor T\Delta_{n}\rfloor\Delta_{n}
and recall from (
3
) that we define the realised variance of
Y
as

\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta_{n}\rfloor}(\Delta_{i}^{n}Y)^{2},\quad\mathrm{for}\;t\in[0,T].

As discussed in Section
2
, in the case where
Y
is a semimartingale, the realised variance
\mathrm{RV}^{n}(t)
converges in probability to the quadratic variation
[Y]_{t}
of
Y_{t}
. Hence,
[Y]_{t}
is a natural candidate to use as the
definition of the integrated energy dissipation.

Outside the semimartingale framework, the situation is more challenging.
When taking the BSS process with a gamma kernel as our underlying model, then the non-semimartingale case is obtained for

\alpha\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)\cup(0,\frac{1}{2})
,
see
Barndorff-Nielsen & Schmiegel (2009)
.
Early work by

Onsager (1949)
established that Kolmogorov’s statistical theory of turbulence implies that the sample paths must
be Hölder continuous of order
\frac{1}{3}
.
It turns out that, when
\alpha\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
, the sample paths of the leading term of
X
are Hölder continuous of order

\alpha+\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, when
\alpha=-\frac{1}{6}
,
the order of Hölder continuity equals
\frac{1}{3}
.
This finding makes this particular BSS process an interesting contender for a stochastic model for turbulence. It remains to address the challenge of defining the integrated energy dissipation in an appropriate way. Given the limit theory discussed in Section
2
and in Theorem
2.1
, in particular, a promising approach was found to be taking the limit of an appropriately scaled realised variance as the integrated energy dissipation.

We note that much of the original limit theory developed for realised measures of BSS and related processes outside the semimartingale framework was driven by the search for a suitable definition of the integrated energy dissipation in turbulence studies. However, as the resulting limit theory introduced novel mathematical concepts and insights of independent interest, this research area has subsequently grown substantially as the references in Section
2
document.

4.1.3
Further reading

Purely spatial models based on ambit fields have been discussed in
Hedevang & Schmiegel (2014)
. Also,
Sauri (2020)
investigates divergence and vorticity theorems for vector ambit fields and proposes a class of spatio-temporal
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued ambit fields with stationary and isotropic increments that are relevant for turbulence studies.

Birnir (2013)
relates the Kolmogorov-Obukhov theory of turbulence to stochastic Navier-Stokes equations.
Also, we note that Ole, together with Mikkel Bennedsen and Jürgen Schmiegel, was working on tailoring ambit fields to turbulent boundary layer problems.
More precisely, they were investigating
a universal statistical description of the main velocity component in a turbulent boundary layer, see
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2022b)
; here they were exploring relevant distributions, with a particular focus on the NIG distribution, and could demonstrate that there is some ‘universality’ property in the statistical description of the turbulence.
In another project, see
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2022a)
, they study a spatio-temporal BSS model of the main velocity component in a turbulent boundary layer. They could show that the two-dimensional correlations in the data could be described well using relatively simple shapes for the ambit sets.

4.2
Stochastic growth models

In spatial statistics and stochastic geometry one is interested in modelling the evolution of objects. Given a star-shaped object
S(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that evolves with time
t\geq 0
, one can define its boundary by the radial function

R(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)=\max\left\{r\,|\,\mathbf{z}+r(e_{1}(\phi),\ldots,e_{n}(\phi))\in S(t)\right\}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n-2},\phi_{n-1})\in[0,\pi]^{n-2}\times[0,2\pi]
,
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reference point of
S(t)
and the functions
e_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
are defined as

\displaystyle e_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\cos(\phi_{1}),

\displaystyle e_{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sin(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2}),

\displaystyle e_{3}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sin(\phi_{1})\sin(\phi_{2})\cos(\phi_{3}),

\displaystyle e_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sin(\phi_{1})\cdots\sin(\phi_{n-2})\cos(\phi_{n-1}),

\displaystyle e_{n}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sin(\phi_{1})\cdots\sin(\phi_{n-2})\sin(\phi_{n-1}).

Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
suggest a growth model for star-shaped objects by modelling the speed of the
radial function by an ambit field. In full generality, let
L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds)
be a Lévy basis on
[0,\pi]^{n-2}\times[0,2\pi]\times\mathbb{R}
(hence,
d=n-1
) and
g
a kernel function, and define the dynamical model for the radial function as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}R(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)=\mu(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)+\int_{A(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)}g(\boldsymbol{\phi},t;\boldsymbol{\xi},s)\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\xi})L(d\boldsymbol{\xi},ds).

(17)

The general trend in the growth of the star-shaped object is modelled by the deterministic function
\mu(\cdot,t):[0,\pi]^{n-2}\times[0,2\pi]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The ambit set
A(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)\subset[0,\pi]^{n-2}\times[0,2\pi]\times(-\infty,t]
relates past events to the current growth rate.

The growth model in (
17
) makes sense also with negative values of the right-hand side, describing that the star-shaped object is shrinking. However, to have
growth
, one would like
\partial R(\boldsymbol{\phi},t)/\partial t
to be non-negative. This can be achieved by considering Lévy bases
L
which are non-negative along with non-negative kernel functions
g
and intermittency processes
\sigma
. Moreover, the general trend
\mu
is also natural to assume non-negative in this case.

4.2.1
A tumour growth model in the plane

In
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
, a growth model on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(i.e.,
n=2
) is studied in detail based on a special case of the ambit field model in (
17
).
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
suppose that the Lévy basis is only depending on the angle,
L(d\mathbf{\xi})
and the kernel function is therefore not depending on
s
. Furthermore,
intermittency is set to
\sigma=1
. Since
n=2
, we have
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
, with
e_{1}(\phi)=\cos(\phi)
and
e_{2}(\phi)=\sin(\phi)
. Thus, their planar tumour growth model is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}R(\phi,t)=\mu(\phi,t)+\int_{A(\phi,t)}g(\phi,t;\mathbf{\xi})L(d\mathbf{\xi}).

(18)

We now take a closer look at some of their modelling choices and analysis for the model (
18
).

The random field describing the evolution of the
radial function
R
is given by

\displaystyle R(\phi,t)

\displaystyle=R_{0}(\phi)+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\phi,s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{A(\phi,s)}g(\phi,s;\xi)L(d\xi)ds

\displaystyle=R_{0}(\phi)+\overline{\mu}(\phi,t)+\int_{\overline{A}(\phi,t)}\overline{g}(\phi,t;\xi)L(d\xi).

(19)

Here,
\overline{\mu}(\phi,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\phi,s)ds
,

\overline{A}(\phi,t)=\bigcup_{0\leq s\leq t}A(\phi,s),

and

\overline{g}(\phi,t;\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{A(\phi,s)}g(\phi,s;\xi)ds.

To commute the integrals to reach (
19
) we need to appeal to a stochastic Fubini theorem. We refer to
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. (2018, Prop. 45)
for a Fubini theorem for ambit fields and for precise conditions that allows for commuting the
ds
-integral with that of
L(d\xi)
.

The specification of the ambit set is an important piece in the growth model specification.
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
suggest for example a model for the sphere of influence where a minimal time lag is introduced in the time-component,

A(\phi,t)\subset[0,2\pi]\times[t-\tau(t),t],

where
t\mapsto\tau(t)\geq 0
is some measurable function. One may let this function be constant,
\tau(t)=\delta
, meaning that the sphere of influence goes back a fixed time step
\delta
with respect to current time
t
.
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
also suggest to specify
t\mapsto t-\tau(t)
being non-decreasing, which, in other words, means to have
\tau
such that
t_{1}-t_{2}\geq\tau(t_{1})-\tau(t_{2})
for any
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
. For such ambit sets,
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008, Prop. 7 and Ex. 8)
compute explicit
expressions for the covariance between

R(\phi_{1},t_{1})
and
R(\phi_{2},t_{2})
given the Fourier representation of the kernel function
g
. The covariance is in general non-separable. However, it is separable in special cases of the kernel function
\overline{g}
, i.e., when the Fourier expansion of the kernel function only consists of its first basis term, in which case

\text{Cov}(R(\phi_{1},t_{1}),R(\phi_{2},t_{2}))\sim\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})h(t_{1},t_{2}),

for some function
h
depending on
\tau
and the kernel function. If
\tau(t)=\delta
is constant, then
h(t_{1},t_{2})=h(t_{1}-t_{2})
and we obtain a covariance function being stationary in angle and time. We refer to
(
Jónsdóttir et al., 2008
, Prop. 7)
for details, where also a possible restriction in the angular dimension of the
ambit set is considered (see Sect. 5.2 in that paper). Noteworthy is the close link between the dependency structures obtained by a carefully selected ambit set and kernel function, and typical covariance functions appearing for other modeling approaches in stochastic geometry (for instance, see Ex. 10 and the discussion following in
Jónsdóttir et al. (2008)
).

5
Conclusion and outlook

Ambit fields constitute a rich and analytically tractable class of random fields that are suitable for modelling spatio-temporal phenomena.
We recall that four aspects of ambit stochastics set it apart from much of
the traditional literature on stochastic analysis:
First, the inclusion of stochastic volatility/intermittency
as an integrand allows for stochastic scaling of the noise component, providing great flexibility in modelling.
Second, the concept of metatimes naturally extends the idea of a time change of a stochastic process to spatio-temporal settings and constitutes a complementary method for incorporating stochastic volatility.
Third,
ambit sets are a new concept used to describe the region of interest where random shocks need to be taken into account.
Fourth, ambit processes and fields can allow for both semimartingale and non-semimartingale settings and can therefore be tailored to a wide range of applications.

Ole’s work on ambit stochastics had a lasting impact on probabilistic and statistical theory, methodology, and a wide range of applications, with turbulence always being of particular interest to Ole.

Research in ambit stochastics has developed further recently, so let us briefly outline some of the current trends and directions. This outlook is not intended to be exhaustive.

Inference for multivariate and high-dimensional settings:
The asymptotic theory presented in Section
2
predominantly focused on one-dimensional settings. In recent years, the theory was further adapted to multivariate settings, see e.g.
Heiny & Podolskij (2021)
.
Also, going back to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which is a classical example within the class of Lévy semistationary processes, the question of drift estimation in high-dimensional settings was tackled, see e.g.
Ciołek et al. (2020)
,
and its extensions to general diffusion settings, see
Ciolek et al. (2024)
.

Related work includes a network setting or graph structure into Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (and related) processes.
In this realm,
Courgeau & Veraart (2022a)
;
Courgeau & Veraart (2022b)
introduced the graphOU process, which was extended to multivariate and graph continuous time autoregressive processes in
Lucchese et al. (2023)
. Also, very much in the spirit of Ole’s and Robert Stelzer’s work on multivariate supOU processes, see
Barndorff-Nielsen & Stelzer (2011)
, a graph version of such processes has been proposed in
Mehta & Veraart (2024)
.
Also, cluster algorithms for determining groups in graphOU processes are currently being developed in
Norlyk & Veraart (2024)
.

Beyond the high-dimensional setting, work on ambit fields has been extended to the infinite-dimensional setting, see
Benth & Eyjolfsson (2017)
. Also,
Benth & Süss (2018)
developed continuous-time autoregressive moving-average processes in Hilbert spaces, which are connected to higher-order SPDEs like the stochastic wave equation.

packages

Ambit-Stochastics
