Meinardus

get precise results in studying the asymptotic behavior of the

He obtained asymptotic formulas of such partition numbers

to weight functions

(we also refer to
[

]

Debruyne and Tenenbaum
[

]

extended Meinardus’ results to partitions with restrictions on the parts
with support
\mathbb{N}\setminus f^{-1}\left(\left\{0\right\}\right)
. Granovsky, Stark, and Erlihson
introduced a probabilistic method to extend Meinardus’ result due to Khintchine
which applies to a variety of models in physics and combinatorics

[
GSE08
,
GS06
,
GS12
,
GS15
]
.

Recently, Bridges, Brindle, Bringmann, and Franke
[
B3F24
]
proved asymptotic formulas if the support is a multiset of integers and the zeta function has multiple poles.
This had been successfully applied
[
BFH24
]
to certain weight functions
f_{\ell}(n)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{\ell}(n)\,q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{-f_{\ell}(n)}

(e. g.
n^{\ell}
and

\lambda_{\ell}
, where
\lambda_{\ell}(n)
counts the number of subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
of index
n

In this paper we
consider sequences of weight functions
\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}

and invest in the log-concavity and log-convexity
of the partition functions
p_{E,{\ell}}\left(n\right)
and its
stability with respect to the configuration of the exceptional set
E
.
We restrict the infinite product to
n\in S\subset\mathbb{N}
and
denote by

E:=\mathbb{N}\setminus S
the exception set.
We assume that
1\in S
.

To state our results, we introduce some notation.

Definition 1
.

Throughout the paper, let
\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be a sequence of normalized arithmetic functions. We assume that
f_{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
and that
the initial function
f_{1}\equiv 1
.
Further, let
real valued functions
\Phi\left({\ell}\right),\Psi\left({\ell}\right)
on
\mathbb{N}
exist such that
the following hold true.

(1)

For all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have

n^{\Phi(\ell)}\leq f_{\ell}(n)\leq n^{\Psi(\ell)}
.

(2)

There exists a positive number
B
independent of
\ell
such that
0\leq\Psi(\ell)-\Phi(\ell)\leq B
.

(3)

We have
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\Phi(\ell)=\infty
.

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{N}
with
1\in S
. Then we
have the double sequence

\{p_{E,\ell}(n)\}_{\ell,n}
of partition numbers by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{E,\ell}(n)\,q^{n}=\prod_{n\in S}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{-f_{\ell}(n)}.

Moreover, we define

\Delta_{E,\ell}(n):=\left(p_{E,\ell}(n)\right)^{2}-p_{E,\ell}(n-1)\,p_{E,\ell}(n+1).

The double sequence

\{p_{E,\ell}(n)\}_{\ell,n}
is log-concave at
n
for the parameter
\ell
if
\Delta_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)\geq 0
and strictly log-concave if
\Delta_{E,\ell}(n)>0
.
We have the same definition for log-convexity if
\Delta_{E,\ell}(n)\leq 0
, and
strict log-convexity if
\Delta_{E,\ell}(n)<0
.

We begin by demonstrating the scope of our results on generalized

k
-regular partitions. Let
f_{\ell}(n):=n^{\ell-1}
and

E_{k}:=\{k^{m}\,:\,m\in\mathbb{N}\}

. Let

n
.
We denote by

is given by

\Big|\left\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in P\,:\,\lambda_{j}\neq k^{m},\,d,m\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }1\leq j\leq d\right\}\Big|.

Further,
p_{E_{k},2}(n)
is related to restricted plane partitions.
Note, that for
E=\emptyset
(no exceptions) and

that
\Delta_{\emptyset,\ell}(n)

is positive for almost all
\ell
and

. Further,

Let

3.
Preliminaries

Let
\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be given as in Definition
1
.
We associate
a sequence
\{g_{E,\ell}\}_{\ell}
of functions on
\mathbb{N}

via the following well-known formula

(3.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{E,\ell}(n)\,q^{n}=\prod_{n\in{S}}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{-f_{\ell}(n)}=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{E,\ell}(n)\frac{q^{n}}{n}\right).

We have

(3.2)

g_{E,\ell}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\notin E\end{subarray}}d\,f_{\ell}(d).

For example, let
f_{\ell}(n)=n^{\ell-1}
and an exception set
E
be given.
Then
g_{E,\ell}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\notin E\end{subarray}}d^{\ell}
which we denote by

\sigma_{E,\ell}(n)
. If
\ell=1
we usually drop the index and as usual we have

\sigma_{\ell}(n)=\sigma_{\emptyset,\ell}(n)
if we have no exceptions.
Since
1
is no exception we have ensured that the largest divisor

n_{{S}}

of
n
in
S
exists. The following bounds for
g_{E,\ell}(n)

will turn out to be very useful.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
E
and
\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be given. Then

(3.3)

\left(n_{S}\right)^{\Phi(\ell)+1}\leq g_{E,\ell}(n)\leq\sigma_{E,1}(n)\,\left(n_{S}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}

Proof.

We have

g_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\notin E\end{subarray}}df_{\ell}\left(d\right)\geq n_{S}f_{\ell}\left(n_{S}\right)\geq n_{S}^{\Phi\left(\ell\right)+1}

and

g_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\notin E\end{subarray}}dn_{S}^{\Psi\left(\ell\right)}\leq\sigma_{E,1}\left(n\right)n_{S}^{\Psi\left(\ell\right)}
.
∎

From the second equation of (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), we get that

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\left(m_{1,S}\cdots m_{k,S}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)},

where
m_{j,S}
is the largest divisor of
m_{j}
belonging to the support
S

(
S=\mathbb{N}\setminus E
). Now, let us set

M_{E}\left(n\right):=\max_{k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}m_{1}\cdots m_{k}.

If it is clear from the context, we will write
M_{E}
instead of
M_{E}(n)
. From the above discussion, we obtain the upper bound of the form

p_{E,\ell}(n)\leq\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,M_{E}^{\Psi(\ell)}=p_{E}(n)\,\left(M_{E}(n)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}.

Next, we derive the lower bound. It follows that

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(m_{1})\cdots g_{E,\ell}(m_{k})}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\left(m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}(n)\end{subarray}}\left(M_{E}(n)\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}.

This leads us to the following general result.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Let
\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be given as in Definition
1
.
Let
E
be the exception set.
Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
determine the growth of
f_{\ell}
as given in Definition
1
.
Then there exist positive
constants
C_{E}^{\downarrow}(n)
and
C_{E}^{\uparrow}(n)

dependent only on
E
and
n
such that:

\frac{C_{E}^{\downarrow}(n)\,M_{E}(n)^{2\Phi(\ell)}}{\left(M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}\leq\frac{p_{E,\ell}(n)^{2}}{p_{E,\ell}(n-1)\,p_{E,\ell}(n+1)}\leq\frac{C_{E}^{\uparrow}(n)\,M_{E}(n)^{2\Psi(\ell)}}{\left(M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}}.

Throughout the manuscript, we assume that
\Psi(\ell)-\Phi(\ell)\leq B
, where
B\geq 0
is fixed and independent of
\ell
. That requirement together with Proposition
3.2
delivers an efficient
criterion for the log-concavity or the log-convexity of

p_{E,\ell}
at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
. More precisely, it turns out that the key information
lies at the value of the quotient

Q_{E}(n):=\frac{M_{E}(n)^{2}}{M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)}

as the following criterion exhibits.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
E
and

\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be given as in Definition
1
.
Then
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is strictly

(1)

log-concave at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
if
Q_{E}(n)>1
;

(2)

log-convex at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
if
Q_{E}(n)<1
.

Proof.

Since both of the cases are very similar, we only prove the criterion for the log-concavity property. Thus, let us require that
Q_{E}(n)>1
. Proposition
3.2
asserts that

\displaystyle\frac{p_{E,\ell}(n)^{2}}{p_{E,\ell}(n-1)\,p_{E,\ell}(n+1)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{E}^{\downarrow}(n)\,M_{E}(n)^{2\Phi(\ell)}}{\left(M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{E}^{\downarrow}(n)}{\left(M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{B}}\left(\frac{M_{E}(n)^{2}}{M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)}\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{E}^{\downarrow}(n)}{\left(M_{E}(n-1)\,M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{B}}Q_{E}(n)^{\Phi(\ell)}\xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}\infty,

where the last line is the consequence of the facts that
\Phi(\ell)\xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}\infty
and
Q_{E}(n)>1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

There is a natural question arising from the statement of Theorem
3.3
, namely, what can one say about the log-behavior of
p_{E,\ell}(n)
if
Q_{E}(n)=1.
This situation is more intricate, and we
saw in Example
3
that both phenomena (log-concavity and log-convexity) might occur in such a setting.

Remark
.

If there is a prime number
p
such that
p\mid M_{E}(n)^{2}
and
p\nmid M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)
or conversely, then
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is either strictly log-concave or strictly log-convex for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
.

4.
Varying the
quotient
Q_{E}(n)

For various choices of the exception set
E
, we now apply Theorem
3.3
to describe the log-behavior of
p_{E,\ell}(n)
at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
. Therefore, we need to determine the quotients

Q_{E}(n)
. Actually, it turns out that their values only depend on the
exception set
E
and the residue class of
n\pmod{r}
, where
r
is
directly related to
E
.

4.1.
The case of
\mathbf{1,2,3\not\in E}

Here, Lemma
6.3
maintains that

r=3
—that is because the largest products heavily depend on the number
of
3
s appearing in their corresponding compositions. For
n\geq 3
, it also guarantees that

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}3^{n/3},&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
4\cdot 3^{(n-4)/3},&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\\
2\cdot 3^{(n-2)/3},&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{array}\right.

In consequence, this leads to

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{9}{8},&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
\frac{8}{9},&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\text{ and}\\
1,&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, Theorem
3.3
asserts that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
and log-convex if
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.
This proves Theorem
2.1
.
On the other hand, the case of
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
needs some additional investigation.

4.2.
The case of
\mathbf{1,2,5\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{3\in E}

In such a setting, Lemma
6.3
points out that
r=4
and

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{n/2},&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
5\cdot 2^{(n-5)/2},&n\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
2^{n/2},&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
5\cdot 2^{(n-5)/2},&n\equiv 3\pmod{4},\end{array}\right.

for any
n\geq 4
. This gives us that

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2^{5}}{5^{2}},&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
\frac{5^{2}}{2^{5}},&n\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
\frac{2^{5}}{5^{2}},&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\text{ and}\\
\frac{5^{2}}{2^{5}},&n\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{array}\right.

Hence, Theorem
3.3
maintains that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and log-convex if
n\equiv 1\pmod{2}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
, and this is a complete characterization in that case.
This proves Theorem
2.3
in
the case
5\in S
.

4.3.
The case of
\mathbf{1,2\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{3,5\in E}

Once again, Lemma
6.3
guarantees that
r=4
and

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{n/2},&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
2^{(n-1)/2},&n\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
2^{n/2},&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
2^{(n-1)/2},&n\equiv 3\pmod{4}\end{array}\right.

for any non-negative integer
n
. This leads to

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2,&n\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
\frac{1}{2},&n\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
2,&n\equiv 2\pmod{4},\text{ and}\\
\frac{1}{2},&n\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, we conclude that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and log-convex if
n\equiv 1\pmod{2}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
, and this is a complete characterization in that case.
This finishes the proof of
Theorem
2.3
with the
case
5\in E
.

4.4.
The case of
\mathbf{1,3,5\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{2,4\in E}

In order to determine the maximal product
M_{E}(n)
here, we use Lemma
6.1
. It says that each number
u\geq 6
can not occur as a part of the compositions corresponding to
M_{E}(n)
for any
n\geq 3
. Moreover, the number
5
may appear at most once—otherwise it is always better to replace
(5,5)
by
(3,3,3,1)
. Thus, we put
r=3
, and obtain

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}3^{n/3},&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
3^{(n-1)/3},&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\\
5\cdot 3^{(n-5)/3},&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}\end{array}\right.

for any
n\geq 3
.
This further leads to

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{3^{2}}{5},&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
\frac{3}{5},&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\text{ and}\\
\frac{5^{2}}{3^{3}},&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{array}\right.

In conclusion,
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
and log-convex if
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
, and this is a complete characterization in that case.
This finishes the proofs of
Theorem
2.4

and Theorem
2.5
in the
case
5\in S
.

4.5.
The case of
\mathbf{1,3\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{2,4,5\in E}

Here, the direct application of Corollary
6.2
gives us
r=3
and

(4.1)

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}3^{n/3},&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
3^{(n-1)/3},&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\\
3^{(n-2)/3},&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}\end{array}\right.

for every non-negative integer
n
.
This, in consequence, leads to

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}3,&n\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
1,&n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\text{ and}\\
\frac{1}{3},&n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{array}\right.

Again, Theorem
3.3
asserts that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
and log-convex if
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.
This finishes the proof of
Theorem
2.4
in the case

5\in E
.
On the other hand, the case of
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
needs some additional investigation.

4.6.
The case of
\mathbf{s\geq 3}
,
\mathbf{1,s,s+1\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{2,3,\ldots,s-1\in E}

Here, Lemma
6.5
maintains that
r=s
and

M_{E}\left(n\right)=\left(r+1\right)^{j}r^{\frac{n-j(r+1)}{r}},\qquad n\equiv j\pmod{r}

for all
n>r(r-1)(3r-1)/2
and
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}
. Further, this leads to

Q_{E}\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{r+1}}{(r+1)^{r}},&n\equiv 0\pmod{r},\\
1,&n\equiv 1,\ldots,r-2\pmod{r},\text{ and}\\
\frac{(r+1)^{r}}{r^{r+1}},&n\equiv r-1\pmod{r}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, Theorem
3.3
asserts that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave if
n\equiv 0\pmod{r}
and log-convex if
n\equiv-1\pmod{r}
for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.
This finishes the proof of
Theorem
2.6
in the cases

n\equiv 0,r-1\pmod{r}
.
On the other hand, the cases of
n\not\equiv 0,-1\pmod{r}
need some additional investigation.

5.
The problematic quotient case:
Q_{E}(n)=1

In the previous section, we got a few problematic cases with
Q_{E}\left(n\right)=1
(
4.1
,
4.5
,
4.6
). Since it is not clear what kind of log-behavior one should predict in these instances, we slightly extend the approach from Section
3

to obtain a
more general criterion than Theorem
3.3
. In order to do that, an additional assumption throughout this section is needed, namely, we require that

(5.1)

\displaystyle M_{E}(n)>M_{E}(n-1)

for all
n\geq n_{0}
, where
n_{0}
is some positive integer.

Remark
.

Let us notice that the inequality (
5.1
) is satisfied in both
cases
4.1

and
4.6
.
However, it is easy to notice that this condition does not hold in the
case
4.5
—there we have
M_{E}(n)=M_{E}(n-1)
whenever

n\geq 4
and
n\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.

We start by determining more sophisticated estimations for both the lower and the upper bounds of
p_{E,\ell}(n)
. For the upper bound, we have that

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\not=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\left(m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
\exists 1\leq i\leq k:m_{i}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\left(m_{1,S}\cdots m_{k,S}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}.

Now, let us observe that the expression in the last line might be estimated from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
\exists 1\leq i\leq k:m_{i}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\left(\max_{1\leq l\leq n-1}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in S\\
n_{1}+\ldots+n_{l}\leq n-1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{l}n_{i}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
\exists 1\leq i\leq k:m_{i}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
\exists 1\leq i\leq k:m_{i}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\tilde{M}_{E}(n)^{\Psi(\ell)}

where

\tilde{M}_{E}\left(n\right):=\max_{k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\not=M_{E}\end{subarray}}m_{1}\cdots m_{k},

and the last inequality is a consequence of (
5.1
) and the fact that we can always put
1
as an additional part to any composition of
n-1
. Thus, we get that

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\not=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\left(m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
\exists 1\leq i\leq k:m_{i}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots\sigma_{E,1}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}\,\tilde{M}_{E}(n)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}+p_{E,1}(n)\tilde{M}_{E}(n)^{\Psi(\ell)}.

The lower bound takes the form

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}.

If the maximal product
M_{E}(n)
is
attained by a unique
S
-partition, say
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
, then one can
rewrite the above estimations as follows:

(5.2)

\displaystyle A_{E}(n)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(m_{i})}{m_{i}}\leq p_{E,\ell}(n)\leq A_{E}(n)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(m_{i})}{m_{i}}+p_{E,1}(n)\tilde{M}_{E}(n)^{\Psi(\ell)},

where
A_{E}(n)
is the number of all compositions of
n
with parts in
S
for
which the maximal value
M_{E}(n)
is attained divided by
k!
.

Now, we are ready to present an extension of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
E
,
\Phi
,
\Psi
, and

\{f_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
be given. Suppose further that the inequality

M_{E}(n)>M_{E}(n-1)
holds for every
n\geq n_{0}
and some positive integer

n_{0}
. Let us also assume that
Q_{E}\left(n\right)=1
for some

n>n_{0}
, and the maximal products
M_{E}(n),M_{E}(n-1)
,
and
M_{E}(n+1)
are attained by the unique
S
-partitions
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a})
,

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{b})
, and
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{c})
(respectively) such that

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\ell}(x_{1})^{2}\cdots g_{\ell}(x_{a})^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}\cdots x_{a}^{2}}\sim\frac{g_{\ell}(y_{1})\cdots g_{\ell}(y_{b})g_{\ell}(z_{1})\cdots g_{\ell}(z_{c})}{y_{1}\cdots y_{b}z_{1}\cdots z_{c}}

for large
\ell
.
Then
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is strictly

(1)

log-concave at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
if
\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}>1
,

(2)

log-convex at
n
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
if
\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}<1
,

where the values
A_{E}(n),A_{E}(n-1)
, and
A_{E}(n+1)
are defined as in (
5.2
).

Proof.

Since both cases are very similar, let us only deal with the criterion for the log-concavity property. The assumptions from the statement together with the inequalities (
5.2
) assert that

p_{E,\ell}(n)^{2}\geq A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(x_{i})}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}

and

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n-1)\,p_{E,\ell}(n+1)\leq

\displaystyle\left(A_{E}(n-1)\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}+p_{E,1}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle{}\cdot\left(A_{E}(n+1)\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}+p_{E,1}(n+1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}

\displaystyle{}+A_{E}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle{}+A_{E}(n+1)p_{E,1}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}

\displaystyle{}+p_{E,1}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)\left(\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}.

Next, we want to compare all of the above four summands with the estimations for
p_{\ell,S}(n)
.
In the first case, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(x_{i})}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}}\sim\frac{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}{A_{E}(n)^{2}}<1

for sufficiently large values of

\ell
.
The second one, on the other hand, requires a little bit more computations:

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(x_{i})}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)M_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{-B}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{1}(n)\left(\frac{\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)}{M_{E}(n+1)}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}\rightarrow 0

for sufficiently large
\ell
.
The third case is similar to the second one and presents as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n+1)p_{E,1}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(x_{i})}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n+1)p_{E,1}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n+1)p_{E,1}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{A_{E}(n+1)p_{E,1}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)^{\Psi(\ell)+1}\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{-B}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{2}(n)\left(\frac{\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)}{M_{E}(n-1)}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}\rightarrow 0

for sufficiently large
\ell
.
The last instance can be estimated in the following way

\displaystyle\frac{p_{E,1}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)\left(\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(x_{i})}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{p_{E,1}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)\left(\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{g_{E,\ell}(z_{i})}{z_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{p_{E,1}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)\left(\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Phi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{p_{E,1}(n-1)p_{E,1}(n+1)\left(\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}{A_{E}(n)^{2}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{-B}\left(M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{3}(n)\left(\frac{\tilde{M}_{E}(n-1)\tilde{M}_{E}(n+1)}{M_{E}(n-1)M_{E}(n+1)}\right)^{\Psi(\ell)}\rightarrow 0

for sufficiently large values of

\ell
.
All of the above calculations lead us finally to the conclusion that

\displaystyle\frac{p_{E,\ell}(n-1)p_{E,\ell}(n+1)}{p_{E,\ell}(n)^{2}}\rightarrow\frac{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}{A_{E}(n)^{2}}<1,

for sufficiently large values of

\ell

and this finishes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

Once again, the natural question arises from the statement of Theorem
5.1
, namely, what can one say about the log-behavior of
p_{E,\ell}(n)
if
\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}=1
. That case becomes even more difficult, and we saw
in Example
3
that there is no
easy answer to that problem.

Now, we apply Theorem
5.1
to investigate some of the remaining cases from Section
4
. At this point, let us observe that we can not simply use our new criterion to deal with the instance
4.1
. Mainly because, we do not have the property with unique partition for large
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
whenever
4\not\in E
—that directly follows from Lemma
6.3
. Therefore, we divide the case
4.1
into two sub-cases depending on belonging of the number
4
to the exception set.

5.1.
The case of
\mathbf{1,2,3\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{4\in E}

Here, Lemma
6.3
guarantees that the assumption from the statement of Theorem
5.1
hold, and one can apply it for any
n>3
with
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}=\frac{\frac{1}{\left(\frac{n-2}{3}\right)!^{2}}}{\frac{1}{2!\left(\frac{n-5}{3}\right)!\left(\frac{n+1}{3}\right)!}}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-2}>1.

Hence, Theorem
5.1
points out that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.
This finishes the proof of
Theorem
2.2
in the case

4\in E
.

5.2.
The case of
\mathbf{1,2,3,4\not\in E}

In order to use Theorem
5.1
to deal with that instance, we need to consider two additional cases depending on the relation between
g_{\ell}(2)
and
g_{\ell}(4)
for all sufficiently large values of
\ell
.

5.2.1.

Let us assume that there exists
\ell_{0}>0
such that
2g_{\ell}(4)>\delta g_{\ell}(2)^{2}
for some fixed
\delta>1
and every
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
. Moreover, let
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
and
n>2(\delta+2)/(\delta-1)
. Here, all of the conditions from the statement of Theorem
5.1
are satisfied except the uniqueness of the

S
-partition for which
M_{E}(n-1)
is attained (as Lemma
6.3
indicates). But it does not make a big difference. In fact, we just need to replace the value of
A_{E}(n-1)
by the number
A_{E}^{\star}(n-1)
that takes into account all of the compositions
with parts in
S
, for which
M_{E}(n-1)
is attained. We have that

\displaystyle p_{E,\ell}(n-1)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\leq n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n-1\\
m_{1}\cdots m_{k}=M_{E}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k!}\,\frac{g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{1}\right)\cdots g_{E,\ell}\left(m_{k}\right)}{m_{1}\cdots m_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2!\left(\frac{n-5}{3}\right)!}\frac{g_{\ell}(2)^{2}}{2^{2}}\left(\frac{g_{\ell}(3)}{3}\right)^{\frac{n-5}{3}}+\frac{1}{\left(\frac{n-5}{3}\right)!}\frac{g_{\ell}(4)}{4}\left(\frac{g_{\ell}(3)}{3}\right)^{\frac{n-5}{3}}

\displaystyle>\frac{1+\delta}{2!\left(\frac{n-5}{3}\right)!}\frac{g_{\ell}(2)^{2}}{2^{2}}\left(\frac{g_{\ell}(3)}{3}\right)^{\frac{n-5}{3}}=:A_{E}^{\star}(n-1)\frac{\left(g_{\ell}\left(2\right)\right)^{2}}{2^{2}}\left(\frac{g_{\ell}\left(3\right)}{3}\right)^{\frac{n-5}{3}},

where the last inequality holds for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
. Now, we can use
Theorem
5.1
for the values
A_{E}(n)
,
A_{E}(n-1)^{\star}
,
and
A_{E}(n+1)
and obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}^{\star}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}\sim\frac{2}{1+\delta}\frac{n+1}{n-2}<1,

where the last inequality follows from our assumption that
n>2(\delta+2)/(\delta-1)
. In conclusion, we get that
p_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)
is log-convex for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.

5.2.2.

Let us assume that
n>3
,
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, and there exists
\ell_{0}>0
such that
2g_{\ell}(4)<\delta g_{\ell}\left(2\right)^{2}
for some fixed
\delta<1
and every
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
. Since the reasoning is analogous to the previous one, we omit it here and only notice that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{E}(n)^{2}}{A_{E}^{\star}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}\sim\frac{2}{1+\delta}\frac{n+1}{n-2}>1,

which indicates that
p_{E,\ell}\left(n\right)
is log-concave for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.

At this point, it is worth saying that our approach does not cover the cases investigated by Abdesselam, Heim, and Neuhauser
[
AHN24
,
HN22
]
, because the appropriate parameters
\delta
and
\ell_{0}
do not exist there.

5.3.
The case of
\mathbf{s\geq 3}
,
\mathbf{1,s,s+1\not\in E}
and
\mathbf{2,3,\ldots,s-1\in E}

Here, Lemma
6.5
maintains that one can directly apply Theorem
5.1
for any
n>r(r-1)(3r-1)/2+1
,
n\equiv j\pmod{r}
with
j\in\{1,\ldots,r-2\}
, where
r=s
. We get that

\displaystyle\frac{\left(A_{E}\left(n\right)\right)^{2}}{A_{E}(n-1)A_{E}(n+1)}=\frac{j+1}{j}\frac{n-j(r+1)+r}{n-j(r+1)}>1,

which means that
p_{E,\ell}(n)
is log-concave for all but finitely many numbers
\ell
.

At the end of this section, it is worth pointing out that the approach presented here can not be explicitly applied to deal with the last remaining case from Section
4.5
.

6.
Lemmata

Here, we collect all auxiliary properties that are used throughout the manuscript.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
S=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be such a set of positive integers that
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
,
a_{2}\geq 3
,
a_{j-1}<2a_{2}
and
a_{j}\geq 2a_{2}
for some
j\geq 3
. Then the largest value

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}

can not be attained at the partition
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
if there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
such that
m_{i}=a_{l}
for some
l\geq j
.

Proof.

Let us suppose that
a_{l}
occurs as a part of
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
. Thus, we
may write
a_{l}=ua_{2}+v
for some non-negative integers
u\geq 2
and

v\in\{0,1,\ldots,a_{2}-1\}
. In particular, it follows that one can replace
the part
a_{l}
by
u

a_{2}
s and
v
ones. After such a switch it suffices to show that

\displaystyle(u+1)a_{2}\leq a_{2}^{u},

and that is an elementary exercise. This completes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence of the preceeding lemma, we get the following property.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
S=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be such a set of positive integers that
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
,
a_{2}\geq 3
and
2a_{2}\leq a_{3}
. Then we have that

\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=a_{2}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{a_{2}}\right\rfloor},

which is attained at the partition

(a_{2}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{a_{2}}\right\rfloor},1^{n-a_{2}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{a_{2}}\right\rfloor}).

Despite the fact that Corollary
6.2
might be
applied for various sets
S
, we still do not have any information about the
considered maximum value, when
a_{2}<a_{3}<2a_{2}
or
a_{2}=2
. Let us investigate these cases separately. At first, we assume that
a_{2}=2
. In such a setting, one can deduce the following.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
S=\{1,2,a_{3},\ldots\}
be such a set of positive integers that
2<a_{3}<a_{4}<\cdots
. For a natural number
n
, we have the following description of

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}.

(1)

If
a_{3}=3
and
4\not\in S
, then it is attained at

\displaystyle(1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n=1,

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n}{3}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{3},

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n-4}{3}},2,2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\geq 4\text{ and }n\equiv 1\pmod{3},

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n-2}{3}},2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.

Similarly, if
a_{3}=3
and
a_{4}=4
, then the maximum value is attained at

\displaystyle(1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n=1,

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n}{3}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{3},

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n-4}{3}},2,2),\left(4,3^{\frac{n-4}{3}}\right),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\geq 4\text{ and }n\equiv 1\pmod{3},

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n-2}{3}},2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3}.

(2)

If
a_{3}=4
and
5\not\in S
, then it is attained at

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n}{2}}),(4,2^{\frac{n-4}{2}}),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n}{4}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n-1}{2}},1),(4,2^{\frac{n-5}{2}},1),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n-1}{4}},1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 1\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n}{2}}),(4,2^{\frac{n-4}{2}}),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n-2}{4}},2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 2\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n-1}{2}},1),(4,2^{\frac{n-5}{2}},1),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n-3}{4}},2,1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 3\pmod{4}.

On the other hand, if
a_{3}=4
and
a_{4}=5
, then the relevant partitions take the forms
(1),(2)
and

(2,1)
for
n\leq 3
, and

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n}{2}}),(4,2^{\frac{n-4}{2}}),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n}{4}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(5,2^{\frac{n-5}{2}}),(5,4,2^{\frac{n-9}{2}}),\ldots,(5,4^{\frac{n-5}{4}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 1\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n}{2}}),(4,2^{\frac{n-4}{2}}),\ldots,(4^{\frac{n-2}{4}},2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 2\pmod{4},

\displaystyle(5,2^{\frac{n-5}{2}}),(5,4,2^{\frac{n-9}{2}}),\ldots,(5,4^{\frac{n-7}{4}},2),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 3\pmod{4},

for
n\geq 4
.

(3)

If
a_{3}=5
, then it is attained at

\displaystyle(1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n=1,

\displaystyle(2,1),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n=3,

\displaystyle(5,2^{\frac{n-5}{2}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\geq 5\text{ and }n\equiv 1\pmod{2},

\displaystyle(2^{\frac{n}{2}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

(4)

If
a_{3}\geq 6
, then it is attained at

\displaystyle(2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor},1^{n-2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}).

Proof.

The proof of the point
(4)
is an analogue
of the proof of Lemma
6.1
. Therefore, we omit it here. On the other hand, the remaining cases are similar to each other. Hence, we show
(2)
and leave the rest as an exercise to the reader.

At first, let us suppose that
5\notin S
. Since we can always replace
4
by
two
2
s and vice versa,
1
can not occur more than once as a part, and we know that the point
(4)
is true, it follows that the desired maximum is attained at the appropriate partitions.

On the other hand, if
a_{4}=5
, then the number
5
can occur at most once as
a part. Otherwise, it is better to replace two
5
s by, for example, five

2
s (
5^{2}<2^{5}
). Additionally, it is easy to see that
1
can not appear as a part if
n\geq 5
. Indeed, we may just simply replace
(2,2,1)
or
(4,1)
by
(5)
, or
(5,1)
by
(2,2,2)
. This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
S=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be such a set of positive integers that
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
,
a_{2}\geq 3
and
a_{j}<2a_{2}
and
a_{j+1}\geq 2a_{2}
for some
j\geq 3
. For a natural number
n
, the value of

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}.

can not be attained at the partition
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
if there exists
a_{i}
for
i\in\{1,3,4,\ldots,j\}
which occurs more than
a_{2}-1
times as a part in
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
.

Proof.

Clearly,
1
can not appear as a part more than
a_{2}-1
times. Let us suppose
that there is some
a_{i}
for
i\in\{3,4,\ldots,j\}
with
multiplicity

a_{2}
in the partition. Thus, we may replace
a_{2}

a_{i}
s by
a_{i}

a_{2}
s and examine whether the inequality

\displaystyle a_{i}^{a_{2}}<a_{2}^{a_{i}}

is true. For the sake of convenience, let us set
u:=a_{2}
and
v:=a_{i}
, and notice that
u<v
. Hence, we may rewrite the above inequality as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\ln{v}}{v}<\frac{\ln{u}}{u}.

Finally, let us observe that the function
\ln{x}/x
is decreasing for
x>e
. This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
S=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be such a set of positive integers that
1<a_{2}<\cdots
,
a_{2}\geq 3
,
a_{3}=a_{2}+1
,
a_{j}<2a_{2}
and
a_{j+1}\geq 2a_{2}
for some
j\geq 3
. Suppose further that
n\geq a_{2}(a_{2}-1)(3a_{2}-1)/2
. Then the value of

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in S\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}.

is attained at

\displaystyle(a_{3}^{i},a_{2}^{\frac{n-i\cdot a_{3}}{a_{2}}}),

\displaystyle\text{ if }n\equiv i\pmod{a_{2}}

for every
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,a_{2}-1\}
.

Proof.

At first, let us note that Lemma
6.1
ensures that
a_{i}
