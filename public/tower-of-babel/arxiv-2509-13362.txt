From Quantum Tsallis Entropy to Strange Metals
Xian-Hui Ge

Abstract
We develop a unified framework connecting quantum Tsallis statistics to electronic transport in strongly interacting systems. Starting from Rényi and Tsallis entropies, we construct a quantum Tsallis distribution that reduces to the conventional Fermi–Dirac distribution when q = 1 q=1 . For q q slightly deviating from unity, the correction term in the occupation function can be mapped to a q q -deformed Schwarzian action, corresponding to soft reparametrization modes. Coupling these soft modes to electrons via the Fermi Golden Rule yields a modified scattering rate, which reproduces conventional Fermi-liquid behavior at low temperatures and linear-in-temperature resistivity at high temperatures. Using the memory matrix formalism, we analyze magnetotransport, finding a linear-in-field magnetoresistance and a Hall angle consistent with Anderson’s two-lifetime scenario. At sufficiently low temperatures, both magnetoresistance and Hall response smoothly recover Fermi-liquid quadratic behaviors. This approach provides a controlled interpolation between Fermi-liquid and non-Fermi-liquid regimes, quantitatively linking q q -deformation, soft-mode dynamics, and experimentally measurable transport coefficients in strange metals.

Quantum Tsallis statistics
-
For a quantum system described by a density matrix
\rho
, the Tsallis entropy of order
q\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}=\frac{1-\Tr(\rho^q)}{q-1},\qquad(q\neq 1),

(2)

The Tsallis entropy is closely related to the Rényi entropy, defined in equation (
1
)
and the two obey the following relation

S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}=\frac{1}{q-1}\left[1-(1-q)e^{S_{q}^{\mathrm{R\'{e}nyi}}}\right].

(3)

This expression shows that the Tsallis entropy provides a power-law expansion of the exponential Rényi entropy. In the limit
q\to 1
, both Tsallis and Rényi entropies smoothly reduce to the von Neumann entropy,

S_{\rm vN}=\lim_{q\to 1}S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}=-\Tr(\rho\log\rho).

The Tsallis entropy is non-additive for independent systems, reflecting possible long-range correlations

S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}(\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}(\rho_{A})+S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}(\rho_{B})+(1-q)\,S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}(\rho_{A})\,S_{q}^{\mathrm{Tsallis}}(\rho_{B}).

From the Tsallis and Rényi entropies, we can obtain the corresponding distribution. Using the Langrangian multiplier method with constraints
Tr\rho=1
and
{\rm Tr}(\rho H)=\langle E\rangle
, we can write down the Langrangian as follows

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{1-q}\ln\bigg({\rm Tr} \rho^q\bigg)-\alpha({\rm Tr}\rho-1)-\beta\bigg({\rm Tr}(\rho H)-\langle E\rangle\bigg).

(4)

We then obtain

\delta\mathcal{L}=\frac{q}{1-q}\frac{{\rm Tr}(\rho^{q-1}\delta\rho)}{{\rm Tr}(\rho^{q})}-\alpha{\rm Tr}\delta\rho-\beta{\rm Tr}(H\delta\rho).

(5)

From maximal entropy
\delta\mathcal{L}=0
and after some manipulation, we find

\;\rho\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{q}}\,\Big[\,1-(1-q)\beta H\,\Big]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\;,

where
[X]_{+}
denotes the positive part of a self-adjoint operator
X
: in the spectral resolution
H=\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}|\varepsilon_{i}\rangle\langle\varepsilon_{i}|
, the operator inside the bracket has eigenvalues
1-(1-q)\beta\varepsilon_{i}
, which are replaced by
\max\{\,1-(1-q)\beta\varepsilon_{i},\,0\,\}
; this enforces
\rho\geq 0
and implements the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker boundary condition of the constrained maximization
[
29
,
30
]
. The normalization
\Tr\rho=1
fixes the partition function,

\;Z_{q}\;=\;\Tr\!\left(\Big[\,1-(1-q)\beta H\,\Big]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\right)\;,

which is finite whenever the trace exists; for unbounded
H
, one either has a high-energy cutoff for
q<1
(since
1-(1-q)\beta\varepsilon\geq 0
implies
\varepsilon\leq 1/[(1-q)\beta]
) or a power-law tail for
q>1
with convergence controlled by the growth of the spectral density. In the Gibbs limit
q\to 1
, the well-known identity
\lim_{q\to 1}\big[1-(1-q)\beta H\big]^{1/(q-1)}=e^{-\beta H}
applies eigenvalue-wise and gives

\rho\;\to\;\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{{\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta H})},\qquad Z_{q}\;\to\;Z={\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta H}),

so the Tsallis ensemble continuously deforms the canonical ensemble and recovers it at
q=1
.

For quantum statistics with indistinguishable particles, the same
q
-deformation propagates to the grand-canonical occupation numbers once one replaces the ordinary exponential by the
q
-exponential while enforcing the exchange symmetry. The generalized quantum occupations can be written compactly as

n_{q}(\varepsilon)\;=\;\frac{1}{\big[\,1-(1-q)\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)\,\big]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-q}}+a},

where
\beta=1/T
and
\mu
is the chemical potential; the parameter
a=+1
gives the Tsallis–Fermi–Dirac case (Pauli exclusion),
a=-1
the Tsallis–Bose–Einstein case (Bose enhancement), and
a=0
the Tsallis–Boltzmann classical limit. This expression reduces to the standard Fermi–Dirac/Bose–Einstein/Boltzmann forms as
q\to 1
. If one prefers the alternative but equivalent representation used in some conventions, the denominator can be written as
\big[1+(q-1)\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)\big]^{1/(q-1)}+a
, noting that
[1+(1-q)x]^{1/(q-1)}=\big([1+(q-1)(-x)]^{1/(q-1)}\big)^{-1}
; either choice is consistent with the operator formula for
\rho
above, provided the same convention for the
q
-exponential is used throughout and the spectral positive-part enforces positivity and, when
q<1
, the finite-energy support of the ensemble.

q-deformed Schwarzian action-
We show that the Tsallis-modified fermion distribution can be interpreted as arising from an effective reparametrization theory for the low-energy modes of nearly-
\mathrm{AdS}_{2}
gravity.
In the Tsallis framework, deviations from the Fermi-Dirac (FD) distribution are parameterized by
(q-1)
, which controls the strength of non-extensive effects.
At
q=1
, the standard FD form is recovered.
For
q\neq 1
, the distribution acquires a correction term
\phi(x)
that is quadratic in the dimensionless energy variable
x
and weighted by the thermal factor
n_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)[1-n_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)]
.
We reinterpret
x
as the Euclidean frequency operator,
x\leftrightarrow-i\partial_{\tau}
, and promote
\phi(x)
to a local functional
\Phi[f](\tau)
of the time-reparametrization mode
f(\tau)
.
This functional encodes the same fluctuation structure as
\phi(x)
, but now in the geometric language of boundary diffeomorphisms.
In the
q\to 1
limit,
\phi(x)
vanishes and
\Phi[f](\tau)
disappears, leaving only the Schwarzian sector; this ensures a smooth interpolation between the Tsallis-deformed and undeformed cases.

We start from the expansion of the quantum Tsallis distribution around
q=1
:

n_{q}(x)=n_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)+(q-1)\,\phi(x)+\cdots,\qquad x=\beta(\epsilon-\mu),

(6)

where

\phi(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}\,n_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)\big[1-n_{\mathrm{FD}}(x)\big].

(7)

Thus,
\phi(x)
can be interpreted as the variance of the Fermi–Dirac occupation number, modulated by a quadratic prefactor
x^{2}
. Since
n_{\mathrm{FD}}(1-n_{\mathrm{FD}})
is maximized at
x=0
and decays away from the Fermi surface, this thermal weight confines the correction to states near the Fermi level. In the frequency–time correspondence (taking the Euclidean Matsubara convention), we have
x=\beta\omega
and
i\partial_{\tau}\leftrightarrow\omega
. Hence,

x^{2}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \beta^{2}\omega^{2}\ \longleftrightarrow\ -\beta^{2}\partial_{\tau}^{2}.

(8)

This indicates that the quadratic factor
x^{2}
corresponds to the
\omega^{2}
contribution in frequency space, which in the time domain is equivalent to a second-order Euclidean time derivative (with an overall sign depending on Fourier conventions). Under the assumption of low-frequency dynamics and a sufficiently smooth response kernel, the frequency-domain correction
\phi(x)
can be promoted to a local time-domain functional
\Phi[f](\tau)
. In this sense, the
(q-1)
correction to the distribution can be reinterpreted as a soft-mode contribution involving second-order time derivatives of the reparametrization mode
f(\tau)
. This identification is approximate: for strongly nonlocal frequency responses
|x|\gg 1
, the local replacement by
\partial_{\tau}^{2}
is no longer valid. Motivated by the structure of
\phi(x)
, we define the local functional

\Phi[f](\tau)=\big(u^{\prime}(\tau)\big)^{2}-2\,u^{\prime\prime}(\tau),\qquad u(\tau)=\log f^{\prime}(\tau),

where
\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}=(u^{\prime})^{2}
and
\partial_{\tau}\log f^{\prime}(\tau)=u^{\prime}(\tau)
. The quadratic term in
\phi(x)
corresponds to
(u^{\prime})^{2}
in the geometric language, while the remaining structure naturally maps to the second derivative term
u^{\prime\prime}(\tau)
. The
q
-deformed effective action is then schematically written as

S_{q}[f]=-C\int\{f,\tau\}\,+\,(q-1)\,\lambda\int\Phi[f](\tau)\,+\,\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2}),

where
\{f,\tau\}
is the Schwarzian derivative and
\lambda
is a coupling determined by the microscopic model. For small deviations from the identity reparametrization,

f(\tau)=\tau+\varepsilon(\tau)
, the quadratic effective action for the
soft-mode fluctuations takes the form

S_{q}^{(2)}[\varepsilon]=\frac{\bar{C}_{\rm eff}}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\,(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\tau))^{2},

(9)

where the effective coupling is renormalized by the
q
-deformation,

\bar{C}_{\rm eff}=\bar{C}+\delta C\,(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2}).

In the limit
q\to 1
, one recovers the standard Schwarzian coupling
\bar{C}

and the familiar zero-mode solutions

f(\tau)=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\qquad ad-bc\neq 0,

up to small fluctuations of the soft mode
\varepsilon(\tau)
.
The quadratic action and its solution thus provide the leading approximation for
small
q-1
deformations. For
q\to 1
, the deformation term vanishes and the partition function describes
the motion of a particle along the boundary of
\mathrm{AdS}_{2}
with configuration
space
SL(2,\mathbb{R})/U(1)
.
Incorporating the Tsallis correction, the
q
-deformed Euclidean partition function
takes the form

Z_{q}=\int\frac{\mathcal{D}f}{SL(2,\mathbb{R})}\exp\left\{-\bar{C}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\left[\{f(\tau),\tau\}-(q-1)\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{f^{\prime}(\tau)}\right)^{2}\right]\right\},

(10)

where
\bar{C}
is the Schwarzian coupling constant.
The
q
-deformation of Eq. (
10
) contributes an additional quadratic term
to the effective action, which can be interpreted as a renormalization of the
Schwarzian stiffness and modifies the dynamics of the soft reparametrization mode.
The corresponding effective Hamiltonian reads

H=-\frac{1}{2\bar{C}}\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+\frac{1}{8\bar{C}\sinh^{2}(u)},

(11)

where
u
is the geodesic distance on the group manifold, and
u\to\infty

corresponds to asymptotically free motion.
The Hilbert space is given by the continuous unitary series of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. The corresponding Schrödinger equation reads

\left[-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+\frac{1}{4\sinh^{2}(u)}\right]\phi(u)=E\,\phi(u),

(12)

with eigenfunctions

\phi_{E}(u)=\sqrt{\sinh u}\,P^{-1/2+ik}_{1/2}(\cosh u),\qquad E=k^{2}.

(13)

The spectral density is determined by the Plancherel measure of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
representations
[
27
,
28
,
31
,
32
,
33
]

\rho(E)=\frac{\bar{C}}{2\pi^{2}}\sinh\!\left(2\pi\sqrt{2\bar{C}E}\right),

(14)

with asymptotic behaviors

\displaystyle\rho(E)\approx\frac{\bar{C}^{3/2}}{\pi}\sqrt{2E},~E\ll 1;~~~\rho(E)\sim\frac{\bar{C}}{4\pi^{2}}\exp\!\left(2\pi\sqrt{2\bar{C}E}\right),~E\gg 1.

(15)

Equation (
15
) exhibits the familiar square-root scaling of the density of states at low energies, analogous to the
1D
density of states in a quantum well. In the high-energy limit
E\gg 1
, the spectral density grows exponentially, which is consistent with a Cardy-like behavior
[
34
]
. This exponential growth reflects the rapid proliferation of high-energy states in the system, which can be interpreted as a manifestation of a large number of chaotic degrees of freedom
[
35
,
36
]
. In the context of the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model and its dual nearly-
AdS_{2}
gravity description, this corresponds to the presence of strongly interacting modes whose dynamics are effectively governed by the Schwarzian action. The Plancherel measure thus provides a precise connection between the group-theoretical structure of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and the statistical distribution of energy levels in these quantum chaotic systems
[
37
,
38
]
.

Electron–Schwarzian Soft-Mode Coupling-
We expand reparametrizations around the thermal saddle
f(\tau)=\tau+\varepsilon(\tau)

and fix the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
zero modes. Writing Fourier components

\varepsilon(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\varepsilon_{n}\,e^{-i\omega_{n}\tau},\qquad\omega_{n}=2\pi nT,\;n\neq 0,\pm 1,

the quadratic Schwarzian action takes the form

S^{(2)}_{\rm Sch}=2\pi\bar{C}T\sum_{n\geq 2}n(n^{2}-1)\,|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2},\qquad\varepsilon_{-n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{*}.

(16)

The modes
n=0,\pm 1
are excluded because they generate the

SL(2,\mathbb{R})
transformations (constant shift, scaling, and inversion)
under which the Schwarzian action is invariant. The
q
-deformation contributes an additional quadratic correction,

\Delta S^{(2)}_{q}=2\pi\bar{C}T\,\gamma_{q}\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{2}\,|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2},\qquad\gamma_{q}=\alpha\,(q-1),

(17)

where
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(1)
is a non-universal constant determined by the microscopic embedding. Combining Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
), the total quadratic action for the soft reparametrization modes becomes

S^{(2)}_{\rm tot}=2\pi\bar{C}T\sum_{n\geq 2}\Big[n(n^{2}-1)+\gamma_{q}n^{2}\Big]\,|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2},

(18)

where the first term
n(n^{2}-1)
is the standard Schwarzian spectrum after
fixing the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
zero modes, while the second term
\gamma_{q}n^{2}

represents the leading
q
-deformation effect.
Physically, the additive
\gamma_{q}n^{2}
term modifies the dispersion of the
soft reparametrization modes. While the Schwarzian spectrum

n(n^{2}-1)
grows cubically with
n
, the
q
-deformation introduces a
quadratic contribution that enhances the stiffness of low-
n
modes,
most prominently the
n=2
component. As a result, the
q
-deformation
shifts the balance between universal Schwarzian fluctuations and
non-Gaussian corrections inherited from the microscopic dynamics. The Matsubara propagator for the reparametrization (soft) modes is defined as the frequency-space two-point function

D(i\omega_{n})\equiv\langle|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2}\rangle=\frac{\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\varepsilon\;|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2}\,e^{-S^{(2)}_{\rm tot}[\varepsilon]}}{\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\varepsilon\;e^{-S^{(2)}_{\rm tot}[\varepsilon]}}.

(19)

Here
\varepsilon_{-n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
and the functional measure factorizes into independent integrals over the complex Fourier modes
\varepsilon_{n}
for each
n\geq 2
. Because the total action (
18
) is quadratic and diagonal in Fourier space, the functional integral factorizes into a product of complex Gaussian integrals. For a single complex variable
z\in\mathbb{C}
the standard identities

\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}z\,e^{-A|z|^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{A},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}z\,|z|^{2}e^{-A|z|^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{A^{2}}\quad(\real A>0)

imply

\frac{\int d^{2}z\,|z|^{2}e^{-A|z|^{2}}}{\int d^{2}z\,e^{-A|z|^{2}}}=\frac{1}{A}.

Applying this to each Fourier mode with quadratic kernel
2\pi\bar{C}TA_{n}
(where
A_{n}\equiv n(n^{2}-1)+\gamma_{q}n^{2}
) yields the exact Matsubara propagator in the Gaussian approximation

D(i\omega_{n})=\langle|\varepsilon_{n}|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi\bar{C}T}\,\frac{1}{\,A_{n}\,}=\frac{1}{2\pi\bar{C}T}\;\frac{1}{n(n^{2}-1)+\gamma_{q}n^{2}}.

(20)

Analytic continuation
i\omega_{n}\to\omega+i0^{+}
and interpolation to the continuum
n\sim\omega/(2\pi T)
yield the retarded propagator
D^{R}(\omega)=D(i\omega_{n}\to\omega+i0^{+})
, whose imaginary part defines the spectral density

A_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\equiv-2\,\imaginary D^{R}(\omega)\;\simeq\;\frac{\kappa_{b}}{\bar{C}}\,\frac{|\omega|}{T+T_{0}},\qquad|\omega|\ll 2\pi T,

(21)

with a non-universal prefactor
\kappa_{b}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The deformation introduces an effective scale

T_{0}\equiv\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}(q-1)\Lambda\;\;\propto\;\;\gamma_{q}T,

(22)

where
\Lambda\sim 2\pi T
is the thermal UV cutoff. The emergent Tsallis scale
T_{0}
is quantitatively anchored by the Raman low-frequency slope, whose ratios across doping levels yield consistent values around
100\text{K}
, as shown in the Appendix. Equation (
21
) shows that the
q
-deformation regularizes the infrared behavior of the soft modes by shifting the effective bath temperature to
T+T_{0}
. In the undeformed limit
q\to 1
, one recovers the canonical Schwarzian bath with Ohmic spectral density
A_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\propto|\omega|/T

[
39
]
.

Having established the quadratic dynamics of the soft reparametrization
modes and their Matsubara propagator, we now turn to their coupling to
microscopic matter fields. In particular, under a boundary
reparametrization
\tau\!\to\!f(\tau)
, a fermion transforms as

\psi(\tau)\;\longrightarrow\;[f^{\prime}(\tau)]^{1/2}\,\psi\!\big(f(\tau)\big).

(23)

Expanding
f(\tau)=\tau+\varepsilon(\tau)
to linear order in the soft
mode
\varepsilon(\tau)
and gauge-fixing the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
zero
modes, one finds a derivative coupling between the soft mode and the
local fermionic energy density
T_{\tau\tau}=\psi^{\dagger}i\partial_{\tau}\psi
:

S_{\rm int}=g\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\;\varepsilon^{\prime}(\tau)\,\psi^{\dagger}(\tau)\,i\partial_{\tau}\psi(\tau),

(24)

where
g=\mathcal{O}(1)
in natural units (
\hbar=k_{B}=1
).
Fourier transforming to frequency space,

\varepsilon(\tau)=\sum_{\omega}\varepsilon_{\omega}e^{-i\omega\tau}

and
\psi(\tau)=\sum_{\varepsilon}\psi_{\varepsilon}e^{-i\varepsilon\tau}
,
the vertex for soft-mode exchange takes the form

\Gamma(\varepsilon,\omega;\varepsilon-\omega)=g\,\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\omega),

(25)

where
\varepsilon
denotes the fermionic Matsubara frequency (measured
from the chemical potential) and
\omega
the soft-mode frequency. The
leading dependence
\propto\varepsilon
reflects that reparametrizations
couple directly to the stress tensor
T_{\tau\tau}
, while the subleading
terms
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
represent higher derivative corrections
suppressed at low frequencies. The linear-in-frequency coupling structure in Eq. (
25
) naturally leads to an Ohmic-type spectral density at low energies, consistent with the universal dissipative behavior expected from soft reparametrization modes
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

When a fermion couples to a bosonic bath such as the Schwarzian soft mode, the fermionic self-energy in real time can be computed by summing all contour contractions according to Wick’s theorem. At second order, the retarded self-energy is given by the standard Keldysh expression

\Sigma^{R}(\varepsilon)=i\int\!\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\Big[\Gamma^{2}D^{K}(\omega)G^{R}_{0}(\varepsilon-\omega)+\Gamma^{2}D^{R}(\omega)G^{K}_{0}(\varepsilon-\omega)\Big],

(26)

where
G^{R/K}_{0}
are the bare fermionic Green’s functions and
D^{R/K}
are the retarded and Keldysh soft-mode propagators. Note that the bare fermionic Green’s functions in the time domain are

G_{0}^{R}(t)=-i\,\theta(t)\,\langle\{c(t),c^{\dagger}(0)\}\rangle

and

G_{0}^{A}(t)=i\,\theta(-t)\,\langle\{c(t),c^{\dagger}(0)\}\rangle
. Using the fluctuation–dissipation relations

D^{K}(\omega)=\big[D^{R}(\omega)-D^{A}(\omega)\big]\coth\!\left(\frac{\omega}{2T}\right),\qquad G^{K}_{0}(\varepsilon)=\big[G^{R}_{0}(\varepsilon)-G^{A}_{0}(\varepsilon)\big]\tanh\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\right),

(27)

the fermionic scattering rate
\Gamma(\varepsilon)\equiv-\imaginary\Sigma^{R}(\varepsilon)
simplifies to the Golden-Rule form

\Gamma(\varepsilon)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;\underbrace{\big[g^{2}\,A_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\big]}_{K(\omega)}\big[n_{B}(\omega)+n_{FD}(\omega-\varepsilon)\big],

(28)

where
A_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=-2\,\imaginary D^{R}(\omega)
is the soft-mode spectral function introduced in Eq. (
21
).
This identifies the Golden-Rule kernel

K(\omega)=\frac{\kappa}{\bar{C}}\,\frac{|\omega|}{T_{0}+T},\qquad\kappa=\frac{g^{2}\kappa_{b}}{2\pi},

(29)

which makes explicit that the effective interaction strength scales inversely with the renormalized Schwarzian stiffness
\bar{C}
.

After integration, we obtain the compact form

\Gamma(T)=\frac{\pi^{2}\tilde{g}^{2}}{4}\,\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0}+T},

(30)

which precisely matches the Golden-Rule scaling widely used in phenomenological analyses of strange-metal transport. Corrections arising from finite-
\delta
deformations or higher-order interactions contribute only subleading terms, such as
\mathcal{O}(T/T_{0})
or logarithmic corrections at strong coupling.
The controlled Keldysh derivation clarifies that the Ohmic kernel

K(\omega)\propto\omega/(T_{0}+T)

originates from the soft-mode spectral density and derivative coupling in Eq. (
24
). The temperature dependence
T^{2}/(T_{0}+T)
in Eq. (
30
) follows from integrating over thermal phase space up to
\omega\sim T
and is consistent with both perturbative and phenomenological treatments of electron–Schwarzian scattering.

Substituting the soft-mode scattering rate into the semiclassical relaxation-time formula,

\sigma_{xx}\simeq e^{2}\int d\epsilon\,(-\partial_{\epsilon}f)\,N(\epsilon)\,v_{x}^{2}(\epsilon)\,\tau_{\rm tr}(\epsilon),\qquad\tau_{\rm tr}(\epsilon)\sim\Gamma^{-1}(\epsilon),

(31)

one obtains a temperature-dependent conductivity. Using the result of Eq. (
30
), the corresponding resistivity takes the form

\rho_{xx}(T)\equiv\sigma_{xx}^{-1}(T)\;\propto\;\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0}+T}.

(32)

This scaling law reveals a smooth crossover: at low temperatures (
T\ll T_{0}
), the resistivity follows the Fermi-liquid form
\rho\!\sim\!T^{2}
, while at high temperatures (
T\gg T_{0}
) it becomes linear,
\rho\!\sim\!T
, as commonly observed in strange metals.
The interpolation
\rho(T)\!\sim\!T^{2}/(T_{0}+T)
therefore provides a natural explanation for the empirical crossover from quadratic to linear resistivity, arising microscopically from Schwarzian soft-mode scattering
[
28
,
33
]
.More broadly, experimental studies have suggested that the linear-in-
T
resistivity slope may correlate with superconducting scales in certain cuprates and pnictides
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
, although such connections lie beyond the scope of the present analysis.

A caveat in the above transport estimate is that the Schwarzian soft modes
are intrinsically 0+1 dimensional (they possess no spatial momentum),
whereas electrical conduction is a spatial transport phenomenon. To make
contact with conductivity one therefore needs an additional ingredient
that converts local (time-only) scattering into momentum/charge relaxation. In this work we adopt the physically transparent assumption that the Schwarzian modes act as a
local
(momentum-independent)
dissipative bath for spatially extended fermions; the resulting single-particle
inelastic rate
\Gamma(\varepsilon)
computed above is then supplemented by
a separate momentum-relaxing channel (e.g.
Umklapp processes) that ensures finite DC conductivity. Under the further
assumption that vertex corrections are subleading (or that the scattering
is approximately angle-independent on the Fermi surface), the transport
time can be taken as
\tau_{\rm tr}(\varepsilon)\!\sim\!\Gamma^{-1}(\varepsilon)
,
and the semiclassical Drude estimate applies, yielding

\rho(T)\propto T^{2}/(T_{0}+T)
.

Hall angle from the memory matrix-
The memory-matrix formalism provides a controlled framework for transport in systems with slow or nearly conserved operators, where standard Boltzmann theory fails
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
. The central idea is that dc conductivities are determined by the relaxation of such slow operators through their couplings to a dissipative bath. For strange metals, this bath is supplied by Schwarzian soft modes, whose Ohmic spectrum ensures smooth low-frequency behavior and introduces an additional channel for energy dissipation. Concretely, for a basis of slow operators
\{A_{\alpha}\}
the conductivity tensor reads

\sigma_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\chi_{J_{i}A_{\alpha}}\,\big[M(0)+N\big]^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}\,\chi_{A_{\beta}J_{j}},

(33)

where
M
encode the dissipative couplings to the bath,
\chi_{\alpha\beta}=\langle A_{\alpha};A_{\beta}\rangle
the static susceptibilities. This expression applies equally to Fermi liquids and non–Fermi liquids. In the Fermi-liquid case,
M
reduces to the standard quasiparticle collision integral, recovering the textbook Kubo–bubble results.
N
is the antisymmetric block induced by the Lorentz force, with
N_{P_{x}P_{y}}=-N_{P_{y}P_{x}}=\omega_{c}\chi_{PP}
and
\omega_{c}=eB/m^{\ast}
. For completeness, we rederive the dc conductivity and the quadratic magnetoresistance of a conventional Fermi liquid in Appendices A and B. The key quantities are the relaxation rates

\Gamma_{Q}\equiv\frac{M_{QQ}(0)}{\chi_{QQ}},\qquad\Gamma_{P}\equiv\frac{M_{PP}(0)}{\chi_{PP}},

which characterize dissipation in the energy-like channel
Q
and in the momentum channel
P
, respectively. When
Q
is chosen as the current-like soft mode, Eq. (
33
) reduces to

\sigma_{xx}=\frac{\chi_{JJ}}{\Gamma_{Q}},\qquad\rho_{xx}=\frac{\Gamma_{Q}}{\chi_{JJ}}.

(34)

The memory element
M_{QQ}(0)=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\tfrac{1}{\omega}\imaginary G^{R}_{\dot{Q}\dot{Q}}(\omega)
is governed by the spectral density of Schwarzian soft modes. Since
A(\omega)\sim|\omega|/(T_{0}+T)
, thermal integration yields

M_{QQ}(0)\propto T\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\rho_{xx}\propto T.

(35)

Physically, the soft reparametrization modes relax energy efficiently without relaxing total momentum, giving rise to a Planckian-like linear resistivity channel. For the momentum block,

M_{PP}(0)=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{1}{\omega}\imaginary G^{R}_{\dot{P}_{x}\dot{P}_{x}}(\omega),\qquad\dot{P}_{x}=i[H,P_{x}].

(36)

In a continuum model one has
\dot{P}_{x}=0
, so momentum relaxation arises solely from translation-breaking terms such as static disorder
V(\mathbf{r})
and lattice umklapp. For a weak periodic potential
V(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}U_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
, the coefficients
U_{\mathbf{G}}
are Fourier components of the lattice potential at reciprocal vectors
\mathbf{G}
. The operator
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G},\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
transfers electronic density by momentum
\mathbf{G}
, while
G_{x}
denotes the
x
-component of
\mathbf{G}
, quantifying the amount of momentum lost along the transport direction. The leading memory-matrix element controlling momentum relaxation then reads

M_{PP}(0)\;\sim\;\sum_{\mathbf{G}}G_{x}^{2}|U_{\mathbf{G}}|^{2}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{1}{\omega}\imaginary\chi^{R}_{\rho_{\mathbf{G}}\rho_{-\mathbf{G}}}(\omega),

(37)

where
\chi^{R}
is the retarded density–density correlator of the interacting system. In clean correlated metals the dominant temperature dependence arises from electron–electron umklapp scattering at finite
\mathbf{G}
. Phase-space integrals over two quasiparticles near the Fermi surface yield the familiar Fermi-liquid factor

\int d\epsilon_{1}d\epsilon_{2}\,\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\,(-\partial_{\epsilon_{1}}f)(-\partial_{\epsilon_{2}}f)\;\propto\;T^{2},

(38)

so that

M_{PP}(0)\propto T^{2},\qquad\Gamma_{P}=\frac{M_{PP}}{\chi_{PP}}\propto T^{2}.

(39)

Hence momentum relaxation is parametrically slower than energy relaxation, consistent with conventional large-angle umklapp scattering.
Because
Q
couples strongly to the current but weakly to the Lorentz-force channel, while
P
couples directly to the Lorentz block
N
, the transport coefficients separate naturally:

\rho_{xx}\propto\Gamma_{Q}\sim T,\qquad\cot\theta_{H}\simeq\frac{M_{PP}}{\omega_{c}\chi_{PP}}\propto T^{2}.

(40)

This dichotomy encapsulates the two-lifetime phenomenology of strange metals. Deviations from
\cot\theta_{H}\propto T^{2}
in electron-doped cuprates
[
51
]
and ruthenates
[
52
]
reflect orbital selectivity and multi-band scattering, but the separation of channels remains a robust organizing principle
[
53
]
.

The canonical scaling of the Hall angle,
\cot\theta_{H}\propto T^{2}
, observed in optimally doped cuprates and several strange metals, has been widely interpreted as evidence for distinct transport and Hall scattering rates
[
53
]
. This behavior, however, is not universal. In electron-doped cuprates, such as
\mathrm{Pr_{2-x}Ce_{x}CuO_{4-\delta}}

[
51
]
, and in layered ruthenates including
\mathrm{Sr_{2}RuO_{4}}
and
\mathrm{Sr_{3}Ru_{2}O_{7}}

[
52
]
, clear departures from the
T^{2}
law have been reported. The coexistence of multiple Fermi-surface sheets with distinct orbital characters leads to orbital-dependent scattering lifetimes that evolve differently with temperature and magnetic field. In
\mathrm{Sr_{2}RuO_{4}}
, for instance, the
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
bands derived from different Ru
4d
orbitals exhibit markedly different coherence scales and gap-like features
[
52
]
. Such orbital-selective effects effectively renormalize the temperature dependence of the transport tensors, producing an apparent breakdown of the simple scaling. These observations point to the necessity of introducing a material-dependent cutoff scale
T_{0}
, which encodes band-structure and orbital complexity within a unified transport framework.

Within the memory-matrix framework, once Schwarzian soft modes are incorporated, the separation between energy relaxation (
\Gamma_{Q}\sim T
) and momentum relaxation (
\Gamma_{P}\sim T^{2}
) arises naturally. This accounts for the canonical
T
–linear resistivity and
T^{2}
Hall angle in single-band strange metals, while also providing a platform to understand deviations in multiband systems. Orbital selectivity modifies the effective relaxation rates but does not alter the fundamental two-channel structure that distinguishes
\rho_{xx}
and
\cot\theta_{H}
.

Having established how distinct scattering channels control the longitudinal and transverse responses, we now turn to the role of an external magnetic field. The same formalism offers a natural route to magnetotransport, and in particular to the microscopic origin of the scale-invariant, linear-in-
B
magnetoresistance observed in quantum-critical strange metals. The scattering rate is given by

\Gamma(\varepsilon)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;K(\omega;B)\big[n_{B}(\omega)+n_{FD}(\omega-\varepsilon)\big],

(41)

with
K(\omega;B)=g^{2}(B)\,A(\omega;B)
and
A(\omega;B)=-2\,\imaginary D^{R}(\omega;B)
the bosonic spectral function. If magnetic-field–induced processes (such as vortexlike excitations, chiral soft modes, or orbital-matrix-element effects) enhance the soft-mode spectrum to acquire a
B
-linear form,

A(\omega;B)\simeq\frac{\kappa_{b}}{\bar{C}}\frac{|B|\,|\omega|}{T_{0}+T},

(42)

then the kernel
K(\omega;B)=g^{2}(B)A(\omega;B)
directly yields

\Gamma(0)=\frac{\kappa\pi}{8\bar{C}}\frac{|B|\,T^{2}}{T_{0}+T},

(43)

so that at fixed
T
the longitudinal resistivity scales linearly with field,
\rho_{xx}\propto|B|
. This mechanism captures key experimental observations in quantum-critical strange metals. In BaFe
2
(As
1-x
P
x
)
2
, near the QCP the scattering rate follows
[
54
]

\Gamma\approx\sqrt{(\alpha k_{B}T)^{2}+(\eta\mu_{B}B)^{2}},\quad\frac{\eta}{\alpha}\approx 1,

(44)

demonstrating nearly identical thermal and magnetic energy scales. Similarly, in overdoped La
2-x
Sr
x
CuO
4
, scale-invariant linear-in-
B
resistivity is observed up to
80~\mathrm{T}

[
55
]
, with slope
\beta\sim\alpha k_{B}/\mu_{B}
, consistent with Eq. (
43
). Together, these results are consistent with a Planckian-limited scaling,

\Gamma(T,B)\propto\max\{\alpha k_{B}T,\eta\mu_{B}B\},

(45)

operating across a fan-shaped quantum-critical region. This scaling suggests that field-enhanced electron–soft mode scattering provides a natural microscopic route to linear magnetoresistance, in line with experimental observations in pnictides
[
54
]
, cuprates
[
55
]
, and the broader phenomenology of Planckian dissipation
[
12
,
13
]
. The memory-matrix analysis unifies the two-lifetime phenomenology (
\rho_{xx}\sim T
,
\cot\theta_{H}\sim T^{2}
) with the scale-invariant magnetoresistance (
\rho_{xx}\sim|B|
), underscoring the central role of soft-mode physics in non-Fermi-liquid transport.

Discussion and Conclusion-
Starting from the quantum Tsallis distribution, we have developed a unified framework for strange-metal transport.
The
q
-deformation naturally introduces soft reparametrization modes whose dynamics can be captured by a Schwarzian-type action.
These collective modes provide an intrinsic channel for energy relaxation, interpolating smoothly between Fermi-liquid and non-Fermi-liquid regimes.

Within this framework, the resistivity evolves from the familiar quadratic temperature dependence at low temperatures to a linear behavior at high temperatures, reproducing the hallmark of strange metals.
The memory-matrix analysis further clarifies that longitudinal transport is controlled by energy relaxation into soft modes, while the Hall response is dictated by the slower momentum relaxation associated with weak translation-symmetry breaking.
This separation leads to two distinct lifetimes, a feature widely observed experimentally.
The framework also accounts for the crossover from linear-in-field magnetoresistivity to the quadratic regime, in agreement with Kohler scaling analyses.

Beyond transport, the present approach suggests new directions for understanding the interplay between strange-metal behavior and high-temperature superconductivity.
Soft-mode fluctuations associated with the Tsallis deformation may enhance pairing interactions or reshape the electronic density of states, thereby influencing the superconducting transition.
Future work should focus on quantitative calculations of the gap, critical temperature, and coherence length, and on systematic comparisons with experimental observables such as resistivity slopes, Hall angles, and magnetoresistance across different families of high-
T_{c}
materials.

Appendix A
DC Conductivity of Fermi liquid

We present the standard Kubo-bubble derivation and show its reduction to the semi-classical (relaxation-time) form used in the main text.

A.1
Kubo bubble and analytic continuation

The optical conductivity
\sigma_{xx}(\omega)
of an interacting electron system can be obtained from the retarded current–current correlation function
[
50
]
:

\Pi^{R}_{xx}(\omega)=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\langle[J_{x}(t),J_{x}(0)]\rangle,

(46)

where
J_{x}=-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}}v_{x}(\mathbf{k})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k}}
is the
x
-direction current operator and
v_{x}(\mathbf{k})=\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial k_{x}
is the group velocity for band dispersion
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
. Within the bubble (one-loop) approximation in the Matsubara formalism, the correlation reads

\Pi_{xx}(i\Omega_{n})=-e^{2}T\sum_{i\omega_{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}v_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{m})G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{m}+i\Omega_{n}),

(47)

where
G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{m})
is the full single-particle Green’s function, and
T
is the temperature (natural units
\hbar=k_{B}=1
are used). The spectral representation,

G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{m})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\frac{A(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)}{i\omega_{m}-\epsilon},

(48)

introduces the spectral function
A(\mathbf{k},\omega)=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
and the Fermi distribution

f(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/T}+1}.

(49)

After analytic continuation
i\Omega_{n}\to\omega+i0^{+}
, the retarded correlator becomes

\Pi^{R}_{xx}(\omega)=-e^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}v_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\frac{f(\epsilon)-f(\epsilon+\omega)}{\omega+i0^{+}}A(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)A(\mathbf{k},\epsilon+\omega).

(50)

The optical conductivity is then

\sigma_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{R}_{xx}(\omega),

(51)

where the spectral function
A(\mathbf{k},\omega)
encodes interaction effects and quasiparticle lifetimes.

A.2
DC limit and the quasi-particle approximation

Taking the dc limit
\omega\to 0
in Eq. (
50
), the difference in Fermi distributions can be expanded as

f(\epsilon)-f(\epsilon+\omega)\simeq-\omega\,\partial_{\epsilon}f(\epsilon),

yielding

\sigma_{xx}=e^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}v_{x}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\epsilon}{2\pi}\big(-\partial_{\epsilon}f(\epsilon)\big)A^{2}(\mathbf{k},\epsilon),

(52)

where
-\partial_{\epsilon}f(\epsilon)
restricts contributions to energies within a thermal window
\sim T
around the Fermi level. Here,
A(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)
is the single-particle spectral function, and
v_{x}(\mathbf{k})=\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial k_{x}
is the group velocity along the
x
-direction.

Near the Fermi surface, the spectral function is well approximated by a Lorentzian form

A(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)}{(\epsilon-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}+\Gamma^{2}(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)},

where
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu
is the band energy measured from the chemical potential
\mu
, and

\Gamma(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)=-\imaginary\Sigma^{R}(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)

is the quasiparticle scattering rate determined by the imaginary part of the retarded self-energy
\Sigma^{R}
. In the sharp-quasiparticle limit (
\Gamma\ll T
), the squared spectral function simplifies to

A^{2}(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)\simeq\frac{2\pi}{\Gamma(\mathbf{k},\epsilon)}\,\delta(\epsilon-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})+\mathcal{O}(1).

Substituting this expression into Eq. (
52
) and integrating over energy and momentum yields the relaxation-time formula

\boxed{\sigma_{xx}\simeq e^{2}\int d\epsilon\;(-\partial_{\epsilon}f(\epsilon))\,N(\epsilon)v_{x}^{2}(\epsilon)\tau_{\rm tr}(\epsilon)},

(53)

where

\tau_{\rm tr}(\epsilon)\simeq\frac{1}{2\Gamma_{\rm tr}(\epsilon)}

(54)

is the transport relaxation time and

N(\epsilon)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(\epsilon-\xi_{\mathbf{k}})

is the single-particle density of states (DOS). Equation (
53
) is equivalent to the Boltzmann relaxation-time approximation when vertex corrections are negligible, capturing the essential semiclassical physics of momentum relaxation
[
50
]
.

In a Landau Fermi liquid, the low-energy scattering rate scales as

\Gamma_{\rm tr}(\epsilon,T)\propto(\epsilon-\mu)^{2}+(\pi T)^{2},

(55)

reflecting phase-space restrictions for electron–electron scattering. Inserting this into Eq. (
53
), and evaluating near the Fermi level (
\epsilon\simeq\mu
), one obtains

\rho_{xx}(T)\equiv\frac{1}{\sigma_{xx}(T)}\propto T^{2},

(56)

the hallmark quadratic temperature dependence of resistivity in a conventional Fermi liquid
[
2
]
. Deviations from this
T^{2}
law signal non-Fermi-liquid behavior, often arising near quantum critical points or in strongly correlated regimes.

Appendix B
Fermi-liquid limit: Quadratic magnetoresistance

In the memory-matrix framework, the formal expression

\sigma_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\chi_{J_{i}A_{\alpha}}\big[M(0)+N\big]^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}\chi_{A_{\beta}J_{j}}

remains valid for both Fermi-liquid (FL) and non-Fermi-liquid (non-FL) scenarios. The distinction lies in the microscopic
B
-dependence of the memory-matrix entries.

For a conventional Fermi liquid, taking the minimal momentum basis
\{P_{x},P_{y}\}
and assuming field-independent susceptibilities, the dominant momentum-relaxation element admits a weak-field expansion

M_{PP}(B)=M_{0}+\alpha B^{2}+O(B^{4}),

with no linear-in-
|B|
contribution. Inverting the
2\times 2
block then yields the standard conductivity formulas, leading to the quadratic magnetoresistance

\rho_{xx}(B)=\rho_{0}+\Delta\rho(B),\qquad\Delta\rho(B)\propto(\omega_{c}\tau_{\rm tr})^{2}\propto B^{2},

which arises when the magnetic field does not generate new low-frequency scattering channels. This includes impurity- or phonon-dominated elastic scattering (field-independent
\tau_{\rm tr}
) and conventional FL inelastic scattering with
\tau_{\rm tr}^{-1}\propto T^{2}
. The expansion is valid in the weak-field limit
\omega_{c}\tau_{\rm tr}\ll 1
and continuous spectral densities. Departures from this behavior—such as Landau quantization or field-enhanced soft modes—lead to non-FL magnetoresistance, including the linear-in-
|B|
response discussed in the main text.

More generally, within the same framework the momentum-relaxation element may be written as

M_{PP}(B)=M_{0}+c|B|+\alpha B^{2}+\cdots,

(57)

where the coefficient
c
encodes scattering enhanced by soft modes. The longitudinal resistivity then takes the form

\rho_{xx}(B)=\frac{M_{PP}(B)}{\chi_{JP}^{2}}+\frac{\omega_{c}^{2}\chi_{PP}^{2}}{\chi_{JP}^{2}M_{PP}(B)},\qquad\omega_{c}=\frac{eB}{m^{\ast}},

(58)

which, upon expansion for small to moderate fields, yields

\rho_{xx}(B)\simeq\rho_{0}+\frac{c}{\chi_{JP}^{2}}|B|+\left(\frac{e^{2}\chi_{PP}^{2}}{m^{\ast 2}\chi_{JP}^{2}M_{0}}+\frac{\alpha}{\chi_{JP}^{2}}\right)B^{2}+O(B^{3}),\qquad\rho_{0}=\frac{M_{0}}{\chi_{JP}^{2}}.

(59)

The crossover field
B^{\ast}\sim cM_{0}/\beta
, with
\beta=e^{2}\chi_{PP}^{2}/(m^{\ast 2}\chi_{JP}^{2})
, separates the linear-in-
|B|
regime (
|B|\ll B^{\ast}
) from the quadratic FL regime (
|B|\gg B^{\ast}
). Experimentally, Kohler plots collapse
\Delta\rho/\rho_{0}
versus
(\omega_{c}\tau_{\rm tr})^{2}
in the FL regime, while a robust linear
|B|
slope signals non-FL scattering channels
[
57
,
49
,
56
,
54
,
55
]
. This demonstrates that the memory-matrix approach interpolates continuously between quadratic FL magnetoresistance and linear non-FL magnetoresistance within a single microscopic framework.

T

Co

)
2
As
2

Taken together, these results show that in both iron-based and cuprate superconductors the extracted Tsallis scale
