Massive acute cervicofacial emphysema following attempted third molar extraction: A case report in effective clinical intervention
Authors: Khushboo Khan, Nancy Mathew, Deborah Sybil, Imran Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 31-year-old man presented to the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMFS) outpatient department with diffuse cervicofacial swelling on the left side of his face, which developed a few hours after an attempted surgical extraction of the lower left third molar tooth. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed significant emphysema of the left cervicofacial space. The clinical signs nearly resolved within three weeks of onset. This postoperative swelling was likely due to either an air embolism or an inflammatory reaction from the use of an air-driven handpiece during the procedure. The condition responded well to antibiotic treatment, underscoring the importance of appropriate management. This case highlights the need for patient education on potential postoperative complications and the importance of careful technique to prevent such issues in the future.

INTRODUCTION

Cervicofacial subcutaneous emphysema (CSE) is the abnormal accumulation of air in the soft tissues of the head and neck, capable of spreading to the mediastinum through cervical fascial planes.[1] Although rare, CSE can be a serious risk after dental procedures, particularly following the extraction of mandibular third molars, due to their proximity to various neck spaces.

CASE REPORT

A 31-year-old man presented with significant left facial swelling, involving the lip, periorbital, and buccal region, obliterating the nasolabial fold and extending to the cervical region. Periorbital edema and ecchymosis led to eyelid apraxia [Figure 1a-d]. This acute condition developed just an hour after an attempted extraction of an impacted mandibular third molar using a high-speed dental handpiece.

(a-d) Severe left facial edema one hour after attempted lower third molar extraction (e-h) CT scan shows fat stranding, soft tissue thickening in multiple spaces, left parotid and submandibular gland attenuation, and thickening of the left lateral wall of the oropharynx, thickening of the aryepiglottic fold with medialization. CT = Computed tomography

On examination, the patient exhibited pronounced crepitation and mild tenderness. The intraoral assessment revealed tenderness and ecchymosis, with significant obliteration of the left upper and lower buccal sulcus; mouth opening was severely restricted. Any history of trauma, road traffic accidents, or allergies was meticulously ruled out. The patient complained of dysphagia and dysphonia. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed extensive air entrapment in subcutaneous fascial spaces and inflammatory fat stranding in the left perimandibular, pre-maxillary, masticator, buccal, and submandibular regions [Figure 1e-h]. Heterogeneous attenuation of the left parotid and submandibular glands, along with thickening of the left oropharyngeal wall, highlighted the condition’s severity. Blood tests showed leucocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein, and a normal coagulation profile.

Following the initial examination, the ecchymosis progressively increased, showing extensive involvement of the peri-orbital region, buccal area, lip on the left side, extending to the submandibular, sublingual, and suprasternal notch. Subconjunctival hemorrhage was also noted.

Upon admission, the patient was started on IV penicillin and amikacin, oral enzymatic therapy like chymotrypsin, and was kept under strict vital sign monitoring to preempt any potential airway compromise. Although ecchymosis expanded within the first day, extending till the anterior neck, edema reduced significantly, and the patient remained stable and comfortable.

At one-week follow-up, the patient showed impressive recovery, becoming asymptomatic with a significant reduction in emphysema. He was discharged after 5 days, achieving complete resolution within three weeks [Figure 2].

DISCUSSION

The first case of subcutaneous emphysema following a dental procedure was documented by Turnbull et al. in 1900.[2] Although cervicofacial emphysema is rare, it may develop after dental procedures involving high-speed air-turbine handpieces, air-water syringes, or gas-forming solutions. Other reported causes include cervicofacial surgeries, laser applications, traumatic intubation, and positive pressure ventilation. Extractions of mandibular third molars are particularly linked with this condition.[34]

Symptoms of CSE typically arise immediately after a procedure. This air accumulation can pressure vital structures, leading to rapid swelling that may compromise the airway. High-pressure instruments can further allow air to dissect through anatomical spaces, risking necrotizing fasciitis.[5] Additionally, air from the oral cavity increases infection risks, with pathogens like Fusobacterium necrophorum, Escherichia coli, and Eikenella corrodens potentially causing severe infections.[34678910]

Diagnosis of subcutaneous emphysema is primarily clinical, characterized by sudden swelling, dysphagia, and crepitus upon palpation.[11] Differential diagnoses may include allergic reactions, angioedema, hematomas, and cellulitis.

While CSE is typically self-limiting, serious complications can occur. Air spread through fascial planes in the neck can lead to pneumomediastinum or pneumopericardium, with a risk of mediastinitis, which has a 50% mortality rate. Signs of mediastinal involvement may include a friction rub during cardiac auscultation (Hamman’s sign).[411]

Management of cervicofacial emphysema is primarily supportive. Broad-spectrum antibiotics are typically administered to prevent oral bacterial contamination, and intubation may be necessary in cases of airway obstruction. Most mild to moderate cases can be managed with observation and reassurance, while prophylactic antibiotics are recommended to reduce infection risks.[11]

In severe cases, securing the airway takes precedence, and micro-drainage may be considered for symptom relief. This involves the insertion of a fenestrated catheter and sequential massage to help alleviate discomfort, typically carrying low risk.[3411]

To prevent cervicofacial emphysema, practitioners should ensure optimal surgical exposure and use gentle techniques to protect soft tissues. Avoiding indiscriminate use of air-water syringes, especially in infected areas, is crucial; gentle irrigation methods should be used instead.[3]

In conclusion, cervicofacial emphysema, though rare, presents significant risks if not promptly recognized and managed. It is crucial for dental practitioners to be aware of its signs, symptoms, and potential complications for effective intervention. This case underscores the importance of timely treatment and demonstrates the resilience of the human body and the critical role of careful medical management.
