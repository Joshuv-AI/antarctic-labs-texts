Marked Clinical Improvement in Parkinson’s Disease Following Double-Filtration Apheresis: A Case Report
Authors: Harald Burgard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is the fastest growing progressive neurological disorder globally, characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor as well as a wide range of non-motor manifestations. Here, we report the case of a patient with advanced Parkinson’s disease diagnosed for PD more than 12 years ago; clinical symptoms included a coarse resting tremor with a focus on the right arm and leg, gait and postural disturbances, sleep disorders and autonomic disfunctions. The patient received several medications for correction of dopamine deficiency, but clinical symptoms progressed over the years. We applied a series of double-filtration apheresis treatments and experienced marked symptomatic improvement, with near-complete resolution of motor deficits and strong decrease of MDS-UPDRS part III score. This case suggests a potential role of extracorporeal blood purification in the management of PD.Keywords: neurodegenerative disease, double-filtration apheresis, environmental toxin

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, leading to motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability, as well as diverse non-motor complications such as cognition, gait and balance problems, gastrointestinal motility and sleep behaviour disorder.1 It is the fastest growing neurological disorder worldwide.2 Diagnosis is based on the patient’s clinical symptoms and exclusion of other possible causes of PD since there are no evident and routine laboratory tests available for a clear diagnosis.3 Due to dopaminergic neuronal death in the midbrain’s substantia nigra resulting in dopamine deficiency, treatment is mainly based on levodopa and dopamine agonists.4,5 There is currently no therapeutical intervention that can prevent/modify disease progression;4 individual customization of treatment at all stages is currently necessary as the disease progresses and levodopa-resistant symptoms emerge that significantly impair the patients quality of life.

The misfolding of αlpha-Synuclein (α-syn) protein leading to oligomers, fibrils and Lewy bodies plays an important role in PD disease pathogenesis.4 α-syn aggregates disturb dopaminerg transmission leading to presynaptic and postsynaptic dysfunctions.6 Further evidence suggests that inflammation may play a relevant role in the pathophysiology of Parkinson’s disease.7 Both neuroinflammatory processes and systemic immune activation have been associated with disease progression. Based on recent studies, a potential role of the gut–brain axis in Parkinson’s disease can be supported. Dysbiosis-induced inflammation and Impaired intestinal barrier integrity (“leaky gut”) may promote the translocation of microbial products and pro-inflammatory molecules into the systemic circulation, thereby contributing to chronic immune activation and neuroinflammatory signaling.8 Environmental toxicants as metals, pesticides, trichloroethylene and perchloroethylene, and air pollution have also been proposed to play an important role in the cause of the disease.9,10

Beyond established pharmacological and surgical approaches, alternative therapeutic modalities targeting immunological and metabolic mechanisms have been explored. Therapeutic apheresis has been used in modern medicine since decades with therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) as the most established form. TPE is a plasma-based procedure were the plasma is first separated from the cell-rich fraction using a separation filter; in order to remove pathological substances such as circulating autoantibodies, immune complexes, cytokines, toxins and other inflammatory mediators from the blood, the plasma is discarded and replaced with replacement fluid.11 This procedure is well-established in the treatment of patients with neuroimmunological disorders as myasthenia gravis, multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, and Guillain–Barré syndrome.12–18 We propose Toxopherese® (extracorporeal double-filtration apheresis) as a therapeutic intervention for treatment of PD, a newer, well-tolerated technique used during the last 20 years and in which the plasma is not entirely removed. Instead, after separation the plasma is filtered by a second filter to selectively remove substances of high molecular weight such as circulating pathogenic molecules, cytokines, immune complexes and proteins of high-molecular weight and to accordingly improve the viscosity of the blood; after filtration, the purified plasma is mixed with the blood cells and transfused back into the body without additional plasma/liquid substitution. Double-filtration apheresis has previously been shown to reduce Apolipoprotein E, lipoprotein(a), oxidized LDL and large LDL particles, as well as triglycerides and other proinflammatory lipids and stress hormones implicated in Alzheimer Disease.19 It has also been shown to strongly decrease ß2-adrenerg autoantibodies, immunoglobulins and antinuclear antibodies and to improve clinical symptoms in a case of long-term ME/CFS.20 Literature describes promising potential in the treatment of various neuroimmune disorders and improvement of neurological function.21

Here, we report the case of a patient with advanced Parkinson’s disease with severe tremor who underwent a series of double-filtration apheresis treatments and experienced striking symptomatic improvement, with near-complete resolution of motor and non-motor deficits.

This case highlights the potential role of extracorporeal blood purification strategies in the management of Parkinson’s disease, warranting further systematic investigation. As this is a single case, definitive conclusions regarding causality or generalizability cannot be drawn. Nevertheless, it may provide clinical insights and serve as a basis for further systemic investigations.

Case Presentation

Patient Description

A 62-year old patient presented in our practice who was diagnosed with PD more than 10 years ago. Initial diagnosis at that time was made in a specialized clinic based on clinical symptoms; supplementary investigation with MRT and DATscan showed typical alterations for Parkinson. He was prescribed rasagiline. With progression of symptoms, as Hypomimia and a high frequent resting tremor of the right hand, further treatments as well as continuous dose alignments followed. Previous treatments did not result in significant improvement of clinical symptoms.

When the patient presented in our practice he received the following medication: beta blocker, levodopa/benserazide, levodopa/carbidopa, rasagiline and pramipexole. Clinical symptoms at first presentation included gait disorders, sleeping disturbances and a high-frequent, coarse resting tremor of the right hand and arm and leg; he required a walking stick for ambulation. The Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III score revealed 34 in the OFF-medication.22 Clinical symptoms furthermore included impaired olfactory sense. He was no longer capable to drive a car or to write/sign. Physical examination revealed muscular tension with elevated shoulders and right lateral head tilt. In addition, the resting heart rate was increased.

Main findings of a comprehensive laboratory analysis before treatment in our center showed complete blood count with differential within normal limits. Results showed increased values for LDL cholesterol (131 mg/dl), total cholesterol (219 mg/dl) while malondialdehyde-modified (MDA)-LDL a marker for fat oxidation was in the upper normal range (71 U/l), increased RANTES (Regulated And Normal T cell Expressed and Secreted) a marker for dental foci (58.4 ng/mL) and leaky gut with increased zonuline (48 ng/mL). Excess immunoglobulin was found for laboratory allergy marker IgE (350 kU/l), while the antioxidative thiol status was decreased. The Lymphocyte Transformation Test (LTT) showed increased T-lymphocyte activity specific for herpes Zoster and staphylococcus indicating ongoing infection. The patient showed a deficiency in selenium, copper and manganese, and he was prescribed supplements accordingly. Vitamin D was determined and found to be in the lower normal range. Treatment Procedure Using Double-Filtration Apheresis

Toxopherese® was used as a treatment procedure. Overall, the patient received a series of 10 aphereses within a time frame of 1,5 years. For each apheresis application, plasma was separated from the cell-rich fraction using a separation filter and then filtered by a second filter (for filtration and immunoadsorption). The amount of plasma to be filtered was calculated taking into consideration the sex, height, body weight and hematocrit of the patient, while heparin was given as anticoagulant. The filtered plasma was combined again with the remaining blood components, reinfused, and during each treatment, the elugate was collected. The eluate is a liquid that is flushed out of the filter and thus contains the extracted immune complexes, toxicants and inflammatory cytokines. After the conduction of many aphereses in our center we see a strong correlation of the appearance of the eluate and the patients’ clinical condition/laboratory findings. The eluate of chronically ill patients generally may contain visible precipitates resulting from the presence of excess immune complexes and toxicants. Additionally, it can range in color from dark yellow to brown. Accordingly, the appearance of the eluate was visually evaluated and photographed after each extracorporeal apheresis. As an advantage of this treatment, which lasted for an average of 2 to 3 hours, no plasma/replacement fluid had to be given afterwards. The patient’s vital signs were monitored throughout the procedure. No side effects occurred during the treatments.

The patient received the first apheresis, followed by the second one two days later. The third and fourth treatments followed 3 months later at the same frequency as treatments 1 and 2.

After the first apheresis, the patient reported that his sense of smell got better and that he could smell while eating a banana. Additionally, convulsions of his right leg and arm disappeared. After 2nd treatment on the contrary the tremor increased; the evaluation of the eluate showed foam on the surface and streaks within the liquid. Third and fourth apheresis followed 3 months later. Within the 3-months period between treatment sessions the patient reported about episodes of fatigue; tremor improved at first but then got worse again. Olfaction significantly improved though not yet constant with a slightly impaired sense of smell in the evenings. The eluate after 3rd and 4th treatment was brighter and had less foam formation and less streaks.

The fifth and sixth double-filtration apheresis was applied 7 months later. Before treatment was started the patient reported that the high-frequent tremor had significantly improved since 3–4 months; he had been able to sign documents again at work, and he could drive a car again without problems. This was also recognized by family members. He could also sleep better and his olfactory sense had been constantly normal over several days. He still adhered to his drug regimen. Laboratory analysis of the blood revealed that the lipid profile showed a decrease for MDA-LDL, LDL and total cholesterol; zonuline indicating leaky gut decreased into the normal range with a value of 22.8 ng/mL (reference range <25 ng/mL) as well as histamine in blood (28.8 ng/mL; reference range <65.5 ng/mL), which was in the upper normal range at first lab analysis. Also, IgE decreased (191.9 kU/l) while there was only a small decrease for RANTES (49 ng/mL; reference range < 30 ng/mL) still indicating dental foci. Selenium was still too low in the blood while the deficit in manganese and copper was compensated. Poor adherence to medication plans, including intake of selenium may explain insufficient increase of levels in the blood. The antioxidative thiol status increased from 320 to 373 µmol/l.

The 7th and 8th double-filtration aphereses followed 5 months later. Before the start of the treatment the patient reported that the tremor got worse again. 60% of the eluate was precipitate and there was a stronger foam formation at the surface of the liquid (Figure 1). After conduction of 9th apheresis 5 months later almost 100% of eluate was filled with precipitate with foam formation on the surface. The increased amount of precipitate may be explained by rediffusion of toxins from storage sites (muscles, organs, bones, brain, lymphatic system) into the blood. Typically, the body does not release toxins that are “stored” within the body immediately. We have experienced that it is often only after several treatments that clearly visible streaks or sludge formations can be seen. The color of the eluate, however, was brighter. The patient reported that his sleep got deeper since a couple of weeks; an experience he no longer knew. The physical examination revealed that the tremor had disappeared and during consultation in our practice the patient sat there completely still. Before 10th apheresis, which was applied 2 days later, results of laboratory analysis were discussed; the lipid profile showed a further decrease for LDL and total cholesterol. IgE level was reduced further to 108,6 ku/l. Results for thiol status further increased to 440 μmol/l. Levels of selenium were still far too low due to inadequate intake. As of note, MDS-UPDRS part III score decreased from 34 at baseline to 1 after treatment procedure.

As an overall outcome, the application of a series of double-filtration aphereses resulted in a marked improvement of the clinical symptoms as well as of the laboratory finding. The patient is currently essentially asymptomatic and able to live a normal life again. A regular follow-up appointment is scheduled on a yearly basis.

Discussion

Parkinson’s disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that can substantially impair quality of life and functional independence. Although established pharmacological and interventional therapies often provide meaningful symptomatic benefit, treatment responses may vary considerably between individuals, and sustained improvement can be challenging in advanced or complex cases. In the present case report, a patient with Parkinson’s disease demonstrated a pronounced clinical improvement following treatment with double-filtration apheresis, exceeding the degree typically anticipated from routine fluctuation alone. This observation warrants careful discussion in the context of existing evidence, possible mechanistic explanations, and the inherent limitations of single-case observations.

Increasing attention has been directed toward the potential contribution of inflammatory processes in PD. There is strong evidence from human samples and from animal models that support the involvement of inflammation in the development of PD. Apart from prominent immune alterations in the central nervous system also peripheral inflammation has been identified as an important contributor of the chronic inflammatory response on disease progression.7 In a systematic review and meta-analysis peripheral inflammatory cytokines, specifically concentrations of IL-6, tumor necrosis factor, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, C-reactive protein, and RANTES were found to be increased in patients with PD.23 In our patients we have determined IL-6 and TNFalpha at baseline but results were found to be in the normal range. However, we have found inflammatory chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), also known as RANTES to be increased. RANTES has been shown to be involved in the recruitment of immune cells and maintenance of inflammatory state.24 Literature demonstrates the involvement of RANTES and its receptor CCR5 in pathological conditions with neurodegenerative diseases such as PD, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), multiple sclerosis, stroke, and HIV-associated dementia.25,26 Tang et al found that serum RANTES levels strongly correlate with disease severity and duration in PD patients.27 In our patient the increased RANTES level could be lowered after application of treatment procedure; even though the value was still above the normal range, it may have contributed to the improvement of clinical symptoms by lowering systemic inflammatory state.

Additionally, the role of intestinal barrier dysfunction and increased gut permeability in the pathophysiology of PD has been discussed. The enteric nervous system and the immune system are tightly connected and influenced by gut microbiota. Studies have documented that patients with PD exhibit disruptions in epithelial tight junction proteins and increased translocation of luminal contents into the systemic circulation, a phenomenon often referred to as “leaky gut.” This increased intestinal permeability has been associated with heightened systemic inflammation and microbial metabolite levels, and may contribute to α-syn pathology via immune and gut brain axis mechanisms, emphasizing the potential role of the gut in both the onset and progression of PD.28–30 In the present case, the patient exhibited an increased value for zonuline and showed dysbiosis, suggesting a compromised gut barrier that may reflect or contribute to systemic inflammatory processes relevant to PD. Following a course of double-filtration apheresis, the patient’s intestinal permeability strongly improved. However, the specific mechanisms by which therapeutic apheresis may influence gut barrier integrity have not been directly studied; it could be speculated that the observed clinical improvement may reflect attenuation of circulating pro-inflammatory factors that otherwise disrupt epithelial tight junctions and promote systemic inflammation.

In addition, environmental factors have increasingly been discussed as potential contributors to both the development and progression of PD. A growing body of evidence supports the involvement of environmental toxins—pesticides, industrial solvents, and heavy metals—in both initiating and accelerating PD. Work by Dorsey and colleagues has emphasized that PD is increasingly understood as being strongly influenced by environmental toxicants, with pesticides featuring prominently among them.9 Epidemiological and experimental research has identified pesticides as significant contributors to the development of PD in people working with it but also to those living in rural areas near their application. Several studies have demonstrated associations between chronic exposure to neurotoxic pesticides and increased disease risk,31–34 and mechanistic work has shown that such compounds can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and dopaminergic neuron loss in experimental models.35,36 Heavy metals and their abnormal accumulation in the brain have also been described as being contributing factors to mitochondrial injury, oxidative stress, DNA damage and neuroinflammation, all central to PD pathology.37 In addition to pesticides, mounting evidence implicates environmental solvents and industrial chemicals in the etiology of Parkinson’s Diseases some of these substances are persisting in air, water, and soil, and have been associated with neurological outcomes relevant to PD pathology.9,38 In a case series of patients with hyperlipoproteinemia environmental toxins including certain heavy metals and organic pollutants could be removed by using double-filtration plasmapheresis.39 In a similar manner, it may be hypothesized that our therapeutic approach could also have contributed to removal of selected toxicant-containing fractions from the circulation through size-and plasma dependent separation mechanisms. However, this remains speculative in the context of the present case and was not directly assessed, warranting further analytical investigation.

Furthermore, α-synuclein aggregation is considered a central pathological feature of Parkinson’s disease, and increasing interest has focused on relevance of extracellular and circulating α-synuclein species in disease propagation and systemic signaling.6,40,41 It may therefore be hypothesized that therapies capable of modifying circulating plasma fractions could also influence the burden of selected α-synuclein–associated components. However, this remains highly speculative and was not evaluated in the present case.

Taken together, the pronounced clinical improvement observed in this patient may reflect a multifactorial response involving modulation of inflammatory activity, improvement of gut barrier dysfunction, and possibly the reduction of other circulating pathogenic factors. While no causal conclusions can be drawn from a single case, it is noteworthy that the patient experienced a substantial and clinically meaningful improvement in symptoms during the course of treatment. The observation highlights the need for further mechanistic studies with larger sample sizes and controlled designs to better define the potential therapeutic role of this approach in Parkinson’s disease.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that PD symptoms, even in patients with advanced PD, and concomitant laboratory findings may be markedly improved through application of therapeutic apheresis such as double-filtration apheresis. This therapeutic, well-tolerated procedure may represent a promising alternative treatment approach for PD patients particularly in cases where conventional therapies provide limited effects. However, this case report describes a single-patient, uncontrolled case. While the marked clinical improvement is noteworthy, larger prospective and controlled studies are required to validate these findings and to further evaluate underlying mechanisms.
