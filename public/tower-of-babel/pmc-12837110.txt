Epidemiological profile and environmental determinants of Plasmodium falciparum infection in villages surrounding the Soum Agropole, Burkina Faso
Authors: Nathalie Ouare, Hermann Sorgho, Helle Hansson, Toussaint Rouamba, Hamidou Ilboudo, Karim Derra, Bérenger Kaboré, Marc Christian Tahita, Eli Rouamba, Michael Alifrangis, Adama Zida, Pascal Magnussen, Halidou Tinto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBuilding dams and irrigation schemes as resilience measures against growing food insecurity and water scarcity may increase malaria risk in endemic settings. This study aims to assess the clinical and asymptomatic carriage of Plasmodium falciparum throughout the year and explores their association with socio-economic and weather factors in Nanoro, Burkina Faso.MethodsA monthly household-based surveys were conducted from March 2022 to February 2023 in five villages located at variable distance (3–40 km) from the Soum dam located in the Nanoro Health district catchment area. Socio-demographic, weather, and economic data were collected using a standardized questionnaire. Capillary blood samples were analyzed for P. falciparum infection by microscopy. A mixed-effects logistic regression model adjusted for socio-economic and environmental factors was used to assess the infection probability and to analyze how village-level and month-level factors contributed to the malaria infection risk.ResultsA total of 13,786 participants were included. The overall prevalence of asymptomatic carriage (24.5%) was higher than clinical cases (19.5%). The 12-month malaria prevalence (clinical cases and asymptomatic carriage) were 39.6% (95%CI 38.79–40.42) with the highest rate (42.1% with 95% CI 40.7–43.47) reported from Soum near the dam and lowest rate (33.2%;95% CI 31.21–35.32) from Séguédin located far from the dam. Regardless of village location and transmission period, participants aged 5–19 years old had the highest prevalence of malaria infection with values ranged from 43.6% (95% CI 38.34–49.01) in Séguédin to 69.4% (95% CI 64.94–73.57) in Soum. Monthly rainfall measured in millimeters, was unexpectedly associated with decreased risk of malaria infection (OR = 0.77, 95%CI 0.66–0.9).ConclusionThe risk of malaria infection was higher in villages close to the dam, with Soum having the highest prevalence and Séguédin the lowest. This study highlights the impact of dam construction on the epidemiological profile of malaria and underscores the need for multi-sectoral approaches integrating health, water management, and socio-economic interventions to tackle the risk of the disease occurrence.

Background

Over the past 50 years, Burkina Faso, like many other Africa sub-Saharan countries, has experienced a significant decline in rainfall and an increase of average temperatures, leading to a drop in agricultural yields and growing water scarcity [1–3]. To mitigate these challenges, the construction of artificial dams and the promotion of irrigated agriculture has emerged as a key strategy for sustaining economic growth, ensuring food security, and enhancing resilience to climate change [4–6]. Since 2001, the Pilot Private Irrigation Development Project (PIDP) has contributed to the construction of several dams in Burkina Faso, leading to a significant expansion in irrigated land [7, 8]. The construction of the Soum dam in 2006 in the province of Boulkiemdé, and the subsequent irrigation of farmland is part of the latter. However, while irrigation boosts food production, it also increases the risk of water-related diseases like malaria [9–11]. Furthermore, new mosquito breeding sites as a resulting of the dams and dry-season irrigation raise Anopheles populations, extending breeding cycles and malaria risk [11]. Malaria transmission dynamic may vary with environmental, climatic, and socio-economic factors, creating localized hotspots [12–14]. Beyond environmental modifications, the exploitation of water resources is generally associated with demographic shifts, behavioral changes, and changes in socio-economic conditions, all of which could affect human-vector-parasite interactions and the overall dynamics of malaria transmission [15]. In Burkina Faso, malaria remains a public health problem and is endemic with seasonal variations. To reduce the disease burden, the government and its international partners have implemented control measures including prompt diagnosis and treatment, vector control, chemoprevention among children less than 5 and pregnant women [16, 17]. Reducing the burden of malaria also depends on accurately estimating the intensity of transmission. Accurate estimation of malaria prevalence, along with a better understanding of the environmental and socio-demographic factors influencing it are essential for predicting malaria transmission patterns and optimizing resources allocation for the disease control [18–20]. The impact of environmental modifications, such as large and small dams, on malaria transmission dynamics at the global and regional level has been extensively studied [21–27]. However, few studies have specifically assessed the combined influence of socio-economic and environmental factors on the spatio-temporal dynamics of malaria transmission, particularly at the household level [12, 22, 28]. This study aims to determine the magnitude of P. falciparum infections in five villages according to their proximity to the Soum dam, and to assess the associations between the intensity of malaria transmission and socio-demographic and environmental factors at household level.

Methods

Study site

The study was carried out in the Nanoro health district and within the Health and Demographic Surveillance System coverage area (Nanoro and Soaw) [36]. Nanoro health district is a rural setting of the central-west region of Burkina Faso (Fig. 1) located in the Sudano-Sahelian region of Burkina Faso where malaria transmission is characterized by three transmission intensity periods [29]. The high intensity season runs from late June to mid-November and overlaps with part of the rainy season, which runs from July to October. The peak incidence is observed approximately 9 weeks after the onset of rainfall. The intermediate intensity period, during which there is almost no rainfall, but surface water creates wetlands and the temperature favorable for mosquito development, maintains moderate intensity. This period lasts from mid-November to March. The low intensity period extends from April to the end of June [29] when the transmission is extremely low. Nanoro health district comprises 14 villages, and only five villages were included in this study. They were intentionally selected according to unpublished data and predefined criteria: inclusion within the HDSS catchment area to ensure availability of high-quality surveillance data, variation in malaria incidence intensity, and different levels of proximity to the Soum dam (near, intermediate, far) to assess its potential impact on malaria transmission. Soum and Gouloure located at the vicinity of the dam, the village of Nazoanga located at 14 km from the dam, and the villages of Séguédin and Soala located far at 33 km and 40 km respectively from the dam. Hydro-agricultural developments are carried out in villages located close to the dam, which modifies the ecological environment of these areas, whereas the ecological environment of villages located at intermediate or far from the dam are less affected. The majority of the population living in the five villages is involved in agriculture during the rainy season. However, irrigated rice cultivation, market gardening and vegetable growing are the main activities of the people living near Soum dam during the dry season.Fig. 1Map of the study area showing the study villages in the Nanoro health district

Study design and population

Monthly cross-sectional household-based surveys was conducted over a 12-month period from March 2022 to February 2023 in each of the five villages (Soum, Goulouré, Nazoanga, Séguédin and Soala). Household members from the five villages were included in the study using a two-stage sampling procedure. First, during each monthly survey, a total of 242 households were randomly selected without replacement. The number of households selected in each village was distributed proportionally to the size of its population in the Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) to ensure demographic representativeness in all villages, both located near and far from the dam. All individuals living in each selected household were interviewed, resulting in a sample size that exceeded the adjusted requirements. Second, all members of the selected households were invited to participate, provided they met the study’s eligibility criteria. During the household visits, individuals present were enrolled after obtaining free and informed consent (or parental consent for minors). Absent eligible members were revisited, when possible, to minimize selection bias.

Inclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for the study were all individuals, regardless of gender and age, living in the five selected villages, from whom free and informed consent was obtained. Parental or legal guardian consent was also required for children aged 6 months to 15 years. For microscopy analysis, all participants for whom a slide had been prepared were included.

Non-inclusion criteria

All individuals whose consent was not obtained and who did not live in the area of selected villages for the study were not included in the study. Participants with severe symptoms of illness were not included.

Sample size estimation and sampling

The recent malaria prevalence in Nanoro was 35.7% (31.4–40.0%) according to Bassinga et al. [30]. However, at the time of designing the study, no prior studies using the same approach had estimated malaria infection prevalence in the specific study population or in the five selected villages. Therefore, an assumed prevalence of 50% was used solely for sample size calculation purposes, following standard practice when the true proportion is unknown. With a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a 10% anticipated non-response rate. This estimate was calculated per survey for the five villages. A total of 423 households were selected per month in the five villages from HDSS database, However, because data were collected using a household-based cluster sampling design, a design effect of 2 were applied to account for intra-household correlation and the expected reduction in statistical efficiency. After adjusting for this design effect and considering an average household size of 7 persons, the required sample size increased to approximately 1694 persons for the five villages per survey.

Study procedures and data collection

Field data collection

The study included two levels of standard questionnaires for socio-demographic and environmental data collection: a household questionnaire and an individual questionnaire. The household questionnaire was administered to the head of household and collected information on socioeconomic characteristics, household composition, GPS coordinates, housing conditions, proximity to the dam and irrigation sites, and access to health services. These variables constitute the environmental data collected by questionnaire, particularly the geographic and environmental characteristics surrounding each household. In addition, rainfall data were obtained from the meteorological station located in Nanoro. These data were recorded daily and then aggregated month by month in order to align with the temporal structure of the malaria infection data. The individual questionnaire was administered to each household member and included demographic information, recent history of fever, healthcare-seeking, and other individual-level covariates relevant to malaria risk.

Approximately 250 µL of capillary blood samples from each participant was collected in an EDTA micro-tubes labelled with anonymous identifiers and transported to the Nanoro Clinical Research Unit laboratory for microscopic diagnosis.

Laboratory analysis

Microscopic malaria diagnosis was performed according to World Health Organization standard procedures [31]. Thin and thick blood smears for each participant were air dried. The thin smears were fixed in ethanol, stained with 10% Giemsa (Sigma) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 30 min and read under a light microscope at 100 × magnification using immersion oil. Each slide was examined by two independent expert microscopists, in a double-blind procedure. Parasite density was estimated from thick smears using the standard method by counting the number of asexual parasites of P. falciparum relative to leukocytes. The number of asexual parasites (n) was counted for a given number of leukocytes (X), then parasite density (PD, parasites/µL) was calculated using the following formula: PD = (n × 8000)/X, where 8000 corresponds to the average number of leukocytes per microliter of blood. This method allows parasite density to be estimated even for infections with low parasitemia. When the thick smear was of poor quality, PD was calculated from the thin smear by counting the number of parasitized red blood cells relative to the total number of red blood cells, taking into account the average concentration of red blood cells in the blood (≈4,000,000/µL). A negative or positive result was confirmed only after examination of 100 fields containing 200 white blood cells by the two readers. In case of disagreement between the two readers the slide was examined by a third microscopist to confirm the result. The final result was defined if at least two readers agreed on positivity or negativity. When all three readers had a positive result but the parasitic densities were considerably different, the final parasite density was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the two closest readers. An individual was considered positive for malaria infection if at least one parasite was detected in either the thin smear or the thick drop. Clinical malaria was defined as any positive microscopy result associated with fever (forehead temperature ≥ 37.5 °C) or a history of fever in the previous 48 h. Asymptomatic carriage is defined as the presence of Plasmodium on microscopy without fever at the time of the survey. The transmission season was subdivided into three periods: a high transmission period, a low transmission period, and an intermediate transmission period, according to the study carried out by [29] in Nanoro [29].

Data management and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted on the data of all participants who had a microcopy result. The cross-sectional survey data were collected using electronic questionnaire build on REDCap. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (2024-10-31) (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize socio-economic, and environmental characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions, stratified by village, age group, and intensity period (used as proxy of transmission season). The prevalence of malaria infection was calculated and summarized across villages and age categories as well as the intensity period. A mixed-effects logistic regression model was used to assess the probability of infection and analyze how village-level and transmission period level factors contributed to the risk of malaria infection. The model included fixed-effects represented by all variables hypothesized or previously documented to influence malaria risk (socio-demographic, environmental, and household-level factors collected in the study). While the random-effects structure accounted for intra-village and intra-period (monthly) correlation. “Month” variable was incorporated as a random effect to capture the unobserved temporal variability and month-to-month fluctuations in malaria prevalence. While the transmission period was included as a fixed effect, as it represents a broader seasonal classification derived from the intensity levels of malaria prevalence (high vs. low transmission periods). These two variables therefore operate at different levels: the month random effect accounts for fine-scale temporal variation, while the transmission period fixed effect captures the overall seasonal pattern. Different model specifications were tested, including models with the transmission period alone and models with the month random effect alone. The final model was selected based on goodness-of-fit criteria and retained both components because they contributed complementary information to the temporal structure of malaria transmission. The random effect at the village level also was incorporated into the model to account for the correlation between outcome measures among individuals or households in the same village. These correlations arise because observations from the same village often share unmeasured or unobservable characteristics, such as local environmental conditions, vector density patterns, socioeconomic factors, or access to health care, which may influence malaria risk. The model was appropriately adjusted to account for this intra-village correlation and avoid biased estimates that could occur if these unobserved factors at the village level were ignored. This approach also allows the fixed effects to be estimated more accurately by separating the between-village variability from the within-village variability. Monthly precipitation (mm) was rescaled in increments of 100 mm and included in the model as a continuous covariate. Odds ratios adjusted (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for the overall population and separately for each village.

Results

Study population

The demographic and socio-economic characteristics of the study participants by village are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 13,786 participants were included in the study including 59.2% females. Children under 5 years old represented 17.6% of the participants and those aged 5–19, and adults aged ≥ 20 represented 40.9% and 41.5% of the population respectively. The majority of the study participants were from the Mossi ethnic group (70.1%), except in Gouloure and Soum, where the Gourounsi were predominant. Agriculture and livestock husbandry were the main occupation (26.9%), particularly near the dam (37.7% in Gouloure, 37.4% in Soum). More than half of the population had no formal education (60.8%), although Gouloure had slightly higher primary education rates (25.1%) and Soala had secondary education rates of 13.1%. A total of 34.2% of our study population were single, while 40.1% were classified as ‘not applicable,’ mainly children. Villages near the dam had a slightly higher proportion of married couples. Regarding care seeking, only 2.2% of participants went to a medical center when they presented clinical signs and symptoms of malaria, such as fever. Socio-demographic characteristics of study population by study site

Plasmodium falciparum infection in the five villages according to transmission season

The overall prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum infection over the 12 months period and across the five villages was 39.6% (95%CI 38.79–40.42), with some variations by village, transmission period, and age group (Table 2). Soum, Goulouré and Nazoanga had similar malaria infection prevalences, ranging from 39.6% (95% CI 37.74–41.51) to 42.0% (95% CI 40.70–43.47). These proportions were higher than those observed in Séguedin and Soala, where malaria infection prevalence ranged from 33.2% (95% CI 31.21–35.32) to 34.6% (95% CI 32.05–37.29). Socio-economic and environmental determinants associated with the risk of malaria infection

The Table 5 summarizes the multivariable analysis of the mixed model of all factors associated with the risk of malaria infection (Table 5). The results showed that age was a factor associated with the risk of malaria infection. Compared with people over 20 years of age, the 5–19 age group were more likelihood to be infected by Plasmodium falciparum (p < 0.001) with an odds ratio (OR) of 4.39 (95% CI 3.80–5.08), followed by children under five (OR = 2.11, 95%CI 1.72–2.60). Among occupations, being a pupil/student was significantly associated with a higher risk of malaria infection (p < 0.001) with an OR of 1.48 (95% CI 1.24–1.76) compared to farmers/herders. Furthermore, higher levels of education were associated with a lower probability of malaria infection (OR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.58–0.82). Unexpectedly, rainfall was associated with a reduced probability of malaria infection (OR = 0.77, 95%CI 0.66–0.9). As expected, the model confirmed seasonal fluctuations in the probability of malaria during the study period. The low transmission period included the months of April, May and June were associated with reduced risks of malaria infection (OR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.49–0.74; p < 0.001). While the months of August and September (rainy season) were associated with an increased probability of malaria infection. Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) period, determined based on the age of each participant and the official schedule of Burkina Faso’s National Malaria Control Program, was significantly linked to a reduced of malaria infection probability (OR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.49–0.74; p < 0.001). Discussion

Our study showed that the highest prevalence of malaria infection was reported in villages close to the dam (Soum and Goulouré) as well as in the village located at an intermediate distance from the dam (Nazoanga), while the lowest prevalence was observed in villages located far from the dam (Séguédin and Soala). Similar trends have been reported in several studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa which have reported an increase in malaria prevalence in the vicinity of large and small dams and irrigation areas [24, 32–35]. Importantly, during the dry season (period of low transmission), the highest prevalences of clinical cases were also reported in villages close to the dam whereas villages far from the dam were characterized by a predominance of asymptomatic infections during the same period. Although higher acquired immunity and fewer symptoms might be expected in areas where the malaria burden is consistently higher, the study setting is a micro-environment that is broadly hyperendemic for malaria. These unexpected results possibly reflect the impact of the dam and irrigated agricultural developments on the intensification of malaria transmission. The Soum agropole covers more than 1000 hectares dedicated to irrigated rice cultivation and started its activities in 2019, according to unpublished data. This hydro-agricultural structure and irrigation activities seem to be the main factors of ecological change affecting mosquito reproduction and malaria transmission [37, 38]. Indeed, the deliberate flooding of irrigated fields, favor the formation of wetlands. These wetlands, combined with the optimal temperature [28.41 °C (18°–38°)], relative humidity [30.3% (12–40%)] create the conditions favorable to the formation and maintenance of mosquito breeding. This maintain a stable malaria transmission and expose the population living close the dam at a high risk of infection [11, 29, 39]. In addition, the high prevalence of malaria in Soum could be explained by the demographic structure, characterized by high population density, both at the village and household levels according to unpublished HDSS data (2024), which could promote intense contact between human hosts and vectors, thereby increasing parasite transmission. Furthermore, the high proportion of young individuals who have not yet developed strong immunity could also explain the persistence of clinical cases of malaria, even in areas where transmission is higher, such as villages near the dam. Finally, inter-community migration is frequent between villages, leading to a relatively homogeneous level of exposure and naturally acquired immunity. This mobility may reduce the expected contrast in immunity or symptoms between villages located near the dam on the one hand, and villages located far from the dam and non-irrigated villages on the other, even if the intensity of transmission is different. A predominance of asymptomatic infections in villages far from the dam during the low transmission season, suggests silent parasite circulation, with no major upsurge in clinical cases. In opposite, in villages close to the dam, the trend in the prevalence of clinical cases was stable, with an increase during the intermediate transmission period. This suggests that the dam may extend the transmission cycle and thus make it more persistent. Previous studies have shown that dams and irrigation systems can prolong the malaria transmission season by creating favorable ecological niches for vector survival during normally dry periods [27, 40–42], Thus, villages near the dam could constitute hot spots of active malaria transmission [29]. This finding calls into question the efficiency of the current standard malaria control strategies in such areas when we know that most of these strategies such as Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (SMC), the Indoor spray was mainly deployed during the rainy season. Moreover, this study shows the importance of a continuous surveillance and the implementation of targeted strategies in such areas to limit the transmission and prevent outbreaks during the low transmission period [43, 44]. Spatial heterogeneity of factors associated with the risk of malaria infection was observed, with a higher risk for the populations living in Soum and Nazoanga, while no significant factor associated with risk of malaria infection was observed in Goulouré, despite its immediate proximity to the dam. In the case of Nazoanga, this could be explained by the village’s proximity to a tributary of the dam, creating a favorable environment for vector reproduction. In addition, seasonal worker migration from Nazoanga to Soum, a village associated with a higher risk of malaria infection, for agricultural or fishing activities, could favor an active transmission dynamic between the two localities, thus reinforcing the risk of exposure to the parasite. The apparent discrepancy between the high risk of malaria infection in Nazoanga, which is relatively far from the dam, and Goulouré, which is close to the dam but did not show a significant risk of infection, questions that proximity to a dam or to hydro-agricultural facilities entails is associated with an increased risk of malaria. Similar results was reported by Lindblade et al. (1991) in Tanzania (35), and by Baldet et al. in Burkina Faso[11]. 43] Lindblade et al., 1991 suggested that extremely high daily temperatures during the dry season, often exceeding 40 °C, could reduce adult mosquito survival, affect sporozoite maturation and thus limit transmission in areas close to water reservoirs. Although temperature was not collected in our study, a study conducted by Rouamba et al. in 2019 in Nanoro heath district area observed that atmospheric temperatures could vary from 18 °C to 38 °C. However, these temperatures could be factor favorable for mosquito development and parasite maturation [29]. Moreover, the study conducted by Baldet et al. attributes their results to a predominance of Anopheles funestus, a less competent vector species than An. gambiae, which can develop in earthen drains and would transmit less parasites [11]. In our study, the particular situation of Goulouré could be explained by the specific management of surface water from the dam by the inhabitants. Indeed, market gardening in irrigated schemes is based on a more rational use of water, with smaller volumes rapidly absorbed by infiltration. In addition, excess water is evacuated through earthen drains, enabling efficient water run-off without the prolonged creation of stagnant zones. This system limits the formation of temporary pools, particularly during the dry season which could prevent the creation of breeding grounds for mosquitoes.

A reduction in the prevalence of P. falciparum in children under 5 years old was observed compared to those aged 5–19 years. This result is coherent with general data underlining the effectiveness of malaria control strategies [45–47]. Indeed, this decline could be attributed to several interventions implemented in Burkina Faso in this age group, including the deployment of the seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) since 2014 and the increasing use of insecticide-treated mosquito nets [48, 49]. However, a high risk of malaria infection has been observed in children under 5 years old, reflecting their immune vulnerability to malaria. This highlights the importance of reinforcing the existing prevention strategies to consolidate the progress achieved and protect this age group in the long term. It is also essential to adapt these strategies to the changes in transmission dynamics observed in areas close to the dams.

Participants aged between 5 and 19-year-old had the highest risk of malaria infection. The increased risk of malaria in this group has been also reported in another study conducted in the Nanoro area [48, 50], suggesting that this age group represent a major parasite reservoir and may actively contribute to maintaining transmission. This finding could be the result of limited access to targeted prevention measures as most of the malaria intervention strategies are focused in children under 5 years of age. This could be also explained by the partial immunity of this age group, which is insufficiently developed to provide effective protection, and increased exposure to mosquito bites due to regular outdoor activities and less protective clothing habits (51). These factors could explain the persistence of the transmission in this population, which thus represents an important target for malaria control. Mass treatment campaigns targeting this age group should be considered as a complement malaria control intervention to reduce to reduce the parasite reservoir and limit malaria transmission within the community.

One of the main limitations of our study is the use of cross-sectional surveys and microscopy for parasite detection, which may have influenced the estimate of malaria infection prevalence. Microscopy, although the gold standard method, has limited sensitivity for sub patent parasite detection, particularly in villages far from the dam, where asymptomatic infections are most prevalent. Consequently, a proportionally lower number of Plasmodium infections was probably detected in these areas, which could overestimate the apparent impact of the dam on malaria infection. The use of a molecular technic such as PCR would have been more appropriate, but due to financial constraints, this was not possible. A longitudinal study would enable us to study the incidence of malaria rather than its prevalence and to follow the same cohort of individuals over the 12 months of the study period, to better capture individual, village and environmental variations, and thus offer a more in-depth understanding of the malaria transmission dynamic and its determinants. It would have also enable to distinguish whether the persistence of asymptomatic infections was due to repeated reinfections, favored by the permanent presence of surface water linked to the dam (thus maintaining a permanent breeding ground for mosquitoes), or whether these infections were caused by strains that persist throughout the year. In addition, several potentially determining factors were not considered in the logistic regression analysis. These include some more environmental parameters (temperature, humidity), entomological data (culicid population density), use of as impregnated mosquito nets, types of agricultural practices, access to health services, dam water management, and access to electricity. Incorporating these elements would have made it possible to explain some of the unexpected spatial variations observed in this study.

Conclusion

Our study results highlight the spatio-temporal heterogeneity of malaria transmission between villages, likely influenced by the combined effect of the Soum agropole and the socio-economic factors. The 5–19 years old associated with high risk of malaria infection shows that they are a key reservoir of the parasite. This age group, which is often excluded from existing prevention strategies, requires targeted, tailored interventions. It’s also highlighted the need to Enhanced malaria surveillance using new-generation Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs), to detect small-scale variations in transmission patterns. Multi-sectoral approach integrating water management, vector control and community engagement are crucial to limiting the impact of dams and reducing malaria prevalence.
