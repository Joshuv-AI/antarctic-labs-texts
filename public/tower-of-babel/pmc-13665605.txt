Validation of a MALDI Intact Cell Mass Spectrometry Workflow for Routine Application in Monitoring Industrial E. coli Fermentations
Authors: Cristian Zanetti, Christopher Stephan, Rudolf Bednar, Alexandra Foettinger‐Vacha, Martina Marchetti‐Deschmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTToday, matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization‐intact cell mass spectrometry (MALDI‐ICMS) is used in clinical environments for rapid pathogen identification, referred to as MALDI biotyping. This method relies on the generation of characteristic mass spectrometric profiles of intact microorganisms. Single‐celled organisms are often used for pharmaceutical production, while this allows for the production of complex products, close monitoring and control of these processes is essential. In this study, we propose the use of MALDI‐ICMS as a potential at‐line approach for fast monitoring of the progress of a biotechnologically relevant fermentation process employing E. coli as a host. We demonstrate the reproducibility of the method, thereby indicating its potential to predict the progress of antibody fragment production in E. coli in an industrial setting. We evaluated various aspects of sample preparation, including the choice of matrix and solvent concentration, sample loading, and sample‐to‐matrix ratios. Our workflow allowed monitoring of over 100 signals across a 32‐h span of E. coli fermentation. To control instrumental variability, we performed eight technical replicates and achieved excellent repeatability, with 9% median relative standard deviation of peak intensities. Principal component analysis revealed strong time‐related differences of the mass spectra, allowing to differentiate the data based on fermentation phases. Furthermore, to assess direct monitoring of product expression, we adapted acquisition parameters to enable product detection via MALDI‐ICMS. The presented MALDI‐ICMS workflow allowed reliable monitoring of cellular changes during fermentation, thereby expanding the applications of MALDI‐ICMS to process analytical technologies (PAT).

Introduction

The initial work on bacterial identification utilizing cell lysates and matrix‐assisted laser/desorption ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI‐MS) was presented by Cain et al. in 1994 [1], and in 1996, identification approaches employing intact cells were introduced [2, 3]. Over the following decades, MALDI intact cell mass spectrometry (MALDI‐ICMS) has become a well‐established technique for the rapid routine identification of pathogens in clinical settings, referred to as MALDI Biotyping [4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

MALDI‐ICMS requires the mixing of intact cells with a MALDI matrix. This can be performed by sequentially adding the two components on a MALDI target (e.g., stainless steel) or by mixing the two components in liquid form and applying the mixture on a MALDI target [9]. The contact of matrix with intact cells induces lysis, supported by the presence of an acidic environment and organic solvents, resulting in the release of cellular components. The obtained matrix‐sample mixture is then allowed to dry, and a fraction of the analytes will co‐crystallize with the matrix. A linear time‐of‐flight mass analyzer is typically employed to cover a wide mass range for such analyses.

With straightforward sample preparation, MALDI‐ICMS offers faster sample preparation compared with cell lysate analysis. However, the quality of the resulting spectra is highly dependent on sample preparation. Various factors can influence the analyte's solubility, crystal size and distribution of the matrix, and ionization efficiency. Therefore, achieving optimal ionization and high shot‐to‐shot reproducibility requires careful consideration of several factors such as sample clean‐up step, selection of MALDI matrices and solvents (including acid type and concentration), sample‐to‐matrix ratio, cell density, and sample application/drying techniques [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Although the detected signals in the acquired mass spectra are considered to be proteins, only a part of the proteome/peptidome is ionized by this approach, primarily basic proteins and peptides [15]. Even though this means that this technique cannot compete in terms of proteome coverage with other methods like shotgun proteomics, the generated mass spectra contain species‐specific fingerprints that can be used for prompt taxonomic identification [4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

In addition to its well‐established use for pathogen identification, MALDI‐ICMS has been explored for modeling various characteristics of intact cell samples. It has been employed in mammalian cell cultures to detect contamination [16], monitor phenotypic changes [17] and differentiation [18], and identify cancerous subpopulations in cell mixtures [19]. Furthermore, its application to assess the ecotoxicological status of water bodies was presented [20]. Worth mentioning is the implementation of MALDI‐ICMS in MALDI imaging approaches, enabling the identification of cell‐specific biomarkers and facilitating in‐tissue localization [21].

Other than these applications, MALDI‐ICMS has been used to study and monitor microorganism growth [12, 22]. This is of particular interest for biopharmaceutical production, where microorganisms are often employed to express products of interest. Among the diverse host organisms available, E. coli represents a widely used organism in biotechnology due to its rapid growth rate and well‐documented biology. Furthermore, the existence of well‐established procedures for its genetic engineering and cultivation elevates the significance of E. coli in biotechnological industries.

During E. coli industrial fermentations [23, 24], which are typically performed in fed‐batch mode, multiple parameters are continuously monitored and controlled to ensure optimal and reproducible production [25]. Nevertheless, the intrinsic complexity of biological processes presents a challenge for industrial production and may fail or result in sub‐optimal production caused by, e.g., contamination but more often due to unwanted variations of unknown origin. In the pursuit of detecting and ideally correcting process anomalies, research and exploration of novel process analytical technologies (PAT) tools are critical. Considering that protein expression profiles convey complex biological information from a living organism, we envision the use of MALDI‐ICMS not just for species identification, but for detecting proteomic‐based anomalies and to monitor product expression.

To date, only a few lab scale studies on E. coli have been published, demonstrating that good signals can be obtained from E. coli , and that cultivation time influences MALDI‐MS spectra [12, 26, 27]. However, a systematic, comprehensive characterization of these time‐related spectral changes, which can be exploited for process monitoring, is lacking.

In this study, we have aimed to develop a robust and quick method as a PAT tool for at‐line monitoring of industrially relevant E. coli fermentations via MALDI‐ICMS. We use a linear MALDI‐TOF instrument with automated mass spectra acquisition to acquire MALDI‐ICMS spectra of industrially relevant E. coli fermentations. Data were processed in R [28, 29, 30] for method optimization and analytical process evaluation. Additionally, we exploited the potential of MALDI‐ICMS to estimate product expression by directly monitoring the respective m/z signal of a Fab fragment.

Methods

Organism

Experiments were performed with E. coli BL21(DE3) (Novagen, Merck KGaA). The gene encoding for FabZ including the wild‐type leader sequence of the OmpA protein was placed under the control of the T7 promoter and the T7 terminator and was integrated into the attTn7 site of the bacterial chromosome as described by Sharan et al. [31].

Cultivation Conditions

Fed‐batch cultivations were performed similarly to previously published works [32, 33]. Four fully controlled 5 L standard stirred‐tank bioreactor systems were used (BIOSTAT Cplus, Sartorius Stedim) with the manufacturer provided process control system (Siemens PCS‐7). A constant aeration of 5 L/min with sterile air at 1.0 bar overhead pressure was applied. The dissolved oxygen (DO) level was set to ≥ 20% and was regulated in a control cascade, where the stirrer speed was increased from 400 to 1200 rpm followed by additional oxygen supplementation up to 50% v/v. To avoid foaming, a sterile antifoam agent was added automatically on demand. In the batch phase the temperature was set to 37°C ± 1°C and the pH at 6.8 ± 0.2 using 25% ammonia and 3 M phosphoric acid. Temperature and pH were shifted to optimal settings for maximum expression in the production phase. Thirty‐five milliliters of preculture with an OD550 of 2 was used to inoculate the fermenter using a sterile syringe.

Synthetic media used for the cultivations were prepared as described by Striedner et al. [34]. The medium was supplemented with 0.1% v/v antifoam agent (PPG 2000, Dow Chemical Co.) and was autoclaved for sterilization in place prior to the start of cultivation.

Feeding was initiated when the culture had a cell dry mass (CDM) of 5.7 g/L in 2.5 L of batch medium and entered the stationary phase, as indicated by a DO peak. After 12 h of exponential feed phase, a constant feed was set for 15 h. The CDM yield coefficient on the glucose was 0.35 g/g, and the 2.0 L feed medium provided glucose and components sufficient to yield a final CDM of 97 g/L. Production was induced with 17.7 mL isopropyl β‐d‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (50 g/L stock).

Sample Collection

Four parallel cultivations were performed, with a total fermentation time of 36 h each (batch phase 0–9 h, fed‐batch phase 9–22 h, induction phase 22–36 h). Fermentation suspension samples (2 mL) were manually collected and frozen at −20°C, sampling was performed at 11 timepoints (T01–11). T01–T03 were collected in the batch phase (4, 6, and 9 h after cultivation start), T04–T05 were collected in the fed‐batch phase (9 h, 11 h), and T06–T11 were collected in the induction phase (23 h, 24 h, 27 h, 29 h, 33 h, 36 h), with T11 being the end of fermentation.

Photometric Measurements

During mass spectrometry sample preparation, optical density at 550 nm (OD550) was measured after diluting the samples 1:10 with a NH4HCO3 solution (LC–MS grade, Merck, 10 mM, pH 7, filtered with 0.2 μm Minisart NML filters, Sartorius), filling the sample in microcuvettes (1 cm path length, 70 μL UV microcuvettes, BRAND) and reading out the absorbance on a benchtop spectrophotometer (Nanophotometer, Implen). The final optimized procedure utilized cell suspensions equalized to an OD550 of 2, which were frozen at −20°C and analyzed within 4 days.

Protein Determination

Cleaned and resuspended samples were sonicated with a cell sonifier (S250A sonifier, BRANSON), equipped with a micro‐horn tip. Sonication was performed with an output power of 30%, duty cycle of 30% using conventional 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes (Safe Lock tubes, Eppendorf). A total of 30 pulses were applied, allowing a cooldown of 30 s every 10 pulses. Samples were kept on ice during sonication. Protein concentration was determined with a Pierce Assay 660 nm kit (ThermoScientific) using bovine serum albumin as a calibrant (0–2 mg/mL) (ThermoScientific).

MALDI‐TOF‐MS Sample Preparation

E. coli suspensions were thawed and kept on ice and, similarly to previous publications [22, 35], washed twice by pelletization (12 800 rcf, 5 min, 4°C) and resuspension in 10 mM NH4HCO3 (pH 7; 0.2 μm filtered with Minisart NML, Sartorius). Cleaned samples were equalized to the desired OD550 value by dilution with 10 mM NH4HCO3. Equalized samples were pre‐mixed in a microcentrifuge tube in the desired ratio with freshly prepared matrix. The matrix, if not stated otherwise, consisted of 7.5 mg/mL sinapinic acid (SA, Sigma‐Aldrich) and 0.8 mg/mL ferulic acid (FA, Sigma‐Aldrich); in a 70/29.5/0.5 v/v/v mixture of acetonitrile (LC–MS grade, Honeywell)/UHQ water (Simplicity UV system, Merck)/trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, HPLC grade, Fisher Scientific). Following brief mixing, the sample was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After this, 1 μL of the mixture was spotted on a MALDI stainless steel target (eight replicates, 384 ground steel MALDI target, Bruker Daltonik GmbH) and dried under ambient conditions. Each sample had a vicinal spot for external calibration (2–20 kDa range ProtMix1, LaserBio). After acquisition of the calibrant spectrum, quadratic calibration was automatically applied to the vicinal sample spots (up to eight, see Figure S1). For fermentation monitoring, the equalized samples were thawed and mixed with freshly prepared matrix as described above in a sample‐to‐matrix ratio of 1:2 (10 μL of sample + 20 μL matrix). The mixture was then treated as described. For analyzing samples in the mass range of 10–70 kDa, the respective protein standard was used for calibration (ProtMix3, LaserBio).

MALDI‐TOF MS Automated Measurements

The spotted and dry target was inserted in a MALDI‐TOF instrument (UltrafleXtreme, Bruker Daltonik GmbH) equipped with a pulsed Nd:YAG laser (Smartbeam II, tripled frequency, 335 nm wavelength). Mass spectra were acquired in the linear positive ion mode in the mass range from 2 to 20 kDa. Automated acquisition was performed by accumulating 1000 shots per sample spot (five times 200 shots, random rastering every 100 shots) at a laser power of 90% or higher (laser parameter: ultra, focus position: 8, flexControl v. 3.4, Bruker Daltonik GmbH). Before accumulation, mass spectra of every 200 shots were automatically smoothed (Savitzky–Golay algorithm; width 2 m/z, 2 cycles), baseline subtracted (TopHat algorithm) and peak picked (centroid method, signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) ≥ 6, intensity ≥ 200 a.u., peak width 10 m/z) by the acquisition software. Mass resolution (min. 100, max. 10× threshold) and the fuzzy control set for proteins/oligonucleotides were the defined inclusion parameters. Only spectra meeting these criteria were accepted for accumulation.

Measurements targeting the product were performed in the higher mass range (10–70 kDa), using 3000 shots that were manually accumulated. Detailed instrumental parameters are presented in Table 1.

Data Analysis Workflow

In‐depth details are provided in the SI (Table S1). Briefly, MALDI‐ICMS data were processed in the R environment (version 4.1.2, https://cran.r‐project.org/) [28], using the packages MALDIquant [29] (v. 1.21) and mdatools [30] (v. 0.12.0). Spectra were imported, following several preprocessing steps including variance stabilization via square root transformation [36, 37], smoothing (Saviztky‐Golay), background subtraction (SNIP), total‐ion‐current normalization, spectral realignment of technical replicates (0.2% tolerance), averaging of technical replicates (mean), and spectral realignment (0.1% tolerance) of averaged spectra. Peak picking was performed (“Supersmoother” algorithm, SNR = 1.5), followed by peak binning (0.1% tolerance) and filtering (removed peaks found in less than four samples). Peak intensities were saved in an intensity matrix.

Data preprocessing was based on the template from the MALDIquant package [29]. Variance stabilization was performed to reduce heteroscedasticity of the data [36, 37]. Spectral realignment was performed twice to allow different tolerances to be applied for intra‐sample and inter‐sample alignment.

Results and Discussion

MALDI‐ICMS has already been proposed as a tracking tool for biotechnological fermentations in 2015 by Helmel et al. [22]. Based on the fact that MALDI‐ICMS has the potential to measure the change in cell status according to the growth stage of E. coli [12], we tested its potential to be used as a PAT tool to monitor the fermentation process of an industrial‐scale E. coli strain producing a Fab fragment.

Sample Preparation

Based on previous findings [35], we carefully evaluated the effect of different MALDI matrices on MALDI‐ICMS peak number, mass resolution, and SNR. We pelletized E. coli suspensions from fed‐batch processes, followed by two washing steps of the pellets with an MS‐compatible solution (NH4HCO3, 10 mM, pH 7) to prevent artefacts and interferences from remaining fermentation broth components (e.g., phosphates [34, 38]). Samples were equalized to the same protein concentration using a Bradford assay [39] after lysis via sonication. At this stage, protein loading per spot ranged between 0.125 and 0.38 μg, which was fine‐tuned after optimizing MALDI matrix and acetonitrile concentrations. Normally, multiple matrices are tested during preliminary MALDI‐MS experiments. SA in a mixture of acetonitrile/UHQ/TFA is commonly used in literature for microbial MALDI analysis [14, 15, 35, 40]; therefore, we expected it to perform well. Furthermore, previous findings [35, 41, 42] highlighted that a binary mixture of SA with FA allowed for improved performance for microbial samples. For confirmation, multiple matrices were tested: SA, FA, α‐cyano‐4‐hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA), 2,5‐dihydroxy‐benzoic acid (DHB), 2,6‐dihydroxyacetophenone (DHAP), and their binary combinations (in 7:3 acetonitrile/0.1% TFA, details in Table S2). We compared two sample‐matrix mixing methods: (I) the pre‐mixing method, where sample and matrix are mixed together in a microcentrifuge tube and spotted; (II) a three‐layer method, also referred to as “sandwich” [9, 43], where the matrix, the sample, and a final matrix layer are sequentially applied, with drying steps in between. Different TFA concentrations (0.5–2.5 vol%) were tested for their influences on the desorption/ionization process.

Similar to previous findings [35, 41, 42], a binary matrix mixture of SA (7.5 mg/mL) and FA (0.8 mg/mL) in acetonitrile/UHQ water/TFA (70/29.5/0.5, v/v/v), pre‐mixed 1:1 with the sample, exhibited the best overall performance. While the best resolution was found for the SA matrix with the pre‐mixing technique, the binary mixture of SA and FA was found best in terms of SNR at higher masses (> 15 kDa), had good crystallization homogeneity and vacuum stability, and was therefore selected. Resolution and SNRs were comparable between pre‐mixing and sandwich methods. Pre‐mixing was therefore selected for faster preparation times. Other matrix combinations showed suboptimal results, like low peak resolution (e.g., CHCA), insufficient crystallization homogeneity (e.g., DHB), or inferior vacuum stability for automated high‐throughput analysis (e.g., DHAP). The effect of TFA concentration on signal quality was also studied. Base peak intensities were highest when acid concentration was ≥ 1 vol%; this can be explained by improved ionization. Nevertheless, the intensity of higher mass compounds suffered when TFA concentrations were > 0.5 vol%, attributed to multiple protonation effects [14]. The selected TFA concentration was therefore 0.5 vol% to allow optimal detection also at higher mass ranges [44]. Based on preliminary experiments, incubation times of up to 10 min did not significantly affect peak number or SNR; a 10‐min incubation was therefore selected to allow a standardized matrix‐analyte contact time across all samples.

Further optimization steps included sample‐to‐matrix ratios, acetonitrile content, and sample amount. In MALDI‐MS, the matrix is normally present in large molar excess; nevertheless, a too high (or too low) amount of matrix can influence negatively the ionization [45]. For this reason, we evaluated the optimal sample‐to‐matrix ratio by checking its influence on SNR after pre‐mixing 10 μL of cell suspensions with different volumes of the binary MALDI matrix to achieve sample‐to‐matrix ratios between 1:2 and 2:1. The best SNR was observed for the 1:2 ratio (10 μL sample + 20 μL of matrix) (Figure S2A). Increasing further the amount of matrix to 1:4 did not have a significant difference in peak intensities. A 1:9 ratio resulted in a decrease in peak intensities compared with the optimum 1:2 ratio, attributed to matrix suppression effects [45]. For these reasons, the selected sample‐to‐matrix ratio was 1:2.

Acetonitrile is often chosen in MALDI‐ICMS [9, 14, 35], for this reason, it was the selected solvent and its concentration in the matrix solution was optimized. Different proportions of acetonitrile (30, 50, or 70 vol%) were tested in the matrix solution (SA/FA 7.5/0.8 mg/mL, sample‐to‐matrix 1:2). While high amounts of organic solvent proportion are expected to reduce protein solubility [46], they also reduce crystal size thanks to faster drying times, improving crystallization homogeneity. In Figure 2A, it is shown that 30 vol% acetonitrile has the most abundant signals, but crystals were biggest and more inhomogeneous, hampering automatic acquisition and resulting in significantly worse shot‐to‐shot reproducibility. Despite the lower signal intensities, attributed to lower protein solubility, spectral reproducibility significantly improved for 50 and 70 vol%. Considering the fastest drying time, superior crystallization homogeneity, small crystal size, and very good shot‐to‐shot reproducibility, 70 vol% acetonitrile was selected as the optimal concentration. Higher amounts of solvent were not tested to avoid sample spreading due to low surface tension and further decrease in protein solubility.

Another aspect that influences MALDI‐ICMS spectral quality is the amount of biomass loaded [40, 47, 48]. For best results, the optimal concentration of biomass was determined, and samples were equalized to this value for reproducible results. One option is to determine the total protein content after cell lysis, a time‐consuming, tedious work. Alternatively, biomass concentration can be estimated by measuring optical density at around 550–600 nm, a fast and uncomplicated parameter commonly used in industrial settings [49, 50]. To determine the optimal amount of biomass, cell suspensions were prepared with different OD550 values using the binary MALDI matrix system. Thirty‐three spectra were generated from samples with OD550 values between 2 and 20. While little influence was observed for the spectral quality, the highest SNRs were found in spectra originating from suspensions (n = 18) with OD550 values ranging between 3 and 18, with an average of 7.8 (Figure S2B). For this reason, the optimal OD550 was considered to be 8. Although this value represents the best option in terms of signal quality, it was observed that samples from a very early time point of the fermentation did not reach this value. With this in mind, the final OD550 value was lowered to 2, to ensure both good quality spectra and inclusion of all fermentation timepoints. This is in accordance with a previous work [14], suggesting using samples with an OD610 of 1.0. All further experiments were carried out by equalizing cleaned and resuspended samples to OD550 2 with NH4HCO3.

To estimate the amount of protein loaded per prepared sample spot, the protein concentration for nine equalized samples was measured. Cell suspensions were lysed by sonication and the protein concentration was evaluated. The resulting amount of protein loaded on each MALDI spot was 0.28 ± 0.05 μg (mean ± C.I., n = 9, α = 5%). The confidence interval was considered to be acceptable for the method. This conclusion was supported by MALDI‐ICMS experiments, which established only minimal influence of biomass loading on SNR (Figure S2B).

In Figure 2B representative, unprocessed spectra of three timepoints following matrix and sample‐to‐matrix optimization are shown. These spectra display signals of high intensity, SNR, and a high number of detected peaks (> 60). From these spectra, it is evident that some signals change with time (highlighted in Figure 2B). Additionally, the occasional presence of cell aggregates (indicated by arrows) did not influence the signal intensity, demonstrating the robustness of the automated spectral acquisition process.

Taking the outcome for all considerations into account, the following optimized sample preparation was established: E. coli samples are pelletized and resuspended twice in 10 mM NH4HCO3 (pH 7). After equalizing the biomass to OD550 2 with NH4HCO3, 10 μL of suspension are mixed with 20 μL of matrix (7.5 mg/mL SA, 0.8 mg/mL FA in acetonitrile/UHQ water/TFA 70/29.5/0.5 v/v/v). The mixture is briefly mixed and after 10 min of incubation, 1 μL is spotted on the MALDI target. Eight replicates are prepared for robust statistics.

Method Robustness for Monitoring the Fermentation Process

To evaluate the potential of MALDI‐ICMS to monitor E. coli fermentations, four 5 L fed‐batch processes were conducted, samples were collected at 11 timepoints for each biological replicate, and analyzed with the developed workflow. After washing and equalization, samples were frozen for later analysis. The preparation of 10 E. coli samples can be completed within 1 h, starting with pellet washing and ending with the automated MALDI‐TOF‐MS analysis (2–20 kDa, Table 1). While this time‐span is relatively short compared with the overall process duration of 36 h, the implementation of further automated sample preparation techniques can increase throughput and accelerate analysis even further.

Spectral reproducibility poses a common challenge in MALDI‐ICMS experiments, resulting from both biological aspects and inherent method variability [14]. To address this issue, the repeatability of the method was assessed for both the instrumental and the biological level as illustrated in Figure S1.

To evaluate repeatability, four parallel fermentations were conducted (i.e., four biological replicates), and samples were collected at 11 timepoints of the process, resulting in a total of 44 samples. Each sample was prepared according to the optimized method and spotted eight times on the MALDI target as technical replicates, resulting in a total of 352 MALDI‐ICMS measurements. Figure 3 presents spectra from technical and biological replicates for a single timepoint. To mitigate the peak intensity variance across the technical replicates, averaging was applied (Figure 3A), which indicated the high technical precision (Figure 3B).

To quantitatively assess the technical repeatability of MALDI‐MS data, the RSD of peak intensities [13, 51] across the eight technical replicates for each of the 44 samples was calculated. Only m/z values that were detected in at least 75% of the replicate measurements were considered as relevant, resulting in a total of 3734 RSD calculations. The resulting median RSD was 13% (IQR 9%–19%, Figure S3). As mentioned, MALDI‐ICMS can suffer from shot‐to‐shot reproducibility; crystallization heterogeneity is a recognized factor that results in the formation of “sweet” or “hot” spots, introducing inconsistencies in ionization across MALDI spots [13]. To our knowledge, very few studies exist that evaluate the reproducibility of microbial MALDI‐ICMS; Toh‐Boyo et al. [13] evaluated the reproducibility of E. coli measurements across different matrices and deposition techniques. For the pre‐mixing method, our calculated RSD value is much lower than the work from Toh‐Boyo et al. [13], which is in the range of 46%–55% (peak area, SA matrix), and more comparable with the reported performance of the spray method, with an RSD in the range of 12%–14% (peak area, SA matrix). We attribute this improvement to different aspects: improved spectral quality thanks to automated spectral acquisition with conditional accumulation, good crystallization homogeneity and small crystal size, achieved with the binary mixture of SA and FA matrices [35] in fast‐drying 70 vol% acetonitrile.

While averaging technical replicates improves the accuracy of methods, it significantly influences measurement times, reducing suitability for industrial applications where cost‐effectiveness is crucial. Hence, the minimum number of technical replicates required to obtain robust results was examined. To do so, we evaluated the biological variability for independent fermentations, as it impacts the method's ability to predict process anomalies. The higher the biological repeatability, the higher the chances of detecting deviations from an expected outcome.

For this, eight artificial subsets were generated from the available MALDI‐ICMS data, each by randomly selecting an increasing number of technical replicates. After averaging the selected replicates, the RSD of the peak intensities across the four biological replicates for each timepoint was calculated.

Using only one MALDI spot resulted in the highest peak intensity dispersion across the biological replicates (median RSD1 spot = 32%, IQR = 16%–54%, 3443 evaluations, Figure S4). When an increasing number of technical replicates was averaged, noise levels decreased, enhancing peak detection and significantly reducing RSDs. This improvement became apparent using just five technical replicates, yielding acceptable biological repeatability (median RSD5 spots 10%, IQR 6%–15%, 1540 evaluations). As expected, the lowest median RSD value was obtained after averaging all eight technical replicates (median RSD8 spots 9%, IQR 5%–13%, 1474 evaluations). This demonstrates that with a sufficiently high number of repeated measurements, it is possible to obtain excellent analytical precision using MALDI‐ICMS data. While it was established that a lower number of technical replicates (e.g., five) could be used depending on process needs and throughput, eight technical replicates were ultimately used for all further evaluations to ensure the highest performance.

Monitoring E. coli Fermentation Progression Using Principal Component Analysis for MALDI‐ICMS Data

We applied PCA to the generated intensity matrix, which stored the intensities of 135 peaks monitored across the 11 timepoints. As expected from previous works [12, 22], fermentation phases are already distinguishable when plotting the first two principal components scores (Figure 4A), with a cumulative explained variance of 73%. Since the peaks detected by MALDI‐ICMS are attributed to proteins, the spectral variations displayed by PCA are a result of proteome changes over time and between individual fermentations. Biological replicates cluster closely together at many timepoints, showing that their variation is smaller than the effect introduced by fermentation progression. This corroborates the finding that spectral changes are reproducible across the four parallel processes. It also reveals that MALDI‐ICMS holds a high potential for precisely tracking proteome changes in E. coli , thereby enabling the modeling of the fermentation progress.

The most relevant features emerging from the dataset can be visualized with the PCA loadings plot (Figure S5). Based on the Euclidean distance from the origin, the five most influential features are m/z 9226, 9537, 5096, 6411, and 2833. Figure 4B shows the top three most influential spectral features for six timepoints. The complete plot for the top five features is presented in the SI (Figure S6). These five peaks show significant change with fermentation progress (ANOVA p values < 10−13), and at the same time, exhibit very low levels of variability across four biological replicates (median RSD of peak intensity 6%, IQR 4%–7%, 11 timepoints). Additionally, performing PCA with only the selected five features results in similar timepoint separation (see Figure S7), confirming that these five features alone are highly informative and can represent the time‐related changes detected with this method. Ryzhov and Fenselau [15] tentatively assigned the identity of E. coli MALDI‐ICMS peaks by matching masses to a database ( E. coli K‐12 strain, SwissProt/TrEMBL). Comparing the literature results [15] with the top five features found in this work, four out of the five features were found also in the published work, while one feature (2833 m/z) was not included in the referenced work's analyzed mass range (4–20 kDa, see Table S3 for matches). Nevertheless, considering that the method is optimized for protein detection, it is reasonable to expect this mass too to be a protein/peptide. For these reasons, for the strain used and fermentation parameters employed, these m/z values serve as meaningful biomarkers for monitoring process changes and are promising candidates for targeted monitoring. Furthermore, considering the extensive applicability of MALDI‐ICMS to diverse microorganisms, we expect the method to be adaptable to multiple strains and fermentation parameters, leading to the identification of case‐specific features.

Freeze–Thaw Cycles and Robustness of Analysis

While freezing samples can be useful, it can impact negatively protein analysis [52]. So, it was of interest to evaluate the impact of freeze–thaw cycles on the statistical outcome. Up to five freeze–thaw cycles were induced for three timepoints, using two biological replicates. Each freeze–thaw cycle consisted of freezing the cleaned and equalized samples overnight at −20°C and thawing them on ice the next morning. After thawing, the samples were mixed with matrix and analyzed with the optimized procedure. It was possible to identify signals varying in intensity with the increasing number of freeze–thaws, as exemplified for the feature with highest loading for principal component 1 (m/z 10 383, Figure 5A). Spectral changes, visible in the PCA plot (Figure 5B), can be attributed to cell lysis, the release of newly detected proteins, or protein degradation. Nevertheless, time‐related changes are maintained along the five freeze–thaw cycles, as visualized in Figure 5B. Additionally, in Figure 5A, the feature with highest loading for principal component 2 (m/z 2834) is shown as an example for a compound with a higher resistance to freeze–thaws, while displaying time effects. Overall, while significant time differences are present even at a high number of freeze–thaw cycles, the influence of freeze–thaws is evident on the data. With this considered, best practice is to use a minimal and consistent number of freeze–thaw cycles to ensure reproducible results.

Monitoring Product Expression via MALDI‐ICMS

At production sites, E. coli biopharmaceutical production is commonly monitored with multiple methods, including enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay [53], and requires steps for cell lysis due to the characteristic intracellular production of this organism. This renders quantification time‐consuming and cannot provide quick monitoring of ongoing fermentations. To address this limitation, the capability of MALDI‐ICMS for monitoring product expression was explored. In this study, a Fab antibody was produced with a mass of about 23 kDa. The acquisition parameters of 2–20 kDa were chosen as a generalized MALDI‐ICMS fingerprinting mass range, which did not include the product mass.

A further MALDI‐ICMS acquisition method was set up to include the product mass in the analysis (10–70 kDa). An analysis of 1 pmol (23 ng) of purified product ensured that the detection of the light‐chain was possible (Figure S8, top, purification procedure in SI pg. 9–10). Additionally, a sample collected in the induction phase exhibited a peak at 23 137 m/z, corresponding to the mass of the antibody fragment and was therefore considered the aspired product. Spiking this sample with 1 pmol of purified product resulted in a clear signal increase at the same m/z value, confirming the ability of the developed workflow to detect the product from fermentation samples (Figure S8, bottom). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the potential of MALDI‐ICMS as a rapid and straightforward method for monitoring E. coli product expression.

Conclusions and Outlook

A MALDI‐ICMS workflow was developed aiming at providing an at‐line PAT monitoring tool for ongoing E. coli fermentations. Samples were collected at multiple timepoints during four parallel fermentations. Sample preparation optimizations covered aspects like matrix choice, application technique, acid and solvent concentrations, sample amount, number of technical replicates, and impact of freeze‐thaw cycles. The chosen sample preparation included: (I) cell wash (twice with 10 mM NH4HCO3, pH 7), (II) equalization to OD550 2, (III) mixing with the binary MALDI matrix consisting of SA (7.5 mg/mL) and FA (0.8 mg/mL) in acetonitrile/UHQ water/TFA (70/29.5/0.5, v/v/v), and (IV) spotting of eight technical replicates. Automated acquisition of MALDI‐ICMS data allowed monitoring 135 peaks over time, which displayed significant time‐related changes as highlighted by PCA and ANOVA. Technical repeatability was very good, with a median RSD of peak intensities across eight technical replicates of 13%. To estimate biological repeatability, the median RSD of peak intensities across four biological replicates was calculated, demonstrating excellent biological repeatability with a median RSD of 9%, reduced to 6% when considering only the top five features found via PCA. These time‐effects were attributed to proteome shifts during fermentation, supported by tentative peak assignment based on previously published work. This is a promising indication that MALDI‐ICMS data cannot only be used to identify species, but also to detect and monitor E. coli proteome changes in an industrial context. Additionally, to evaluate the potential of the workflow to monitor product expression of an ongoing process, acquisition parameters were adapted to allow, with the same sample preparation, the successful detection of the expressed product within the prepared sample.

While this technique cannot compete in terms of quickness to in‐line monitoring techniques (e.g., near infrared spectroscopy), or of protein coverage to shotgun proteomics, it offers distinct advantages in precise and relatively fast proteome monitoring (> 100 peaks) for comprehensive process understanding.

Overall, it can be stated that the presented work constitutes basis for future evaluations of MALDI‐ICMS as a novel PAT tool for monitoring E. coli fermentations. In future works, peak identification (e.g., via top–down sequencing) is considered to be useful to strengthen biological understanding of the identified features and to allow the identification of process anomalies. Improvement on speed, throughput, and reproducibility may be achieved with the implementation of automated sample preparation.
