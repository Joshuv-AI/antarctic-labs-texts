Adaptively mixed thin films for advanced optical coatings with reduced stress and tunable refractive index
Authors: Kunio Yoshida, Kana Fujioka, Ryosuke Kodama, Takuya Mikami, Shinji Motokoshi, Takayuki Okamoto, Hidetsugu Yoshida, Kazuo A. Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A wide variety of optical components are used in optical instruments such as laser devices, spectrophotometers, cameras and telescopes. To enhance their performance, these components are often coated with optical thin films including antireflection (AR) coatings, high reflection (HR) coatings, polarizing films, translucent films, and bandpass filters. However, the fabrication of multilayer thin-film coatings is often constrained by stresses that arise during the deposition process. These stresses can lead to delamination, thereby limiting the range of dielectric materials and operating wavelengths available for optical components. We report a novel thin-film fabrication method that enables both refractive index control and significant stress reduction. This approach produces adaptively mixed thin films (AMTFs), consisting of dielectric material, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and depletion layers, with a porous microstructure that lowers refractive index while maintaining high transmittance. For example, AMTF: MgF₂ films exhibit a refractive index as low as 1.3, a 15-fold reduction in stress compared with pure MgF₂, and 95.95% transmittance. In addition to antireflection coatings, highly reflective multilayer mirrors can also be fabricated using structures such as [Al₂O₃/AMTF: Al₂O₃], [ZrO₂/AMTF: SiO₂], and [TiO₂/AMTF: MgF₂]. The range of applicable dielectric materials is thereby significantly expanded. By tailoring the refractive index, these films enable coverage of a broad spectral range from 200 nm to 7000 nm. The demonstrated reduction of stress, control over refractive index, and wide spectral applicability highlight the potential of AMTFs to advance the design and fabrication of next-generation optical coatings, particularly in the field of laser optics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26097-8.

Introduction

Optical mirrors and filters are indispensable components in a wide range of optical systems, where they are commonly fabricated with coatings such as antireflection (AR) films1–3, high-reflection (HR) films4,5, polarizing films6,7, translucent films, and bandpass filters8. Despite their broad utility, there remains a strong demand for coatings that can extend the usable wavelength range while avoiding internal stresses within multilayer structures.

Several critical challenges remain unresolved in the fabrication of optical thin films. First, the dielectric materials available for deposition are limited, which constrains the range of achievable refractive indices. As a result, the refractive index of thin films cannot be freely tailored. Second, during fabrication, substrates are typically heated to 150–300 °C. In the course of vacuum evaporation, thin films are subjected to mechanical stress, which can manifest as tensile stress leading to the formation of microcracks, or compressive stress9–11 resulting in delamination from the substrate. These issues become particularly serious in multilayer structures comprising twenty or more layers—such as polarizers, broadband mirrors, and interference filters or in thick multilayer films designed for infrared applications.

To address these limitations, we have developed an innovative thin-film fabrication method that enables (i) precise control over the refractive index of vacuum-deposited optical thin films and (ii) a significant reduction in deposition-induced stress. This method relies on the formation of a porous film with a refractive index gradient, produced under vacuum conditions, which serves as the low-refractive-index layer. Structurally, the film consists of a composite of dielectric material, polytetrafluoroethylene (Teflon), and associated depletion layers. This approach provides a pathway to overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional dielectric thin films, thereby expanding material flexibility and enabling stress-free multilayer optical coatings across a broad spectral range.

Many evaporation materials used in the fabrication of optical thin films possess high melting points. As a result, during vacuum deposition, the films often develop a columnar microstructure, in which columnar grains grow in the direction perpendicular to the substrate surface. These grains typically exhibit cylindrical shapes with diameters on the order of several tens of nanometers and are arranged in a hexagonal pattern.

Narrow pores are formed along the interfaces between adjacent columns, extending throughout the film cross section. Due to the large interfacial area of the columnar grains, these pores remain in direct contact with the surrounding atmosphere, thereby exposing the thin film to environmental influences.

In the 1980 s, there were a lot of research and development on controlling the packing density ratio, which significantly reduced the packing density ratio to about 50% and produced an antireflection film with a single layer film.

There were two methods for fabricating porous films with refractive index gradients: the sol-gel method and the vapor deposition method13. The sol-gel method has been used to fabricate porous silica thin films (with a reflectance of 0.1–0.3% on a quartz substrate surface)14 by Milam et al. at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in the United States. Thomas of the same institute has also formed porous thin films of the fluorides MgF2 and CaF2 on quartz glass and CaF2 substrate surfaces by the sol-gel method15.

On the other hand, the method that we developed in 1985 combines the vacuum deposition and chemical treatment methods13. In this method, a mixed thin film of water-soluble and non-water-soluble deposition materials is formed by a binary simultaneous vacuum deposition method, and then the water-soluble materials in the mixed film are dissolved and removed with pure water to form a porous film of non-water-soluble materials on the substrate surface. The porous film is formed by dissolving and removing the water-soluble substances in the mixed thin film with pure water. Although the porous film produced by these methods had excellent performance as a single-layer antireflection film, it is difficult to form a multilayer film by alternately layering with a dielectric material with a high refractive index because of its weak mechanical strength.

To solve this problem, we developed a method to form a mixed film of Teflon and a dielectric material (oxide or fluoride) for thin film formation on a substrate by a binary evaporation method, and control the packing density ratio by changing the mixing ratio to reduce the refractive index of the optical thin film16–20. In the case of producing mirrors with the desired reflectance, alternating layers of highly refractive material (e.g., TiO2, ZrO2, HfO2, etc.) and low-refractive material are placed on the substrate surface for evaporation coating. In this case, the larger the difference in refractive index between the two materials, the fewer the number of layers can be obtained. Currently, the lowest refractive material is MgF2, followed by SiO2. For typical applications, the refractive index at 1.06 μm is 1.36 for MgF2 and 1.45 for SiO2.

However MgF2 has been rarely used because the tensile stress from the deposition is too large and causes cracking. Instead SiO2 is used as a low-refractive material. If the stresses in MgF2 can be controlled, then MgF2 can be used because of the refractive index lower than that of SiO2.

The optical thin film fabricated by this method is called “adaptively mixed thin film: AMTF. The porous MgF2 film fabricated by this method is defined as AMTF: MgF2. Since AMTF films can be fabricated in vacuum, multilayer optical films can be easily fabricated by a conventional vapor deposition method. AMTF films can be realized as a porous film made of dielectric material, Teflon, and depletion layer. AMTF with a low refractive index can be fabricated by changing the mixing ratio of Teflon and dielectric material.

Results

We show first AMTF: MgF2 case of transmission optics that makes the refractive index lower than MgF2. Figure 1 shows the results of transmittance of various optical thin films measured by a spectrophotometer. The films were deposited on a quartz glass substrate at 230 °C installed in a vacuum apparatus. The transmittance on both sides of the quartz glass substrate in Fig. 1a at a wavelength of 1.06 μm was 93.54%, with a reflectance Ra=3.2%, refractive index na=1.43 on one side. The transmittance of MgF2 in Fig. 1b was 95.06%, with a reflectance Rb=1.74%, refractive index nb=1.36. The AMTF: MgF2 film in Fig. 1c, a mixture of Teflon and MgF2, had a transmittance of 95.95%, a reflectance Rc=0.85% and a refractive index nc=1.31. The transmittance of Teflon in Fig. 1d was 96.74%, reflectance Rd = 0.06%, and refractive index nd=1.225. These results indicate that the refractive index of a mixed film of dielectric material and Teflon can be made smaller than the dielectric material, and average transmittance of 95% can be obtained between 800 and 1800 nm.

The error bar is typically 0.5% in the spectrophotometer measurement due to the instrumental accuracy.

We show next that the combination of dielectric materials: Al2O3, TiO2, and ZrO2 as reflective mirrors. This kind of mirrors of multi-layer coating with Teflon plastic has not been possible with conventional coating techniques. Figure 2a shows the spectral characteristics of a highly reflective mirror fabricated by alternately stacking seven layers of dielectric material Al2O3 and AMTF: MgF2 on a quartz glass substrate. The reflectivity is 28% at a wavelength of 1.06 μm. The layering of the high refractive index material Al2O3 and the low refractive index material AMTF: MgF2, which was not possible with conventional technology, is now possible. The damage threshold of this mirror was measured to be 49.8 J/cm224. Nd: YAG laser system was used at 1064 nm wavelength with a 10 nsec pulse width for the damage threshold measurement. A fixed site was irradiated with pulses of increasing fluence until the plasma emission was detected with a CCD camera. In previous studies damage threshold of single layer Ta2O5 was 6.1 J/cm2. Similarly damage threshold was 4.8 J/cm2 for Ta2O5/SiO2 multilayer at the same laser irradiation condition as ours25.

Fig. 2b shows the spectral characteristics of a highly reflective mirror fabricated by alternately layering 15 layers of two evaporation materials, TiO2 and AMTF: MgF2, on a quartz glass substrate at 230 °C. The reflectance at a wavelength of 1.06 μm was 99.75%. In the conventional vapor deposition method, when a highly reflective mirror is fabricated by alternating layers of MgF2 and a high refractive index material, the thin film cracks due to large tensile stress. However, the porous AMTF: MgF2 thin film, which is a mixture of a dielectric material and Teflon, can be used as a low-refractive material for high-reflection mirrors because the stress is significantly reduced as shown in Table 2. Therefore, AMTF: MgF2 thin film is also useful as a low-refractive index material for high-reflection mirrors in the UV to IR wavelength range.

Figure 2c shows the highly reflective mirror spectral characteristics fabricated by alternating 11 layers of the dielectric material ZrO2 and AMTF: SiO2 on a quartz glass substrate. The reflectance of the mirror at a wavelength of 1.06 μm is 83%. Porous thin films of oxide dielectric materials can be easily fabricated with this technology.

Figure 2d shows the spectral characteristics of a highly reflective mirror fabricated by alternately stacking nine layers of the dielectric materials Al2O3 and AMTF: Al2O3 on a quartz glass substrate. The technique proposed in this paper enabled us to fabricate a highly reflective mirror using only one type of dielectric material.

Methods

Fabrication procedure of porous thin film (AMTF : MgF2)

Figure 3 shows the set up for fabricating porous dielectric thin films. A commercial vacuum deposition equipment of electron-gun and tungsten boat was prepared to fabricate the porous thin film. The diameter of the vacuum equipment is 45 cm, and the distance between the E-gun and the substrate holder is 50 cm. An optical thickness monitor was used to monitor the thickness of the thin films. This device consists of a light source to illuminate the glass substrate for monitoring and a detector to analyze the reflected light.

Using this set up, the dielectric material MgF2 was heated with an E-beam and Teflon heated in a Tungsten boat to form a porous mixed film on the substrate surface. Oxide and fluoride dielectric materials were used to fabricate the porous mixed film. The decomposition temperature of Teflon is as low as 390 °C, which makes it difficult to form a thin film because of the slow velocity of the dissolved particles. This problem was solved by moving the Tungsten boat 20 cm closer to the substrate holder than the electron-gun. The vacuum pressure is maintained at 10− 3 Pascal with a rotary and oil diffusion pumps.

Vacuum chamber for porous thin film formation.

The AMTF: MgF2 thin film is composed of the dielectric material MgF2, porous Teflon, and a depletion layer, so by changing the mixing ratio of MgF2 and Teflon, the packing density ratio of the mixed film, i.e., the refractive index of AMTF: MgF2, can be changed. Since Teflon is transparent over a wide bandwidth from 200 nm to 7000 nm, the absorption and other effects of the AMTF thin film in this bandwidth depend on the dielectric material.

Discussion

The stress generated in AMTF films fabricated by this method is reduced to one-fifteenth of that in MgF₂ films produced by conventional deposition. Moreover, the refractive index can be lowered to 1.3. As noted earlier, MgF₂ is seldom used in multilayer optical coatings because, despite its refractive index being lower than that of SiO₂, it induces strong internal stress. In this work, we demonstrate for the first time a multilayer optical thin-film reflector composed of AMTF with a low refractive index combined with a high-index dielectric material, fabricated by vacuum evaporation.

The thin film is much thinner than the ones with conventional technology. As a result, the number of layers in the thin film can be reduced by about 20%, and the bandwidth can be increased by more than 10%.

Multilayer optical thin films can be fabricated using MgF2 and optical material with a low refractive index.

Examples are shown of Al2O3 and AMTF: MgF2, TiO2 and AMTF: MgF2 and ZrO2 and AMTF: SiO2 as reflectors. Highly reflective mirrors can be fabricated using this AMTF method.

Summary

Optical thin-film coatings—such as high-reflective, anti-reflective, polarizing, and interference films—are widely applied across science, engineering, medical technology, information and communication, and optical measurement. We have developed a method for fabricating mixed films of dielectric materials and Teflon (PTFE: Polytetrafluoroethylene), enabling their use as low refractive index layers in multilayer optical reflection mirrors. This method has a potential to widen the limit of the existing optical coating.

A major challenge in conventional high-temperature coating processes is the stress generated within thin films, which can lead to delamination, microcracks, and restrictions on usable dielectric materials. In addition, the refractive indices of available materials are discrete, limiting wavelength design flexibility. For multilayer mirrors, alternating layers of high- and low-index materials can achieve high reflectance more efficiently when the refractive index contrast is large, also resulting in broader bandwidths.

To satisfy these conditions, we developed a binary deposition method that forms a mixed film of Teflon and dielectric materials (oxides or fluorides) on the substrate. By controlling the mixing ratio, the packing density and refractive index of the thin film can be precisely tuned, expanding the design freedom and performance of multilayer optical coatings.

Teflon was chosen for the following reasons:

1. The porous nature of Teflon reduces the stress in the optical thin film.

2. The transmission band of Teflon covers a wide range from 200 nm to 7000 nm, expanding the range of usable dielectric materials.

3. The high bonding energy of Teflon (C-C: 3.6 eV, C-F: 4.6 eV) increases the laser damage threshold of the mixed film.
