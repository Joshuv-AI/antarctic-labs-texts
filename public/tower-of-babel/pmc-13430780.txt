Prevalence and molecular characterization of colistin-resistant gram-negative bacteria from a tertiary care hospital in Eastern India
Authors: Shreya Singh, Rajesh Kumar Sahoo, Ishwar Chandra Behera, Biranchi Narayan Sahoo, Parthasarathi Barik, Mahesh Chandra Sahu, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColistin, a last resort antibiotic for multidrug resistant gram-negative infections, is facing enhanced resistance. Data from Eastern India, including Odisha, is limited, highlighting the importance for enhanced surveillance to monitor emerging resistance and guide appropriate empirical therapy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to screen for colistin resistance and to evaluate the antimicrobial susceptibility patterns among gram-negative bacteria isolated from clinical samples.MethodThe study period was from February 2024 to January 2025, during which 366 non-duplicate identified clinical samples were collected from SUM hospital, Bhubaneswar. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, including colistin tested at three concentrations (10, 25, and 50 µg), was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. As CLSI and EUCAST guidelines do not recommend disk diffusion for colistin due to poor agar diffusion, the results were considered screening-only and were not used to determine confirmed susceptibility or resistance, and mcr-1 to mcr-9 genes were screened by PCR; however, mcr-1, mcr-3, and mcr-4 were not detected in any of the isolates. Descriptive statistics, including percentages and 95% confidence intervals, were used to summarize prevalence and screening-based colistin non-susceptibility findings.ResultMost frequently isolated organisms were E. coli (36.88%) and K. pneumoniae (19.67%). Screen-positive colistin non-susceptibility was observed highest in K. pneumoniae (59.72%), P. aeruginosa (29.62%) and E. coli (8.14%). Multiple mcr genes were detected among screen-positive isolates, with K. pneumoniae positive for mcr-2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, E. coli was positive for mcr-5 and mcr-8, A. baumannii for mcr-2, 6, 7 and mcr-1, mcr-3, and mcr-4 were absent in all isolates; P. aeruginosa harboured mcr-7 & mcr-9 only. K. pneumoniae, E. coli and A. baumannii showed high multidrug resistance patterns. These findings are based on screening-positive results only. Prevalence estimates of screening-based colistin non-susceptibility were interpreted descriptively, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for bacterial isolates and clinical sample sources where appropriate.ConclusionThis study highlights screen-positive colistin non-susceptibility among gram-negative isolates in Odisha, India. These screening-based observations underscore the need for confirmatory testing using broth microdilution and ongoing molecular surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05226-x.Keywords: Colistin resistance, Gram-negative bacteria, Antimicrobial susceptibility, mcr genes, Multidrug resistance

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a major public health challenge of the 21st century, which is largely associated with infections related to gram negative bacteria such as E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii. These organisms cause hospital acquired infections and are increasingly reported in India, like in tertiary care facilities [1–3]. While K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa are commonly linked to respiratory and wound infections, E. coli remains the leading cause of urinary tract infections [4]. Gram-negative bacteria are commonly isolated from clinical samples such as urine, pus, wound swabs, respiratory secretions, and blood. Treatment options are becoming limited due to the rising multidrug resistance, with high levels of resistance reported against commonly used antibiotics, including sulphonamides, fluoroquinolones, third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, and other β-lactams. Importantly, global ESBL production in E. coli and K. pneumoniae has rendered many first-line antibiotics ineffective [2, 4, 5].

Non-fermenting bacteria such as A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa are well-known causes of ventilator-associated pneumonia, bloodstream infections, device-associated infections, and other severe clinical complications. Their ability to cause persistent infections is largely attributed to a combination of intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms. As a result, these infections are often resistant to multiple classes of antibiotics, leading to increased patient morbidity and mortality, as well as substantially higher healthcare costs [6–8]. The emergence of plasmid-mediated mcr genes, along with chromosomal mutations, has jeopardized the effectiveness of colistin a drug once reserved as the last line of defence against carbapenem-resistant gram-negative infections [9]. Recent data from India indicate colistin resistance rates of 10–40% among Enterobacterales and as high as 50–60% in K. pneumoniae, highlighting the rapid spread of resistance within hospital environments [9, 10].

In both environmental and clinical isolates, positive strains of multiple mcr have been identified, underscoring the urgent need for continuous and robust surveillance [11, 12]. Colistin resistance is an evolving clinical challenge, driven by chromosomal alterations affecting lipid A, and modification by plasmid-mediated mcr genes [11, 13]. In India, incidence of colistin resistance is increasingly reported across various clinical settings. Among gram negative pathogens, K. pneumoniae is the most commonly identified colistin-resistant organism and often shows simultaneous resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics [14]. High level of resistance to colistin is also observed in A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa, particularly in intensive care units (ICU), where ventilated, critically ill, or immunocompromised patients are more susceptible [14–17]. Colistin-resistant bacterial infections present a serious clinical challenge because of limited treatment options. Treatment often depends on combination of antibiotics involving tigecycline, ceftazidime–avibactam, fosfomycin, or high-dose colistin derivatives, which may be associated with toxicity or reduced effectiveness [13, 15]. Despite these strategies, mortality rates remain as high as 40–50%, especially in patients with bloodstream infections, ventilator-associated pneumonia, septic shock, and in immunocompromised patients [18]. The combination of multiple resistance mechanisms within a single isolate further causes treatment failure [13, 18–21].

In this scenario, continuous monitoring of colistin resistance is essential. Understanding species-specific prevalence, sample distribution, antibiotic susceptibility patterns, and the associated clinical burden is important for improving antibiotic stewardship and enabling timely therapeutic interventions. Despite increasing incidence of colistin resistance in India, data from Eastern part of India like Odisha remains limited. Therefore, continuous surveillance in this region will help to guide proper empirical therapy. This study aims to evaluate the profiles of gram-negative bacteria antimicrobial susceptibility pattern from diverse clinical samples, analyse their distribution across infection types, and to know the prevalence of colistin resistance, thereby providing important insight into the evolving resistance mechanism and supporting informed clinical and public health interventions.

Methods

Sample collection and bacterial isolation

The study was conducted over a one-year period from February 2024 to January 2025. The clinical samples had already been identified by the Department of Microbiology, SUM Hospital, Bhubaneswar and gram-negative isolates were obtained from these confirmed samples for further analysis. Consecutive sampling method was used for all eligible non-repeating gram-negative isolates obtained throughout the course of the study while the selection criteria were positive isolates of gram-negative bacteria from clinical specimens with no repeats from the same patient. All procedures were carried out in accordance with CLSI M100 guidelines [22]. According to CLSI M100 and EUCAST polymyxin susceptibility testing recommendations, disk diffusion is not recommended for colistin due to its poor diffusion characteristics. Therefore, the colistin results obtained in this study were interpreted as screening-based only.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

Antibiotic susceptibility was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller Hinton agar as per CLSI guidelines (CLSI M100). A panel of antibiotics representing major classes β-lactams (cephalosporins), monobactams, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, sulphonamides, amphenicols, and polymyxins was used to evaluate the resistance pattern among the isolates. The antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates was evaluated using a panel of antibiotics including chloramphenicol (50 µg), cefoperazone/sulbactam (75/10 µg), cefpodoxime (5 µg), azithromycin (15 µg), gatifloxacin (30 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), aztreonam (50 µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100/10 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), imipenem/cilastatin (10/10 µg), amoxicillin (10 µg), co-trimoxazole (25 µg), colistin (10 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg). Colistin screening was performed using 10 µg, 25 µg, and 50 µg disks to observe inhibitory trends among isolates. In accordance with CLSI M100 and EUCAST guidelines, disk diffusion is not a validated method for colistin susceptibility testing; therefore, the results were appropriately interpreted as screening-based or screen-positive findings only.

Molecular detection of mcr Genes (mcr-1 to mcr-9)

All gram-negative bacterial isolates obtained during the study period were initially included for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, including colistin screening, irrespective of their known intrinsic resistance profiles. This approach was adopted to provide a comprehensive overview of isolate distribution. However, organisms such as P. mirabilis and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, which are known to exhibit intrinsic resistance or reduced susceptibility to polymyxins, were interpreted cautiously during downstream analysis. DNA was extracted from isolates showing screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility, by boiling method, and PCR amplification was performed to detect the presence of mcr-1 to mcr-9 genes using specific primers. Only colistin screen-positive gram-negative isolates were selected for mcr gene detection, and percentages were calculated species-wise based on the respective number of resistant isolates & positive gene amplification. Thermocycling conditions for all mcr gene targets (mcr-1 to mcr-9) were standardized with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30–35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, gene-specific annealing temperatures ranging from 58.01 °C to 62.43 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Negative controls (nuclease-free water as no-template control) were included in each PCR run to ensure assay validity and rule out contamination. No positive control strains were used; nuclease-free water served as the no-template negative control in all PCR runs. All primers for mcr-1 to mcr-9 were obtained from previously published literature and are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The primer sequences were further checked and validated in silico using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool to confirm specificity and target alignment. Amplified products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm the presence of corresponding mcr genes.

Multiple Antibiotic Resistance Index (MARI) assay

MARI was calculated using a panel of 16 antibiotics, defined as the ratio of the number of antibiotics to which an isolate was resistant to the total number of antibiotics tested (n = 16). The resistance patterns were obtained from the antibiotic susceptibility test results, and the resulting MARI values were recorded for gram negative bacteria. A MARI value > 0.2 was considered indicative of high antibiotic exposure, as commonly applied in environmental microbiology; however, this threshold was used here for comparative purposes only. Colistin findings were based on screening methods and should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of gram-negative bacterial isolates, screening-based colistin non-susceptibility patterns, antibiotic resistance profiles, and mcr gene findings. Percentages and 95% confidence intervals (Wilson method) were calculated for prevalence estimates of gram-negative bacteria and clinical sample sources. A chi-square test was performed to compare screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility among major bacterial species. Due to limited sample sizes, unequal subgroup distribution, and the descriptive nature of PCR-based screening observations, additional inferential statistical analyses were not performed for mcr gene distribution, concentration-based screening patterns, or antibiotic resistance comparisons. Therefore, these findings were interpreted descriptively and with appropriate caution.

Results

Prevalence of gram-negative bacteria

The most prevalent species was E. coli (36.88%), followed by K. pneumoniae (19.67%), P. aeruginosa (14.75%), and A. baumannii (10.10%). Lower prevalence was observed for P. mirabilis (9.83%), P. vulgaris (3.82%), C. freundii (1.36%), S. sonnei (1.36%), E. cloacae (0.81%), Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (0.81%), and K. oxytoca (0.54%) (Fig. 1). Screen-positive colistin findings differed significantly among major species (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa) (χ² = 63.9, df = 2, p < 0.001).

Screening-based prevalence of reduced colistin susceptibility

Screen-positive colistin non-susceptibility was most frequently observed in K. pneumoniae (59.72%), followed by P. aeruginosa (29.62%), E. coli (8.14%), and A. baumannii (13.51%). Notably, E. cloacae exhibited the highest relative screen-positive rates (66.66%) despite the small number of isolates (Table 1). High rates of screening-based reduced colistin susceptibility were observed in P. mirabilis (19.44%) and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (66.66%). These findings are consistent with the known intrinsic resistance or reduced susceptibility of these organisms to polymyxins. The highest percentage of screen-positive colistin non-susceptibility isolates was found in drain fluid, ET aspirate, and tissue samples (100%) but the samples were also few in comparison to others, followed by blood (87.5%), wound swab (75%), sputum (72.72%), and pus (55.17%). Urine samples showed a lower rate of screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility (12.32%) (Table 2). Screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility appeared to vary across clinical sources, with highest proportions observed in respiratory and blood isolates; however, formal statistical comparison was not performed due to small and unequal subgroup sizes.

Molecular detection of mcr genes

Multiple mcr genes (mcr-1 to mcr-9) were screened among isolates showing screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility. No isolate carried mcr-1, mcr-3, or mcr-4. A. baumannii harboured mcr-2, mcr-6, and mcr-7, while E. coli carried mcr-5 and mcr-8. K. pneumoniae harboured mcr-2, mcr-5, mcr-6, mcr-7, mcr-8, and mcr-9. P. aeruginosa carried mcr-7 and mcr-9, whereas Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi showed the presence of mcr-2 and mcr-9; however, this organism is intrinsically less susceptible to colistin (Table 4). The detection of mcr genes (such as mcr-2 and mcr-9) in Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi should be interpreted with caution, as this organism is known to exhibit intrinsic reduced susceptibility to colistin. The presence of these genes may not necessarily correlate with clinically significant reduced colistin susceptibility and could represent background genetic carriage rather than active phenotypic expression. Therefore, further functional studies are needed to determine their actual contribution to reduced colistin susceptibility. A combination of multiple mcr genes was also observed in A. baumannii, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa, indicating the presence of multiple mcr gene variants among the screened isolates, as detected by PCR (Table 5). Due to the limited number of isolates and the descriptive nature of the dataset, no formal statistical association analysis between mcr gene carriage and phenotypic reduced colistin susceptibility was performed. Findings are based on PCR detection and were not confirmed by sequencing.

Descriptive antibiotic susceptibility pattern

K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and A. baumannii were the most multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria found in this study, they were especially resistant to β-lactam antibiotics such as cefepime, ceftriaxone, ampicillin, and amoxicillin. A. baumannii was identified as a high-risk pathogen due to its widespread resistance to nearly every class of antibiotics, including aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and β-lactams. With strong non-susceptibility to cefepime (86%), ceftriaxone (54%), ampicillin (63%), and colistin (59%), K. pneumoniae showed comparatively high levels of resistance, consistent with previous reports describing this organism as a therapeutically challenging gram-negative pathogen. E. coli demonstrated extensive resistance to β-lactams, ranging from 44% to 69%, but modest resistance to gentamicin and fluoroquinolones, possibly reflecting increased antibiotic exposure and multidrug resistance within this species. In comparison to the Enterobacterales group, P. aeruginosa exhibited moderate resistance overall, but it was still less resistant to β-lactams. P. vulgaris and P. mirabilis showed moderate to high resistance to a number of antibiotic families, especially β-lactams and fluoroquinolones. Ceftriaxone and fluoroquinolone resistance was evident in E. cloacae and C. freundii, which showed intermediate resistance patterns. Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi and S. sonnei showed notable resistance to gentamicin, levofloxacin, and certain β-lactams. Klebsiella species showed comparatively higher resistance patterns among the organisms studied, including resistance to multiple antibiotic classes such as aminoglycosides, β-lactams, and macrolides. However, these observations should be interpreted cautiously because of unequal and limited sample sizes among some groups. Overall, all organisms displayed β-lactam resistance; however, E. coli and K. pneumoniae, showed higher levels of screening-based colistin non-susceptibility (Table 6). Comparisons of resistance patterns across species are descriptive, and no formal statistical testing was performed.

Multiple Antibiotic Resistance Index (MARI)

The majority of the gram-negative bacterial isolates in this investigation showed moderate to high degrees of antibiotic exposure, according to the Multiple Antibiotic Resistance Index (MARI) values. With a strong multidrug-resistant profile and high antibiotic pressure, K. oxytoca showed the greatest MARI value. Elevated MARI scores were also observed in A. baumannii, C. freundii, E. cloacae, K. pneumoniae, P. mirabilis, P. vulgaris, and Pseudomonas spp., suggesting substantial exposure to multiple antibiotic classes. S. sonnei, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, and E. coli, on the other hand, displayed somewhat lower but still alarming MARI values. Crucially, all isolates exhibited MARI values above the 0.2 threshold; however, this threshold was applied for comparative purposes only. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined according to EUCAST/CDC criteria as resistance to ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics, and MARI values were interpreted only as supportive indicators of antibiotic exposure (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The distribution of gram-negative bacteria seen in our study, with E. coli (36.88%) and K. pneumoniae (19.67%) appearing as the most often isolated pathogens, closely matches studies from other tertiary-care facilities throughout India. For example, the most common species found at a large tertiary-care hospital in Chennai were K. pneumoniae (62.4%), E. coli (19.7%), P. aeruginosa (10.7%), and A. baumannii (4.8%). The majority of gram-negative clinical isolates continue to be Enterobacterales; however, the cited studies primarily reflect specific clinical and ICU-based settings rather than broader epidemiological distribution, and thus may not fully represent national trends [17–20]. The high frequency of screening-based colistin non-susceptibility observed in our study for K. pneumoniae (60%) is consistent with reports of colistin non-susceptibility among Enterobacterales in India, although such comparisons should be interpreted cautiously in light of differences in study design and clinical context [23, 24]. The precise incidence varies by geography and clinical situation, despite the fact that plasmid-mediated resistance has been extensively established in numerous Indian studies. For instance, a study conducted in Chennai reported reduced colistin susceptibility in 13.08% of gram-negative isolates overall [25]. Similarly, colistin non-susceptible E. cloacae complex isolates with the plasmid-encoded mcr-9 gene were found in a multicentre critical care unit study from Kolkata. These studies demonstrate the increasing occurrence of colistin non-susceptibility reported in India, as well as the possible contribution of transferable resistance determinants to its dissemination, which is in agreement with the observational detection of multiple mcr variants in this study. However, these findings were based on PCR screening only and were not confirmed by sequencing. Emerging research has also reported a variety of mcr variations, despite the fact that some mcr-positive isolates did not show phenotypic resistance. Recent studies from India further strengthen our observations. A study from Chennai reported the presence of mcr-5 in clinical isolates, going beyond the commonly recognised mcr-1 variant. Similarly, an ICU-based bloodstream infection study from Kolkata reported the presence of mcr-9 among colistin non-susceptible isolates, suggesting its possible clinical relevance [26–31]. These findings are consistent with our observations and may suggest the presence of a diverse mcr gene reservoir, including several non-mcr-1 variants, in Indian hospital settings. However, as the findings were based on PCR screening alone without sequencing confirmation, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution. These observations may suggest the possible presence of diverse mobile genetic mechanisms associated with colistin non-susceptibility in clinical settings; however, further confirmation studies are required. As per the recent data available from India, the prevalence of mcr genes is still lower (8–9% amongst isolates showing colistin non-susceptibility) and, more significantly, it shows very uneven geographic distribution. While it is true that mcr-1 is generally considered the most common variant in India, there are also instances where other mcr genes have not been detected uniformly across different geographic locations due to poor sampling and limited spread [32]. Hence, the absence of mcr-1, mcr-3, and mcr-4 in this study may reflect limited detection or dissemination of these variants within this region of India. However, as these observations were based solely on PCR screening without sequencing confirmation, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

However, important differences are also observed. For instance, a recent study on uropathogenic E. coli isolated from urinary tract infections reported that all 88 multidrug-resistant strains remained colistin-susceptible, and none carried mcr-9. This contrast may suggest that colistin non-susceptibility and mcr gene carriage are more frequently observed in certain infection types such as ICU, bloodstream, or device-associated infections compared to community-acquired UTI isolates. In contrast to reports where all MDR E. coli isolates remained colistin-susceptible [29], the presence of isolates showing screening-positive colistin non-susceptibility in our study may reflect geographic variation in resistance patterns and local antimicrobial usage, despite similarities in clinical setting and isolate sources [29–31]. It is important to note that P. mirabilis is intrinsically resistant to colistin due to constitutive modifications in the lipid A component of lipopolysaccharides, which reduce polymyxin binding. Therefore, the observed resistance in this species is expected and does not represent acquired resistance. Similarly, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi exhibits reduced susceptibility to polymyxins and is not considered a primary target organism in colistin resistance surveillance studies [33]. The inclusion of these intrinsically resistant organisms in colistin screening analysis may lead to overestimation of resistance rates if not interpreted cautiously. In this study, their results are presented to reflect overall isolate behaviour but are not indicative of clinically relevant acquired colistin resistance.

In terms of broader antimicrobial susceptibility, the extensive β-lactam resistance, moderate to high resistance to aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones, and variable susceptibility to other antibiotic classes observed in our study align well with national resistance patterns reported from India. According to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) national surveillance data, Enterobacterales and non-fermenting gram-negative bacteria continue to show high levels of resistance to cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and several other commonly used antibiotics. In many centres, colistin remains one of the few reliably active therapeutic options [34, 35].

However, the fact that some Indian studies still report preserved susceptibility to carbapenems or even to colistin in certain isolates, such as the uro-pathogenic E. coli cohort referenced earlier, highlights the influence of sample type, infection-control practices, and local antibiotic usage patterns on resistance trends [31, 34]. This variation underscores the heterogeneous nature of AMR within the country.

Several molecular studies from India have reported that colistin non-susceptibility in K. pneumoniae is more commonly driven by chromosomal alterations, such as disruption of mgrB gene and changes in two-component regulatory systems, and few by plasmid-mediated mcr genes [36–38]. These observations suggest that mechanisms associated with colistin non-susceptibility may be diverse and heterogeneous, with underlying mechanisms differing considerably between hospitals and across different geographical regions in India [13, 36, 39, 40]. In this context, the detection of multiple mcr variants in our isolates further enhances the importance of comprehensive genotypic surveillance, that goes beyond mcr-1 alone. As several subgroup analyses involved limited sample sizes and PCR-based screening observations, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered primarily descriptive and exploratory in nature.

Limitations

A major limitation of this study is that, the PCR-based detection of mcr genes was not confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Therefore, the possibility of non-specific amplification, particularly for less-characterized variants (mcr-5 to mcr-9), cannot be excluded. In addition, disk diffusion was used only as a screening method for colistin susceptibility assessment. Both CLSI M100 (p.239) and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing explicitly state that disk diffusion is not suitable for polymyxin testing due to poor diffusion characteristics, which can lead to inaccurate and non-reproducible results. Consequently, the screening-based colistin non-susceptibility findings presented here should be considered preliminary, and the true prevalence of confirmed resistance cannot be reliably determined without broth microdilution confirmation. PCR detection of mcr genes was performed without positive controls, and amplicons were not confirmed by sequencing; therefore, the possibility of non-specific amplification cannot be excluded. This limitation may have led to over- or underestimation of resistance rates. MARI values for species with a low number of isolates (e.g., K. oxytoca, n = 2) should be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size may limit the reliability of these estimates. As the study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, the findings may not be fully representative of other clinical settings. In addition, prior antibiotic exposure, treatment outcomes of the infected patients were not evaluated, limiting clinical interpretation of the results. One more limitation of the present study is that chromosomal resistance mechanisms (e.g., mgrB, phoPQ, and pmrAB) were not evaluated, though they may play a role in colistin resistance.

Conclusion

The study highlights a notable frequency of screening-based colistin non-susceptibility in Eastern part of India, Odisha. Pathogens such as K. pneumoniae, E. coli, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa may pose therapeutic challenges due to their multidrug resistance and screening-based colistin non-susceptibility profiles. However, these observations were based solely on PCR screening, and the findings are limited to molecular-level detection without sequencing confirmation or further detailed characterization. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the necessity of improving antimicrobial resistance surveillance systems, encouraging molecular surveillance for mcr genes, and enhancing antibiotic stewardship programs to facilitate appropriate infection control and empirical therapy within the region.
