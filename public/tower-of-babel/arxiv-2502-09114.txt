Interlacing sequences resulting from an interval split-merge dynamics and the induced probability measures
Serge Cohen, James Norris, Michel Pain, Gennady Samorodnitsky

Abstract
We study sequences of partitions of the unit interval into subintervals, starting from the trivial partition, in which each partition is obtained from the one before by splitting its subintervals in two, according to a given rule, and then merging pairs of subintervals at the break points of the old partition. The n n th partition then comprises n + 1 n+1 subintervals with n n break points, which inherently possess an interlacing property. The empirical distribution of these points reveals a surprisingly rich structure, even when the splitting rule is completely deterministic. We consider both deterministic and randomized splitting rules and we study from multiple angles the limiting behavior of the empirical distribution of the break points.



Keywords
: interlacing sequences, splitting and merging intervals, random walk in random environment, Markov chain, limit theorems, large deviations.

AMS classification (2020)
: 60J05, 60F05.

1.1
Definition of the model

We consider sequences of partitions
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of the interval
(0,1]
, of the form

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{(a_{n,0},a_{n,1}],\dots,(a_{n,n},a_{n,n+1}]\}

where
a_{n,0}=0
and
a_{n,n+1}=1

n

a_{n,1},\dots,a_{n,n}
satisfy

0\leq a_{n,1}\leq\dots\leq a_{n,n}\leq 1.

Thus
\mathcal{P}_{0}=(0,1]
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
partitions
(0,1]
into
n+1
subintervals

(0,1]=(a_{n,0},a_{n,1}]\cup\dots\cup(a_{n,n},a_{n,n+1}].

Suppose we are given a family of splitting proportions

{\bm{p}}=(p_{n,k}:n\geq 1,\,1\leq k\leq n)

with
p_{n,k}\in[0,1]
for all
k
.
For
n\geq 1
, we define the break points of the partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
recursively by

a_{n,k}=p_{n,k}a_{n-1,k-1}+(1-p_{n,k})a_{n-1,k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n.

We sometimes choose the family of splitting proportions randomly.
In this case, we will use upper-case letters for both the splitting proportions
{\bm{P}}=(P_{n,k}:n\geq 1,\,1\leq k\leq n)

and the break points
A_{n,k}
, but their relation will be the same, namely
A_{n,0}=A_{n,n+1}=0
for all
n
and

A_{n,k}=P_{n,k}A_{n-1,k-1}+(1-P_{n,k})A_{n-1,k},\quad k=1,\ldots,n.

(3)

In this paper, we mainly consider the following three choices for the splitting proportions.

•

Deterministic stratified case:
we choose a
deterministic sequence
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
[0,1]
and set
p_{n,k}=p_{n}
for all
k
.
An interesting special case is the choice
p_{n}\equiv p\in(0,1)
.

•

Random stratified case:
we set
P_{n,k}=P_{n}
for all
k
,
where
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are i.i.d. random variables in
[0,1]
.

•

Fully random case:
we take
(P_{n,k}:n\geq 1,\,1\leq k\leq n)
to be an
array of i.i.d. random variables in
[0,1]
.

In each of these three cases,
the sequence
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
forms a Markov chain whose state-space is the set of finite partitions
of the unit interval into subintervals.

1.2
Motivation and related literature

The motivation for our work comes from an analysis of the asymptotic behavior, in a certain regime,
of a Markov chain on
\{-1,1\}^{K}
with
K
large, considered in
[
7
]
.
This latter Markov chain moves from a state
a\in\{-1,1\}^{K}
to a state
b\in\{-1,1\}^{K}
according to the following rule:
choose, uniformly at random, a subsequence
J
of
\{1,\ldots,K\}

from the set of subsequences
j=(j(1),\dots,j(\ell))
of
\{1,\dots,K\}
such that
a_{j(i+1)}\neq a_{j(i)}
for all
i
.
Once a subsequence
J
is sampled,
the Markov chain moves to the state
b\in\{-1,1\}^{K}
defined by
b_{k}=a_{k}
if
k
is not in the range of
J
,
and
b_{k}=-a_{k}
otherwise. Starting from the initial state
a=(1,\dots,1)
,
the first transition leads to a state
b
that has at most one digit equal to
-1
,
and this
b
is chosen uniformly among the
K+1
possible choices;
furthermore, for
n\ll\log K
the behavior of this Markov chain can be approximated by
the fully random case of the model we consider in the present paper,
where the proportions have the standard uniform distribution.
For more related results concerning this Markov chain refer to
[
6
]
.

The splitting part of our dynamics appears in fragmentation processes, a well-studied class of stochastic processes (see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
3
]
),
and also bears some resemblance to the definition of multiplicative cascades (see e.g.
[
13
,
1
,
11
]
).
However, the merging feature prominent in our model does not seem to have been studied yet and produces a radically different behavior. In particular, the number of intervals grows linearly in the number of steps for our model, whereas it grows exponentially for fragmentation processes or multiplicative cascades.

We have already mentioned that our dynamics produces interlacing sequences.
We would like to point out that interlacing sequences appear naturally in other contexts as well, both deterministically and randomly.
A common name for a family of sequences satisfying (
2
) is a
Gelfand–Tsetlin pattern
, which is
in many situations a finite pattern (i.e. extends over a finite range of
n
), but sometimes is infinite, like our pattern is.
Important examples of Gelfand–Tsetlin patterns are the eigenvalues of the successive principal minors of an
N\times N
Hermitian matrix (
[
12
,
2
]
) or the roots of the successive derivatives of a degree
N
polynomial with real roots (
[
10
]
).
These models differ from our model both in the manner interlacing structures are constructed and in the type of questions that are being asked.

1.3
Content of the paper

We are interested in the behavior of the empirical distribution of the break points of the partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
as
n\to\infty
.
We will set

g_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{a_{n,k}},\quad G_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{A_{n,k}}

(4)

in the deterministic and random cases respectively.
Here
\delta_{a}
denotes the unit mass at point
a
.
Note that, when the splitting proportions
{\bm{P}}
are random, the empirical distribution
G_{n}
is a random probability measure.
We will study both the overall behavior of the empirical distribution and its restriction to certain parts of the unit interval.

We begin in Section
2
by establishing a key representation of the break points in terms of the cumulative
distribution function of a certain random walk, whose steps are governed by the splitting proportions.
We denote this walk by
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
when we restrict to the case of deterministic splitting proportions,
and by
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in the general random case.
The walk
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
will be random in both cases, even when the splitting proportions are deterministic.
Interestingly, this representation is useful even in the deterministic case.
In the random stratified case,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
becomes a random walk with a random environment in time,
where the environment consists of the random splitting proportions
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Similarly, in the fully random case,

(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
becomes a random walk with a random environment in time and space.

In Section
3
, weak convergence of the empirical distribution is established,
as a consequence of the law of large numbers satisfied by the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
The weak limit turns out always to be supported by the endpoints of the unit interval.
Nonetheless, the number of points of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
in any given open subset of
(0,1)
diverges,
and we proceed in Section
4.1
to investigate the behavior of the empirical distribution
away from the endpoints.
This study relies on the central limit theorem satisfied by
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}

and we will show vague convergence, under suitable renormalization, to an infinite measure on
(0,1)
,
whose density is the derivative of the quantile function of a standard normal distribution.
The exact scaling is however sensitive to the case considered.
Finally, in Section
4.2
, we investigate more precisely the behavior of the
empirical distribution near the endpoints,
by studying for example the number of break points of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
in intervals of the type
[0,x^{n}]
for
0<x<1
.
This behavior turns out to be related to the large deviation principle for the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

2
Representation of the break points

Set

I=\{(n,k):n\geq 1,\,1\leq k\leq n\}

and fix

{\bm{p}}=(p_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)

with
p_{n,k}\in[0,1]
for all
n
and
k
.
Let
(U_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
be a family of independent random variables, all uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
.
Set

y_{n,k}=1_{\{U_{n,k}\leq p_{n,k}\}}

so that
y_{n,k}
is a Bernoulli random variable with success probability
p_{n,k}
.
Write
\Omega_{0}
for the set of sequences
(y_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
with
y_{n,k}\in\{0,1\}
for all
n
and
k
.
Denote by
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
the law of
(y_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
on
\Omega_{0}
.
Define a random process
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\Omega_{0}
by setting
x_{0}=0
and then, recursively for
n\geq 1
,

x_{n}=x_{n-1}+y_{n,x_{n-1}+1}.

(5)

Note that
x_{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
for all
n
and
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain under
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
.
The following proposition provides a useful formula for the break points of the deterministic split-merge process
with splitting proportions
{\bm{p}}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
(n,k)\in I
, we have

a_{n,k}=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}\leq k-1).

Proof.

Define for
n\geq 0
and
0\leq k\leq n+1

\alpha_{n,k}=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}\leq k-1).

Then
\alpha_{n,0}=0
and
\alpha_{n,n+1}=1
for all
n
.
For
(n,k)\in I
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}\leq k-1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n-1}\leq k-2)+\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n-1}=k-1,y_{n,k}=0)

\displaystyle=p_{n,k}\alpha_{n-1,k-1}+(1-p_{n,k})\alpha_{n-1,k}.

The same recursion (
1
) defines
a_{n,k}
so
\alpha_{n,k}=a_{n,k}
for all
n
and
k
.
∎

We turn now to the more general case where the splitting proportions
{\bm{P}}
are random.
By augmenting our probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
if necessary,
we can assume that
\Omega
supports a family of independent random variables
(U_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
as above,
which is moreover independent of
{\bm{P}}
.
Set

Y_{n,k}=1_{\{U_{n,k}\leq P_{n,k}\}}

and define a random process
X=(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\Omega
by setting
X_{0}=0
and then, recursively for
n\geq 1
,

X_{n}=X_{n-1}+Y_{n,X_{n-1}+1}.

Note that if
\Phi
is a bounded measurable function on
(\mathbb{Z}^{+})^{\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
and
\phi:[0,1]^{I}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\phi({\bm{p}})=\mathbb{E}^{\bm{p}}(\Phi(x)),

where
x=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is the time-inhomogeneous Markov chain defined at (
5
),
then
\phi
is measurable and we have,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

\mathbb{E}(\Phi(X)|{\bm{P}})=\phi({\bm{P}}).

(6)

It will be convenient sometimes to regard (
1
) and (
4
)
as defining measurable functions
a_{n,k}({\bm{p}})
and
g_{n}({\bm{p}})
of
{\bm{p}}
, so we can write

A_{n,k}=a_{n,k}({\bm{P}}),\quad G_{n}=g_{n}({\bm{P}}).

Note that, on taking
\Phi(x)=1_{\{x_{n}\leq k-1\}}
, we have
\phi({\bm{p}})=a_{n,k}({\bm{p}})
by Proposition
2.1
.
Hence, the random break points
(A_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
associated with the random splitting proportions
{\bm{P}}
satisfy,
almost surely,

A_{n,k}=a_{n,k}({\bm{P}})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\leq k-1|{\bm{P}})

We remark on two special cases where
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
takes a simple form.

•

In the case where
p_{n,k}\equiv p\in[0,1]
for all
n
and
k
,
which is a special case of the deterministic stratified case,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a simple (biased) random walk.
In particular
X_{n}
has the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
.

•

In the fully random case, the process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Random Walk in Dynamic Random Environment (RWDRE).
See for example
[
14
]
.
We will be interested not only in its annealed distribution, which is a simple random walk,
but also in its quenched distribution, conditional on the splitting proportions
{\bm{P}}
.

3
Weak convergence of the empirical distributions

Our first use of the representation of the partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
developed in the previous section shows that,
perhaps somewhat unexpectedly,
under quite general conditions,
the sequence of empirical distributions of break points
(g_{n})_{n\geq 0}
(or
(G_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in the random case)
has a weak limit supported on
\{0,1\}
.
This is the content of the main result of this section, Theorem
3.2
below.

We start with a key lemma stating that this weak convergence is a consequence of
a law of large numbers for the auxiliary walk
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix a family of splitting proportions
{\bm{p}}=(p_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)

and consider the associated random process
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined on
\Omega_{0}
by (
5
).
Assume that, for some
\bar{p}\in[0,1]
, under
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
, as
n\to\infty
,

x_{n}/n\to\bar{p}\quad\text{in probability}.

(7)

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

g_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{p}\delta_{0}+(1-\bar{p})\delta_{1}\quad\text{weakly on $[0,1]$}.

Proof.

It follows from the assumption that, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, by Proposition
2.1
,

a_{n,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}\leq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1)\to\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\alpha<\bar{p},\\
1&\text{if }\alpha>\bar{p},\\
\end{cases}

and so
g_{n}([0,x])\to\bar{p}
for all
x\in(0,1)
, which implies the claimed weak convergence.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

(a)

Deterministic stratified case
.
Assume that the averages
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}
converge to some limit
\bar{p}
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

g_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{p}\delta_{0}+(1-\bar{p})\delta_{1}\quad\text{weakly on $[0,1]$}.

(b)

Random stratified and fully random cases
.
Define
\bar{p}=\mathbb{E}(P_{1})
or
\bar{p}=\mathbb{E}(P_{1,1})
according to the case in hand.
Then, almost surely, as
n\to\infty
,

G_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{p}\delta_{0}+(1-\bar{p})\delta_{1}\quad\text{weakly on $[0,1]$}.

Proof.

For part (a), under
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
, the process
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables,
with success probabilities
p_{1},p_{2},\dots
.
Then, by Hoeffding’s inequality, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{x_{n}}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}}}\right|\geq\varepsilon}}\right)\leq 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-2n\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}.

Hence
x_{n}/n\to\bar{p}
in probability and Lemma
3.1
applies.

For part (b), note that, in both cases,
the process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a random walk with mean step size
\bar{p}
,
so
X_{n}/n\to\bar{p}
almost surely by the strong law of large numbers.
Set

\Phi(x)=1_{\{x_{n}/n\to\bar{p}\text{ as }n\to\infty\}}

then

\phi({\bm{p}})=\mathbb{E}^{\bm{p}}(\Phi(x))=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}/n\to\bar{p}\text{ as }n\to\infty).

By (
6
), almost surely,

\phi({\bm{P}})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}/n\to\bar{p}\text{ as }n\to\infty|{\bm{P}}).

Hence
\phi({\bm{P}})=1
almost surely.
Consider the event
\Omega^{*}=\{{\bm{P}}\in B\}
, where

B=\{{\bm{p}}:x_{n}/n\to\bar{p}\text{ in probability as $n\to\infty$ under $\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}$}\}.

By Lemma
3.1
, for
{\bm{p}}\in B
, we have
g_{n}({\bm{p}})\Rightarrow\bar{p}\delta_{0}+(1-\bar{p})\delta_{1}
weakly as
n\to\infty
.
Then, since convergence almost surely implies convergence in probability,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{*})\geq\mathbb{P}(\phi({\bm{P}})=1)=1

and on
\Omega^{*}
we have

G_{n}=g_{n}({\bm{P}})\Rightarrow\bar{p}\delta_{0}+(1-\bar{p})\delta_{1}\quad,

weakly as
n\to\infty
.
∎

4
Taking a closer look

The main result of the previous section, Theorem
3.2
, shows that, under general conditions,
the empirical probability distribution of break points
G_{n}
, defined at (
4
), converges weakly on
[0,1]

(on an event of probability 1 in the random cases) to a limit supported by the endpoints of the interval.
That is, most of the break points
A_{n,k}
‘move’ either to 0 or to 1 as the split-merge dynamics proceeds.
Still, it is clear that this result does not provide a completely satisfactory picture of the distribution of the break points,
even for large
n
.
For example, as
n\to\infty
, there will be more and more break points away from the endpoints of the interval.
How are these points distributed?
Furthermore, even though most of the mass of
G_{n}
moves towards the endpoints, how fast does this movement happen?
We address these questions in this section.

4.1
Away from the endpoints

The results in this subsection will be expressed in terms of the quantile function

Q=\Phi^{-1}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}

associated with the standard normal cumulative distribution function
\Phi
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix a family of splitting proportions
{\bm{p}}=(p_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)

and write
(g_{n})_{n\geq 0}
for the associated sequence of empirical distributions of the break points.
Consider the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
on
\Omega_{0}
defined in Section
2
and the random process
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\Omega_{0}
defined at (
5
).
Assume that there exist sequences
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}
and
(\sigma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
(0,\infty)
,
such that, as
n\to\infty
, we have
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
and, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x_{n}-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq t}}\right)\to\Phi(t).

(8)

Then, for all
x,y\in(0,1)
with
x\leq y
, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{ng_{n}([x,y])}{\sigma_{n}}\to Q(y)-Q(x).

That is, the sequence
(ng_{n}/\sigma_{n})
of Radon measures on
(0,1)

converges vaguely on that space to the Radon measure with density
Q^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

For
x\in(0,1)
, define

k_{n}(x)=\min\{k\in\{0,1,\dots,n+1\}:a_{n,k}\geq x\}.

Then

\frac{ng_{n}([0,x))}{\sigma_{n}}=\frac{k_{n}(x)}{\sigma_{n}}.

(9)

We now focus on estimating the asymptotic behavior of
k_{n}(x)
.

By Proposition
2.1
, we have

a_{n,k_{n}(x)}=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{n}\leq k_{n}(x)-1}}\right)=\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x_{n}-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq\frac{k_{n}(x)-1-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}}}\right).

Moreover, the convergence in (
8
) actually holds uniformly in
t\in\mathbb{R}
by Pólya’s theorem
(pointwise convergence of a sequence of bounded non-decreasing functions on
\mathbb{R}
towards a continuous function implies uniform convergence).
So we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a_{n,k_{n}(x)}-\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{k_{n}(x)-1-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}}}\right)}}\right\rvert\to 0.

(10)

On the other hand, by definition of
k_{n}(x)
, we have
x\leq a_{n,k_{n}(x)}\leq x+\ell_{n}
,
where
\ell_{n}=\max_{i=0}^{n}(a_{n,i+1}-a_{n,i})
is the length of the longest interval in the partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Moreover, by Proposition
2.1
again

\displaystyle a_{n,i+1}-a_{n,i}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}}}\right)-\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{i-1-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+2\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{X_{n}-m_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq t}}\right)-\Phi(t)}}\right\rvert.

(11)

Using (
8
) and that
\Phi
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
together with

∎

We now state and prove convergence results for our three main cases of our interval split-merge dynamics.
The first result addresses the deterministic stratified case.
Note that the assumptions of this result may hold even when the assumption in part (a) of Theorem
3.2
does not hold.
Recall that we write

for the quantile function of the standard normal distribution.

Theorem 4.3
.

Deterministic stratified case:

Set

s_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}(1-p_{k})

and assume that
s_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Then the sequence
(ng_{n}/\sqrt{s_{n}})
of Radon measures on
(0,1)

converges vaguely on that space to the Radon measure with density
Q^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

In this case, under
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
,

is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables with success probabilities

,
so (
8
) holds by Lindeberg’s central limit theorem, with

m_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k},\quad\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{s_{n}}.

Hence the result follows from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

We obtain as a corollary the following result for the random stratified case.
This can alternatively be shown directly from Lemma
4.1
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Random stratified case:

Set

s=\mathbb{E}(P_{1}(1-P_{1}))

and assume that
\mathbb{P}(P_{1}\in(0,1))>0
.
Then, almost surely, the sequence
(\sqrt{n}G_{n}/\sqrt{s})
of Radon measures on
(0,1)

converges vaguely on that space to the Radon measure with density
Q^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

Condition on the stratified family of splitting proportions
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and write

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n}G_{n}}{\sqrt{s}}=\frac{nG_{n}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}P_{k}(1-P_{k})}}\times\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}P_{k}(1-P_{k})}{ns}}.

Theorem
4.3
applies to the first factor on the right,
while the second factor tends to

almost surely by the strong law of large numbers.
∎

Our final result in this subsection describes what happens in the case of fully random case.

Theorem 4.5
.

Fully random case:

Set

\bar{p}=\mathbb{E}(P_{1,1}),\quad s=\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})

and assume that
\mathbb{P}(P_{1,1}\in(0,1))>0
.
Then, almost surely, the sequence
(\sqrt{n}G_{n}/\sqrt{s})

(0,1)

converges vaguely on that space to the Radon measure with density
Q^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

Set

and consider the event
\Omega^{*}=\{{\bm{P}}\in B\}
, where

B=\{{\bm{p}}:\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}((x_{n}-m_{n})/\sigma_{n}\leq t)\to\Phi(t)\text{ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}\}.

This event can be rewritten as follows

\Omega^{*}=\{\mathbb{P}((X_{n}-m_{n})/\sigma_{n}\leq t|{\bm{P}})\to\Phi(t)\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{Q}\}.

Therefore, by the almost sure central limit theorem for the RWDRE, which has been proved in
[

]
, we have

.
In particular, the assumption

, on

\frac{\sqrt{n}G_{n}([x,y])}{\sqrt{s}}=\frac{ng_{n}({\bm{P}})([x,y])}{\sigma_{n}}\to Q(y)-Q(x),

∎

Remark 4.6
.

The limits in Theorem

Comparing with Theorems

depends on other factors.
For example:

•

for all

becomes

;

•

to be uniformly distributed on

becomes

•

4.2
Near the endpoints

We now look more closely at how the mass of the empirical distribution of break points
moves towards the endpoints of the interval
[0,1]
.
We make statements for the behavior close to 0.
Analogous statements near 1 can be obtained by flipping the interval.

Consider the transformed empirical distributions
\tilde{g}_{n}
defined by

\tilde{g}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\tilde{a}_{n,k}},\quad\tilde{a}_{n,k}=a_{n,k}^{1/n}.

As usual, we will change to upper-case and write
\tilde{G}_{n}
when the splitting proportions are random.
Note that
\tilde{g}_{n}
is simply the pushforward of
g_{n}
by
x\mapsto x^{1/n}
and the following relation holds, for
x,y\in[0,1]
with
x\leq y
,

\tilde{g}_{n}([x,y])=g_{n}([x^{n},y^{n}]).

We start with a lemma relating the limiting behavior of
\tilde{g}_{n}
to large deviation estimates on the lower tail
for the associated random walk.

Lemma 4.7
.

Fix a family of splitting proportions
{\bm{p}}=(p_{n,k}:(n,k)\in I)
.
Consider the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}
on
\Omega_{0}
defined in Section
2
and the
random process
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\Omega_{0}
defined at (
5
).
Assume that there exists
\bar{p}\in(0,1]
and a function
I:(0,\bar{p}]\to[0,\infty)
,
continuous and decreasing with
I(\bar{p})=0
, and such that, for all
\alpha\in(0,\bar{p})
,

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{n}\leq\alpha n}}\right)\to-I(\alpha),\quad\text{ as $n\to\infty$}.

(16)

Define
I(0)\in(0,\infty]
and
x_{*}\in[0,1)
by

I(0)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}I(\alpha),\quad x_{*}=e^{-I(0)}.

For
x\in[x_{*},1]
, let
\alpha_{I}(x)
be the unique solution
\alpha\in[0,\bar{p}]
of the equation
I(\alpha)=\log(1/x)
.
Write
\tilde{g}
for the probability measure on
[0,1]
with distribution given by

\tilde{g}([0,x])=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x\in[0,x_{*}],\\
\alpha_{I}(x),&\text{if }x\in(x_{*},1).\end{cases}

(17)

Then, as
n\to\infty
, we have

\tilde{g}_{n}\Rightarrow\tilde{g}\quad\text{weakly on $[0,1]$}.

Remark 4.8
.

Under the assumptions of the lemma, the map
I:[0,\bar{p}]\to[0,\log(1/x_{*})]
is one-to-one,
which implies that
\alpha_{I}(x)
is well-defined for
x\in[x_{*},1]
.
Moreover,
\alpha_{I}
is continuous and increasing on
[x_{*},1]
with
\alpha_{I}(x_{*})=0
and
\alpha_{I}(1)=\bar{p}
.
Hence
x\mapsto\tilde{g}([0,x])
is continuous on
[0,1)
, so
g
is atomless on
[0,1)
, and

\tilde{g}(\{1\})=1-\bar{p}.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{g}_{n}
and
\tilde{g}
are supported on
[0,1]
, and
\alpha_{I}
is continuous on
[x_{*},1]
with
\alpha_{I}(x_{*})=0
,
it will suffice to show that
\tilde{g}_{n}([0,x])\to\alpha_{I}(x)
for all
x\in(x_{*},1)
.
Fix
x\in(x_{*},1)
and note that

\tilde{g}_{n}([0,x])=\frac{1}{n}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{k\geq 0:a_{n,k}^{1/n}\leq x}}\right\}.

We use Proposition
2.1
and (
16
), together with continuity of
I
, to see that,
for all
\alpha\in(0,\bar{p})
, as
n\to\infty
,

\log a_{n,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}^{1/n}=\frac{1}{n}\log a_{n,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}=\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}^{\bm{p}}(x_{n}\leq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1)\to-I(\alpha)

so

a_{n,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}^{1/n}\to e^{-I(\alpha)}.

This implies the desired limit for
\tilde{g}_{n}([0,x])
.
∎

We now apply this lemma to the case of deterministic stratified case.

Theorem 4.9
.

Deterministic stratified case:

Assume that, for some probability measure
H
on
[0,1]
with
H(\{0,1\})<1
,
the following weak limit holds on
[0,1]
as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{p_{k}}\Rightarrow H.

Set

\bar{p}=\int_{0}^{1}t\,H(dt).

\bar{p}\in(0,1)
and, for all
\alpha\in(0,\bar{p})
, there is a unique

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{te^{\theta}}{1-t+te^{\theta}}\,H(dt)=\alpha.

Define, for
\alpha\in(0,\bar{p})

I(\alpha)=\alpha\theta(\alpha)-\int_{0}^{1}\log\bigl(1-t+te^{\theta(\alpha)}\bigr)\,H(dt).

Then

is continuous and decreasing on
(0,\bar{p})

.
Moreover, in the limit

\tilde{g}_{n}\Rightarrow\tilde{g}\quad\text{ weakly on $[0,1]$}

is defined as in Lemma

.

Remark 4.10
.

The hypothesis (

converges to

Moreover (

Remark 4.11
.

It is straightforward to check from (

\theta(\alpha)\to-\infty
and
\alpha\theta(\alpha)\to 0
, so

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}I(\alpha)=-\int_{0}^{1}\log(1-t)\,H(dt).

Hence, in the notation of Lemma

, we have

x_{*}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\log(1-t)\,H(dt)}}\right\}\in[0,1-\bar{p}].

(20)

Proof.

We check the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
.
Note that, for

\frac{1}{n}\Lambda_{n}(n\theta)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{1}\log\bigl(1-x+xe^{\theta}\bigr)\,H(dx)=:\Lambda(\theta).

Since

,
it follows from the Gärtner-Ellis theorem (see e.g. Theorem 2.3.6 in

)
that, under

satisfies a large deviation principle with speed

and rate function

\Lambda^{*}(\alpha)=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta\alpha-\int_{0}^{1}\log\bigl(1-x+xe^{\theta}\bigr)\,H(dx)}}\right)

In particular,
the function

If
\alpha\in[0,\bar{p}]
, then the supremum in the definition of

and satisfies

∎

Consider the fully random case with splitting proportions

Remark 4.14
.

The limit distribution
\tilde{g}
in Theorem

.
Since a full description of the rate function

is not available, neither is a full description of

Nonetheless, some information is available in

]

Under
\mathbb{P}

So
(X_{n}/n)_{n\geq 1}
satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function

and that the two functions share the same unique global minimum, at the point

Let

Define a probability law supported by

F_{a}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{a}(x)}{\bar{p}},\ x\in[x_{*,a},1]

\alpha_{a}(x)

.
