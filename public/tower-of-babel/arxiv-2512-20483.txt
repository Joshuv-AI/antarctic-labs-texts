1.
Introduction

The study of central values of
L
-functions is a classical theme in number theory. For many problems in number theory, the size of the central values is of particular importance and the general theme of bounding these
L
-values is commonly referred to as the
subconvexity
problem. More precisely, given an
L
-function
L(s,\pi)
satisfying analytic continuation and a functional equation, one expects:

L(1/2,\pi)\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{C}(\pi)^{1/4-\delta+\varepsilon},

for some
\delta>0.
Here
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
is called the analytic conductor of the
L
-function (see
[
12
, Chapter 5]
for a thorough treatment).
The trivial bound corresponding to
\delta=0
is called the convexity bound and can be achieved by applying the Phragmén-Lindelöf principle.
A
subconvexity
bound is an improvement of the trivial bound corresponding to some
\delta>0
. The best possible bound corresponds to
\delta=\frac{1}{4}
; this is known as the Lindelöf Hypothesis and follows in the cases where
L(s,\pi)
satisfies the Riemann Hypothesis. Subconvexity bounds have far-reaching applications to deep arithmetic problems, including the Quantum Unique Ergodicity conjecture and the equidistribution of Heegener points (see e.g.,
[
26
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
20
, Lecture 5]
).

This paper investigates the subconvexity problem for
L
-functions associated to modular forms of half-integral weight. These
L
-functions do not admit Euler products and are known to not satisfy the Riemann Hypothesis (see
[
31
]
).

There are examples of Dirichlet series without an Euler product that fail to be subconvex at the central point (see
[
6
]
). This would suggest that the Euler product may be essential for subconvexity. However, for automorphic
L
-functions attached to modular forms of integral weight, the Euler product arises from the fact that the underlying form is a “simultaneous eigenfunction” for the Hecke operators. Jeffrey Hoffstein informally conjectured at Oberwolfach in 2011 that such a “simultaneous eigenfunction” property is crucial for implying a Lindelöf Hypothesis. Although half-integral weight forms lack an Euler product, they still have Hecke operators; thus, one might expect that they satisfy the Lindelöf Hypothesis. In
[
16
]
, Kiral studied the subconvexity problems for
L
-functions associated to half-integral weight eigenforms twisted by a character in the conductor aspect, and a corresponding second moment was later derived in
[
8
]
. Subconvexity for other
L
-functions without Euler products has also been successfully investigated, notably by Blomer in
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
.
In this paper, we study the subconvexity problem for
L
-functions associated with half-integral weight modular forms in the weight aspect. In the elliptic modular form case, the subconvexity problem in the weight aspect has been extensively studied (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
and
[
32
]
). In contrast, no subconvexity bound in the weight aspect is known for
L
-functions associated with half-integral weight modular forms as the classical method requires the Euler product.

The goal of this paper is to establish the first such result. Instead of the classical method, we utilize the relative trace formula method to derive a second moment formula for central values of half-integral weight modular forms. This can be regarded as an average Lindelöf result in the weight aspect. As applications, we establish the desired subconvexity bound as well as a simultaneous non-vanishing result for the central values in the weight aspect.

In Corollary

Remark 1.3
.

When

we obtain a stronger asymptotic formula for the second moment, which is Proposition

.

Let
f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)
be a Hecke eigenform in the Kohnen plus space. Then it has a Fourier expansion

(1.1)

f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}e(nz).

We can normalize
f
such that
a_{f}(n)
are always real. Let
\Lambda_{f}(n)
be the
n
-th Hecke eigenvalue of
f
so that
T_{n}f=\Lambda_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}f
. We normalize
f
such that, for any fundamental discriminant
D
satisfying
(-1)^{\kappa-\frac{1}{2}}D>0,

(1.2)

a_{f}(|D|)^{2}=\pi^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},F\times\chi_{D}\right),

where
F:=F_{f}\in H_{2\kappa-1}(1)
is the Shimura correspondent of

, which is an integral-weight

. The normalization is well-defined by the Kohnen-Zagier formula
[

, Theorem 1]
.

Let
f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)
be a Hecke eigenform in the Kohnen plus space. The analytic conductor of
f
is of size
\kappa^{2}.
By the amplification method, we prove the following subconvexity result:

Theorem B
.

Let
f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)
satisfying the normalization (
1.2
). Then for any
\varepsilon>0,

L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}(\kappa^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{40}+\varepsilon}.

This result establishes the first subconvexity result for half-integral weight modular forms in the weight aspect. From the proof of Theorem

, this subconvexity bound is true for all eigenforms

Applying Hölder’s inequality, we establish the following simultaneous nonvanishing result for the central values:

Theorem C
.

Let
D
be a fixed fundamental discriminant satisfying
(-1)^{\kappa-\frac{1}{2}}D>0.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0,

\#\{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4):L(1/2,f)L(1/2,F\times\chi_{D})\neq 0\}\gg\frac{\kappa}{(\log\kappa)^{3+\varepsilon}}.

Prior to Theorem

, only the existence of half integral Hecke eigenforms with nonvanishing central values was known (see
[
24
,
14
]
). Our result provides the first quantitative lower bound. Additionally, this implies that, for
D
a fundamental discriminant satisfying
(-1)^{\kappa-\frac{1}{2}}D>0,
the number of integral weight eigenforms
F\in H_{2\kappa-1}(1)
for which
L(1/2,F\times\chi_{D})\neq 0
is at least of order
\frac{\kappa}{(\log\kappa)^{3+\varepsilon}}
.

1.2.
Strategy of the Proof

In the integral weight set up, the eigenvalue-weighted second moment, which is known as Kuznetsov’s identity (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
), has been extensively studied. The main idea is to apply the Hecke relations to produce divisor functions, and subsequently apply the Petersson trace formula and the divisor-type Voronoi summation formula. For the details in this classical set up, one can refer to
[
1
, Section 4]
.

This method is difficult to adapt to the half-integral weight setting because the associated
L
-functions lack Euler products and the corresponding multiplicative relations. Consequently, divisor functions do not emerge naturally, and the classical method fails. In this paper, we instead employ the "relative trace formula" (RTF) to establish a half-integral weight Kuznetsov’s identity, which is Theorem
A
.

The RTF method has been extensively studied in analytic number theory (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
9
,
30
]
). It provides an effective tool for bypassing certain intermediate steps in the classical derivation of Kuznetsov’s identity. The main idea of the RTF method is to integrate the pre-trace formula (see, e.g.,
[
33
, Theorem 1]
) along “proper subgroups.” In our setting, the proper subgroup corresponds to the diagonal group in
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}
, or, classically, the positive imaginary axis. Integrating the spectral side of the pre-trace formula–often referred to as the
spectral side
of the RTF–yields the desired second moment. Consequently, it suffices to analyze the integral of the geometric side, commonly called the
geometric side
of the RTF. By applying the Bruhat decomposition, one finds that the geometric side decomposes into a sum of integrals, known as orbital integrals. A key difference between the representation-theoretic RTF and our approach arises in the half-integral weight setting: here, the orbital integrals cannot generally be expressed as products of local orbital integrals. Nevertheless, one can employ a classical version of the RTF, previously studied in the integral weight case in
[
29
]
.

We give a brief overview of the proof focusing on the essential details. Notice that the
L
-functions of half-integral weight modular forms can be defined by the Mellin transformation (
1.4
). This motivates us to consider the following double integral:

J(\mathbf{s},n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{(T_{n}f)(iy_{1})\overline{f(iy_{2})}}{\|f\|^{2}}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}},

where
n
is an odd square integer, and
T_{n}
is the half-integral weight Hecke operator. Then by (
1.4
),
J(\textbf{0},n)
is the left hand side of Theorem
A
. Similar to
[
33
]
, we can deduce the reproducing kernel for the space
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
, which implies:

J(\mathbf{s},n)=C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in G_{4}(n)}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

Here
G_{4}(n)=\Gamma_{0}(4)\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&n\end{pmatrix}\Gamma_{0}(4)
and
C_{\kappa}
and
R_{\gamma}(z,z^{\prime})
are defined in §
2.3
. Depending on if
\gamma\in G_{4}(n)
is a triangular matrix or not, we write
G_{4}(n)
as a disjoint union and hence express
J(\mathbf{s},n)
as the sum of singular orbital integrals
J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{s},n)
and regular orbital integrals
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\mathbf{s},n)
.

The singular orbital integral can be computed directly and will contribute the main term (Proposition
3.11
). For the regular orbital integral, we write them as sums of hypergeometric functions, which will later be transformed to Legendre functions. The upper bounds of the Legendre functions in different regions will imply that the regular orbital integrals contribute the error term (Proposition
4.7
).

For Theorem
B
, we apply the amplification method, which was established in
[
11
]
. Here we use the version in
[
28
, Section 11.2.2]
. Instead of using the Fourier coefficients of half-integral weight modular forms, we utilized the Fourier coefficients of their Shimura correspondents as the amplifier. This new amplifier works as the Shimura correspondent is Hecke equivariant, which can also be regarded as an evidence for Hoffstein’s “simultaneous eigenfunction” philosophy.

For Theorem
C
, we apply Hölder’s inequality for the first moment. Then Theorem
A
will yield the desired result. Notice that for Theorem
C
, we only need
n=1,
and Proposition
5.1
provides a better error term in this case.

Finally, we outline the organization of the paper:

In §
2
, we do the preparation work. In §
2.2
, we briefly introduce the Hecke theory of half-integral weight modular forms. In §
2.3
, we prove Proposition
2.6
, which can be regarded as a Zagier’s kernel function in the half-integral weight set up. Then we set up the integral
J(\mathbf{s},n)
and express it as the summation of singular orbital integrals
J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\mathbf{s},n)
and the regular orbital integrals
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\mathbf{s},n).

•

In §
3
, we study the singular orbital integrals
J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\mathbf{s},n)
, which will be written as the summation of small cell orbital integral
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\mathbf{s},n)
and the dual orbital integral
J_{\operatorname{Dual}}(\mathbf{s},n).
They will be treated separately in §
3.1
and §
3.2
. In §

, we combine the small cell orbital integral and the dual orbital integral to deduce Proposition
3.11
. We also give the estimation of the singular orbital integral, which is Corollary
3.12
.

•

which is a summation of three types of regular orbital integrals. We study them separately in §
4.1
, §
4.2
and §

. The explicit regular orbital integral

1.3.
Notation Guide

1.3.1.
The half-integral weight modular forms

Denote by
\Gamma_{0}(4)
the Hecke congruence subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of level
4.
Here we introduce the half-integral weight automorphic forms on
\Gamma_{0}(4)\backslash\mathbb{H}
. For any
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
, we set

j_{\gamma}(z)=\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)\varepsilon_{d}^{-1}\sqrt{cz+d},

where we take the principal branch of the square-root,
\textstyle{\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)}
is the Jacobi symbol with the stipulations
\textstyle{\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)}=\textstyle{\left(\frac{-c}{-d}\right)}
and
\textstyle{\left(\frac{0}{d}\right)}=1
, and
\varepsilon_{d}=1,i
depending on whether
d\equiv 1,3\ \left(\text{mod }4\right)
, respectively. Moreover, we set
j_{\gamma}(z)=j_{-\gamma}(z)
if
d<0.
This makes
j_{\gamma}(z)
into the theta multiplier. We also set

j(\gamma,z)=cz+d.

Then
|j_{\gamma}(z)|^{2}=|j(\gamma,z)|
. Let
\kappa
be a positive half-integer. A holomorphic form of half-integral weight
\kappa
is a holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

(1.3)

f(\gamma z)=j_{\gamma}(z)^{2\kappa}f(z),

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
, and such that
f(\alpha z)\ll 1
as
y\to\infty
for all
\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
. We say
f(z)
is a cusp form if the last condition can be replaced by
f(\alpha z)\to 0
. Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
the vector space of holomorphic cusp forms of weight
\kappa.
This is a Euclidean space endowed with the Petersson inner product. That is, for

f,g\in\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
their inner product is given by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Gamma_{0}(4)\backslash\mathbb{H}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}y^{\kappa}\,d\mu(z).

1.3.2.
The
L
-functions

For any
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
, we associate the
L
-function

L(s,f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{f}(n)}{n^{s}}.

This Dirichlet series is absolutely convergent when
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
. Moreover,
L(s,f)
admits analytic continuation to

and can be expressed via the following Mellin transform:

(1.4)

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(iy)y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+s}\frac{\,dy}{y}=(2\pi)^{-(s+\frac{\kappa}{2})}\Gamma\left(s+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)L\left(\frac{1}{2}+s,f\right).

1.3.5.
The upper and lower triangular matrices

Fix an odd square integer

. Throughout we will consider upper and lower triangular matrices of the form

\gamma^{a,d,m}=\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\

We have the following results for the complex power function:

.

Let

2.2.

In this section, we introduce the Hecke operators on

M_{4}^{+}(n):=\left\{\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\

Notice that
M_{4}^{+}(1)=\Gamma_{0}(4).
The following proposition allows us to extend the definition of

\Gamma_{0}(4)

For any
\gamma\in M_{4}^{+}
, there exists a function
j_{\gamma}(z)
such that the following properties hold:

•

Cocycle condition:

(2.1)

j_{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}(z)=j_{\gamma_{1}}(\gamma_{2}z)j_{\gamma_{2}}(z).

•

Compatibility condition: if
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4),
then

(2.2)

j_{\gamma}(z)=\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)\varepsilon_{d}^{-1}\sqrt{cz+d}.

•

Modulus condition: for
\gamma\in M_{4}^{+},

(2.3)

j_{\gamma}(z)=t_{\gamma}\sqrt{cz+d},

with
t_{\gamma}\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
so that
|t_{\gamma}|=1
. Moreover,
t_{\gamma}
is independent of the choice of
z.

Remark 2.3
.

Our choice of
j_{\gamma}(z)
differs by a factor of
\det(\gamma)^{1/4}
from that in
[
27
]
.

Let
n
be an odd integer. The following two lemmas will be used to prove a coset decomposition of the form

(2.4)

G_{4}(n):=\Gamma_{0}(4)\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&n\end{pmatrix}\Gamma_{0}(4)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma_{0}(4)\gamma_{j},

for some matrices
\gamma_{j}\in M_{4}^{+}
and
r\geq 1
. This coset decomposition is essential as it allows us to define the Hecke operators. Notice that
G_{4}(n)
is not the same as
M_{4}^{+}(n)
and
G_{4}(1)=\Gamma_{0}(4)
. We first prove the following useful lemma:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. Let
\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4}^{+}(n)
be such that
\gcd(a,d,m)=1.
Then we can find
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
satisfying

\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}=\gamma_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&ad\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{2}.

Moreover,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
satisfy (
2.5
).

Proof.

Recall from §
1.3.5
that
g_{1}=\gcd(m,d)
, so we can find
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
mx+dy=g_{1}.
We claim that
x
may be assumed to be a positive prime satisfying
\gcd(x,d)=1
and
x\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4).
We will assume this condition on
x
for now and delay its proof to the end. Since
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
and
ad=n
is odd, we have
\gcd(ax,4g_{1})=1
(notice that
\gcd(x,d)=1
implies
(x,g_{1})=1
). Then we can find integers
s
and
r
such that
axs-4g_{1}r=1.
Now let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
be the matrices

\gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}as-4rm&-y\\
-4dr&x\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}ax&g_{1}\\
4r&s\end{pmatrix},

and note that

(2.5)

\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}=\gamma_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&ad\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}as-4rm&-y\\
-4dr&x\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&ad\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}ax&g_{1}\\
4r&s\end{pmatrix},

as desired.

Finally, we prove the claim on the conditions we assumed that
x
satisfied. Our
x
and
y
from above satisfy
\frac{m}{g_{1}}x+\frac{d}{g_{1}}y=1.
Let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be a solution to
\frac{m}{g_{1}}x+\frac{d}{g_{1}}y=1.
Then
\gcd\left(x_{0},\frac{d}{g_{1}}\right)=1
. By elementary number theory,
x=x_{0}+t\frac{d}{g_{1}}
and
y=y_{0}-t\frac{m}{g_{1}}
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}
are all the solutions of the linear equation
\frac{m}{g_{1}}x+\frac{d}{g_{1}}y=1.
As
\gcd\left(x_{0},\frac{d}{g_{1}}\right)=1
, Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions implies that there are infinitely many
t
such that
x=x_{0}+t\frac{d}{g_{1}}
is prime. Then we can choose large enough
t
such that
x>0
,
\gcd(x,d)=1
, and
x\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
.
∎

We can use Lemma
2.4
to furnish different decompositions of
G_{4}(n):

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. Then the following sets of matrices are the same:

G_{4}(n)=\Gamma_{0}(4)\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&n\end{pmatrix}\Gamma_{0}(4),

G_{4}^{\prime}(n)=M_{4}^{+}(n)\bigcap\left\{\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4}^{+}:\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\right\},

and

G_{4}^{\prime\prime}(n)=\bigsqcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n\\
m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d),\\
\gcd(a,d,m)=1\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{0}(4)\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

To prove this, it suffices to show the following chain of inclusions
G_{4}(n)\subseteq G_{4}^{\prime}(n)\subseteq G_{4}^{\prime\prime}(n)\subseteq G_{4}(n)
.

We begin by showing that
G_{4}(n)\subseteq G_{4}^{\prime}(n)
. Take
\gamma\in G_{4}(n).
Then we can find
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
such that

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ax+bzn&ay+bwn\\
cx+dzn&cy+dwn\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4}^{+}(n).

Assume that
p|\gcd(ax+bzn,ay+bwn,cx+dzn,cy+dwn).
Then
p
divides the determinant of
\gamma
and hence
p|n
, which implies
p|\gcd(ax,ay,cx,cy).
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
p|a
and
p\nmid c
, as
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
has determinant
1
. This forces
p|\gcd(x,y)
which contradicts that
\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
having determinant
1
. Therefore
\gcd(ax+bzn,ay+bwn,cx+dzn,cy+dwn)=1
as desired.

Now we show that
G_{4}^{\prime}(n)\subseteq G_{4}^{\prime\prime}(n)
. This is obvious upon noting that

M_{4}^{+}(n)=\bigsqcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{0}(4)\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, adding the
\gcd
condition makes it clear that
G_{4}^{\prime}(n)\subseteq G_{4}^{\prime\prime}(n)
.

Finally, we shall show that
G_{4}^{\prime\prime}(n)\subseteq G_{4}(n)
. It suffices to show that if
\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}
satisfies
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
then we can find
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
such that
\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}=\gamma_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&ad\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{2}.
This is exactly what was proved in Lemma
2.4
.
∎

With the decomposition in (
2.4
) in hand, we define the Hecke operator
T_{n}
by

T_{n}f(z)=n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}j_{\gamma_{j}}(z)^{-2\kappa}f(\gamma_{j}z).

When
n
is not a square number, Shimura
[
27
, Page 450]
showed that the Hecke operator vanishes.

Similar to the integral weight Hecke theory, we can find a Hecke orthogonal basis
H_{\kappa}(4)
. In
[
27
,
21
]
, a correspondence between
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
and
\mathcal{S}_{2\kappa-1}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
is given, which is now known as the Shimura correspondence. For
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
, denote by
F:=F_{f}\in\mathcal{S}_{2\kappa-1}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
the Shimura correspondent of
f.
The Shimura correspondence sends a Hecke eigenform of
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
to that of
\mathcal{S}_{2\kappa-1}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
. We write
\widetilde{T}_{n}
for the
n
-th Hecke operator on
\mathcal{S}_{2\kappa-1}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
. Then for a Hecke eigenform
F
of
\mathcal{S}_{2\kappa-1}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
,
\widetilde{T}_{n}F=\lambda_{F}(n)n^{\kappa-1}F.
By
[
15
]
(or
[
23
, Theorem 1.1, 1.2]
), the Hecke operators on the half-integral weight modular forms are multiplicative. Moreover, if
n=p^{2\ell}
, then

(2.6)

T_{p^{2\ell}}f=\Lambda_{f}(p^{2\ell})f=(\lambda_{F}(p^{\ell})p^{(\kappa-1)\ell}-\lambda_{F}(p^{\ell-2})p^{\ell(\kappa-1)-1})f.

2.3.
The Kernel Function

The goal of this section is to set up the kernel function that we will examine. In particular, we shall prove an analog of
[
33
, Theorem 1]
for half-integers. We begin by setting some notation. For
\gamma\in M_{4}^{+},
we define the functions

(2.7)

R_{\gamma}(z,z^{\prime})=j_{\gamma}(z)^{2\kappa}(\gamma z+z^{\prime})^{\kappa},

and

(2.8)

h_{n}(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{\gamma\in G_{4}(n)}\frac{1}{R_{\gamma}(z,z^{\prime})}.

Notice that, when
n=1,

G_{4}(1)=\Gamma_{0}(4).

We also set

(2.9)

C_{\kappa}=\frac{(-i)^{\kappa}\pi}{2^{\kappa-3}(\kappa-1)}.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
H_{\kappa}(4)
be an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4))
. Let
n\geq 1
be an odd square integer. Then for
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}

(2.10)

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{(T_{n}f)(z)\overline{f(z^{\prime})}}{\|f\|^{2}}=\frac{n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}h_{n}(z,-\overline{z^{\prime}}).

In particular, if we set
z=iy_{1}
and
z^{\prime}=iy_{2}:

(2.11)

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{(T_{n}f)(iy_{1})\overline{f(iy_{2})}}{\|f\|^{2}}=\frac{n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}h_{n}(iy_{1},iy_{2}).

Proof.

Our proof begins like that of Zagier
[
33
, Theorem 1]
, but diverges when we examine the integral that we denote
I(y)
as Zagier uses Cauchy’s theorem which can only be done in the integral weight case.

Just like Zagier, it suffices to prove the statement for
n=1.
As for larger
n
applying the Hecke operator gives the result. Moreover, it suffices to show that for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}(\Gamma_{0}(4)),
we have

(2.12)

(f*h_{1})(z^{\prime})=\int_{\Gamma_{0}(4)\backslash\mathbb{H}}f(z)\overline{h_{1}(z,-\overline{z^{\prime}})}(\Im z)^{\kappa}\,d\mu(z)=\overline{C_{\kappa}}f(z^{\prime}).

By (
1.3
), we have

\overline{j_{\gamma}(z)}^{2\kappa}f(z)(\Im z)^{\kappa}=(\Im\gamma z)^{\kappa}f(\gamma z),

for
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(4).
Summing over all matrices
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
yields

f(z)\overline{h_{1}(z,z^{\prime})}(\Im z)^{\kappa}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(4)}(\gamma\overline{z}+\overline{z^{\prime}})^{-\kappa}f(\gamma z)(\Im\gamma z)^{\kappa}.

We substitute this into (
2.12
) to obtain

(f*h_{1})(z^{\prime})=\int_{\Gamma_{0}(4)\backslash\mathbb{H}}f(z)\overline{h_{1}(z,-\overline{z^{\prime}})}(\Im z)^{\kappa}\,d\mu(z)=\int_{\Gamma_{0}(4)\backslash\mathbb{H}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(4)}(\gamma\overline{z}-z^{\prime})^{-\kappa}f(\gamma z)(\Im\gamma z)^{\kappa}\,d\mu(z).

Then unfolding the integral gives us that

(2.13)

(f*h_{1})(z^{\prime})=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x+iy)y^{\kappa-1}}{(x-iy-z^{\prime})^{\kappa}}\,dx\frac{\,dy}{y}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\kappa-1}I(y)\frac{\,dy}{y},

where

I(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x+iy)}{(x-iy-z^{\prime})^{\kappa}}\,dx.

We insert the Fourier expansion of
f
into the integral and obtain

I(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}e^{-2\pi ny}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e(nx)}{(x-iy-z^{\prime})^{\kappa}}\,dx.

Now, by the change of variable
x\mapsto-x
, and multiplying the integral by
(-i)^{\kappa}
, the integral will be of the form
[
10
, Equation 3.382(7)]
with
\nu=\kappa
,
p=2\pi n
, and
\beta=y-iz^{\prime}
, so we get that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e(nx)}{(x-iy-z^{\prime})^{\kappa}}\,dx=2\pi i\frac{e(n(iy+z^{\prime}))(2\pi in)^{\kappa-1}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}.

Plugging this into
I(y)
yields

I(y)=\frac{(2\pi i)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}e(nz^{\prime})e^{-4\pi ny}n^{\kappa-1}.

Inserting this into (
2.13
), and we conclude that

\displaystyle(f*h_{1})(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(2\pi i)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}e(nz^{\prime})n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}y^{\kappa-1}\frac{\,dy}{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(2\pi i)^{\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa-1)}{(4\pi)^{\kappa-1}\Gamma(\kappa)}f(z^{\prime})=\overline{C_{\kappa}}f(z^{\prime}).

This completes the proof of (
2.12
) and hence Proposition
2.6
.
∎

For
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, we define the function
J(\mathbf{s},n)
. This is the function that we shall study to derive our weighted second moment result. In light of Proposition
2.6
, we write
J(\mathbf{s},n)
in the following two different ways:

(2.14)

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{(T_{n}f)(iy_{1})\overline{f(iy_{2})}}{\|f\|^{2}}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}

\displaystyle=:J_{\mathrm{Spec}}(\mathbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in G_{4}(n)}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}

\displaystyle=:J_{\mathrm{Geo}}(\mathbf{s},n).

For accuracy, we refer to
J_{\mathrm{Spec}}(\textbf{s},n)
as the
spectral side
of the relative trace formula and
J_{\mathrm{Geo}}(\textbf{s},n)
as the
geometric side
of the relative trace formula.

2.4.
The Spectral Side

Let
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})\gg 1
. Using the assumption that
f
is an eigenfunction of the Hecke operator (
2.6
) and (
1.4
) we get

(2.15)

J_{\mathrm{Spec}}(\mathbf{s},n)=n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(s_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}+\kappa}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(n)L(1/2+s_{1},f)\overline{L(1/2+\overline{s_{2}},f)}}{\|f\|^{2}},

where

(2.16)

\Lambda_{f}(n)=\prod_{p^{2\ell}\parallel n}(\lambda_{F}(p^{\ell})-p^{-1}\lambda_{F}(p^{\ell-2})).

This is absolutely convergent when
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})>\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, it admits meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
via the analytic continuation of automorphic
L
-functions. In particular, we may take
s_{1}=s_{2}=0
to get the weighted second moment.

2.5.
The Geometric Side

We set

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}:\mbox{$ad=n,$ $a,d\in\mathbb{Z}$, $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, and $\gcd(a,d,m)=1$}\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=B_{n}/{\pm I_{2}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}:\mbox{$ad=n,$ $a,d>0,$ $a,d\in\mathbb{Z}$, $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, and $\gcd(a,d,m)=1$}\right\},

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\\
m&d\end{pmatrix}:\mbox{$ad=n$, $a,d\in\mathbb{Z}$, $0\neq m\in 4\mathbb{Z}$, and $\gcd(a,d,m)=1$}\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{L}_{n}

\displaystyle=L_{n}/\{\pm I_{2}\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\\
m&d\end{pmatrix}:\mbox{$ad=n,$ $a,d>0,$ $a,d\in\mathbb{Z}$ $0\neq m\in 4\mathbb{Z}$, and $\gcd(a,d,m)=1$}\right\}.

With these matrix sets, we prove the following Bruhat decomposition of
G_{4}(n):

Lemma 2.7

(Bruhat decomposition)
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. The Bruhat decomposition for
G_{4}(n)
is

\displaystyle G_{4}(n)=B_{n}\bigsqcup L_{n}\bigsqcup\left(\bigsqcup_{m\neq 0,-n}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b\in\mathbb{Z},c\in 4\mathbb{Z}\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\right).

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
2.5
that we have that
G_{4}(n)=G_{4}(n)^{\prime}
. The first component contains matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
&*\end{pmatrix}
. The second component contains matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}*&\\
*&*\end{pmatrix},
with bottom left corner being nonzero. The last component contains matrices with each entry being nonzero. Now observe that matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
*&\end{pmatrix}
or
\begin{pmatrix}&*\\
*&*\end{pmatrix}
will disappear as
(n,4)=1
. This proves the claim.
∎

We insert the Bruhat decomposition for
G_{4}(n)
into (
2.14
) and by the definition of
j_{\gamma}(z)
for
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in G_{4}(n)
, we obtain the decomposition

(2.17)

J_{\operatorname{Geo}}(\textbf{s},n)=J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Dual}}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n),

where

(2.18)

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)=2C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{B}_{n}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}},

(2.19)

J_{\operatorname{Dual}}(\textbf{s},n)=2C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{L}_{n}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}},

and

(2.20)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)=C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m\neq 0,-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b\in\mathbb{Z},c\in 4\mathbb{Z}\\
(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

Remark 2.8
.

Finding the region of absolute convergence for
J_{\operatorname{Geo}}(\textbf{s},n)
is commonly referred to as the “regularization” process, which is a central ingredient of the relative trace formula, see
[
30
]
. In the holomorphic case, however, the regularization is simpler, and
J_{\operatorname{Geo}}(\textbf{s},n)
admits an analytic continuation to a neighborhood of
\textbf{s}=\textbf{0}.
For further details, see
[
29
, Section 5.1]
.

We will call
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
the
small cell orbital integrals
,
J_{\operatorname{Dual}}(\textbf{s},n)
the
dual orbital integrals
, and
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)
the
regular orbital integral
. We also define

J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{s},n)=J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Dual}}(\textbf{s},n),

which is the
singular orbital integrals
. Combining this decomposition with (
2.14
), (
2.15
), and (
2.17
), and we conclude that for
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})\gg 1
, we have

(2.21)

n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(s_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}+\kappa}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(n)L(1/2+s_{1},f)\overline{L(1/2+\overline{s_{2}},f)}}{\|f\|^{2}}=J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\mathbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\mathbf{s},n).

3.
The Singular Orbital Integral
J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{s},n)

In this section, we always assume that
n
is an odd square integer.

3.1.
The Small Cell Orbital Integral
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)

Before the calculation of the singular orbital integrals, we introduce several useful lemmas. The first lemma is a generalization of the Lipschitz summation formula:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{H}
and
\kappa>1
. Then for any positive integers
r
and
s
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ s)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(z+m)^{\kappa}}=\frac{(-2\pi i)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)s^{\kappa}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{\kappa-1}e\left(\frac{rm}{s}\right)e\left(\frac{mz}{s}\right).

Proof.

We rewrite the left hand side as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ s)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(z+m)^{\kappa}}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(z+r+ms)^{\kappa}}=\frac{1}{s^{\kappa}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{z+r}{s}+m\right)^{\kappa}}.

Applying the Lipschitz summation formula (e.g.,
[
24
, Equation (7)]
) completes the proof.
∎

Recall from (
2.2
) that for any
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4}^{+},
there exists a complex number
t_{\gamma}
satisfying
j_{\gamma}(z)=t_{\gamma}\sqrt{cz+d}
and such that
|t_{\gamma}|=1
. The following lemma gives an explicit formula for
t_{\gamma}
when
\gamma=\gamma_{j}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\gamma=\gamma^{a,d,m}=\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}
satisfying
a,d>0,

ad=n
, and
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
, one has

t^{a,d,m}:=t_{\gamma}=\varepsilon_{g}^{-1}\left(\frac{-m}{g}\right),

where
g=\gcd(a,d)
from §
1.3.5
.

Proof.

Notice

\begin{pmatrix}a&m+\ell d\\
&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\\
&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&m\\
&d\end{pmatrix}.

So by the cocycle condition, we can reduce the upper right entry modulo
d
without changing the value of
t^{a,d,m}
. Thus, without loss of generality, it suffices to prove the theorem when
0\leq m<d
. Now we shall apply Lemma
2.4
where we will let
g_{1},r,s,x,y
define
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
as in (
2.5
) and §
1.3.5
; in particular,
g_{1}=\gcd(m,d)
. From the proof of Lemma
2.4
, we may further assume
x
is a prime such that
x\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
. By the cocycle condition, we have

t^{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}\varepsilon_{s}^{-1}\left(\frac{-4dr}{x}\right)\left(\frac{4r}{s}\right)=\varepsilon_{s}^{-1}\left(\frac{4r}{xs}\right)\left(\frac{d}{x}\right).

The determinant of
\gamma_{2}
from (
2.5
) implies
axs-4rg_{1}=1
. In particular,
as\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
and so
\varepsilon_{s}=\varepsilon_{a}
. As
ad=n
is a square it further follows that
a\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
and thus
\varepsilon_{s}=\varepsilon_{a}=\varepsilon_{d}
.

Since
ad=n
is an odd square, we will have that
a\equiv d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
and so
\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{a}\right)
. Upon writing
4rg_{1}=-1+axs
, we see that
axs\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
, thus
\left(\frac{-1}{axs}\right)=1
, and in particular
\left(\frac{-1}{a}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{xs}\right)
. Putting this together gives
\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{a}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{-1+axs}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{4rg_{1}}{xs}\right)
. This implies
\left(\frac{4r}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{g_{1}}{xs}\right)
. A similar argument will show
\left(\frac{g_{1}}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{g_{1}}{a}\right)
. Then

\left(\frac{4r}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{g_{1}}{xs}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{g_{1}}{a}\right).

As
x\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4),
we have
\left(\frac{d}{x}\right)=\left(\frac{x}{d}\right).
Setting
d=d_{*}(d^{\prime})^{2}=p_{1}\cdots p_{s}(d^{\prime})^{2}
, we can rewrite this identity in the form
\left(\frac{d}{x}\right)=\left(\frac{x}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right)
. Recalling that
y
was chosen so that
\frac{m}{g_{1}}x+\frac{d}{g_{1}}y=1
, we have

\left(\frac{x}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right)\left(\frac{m/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right)=\left(\frac{1-yd/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right).

Now as
ad=n
is an odd square, we must have
p_{1}\cdots p_{s}\mid a
. Because
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
, we also have
\ord_{p_{1}}(g_{1})=\cdots\ord_{p_{s}}(g_{1})=0.
In other words,
p_{1}\cdots p_{s}|\frac{d}{g_{1}}
which means
\left(\frac{1-yd/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right)=1
and we conclude that

\left(\frac{d}{x}\right)=\left(\frac{m/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{2}}\right).

Therefore

t^{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{s}^{-1}\left(\frac{4r}{xs}\right)\left(\frac{d}{x}\right)=\varepsilon_{d}^{-1}\left(\frac{-1}{d}\right)\left(\frac{g_{1}}{a}\right)\left(\frac{m/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right)=\varepsilon_{d}^{-1}\left(\frac{g_{1}}{a}\right)\left(\frac{-m/g_{1}}{p_{1}\cdots p_{s}}\right).

Again recalling that
ad=n
is a square number,
\ord_{p}(g)
is odd if and only if
\ord_{p}(d)
is odd if and only if
\ord_{p}(a)
is odd. This implies the relations
\varepsilon_{d}^{-1}=\varepsilon_{g}^{-1},

\left(\frac{g_{1}}{a}\right)=\left(\frac{g_{1}}{g}\right)
, and
\left(\frac{-m/g_{1}}{d}\right)=\left(\frac{-m/g_{1}}{g}\right)
. Together, these complete the proof.
∎

We now investigate the small cell orbital integral (

\gcd(a,d,m)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(t^{a,d,m})^{-2\kappa}}{(iay_{1}+idy_{2}+m)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}},

The main result of §

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
is absolutely convergent in the region

\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}+s_{2})>0,\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})>-\frac{\kappa}{2},\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})>-\frac{\kappa}{2}\right\}.

Moreover, it admits a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{g_{*}})g_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}+s_{1}+s_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa+1}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}g^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\prod_{p|g_{0}}\left(1-\chi_{g_{*}}(p)p^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\right).

Proof.

Breaking up the sum over
ad=n
based on
g=\gcd(a,d)
, we can apply Lemma
3.2
to get

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)=2C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\gcd(g,m)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{-m}{g}\right)\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{(iagy_{1}+idgy_{2}+m)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

Now we break up the sum over
m\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(g,m)=1
into congruence classes
r
modulo
g
with
(r,g)=1
. This gives

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)=2C_{\kappa}^{-1}n^{\kappa-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g)\\
\gcd(r,g)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{-r}{g}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g)\end{subarray}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{(iagy_{1}+idgy_{2}+m)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

Now apply Lemma
3.1
to obtain

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-2\pi i)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g)\\
\gcd(r,g)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{-r}{g}\right)\sum_{m\geq 1}e\left(\frac{mr}{g}\right)\frac{m^{\kappa-1}}{g^{\kappa}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi amy_{1}}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi dmy_{2}}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

Making the change of variable
y_{1}\mapsto\frac{y_{1}}{2\pi am}
,
y_{2}\mapsto\frac{y_{2}}{2\pi dm},

and recalling the definition of the Gamma function

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{s}\frac{\,dy}{y}=\Gamma(s),

yields

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{\chi_{g}}(m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}},

where

c_{\chi}(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g)\\
\gcd(r,g)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(r)e\left(\frac{rm}{g}\right).

Next, we need to investigate the sums
c_{\chi_{g}}(m),
which was studied in
[
2
]
. Recall the notations in §
1.3.5
. By
[
2
, Lemma 4.11]
,
c_{\chi_{g}}(m)=0
if
g_{2}\nmid m,
and otherwise

c_{\chi_{g}}(mg_{2})=g_{2}\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})c_{\chi_{g_{*}}}(m)c_{g_{0}}(m),

where
c_{g_{0}}(m)
is the Ramanujan sum:

(3.2)

c_{g_{0}}(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ g_{0})\\
\gcd(r,g_{0})=1\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{mr}{g_{0}}\right)=\sum_{\ell\mid\gcd(m,g_{0})}\ell\mu\left(\frac{g_{0}}{\ell}\right).

Notice that
\chi_{g_{*}}
is a real primitive character. Then
c_{\chi_{g_{*}}}(m)=\chi_{g_{*}}(m)\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{\ast}}.
This implies

\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{\chi_{g}}(m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}=\frac{1}{g_{2}^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{\chi_{g}}(g_{2}m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}=\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{*}}}{g_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(m)c_{g_{0}}(m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}.

Combining with (
3.2
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{c_{\chi_{g}}(m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{*}}}{g_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(m)}{m^{1+s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{\ell\mid\gcd(m,g_{0})}\ell\mu\left(\frac{g_{0}}{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{g_{*}})\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})\mu(g_{0})\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{*}}}{g_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{\ell|g_{0}}\frac{\mu(\ell)\chi_{g_{*}}(\ell)}{\ell^{s_{1}+s_{2}}},

where in the second line we have interchanged the order of summation and then made the change of variables
m\mapsto m\ell
. Insert this result into
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
to obtain the following:

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{g_{*}})\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}}\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})\mu(g_{0})\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{*}}}{g_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{\ell|g_{0}}\frac{\mu(\ell)\chi_{g_{*}}(\ell)}{\ell^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}.

Applying the change of variable
\ell\mapsto\frac{g_{0}}{\ell}
and noticing that
g_{*}
is the squarefree part of
g
, yields

\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}}\frac{\chi_{g_{*}}(g_{0})\mu(g_{0})\varepsilon_{g_{*}}\sqrt{g_{*}}}{g_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\sum_{\ell|g_{0}}\frac{\mu(\ell)\chi_{g_{*}}(\ell)}{\ell^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}=g_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}+s_{1}+s_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa+1}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}g^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\prod_{p|g_{0}}\left(1-\chi_{g_{*}}(p)p^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\right).

Inserting this last expression into
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

When
n=1
, we obtain:

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},1)=\frac{2}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}\zeta(1+s_{1}+s_{2}).

Notice that
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
has a pole at
(s_{1},s_{2})=(0,0)
when

is the trivial character. This occurs precisely when
g
is a square. In the following corollary, we separate the terms with and without poles:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. Then

J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)=J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n),

where
J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
is given by

J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}\zeta(1+s_{1}+s_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{4}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}g^{2s_{1}+2s_{2}}}\prod_{p|g}(1-p^{s_{1}+s_{2}}),

and
J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)
is entire in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
satisfying

J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\mathbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon}}{|C_{\kappa}|}B\left(\frac{\kappa}{2},\frac{\kappa}{2}\right).

Proof.

Notice that
L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{g_{*}})
has a pole at
(s_{1},s_{2})=(0,0)
if and only if
\chi_{g_{*}}=\chi_{1}
which occurs if and only if
g
is a square, and in this case we have
g_{*}=1
so that
L(s,\chi_{g_{*}})=\zeta(s)
. This motivates us to break up
J_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
according to when
g
is a square or not. Therefore we define

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\\
g=\square\end{subarray}}L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{{g_{*}}})g_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}+s_{1}+s_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa+1}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}g^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\prod_{p|g_{0}}\left(1-\chi_{g_{*}}(p)p^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\right),

and

(3.3)

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{2}ad=n\\
\gcd(a,d)=1\\
g\neq\square\end{subarray}}L(1+s_{1}+s_{2},\chi_{g_{*}})g_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}+s_{1}+s_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{g}^{2\kappa+1}\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)}{a^{s_{1}}d^{s_{2}}g^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}\prod_{p|g_{0}}\left(1-\chi_{g_{*}}(p)p^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\right).

Now we derive the formula for
J^{1}_{\operatorname{Small}}(\textbf{s},n)
. When
g
is a square,
\varepsilon_{g}=\left(\frac{-1}{g}\right)=1
and
\chi_{g_{*}}=1.
Moreover,
g_{*}=1
,
g_{0}=\rad(g)
and
g_{2}=\frac{g}{\rad(g)}.
Since in
J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
, we have that
g
must be a square, we substituting
g
by

, observe that
g_{2}
becomes
\frac{g^{2}}{\rad(g)}
. Doing this, we obtain

J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)=\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s_{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{(2\pi)^{s_{1}+s_{2}}\Gamma(\kappa)}\zeta(1+s_{1}+s_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g^{4}ad=n\\

3.2.
The Dual Orbital Integral

Before the calculation of the dual orbital integrals, we introduce the following useful lemmas:

Lemma 3.6
.

For
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})\gg 1,
we set:

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{-s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{(1+iy_{1}+iy_{2})^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}},

and

J_{2}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{-s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{(-1+iy_{1}+iy_{2})^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

The integrals are convergent when
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})>-\frac{\kappa}{2}+1
and
\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})<\frac{\kappa}{2}.
Moreover, for
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
and
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}+s_{2})>0,
we have

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\frac{e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})}}{i^{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma(-s_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(s_{1}+s_{2})}{\Gamma(\kappa)},

and

J_{2}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\frac{e^{-\frac{\pi i}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})}}{i^{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma(-s_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(s_{1}+s_{2})}{\Gamma(\kappa)}.

Proof.

We only prove the claim for
J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)
as the proof for
J_{2}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)
is handled similarly. The change of variable
y_{2}\mapsto\frac{1}{y_{2}}
and
y_{1}\mapsto\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}
yields

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}y_{1}^{-s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(y_{2}+i(y_{1}+1))^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Following the method in
[
25
, Proposition 2.2]
, the integral is convergent when
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})>-\frac{\kappa}{2}+1
and
\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})<\frac{\kappa}{2}.

We now examine the inner integral of
y_{2}.
Define the function
h(z)=(z+i(y_{1}+1))^{-\kappa}e^{(s_{1}+s_{2}-1)\log z}.
This is holomorphic when
-\frac{1}{10}<\arg(z)<\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{1}{10}.
Let
R>0.
We consider the sector contour constructed by the line segment connecting
0
and
R,
the arc connecting
R
and
iR
and the line segment connecting
iR
and
0.
By Cauchy’s theorem, integrating over this contour will be
0
. Upon taking
R\rightarrow\infty
, the integral over the arc connecting
R
and
iR
will go to
0
. Thus, the sum of the integral along the real axis from
0
to
\infty
and the integral along the imaginary axis from
i\infty
to
0
will be
0
. Using this, we have that:

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\frac{e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})}}{i^{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{-s_{1}+\kappa/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}{(y_{2}+y_{1}+1)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Then the change of variable
y_{2}\mapsto y_{2}(y_{1}+1)
implies

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\frac{e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})}}{i^{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{-s_{1}+\kappa/2}(y_{1}+1)^{s_{1}+s_{2}-\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}}{(y_{2}+1)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Recall that, for
\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(z_{2})>0,
we have

(3.4)

\frac{\Gamma(z_{1})\Gamma(z_{2})}{\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})}=B(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{z_{1}}}{(t+1)^{z_{1}+z_{2}}}\frac{\,dt}{t}.

This implies

J_{1}(s_{1},s_{2},\kappa)=\frac{e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})}}{i^{\kappa}}\frac{\Gamma(-s_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(s_{1}+s_{2})}{\Gamma(\kappa)},

when
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}),\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
and
\operatorname{Re}(s_{1}+s_{2})>0
.
∎

The next lemma gives the explicit value for
t_{\gamma}
when
\gamma\in\overline{L}_{n}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. Let
\gamma:=\gamma_{a,d,m}=\begin{pmatrix}a&\\
m&d\end{pmatrix}
be a matrix satisfying
a,d>0,

ad=n
,
m\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
, and
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
. Let
g=\gcd(a,d).
Then

t_{a,d,m}:=t_{\gamma}=\varepsilon_{g}^{-1}\left(\frac{m}{g}\right).

Proof.

For
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&\\
m&d\end{pmatrix},
we recall from §

that

and
h_{1}=\gcd\left(h,\frac{ad}{h}\right)
, so we can find

such that
\frac{a}{h}x+\frac{m}{h}y=1.
Furthermore, we can assume that

is a prime and
\gcd(y,mn)=1.
Thus, we can write

\begin{pmatrix}a&\\
m&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a/h&-y\\
m/h&x\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}h&yd\\
&ad/h\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that as
ad=n
is odd so is

\begin{pmatrix}a/h&-y\\
m/h&x\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(4).
If we apply the cocycle relation, we see that

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{x}\right)t^{h,\frac{ad}{h},yd}.

Now we claim that
\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{x}\right)=\varepsilon_{a/h}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{a/h}\right)
. Indeed,
\frac{a}{h}x\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
because
\frac{a}{h}x=1-\frac{m}{h}y
. Hence
\varepsilon_{x}=\varepsilon_{a/h}
and
\left(\frac{m/h}{a/h}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{x}\right)=\left(\frac{m/h}{ax/h}\right)=\left(\frac{m/h}{1-my/h}\right)=1
. These two facts together imply
\varepsilon_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{x}\right)=\varepsilon_{a/h}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{a/h}\right)
. If we use this and apply Lemma
3.2
, we obtain

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{a/h}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h_{1}}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{a/h}\right)\left(\frac{-yd}{h_{1}}\right).

Now we shall write
h=h_{*}(h^{\prime})^{2}
and
d=d_{*}(d^{\prime})^{2}
with the goal of showing that
\varepsilon_{a/h}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{a/h}\right)=\varepsilon_{h_{*}d_{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{h_{*}d_{*}}\right)
and
\varepsilon_{h_{1}}^{-1}\left(\frac{-yd}{h_{1}}\right)=\varepsilon_{h_{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{-yd}{h_{*}}\right)
. We will do this by studying prime by prime. In particular, for the first claim it suffices to show that
\ord_{p}(a/h)
is odd if and only if
p\mid h_{*}d_{*}
, and for the second claim it suffices to show that
\ord_{p}(h_{1})
is odd if and only if
p\mid h_{*}
. For the first claim, we shall have two cases depending on if for a prime
p
if
p\mid h
or
p\nmid h
. These two cases will correspond to the
p\mid h_{*}
and
p\mid d_{*}
conditions.

Thus, suppose
p
is a prime such that
\ord_{p}(h)\geq 1
. Then
\gcd(a,d,m)=1
implies
\ord_{p}(d)=0
and that
\ord_{p}(a)=\ord_{p}(n)
. In particular,
\ord_{p}(a)=\ord_{p}(n)
is even because
n
is a square. Now
\ord_{p}(a/h)
is odd if and only if
\ord_{p}(h)
is odd which is equivalent to
p\mid h_{*}
.

Now suppose that
p
is a prime such that
p\nmid h
, but
p\mid d
. We wish to show that
\ord_{p}(a/h)
is odd if and only if
p\mid d_{*}
. Then we will have that
\ord_{p}(a/h)=\ord_{p}(a)
. Now as
ad=n
is a square, we have that
\ord_{p}(a)
is odd if and only if
\ord_{p}(d)
is odd which is equivalent to
p\mid d_{*}
. These two cases allow us to prove the first claim; as for the second claim, assume that
p\mid h_{1}
. We have two cases as
h_{1}=\gcd\left(h,\frac{a}{h}\right)
. If
\ord_{p}(h_{1})=\ord_{p}(h)
, then it is clear that
\ord_{p}(h_{1})
is odd if and only if
p\mid h_{*}
. Now if
\ord_{p}(h_{1})=\ord_{p}(a/h)
, then as
\ord_{p}(h)\geq 1
, we may use the first case above to conclude that
\ord_{p}(h_{1})
is odd if and only if
p\mid h_{*}
. This finishes the proof of the second claim, putting this together yields:

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{h_{*}d_{*}}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h_{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{m/h}{h_{*}d_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{-yd}{h_{*}}\right).

Notice that
\ord_{p}(a)>\ord_{p}(h)
if
p|h_{*}
, which implies:

\left(\frac{y}{h_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{h_{*}}\right)=\left(\frac{1-xa/h}{h_{*}}\right)=1

and

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{h_{*}d_{*}}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h_{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{-d}{h_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{d_{*}}\right)=\varepsilon_{h_{*}d_{*}}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h_{*}}^{-1}\left(\frac{-d_{*}}{h_{*}}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{d_{*}}\right).

Finally, notice that
d_{*}
is the square-free part of
d
and
ad
is square. This implies that
d_{*}
is also the square-free part of
g=\gcd(a,d).
Then notice that
h_{*}
is the square-free part of
h
and
\gcd(m,g)=1.
Then we can replace
h_{*}
by
h
and
d_{*}
by
g
in

which yields:

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{hd}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h}^{-1}\left(\frac{-1}{h}\right)\left(\frac{g}{h}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{g}\right).

By the quadratic reciprocity
\left(\frac{g}{h}\right)\left(\frac{h}{g}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{h-1}{2}\cdot\frac{g-1}{2}}
and the fact

\varepsilon_{hg}(-1)^{\frac{h-1}{2}\cdot\frac{g-1}{2}}=\varepsilon_{h}\varepsilon_{g},

we obtain:

t_{a,d,m}=\varepsilon_{g}^{-1}\varepsilon_{h}^{-2}\left(\frac{-1}{h}\right)\left(\frac{h}{g}\right)\left(\frac{m/h}{g}\right).

Then apply
\varepsilon_{h}^{-2}\left(\frac{-1}{h}\right)=1
and we complete the proof.

be an odd square integer. Then for any
\varepsilon>0,

J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon}\frac{\kappa^{\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|}.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
3.11
, with
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
to see that

J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{0},n)=J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)+J_{\operatorname{Dual}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)-\frac{2n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa)}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\oint\displaylimits_{|z_{1}|=1}\oint\displaylimits_{|z_{2}|=1}\frac{(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})^{2}}{z_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{3}}K(z_{1},z_{2};n)\,dz_{1}\,dz_{2}.

By Corollary
3.5
and Corollary
3.10
, we obtain

J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)+J_{\operatorname{Dual}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon}\frac{B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|}.

For the last term, apply the residue theorem and the estimate

\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}\ll\log\kappa\ll_{\varepsilon}\kappa^{\varepsilon}.

This implies that the last term is
O_{\varepsilon}\left(n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1+\varepsilon}\frac{\kappa^{\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|}\right)
which is an admissible error in comparison to the bound obtained for the first two terms.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

When
n=1,
we have that
J_{\operatorname{Small}}^{2}(\textbf{0},1)
and
J_{\operatorname{Dual}}^{2}(\textbf{0},1)
vanish since in these terms we must have that
n=a=d=g=1
, and the terms defining these sums are over
g
not a square. Now a similar argument to Remark
3.4
and Remark
3.9
will show that

(3.8)

J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{0},1)=-\frac{2}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa)}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\oint\displaylimits_{|z_{1}|=1}\oint\displaylimits_{|z_{2}|=1}\frac{(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})^{2}}{(z_{1}z_{2})^{3}}\frac{\Gamma(z_{1}+\kappa/2)\Gamma(z_{2}+\kappa/2)}{\pi^{z_{1}+z_{2}}}\zeta(1+z_{1}+z_{2})\,dz_{1}\,dz_{2}.

Applying the Laurent expansion for
\zeta(s)
(at
s=1
) and the residue theorem, we can show that

J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{0},1)=\frac{4\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa)}\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}+O(1)\right)=\frac{4\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}}{i^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}\Gamma(\kappa)}\left(\log\kappa+O\left(1+\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\right).

4.
The Regular Orbital Integrals
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)

In this section, let
n
be an odd square integer. We study
J_{\mathrm{Reg}}(\mathbf{s},n)
. For any integer
m\neq 0,-n
, let
\mathcal{M}_{m}
denote the set of matrices

\mathcal{M}_{m}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\;:\;ad=n+m,\,bc=m,4\mid c,\,\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\right\}.

We define
\Omega=\bigsqcup_{m\neq 0,-n}\mathcal{M}_{m}
; this is precisely the last union of matrices in Lemma
2.7
. We further decompose
\Omega
into the disjoint union
\Omega_{1}\sqcup\Omega_{2}\sqcup\Omega_{3}
, where

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{m\geq 1}\mathcal{M}_{m},

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{m\leq-(n+1)}\mathcal{M}_{m},

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{-n+1\leq m\leq-1}\mathcal{M}_{m}.

Then set
\overline{\Omega}_{j}=\Omega_{j}/\{\pm I\}
. We define

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{j}(\textbf{s},n)=\frac{n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in\Omega_{j}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{\Omega}_{j}}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{R_{\gamma}(iy_{1},iy_{2})}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}.

The disjoint union of matrices implies

(4.1)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)=J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n).

The main result of this section is the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

We adopt the notation established above. The regular orbital integral
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)
converges absolutely in the region

\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1,|\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1.\right\}.

In particular, if we set
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
then

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{0},n)=J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n),

with

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4\mid c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}i^{\kappa}+t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}i^{-\kappa}),

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{\overline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}+t_{\underline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}),

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/m)}{m^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{{}^{\iota}{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}+t_{{}{\gamma}^{\iota}}^{-2\kappa}).

Proof.

The region of absolute convergence follows from
[
25
, Theorem 2.1]
. The second part of the proposition is a direct corollary of Lemma
4.4
, Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
.
∎

We analyze
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{j}(\textbf{s},n)
separately in the subsequent sections. As a preliminary step, we make a reduction using the following lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\gamma\in G_{4}(n).
Then for
y_{1},y_{2}>0
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{R},

\gamma(iy_{1})^{\kappa}\left(\frac{iy_{2}}{\gamma(iy_{1})}+1\right)^{\kappa}=(iy_{2}+\gamma(iy_{1}))^{\kappa}.

Proof.

We first check the arguments of each complex number. We can show that
\arg(\gamma(iy_{1}))\in(0,\pi)
and
\arg(iy_{2}+\gamma(iy_{1}))\in(0,\pi).
This will force

\arg(\gamma(iy_{1}))+\arg\left(\frac{iy_{2}}{\gamma(iy_{1})}+1\right)\in(-\pi,\pi).

Applying Lemma
2.1
completes the proof.
∎

To simplify notation in the subsequent sections, we introduce some notation. For
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2},
we will set

\mathcal{B}(s_{2}):=\mathcal{B}(s_{2};n,\kappa)=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(s_{2}+\kappa/2,-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}{i^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}C_{\kappa}}.

By Lemma
4.2
and the definition of
R_{\gamma}
in (
2.7
), we obtain

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{j}(\textbf{s},n)=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}}{C_{\kappa}}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{\Omega}_{j}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{j(\gamma,iy_{1})^{\kappa}\gamma(iy_{1})^{\kappa}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2}^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}}{\left(\frac{i}{\gamma(iy_{1})}y_{2}+1\right)^{\kappa}}\frac{\,dy_{2}}{y_{2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Here
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in G_{4}(n)
and
t_{\gamma}=\frac{j_{\gamma}(z)}{(cz+d)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.
We apply
[
10
, Equation 3.194(3)]
with
\mu=s_{2}+\kappa/2
to rewrite the integral in the form

(4.2)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{j}(\textbf{s},n)=\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{\Omega}_{j}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{j(\gamma,iy_{1})^{\kappa}\gamma(iy_{1})^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Remark 4.3
.

We will now prove the decompositions for
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{j}(\textbf{s},n)
. While the details are different in each case, the strategy remains the same. We consider the sum over the matrices in
\Omega_{j}
; these are matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Our goal is to rewrite the corresponding sums for
a,b,c,d>0
. Furthermore, for each
\Omega_{j}
, we determine the signs of the matrix entries. This is the motivation of the notation in §
1.3.3
as these will be the corresponding locations of the negative signs in the matrices when we reindex our sums to be over positive integers.

4.1.
The Calculation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)

First, we consider the set
\Omega_{1}
. For positive integers
a,b,c,d
we define the matrices

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\quad and\quad\widehat{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}-a&b\\
c&-d\end{pmatrix}.

These matrices belong to
G_{4}(n)
and the multipliers
t_{\gamma}=\frac{j_{\gamma}(z)}{(cz+d)^{1/2}}
and
t_{\widehat{\gamma}}
are well-defined. In this section, we prove the following lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

The orbital integral

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
converges absolutely in the region

\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1,\quad|\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1\right\}.

In particular, if we set
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
then

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4\mid c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}i^{\kappa}+t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}i^{-\kappa}).

Proof.

Recall that for matrices in
\Omega_{1}
we have
ad>0
. We decompose the expression
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
into parts where
a,d>0
(associated with
\gamma
) and into parts with
a,d<0
(associated with
\widehat{\gamma}
). Using equation (
4.2
) yields

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}+d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{iay_{1}+b}{icy_{1}+d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}-d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{-iay_{1}+b}{icy_{1}-d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle=:J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n).

Evaluation of
J_{\mathrm{Reg}}^{1,1}(\textbf{s},n)
.

We use Lemma
2.1
to handle complex powers and rewrite

(icy_{1}+d)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(iay_{1}+b)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}=i^{\kappa}c^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}a^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}\Big(y_{1}+\frac{d}{ic}\Big)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}\Big(y_{1}+\frac{b}{ia}\Big)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}.

Expressing the resulting integral in terms of hypergeometric functions, using
[
10
, Equation 3.197(1)]
with
\nu=s_{1}+\kappa/2,

\mu=s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\rho=-s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\beta=\frac{d}{ic}
,
\gamma=\frac{b}{ia}
for
-\frac{\kappa}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,1}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(n+m))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}b^{s_{1}+s_{2}}a^{-s_{1}}d^{-s_{2}}.

Evaluating
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n)

We proceed in a similar way for
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n)
. Notably, we pick up the phase factor
e^{2\pi i(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}
when we apply Lemma
2.1
to factor
(\frac{-iay_{1}+b}{icy_{1}-d})^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}
. Then

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})}{e^{2\pi i(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4\mid c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}-d)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(-iay_{1}+b)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})}{i^{\kappa}e^{\pi i(-s_{2}+\kappa)}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}}{c^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}a^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\left(y_{1}+\frac{id}{c}\right)^{-(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\left(y_{1}+\frac{ib}{a}\right)^{-(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Invoking the integral relation
[
10
, Equation 3.197(1)]
with
\nu=s_{1}+\kappa/2,

\mu=s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\rho=-s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\beta=\frac{id}{c}
and
\gamma=\frac{ib}{a}
for
-\frac{\kappa}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
, gives

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{-s_{1}-2s_{2}+5\kappa/2}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\widehat{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}a^{-s_{1}}d^{-s_{2}}b^{s_{1}+s_{2}}.

Finally, we substitute the results for
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,1}(\textbf{s},n)
and
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1,2}(\textbf{s},n)
into
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
, and we set
\textbf{s}=\textbf{0}
to complete the proof.
∎

4.2.
The Calculation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)

Next, we consider the set
\Omega_{2}.
To simplify computations, we introduce the following matrices

\overline{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}-a&-b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{ and }\underline{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
c&-d\end{pmatrix}.

Since
4\mid c
,
\overline{\gamma},\underline{\gamma}\in G_{4}(n)
, which implies that the multipliers
t_{\overline{\gamma}}
and
t_{\underline{\gamma}}
are well defined.

Lemma 4.5
.

The orbital integral

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)
converges absolutely in the region

\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1,\quad|\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1\right\}.

In particular, if we set
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
then

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{\overline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}+t_{\underline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}).

Proof.

From the definition of
\Omega_{2}
and expression (
4.2
), we have

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)=\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\leq-(n+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
c\geq 1,4|c,b<0\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{j(\gamma,iy_{1})^{\kappa}\gamma(iy_{1})^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

We apply the change in variable
m\mapsto-m-n
, which results in the new summation range
m\geq 1
and the conditions
ad=-m,bc=-(m+n).
The condition
ad=-m
implies that the integers
a,d
have opposite signs and so we separate the summation into two cases:

(1)

d>0\implies a<0
. We transform variables:
a\mapsto-a,b\mapsto-b
(associated matrix
\overline{\gamma}
).

(2)

d<0\implies a>0
. We transform variables:
d\mapsto-d,b\mapsto-b
(associated matrix
\underline{\gamma}
).

This decomposition yields

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\overline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}+d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{-iay_{1}-b}{icy_{1}+d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\underline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}-d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{iay_{1}-b}{icy_{1}-d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle:J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,2}(\textbf{s},n).

Evaluation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,1}(\textbf{s},n)
:

Similarly to the computations in the prior section, we use Lemma
2.1
to handle the factorization of complex powers and obtain

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,1}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})}{e^{2\pi i(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{t_{\overline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}}{(ic)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(-ia)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\left(y_{1}+\frac{d}{ic}\right)^{-(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\left(y_{1}+\frac{b}{ia}\right)^{-(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Applying the integral relation
[
10
, Equation 3.197(1)]
with
\nu=s_{1}+\kappa/2,

\mu=-s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\rho=s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\beta=\frac{b}{ia}
and
\gamma=\frac{d}{ic}
for
-\frac{\kappa}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
) implies

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,1}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{s_{1}-2s_{2}+3\kappa/2}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(-s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4\mid c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\overline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}d^{s_{1}-s_{2}}c^{-s_{1}}b^{s_{2}}.

Evaluation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,2}(\textbf{s},n):
We again use Lemma
2.1
and rewrite

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,2}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{t_{\underline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}}{(ic)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(ia)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\left(y_{1}+\frac{id}{c}\right)^{-(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\left(y_{1}+\frac{ib}{a}\right)^{-(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Applying the relation
[
10
, Equation 3.197(1)]
with
\nu=s_{1}+\kappa/2,

\mu=-s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\rho=s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\beta=\frac{ib}{a}
and
\gamma=\frac{id}{c}
for
-\frac{\kappa}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
) yields

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,2}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{-s_{1}+3\kappa/2}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{F(-s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\underline{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}d^{s_{1}-s_{2}}c^{-s_{1}}b^{s_{2}}.

Substituting
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,1}(\textbf{s},n)
and
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2,2}(\textbf{s},n)
into
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)
, and setting

proves the claim.
∎

4.3.
The Calculation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n)

Finally, we treat the case of
\Omega_{3}
. We introduce the matrices

{}^{\iota}{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma^{\iota}=\begin{pmatrix}-a&-b\\
c&-d\end{pmatrix},

belonging to
G_{4}(n)
. As in the prior sections this implies that the multipliers
t_{{{}^{\iota}\gamma}}
and
t_{\gamma^{\iota}}
are well defined.

Lemma 4.6
.

The orbital integral

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n)
converges absolutely in the region

\left\{\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1,\quad|\operatorname{Re}(s_{2})|<\frac{\kappa}{2}-1\right\}.

In particular, if we set
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
then

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\frac{F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/m)}{m^{\kappa/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}(t_{{}^{\iota}{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}+t_{{}{\gamma}^{\iota}}^{-2\kappa}).

Proof.

From the definition of
\Omega_{3}
and expression (
4.2
), we obtain

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n)=\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{-(n-1)\leq m\leq-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n+m\\
a,d\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
c\geq 1,4|c,b<0\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{j(\gamma,iy_{1})^{\kappa}\gamma(iy_{1})^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Changing the variable
m\mapsto-m
and
b\mapsto-b
yields the new summation range
1\leq m\leq n-1
and conditions
ad=n-m
,
b\geq 1
. We divide the summation again into two cases, depending on the sign of
d
:

(1)

d>0\implies a>0
(associated matrix
{}^{\iota}{\gamma}
).

(2)

d<0\implies a<0
. We map
a\mapsto-a,d\mapsto-d
(associated matrix
\gamma^{\iota}
).

This decomposition yields

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{{}^{\iota}\gamma}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}+d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{iay_{1}-b}{icy_{1}+d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{\gamma^{\iota}}^{-2\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}}{(icy_{1}-d)^{\kappa}\left(\frac{-iay_{1}-b}{icy_{1}-d}\right)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,2}(\textbf{s},n).

Evaluation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,1}(\textbf{s},n)
:

Using Lemma
2.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,1}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(s_{2})\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{t_{{}^{\iota}{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}}{(ic)^{s_{2}+\kappa/2}(ia)^{-s_{2}+\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\left(y_{1}+\frac{d}{ic}\right)^{-(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\left(y_{1}+\frac{ib}{a}\right)^{-(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

Invoking the relation
[
10
, Equation 3.197(1)]
with
\nu=s_{1}+\kappa/2,

\mu=-s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\rho=s_{2}+\kappa/2
,
\beta=\frac{ib}{a}
and
\gamma=\frac{d}{ic}
for
-\frac{\kappa}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s_{1})<\frac{\kappa}{2}
, implies

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,1}(\textbf{s},n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{s_{1}-2s_{2}+3\kappa/2}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\frac{F(-s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/m)}{m^{\kappa/2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n-m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}t_{{}^{\iota}{\gamma}}^{-2\kappa}d^{s_{1}-s_{2}}c^{-s_{1}}b^{s_{2}}.

Evaluation of

Finally, we investigate
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,2}(\textbf{s},n)
. We proceed with a similar computation as in previous sections, using Lemma
2.1
and obtain

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3,2}(\textbf{s},n)=

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y_{1}^{s_{1}+\kappa/2}\left(y_{1}+\frac{id}{c}\right)^{-(s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\left(y_{1}+\frac{b}{ia}\right)^{-(-s_{2}+\kappa/2)}\frac{\,dy_{1}}{y_{1}}.

with

,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{B}(s_{2})B(s_{1}+\kappa/2,-s_{1}+\kappa/2)}{i^{-s_{1}+3\kappa/2}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\frac{F(-s_{2}+\kappa/2,s_{1}+\kappa/2;\kappa;n/m)}{m^{\kappa/2}}

b,c\geq 1,4|c\\

4.4.
Estimation of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\mathbf{0},n)

In this section, we establish an upper bound for the regular orbital integral. In particular, our primary aim is to prove the following result:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
n
be an odd square integer. For any
\varepsilon>0
, the regular orbital integral satisfies:

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

Notice that

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{s},n)=J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n).

The result follows immediately from the bounds on the individual components given in Lemma
4.8
and Lemma
4.10
.
∎

The main contribution to the error term will arise from
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)
, and so the following, rather crude, bound for
J^{1}_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{0},n)+J^{2}_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{0},n)
will suffice for our purposes:

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{s},n)
and
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{s},n)
be defined as in Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
respectively. Then for any
\varepsilon>0,

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

We restrict our attention to bounding
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)
, the case of
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},n)
being treated similarly. Since
|t_{\gamma}|=1
for
\gamma\in G_{4}(n)
, and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=m\\
a,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}bc=m+n\\
b,c\geq 1,4|c\\
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1\end{subarray}}1\ll_{\varepsilon}m^{\varepsilon},

we have

(4.3)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\kappa-1+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{|C_{\kappa}|}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{m^{\varepsilon}}{(m+n)^{\kappa/2}}|F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/(m+n))|.

Next we relate the hypergeometric function to the Legendre function of the second kind,
Q_{\nu}(z).

Applying the transformation formulas
[
10
, 9.134(1) and 8.820(2)]
and the duplication formula for the Gamma function, we obtain

(4.4)

F\left(\frac{\kappa}{2},\frac{\kappa}{2};\kappa;1-z\right)=\frac{2(1-z)^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}}}{B\left(\frac{\kappa}{2},\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)}Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right).

Setting
z=\frac{m}{m+n}
and substituting relation (
4.4
) into inequality (
4.3
) yields

(4.5)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|}\sum_{m\geq 1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\right|.

To estimate the Legendre function we use the following integral representation given in
[
10
, Equation 8.712]
:

Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\,dt.

We now split the analysis of this integral into two ranges.

Case 1:
m\leq 4n
.
We have

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\,dt\ll\frac{n}{m}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{\left(1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}\,dt\ll\frac{n}{m}\frac{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}{\kappa}.

Consequently,

Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\ll\frac{n}{m\kappa}.

Case 2:
m>4n
.

Suppose that
2^{\ell}n<m\leq 2^{\ell+1}n
with
\ell\geq 2.
Then

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\,dt\ll\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}\ell}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\,dt.

This integral is even. So after the change of variable
t\mapsto\sqrt{t},
together with an application of Stirling’s formula, we obtain

\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\right)}\ll\frac{1}{\kappa^{1/2}}.

When
2^{\ell}n<m\leq 2^{\ell+1}n
, the above computations result in

Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\ll\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}(\ell+1)}}\frac{1}{\kappa}.

Next, we sum over dyadic intervals. This gives

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\leq 4n}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\right|+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\quad\sum_{2^{\ell}n<m\leq 2^{\ell}n}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}+1\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{m\leq 4n}\frac{n}{m^{1-\varepsilon}}+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\quad\sum_{2^{\ell}n<m\leq 2^{\ell+1}n}m^{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}(\ell+1)}}\frac{1}{\kappa^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{1+\varepsilon}}{\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Upon inserting this result into inequality (
4.5
), we conclude

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

∎

Remark 4.9
.

When
n=1,
we claim that

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}(\textbf{0},1)=J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},1)+J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{2}(\textbf{0},1)\ll\frac{B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}.

By Proposition
4.1
, we have
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},1)=0
. It suffices to study
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\textbf{0},1).
Notice that we have the inequality:

2m+1-t\geq 2|1-t|,

when
t\in[-1,1]
. Then we have

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{\left(2m+1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\,dt\ll\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\left(1-t\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}}\,dt\ll\frac{1}{\kappa}.

This implies

Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(2m+1\right)\ll\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\kappa},

and consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(2m+1\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\leq 4}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(2m+1\right)\right|+\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\quad\sum_{2^{\ell}<m\leq 2^{\ell}}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(2m+1\right)\right|\ll\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}.

Insert it into
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{1}(\mathbf{0},1)
and we prove the claim.

Finally, we investigate
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\mathbf{0},n).

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{s},n)
be defined as in Lemma
4.6
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

First, we notice again that
|t_{\gamma}|=1
for
\gamma\in G_{4}(n),
so that

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{2n^{\kappa-1}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)^{2}}{i^{2\kappa}C_{\kappa}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\frac{m^{\varepsilon}}{m^{\kappa/2}}|F(\kappa/2,\kappa/2;\kappa;n/m)|.

As before, we use (
4.4
) and obtain

(4.6)

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)\right|.

Since
1\leq m\leq n-1
, we have
\frac{2m}{n}-1\in(-1,1).
Applying the bound
[
10
, Equation 8.724(2)]
to the Legendre function yields

Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\frac{1}{(m/n)^{1/4}(1-m/n)^{1/4}}.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq m\leq n-1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq m\leq n/2}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)\right|+\sum_{n/2<m\leq n-1}m^{\varepsilon}\left|Q_{\frac{\kappa}{2}-1}\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{1\leq m\leq n/2}\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{n/2<m\leq n}\left(\frac{n}{n-m}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{1+\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}.

Upon inserting this bound into (
4.6
) we conclude

J_{\operatorname{Reg}}^{3}(\textbf{0},n)\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

∎

) into this identity completes the proof.

Let
m

A
, we have

(m^{2})^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\kappa/2)^{2}}{(2\pi)^{\kappa}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(m^{2})|L(1/2,f)|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}}=J_{\operatorname{Sing}}(\textbf{0},m^{2})+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{(m^{2})^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}B(\kappa/2,\kappa/2)}{|C_{\kappa}|\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

Proof.

|\lambda_{F_{0}}(p^{\alpha_{p}})|\geq\frac{1}{4},

for some
\alpha_{p}\in\{1,2\}.
If both
|\lambda_{F_{0}}(p)|,|\lambda_{F_{0}}(p^{2})|\geq\frac{1}{4},
we set
\alpha_{p}=1.
Let
X\gg 1
and set

\mathcal{P}=\left\{p^{\alpha_{p}}:p\in[X,2X],\alpha_{p}\in\{1,2\}\hskip 5.69054pt\mbox{$p$ is a prime},\hskip 5.69054pt|\lambda_{F_{0}}(p^{\alpha_{p}})|\geq\frac{1}{4}\right\}.

This implies that if
\ell=p^{\alpha_{p}}\in\mathcal{P}
then
\ell\leq 4X^{2}
and
|\lambda_{F_{0}}(\ell)|\geq\frac{1}{4}.
Let
\{c_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{P}}
be a complex sequence satisfying
|c_{\ell}|=1
for
\ell\in\mathcal{P}.
Consider the sum

\mathcal{J}=\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{|L(1/2,f)|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{P}}c_{\ell}\lambda_{F}(\ell)\right|^{2},

where we take
c_{\ell}=\frac{\lambda_{F_{0}}(\ell)}{|\lambda_{F_{0}}(\ell)|}
which is the sign of
\lambda_{F_{0}}(\ell).
The positivity of summands in
\mathcal{J}
and that
|\lambda_{F_{0}}(\ell)|\geq\frac{1}{4}
together yield the lower bound

\left(\frac{X}{\log X}\right)^{2}\frac{L(1/2,f_{0})^{2}}{\|f_{0}\|^{2}}\ll\mathcal{J}.

On the other hand, open the square in
\mathcal{J}
and interchange the order of summation to obtain

\mathcal{J}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{P}}|c_{\ell}|^{2}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\lambda_{F}(\ell)^{2}|L(1/2,f)|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}}+\sum_{\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}c_{\ell_{1}}\overline{c_{\ell_{2}}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\lambda_{F}(\ell_{1}\ell_{2})|L(1/2,f)|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}}.

Applying the aforementioned Hecke relation and (
5.1
) yields the upper bound

\mathcal{J}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{(4\pi)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}\left(X\left(\frac{\kappa^{1+\varepsilon}}{X^{\frac{1}{2}}}+X^{2}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\right)+X^{2}\left(\frac{\kappa^{1+\varepsilon}}{X}+X^{4}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\right)\right).

Combining the lower and upper bound for
\mathcal{J}
gives

\frac{L(1/2,f_{0})^{2}}{\|f_{0}\|^{2}}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{(4\pi)^{\kappa}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}\left(\frac{\kappa^{1+\varepsilon}}{X}+X^{4}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

Now apply Lemma
5.2
and set
X=\kappa^{\frac{1}{10}}
to obtain

L\left(\frac{1}{2},f_{0}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}\kappa^{\frac{9}{20}+\varepsilon}=(\kappa^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{40}+\varepsilon}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
B
.
∎

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
C

Before the proof of Theorem
C
, we prove a result on the first moment of central values. This result is extensively studied in
[
24
,
14
]
and we only sketch the proof.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
D
be a fixed fundamental discriminant satisfying
(-1)^{\kappa-\frac{1}{2}}D>0.
Then

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\gg\kappa.

Proof.

By
[
14
, Equation (18),(21)]
, we obtain

\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma(\kappa-1)}{2(4\pi)^{\kappa-1}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)}{\|f\|^{2}}f(z)=R_{\frac{\kappa}{2},\kappa-1}|\textrm{pr}(z),

where
|\textrm{pr}
is the projection operator defined in
[
14
, Equation (4)]
. We note that our normalization differs slightly from that used in
[
14
]
. Taking the
|D|
-th Fourier coefficient on each side and applying
[
14
, Lemma 1]
gives

(5.2)

\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma(\kappa-1)}{2(4\pi)^{\kappa-1}}|D|^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)}{\|f\|^{2}}=\int_{iC}^{iC+1}R_{\frac{\kappa}{2},\kappa-1}|\textrm{pr}(z)e(-|D|z)\,dz.

Then by
[
14
, Lemma 1]
and the bound

\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(\kappa)}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{\kappa}{2},\kappa;z\right)\ll\frac{1}{2^{\kappa}},

(see
[
10
, Equation 9.211(2)]
), we conclude that

\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\Gamma(\kappa-1)}{2(4\pi)^{\kappa-1}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)}{\|f\|^{2}}\gg(2\pi)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\right).

Notice that
D
is fixed. Then inserting Lemma
5.2
we complete the proof.
∎

We introduce the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{a\neq b}
, which takes the value
1
if
a\neq b
and
0
if
a=b
. In particular, we will use Hölder’s inequality with
\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)a_{f}(|D|)\neq 0}
. Furthermore, by the Kohnen-Zagier formula, we have

(5.3)

\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)a_{f}(|D|)\neq 0}=\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)L(1/2,F\times\chi_{D})\neq 0}.

This will allow us to detect simultaneous non-vanishing of the
L
-values. Finally, we prove Theorem
C
:

Proof.

We apply Proposition
5.3
and Hölder’s inequality, and obtain

(5.4)

\begin{split}\kappa\ll\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}&\leq\left(\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)a_{f}(|D|)\neq 0}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt\times\left(\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)^{4}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)^{2}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

For the second term, we apply (
1.2
) so that it becomes the second moment of
L\left(\frac{1}{2},F\times\chi_{D}\right)
. Then we have

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{a_{f}(|D|)^{4}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\ll\sum_{F\in H_{2\kappa-1}(1)}\frac{L\left(\frac{1}{2},F\times\chi_{D}\right)^{2}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\ll\kappa\log\kappa.

Here
H_{2\kappa-1}(1)
is the set of Hecke eigenforms of (integral) weight
2\kappa-1
and level
1.
For
D=1,
the proof of the second moment can be found in
[
1
]
. Since the fundamental discriminant
D
is fixed, the proof is identical to that of the
D=1
case. For the last term, we apply Proposition
5.1
, together with Lemma
5.2
and (
2.9
) to obtain

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)^{2}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\ll\frac{\Gamma(\kappa)}{(4\pi)^{\kappa}}\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}(4)}\frac{\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},f\right)\right|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}}\ll\kappa\log\kappa.

Inserting these estimates into (
5.4
) gives

\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)a_{f}(|D|)\neq 0}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\gg\frac{\kappa}{(\log\kappa)^{3}}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\frac{\kappa}{(\log\kappa)^{3}}\ll\left(\sum_{F\in H_{2\kappa-1}(1)}\frac{1}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)^{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\sum_{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4)}\mathbf{1}_{L(1/2,f)a_{f}(|D|)\neq 0}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{n}},

for any fixed
n\gg 1
. Following the method in
[
19
, Section 5]
(see also
[
17
, Proposition 6.1]
), we can show

\sum_{F\in H_{2\kappa-1}(1)}\frac{1}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)^{n}}\ll\kappa.

Together with (
5.3
), this implies

\#\{f\in H_{\kappa}^{+}(4):L(1/2,f)L(1/2,F\times\chi_{D})\neq 0\}\gg\frac{\kappa}{(\log\kappa)^{\frac{3n}{n-1}}}.

Choosing
n
large enough completes the proof of Theorem
C
.
∎
