High-performance quatrefoil-slotted THz MIMO antenna for 6G applications with regression-based machine learning validation
Authors: Md Ashraful Haque, Md Shamiur Rahman Nishan, Isha Das, Geamel Alyami, Maruf Billah, Marji A. Alshammari, Narinderjit Singh Sawaran Singh, Abeer D. Algarni, Abdelhamied A. Ateya, Hussein Shaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the emergence of every new generation of wireless communication, 6G will require ultra-high-speed, low-latency, and high-spectrum efficiency techniques, which would need sophisticated antenna designs working on the terahertz (THz) spectrum range. This article introduces a miniaturized 2-element MIMO microstrip antenna with the graphene radiation patch on a polyimide substrate and copper ground for the THz regime. The proposed antenna exhibits a resonant frequency at 4.78THz with the ultra-low return loss of − 52dB and has an operation bandwidth of 3.81–5.13THz, which achieves a wide bandwidth of 1.32THz in totality. The proposed antenna has a maximum gain of 11.97dB, high radiation efficiency reaching as high as about 90%, and a good isolation (− 40dB) is achieved between ports. The diversity gain (DG) and the envelope correlation coefficient (ECC) are 9.999 and 0.0000175, which confirms that the diversity performance is a good candidate for future-generation high data rate systems. An equivalent RLC circuit is proposed and verified by full-wave simulations in order to describe the impedance behavior of the antenna accurately. Moreover, ML regression algorithms such as Extra Trees Regression are incorporated in the design flow, which efficiently reduces the evaluation time and allows multi-parameter optimization. Comparative studies also verify that the proposed antenna outperforms existing antennas in terms of bandwidth, gain, isolation, and flexibility and is therefore a potential candidate for 6G communication, biomedical imaging, and high-resolution sensing. The proposed antenna, which exhibits compact size, superior isolation, and high efficiency, yields tremendous potential for 6G applications at high Data Rate and presents a strong candidate to operate in the next generation of wireless communication devices.

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of smart devices, automation, and data-intensive applications such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and ultra-high-definition video streaming is pushing existing 5G wireless networks to their limits in terms of performance and scalability1. Despite significant advancements in enhanced mobile broadband, ultra-low latency, and massive IoT connectivity, 5G faces critical challenges, including limited spectrum availability, coverage constraints in millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands, and insufficient support for ultra-reliable, real-time applications2.

To overcome these limitations, the next generation of wireless communication—6G—is being actively developed, promising breakthrough capabilities such as data rates exceeding 1 terabit per second, latency below 0.1 milliseconds, and massive device connectivity up to 10 million devices per square kilometer. Central to achieving these ambitious goals is the exploitation of the terahertz (THz) frequency spectrum (100 GHz to 10 THz), which offers vast bandwidth resources capable of supporting ultra-high-speed, short-range communication links essential for environments like smart factories, autonomous vehicle networks, and advanced healthcare systems3,4.

Efficient operation at THz frequencies necessitates advanced antenna technologies that can meet stringent requirements for bandwidth, gain, isolation, and efficiency5. Microstrip patch antennas are particularly attractive for THz applications due to their low profile, ease of fabrication, and compatibility with integrated circuits, making them suitable for compact wireless devices operating in GHz and THz regimes6,7. In parallel, Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) antenna systems play a vital role in enhancing data throughput and link reliability through spatial multiplexing and beamforming techniques indispensable for maximizing spectral efficiency and network capacity in 6G’s high-frequency regime8.

A promising frontier in THz antenna design is the integration of graphene, a two-dimensional carbon material distinguished by exceptional electrical, mechanical, and thermal properties9. Graphene’s high carrier mobility, tunable conductivity, and ability to support surface plasmon polaritons enable the development of miniaturized, reconfigurable antennas10. When combined with MIMO architectures, graphene-based antennas facilitate dynamic tuning of operational frequency and radiation patterns, thereby enhancing system adaptability and energy efficiency11. Furthermore, graphene’s mechanical flexibility and transparency open novel opportunities for wearable and embedded THz communication devices, enabling next-generation wireless communication systems to be more adaptable and versatile12.

This research paper focuses on the design and analysis of a THz microstrip patch antenna employing graphene material within a MIMO framework. Through detailed comparative study and optimization, the proposed design aims to address critical performance challenges and contribute to the advancement of high-efficiency, compact, and adaptive antenna systems for next-generation 6G wireless networks13. The proposed antenna features a prominent and well-defined resonance at 4.78 THz, positioning it at the core of several critical THz applications14,15, reinforcing its compatibility with 6G communication and advanced THz imaging. This frequency falls squarely within the 4.4–5.15 THz band, which has been previously demonstrated to support high-performance optical-domain wireless communication systems requiring sharp frequency selectivity and low propagation loss16. Additionally, the antenna’s resonance lies within the operational range of fractal graphene-based designs that operate efficiently across 2.25–6.0 THz, reinforcing its compatibility with 6G communication, advanced THz imaging, and sensing platforms where spectral agility and material tunability are key17,18. The 4.78 THz resonance is also situated within the ultra-wideband performance envelope (0.9–10.1 THz) of antennas used in biomedical diagnostics, such as breast cancer detection, drug analysis, and sugar measurement, confirming its suitability for health-monitoring and spectroscopic applications19–21. More specifically, antennas designed to operate between 4.67 and 4.87 THz have been applied in biomedical sensing, validating the exact resonance at 4.78 THz for non-invasive, molecular-level imaging tasks22. Furthermore, multi-resonant MIMO systems functional from 0.1 to 5.0 THz demonstrate the relevance of the 4.78 THz range in high-resolution THz imaging, spatial sensing, and secure multi-user transmission, all of which benefit from the high isolation and signal integrity seen at this frequency23. Antennas operating in the 4.55–5.85 THz band further support the role of 4.78 THz in indoor THz WLAN environments, where broadside directional gain and diversity performance are essential24. Dual-band antennas tuned between 4.05 and 5.05 THz also encompass the proposed antenna’s resonance, confirming its utility in 6G, bio-sensing, and multi-channel biomedical imaging systems25. Collectively, these validations affirm the efficacy and domain relevance of the proposed antenna’s resonance at 4.78 THz within the terahertz spectrum, bridging next-generation communication and biomedical technologies.

Recent studies have thoroughly recorded advancements in THz communication and MIMO antenna technology, highlighting their increasing importance in facilitating next-generation 6G systems26,27. The suggested antenna design has been rigorously evaluated against existing alternatives to emphasize its enhanced performance in essential parameters, including bandwidth, gain, efficiency, isolation, and diversity. It attains a respectable gain (7.9 dB) and notable efficiency (85.64%), although it is hindered by inadequate isolation (− 20 dB), a high ECC (0.07), and constrained diversity (DG: ≈10 dB), which significantly impairs its MIMO performance. Despite its remarkable isolation (− 25 dB), great efficiency (90%), and substantial diversity (DG: 9.999 dB), its performance is constrained by the lack of ML/RLC and exceedingly low bandwidth. Reference35 discusses dual frequencies (0.395 THz and 0.629 THz) with narrow bandwidths (0.01 THz and 0.025 THz). Despite great efficiency (92.48%) and sufficient isolation (− 20 dB), the gain is low (5.17 dB), and the lack of machine learning capability constrains adaptation, even with RLC integration and a 4 × 4 MIMO configuration.

The proposed antenna represents a notable improvement with the center frequency at 4.78 THz and the operating frequency band between 3.857 THz and 5.177 THz, which results in a splendid bandwidth of 1.32 THz. It has excellent isolation around − 40 dB, and its gain of 11.97 dB and efficiency of 90% are much higher than those of ever-reported ones. The antenna has excellent MIMO performance with a very low ECC on the order of 10 − 4 (0.00015) and a high diversity gain of 9.99 dB. The architecture uniquely combines machine learning optimization with RLC circuits and enables real-time tuning and adaptability based on varying application demands. The compact 2 × 2 MIMO antenna is implemented on a polyimide substrate that enhances the integrability in miniaturized systems, making it more appropriate for futuristic 6G THz communication, imaging, and high-resolution sensing applications. The combination of wide bandwidth, high isolation, high efficiency, and complex design in the proposed antenna makes it a leading candidate in THz MIMO solutions.

Research gap and contribution

The proposed compact MIMO antenna design included machine learning, which is rarely applied in THz antennas at present. This architecture experiences remarkable gains in gain, isolation, and efficiency, which is a good candidate for next-generation 6G networks. In addition, the RLC equivalent circuit model is simulated and validated by multiple machine learning algorithms, from different perspectives of performance evaluation. Performance comparison with the past works highlighted the uniqueness of the design. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

ML-based regression algorithms speed up the evaluation time by orders of magnitude with respect to the traditional numerical simulations, and are extremely powerful for design optimization. These models also contain information about the unique effect each design parameter has on performance. Remarkably, the predictions of the regression models are quite similar to those of the simulations.

The derived RLC equivalent circuit yields results that are in excellent agreement with those of full-wave simulations, which confirms the accuracy and reliability of the model.

Characteristics of graphene material

Graphene consists of a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional hexagonal honeycomb lattice, which makes it one of the most extraordinary members of the family of carbon allotropes that also includes graphite, fullerenes, carbon nanotubes, and others. The unique or excellent electrical performance of the graphene layer can be attributed mainly to sp^2 hybridized carbon bonds providing extremely high electron mobility, which makes it a good candidate for high-end electronic and electromagnetic applications36. In antennas, some relevant parameters of graphene conductivity can be tuned, such as the chemical potential (µc) or the relaxation time (τ). For simulation, typical parameters include a chemical potential of 0.5 eV, a scattering time of 1 ps, and a film thickness of 0.01 μm37. In view of the Exceptional Conductivity of Graphene and its Properties that can be modified using electrical gating, chemical doping, and so on, it permits LF-active frequency tuning and impedance matching, thus this is an application for 6G communications38. The state-of-the-art shows that the features of graphene can embrace not only programmable beam-steering and multi-resonant properties that will enhance the radiation efficiency39, but also an achievable working band of a MIMO system. This tunability implies better adaptive antenna systems with beamforming, and hence high-performance communications in THz MIMO systems40. Such tunability is of paramount importance for the realization of high-performance antennas operating in the 0.1–10 THz range, which can enable miniaturized, reconfigurable wireless systems. These devices can achieve extraordinary radiation characteristics at scales consistent with conventional antenna technology.

Design methodology

In this section, we review the history of antenna evolution: from single-element antennas to MIMO configurations Fig. 1, also analyzing more sophisticated examples for one- and two-layer multi-feed systems. A wireless multi-input multi-output (MIMO) antenna is currently under investigation. These developments are becoming more important for data capacity, signal quality, bandwidth, and system efficiency potential. This paper defines the major technical challenge in antenna design, shows how inventive new architectures are changing the very logic of wireless communications systems, and illustrates how such paradigm shifts are now emerging at the system level.

Considering that the proposed antenna is designed to operate in the terahertz (THz) region of frequency, a compact dimension is necessary for the performance enhancement. The radiating patch is graphene, which exhibits an outstanding electrical conductivity and is tangentially tunable at higher frequencies. For good conductivity and reflection of signals, the ground plane is fabricated with copper. The substrate is the 5 μm polyimide film with a dielectric constant (εr) of 3.5 and a lower loss tangent (tan δ = 0.0027) is employed41. This material has perfect mechanical, chemical, and electrical properties in a wide frequency band, which is very suitable for the application in THz.

Flowchart of design and development methodology of the proposed antenna

The flowchart describes a procedure with the steps required to use the CST software to create a MIMO antenna array system. First, the antenna geometry is modeled and simulated. Thereafter, the Patch Antenna is analyzed and simulated with respect to critical performance parameters like S11 (return loss), gain, and efficiency to verify the performance of the antenna. If the results are not satisfactory, a parameter sweep is executed to find the optimum values by changing certain physical and material parameters. After the antenna has passed the performance tests, it is then fixed as a single-element antenna.

The subsequent step is to design a two-element MIMO antenna configuration. In this step, it is important to calculate S11, S21 (to determine the inter-element interaction), ECC (Envelope Correlation Coefficient), DG (Diversity Gain), gain, and the radiation pattern as well. If performance is not satisfactory, the spacing between the antennas can be adjusted to reduce the interference and maximize the diversity. The MIMO configuration is regarded as finished for backward development once it has successfully passed all screening.

In the final step, an equivalent RLC circuit for the antenna system is synthesized. This model enables the seamless connection of the antenna to other electronics and allows for circuit-level simulations. At this stage, the antenna design is considered final, and it has been rigorously tested for consistency in the application of wireless communications. The proposed antenna is analyzed on a polyimide substrate of 140 × 140 µm2, with a 100 × 100 µm2 graphene patch of 3 μm thickness on the top surface and a copper ground plane on the bottom surface. This stack of materials exploits the good dielectric stability of polyimide, the excellent electrical conductivity of graphene, and the good grounding properties of copper. The combination of these materials makes it a well-balanced solution, mechanically flexible, thermally stable, highly radiative, and inexpensive, which can be adequately used for miniaturized high-frequency applications. A symmetrical quatrefoil-inspired pattern slot, which consists of five perfectly aligned square-shaped apertures in a cross manner, is etched into the graphene patch. Four identical squares are positioned diagonally around a central square, rotated 45 degrees about the center, forming a cohesive design with strong fourfold symmetry. Nonetheless, all squares share the same side length and are right-angled, assuring manufacturing accuracy. The adjacent squares’ corners are connected to create a star-like aperture, which offers a symmetrical surface current distribution, which can contribute, as inspired by the literature, to multi-resonant features within the operational frequency band. If the symmetric quatrefoil-inspired slot is viewed as the one functional unit, the antenna has five such slots with the arrangement of strict diagonal symmetry. One is at the center of the patch, the rest four are symmetrically placed in the top-left, top-right, bottom-left, and bottom-right quadrants— each of them aligned with ± 45° axes, and the distance from the center is the same. To improve the impedance matching and the electromagnetic energy coupling, two equal-sized inset notches are symmetrically cut on both sides of the microstrip line. These insets are located so that the central slot is excited in a balanced manner and that the reflection can be minimized. This X-composite slot arrangement, together with the inset tuning features, enables structural and electromagnetic symmetry and uniform surface current flow, which leads to stable impedance and enhanced broadband radiation performance. Evolution of the single-element antenna

The development of the single-element antenna design was a process of successive modifications of the antenna shape and layout. This enabled us to investigate design options in systematic iterations so we could identify the key determinants of antenna performance. As the design evolved in stages, results from prior iterations were used to inform subsequent iterations, and the performance objectives were gradually converging. The design and performance evaluation of the single-element antenna is shown in Fig. 4, where the stepwise development is accompanied by the influence of each modification on the most relevant parameters: return loss, bandwidth, and gain.

In the first step, the antenna design starts from a simple rectangular shape with one symmetrical quatrefoil-shaped slot etched in the center of the graphene patch. This configuration was composed of five connected square-shaped apertures, one being in the center, and the other four surrounding it diagonally were rotated 45 degrees relative to the center. When combined, they appeared as a single geometry similar to a quatrefoil with strong fourfold symmetry. All squares have the same side lengths and angles, and the connecting corners of the squares also produce a star-shaped aperture that adds to the geometrical precision and the symmetry throughout the whole structure. For better impedance matching and stronger electromagnetic coupling, two mirrored symmetrical inset notches are introduced at both sides of the feedline. Nevertheless, the antenna did not show any notable resonance frequency at this stage of its development, as depicted in (Fig. 4a). Absence of any outstanding performance reveals that the proposed configuration is not enough for effective radiation. Therefore, the initial results were considered to be not so satisfying, which implies that further improvement of the structure and the design is called for.

The two were combined to а single geometry corresponding to a quatrefoil with a fourfold symmetry axis. All squares have the same edge length and are rotated at right angles, and the joined corners of the squares form a star-like aperture, which enhances the geometric and structural symmetry of the figure. In order to improve impedance matching and electromagnetic coupling, two symmetrical inset notches are introduced on both sides of the feedline. But the antenna would not, at this point, produce a meaningful resonance frequency as can be seen in (Fig. 4a). The absence of conspicuous performance results suggested that the initial design did not effectively radiate. Therefore, the initial results were considered poor; thus, further investigation on the structure as well as optimization design was required. In the second stage, the design was modified by introducing two additional symmetrical quatrefoil-inspired unit slots, positioned at the upper side corners of the graphene patch. This modification increased the effective electrical length of the antenna without expanding its physical footprint. As a result, the antenna exhibited a distinct resonance at 4.67 THz with an S₁₁ value of − 33 dB. Although the frequency range began at 3.63 THz, the S₁₁ curve remained below the − 10 dB line throughout the rest of the frequency band, indicating the absence of usable bandwidth. Nevertheless, the gain performance demonstrated a noticeable improvement over the initial design, as shown in (Fig. 4b).

The final stage of development for the antenna went into more details of structural modifications, where two more quatrefoil-shaped slots are added at the lower side corners of the graphene patch, and the feed line width is also increased. This modification produced an X-shaped U-slot composite configuration as depicted in (Fig. 4c), which is obviously different from the previous stage of design. The proposed structure gave a bandwidth of 1.4 THz (3.7 THz − 5.1 THz) with a dominant resonant frequency of 4.76 THz and a good return loss of – 47 dB. This performance improvement made it possible for the antenna to be used effectively over a broader frequency range, although it still operated in a single band only. As a consequence, the antenna reached a well-defined resonance, and the performances of the antenna were further improved with a maximum gain of 11.538 dB in the working bandwidth. The contribution of incorporating five symmetrical quatrefoil-shaped slots was found to be instrumental in maximizing radiation efficiency and impedance. This particular repeated tuning confirms the significance of gradual structural changes in enhancing the terahertz antenna system.

Parametric analysis

Parametric analysis is a critical part of the antenna development process, which enables one to predict the effect of variations in physical dimensions of design parameters on the antenna performance. In this contribution, substrate thickness and length on the hummingbird antenna for its electromagnetic properties are investigated. The effect on the key performance parameters, including return loss, bandwidth, and gain, was studied with the gradual change of the radiation patch dimensions. The effect of substrate thickness and length variation on the performance of the antenna is studied by using full-wave simulation. It has been reported that the resonant frequency and bandwidth are quite sensitive to these parameters.

Substrate thickness

In the parametric analysis, the antenna performance was studied in terms of substrate thickness, st, when all other parameters of the design were kept constant. To investigate the effect on return loss, bandwidth, and gain. Substrate thickness plays an important role in determining the antenna performance, as can be seen from (Fig. 5a,b), which show the reflection coefficient and gain, respectively. At h = 3 μm, there is no resonance, and the gain curve falls off steeply, which means that it is not suitable in the THz region. Increasing the thickness to 7 μm, a reflection coefficient peak appears at 4.7 THz, with a return loss of only − 20 dB and a bandwidth of 0.9 THz (from 4.3 to 5.2 THz), with a reasonable gain of 9 dB over this band. However, in both situations, the return loss is too low. The substrate thickness of 5 μm yields the best trade-off between the resonant frequency, return loss, bandwidth, and gain, and thus, this is the best choice for the proposed design. This study highlights the high sensitivity of antenna performance to substrate thickness.

Substrate length analysis

In this work, we investigate the impact of changing the substrate length (sl) on antenna characteristics, for sl values in the range 130 –150 μm. The two main parameters versus frequency: the reflection coefficient, see (Fig. 6a), and the gain, see (Fig. 6b). The impact on antenna performance of the substrate length that is gradually increased and decreased from the desired value of 140 μm was thoroughly investigated. A single resonant frequency of 5 THz was observed when the substrate length was decreased to less than 140 μm, which results in an unacceptable return loss of − 27 dB. Although the frequency interval prorogates from 4.7 THz, the S₁₁ curve hereafter remained below the − 10 dB line for the rest of the band, since there was no usable bandwidth. Moreover, the gain curve is moderate and pretty fat. In contrast, no significant resonance was found when the substrate length was extended over 140 μm; the gain response appeared similar to the case of truncated length. The optimal substrate length of 140 μm provided the best ideal performance in terms of resonance and gain for all tested cases. This result highlights the innate sensitivity of antenna performance to geometric parameters and underscores the need to maintain an appropriate substrate length to assure efficient operation and high efficiency of the design.

MIMO antenna design

For a better signal in terahertz (THz) communication systems, the graphene microstrip MIMO antenna, which is developed based on a single patch element, a highly efficient 2-port configuration is proposed. This structure takes advantage of such factors as spatial diversity, interference mitigation, data throughput enhancement, multipath exploitation, and security enhancement45. The performance of the design depends on the physical orientation of the antenna elements for best isolation and minimum coupling. The antenna element positions and orientations in MIMO layouts were methodically derived from the optimized single-element structure. At the first step, the surface current and the radiation pattern of a single-element antenna were studied to determine the main radiation direction and the minimum coupling regions. On the basis of these results, a number of placement arrangements—for the parallel, mirrored, and co-directional orientations—are considered. A 55 μm edge-to-edge distance was selected by parametric study, which guarantees a low EM interference and high isolation as well as compactness. This is the method that leads to the MIMO configurations seen in Fig. 11. RLC equivalent circuit

The RLC circuit-equivalent model, which includes resistive (R), inductive (L), and capacitive (C) components, is necessary to be considered for the antenna performance compensation. This model describes the inductance L as the ability of the antenna to store magnetic energy, the capacitance C as its ability to store electrical energy, and the resistance R as the energy lost to heat and radiation53. A full impedance analysis is performed in CST Studio Suite and Agilent Advanced Design System (ADS) in order to obtain accurate R-L-C parameters and to evaluate the response of the antenna to the incident wave in a wide frequency band54. The RLC circuit, although a crude approximation that does not fully capture the complex current distribution of the antenna, provides useful information about its behavior. The validity of the model is proved by the RLC equivalent circuit formulated in ADS55. The final RLC circuit for the proposed MIMO antenna is shown in Fig. 18. The process begins with analysis of the antenna’s electromagnetic behavior in the CST Studio Suite, with critical parameters such as return loss (S11), impedance curve, and resonant frequencies56. Simulation results, in Touchstone (s2p) format, are imported into ADS for the circuit-level representation. The RLC model is built in a bottom-up manner, starting from the modified antenna element. The resonant dip observed in the S11 of CST is represented by a parallel RLC circuit branch. Machine learning (ML) based optimization

Machine learning (ML) is increasingly being utilized in antenna design due to its speed, efficiency, and ability to tackle complex problems57–59. Traditional electromagnetic solutions, such as the Finite Element Method (FEM) and Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD), are accurate but often inefficient and resource-intensive, frequently requiring hours or even days for a single simulation. In contrast, ML models can quickly estimate antenna performance after training, making them ideal for rapid prototyping in fields such as telecommunications, aerospace, and IoT60,61.

Beyond speed, machine learning can explore vast and intricate design spaces that are challenging to navigate through manual adjustments and repeated simulations. It autonomously identifies optimal solutions from historical data, thus reducing design time and minimizing human error62. Moreover, machine learning demonstrates a superior ability to adapt to real-world conditions, including material imperfections and environmental variations, which traditional solvers often fail to account for.

A notable advantage of machine learning is its capacity for multi-objective optimization. Conventional methods typically focus on one or two parameters at a time, while machine learning can simultaneously optimize for multiple factors such as gain, bandwidth, and size, resulting in more balanced and high-performing designs63,64.

In summary, machine learning offers a powerful alternative to traditional electromagnetic solvers, providing faster predictions, improved design exploration, enhanced real-world performance, and efficient multi-parameter optimization—essential for modern antenna development.

Selection of algorithm

In machine learning- based antenna design, the choice of regression model is also important to allow the performance to be correctly predicted and optimized. A number of well-known algorithms are often considered to predict nonlinear models for the relationship between antenna parameters and performance metrics, for example, gain or resonant frequency. These are Decision Tree Regression, Extra Tree Regression, XGBoost Regression, Gaussian Process Regression, and Poisson Regression.

XGBoost is more accurate for large-scale data sets, while Gaussian Process Regression is more suitable for small-scale data sets with uncertainty predictions. The final strategy selection is from the standpoint of mean squared error and the R2 score of performance, which ensures the forecasting model selected can provide reliable and robust predictions of the antenna performance.

Decision tree regression (DTR)

Decision Tree Regression is a supervised learning method that partitions the space of input into a number of regions determined by a set of decision rules dependent on the features. In antenna design, it focuses on how factors such as patch width, substrate thickness, and feed width influence the output, which could be antenna gain or resonance frequency. The tree is grown by choosing the feature and the threshold to split on at each node such that some measure of the prediction error is minimized, typically using the Mean Squared Error (MSE).

One important benefit of DTR for antenna modeling is that it is interpretable. Engineers can observe how the antenna works in the field under real conditions, as they are able to explain different performances with different designs. Still, a common limitation is that “decision trees overfit the training data, especially if the tree is deep enough. In those cases, prune the tree or impose a maximum depth limit. While it doesn’t always match up to the best accuracy due to ensemble methods, it does make a very strong baseline model (and also a way to test out sensitivity to different things in parameters)65.

XGBoost regression (XGBR)

XGBoost is a gradient boosting library designed to be efficient, flexible, and portable, which builds prediction models in the form of an ensemble of weak learners, typically decision trees. Every single tree tries to fix errors made by the previous trees. XGBoost is particularly good at modeling complex nonlinear interactions between variables that are difficult to anticipate in antenna design. It supports linear and nonlinear models and employs regularization terms to avoid overfitting.

XGBoost is one of the most popular machine learning models for engineering problems, as it scales well to large datasets, provides high accuracy, and is very robust. For instance, it can accurately predict how gain changes as a function of shape, material properties, or fabrication tolerances. Engineers can also customize it for specific antenna types or frequency ranges by modifying parameters such as the learning rate, maximum depth, and number of estimators. The biggest downside is that it’s slower to train and more difficult to tune. However, in the real world, the benefits of performance are often worth the hassle66.

Regression with extra trees (ETR)

Extra Trees Regression (Extremely Randomized Trees) generates multiple decision trees by making random splits both in terms of features and values. This approach introduces more randomness into the model compared to standard decision trees and even random forests, typically resulting in lower variance. Consequently, ETR can better generalize to new combinations of parameters and provide consistent results even in noisy or overlapping input spaces.

One of the key advantages of ETR for antenna design is its speed. Because it employs random split points, it trains quickly than other ensemble methods. Another benefit is its resistance to overfitting, particularly when there are many design variables. It performs well for parameter sweeps, where numerous simulations create different but related datasets. The aggregate predictions from this ensemble method are reliable, making it an excellent choice when model performance takes precedence over the interpretability of individual tree selections67.

Gaussian process regression (GPR)

Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) is a parameter-free probabilistic model. It can give predictions and confidence intervals for these predictions. Antenna design is a typical application area for GPR because the performance can be compromised by uncertainties related to, e.g., production tolerances, external environment variations, or dielectric material variations. It is based on applying a kernel such as RBF or Marten to capture the relations between input variables, and it assumes that data follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution.

GPR does not scale up well to large datasets where excessive attention is paid more on training data rather than prediction. For example, when GPR predicts gain or frequency response, it is not restricted to produce only a point estimate, but rather the approach yields a “confidence band”, which engineers can use for risk assessment. This characteristic also assists in finding “safe zones” of design space where the antenna will work well, despite variation of some parameters. However, one of GPR’s drawbacks is its lack of scalability as the size of the dataset grows: when the size n becomes larger, it is computationally expensive (and infeasible) to apply GPR, since it has an O(n³) time complexity. As a consequence, GPR is most appropriate as an early design tool or when accuracy is of utmost importance, perhaps despite unknowns. Requirements for GPR68.

Poisson regression

We use Poisson Regression to model count data where the response variable signifies the number of occurrences in fixed intervals. While not the best option for designing an antenna, it could be helpful when trying to predict discrete results such as the number of resonance peaks, slot counts, or quantized gain levels. This model requires that the target’s mean and variance be equal, which is not necessarily true for EM problems.

When properly pre-processed, Poisson Regression can serve as a benchmark model or be tailored to training for given antenna features. The simplicity of BPNN will allow for easy application and explanation, which is useful in some cases. But the assumptions underpinning it require it to be too ineffective at typical regression tasks, such as predicting gain or frequency. The model also suffers from the overdispersion problem, in which variance exceeds mean, common for real-world antenna data. Because of this, DS can usually only be utilized in specific scenarios and tends to underperform when the task is regression for continuous outcomes compared with more powerful models such as XGBoost or GPR69.

Conclusion

In this article, we propose a high-efficiency graphene-based THz MIMO antenna, which is carefully engineered for the application of future 6G communication technology. By employing a quatrefoil-shaped slotted radiating patch on a polyimide substrate, with a sincerely optimized symmetric inset fed, this antenna exhibits a strong resonance at 4.78 THz with an ultra-wide bandwidth of 1.32 THz extending from 3.81 THz to 5.13 THz. The proposed two-port structure allows for a very small size and high electromagnetic performance, such as the exceptional isolation of − 40 dB, a peak gain of 11.9 dB, and radiation efficiency as high as 90%. The design also demonstrates excellent diversity performance with extremely low ECC of 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ and almost ideal diversity gain of 9.999, which is a perfect match for ultra-reliable, high-data-rate applications in THz wireless networks. To facilitate system-level integration, an equivalent RLC circuit was developed in ADS, which closely approximates the CST-simulated S-parameters and provides a simple yet accurate model of the antenna’s characteristics. The gain performance was further predicted by a machine learning-based regression model from the geometric and material parameters, and the Extra Trees algorithm exhibited the best robustness with very small prediction errors, resulting in an MAE of 0.74%, MSE of 0.02%, and RMSE of 1.25%, promoting the design process without sacrificing accuracy. The convergence of novel materials design and processing, full-wave and circuit modeling, and AI-based performance prediction provides a cohesive approach to multi-band/next-generation antenna design. Future work will also include experimental realization and validation in the THz regime, bias-driven dynamic reconfiguration of graphene, the scaling to larger MIMO arrays (e.g., 4 × 4 and beyond) augmented with decoupling structures or metamaterials, and the incorporation of flexible or on chip platforms to further facilitate seamless integration, flexibility, and application in miniaturized 6G communication gadgets and super-resolution sensing systems.
