Introduction

1.1
The Hastings–Levitov model

Description via iterated conformal maps

The Hastings–Levitov model was introduced
[
15
,
8
]
in an attempt to describe stochastic Laplacian growth by means of a sequence of random conformal maps. Let
\mathbb{D}
be the unit disc in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and write
\Delta=\mathbb{\hat{C}}\smallsetminus\mathbb{\overline{D}}
for the exterior disc. Consider the conformal map

\varphi_{d}:\Delta\to\Delta\smallsetminus[1,1+d]

fixing
\infty
with positive derivative there. We write
\varphi_{d,\theta}(z)=e^{i\theta}\varphi_{d}\left(e^{-i\theta}z\right)
and think about
\varphi_{d,\theta}
as attaching a “particle” (i.e., a slit) of size
d
at an angle
\theta
to the unit disk. Composing these maps gives a sequence
\{\Phi_{n}\}
of conformal maps

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}=\varphi_{d_{1},\theta_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\varphi_{d_{n},\theta_{n}}.

(1.1)

The
\Phi_{n}
maps the exterior disk to the exterior of a growing cluster
K_{n}
whose shape will depend on the choice of angles
\{\theta_{k}\}
and particle sizes
\{d_{k}\}
.

In the Hastings–Levitov model HL
(\alpha)
,
\alpha>0
, the angles
\{\theta_{k}\}
are chosen to be i.i.d. uniform random variables, which corresponds to attaching particles independently, according to harmonic measure seen from
\infty
. The particle sizes
\{d_{k}\}
are given by

\displaystyle d_{k}=\varepsilon|\Phi_{k-1}^{\prime}(e^{i\theta_{k}})|^{-\alpha/2}.

(1.2)

Here
\alpha,\varepsilon>0
are parameters, the latter describing the overall scale of particles.

Different values of the parameter
\alpha
(heuristically) correspond to different lattice growth models. For instance, it has been argued that
\alpha=1
corresponds to a continuum version of the Eden model while
\alpha=2
corresponds to a continuum version of diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA), see
[
15
]
which also conjectures an intriguing phase transition at
\alpha=1
. In practice, one needs to regularize the model due to the difficulty in evaluating the derivative of the conformal map on the irregular cluster boundary, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
24
,
18
]
. In this paper we only consider the HL
(0)
model which requires no regularization, and in this case the mapping (
1.1
) is a composition of i.i.d. random uniform rotations of the fixed conformal map
\varphi_{d}
. This model is the easiest to analyze and it has received substantial attention in the literature, see,
[
21
,
24
,
25
,
17
,
4
,
16
,
5
]
.

Description via the Loewner-Kufarev equation

It is useful to describe the HL
(0)
growth process in continuous time using the Loewner–Kufarev equation, see
[
8
]
.
Briefly put, letting each slit particle grow continuously one after the other (keeping the overall order of the particles) and parametrizing appropriately, we obtain a family of exterior conformal isomorphisms
f_{t}:\Delta\to\Delta_{t}
, where
\Delta_{t}=\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus K_{t}
are decreasing simply connected domains and
f_{t}(z)=e^{t}z+o(1)
as
z\to\infty
. The family
(f_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is known as a Loewner chain and satisfies the Loewner–Kufarev equation:

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{t}(z)=zf^{\prime}_{t}(z)p_{t}(z),\qquad f_{0}(z)=z,\qquad z\in\Delta,

(1.3)

understood in the absolutely continuous (integrated) sense, where

p_{t}(z)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{z+e^{i\theta}}{z-e^{i\theta}}\rho_{t}(d\theta)

is an analytic function with positive real part and
p(\infty)=1
.
Here
(\rho_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a family of probability measures on
S^{1}=[0,2\pi]
which we shall often identify with the unit circle.

Conversely, under suitable assumptions, a given family of probability measures
(\rho_{t})
(defined for a.e.
t
) can serve as an input, refereed to as driving measure, for the Loewner–Kufarev equation which can be solved to produce a Loewner chain
(f_{t})
. The clusters
K_{t}=\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\Delta_{t}
of the family of decreasing domains
\Delta_{t}:=f_{t}(\Delta)
is a family of growing compact sets. For example, if
\rho_{t}\equiv d\theta/2\pi
, we have
f_{t}(z)=e^{t}z
and
K_{t}=e^{t}\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. In general, there is a one-to-one correspondence between growing clusters, Loewner chains, and driving measures. See, e.g.,
[
28
]
and the references therein.

Remark
.

The most classical setup, used for instance when constructing Schramm-Loewner evolution curves, is that the measure is a continuously moving point mass on the circle:
\rho_{t}=\delta_{W_{t}}(\theta)
, where the continuous function
t\mapsto W_{t}
takes values in
S^{1}
.

Note that the Loewner–Kufarev equation (
1.3
) ensures that time is parametrized in such a way that the cluster
K_{t}
has
\log
-capacity
c(t)=\int_{0}^{t}|\rho_{s}|ds
at time
t
, where
|\rho_{s}|
is the total mass of
\rho_{s}
. In particular, if
\rho_{s}
is a probability measure for every
s\geqslant 0
, then
K_{t}
has capacity equal to
e^{t}
for any
t\geqslant 0
, but one may also consider more general finite measures. In general,
f_{t}(z)=e^{c(t)}z+o(1)
as
z\to\infty
.

The Hastings–Levitov HL(
\alpha
) model
(K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})
described above fits in the framework of Loewner chains. Reparametrizing time so that the corresponding cluster grown at time
t\geqslant 0
(denoted with a small abuse of notation by
K_{t}
) has capacity given by
\text{cap}(K_{t})=e^{t}
for all
t\geqslant 0
, its family of driving measures
(\rho_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
depends on
\varepsilon
and we call it
\mu_{\varepsilon}=(\mu_{\varepsilon,t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon,t}(d\theta)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\theta_{k}}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[T_{k-1},T_{k})}(t),

(1.4)

where
T_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}t_{i}
, with capacity increments
\{t_{k}\}
given by

e^{t_{k}}=1+\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{4(1+d_{k})}

(1.5)

and
\{d_{k}\}
defined as in (
1.2
). When the parameter
\alpha=0
, the slit length
d_{k}\equiv\varepsilon
is constant. Also,
\delta_{\theta}
denotes the Dirac measure at
\theta
. (Note that with this time-parametrization,
\mu_{\varepsilon,t}
is a probability measure for each
0\leqslant t<T_{n}
and for all
\varepsilon>0
.) With this choice of parametrisation, the relation between the map
\Phi_{n}
of (
1.1
) and the Loewner map
f_{t}
is that
\Phi_{n}=f_{T_{n}}
.

The small particle scaling limit

We shall be concerned with the small particle scaling limit of HL
(0)
-clusters, see, e.g.,
[
21
,
17
,
18
]
. This is the limit where the particle size
\varepsilon\to 0
and the number of particles
n\to+\infty
simultaneously in such a way that the limiting cluster becomes non-trivial, in the sense that it has positive (and finite) log-capacity. That is, let
t_{k}
be as in (
1.5
) the individual log-capacity of each particle. (Recall that since
\alpha=0
, we have
d_{k}=\varepsilon
and thus
t_{k}=c(\varepsilon):=\tfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{2}+o(\varepsilon)
.)
For some given
T>0
we take
n=n(\varepsilon)
such that
nc(\varepsilon)\to T
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This ensures that, after
n
particles have been added, the capacity of
K_{n}
converges (in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
) to
e^{T}
. We are interested in the limiting cluster.

1.2
Large deviation principles

Large deviations in random geometry

A large deviation principle (LDP) describes probabilities of rare events on an exponential scale. Let
(X_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
, be a family of random variables. Roughly speaking,
(X_{\varepsilon})
is said to satisfy an LDP if there exists a non-negative and lower semi-continuous function
I
, called rate function, and a function
r
, called the rate, with
r(\varepsilon)\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, such that

P(X_{\varepsilon}\in A)\approx e^{-r(\varepsilon)\inf_{x\in A}I(x)},\quad\varepsilon\to 0+.

To make such a statement precise one has to specify the topology and replace
\approx
by suitable estimates. A classical example is when
X_{\varepsilon}
is taken to be
(\sqrt{\varepsilon}B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, where
B
is standard Brownian motion. In this case the rate function is the one-dimensional Dirichlet energy, which is finite for functions in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}
(the Cameron-Martin space); this is known as Schilder’s theorem.
Recently there has been substantial interest in large deviation principles in random planar geometry, in particular in relation to Schramm-Loewner evolutions
[
30
,
2
,
14
,
20
,
1
]
. Both the Loewner energy and Loewner–Kufarev energy (see below) arise as rate functions in this context. The study of these rate functions and the geometry of configurations (curves, loops, foliations) which are assigned finite energy has been fruitful, see
[
31
]
and the references therein. In this paper we study the Hastings–Levitov model from a similar perspective: we prove several LDPs and identify the corresponding rate functions. We then analyze the geometry of configurations that are assigned finite values by the rate function.

LDP for HL(0) in discrete time

To state our first result we need some additional notation. We write
S^{1}=[0,2\pi)
(recall also that we sometimes identify
S^{1}
with the unit circle in the complex plane). For
T>0
, let
\mathcal{M}(S^{1}\times[0,T])
be the space of Borel measures on the cylinder
S^{1}\times[0,T]
and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{T}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(S^{1}\times[0,T]):\mu(S^{1}\times I)=|I|\text{ for any interval }I\subset[0,T]\},

where
|I|
denotes Lebesgue measure of
I
. When
T=+\infty
we write
\mathcal{N}_{+}
. Elements of
\mathcal{N}_{T}
will be referred to as (normalized) driving measures. By disintegration every
\rho\in\mathcal{N}_{T}
corresponds to a family of probability measures
(\rho_{t})
as above. We equip
\mathcal{N}_{T}
with the weak topology. We also view the driving measures
\mu_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.4
)
as a measure in
\mathcal{N}_{T}
by setting
\mu_{\varepsilon}(d\theta,dt)=\mu_{\varepsilon,t}(d\theta)dt
.

It is known that in the small particle limit (described above) as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the HL
(0)
driving measures for the Loewner–Kufarev equation (
1.3
) converge towards the deterministic limit
\mu_{0}(d\theta,dt)=d\theta dt/2\pi
almost surely, in the topology of weak convergence in
\mathcal{N}_{T}
. By continuity the corresponding Loewner chains also converge almost surely, in the Carathéodory sense. See
[
17
, Theorem 2]
for these facts. As pointed out above, the limiting driving measure
\mu_{0}
generates growing discs
e^{t}\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. This statement can be interpreted as a shape theorem: the HL
(0)
clusters converge towards a family of growing concentric discs.

In this article, we are interested in the large deviation event that the HL
(0)
cluster process is “close” to a given growth process, potentially different from concentric discs, in the small particle limit. One way to make this precise is via the driving measures. The corresponding large deviation result is stated below, and is the first main result of this article.

Theorem 1.1
.

The family of HL
(0)
driving measures
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
defined in (
1.4
) satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{N}_{T}
, equipped with the weak topology, with rate
4/\varepsilon^{2}
, and convex, good rate function
\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{N}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by the relative entropy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{S^{1}\times[0,T]}\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)\log\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)d\theta dt

(1.6)

whenever
\rho=\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)d\theta dt/2\pi
and the integral exists, and set to
+\infty
otherwise.

In other words,
\mathcal{H}
is a convex, lower semi-continuous function with compact level sets
\{\mathcal{H}(\rho)\leqslant a\}
, such that for any open set
O
and any closed set
C
of
\mathcal{N}_{T}
,

\text{(i)\quad}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\log\mathbb{P}(\mu_{\varepsilon}\in C)\leqslant-\inf_{C}\mathcal{H}(\rho),

and

\text{(ii)\quad}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\log\mathbb{P}(\mu_{\varepsilon}\in O)\geqslant-\inf_{O}\mathcal{H}(\rho).

Remark
.

The relative entropy functional arises from Sanov’s classical theorem, see
[
9
]
, which is applied in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that, given two measures
\mu
and
\nu
, such that
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
with Radon-Nikodym derivative given by
f
, the relative entropy of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
(or Kullback–Leibler divergence) is the quantity

H(\nu|\mu):=\int\log fd\nu=\int f\log fd\mu.

Thus the rate function
\mathcal{H}
in the above theorem is the relative entropy with respect to the measure
\mu_{0}(d\theta,dt)=d\theta dt/2\pi
on
S^{1}\times[0,T]
, whose total mass of its
t
marginal is 1. That is,

\mathcal{H}(\rho)=H(\rho|\mu_{0})=\iint_{S^{1}\times[0,T]}\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)\log\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)\mu_{0}(d\theta,dt),

where
\bar{\rho}_{t}(\theta)
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\rho
with respect to
\mu_{0}
.
By Jensen’s inequality, since
x\mapsto x\log x
is convex on
(0,\infty)
and
d\theta/2\pi
is a probability measure,
\mathcal{H}(\rho)\geqslant 0
.

Remark
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
also gives the corresponding result for the anisotropic HL(0) model of
[
17
]
. In this model the distribution of angles is chosen according to some given density
\lambda
as opposed to being uniform. In this case, the rate function is

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{S^{1}\times[0,T]}\rho_{t}^{\lambda}(\theta)\log\rho_{t}^{\lambda}(\theta)\lambda(\theta)d\theta dt

and
\rho_{t}^{\lambda}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\rho
with respect to
\lambda(\theta)d\theta dt/2\pi
.

LDP for HL(0) in continuous time

We now consider the Hastings–Levitov HL(
0
) model, where particles arrive in continuous time according to a Poisson process rather than discrete time, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
. More precisely, let
(N(t),t\geqslant 0)
be a Poisson process with intensity
\lambda=4/\varepsilon^{2}
, independent of the i.i.d. angles
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
defining the discrete time model in (
1.2
), (
1.1
). We then set
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}:=\Phi_{N(t)}
for
t\geqslant 0
. We encode this growth process via a Loewner evolution
(\tilde{f}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. It is driven by a family of measures
\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}=(\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon,t})_{t\geqslant 0}
as in (
1.4
), but the form of these driving measures is slightly more complicated, as we are not simply reparametrizing by capacity:

\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon,t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{t_{k}}{X_{k}}\delta_{\theta_{k}}(d\theta)1_{(\tau_{k-1},\tau_{k}]}(t).

(1.7)

Here the
(X_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
are the independent exponential random variables with parameter
\lambda
defining the intervals of time during successive arrivals of the Poisson process
(N(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, and for
k\geqslant 0
,
\tau_{k}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{k}
is the time of the
k
th arrival in the Poisson process (so
N(\tau_{k})=k
, and
\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}=X_{k}
).

The coefficient of the Dirac mass in the right hand side of (
1.7
) ensures that the log-capacity of
\tilde{K}_{t}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\tilde{f}_{t}(\Delta)
grows by a total of
t_{k}
(depending on
\varepsilon
as in (
1.5
)) during the interval between the time of arrival of the
(k-1)
th particle and that of the
k
th particle, as in (
1.4
). With these choices, for any
k\geqslant 0
we have

\tilde{\Phi}_{\tau_{k}}=\tilde{f}_{\tau_{k}}

and
\tilde{K}_{t}
has capacity
\exp(\lambda^{-1}N(t)+o(1))
, where
o(1)
is a term which is uniformly bounded by a quantity tending to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
(and comes from the fact that in between the two successive values of
\tau_{k}
the capacity grows by a fraction of
t_{k}
and so is bounded by
t_{k}=O(\varepsilon^{2})
). That is, for every fixed
t\geqslant 0
we have
\tilde{f}_{t}(z)=e^{c(t)}z+o(1)
as
z\to\infty
, where the log-capacity
c(t)
of
\tilde{K}_{t}
satisfies
c(t)=\lambda^{-1}N(t)+o(1)
, and
o(1)
converges to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly.

Note that by the law of large numbers, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
c(t)\to t
in probability. But at the level of large deviations, it is perfectly possible for
c(t)
to grow at a different rate. Denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}:

\displaystyle=\big\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}):\text{ there exists a differentiable function $m:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that }

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\mu(S^{1}\times I)=\int_{I}m_{t}dt\text{ for any interval }I\subset\mathbb{R}\big\}.

Given
\tilde{\rho}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
, let
s\mapsto t(s)
be an increasing diffeomorphism so that if we define
\rho
via

\tilde{\rho}_{s}=t^{\prime}(s)\rho_{t(s)},

(1.8)

then
\rho
has marginal mass
t\mapsto m_{t}\equiv 1
, i.e.,
\rho\in\mathcal{N}
and the corresponding Loewner chains satisfy
f^{\tilde{\rho}}_{s}=f_{t(s)}^{\rho}
, see
[
28
]
.
To state
our large deviation principle for this continuous-time HL
(0)
model, we shall impose a restriction that the total number of particles (or equivalently the total capacity) is fixed, equal to what one would have if growing the shape at constant rate.
Set

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{T}=\big\{\tilde{\rho}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}:\tilde{m}\text{ has support in $[0,T]$ and }\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{m}(s)ds=T\big\}

and extend the definition of
\mathcal{H}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{T}
by setting

\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\rho})=\iint_{S^{1}\times[0,T]}\tilde{\rho}_{s}\log(2\pi\tilde{\rho}_{s})d\theta ds.

Finally, let

h_{T}(f)=\int_{0}^{T}f\log fdt.

Proposition 1.2
.

The family of continuous-time HL
(0)
driving measures
\{\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
defined in (
1.7
) satisfies an LDP in
\tilde{\mathcal{N}_{T}}
, equipped with the weak topology, with rate
\lambda=4/\varepsilon^{2}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and convex, good rate function

\tilde{\rho}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{T}\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\rho})=\mathcal{H}(\rho)+h_{T}(\tilde{m}),

where
m
is an increasing diffeomorphism that relates
\tilde{\rho}
and
\rho
in the equation (
1.8
).

The proof is sketched in Section
2.1
.

LDP for HL(0) shapes

Theorem
1.1
and Proposition
1.2
take into account the whole history of the growth process. It is also natural to consider only the shape of the cluster at a given time
T>0
,
and ask for a corresponding large deviation event. One way to phrase such a result is in terms of the discrete time HL
(0)
-map
\Phi_{n}
which induces a probability distribution on the space of normalized conformal maps

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}_{T}=\big\{g\text{ univalent in $\Delta$, omits a neighborhood of $0$, and }g(z)=e^{T}z+O(1/z)\big\},

equipped with the locally uniform topology.

Corollary 1.3
.

The maps
\Phi_{n}
satisfy an LDP in
\Sigma^{\prime}_{T}
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
\Delta
, with rate

, as

, and good rate function given by

\mathcal{H}^{*}_{T}(\Phi)=\inf_{\rho}\mathcal{H}(\rho),

(1.9)

where

is the capacity of

, and the infimum is over driving measures generating

via the Loewner–Kufarev equation on

.

The result follows from Theorem
1.1
and the contraction principle
[
9
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. Indeed, solutions to the Loewner–Kufarev equation depend continuously on their driving measures with respect to the weak topology and the locally uniform topology, respectively, see
[

]
.

1.3
Finite entropy shapes

We are interested in describing the class of shapes that can be grown by a measure of finite entropy, where by shape we mean the boundary of a fixed compact hull, and by entropy we mean the large deviation functional
\mathcal{H}
appearing in Theorem
1.1
.

Loewner–Kufarev entropy and energy

We begin by describing some parallels with the notion of Loewner–Kufarev energy, see
[
2
,
28
]
, with a view of discussing shapes with finite entropy below. To simplify this comparison it is useful to consider a slightly more general setting, in which, instead of a family of growing hulls
(K_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of capacity
e^{t}
for
t\in[0,T]
, and starting at time 0 from
K_{0}=\mathbb{D}
, we have a growing family
(K_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
indexed by all of
\mathbb{R}
, and of capacity
e^{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In this manner, the hulls start at time
t=-\infty
from a ‘single seed’ (the origin), in the sense that
\cap_{t\in\mathbb{R}}K_{t}=\{0\}
.

We thus introduce an enlarged space on which the Loewner–Kufarev entropy will be defined.
We first define

\mathcal{N}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}):\mu(S^{1}\times I)=|I|\text{ for any interval }I\subset\mathbb{R}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{N}_{T}
can be embedded in
\mathcal{N}
by extending
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{T}
to a measure in
\mathcal{N}
by defining it to equal
d\theta dt/2\pi
where not originally defined. We will always consider
\mathcal{N}_{T}
embedded in
\mathcal{N}
in this way.

A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{N}
defines a growing family of hulls
(K_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, known as a whole-plane Loewner chain, via the Loewner–Kufarev equation and the initial condition

\lim_{t\to-\infty}e^{t}f_{t}(z)=z,

where
f_{t}:\Delta\to\Delta_{t}=\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus K_{t}
is the corresponding conformal map. As in
[
28
]
, under regularity assumptions, one may think of

1.4
Minimal entropy driving measures

Given a shape with finite entropy, can one describe a measure of minimal entropy that generates it via the Loewner equation? Is such a measure unique? This optimization problem is reminiscent of an entropic optimal transport problem with a rather complicated constraint. Solving this in some generality appears difficult, and we do not answer these questions in this article. However, we give a counterexample that shows that a natural guess which is correct for the Loewner–Kufarev energy (given by the “equipotentials” of
K
) cannot in general be the answer to this optimization problem.
In Section
5
we study the minimal entropy functional
\mathcal{H}^{*}(\Phi)
(which can be viewed as a function of the shape
K
) along with several related optimization problems.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

\displaystyle=\iint_{S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}_{s}\log(2\pi\tilde{\rho}_{s})d\theta ds

where we used that

and any closed set
C
of
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})
,

\text{(i)\quad}\limsup\limits_{n/m\to\infty}\frac{1}{n/m}\log\mathbb{P}\left[L_{i}^{n,m}(d\theta)\in C\right]\leqslant-\inf\limits_{\nu\in C}H(\nu|\lambda)

and

\text{(ii)\quad}\limsup\limits_{n/m\to\infty}\frac{1}{n/m}\log\mathbb{P}\left[L_{i}^{n,m}(d\theta)\in O\right]\geqslant-\inf\limits_{\nu\in O}H(\nu|\lambda).

Note that if the rate of convergence is considered to be
n
, then the rate function is simply scaled by
1/m
.

\mathbf{2^{\circ}}.
Every sequence, indexed by
n
, in the collection
L_{1}^{n,m}
, …,
L_{m}^{n,m}
satisfies the LDP in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})
with rate
n
and common rate function
\frac{1}{m}H(\cdot|\lambda)
. The sequence
(L_{1}^{n,m}
, …,
L_{m}^{n,m})
as an element of
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})^{m}
is made out of mutually independent empirical measures and thus satisfies the LDP with the rate function
I_{m}:\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})^{m}\to[0,\infty]
given by

\displaystyle I_{m}(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}H(\nu_{i}|\lambda).

This general fact relies only on independence, see for example
[
23
, Corollary 1.3]
.

\mathbf{3^{\circ}}.
The passage from the vector
(L_{1}^{n,m}
, …,
L_{m}^{n,m})
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})^{m}
to the measure
\tilde{\mu}_{n,m}
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
via the mapping

(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})\to\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}\right)}(t)dt

is a direct application of the contraction principle,
[
9
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. The continuity of the mapping is proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

The mapping from
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1})^{m}
to
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
given by

(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})\to\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt

is continuous in the weak topology.

Proof.

The distance between the two measures
\mu(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{n})}(t)dt
and
\tilde{\mu}(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\nu}_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m}\frac{i}{n})}(t)dt
is

\displaystyle d_{\text{KR}}\left(\mu,\tilde{\mu}\right)=\sup\limits_{f}\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f(\theta,t)dt\nu_{i}(d\theta)-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f(\theta,t)dt\tilde{\nu}_{i}(d\theta)\right)\right|,

where the supremum is taken over Lip
{}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
functions with
\sup_{(\theta,t)\in S^{1}\times[0,1]}|f(\theta,t)|\leqslant 1
. The function
\theta\mapsto m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f(\theta,t)dt
is again Lipschitz continuous, and moreover the norm does not increase. Thus the distance can be bounded by

\displaystyle d_{\text{KR}}\left(\mu,\tilde{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sup\limits_{f}\left|\nu_{i}\left(m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f(\cdot,t)dt\right)-\tilde{\nu}_{i}\left(m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f(\cdot,t)dt\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sup\limits_{g}\left|\nu_{i}(g)-\tilde{\nu}_{i}(g)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}d_{\text{KR}}(\nu_{i},\tilde{\nu}_{i}).\qed

Lemma 3.5
.

The measures
\tilde{\mu}_{n,m}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}^{n,m}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}\right)}(t)dt
, indexed by
n
, satisfy a large deviation principle in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
with the rate
n
and the rate function

\displaystyle I_{m}(\mu)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}H(\nu_{i}|\lambda),\text{ if }\mu(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt\text{ with }\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}),\\
+\infty,\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}:(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})\to\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}\right)}(t)dt
denote the continuous mapping of Lemma
3.4
.
By the contraction principle, see
[
9
, Theorem 4.2.1]
, the rate function is given by

\displaystyle I_{m}(\mu)=\inf\{I_{m}(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m}):(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})\in\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\mu)\},\quad\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1]).

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
has the form

\mu(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}\right)}(t)dt,\quad\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}),

then
\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\mu)=\{(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{m})\}
, and

I_{m}(\mu)=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}H(\nu_{i}|\lambda).

Otherwise,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}(\mu)=\varnothing
, and the convention is that the infinum over an empty set is
+\infty
.
∎

\mathbf{4^{\circ}}.
Lemma
3.1
implies that
\mu_{n,m}
is within distance
1/m
to
\mu_{n}
uniformly in
n
, since
\mu_{n}(S^{1}\times[0,1])=1
. This, in turn, implies exponential closeness, that is, for any
\delta>0

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}\uplim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\left[d_{\text{KR}}(\mu_{n},\mu_{n,m})>\delta\right]=-\infty,

since
\mathbb{P}\left[d_{\text{KR}}(\mu_{n},\mu_{n,m})>\delta\right]=0
for any
m>1/\delta
.

The combination of the exponential closeness of
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n,m}
, the exponential equivalence of
\mu_{n,m}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{n,m}
, Lemma
3.2
, and the LDP of
\tilde{\mu}_{n,m}
, Lemma
3.5
, together with the standard result on exponential approximations
[
9
, Theorem 4.2.16]
implies that the driving measures
\mu_{n}
satisfy the weak LDP with rate
n
and the rate function given by

\displaystyle I(\mu)=\sup\limits_{\delta>0}\lowlim\limits_{m\to\infty}I_{m}(B(\mu,\delta)).

The next lemma shows that
I
equals the relative entropy and as a consequence is a good rate function. First, recall the definition of the relative entropy: for any
\mu,\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])

\displaystyle H(\mu|\tilde{\mu})=\begin{cases}\int_{S^{1}\times[0,1]}\frac{d\mu}{d\tilde{\mu}}\log\frac{d\mu}{d\tilde{\mu}}d\tilde{\mu},&\text{ if }\mu\ll\tilde{\mu}\\
+\infty,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
,
I(\mu)=H(\mu(d\theta,dt)|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
, where
H
is the relative entropy. Moreover,
I
is a good rate function.

Proof.

Recall the rate function of
\mu_{n,m}
:

\displaystyle I_{m}(\mu)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}H(\nu_{i}|\lambda),&\text{ if }\mu(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt\text{ with }\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}),\\
\infty,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

From the approximate contraction principle the rate function of
\mu_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle I(\mu)=\sup\limits_{\delta>0}\lowlim\limits_{m\to\infty}I_{m}\left(B(\mu,\delta)\right).

First, we prove that
I(\mu)\leqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
. Assume
\mu(d\theta,dt)=\rho_{t}(\theta)\lambda(d\theta)dt
and that
\iint\rho_{t}(\theta)\log\rho_{t}(\theta)\lambda(d\theta)dt<\infty
, for otherwise the inequality is trivially satisfied. By Lemma
3.1
, the approximation
\langle\mu\rangle_{m}
can be made arbitrary close to
\mu
in the Kantorovich–Rubinshtein distance, i.e., for any
\delta>0
there exists
m_{\delta}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
m\geqslant m_{\delta}
it holds that
d_{KR}(\mu,\langle\mu\rangle_{m})<\delta
. Thus, the inclusion
\langle\mu\rangle_{m}\in B(\mu,\delta)
yields
I_{m}(B(\mu,\delta))\leqslant I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})
. However, the bound
I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})
allows for the explicit formula

\displaystyle I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}H\left(m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}\rho_{s}(\theta)ds\lambda(d\theta)\Bigg|\lambda(d\theta)\right).

The summands can be bounded with the help of Jensen’s inequality as the function
f(x)=x\log x
is convex:

\displaystyle f\left(m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}\rho_{s}(\theta)ds\right)\leqslant m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f\left(\rho_{s}(\theta)\right)ds.

Integrating with respect to
\lambda(d\theta)
gives

\displaystyle H\left(m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}\rho_{s}(\theta)ds\lambda(d\theta)\Bigg|\lambda(d\theta)\right)\leqslant\int_{0}^{2\pi}m\int_{\frac{i-1}{m}}^{\frac{i}{m}}f\left(\rho_{s}(\theta)\right)ds\lambda(d\theta),

which translates to
I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})\leqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
. Thus, the bound

\displaystyle\lowlim\limits_{m\to\infty}I_{m}(B(\mu,\delta))\leqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt),

is obtained, and after taking taking the supremum in
\delta>0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle I(\mu)\leqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt).

Second, we prove the reverse inequality
I(\mu)\geqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
. Lower semi-continuity of the relative entropy
H
can be formulated as

\displaystyle H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)=\lim\limits_{\delta\downarrow 0}\inf\limits_{\tilde{\mu}\in B(\mu,\delta)}H(\tilde{\mu}|\lambda(d\theta)dt).

Combining it with the identity

\displaystyle H(\tilde{\mu}|\lambda(d\theta)dt)=I_{m}(\tilde{\mu}),\text{ whenever }\tilde{\mu}(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt,

we obtain the following chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\tilde{\mu}\in B(\mu,\delta)}H(\tilde{\mu}|\lambda(d\theta)dt)

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf\limits\left\{H(\tilde{\mu}|\lambda(d\theta)dt):\tilde{\mu}\in B(\mu,\delta)\text{ and }\tilde{\mu}(d\theta,dt)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf\limits_{\tilde{\mu}\in B(\mu,\delta)}I_{m}(\tilde{\mu}).

The last inequality uses the fact that
I_{m}(\tilde{\mu})=\infty
if
\tilde{\mu}
is not of the form

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(d\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m})}(t)dt

, thus allowing the addition of such measures to set, over which the infinum is taken, without lowering the value of the infinum. After taking the limit in
m
and then in
\delta
, we arrive at the desired bound

\displaystyle H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)\leqslant\sup\limits_{\delta>0}\lowlim\limits_{m\to\infty}\inf\limits_{\tilde{\mu}\in B(\mu,\delta)}I_{m}(\tilde{\mu})=I(\mu).

Therefore, combining the two bounds together, the equality
I(\mu)=H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
is obtained. Moreover, since the relative entropy is a convex good rate function, so is
I
.

∎

Remark
.

In the proof above we have made use of the inequality
I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})\leqslant H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt))
, which is direct consequence of the Jensen inequality. In fact, one can show the convergence
\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}I_{m}(\langle\mu\rangle_{m})=H(\mu|\lambda(d\theta)dt)
, see
[
19
]
, where the latter and the former quantities are called coarse-grained and fine-grained entropies correspondingly.

By
[
7
, Theorem 8.9.3]
,
\mathcal{M}_{1}(S^{1}\times[0,1])
is a compact space. Thus, one can conclude that the family of probability laws of
\mu_{n}
is exponentially tight. The weak LDP of
\mu_{n}
together with exponential tightness imply the full LDP of
\mu_{n}
with a good rate function
I=H
, see
[
10
, Lemma 2.1.5]
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
. ∎

3.2
Proof sketch for Proposition
1.2

We will not provide a full proof of Proposition
1.2
, the large deviations result in continuous time, but we will content ourselves with sketching its proof, as it shares many ideas with the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Sketch of proof of Proposition
1.2
.

Since the Poisson process
(N(t),t\geqslant 0)
is independent of the discrete time dynamics (
1.1
), and given Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to consider the large deviations of the total mass, i.e., we wish to obtain the asymptotics as
\delta\to 0
of

\log\mathbb{P}(\lambda^{-1}N(t)\in E)

for any Borel measurable set of functions
\tilde{m}:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence, and where we recall that
\lambda=4/\delta^{2}
is the rate of the Poisson process (but is also the rate of the LDP in Theorem
1.1
).

Now we recall that if
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d. Poisson random variable with rate 1 then
\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with rate
n
and rate function

I(x)=x\log x-(x-1).

Using the fact that arrivals of particles during
[t,t+\varepsilon]
are given by a Poisson number of particles with mean
\lambda\varepsilon
(or, alternatively, a sum of
n=\lambda\varepsilon
i.i.d. Poisson random variables of rate 1), we deduce that
\lambda^{-1}N(t)
satisfies an LDP with rate function

\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{m}_{t}\log\tilde{m}_{t}dt-\int_{0}^{T}(\tilde{m}_{t}-1)dt

and rate
\lambda
. However, on the space
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{T}
we have necessarily
\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{m}_{t}dt=T
so that the rate function is just
\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{m}_{t}\log\tilde{m}_{t}dt=h_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{m})
, as desired.
∎

4
Geometric properties of finite entropy Loewner chains

This section completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

4.1
Loewner–Kufarev energy and Weil-Petersson quasicircles

We now prove the entropy-energy inequality of Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

We claim that if
\rho
is a probability density on
S^{1}
such that
\sqrt{\rho}
is absolutely continuous and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}/\rho(\theta)d\theta<\infty
then the following inequality is satisfied:

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho)\leqslant 2\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{\rho}),

where
h_{S^{1}}(\rho)
is the relative entropy of
\rho d\theta
with respect to
d\theta/2\pi
. This immediately implies the first claim of Theorem
1.7
.
To prove the estimate, we use the following logarithmic Sobolev inequality

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{2}\log f^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}-\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\log\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\leqslant 2\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f^{\prime}|^{2}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi},

which holds for all real
f
such that
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(S^{1})
,
\int_{S^{1}}f^{\prime}d\theta=0
, and
f
is continuous and is determined by
f^{\prime}
up to a constant; see
[
12
]
. Then, for
f(\theta)=\sqrt{2\pi\rho(\theta)}
we obtain the desired inequality:

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left(2\pi\rho(\theta)\right)\rho(\theta)d\theta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}}{\rho(\theta)}d\theta=2\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{\rho}).

The last statement of Theorem
1.7
, i.e., if
K
is any compact hull bounded by a Weil–Petersson quasicircle, then
K
can be generated by a measure with finite Loewner–Kufarev entropy, now follows from the inequality and Theorem 1.4 of
[
28
]
.
∎

Remark
.

Another link between the entropy
\mathcal{H}
and energy
S
comes from the de Bruijn identity. Let
\rho_{0}
be a probability density on
S^{1}
with
\rho_{0}^{\prime}(0)=\rho_{0}^{\prime}(2\pi)=0
. Let
\rho_{t}
solve the heat equation for
t\geqslant 0
, so that
\dot{\rho_{t}}=\rho_{t}^{\prime\prime}/2
, with Neumann boundary condition
\rho_{t}^{\prime}(0)=\rho_{t}^{\prime}(2\pi)=0
. Then
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{t}dx/2\pi\equiv 1
so we obtain a measure
\rho=(\rho_{t})_{\rho\geqslant 0}
whose marginals interpolate between
\rho_{0}
and the stationary solution
\rho_{\infty}\equiv 2\pi
. Using the heat equation and integration by parts,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{t}\log\rho_{t}d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\dot{\rho_{t}}(\log\rho_{t}+1)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{\prime\prime}_{t}\log\rho_{t}d\theta

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}(\sqrt{\rho_{t}})\leqslant 0,

with strict inequality unless
\rho_{0}\equiv 2\pi
. In this setting the Dirichlet integral on the right is sometimes called the Fisher information.
Hence, for
T\geqslant 0
, for this particular choice of measure,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{T}\log\rho_{T}d\theta-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0}d\theta=-S_{+}(\rho\mid_{[0,T]}).

Since
\rho_{t}
converges to the stationary solution with vanishing entropy as
t\to\infty
, by dominated convergence we obtain interesting formula

S_{+}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0}d\theta.

4.2
Becker quasicircles

Becker
[
3
]
gave a sufficient condition on the driving measure
\rho
to generate quasicircles via the Loewner equation, see also
[
13
]
. Consider the Loewner–Kufarev equation (
1.3
). If
w
is on the unit circle, the Möbius map
z\mapsto\tfrac{z+w}{z-w}
maps the exterior disc
\Delta=\hat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\mathbb{D}
to the half plane of points with positive real parts. Therefore, the function
H_{t}
appearing in (
1.3
)
is analytic and has positive real part in
\Delta
(such a function is sometimes called a Herglotz function). One version of Becker’s condition can be stated as follows. If there exists
\kappa\in[0,1)
such that for a.e.,
t\in[0,T]
,

H_{t}(\Delta)\subset\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\left|\frac{1-w}{1+w}\right|\leqslant\kappa\right\},

(4.1)

then
f_{t}(S^{1})
is a quasicircle for every
t\in[0,T]
, where
(f_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the solution to (
1.3
). Note that
w\mapsto(1-w)/(1+w)
is a Möbius transformation of the right half-plane onto the unit disc with
1
mapped to
0
. Since
\kappa<1
, (
4.1
) implies that
H_{t}(\Delta)
is contained in a compact subset of the right half-plane containing
1
. We say that
\gamma
is a (finite time) Becker quasicircle if
\gamma=f_{t}(S^{1})
for some Loewner chain satisfying (
4.1
). Neither the Becker condition nor the
S(\rho)<\infty
Weil-Petersson condition implies the other. Note that
\kappa=0
if and only if
\rho_{t}=d\theta dt/2\pi
.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\rho\in\mathcal{N}_{T}
satisfies (
4.1
), then
\mathcal{H}(\rho)<CT
, where
C<\infty
depends only on
\kappa
.

Proof.

Suppose
H_{t}
satisfies (
4.1
). Then
H_{t}
is a bounded analytic function in
\Delta
. It follows (see e.g. Corollary 2 to Theorem 3.1 of
[
11
]
) that
\Re\,H_{t}
is the Poisson integral of an
L^{\infty}(S^{1})
function
\rho_{t}
, that is, the measure
\rho_{t}(d\theta)
is absolutely continuous with
L^{\infty}
density
\rho_{t}
. Moreover,
M:=\|\rho_{t}\|_{\infty}
depends only on
\kappa
. Next we note that there exists
C
depending only on
\kappa
such that,

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{S^{1}}\rho_{t}(\theta)\log\rho_{t}(\theta)d\theta\leqslant\sup_{r\in[0,M]}\left|r\log r\right|<C.

Integrating over
[0,T]
, we see that
\mathcal{H}(\rho)<CT<\infty
, as claimed. (In fact, a similar argument can be used to show that if
\Re H_{t}(\Delta)
is bounded, then the same conclusion applies.)
∎

4.3
Examples of finite entropy shapes

We now discuss several examples where the entropy can be computed or estimated. These examples combined with Theorem
1.7
prove Theorem
1.8
.

Inward-pointing corner

This example is based on
[
27
]
.
The conformal map
\varphi_{t}(z)=e^{t}\frac{z-1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4z+e^{2t}(1-z)^{2}}
takes the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
to the union
\mathbb{D}\cup B(e^{t},\sqrt{e^{2t}-1})
. Rotating the hull and applying the Schwarz reflection we get the map

f_{t}(z)=-1/\varphi_{t}(-1/z)
that takes the exterior disk
\Delta
to the complement of the compact hull. Figure
2
illustrates the hull for different values of
t>0
.

Figure 2
:
The growing hull given by the map
f_{t}:\Delta\to\Delta\smallsetminus B(e^{t},\sqrt{e^{2t}-1})

The probability density that generates such hulls is

\rho_{t}(\theta)=\tilde{\rho}_{t}(\pi+\theta)\mathds{1}_{[0,\pi)}(\theta)+\tilde{\rho}(\pi-\theta)\mathds{1}_{[\pi,2\pi]}(\theta),

where

\tilde{\rho}_{t}(\theta)=\left(\frac{e^{-t}}{\pi(1-e^{-2t})}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\sqrt{e^{2t}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-1}\right)\mathds{1}_{I_{t}}(\theta),

and
I_{t}=[2\arcsin(e^{-t}),2\pi-2\arcsin(e^{-t})].

For a fixed time
t>0
, the relative entropy of this density with respect to the uniform measure on
S^{1}
equals

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\rho}_{t}(\theta)\log(2\pi\tilde{\rho}_{t}(\theta))d\theta

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{2}{e^{2t}-1}\right)+\frac{2}{\pi(e^{2t}-1)}\int_{1}^{e^{t}}x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\log\left(x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)\frac{dx}{\sqrt{e^{2t}-x^{2}}}<+\infty.

As
t\to 0
the probability density
\rho_{t}
localizes at one point and the entropy
h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})
diverges. However, as it diverges slower than
1/\sqrt{t}
, the total entropy
\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})dt
is finite.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\rho)\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\log\left(\frac{2}{e^{2t}-1}\right)}_{\sim\log\frac{1}{t}}dt+\frac{2e\sqrt{e^{2}-1}\log(e\sqrt{e^{2}-1})}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\frac{\arccos(e^{-t})}{e^{2t}-1}}_{\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}dt<+\infty.

Inward-pointing cusp and non-Jordan curve

This example is also based on
[
27
]
.
Consider the conformal maps

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{s}(z)=1+\frac{2\pi is}{\log\left(\frac{z-e^{-i\pi s}}{z-e^{i\pi s}}\right)},\quad 0\leqslant s<1,

that map the exterior disk
\Delta=\{|z|>1\}
to the complement of hulls depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3
:
The hulls grown by the conformal map
\tilde{f}_{s}:\Delta\to\Delta\smallsetminus B(\frac{1}{1-s},\frac{1+s}{1-s})
. Note that the interfaces are not Jordan curves.

Note that the time-parametrization differs from the standard one. We have
\tilde{f}_{s}(z)=\beta(s)z+o(z)
, where

\beta(s)=\frac{\pi s}{\sin(\pi s)}
. One can go back to the standard capacity parametrization
f_{t}(z)=\tilde{f}_{\alpha(t)}(z)=e^{t}z+o(z)
with the function
\alpha(t)=\beta^{-1}(e^{t})
.
The probability density is

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{s}(\theta)=\frac{\cos(\theta)-\cos(\pi s)}{2s\sin(\pi s)}\mathds{1}_{(-\pi s,\pi s)}(\theta),\quad 0\leqslant s<1,\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{\rho}_{s}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{\beta^{\prime}(s)}{\beta(s)};

or after time reparametrization one obtains

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\theta)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha(t)}(\theta)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)\frac{\cos(\theta)-\cos(\pi\alpha(t))}{2\alpha(t)\sin(\pi\alpha(t))}\mathds{1}_{(-\pi\alpha(t),\pi\alpha(t))}(\theta),\quad t>0.

For a fixed time
t>0
, the relative entropy of the density with respect to the uniform measure on
S^{1}
equals

h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})=\log\left(\frac{\pi\alpha^{\prime}(t)}{\alpha(t)\sin(\pi\alpha(t))}\right)+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}{\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha(t))}I(\alpha(t))<\infty,

where

\displaystyle I(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi\alpha}(\cos(\theta)-\cos(\pi\alpha))\log(\cos(\theta)-\cos(\pi\alpha))d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\cos(\pi\alpha)}\frac{x\log x}{\sqrt{1-(x+\cos(\pi\alpha))^{2}}}dx.

The function
I
can be bounded by

\displaystyle|I(\alpha)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\cos(\pi\alpha)}}\left|\int_{0}^{1-\cos(\pi\alpha)}\frac{x\log x}{\sqrt{(1-\cos(\pi\alpha))-x}}dx\right|,

where the integral is computed explicitly; for
a>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{x\log x}{\sqrt{a-x}}dx=\frac{4}{9}a^{3/2}(3\log a-5+\log 64).

Thus, as
\alpha\to 0
,
|I(\alpha)|\sim\alpha^{3}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}
.
The derivative of
\alpha
is given by

\alpha^{\prime}(t)=\frac{e^{t}\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)}{\pi(\sin(\pi\alpha)-\pi\alpha\cos(\pi\alpha))},

so that

\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)}=\frac{e^{t}\frac{\sin(\pi\alpha)}{\pi\alpha}}{\sin(\pi\alpha)-\pi\alpha\cos(\pi\alpha)}=\frac{1}{\sin(\pi\alpha)-\pi\alpha\cos(\pi\alpha)}.

As
\alpha\to 0

\displaystyle\sin(\pi\alpha)-\pi\alpha\cos(\pi\alpha)\sim\frac{(\pi\alpha)^{3}}{3}+o(\alpha^{3}).

Hence, it follows that the total entropy
\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})dt
is finite

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\alpha(t))-\pi\alpha(t)\cos(\pi\alpha(t))}\right)}_{\sim\log t}dt+\int_{0}^{\alpha(1)}\underbrace{\frac{I(\alpha)}{\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)}}_{\sim\alpha\log\alpha}d\alpha<+\infty.

Outward-pointing cusps

Consider the time-independent probability density given by the Poisson kernel:

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\theta)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{R^{2}-1}{1-2R\cos(\theta)+R^{2}},

where
R>1
is a fixed constant.
The conformal map that solves Loewner–Kufarev equation, driven by this density, is given by

\displaystyle f_{t}(z)=\frac{1}{R}\left[e^{t}\frac{Rz+1}{2Rz}\left(Rz+1+\sqrt{R^{2}z^{2}+1+2Rz(1-2e^{-t})}\right)-1\right],\quad z\in\Delta.

The derivation of this conformal map can be found, for example, in
[
26
]
along with many other examples of Loewner chains driven by densities. Figure
4
illustrates the hulls generated by the Poisson kernel density for different values of the parameter
R>1
.

(a)

R=1.1

(b)

R=1.5

(c)

R=2

Figure 4
:
The hulls generated by the Poisson kernel density with constant parameter
R>1
.

For a fixed-time
t>0
, the relative entropy
h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})
equals

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{R^{2}-1}{1-2R\cos(\theta)+R^{2}}\log\left(\frac{R^{2}-1}{1-2R\cos(\theta)+R^{2}}\right)\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\frac{R-1}{R+1}}^{\frac{R+1}{R-1}}\frac{\frac{1}{x}\log\frac{1}{x}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{R+1}{R-1}-x\right)\left(x-\frac{R-1}{R+1}\right)}}dx.

After the change of variable
y=(x-\frac{R-1}{R+1})\frac{R^{2}-1}{4R}
we obtain

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}+\frac{4R}{R^{2}-1}y}\log\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}+\frac{4R}{R^{2}-1}y}}{\sqrt{(1-y)y}}dy.

Since
\frac{R-1}{R+1}\leqslant\frac{R-1}{R+1}+\frac{4R}{R^{2}-1}y\leqslant\frac{R+1}{R-1}
, the logarithm takes both positive and negative values, and changes the sign at
y=\frac{R-1}{2R}
. We can write down the following estimate

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{R-1}{2R}}\frac{\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}+\frac{4R}{R^{2}-1}y}\log\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}+\frac{4R}{R^{2}-1}y}}{\sqrt{(1-y)y}}dy

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}}\log\frac{1}{\frac{R-1}{R+1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{R-1}{2R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-y)y}}dy

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{R-1}{2R}}\right)\frac{R+1}{R-1}\log\frac{R+1}{R-1}.

As
R\downarrow 1
, the upper bound essentially behaves like
\frac{1}{\sqrt{R-1}}\log\frac{1}{R-1}
.

Now, let us consider the probability density given by the Poisson kernel where the parameter
R=R(t)
is time-dependent:

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{R(t)^{2}-1}{1-2R(t)\cos(\theta)+R(t)^{2}}.

Three different choices of
R=R(t)
are illustrated in Figure
5
.

(a)

R(t)=1+\sqrt{1-t}

(b)

R(t)=\frac{1}{t}

(c)

R(t)=1+e^{-\frac{1}{1-t}}

Figure 5
:
The hulls grown by the Poisson kernel density with different choices of
R=R(t)
.

The idea is that if we chose
R=R(t)
appropriately, so that
\lim\limits_{t\to 1}R(t)=1
, then at time
t=1
the hull will form a cusp.

For a fixed
t>0
, the relative entropy
h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})
equals the same expression that we have computed for a constant parameter
R>1
above:

\displaystyle h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho_{t}(\theta)\log\rho_{t}(\theta)\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\leqslant\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{R(t)-1}{2R(t)}}\right)\frac{R(t)+1}{R(t)-1}\log\frac{R(t)+1}{R(t)-1}.

The total entropy
\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})dt
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\rho)\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{R(t)-1}{2R(t)}}\right)\frac{R(t)+1}{R(t)-1}\log\frac{R(t)+1}{R(t)-1}dt.

For both
R(t)=1+\sqrt{1-t}
and
R(t)=1/t
, the upper bound if finite, so they provide examples of finite entropy hulls.

Next step is to investigate the derivative of the conformal map
f_{t}
at time
t=1
around the point
z=1
. The conformal map
g_{t}=f_{t}^{-1}
, from
\Delta_{t}=f_{t}(\Delta)
to
\Delta
, satisfies the Loewner equation

\dot{g}_{t}(z)=-g_{t}(z)p_{t}(g_{t}(z)),\quad z\in\Delta_{t},

with
g_{0}(z)=z
, and where

p_{t}(z)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{z+e^{i\theta}}{z-e^{i\theta}}\rho_{t}(d\theta)

is an analytic function with positive real part and
p_{t}(\infty)=1
. Differentiating the Loewner equation with respect to
z
and then integrating with respect to
t
one gets

\displaystyle g_{t}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{g_{t}(z)}{z}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}p_{s}^{\prime}(g_{s})ds\right).

This translates to the derivative of
f_{t}
via
f_{t}^{\prime}(z)=1/(g_{t}^{\prime}(f_{t}(z)))
:

\displaystyle f_{t}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{f_{t}(z)}{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}(f_{t})p_{s}^{\prime}(g_{s}(f_{t}))ds\right).

With the help of the identity

\log\frac{p_{t}(g_{t}(z))}{p_{0}(z)}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\dot{p}_{s}(g_{s})+p_{s}^{\prime}(s_{s})\dot{g}_{s}}{p_{s}(g_{s})}ds,

which combined with the Loewner equation becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}p_{s}^{\prime}(g_{s})ds=\log\frac{p_{0}(z)}{p_{t}(g_{t})}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\dot{p}_{s}(g_{s})}{p_{s}(g_{s})}ds,

the expression
f_{t}^{\prime}(z)
transforms into

\displaystyle f_{t}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{f_{t}(z)p_{0}(f_{t}(z))}{zp_{t}(z)}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\dot{p}_{s}(g_{s}\circ f_{t})}{p_{s}(g_{s}\circ f_{t})}ds\right).

Specifying
\rho_{t}
to be the Poisson kernel density one can compute
p_{t}(z)
explicitly, see
[
26
]
for details:

p_{t}(z)=\frac{R_{t}z+1}{R_{t}z-1}.

Then,
f_{t}^{\prime}
becomes

\displaystyle f_{t}^{\prime}(z)=(R_{t}z-1)\frac{f_{t}(z)(R_{0}f_{t}+1)}{z(R_{0}f_{t}-1)(R_{t}z+1)}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{-2\dot{R}_{s}(g_{s}\circ f_{t})(z)}{R^{2}_{s}(g_{s}\circ f_{t})^{2}(z)-1}ds\right),

and at
t=1
, that is, when
R_{1}=1
:

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime}(z)=(z-1)\frac{f_{1}(z)(R_{0}f_{1}(z)+1)}{z(R_{0}f_{1}(z)-1)(z+1)}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{-2(g_{s}\circ f_{1})(z)}{R^{2}_{s}(g_{s}\circ f_{1})^{2}(z)-1}\dot{R}_{s}ds\right).

To control the function inside the exponent denote
\phi_{s}=g_{s}\circ f_{1}
and write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{-2\phi_{s}(z)}{R^{2}_{s}\phi_{s}(z)^{2}-1}\dot{R}_{s}ds=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2\frac{\dot{R}_{s}}{R_{s}^{2}}}{(\phi_{s}(z)+\frac{1}{R_{s}})^{2}}\dot{\phi}_{s}(z)ds,

where we used the identity

\frac{-\phi_{s}(z)}{R_{s}\phi_{s}(z)-1}=\frac{\dot{\phi}_{s}(z)}{\phi_{s}(z)+1}

which follows from the Loewner equation.

For the case
R_{t}=1/t
, the integral inside the exponent in the expression for
f_{1}^{\prime}
is well-behaved:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{-2}{(\phi_{s}(z)+s)^{2}}\dot{\phi}_{s}(z)ds=\frac{2}{z+1}-\frac{2}{f_{t}(z)}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{(\phi_{s}(z)+s)^{2}}ds,

where

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{(\phi_{s}(z)+s)^{2}}ds\right|\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{|z+s|^{2}}ds=\frac{2}{\Im(z)}\left(\text{arctan}\left(\frac{\Re(z)+1}{\Im(z)}\right)-\text{arctan}\left(\frac{\Re(z)}{\Im(z)}\right)\right).

Hence, the derivative can be factored as

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime}(z)=(z-1)q(z),

where

\displaystyle q(z)=\frac{f_{1}(z)(R_{0}f_{1}(z)+1)}{z(R_{0}f_{1}(z)-1)(z+1)}\exp\left(\frac{2}{z+1}-\frac{2}{f_{t}(z)}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2}{(\phi_{s}(z)+s)^{2}}ds\right).

Note that
\lim\limits_{z\to 1}q(z)\neq 0
. Therefore, around
z=1
, the derivative behaves like
f_{1}^{\prime}(z)\sim z-1
. Moreover, the limit
\lim\limits_{z\to 1}q^{\prime}(z)
exists and does not equal
\infty
.

Now we are in a position to show that the choice
R(t)=1/t
produces a hull with a cusp at time
t=1
. Recall that
f(\partial\Delta)
is said to have an
inward-pointing cusp
at
f(1)\neq\infty
if

\displaystyle\arg(f(e^{i\vartheta})-f(1))\to\begin{cases}\beta\quad&\text{as }\vartheta\to 0+,\\
\beta+2\pi&\text{as }\vartheta\to 0-.\end{cases}

Remark
.

Note that here
inward-pointing
is understood from the perspective of the conformal map
f:\Delta\to D
, and it would be
outward-pointing
from the perspective of a conformal map from the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
to the inside of the hull.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
22
]
, Theorem 3.7)
.

Let
f:\Delta\to D
be conformal and
\partial D
be locally connected. Then
f(\partial\Delta)
has a cusp at
f(1)\neq\infty
if and only if

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{z\to 1}\arg\frac{f(z)-f(1)}{(z-1)^{2}}=\beta-\pi.

The Loewner map
f_{t}
at time
t=1
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{z\to 1}\frac{f_{1}(z)-f_{1}(1)}{(z-1)^{2}}=\lim\limits_{z\to 1}\frac{f_{1}^{\prime}(z)}{2(z-1)}=\lim\limits_{z\to 1}\frac{q(z)}{2}=\frac{q(1)}{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{z\to 1}\arg\frac{f_{1}(z)-f_{1}(1)}{(z-1)^{2}}=0,

and by continuity

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\vartheta\to 0}\arg\frac{f_{1}(e^{i\vartheta})-f_{1}(1)}{(e^{i\vartheta}-1)^{2}}=0.

This immediately implies

\displaystyle\arg\left(f_{1}(e^{i\vartheta})-f_{1}(1)\right)=\begin{cases}\pi&\vartheta\to 0+,\\
\pi+2\pi&\vartheta\to 0-.\end{cases}

Therefore, the family
(f_{t}(\partial\mathbb{D}))_{t\in[0,1]}
forms an inwards-pointing cusp at time
t=1
at the point
f_{1}(1)
, and confirms the simulation in Figure
6
.

R(t)=1/t
.

5
Minimizing the entropy

In this section we discuss several optimization problems motivated by the problem to compute more explicitly the minimizing rate function
\mathcal{H}^{*}
in Corollary
1.3
. We will also discuss the example showing that the equipotential measure does not in general give the minimal entropy.

5.1
Loewner–Kufarev evolution with constraint

While not exactly the setting of Corollary
1.3
, the following formulation is convenient to work with.

Problem 5.1

(Whole-plane Loewner–Kufarev entropy with prescribed interface)
.

Let
\gamma
be a Jordan curve separating
0
from
\infty
and assume that the capacity of
\gamma
equals
e^{T}
.

\displaystyle\textrm{Minimize}\quad\mathcal{H}(\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{T}h_{S^{1}}(\rho_{t})dt,

(5.1)

where
\rho\in\mathcal{N}
is subject to the constraint that
\partial\Delta_{T}=\gamma
, where
\Delta_{t}=f_{t}(\Delta)
and
(f_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the Loewner chain of
\rho
.

The analog of Problem
5.1
with entropy replaced by energy was solved in
[
28
]
and a solution exists if and only if
\gamma
is a Weil-Petersson quasicircle, i.e., has finite Loewner energy. Let
D
be the bounded component of
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\gamma
. Consider the
equipotential foliation
of
D
defined as follows. Let
f:\mathbb{D}\to D
be the conformal map such that
f(0)=0,f^{\prime}(0)>0
. The images under
f
of concentric circles form a foliation of
D
which can be described by Loewner evolution. Similarly, we can use the exterior normalized conformal map
g
to foliate the exterior
\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus D
by equipotentials. Write
\rho^{\gamma}_{\textrm{eq.}}
for the corresponding driving measure with marginals integrating to
1
. Note that
\rho^{\gamma}_{\textrm{eq.}}\equiv 1/2\pi d\theta dt
for
t\geqslant 0
(this uses that
\gamma
has capacity
1
).

Theorem 5.2

(
[
28
]
, Theorem 1.4)
.

We have

\inf S(\rho)=S(\rho^{\gamma}_{\textrm{eq.}})=\frac{1}{16}I^{L}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{8}\log\frac{|f^{\prime}(0)|}{|g^{\prime}(\infty)|},

where the infimum is taken over
\rho\in\mathcal{N}
generating
\gamma
as a shape and
I^{L}
is the Loewner energy of
\gamma
.

Corollary 5.3
.

The minimal entropy
\mathcal{H}^{*}(\gamma)
needed to generate
\gamma
satisfies

\mathcal{H}^{*}(\gamma)\leqslant\frac{1}{8}I^{L}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{|f^{\prime}(0)|}{|g^{\prime}(\infty)|}.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.7
we know that
\mathcal{H}(\rho)\leqslant 2S(\rho)
, so the estimate follows immediately from this estimate combined with Theorem
5.2
.
∎

Given the corollary, one may ask whether equipotentials provide the solution to Problem
5.1
as well. This is however not the case.

Proposition 5.4
.

Consider Problem
5.1
. There exists a Jordan curve
\gamma
such that

\mathcal{H}^{*}(\gamma)<\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\gamma}_{\textrm{eq.}}),

where
\rho^{\gamma}_{\textrm{eq.}}
is the driving measure of the equipotential foliation corresponding to
\gamma
.

Sketch of proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be very small and consider the measure
\rho^{\epsilon}
defined by
\rho_{t}^{\epsilon}\equiv 1/2\pi
for
t\leqslant 0
and

\rho_{t}^{\epsilon}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi(1-2\epsilon)}\mathds{1}_{(\epsilon,2\pi-\epsilon)}(\theta),\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1.

The hull at time at time
1
is a “Pac-Man” shaped compact set
K_{\epsilon}\supset\mathbb{D}
whose boundary is a Jordan curve
\gamma_{\epsilon}
containing the interval
\{e^{i\theta}:\,\theta\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\}
. Write
D_{\epsilon}
for the simply connected interior of
K_{\epsilon}
. Note that
\gamma_{\epsilon}\cap[1,\infty)=\{1\}
and, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the interiors
D_{\epsilon}
converge in the Carathéordory sense towards the slit disc
D_{0}:=(e\mathbb{D})\smallsetminus[1,e]
. Since the conformal maps from the unit disc converge uniformly on compacts, the equipotential foliations converge and so do the corresponding driving measures.

By Lemma
2.1

\mathcal{H}
is lower semi-continuous. Therefore, if we let
\rho_{\textrm{eq.}}^{D_{\epsilon}}
be the driving measure of the equipotential foliation of
D_{\epsilon}
considered up to time
1
,

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathcal{H}(\rho_{\textrm{eq.}}^{D_{\epsilon}})\geqslant\mathcal{H}(\rho_{\textrm{eq.}}^{D_{0}})>0.

On the other hand, we compute that
\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\epsilon})=O(\epsilon)
. It follows that if
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small then

\mathcal{H}(\rho^{\epsilon})<\mathcal{H}(\rho_{\textrm{eq.}}^{D_{\epsilon}}),

as claimed.
∎

5.2
Non-analytic Loewner evolution as a transport equation

We now consider a simpler optimization problem. It is convenient to work in the half-plane,
chordal
, setting.
First recall that the chordal Loewner–Kufarev PDE is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{t}(z)=-f^{\prime}_{t}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\mu_{t}(x)}{z-x},\qquad z\in\mathbb{H},

(5.2)

where
(\mu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is usually assumed to be a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose that
(\rho_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a measurable family of real densities
d\mu_{t}=\rho_{t}(x)dx
, smooth say, such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)dx=1
.
For each
t
, let
P_{t}
be the Poisson integral of
\rho_{t}
, that is,

P_{t}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho_{t}(\xi)d\xi}{z-\xi},\qquad z=x+iy.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the following real PDE,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,y)=P_{t}(x,y)\partial_{y}u(t,y),\qquad u(0,y)=y.

(5.3)

This is a transport equation in non-conservative form, with velocity field
t\mapsto P_{t}(x,y)
, which depends on
\rho_{t}
in a non-local manner. Note that
u
depends implicitly on
x
.
A similar equation was considered by Carleson and Makarov who interpreted it as a non-analytic simplification of the Loewner–Kufarev equation:
(
5.3
) arises up to a factor
\pi
after taking the imaginary part of (
5.2
) (with
\mu_{t}
replaced by
\rho_{t}
) and ignoring the
x
-dependence, see Section 1.5 of
[
8
]
. In this context we view (
5.3
) as describing the evolution of a non-analytic map

U_{t}:x+iy\mapsto x+iu^{x}(t,y),\quad U_{0}(x+iy)=x+iy,

and the image of the unit interval describes an interface or “shape”.

We are interested in the following optimization problem, where we recall the notation
h_{\mathbb{R}}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\log fdx
.

Problem 5.5

(Non-analytic Loewner evolution)
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a smooth function.

\displaystyle\textrm{Minimize}\quad H(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}h_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho_{t})dt,

(5.4)

where
\rho
is subject to the following constraints:

•

For each
t\in[0,1]
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)dx=1,

•

\rho
generates
\gamma
in the sense that for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
u(t,y)=u^{x}(t,y)
satisfies (
5.3
) with velocity field
P_{t}
and

\lim_{y\to 0+}u(1,y)=\gamma(x).

In other words, we prescribe the interface
U_{1}(x)=\gamma(x)
at time
1
and ask for a measure with minimal entropy that produces this interface via (
5.3
). This is a version of Problem
5.1
. However, while the dynamics is simpler, solutions do not generate conformal maps which results in loss of a source of integrability. Moreover, even though (
5.3
) can be solved by characteristics via the ODE

\dot{Y}=-P_{t}(x,Y),\qquad Y(0)=y_{0},

the non-linear dependence on
\rho
still results in a complicated constraint. Even determining a useful condition for
\gamma
to be generated by (
5.3
) with some
\rho
appears non-trivial.
To make progress, we will further simplify (
5.3
) by replacing
P_{t}(x,y)
by
\rho_{t}(x)=\lim_{y\to 0+}P_{t}(x,y)
and for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain the following transport equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,y)=\rho_{t}(x)\partial_{y}v(t,y),\qquad v(0,y)=y.

(5.5)

Since the right-hand side of (
5.5
) can be written
\partial_{t}u=\partial_{y}(\rho_{t}v)
the equation is conservative. (Recall that
x
is a parameter and not a variable.) Now it is not hard to determine what
\gamma
can be generated and we formulate the following version of the problem.

Problem 5.6

(Conservative non-analytic Loewner evolution)
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a smooth function.

\displaystyle\textrm{Minimize}\quad H(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}h_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho_{t})dt,

(5.6)

where
\rho
is subject to the following constraints:

•

For each
t\in[0,1]
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)dx=1,

•

\rho
generates
\gamma
in the sense that for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
v(t,y)=v^{x}(t,y)
satisfies (
5.5
) with velocity field
\rho
and

\lim_{y\to 0+}v(1,y)=\gamma(x).

Proposition 5.7
.

A solution to Problem
5.6
exists if and only if
\gamma
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(x)dx=1
and
h_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma)<\infty
, in which case the solution is constant in
t
,

\rho^{*}_{t}(x)\equiv\gamma(x).

The minimal entropy equals the entropy of
\gamma
,

H^{*}=h_{\mathbb{R}}(\gamma).

Proof.

The solution to (
5.5
) is

v(t,y)=y+\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{t}(x)dt.

Therefore, the condition that
\rho
generates
\gamma
becomes

v(1,0)=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}(x)dt=\gamma(x)

and so all constraints are linear.
Combined with the requirement that
\int\rho_{t}dx=1
this gives the necessary condition
\int\gamma(x)dx=1
. Consider now the action functional with Lagrange multipliers corresponding to the constraints

L(\rho,\lambda,\mu)=H(\rho)+\int_{0}^{1}\lambda(t)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho_{t}(x)dx-1\right)dt+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mu(x)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}(x)dt-\gamma(x)\right)dx.

The Euler-Lagrange equation for
\rho
is

\log\rho+1+\lambda+\mu=0,

so a stationary solution can be written in the form

\rho_{t}(x)=\exp\{-1-\lambda(t)-\mu(x)\}=:T(t)X(x).

On the other hand, the condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)dx=1
implies
T(t)\equiv 1
and finally the condition
\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{t}(x)dt=\gamma(x)
implies
X(x)=\gamma(x)
.
∎
