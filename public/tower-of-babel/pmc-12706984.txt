Evaluation of protein profile in vitreous samples of patients with naive age-related macular degeneration using proteomic approaches
Authors: Hamza Kose, Berna Ozkan, Aylin Kanli, Levent V. Karabas, Gurler Akpinar, Murat Kasap, Fatma Sumer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge-related macular degeneration (AMD) represents one of the most common causes of permanent vision impairment in individuals over 50 years of age.MethodsThis study aims to characterize AMD using proteomic analysis to enhance diagnosis and treatment strategies. In a prospective case-control clinical trial, vitreous fluids (VF) from thirteen AMD patients were collected during surgery and analyzed by 2DE-based MALDI TOF-TOF MS/MS. PANTHER and STRING analyses were performed to investigate the biological relationships between the identified proteins and to determine relevant cellular pathways.ResultsA total of 11 proteins were differentially regulated between AMD patients and healthy controls. Among them, Apolipoprotein E was significantly up-regulated (↑3-fold), while ten proteins, including alpha-crystallin A chain (↓779-fold), beta-crystallin B2 (↓232-fold), and haptoglobin (↓15-fold), were markedly down-regulated. These quantitative differences underscore the critical role of lipid metabolism, oxidative stress response, and inflammation in AMD pathogenesis.ConclusionThe identified proteins are related to biological regulation, retinal protection, and regulation of inflammation and angiogenesis processes. The up-regulation of Apolipoprotein E highlights its involvement in lipid metabolism and inflammatory modulation, while the sharp down-regulation of crystallin family proteins suggests compromised retinal protection against oxidative stress and apoptosis. These protein alterations provide new insights into AMD pathogenesis and may serve as potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and novel therapeutic targets in managing the disease.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of irreversible vision loss among the elderly, particularly affecting individuals over the age of 50. This multifactorial disease is characterized by progressive degeneration of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptors, leading to the formation of drusen and, in advanced stages, choroidal neovascularization (CNV) [1, 2]. The two primary forms of AMD, dry (atrophic) and wet (neovascular), exhibit distinct pathological features, with wet AMD being responsible for the majority of severe visual impairment [1, 2]. Given the increasing prevalence of AMD in aging populations worldwide, understanding its underlying mechanisms is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Globally, AMD affects nearly 196 million people, and this number is expected to rise to 288 million by 2040 due to an aging population [3]. In Turkey, the prevalence of AMD among individuals older than 60 years is estimated to range between 8 and 15% based on regional European data, as no specific population-based studies have been conducted in the Turkish population [3]. The disease is more frequently observed in women compared to men, likely due to longer life expectancy, and its prevalence increases sharply with age. AMD has also been associated with systemic comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, suggesting shared pathogenic mechanisms involving oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction. Currently, anti-VEGF therapy remains the primary treatment option for wet AMD, while there are limited effective therapeutic strategies for the dry form, emphasizing the need for novel approaches.

Proteomic analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating the complex molecular landscape of AMD. By employing advanced techniques such as mass spectrometry and two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, researchers can identify and quantify proteins that are differentially expressed in AMD patients compared to healthy controls [4]. This approach not only aids in the discovery of potential biomarkers for early diagnosis but also enhances our understanding of the pathophysiological processes involved in AMD, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune responses [5]. For instance, studies have highlighted the role of complement system proteins in the progression of AMD, suggesting that genetic predispositions may influence individual susceptibility to the disease [6, 7].

In the contemporary context of a steadily growing elderly population, the likelihood of developing AMD is correspondingly increasing. Consequently, research aimed at uncovering the etiology and pathogenesis of this disease is becoming increasingly critical. Despite recent advances in proteomic research on exudative AMD, a comprehensive analysis of vitreous humor in the treatment-naive AMD patients, particularly focusing on early-stage inflammatory and metabolic protein alterations, remains underexplored. For instance, Koss et al. performed proteomic profiling of vitreous humor in the exudative AMD and found several up-regulated proteins involved in acute phase inflammatory reaction, coagulation and ion transport, but did not emphasize early metabolic markers [8]. Moreover, more recent work by dos Santos et al. showed that vitreous proteome alterations include complement, coagulation and neurodegeneration-related proteins across vitreoretinal diseases including AMD, highlighting the importance of exploring local vitreous changes [9]. Vitreous fluid offers unique advantages including direct anatomical proximity to the retina and macula, minimal systemic interference, and real-time reflection of local pathological processes compared to systemic fluids such as serum or plasma. However, it should also be noted that vitreous sampling is invasive and protein concentration is relatively low, which may limit sensitivity and require careful methodological design. By focusing on vitreous proteomics in naïve AMD patients using 2D gel-based MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometry, this study aims to address these gaps and provide new insights into the early molecular mechanisms underlying AMD. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to characterize the vitreous humor proteome in treatment-naïve AMD patients using 2D gel electrophoresis coupled with MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometry, with specific focus on identifying early-stage inflammatory and metabolic protein alterations that may serve as potential biomarkers for disease progression and novel therapeutic targets.

Material and method

Study population

This study was designed as a prospective case-control study. The study included thirteen eyes of thirteen patients who were evaluated in Kocaeli University Ophthalmology Clinic, especially in the Retina Department between May 2016 and January 2018. The inclusion criteria were naive patients diagnosed with age-related macular degeneration and scheduled for anti-VEGF injection. The diagnosis of nAMD and the determination of the need for treatment was made by two independent retina specialists and a rigorous evaluation process was ensured.

The control group consisted of the eyes of eleven patients, specifically selected from individuals scheduled to undergo standard cataract surgery. Importantly, none of the patients in this control group had been diagnosed with AMD. To ensure comparability, the same exclusion criteria were applied equally to both control and study groups. The exclusion criteria included any previous treatment for CNV lesions, previous vitrectomy, laser coagulation, previous participation in any studies using anti-VEGF drugs, intraocular surgery (including cataract surgery), glaucoma in the study eye, diabetes mellitus, use of immunosuppressive drugs, or malignant tumors in any location.

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: all neovascular AMD subtypes (type 1-hidden choroidal neovascularization and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, type 2-classical choroidal neovascularization, and type 3-retinal angiomatous proliferation). The diagnosis was initially made by fluorescein angiography and SD-OCT. Only naive patients with recent disease onset without advanced fibrosis or hemorrhage, confirmed by history and clinical findings, were included in the study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to treatment and all procedures were performed at Kocaeli University Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kocaeli University Hospital (Kocaeli, Turkey). The study was conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 13 the AMD patients and 11 control subjects were included. We acknowledge that this represents a relatively small cohort for proteomic studies. Therefore, this study should be considered a pilot exploratory analysis aimed at generating hypotheses and identifying candidate proteins for subsequent validation in larger, independent cohorts. While a formal power analysis was not feasible for this discovery-based design, the primary goal was to explore proteomic alterations and provide a preliminary dataset for future studies.

Clinical assessment

All the subjects underwent a complete ophthalmic examination consisting of best corrected visual acuity; slit lamp biomicroscopy of the anterior segment; fundoscopic examination after dilation of the pupils with 0.5% tropicamide and 5% phenylephrine hydrochloride; and fundus photography via optical coherence tomography (spectral domain OCT, software version 5.6.3.0, Heidelberg, Germany) and fundus fluorescein angiography (Topcon TRC 50DX retinal camera [FFA]). The diagnosis was made using clinical examination, optical coherence tomography and fundus fluorescein angiography by two independent retina specialists (BO-LK).

The AMD group was included in the study according to their clinical classification. As shown in Table 1, the severity stages (i.e., early, intermediate, and late (exudative) AMD) were established according to drusen size and AMD pigmentary abnormalities. AMD pigmentary abnormalities were defined as hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation present within 2 disc diameters of the center of the macula in eyes with drusen ≤ 63 μm in diameter and without known retinal disease entities or other reasons for such abnormalities. Exudative type AMD was defined as the presence of choroidal neovascularization in patients older than 50 years of age and without any other conditions related to choroidal neovascularization, such as degenerative myopia, ocular histoplasmosis, or angioid streaks. Patients with exudative-type AMD can be divided into 3 groups according to clinical, angiographic and spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) findings: type 1 neovascularization (hidden choroidal neovascularization and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy), type 2 neovascularization (classical choroidal neovascularization), and type 3 neovascularization (retinal angiomatous proliferation) [10]. Dry-type AMD was defined as the presence of bilateral soft drusen with or without retinal pigment abnormalities or geographic atrophy. Vitreous collection

In the control group and study group after 2 cc (lidocaine 50% + bupivacaine 50%) peribulbar anesthesia, the eyelids, and periocular area were wiped with a 10% poviodine and iodine-soaked gauze swab. The surgical area was covered with a sterile drape, and a sterile eyelid retractor was placed. Then, the ocular surface and conjunctival fornix were washed with 5% poviodine iodine. After 3 min, the ocular surface was washed with a sterile isotonic solution. In the AMD groups, the transconjunctival 23-gauge trockar was located, and a 0.5 mL vitreous sample was collected with the vitrectomy probe. The transconjunctival sclerotomy trocar was removed, and the pressure was applied with a cotton swab. Then, 0.5 mg ranibizumab or aflibercept was injected at 4 mm from the limbus.

In control group, before starting routine cataract surgery, a vitreous sample (approximately 0.5–0.6 mL) was obtained by entering the pars plana with an insulin injector at a distance of 3.5 mm. Anterior chamber protection and infusion were not used in any of these procedures. The routine surgical procedure was then continued. Samples were injected into sterile Eppendorf tubes frozen in a liquid nitrogen tank and kept in a – 80 °C refrigerator. All interventions were done in operating room conditions. Samples were always taken by the same surgeon (BO). No complications were developed in any operation.

Sample preparation and 2D gel electrophoresis

Since the sample amounts and concentrations of both the patient and control groups were very low in the 1D-gel electrophoresis studies, it was decided to create the protein pools to be used in two-dimensional (2D) gel electrophoresis studies according to the volume, not the sample concentration. This strategy was adopted because the collected vitreous volumes varied substantially among patients, and pooling by concentration would have led to the exclusion of samples with insufficient yield. Pooling by volume allowed us to incorporate all available samples, thereby maximizing the representativeness of the patient group. However, we recognize that this approach emphasizes overall group-level proteomic trends and may mask inter-individual variability, which we have indicated as a limitation of this study design. While creating the sample pools, it was created based on the sample with the least volume. After measuring the concentration of the protein pools with the Bradford method, our measurement was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (180 V, 50 min) (Fig. 1a). As a result of the experiment, it was observed that the protein pools of the AMD and control groups contained equal amounts of protein (Fig. 1b). This preliminary analysis showed that the samples were suitable for comparative 2D-gel studies and that the information to be obtained would be reliable.

Protein samples were rehydrated with the strips so that they could be transferred to the IPG strips in the first step. In isoelectric focusing, 7 cm IPG strips (pH 3–10 NL) for preliminary trials, and 17 cm IPG strips (pH 5–8 NL) for analysis. At this stage, a 100 µg protein sample was used for 7 cm IPG strips, and a 750 µg protein sample was used for 17 cm IPG strips. The required amount of protein sample was mixed with 2D rehydration buffer and 1% Amphylote and 1% TBP suitable for the pH of the strip were added. After this stage, the protein samples were placed in the focusing trays and the appropriate IPG strips were carefully placed on the samples and kept for active rehydration (20 °C) in the PROTEAN IEF (BioRad) with a current of 50µA/IPG Strip. During active rehydration, at the end of the 1 st hour, 2–3 ml of mineral oil was added to the IPG strips to prevent evaporation of the sample, and the rehydration process was continued for approximately 16 h. After the rehydration process, isoelectric focusing of the IPG strips was started. At the end of the rehydration, the IPG strips were taken and the excess oil on them was cleaned with the help of blotting paper and water, then they were placed in a clean focusing tray and new mineral oil was added to them. The trailer was then placed in the PROTEAN IEF (BioRad) and focused at 20 °C with a three-stage program that increases the current step by step (S1:250 V 20 min Linear for 7 cm IPG strips, S2: 4000 V 2 h Linear, S3: 4000 V, 10000 V-hours, Rapid, 5 h total 14000 V-hours For 11 cm IPG strips S1:250 V 20 min Linear, S2: 8000 V 2.5 h Linear, S3: 8000volts, 20000 V-hours, Rapid, 5.3 h total 30000 V-hours for 17 cm IPG strips S1:250 V 20 min Linear, S2: 10000 V 2.5 h Linear, S3: 10000volts, 40000 V-hours, Rapid, 7 h total − 50000 V-hours.). During focusing, the proteins are aligned on the IPG strip according to their isoelectric points. After focusing, the strips were stripped with buffer I (6 M urea, 0.375 M tris-HCL pH8.8, 2% SDS, 20% glycerol, 2% (w/v) DTT) and buffer II (6 M urea, 0.375 M tris-HCL pH8.8)., 2% SDS, 20% glycerol, 2% (w/v) iodoacetamide) for 30 min. After this step, the strips are placed on polyacrylamide gels (12% or at different concentrations) prepared or precast, using electrophoresis (Mini Protean Tetra Cell (Bio-Rad) for 7 cm strips and Dodeca Cell (Bio-Rad) for 17 cm strips), 35 mA 30 min, 48 mA 5 h at 16 °C), and second dimension (2D) separation according to their molecular size (MW) [Laemmli et al. 1970]. DODECA (BioRad) gel pouring, gel running, and staining system was used for 17 cm strips in multiple sample studies to eliminate gel-gel differences for the gels to be used during the second dimension separation. After electrophoresis, the gels were fixed in 40% methanol and 10% acetic acid for at least one hour, followed by overnight staining with Murafast colloidal Coomassie blue dye, which was prepared in-house.

2DE gel image analysis

2DE gels were visualized with VersaDoc MP 4000 (Bio-Rad, USA), and PDQuest 2DE Analysis Software (Bio-Rad, USA) was used for spot intensity calibration, spot detection, and background subtraction. The number of spots was normalized to the total valid spot intensity by using a linear regression model. The spots on gels were detected automatically. 2D gel image comparison and protein spot intensities with more than a two-fold significant change in a consistently increased or decreased pattern were considered differentially expressed. The statistically significant spots were cut with the Spot-cutter robot (BioRad). Student’s t-tests were used to assess differences in the average protein abundance between the gels.

Sample preparation for MALDI analysis

Protein spot samples are destained in 50% acetonitrile (ACN) and 50% 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution and washed on a shaking vortex. At the end of the washing, the samples are reduced to 10% TCEP in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate at 60 C for 10 min, thus breaking the disulfide bonds. Then, the reduced cystine will be methylated from its side chains (Alkylation) by keeping it in the dark for 1 h at room temperature in 25 ammonium bicarbonate with a final concentration of 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA). At the end of this process, the spots taken into 100% ACN are cut overnight at 30 °C with trypsin with a final concentration of 10ng/µl after the ACN is removed. Zip-Tips of the samples begin with equilibration of the ends with ACN and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) first. Before deposition onto a MALDI plate, the digested peptide mixes were desalted with a 10 µL ZipTipC18 (Millipore, USA) and eluted with the matrix solution containing 10 mg/mL α-Cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid before spotting onto the MALDI target plate.

MALDI TOF-TOF analysis

Protein identification experiments were performed at Kocaeli University Proteomics Laboratory by using ABSCIEX MALDI-TOF/TOF 5800 system (Applied Biosystems, Framingham, MA, USA). In-gel tryptic digestion of the proteins was performed using an in-gel digestion kit following the recommended protocol (Pierce, USA). Before deposition onto a MALDI plate, all samples were desalted with a 10 µL ZipTipC18 (Millipore®, USA). Peptides were eluted in a volume of 1 µL using a concentrated solution of α-Cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) in 50% acetonitrile and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in water and spotted onto the MALDI target plate. MALDI calibration will be done using external or internal calibrant, an average of 2000 laser pulses will be used for each spectrum. All obtained spectra (m/z), search parameters; A miscut site, carbamidomethyl (cysteine) as fixed modification, Met oxidation as variable modification, peptide charge + 1, monoisotopic mass values, peptide mass tolerance ± 50 ppm, fragment mass tolerance ± 0.25 Da were analyzed in the MASCOT database.

Statistical and bioinformatics analysis

Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For demographic and clinical variables, comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed continuous data and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables when expected cell frequencies were less than 5 [11].

For proteomic analysis, statistical evaluation of protein abundance changes between samples was performed using PDQuest 2DE Analysis Software (Bio-Rad, USA). Independent samples t-tests were applied to assess differences in average protein spot intensities between AMD and control groups using pooled samples.

Results for quantitative variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Demographic and clinical data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) [12].

PANTHER and STRING programs were used to reveal the biological relationship between the identified proteins and to determine the cellular pathways. Both of these programs are programs that can be used free of charge over the internet and can be accessed from the web addresses http://www.pantherdb.org/ and https://string-db.org/.

Proteomic analysis

In this study, vitreous fluid samples of 13 patients diagnosed with Age-Related Macular Degeneration and 11 healthy individuals who constituted the control group without any systemic and retinal disease were studied. The vitreous humor is primarily composed of approximately 95% water, with the remaining 5% consisting of hyaluronic acid and various proteins.

Using pooled protein samples, well-resolved and reproducible 2D gels were generated (Fig. 2a). Comparative analysis of the gels revealed an inter-gel match rate of 98%. High molecular weight proteins localized near a pH of 8.0 were detected in the vitreous proteome. However, these proteins were excluded from further differential analysis. This decision was based on the fact that highly abundant proteins, such as albumin and immunoglobulins, can dominate proteomic profiles and obscure the detection of lower-abundance regulatory proteins that are more likely to provide mechanistic insights into AMD [13, 14]. In addition, some of these proteins represent common plasma-derived components or technical contaminants (e.g., keratins), which reduces their specificity for AMD-related pathophysiological processes [15]. For these reasons, we focused on lower-abundance proteins with greater potential relevance for disease mechanisms and biomarker discovery, while still acknowledging the presence of abundant proteins in the vitreous proteome. In total, approximately 150 well-resolved protein spots were detected, among which 17 demonstrated significant differences in expression between AMD patients and controls. These differentially regulated protein spots were excised (Fig. 2b) and identified by MALDI-TOF/TOF MS/MS. Discussion

Proteins in a tissue may change in response to different conditions such as diseases and environmental influences. In our study, we evaluated protein changes in the vitreous of patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and compared the results with normal subjects. AMD is a multifactorial disease, and predisposing factors may induce alterations in the vitreous proteome. Using a 2D-PAGE approach, we analyzed differentially regulated proteins between AMD and control groups and identified 17 protein spots with significant expression changes. Interestingly, some proteins were represented by multiple spots on the gels, such as Haptoglobin (four distinct spots), LRG1/A2GL, CRYBA1, and CRYGS (two spots each). This phenomenon is not an experimental artifact but is likely the result of post-translational modifications (PTMs) or amino acid residue adducts, which alter the focusing positions of proteins on 2D gels. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [16–18]. In our case, the regulation trends of these multiple spots were consistent, supporting the validity of the results. Although the precise types of PTMs were not analyzed in this study, further investigation of these modifications may provide additional insights into the role of protein function in AMD. After omitting repeated identifications of the same proteins, a total of 11 unique proteins were determined to be differentially regulated and were subjected to detailed analysis.

The results of the study showed that the amount of Apolipoprotein E (APOE) was significantly increased in the vitreous of AMD patients compared to the control group. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is a critical protein involved in lipid metabolism and has garnered significant attention in the context of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a leading cause of vision loss in the elderly. The role of APOE in AMD is multifaceted, as it not only participates in lipid transport but also influences inflammatory processes that are pivotal in the pathogenesis of the disease. Research has demonstrated that different isoforms of APOE (APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4) exhibit varying effects on the risk and progression of AMD, with the APOE4 allele being associated with a reduced risk for the disease compared to APOE2, which appears to increase susceptibility [19–21]. Inflammation is a hallmark of AMD, and studies have shown that APOE plays a significant role in modulating inflammatory responses within the retina. For instance, Levy et al. highlighted that APOE promotes the survival of subretinal mononuclear phagocytes, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation in AMD [22]. This chronic inflammatory state is characterized by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and chemokines like CCL2, which are implicated in the progression of retinal degeneration [22]. Furthermore, the presence of APOE in drusen and basal laminar deposits within the macula suggests its involvement in the pathological processes associated with AMD [19]. The interplay between APOE and inflammation is further complicated by environmental factors and genetic predispositions. Age-related changes and lifestyle factors, such as high-fat diets and smoking, can exacerbate the inflammatory response in the retina, potentially interacting with the genetic risk conferred by APOE variants [21]. Understanding these interactions is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying AMD and developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Beyond its genetic isoform associations with AMD, elevated vitreous APOE levels may reflect increased local lipid transport and remodeling, as well as enhanced inflammatory signaling. Such changes could contribute to drusen formation and chronic inflammation in the macular environment, both central features of AMD pathogenesis. Previous studies, such as Koss et al., reported decreased APOE in AMD vitreous samples [8]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in patient characteristics: our cohort consisted of treatment-naïve patients, whereas their study included patients with more advanced disease or prior treatment exposure. Taken together, our findings suggest that APOE up-regulation may represent an early molecular signature of AMD, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for early diagnosis or therapeutic targeting. Another key factor contributing to differences with the Koss et al. dataset may be the age distribution and naïve status of the patients [8]. Our cohort had a mean age of approximately 69 ± 2 years (50–80 years) and included only treatment-naïve patients, whereas Koss et al. analyzed older patients, some of whom may have had prior interventions. Age-dependent proteomic changes and therapy-related alterations are known to influence protein abundance in ocular fluids [23]. Therefore, our results may better capture molecular alterations characteristic of earlier disease stages or specific patient subgroups, adding a complementary perspective to existing literature. In summary, APOE is a significant player in the inflammatory landscape of age-related macular degeneration. Its isoforms influence both lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, which are critical in the pathogenesis of AMD. Ongoing research into the role of APOE in AMD may provide insights into potential biomarkers for disease progression and novel therapeutic targets aimed at mitigating inflammation and preserving vision in affected individuals.

The proteins that attracted our attention by showing the most differentiated expression level changes in the 2DE analyzes were those belonging to the crystallin family. Alpha crystalline A chain and alpha crystalline B chain are the proteins that are regulated by oxidative stress. They protect the cells by increasing the effects of the anti-oxidants, and this protection may vary depending on the type of stress stimulus [24]. Alpha crystalline A chain can be produced by many tissues including the retina, brain, muscle, spleen, lung, and skin. Both Alpha crystalline A chain and B chain have regulatory roles in apoptosis, angiogenesis, and production of b-amyloid fibrils [25]. In this study, we found that alpha crystallin A chain and alpha crystallin B chain were downregulated in AMD patients compared with controls. This decrease in the expression of crystallin proteins suggested that it may play a role in the lack of a response to inflammation in AMD patients and in the emergence of the pathological condition that causes inflammation in patients.

Alpha-crystallins, specifically alpha-A (CRYAA) and alpha-B (CRYAB), are small heat shock proteins that function primarily as molecular chaperones. They play a critical role in protecting cells from stress-induced apoptosis, particularly in the RPE, which is essential for maintaining retinal health [24]. In the context of AMD, increased expression of alpha-crystallins has been shown to counteract apoptotic stimuli caused by oxidative stress, inflammation, and other damaging factors [24]. For instance, studies have indicated that alpha-crystallins can inhibit apoptosis in RPE cells exposed to various stressors, thereby preserving cell viability and function [24]. This protective mechanism is particularly relevant given the chronic low-grade inflammation associated with AMD, which contributes to RPE dysfunction and photoreceptor degeneration [26, 27].

Moreover, the phosphorylation of crystallins in the RPE has been implicated in protective signaling pathways that may mitigate the effects of light-induced damage, a known risk factor for AMD [28]. The ability of alpha-crystallins to modulate cellular responses to stress and inflammation positions them as potential therapeutic targets for AMD. Their role in regulating apoptosis and inflammation suggests that enhancing alpha-crystallin expression or function could be beneficial in slowing the progression of AMD and preserving vision [24, 29].

In summary, alpha-crystallins are integral to the cellular defense mechanisms in the RPE, particularly in the context of age-related macular degeneration. Their ability to inhibit apoptosis and modulate inflammatory responses highlights their potential as therapeutic agents in managing AMD. Further research into the mechanisms by which alpha-crystallins exert their protective effects may provide valuable insights into novel strategies for preventing or treating this debilitating condition.

In addition to notable alterations in the expression of crystalline family proteins, changes in the expression of the immunoglobulin kappa chain C region protein were also observed. The immunoglobulin kappa constant globulin plays a crucial role in maintaining tissue homeostasis within the retina, regulating essential processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic functions [21].

Haptoglobin, an acute-phase protein, is involved in the clearance of hemoglobin released from lysed red blood cells and serves as a potent antioxidant against oxidative stress [22]. This protein can be synthesized in various retinal layers, including photoreceptors, the inner nuclear layer, and the ganglion cell layer of the neuroretina [22]. Haptoglobin exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the production of prostaglandins and nitric oxide [24]. Additionally, it plays a role in the immune response by modulating neutrophil metabolism and influencing the production of B lymphocytes and antibodies. As a positive acute-phase reactant, haptoglobin exhibits different phenotypes, with their distribution varying with age [24].

Angiogenesis is a critical factor in the pathogenesis of AMD, and evidence suggests that haptoglobin may also influence angiogenic processes. Specifically, the haptoglobin phenotype 1–1 has been associated with protective effects against diabetic retinopathy, with lower levels observed in affected patients, a phenomenon attributed to its antioxidant properties [26]. In our current study, we identified four distinct spots corresponding to haptoglobin, leading us to hypothesize that its antioxidant activity and anti-inflammatory role may contribute to protection against AMD.

Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP) and alpha-2-HS-glycoprotein (AHSG) have been found to be two acute phase proteins involved in the pathophysiology of age-related macular degeneration (AMD). AGP, also known as orosomucoid, is produced in response to inflammation and infection, and plasma levels increase during these conditions [30]. This protein has been associated with various health outcomes, including cardiovascular mortality and prognosis of some cancers, suggesting a role as a biomarker of systemic inflammation [30, 31]. In the context of AMD, elevated AGP levels may reflect the chronic inflammatory state that characterizes the disease and potentially contribute to retinal damage and progression [26].

The interaction between these glycoproteins and inflammation is particularly important in AMD, where chronic low-grade inflammation is a major contributor to retinal degeneration [26]. Elevated AGP levels and altered AHSG expression may serve as indicators of inflammatory processes in AMD, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for disease progression. Furthermore, the antioxidant properties attributed to these proteins may offer insights for therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in AMD patients [30].

The altered expressions of both alpha-1-acid glycoprotein and alpha-2-HS-glycoprotein in AMD patients highlight the importance of inflammation and oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of the disease and suggest that these proteins may serve as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets in the treatment of AMD.

Leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein (LRG) has emerged as a significant protein in the context of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), primarily due to its involvement in inflammatory processes and potential roles in retinal pathology. LRG is a glycoprotein of approximately 50 kDa that contains repetitive leucine-rich motifs and is known to be upregulated in various inflammatory conditions [32, 33]. Its expression is associated with acute-phase responses, indicating its role as a biomarker for systemic inflammation [34].

In AMD, chronic inflammation is a critical factor contributing to the degeneration of retinal cells and the progression of the disease. Elevated levels of LRG have been observed in patients with inflammatory diseases, suggesting that it may also play a role in the inflammatory milieu of AMD [34].The protein’s involvement in modulating inflammatory responses could be particularly relevant, as AMD is characterized by the activation of microglia and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that contribute to retinal damage [33].

Moreover, LRG has been implicated in processes such as angiogenesis, which is a key feature of wet AMD. Studies have shown that LRG can influence endothelial cell behavior and promote angiogenic pathways through its interaction with transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling [35, 36]. This suggests that LRG may not only serve as a marker of inflammation but could also actively participate in the pathological processes that lead to neovascularization in AMD.

Leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein is a protein of interest in the study of age-related macular degeneration due to its roles in inflammation and angiogenesis. Its elevated levels in inflammatory conditions and potential involvement in retinal pathology highlight the need for further research to elucidate its precise contributions to AMD and its potential as a therapeutic target or biomarker for disease progression.

Recent studies have investigated the relationships between vitreous proteins and age-related macular degeneration (AMD). Koss et al., identified 19 proteins in the vitreous that were found to be upregulated in patients with AMD [8]. In our study, however, only three of these proteins Ig kappa chain C region, alpha-2-HS-glycoprotein, and haptoglobin were detected, and notably, each of these proteins exhibited downregulation in our findings. Upon reviewing the Koss study, we noted that the mean ages of the AMD patients and control groups were 77.8 ± 8.9 years and 60 ± 16 years, respectively, indicating a lack of demographic matching between the groups. Given that the composition of vitreous proteins can change with age, these age disparities may explain the differences observed between the studies.

Each of these proteins provides important insights into the pathogenesis of AMD. For instance, the upregulation of Apolipoprotein E highlights its dual role in lipid metabolism and inflammation, while the downregulation of alpha- and beta-crystallins suggests impaired cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress and apoptosis. Similarly, reduced levels of haptoglobin and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein indicate compromised antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses, which are crucial in retinal protection. The STRING and PANTHER analyses further revealed that these proteins are functionally linked through networks related to angiogenesis, immune regulation, and metabolic control. Together, these findings support the concept that AMD is not caused by a single pathway, but rather by a complex interplay of oxidative, inflammatory, and angiogenic processes.

Regarding peptide identification, the coverage percentage achieved in our MALDI-TOF/TOF analyses ranged between 20 and 67% for most proteins (Table 2). This level of sequence coverage is consistent with previous proteomic studies on ocular fluids and provides confidence in the reliability of our protein identifications. Higher coverage percentages, such as those obtained for beta-crystallin B2 (66%) and alpha-crystallin A chain (45%), strengthen the accuracy of these identifications, while even lower coverage values remain acceptable when combined with high Mascot scores and multiple peptide matches. Therefore, the peptide coverage data not only validates the identified proteins but also enhances the robustness of our findings.

This study presents several methodological limitations that warrant careful consideration in the interpretation of findings. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 13 AMD patients, n = 11 controls) significantly constrains statistical power and limits the generalizability of results. This cohort size, while appropriate for an exploratory pilot investigation, necessitates validation in larger, multi-center cohorts before definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the clinical significance of identified protein alterations.

Second, the pooling strategy employed for proteomic analysis, though necessitated by inherently low protein concentrations in vitreous samples, introduces fundamental limitations in data interpretation. While pooling by volume maximized sample utilization and facilitated group-level comparisons, this approach inevitably obscures inter-individual proteomic variability and precludes patient-level statistical analyses. Consequently, sophisticated analytical approaches such as Kendall correlation matrices, multivariate modeling, or personalized biomarker profiling could not be implemented. The resulting data reflect group-level proteomic patterns rather than individual molecular signatures, which limits insights into patient-specific disease mechanisms.

Third, a significant confounding factor emerges from the heterogeneous lens status between study groups. The control cohort consisted entirely of cataract patients scheduled for surgical intervention, whereas the AMD group exhibited mixed lens conditions including clear lenses, cataracts, and pseudophakic eyes. This demographic disparity may influence vitreous protein composition independent of AMD pathology, potentially introducing bias in proteomic comparisons.

Fourth, the cross-sectional study design prevents evaluation of temporal protein dynamics during disease progression. This limitation is particularly relevant given AMD’s chronic, progressive nature, as it restricts understanding of whether observed protein alterations represent early pathogenic events, secondary inflammatory responses, or late-stage disease manifestations.

Fifth, the exclusion of high-abundance proteins, while methodologically justified to enhance detection of regulatory proteins, may have eliminated potentially relevant biomarkers. Although albumin, immunoglobulins, and other abundant species can mask low-abundance signals, they may themselves undergo disease-specific modifications worthy of investigation.

Finally, as a single-center discovery-based proteomic investigation, these findings require independent replication through targeted quantitative methodologies before clinical translation can be considered. The exploratory nature of this work, while providing valuable preliminary insights into vitreous proteomic alterations in treatment-naïve AMD patients, establishes hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions.

Interpretive implications: Given these methodological constraints, results should be interpreted as suggestive group-level observations requiring validation in larger, independently replicated cohorts with individual sample analysis to confirm biological significance. Future investigations employing individual-level proteomic profiling will be essential to validate identified proteins, assess inter-patient variability, and establish robust associations with clinical parameters and disease outcomes.

In conclusion, we have characterized the alterations in protein composition of the vitreous humor in patients with treatment-naïve AMD compared to non-AMD controls. Our proteomic analysis identified significant changes, including the up-regulation of Apolipoprotein E and the marked down-regulation of crystallin family proteins, suggesting disrupted lipid transport, heightened inflammatory signaling, and impaired protection against oxidative stress as early molecular events in AMD pathogenesis. Through bioinformatics analyses, we further demonstrated that, in addition to VEGF and its known mediators, these proteins are closely associated with retinal protection, inflammatory responses, and angiogenic processes. By focusing on vitreous humor, which directly reflects the retinal microenvironment, this study provides novel insights into local disease mechanisms. Although limited by the relatively small sample size, pooling strategy, and single-center design, our findings are exploratory yet hypothesis-generating. They highlight candidate proteins that may serve as early diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of AMD etiopathology and providing a valuable foundation for future investigations. Larger, independent cohorts with individual sample analyses are needed to validate these findings and establish their clinical significance.
