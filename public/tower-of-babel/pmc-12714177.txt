Needle-free Subcision and Hyaluronic Acid Injection Using CureJet for Horizontal Neck Fold Reduction Assessed by Ultrasound and 3-dimensional Analysis
Authors: JongSeo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Horizontal neck folds (HNF) result from fibrous connections between the platysma and skin, often becoming more prominent with aging and skin laxity. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of combining needle-free subcision and hyaluronic acid (HA) filler injections in improving the dermal and subcutaneous architecture of HNF using sonographic and 3-dimensional (3D) imaging tools.Methods:Twenty patients underwent 3 sessions of HA (Cleviel Wrinkle) injections for HNF. A needle-free injector (CureJet) was used in the first session to perform subcision via high-pressure microjets of HA, mechanically disrupting adhesions in the subcutaneous tissue. The second and third sessions involved 29G needle injections for volumization. Ultrasound and 3D imaging measured changes in dermal thickness, subcutaneous fat, and skin roughness.Results:Ultrasound showed significantly thinner subcutaneous fat in wrinkled areas (0.67 ± 0.26 mm) compared with normal regions (1.73 ± 0.32 mm), confirming fat loss as a key contributor to HNF. Posttreatment, subcutaneous fat thickness increased to 2.05 ± 0.41 mm, whereas dermal thickness improved from 1.02 ± 0.17 to 1.58 ± 0.26 mm. 3D analysis revealed a reduction in skin roughness, with the roughness average decreasing from 3.43 ± 0.73 to 1.79 ± 0.49. Mild bruising from subcision resolved within 7 days.Conclusions:HA injections effectively increased dermal and subcutaneous fat thickness, reducing the appearance of HNF without complications such as nodules or lumps. The combination of needle-free subcision for adhesion release and subsequent HA volumization provided both structural and aesthetic benefits, making it a promising approach for noninvasive neck rejuvenation.

Takeaways

Question: Can needle-free subcision combined with hyaluronic acid (HA) filler effectively improve both the subcutaneous architecture and surface roughness of horizontal neck folds?

Findings: In 20 patients, needle-free injector subcision followed by HA filler significantly increased dermal and subcutaneous thickness and reduced skin roughness, as confirmed by ultrasound and 3-dimensional imaging, with only mild, self-limited bruising and no major complications.

Meaning: Needle-free subcision combined with HA filler provides a safe, minimally invasive treatment option that improves both structural and surface features of horizontal neck folds.

INTRODUCTION

Horizontal neck folds (HNF) are characterized by fibrous connections between the platysma and the skin. HNFs are a common aesthetic concern with diverse etiologies, including congenital predisposition, weight gain, and aging-related structural changes such as dermal thinning and subcutaneous fat loss. Unlike dynamic wrinkles, these folds act as scar-like structures that resist yielding to weight gain, often deepening with increased skin tension. Many patients only seek treatment when aging exacerbates these lines with additional issues such as skin laxity. These factors lead to deepening horizontal lines, affecting overall neck and facial aesthetics.1 Current treatments include minimally invasive techniques such as botulinum toxin, radiofrequency, laser treatments, and dermal fillers, with hyaluronic acid (HA) fillers widely used for volume restoration and skin smoothing.2

However, the effectiveness of HA fillers for HNF is not well studied, especially with innovative delivery methods such as needle-free injectors.3 Previous studies have attempted to treat HNF using a range of approaches. Lee and Kim4 reported improvement in horizontal necklines following HA filler injections, but noted the challenges in treating congenital or deeply fibrotic folds. Tseng and Yu1 retrospectively analyzed HA treatment for HNF and emphasized variability in response depending on the depth and elasticity of the skin. Although these studies demonstrated efficacy in superficial cases, they lacked imaging-based evaluation or mechanistic insights into filler–tissue interactions.

This study evaluated HNF improvement using a needle-free injector designed to perform subcision by delivering high-velocity microjets of HA. Unlike conventional needle-based techniques, this mechanism creates a controlled mechanical disruption of scar-like bands and adhesions, enabling improved tissue pliability and effective volume restoration with subsequent injections.

Advanced 3-dimensional (3D) imaging and high-frequency ultrasound were used to measure changes in skin roughness, dermal thickness, and subcutaneous fat, providing a detailed assessment of structural and surface skin improvements posttreatment.5 This study aimed to fill a gap in the literature by systematically analyzing the impact of needle-free injectors in HNF treatment and comparing their outcomes with conventional methods. This retrospective study aimed to shed light on the use of HA fillers for HNF, highlighting their potential in enhancing skin integrity and appearance without complications such as nodules, lumps, or unintended filler migration.

METHODS

This retrospective observational study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and structural effects of HA filler for HNFs using a needle-free injection technique, and absence of prior neck treatments in the author’s clinic for treating neck folds from January 2023 to October 2024. The inclusion criteria were (1) patients aged 25–60 years with moderate to severe HNF and (2) no prior neck filler treatments in the past 6 months. The exclusion criteria were (1) a history of keloid formation, (2) prior neck surgery, (3) systemic conditions affecting skin healing, and (4) any cosmetic procedures involving the abdominal skin—including liposuction, fillers, or botulinum toxin—performed within the past 6 months. This study was approved by the institutional review board (no. P01-202412-10-042), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before participation. This retrospective study analyzed the improvement of HNF after HA injection using the 3D scanner and ultrasound evaluation. Cleviel Wrinkle (Pharma Research, Korea), a cross-linked HA filler, has shown success in facial rejuvenation, yet its use for HNF is underexplored. Patients received HA filler material using needle-free injection during the first session for subcision of adhesion and scar-like band along the HNFs and 30G needle injection during the second and third sessions.

Sonographic Examination of HNF

High-frequency ultrasound has been widely used to evaluate dermal and subcutaneous structural changes after injectable treatments.6 In this study, pre- and posttreatment 33-MHz ultrasound images (Aplio i-700, Cannon Medical System, Tokyo, Japan) were used to measure and analyze injection depths and improvements for all patients. Pre-procedure ultrasound imaging was performed to assess the soft tissue layers at the author’s clinic. Images were retrospectively collected to ensure consistency in injection depth. The thickness of the dermis and subcutaneous fat was measured before and after the procedure.

Injection Methods

The needle-free injector (CureJet: C-Jet, Baz Biomedic, Seoul, Korea) uses electromagnetic force to drive a piston forward, creating high-pressure microjets of HA. Upon impact, these jets penetrate the tissue at high speed, generating a controlled mechanical effect. This disrupts adhesions and scar-like bands within the subcutaneous layer, mimicking the traditional subcision technique. The high-velocity injection produces a Venturi effect, enhancing distribution and enabling simultaneous subcision and filler delivery. The needle-free injector achieves subcision by delivering high-velocity microjets of HA. These jets generate a shearing effect within the subcutaneous tissue, disrupting adhesions and scar-like bands, as evidenced by ultrasound imaging showing loosening of tissue planes.7–10

First Session

For each patient, 1000 shots were administered using the needle-free injector.7–10 The C-Jet operates through an electromagnetic force that pushes a piston hammer forward very fast. When the hammer strikes the membrane of the needle free injector, its momentum drives the drug chamber to expel the filler through the nozzle into the tissue. Following the Venturi effect, the injecting fluid exits through the narrow nozzle at a very high speed.11 Using the C-Jet, microjet injections can be repeated at a frequency of up to 20 Hz. In this study, the author used a rate of 3–10 Hz to maintain strong injection speed and penetration force. The C-Jet produces a stronger and deeper jet when applied in full contact with the skin. All shots were performed using the contact mode. A 1:2 diluted HA solution was injected using the contact method. The injectable used was HA, composed of 15 mg of HA. To avoid sticking of the injector nozzle, the HA was diluted at a 1:2 ratio. The nozzle tip was maintained in contact with the skin at a 45-degree angle to ensure deep and effective injections. When injections, the skin was stretched with the nondominant hand to allow complete coverage, whereas patients with smaller areas required approximately 1000 shots. Second and Third Sessions

Treatments were administered every 2 weeks, with an additional 2 sessions being the routine. During the second and third sessions, prefilled HA 1 mL was injected along the HNF into the subcutaneous fat layer using a 30G needle. Three-dimensional Analysis

To evaluate treatment efficacy for HNF, a 3D scanner (3D LifeViz Mini; QuantifiCare, Sophia Antipolis, France) was used to measure skin changes immediately after the procedures.12 The scanner captured standardized images with consistent lighting, distance, and angles, ensuring reliable and quantifiable data collection.

Roughness Evaluation of HNF

Open-source computer analysis software (Gwyddion data analysis software; Czech Metrology Institute, Jihlava, Czechia) was used to measure various skin roughness parameters and evaluate the degree of depression along the HNF.13 These images were then analyzed using an online computer program for measuring skin roughness, roughness average, root mean square roughness, maximum height of roughness, maximum roughness valley depth, and waviness average. The figure shows the roughness average, root mean square roughness, maximum height of roughness, maximum roughness valley depth, and waviness average before and after the treatment on the same areas using the Gwyddion computer program.14 After 3 sessions, patients visited the clinic without applying makeup or any other products to their faces. Three-dimensional photographs and sonographic images were then obtained. Paired t tests were conducted to compare pre- and posttreatment values for roughness, dermal thickness, and subcutaneous fat thickness. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

After Treatment

After the treatment and taking the 3D images, 0.5 mL of a cosmetic product (ExtraGoldStem ample; Reteenage, Seoul, South Korea) was sprayed under high pressure to allow penetration of the ampoule into the needle holes and epidermis. The ampoule, containing growth factors and peptides, was applied to promote epidermal repair and hydration posttreatment, enhancing the overall healing process. After the first session, the treated area was covered with a Duoderm hydrogel bandage, and this occlusive dressing was maintained for 2 days.

Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale

The Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale was used for a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of the HA injection on the improvement of HNF (very much improved, 5; much improved, 4; improved, 3; no change, 2; worse, 1). Data were analyzed using paired t tests to compare pre- and posttreatment values for dermal thickness, subcutaneous fat thickness, and skin roughness parameters. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

This study included 20 patients (14 women, 6 men) with a mean age of 35.4 years (range 27–59 y). All participants were healthy without underlying systemic disease and had Fitzpatrick skin types II–IV. None had a history of previous neck filler treatments or neck surgery.

Three-dimensional Analysis of Neck Fold

The 3D images showed the results of neck wrinkle reduction after a series of injections, captured using QuantifiCare 3D analysis technology.

Pretreatment Observations

In the pretreatment images, there are noticeable deep folds and wrinkles, particularly in the lower neck area. These creases suggest loss of specific soft tissue, which is typically unrelated to aging or skin sagging.

Posttreatment Observations: Wrinkle Reduction

After 3 sessions of injections, there was a marked reduction in the depth and prominence of the neck wrinkles. The folds that were visible in the lower neck had diminished significantly. The skin appeared smoother and more even, with reduced furrows in the treated area.

Improvement in Texture and Tightness

The skin texture appeared much more uniform posttreatment, as indicated by the lack of deep, obvious ridges or valleys. This suggests that the soft tissue loss was treated in 2 layers (the dermis and the subcutaneous fat layer), which may have contributed to the improved appearance. The results provide clear evidence that the treatment has significantly reduced the visibility of neck wrinkles. The improvements are not just in the reduction of wrinkle depth but also in the overall smoothness, texture, and skin tightness. The 3D imaging highlighted the profound differences between the pre- and posttreatment stages, making it a valuable visual tool to support the efficacy of the procedure (Figs. 1–3). Topographic Analysis of Neck Fold

The graphs compare the pre- and posttreatment values for key surface parameters, including roughness average, root mean square roughness, maximum height of roughness, maximum roughness valley depth, and waviness average. Before treatment, the roughness average was measured at 3.43 ± 0.73, indicating a relatively uneven surface texture. Following the procedure, this value significantly decreased to 1.79 ± 0.49, demonstrating a much smoother skin surface. Similarly, the root mean square roughness, which reflects the variability in surface texture, showed a substantial reduction from 4.49 ± 1.22 before treatment to 2.21 ± 0.60 afterward. This decline in variability further supports the effectiveness of the treatment in achieving a more uniform skin surface.

The maximum height of roughness, which represents the presence of high peaks on the skin surface, was initially 17.22 ± 6.01. After treatment, this value was markedly reduced to 7.83 ± 2.66, indicating that the intervention effectively diminished the surface irregularities. Additionally, the maximum roughness valley depth, which quantifies the depth of depressions in the skin, showed a reduction from 8.49 ± 3.15 to 4.15 ± 1.51 posttreatment. This suggests that the valleys on the skin surface became significantly shallower, contributing to a smoother appearance.

The waviness average, which measures the overall undulation of the surface, also demonstrated improvement. Pretreatment values were 15.84 ± 4.69, reflecting significant variations in the skin’s surface profile. After treatment, the value decreased to 10.09 ± 3.30, showing that the treatment effectively reduced overall waviness, although some variability remained.

These trends collectively indicate that the treatment led to a noticeable improvement in skin surface smoothness by reducing both roughness and waviness, as evidenced by the significant reductions in each parameter (Table 1) (Figs. 4, 5). To assess the statistical significance of these improvements, a paired t test was conducted, confirming that the reductions in roughness, valley depth, and waviness between pre- and posttreatment were statistically significant (P < 0.001). This suggests that the observed smoothing effect was highly unlikely to be due to random variation. The paired t test was chosen as the appropriate statistical method because it compares related pre- and posttreatment measurements within the same individuals.

The statistical analysis was conducted under the framework of hypothesis testing, where the null hypothesis (H₀) assumed no difference between the pretreatment and posttreatment means, whereas the alternative hypothesis (H₁) proposed that a significant difference existed. The t statistic was calculated as the ratio of the mean of the differences to the SD of differences, adjusted for sample size.

The resulting P value provided a probability measure of whether the observed differences could have occurred by chance. Given that the P value was well under 0.05, the null hypothesis was rejected, confirming that the treatment had a statistically significant impact on improving skin texture. The consistent and significant reductions across all measured parameters underscore the effectiveness of the intervention in enhancing skin smoothness and reducing the prominence of HNFs.

The t statistic measures the size of the difference relative to the variation within the sample data. The P value helps determine whether that difference is statistically significant. In this case, the very low P values suggest that the treatment led to a real improvement in the skin surface properties, not just random fluctuations. In the results calculation, the P values for all metrics (roughness average, root mean square roughness, maximum height of roughness, etc.) were very small (<0.001). This means the likelihood of the differences between pre- and posttreatment measurements being due to chance is extremely low. Therefore, we can confidently say that the treatment had a significant impact on these skin parameters (Table 2).

Sonographic Evaluation

Subcision achieved with the needle-free injector disrupted adhesions and scar-like bands, as evidenced by ultrasound imaging showing loosening of subcutaneous tissue planes. This effect allowed for uniform filler distribution and contributed to improved structural support in the treated areas. The observed increase in subcutaneous fat thickness posttreatment was attributed to the volumizing effect of HA filler, which effectively restored lost volume and improved support for the overlying skin.

Results of Subcutaneous Fat Analysis Before and After Treatment Using 30G Needle Injection in the Fat Layer

This retrospective study found a significant difference in subcutaneous fat thickness between normal neck areas and regions with HNF. Normal areas showed an average subcutaneous thickness of 1.73 ± 0.32 mm, whereas HNF regions had only 0.67 ± 0.26 mm, highlighting the role of reduced subcutaneous fat in wrinkle formation. Posttreatment, subcutaneous thickness in HNF regions increased to 2.05 ± 0.41 mm. A paired t test showed this increase to be statistically significant (t = −13.90, P = 2.25 × 10−12), confirming the treatment’s effectiveness in enhancing subcutaneous thickness (Tables 2 and 3) (Figs. 6–8).

Pretreatment Findings

Normal Areas

The average dermal thickness in normal skin areas was 1.51 ± 0.23 mm, representing healthy, intact skin structure.

Wrinkled Areas (Before Treatment)

In contrast, the dermal thickness in the wrinkle folds was significantly thinner, averaging 1.02 ± 0.17 mm. This reduction in dermal thickness likely contributes to the formation and deepening of wrinkles, as thinner skin is more prone to folding and sagging.

Posttreatment Findings

Wrinkled Areas (After Treatment)

After the treatment, the dermal thickness in the wrinkle areas increased to an average of 1.58 ± 0.26 mm. This represents a substantial improvement, bringing the thickness of the dermal layer in the treated areas closer to that of normal, nonwrinkled skin.

The increase in dermal thickness suggests a successful therapeutic outcome, likely due to enhanced collagen production and dermal regeneration stimulated by the treatment. This thicker dermis may provide better structural support to the overlying skin, reducing the appearance of wrinkles.

The study demonstrates that the use of a needle-free injector led to a statistically significant increase in dermal thickness in the wrinkle-prone areas of the neck. This increase can be interpreted as a positive skin remodeling effect, as thicker dermal layers are associated with better skin elasticity, resilience, and a smoother surface appearance. The treatment’s ability to restore dermal thickness to near-normal levels indicates its potential in noninvasive rejuvenation therapies. By increasing dermal thickness, the skin gains more structural integrity, reducing the likelihood of deep wrinkle formation and improving overall skin appearance. This finding suggests that the needle-free injection method may offer a valuable alternative to traditional invasive techniques for patients seeking to reduce neck wrinkles and enhance skin quality.

The paired t test comparing dermal thickness before and after treatment resulted in a t statistic of −9.24 and a P value of 4.99 × 10−9. This extremely low P value indicates that the increase in dermal thickness posttreatment is statistically significant, meaning that the observed improvement in dermal thickness is highly unlikely to be due to chance. Therefore, we can confidently conclude that the needle-free injection treatment effectively increased dermal thickness in the wrinkle areas (Tables 4, 5) (Figs. 10–12).

DISCUSSION

This study provided valuable insights into the role of subcutaneous fat in the development of neck wrinkles and demonstrated the efficacy of HA fillers in both subcision and volumization.1–3 Cleviel Wrinkle features a high HA concentration of 15 mg/mL, providing enhanced volumizing effects with minimal migration (<1 mm observed during follow-up). This compares favorably with standard fillers, which report migration up to 2.5 cm. Long-term studies are ongoing to evaluate its safety and efficacy. Although preliminary results demonstrate the safety of the HA for neck rejuvenation, longer term follow-up is required to confirm the durability of outcomes and the absence of delayed adverse effects.

One of the most important findings from this study is the significant reduction in subcutaneous fat thickness in the wrinkled areas of the neck compared with normal skin, as confirmed by high-frequency ultrasound imaging. This finding highlights a key underlying cause of neck wrinkles: loss of subcutaneous fat, which diminishes the support for the overlying skin, leading to the formation of deeper creases and folds. By restoring this lost fat volume through HA injections, the skin can regain a more youthful and smooth appearance. Moreover, the use of a needle-free injector in the first session provided a crucial advantage by performing subcision to release scar-like bands and adhesions along the HNF.7–10 This technique helped loosen the tight attachments in the subcutaneous tissue, allowing the injected HA filler to distribute more evenly and effectively within the fat layer in subsequent sessions. Subcision performed with the needle-free injector is especially advantageous because it is extremely fast (20 shots/s), enabling the operator to perform the procedure efficiently. However, 1 notable downside of this method is the occurrence of bruising at the treated sites, which is expected as part of the subcision process. Bruising was observed in all patients and typically resolved within 7–14 days, consistent with superficial trauma from subcision. Despite this temporary side effect, subcision remains a crucial first step in the treatment protocol, ensuring optimal outcomes for volumization in the subsequent sessions.15,16

Our findings suggest that a multimodal approach, combining needle-free injectors for subcision and traditional needle injections for volumization, offers a robust solution for patients seeking nonsurgical neck rejuvenation. This approach ensures both structural release of adhesions and restoration of lost volume, leading to more durable and aesthetically pleasing results.

This study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of HA fillers in neck rejuvenation and highlights the innovative application of needle-free injection technology in combination with traditional techniques. Future studies may explore the long-term effects of these treatments and their potential applications for other anatomical areas where adhesion and volume loss contribute to wrinkle formation. This combined approach offers a promising pathway for achieving noninvasive yet highly effective aesthetic improvements in the neck.

Limitations of this study include a small sample size and short-term follow-up. Future studies should explore long-term outcomes and compare needle-free versus traditional injection methods in a larger cohort.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated the efficacy of combining needle-free subcision with 30G needle-based HA injections for treating HNFs. Three-dimensional imaging and ultrasound confirmed improvements in skin texture and dermal and subcutaneous thickness. Subcision using high-velocity microjets disrupted scar-like adhesions, enhancing filler integration and skin smoothness. Although short-term outcomes are promising, further studies with long-term follow-up and histological analysis are needed to determine whether tissue thickening reflects true remodeling or filler deposition. This dual-action, minimally invasive technique offers a safe and effective option for neck rejuvenation.
