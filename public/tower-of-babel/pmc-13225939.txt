The Functional Study of Prostaglandin E2 via EP Receptor on Pacemaker Activity in Interstitial Cells of Cajal from Murine Colon
Authors: Hongyang Gong, Han Yi Jiao, Yuan Chang, Seok Choi, Chan Sik Hong, Jae Yeoul Jun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC) generate spontaneous pacemaker activities responsible for producing slow waves in the gut smooth muscles. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is one of the most important prostanoids in the gut, playing a crucial role in multiple physiological and pathological processes. Because information regarding the effects of PGE2 on colonic ICC is limited, we investigated the effects of PGE2 and its underlying signaling pathways in murine colonic ICC. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analyses were performed to evaluate the effects of PGE2 on mouse colonic ICC. PGE2 had a dual effect on pacemaker potential in the current-clamp mode. RT-PCR data suggested the expression of EP3 and EP4 receptors in colonic ICC. Low PGE2 concentrations induced membrane depolarization and generation of pacemaker activities. And this effect is mimicked by sulprostone (an EP3 agonist). The low PGE2 concentration-induced effect was inhibited by anoctamin-1 (ANO1) or a T-type Ca2+ channel blocker. Conversely, high PGE2 concentrations induced membrane hyperpolarization and blocked pacemaker potential generation. However, this inhibitory effect was blocked by an ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel blocker but not by other K+ channel blockers. Low concentrations of PGE2 activated EP3 receptors, leading to excitation of pacemaker activity through regulation of ANO1 and T-type Ca2+ channels in colonic ICC. In contrast, higher concentrations of PGE2 activated EP4 receptors and opened ATP-sensitive K+ channels, resulting in inhibition of pacemaker activity in colonic ICC.Keywords: Interstitial Cells of Cajal, Dinoprostone, Colon, Gastrointestinal Motility

INTRODUCTION

Interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC) act as pacemaker cells and form a cellular network that is embedded between the different layers of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.1 ICC spontaneously generate pacemaker currents, which are transmitted to smooth muscle in the form of slow waves, to induce rhythmic gut contraction.2,3 In addition, ICC can also transduce excitatory and inhibitory signals from the enteric nervous system to the smooth muscle.2 ANO1, transient receptor potential (TRP), voltage-dependent Ca2+, and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide (HCN) channels were thought to be important channels in the ICC for the generation or regulation of pacemaker activity.4,5,6 Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels are expressed in colonic ICC and contribute to the generation of spontaneous pacemaker activity.

Prostaglandins (PGs) are ubiquitous in all tissues and mediate different varieties of physiological and pathological processes. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is one of the most important prostanoids produced within the whole GI tract. It regulates various physiological functions (such as mucosal protection, secretion, and motility) and plays pathophysiological roles in various GI tract dysfunctions (such as inflammatory bowel diseases and colorectal neoplasia).7,8,9 The motility of the GI tract is maintained through the contraction and relaxation of GI smooth muscles. PGE2 is known to contract longitudinal muscle and to relax circular muscle in diverse species.10,11 This depends on PGE2 concentration, species, and so on.12,13,14

The patterns of contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle cells in the GI are regulated by the pacemaker activity of ICC. This means the effects of PGE2 on smooth muscle may be mediated by acting on the ICC. Meanwhile, the report found that the frequency of peristaltic contraction and the appearance of ectopic sites of waves increased by PGE2 was not observed in ICC-deficient mice, suggesting a possible effect of PGE2 on stomach ICC.15 Furthermore, a previous study showed that PGE2 regulates pacemaker activity in small intestinal ICC.16 Although numerous studies have examined the effects of PGE2 on gastrointestinal function, including its actions on small intestinal ICC, the role of PGE2 in regulating pacemaker activity in colonic ICC remains unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of PGE2 on pacemaker potentials in colonic ICC and to elucidate the associated EP receptor–mediated signaling pathways.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Experimental animals

All animal care and handling protocols used in these experiments were carried out according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and approved by the Ethics Committee of Chosun University and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

2. Preparation of cells and tissues

Either sex of ICR mice aged 4-7 days were sedated with diethyl ether and killed by cervical dislocation. Small intestine (from 1 cm below the pyloric ring to the cecum) and colon (from below the cecum to the rectum) were cut and pinned to the base of a sylgard dish, which was full of ice-cold Ca2+ free Hank’s solution (see solutions). Tissues were opened along the mesenteric border. After removing luminal contents, the mucosa and submucosa of tissues were peeled away by sharp dissection. Stripes of intestinal or colonic muscle were equilibrated in Ca2+ free Hank’s solution for 30 minutes. The muscle strips were transferred into an enzymic solution (see solutions) and incubated in 37℃water bath for 14 minutes. Then tissues were washed out 3 times with Ca2+ free solution and triturated with ablunt pipettes to disperse cell lumps. Cells were plated on poly-L-lysine (200 µL, sigma) coated sterile glass coverslips in 35 mm culture dishes and incubated at 37℃ in a 95% O2-5% CO2 incubator in smooth muscle growth medium (SMGM, Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) supplemented with 2% antibiotic-antimycotics (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and stem cell factor (SCF, 5 ng/mL, sigma). After a 1 day incubation, the medium was replaced with SMGM without SCF, then incubated further 24 hours under the same conditions till performing the following experiments.

3. Whole-cell patch clamp

ICC were isolated from at least 5-8 mice for each experimental series. The reported n values represent the number of recorded cells obtained from at least three independent culture preparations derived from different animals. The cells in 35 mm dishes were taken out from the CO2 incubator after 48 hours of culture and placed into the recording chamber on the inverted microscope. Whole-cell patch clamp technique was used to measure the alterations of membrane potentials from cultured intestinal and colonic ICC. A superfusion system with a temperature controller (Harvard Apparatus) was used to perfuse solutions and electronic equipment such as head stage (CV203BU headstage, Axon instruments, Foster, CA, USA), amplifier (Axopatch 200B, Axon instruments), digitizer (Digidata 1322A, Axon instruments), computer and software (pClamp 9.2, Axon instruments) to record and analyze data. n indicates the number of cells recorded, derived from at least three independent culture preparations.

4. DNA synthesis and PCR

Isolated RNA from single cells and whole dish underwent cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript™ 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (6110A, TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. The cDNA was purified with PCR PreMix iStar Taq (iNtRON Biotechnology Inc., Korea) and amplified with specific primers using PCR, using the following amplification conditions: 94℃ for 5 minutes to activate i-Star Taq DNA polymerase and then 30 cycles at 94℃ for 30 s to desaturation, 59–63℃ for 30 s depends on different primers to annealing, 72℃ for 30 s to extension. Followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 10 minutes. The PCR products (5 µL) were separated by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel and visualized by ethidium bromide staining. The primers used were as follows: KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (c-Kit) (Accession number AY536430), 5′-GCA CAG AAG GAG GCA CTT ATA CCT-3′ and 5′-TGA GAC AGG AGT GGT ACA CCT TTG-3′ (with a product size of 215 bp); Ano1 (Accession number NM-178642), 5′-AGG CCA AGT ACA GCA TGG GTA TCA-3′ and 5′-AGT ACA GGC CAA CCT TCT CAC CAA -3′ (with a product size of 213 bp); and ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase PGP9.5 (Uch-L1) (Accession number AF172334), 5′-GCC AAC AAC CAA GAC AAG CTG GAA-3′ and 5′-GCC GTC CAC GTT GTT GAA CAG AAT-3′ (with a product size of 213 bp); myosin, heavy polypeptide 11, smooth muscle (Myh11) (Accession number NM_013607), 5′-GTACCATCGAGGTCATGGAG-3′ and 5′-GGTCTCTCTCATCCGCATAC-3′ (with a product size of 264 bp); EP1 (Accession number NM_013641), forward 5′–CTGGGCTCAAGGGTATAGAG-3′, reverse 5′–GGTGACAGAGATGGGAAGTG–3′, (with a product size of 290 bp); EP2 (Accession number NM_008964), forward 5′–CAGGAGAGGAGAGAGGACTT-3′, reverse 5′–GAAGTC CGACAACAGAGGAC–3′, (with a product size of 261 bp); EP3 (Accession number NM_011196), forward 5′–CAGG GAAGGATGACTGAGTA-3′, reverse 5′–GGTTCTGAGGCTGGAGATA–3′, (with a product size of 225 bp); EP4 (Accession number NM_008965), forward 5′–GTTGGTGGATGAGGTTAGTG-3′, reverse 5′–CTAGTGGGAGTCCAGATGAA–3′, (with a product size of 220 bp). Single-cell RT-PCR analysis was performed in at least 6 independent ICC preparations. EP3 and EP4 transcripts were consistently detected, whereas EP1 and EP2 were not observed in the majority of analyzed single ICC samples. ICC identity was confirmed by c-Kit and Ano1 expression.

5. Reagents

The normal solution contained 1.2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM KCl, 135 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 using Tris. The pipette solution contained 5 mM MgCl2, 140 mM KCl, 2.7 mM K2ATP, 0.1 mM Na2GTP, 2.5 mM creatine phosphate disodium, 5 mM HEPES, and 0.1 mM EGTA, adjusted to pH 7.2 with Tris. PGE2, sulprostone, CAY10580, TMEM16A inhibitor, MONNA, NiCl2, mibefradil, glibenclamide, TEA, 4-AP, and apamin were used for the study, and all drugs were purchased from Sigma.

6. Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean±SEM. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups or dose–response analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1. PGE2 shows a dual effect with different concentrations in colonic ICC

In the present study, we evaluated the actions of PGE2 on pacemaker potential by using a current clamp in cultured colonic ICC. Under control conditions, the membrane potential and pacemaker potential frequency were −55.7±0.7 mV and 9.7±0.4 cycles/5 min, respectively (n=50). Treating the cells with low-dose PGE2 (0.05 nM to 0.3 nM, Fig. 1A-C) slightly increased the membrane potential and frequency. The membrane potential was −52.4±1.3 mV at 0.05 nM, −49.5±1.8 mV at 0.1 nM, and −49.8±2.5 mV at 0.3 nM. Meanwhile, the pacemaker potential frequency was 12.3±2.9 cycles/5 min at 0.05 nM, 12.7±1 cycles/5 min at 0.1 nM, and 17.3±6.4 cycles/5 min at 0.3 nM (n=5-6). However, at high concentrations (0.5 nM to 10 nM, Fig. 1D-F), PGE2 induced membrane hyperpolarization and inhibited the generation of pacemaker potentials in a dosedependent manner. The summarized changes in membrane potential and pacemaker frequency induced by high concentrations of PGE2 are shown in Fig. 1G and H. These results suggest that PGE2 activates pacemaker potentials at low concentrations and inhibits pacemaker potentials at high concentrations.

(A-C) Pacemaker potentials of ICC under current-clamp mode in control condition and exposed to low concentration of PGE2 (0.05, 0.1 and 0.3 nM); (D-F) Pacemaker potentials of ICC under current-clamp mode in control condition and exposed to high concentration of PGE2 (0.5, 1 and 10 nM); (G, H) Summarized resting membrane potentials and frequency changing of PGE2 action; bars represent mean±SE values. *p<0.05 is significantly different from Control. RMP: resting membrane potentials.

2. Prostanoid EP3 and EP4 receptors are involved in PGE2-induced effects on colonic ICC

It is well known that PGE2 has four EP receptors: EP1, EP2, EP3, and EP4.16 However, the expression of the EP receptors varies depending on the tissues and cell types. To know the EP receptor subtypes found in colonic ICC, we examined the expression of EP receptor subtypes in the total mRNA extracted from the whole-culture colonic cells and the single colonic ICC cells, by performing RT-PCR. From the total RNA extracted from the whole-culture colonic cells, we detected all four subtypes of EP receptors (Fig. 2A). Conversely, the total RNA extracted from a single colonic ICC expressed mRNA encoding for EP3 and EP4 receptor only. This result suggests that the analyzed samples were highly enriched with ICC-specific transcripts. (Fig. 2B). To determine whether EP3 and EP4 receptors are involved in pacemaker activity, we evaluated the effects of a selective EP3 receptor agonist (sulprostone, 100 nM) and an EP4 receptor agonist (CAY10580, 1 µM). Sulprostone depolarized the membranes from −56.6±1.5 mV to −51±2.1 mV and increased pacemaker frequency from 11.2±1.1 cycles/5 min to 29.3±5.3 cycles/5 min. CAY10580 almost totally blocked the generation of pacemaker potentials; the frequency decreased from 11.2±1.1 cycles/5 min to 0.3±0.2 cycles/5 min, but the membrane potential did not change significantly (from −56.6±1.5 mV to −57.9±1.7 mV) (Fig. 2C, D). The effects of sulprostone and CAY10580 on pacemaker potentials were summarized in Fig. 2E and F. These RT-PCR and pharmacological results indicate that PGE2 may mediate pacemaker activity through its action on EP3 and EP4 receptors in colonic ICC.

3. Low dose PGE2 activated pacemaker potentials through the ANO1 channel and the T-type Ca2+ channel

To determine whether or not the ANO1 channel is involved in the effects of low-dose PGE2, ICC were exposed to a specific ANO1 channel inhibitors. The two ANO1 channel blockers (TMEM16A inhibitor 5 µM or MONNA 5 µM) hyperpolarized the cell membrane and inhibited the generation of pacemaker potentials. ICC were further co-treated with ANO1 channel blockers and low-dose PGE2. TMEM16A inhibitor and MONNA significantly blocked the low-dose PGE2-induced augmentation in pacemaker activity (Fig. 3A, B). The effects of the TMEM16A inhibitor and MONNA on the low-dose PGE2-induced effect were summarized in Fig. 3C and D. To confirm whether T-type Ca2+ channels are involved in the PGE2-induced enhancement of pacemaker activity, NiCl2 (50 µM, n=4) and mibefradil (1 µM, n=4) (both are T-type Ca2+ channel blockers) were used. After treating the ICC with these substances, we found that NiCl2 or mibefradil inhibited pacemaker potential frequency but did not alter the membrane potential (Fig. 4A and B). These findings may imply that the activation of T-type Ca2+ channels is vital for the generation of pacemaker activity. This blocked the low-dose PGE2-induced effect on pacemaker activity. The effects of NiCl2 or mibefradil on low-dose PGE2-induced effect were summarized in Fig. 4C and D.

4. High dose of PGE2 activated KATP channels in colonic ICC

It has been reported that KATP channels are activated in colonic ICC during the resting state and contribute to the maintenance of the resting membrane potential. Due to the hyperpolarization effects of PGE2 (10 nM) on ICC, we pre-treated the ICC with glibenclamide (an ATP-sensitive K+ channel blocker) to determine whether the high-concentration effects of PGE2 are linked to its action on KATP channels in colonic ICC. ICC were exposed to glibenclamide (10 µM) and were treated with PGE2 (10 nM) in the continuous presence of glibenclamide (Fig. 5A). Compared to the control, glibenclamide itself showed slight depolarization and frequency increasing, and the PGE2-induced inhibitory effects were significantly blocked by glibenclamide. The effects of glibenclamide on the high-dose PGE2-induced effects were summarized in Fig. 5B and C. These results suggest that KATP channels are involved in high dose PGE2-induced effects in colonic ICC.

Con: control, GBC: glibenclamide, RMP: resting membrane potentials.

5. Voltage-dependent and Ca2+-activated K+ channels are not involved in high dose PGE2 induced effects in colonic ICC

To explore whether PGE2 affects other potassium channels in the ICC, we treated colonic ICC with tetraethylammonium (TEA, a voltage-dependent K+ channel blocker), 4-aminopyridine (4-AP, another voltage-dependent K+ channel blocker), and apamin (a Ca2+-activated K+ channel blocker). TEA 5 mM (n=4), 4-AP 5 mM (n=4), and Apamin 100 nM (n=4) did not block the inhibitory effects of 10 nM PGE2 (Fig. 6A-C). The effects of those blockers on high-dose PGE2-induced effect were summarized in Fig. 6D and E. These results suggest that voltage-dependent K+ channels and Ca2+-activated K+ channels may not be involved in the PGE2-induced inhibition of pacemaker potential in colonic ICC.

DISCUSSION

Basal PGE2 levels in normal colonic tissue are generally within the subnanomolar to low nanomolar range, whereas inflammatory conditions such as colitis may markedly increase PGE2 concentrations due to upregulated COX-2 expression. Accordingly, the low concentrations used in this study (0.05–0.3 nM) may reflect physiological conditions, whereas higher concentrations (0.5–10 nM) nM) may mimic inflammatory states. The differential activation of EP3 and EP4 receptors may partly reflect differences in receptor binding affinity and downstream signaling efficiency, although further receptor-binding studies in colonic ICC are needed.17,18,19 This study has shown that PGE2 mediates pacemaker activity through concentration-dependent actions on EP3 and EP4 receptors in colonic ICC. The membrane depolarization and pacemaker frequency increase induced by low-dose PGE2, and, in contrast, high concentrations of PGE2 induced membrane hyperpolarization and suppressed the generation of pacemaker potentials.

PGE2 was proven to have a dual effect (depending on its concentration) on colonic ICC.20,21 In this study, relatively high concentrations of PGE2 (0.5-10 nM) caused colonic ICC hyperpolarization and the slowing down of pacemaker potential frequency. Contrarily, low concentrations of PGE2 (0.05–0.3 nM) induced membrane depolarization and pacemaker potential enhancement. PGE2 exerts its effects by binding to EP receptors, and its interactions with the different types of EP receptors account for the various effects of PGE2. It has been reported that PGE2 enhanced rat colon contractions via EP1 and EP3 receptors.20 Conversely, PGE2 relaxed mice colon through EP2 and EP4 receptors.21 Based on these initial findings, we performed RT-PCR assays with a single ICC cell. EP3 and EP4 receptors were identified in mRNA transcripts from colonic ICC. Furthermore, the EP3 agonist (sulprostone) induced an enhancement of pacemaker potential and mimicked the effects induced by low concentrations of PGE2. Conversely, the EP4 agonist (CAY10580) inhibited pacemaker potentials, and this effect was similar to that of high concentrations of PGE2. These results suggest that the dual effects induced by PGE2 on colonic ICC are probably due to the activation of EP3 and EP4 receptors. Additionally, one possible reason why PGE2 binds to EP3 and EP4 receptors at different concentrations is the fact that low concentrations of PGE2 have a higher binding affinity to EP3 than EP4.22 In a previous study, it was found that PGE2 usually evokes contraction by acting on EP1 and EP3 receptors in the small intestine and colon.23,24 And in the stomach, the EP3 receptor is expressed in stomach ICC, and this activation by PGE2 leads to an increase in slow wave frequencies.15 These reports support our suggestion that PGE2 regulates the contractile activity of colonic ICC by acting on the EP3 receptor. We reported the exposure of PGE2 in small intestinal ICC generated hyperpolarization of membrane potential and frequency inhibition of pacemaker activity.16 Notably, only EP2 receptor expression was detected in small intestinal ICC. This indicates that the expression of the EP receptor subtype determines the pattern of effect of PGE2. This can be explained by the absence of EP1 or EP3 receptors, which results in only inhibitory action by PGE2 on pacemaker activity in small intestinal ICC. Although selective EP3 and EP4 agonists reproduced the bidirectional effects of PGE2, receptor antagonists were not used in the present study. Therefore, the pharmacological specificity remains partially inferential. Future studies using selective EP receptor antagonists or genetic approaches such as receptor knockdown or knockout models would further strengthen the receptor-specific interpretation of these findings.

To find the signaling of EP3 receptor activation by PGE2, we focused on [Ca2+]i increase by PGE2. Two definite mechanisms for increasing [Ca2+]i by PGE2 have been reported. PGE2 caused both Ca2+ entry through the plasma membrane and Ca2+ release from internal stores25,26,27 and the G protein-phospholipase C (PLC)-inositol triphosphate pathway was suggested for PGE2-induced [Ca2+]i release.23 Furthermore, we already suggested that PLC and [Ca2+]i increase in colonic ICC is important for activation of EP3 receptor in our previous study.6 Because the mechanism of pacemaker activity in ICC is by periodic [Ca2+]i release and uptake of [Ca2+]i from the endoplasmic reticulum, this explains how EP3 receptor activation can lead to depolarization and increasing the pacemaker activity.

It is well known that ANO1 and T-type Ca2+ are important channels for the generation or regulation of pacemaker activity from ICC.4,5 Release of Ca2+ from intracellular stores in ICC activates T-type Ca2+ channels for initiation of pacemaker activity and this leads to ANO1 activation.26 To investigate the involvement of those channels for low-dose PGE2 action, we tested with specific ANO1 or T-type Ca2+ blockers and found they blocked the pacemaker activity of colonic ICC. Recently, we suggested HCN exists in colonic ICC, and this channel regulates the pacemaker activity, but small intestinal ICC did not have a functional HCN channel.6 In this study, we did not examine the involvement of HCN channel for the regulation of pacemaker activity in colonic ICC, but our previous report showed HCN blocker blocked the sulprostone-induced effects on pacemaker potentials in colonic ICC. Therefore, these indicate ANO1, T-type Ca2+, and HCN channels are involved in low-dose PGE2 action on pacemaker activity in colonic ICC. However, further studies will be required to clarify the potential role of those channels in the PGE2-induced modulation of pacemaker activity in colonic ICC.

We proceeded to investigate the effects of high-dose PGE2 on the pacemaker activity of colonic ICC. KATP channels have been reported to play a role in the maintenance of the resting membrane potential and the regulation of cell excitability in various tissues.28,29 Our previous work has also proved that KATP channels play the same role in colonic ICC.30 Activation of KATP channels induces membrane hyperpolarization, whereas inhibition of KATP channels leads to membrane depolarization. Moreover, the action of PGE2 in the small intestine ICC depends on the activation of KATP channels.16 In this study, high-dose PGE2 did not induce a significant inhibition of pacemaker potential after blocking the KATP channel with a KATP channel blocker. And voltage-dependent K+ channel blockers and Ca2+-activated K+ channel blockers did not block the effects induced by high-dose PGE2. Therefore, this suggests that high-dose PGE2 may activate KATP channels, thereby hyperpolarizing the membranes of colonic ICC, just as in small intestinal ICC.

In conclusion, PGE2 was found to have a dual effect on colonic ICC. It may activate EP3 receptors (at low concentrations) and EP4 receptors (at high concentrations). The activation of EP3 receptors by low-dose PGE2 could induce membrane depolarization and increase pacemaker potential frequency. This activation may involve the regulation of ANO1, T-type Ca2+ channels. Conversely, the activation of EP4 receptors by high-dose (relatively) PGE2 induced membrane hyperpolarization and blocked the pacemaker potential frequency. In this condition, PGE2 may open KATP channels. Thus, considering the distribution of EP receptors in the colon, PGE2 may play dual roles in regulating colonic motility. However, the present study was conducted using cultured murine colonic ICC under controlled in vitro conditions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to complex in vivo pathological conditions. In inflammatory bowel disease or colorectal neoplasia, multiple mediators, including cytokines, nitric oxide, and prostanoids, interact simultaneously and may influence ICC function and gastrointestinal motility. Future studies using disease models, such as colitis-induced mice, will be required to determine whether the EP3/EP4-mediated dual regulation of pacemaker activity is maintained under pathological conditions.
