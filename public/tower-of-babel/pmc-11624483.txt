Radiomic‐based biomarkers: Transforming age and body composition metrics into personalized age‐informed indices
Authors: Radin Alikhani, Steven R. Horbal, Amy E. Rothberg, Manjunath P. Pai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronological age has been the standard for quantifying the aging process. While it is simple to quantify it cannot fully discern the biological variability of aging between individuals. The growing body of interest in this variability of human aging has led to the introduction of new biomarkers to operationalize biological age. The inclusion of body composition may provide additional value to biological aging as a prediction and estimation factor of individual health outcomes. Diagnostic images based on radiomic techniques such as Computed Tomography contain an untapped wealth of patient‐specific data that remain inaccessible to healthcare providers. These images are beneficial for collecting information from body composition that adds precision and granularity when compared to traditional measures. This information can subsequently be aggregated to construct models for changes in the human body associated with aging. In addition, aging leads to a natural decline in the best parameter of drug dosing in older adults, glomerular filtration rate. Since the conventional models of kidney function are correlated with age and body composition, the radiomic biomarkers representing age‐related changes in body composition may also serve as potential new imaging biomarkers of kidney function for personalized dosing. Our review introduces potential radiomic biomarkers as measures of body composition change targeting the aging processes. As a functional example, we have hypothesized an age‐related model of radiomics as a covariate of kidney function to improve personalized dosing. Future research focusing on evaluating this hypothesis in human subject studies is acknowledged.

INTRODUCTION

Changes in body composition are among the most prominent physical signs of aging. 1 These structural and functional changes to the body can be monitored and quantified over time to track serious clinical implications and disease prognoses. 2 Body composition measurement has evolved from traditional anthropometric human body measures such as weight, height, and waist circumference to advanced radiologic imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA). 3 These imaging techniques provide extensive clinical value, revealing patient and disease characteristics not accessible by physical examination. 4 Additionally, chronological age, the traditional measure of aging, cannot precisely distinguish physiological differences among people of the same age group, leading to the introduction of biological age. 5 Radiologic imaging biomarkers or “radiomics” representing body composition and aging can shift the paradigm of clinical measures towards personalized treatment plans, improve diagnostic accuracy, and streamline healthcare workflows. 6 , 7 Despite their potential, radiomic biomarkers are understudied in the context of human aging and pharmacotherapy in older adults.

Several radiomic markers have been established for different organs and tissues, which can predict organ function or report survival, morbidity, and mortality after a major clinical event or surgery. 8 , 9 , 10 The kidneys, for example, undergo major changes attributed to aging, and their function is crucial for drug dosing in older adults. 11 New age‐related radiomic biomarkers of body composition can be used to quantify these changes in kidney function with aging. To validate this approach, pharmacokinetic studies will need to be conducted to confirm the accuracy of approximating kidney function using a combination of biomarkers. This review focuses on conventional body composition measures from an aging perspective and proposes a model of age‐informed body composition biomarkers derived from CT scans (BAMor) as a factor of kidney function for future dosing optimization and translational studies.

RADIOMIC TRENDS OF AGING

Given the variability in how individuals age, there is an increasing demand for tools that accurately measure biological age to foster and support healthy aging practices. 12 The preliminary findings in employing radiomic features for measuring biological age are promising. For instance, one study suggests that radiomic features obtained from MRI scans can estimate the biological age of different cardiac regions such as left and right ventricles (LV, RV), atria (LA, RA), and the LV myocardium. 13 Of the cardiac regions modeled, the LV age model performed the best, whereas the RV model showed the largest discrepancies between estimated BA and CA and the highest errors. Also, the age gap for each cardiac region, determined as the difference between biological and chronological age, was strongly correlated with increased visceral fat and higher bone mineral density (BMD) for larger and smaller age gaps, respectively. These results indicate that different regions of the heart age at different rates. By highlighting the different aging rates in heart regions, these findings underscore the valuable role of radiomic techniques in studying biological aging through body composition.

The opportunities that radiomic techniques provide for studying biological aging with body composition are just as important as their uses in clinical medicine. They can monitor body composition at various spatial scales ranging from the whole body to the cellular level and quantify the geometry of the region of interest in terms of volume, surface area, density, depth, etc. 4 Cross‐sectional analyses of body composition with CT and MRI techniques are considered the gold standard methods. 14 CT scans are generally faster and less expensive than MRI scans but use ionizing radiation. 15 This cost‐effectiveness can make CT a more feasible option for routine body composition analysis, especially in settings where resources are limited. 16 The annual number of CT scans performed is double that of MRI scans in the United States. 17 , 18 Therefore, in this review, our focus is mostly on CT‐based markers rather than markers from MRI or other imaging tools.

The application of cross‐sectional CT imaging to assess skeletal muscle and other radiomic biomarkers offers a powerful tool for enhancing clinical measurements, though it comes with significant considerations related to both cost and patient safety due to radiation exposure. 21 The average cost of a lumbar spine CT scan, where the psoas muscle is typically measured, is around $540. 22 This cost, alongside other common CT procedures like chest ($711) and brain ($446) scans, highlights the financial considerations that must be accounted for in clinical practice. 23 However, once the CT scan is performed, the remaining workflow—using semi‐automated HU thresholds for image analysis to segment and measure the muscle area—is relatively low‐cost, involving mainly the time and expertise of the clinician or researcher. 24 , 25 Another important factor is radiation exposure. A lumbar spine CT scan delivers approximately 6 millisieverts (mSv) of radiation, which remains well within the safety thresholds established by the Radiologic Society of North America, which suggests low risks at doses below 50 mSv for single procedures or 100 mSv for multiple procedures. 26 , 27 In a similar context, other common CT procedures such as chest scans (7 mSv), abdominal and pelvic scans (10 mSv), and Cardiac for calcium scoring (3 mSv) all fall well below the 50 mSv threshold for single procedures, reinforcing the assertion by the Radiologic Society of North America. 26 , 27 While the costs and radiation exposure are non‐negligible, the precision and reproducibility of CT imaging render it an invaluable resource for assessing body composition.

Muscle

Aging is associated with a simultaneous decrease in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and quality. 28 Longitudinal studies have reported an annual 1%–2% decline in muscle mass after age 50, leading to up to 40% loss in the seventh decade of life compared to young adults. 29 The decline in muscle strength is even greater, peaking around 25–35 years and then decreasing at an estimated rate of 1.5%–3% annually after age 50. 29 This trajectory of muscle changes over time varies by sex, with men experiencing a higher rate of muscle mass decline and a higher prevalence of sarcopenia. 30 Age‐related loss of muscle mass may also be accompanied by increased body fat, resulting in significant weakness even if body weight appears normal. 31

Apart from the SMA and SMI mentioned earlier, single‐muscle measures for the psoas muscle are also recognized as biomarkers for sarcopenia. The psoas muscle, a long, ribbon‐shaped muscle found on both sides of the spine and the edge of the lower pelvis, is one of the most important muscles covering the vertebral column. 32 Psoas muscle area (PMA), the cross‐sectional bilateral surface area of the psoas muscle, psoas muscle index (PMI), the PMA divided by the patient's height, and psoas muscle density (PMD), the average cross‐sectional muscle attenuation in Hounsfield units (HU), are determined to provide information about the muscle status and diagnosis of sarcopenia. 33

The psoas muscle can only be measured using abdominal CT or MRI scans. Because of its central location, it is difficult to image with ultrasound, which complicates prospective and repeated data collection. Unlike the psoas muscle, the dorsal muscle group (DMG) is easier to capture using both CT and ultrasound imaging, making it a better option for facilitating prospective studies on sarcopenia. 34 DMG area refers to any muscles located behind the spine and ribs, extending no farther outwards than the outer edges of the erector spinae muscles. 34 The cross‐sectional area of DMG can be commonly measured using both thoracic and abdominal scans and is reported to be a significant independent predictor of survival and mortality in liver transplantation recipients. 34 It has also been used with other CT‐based biomarkers (psoas muscle area and subcutaneous fat area) to screen severely malnourished patients. 35 Using the Reference Analytic Morphomic Population (RAMP) data developed at the University of Michigan from trauma patients across all age groups, the trajectory of changes in skeletal muscle and DMG expanded muscle areas with age shows that these areas peak around age 30 in men and age 20 in women, and then decrease throughout the rest of life (Figure 1a).

Aging is also associated with fat infiltration into skeletal muscle, known as myosteatosis. 36 Given that increased fat reduces the density of muscles, myosteatosis can be characterized as decreased skeletal muscle density. Myosteatosis is associated with several complications such as inflammation, severe liver disease, insulin resistance, metabolic dysfunction, hip fractures, and mortality in older adult individuals. 36 , 37 Myosteatosis is shown to be correlated with other CT scan measurements such as muscle density, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat. 38 Recent findings have highlighted it as a key factor in staging chronic kidney disease (CKD), along with high levels of aortic calcium, which is discussed in the next paragraph. 39

As people age after 40 years, the likelihood of calcified plaque formation and vessel narrowing increases, leading to atherosclerosis and promoting further plaque formation. 40 , 41 Evidence supporting abdominal aortic calcifications (AAC) as a clinical correlate and useful predictor of cardiovascular events is growing. 42 AAC is commonly seen on CT scans, which can reliably determine the presence and extent of these calcifications. AAC can also serve as an indicator of worsening CKD. The prevalence and progression of aortic calcification increase with more advanced stages of CKD. 43 AAC and kidney function are interrelated through shared pathophysiological processes, common risk factors, and their impact on vascular health. Both conditions often coexist in patients with CKD, where factors like hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia contribute to calcification in both the aorta and renal arteries. 44 Aortic calcification, particularly when leading to aortic stenosis, can impair renal blood flow, increasing vascular resistance and leading to a decline in kidney function. 45

Bone

At any given age, bone density depends on the maximum bone mass and the subsequent bone loss. 46 As people age, their bones lose mass and density and contain fewer minerals, making them less strong. Men and women generally reach their highest bone mass by age 30. After this age, men tend to lose about 0.3% of their bone mass annually, while women lose slightly more, about 0.5% per year. 47 Similar to muscle mass, the average rate of bone loss is reported as 1%–2% per year after middle age, with women experiencing a greater decrease of 2%–3% per year from 1 to 3 years pre‐menopause until 5–8 years post‐menopause, then slowing to 1% per year. 48 , 49

Genetic factors and race, along with controllable lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, and optimal body weight are the major determinants of bone health. 50 BMD is significantly influenced by genetics, with heritability estimates ranging from 50% to 85%. 51 Among the racial groups, in general, increased African genetic admixture is linked to higher BMD but greater declines in bone strength with aging. 51 Accordingly, black adults typically have the highest BMD and the lowest rates of bone loss compared to White and Asian adults. Asians, on the contrast, often have lower BMD than other racial groups. 52 Nevertheless, some inconsistencies exist in the reported mean BMD differences among these racial groups. Racial disparities in bone density remain even after controlling for body mass. 53 Studies suggest that a 10% increase in bone mass may decrease the likelihood of fractures by up to 50%. 54 Traditionally, single‐ or dual‐photon absorptiometry (SPA/DPA) followed by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) has been employed as the gold standard for measuring BMD, serving as a proxy for bone strength and fracture risk. 55 , 56 DEXA offers numerous benefits such as low radiation exposure and scanning skeletal areas most susceptible to osteoporotic fractures. However, DEXA cannot provide a complete assessment of bone size as it does not account for differences in bone depth. 56 Therefore, researchers have started using HU measurements from CT scans to evaluate BMD in specific chest, abdomen, or spine regions. 38 CT scans now allow for measuring volumetric BMD and assessing the structural dimensions and internal organization of cortical and trabecular bone, providing estimates of bone strength (Figure 2). 56 Figure 2 provides a schematic view of measuring BMD through DEXA or CT scans. Bone density HU values below 100 indicate osteoporosis, values between 100 and 160 suggest osteopenia, and values above 160 reflect normal bone mineral density. 58 On the contrary, DEXA is a real measurement of BMD (g/cm2), which can be influenced by bone size, thickness, and geometry. Larger bones may appear to have higher BMD simply because the area is larger, not necessarily because the bone is denser. 59 DEXA BMD is often reported using T‐scores, which compare a patient's BMD to the average peak BMD of a young, healthy reference population: Normal bone density: T‐score ≥−1; Low bone density (Osteopenia): T‐score between −1 and −2.5; and Severe bone loss (Osteoporosis): T‐score ≤−2.5.

Adipose tissue (body fat)

Adipose tissue plays a crucial role in physiology beyond merely storing energy, making its assessment essential in body composition analysis. Therefore, fat quantification should involve compartment‐specific analyses, which cannot be provided by BMI. 60 BMI is likely to misrepresent the risk of obesity‐related chronic diseases because it does not accurately reflect body fat and muscle levels, particularly at higher BMI levels. 61 CT is viewed as the gold standard technique for accurately measuring the area of adipose tissue. 62 It enables the distinct examination of the subcutaneous fat (SAT), known as the fat beneath the skin, and visceral fat (VAT), which is fat stored around intra‐abdominal organs. 63 Quantifying VAT generally involves segmenting the intra‐abdominal compartment and calculating the cross‐sectional area of tissue. These measurements are typically taken at abdominal levels ranging from L1 to L4. 64 Also, subcutaneous fat volume is measured by segmenting the area from the abdominal wall muscles to the skin at a given level. The visceral and subcutaneous fat areas can then be combined to determine the total fat area. 60 , 64

The accumulation and redistribution of the adipose tissue in the human body could be affected by aging. 65 Anatomically, the distribution of adipose tissue is often characterized by sex: Men tend to accumulate more VAT, and women usually have considerably more SAT. 66 CT assessments of abdominal VAT have confirmed that men accumulate more VAT than women at the same weight (Figure 1b). Additionally, the findings from the RAMP data demonstrate that SAT area measures at L3 peak approximately at age 40 in men and women, while VAT area measures at L3 are greater in older adulthood than in young adulthood on average. After menopause, women's fat distribution shifts to resemble men's, leading to increased central obesity. 66 , 67 Studies have shown a longitudinal association between visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and the development of metabolic syndrome, whereas subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) appears to have a protective effect against the incidence of individual components of metabolic syndrome. 68 , 69 Greater SAT distribution is correlated with reduced rates of cardiometabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in both sexes. 66

Furthermore, the VAT/SAT ratio can be utilized for prognostic purposes. One study showed that an increased VAT/SAT ratio is associated with decreased overall survival in patients with pancreatic cancer. 70 Another study reported an increased major adverse cardiac events in patients with low BMI having a high VAT/SAT ratio. 71 Finally, patients with gastric cancer who had a higher VAT/SAT ratio were at a greater risk of developing infections at the surgical site after gastrectomy. 72 Radiologic measurement of SAT and VAT at different vertebra levels can be useful in predicting several clinical outcomes such as nonmetastatic breast cancer survival, Crohn's disease prognosis, COVID‐19 hospitalization, and mortality, and guiding personalized treatment strategies. 73 , 74 , 75

A MODEL OF AGE‐INFORMED RADIOMIC BIOMARKERS AS A COVARIATE OF KIDNEY FUNCTION

Kidney function declines with aging, with anatomical changes that are consistent with this decline. 11 As age increases, the kidney's cortex decreases in size, weight, volume, and number of glomeruli. 76 Age‐related congestive degeneration and hardening of the glomeruli lead to reduced renal tubule function and decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which determines kidney function and CKD stages. 76 Changes in kidney function with age have profound importance in dosing optimization, especially in older adults. 77 Despite numerous publications improving models for estimating kidney function, we still rely on the primarily originated models (e.g., Cockcroft‐Gault) for drug dosing, especially in older adults. 78

As people age, there is a concurrent decline in muscle mass and urinary creatinine clearance (CrCl). 79 Due to increased heterogeneity in muscle mass and creatinine production with aging, CrCl is a less accurate measure of muscle mass and lean body mass in older adults compared to young adults. 80 Despite the age‐related decline in CrCl, plasma creatinine levels do not rise with aging. 81 In healthy older adults, the true GFR measured by inulin clearance is significantly lower than in young adults but remains within the normal range. Inulin is the gold standard marker for evaluating renal clearance and GFR. Both measured CrCl and the Cockcroft‐Gault formula underestimates the true GFR. 78 , 82 Research continues to find new biomarkers of kidney function to develop better equations.

Apart from filtration, the kidneys also play an important role in the endocrine system by secreting hormones to maintain blood pressure, regulate fluid and salt homeostasis, and promote bone integrity by activating vitamin D3. 83 , 84 The advent of radiomics and the opportunistic screening of radiomic images opened new horizons to finding biomarkers that provide an extensive kidney health and function assessment. Nevertheless, sparse studies have been conducted for this purpose. Among the radiomic techniques, CT scans are the preferred diagnostic tool for CKD since they can provide high‐quality images of the renal parenchyma. 85 , 86

The radiomic‐based analysis of major body composition components—especially muscles involved in creatinine production—with aging can potentially introduce new biomarkers for kidney function that can be validated against the existing models of kidney function for greater precision. One study demonstrated that patient‐specific measures of the skeletal muscle area from their CT scans were strongly correlated with the Cl of aminoglycoside, a surrogate for kidney function, in a retrospective cohort of inpatient adults with a median age of 57 years. 87 Additionally, a recent cross‐sectional study of 400 healthy subjects with a mean age of 56.2 years found that adipose tissue and VAT were positively correlated with cystatin C (an endogenous biomarker of kidney function) metabolism, and inversely correlated with eGFR estimated via cystatin C serum concentration (eGFRcys). 88 Furthermore, reduced kidney function with aging can cause lower vitamin D3 levels, calcium reabsorption, and bone density, which are the risk factors for bone pain, muscle twitching, and fragility fractures. 89

The correlations between the covered radiomic biomarkers and kidney function illuminate the potential to refine conventional models of estimating kidney function by replacing body weight‐based measures. Each age‐informed radiomic biomarker discussed in this review can be evaluated in the population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) models of kidney function surrogates alone or with other covariates (age, sex, Scr, etc.) to determine whether they can improve the dosing of medications. Given the diversity of the radiomic biomarkers and their personalized identity, it would be advantageous to design a model or index using these biomarkers. This index, derived from radiomics and representing changes in body composition with aging, may be classified as a type of biological age. Efforts in quantifying biological age have dramatically increased in the past decade. Epigenetic clocks and metabolic measurements are trendy examples of quantifying biological age. 90 , 91

Some models of radiomic biological age have been constructed over the past decade to predict essential clinical outcomes. One of the earliest models was introduced by researchers at the Morphomic Analysis Group at the University of Michigan in 2013 to predict postoperative survival. 92 Additionally, Cron et al. introduced a model of morphomic age consisting of visceral fat area, body depth, and total body area as pre‐transplant measures predictive of post‐kidney transplantation diabetes mellitus in 99 kidney recipients. 93 In another study, psoas muscle area, psoas muscle density, and aortic calcification were significantly associated with surgical risk in terms of 1‐year postoperative mortality and the length of stay in a cohort of 1370 patients who underwent general and vascular surgery at the University of Michigan. 18 Nevertheless, a model of morphomic age has not been assessed as a covariate of kidney function. The capability of morphomic age to predict clinical outcomes has led to interest in the potential assessment of the pharmacokinetic changes of drugs. As mentioned earlier, the skeletal muscle area can improve the estimation of aminoglycoside Cl. In a recent study, Pieters et al. developed an automated deep learning‐based algorithm for the body composition (muscle and fat) analysis of the abdominal CT scans at the 3rd lumbar vertebra (L3) and introduced two validated equations (CRAFT 1 and CRAFT 2) to estimate the creatinine excretion. 94 CRAFT 1 equation includes CT parameters, age, weight, and stature, whereas CRAFT 2 eliminates weight and stature. These equations demonstrated more accurate results than Cockcroft‐Gault and Ix equations. These promising results highlight the potential application of CT radiomics as a low‐cost, reliable, and safe tool to identify and predict aging consequences on drug pharmacokinetics, organ functions, and clinical outcomes.

Integrating more precise biomarkers of body composition in translational studies can benefit clinicians and patients. 95 The promise of these biomarkers includes assistance in diagnosing serious complications of aging and providing patient‐specific therapeutic and non‐therapeutic treatment plans. Patients can also get pragmatic recommendations for healthier aging, safer medications, and better outcomes. According to Table 2, BAMor excels in specificity and age‐informativeness, offering a nuanced picture of an individual's aging process by quantitatively capturing physiological changes like bone density, muscle damage, visceral fat accumulation, vascular calcification, etc. BAMor's effectiveness in predicting morbidity and mortality risks is proven in previous studies. This tailored approach holds the potential to reflect lifestyle influence and the effectiveness of clinical interventions on an individual's biological state more accurately than chronological age.

While chronological age is known as the common measure of time, it is less sensitive to immediate biological shifts but remains consistent over time. The ease of ascertainment, low cost, and widespread standardization render chronological age the solid foundation upon which policies and health system frameworks are built. Its universal applicability, without the need for complex imaging and software‐based analysis, facilitates its continued use across human subject studies.

Although radiomic data is compelling, blending its depth with the broad applicability of chronological age proposes an elegant symbiosis for future aging research. The integration is likely to yield more robust aging models and refine our understanding of aging as a multifactorial process. The use of radiomic data in personalizing healthcare, predicting disease onset and progression, and crafting preemptive interventions represents a frontier in pharmacotherapy that harnesses the promise of precision dosing. This integration aligns with the aspiration to craft more deeply informed health strategies that account for the biological uniqueness of individuals as they age. Nevertheless, using cross‐sectional imaging for serial assessments of BAMor presents considerable challenges, particularly in terms of cost, time, and accessibility for patients and clinicians, which currently limits its widespread use. In light of these challenges, prioritizing the use of this approach in patients who could benefit most from precise BAMor assessments, such as those with advanced CKD or metabolic conditions would be more beneficial. Ongoing advancements in imaging technology and artificial intelligence hold the potential to streamline these processes, making them more cost‐effective and accessible to a broader patient population in the future.

In conclusion, medical imaging technology offers valuable insights into the physical manifestations of aging on body composition, enhancing the precision of pharmacotherapy in clinical practice. A composite model of morphomic age can provide impactful information about age‐related changes in body composition and different organ functions. Future investigations on the correlation between morphomic age and kidney function may help refine dosing regimens in the aging population, optimizing therapeutic outcomes.
