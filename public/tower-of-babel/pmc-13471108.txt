HSV-1 metabolic hijacking: Mechanisms to precision therapeutics
Authors: Siping Kuang, Jingjing Zhang, Junjie Chen, Zhiyang He, Junfeng Yang, Shuli Li, Juntao Ding, Zhenghai Ma, Beibei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes Simplex Virus Type 1 (HSV-1) strategically reprograms host cell metabolism to fulfill its replicative needs and establish persistent infection. This hijacking extends across glucose, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide pathways, diverting resources toward viral biosynthesis. Critically, the resulting metabolic landscape is not neutral but actively immunosuppressive. By altering the availability of key metabolites and cellular energy states, HSV-1 impairs the function of innate and adaptive immune cells, including antigen presentation by dendritic cells and the effector response of T cells, thereby enabling immune evasion. This review highlights that a systematic understanding of HSV-1-induced immunometabolic dysregulation reveals novel therapeutic targets. Exploiting these metabolic vulnerabilities – through pharmacological intervention or engineered oncolytic viruses – offers a promising precision medicine approach against HSV-1 infection and related pathologies.

Introduction

HSV-1 is a widely disseminated human pathogen that establishes lifelong latent infection and can cause a spectrum of diseases. HSV-1 belongs to the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae, genus Simplexvirus, and is classified as Human alphaherpesvirus 1 (HHV-1) [1]. Globally, approximately 3.7 billion individuals are estimated to be infected with HSV-1, making it a pervasive lifelong pathogen [2]. It is responsible for recurrent orolabial herpes, sight-threatening keratitis, fatal encephalitis, and potentially contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, representing a significant and persistent public health burden [3].

The lifecycle of HSV-1 is highly dependent on the metabolic resources of the host cell. Viral reprogramming of host metabolic networks to obtain energy, macromolecular precursors, and membrane structures necessary for replication has emerged as a central aspect of its pathogenic mechanism [4]. This mirrors strategies observed with other viruses; for instance, Human papillomavirus (HPV) modulates host glycolytic rates through proteins such as E2 and E7, while Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection in HepG2.2.15 cells significantly increases phosphatidylcholine (PC) expression [5]. Although it is known that HSV-1 activates certain metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis [6], a comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the virus’s systematic remodeling of multiple core host metabolic pathways – including carbohydrate, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism, as well as energy metabolism – and how these metabolic alterations actively contribute to immune evasion, remains lacking.

This review aims to summarize the extensive metabolic reprogramming induced by HSV-1 infection in host cells, with a focus on its impact on the key metabolic pathways mentioned above. Importantly, we will delve into how these virus-driven metabolic changes are exploited to promote HSV-1 immune evasion and persistent infection by interfering with the recognition, activation, or effector functions of immune cells, and by altering the metabolomic composition of the immune microenvironment. Systematically deciphering the metabolic dependencies of HSV-1 and its immunomodulatory mechanisms will not only enhance our comprehensive understanding of its pathogenesis but also provide an important theoretical foundation and potential therapeutic targets for developing novel antiviral strategies that target virus – host metabolic interactions.

HSV-1-induced host metabolic reprogramming

Previous studies have shown that various viruses reprogram host cell metabolism to promote their replication [7]. Recent metabolomics investigations reveal that HSV-1 infection alters metabolites associated with central carbon metabolism, choline metabolism, amino acid metabolism, purine and pyrimidine metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, bile secretion, and prolactin signaling pathways in Human embryonic lung diploid fibroblast cell line (KMB17) [8]. These changes are confirmed in patients with herpes simplex keratitis (HSK) [9]. HSV-1 infection downregulates most metabolites (particularly spermine and purine/pyrimidine nucleobases) in the host trigeminal ganglia, with only deoxyinosine and cytidine being upregulated. This results in a depletion of DNA/RNA synthesis substrates, creating a nutritionally depleted neuronal environment that may restrict viral replication and facilitate latency maintenance [10]. Similarly, the Human Cytomegalovirus (HCMV) unique long 38 (UL38) protein drives glycolysis and specific amino acid catabolism by inhibiting tuberous sclerosis complex 2 protein (TSC2). The UL38-mediated metabolic flux activation is independent of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), whereas the induction of metabolite concentrations is mTOR-dependent, establishing a proviral environment [11].

In human microglia clone 3 (HMC3), HSV-1 inhibits PTEN induced kinase 1/Parkin (PINK1/Parkin)-mediated mitophagy through its encoded protein kinase unique short 3 protein (US3), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction (including mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) accumulation, structural damage, and reduced oxygen consumption rate (OCR)) and neuroinflammation. In contrast, the compound activates Unc-51-like Kinase 1/Ras-related Protein Rab-9A (ULK1/Rab9) dependent mitophagy by promoting ULK1 Ser555 phosphorylation and Rab9 puncta formation, thereby reversing HSV-1-induced mitophagy inhibition, improving mitochondrial function and phagocytosis, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)), and enhancing interferon-mediated anti-HSV-1 immunity [12].

Furthermore, HSV-1 infection induces significant host metabolic alterations, characterized by suppressed viral replication through modulation of inhibitor of nuclear factor Kappa-B kinase subunit β (IKKβ) phosphorylation, concomitant promotion of collagen synthesis, and reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [13]. Collectively, these metabolic changes constitute key pathophysiological features of HSV-1 infection.

Glucose metabolic reprogramming: hijacking energy supply and sculpting the immune microenvironment

The metabolic reprogramming induced by HSV-1 infection is a dynamic, virus-orchestrated process. In primary human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs), kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis reveals significant remodeling at 6 and 24 hours post-infection (hpi), characterized by reduced tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity and enhanced glycolysis, linked to dysregulated carbon and pyruvate metabolism. At 6 hpi, upregulated proteins enrich ribosomal and proteasomal pathways, while downregulated proteins enrich the spliceosome and TCA cycle. By 24 hpi, upregulated proteins shift to glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and amino acid biosynthesis, with downregulated proteins linked to the spliceosome and bile secretion [14].

In contrast to the local corneal epithelial cell response, in vivo, during ocular infection, HSV-1 elevates blood glucose, promotes glucose uptake and glycolysis in CD4+ T cells, and exacerbates corneal inflammation. Systemic inhibition of glucose metabolism with 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) alleviates corneal pathology but increases fatal encephalitis risk during acute replication, underscoring intervention complexity [15]. This glycolytic enhancement is confirmed in Uppsala 373 malignant glioma (U373) and HMC3 cells [16]. Although certain metabolic features (e.g. enhanced glycolysis) appear conserved between corneal epithelial cells and systemic CD4+ T cells, the functional outcomes differ markedly, highlighting the need to distinguish local ocular from systemic immune responses.

Analogous reprogramming occurs with other herpesviruses. Reactivation of HHV-6 in myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) patients induces mitochondrial fragmentation, enhances one-carbon metabolism, suppresses superoxide dismutase (SOD2) and amino acid/glucose oxidation proteins, and is driven by increased release of ROS and mtDNA. Meanwhile, downregulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase 1 (PDP1), interferon-β (IFN-β), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), TNF-α, SOD2, and IFN-γ ultimately triggers a pro-inflammatory cell danger response. This establishes a broad-spectrum antiviral state but severely impairs cellular energy metabolism [17].

HSV-1 employs specific mechanisms to modulate host metabolism. Its UL43 protein, via a Tyr-Pro-Leu-Phe (YPLF) motif, interacts with host ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 2 (ARL2), interfering with its mitochondrial localization. This recruits and phosphorylates adenine nucleotide translocator 1 (ANT1), increasing expression of oxidative phosphorylation components (Complexes I, III, IV, ATP synthase), influencing mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, and boosting ATP production [18]. Similarly, in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), UL16 interacts with ANT2 to promote glucose oxidative phosphorylation, increase ATP, and restore D-galactose-induced mitochondrial dysfunction [19]. Host cells also leverage metabolic shifts for defense. HSV infection activates the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to initiate antiviral immunity. In the human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cell line (KYSE-30), stimulator of interferon genes (STING) positively regulates this pathway by stabilizing transketolase (TKT), thereby enhancing host restriction. Inhibiting the PPP or TKT impairs STING‑mediated immunity [20].

Metabolites form a regulatory interface. Lactate from HSV-1-induced aerobic glycolysis triggers protein lactylation, targeting proteins like RNA binding motif protein 14 (RBM14) and interferon gamma inducible protein 16 (IFI16) to modulate immune signaling and facilitate evasion [21]. Conversely, in Tohoku Hospital Psychiatry-1 (THP-1) cells exploit this modification: HSV-1 infection enhances lactylation of the RNA m6A demethylase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), altering its interaction with establishment of sister chromatid cohesion N-acetyltransferase 2 (ESCO2) and sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) to augment IFN-β mRNA synthesis [22]. In primary peritoneal macrophages, the intracellular L-lactate sensor alanyl-tRNA synthetase 2 (AARS2) mediates lactylation of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) at its N-terminus, switching it from DNA binding to rejection, inhibiting cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthesis and enhancing replication. Inhibiting cGAS lactylation by blocking monocarboxylate transporter 1/solute carrier family 16 member 1 (MCT1/SLC16A1) holds therapeutic promise [23]. A similar lactylation axis exists in other herpesviruses. In Kaposi’s Sarcoma-Associated Herpesvirus (KSHV), lactylation of N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) at lysine 290, facilitated by the viral polyadenylated nuclear RNA (PAN), promotes NAT10-α-tubulin acetyltransferase 1 (ATAT1) interaction, driving ac4C modification of tRNASer-CGA–1–1 and viral reactivation [24]. HCMV infection induces lactylation of host AARS1 and gluconeogenic proteins, including upregulation of lactylation at lysine 62 of pyruvate kinase M, promoting dissemination [21] (Figure 1). The transcription factor hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), regulating key glycolytic enzymes (e.g. glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)), is critical for efficient replication. In Caco2 cells, inhibiting HIF-1α with BAY87-2243 significantly reduces HSV-1 mRNA, protein, and cytopathic effect (CPE) [25]. And in KYSE-30 cells, HSV-1 enhances the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), mediating O-GlcNAcylation of STING to activate an antiviral response [26]. Conversely, upregulation of this pathway and its key metabolite uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine enhances O-GlcNAcylation of the host restriction factor sterile alpha motif and histidine/D-aspartic acid domain-containing protein 1 (SAMHD1) at serine 93, potentially promoting its stability and inhibiting HSV-1 replication, illustrating bidirectional metabolic-immune crosstalk [27]. Late in infection, restored ATP production in Mouse Embryonic Fibroblast (MEF) cells coincides with increased mitochondrial Ca2 + and proton leak, indicating a further shift toward glycolysis to meet replication demands [28]. Mechanistically, HSV-1 upregulates phosphofructokinase-1 and promotes its serine phosphorylation to activate glycolysis, increasing ATP generation [29]. In HSV-1 induced HSK, corneal HK2 is upregulated while mitochondrial complex activity is reduced. Lonidamine inhibits HK2, reversing aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), decreasing lactate, restoring mitochondrial function, and reducing viral protein (e.g. glycoprotein D (gD), infected cell protein 0 (ICP0)) expression, identifying HK2-mediated glycolysis as key to HSK pathogenesis [30]. HK2 function is context-dependent; its overexpression in neurodegenerative models attenuates dopaminergic neuron death, suggesting a neuroprotective role [31] (Figure 1).

Metabolic reprogramming is a shared strategy across Herpesviridae. HHV-6A activates the AKT serine/threonine kinase- mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (AKT-mTORC1) pathway in T cells (HSB-2), enhancing AKT phosphorylation and downstream effectors p70 ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), inducing glycolytic gene upregulation [32]. Similarly, KSHV viral G protein-coupled receptor (vGPCR) stabilizes HIF-1α under hypoxia, inducing a Warburg effect and enhancing proliferation [33,34]. Hosts have evolved metabolism-based defenses. Immune responsive gene 1 (IRG1) catalyzes itaconate production, and the IRG1-itaconate axis modulates immunity in an HSV-1 infection in an type I interferon (IFN-I) dependent manner [35]. Conversely, thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) in Human Embryonic Kidney 293 cells expressing the SV40 large T antigen (HEK293T) promotes viral replication by inhibiting mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) aggregation, disrupting the TANK-binding kinase 1/ interferon regulatory factor 3 (TBK1/IRF3) pathway and reducing IFN-β production. TXNIP knockout significantly inhibits replication [36] (Figure 1).

Amino acid and translational reprogramming: commanding metabolic-immune checkpoints and hijacking the translational machinery

HCMV and HSV-1, among other viruses, promote their replication and achieve immune evasion by manipulating host amino acid and polyamine metabolism. The tryptophan-kynurenine (Kyn) metabolic axis is crucial. HCMV infection induces indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), increasing Kyn production, which activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway. The activated AhR/aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) complex represses cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A/1B (CDKN1Aand CDKN1B) expression in Human Foreskin Fibroblasts (HFFs), relieving inhibition on cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2). This drives the cell cycle past the G1/S checkpoint, facilitating viral DNA replication and aiding immune evasion from natural killer (NK) cells [37]. Similarly, HSV-1, in cell lines such as Human Hepatocellular Carcinoma (Huh7), Human Neuroblastoma (SK-N-SH), and African Green Monkey Kidney Epithelial Cells (Vero), activates tryptophan-Kyn metabolism via an IFN-dependent mechanism, driving AhR signaling to promote immediate-early gene transcription and upregulate viral receptor expression, enhancing replication. AhR antagonists or metabolic inhibitors are thus promising anti-HSV-1 strategies [38]. In KMB17 cells, HSV-1 infection downregulates tryptophan, while exogenous Kyn pretreatment increases viral protein and titer, confirming this pathway’s pivotal role [8]. Furthermore, in infected Human Corneal Epithelial Cells (HCECs), HSV-1 upregulates key enzymes (tryptophan hydroxylase 1/2 (TPH-1/2), dopa decarboxylase (DDC)) and the serotonin transporter (SERT), promoting serotonin synthesis and uptake to enhance replication. HSV-1-induced serotonin levels in aqueous humor correlate with viral load and keratitis severity, suggesting this pathway as a therapeutic target [39] (Figure 1).

Beyond tryptophan, arginine and polyamine metabolism are significant. Polyamines like spermine and spermidine modulate transcription and translation. The spermine analog DenSpm inhibits HSV-1 in vitro. Conversely, the catabolic enzyme spermidine/spermine N1 -acetyltransferase 1 (SAT1) reduces intracellular polyamine levels in Hela cells and inhibits Z-form DNA (Z-DNA) accumulation, enhancing cGAS-mediated innate immunity [40]. SAT1 expression also benefits the host in suppressing HSV-1 replication in Human Corneal Epithelial (HCE) cells [10]. HSV-1 transcriptionally activates peptidyl arginine deiminase 2/3/4 (PAD2/3/4), enhancing citrullination of interferon-induced proteins Interferon-Induced Protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 1/2 (IFIT1/2). This weakens the host interferon response and promotes immune evasion. Small-molecule PAD inhibitors host-targeting antivirals (HTAs) can restore host defense in various cell lines (e.g. Adult Retinal Pigment Epithelial cell line 19 (ARPE-19); SH-SY5Y; HFFs) [41]. (Figure 1).

Viruses hijack glutamine metabolism for biosynthetic demands. Activated T cells rely on glutamine to enhance function, and glutamine supplementation can inhibit HSV-1 reactivation by strengthening IFN-γ production in CD8+ T cells [42]. However, HCMV infection enhances glutamine catabolism, generating α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) to replenish the TCA cycle and promote replication [43]. Similarly, KSHV upregulates the glutamine transporter solute carrier family 15 member 5 (SLC15A5) and glutaminase, driving glutaminolysis to sustain the TCA cycle and support infected cell survival; targeting this metabolism inhibits KSHV proliferation [44,45]. In a herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) model, inhibiting glutamine metabolism (e.g. with 6-diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine (DON)) alleviated HSV-1-induced murine stromal keratitis and angiogenesis. It delayed replication by rebalancing Th1/Th17 and Treg responses, indicating therapeutic potential [46] (Figure 1).

To utilize host resources, viruses reprogram the translation machinery. The HSV-1-encoded virion host shutoff (Vhs) protein selectively degrades host mRNAs early in infection in Human Fetal Foreskin Fibroblast (HFFF2) cells, sparing viral mRNAs to monopolize translational resources; its activity is regulated by Viral Protein 22 (VP22) [47]. The virus hijacks the eukaryotic initiation factor 4F (eIF4F) complex (eIF4E, eIF4G, eIF4A), recruiting it to viral mRNAs for efficient translation. In microglial cells, HSV-1 also uses infected cell protein 34.5 (ICP34.5 or γ34.5) and US11 to inhibit the protein kinase R-eukaryotic initiation factor 2α subunit-activating transcription factor 4 (PKR-eIF2α-ATF4)axis, blocking host translation initiation while ensuring its own [48,49]. Furthermore, viral proteins (e.g. ICP0, ICP34.5) optimize the translational environment by degrading host factors (e.g. methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14)) and inhibiting signaling molecules like retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) [50–52]. The mTORC1 pathway is central. HSV-1, via UL46 and Us3, activates the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mTORC1 pathway. This releases mTORC1 from amino acid dependence in normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDF), keeping it active under nutrient deprivation to sustain viral protein translation and replication under metabolic stress [53] (Figure 1).

Viruses also influence antiviral responses by modulating specific metabolic enzymes. HSV-1 upregulates protein arginine methyltransferase 6 (Prmt6), which suppresses the cGAS-STING pathway to evade immunity and maintain latency. The Prmt6 inhibitor EPZ020411 enhances innate immunity, reducing viral load [54]. Virus-induced metabolic reprogramming has systemic effects: HSV-1 neurotropic infection can induce gut microbiota dysbiosis and tryptophan-nicotinamide metabolism abnormalities. This causes a microglial mitophagy defect-dependent activation, promoting HSE progression. The gut microbial metabolite nicotinamide n-oxide (NAMO) – primarily from Lactobacillus reuteri and L. raoultii—can restore nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (oxidized form) (NAD+)-dependent mitophagy, inhibiting microglia-triggered neuroinflammation and infection, offering a novel therapeutic strategy [55] (Figure 1).

Lipid metabolic reprogramming: remodeling membrane architecture and mediating neuroimmunopathology

Although viruses differ in their genome types (including RNA viruses and DNA viruses), a large number of studies have shown that the replication of many viruses, including Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), Zika virus (ZIKV), Ebola virus (EBOV), and Peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV), depends on the host cell’s fatty acid synthesis pathway [56]. As a representative DNA virus, HSV-1 replication is also closely linked to lipid metabolism, involving molecules such as cholesterol, fatty acids, and ceramides. Through metabolic reprogramming, HSV-1 can modulate immune responses, affecting its pathogenicity.

Cholesterol is pivotal for HSV-1 replication. Depleting cellular cholesterol with methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) or using DHCR24–/– cell models impairs viral protein expression, genome packaging, virion release, and cell-to-cell spread [57]. Studies indicate cholesterol’s hydrocarbon tail promotes viral synthesis. While desmosterol can partially substitute for cholesterol, viral yield is significantly reduced [57]. RNA sequencing analysis in neural precursor cells showed a > 50% reduction in cholesterol synthesis genes (e.g. 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), squalene epoxidase (SQLE)). Adding exogenous cholesterol partially restored replication, confirming HSV-1’s reliance on disrupting cholesterol homeostasis [58]. Furthermore, dysregulated cholesterol metabolism is linked to neurodegenerative diseases. HSV-1 activates the transcription factor sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2) to upregulate key synthesis enzymes (e.g. HMGCR) while inhibiting the efflux protein ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCG1). This leads to intracellular cholesterol accumulation, potentially promoting β-amyloid deposition and increasing Alzheimer’s disease (AD) risk. Cholesterol depletion by MβCD can also block HSV-1 entry [59], which depends on host lipid rafts [60]. The HSV-1 protein US9 mimics amyloid precursor protein (APP) behavior, accumulating in lipid rafts and shuttling between endosomes and the plasma membrane to promote amyloidogenic cleavage, contributing to AD pathogenesis [61]. HSV-1 can also leverage Apolipoprotein E4 (ApoE4) and its interaction with heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) to enhance brain colonization, synergistically increasing neurodegenerative risk [62,63]. These findings collectively confirm the crucial role of cholesterol metabolism in viral entry (Figure 2). Fatty acid metabolism is also central to HSV-1 infection. The virus upregulates Fatty Acid Synthase (FASN) to promote de novo lipid synthesis, essential for virion structure and infectivity. Inhibiting FASN leads the virus to compensate via CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake. In SH-SY5Y cells, blocking CD36 with sulfo-N-succinimidyl oleate (SSO) further diminishes infectivity [64]. HSV-1 induces peroxisome biogenesis and upregulates related proteins (e.g. peroxins (PEXs), mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), mitochondrial fission 1 protein (FIS1), glyceronephosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT), alkylglycerone phosphate synthase (AGPS)), enhancing plasmalogen synthesis to boost virus production – a mechanism potentially common to all enveloped viruses [65]. HSV-1 also upregulates Transmembrane Protein 41B (TMEM41B) via octamer-binding protein 1 (OCT-1) in Vascular Smooth Muscle (VSMCs) cells, stabilizing FASN and promoting lipid droplet formation [66]. A similar mechanism exists in KSHV, where infection elevates fatty acid synthase (FAS) metabolites; inhibiting acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) or FASN induces apoptosis [67]. In HCMV, immediate early (IE) and early (E) gene expression promotes de novo PC synthesis [68]. HCMV infection upregulates central carbon metabolic flux, diverting the TCA cycle into fatty acid biosynthesis. Pharmacologically inhibiting this pathway suppresses HCMV replication. Notably, the same inhibition also suppresses replication of another enveloped virus, Influenza A Virus (IAV), indicating that the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway is critical for diverse enveloped viruses [69]. HCMV uses pUL38 to maintain mTOR activity and stimulate SREBP maturation, while pUL37×1 upregulates the elongase elongation of very long chain fatty acids protein 7 (ELOVL7). This promotes the synthesis of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) enriched in the viral envelope, enhancing progeny infectivity [70,71]. In contrast, Herpes Simplex Virus type 2 (HSV-2) infection downregulates genes like stearoyl‑CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), sterol regulatory element binding protein 1 (SREBP1), and ACC1 in D407 cells. The inhibitor UCM05 exhibits broad spectrum antiviral activity, inhibiting Human Immunodeficiency Virus Type 1 (HIV-1), HIV-1/HSV-2 co-infection, and HSV-1 by binding viral gB/gD and suppressing both viral protein and fatty acid synthesis [72]. Its efficacy against HSV-1 underscores the potential of targeting viral envelope glycoproteins as a cross-viral strategy. HSV-1 infection also suppresses host cell β-oxidation, possibly due to mitochondrial dysfunction or perturbations akin to the “Randall cycle” [73,74] (Figure 2).

Viruses mediate immune evasion by intervening in lipid metabolism [75]. HSV-1-infected dendritic cells (DCs) show suppressed lipolysis and promoted lipogenesis, leading to lipid droplet accumulation. This impairs antigen presentation and reduces virus-specific cluster of differentiation 8 positive T (CD8+ T) cell responses. Inhibiting acyl-coenzyme A:cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT) and fatty acid transport protein (FATP) partially restores DC function, whereas inhibition of diacylglycerol acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) has only limited effects [76]. In contrast, in human and murine astrocytes, HSV-1 infection induces rapid lipid droplet (LD) accumulation as early as 2–8 hpi via an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-dependent pathway. These LDs enhance type I and III interferon production, thereby promoting antiviral immunity and restricting viral replication [77]. Together, these findings indicate that LDs can exert either immunosuppressive effects (in DCs) or protective antiviral effects (in astrocytes), highlighting the critical impact of cell type on the outcome of immunometabolic reprogramming during HSV-1 infection. Lipidomics reveals increased lipid diversity early, followed by significant accumulation of glycerolipids like triacylglycerols (TAGs), and rises in specific lipids such as saturated diacylglycerols and ceramides [76]. Additionally, HSV-1 tegument protein pUL21 competitively binds vesicle-associated membrane protein-associated protein A (VAP-A), inhibiting ceramide transfer protein (CERT)-mediated ceramide transport to the golgi apparatus (GA). This increases the ceramide /sphingomyelin ratio, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and enhancing viral replication [78]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) maintains lipid redox homeostasis; in the context of GPX4 deficiency, HSV-1 infection leads to increased lipid peroxidation levels, which in turn promotes carboxyalkylation of cysteine 88 on STING, inhibits its intracellular trafficking, and blocks the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby facilitating viral replication [79]. Such peroxidation is detected in HSV-1-infected mouse neurons as elevated 4-Hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a marker reaching levels three times higher than normal [80] (Figure 2).

Viral replication depends on membrane biosynthesis and reorganization. HSV-1 infection stimulates phospholipid synthesis. Newly synthesized phospholipids are incorporated into the nuclear envelope and plasma membrane, maintaining membrane integrity and providing lipids for capsid budding and envelopment at nuclear and Golgi membranes [81]. The HSV-1 US3 protein regulates this process; its absence causes abnormal phospholipid metabolism, delayed [3 H]-choline incorporation, abnormal nuclear envelope expansion, and GA dilation, disrupting viral envelopment [82].

Metabolic reprogramming strategies vary across viruses and cell types. In human foreskin fibroblasts (HFF-1) cells, HSV-1 upregulates antiviral effectors (e.g. interferon-stimulated gene 20 kDa protein (ISG20), IRF7) but downregulates chemokine signaling (e.g. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), differentially expressed in FDCP 8 (DEF8)) and phosphatidylserine synthase 1 (PTDSS1), facilitating immune evasion. HSV-2 upregulates inflammatory effectors (e.g. immunoglobulin heavy variable 3–9 (IGHV3-9), serpin family A member 1 (SERPINA1)), enhances nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling, and induces glycolytic reprogramming [83]. KSHV hijacks LY6/PLAUR domain-containing protein(palmitoyl-protein thioesterase) (PINLYP) to inhibit cytosolic phospholipase A2α (cPLA2α) activity and AKT phosphorylation, remodeling phospholipid metabolism to maintain latency [84]. Regarding energy metabolism, inhibiting fatty acid oxidation (e.g. with Etox) or glycolysis (e.g. with 2-DG) early in infection blocks energy supply but 2-DG reduces CD8+ and Th1 T cells in the trigeminal ganglion (TG) and can induce encephalitis. Metformin preserves IFN-γ-producing T cell function and maintains latent infection safely, while Etomoxir has minimal impact on such cells. Initiating Etomoxir after clinical onset is largely ineffective [85–87] (Figure 2).

Viruses use specific lipid modifications to promote infection. Palmitoylation attaches palmitic acid to target proteins. HSV-1, via its UL20 protein and gK, interacts with the host Asp-His-His-Cys zinc finger (DHHC) protein golgi apparatus-specific Asp-His-His-Cys (DHHC) zinc finger protein 3; DHHC3 (GODZ), regulating UL20 trafficking and palmitoylation to affect gK surface expression, membrane fusion, and Golgi structure [88]. Bovine Herpesvirus 1 (BoHV-1) uses its US3 kinase to phosphorylate VP8, mediating its nuclear export and cis-Golgi enrichment. This inhibits intranuclear lipid droplet formation and promotes cholesterol accumulation [89]. Stress-induced corticosterone (CORT) triggers arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15)-mediated lipid peroxidation and 4-HNE modification, impairing STING trafficking and increasing HSV-1 susceptibility. Rosmarinic acid (RA) targets ALOX15 to restore innate immunity [90] (Figure 2).

Certain lipid molecules show antiviral potential. In KMB17 cells, 25-hydroxycholesterol (25HC) inhibits HSV-1 protein expression and titer [8], acting via liver X receptor (LXR) pathway activation and synergy with IFN-γ [91]. In endothelial cells, inhibiting adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase, α1/2 subunit (AMPKα1/2) enhances HSV-1 replication, while activating AMPK (with MK-8722) suppresses it by inhibiting mTORC1 and ACC1 [92]. Tamoxifen, a type II chloride channel inhibitor, synergizes with 5-nitro-2-(3-phenylpropylamino) benzoic acid (NPPB) in lipid rafts to inhibit volume-regulated anion currents and calcium-activated chloride channel (CaCC), disrupting calcium homeostasis and blocking viral entry [93].

HSV infection is associated with choline metabolism abnormalities. Choline kinase deficiency leading to choline accumulation can promote replication (e.g. in RAW264.7 cells), though host cells may also suppress the virus by downregulating choline (e.g. in KMB17 cells) [8]. In PC12 cells, HSV-1 decreases acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity [94] and affects α-7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) expression in microglia, interfering with cholinergic neurotransmission [95] (Figure 2).

Nucleic acid metabolism and gene expression reprogramming: multifaceted hijacking of the host gene expression program

During infection, HSV-1 employs sophisticated mechanisms to hijack and reprogram host gene expression to achieve efficient replication and immune evasion. These mechanisms span transcriptional initiation, epigenetic regulation, mRNA processing, and degradation.

At the transcriptional level, HSV-1 heavily relies on host machinery. Upon entry, it commandeers host RNA polymerase II (RNAP II) and DNA topoisomerase I (TOP1), relocating them to viral replication compartments (VRCs) to transcribe viral genes and suppress host transcription [96]. The immediate-early protein VP16 forms a complex with host cell factor 1 (HCF-1) and Oct-1 to stimulate IE gene transcription [97]. The IE protein ICP22 induces open chromatin regions and disrupts RNAP II termination, facilitating sustained viral expression [98]. Specifically, ICP22 inhibits RNAP II C-terminal domain (CTD) Ser-2 phosphorylation, blocking host transcriptional elongation while redirecting RNAP II to viral promoters [99,100]. Furthermore, ICP22, in Medical Research Council cell strain 5 (MRC-5) cells, forms biomolecular condensates that selectively deplete RNA polymerase II phosphorylated at serine 2 (RNAPII Ser-2P) occupancy at host gene transcription start sites (TSSs), suppressing global host transcription. This is dependent on condensate formation, as point mutations disrupting it abolish the effect [101]. Additionally, the E3 ubiquitin ligase ICP0 dissociates histone deacetylase 1/2 (HDAC1/2) from the co-repressor of RE1-Silencing transcription factor / RE1-silencing transcription factor (CoREST/REST) complex, while US3 in U2OS (Human osteosarcoma cells) modifies HDACs to block their activity, countering epigenetic silencing [102]. HSV-1 also induces endogenous retroviral elements (like germline transcription factor double homeobox 4 (DUX4)) via ICP0 and ICP4, mimicking zygotic genome activation (ZGA) to prevent viral genome silencing and promote transcription [103] (Figure 3). At the epigenetic level, HSV-1 uses multifaceted strategies. The incoming viral genome is rapidly targeted by host heterochromatinization (e.g. Histone H3 trimethylated at Lysine 27 (H3K27me3), Histone H3 trimethylated at Lysine 9 (H3K9me3)). The virus utilizes CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) insulator elements (e.g. CTCF repeat LAT region 2 (CTRL2)) to modulate chromatin accessibility and lift these repressive marks [104]. ICP0 counteracts the human silencing hub-three types of methyltransferases (SUV39H1/2 and ESET/SETDB1)-microrchidia CW-type zinc finger 2 (HUSH-SETDB1-MORC2) complex, reducing H3K9me3 enrichment on its genome [105]. The viral protein pUL12 hijacks the deubiquitinase ubiquitin specific peptidase 15 (USP15), recruiting it to VRCs. USP15 promotes viral transcription by deubiquitinating histone H2B, altering chromatin structure. pUL12 cooperates with USP15 to modulate the host DNA damage response (DDR) and nucleotide metabolism, providing precursors for genome replication [106]. HSV-1 infection also activates the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway, causing the epigenetic reader bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) to dissociate from host chromatin and relocate to the viral genome. There, by interacting with positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb), it drives viral gene expression [107] (Figure 3).

At the post-transcriptional and metabolic level, HSV-1 executes profound reprogramming. ICP22 condensates suppress host transcript export [101]. The virus strongly stimulates host tRNA expression via RNAP III activation to support its protein synthesis [108]. Regarding mRNA modification, ICP0 interacts with the m6A methyltransferase complex component METTL14. Acting as an E3 ligase, ICP0 ubiquitinates METTL14 at K156/K162, leading to its proteasomal degradation. This reduces cellular m6A levels, enhancing viral gene expression by inhibiting the METTL14/ ISG15 pathway [51]. The virus also redistributes the host m6A machinery in human primary dermal fibroblasts, reducing modifications on host mRNAs to prioritize viral mRNA translation [109].

In DNA metabolism and genome remodeling, HSV-1 manipulates host DDR pathways. Infection exploits viral DNA entry to activate the checkpoint kinase 2 (Chk2)-DDR and replication to activate the ATM-p53 pathway, promoting viral processes while suppressing antiviral components like meiotic recombination 11(Mer11). ICP0 orchestrates the DDR to optimize replication by antagonizing both the proviral effects of ataxia-telangiectasia mutated/tumor protein p53 (ATM/p53) and the antiviral actions of Mre11 [110]. In Hep-2 cells, the HSV-1-encoded uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG), in its phosphorylated form, antagonizes the editing activity of host apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic subunit 1 (APOBEC1) on the viral genome, protecting its integrity and promoting replication [111]. HSV-1 also promotes centromeric DNA amplification in quiescent cells via the translesion DNA synthesis (TLS) pathway through ICP0-mediated degradation of centromere proteins, activating nuclear cGAS. It counteracts this by expressing UL36USP to inhibit the TLS pathway [112] (Figure 3).

Furthermore, the HSV-1 in umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) counteracts the host integrated stress response (ISR) and promotes translation via ICP34.5, which mediates eIF2α dephosphorylation [113]. The Arg-Gly-Gly (RGG) motif in ICP27 may indirectly interfere with host metabolism gene expression by affecting nucleocytoplasmic transport of intronless mRNAs [114].

In summary, HSV-1 employs a multi-layered, coordinated set of strategies to hijack the host gene expression network across transcription, epigenetics, post-transcriptional modification, and DNA metabolism. Key nodes in these processes present promising avenues for novel antiviral strategies, including the potential modulation of nucleotide metabolism to enhance antigen presentation by dendritic cells or macrophages (a strategy supported by tumor immunotherapy studies [115], although its direct application to HSV-1 infection awaits further investigation), inhibiting enzymes such as dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPYD) or thymidine phosphorylase (TYMP) [116], and developing UNG [111] or JNK inhibitors [107] (Figure 3).

Reprogramming of additional metabolic pathways: targeting cofactor homeostasis and cell death decisions

HSV-1 hijacks host metabolic and cell death pathways to favor its replication, often causing cellular dysfunction. The manipulation of the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) pathway and NAD+ metabolism is a core viral strategy.

HSV-1 infection activates PARP-1/2 via viral DNA replication, consuming NAD+ and accumulating poly (ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase (PAR) chains, which can induce caspase-independent cell death. The viral protein ICP0 counteracts this by mediating PAR glycohydrolase (PARG) degradation. This enhances PARP automodification inhibition in human fibroblasts, preventing premature host cell death and buying time for replication [117]. ICP0 can also mediate sustained PARP activation, exacerbating NAD+ depletion while inhibiting PAR clearance, depleting cellular energy and disrupting DNA repair [117]. Genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) screening identified PARP1 as an HSV-1 restriction factor via DNA repair and NAD+ metabolism. Inhibiting PARP enhances HSV-1-mediated oncolysis and combination anti-tumor therapies [118]. In other models, HSV-1 can induce classical apoptosis in NCTC clone 929 cells, cleaving PARP, caspase-8, and caspase-3 [119]. Furthermore, In HeLa cells, infection induces phosphorylation of PARP1 at threonine 594, regulating its nucleocytoplasmic translocation [120]. Notably, high-concentration PARP-1 inhibitors moderately reduce viral yield, as NAD+ consumption precipitates a cellular energy crisis. During HCMV infection, PARP-1 activity promotes viral replication, a process enhanced by PARG inhibition. The HCMV UL76 protein binds PARP-1’s BRCA1 C-terminus (BRCT) domain to suppress its overactivation [121] (Figure 4). NAD+ metabolism plays a dual role. The virus activates the host salvage enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) to acquire metabolic intermediates. However, NAMPT possesses an NAD synthesis-independent phosphoribosylhydrolase activity that restricts viral glycoprotein/tegument incorporation, inhibiting replication. NAMPT-deficient mice show increased susceptibility [122]. In neurons, infection triggers axonal degeneration, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation via sterile alpha and TIR motif containing 1 (SARM1) pathway activation, associated with an elevated nicotinamide mononucleotide/nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NMN/NAD) ratio, these results have been validated in mouse models. Inhibiting SARM1 preserves axonal integrity and mitochondrial function [123].

The virus finely regulates programmed cell death. HSV-1 induces in A549 (Human non-small cell lung cancer) metabolic abnormalities via the ferroptosis pathway, mediated by the p53/ solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) axis. Infection upregulates DNA methyltransferase 3B (DNMT3b), which methylates the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) promoter, inhibiting c-JUN binding and downregulating FcRn. This activates ferroptosis via p53/SLC7A11, exacerbating inflammation and injury in lung epithelial cells, reversible by ferroptosis inhibitors or the demethylating agent 5-Aza-2 [124]. HSV-1 also promotes corneal epithelial cell apoptosis by activating the ROS/jagged 1/neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1/phosphorylated unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (JAG1/NOTCH1/pULK1) pathway, inhibiting ULK1-mediated autophagy while promoting ROS accumulation [125]. Autophagy and metabolic homeostasis are altered. Infection inhibits autophagic flux, particularly in glial cells, accompanied by a broad inflammatory response. Autophagy activators can inhibit replication and rescue neurovascular damage [126] (Figure 4).

Viral interference with vitamin metabolism is another aspect. HSV-1 upregulates retinol reductase 3 (DHRS3) by activating p53, inhibiting RA metabolism. This relies on virus-triggered DDR, HDAC1/2 degradation, and histone H3/H4 hyperacetylation, promoting replication [127]. However, Exogenous RA exerts an antiviral effect by increasing tritiated ([3 H]) mannose incorporation, interfering with N-glycosylation of viral glycoproteins [128]. HSV-1 infection causes rapid disappearance of the folate transporter solute carrier family 19 member 1 (SLC19A1) from the plasma membrane, potentially impacting one-carbon metabolism [129]. Additionally, the HSV-1 US2 protein cluster shares structural features with dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), suggesting HSV-1 may express mimics to interfere with host folate metabolism [130].

Finally, proteomic changes in HSV-1-infected neurons show significant overlap with those in AD brain tissue. These convergent changes are in key neuronal pathways, suggesting infection may contribute to neurodegeneration by interfering with homeostatic pathways like β-amyloid metabolism and tau phosphorylation [131]. Following HSV-1 binding to astrocytes, ATP is released via the phospholipase C-inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (PLC-IP3)-Ca2 + pathway. This ATP activates neuronal P2 receptors(P2R), elevating Ca2 + and activating glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), essential for viral entry/replication. Inhibiting astrocyte metabolism or blocking P2R/GSK-3β reduces neuronal infection [132] (Figure 4).

Immunometabolic reprogramming by HSV-1: a mechanism for host immune evasion

HSV-1 evades innate and adaptive immune responses and establishes persistent infection through multi-layered immunometabolic reprogramming. Its precise modulation of immune cell function and metabolic state is a core immune escape strategy.

Innate immune reprogramming

Macrophage polarization into pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes is crucial for antiviral immunity [133]. HSV-1 replicates less efficiently in antiviral M1 macrophages than in M2 macrophages [134], and M1-deficient mice show higher susceptibility [135]. A key M1 metabolic signature is enhanced glycolysis, which overlaps and competes with HSV-1’s own glycolytic reprogramming [136]. HIF-1α is central: its overexpression drives M1 polarization by upregulating glycolytic genes (LDHA, PKM2), increasing glycolysis and PPP metabolites, an effect potentiated by HSV-1 infection. Conversely, HIF-1α knockdown weakens antiviral response by downregulating STING and enhances infectivity [137–139]. The glycolytic metabolite lactate upregulates serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 10 (SRSF10), increasing circular RNA derived from METTL3 (Circular RNA Derived from METTL3 Gene (circMETTL3)). The encoded peptide METTL3-156aa binds LDHA, enhancing macrophage glycolysis and promoting M1 polarization [137]. Inhibiting glycolysis with 2-DG reduces M1 polarization and inflammatory damage, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting glucose metabolism [140].

After HSV-1 infection of macrophages, virus replication drives glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, which negatively regulates the production of CXCL1. Treatment with 2-DG or loss of the catalytic activity of cGAS relieves the inhibitory effect of glycolysis on CXCL1, leading to its excessive secretion. This, in turn, recruits a large number of neutrophils (Ly6G+) into the central nervous system, further exacerbating tissue damage and viral spread, ultimately resulting in severe herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) [141].

Following HSV-1 infection, basal metabolic genes (ATP Synthase F1 Subunit gamma 1 (Atpc1), NADH:Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Subunit A2 (Ndufa2)) are upregulated in the infiltrating monocyte subset Mono1, suggesting enhanced oxidative phosphorylation. Meanwhile, the infection-induced “transitional” microglia/microglia-like cells show increased expression of the immunometabolic enzyme Acod1, which catalyzes the production of itaconate to modulate antiviral immunity [142].

HSV-1 regulates innate immunity via epitranscriptomic modifications. Infection of HIOECs downregulates fat mass and obesity associated protein (FTO) and ALKBH5, increasing global m6A levels. This enhances IFN-I production and promotes viral clearance [143].

In nervous system infections, HSV-1 modulates neuroimmune homeostasis by altering tryptophan metabolism[144]. It shifts tryptophan-nicotinamide conversion toward tryptophan-phenylalanine, increasing phenylalanine. The gut microbial metabolite NAMO crosses the blood-brain barrier and, in a PINK1-parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (PRKN)-independent manner, restores NAD+ -dependent mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria, suppressing microglial activation and cytokine release. SCFAs synergize with NAMMO to regulate microglial maturation and function, maintaining immune balance and blocking early neuronal infection [145] (Figure 5). HSV-1 infection leads to mitochondrial dynamics dysregulation and host immune surveillance dysfunction [3]. Adaptive immune reprogramming

The function of DCs, as key antigen-presenting cells, is severely compromised. HSV-1 disrupts DC maturation, antigen presentation, migration, and induces apoptosis. Inhibiting ACAT and fatty acid transport proteins (FATPs) impacts infected DC viability and promotes their migration to draining lymph nodes. ACAT inhibition further enhances virus-specific CD8+ T cell activation [76] (Figure 5).

In B cells, EBV activates cardiolipin synthase via EBNA2 to promote cardiolipin synthesis, reshape mitochondrial cristae and the electron transport chain, and support oxidative phosphorylation and one-carbon metabolism. Inhibition of cardiolipin synthesis leads to destabilization of serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 (SHMT2), aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member L2 (ALDH1L2), and glutamic‑oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2), resulting in NADPH depletion, oxidative stress, and aspartate deficiency, thereby suppressing B cell proliferation. The synthetic lethality observed upon combined inhibition of cardiolipin synthesis and one-carbon metabolism suggests that targeting cardiolipin metabolism represents a potential therapeutic strategy for EBV-associated B cell lymphoma [148]. In KSHV-infected B cell lymphoma lines (Body Cavity‑Based Lymphoma 1 (BCBL-1), Body Cavity‑Based Lymphoma 3 (BC-3), JSC‑1 cell line (JSC-1)), viral vCyclin promotes cyclin‑dependent kinase 6 (CDK6) mediated phosphorylation of carbamoyl‑phosphate synthetase 2, aspartate transcarbamylase, and dihydroorotase (CAD) at Ser-1900, thereby enhancing pyrimidine synthesis and activating CAD-mediated deamidation of v‑rel avian reticuloendotheliosis viral oncogene homolog A (RelA). RelA deamidation suppresses NF-κB-driven inflammation and upregulates enzymes such as hexokinase 4 (HK4), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 2 and 3 (PDK2/3), and PC, driving aerobic glycolysis to provide energy and precursors for viral replication and tumorigenesis, while also facilitating immune evasion [149]

Under hypoxic conditions, KSHV-infected B cells stabilize HIF1α to upregulate viral G protein-coupled receptor (vGPCR), driving aerobic glycolysis (increased glucose uptake and lactate production), coordinately regulating genes involved in glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, while suppressing DNMT3A/3B expression. Silencing HIF1α reverses this phenotype, revealing the mechanism by which KSHV hijacks B cell metabolism via the HIF1α-vGPCR axis in the hypoxic microenvironment to support viral latency, replication, and tumorigenesis [33].

As the core effector cells of adaptive immunity, T cells are also susceptible to metabolic hijacking by viruses. HHV-6A infection of human T cells (HSB-2) activates AKT-mTORC1, upregulates GLUT1/3 expression and membrane translocation, and induces glycolytic enzymes (phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1), lactate dehydrogenase α (LDHα), HK2,), leading to increased glucose uptake, consumption, and lactate production, thus driving aerobic glycolysis to supply energy and precursors for viral replication and progeny release. Inhibition of 2-DG or mTORC1 (rapamycin, Torin1) effectively suppresses viral proliferation, revealing a mechanism by which the virus hijacks host T cell metabolism to support its replication and immune evasion [32].

Regarding adaptive immunity, HSV-1 disrupts amino acid metabolism in the nervous system, affecting T cell function. Infection downregulates central carbon metabolism pathways (e.g. glycolysis) in the TG microenvironment, potentially limiting energy for immune cells (T cells, NK cells) and affecting immune checkpoints like programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) [150,151]. The microbiota-metabolite-immune axis plays a key role. Microbial SCFAs regulate CD8+ T cells by activating G protein-coupled receptor 41/43 (GPR41/43). Following epicutaneous HSV-1 infection, GPR41/43 deficiency impairs MPEC differentiation, reduces effector molecules (TNF-α, granzyme B, IFN-γ, perforin), and compromises viral control via the MAPK/ERK pathway. Restoring GPR41/43 expression in HSV-specific CD8+ T cells rescues their antiviral capacity, indicating that both intrinsic and extrinsic GPR41/43 signals optimize CD8+ T cell immunity against HSV-1 [152]. Sodium propionate (SP) supplementation exploits metabolic disparities, preferentially fueling Treg energy metabolism to promote Treg expansion while suppressing Th1/Th17 differentiation. This increases the Treg/Th17 ratio, reshaping the immune landscape and ameliorating pathology [153] (Figure 5).

From metabolic vulnerabilities to therapeutic intervention: targeting HSV-1 dependencies for both antiviral and oncolytic strategies

HSV-1 and its engineered oncolytic derivatives oncolytic Herpes Simplex Virus (oHSV) profoundly reshape the metabolic network of host cells to support viral replication and modulate anti-tumor immune responses, concurrently revealing novel therapeutic targets.

In oncotherapy, oHSV demonstrates a potent ability to exert oncolysis through metabolic reprogramming. In identification of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wildtype glioma, oHSV remodels host cell central carbon metabolism, shifting glucose utilization from glycolysis toward oxidative phosphorylation while redirecting glutamine metabolism toward reductive carboxylation. This reprogramming leads to increased lipid synthesis and elevated intracellular ROS. Through the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway, it induces Acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 4 (ACSL4) expression, ultimately triggering lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [154]. In glioblastoma (GBM), oncolytic HSV expressing short hairpin RNA against PKR (oHSV-shPKR), while enhancing oncolysis, activates the IDO pathway in DCs (IDO-positive, CD11c-positive DCs (IDO+ CD11c+ DCs)) via IFN-I signaling, promoting tryptophan depletion. Combination with an IDO inhibitor (indoximod) blocks this immunosuppressive feedback, reduces tryptophan consumption, enhances DC activation, and ultimately synergistically augments antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses and anti-tumor efficacy [155]. In a model of non-small cell lung cancer brain metastasis, engineered stem cells loaded with oHSV and single-chain variable fragment against PD-1 (scFvPD-1) achieve enhanced therapeutic effects by inducing oHSV-mediated immunogenic cell death, activating anti-tumor T cells, and disrupting oxidative phosphorylation, while also sensitizing tumors to the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin [156]. The oncolytic viral strain OncoDelta (OncoD), engineered by deleting the viral ribonucleotide reductase (RR) gene, forces the virus to rely on host cell RR for replication. This modification enables selective replication in actively dividing tumor cells with active nucleotide metabolism while restricting replication in normal cells. Furthermore, transcriptomics reveals that this virus also disrupts host DNA damage repair pathways, indirectly affecting cellular metabolic state [157] (Figure 6). The remodeling of host metabolism by HSV-1 and its oncolytic virus variant (oHSV) constitutes a multidimensional process. At the fundamental metabolic level, the virus hijacks the host’s central carbon metabolism (e.g. driving glucose toward oxidative phosphorylation), nucleotide metabolism (e.g. relying on host RR), and amino acid metabolism to acquire the materials and energy necessary for replication (①). At the regulatory mechanism level, the virus precisely modulates the host environment through strategies such as ICP0-mediated degradation of METTL14 to reduce m6 a modification and evade interferon responses, as well as manipulating lipid metabolism (e.g. affecting OSBP) to interfere with pathways like cGAS-STING (②). At the therapeutic application level, oHSV designed based on these mechanisms can selectively replicate within tumors and induce oncolysis through processes like ferroptosis. Concurrently, combination strategies that target these metabolic vulnerabilities (e.g. using IDO inhibitors or arginase) can enhance therapeutic efficacy or overcome immune suppression (③). These three levels systematically elucidate the metabolic logic underlying the transition from viral infection to therapeutic tool, providing a rationale for developing novel therapies that target host metabolism.

Viral replication itself is critically dependent on and reprograms host nucleotide and amino acid metabolism [7]. Cancer cells exhibit high demand for amino acids like arginine [158], and HSV-1 infection alters arginine metabolic pathways. Pegylated recombinant human arginase I (peg-ArgI), by targeting this host metabolic vulnerability, can effectively control viral replication [159,160]. Transcriptomic analysis indicates that following oHSV-1 infection of tumor cells, the proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process is significantly affected. Proteasome-related genes such as proteasome 26S subunit, non-ATPase 2/4 (PSMD2, PSMD4), proteasome 20S subunit α2 (PSMA2), and GADD45g play important roles in viral proliferation, suggesting the virus reprograms metabolism by modulating proteostasis [161].

Epitranscriptomic modifications, particularly m6A methylation, constitute a key mechanism by which HSV-1 regulates host metabolism. HSV-1 infection, through its immediate-early protein ICP0, interacts with METTL14, a core component of the m6A methyltransferase complex, within nuclear domain 10 (ND10) bodies. Acting as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, ICP0 targets and ubiquitinates METTL14 at lysine residues K156 and K162, leading to its proteasomal degradation and a global reduction in host cell m6A levels. This m6A reprogramming significantly enhances the anti-tumor activity of oncolytic HSV-1 in glioma therapy by inhibiting the METTL14/ISG15 pathway [51]. The global decrease in m6A modification directly affects RNA stability (e.g. ISG15 mRNA), thereby interfering with multiple host metabolic pathways, including the interferon response (Figure 6).

Viruses optimize their replication environment by manipulating lipid metabolism and key signaling pathways. The interferon gamma inducible protein 16 (IFI16), in cooperation with the carbohydrate response element binding protein (ChREBP), downregulates the transcriptional activation of GLUT4. This reduces the transcription of HCMV-induced lipogenic enzymes, ultimately inhibiting the de novo formation of enveloped viral particles [162], offering a novel avenue for antiviral therapy. HSV-1 infection stimulates the generation of cytosolic DNA, activating the cGAS-STING pathway. Oxiconazole, by targeting oxysterol binding protein (OSBP) – a protein involved in lipid metabolism and membrane trafficking – interferes with STING trafficking and suppresses IFN-I production, indicating that HSV-1 may influence innate immunity by altering host lipid metabolism [163,164].

Therapeutic strategies based on metabolic reprogramming hold broad promise. The genetically modified oncolytic virus talimogene iaherparepvec (T-VEC), with deletions in the ICP34.5 and ICP47 genes and insertion of granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), selectively replicates in and lyses tumor cells. It exploits metabolic features of tumor cells such as dysregulated rat sarcoma virus oncogene (Ras) signaling and impaired interferon pathways for selective replication. Given the close link between the Ras pathway and metabolic reprogramming (e.g. the Warburg effect), this suggests viral hijacking of these pathways [165,166]. Acetaldehyde has been identified as an inhibitor of the cGAS-STING pathway, impairing cGAS-DNA binding and phase separation [167]. In HSV-1 infection models, acetaldehyde suppresses antiviral cytokine production and promotes viral replication, suggesting that increasing acetaldehyde production by enhancing alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) activity in specific cells might be a novel intervention strategy [168]. Inositol hexakisphosphate kinase 2 (IP6K2), an inositol phosphate metabolic kinase, influences apoptosis by regulating the p53/p21 pathway [169]. HSV-1 infection engages the IP6K2-p53-p21 signaling axis to promote efficient viral replication and subsequent apoptosis. Tumor cells lacking IP6K2 exhibit resistance to HSV-1 oncolysis, indicating viral exploitation of this host metabolic network, and positioning IP6K2 as a potential therapeutic target [170]. Furthermore, In vitro infection with an ICP34.5-deletion mutant (HSV-ΔICP34.5) in J-Lat 10.6 and 293T cells promotes HSF1 phosphorylation and NF-κB pathway activation by reducing the recruitment of protein phosphatase 1α (PP1α), effectively reactivating latent HIV reservoirs; in vivo, this strategy also reactivates latent SIV in chronically infected rhesus macaques [171]. This demonstrates the potential for cross-regulating metabolism and signaling to treat diverse viral infections (Figure 6).

Discussion

Regarding the metabolic reprogramming induced by HSV-1, we have constructed an integrated view of the metabolism-immune axis. Detailed elucidation of glucose, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism has revealed cross-regulatory mechanisms. The overarching aim is to explore the therapeutic applications of metabolism-triggered immunomodulation for treating HSV-1 infection and cancer. However, numerous questions remain.

The spatiotemporal dynamics and tissue specificity of metabolic reprogramming are unclear. Current research focuses on specific time points and cell lines, yet viral infection in vivo is dynamic. Metabolic demands likely differ across cell types (e.g. neurons, corneal epithelial cells, DCs) and infection stages (lytic, latent, reactivation) [172–174]. This specificity is crucial for therapy, as illustrated by the divergent mechanisms of oHSV in tumor vs normal cells. Techniques like single-cell metabolomics and spatial metabolomics hold potential for mapping the dynamic landscape in vivo [175,176].

As an upstream driver, metabolic regulation plays a pivotal, yet incompletely understood, role in controlling HSV-1 latency and reactivation [177]. Cellular metabolic states modulate key metabolites (acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), α-KG, NAD+), directly influencing epigenetic enzymes (histone acetyltransferases (HATs), histone methyltransferases (HMTs), HDACs demethylases). This reshapes the viral genome’s chromatin landscape, determining viral fate [178,179]. Current research lacks quantitative evidence directly linking specific metabolites to dynamic epigenetic changes at precise viral loci. Integrating assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq), chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), and metabolomics data could help reconstruct regulatory networks [180].

The role of the “Microbiota-Metabolite-Immune” axis is another frontier. Gut microbiota-derived metabolites like SCFAs remotely regulate CNS immunometabolic homeostasis, influencing viral pathology [181,182]. However, SCFAs can also promote reactivation of viruses like EBV and KSHV, suggesting a potentially similar mechanism for HSV-1 [183]. Understanding this balance is critical. Significant differences between mouse models and humans, alongside limitations of artificial reactivation models, highlight the need for novel systems [184].

HSV-1’s diverse regulatory mechanisms offer numerous therapeutic avenues. Strategies targeting the metabolism-immune interface show promise but face challenges due to pathway interconnectivity.

One strategy involves repurposing existing compounds for multi-target therapies. Examples include combining acyclovir (ACV) with 2-DG or the FASN inhibitor TVB-2640. Although no clinical trials for such HSV-1 combinations are reported [185], prior research supports this synergistic approach [186]. Within the metabolism-immune network, mechanisms like energy competition and metabolite-mediated immunosuppression have been identified. Discovered nodes (e.g. dehydrogenase/reductase member 3 (DHRS3) [127], argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (AS1) [187], IDO1 [188]) and PC, constitute novel “metabolic checkpoints” for intervention.

Combination therapies are likely superior. Combining oHSV with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g. anti-PD-1) or metabolic modulators (e.g. IDO inhibitors) shows synergistic efficacy in models [189], promising to overcome immune evasion.

Systemic inhibition of glycolysis may impair antiviral immune cells, causing side effects [190]. Future research should focus on developing cell-type-specific targeted delivery systems.

Finally, research into HSV-1’s metabolic-immune mechanisms has broad implications. HSV-1 infection is associated with an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s, potentially via dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and β-amyloid deposition [191]. Conversely, studies on oHSV inform how to exploit viral reprogramming of tumor metabolism (e.g. inducing ferroptosis) to treat cancer [192]. A deeper understanding not only paves new avenues for antiviral therapy but may also provide novel targets for treating metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer.

Conclusion

HSV-1 reprograms multiple host metabolic pathways – carbohydrate, lipid, amino acid, nucleotide, and energy metabolism – to promote viral replication and immune evasion. This metabolic remodeling is central to HSV-1 pathogenesis and persistence, highlighting intricate virus – host metabolic interactions and offering a foundation for novel therapies targeting the metabolism – immune interface. Future studies should investigate the spatiotemporal and cell-type specificity of this reprogramming, explore metabolism – epigenetic crosstalk in latency and reactivation, and examine the role of the microbiota – metabolite – immune axis to enable precise therapeutic strategies.
