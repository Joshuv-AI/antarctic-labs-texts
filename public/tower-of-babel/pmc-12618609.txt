Quantum dots enabling all-in-one controllable polymerization through triplet energy transfer
Authors: Yongshun Lyu, Heran Nie, Jun Du, Kaifeng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) have recently emerged as a class of initiators or activators for photopolymerization, typically relying on photoinduced charge transfer from QDs to co-additives. Here we report efficient photopolymerization of acrylates using QDs alone without any co-additives, triggered by energy transfer from photoexcited QDs to the triplet excited-states of acrylates on sub-nanosecond timescales. The diradical-like triplets are converted to free radicals through interaction with QDs’ surface dangling bonds, thereby initiating chain propagation. The radical-surface interaction enables controlled polymerization with narrow polydispersity and block copolymerization capability. This study demonstrates how the interior excitons and surface atoms of QDs can synergistically drive controllable photopolymerization, distinguishing QDs from conventional molecular initiators. Practical advantages of this all-in-one approach include its streamlined reaction system, well-preserved emission of QDs, and enhanced stability of QDs in nanocomposites that are well suited for display and optical waveguiding applications.

Introduction

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) are small-size semiconductor nanocrystals made in solution, whose readily tunable optical and electronic properties have enabled a promising platform to explore diverse applications ranging from biomarkers to optoelectronic devices, and to quantum sciences1,2. In recent years, the potential of colloidal QDs in driving photochemical reactions is also being gradually unlocked. QDs feature the advantages of facilely tailored excited-state energies and redox potentials, and their large surface areas afford rich binding sites and/or scaffolds for selective chemical transformations. Within the large volume of works on chemical reactions catalyzed by photoexcited QDs3–10, only a limited fraction has been devoted to photopolymerization11–20. Nevertheless, this direction is particularly promising for rapid adoption in real-life technologies, considering the large-scale demand of functional polymers, the well-established application of high-quality QD-polymer composites in displays21,22 and luminescent solar concentrators23, as well the potential in emerging technologies such as 3D printing17. For these applications, a delicate spatiotemporal control over the reaction kinetics is required for the formation of polymers with precise molecular weight, narrow dispersity and designated structures24.

Early reports on photopolymerization using QDs and related nanoparticles are mostly free-radical polymerization resulting in uncontrolled formation of polymer chains16. Only until recent years have the strategies of reversible-deactivation radical polymerization (RDRP), such as atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) and reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT), been introduced to QD-based photopolymerization reactions11–15. These systems usually rely on electron transfer from photoexcited QDs to atom or chain transfer reagents, which cleaves the latter into radicals responsible for chain propagation and accompanying anions (Fig. 1a). The photogenerated holes in QDs can be scavenged by the anions, regenerating the ground state QDs and creating the radicals that can reversibly trap the propagating polymer chains to form the dormant species. In practice, excessive amounts of sacrificial amines11–14 or thiol ligands15 were also added to scavenge the holes due to the requirement of a balance between charge separation and recombination for efficient polymerization. Inclusion of transfer reagents and sacrificial donors, however, may result in formation of highly reactive side products that impair the functionality and long-term stability of the polymers. In an effort to seek an alternate mechanism that can completely eliminate atom/chain transfer reagents and sacrificial donors, thereby greatly streamlining QD-based photopolymerization reactions, we considered triplet energy transfer (TET), which is an intrinsically charge-neutral process. TET from photoexcited QDs to organic molecules is a novel mechanism26 that has found applications in photochemical upconversion27–31 and organic photocatalysis32–34. A unique advantage of QDs compared to traditional molecular triplet sensitizers is the negligible intersystem crossing energy loss in QDs35, allowing them to sensitize very energetic triplets under relatively low photon energy36. Therefore, it is possible to use QDs to directly sensitize the triplet excited states of common monomers such as acrylates. Conventionally, triplet states are not active species for polymerization because they are at best considered as “diradicals” but not free radicals. However, there have been several reports on direct photopolymerization of acrylates under short-wavelength (typically ≤250 nm) ultraviolet (UV) irradiation37–40, for which the reaction of triplet diradicals with nearby atoms or molecules (e.g., inter and intramolecular hydrogen-transfer or bimolecular addition) has been proposed as the initiation mechanism40. We, therefore, envision that the abundant dangling bonds found on the surfaces of colloidal nanoparticles41–43 could readily interact with the triplet diradicals to produce free radicals for chain propagation.

Here, we report that the synergistic utilization of excitons of QDs for triplet sensitization and surface dangling bonds of QDs for radical generation can indeed enable efficient photopolymerization (scheme in Fig. 1b). The surface dangling bonds also participate in reversible-deactivation of the polymer chains, giving rise to acrylate polymer products with narrow polydispersity and block copolymers. The emission efficiency of the starting ZnSe/ZnS core/shell QDs is well preserved in the resulting QD-polymer composites, and the photo and chemical stability is strongly enhanced. Therefore, this study not only establishes a novel mechanism of photopolymerization initiated by TET and controlled by surface atoms of QDs, but also offers a practical route to fabricating high-optical-quality QD-polymer composites.

Results

All-in-one photopolymerization using QDs

For reactions driven by TET from QDs, the exciton energy of QDs is a key parameter. In order to tune the exciton energy, we synthesized a series of QDs (ZnSe/ZnS core/shell, CdS and CdSe) of varying sizes; see Methods for details. By controlling the size of these QDs, the first excitonic absorption peaks can be tuned in the range of ca. 370 nm to 620 nm (Fig. 2a). In general, the light harvesting ranges of the ZnSe/ZnS core/shell and CdS QDs are similar, which are situated in the UV to blue region, whereas the CdSe QDs covers the cyan to red region. Supplementary Fig. 1 displays a representative transmission electron microscope (TEM) image of the ZnSe/ZnS QDs with an absorption peak at 405 nm, which shows the high monodispersity of the particles with an average diameter of 5.2 nm. The core diameter is ca. 3.7 nm, and the shell thickness is ca. 0.7 nm. We first carried out the polymerization of methyl methacrylate (MMA; 2.35 M concentration) using 405-nm-peak ZnSe/ZnS QDs (1.23 μM concentration) in toluene, without any extra additives; see Methods. The mixture was illuminated by a UV light source (365 nm, 11 mW cm−2) at room temperature. After 12 h, the monomer conversion yield reaches 73%, which is calculated from the weight of the product polymer after removing the QDs (see Methods), and is consistent with the value determined using 1H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR). The number-average molecular weight (Mn) is 25.7 kDa, and the polydispersity (PDI) is 1.12; both were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). The quantum yield of the polymerization reaction is estimated to be ~0.77% (see Supplementary Note 1). Control experiments confirmed that polymerization did not occur in the absence of QDs (only MMA) or light irradiation (with QDs and MMA). In addition, the temperature of the reactor showed no obvious change (~30 °C) over 12 hours under 365 nm illumination for both QD-MMA and MMA-only reaction systems (Supplementary Fig. 2a). These results confirm that polymerization is triggered by the excited states of QDs and not by thermal effects. Besides MMA, the ZnSe/ZnS QDs can polymerize various functional monomers, including benzyl methacrylate (BZMA), 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate (TFEMA), tert-butyl methacrylate (t-BUMA), and methacrylate (MA), all of which display relatively narrow polydispersity (summarized in Fig. 2b). These reactions do not necessitate the use of UV light source. We found that blue light (~405 nm) illumination also worked, as long as it was able to excite the QDs. In fact, due to the large absorption cross section of ZnSe/ZnS QDs (ca. 8.77 × 10-15 cm-2 at 405 nm; see Supplementary Note 1), we were able to conduct polymerization under natural sunlight (Fig. 2c). The conversion yield of MMA reaches 20% after 3-hour illumination by outdoor sunlight.

The monomer conversion yield depends on the concentration of ZnSe/ZnS QDs (Fig. 2d), as the latter dictates the number of harvested photons in a unit time. When the QD concentration increases from 0.05 to 3.69 μM, the conversion yield of MMA monomers is enhanced from 37% to 96%. Importantly, when 2,2-Dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA; a commonly used photoinitiator) with the concentration of 1.23 μM is used for free-radical polymerization of MMA, the monomer conversion yield is only 12%, which is ~six-fold lower than that of ZnSe/ZnS QDs with identical concentration and reaction conditions. Besides the QD concentration, the exciton energy of QDs also substantially affects the monomer conversion. A general trend is that higher exciton energy of QDs tends to result in higher MMA monomer conversion yields, which holds across the ZnSe/ZnS, CdS and CdSe QDs of similar concentrations (Fig. 2e). This is consistent with the expectation for reactions driven by energy transfer.

Evidence for TET from QDs

In order to establish the mechanism of TET from photoexcited QDs to MMA monomers, we conducted a series of steady-state and transient spectroscopic measurements; see Methods. The photoluminescence (PL) of QDs was substantially quenched by MMA, with a higher MMA:QD ratio resulting in stronger quenching (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The quenching dynamics was further directly observed by femtosecond transient absorption (TA). As shown in Fig. 3a and b, the exciton bleach feature of ZnSe/ZnS QDs at ca. 405 nm shows faster recovery in the presence of MMA, implying charge and/or energy transfer from QDs to MMA. The higher the MMA concentration is, the faster the recovery is (Supplementary Fig. 3b). A schematic energy level alignment between MMA and ZnSe/ZnS QDs is provided in Supplementary Fig. 4, on the basis of which TET and electron transfer (ET) from photoexcited QDs to MMA are both likely to occur. Note that although the triplet energy of MMA was reported to be as high as 3.7 eV44, it was also found that structural relaxation (such as torsional motion about the C = C bond) can lower the triplet energy to ca. 2.6 eV39, which then becomes suitable for sensitization using ZnSe/ZnS QDs with exciton energy of ca. 3 eV. Scrutiny of the TA data in Fig. 3a, as well as spectra in other visible to near-infrared spectral ranges, however, yields no new spectral features associated with MMA anion radicals (MMA-) or triplets (3MMA*). The latter was reported to absorb at <400 nm40, which could be covered by the overlapping intense signals of QDs. In order to differentiate between the TET and ET pathways, we introduced to the polymerization reactions various types of amines as hole scavengers. PL quenching measurements proved that these amines can effectively capture the photogenerated holes of ZnSe/ZnS QDs (Supplementary Fig. 5). As demonstrated in prior studies, if the reaction is triggered by ET from QDs, addition of amines can enhance the polymerization efficiency11,13,16. By contrast, here amines strongly reduce the monomer conversion yields (Fig. 3c), thereby evidencing that the polymerization reaction is initiated by TET rather than ET from photoexcited ZnSe/ZnS QDs to MMA.

The mechanism of hole transfer from photoexcited ZnSe/ZnS QDs to MMA can be ruled out not only by the energy level alignment shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, but also based on several aspects of analysis. To begin with, as a II-VI semiconductor material, the exciton bleach on the TA spectra of ZnSe QDs should be dominated by the state filling effect of the electrons at the conduction band edge, with negligible contribution from valence band holes, which is similar to early studies on CdSe and CdS QDs45–47 and is also directly proved by our previous studies on electron transfer from ZnSe-bases QDs48. Therefore, hole transfer should not lead to such an obvious decay of exciton bleach as observed in Fig. 3b. Additionally, the comparison among ZnSe, CdS and CdSe QDs in Fig. 2e is consistent with an energy transfer instead of hole transfer mechanism. Specifically, if the reaction is mainly driven by hole transfer, we should not observe a large difference between ZnSe and CdSe QDs which have a large conduction band offset and a very small valence band offset49.

We further performed photo-illuminated electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements to investigate the TET mechanism; see Methods for details. For a sample comprising ZnSe/ZnS QDs and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP), after 5 minutes of 365 nm illumination, we can clearly detect the EPR pattern of TEMPO radicals as the oxidized product of TEMP (Fig. 3d). This indicates TET from QDs to ground-state O2, generating singlet oxygen (1O2*) that can selectively oxidize TEMP to TEMPO50. The formation of 1O2* is further confirmed by using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as a chemical probe (Supplementary Fig. 6). This TET process is energetically favored due to the low energy of 1O2* (ca. 0.98 eV). In contrast, if we further add MMA (with an MMA:QD ratio of 1.9 × 106:1), the TEMPO signals are strongly reduced. This is consistent with rapid TET from QDs to MMA, thereby intercepting the TET pathway between QDs and O2.

An interesting question remains as to why the QD-sensitized 3MMA* cannot donate energy to O2 to generate O2*, which is thermodynamically allowed. We suspect that it is kinetically disrupted by other reaction pathways of 3MMA*. As mentioned in the introduction, triplets can be viewed as diradicals and they are usually not as reactive as free radicals due to the strong spin correlation between the two radicals. However, near the surface of QDs, a triplet diradical could interact with an under-coordinated atom to quench one of the radicals. This can be understood in terms of pairing of one of the diradical spins with a dangling bond spin. We speculate that the transformation of 3MMA* to MMA radicals is responsible for the reduction of the EPR signals of TEMPO in Fig. 3d. The resulting carbon radical signals are indeed captured in EPR measurements (Fig. 3e). The absence of these signals in the control sample (without QDs) and in the presence of TEMP (Fig. 3d) is consistent with the generation of carbon radicals by photoexcited QDs.

The radical initiation mechanism is schematically depicted in Fig. 3f. The QD-sensitized 3MMA* diradicals are mainly localized near the vinyl group, but the carbonyl group also has appreciable spin density40. Therefore, there exists a resonance structure in which an unpaired electron is localized at the oxygen atom on the carbonyl group. This unpaired electron can interact with a Zn atom (also having an unpaired electron) on the QD surface to form a Zn-O bond, resulting in a free radical located on the terminal carbon that can initiate chain propagation. The mechanism of transformation from triplets to free radicals proposed here can borrow evidence from previous studies in other fields. For example, in organic photochemistry, a classical stepwise mechanism for [2 + 2] cycloaddition involves interaction of a triplet with an ene species to form a 1,4-biradical51,52. A more related example is found in organometallic photochemical synthesis, where triplet sensitization of a copper (I)-organic species resulted in formation of copper (II) and a free radical that participated in radical C-H amination reactions53.

We conducted X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements to capture evidence for the bonding between the surface Zn atoms of QDs and the carbonyl oxygen depicted in Fig. 3f. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the XPS of the Zn−2p binding energy shows a redshift of ca. 0.8 eV in the ZnSe/ZnS QD-PMMA composites prepared by photopolymerization as compared to the free ZnSe/ZnS QDs, which is consistent with Zn-O bond formation in the composites increasing the charge density around Zn atoms. The Zn K-edge extended XAFS (EXAFS) spectra in the composites and free QDs are also different (Supplementary Fig. 8). Spectral fitting gives a higher Zn-O coordination number in the composites (ca. 1.9) than in free QDs (ca. 1.5), indicating the formation of new Zn-O bonds after polymerization.

Mechanism for controllable photopolymerization

With the mechanism for the generation of carbon radicals proposed, we further examined the chain propagation process and the source of controllability. As shown in Fig. 4a, the polymerization reaction using ZnSe/ZnS QDs exhibits a pseudo-first-order kinetics for the consumption of the MMA monomers, with an apparent propagation rate constant of 2.37 × 10-5 s-1. This observation indicates that the concentration of propagating radicals is relatively constant over the whole polymerization period, which is one of the signatures to evaluate the “livingness/controllability” of polymerization and has been reported in previous QD-based RDRP systems11–15. We further implemented the light-controlled “ON/OFF” experiments (Fig. 4b). The monomer conversion shows an increase with the light on, whereas negligible polymerization occurs when the light is off. These “ON/OFF” cycles can be repeated for many times, further substantiating the controllability of this polymerization process. Thanks to the controllability, we were able to conduct chain-extension to produce block copolymers. In these experiments, the isolated PMMA product was used to reinitiate polymerization of butyl methacrylate (BMA), benzyl methacrylate (BZMA), and butyl acrylate (BA), under the assistance of ZnSe/ZnS QDs and UV light. The GPC results and NMR measurements (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 9) indicate a substantial increase in Mn. Importantly, the controllability of the present system depends sensitively on the surface-terminating atoms of our QDs. When Zn-terminated ZnSe QDs (with first absorption peak also at 405 nm, Supplementary Fig. 11) were used for MMA polymerization, the reaction kinetics did not follow the pseudo-first-order behavior (Fig. 4a). Instead, the rapid initial consumption of monomers, followed by saturation at later times, is more consistent with free radical polymerization. The distinction between ZnSe/ZnS QDs and Zn-terminated ZnSe QDs suggests that the surface S atoms are most likely responsible for the controllability of photopolymerization. Therefore, there likely exists a reversible interaction between the carbon radicals on the propagation chains with the S dangling bonds on the surfaces of QDs. As a related example, thiocarbonates are one of the most commonly used RAFT agents for polymerization12–15,56. In fact, fitting the EXAFS data in Supplementary Fig. 8 yields a reduced Zn-S coordination number in the QD-PMMA composites (ca. 2.9) than free QDs (ca. 4.0), which is consistent with this C-S bonding interaction weakening the original Zn-S bonds on the surface of QDs.

In addition to the surface atom termination, we also examined the influence of surface ligands on polymerization controllability. Specifically, we employed ZnSe/ZnS QDs with trioctylphosphine (TOP) (see method for details) as the capping ligands for the polymerization reaction. The successful light-controlled “ON/OFF” experiments and pseudo-first-order kinetics indicate that the polymerization controllability is unaffected by the ligands (Supplementary Fig. 12).

While the constantly narrow polydispersity during the reaction time is consistent with the controllability of our polymerization system, the constant Mn of PMMA observed herein (Fig. 4d) is distinct from conventional RDRP systems for which Mn is expected to increase linearly with reaction time. One way to simultaneously rationalize the controllability and the constant Mn is to consider that the chain growth is very rapid, but the ultimate chain length is controlled by a “template” that can accommodate a narrow distribution of chain lengths. As aforementioned, the dangling bonds of surface S atoms on QDs can terminate the active PMMA chains to form the dormant species. Taking into account that the QDs are colloidally stable and well dispersed during the polymerization, the ultimate chain length should be limited by the QD-to-QD distance. A product polymer chain starts from the Zn atom of one QD and ends at the S atom of a neighboring QD. Therefore, the polymer length should be inversely proportional to the concentration of QD. This is proved by the increase of the Mn of PMMA with decreasing concentration of ZnSe/ZnS QDs (Supplementary Fig. 13). Consistently, dynamic light scattering shows that the particle size after polymerization is increased and correlates inversely with the concentration of QD as well (Fig. 4e). This measurement also implies that the PMMA chains are bound to the surfaces of QDs. As a result, the final QD-PMMA composites can be dispersed in dimethylformamide (DMF), standing in contrast to original ZnSe/ZnS QDs that are dispersible in non-polar hexane but not DMF (Fig. 4f).

The mechanism of controllable polymerization of acrylates using ZnSe/ZnS QDs is schematically summarized in Fig. 4g. The reaction kinetics can be described as “rapid initiation and rapid propagation”, which sets itself apart from both free radical polymerizations characterized by “slow initiation and rapid propagation” and conventional living polymerization by “rapid initiation and slow propagation”. After an MMA radical is initiated on the QD surface through sequential TET and dangling bond interaction processes, the chain propagates rapidly, but it will terminate once it reaches the S atom on a neighboring QD’s surface. The time-dependent consumption of monomers observed on the minute to hour timescales corresponds to the growth of more polymer chains in the dispersion. A quantitative analysis of the reaction kinetics is provided in Supplementary Note 2. We note that there is a dynamic equilibrium for the making and breaking of the C-S bonds, and this equilibrium can be shifted once the concentrations of QDs and monomers are altered or once a different type of monomers with a distinct binding affinity towards the S atoms are added to the mixture. This explains the chain extension experiments that led to block copolymer formation in Fig. 4c. The synthesized polymers and copolymers can be isolated by removing the QDs from the mixture (Methods).

Optical quality of QD-PMMA composites

On the basis of the fundamental understanding of the initiation and polymerization mechanisms using QDs, we further examine the optical quality of the QD-PMMA composites in the context of real-life applications. As shown in Fig. 5a, a 100 cm2 square-shaped ZnSe/ZnS QD-PMMA composite has uniform absorbances across multiple spots on the sample, and their extinction spectra indeed display negligible scattering tail. Moreover, because the radicals involved in our polymerization method are generated and utilized in chain growth in controllable fashions, we did not observe emission quenching of the ZnSe/ZnS QDs by the radicals. In fact, the PL intensity of QDs is slightly enhanced (by ca. 20%) after polymerization in comparison to free QDs in toluene (Fig. 5b). The preservation of QDs’ optical properties during polymerization is also observed in the case of Zn-terminated (ZnSe/Zn) QDs (Supplementary Fig. 14). By contrast, introduction of a commonly used photoinitiator, DMPA, caused substantial PL quenching; the PL of QDs is reduced by ca. 26% upon addition of 0.1% DMPA (molar ratio of DMPA:MMA). The optical and chemical stabilities of the ZnSe/ZnS QD-PMMA composites are also strongly enhanced compared to standalone QDs. Under AM 1.5 G (one sun) illumination, the PL intensity of the composite does not display any degradation after 25 hours, whereas a film of drop-casted QDs loses almost half of its PL intensity within 5 hours (Fig. 5c). Even more remarkably, the PL of the QD-PMMA composite does not degrade at all after being immersed in an acidic aqueous solution (pH = 1) for 25 hours (Fig. 5d). In contrast, free ZnSe/ZnS QDs were etched away by the acidic solution within 1 hour.

The superior optical property and stability of our QD-PMMA composites make them well-suited for photonic applications such as optical waveguides or luminescent solar concentrators. For this demonstration, we used Mn2+-doped Cd1-xZnxS QDs (Mn:QDs)57, which have sufficiently high exciton energy (ca. 3.02 eV; Supplementary Fig. 15) to drive photopolymerization of MMA, and meanwhile, the exciton energy of the host QDs can be transferred to Mn2+ dopants, promoting the dopants from their 6A1 ground state to their 4T1 excited state58. The emission of Mn2+ dopants arises from the 4T1 → 6A1 forbidden transition at an energy of ∼2.1 eV (~586 nm), which experiences negligible reabsorption loss from either the QD exciton absorption at ~3.02 eV and its own forbidden absorptive transition58,59. A simple mixture of Mn:QDs with commercial PMMA leads to severe particle aggregation and optical scattering, causing a hazy appearance of the composite (Fig. 5e, bottom). In contrast, in the photopolymerized Mn:QD-PMMA composite, QDs are homogeneously dispersed by the atomic scale interaction between PMMA chains and QDs’ surfaces (Fig. 5e, top). To evaluate the optical waveguiding performances of these two luminescent composites, we systemically vary the distance (d) between the excitation spot and one end of the luminescent rod, and collect the PL spectra at the end; see scheme in Fig. 5f inset. For the physically mixed Mn:QD + PMMA rod, the PL intensity decreases substantially with increasing d, indicating strong scattering loss caused by QD aggregation. In contrast, the photopolymerized Mn:QD-PMMA rod exhibits nearly identical PL intensity over d up to 20 cm, reflecting minimized scattering loss due to uniform QD dispersion in the polymer enabled by the QD-polymer interfacial bonding60.

Discussion

This work establishes a mechanism for polymer synthesis mediated by nanoscale interactions at both excitonic and atomic levels. We demonstrated that triplet energy transfer from photogenerated excitons of colloidal QDs to acrylate monomers, followed by interaction of the acrylate triplets with surface atoms of QDs, can trigger controllable photopolymerization. Nontoxic ZnSe/ZnS QDs alone are particularly efficient for initiating diverse acrylate monomers under both LED light and sunlight, with the polymerization performance being comparable to previous QD- or non-QD-based photopolymerization systems relying on external RDRP reagents or sacrificial donors (Supplementary Table 1). Reversible bonding of PMMA chains with under-coordinated sulfur atoms on these QDs enables precise control over molecular weight and high-fidelity formation of block copolymers. Importantly, this atomic-level interaction not only preserves the excellent luminescent properties of the original QDs after polymerization but also imparts superior optical and chemical stability, giving rise to high-quality QD-PMMA composites with strong promise for optical display and waveguiding applications. These results provide valuable insights into the design and synthesis of advanced polymeric materials with tailored properties. Full utilization of interfacial charge/energy flow and atomic interactions between colloidal QDs and organic monomers will greatly enrich the toolbox of photopolymerization and expand the functional reach of composite nanomaterials.

Methods

Chemicals

Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(Ac)2 ∙ 2H2O; 99%), zinc stearate (Zn(St)2; 99%), oleic acid (OA; 90% technical grade), selenium powder (Se; 99.999%, 100 mesh), sulfur powder (S; 99.98% trace metals basis), cadmium oxide (CdO; 99.99% trace metal basis), 1-octadecene (ODE; 90% technical grade), 1-octanethiol (OT; ≥98.5%), trioctylphosphine (TOP; 97%), 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP; 99%), 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO; 98% GC) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Diisopropylamine (DIPA; 99.5%), trimethylamine (TEA; 99.5%), butylamine (BRN; 99%) were purchased from Macklin. Hexane, hydrochloric acid (HCl), toluene, chloroform, acetone, methanol, ethanol, and ethyl acetate were purchased from Tianjin Damao Reagents. All the chemicals were used without further purification. Methyl methacrylate (MMA; 99.5%), benzyl methacrylate (BZMA; 98%), 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate (TFEMA; 98%), lauryl methacrylate (LMA; 97%), tert-butyl methacrylate (t-BUMA; 98%), butylmethacrylate (BMA; 99%) and butyl acrylate (BA; 99%) purchased from Macklin were purified with aluminum oxide basic before being used for polymerization.

Preparation of ZnSe cores

In a typical synthesis, 2 mmol Zn(St)2 (1.26 g) and 30 mL ODE were loaded in a three neck flask and degassed at 110 °C in vacuum for 1 hr. The flask was then filled with nitrogen and heated to 300 oC. During the heating, 1 mL of 1 M DPP-Se stock solution (prepared by dissolving 1 mmol Se in 1 mL DPP) was swiftly injected into the reaction when the temperature reached 200 oC. The reaction was maintained at 300 oC for 2 h to form the ZnSe cores with the first excitonic absorption peak at 395 nm. To obtain larger ZnSe cores, we sequentially injected 1 mL of 0.5 M Zn-oleate (prepared by dissolving 10 mmol Zn(Ac)2 ∙ 2H2O in 15 mL OA and 5 mL ODE at 120 oC) and 1 mL of 0.5 M ODE-Se suspension (prepared by dispersing 5 mmol Se powder in 10 mL ODE) into the reaction at 300 oC for 1 h. The first excitonic absorption peak of the obtained ZnSe core QDs was at 410 nm. Injecting further larger amounts of Zn-oleate and ODE-Se and reacting for longer time gave ZnSe core QDs with absorption peak at 425 nm.

ZnS shelling for ZnSe cores

We conducted the same ZnS shelling procedure for all ZnSe core QDs. The ZnS shell was formed by sequentially injecting 3mL of 0.5M Zn-oleate and 1mL of 1.5M TOP-S (prepared by dissolving 15mmol S powder in 10mL TOP) into the above ZnSe core QDs at 280 oC for 10min. The shelling process was repeated 3 times and finalized by injecting 3 mL of 0.5 M Zn-oleate and 0.5 mL OT. The reaction was maintained at 280 oC for 10 min before being cooled to room temperature. The ZnSe/ZnS QDs were purified with acetone and hexane and redissolved in toluene for future use. Zn-terminated ZnSe QDs (ZnSe/Zn) were made by shelling using Zn-oleate alone without any TOP-S or OT.

Synthesis of CdSe QDs

CdSe QDs with first excitonic absorption peaks at 500, 520 and 560 nm were synthesized by modifying the hot injection method reported in the literature61. 0.5 M Cd-oleate stock solution was prepared first. Typically, 10 mmol CdO, 10 ml OA, and 10 ml ODE were loaded in a three-neck flask and degassed at 100 oC for 10 min. The mixture was saturated with N2 and heated to 280 oC. The reaction was maintained at 280 oC for 20 min before being cooled down to room temperature. The obtained 0.5 M Cd-oleate solution was kept at 100 oC for further use. To synthesize CdSe cores, 1 ml of 0.5 M Cd-oleate and 10 ml ODE were loaded in a three-neck flask and degassed at 100 oC in vacuum for 10 min. The reaction was filled with N2 and heated to 260 oC. 2.5 ml of 0.1 M Se-ODE suspension was injected into the mixture and the reaction was allowed to proceed at 250 oC for 8 min. We then added the Se-ODE suspension dropwise into the reaction and monitored the absorption of the CdSe QDs by taking the aliquots. The reaction was stopped and cooled to room temperature, when the first exciton absorption peaks of CdSe QDs reached the described values. We purified the QDs with ethanol and toluene and redissolved the QDs in toluene for further use.

CdSe QDs with first excitonic absorption peaks at 590 and 620 nm were synthesized according to ref. 62. In a typical reaction, 6 ml ODE and 0.2 ml of 0.5 M Cd oleate were loaded in a three-neck flask and degassed at 120 oC in vacuum for 10 min. We then filled the reaction with N2 and heated it to 285 oC. We swiftly injected 0.1 ml of 2 M TOP-Se and then added dropwise 1 ml TOP over 20 sec into the reaction and maintained the reaction at 285 oC for 3 min. To obtain CdSe QDs with first exciton absorption peaks at 590 nm and 620 nm, we injected 0.6 ml and 1 ml of mixture solution (prepared by mixing 0.7 ml ODE, 1 ml of 0.5 M Cd oleate and 0.25 ml of 2 M TOP-Se) into the reaction, respectively. The obtained CdSe cores were purified with ethanol and toluene and dispersed in toluene for further use.

Synthesis of CdS QDs

The CdS QDs were synthesized through a literature method63 with slight modifications. Typically, 0.077 g CdO, 15 mL ODE, and a desired amount of OA were loaded in a three-neck flask and degassed at 80 oC for 10 min in vacuum, after which the mixture was filled with N2 and heated to 270 oC. 3 mL of 0.1 M ODE-S (prepared by dissolving 1.5 mmol S powder in 15 mL ODE) was swiftly injected into the reaction at 270 oC, followed by injection of 6 mL ODE. The reaction was cooled rapidly by removing the heating mantle and placing the flask in a water bath. The CdS QDs with first exciton absorption peaks at 370 nm, 390 nm, 400 nm, and 420 nm were obtained by using 4 mL, 2.4 mL, 2 mL, and 1.3 mL OA in the synthesis, respectively. The QDs were purified with acetone and hexane and dissolved in toluene for further use.

General procedures of QD-induced polymerization

We sealed a desired amount of QD powder (prepared by drying the QD-toluene solution in a vacuum) in 0.375 mL MMA and 1.125 mL toluene in a reaction vial in a glovebox. The polymerization was performed with irradiation by 365 nm, 50 W LED (the light intensity at the sample position was measured to be 11 mW cm-2) for 0-10 hr before being terminated by exposure to air. QDs in the resulting PMMA were removed with acid solution (200 μL 37% HCl in 1 ml methanol). The polymer was extracted with water and chloroform to remove excess acids and ions, followed by purification using chloroform and methanol for four times and drying in vacuum at 60 oC for 24 h. The isolated polymer was then weighed to determine the conversion yields. As to the synthesis of block copolymers, the polymerization was performed for 4 hr under 365 nm radiation, after which the mixture was vacuumed to remove residual monomer to obtain the QD-PMMS macroinitiators. Subsequently, a mixture of 2 ml of monomers for the second block and 1 mL toluene was injected into the macroinitiators in glovebox for 8 hr reaction under 365 nm radiation.

General characterizations

Steady-state absorption spectra were acquired using an Agilent Cary 60 spectrometer. PL spectra were acquired using an Agilent Eclipse fluorescence spectrometer. TEM images were taken with a JEOL 2100 F field emission electron microscope operating at 200 kV accelerating voltage. GPC measurements were performed on a Malvern Panalytical’s Viscotek with HPLC-grade solvents as eluents: tetrahydrofuran at room temperature. The sample concentration was 5 mg mL-1 and the injection volume was 150 μL. NMR quantification was performed using a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz for 1H using CDCl3 as a solvent.

EPR spectra were recorded at room temperature on a Bruker A200. For singlet oxygen signal capturing, 2 mL of 80 μM ZnSe/ZnS QDs in toluene was mixed with 3.5 μL of TEMP. The mixture was bubbled with oxygen for 5 min, loaded into an EPR tube and irradiated by 365 nm light for 5 min before EPR measurements. The EPR parameters applied were: modulation amplitude 2.0 G; microwave power 5.1 mW; at ~9.33 GHz. For carbon radical signal capturing, 0.1 ml of 80 μM ZnSe/ZnS QDs and 0.01 M DMPO in 1:3 toluene/MMA was sealed into an EPR tube in a glovebox, and was irradiated by 365 nm light for 5 min before EPR measurements; for the control, MMA was removed with the rest conditions remaining identical. The EPR parameters applied were: modulation amplitude 1.0 G; microwave power 5.2 mW; at ~9.33 GHz.

XPS spectra were recorded on an ESCALAB 250 XI XPS instrument (Thermo Scientific). Ultra-high vacuum system was provided through monochromate Al Kα (150 W, 20 eV pass energy) radiation. The spectra were recorded at 191 energy steps with the energy step size of 0.100 eV for the survey scan. The sample surface was cleaned to remove the carbon and oxygen of the carbohydrate that were deposited during the XPS sample preparation process. The peaks were fitted and analyzed with Thermo Scientific Avantage 5.0 software. Zn K-edge XAFS spectra were collected on beamline BL14W1 specializes in XAFS in Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF).

Transient absorption

The fs-TA measurements were conducted based on a regeneratively amplified Ti:sapphire laser system (Coherent; 800 nm, ~100 fs, 7 mJ per pulse, 1 kHz) as the laser source. The pump pulse at 360 nm was generated from an optical parametric amplifier pumped by the Ti:sapphire amplifier, and was modulated at 500 Hz by a mechanical chopper. The white-light probe pulse was generated by focusing a weak 800-nm fundamental beam onto a CaF2 crystal. The pump-probe time delay (0-8 ns) was controlled by a motorized optical delay line. The samples were placed in sealed 1-mm quartz cuvettes for measurements at room temperature.
