The relationship between resilience and burnout in elite athletes: the mediating role of coping strategies and the moderating role of psychosocial resources
Authors: Zhengyang Mei, Chenyi Cai, Tingfeng Wang, Shulai Luo, Yang Yang, Chifong Lam, Zirui Wang, Shi Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGuided by the unifying model of the stress process, this study examines the impact of resilience on athlete burnout and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsA sample of 756 elite athletes was surveyed using the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale, Athlete Burnout Questionnaire, Modified COPE, and Psychosocial Resources Scale. Data were processed and analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 27.0.Results(1) After controlling for gender and age, resilience was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout; (2) Both problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping strategies partially mediated the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout; (3) Psychosocial resources moderated the relationship between coping strategies and athlete burnout in the context of resilience.ConclusionsResilience is a crucial proximal factor in athlete burnout, with coping strategies partially mediating their relationship. Psychosocial resources play a protective role in mitigating burnout risk. However, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to fully infer causal relationships between variables; future research is encouraged to adopt experimental or longitudinal designs to further examine the rationality and scientificity of the proposed hypotheses.

Introduction

Athlete burnout refers to the immediate and temporary physical and psychological stress response that athletes experience when there is a significant imbalance between their individual resources and situational demands [1]. When effective recovery is difficult over an extended period, this stress response accumulates and eventually develops into physical and psychological symptoms, leading to dysfunction. The key characteristics of burnout include emotional and physical exhaustion, a reduced sense of achievement, and devaluation [1].

Over the past two decades, the average level of athlete burnout has steadily increased [2], and involves a range of psychological states and behavioral patterns that develops during training and competition [3–5]. Specifically, athletes with higher burnout levels tend to experience more prominent psychological stress [6], sports injuries [7], anxiety [8], and depression [9], while exhibiting lower satisfaction levels with their sports performance [10], coping resources [11], well-being [12], and hope [13]. Thus, athlete burnout may not only have a detrimental impact on athletes’ physical and mental health and career development, but also pose broader challenges for the athletic community.

Athlete burnout may be linked to the stressors that athletes encounter or experience [14]. The unifying model of the stress process suggests that, when confronted with stressors, individuals assess whether the stressor represents a potential threat and whether they have the resources to cope with it [15]. If the level of exposure to stressors exceeds the available coping resources, it leads to overwhelming stress or difficulty in managing the situation, triggering negative emotional, physiological, or behavioral responses.

Athletes undergo significant changes in their physical and mental states due to the demands of long-term training and competition and face biological, psychological, and social stressors, including training load, injuries, social expectations, fear of failure, coach-athlete relationships, cyber-bullying, and family conflict [16–18]. Some athletes are more sensitive to these stressors and often resort to maladaptive coping strategies, making their psychological states and behavior patterns more vulnerable to the negative impact of stressors, which contributes to burnout. Other athletes can actively mobilize internal and external resources, adopting adaptive coping strategies to manage stressors, with burnout symptoms remaining minimal. In this context, resilience—the ability to cope with and overcome adversity and stress—becomes crucial in preventing athlete burnout.

Higher-resilience athletes may have greater coping resources to protect their physical and mental well-being from the detrimental effects of stressors [19, 20]. However, existing research on athlete burnout has predominantly focused on the direct impact of resilience. The potential mediators or moderators in this process remain underexplored, limiting our understanding of the mechanism underlying athlete burnout. Based on this evidence gap, this study addresses the following research questions: Does the positive impact of resilience on athlete burnout occur through the mediating process of coping strategies? How do different coping strategies relate to resilience and athlete burnout? Do psychosocial resources play a facilitating or buffering role, thereby influencing the development of athlete burnout in this process?

Resilience and athlete burnout

Resilience refers to an athlete’s ability to evaluate their own thoughts, feelings, and behaviors during sports-related adversity, and it has the effects of stimulating potential, invigorating emotions and improving health [21, 22]. During stressful situations, resilience serves as the ability to return to an adaptive and healthy behavioral pattern following adversity [23]. The meta-theory of resilience suggests that resilience, as a psychological factor of positive adaptation, proactive coping, and optimism, directly influences whether an athlete will experience maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses when coping with stressors [24]. That is, high resilience can help athletes effectively cope with and overcome adversity and stress, protecting their physical and mental well-being from the negative impact of stressors and reducing burnout risk. A cross-sectional study involving young athletes confirmed that those with higher resilience levels exhibit lower burnout levels and higher psychological well-being levels when exposed to stress [25]. Another cross-sectional study involving adolescent athletes indicated that resilience impacts the relationship between stressors and burnout, reducing burnout resulting from high stress exposure [26]. Moreover, most studies have established a positive association between resilience and protective factors related to athlete burnout, including basic psychological needs [27], self-determined motivation [28], optimism [29], and social support [14]. Based on the previous arguments, hypothesis 1 is proposed.

Mediating role of coping strategies

Coping strategies refer to the constantly changing cognitive and behavioral efforts employed by athletes to manage internal and external demands that are appraised as exceeding their physical and psychological resources [30]. The meta-model of stress, emotions, and performance suggests that coping strategies serve as an important mediator in the stress process. The influence of personality traits on athletes’ psychological states and behavioral patterns is often realized through coping strategies, leading to adaptive or maladaptive coping outcomes [31]. Coping strategies include problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping, and the relationship patterns between them and resilience vary [32, 33]. High-resilience athletes tend to adopt problem-focused coping strategies (e.g., proactive coping, positive adjustment, planning, and seeking instrumental assistance), thereby alleviating or eliminating the negative outcomes triggered by stress [34, 35]. Low-resilience athletes may use avoidance-focused strategies (e.g., giving up, venting unpleasant emotions, mental distraction, or disengagement), which directly weaken their ability to cope with situational demands and increase the potential risk of maladaptive outcomes like burnout [34].

The relationship between coping strategies and athlete burnout has been empirically examined. Specifically, a cross-sectional study involving elite athletes indicated that problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping is associated with low and high burnout levels, respectively [36]. Similarly, a longitudinal study involving college athletes demonstrated that coping strategies can predict changes in burnout levels, with problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping negatively and positively correlating with burnout, respectively [37]. In summary, resilience can influence which coping strategies athletes adopt, and different coping strategies, in turn, further influence their burnout levels. Based on the previous arguments, hypothesis 2 is proposed.

H2: Coping strategies mediate the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout.

Moderating role of psychosocial resources

Psychosocial resources refer to the individual and social factors that promote physical and mental health [38]. The resilience meta-model suggests that when experiencing stressors, specific psychological and social resources can “filter” or “buffer” the negative impact of stressors on athletes’ physical and mental well-being [21]. Psychological resources primarily include an individual’s positive internal characteristics, belief systems, and emotional states; social resources mainly involve social networks and social support [39]. Significant differences exist in the specific observational indicators used to measure psychosocial resources across various fields. For example, Pinquart and Fröhlich [40] characterized psychosocial resources using optimism, sense of control, self-esteem, purpose in life, and social support. Ponce-Garcia, et al. [41] characterized psychosocial resources using social support, social skills, planning behavior, and goal efficacy. Manove, et al. [42] characterized psychosocial resources using social support, religious importance, religious attendance, and purpose in life. Based on a comprehensive systematic review of the previous literature, this study selects the four indicators most strongly related to positive psychology to represent psychosocial resources, including optimism, sense of control, self-esteem, and extraversion [43].

In the context of competitive sports, the environment is primarily characterized by constant pressure and resource demands [20]. Resource shortages may intensify the negative impact of stressors on athletes’ physical and mental well-being. That is, psychosocial resources may be a moderator in this process. On the one hand, psychosocial resources may enhance the direct relationship between resilience and adverse outcomes. The integrated model of self-regulation based on processes of motivated effort allocation suggests that to mitigate the depletion caused by stressors and their adverse outcomes, individuals tend to effectively allocate their resources to enhance coping effectiveness [44]. Psychosocial resources can strengthen resilience’s positive impact on adverse outcomes, such as perceived stress [45, 46], pain perception [47], and internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [48]. On the other hand, psychosocial resources may moderate the mediating role of the relationship between coping strategies and adverse outcomes. The resources and perception model suggests that individuals with abundant psychosocial resources are more adept at perceiving and assessing stressors rationally and appropriately, enabling them to adopt adaptive coping strategies and reduce the risk of maladaptive coping outcomes [49]. Psychosocial resources can enhance the positive impact of problem-focused coping on adverse outcomes such as psychological stress [50], anxiety and depression [51, 52], bullying and victimization [53], while buffering the negative impact of avoidance-focused coping on these outcomes. In summary, psychosocial resources may not only strengthen the direct effect of resilience on adverse outcomes but also moderate the mediating effect of different coping strategies on these outcomes. Based on this, hypotheses 3 and 4 are proposed. Given that significant differences exist in athlete burnout levels across gender [54] and age [6], these variables are controlled for in the analysis.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A two-stage cluster sampling method was employed. In the first stage, five representative regions—Beijing, Jiangsu, Sichuan, Chongqing, and Jiangxi—were selected based on the diversity of their geographical distribution and the development level of competitive sports. In the second stage, professional sports teams from each region were used as sampling clusters, and elite athletes within each cluster who met the research criteria were invited to participate. Elite athletes are individuals who compete at a national or international performance level in their respective sports [55]. To minimize potential interference caused by training- or competition-related stress, all questionnaires were completed before the athletes’ training sessions, and outliers were excluded during data collection. To ensure the linguistic and conceptual accuracy of the original English instruments, a standardized translation and back-translation procedure was employed. Two bilingual experts independently translated the scales into Chinese, and a professional translator subsequently back-translated them into English to assess semantic equivalence and cultural appropriateness.

A total of 829 questionnaires were distributed, and after excluding invalid responses, 756 valid questionnaires were collected, resulting in an effective response rate of 91.2%. The sample included 399 male athletes (52.8%) and 357 female athletes (47.2%). The average age of the athletes was 20.06 years (range: 16–25 years), with a standard deviation of 2.19, and their average training period was 7.63 years. Additionally, 112 athletes (14.8%) were classified as national master sportsmen, 414 (54.8%) as national first-level athletes, and 230 (30.4%) as national second-level athletes. The sports represented in the sample included athletics, basketball, football, volleyball, badminton, table tennis, tennis, and swimming. Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in this work. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the School of Physical Education of Southwest University (ethical approval number: SWU20180601).

Instrument development

Connor–Davidson resilience scale

The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), developed by Connor and Davidson [56], was selected to assess resilience across five dimensions: personal competence, stress reaction, positive adaptation, perceived control, and spiritual influence. The CD-RISC consists of 25 items and utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, where 0 represents “not true at all,” and 4 represents “true nearly all of the time.” The total score represents the overall level of resilience, with higher scores indicating greater resilience in the athlete. The CD-RISC has demonstrated reliability and validity in numerous previous studies involving Chinese populations (e.g., Wu, et al. [57]). Permission to use the CD-RISC was obtained from the copyright owner. During the validity and reliability testing, item 20 (“Have to act on a hunch”) was removed due to its factor loading being below 0.4. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the CD-RISC was 0.97. The results of confirmatory factor analysis were as follows: χ2/df = 4.769; RMSEA = 0.071; SRMR = 0.023; CFI = 0.948; NFI = 0.935; IFI = 0.948; and GFI = 0.897, indicating that the scale has good structural validity [58].

Athlete burnout questionnaire

The Athlete Burnout Questionnaire (ABQ), developed by Raedeke and Smith [59], was selected to assess athlete burnout across three dimensions: emotional and physical exhaustion, reduced sense of achievement, and devaluation. The ABQ consists of 15 items, with items 1 and 14 reverse scored, and utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “almost never,” and 5 represents “almost always.” The total score represents the overall level of burnout, with higher scores indicating greater burnout in the athlete. The ABQ has demonstrated reliability and validity in numerous previous studies involving Chinese populations (e.g., Liu, et al. [60]). During validity and reliability testing, items 1 (“I am accomplishing many worthwhile things in sport”) and 14 (“I feel successful at sport”) were removed due to their factor loadings being below 0.4. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the ABQ was 0.93, while the Cronbach’s α for the subscales were 0.87 (emotional and physical exhaustion), 0.84 (reduced sense of achievement), and 0.75 (devaluation). The results of confirmatory factor analysis were as follows: χ2/df = 4.559; RMSEA = 0.069; SRMR = 0.040; CFI = 0.979; NFI = 0.973; IFI = 0.979; and GFI = 0.967, indicating that the scale has good structural validity [58].

Modified COPE

The Modified COPE, developed by Carver, et al. [61] and revised by Crocker and Graham [32], was selected to assess coping strategies across two dimensions: problem-focused coping and avoidance-focused coping. The items for each dimension comprise corresponding subscales. Problem-focused coping includes planning, active coping, and suppression of competing activities, while avoidance-focused coping includes denial and behavioral disengagement [33, 36]. All items utilize a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “used not at all/very little” and 5 represents “used very much.” The total score represents the overall level of problem-focused or avoidance-focused coping strategies, with higher scores indicating greater use of each respective strategy in the athlete. The Modified COPE has demonstrated reliability and validity in numerous previous studies involving Chinese populations (e.g., Huang, et al. [62]). In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the Modified COPE was 0.88, while the Cronbach’s α for the subscales were 0.89 (problem-focused coping) and 0.88 (avoidance-focused coping). The results of confirmatory factor analysis were as follows: χ2/df = 3.856; RMSEA = 0.062; SRMR = 0.048; CFI = 0.947; NFI = 0.930; IFI = 0.947; and GFI = 0.925, indicating that the scale has good structural validity [58].

Psychosocial resources scale

Psychosocial resources comprise optimism, sense of control, self-esteem, and extraversion. Optimism was assessed using the Revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R), developed by Scheier and Carver [63] and revised by Scheier, et al. [64]. The LOT-R consists of 10 items, with items 3, 7, and 9 reverse scored, and utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “strongly disagree” and 5 represents “strongly agree.” A higher score indicates a greater level of optimism in the athlete. Sense of control was assessed using the Sense of Control Scale (SCS) developed by Lachman and Weaver [65], which measures two dimensions: mastery and perceived constraints. The SCS consists of 12 items and utilizes a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “strongly disagree” and 7 represents “strongly agree.” A higher score indicates a greater sense of control in the athlete. Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSS), developed by Rosenberg [66]. The RSS consists of 10 items, with items 2, 5, 6, 8, and 9 reverse scored, and utilizes a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “strongly disagree” and 7 represents “strongly agree.” A higher score indicates a greater level of self-esteem in the athlete. Extraversion was assessed using the extraversion subscale of the Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2), developed by John, et al. [67] and revised by Soto and John [68]. The subscale consists of 12 items, with items 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 11 reverse scored, and utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “strongly disagree” and 5 represents “strongly agree.” A higher score indicates a greater level of extraversion in the athlete.

The psychosocial resources scale has demonstrated reliability and validity in numerous previous studies involving Chinese populations (e.g., Li, et al. [69]). In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the overall scale was 0.89 while it was 0.72 (optimism), 0.82 (sense of control), 0.82 (self-esteem), and 0.85 (extraversion) for the subscales. To compute psychosocial resource scores, principal component analysis was conducted for factor extraction using the criterion of eigenvalues greater than 1 [70]. One principal component with an eigenvalue of 2.213 was extracted, accounting for 55.315% of the cumulative variance. Based on Z-scores, factor loadings, and the eigenvalue of the four indicators, the formula for calculating psychosocial resources is as follows: (0.768×Zoptimism−0.545×Zsense of control+0.864×Zself−esteem+0.761×Zextraversion)/2.213, where 0.768,−0.545, 0.864, and 0.761 represent the factor loadings for optimism, sense of control, self-esteem, and extraversion, respectively.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 27.0 were used to process and analyze the research data. The statistical methods included reliability and validity analysis, descriptive analysis, correlation analysis, analysis of variance, regression analysis, and bias-corrected percentile bootstrap analysis. The macro program PROCESS in SPSS 27.0 was employed to examine the mediating role of different coping strategies in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout, as well as the moderating role of psychosocial resources in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout and between different coping strategies and athlete burnout. Compared to full structural equation modeling, the macro program PROCESS, as a regression-based analytical tool, provides the advantages of operational simplicity and flexible model specification for mediation and moderation analysis. The built-in bootstrapping of this tool enhances the reliability and robustness of effect estimation, making it well-suited for research contexts centered on theory-driven hypothesis testing. The research data were analyzed based on standardized scores, and bootstrapping was used to test the significance of all effects. Bootstrapping was conducted using 10,000 resamples, with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the significance of path coefficients. An effect was considered significant if the CI did not include zero.

Results

Common method bias test

In this study, common method bias was controlled by ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and standardized measurement procedures during data collection. Exploratory factor analysis using Harman’s single-factor test was conducted, and the results indicated that 17 principal components had eigenvalues greater than 1. The first principal component accounted for only 22.971% of the total variance, which was below the critical threshold of 40%, suggesting that common method bias was not a significant concern in the research data.

Mediation and moderation analysis

First, using PROCESS macro-Model 4 [71], the mediating role of different coping strategies (problem-focused coping and avoidance-focused coping) in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout was examined, controlling for gender and age. The results indicated that resilience was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout in the absence of mediators (β = −0.50, t = −15.41, p < 0.001). After including the mediators, resilience continued to significantly negatively associate with athlete burnout (problem-focused coping: β = −0.33, t = −8.96, p < 0.001; avoidance-focused coping: β = −0.31, t = −11.62, p < 0.001). Additionally, resilience was significantly positively associated with problem-focused coping (β = 0.54, t = 17.45, p < 0.001) and significantly negatively associated with avoidance-focused coping (β = −0.33, t = −9.32, p < 0.001). Furthermore, problem-focused coping was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout (β = −0.32, t = −8.71, p < 0.001), whereas avoidance-focused coping was significantly positively associated with athlete burnout (β = 0.57, t = 21.41, p < 0.001). The bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping method indicated that problem-focused coping and avoidance-focused coping partially mediated the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout, with their indirect effects accounting for 34.00% and 38.00% of the total effects, respectively (see Tables 2 and 3).

Second, using PROCESS macro-Model 15 [71], the moderating role of psychosocial resources was examined in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout and between different coping strategies and athlete burnout while controlling for gender and age. The bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping method indicated that different coping strategies and psychosocial resources played a moderated mediation role in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout (see Figs. 1 and 2). Specifically, without including the moderator, resilience was significantly positively associated with problem-focused coping (β = 0.54, t = 17.45, p < 0.001) and significantly negatively associated with avoidance-focused coping (β = −0.33, t = −9.32, p < 0.001). After including the moderator, resilience was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout (problem-focused coping: β = −0.22, t = −6.01, p < 0.001; avoidance-focused coping: β = −0.26, t = −9.40, p < 0.001). Furthermore, problem-focused coping was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout (β = −0.26, t = −7.42, p < 0.001), while avoidance-focused coping was significantly positively associated with athlete burnout (β = 0.50, t = 18.56, p < 0.001). Psychosocial resources were significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout (problem-focused coping: β = −0.32, t = −9.98, p < 0.001; avoidance-focused coping: β = −0.19, t = −6.73, p < 0.001). Additionally, the interaction between resilience and psychosocial resources were not significantly associate with athlete burnout (problem-focused coping: β = 0.02, t = 0.58, p > 0.05; avoidance-focused coping: β = 0.04, t = 1.78, p > 0.05). However, the interaction between different coping strategies and psychosocial resources were significantly associated with athlete burnout (problem-focused coping: β = 0.07, t = 2.47, p < 0.05; avoidance-focused coping: β = −0.07, t = −2.91, p < 0.01). A simple slope test was conducted to further examine the moderating role of psychosocial resources in the relationship between different coping strategies and athlete burnout (see Fig. 3). Participants were categorized into high-level and low-level psychosocial resource groups by adding and subtracting one standard deviation from the mean level of psychosocial resources. The results indicated that as the level of psychosocial resources increased, the negative impact of problem-focused coping on athlete burnout gradually weakened, though it remained significant (from β = −0.33, p < 0.001 to β = −0.19, p < 0.001). Discussion

From the perspective of the unifying model of the stress process, this study constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the impact of resilience on athlete burnout and its underlying mechanisms. After controlling for gender and age, resilience was significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout. Both problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping strategies partially mediated the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout. Psychosocial resources moderated the relationship between coping strategies and athlete burnout in the context of resilience.

Resilience and athlete burnout

Our findings support the meta-theory of resilience by demonstrating that resilience is significantly negatively associated with athlete burnout. This provides empirical support for the theory’s proposition that resilience reduces maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses when athletes encounter stressors [24]. Previous research has shown that athletes with lower resilience levels are more likely to perceive stressors as threats, leading to frequent worry, physiological tension, and attention problems, which ultimately increases the risk of athlete burnout. In contrast, athletes with higher resilience levels tend to view stressors as challenges and opportunities for career growth. This positive internal motivation encourages them to actively cope with stress, thereby reducing the risk of athlete burnout [25, 72]. Furthermore, athletes with higher resilience levels are typically better able to satisfy their basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and belonging [73]. Their motivation for training and competition is primarily driven by a love for the sport and intrinsic satisfaction rather than external rewards and outcome-oriented goals. This intrinsic motivation enables them to maintain an optimistic mindset, regulate their emotions and behaviors more effectively, and protect their physical and mental well-being from the negative impact of stressors [74]. Notably, a lack of resilience may be a potential contributor to athlete burnout. As a key psychological factor, resilience plays a crucial role in enabling athletes to effectively cope with and overcome adversity and stress in competitive sports, as well as in developing their ability to adapt to negative stimuli. The absence of resilience undoubtedly places athletes at greater risk for burnout. Therefore, when examining the factors influencing maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses, it is essential to consider not only objective environmental factors such as training load and competition pressure but also the development of athletes’ psychological attributes. Drawing from theories of stress inoculation [75], it has been suggested that moderate exposure to adversity and stress can enhance individuals’ ability to successfully manage future stressors. This highlights the need for intervention strategies aimed at fostering resilience as a critical avenue for research and practice, ultimately helping to prevent athlete burnout.

Mediating role of coping strategies

Our findings support the meta-model of stress, emotions, and performance by demonstrating that both problem-focused and avoidance-focused coping strategies partially mediate the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout. This provides empirical support for the model’s proposition that coping strategies serve as key mediators in the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout [31]. Problem-focused coping and avoidance-focused coping exert opposite effects on the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout. The former involves strategies aimed at modifying or eliminating stressors, while the latter involves strategies that attempt to escape from them [76]. Previous research has demonstrated that problem-focused coping helps athletes develop a sense of purpose in training and competition, allowing them to actively mobilize coping resources to meet situational demands [77]. In contrast, avoidance-focused coping may lead to delays or neglect in addressing situational demands and personal emotional stress responses, ultimately exacerbating emotional and physical exhaustion as well as devaluation [30, 78]. Additionally, avoidance-focused coping may contribute to a withdrawal from achievement-oriented goals, leading to a diminished sense of accomplishment [77]. According to the results of this study, resilience, as an antecedent and distal influencing factor of coping strategies, plays a crucial role in this process. On the one hand, resilience enables athletes to adopt problem-focused coping strategies—such as adjusting training plans, engaging in positive self-talk, or seeking support and feedback—to effectively manage adversity and stress in competitive sports, thereby alleviating burnout symptoms. On the other hand, resilience helps reduce athletes’ reliance on avoidance-focused coping strategies—such as self-denial, emotional suppression, or excessive dependence on external reassurance—thereby mitigating burnout symptoms associated with maladaptation. Thus, when faced with stressful situations, athletes should remain fully aware of the “trap” of avoidance-focused coping strategies and their potential negative impact. In turn, coaches should actively encourage athletes to develop and apply problem-focused coping strategies in specific situations, which is particularly essential for effectively managing athlete burnout [37].

Moderating role of psychosocial resources

Our findings support the resources and perception model by demonstrating that psychosocial resources moderate the relationship between coping strategies and athlete burnout in the context of resilience. This provides empirical support for the model’s proposition that the likelihood of athletes exhibiting maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses decreases as their level of psychosocial resources increases [49]. Our findings suggest that psychosocial resources can alleviate athletes’ acute emotional distress in the short term by providing immediate emotional support and constructive feedback. This process helps athletes reappraise stressors, thereby optimizing the coping strategies they adopt. Previous research has shown that the accumulation of specific situational resources (e.g., social support) helps athletes to more actively evaluate the relationship between challenges and threats, mitigating the negative impact of stressors on their psychological states and behavioral patterns, including athlete burnout [79–81]. Among athletes who adopt problem-focused coping, psychosocial resources facilitate a more active approach to identifying and addressing challenges. These athletes typically exhibit stronger positive traits, such as optimism and a sense of control, when faced with stressful situations, which effectively reduces stress accumulation and negative emotions, thereby preventing the onset of burnout symptoms. Similarly, for athletes who rely on avoidance-focused coping, psychosocial resources help alleviate maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses. While these athletes may avoid confronting problems and stress, psychosocial resources can still provide emotional support and reassurance, helping to reduce negative emotions and feelings of helplessness. Consequently, psychosocial resources play a crucial role in mitigating stress accumulation and burnout symptoms associated with avoidance-focused coping.

Notably, psychosocial resources did not moderate the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout, which may be attributed to the temporal characteristics of resilience and athlete burnout. First, as a consequence of prolonged exposure to stress, athlete burnout develops gradually and exhibits a certain degree of temporal stability. Second, resilience is a psychological factor that athletes build over time as they cope with stressful situations, requiring long-term adaptation and cultivation. Its formation process also follows a pattern of temporal continuity. However, psychosocial resources involve the dynamic interaction between the individual and the environment, primarily relying on the continuous acquisition of resources and external support. This suggests that short-term fluctuations in psychosocial resources are unlikely to exert a direct and significant impact on the relationship between resilience and athlete burnout. However, the long-term support and stable accumulation of psychosocial resources over time may influence this relationship, highlighting the need for future research to explore how psychosocial resources moderate the resilience–burnout relationship from a longitudinal perspective to better understand its potential long-term effects. In this regard, the moderating role of psychosocial resources in preventing athlete burnout offers a new perspective and research avenue for theoretical advancements in this field. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying resilience’s influence on athlete burnout, laying an important foundation for the theoretical development of psychological health interventions for athletes.

Applied implications

The present findings have theoretical significance and practical implications for advancing research on the relationship between resilience and maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses. Moreover, they contribute to the development of evidence-based prevention and intervention strategies aimed at reducing the risk of athlete burnout. It is suggested that psychologists design and implement intervention programs for managing athlete burnout, focusing on developing problem-focused coping and reducing avoidance-focused coping of athletes in stressful situations. For example, mindfulness-based interventions have been proven to help athletes identify and adjust maladaptive coping strategies, effectively manage distractions, and stay focused on the task at hand, thereby reducing the risk of athlete burnout [82–85]. For coaches and sports administrators, it is important to collaboratively promote the establishment of social support networks, encourage athletes to adopt adaptive coping strategies during training and competition, and foster a supportive team environment. These approaches can ensure that athletes receive adequate psychosocial resource support, alleviating their maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses to stressors. Notably, athletes from different regions and cultural backgrounds may have different coping strategies or psychosocial resources that should be prioritized for development. Specifically, athletes from Western cultural backgrounds typically place greater emphasis on developing psychosocial resources such as self-efficacy and self-esteem, making them more inclined to adopt individual-centered coping strategies in stressful situations. In contrast, Eastern cultures tend to place greater value on social support and team cohesion, suggesting that athletes from these backgrounds may be better suited to collective-centered coping strategies. Therefore, the influence of cultural background on athletes’ psychological states and behavioral patterns must be considered when helping athletes to manage and reduce burnout. This enables the development of culturally adaptive intervention programs, enhancing the overall effectiveness of the interventions.

Limitations and future suggestions

The findings of this study should be interpreted with consideration of its limitations. First, although the cross-sectional design adopted in this study is grounded in existing theories and research, it limits the ability to fully infer causal relationships between variables. Future research should consider adopting a randomized controlled trial design, incorporating experimental and control groups to further validate the moderated mediation model proposed in this study. Additionally, longitudinal tracking methods could provide deeper insights into the causal relationships between variables and their dynamic changes. Second, all variables examined were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may not effectively reflect athletes’ true psychological states and behavioral patterns. Future research should employ physiological or behavioral indicators to further examine the rationality and scientificity of the proposed hypotheses. Third, the sample in this study primarily consisted of Chinese athletes, which raises questions about the generalizability of the findings to athletes from other regions and cultural backgrounds. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying the relationship between resilience and burnout symptoms across diverse populations through cross-cultural and cross-disciplinary comparisons to better understand potential variations in psychological processes. Finally, psychosocial resources have a multidimensional structure with complex components. This study selected only four indicators—optimism, sense of control, self-esteem, and extraversion—to represent psychosocial resources, which may not fully capture their broader composition. Future research should consider integrating additional individual and environmental factors, such as athlete identity and perceived social support, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how psychosocial resources contribute to athletes’ resilience in stressful contexts.

Conclusions

Resilience is a crucial proximal factor in athlete burnout, with coping strategies partially mediating their relationship. Psychosocial resources play a protective role in mitigating burnout risk. These findings have theoretical and practical implications for advancing research on resilience and maladaptive psychological or behavioral responses, as well as for guiding the development of interventions to reduce the risk of athlete burnout. However, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to fully infer causal relationships between variables; future research is encouraged to adopt experimental or longitudinal designs to further examine the rationality and scientificity of the proposed hypotheses.
