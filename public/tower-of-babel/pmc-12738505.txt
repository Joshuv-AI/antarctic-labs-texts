Cholestanol promotes tau pathology in a mouse model of tauopathy
Authors: Ting Yu, Yuanmin Li, Sheng Li, Shuke Nie, Hongyan Feng, Zhentao Zhang, Lanxia Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is an autosomal recessive lipid storage disease characterized by the accumulation of cholestanol. CTX patients often suffer from cognitive impairment. We found that serum cholestanol levels are higher in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients than in control subjects. Thus, we tested whether cholestanol regulates the pathogenesis of AD. Cholestanol promotes tau fragmentation and hyperphosphorylation by activating asparagine endopeptidase (AEP). AEP knockdown alleviates cholestanol-induced tau fragmentation and phosphorylation. Feeding cholestanol to tau P301S mice aggravates tau pathology and behavioral defects, while knockout of AEP ameliorates cholestanol-induced tau pathology and behavioral defects in tau P301S mice. These results highlight the role of AEP-mediated tau cleavage in cholestanol-induced tau pathology and cognitive decline. The data also identify the potential therapeutic target of AEP in AD, particularly in AD patients with elevated serum cholestanol levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05978-1.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder and the most common form of dementia. The pathological features of AD are extracellular senile plaques, which are mainly composed of amyloid-β (Aβ), and intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles, which are primarily composed of hyperphosphorylated tau [1, 2]. Tau is believed to regulate microtubule assembly and stabilization. In the AD brain, tau aggregates into insoluble fibrils, leading to neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment [3]. Phosphorylation and truncation have been reported to contribute to tau pathology in AD [4]. However, the underlying mechanisms that regulate tau phosphorylation and truncation have not been fully elucidated.

Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is a rare autosomal recessive lipid metabolism disorder caused by mutations in the CYP27A1 gene. The main pathological feature of CTX is the deposition of sterols in various parts of the body, especially the brain, tendons, and lens. This leads to a range of clinical manifestations, such as neuropsychiatric symptoms, Achilles tendon xanthomatosis, and juvenile cataracts [5]. In the liver, cholesterol is normally metabolized into bile acid by the catalysis of two main enzymes: CYP7A1, which is the rate-limiting enzyme, and CYP27A1. However, in CTX patients, mutation of the CYP27A1 gene impairs the synthesis of bile acid, which negatively feeds back to the enzyme CYP7A1, resulting in a 100-fold increase in the production of the intermediate 7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one. This intermediate can easily cross the blood–brain barrier and is further metabolized into cholestanol, which deposited in the brain [6, 7]. The cholestanol content in plasma and tissues typically accounts for 0.1–0.2% of total cholesterol. However, in patients with CTX, this proportion can be 10–100 times higher (up to 2%) and is significantly enriched in the brain (up to 50%), tendons (up to 10%), and bile (up to 10%) [8, 9]. Many studies support the association between CTX and dementia [10–12]. In the latest clinical study, 44% (4/9) of children with CTX and 78% (14/18) of adults with CTX suffered from severe cognitive impairment. Among these adults with CTX, 16% (3/18) developed dementia after the age of 50 [13]. This finding prompted us to investigate whether cholestanol is involved in the onset of AD.

Asparagine endopeptidase (AEP), also known as legumain (LGMN) or δ-secretase, is a lysosomal cysteine endopeptidase expressed in plants, schistosomes, ticks, and mammals [14]. AEP specifically hydrolyzes its substrate on the C-terminus of asparagine residues, producing functional protein fragments [15]. AEP can cleave α-synuclein, amyloid precursor protein, and tau and is involved in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and AD [16–18]. The activation of AEP shortens lifespan and is involved in cancer metastasis [19, 20]. Our previous study revealed that AEP is activated in the brain in an age-dependent manner under the regulation of CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β (C/EBPβ), a neuroinflammation-related transcription factor [17, 21]. Phosphorylation of C/EBPβ at Thr235 can regulate its activity and its ability to activate gene transcription [22].

In this study, we found that cholestanol activates AEP, promoting the cleavage and phosphorylation of tau and aggravating cognitive impairment. Targeting cholesterol and AEP may be a potential treatment for AD, especially for patients with increased cholestanol levels.

Materials and methods

Mouse treatment

Wild-type C57BL/6 J (WT) mice (stock number: 000664) and tau P301S mice (stock number: 008169) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Tau P301S mice expresses human T34 isoform tau (1N4R), consisting of exons 1 to 13 without exon 3, carrying the P301S mutation [23]. AEP knockout (KO) mice on a mixed 129/Ola and C57BL/6 background were generated as previously reported [24]. All the mice were kept under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, following normal 12 h light/12 h dark cycles with free access to food and water at the Animal Experiment Center of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. All reasonable efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to use the minimum number of animals necessary to perform statistically valid analysis. The experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethical Committee (IACUC) of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University with the IACUC issue number of the WDRM animal (welfare) 20220905A.

Antibodies and reagents

Antibodies against the following targets were used: anti-C/EBPβ (H-7) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-7962), anti-pC/EBPβ (Thr235) (Cell Signaling Technology, 3084), anti-legumain (D6S4H) (Cell Signaling Technology, 93627), anti-tau N368 (Homemade, reported previously in ref. 17), anti-p-tau T181 (Invitrogen, MN1050), anti-p-tau S396 (Abcam, ab109390), anti-p-tau S202 (Abcam, ab108387), anti-tau (tau5) (Invitrogen, MA5-12808), anti-tau (HT7) (Invitrogen, MN1000), anti-MAP2 (Proteintech, 17490–1-AP), anti-MAP2 (Proteintech, 67015–1-Ig), anti-MAP2 (Abcam, ab254144), anti-HRP-conjugated GAPDH (Proteintech, HRP-60004), anti-HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Bio-Rad, 170–6515), anti-HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Bio-Rad, 170–6516), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11034), goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11001), goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A-11005), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A-11012), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, A-21244), and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L, Alexa Fluor 647 (Abcam, ab150115).

The following reagents were used: cholestanol (Sigma-Aldrich, D6128), cholestanol standard (5α-cholestan-3β-ol) (Avanti, 700064P-5MG-E-011, for LC–MS), TRIzol (Takara), ABScript II cDNA first-strand synthesis kit (ABclonal, RK20400), 2X universal SYBR green fast qPCR mix (ABclonal, RK21203), cycloheximide (CHX) (MKBio, MS0035), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay kit (Beyotime, C0016), membrane fluidity PDA fluorescence detection kit (YIJI, YIJI10362.1), DAPI (BioFroxx), DiI dye (Invitrogen, D-282), NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013F), RIPA lysis buffer (Fdbio science, FD009), DMSO (Sigma, D5879), BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227), IHC detection system kit (ZSGB-BIO, PV-6001, PV-6002, ZLI-9019), electron microscope fixative (Servicebio, G1102), FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit (FD NeuroTechnologies, Inc., PK401).

Expression constructs, lentiviral preparation, and AAV preparation

Plasmids encoding HA-tagged human tau 1–441 (referred to as HA-Tau) were used in this study. Lentiviral short hairpin RNA (sh-LGMN) and negative control (sh-NC) were prepared by Obio Technology. The sequences of sh-LGMN were as follows: sense: GCCATGCCTACCAGATCATTC; and sh-NC (sense: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT). Recombinant adeno-associated virus encoding human tau 1–441 P301S-EGFP-WPRE-hGH polyA under the control of the hSyn promoter (rAAV-hsyn-tau P301S-EGFP-WPRE-hGH polyA, referred to as AAV-tau P301S-GFP) was prepared by BrainVTA (Wuhan) Co., Ltd.

18F-FDG PET-SPM/MRI analysis

To more specifically and accurately identify the differences in 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) metabolism in brain regions between CTX patients and controls. The brain 18F-FDG PET images were converted into an analysis format via image format conversion software (MRIcro). On the image processing software MATLAB R2014a platform, statistical parametric mapping 12 (SPM12) was used to standardize the images according to the brain atlas of the Montreal Neurological Institute, and the standardized images were smoothed with a half-height width of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm. The brain 18F-FDG PET images of CTX patients (n = 3) and the control subjects (n = 10) were subjected to intergroup voxel (2-sample t-test) SPM12 statistical analysis to obtain the spatial coordinates of the pixels with statistically significant differences (FWE-corrected P < 0.001). The obtained SPM12 was fused to the standard magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain template to analyze the functional brain areas corresponding to the statistically significant differences.

Cell culture and treatment

HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum. Primary neurons were prepared from the cerebral cortex of WT, tau P301S, or AEP KO E18 embryos, which were removed from the uterus of pregnant mice. The brain tissues were removed, placed in PBS, chopped into pieces, and blown until no obvious tissue pieces were visible. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 2,000 × g, resuspended and cultured in DMEM containing 5% horse serum, 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM GlutaMAX. After the neurons adhered to the slides, the culture medium was replaced with Neurobasal medium containing 2% B27, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM GlutaMAX. All mammalian cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

To overexpress tau in primary neurons, primary neurons were infected with AAV-tau P301S-GFP on 7 days in vitro (DIV) and used for immunofluorescence experiments or DiI staining experiments on DIV 14. To overexpress tau in HEK293 cells, HEK293 cells were transfected with the HA-Tau plasmid using polyethylenimine (PEI) as the DNA transporter. To knock down LGMN in HEK293 cells, HEK293 cells were infected with sh-LGMN or sh-NC at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The knockdown efficiency was assessed via Western blotting. Cholestanol was dissolved and stored in a 1:1 volume mixture of ethanol and DMSO. The final concentration of DMSO or absolute ethanol in the culture medium in all the groups was less than 0.1%.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from HEK293 cells using TRIzol. 1 μg RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using the ABScript II cDNA first-strand synthesis kit. The amplification reaction was calculated by monitoring the fluorescent dye SybrGreen in a real-time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Relative quantification of C/EBPβ and LGMN expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the comparative Ct method. The following primers obtained from PrimerBank and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology, were used: C/EBPβ forward: CTTCAGCCCGTACCTGGAG; C/EBPβ reverse: GGAGAGGAAGTCGTGGTGC; LGMN forward: ATCGTGGCAGGTTCAAATGG; LGMN reverse: GGACTCCCTGATAGACATCTGTG; GAPDH forward: CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC; GAPDH reverse: AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG.

CHX-chase assay

CHX is a potent antibiotic that inhibits protein synthesis in eukaryotes. The CHX chase assay is commonly used to observe intracellular protein degradation and determine the half-life of a specific protein in eukaryotes. In this study, we conducted the CHX chase assay with slight modifications, following a previous study [25], to assess the degradation and half-life of C/EBPβ and AEP. Briefly, HEK293 cells transfected with the HA-Tau plasmid were treated with either vehicle or cholestanol (20 μM) and CHX (50 μg/mL) simultaneously for 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h. The C/EBPβ and AEP proteins were then detected by Western blotting.

LDH release assay

The integrity of the cell membrane was assessed using the LDH release assay, as described in previous study [26]. Briefly, HEK293 cells were seeded at an appropriate density in a 96-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a cell incubator. The cells were then treated with either vehicle or cholestanol for 36 h. After treatment, the LDH release assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After that, the plate was read on a microplate reader (SpectraMax, Molecular Devices) at a wavelength of 490 nm.

Measurements of membrane fluidity

The fluidity of the cell membrane was assessed using the membrane fluidity 1-pyrenedecanoic acid (PDA) fluorescence detection assay kit with slight modifications, as described in a previous study [27]. Briefly, HEK293 cells were seeded in a 96-well black plate at a density of 10,000 cells per well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a cell incubator. The cells were then treated with either vehicle or cholestanol for 36 h. After treatment, the membrane fluidity PDA fluorescence detection assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plate was then read on a microplate reader (SpectraMax, Molecular Devices) by exciting at 350 nm and taking emission values at 370 nm (monomer) and 470 nm (excimer). The relative membrane fluidity was calculated as the ratio of excimer to monomer fluorescence. The higher the ratio, the stronger the membrane fluidity.

DiI staining

DiI (1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate) is a lipophilic membrane dye that can stain the entire cell through lateral diffusion. We performed DiI staining to determine the density of neuronal dendritic spines. DiI dye was evenly sprinkled on the neuron slides for 20 min, and the dye that was not bound to the membrane was washed away with PBS. Furthermore, the slides were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quencher and observed and photographed under a confocal laser microscope (Leica).

Administration of cholestanol

Cholestanol was formulated in standard chow (AIN-93G-D10012G) at 2/1000 (w/w). Two-month-old mice were fed cholestanol or control chow for 4 months and then subjected to behavioral testing and were sacrificed. The dose of cholestanol used was approximately 150 mg/kg/d.

Stereotaxic injection of AAV-tau P301S-GFP

WT mice or AEP KO mice that had been fed cholestanol or control chow for 1 month were stereotaxically injected with AAV-tau P301S-GFP (200 nL) into the left hippocampus dentate gyrus (DG). The coordinates of the injection were as follows: lateral −2.0 mm, posterior −2.5 mm, and ventral −1.7 mm relative to bregma. The AAVs were injected at 50 nL/minute via a 10 μL glass syringe with a fixed needle. After injection, the needles were maintained for 5 min to facilitate absorption of the solution. Postoperative monitoring and care were performed after the injection.

Morris water maze test

The Morris water maze test was conducted to measure learning and memory as described previously [4] with slight modifications. Briefly, the mice were trained in a large cylinder (150 cm in diameter) filled with opaque water dyed with white non-toxic pigment. The cylinder was surrounded by a black curtain with pieces of paper of different colors and shapes pasted on it as clues. ANY-Maze software (San Diego Instruments) was used for the collection and analysis of the behavioral data. During training, the platform was placed 1 cm below the water surface. Each subject was trained for 4 trials/day for 4 consecutive days with a 15-min intertrial interval. If the subjects did not reach the platform within 90 s, they were manually guided to the platform and allowed to stay there for 10 s. A probe trial was performed on day 5. The platform was removed, and the time spent in the quadrant that previously contained the escape platform was measured over 90 s.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze test was conducted via established methods [28]. The mice were placed in a Y-maze with dimensions of 40 cm (length), 12 cm (height), and 3 cm (width), with each arm at a 120-degree angle. Behavioral data were collected and analyzed via ANY-Maze software (San Diego Instruments). During training, one arm (designated the novel arm) was blocked with cardboard, and the mice were placed into the Y-maze from the starting point of another arm (designated the start arm). This allowed the mice to explore only two arms. After a 2-h intertrial interval, the formal test was conducted. The barrier at the entrance of the novel arm was removed, and the mice were free to explore all three arms. The amount of time spent in the novel arm was recorded for 5 min.

LC–MS to detect cholestanol

In our study, the levels of cholestanol in the serum of both AD patients and control subjects, as well as in mouse hippocampus brain tissue, were measured by LC–MS as described before as follows [29], with some modifications. Metabolite extraction: Methanol was added to samples. After vortexing, homogenization, and sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 15,294 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for LC–MS analysis. Methanol was used as a solvent to dissolve the target compound, cholestanol, in the samples. Some other non-target compounds in the tissue are also dissolved in the methanol during this process. Standard solution preparation: Stock solution with a final concentration of 100 mg/L was prepared by dissolving cholestanol standard. A series of calibration standard solutions were then prepared by stepwise dilution of this stock solution. UHPLC-MRM-MS analysis: Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) separation was carried out using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II series UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies) equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 mm, 50 mm, 1.8 μm). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in methanol. The flow rate was 300 μL/minute, the column temperature was set at 35 °C, the auto-sampler temperature was set at 10 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL. An Agilent 6495 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies), equipped with an APCI interface, was applied for assay development. Typical ion source parameters were: capillary voltage = + 4500 V, APCI Needle voltage = 4.5 V, gas (N2) temperature = 200 °C, gas (N2) flow = 14 L/minute, nebulizer = 35 psi. The MRM parameters for the targeted analytes were optimized, by injecting the standard solution of the individual analytes directly, into the APCI source of the mass spectrometer. At least two MRM transitions (i.e., the Q1/Q3 pairs) per analyte were obtained, and the two most sensitive transitions were used in the MRM scan mode to optimize the collision energy for each Q1/Q3 pair. Among the two MRM transitions per analyte, the Q1/Q3 pairs that showed the highest sensitivity and selectivity were used as the MRM transitions for quantitative monitoring. The additional transitions acted as qualifier for the purpose of verifying the identity of the target analytes. Agilent MassHunter Work Station Software (B.10.00, Agilent Technologies) was employed for MRM data acquisition and processing. Calibration Curves: calibration solution was subjected to UPLC-MRM-MS/MS analysis using the method described above. Least squares method was used for the fitting. 1/x weighting was applied in the curve fitting since it provided highest accuracy and correlation coefficient (R2). Calculation of cholestanol: the “retention time” of choletanol standard was used to identify if cholestanol was present in samples. The content of the target compound in the sample was calculated using the calibration curves and compound peak area. The reading unit of the machine is µg/L, and the contents of cholestanol were expressed as ng/mg in mouse tissues and as μmol/L in human serum samples.

Western blot

The cell samples were lysed with NP-40, and the mouse hippocampal tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer, both of which contained protease inhibitor cocktails and phosphatase inhibitors. The samples were lysed on ice for 30 min, vortexed every 5 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration of the supernatant was measured via a BCA protein assay kit. The supernatant was boiled in SDS loading buffer. Equal amounts of proteins were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked, and then incubated with the primary antibody on a shaker at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed in TBST, incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, washed in TBST again, and then visualized via enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrates.

Immunofluorescence

The neurons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Then, the sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min and blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. Next, the neurons were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594-onjugated, 488-onjugated, or 647-conjugated antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. The samples were then washed with PBS and stained with DAPI. Pictures were taken via a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX73) or a confocal microscopy (Leica, Stellaris 5). The positive areas of tau phosphorylated at T181 or S396 and MAP2 were quantified via ImageJ software. The ratio of tau phosphorylated at T181 or S396 to MAP2 was used for statistical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were anesthetized with 5% chloral hydrate and then intracardially perfused with saline and then 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were isolated and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. The brains were subsequently embedded and cut into 4 μm slices. The mouse hippocampal slices were first dewaxed in xylene and then rehydrated in a series of graded ethanol solutions: 100% anhydrous ethanol for 5 min, 95% ethanol for 5 min, 85% ethanol for 5 min, 75% ethanol for 5 min, and double distilled water for 5 min. The slides were then subjected to antigen retrieval in a solution of 0.1 M trisodium citrate (pH 6.0) for 20 min at 94 °C. After the solution cooled to room temperature, the slides were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. To reduce nonspecific background staining caused by endogenous peroxidase, the slides were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Next, the slides were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min and then incubated with primary antibodies against p-tau T181 (Invitrogen, 1:500) or p-tau S396 (Abcam, 1:500) overnight at 4 °C. Positive signals were detected via an IHC detection kit. The nuclei were stained, and the slides were sealed with neutral gum. Light microscopy images were captured, and the optical density was measured via ImageJ software and the IHC Profiler plugin to calculate the optical density score as previously described [30, 31]. The ImageJ software counts the pixels and evaluates the percentage contributions of high positive, positive, low positive, and negative areas. The “optical density score” was calculated as (percentage contribution of high positive*4 + percentage contribution of positive*3 + percentage contribution of low positive*2 + percentage contribution of negative*1)/100.

Transmission electron microscopy of synapses

Transmission electron microscopy was used to quantify the synaptic density in the mouse hippocampus. The mice were anesthetized with 5% chloral hydrate and then perfused intracardially with electron microscope fixative. The mouse brains were removed and immediately placed in electron microscope fixative. Hippocampal tissues were quickly isolated, cut into smaller pieces, and immersed in electron microscope fixative overnight at 4 °C. Following standard procedures (post-fixation, dehydration, resin penetration, embedding, polymerization, and positioning), the hippocampal tissues were cut into ultrathin Sections. (80 nm). These sections were then loaded on cuprum grids, stained with uranyl acetate and lead acetate, dried, and observed using a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, HT7800/HT7700). Synapses were identified by the presence of synaptic vesicles and postsynaptic densities.

Golgi staining

The Golgi dye solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of solutions A and B of the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit at least 24 h before use. The mice were deeply anesthetized, and their brain tissues were quickly isolated. The blood on the surface of the brain tissues was quickly rinsed off with double distilled water. The tissues were then immersed in the dye solution and stored in the dark at room temperature. After one day of storage, the dye solution was renewed. Two weeks later, the tissues were transferred to solution C of the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit for washing, after which they were stored in the dark at room temperature. After one day of storage, the washing mixture was renewed. Seventy-two hours later, the tissues were cut into 60 μm thick slices, placed on slides, and treated with tissue treatment solution (Servicebio, G1069-3). The slices were then washed with ultrapure water and developed with Golgi developer solution (Servicebio, G1069-2) for 30 min. Finally, the slides were sealed with glycerin gelatin (Servicebio, G1402) and observed under a light microscope for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad software 8.3.0. Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses included the 2-tailed Student’s t-test, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypometabolism in the brains of CTX patients

18F-FDG PET is a non-invasive imaging tool for monitoring changes in cerebral brain glucose metabolism. AD patients show typical FDG hypometabolism in brain regions including the posteromedial parietal (precuneus), lateral parietal, lateral temporal, and medial temporal lobes [32]. FDG hypometabolism is parallel to cognitive function along the trajectory of normal, preclinical, prodromal, and established AD [33, 34]. To determine whether CTX-related cognitive impairment is also accompanied by decreased FDG metabolism, we analyzed the intergroup voxel SPM12 of 18F-FDG PET images from 3 CTX patients and 10 healthy volunteers. Similar to the findings in AD patients, we detected reduced FDG uptake in the bilateral frontal lobes, bilateral striatum, bilateral temporal lobes, and cerebellar tonsils in the CTX group compared with that in healthy subjects (Fig. 1A). CTX is characterized by increased cholestanol levels. To further explore the potential role of cholestanol in AD, we performed LC–MS to measure the serum cholestanol level in 15 clinically diagnosed AD patients with moderate or severe cognitive impairment and 19 age-matched control subjects. Our results revealed that the levels of cholestanol were higher in AD patients than in control subjects (Fig. 1B). Hippocampal MRI results showed no hippocampal atrophy in controls, but 7 of 15 AD patients had hippocampal atrophy. Notably, there was no significant difference in metabolic statuses and lipid profiles between controls and AD patients (Supplementary Table 1). The metabolic pathway of cholesterol is described in detail in previous study [35]. Figure 1C shows the cholesterol metabolism process involving CYP enzyme metabolism and cholestanol deposition (Fig. 1C). Previous literature [7] indicates that cholestanol can cross the blood–brain barrier to a certain extent. We hypothesized that elevated cholestanol may deposit in the brain and activate AEP. This further leads to the cleavage of tau into fragments, resulting in increased tau phosphorylation and ultimately leading to cognitive decline. Cholestanol activates the C/EBPβ/AEP pathway and increases tau phosphorylation

Excessive phosphorylation of tau is thought to mediate neuronal dysfunction and cognitive impairment in AD and other tauopathies [3]. To investigate the effect of cholestanol on tau phosphorylation, HEK293 cells were transfected with HA-Tau and treated with 20 µM cholestanol for 36 h. Interestingly, cholestanol increased tau phosphorylation at the T181 and S396 residues, but not at the S202 residue (Fig. 2A-E). Therefore, we focused on the tau phosphorylation at T181 and S396 residues in our subsequent experiments. Previously, we reported that the protease AEP is activated in an age-dependent manner and cleaves tau after N368, generating a tau 1–368 fragment that is more prone to aggregation [17]. The expression of AEP is regulated by the transcription factor C/EBPβ [21]. Therefore, we examined the effects of cholestanol on the C/EBPβ/AEP signaling pathway and tau fragmentation. Interestingly, cholestanol dramatically increased the levels of C/EBPβ, pC/EBPβ, AEP, and the AEP-generated tau N368 fragment (Fig. 2A and F-J). RT-PCR analysis showed that the levels of C/EBPβ and LGMN were elevated after the HEK293 cells were exposed to 20 μM cholestanol for 36 h (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, the CHX chase assay revealed that there was no significant difference in the half-life of C/EBPβ and AEP proteins between the vehicle group and the chostanol group, indicating that cholestanol did not affect the degradation of C/EBPβ and AEP in HEK293 cells (Fig. 2L-M). Using a LDH release assay and a membrane fluidity PDA fluorescence detection assay in HEK293 cells, we found that cholestanol has no effect on both membrane integrity and fluidity (Supplemental Fig. 1A-B). These results suggest that cholestanol increases the protein levels of C/EBPβ and AEP by upregulating their expression rather than by reducing their degradation. Discussion

In this study, we found that AD patients have elevated levels of serum cholestanol. High levels of cholestanol activate the C/EBPβ/AEP pathway and promote the cleavage and phosphorylation of tau, resulting in synaptic damage and cognitive decline. Blockade of AEP reduces cholestanol induced tau pathology, mitigates synaptic damage, and alleviates cognitive deficits. These findings indicate that cholestanol promotes tau phosphorylation and cognitive impairment by activating AEP.

In patients with CTX, cholestanol is deposited in the brain [6, 7]. As previous study, mice were fed a diet rich in cholestanol, and the levels of cholestanol in the cerebellum increased almost linearly in parallel to the feeding time [37], we detected elevated levels of cholestanol in the hippocampus of mice fed diet containing cholestanol. In addition, cholestanol is not easy to metabolize [7, 38]. Moreover, in CTX patients of other study, the blood–brain barrier appeared to be intact [39]. A study utilized an established model of the blood–brain barrier to demonstrate that cholestanol is capable of passing the blood–brain barrier, but at a lower rate than 7a-hydroxy-4cholesten-3-one [7]. These evidences indicate that cholestanol can pass the blood–brain barrier. The physiological function of cholestanol remains unclear. Excessive cholestanol has been shown to induce the apoptosis of corneal endothelial and lens epithelial cells, as well as damage to cerebellar and dopaminergic neurons, which may explain the cataracts, cerebellar ataxia, and PD-like symptoms observed in patients with CTX [40–42]. Studies have shown that 16% of adult CTX patients will develop dementia, and even cognitive impairment can occur in the early stages of the disease [13]. Our PET-MRI image fusion results revealed reduced frontal and temporal metabolism in CTX patients, similar to the findings in AD patients. These findings support a potential link between cholestanol and the onset of AD.

Cholestanol can be synthesized in the liver and absorbed from the intestine [43, 44]. However, the specific pathway by which serum cholestanol levels are elevated in AD patients is currently unknown. CYP7A1 and CYP27A1 enzymes are very important in the synthesis of cholestanol, and CYP27A1 and CYP46A1 are involved in the decomposition of cholestanol in brain [35]. In this study, we attempted to measure the levels of serum CYP7A1, CYP27, and CYP46A1 in both control subjects and AD patients using elisa kits, however, these enzymes were not detected. We are currently developing more sensitive elisa kits and will use RT-PCR, Western blotting and other methods to detect the content and activity of these three enzymes in serum samples and other samples such as liver, cerebrospinal fluid and feces, so as to gain a better understanding of cholesterol homeostasis.

Hyperphosphorylation of tau is a pathological characteristic of AD. It induces synaptic dysfunction, decreases tau degradation, and enhances tau aggregation [45–50]. Tau phosphorylation is regulated by various factors, especially kinases and protein phosphatases [3]. Our previous study found that cholestanol causes mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to a decrease in the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes (specifically complexes II, III, IV, and V) and an increase in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [40]. Furthermore, we observed that inhibiting mitochondrial complexes I, II, or III results in an escalation of ROS levels and activates the C/EBPβ/AEP pathway [51]. Based on our current study and previous findings, it is clear that cholestanol can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, and then activates C/EBPβ/AEP pathway. We found that AEP cleaves tau and generates tau (1–368) fragment, which accelerates tau phosphorylation and deposition [17]. Here, we demonstrate that cholestanol activates AEP, leading to an increase in fragmented tau (1–368) and hyperphosphorylation of tau.

The tau P301S transgenic mouse model is commonly used to study tauopathy, as these mice develop tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, and behavioral disorders [23, 52, 53]. In our study, we fed tau P301S transgenic mice a diet containing cholestanol and observed an increase in C/EBPβ/AEP activation in the hippocampus, as well as increased tau fragmentation and phosphorylation, aggravated synaptic loss, and decreased cognitive function compared with those of control mice. The role of AEP in cholestanol-induced tau pathology and AD-like cognitive decline was further confirmed in AEP KO mice. Injection of AAV-tau P301S-GFP induces tau pathology, reduction in synaptic density, and impairment of cognitive function. Tau pathology was exacerbated by cholestanol and attenuated by AEP knockout. Since the serum cholestanol levels in serum in AD patient are greater than those of in healthy controls, our data suggest that cholestanol promotes tau phosphorylation by activating AEP in AD patients. Study has shown that cholesterol-lowering statins, such as atorvastatin can reduce tau phosphorylation [54], which may also indirectly be caused by reducing cholestanol, a process we will continue to explore in the future.

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the pathological mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment associated with CTX. Our findings demonstrate that AD patients have elevated levels of serum cholestanol. Most significantly, we have discovered that cholestanol promotes tau phosphorylation and cognitive impairment by activating AEP. Our study may facilitate to identify the potential therapeutic target of AEP in AD, particularly in AD patients with elevated serum cholestanol levels.
