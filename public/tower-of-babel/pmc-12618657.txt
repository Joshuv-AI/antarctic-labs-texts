Gestational age-specific sex difference in neurological outcomes of very low birth weight preterm infants—a nationwide study
Authors: Joo Yun Yang, Young Min Youn, Jung In Kang, Ye Jin Han, Do Kyung Lee, So-Yeon Shim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is a population-based study from the Korean Neonatal Network (KNN) for very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs) born at 23+0 and 31+6 weeks of gestation between 2013 and 2019. This study aims to evaluate the effect of sex on neurological outcomes at 18–24 months corrected age, stratified with gestational age (GA) in VLBWIs. A total of 1,829 infants (15.7%) who completed Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, third edition (BSID-III) assessments at 18–24 months corrected age were included and were further stratified into three GA groups: 23+0 to 25+6 weeks, 26+0 to 28+6 weeks, and 29+0 to 31+6 weeks. The primary outcomes were the BSID-III composite scores and neurodevelopmental delay defined as BSID-III composite scores lower than 80. Male sex was an independent risk factor for neurodevelopmental delay, particularly in infants born over 26 weeks of gestation. Among VLBW preterm infants born over 26 weeks of gestation, male infants of the younger GA group were associated with a higher risk of cognitive and motor delay, whereas those of the older GA group had a higher risk of language delay. The extremely low GA group (below 25 weeks of gestation) did not exhibit sex differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23735-z.Subject terms: Paediatric research, Neurodevelopmental disorders

Introduction

Preterm birth is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as any births occurring before the completion of 37 gestation weeks1. Infants were categorized according to birth weight into two groups: the very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs; infants weighing < 1,500 g at birth) and extremely low birth weight infants (infants weighing < 1,000 g at birth). The survival rate of VLBWIs in South Korea has exhibited improvement over the past years, particularly in preterm infants under a gestational age (GA) of 26 weeks2. However, VLBW preterm infants acquire an increased risk of adverse long-term neurological outcomes, with the risk escalating as GA decreases3–8. Concerns about the long-term neurological outcomes of preterm infants are emerging with the improved survival rates of VLBWIs.

Various factors contribute to adverse neurological outcomes in VLBW infants. Neonatal factors, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), and sepsis are recognized as important risk factors9–12. Additionally, maternal factors such as chorioamnionitis, foreign maternal origin, and mothers with a lower educational level have been identified as contributors to the increased risk of adverse neurological outcomes13–16. Furthermore, male sex is also a well-established risk factor for poor neurological outcomes in preterm infants. Male preterm infants have inherently higher risk of adverse neurological outcomes than their female counterparts12,14–23.

While GA remains as the predominant factor influencing neonatal outcomes, limited number of studies have explored the specific effects of sex, adjusting for GA17,24. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, there are no published studies examining the impact of sex on long-term outcomes in preterm infants, especially those that consider various perinatal factors, including multiple births, maternal diabetes, maternal hypertension, chorioamnionitis, and antenatal steroid use.

This retrospective observational study was based on data from the Korean Neonatal Network (KNN), a nationwide database covering VLBWIs across South Korea. This study was aimed to evaluate the effect of sex on neurological outcomes at 18–24 months corrected age in VLBW preterm infants, stratified by GA. We aimed to ascertain the sex-specific effects within distinct GA strata, while adjusting for perinatal risk factors that could influence long-term neurological outcomes. Furthermore, considering previous studies indicating elevated mortality and morbidities among preterm infants born at ≤ 25 weeks of gestation25–29, we performed a subgroup analysis according to GA groups: from 23 to 25 weeks, from 26 to 28 weeks, and from 29 to 31 weeks of gestation.

Results

Population

A total of 12,021 VLBW preterm infants, born between 23+0 and 31+6 weeks of gestation, were registered in the KNN during the study period. Among them, 380 infants were identified with severe congenital anomaly. Follow-up was proposed for all 9,962 surviving infants at the time of hospital discharge. However, 69 infants experienced fatal outcomes before follow-up, resulting in a final cohort of 9,893 eligible survivors for follow-up at 18–24 months corrected age. Unfortunately, follow-up assessments were completed for only 3,035 infants (30.7%). From this subset, only 1,829 survivors (15.7%) who completed Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, third edition (BSID-III) assessments were included in this study. The distribution of male and female infants in the three GA groups is depicted in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the study population from the Korean Neonatal Network database.

Survivors eligible for follow-up but lacked BSID-III scores exhibited higher GA, increased birth weight, and larger head circumference at birth compared with those with BSID-III scores. Additionally, those without BSID-III scores experienced a lower frequency of neonatal morbidities. Mothers of infants without BSID-III scores demonstrated a lower incidence of chrioamnionitis, reduced rate of antenatal steroid use, and lower educational levels compared to mothers of infants with available BSID-III scores. Furthermore, the number of mothers originating from countries other than Korea was higher among those without BSID-III scores, while the number of parents serving as the primary caretaker of the infant was lower (see Supplementary Table S1 online).

Infants’ characteristics and major morbidities

Male infants exhibited a significantly lower GA compared to their female counterparts (P = 0.004), although this difference disappeared upon subgroup analysis. No significant sex differences were observed in delivery mode, outborn status, Apgar score at 5 min, duration on ventilation, and length of hospital stay among all enrolled patients. However, male infants displayed higher weight (P < 0.001) and head circumference (P = 0.002) at birth and at discharge (both P < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis, male infants in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group had higher birth weight (P < 0.001). In the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks of gestation group, male infants exhibited higher weight and head circumference at birth (both P < 0.001) and at discharge (P < 0.001 and P = 0.002, respectively). Within the 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation group, male infants showed greater head circumference at birth (P = 0.006), and greater weight and head circumference at discharge (both P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Among the enrolled infants, male infants exhibited a higher incidence of BPD (P = 0.005).

However, there were no significant differences in the occurrence for IVH grade ≥ 3, PVL, sepsis, and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) stage ≥ 2 between male and female infants. Maternal characteristics and social economic status

The prevalence of maternal diabetes was higher in mothers of male infants (P = 0.012), while the prevalence of maternal hypertension was higher in mothers of female infants (P = 0.013). Subgroup analysis revealed that mothers of male infants in the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks of gestation group had a higher rate of maternal diabetes (P = 0.001) and a lower rate of maternal hypertension (P = 0.037). There were no significant sex differences in the prevalence of multiple births, chorioamnionitis, and the use of antenatal steroids in the overall analysis. However, in the subgroup analysis, the rate of chorioamnionitis was higher in the mothers of female infants in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group and in the mothers of male infants the 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation group (P = 0.031 and P = 0.018, respectively) (Table 2).

Neurological outcomes

In the overall analysis, male infants displayed significantly lower BSID-III composite scores in cognition, language, and motor skills (91.3 ± 16.3 vs. 95.5 ± 15.5, 86.6 ± 16.2 vs. 91.7 ± 15.9, and 91.0 ± 16.3 vs. 94.5 ± 15.7, respectively; all with P < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis, male. infants had significantly lower BSID-III cognition composite scores in the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks and 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation groups (90.8 ± 16.0 vs. 95.7 ± 15.9, and 94.9 ± 15.0 vs. 98.4 ± 13.4, respectively; both P < 0.001) Additionally, BSID-III motor composite scores of males were significantly lower in these same gestation groups (90.5 ± 16.4 vs. 94.9 ± 15.4, P < 0.001; and 95.5 ± 13.2 vs. 97.6 ± 13.8, P = 0.020, respectively). BSID-III language composite scores of male infants were significantly lower in all gestation groups (80.7 ± 16.6 vs. 83.7 ± 15.3, P = 0.048; 86.7 ± 16.4 vs. 91.8 ± 16.1, P < 0.001; and 89.2 ± 15.2 vs. 94.9 ± 15.0, P < 0.001) (Table 3). Table 4 presents the results of multiple linear regression analysis performed to assess the effect of sex on BSID-III composite scores. In the overall analysis, male infants had lower BSID-III cognition, language, and motor scores (adjusted β: −4.67, 95% CI: −6.07 to −3.27; adjusted β: −5.46, 95% CI: −6.90 to −4.01; and adjusted β: −3.65, 95% CI: −4.97 to −2.32, respectively, all with P < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis, male infants also had lower BSID-III cognition, language and motor scores in the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks of gestation group (adjusted β: −5.90, 95% CI: −8.06 to −3.74, P < 0.001; adjusted β: −6.01, 95% CI: −8.26 to −3.77, P < 0.001; and adjusted β: −5.13, 95% CI: −7.16 to −3.10, P < 0.001, respectively) and in the 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation group (adjusted β: −4.10, 95% CI: −6.12 to −2.09, P < 0.001; adjusted β: −6.27, 95% CI: −8.43 to −4.11, P < 0.001; and adjusted β: −2.46, 95% CI: −4.30 to −0.61, P = 0.009, respectively). However, there were no significant differences in BSID-III cognition, language, and motor scores between male and female infants in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group (adjusted β: −1.21, 95% CI: −5.27 to 2.86, P = 0.560; adjusted β: −1.57, 95% CI: −5.45 to 2.32, P = 0.427; and adjusted β: −1.02, 95% CI: −4.97 to 2.93, P = 0.612, respectively).

Sex differences in incidence of neurodevelopmental delay, stratified by GA group are presented in Table 3. In the overall analysis, male infants demonstrated significantly higher rates of cognitive, language, and motor delay (18.3% vs. 11.4%, OR: 1.85, 95% CI: 1.32–2.59, P < 0.001; 35.4% vs. 24.7%, OR: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.43–2.35, P < 0.001; and 21.5% vs. 15.3%, OR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.11–2.14, P = 0.009, respectively). In the subgroup analysis, the rate of cognitive delay was significantly higher in males of the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks and 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation groups (19.0% vs. 12.0%, OR: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.23–3.36, P = 0.006 and 11.8% vs. 6.6%, OR: 1.98, 95% CI: 1.09–3.59, P = 0.026, respectively). Similarly, the rate of language delay was significantly higher in males of the same gestation groups (35.6% vs. 26.1%, OR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.31–2.75, P < 0.001 and 28.8% vs. 16.5%, OR: 2.38, 95% CI: 1.57–3.61, P < 0.001, respectively). The rate of motor delay was significantly higher only in males of the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks of gestation group (23.4% vs. 14.9%, OR: 2.16, 95% CI: 1.30–3.57, P = 0.003). However, the rates of cognitive, language, and motor delay were not significantly different between sexes in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group (30.7% vs. 22.1%, OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.48–2.33, P = 0.880; 49.7% vs. 41.6%, OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 0.79–2.70, P = 0.222; and 41.2% vs. 31.5%, OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.57–2.14, P = 0.760, respectively) (Table 3). These findings remained consistent in the multiple logistic regression analysis after adjusting for clinical and social factors (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of the present study revealed that VLBW male preterm infants exhibited lower BSID-III composite scores and higher risks of neurodevelopment delay at 18–24 months corrected age compared to their female counterparts. The finding of higher risk of neurodevelopmental delay exhibited by VLBW male preterm infants aligns with previous studies12,14. –15,17–22 In a study involving 461 extremely preterm infants born before 27 weeks of gestation, male infants demonstrated significantly lower scores in all BSID-III indices compared to female infants at the corrected age of two and a half years14. Another cohort study involving infants born at 24 to 33 weeks of gestation revealed that male infants were associated with a significantly higher risk of neurodevelopmental impairment at 2 years corrected age21. Male preterm infants are associated with increased risk of morbidities, especially with worse respiratory outcomes such as BPD30. BPD is a well-known important risk factor for unfavorable neurodevelopmental outcome9. The incidence of BPD was higher among male preterm infants enrolled in this study, which could have influenced the different neurodevelopment outcomes between male and female infants.

In this study, a consistent decrease was observed in the rate of neurodevelopmental delay as GA increased, regardless of sex, aligning with findings from previous studies3–8. However, the rates of neurodevelopmental delay in infants with extremely low GA in the present study were higher than that of other studies. In the Australian and New Zealand Neonatal Network, the rates of developmental delay in cognition, language, and motor skills were 22.3%, 34.8%, and 25.1%, respectively, for infants born below 25 weeks of gestation8. In contrast, this study found that the rates of developmental delay in cognition, language, and motor skills were 26.5%, 45.7%, and 36.4%, respectively, for infants born below 25 weeks of gestation. The higher rate of neurodevelopmental delay in infants born below 25 weeks of gestation in the present study may result in the diminished sex effect on neurodevelopmental delay in this GA group.

In contrast to previous studies, the present study conducted a subgroup analysis according to GA to explore the neurological outcomes of VLBW preterm infants. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to perform subgroup analysis according to GA in VLBW preterm infants under 32 weeks of gestation. Interestingly, extremely preterm infants born below 25 weeks of gestation showed no statistically significant sex differences in risks of neurodevelopmental delay. This finding is in contrast with the study by Wood et al., where male infants born below 25 weeks of gestation had a significantly higher rate of motor developmental delay at 30 months corrected age (male: 14% vs. female: 7%, P < 0.001)31. In the present study, both male and female infants in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group exhibited higher rates of motor developmental delay (male: 41.2% vs. female: 31.5%, P = 0.082) than in the study by Wood et al. The high rate of motor development delay in both male and female infants in this study unlike that of Wood’s study, could be an explanation for the diminishing sex effect on neurodevelopmental outcomes of the extremely preterm infants in this study. In the study by Hintz et al., investigating the association of perinatal factors and sex on neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm infants born under 28 weeks of gestation, the risk of motor developmental delay was significantly higher in male infants. However, when an interaction term for sex with IVH grade ≥ 3 and PVL was included in the regression model, the difference in risks of motor developmental delay between sexes disappeared19. In the present study, the rates of IVH grade ≥ 3 and PVL for both sexes in the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group were higher (male: 37.9% vs. female: 30.3%) than those in the other GA groups. This could provide another explanation for the lack of significant differences in motor developmental delay between sexes in the extremely low GA group.

Male infants in the 26+0 to 28+6 weeks of gestation group demonstrated lower composition scores and higher risks of delay in cognition and motor skills than those in the 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation group. The impact of sex on cognitive and motor development appeared to decrease as GA increased in infants born after 26 weeks of gestation. Conversely, male infants in the 29+0 to 31+6 weeks of gestation group had lower composition scores and higher risks of delay in language than those in the lower GA group. A similar finding was observed in a prospective study of extremely preterm infants born under 27 weeks of gestation, where male infants had lower cognition and language BSID-III scores than female infants, while motor skill scores were not significantly different at 30 months corrected age20. The study also examined the associations of sex differences with neonatal brain morphology by conducting an MRI at term equivalent age. Delayed myelination was more common in male infants, while cerebellar abnormalities were more frequent among female infants. However, MRI findings were not significantly associated with the BSID-III scores, and the study failed to explain the sex difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes by brain morphology on MRI. The present study demonstrated that the risk of language delay increased as GA increased in VLBW preterm infants born over 26 weeks of gestation, but it is currently challenging to provide a clear explanation for this trend. The rate of language delay was highest among the three developmental composites in the current study. A possible explanation includes overdiagnosis of language delay because language development tends to not be fully established at this age. Additional studies evaluating the effect of sex on language development through BSID-III at three years of age according to the follow-up schedule of KNN are essential.

In this study, there were no differences in effect of sex on secondary outcomes, including visual impairment, hearing impairment, and CP. The rates of visual and hearing impairment were low across all GA groups, consistent with findings in other studies19,25. However, the rate of CP was relatively high in both sexes of the 23+0 to 25+6 weeks of gestation group (male: 17.2%; female: 13.9%, respectively), while it decreased remarkably in infants over 26 weeks of gestation.

This study found that the effect of sex on neurodevelopmental delay in VLBWIs differs depending on GA on a nationwide scale. However, there is difficulty in understanding the mechanisms of the interaction between sex and GA on each developmental composite. Further studies using advanced MRI or electroencephalography are needed to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

The primary limitation of this study is the inclusion of only 1,829 (18.5%) infants assessed by BSID-III at 18–24 months corrected age. Especially, the sample size for secondary outcomes may have been insufficient for analysis. The low rate of infants assessed by BSID-III at this age may be associated with the overall low follow-up rate of the KNN (30.7%). Also, infants who completed BSID-III assessments in this study exhibited lower GA, lower birth weight and higher frequency of neonatal morbidities compared with those without BSID-III scores. This difference could have had an impact on the higher rates of neurodevelopmental delay in infants with extremely low GA in this study compared with that of other studies8. However, recent efforts have been made to increase follow-up rates, such as increased efforts of coordinators to recruit more infants. This study’s population may not fully represent all VLBW preterm infants in South Korea. Despite this limitation, this study was not deficient to evaluate the effects of sex and GA on neurodevelopmental delay, given the inclusion of a large number of study infants (more than 1,800 VLBW preterm infants) and statistical adjustments for other factors.

The strength of this study is on the coverage of a large geographical cohort that includes approximately 80% of VLBWIs in Korea. Despite the retrospective nature of this study, data collection was conducted prospectively, adhering to the stringent guidelines of the KNN. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the effect of GA on the risk associated with male sex for neurological outcomes in VLBW preterm infants born under 32 weeks of gestation.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight male sex as an independent risk factor for neurodevelopmental delay in VLBW preterm infants at 18–24 months corrected age, particularly in infants born over 26 weeks of gestation. Interestingly, the extremely low GA group (below 25 weeks of gestation) did not exhibit sex differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes. Among VLBW preterm infants born over 26 weeks of gestation, male infants of the younger GA group were associated with a higher risk of cognitive and motor skill delay than those of the older GA group. However, for language delay, the effect of sex was observed to increase as GA increased in VLBW preterm infants born over 26 weeks of gestation. There were no sex differences in visual impairment, hearing impairment, and CP across all gestation groups. These results can serve as valuable references for clinicians and contribute to counseling parents.

Methods

Study population

This population-based study with data retrieved from KNN, focuses on VLBW infants born at 23+0 and 31+6 weeks of gestation between January 2013 and December 2019. The inclusion criteria comprised VLBW preterm infants assessed using the BSID-III at 18–24 months corrected age, while infants with major congenital anomalies were excluded. The VLBW preterm infants were categorized by sex and further stratified into three GA groups: 23+0 to 25+6 weeks, 26+0 to 28+6 weeks, and 29+0 to 31+6 weeks. The KNN is a nationwide database, encompassing information from 78 hospitals across South Korea, and accounts for the data of approximately 80% of VLBW infants in the country. Data were collected at each participating center and entered into a data program. Study variables were defined in accordance with the KNN manual. The present study was approved by the KNN data management committee.

Data collection

Infant and maternal data were extracted from the KNN database. Encompassing information such as sex, GA, birth weight, head circumference at birth and discharge, delivery mode, and outborn status. Additionally, the length of hospital stay and the duration of mechanical ventilation were considered. Various major neonatal morbidities were assessed for each sex, including BPD, IVH grade ≥ 3, PVL, sepsis, and NEC stage ≥ 2. Maternal data collected included factors such as multiple births, diabetes, hypertension, histological chorioamnionitis, and the use of antenatal steroids. Social economic factors, including maternal education level, mother’s country of origin, infant’s attendance at daycare centers, and the primary caretakers of the infant, were also considered. For the classification of maternal education level, two groups were established based on graduation from college. Similarly, the mother’s country of origin was categorized into two groups according to origin from South Korea or other country. The primary caretakers of the infant were divided into two groups based on whether the biological parents assumed the primary caretaker role or not.

Neurological outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study focused on the BSID-III composite scores, particularly in the domains of cognition, language, and motor skills. Neurodevelopmental delay was defined as BSID-III composite scores lower than 80. According to previous studies that compared scores between BSID-II and Bayley-III, defining developmental delay as < 80 in BSID-III showed better predictive power32,33. Secondary outcomes were visual impairment, hearing impairment, and CP.

Definitions

BPD was defined as the requirement for supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks of postmenstrual age34. IVH was determined as the use of Papile’s criteria through cranial ultrasonography35. Given that imaging protocols varied across hospitals, the most severe grade identified in the cranial ultrasonography results during hospitalization was adopted. PVL specifically included cystic PVL, identified through cranial ultrasonography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) conducted during hospitalization. The criteria for sepsis required confirmation through positive blood culture results. For NEC, this was defined according to Bell’s criteria (stage 2 or higher)36.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics, version 29.0.1.0 (IBM, New York, NY, USA). Data were presented as number (%), mean (standard deviation), and median (interquartile range). Linear coefficients and odds ratios (OR) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Univariate analyses of categorical variables were preformed using chi-square test while t-test was utilized for continuous variables. Adjusted sex differences related to clinical and socio-economic factors were examined using multiple linear and logistic regression analyses. Criteria for entry and removal were set at P < 0.05 and P > 0.10, respectively. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit-test37. A P-value < 0.05 with two-tailed comparisons was considered significant.
