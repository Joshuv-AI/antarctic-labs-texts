Machine learning-driven virtual biopsy system may increase organ discards at aggressive kidney transplant centers
Authors: Emmanouil Giorgakis, Hailey Hardgrave, Nicholas Callais, Allison Wells
Published: 2024

arising from D. Yoo et al. Nature Communications 10.1038/s41467-023-44595-z (2024)

Over the recent years, organ shortage has resulted in expansion of kidney allograft acceptance criteria, including the use of organs from donors with high Kidney Donor Profile Index, high terminal creatinine, fibrin thrombi, metabolic disease, cardiovascular comorbidities or older age, with acceptable outcomes1–5. It has become increasingly common to review a preimplantation procurement biopsy, particularly on expanded criteria donors, to better characterize the deceased donor organ quality, optimize donor-recipient match, and obtain baseline kidney allograft histopathological characteristics should a post-transplant biopsy be needed.

Recently, concerns have been raised about inefficiencies and downstream sequelae of preimplantation biopsies: biopsies may be inadequate or result in a complication; they should be interpreted by trained nephropathologists and in a timely fashion, something often non-feasible6. Biopsy misinterpretation may lead to unnecessary organ discards. Indeed, reportedly thousands of deceased-donor kidneys are annually discarded in the United States (US) due to unacceptable procurement biopsy reads7,8. What is more, reproducibility of preimplantation biopsy findings is often poor7. On an international multi-center study, the authors concluded that preimplantation kidney donor histology assessment did not add value to the organ allocation process but resulted in kidney discards of potentially usable organs8. Also, preimplantation biopsy is costly and time-consuming. The precious time wasted awaiting the result, prolongs cold ischemia time. Accumulated cold ischemia time is associated with worse outcome9, increased risk of organ decline by the transplant center, and ultimate non-use10.

Yet, there is still an unmet need for a reliable method to assess the presence and severity of pertinent histopathological findings of the deceased donor kidney. The development of a machine learning tool employing basic donor variables may eliminate the inherent costs, delays, complication risk and interpretation bias11. On this basis, a Machine-Learning Virtual Biopsy (VBx) model was published recently at Nature Communications11. Yoo et al. used basic donor parameters to predict four Banff kidney lesions routinely used in deceased donor kidney transplant decision-making, namely arteriosclerosis (AS), arteriolar hyalinosis (AH), interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA), and percentile glomerular sclerosis (GS%)11,12. The model developed by the authors showed good performance on internal and external validation.

We endeavored to test this VBx model at an aggressive kidney transplant center in the United States.

Methods

Study period was January 1st, 2022–December 31st, 2023. After obtaining institutional ethics approval, we retrieved basic donor characteristics and preimplantation (“day zero”) biopsies (D0Bx) of consecutive deceased donor kidney transplants performed in a single academic transplant institution (University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences, UAMS). All data were anonymized. Cases with no preimplantation biopsy or missing demographic data demanded by the algorithm were excluded. Donor demographic data and preimplantation biopsy reports were retrieved utilizing the UNetSM online platform, provided by the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network system, a property of United Network for Organ Sharing13. Banff lesion severity predictions (AS, AH, IFTA, and GS%) were generated using the online application14. Predictions were then paired with their controls. Histopathological findings (“Banff lesions”) were classified as positive [(+); moderate/severe] or negative [(–); none/mild]. For each category, a confusion matrix was created to calculate specificity and eventually sensitivity. The true positive rates and false positive rates were calculated and evaluated for each measure of severity. In order to determine how well the model preformed at different classification thresholds, an area under the receiver operating characteristic analysis (AUROC) was done, using the virtual predicted probabilities for severity and the true classifications.

In our study, on paired comparison, the VBx model exhibited high false positive rates. 43/231 (18.6%) for IFTA; 50/231 (21.6%) for AS; 62/231 (26.8%) for AH, see Table 1. The model specificity for ruling out IFTA, AS, and AH, was 0.81, 0.78, and 0.72, respectively (Table 1). Sensitivity for detecting moderate/severe IFTA, AS and AH was 0.5, 0.38, and 0.67, respectively. Calculated Receiver Operative Characteristics was 0.54, 0.74, and 0.69 for moderate/ severe IFTA, AS, and AH, respectively (Table 1). GS% was also overestimated (t test, VBx vs D0Bx GS was 8.3% vs 3.4%, respectively, p < 0.001; Table 2). Notably, 51/231 (22.1%) of the cases had false positive VBx findings on 2 or more Banff categories.

Based on the latest Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients reports (SRTR), UAMS maintains higher national organ acceptance rates, while maintaining top-tier post-transplant survival15. This is achieved through diligent review of pertinent donor information, proper donor-recipient match, and careful preimplantation biopsy review, particularly on those offers considered as “extended criteria” or “aggressive”. On such offers, accurate biopsy interpretation is key. Our standard practice is to decline if there are 2 or more moderate/severe or a single severe Banff lesion. Notably, on our study cohort, had the preimplantation biopsy been replaced by a VBx, at least 51 transplanted organs (22.1%) would have been discarded based on predicted organ quality.

In conclusion, the authors agree that a VBx may eliminate the need of preimplantation biopsy on deceased donor kidney offers with low predicted risk of moderate/severe histopathological findings (specificity 0.72–0.81, Table 1). However, we caution that its application may erroneously result in an increase rather than decrease of organ discards, particularly if applied in certain organ acceptance environments. The developed VBx algorithm may therefore prove useful in identifying low-risk cases whereby a preimplantation biopsy can be omitted. However, if the VBx predicts positive moderate/severe histopathological findings, a preimplantation biopsy may still be warranted, to avoid the risk of unnecessary organ discards.

Limitations of this study have been its single center and retrospective nature; inherent selection bias (true positive moderate/severe Banff lesions likely would have been higher if discarded organs had been included); and the decentralized interpretation of the preimplantation biopsies. Finally, according to the SRTR15, the transplant center where the study was based has been ranked above national average on utilizing extended criteria organs; as such, our observations may not reflect the VBx system performance if applied in a different setting.
