The African Initiative for Bioinformatics Online Training in Neurodegenerative Diseases (AI‐BOND): Investing in the next generation of African neuroscientists
Authors: Bernard Fongang, Biniyam A Ayele, Yannick N Wadop, Emmanuel Epenge, Cyrille D Nkouonlack, Wepnyu Y Njamnshi, Xueqiu Jian, Murali Sargurupremraj, Alice B S Nono Djotsa, Paul F Seke Etet, Rebecca Bernal, Abdon Atangana, Jose E Cavazos, Jayandra Jung Himali, Alfred N Fonteh, Gladys Maestre, Alfred K Njamnshi, Sudha Seshadri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer's disease and AD‐related dementias (AD/ADRD), pose significant challenges to health care systems globally, particularly in Africa. With the advances in medical technology and research capabilities, especially in next‐generation sequencing and imaging, vast amounts of data have been generated from AD/ADRD research. Given that the greatest increase in AD/ADRD prevalence is expected to occur in Africa, it is critical to establish comprehensive bioinformatics training programs to help African scientists leverage existing data and collect additional information to untangle AD/ADRD heterogeneity in African populations. The South Texas Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, with efforts from the National Institutes of Health and the Global Brain Health Institute, has partnered with the Brain Research Africa Initiative to develop the African Initiative on Bioinformatics Online Training in Neurodegenerative Disease (AI‐BOND). AI‐BOND is a comprehensive and accessible training program, the aim of which is to advance biostatistics and bioinformatics expertise in Africa in studying neurodegenerative diseases. This expertise is essential to enable African scientists to utilize the extensive AD/ADRD data and enhance the continent's ability to contribute to global research efforts in this field. The training addresses the gap in analyzing neurodegenerative disease data by providing skills and knowledge in genetic epidemiology, biostatistics, and bioinformatics to African students and researchers. This innovative online training program will last 6 months and provide training in skill sets R, SAS, and Python programing, genome‐wide association studies, genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, microbiome analysis, and advanced statistical methods. Additional training will include study design and manuscript and grant writing. The first cohort of the AI‐BOND program will graduate in June 2024. INTRODUCTION

Neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) and AD‐related dementias (AD/ADRD), present a significant challenge globally, affecting millions of individuals and their families. 1 , 2 Although AD/ADRD has been recognized as a leading cause of death in many countries, its burden is particularly pronounced in low‐ and middle‐income countries due to their aging populations and limited health care resources. 3 Africa, with its rapidly growing elderly population and diverse health care landscape, faces unique challenges in addressing the impact of AD/ADRD, which is increasingly recognized as a public health priority. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Indeed, the prevalence of AD/ADRD is expected to rise substantially due to several factors including increasing life expectancy, changing demographics, and lifestyle changes associated with urbanization and globalization. 8 In addition, the lack of awareness, the stigma surrounding brain health, and limited access to health care services further exacerbate the challenges of diagnosing and managing AD/ADRD in many African countries. 3 Furthermore, Africa harbors more human genetic diversity, and yet Africa is underrepresented in studies exploring neurodegenerative disorders. 4 , 9 Therefore, addressing the challenges posed by AD/ADRD in Africa requires a multifaceted approach that encompasses public health initiatives, health care infrastructure development, and building strong research capacities.

Although several research initiatives have been developed to collect AD/ADRD data in Africa, the processing and analysis of these data have been conducted mainly out of the continent, thus limiting the dissemination of the results in Africa and the beneficial outcomes on African populations. Therefore, it is necessary for African scientists to improve their skills and knowledge in AD/ADRD data collection, processing, and analysis in Africa using cutting‐edge technologies and state‐of‐the‐art statistical methods. 4 Addressing the gap requires significant health infrastructure investment, designing large databases, and building computational research capacities to utilize the vast clinical, genomic, biomarker, and neuroimaging data generated.

Indeed, building computational research capacities is a crucial aspect of addressing the challenges of dementia in Africa. Computational research, including bioinformatics, data science, and artificial intelligence, plays a significant role in understanding the underlying mechanisms of neurodegenerative disorders, identifying potential biomarkers, developing predictive models, and exploring novel therapeutic interventions. In the context of AD/ADRD, computational researchers can help analyze large data sets, including genomic, proteomic, and neuroimaging data, to uncover patterns, associations, and potential targets for intervention. By leveraging computational techniques such as machine learning and network analysis, researchers can gain insight into the complex interactions underlying dementia pathophysiology, thereby facilitating the discovery of new diagnostic tools and therapeutic approaches for heterogeneous populations such as those of Africa.

THE AFRICAN INITIATIVE FOR BIOINFORMATICS ONLINE TRAINING IN NEURODEGENERATIVE DISEASES (AI‐BOND)

To address the gap in building computational research capacities in neurodegenerative diseases in Africa, the South Texas Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (STAC), with efforts from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Global Brain Health Institute (GBHI), has partnered with the Brain Research Africa Initiative (BRAIN) 10 to develop a free and comprehensive training program named “African Initiative for Bioinformatics Online training in Neurodegenerative Disease (AI‐BOND).” AI‐BOND aims at training the next generation of African scientists, especially neuroscientists, in study design, cutting‐edge techniques in data analysis, and manuscript and grant writing to build a computational research workforce that will complement the global effort of “Building AD/ADRD Research Capacity in Africa.” The AI‐BOND program addresses this issue by providing accessible and high‐quality online training in genetic epidemiology, biostatistics, and bioinformatics, focusing explicitly on neurodegenerative diseases. The program has brought together a multidisciplinary team of experts—including bioinformaticians, neuroscientists, educators, and mentors—to develop and deliver a curated curriculum combining theoretical knowledge and practical skills (Figure 1).

AI‐BOND training modules. AI‐BOND aims to provide comprehensive training in cutting‐edge programming, genetic epidemiology, and multi‐omics methods applied to neurodegenerative diseases. AI‐BOND, African Initiative for Bioinformatics Online Training in Neurodegenerative Diseases.

This 6‐month online training program aims to bridge the bioinformatics knowledge and skill gap in neurodegenerative diseases, targeting students, neuroscientists, biologists, and clinicians across Africa. The AI‐BOND program is designed to equip African scientists with the necessary bioinformatics tools for cutting‐edge research on neurodegenerative diseases. It fosters a sense of community among researchers and promotes networking opportunities, essential for addressing the challenges posed by these diseases. The program is tailored to the African context, incorporating region‐specific case studies and examples to ensure relevance and applicability to the unique health care landscape of Africa.

Furthermore, AI‐BOND is offered as a free online training program to eliminate financial barriers and accommodate diverse participants, including students, early‐career researchers, and seasoned clinicians. By providing access to bioinformatics resources and tools, including high‐performance computing (HPC) systems, the program aims to enable African scientists to analyze complex biological data and extract valuable insights related to neurodegenerative diseases. 11 The AI‐BOND initiative is a significant step toward enhancing research capacity and addressing the specific challenges of African scientists in addressing neurodegenerative diseases. The program's focus on bioinformatics training tailored to the African context holds promise for advancing research and understanding these complex conditions in the region.

AI‐BOND is designed to help applicants without computational skills to perform large‐scale data analysis focused on AD/ADRD and other neurodegenerative diseases. It covers several topics divided into four modules (Figure 1). The first module, programming, introduces the basics of programming, including the R, Python, and SAS programming languages, the Linux environment, and basic shell scripting. The second module introduces the biology of neurodegenerative diseases, current research, and available data sets to help develop projects and preliminary data for grant submissions, including the National Center for Biotechnology Information – Gene Expression Omnibus, The database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP), The Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine, The Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, and The Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology. We also explain how to access the data and proceed with quality control, visualization, and statistical approaches. The third module, genetic epidemiology, is dedicated to analyzing genetic data using genome‐wide association studies (GWAS), whole‐genome sequencing (GWS), whole‐exome sequencing (WES), and post‐GWAS analysis using different techniques, including Mendelian randomization. Finally, the fourth module, Integrative Omics, covers transcriptomic analysis (microarray, RNA‐Seq, scRNA‐seq), epigenomics, metabolomics, microbiome, and integrative multi‐omics methods. AI‐BOND also provides research article and grant writing training to help trainees develop projects leading to publication and serve as preliminary data for grant applications.

Recruiting the first AI‐BOND cohort

The online enrollment process for the first AI‐BOND cohort started on September 1, 2023, and lasted for 30 days. We received 173 applications from 22 African countries through the dedicated BRAIN web‐based platform (www.brainafrica.org/bioinformatics_training/) during this period. Cameroon and Nigeria had the highest number of candidates (Figure 2A,C). The high number of applications in a relatively short period underscores the need for such programs in Africa. The applicants had diverse educational levels, including PhD students, medical doctors, and senior scientists (Figure 2B). Of the 173 applicants, 34.1% were female. Nearly 70% of the applicants did not have prior training in bioinformatics. Finding a sustainable solution to the significant lack of training in bioinformatics among researchers in the field of neuroscience in Africa is the main driving force behind AI‐BOND, aiming to close this knowledge and skill gap. This force has been propelled by some fertile ground and opportunities that favor the present training program. These include the availability of high‐speed computer access for almost all applicants and a stable internet connection. Furthermore, nearly two‐thirds of the applicants have an ongoing research project that would benefit from bioinformatics training. Hence, the AI‐BOND aims to utilize available resources, enhancing the bioinformatics skills and knowledge of African researchers and health care professionals.

We established a fair review committee in which members did not review candidates from their own country of origin. We reviewed all applications, assessing scientific background, research capabilities, and willingness to complete a 6‐month training program. Our selection criteria focused on three main areas: (1) candidate background—including their educational levels, research experience, and computing abilities; (2) candidate research activity—including their involvement in research, particularly in neuroscience, and their willingness to conduct research on neurodegenerative diseases in Africa; and (3) candidate ability to complete the training—including their availability, access to computers, and high‐speed internet.

After reviewing the applications, 70 applicants were invited to a webinar highlighting the gaps in AD/ADRD research in Africa and the importance of developing computational infrastructure. The webinar featured leadership of the partner institutions: STAC's Drs. Sudha Seshadri and Gladys Maestre, and BRAIN's CEO, Prof. Alfred K. Njamnshi. The webinar was followed by a formal interview of the 70 candidates, from which four interview committees selected 41 applicants to form the first AI‐BOND cohort.

Challenges in the recruitment and implementation phases

The primary challenge is that this free online training program for young African scientists operates without funding from individuals or organizations, relying entirely on volunteer faculty members. Consequently, organizing, monitoring, and following up on the 6‐month online training program has significantly burdened the already busy schedules of the faculty. Furthermore, because the training program is in English, it is difficult for trainees from predominantly French‐speaking African countries to take advantage of this training opportunity, despite no language being listed in the exclusion criteria. Other challenges are related to the significant time zone differences between the trainees and the majority of the faculties, who are based in the United States. In addition, there is a lack of laptops for trainees, as well as low internet speeds and interruptions in internet coverage in most of the countries where the trainees are located. To address the small number of female participants and increase the representation of all countries in sub‐Saharan Africa, the registration period for the second cohort of trainees has been extended to 2 months. The training program and the opportunities were presented by the AI‐BOND course director at the 2024 Alzheimer's Association Symposium in Cape Town, South Africa.

The 2023 AI‐BOND training, faculty, completion, and future recruitments

The online training program commenced on December 4, 2023, with 41 trainees. The graduation is scheduled for June 30, 2024 (Figure 2A). The program (at the time of writing this paper) consists of weekly activities that revolve around the training modules and a 2‐hour online lecture scheduled for every Wednesday. AI‐BOND has developed the “Learn and Apply” concept, which involves developing projects that could lead to manuscripts and grants based on the learning modules. The program's success will be measured through three metrics: (1) the number of candidates that complete the program, (2) the number of manuscripts submitted after the training, and (3) the number of grants submitted using the preliminary data generated during the training.

The AI‐BOND Faculty consists of diverse professionals with expertise in computer programing, neurodegenerative diseases, and research/scientific projects. This diverse group of professionals ensures that applicants undergo comprehensive training that will empower them to develop projects for publications or grant applications.

AI‐BOND is intended to be an annual training program with enrollment windows between May and August for an effective training start in November. After the current cohort, we will additionally use a multifaceted approach to advertise the program. First, leveraging digital platforms such as social media, websites, and email newsletters. Then, we will use targeted advertising on platforms such as Facebook and LinkedIn and collaborate with academic institutions and research organizations across Africa to extend the program's reach through their networks and communication channels. We will also utilize traditional advertising methods like posters and flyers, especially in areas with limited internet access. Finally, because word‐of‐mouth remains a powerful tool in African communities, we will encourage current participants and alumni to spread the word to boost enrollment and make the program as accessible as possible. At the mid‐training period, we held individual discussions with each trainee to gather their feedback on the training program. The majority of the feedback was positive, highlighting the comprehensiveness of the course content, the engaging faculty, and the wide coverage of relevant topics. However, some trainees mentioned challenges, such as the need for a strong understanding of programming languages such as R and Python, as well as basic knowledge of the Linux operating system. These requirements made it difficult for some trainees to keep up with the rest of the group.

The partnership between AI‐BOND and African research institutions is strong. One of the AI‐BOND advisory group members, Prof. Alfred K. Njamnshi, is the CEO of the BRAIN in Cameroon. In addition, two faculty members from African universities are also part of the AI‐BOND team. Despite the lack of publicly available ADRD data from Africa, AI‐BOND faculty and advisory group members contributed data sets on the neurocognition of HIV and epilepsy patients from their respective cohorts for the trainees' project development.

DISCUSSION

Due to Africa's vast genetic diversity and high prevalence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk factors, it is anticipated that future and ongoing research projects in the field of neurodegenerative diseases will yield large amounts of genetic, multi‐omics, and biomarker data. To fully exploit the data and capture the heterogeneity of the disease in the continent, it is imperative to develop a computationally skilled workforce in Africa to process and analyze these data. 3 , 6 , 7 AI‐BOND was designed to fill that gap and provide African scientists with expertise in basic programming, biostatistics, and bioinformatics applied to neurodegenerative diseases.

The AI‐BOND program aligns with the World Health Organization's (WHO) 2017 global action plan, highlighting the significance of strengthening dementia research and innovation worldwide. 7 It also supports WHO's recommendation in Africa that successful dementia research should be aligned with identified priorities and social as well as technological innovations. 7 , 12 This alignment would ultimately increase the likelihood of making adequate progress toward better prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and care for people with dementia in Africa. The primary goal of the AI‐BOND online training is to enhance the knowledge and build the capacity of African neuroscientists in large‐scale data analysis—such as genetics and multi‐omics—of individuals with neurodegenerative disorders. 12 , 13

Finally, AI‐BOND has introduced the “Learn and Apply” concept, which is a crucial motto for any training that requires the practical application of theoretical concepts learned in the classroom environment. Trainees will work closely with faculty members throughout the 6‐month training period to develop research projects based on secondary data, faculty data, or their own data. These projects will lead to research articles publishable in reputable international journals and will serve as preliminary results for grant applications.

The main challenges in AI‐BOND

The significance of interdisciplinary and cost‐effective training programs such as AI‐BOND in resource‐limited settings, particularly in Africa, is immense and cannot be overstated. The high number of applicants to AI‐BOND suggests that the program meets a real and felt need. Such programs play a vital role in developing capacities, bridging skill gaps, and empowering individuals to contribute meaningfully to their research communities. As the world becomes increasingly interconnected, initiatives like AI‐BOND will serve as beacons of hope, paving the way for a brighter, more sustainable future for neuroscience research in resource‐limited regions.
