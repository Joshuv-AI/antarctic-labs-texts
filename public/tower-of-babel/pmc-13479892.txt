Tobacco intercropping with Allium crops reshapes soil microbiome–metabolome interactions and improves plant health and tobacco leaf quality
Authors: Chaojun Shi, Honglin Zhang, Yao Liu, Guoqin Zhou, Yinbiao Zhou, Yuqiu Yan, Xinyi Huang, Xiufang Yang, Lijie Chen, Fen Lü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntercropping systems play important roles in improving soil conditions, enhancing crop performance and suppressing soil-borne diseases. However, the mechanisms by which tobacco–Allium intercropping regulates soil microecology, tobacco growth and leaf quality remain insufficiently understood. In this study, tobacco monoculture and tobacco intercropping systems with onion, garlic or Chinese chive were established to evaluate tobacco agronomic traits, disease occurrence, cured leaf chemical composition and defence-related enzyme activities. Soil physicochemical properties, microbial community structure and metabolomic profiles were further analysed and integrated using a multi-omics approach.ResultsCompared with tobacco monoculture, the tobacco–garlic intercropping treatment (CG) showed the most pronounced effects. CG alleviated soil acidification and increased the contents of available phosphorus and available potassium. The incidence and disease index of tobacco mosaic virus disease (TMVD) were significantly reduced by 27.91% and 31.77%, respectively. CG also significantly improved tobacco agronomic traits and enhanced the activities of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) by 146.50%, 162.65% and 248.87%, respectively. In cured tobacco leaves, total nitrogen, nicotine and reducing sugar contents increased by 20.57%, 28.22% and 10.49%, respectively. In addition, CG significantly increased potassium content and improved the potassium-to-chloride ratio by 55.46%. Correlation analysis showed that intercropping promoted the enrichment of beneficial bacterial genera, including Gemmatimonas and Sphingomonas, and enhanced functional pathways associated with linoleic acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and the biosynthesis of antibiotics and other secondary metabolites.ConclusionsTobacco–Allium intercropping, especially tobacco–garlic intercropping, improved tobacco growth and cured leaf chemical quality and reduced field TMVD occurrence. Background

Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) is an important economic crop whose leaves constitute the primary harvested product, and plant growth status directly determines both yield and leaf quality [1]. Tobacco is susceptible to infection by diverse viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens. Among these diseases, tobacco mosaic virus disease, black shank and bacterial wilt are particularly destructive, causing substantial reductions in yield and quality [2, 3]. These diseases not only impair plant physiological functions and reduce biomass accumulation, but also aggravate lesion development during curing, thereby decreasing the industrial usability of cured tobacco leaves [2, 3]. Current disease management strategies include crop rotation, resistant cultivars, chemical control and biological control [4]. However, chemical pesticides remain widely used in tobacco production, leading to concerns regarding pesticide residues, pathogen resistance and ecological risks [5, 6]. With increasing requirements for tobacco leaf quality and safety, there is an urgent need to develop environmentally friendly and efficient strategies for disease management.

The occurrence of diseases in flue-cured tobacco is jointly driven by multiple factors, including soil moisture, temperature, pH, nutrient availability, microbial community composition and cropping systems [7–9]. The soil environment not only regulates the physiological status of tobacco plants, but also affects the survival, proliferation and transmission of pathogens and antagonistic microorganisms [10]. In addition, several plant viruses can persist or be maintained through plant residues, alternative hosts or vectors, including Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) [11], Tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) [12] and Potato virus Y (PVY) [13]. Therefore, optimizing the soil environment and cropping practices is a key strategy for reducing disease occurrence. Intercropping can significantly increase soil microbial diversity, suppress pathogen spread and reduce the incidence of various crop diseases [14–16]. In flue-cured tobacco production systems, intercropping with companion plants has been reported to improve rhizosphere soil chemical properties and microbial community structure, which may contribute to improved soil health and disease suppression [17]. Similar disease-suppressive effects of intercropping have also been observed in other crop systems [18]. These effects are mainly attributed to the mitigation of continuous cropping obstacles, promotion of nutrient cycling and reduction in autotoxin accumulation, thereby improving tobacco leaf quality and disease resistance [19, 20].

Intercropping has been shown to increase the abundance of rhizosphere bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes, enhance microbial diversity and promote the enrichment of beneficial microbial taxa. Soil microorganisms play central roles in maintaining soil health and suppressing plant diseases [21–23]. Root exudates are key drivers of rhizosphere microecology because they can regulate microbial community composition, soil enzyme activities and pathogen-suppressive capacity [24, 25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that root exudates from various plant species can directly inhibit soil-borne pathogens [26]. By increasing the diversity and quantity of root exudates, intercropping may further enhance the disease-suppressive potential of soil. The rhizosphere microecological system influences plant growth and resistance through the coordinated effects of root exudates, soil enzymes and microbial communities. It can also improve rhizosphere enzyme activities, promote nutrient transformation, enrich plant growth-promoting microorganisms, reduce the accumulation of root-derived autotoxins, improve soil quality and ultimately promote plant growth [27–29].

Biodiversity acts as an important ecological barrier against plant diseases, and biodiversity-based disease management has become an important research focus [30]. Intercropping, defined as the simultaneous cultivation of two or more crop species in the same field during the same growing season, can increase agroecosystem biodiversity and improve crop stress tolerance. It can also reduce the occurrence of diseases and insect pests by modifying the field microclimate, such as improving ventilation, providing shading and reducing canopy temperature, thereby exerting ecological pest- and disease-suppressive effects. Previous studies have shown that intercropping improves resource-use efficiency, enhances disease resistance and increases yield per unit area [31]. In addition, intercropping can increase rhizosphere microbial biomass and diversity, promote nutrient cycling, enhance stress resistance, and improve soil structure and physicochemical properties [32, 33]. Tobacco leaf quality is highly dependent on soil fertility; however, long-term excessive application of chemical fertilizers has led to declines in soil organic matter and nutrient imbalances, thereby constraining the sustainable production of flue-cured tobacco [34]. Studies have shown that intercropping can improve soil structure and physicochemical properties, increase soil nutrient availability and enhance soil enzyme activities [35].

In tobacco cultivation, intercropping can optimize the soil environment and improve tobacco leaf quality and yield. For example, flue-cured tobacco–maize and flue-cured tobacco–soybean intercropping systems have been reported to increase yield and production efficiency [35]. In addition, Allium crops exhibit substantial disease-suppressive potential in intercropping or rotation systems. Their volatile compounds and root exudates can inhibit a range of soil-borne pathogens, thereby enhancing crop health [36]. Garlic (Allium sativum L.) has attracted increasing attention because of its pronounced antimicrobial and allelopathic activities, and garlic extracts have been shown to suppress crop diseases and provide protective effects against pathogen infection [37].

Although previous studies have shown that intercropping can improve tobacco production and that Allium crops have disease-suppressive potential, the mechanisms by which tobacco–Allium intercropping regulates rhizosphere soil properties, microbial community structure, soil metabolites, tobacco disease occurrence and cured leaf quality remain unclear. In particular, it is still uncertain whether the reduced field occurrence of tobacco mosaic virus disease (TMVD) under Allium intercropping is associated with changes in rhizosphere microbiome–metabolome interactions or with other ecological effects of intercropping. We hypothesized that tobacco–Allium intercropping, especially tobacco–garlic intercropping, would improve soil physicochemical conditions, reshape rhizosphere microbial communities and metabolite profiles, and that these changes would be associated with improved tobacco growth, cured leaf chemical quality and reduced field TMVD occurrence. To test this hypothesis, this study established tobacco monoculture and tobacco intercropping systems with garlic, onion and Chinese chive, and integrated soil physicochemical analysis, microbial community profiling, rhizosphere metabolomics, defence-related enzyme assays, disease assessment and tobacco agronomic and quality measurements. The results are expected to provide a theoretical basis for ecological tobacco cultivation and sustainable disease management.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and site description

The field experiment was conducted from April to October 2024 in Fenghe Town, Xundian Hui and Yi Autonomous County, Kunming, Yunnan Province, China (25.81958°N, 103.01977°E). The experimental soil was a sandy loam that had been continuously cropped with flue-cured tobacco for five consecutive years. The soil was slightly acidic, with a pH of 6.32, alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen of 156.21 mg kg⁻¹, organic matter of 123.65 g kg⁻¹, available potassium of 59.07 mg kg⁻¹ and available phosphorus of 148.40 mg kg⁻¹.

The flue-cured tobacco cultivar used in this study was Honghua Dajinyuan, which was provided by the local tobacco company in Xundian County, Yunnan Province, China. The intercrops included Chinese chive (Allium tuberosum Rottler ex Spreng., cv. Xuejiu No. 9), purple-skinned onion (Allium cepa L., cv. Ziyu 88) and multi-clove garlic (Allium sativum L., cv. Yunsuan No. 1). Tobacco-specific compound fertilizer (N–P₂O₅–K₂O, 15–8–25), potassium sulfate (52% K₂O) and potassium nitrate (N–P₂O₅–K₂O, 13.5–0–44.5) were applied. The total pure nitrogen application rate was 60 kg ha⁻¹, and all other field management practices followed local standard practices for flue-cured tobacco cultivation.

Four treatments were established: flue-cured tobacco monoculture (C), flue-cured tobacco–garlic intercropping (CG), flue-cured tobacco–onion intercropping (CO) and flue-cured tobacco–Chinese chive intercropping (CW). Each treatment was replicated three times, resulting in a total of 12 experimental plots. Each plot contained 60 tobacco plants arranged at a plant spacing of 55 cm and a row spacing of 120 cm, with an effective planting area of approximately 39.6 m² per plot. Guard rows were established around the experimental field.

Soil sampling

Before the establishment of the intercropping treatments with Chinese chive, onion and garlic, three composite baseline soil samples were randomly collected using the five-point sampling method. The samples were air-dried and used for the determination of initial soil physicochemical properties.

At the maturity stage of flue-cured tobacco, approximately 90 days after transplantation, five tobacco plants were randomly selected from each plot for rhizosphere soil collection. Rhizosphere soil was collected using the root-shaking method [38]. Briefly, surface weeds and loose topsoil were first removed, and the plants were carefully uprooted. After gently shaking off the loosely attached bulk soil, the soil tightly adhering to the roots was collected as rhizosphere soil. The rhizosphere soil samples from the five plants within each plot were thoroughly mixed to form one composite biological replicate. Thus, each treatment included three biological replicates, with each replicate consisting of a composite rhizosphere soil sample from five plants.

Each composite rhizosphere soil sample was divided into two subsamples. One subsample was placed in a sealed plastic bag, air-dried and used for the determination of soil physicochemical properties. The other subsample was transferred into cryogenic tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory and stored at − 80 °C until deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction, microbial community analysis and rhizosphere metabolomic analysis.

Disease assessment

The occurrence of tobacco mosaic virus disease (TMVD) was monitored in each plot using a systematic field survey method. TMVD occurrence in this study was evaluated under natural field infection conditions, and no artificial TMV inoculation was performed. Initial TMV titres in soil, plant residues or asymptomatic tobacco plants were not quantitatively determined before treatment establishment. To reduce the potential influence of spatial heterogeneity in natural inoculum pressure, the experiment was arranged using a randomized block design, and all plots had the same previous cropping history and were managed using the same agronomic practices. The number of diseased plants and the corresponding disease severity grades were recorded plot by plot. Beginning at the initial stage of disease occurrence, disease assessments were conducted once per week for six consecutive weeks. Disease severity was graded according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 23,222–2008, as previously applied in tobacco viral disease assessment [39]. Determination of defence-related enzyme activities in tobacco leaves

At the maturity stage of flue-cured tobacco, approximately 90 days after transplantation, three flue-cured tobacco plants were randomly selected from each plot. Three fully expanded mature middle leaves were collected from each plant, and leaves collected from the same plot were pooled to form one biological replicate. The midribs were removed, and approximately 0.2 g of fresh leaf tissue from each biological replicate was used for enzyme extraction. The leaf samples were wrapped in aluminium foil, labelled, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and transported to the laboratory. All samples were stored at − 80 °C until enzyme activity analysis.

Before measurement, the frozen leaf samples were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen. Enzyme extracts were prepared by homogenizing fresh leaf tissue with the extraction buffer supplied in the corresponding assay kits, followed by centrifugation at low temperature. The supernatant was collected for enzyme activity determination. The activities of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) were determined spectrophotometrically according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the assay kits provided by Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) and previously described methods [40].

Measurement of agronomic traits

Agronomic traits were measured after the final disease assessment. Ten flue-cured tobacco plants were randomly selected from each plot and used for the determination of agronomic traits, including plant height, stem circumference and leaf number. The measurements were performed according to the tobacco industry standard YC/T 142–2010, Methods for investigation and measurement of agronomic traits of tobacco, as described previously [41].

Measurement of cured tobacco leaf yield

After maturity, tobacco leaves were harvested and cured according to local standard curing practices. Cured leaf yield was recorded for each plot and then calculated for each treatment, as described previously [42].

Sampling and chemical analysis of cured tobacco leaves

After curing, 1 kg of C3F-grade cured tobacco leaves was collected from each plot. The midribs were removed, and the leaf samples were ground and passed through a 0.4-mm sieve for chemical composition analysis.

The contents of total sugar, reducing sugar, total nitrogen, nicotine and chloride in cured tobacco leaves were determined using an AA3 continuous-flow analyser (SEAL Analytical, Norderstedt, Germany) according to YC/T 159–2002, YC/T 161–2002, YC/T 160–2002 and YC/T 162–2002, respectively. Potassium content was determined using an AAnalyst 300 atomic absorption spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) according to YC/T 274–2008. The chemical composition of cured tobacco leaves was further evaluated according to the routine chemical quality requirements for high-quality flue-cured tobacco in Yunnan Province (Q/YZY 1–2009). The reference ranges for the main chemical quality indicators are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Determination of soil physicochemical properties

Soil physicochemical properties were determined using standard analytical methods. Soil pH was measured using the potentiometric method with a soil-to-water ratio of 1:2.5 [43]. Soil organic matter (SOM) content was determined by the potassium dichromate volumetric method [44]. Alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen (AN) was determined using the alkaline hydrolysis diffusion method [45]. Available potassium (AK) was extracted with ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc) and measured using flame photometry [46]. Available phosphorus (AP) was determined using the sodium bicarbonate extraction–molybdenum antimony colorimetric method [47].

Soil bacterial 16 S rRNA gene and fungal ITS amplicon sequencing

Soil microbial DNA was extracted from the rhizosphere soil samples described in Sect. 2.2 using the HiPure Soil DNA Kit (D3142; Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity of extracted genomic DNA was checked by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The DNA samples were then diluted to the required concentration for subsequent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and microbial community analysis.

For bacterial and fungal community analyses, the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene and fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region were amplified using target-specific primers containing unique barcodes according to the selected sequencing regions. PCR amplification was performed using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with a high-efficiency and high-fidelity DNA polymerase from New England Biolabs to ensure amplification efficiency and accuracy.

PCR products were purified using AMPure XP Beads and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. After the second-round amplification, the PCR products were further purified using AMPure XP Beads and quantified using an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, USA). Qualified amplicon libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the PE250 sequencing mode.

Raw sequencing reads were processed using USEARCH software. Low-quality reads were first filtered, and paired-end reads were then merged to generate tags. The merged tags were further quality-filtered to obtain clean tags. Based on the clean tags, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered using USEARCH, and chimeric tags detected during clustering were removed. Finally, OTU abundance tables and representative OTU sequences were generated for subsequent microbial community analyses.

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis of rhizosphere soil metabolites

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis of rhizosphere soil metabolites was performed using liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS). Metabolites were annotated by comparison with the mzCloud, mzVault and MassList databases.

Briefly, 100 mg of each frozen rhizosphere soil sample was thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen, and 500 µL of 80% aqueous methanol was added for metabolite extraction. The mixture was vortexed and incubated on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was diluted to a final methanol concentration of 53% and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant was collected for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis.

LC–MS analysis was performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The scan range was set to m/z 100–1500. The main mass spectrometry parameters were as follows: spray voltage, 3.5 kV; sheath gas flow rate, 35 arbitrary units (arb); auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C; and S-lens radio frequency (RF) level, 60. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode.

Raw LC–MS data were imported into Compound Discoverer 3.3 software for peak extraction, alignment and metabolite annotation. Peaks were filtered according to retention time and mass-to-charge ratio, and peak areas were corrected using quality control (QC) samples. Peak extraction and quantification were performed with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm and a signal intensity tolerance of 30%. Target ions were integrated, and molecular formulas were predicted based on molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. Metabolites were annotated by matching the predicted molecular formulas and MS/MS fragment information against the mzCloud, mzVault and MassList databases. Background ions were removed using blank samples. Metabolite abundance was normalized as follows: normalized abundance = raw abundance / (total abundance of the sample / total abundance of QC1). Compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 30% in the relative peak areas of QC samples were removed. The final dataset, including metabolite annotation information and relative quantitative abundances, was used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data organization and preliminary plotting were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0. All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Differences among treatments were analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Correlation analyses were also performed using SPSS 22.0.

For microbial community analysis, low-quality sequences were filtered, paired-end reads were merged, and clean tags were obtained as described above. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered using USEARCH after the removal of chimeric sequences. Representative OTU sequences were used for taxonomic annotation, and species abundance profiles were generated at different taxonomic levels. Based on the normalized OTU abundance table, microbial abundance and alpha diversity indices, including Sobs, Shannon and Chao1, were calculated. Community structure was further analysed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), which was performed and visualized using the ade4 package in R software.

Metabolomic data were analysed using the MetaboAnalystR package in R software. Metabolite annotation was performed by searching the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and LIPID MAPS databases. Data processing and downstream analyses were conducted in a Linux environment using R and Python.

Results

Comprehensive evaluation of tobacco plants and soil properties

Intercropping significantly reduced the incidence and disease index of TMVD (Table S3). Among the intercropping treatments, CG showed the strongest suppressive effect. At week 6, TMVD incidence and disease index in the CG treatment were significantly reduced by 27.91% and 31.77%, respectively, compared with the monoculture treatment.

Intercropping significantly altered soil physicochemical properties and alleviated soil acidification. Compared with monoculture, intercropping significantly increased AK and AP contents, while significantly decreasing SOM and AN contents (Fig. 1A). Among the treatments, CG showed the greatest decreases in AN and SOM, indicating that tobacco–garlic intercropping may promote nutrient uptake and utilization while increasing the consumption of available nitrogen in the soil.

Intercropping also improved tobacco agronomic traits and yield-related performance (Fig. 1B). The CG and CW treatments increased plant height and leaf number. Plant height increased by 6.10% and 7.64%, and leaf number increased by 10.27% and 9.64% under CG and CW, respectively, compared with monoculture. Cured leaf yield was recorded at the plot level and used as the main yield indicator in this study. Compared with monoculture, cured leaf yield increased by 4.67%, 1.63% and 0.64% under CG, CO and CW, respectively. Among the intercropping treatments, CG showed the highest cured leaf yield and the most favourable overall performance in terms of plant growth, TMVD reduction and cured leaf chemical composition, indicating that tobacco–garlic intercropping was the most effective intercropping system under the present field conditions.

Intercropping enhanced the activities of defence-related enzymes in tobacco leaves (Fig. 1B). The CG treatment showed the strongest effect, significantly increasing the activities of PAL, PPO and SOD by 146.50%, 162.65% and 248.87%, respectively.

Intercropping significantly improved the chemical composition of cured tobacco leaves. Among the treatments, CG showed the most favourable chemical composition balance. Compared with monoculture, CG significantly increased the contents of total nitrogen, nicotine and reducing sugar in cured tobacco leaves by 20.57%, 28.22% and 10.49%, respectively. CG also significantly increased potassium content and improved the potassium-to-chloride ratio by 55.46% (Fig. 1C).

Soil microbial diversity and community structure

Intercropping altered the alpha diversity of bacterial and fungal communities in tobacco rhizosphere soil compared with flue-cured tobacco monoculture (Table 1). In the bacterial community, the Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices were significantly increased under the CO and CW treatments (P < 0.05), indicating that tobacco–onion and tobacco–Chinese chive intercropping enhanced bacterial richness and diversity in the rhizosphere soil. In the fungal community, the CG treatment increased the Chao1 index and significantly increased the Shannon and Simpson indices (P < 0.05), suggesting that tobacco–garlic intercropping mainly enhanced fungal community diversity.

Intercropping significantly affected the bacterial and fungal community structures in tobacco rhizosphere soil (Supplementary Fig. S1). For the bacterial community, PCoA showed that PCoA1 and PCoA2 together explained 46.40% of the total variation. The intercropping treatments were separated from the monoculture treatment in the ordination space, and the different intercropping treatments were also separated from one another, indicating distinct bacterial community compositions among the different cropping systems (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

For the fungal community, PCoA1 and PCoA2 together explained 28.70% of the total variation. The intercropping treatments were separated from the monoculture treatment, indicating differences in fungal community composition between intercropping systems and monoculture. Partial overlap was observed between the CG and CW treatments, suggesting that the fungal community compositions under tobacco–garlic and tobacco–Chinese chive intercropping were relatively similar (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Soil microbial community composition and network structure analysis

Intercropping reshaped the composition of soil microbial communities. In the bacterial community, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Chloroflexota, Gemmatimonadota, Acidobacteriota and Myxococcota were the dominant phyla across treatments (Fig. 2A). At the genus level, Gemmatimonas and Sphingomonas were the dominant bacterial genera (Fig. 2C).

Soil microbial community composition. A Bacterial community composition at the phylum level. B Fungal community composition at the phylum level. C Bacterial community composition at the genus level. D Fungal community composition at the genus level

In the fungal community, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Mortierellomycota, Mucoromycota and Chytridiomycota were the dominant phyla (Fig. 2B). At the genus level, Chaetomium, Fusarium and Penicillium were the dominant fungal genera (Fig. 2D).

Co-occurrence network analysis further showed that intercropping altered the association patterns among soil microorganisms and reshaped the structure of microbial co-occurrence networks (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S3). In the bacterial network, the CO treatment had the highest number of edges (412) and the highest network density (0.336), indicating stronger network connectivity and a more complex bacterial co-occurrence pattern (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the CG treatment had the lowest number of edges (368) and network density (0.300), but showed the highest proportion of positive correlations (62.50%), suggesting that tobacco–garlic intercropping may have promoted more positive associations among bacterial taxa (Fig. 3B). The CW treatment had the lowest proportion of positive correlations (50.50%), indicating a relatively balanced pattern of positive and negative associations within the bacterial community (Fig. 3D). In terms of modularity, the C (0.650) and CW (0.646) treatments showed relatively high values, suggesting a more pronounced modular structure and greater compartmentalization of the bacterial network.

In the fungal network, differences among treatments were more pronounced. The CG treatment exhibited the highest number of edges (516) and the highest network density (0.421), indicating that tobacco–garlic intercropping increased fungal network complexity and strengthened potential associations among fungal taxa. The relatively high proportion of positive correlations (57.75%) further suggested that the fungal network under CG was characterized by more positive associations. However, this treatment had the lowest modularity (0.496), indicating stronger overall network integration but weaker modular partitioning (Fig. 3F). In contrast, the CW treatment had the lowest number of edges (393) and network density (0.321), but the highest modularity (0.662), indicating a more compartmentalized fungal network structure (Fig. 3H). The C and CO treatments showed intermediate network characteristics overall, whereas the CO treatment was characterized by relatively high connectivity (424 edges; density = 0.346) and moderate modularity (0.558) (Fig. 3E, G).

Metabolite composition, differential accumulation and enrichment analysis

A total of 1,408 rhizosphere soil metabolites were detected across the four treatments. Among them, lipids and lipid-like molecules were the most abundant metabolite class, accounting for 28.91% of the total detected metabolites, followed by phenylpropanoids and polyketides (12.00%), organoheterocyclic compounds (9.45%), organic acids and derivatives (6.96%), benzenoids (6.46%), and organic oxygen compounds (5.82%). Other minor classes, including nucleosides, nucleotides and analogues, alkaloids and derivatives, lignans, neolignans and related compounds, and other compounds, together accounted for 5.97% (Fig. 4A).

Intercropping markedly altered the composition of rhizosphere soil metabolites in tobacco plants (Fig. 4B). In the principal component analysis (PCA) score plot, all intercropping treatments were clearly separated from the monoculture treatment along the principal components, indicating distinct metabolite profiles among treatments. To visualize differences in metabolite composition among groups more intuitively, the relative abundance of each metabolite class within each sample was calculated and displayed in clustered heatmaps (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Compared with tobacco monoculture, intercropping increased the abundance of lipids and lipid-like molecules in the rhizosphere soil to different extents. In the CG treatment, the contents of 15-Deoxy-Δ12,14-prostaglandin A1, 11-Deoxy prostaglandin F1α and Thromboxane B2 were relatively high, whereas the CO treatment was characterized by higher levels of Oleamide, Arachidonoyl amide and Hexadecanamide (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Among phenylpropanoids and polyketides, Scopoletin showed a relatively high abundance in the CO treatment, whereas Schisanhenol was more abundant in the CG treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Intercropping reduced the abundance of benzenoid compounds such as Methyl eugenol, Hydroxytyrosol, p-Toluenesulfonic acid and 4-Ethylbenzaldehyde, with the lowest levels generally observed in the CG treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2F). In addition, the contents of organoheterocyclic compounds, organic oxygen compounds, organic acids and derivatives, other compounds (including nucleosides, nucleotides and analogues, alkaloids and derivatives, lignans, neolignans and related compounds), and unclassified compounds were altered by intercropping in tobacco rhizosphere soil (Supplementary Fig. S2B, D, E, G and H), and most of these compounds were present at the highest levels in the CO treatment.

Volcano plot analysis showed that, compared with monoculture, intercropping resulted in 104 metabolites with decreased abundance and 50 metabolites with increased abundance. Among them, 1-methyl-1 H-benzimidazole-2-sulfonic acid (MBSA), 6-chloro-1-ethyl-4-oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic acid (CEQC), Oxidized glutathione, Dihydrofolic acid and Methionine sulfoxide were significantly decreased, whereas Deoxycytidine, Pseudouridine, 9-(β-D-Arabinofuranosyl)-hypoxanthine (Ara-Hx), Cytosine and N6-Me-Adenosine were significantly increased (Fig. 4C).

KEGG enrichment clustering showed that the differential metabolic pathways were mainly grouped into three functional modules: nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, and antibiotic and secondary metabolite metabolism. Linoleic acid metabolism and sphingolipid metabolism were significantly enriched, indicating substantial remodeling of lipid metabolism. In addition, pathways related to Degradation of flavonoids, Plant secondary metabolites biosynthesis and Antibiotics biosynthesis were activated, suggesting that intercropping promoted the accumulation of defence-related metabolites in the rhizosphere, which may contribute to enhanced soil ecological stability (Fig. 4D).

Soil–microbe–metabolite association analysis

In the bacterial community, redundancy analysis (RDA) showed that RDA1 and RDA2 together explained 72.65% of the total variation. Gemmatimonas and Sphingomonas were strongly associated with higher activities of tobacco defence-related enzymes, including SOD, PPO and PAL (Fig. 5A). In the fungal community, RDA1 and RDA2 together explained 62.01% of the total variation. Chaetomium was strongly associated with high SOD, PPO and PAL activities, whereas Fusarium was mainly associated with higher soil pH, AK and AP. In contrast, Penicillium showed a negative association with these soil physicochemical factors, suggesting that increases in soil pH, AK and AP may suppress the relative abundance of Penicillium (Fig. 5B).

Soil–microbe–metabolite associations. A Redundancy analysis (RDA) of bacterial genera and environmental factors. B RDA of fungal genera and environmental factors. C RDA of differential metabolites and environmental factors. D Correlation network between dominant microbial genera and differential metabolites

For differential metabolites, RDA1 and RDA2 together explained 97.29% of the total variation. Environments with higher SOD, PPO and PAL activities were positively associated with the accumulation of Deoxycytidine, Pseudouridine, Ara-Hx, Cytosine and N6-Me-Adenosine. In contrast, higher soil pH, AK and AP were negatively associated with the accumulation of 1-methyl-1 H-benzimidazole-2-sulfonic acid (MBSA), 6-chloro-1-ethyl-4-oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic acid (CEQC), Oxidized glutathione, Dihydrofolic acid and Methionine sulfoxide (Fig. 5C).

Correlation analysis between dominant microbial genera and differential metabolites further revealed close microbe–metabolite associations (Fig. 5D). In the bacterial community, Gemmatimonas was significantly positively correlated with the significantly increased metabolites Deoxycytidine, Pseudouridine, Cytosine and N6-Me-Adenosine (P < 0.05). Sphingomonas was extremely significantly positively correlated with Deoxycytidine, Pseudouridine and Cytosine (P < 0.01), and significantly positively correlated with Ara-Hx and N6-Me-Adenosine (P < 0.05). In the fungal community, Penicillium was extremely significantly positively correlated with the significantly decreased metabolites MBSA, CEQC and Methionine sulfoxide (P < 0.01), and significantly positively correlated with Oxidized glutathione and Dihydrofolic acid (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Tobacco mosaic virus disease (TMVD) is difficult to control because its causal virus has a broad host range and is an obligate intracellular pathogen. Conventional chemical agents are often unable to completely prevent the occurrence and development of this disease, and effective chemical control options remain limited [48]. Under appropriate crop selection and field management, intercropping can effectively reduce disease incidence, improve the soil environment and enhance plant disease resistance [49]. The results of the present study are consistent with these findings. Compared with flue-cured tobacco monoculture, intercropping with garlic, Chinese chive or onion reduced the incidence and disease index of TMVD to varying degrees.

The reduced TMVD incidence and disease index observed under tobacco–Allium intercropping should not be interpreted as evidence that microbiome shifts were the sole cause of disease suppression. Instead, the disease-suppressive effects of intercropping may be explained by several interacting mechanisms. First, intercropping can disrupt the spatial continuity of a single host crop, thereby reducing pathogen transmission and accumulation, diluting pathogen pressure and decreasing the probability of infection in flue-cured tobacco plants [50]. In the present field experiment, tobacco plant density was kept constant among treatments; therefore, the reduced TMVD occurrence was unlikely to be caused by a lower tobacco planting density. However, the presence of Allium intercrops may still have altered canopy structure and host spatial arrangement. Second, intercropping may modify the field microclimate, including humidity, temperature and ventilation conditions, thereby creating a less favourable environment for disease development [51]. Third, intercropping can increase soil microbial diversity, promote the proliferation of beneficial microorganisms and suppress pathogen reproduction [52]. In addition, beneficial compounds released in intercropping systems, including Allium-derived bioactive metabolites and volatiles, may enhance the disease resistance of flue-cured tobacco plants or influence pathogen/vector activity [53]. Therefore, TMVD suppression under tobacco–Allium intercropping should be interpreted as the result of multiple interacting ecological pathways rather than a direct consequence of microbiome changes alone.

Agronomic performance, cured leaf yield and leaf chemical quality are direct indicators of the practical effectiveness of tobacco intercropping systems. In the present study, cured leaf yield was measured at the plot level and used together with plant height, leaf number, TMVD occurrence and cured leaf chemical composition to evaluate treatment performance. Tobacco–garlic intercropping showed the most favourable comprehensive performance, suggesting that CG not only reduced field disease occurrence but also maintained or improved agronomic productivity. This is important because an intercropping system has practical value only when disease reduction is accompanied by stable or improved yield and acceptable cured leaf quality. Therefore, the yield result supports the agronomic feasibility of tobacco–garlic intercropping under the present field conditions.

Previous studies have also reported that intercropping can increase the contents of total sugar, reducing sugar and potassium in tobacco leaves and significantly improve the potassium-to-chloride ratio, thereby contributing to increased tobacco yield [54, 55]. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study. Zhou et al. further showed that intercropping improved the contents of total sugar, reducing sugar and nicotine in tobacco leaves, enhanced the coordination of chemical components and enabled tobacco leaves to meet the production standards for high-quality flue-cured tobacco [56].

The nutrients required for tobacco growth are mainly derived from soil, and an appropriate soil nutrient status is a prerequisite for the production of high-quality tobacco leaves [57]. Soil nutrients and enzyme activities are closely associated with healthy tobacco growth, and the expression of resistance-related genes in tobacco plants also depends on a suitable growth environment. Therefore, soil conditions can substantially influence tobacco growth, field disease resistance, yield and leaf quality [7, 58, 59]. Previous studies have shown that intercropping can improve the utilization of soil water and nutrients, accelerate nutrient turnover and release, increase soil nutrient availability and enhance fertilizer-use efficiency [60].

In the present study, compared with flue-cured tobacco monoculture, intercropping significantly alleviated soil acidification caused by long-term continuous cropping and increased the contents of available potassium and available phosphorus within the agronomically suitable range. However, alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen and soil organic matter contents decreased to some extent. These results suggest that intercropping may alleviate soil acidification by restructuring rhizosphere ecological processes. On the one hand, intercropping may improve nitrogen uptake efficiency and reduce excessive nitrogen accumulation in soil, thereby decreasing nitrification-associated H⁺ release [61]. On the other hand, spatial differentiation in nutrient uptake by different crop root systems may promote the cycling and redistribution of base cations, thereby improving the ionic buffering capacity of soil [62]. Meanwhile, intercropping may also enhance nutrient absorption and utilization by tobacco plants, although this process may consume alkali-hydrolysable nitrogen and soil organic matter. This could be related to increased root exudate input and enhanced microbial activity, which may promote organic matter mineralization and the formation of organic acid buffering systems, jointly contributing to the maintenance of soil pH stability [63].

Soil enzymes are important indicators of nutrient cycling and microbial functional activity [56]. Key soil enzymes, such as urease, dehydrogenase, phosphatase, β-glucosidase, cellulase and catalase, can reflect nitrogen transformation, microbial oxidative activity, phosphorus mobilization, carbon cycling and soil redox processes. In the present study, these soil enzyme activities were not measured. Therefore, although changes in soil physicochemical properties, microbial community structure and rhizosphere metabolites suggest that tobacco–Allium intercropping reshaped the rhizosphere environment, the functional mechanisms related to soil enzymatic processes remain inferential. Future studies should include soil enzyme assays, together with metagenomic or metatranscriptomic analyses, to better link microbial community shifts with soil functional processes.

In addition, intercropping significantly increased the activities of defence-related enzymes, including SOD, PPO, PAL and POD, in tobacco leaves. The enhancement of these enzyme activities may strengthen the antioxidant and defence responses of tobacco plants, thereby contributing to the reduced disease incidence observed under intercropping systems. This finding is consistent with previous reports [64].

The rhizosphere serves as an important interface between plant roots and soil, and changes in the rhizosphere microenvironment strongly influence crop growth, nutrient uptake and disease occurrence, thereby playing a crucial role in maintaining plant health [65]. In flue-cured tobacco production systems, viral inoculum may persist in infected plant residues, contaminated soil or other reservoir materials. After tobacco transplantation in the following season, viruses can enter plants through root or stem wounds caused by transplanting, irrigation or field operations, leading to systemic infection and disease development [66, 67].

Previous studies have shown that plants grown in soils with higher microbial α-diversity generally exhibit stronger disease resistance. Conversely, after disease infection, pathogen proliferation may disrupt the rhizosphere microbial community and suppress the growth of other microorganisms, resulting in reduced microbial diversity [68]. In the present study, intercropping significantly increased the α-diversity of rhizosphere microbial communities in tobacco. Specifically, tobacco–onion and tobacco–Chinese chive intercropping significantly increased bacterial richness and diversity, whereas tobacco–garlic intercropping significantly increased fungal richness and diversity. These findings are consistent with previous reports. Higher microbial diversity may enable plants to respond more rapidly and strongly to pathogen invasion [69]. A highly diverse microbial community usually contains more microbial taxa and has a more stable community structure, making it more difficult for pathogens to become dominant. In addition, diverse microorganisms can produce antibiotics or other antagonistic compounds that inhibit pathogen growth [70].

The β-diversity analysis further showed that the rhizosphere microbial communities under intercropping treatments were clearly separated from those under flue-cured tobacco monoculture along the coordinate axes, with close clustering among replicates and clear separation among different treatments. These results indicate that intercropping significantly altered microbial community composition at the genus level (P < 0.05). In this study, Gemmatimonas, Sphingomonas, Chaetomium, Fusarium and Penicillium were dominant genera in the rhizosphere soil. Among them, Gemmatimonas, Sphingomonas and Chaetomium may contribute to disease suppression and plant health promotion, whereas the ecological roles of Fusarium and Penicillium are more complex and may depend on species identity and environmental context. This is consistent with previous studies showing that shifts in dominant microbial taxa are closely related to plant disease resistance [71].

Soil ecological networks provide an intuitive representation of complex interactions among microbial taxa and can be used to characterize the stability and organization of microbial community structure. Intercropping can modify microbial interaction patterns, thereby leading to differences in network structural properties [72]. In the present study, tobacco–garlic intercropping mainly enhanced positive associations within the microbial network and markedly increased network complexity, especially in the fungal community. Tobacco–onion intercropping increased network connectivity and strengthened associations among taxa, whereas tobacco–Chinese chive intercropping showed higher modularity, suggesting that it may promote the formation of a microbial network with clearer structural compartmentalization.

Plant-derived secondary metabolites, including alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolics and flavonoids, have potential antiviral activities and represent a natural reservoir of bioactive compounds [73]. During plant growth, a wide range of secondary metabolites can be produced to defend against pathogen invasion. However, excessive accumulation of organic acids and phenolic acids may damage root cell structures, increase cell susceptibility and facilitate pathogen entry, thereby promoting disease development [74, 75]. In the present study, 1,408 rhizosphere soil metabolites were detected, among which Lipids and lipid-like molecules accounted for the largest proportion (28.91%), followed by Phenylpropanoids and polyketides (12.00%), Organoheterocyclic compounds (9.45%), Organic acids and derivatives (6.96%) and Benzenoids (6.46%). The metabolites detected in rhizosphere soil may originate from multiple sources, including direct root exudation by tobacco or Allium crops, decomposition of Allium tissues or residues, microbial biosynthesis and microbial transformation of plant-derived compounds. Root exudates are important chemical mediators of plant–soil–microbe interactions, whereas rhizosphere microorganisms can also modify root exudation patterns and produce diverse bioactive metabolites [76]. Therefore, the present non-targeted metabolomic analysis cannot fully distinguish whether specific metabolites were directly released by Allium roots, derived from decomposing plant tissues, or generated indirectly through microbial metabolism. For example, lipid-like molecules, nucleotide-related compounds and phenylpropanoid-related metabolites may reflect both plant-derived exudation and microbial turnover processes. This distinction is essential for understanding microbiome–metabolome crosstalk and should be further verified using separated root exudate collection, sterile or semi-sterile culture systems, isotope tracing, targeted metabolite validation and synthetic microbial community experiments [77].

The metabolomic changes observed in the present study are partly consistent with previous intercropping metabolome studies, but they also show features related to the use of Allium companion crops. Previous metabolomic and metagenomic analyses in tobacco intercropping systems showed that intercropping with maize or soybean can reshape tobacco rhizosphere metabolites and microbial functions, thereby helping to alleviate continuous cropping-related constraints [55]. Similarly, studies on maize–soybean intercropping have shown that intercropping alters root exudate composition and rhizosphere metabolite profiles, which can further influence nutrient availability and microbial community assembly [78]. Consistent with these studies, our results showed that tobacco–Allium intercropping markedly changed the rhizosphere metabolite profile and enriched pathways related to lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. However, unlike non-Allium intercropping systems, Allium crops are characterized by sulfur-containing compounds, strong allelopathic activity and antimicrobial potential. Previous studies have shown that garlic root exudates can mediate allelopathic plant–plant interactions and regulate carbohydrate metabolism in neighbouring plants [79]. Therefore, the enrichment of linoleic acid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, nucleotide-related metabolites and antibiotic/secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways in the present study may reflect a distinct rhizosphere chemical environment formed by tobacco–Allium intercropping. These findings extend previous intercropping metabolome studies by highlighting the potential role of Allium companion crops in regulating tobacco rhizosphere microbiome–metabolome interactions.

Lipids and lipid-like metabolites in soil serve not only as energy carriers and structural components, but also as key mediators of ecological regulation [80]. Tobacco–garlic and tobacco–onion intercropping increased the abundance of Lipids and lipid-like molecules in the rhizosphere soil. Specifically, tobacco–garlic intercropping increased the levels of 11-Deoxy prostaglandin F1α and Thromboxane B2, whereas tobacco–onion intercropping increased the levels of Oleamide, Arachidonoyl amide and Hexadecanamide. These lipid-related changes may contribute to enhanced disease resistance in tobacco plants. Soil lipids and lipid-like compounds are important factors regulating plant disease occurrence. They provide carbon sources and energy substrates for soil microorganisms and are also essential structural components of microbial cell membranes. In addition, some lipid molecules can regulate microbial community behaviours, including biofilm formation and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites such as antibiotics. They may also influence microbial community assembly through selective recruitment, thereby affecting disease development [81, 82].

Phenylpropanoids, polyketides and benzenoid compounds are mainly involved in the construction of basal plant defence systems. They can enhance plant resistance to pathogens and environmental stresses by regulating cell wall structure and inducing the production of defence-related metabolites [83, 84]. In this study, the reduced TMVD incidence and disease index under tobacco–garlic intercropping may be partly associated with the increased abundance of Schisanhenol.

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the differential metabolic pathways could be grouped into three major functional modules. The Nucleotide module, including Purine metabolism, Nucleotide metabolism and Pyrimidine metabolism, showed the strongest enrichment, indicating that intercropping substantially regulated nucleic acid synthesis and overall energy metabolism in the rhizosphere soil microbiome. Meanwhile, lipid-related pathways, such as Linoleic acid metabolism and Sphingolipid metabolism, were significantly enriched, which was consistent with the intercropping-induced accumulation of lipid-related defence metabolites. In addition, the activation of Degradation of flavonoids, Biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites and Biosynthesis of various antibiotics suggested that intercropping enhanced the disease-suppressive potential of the rhizosphere microecological system, which may ultimately contribute to improved resistance of tobacco plants to TMVD.

Soil physicochemical properties can contribute to plant disease resistance by enhancing defence-related enzyme activities, enriching beneficial dominant microbial taxa and driving the accumulation of key differential metabolites, thereby establishing a more stable soil microecological environment [85]. RDA and correlation analyses showed that intercropping promoted the enrichment of Gemmatimonas and Sphingomonas in the bacterial community and was closely associated with the activation of nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, Linoleic acid metabolism, and antibiotic and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. These changes were associated with increased SOD, PPO and PAL activities in tobacco leaves, jointly contributing to the formation of a disease-suppressive rhizosphere microenvironment and enhanced resistance of tobacco plants to TMVD [86, 87]. In the fungal community, increases in soil pH, AK and AP were negatively associated with Penicillium abundance, suggesting that improved soil physicochemical conditions may alter microbial competition and weaken the ecological advantage of some phosphorus-solubilizing fungi, thereby contributing to reduced disease occurrence [88].

Although this study systematically evaluated the ecological effects of tobacco intercropping with onion, garlic and Chinese chive from multiple perspectives, including agronomic traits, disease occurrence, cured leaf chemical composition, defence-related enzyme activities, soil physicochemical properties, microbial communities and metabolomic profiles, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was based on a one-year field experiment conducted at a single site and did not include dynamic monitoring under long-term continuous cropping conditions. Therefore, the long-term effects of intercropping systems on soil microecology and the stability of tobacco leaf quality require further validation. Second, TMVD was assessed under natural field infection conditions, and initial TMV titres in soil, plant residues or asymptomatic tobacco plants were not quantified before treatment establishment. Therefore, background spatial variation in natural inoculum pressure cannot be completely excluded, although a randomized block design and uniform field management were used to reduce this potential bias. Third, although high-throughput sequencing and metabolomic analyses revealed changes in microbial community structure and metabolite profiles, the causal relationships among intercropping, key microbial taxa, specific metabolites and TMVD suppression remain unclear. Future studies should further verify these relationships through controlled inoculation experiments, strain isolation, reinoculation experiments, vector monitoring and metagenomic functional annotation. Fourth, although correlations among soil physicochemical properties, microbial communities and metabolites were analysed, key soil enzyme activities, including urease, dehydrogenase, phosphatase, β-glucosidase, cellulase and catalase, were not measured in this study. Therefore, the mechanisms by which these environmental factors act through the “soil environment–microbe–metabolite–tobacco resistance” pathway remain insufficiently understood, and a complete regulatory network model has not yet been established. This limits our ability to directly link microbiome changes with soil nutrient cycling and functional processes. Future studies should include soil enzyme assays combined with metagenomic or metatranscriptomic analyses to better clarify the functional mechanisms underlying tobacco–Allium intercropping.

In addition, although changes in cured leaf chemical composition and defence-related enzyme activities were measured, the present analysis mainly focused on physiological and biochemical indicators. Further investigation is needed to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying key pathways, such as phenylpropanoid metabolism, antioxidant defence systems and secondary metabolite regulation.

Finally, the intercropping systems examined in this study were limited to three Allium crops, namely onion, garlic and Chinese chive. Therefore, the broader applicability of these findings should be further verified using other crop combinations and in different ecological regions.

Conclusions

Tobacco–Allium intercropping, particularly tobacco–garlic intercropping, improved soil physicochemical properties, reshaped rhizosphere microbial communities and altered key metabolic processes, including lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. These changes were associated with enhanced defence-related enzyme activities and reduced field occurrence of tobacco mosaic virus disease.

Overall, tobacco–Allium intercropping promoted tobacco growth and improved the chemical quality of cured tobacco leaves, showing potential as an ecological strategy for sustainable flue-cured tobacco production. However, the causal relationships among intercropping, microbiome shifts, metabolite changes and TMVD suppression require further experimental verification.
