Finnish physicians’ attitudes towards euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Reetta P Piili, Elina Tolvanen, Minna Hökkä, Jukka Vänskä, Pekka Louhiala, Juho T Lehto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo study Finnish physicians’ attitudes and opinions toward euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide (PAS).DesignA nationwide cross-sectional study.SettingAn online survey was conducted via the Finnish Medical Association in the autumn of 2023 covering all Finnish physicians.Participants8857 Finnish physicians answered the survey, yielding a response rate of 31%.Primary and secondary outcome measuresFinnish physicians’ attitudes towards euthanasia and PAS and background factors influencing these attitudes and opinions.ResultsOf the responders, 29% fully agreed and 29% fully disagreed with the statement ‘Euthanasia should be legalised in Finland’, respectively. Fewer responders (18%) fully agreed that physicians should be able to assist a patient in suicide. Factors such as being female and having experience in end-of-life care were associated with disagreement about legalising euthanasia and assistance in suicide. Conversely, having faced a request for euthanasia or PAS was associated with agreeing on legalising euthanasia and assistance in suicide. One out of seven respondents fully agreed that they could practise euthanasia, and one out of six fully agreed that they could assist a patient in suicide if these practices were legal in Finland.ConclusionsFinnish physicians’ attitudes and opinions towards assistance in dying are highly divided, emphasising the controversy of this challenging subject.Keywords: Physicians, MEDICAL ETHICS, Death, Surveys and Questionnaires

Introduction

Evolving medicine continuously changes the way we die. Assistance in dying, encompassing euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide (PAS), is one of the drivers of this change. Although the legislation of assistance in dying has expanded in different countries worldwide, there remains considerable variation in the terminology used.1 For example, voluntary-assisted dying is predominantly used in Australia and medical assistance in dying in Canada. Other terms include aid in dying, assisted dying and physician-assisted death.2,5 For a long time, the subject of dying has been a taboo in many cultures, but during the 1960s, a weakening of the taboo towards controlled dying has been seen.6 Assistance in dying is a contentious issue, involving ethical, judicial, psycho-social, cultural, religious and societal dimensions.

Currently, euthanasia has been legalised or decriminalised in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Canada, the states of Victoria and Western Australia in Australia, New Zealand, Spain, Portugal, Ecuador and Cuba.57,9 PAS is legal in 10 states in the USA.5 7 Additionally, Germany has decriminalised PAS, Colombia has a court ruling protecting physicians from prosecution for euthanasia or PAS and Switzerland has decriminalised assisted suicide for both residents and non-residents.5 7

Public attitudes towards assistance in dying vary significantly.10,12 For example, the public in the Netherlands and Denmark exhibit more favourable views on euthanasia, whereas countries like Jordan, Romania and Turkey show lower acceptance.10,12 A previous study indicates that euthanasia is perceived as more justifiable in high-income countries compared with low- and middle-income countries.10 Various factors, including religion, economic, sociodemographic, health-related and cultural influences as well as political orientation, influence attitudes towards euthanasia and PAS.10,12

Physicians have historically been less supportive of legalising euthanasia and PAS compared with the general public.4 12 However, changes in these attitudes have been seen in the past decades. In Finland, the ambivalence towards euthanasia and PAS has decreased, with physician support for legalising euthanasia rising from 5% to 25% between 1993 and 2020.13 In a survey study conducted in Estonia, 73% and 71% of physicians agreed that euthanasia and assisted suicide should be legal.14 In Sweden, acceptance of PAS among physicians increased from 35% to 47% between 2007 and 2020.15 Overall, physicians’ attitudes towards assistance in dying vary substantially.412 16,19 In countries where assistance in dying is allowed, the support among physicians is high, whereas in some countries where it is not allowed, the support remains quite low, for example, in Japan, where only 2% of physicians answering a survey were in favour of euthanasia.412 16,19

The debate over the legalisation of assistance in dying is further complicated by discussions on the legal frameworks and guidelines for practising euthanasia and PAS. Controversies include whether euthanasia should be permitted for psychiatric illnesses, based on advance directives, or for underage children. Discussions are ongoing, particularly in countries where assistance in dying is already allowed.20 21 Besides physical suffering, social challenges such as loneliness without social support, uncertainty about the future and hopelessness as well as loss of function are reasons for seeking euthanasia or PAS.20 22 23 One issue in countries where euthanasia is practised is the ambiguous requirements, which allow for varying interpretations.20 23 24

In Finland, a new citizen initiative was launched in the autumn of 2023 and given to the parliament in May 2024, calling for the legalisation of euthanasia.25 A similar initiative was rejected by Parliament in 2018.25 An expert group established by the Finnish Ministry of Social Affairs and Health did not reach a common understanding on legalisation of euthanasia or assisted suicide in Finland.26 The new initiative is expected to spark debate and discussions about assistance in dying, making it crucial to understand the current attitudes of Finnish physicians. There are associations promoting legalisation of euthanasia, which probably shape attitudes and opinions in Finland.27 Similar debates are ongoing in other countries, including the UK, Ireland, Slovenia and Japan.20 28 29

Attitudes towards assistance in dying are influenced by societal changes, public sentiment and advancements in medicine, making alterations in these attitudes very likely also among physicians. Worldwide, practising assistance in dying has become integrated into society in some countries, although in most countries these practices remain illegal.5 7 Therefore, it is essential to gather current data on attitudes and opinions towards euthanasia and PAS in countries where the discussion of whether to legalise assistance in dying or not is ongoing. The aim of this study was to explore Finnish physicians’ current attitudes and opinions toward euthanasia and PAS.

Methods

An electronic survey was distributed to all members of the Finnish Medical Association whose email addresses were available and who had not opted out of participating in surveys, covering approximately 90% of all Finnish physicians. The study population included altogether 28 534 physicians. Medical students, with permission to work as locum doctors under supervision after they have completed the fourth year in medical school, are invited to join the Finnish Medical Association. Membership can continue after retirement, thus both medical students and retired physicians are included in the study population. The survey was conducted from 7 November to 4 December 2023; three reminders were sent to non-respondents (15 November, 22 November and 28 November 2023). Duplicate answers were not possible because we used personal invites and survey software was able to detect participants, so the questionnaire was possible to answer only once.

Before conducting the survey, the content validity of the questionnaire was assessed and several improvements were made. A detailed description of this process and the final version of the questionnaire have been published previously30 and is available as an online supplemental file. The questionnaire included several statements regarding euthanasia and PAS, as well as open questions and background information. Responses to the statements were categorised on a Likert-type scale: fully agree, partly agree, partly disagree, fully disagree or I cannot say. Basic background information included age, gender, fields of medical specialty, employment status, whether a respondent is taking care of patients in palliative and end-of-life care currently, self-reported experience in palliative and end-of-life care, years of experience or special competency in these areas and whether the respondent had ever been asked for PAS or euthanasia.

The possibility of non-response bias was addressed using several approaches, including conducting the study through the official association for Finnish physicians (the Finnish Medical Association), which covers almost all Finnish physicians. The study population comprised all members of the Finnish Medical Association. Reminders were sent three times to enhance response rates. Responses were collected anonymously to encourage participation in a survey addressing sensitive topics. The questionnaire had been previously assessed to ensure that the framing of the questions is relevant and understandable, which facilitates the answering.

The characteristics of the participants and their responses to the statements are described using numbers and proportions. The number of respondents also allowed subgroup analysis. To assess the relationship between different background factors and the statements, the scale was converted into two options: fully/partly agree and fully/partly disagree. The ‘I cannot say’ responses were excluded from these analyses to enhance the comparison between these two groups. A cross-tabulation was performed and Pearson’s χ2 test was used to assess statistical significance.

A logistic regression analysis (enter-model) was used to create a model explaining disagreement with the statements ‘A physician should be able to assist a patient in a suicide’ and ‘Euthanasia should be legalised in Finland’. The responses to the statements were converted into two options as explained above. This analysis was used to examine the associations between multiple background factors and the binary outcome variable. All the background factors shown in table 1 were entered into the model simultaneously to control for potential confounding factors and to assess the individual effect of each factor while holding other factors constant.

Results

The characteristics of the respondents are presented in table 1. The response rate was 31%. Most of the respondents were female (59%). The smallest age group was under 25 years of age (1%), while the largest was over 64 years of age (30%). Most respondents were currently working (66%) and a minority of respondents were students (5%). Respondents had mainly specialised in conservative (including, eg, internal medicine, oncology, geriatric, neurology, pulmonology) fields, general medicine (including occupational medicine and other fields) (24% in both) and operative fields (eg, surgery) (20% of respondents). Approximately one-third of the respondents were currently caring for patients in palliative and end-of-life care at the time of the survey. 73% reported having none or <5 years of experience in palliative or end-of-life care. Conversely, approximately one out of five respondents reported having over 10 years of experience in these areas. Most of the respondents had never faced a request for PAS or euthanasia (88% and 85%, respectively), with requests for euthanasia being slightly more frequent than those for PAS.

Respondents’ answers to the statements concerning euthanasia and PAS are shown in figure 1. The complete table of the results from figure 1 can be found in an online supplemental file. Of the respondents, 18% fully agreed and 30% fully disagreed that a physician should be able to assist a patient in suicide. Only a minority of respondents believed that physicians should be punished for assisting in suicide (7% fully and 8% partly). Most respondents (39% fully and 8% partly) were not willing to assist a patient in suicide if it were allowed in Finland.

Proportions of answers to questionnaire statements.

Opinions on legalising euthanasia in Finland were divided, with 29% fully agreeing and the same amount (29%) fully disagreeing on the statement that euthanasia should be legalised. Half of the respondents (43% fully and 7% partly) were not willing to practice euthanasia if it were legalised in Finland, and one-third were willing (14% fully and 18% partly). Almost one-third of the respondents fully agreed that with sufficient palliative and end-of-life care, there is no need for euthanasia. Approximately 40% of the respondents fully or partly agreed that accepting euthanasia would strengthen the doctor-patient relationship, whereas 33% agreed (fully or partly) that it would harm this relationship. Just over 60% agreed, fully or partly, that it is possible to define adequately clear eligibility criteria for euthanasia in the law, enactments and instructions.

Table 2 represents participants’ background factors concerning five of the statements. Male participants, physicians working in operative and diagnostic fields and students agreed significantly more that assisted suicide should be allowed and euthanasia legalised. They also more frequently agreed that they could assist in suicide and practice euthanasia. This difference is also seen in the logistic regression analysis concerning statements about legalising euthanasia and allowing PAS (tables3 4).

More years of experience in caring for patients in palliative or end-of-life care was associated with lower acceptability of allowing PAS, legalising euthanasia and practising these procedures if they were legal. If a patient or a patient’s closest one had requested PAS or euthanasia, physicians’ attitudes towards euthanasia and PAS were more acceptable and their willingness to practise these procedures was also higher. These findings are further confirmed in the logistic regression analysis (tables3 4). The goodness-of-fit by Nagelkerke R Square was 0.046 for model explaining ‘A physician should be able to assist a patient in a suicide’ and 0.057 for model explaining ‘Euthanasia should be legalised in Finland’.

Being male, over 64 years of age or between 25–34 years of age, working in operative or diagnostic fields and not in the care of patients in palliative or end-of-life care were associated with a positive attitude towards the possibility to define adequately clear eligibility criteria for euthanasia in the law, enactments and instructions (table 2). This was also seen with the respondents who had faced a request for euthanasia or PAS (table 2).

Discussion

Based on our results, attitudes towards euthanasia and PAS are highly divergent among Finnish physicians. Physicians supported legalisation of euthanasia or PAS more often than they were willing to participate in these procedures. Acceptance of legalising euthanasia was greater than agreeing that a physician should be able to assist a patient in suicide, while the willingness to practice euthanasia was lower than the willingness to assist in suicide. These results highlight the controversy surrounding this challenging subject. To enhance the quality of the survey, the validity of the questionnaire was thoroughly studied before its use.30

This study shows a higher acceptance of assistance in dying compared with previous studies on Finnish physicians, although the study populations are not exactly comparable, as previous studies have excluded medical students and retired physicians from the sample.13 31 32 In a study published in 2022, the proportion of Finnish physicians fully agreeing with the legalisation of euthanasia was 25% in 2020, compared with 29% in this study.13 There exists a large global variation in physicians’ attitudes towards euthanasia. The acceptance of euthanasia ranges from over 80% in Belgium and the Netherlands to 2% in Japan.4 12 17 A scoping review from Australia reported a wide range of 9%–60% of physicians supporting legalisation of assistance in dying.18 A recent study from Spain concluded that there is no consensus regarding assistance in dying among healthcare professionals.19 Previous international studies have shown that attitudes towards assistance in dying have become more positive among the public as well as among physicians over the last decades.4 12 While the taboo connected with euthanasia and PAS has weakened, another taboo against the questioning of patient autonomy has emerged in some parts of the world.6

Physicians’ attitudes towards PAS have also become more acceptable. In our study, 18% of physicians fully agreed and 31% partly agreed that physicians should be able to assist a patient in suicide. Most physicians in this study considered that a physician should not be punished for assisting in suicide, while only a small proportion (7% fully and 8% partly) agreed that this should be punishable. Furthermore, almost half of the respondents fully agreed that legislation should even ensure that physicians are not punished for assisting in suicide. These results likely reflect the increased acceptability of PAS in Western society and among physicians.4 12 15 31 Our results are also comparable to a study from Sweden, where 47% of physicians accepted PAS in 2020.15

In this study, physicians were more willing to accept the legalisation of euthanasia than to allow assistance in suicide, which is a rare finding. A similar result was found in Chile, where 75% and 57% of physicians surveyed agreed that euthanasia and assisted suicide should be legalised, respectively.33 The reasons behind this result can only be speculated. In a survey study from the Netherlands, physicians did not have a clear preference between euthanasia and PAS.24 Although they considered PAS to emphasise patient autonomy and responsibility, they also anticipated practical problems with PAS, such as a longer time until death and the unreliability of lethal drinks.24 This finding should be further explored in future studies using qualitative methods as well.

Finnish physicians were more willing to legalise euthanasia and to allow PAS than to practice them. A similar result was found in Kuwait, where 44% of physicians surveyed were willing to legalise euthanasia but only 29% were willing to perform it.34 In Sweden, 47% of physicians accepted PAS, while 33% were prepared to prescribe the needed drug.15 In the USA, 60% of physicians surveyed thought PAS should be legal, but only 13% indicated they would be willing to perform the practice.35 In New South Wales, Australia, at the time of implementation of assistance in dying, 25%–28% were willing to give an opinion on patient eligibility for assisted dying, but only 6%–15% of medical specialists were willing to be actual practitioners of assistance in dying.36 This finding should be taken into account when discussing the practicalities of assistance in dying.

In our study, physicians were more willing to practice PAS than euthanasia, which is similar to a Canadian study where family medicine residents were more willing to participate in assistance in dying by prescribing a lethal drug (31%) than in euthanasia by lethal injection (24%).37 Conversely, our result is contrary to a study from Chile, where only 36% of physicians were willing to participate in assisted suicide, but 54% agreed to participate in euthanasia.33

Willingness to practice assistance in dying was quite low in this study. The exact reasons behind this remain unknown, but suggestions can be reflected through previous studies. Previous qualitative research reveals that physicians’ reasons for not participating in assistance in dying practices include lack of training and expertise, ethical concerns and adherence to the Hippocratic Oath, religious beliefs and legal implications and obstacles.35 38 There are also conflicting findings regarding the impact of participating in assistance in dying on physicians.39,41 Physicians have described both positive feelings, such as satisfaction, success and relief, and negative feelings such as loneliness, anxiety, distress and discomfort.3941,45 Concerns about how practising assistance in dying might affect physicians’ well-being could be one reason behind the lower number of physicians willing to participate in these practices. In a Canadian study, reasons for not participating in assistance in dying included the emotional burden related to this act and the fear of psychological repercussions.40 Qualitative research from this aspect is needed in the future to better understand physicians’ willingness or unwillingness to participate in assistance in dying. This issue has also major practical importance if implementing assistance in dying will happen in Finland.

In Canada, the majority of the deaths through assistance in dying are covered by a minority of physicians, only 2% of all physicians being involved with assistance in dying.46 47 Thus, it is stated that assistance in dying is becoming an area of focused expertise for some physicians.46 47 Reflecting this, perhaps not many physicians are needed to cover the number of deaths through euthanasia and PAS in Finland if these practices are legalised. In countries where assistance in dying is legal, most physicians involved are practising family medicine or are general practitioners.47 48 In Finland, continuity of care has decreased over the past decades, and lifelong doctor-patient relationships rarely exist.49 The citizen initiative, calling for legalising euthanasia, does not specify any specialties for physicians involving assistance in dying.25 Discussions are ongoing about which physicians would perform euthanasia and PAS if they will be allowed in Finland.

In our study, males, physicians working in operative and diagnostic fields and students showed more acceptance towards legalising euthanasia, allowing PAS and willingness to practise assistance in dying compared with females, older physicians and those working in conservative fields. Younger physicians have been more in favour of euthanasia and PAS in previous studies as well.15 16 50 In the general population, males more frequently support the legalisation of euthanasia.4 A Canadian study found that male residents were more willing to participate in assistance in dying, which aligns with our findings.37

Palliative care physicians are known to be least in favour of assistance in dying.1550,53 Similar results were found in this study, as physicians with >10 years of experience in palliative and end-of-life care were least likely to agree that physicians should be able to assist a patient in suicide or that euthanasia should be legalised. They were also least willing to assist in suicide and practise euthanasia. These findings are somewhat contrary to a Canadian study where clinical exposure to death and dying among residents was associated with an agreement to participate in various end-of-life procedures, including withdrawal of treatment, prescribing lethal drugs and administering lethal drugs.37 This discrepancy might be due to different generations of physicians becoming specialists while assistance in dying practice has been legal. However, more studies are needed to understand these differences.

If a patient or a patient’s closest one had requested euthanasia or PAS, physicians were more accepting of these practices and more willing to participate in them. This is most evident in the agreement with the statement, ‘If euthanasia was legalised in Finland, I could sometimes perform it’. The reasons behind this result can only be speculated and, to our knowledge, there exists no previous research on this issue. It might be assumed that witnessing such suffering that leads to a request for assistance in dying could influence physicians’ attitudes. The statement regarding the request for euthanasia or PAS did not separate the request made by a patient and a patient’s closest one. However, it is well known that euthanasia or PAS is not allowed in any country based on a request other than the patient. The statement gives an overview of the number of requests as a whole, and a qualitative study of the answers to the open question concerning these issues would definitely give a broader aspect of the requests.

In our study, 53% of physicians fully or partly agreed that with sufficient palliative and end-of-life care, there is no need for euthanasia. This is comparable to a US study, where almost 40% of physicians surveyed believed that assistance in dying would be unnecessary if all patients had access to excellent palliative care.35 There are arguments both for and against the idea that physician-assisted death should not be allowed until palliative care is available to all patients.21 54 In a Canadian qualitative study, palliative care providers acknowledged that assistance in dying has had a significant impact on their practice, identifying challenges such as symptom control, communication, relationship with patients and the consumption of palliative care resources in relation to assistance in dying.55 The relationship between palliative care and assistance in dying is a complex and demanding subject that requires further research in future.

Physicians also have opposite opinions on whether accepting euthanasia would strengthen or harm the doctor-patient relationship. A significant number of respondents (14%–24%) answered ‘I cannot say’, reflecting the complexity of the subject. In a Canadian study, physicians described how providing assistance in dying felt like rediscovering the art of medicine, and part of this art was a relationship of intimacy and grace.56 They felt more engaged with their patients.56 Conversely, concerns have been raised that accepting assistance in dying could endanger trust in physicians, especially among vulnerable patient groups.57 58

A little over 60% of respondents fully or partly agreed that it is possible to define adequately clear eligibility criteria for euthanasia in the law, enactments and instructions. Although guidelines and instructions have been established in countries where euthanasia is allowed, a systematic review revealed that one of the main problems with euthanasia is ambiguous criteria, which leave room for interpretation.20

Strengths and limitations

The response rate (31%) is comparable to that of other physicians surveys, which have reported rates ranging from 3% to 59%.15 17 35 The proportion of women among the respondents was comparable (59% vs 58%) to the proportion of women in the study population.59 Older age groups responded to the survey slightly more actively than younger physicians, the proportion of the respondents over 55 years of age was 47% while this percentage was 39% in the study population.59 Distinction between students, working and retired physicians was done since differences in experience and working status can have influence in their attitudes. Nevertheless, the sample is sufficiently representative to allow the results to be generalised to all Finnish physicians. The large number of respondents also enabled reliable subgroup analyses. Taking these factors together, along with the procedures implemented prior to data collection, for example, using a reliable distributor (the Finnish Medical Association), not having a random sample, anonymity of the survey and by using a previously validated tool, we estimate that the risk of non-response bias is low. The goodness-of-fit regarding logistic regression analysis was low, which is understandable considering the complexity and multidimensionality of the studied issues. Analysing latent attitudinal dimensions may also be worthwhile in future research. As the study was conducted solely in Finland, comparisons with findings from other countries should be made with caution.

The content validity of the questionnaire used in this study has been thoroughly examined,30 which is one of the study’s strengths. Through content validity testing, we were able to confirm the relevance of the questionnaire items and improve their comprehensibility. However, some ambiguous items remained in the final questionnaire,30 which can be considered a limitation. Given the complexity of the issue of assistance in dying, some amount of interpretation is inevitable.

Future research on euthanasia and PAS should include religious aspects, as various religious groups have shown to have almost universal opposition to euthanasia and PAS in previous studies.4 60

Conclusions

Finnish physicians’ attitudes and opinions towards assistance in dying are highly divergent, emphasising the controversy of this challenging subject. Physicians were reported to be less eager to practice euthanasia or assist in suicide than to allow these practices in Finland. Based on these results and reflections from the literature, it is clear that assistance in dying remains a controversial issue among physicians.
