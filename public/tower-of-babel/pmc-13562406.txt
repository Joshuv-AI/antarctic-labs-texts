TargetQC: A targeted quality control framework for clinical genomic testing
Authors: Fangfang Lan, Yaqiong Wang, Yulan Lu, Bingbing Wu, Xiao Wang, Chuan Li, Bo Liu, Xinran Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReliable genetic testing depends on accurate assessment of sequencing quality in clinically relevant genomic regions that directly influence variant interpretation. We developed TargetQC, a flexible quality control framework that supports user-defined gene sets, coverage thresholds, and variant sets for evaluating sequencing performance across exome sequencing (ES) and genome sequencing (GS) platforms. TargetQC assesses exon and gene coverage, identifies regions meeting predefined coverage thresholds, evaluates variant detection accuracy, and measures sequencing quality at pathogenic variant sites. We applied TargetQC to the reference sample NA12878 and 665 clinical samples across five ES platforms and one GS platform. ES-VendorB and ES-VendorE achieved the most complete coverage of OMIM coding regions in NA12878, whereas ES-VendorD and ES-VendorE showed the highest coverage compliance in clinical samples. ES-VendorB and GS demonstrated the highest variant detection accuracy. TargetQC provides a practical framework for benchmarking sequencing performance and informing platform selection in clinical genomics.

Introduction

Rare diseases, affecting fewer than 1 in 2,000 individuals, are predominantly genetic in origin and often manifest as severe multisystem disorders in children.1 Identifying their molecular causes is critical for accurate diagnosis, effective treatment, and informed genetic counseling. Over the past decade, next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have revolutionized the diagnosis of genetic disorders by enabling high-throughput, sensitive, and specific detection of pathogenic variants.2,3 Among these, capture-based sequencing (e.g., exome sequencing [ES] or gene panels) has become a cost-effective and widely adopted approach, offering high coverage depth, manageable data size, and relatively straightforward interpretation.4 Whole-genome sequencing (WGS), in contrast, offers more comprehensive genomic coverage by interrogating both coding and non-coding regions while avoiding biases introduced by hybrid-capture methods.5,6 Nevertheless, the substantially greater sequencing throughput required for WGS increases both sequencing and computational costs. To balance diagnostic yield and cost, clinical WGS is typically performed at a lower average sequencing depth (DP) than ES. Consequently, the broader genomic coverage of WGS does not necessarily translate into superior coverage of all disease-associated genes or coding regions, highlighting the importance of systematic assessment of coverage performance in clinically relevant genomic targets.

Despite substantial advances in sequencing technologies and analytical pipelines, achieving consistent coverage and reliable variant detection remains challenging. Sequencing performance depends on multiple factors, including capture design, GC content, read length, and mapping efficiency. Consequently, coverage of the same genomic locus or disease-associated gene can vary considerably across sequencing platforms, laboratory protocols, and analytical workflows. Such variability may lead to inconsistent variant calls or failure to detect pathogenic variants, posing a significant challenge for clinical diagnostics.7,8 Insufficient coverage of clinically relevant exons can result in missed variants, incorrect zygosity assignments, and inaccurate assessment of allelic balance, ultimately reducing diagnostic sensitivity.7 For instance, even in samples with high average read depth (>75×), some regions are captured poorly (with coverage as low as 10×), potentially resulting in missed variant calls.9 Similar limitations have been reported in targeted sequencing studies, where variant detection sensitivity decreases substantially in regions with low read depth (5×) compared with adequately covered regions (20×).10,11 Several studies have evaluated the performance of commercial ES platforms. Zhou J et al.12 systematically assessed technical metrics, including probe design, enrichment efficiency, and GC bias across multiple exome capture platforms. Belova V et al.13 further extended such comparisons to include coverage of the 81 genes recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), using platforms such as Agilent SureSelect v8, Roche KAPA HyperExome, Vazyme VAHTS Target Capture Core Exome Panel, and Nanodigmbio NEXome Plus Panel v.1. However, these studies primarily relied on global metrics such as average coverage depth, enrichment efficiency, or the number of uncovered regions. They did not comprehensively evaluate coverage across clinically relevant coding regions or assess the ability of sequencing platforms to detect known pathogenic variants.

Traditional sequencing quality control tools, such as Picard CollectHsMetrics14 and Qualimap,15 provide essential metrics including target coverage, coverage uniformity, on-target rate, depth distribution, and gene-level coverage. While these metrics are valuable for evaluating overall sequencing and target-capture performance, they primarily offer global quality assessments and do not directly address whether clinically relevant genes, exons, or known pathogenic variants are adequately covered for reliable diagnosis. To address this gap, we developed TargetQC, a flexible and target-oriented quality assessment framework that complements existing QC tools by focusing on user-defined genomic regions, disease-associated genes, and curated pathogenic variant sets. Rather than relying solely on genome-wide summary statistics, TargetQC evaluates sequencing performance within genomic regions that directly influence clinical interpretation.

To demonstrate its utility, we applied TargetQC to compare five representative capture-based ES platforms and one WGS platform, with a particular focus on regions and variants associated with genetic diseases. These platforms represent major sequencing technologies currently used in clinical genomics laboratories. Unlike conventional QC approaches, TargetQC integrates exon- and gene-level coverage analyses with variant detection assessment, enabling comprehensive evaluation of sequencing performance within clinically relevant genomic regions. Additionally, we developed an interactive web application (https://rpubs.com/Twilight/1372964); users can assess coverage quality in regions of interest. By linking sequencing quality metrics to clinically actionable genomic targets, TargetQC provides a practical framework for evaluating diagnostic readiness and supports more informed implementation of genomic testing in precision medicine.

At the exon level, the target exon QC module systematically assesses each exon of the target genes. For ES data, it evaluates probe capture efficiency (classifying exons as full-, partial-, near-, or non-captured) and DP uniformity (rating coverage as well, middle, or poor), thereby pinpointing weak regions in target capture or sequencing.

At the gene level, the target gene QC module examines coverage completeness across the coding or exon regions of each gene. Based on predefined thresholds, it assigns an overall quality grade (well, middle, or poor) to determine whether the data are sufficient to support reliable gene-level analysis.

At the variant level, TargetQC provides two complementary modules tailored to different scenarios. For samples with an established gold standard (e.g., NA12878), the variant calling QC module works with benchmarking tools such as hap.py to compute recall, precision, and F1-score and further characterizes miscalled variants to directly evaluate the accuracy of the bioinformatics pipeline. For routine clinical samples without a gold standard, the variant capture QC module indirectly evaluates genotyping reliability at key known pathogenic sites by inspecting DP, allele frequency, and other quality metrics. This allows assessment of the robustness of variant detection in real-world settings. The TargetQC results for an individual sample are shown in Figure S1.

Comparison of the five exome sequencing capture platforms

To evaluate the similarities and differences in target region design among various exome capture technologies, we systematically compared the probe coverage characteristics of five capture-based platforms—ES-VendorA, ES-VendorB, ES-VendorC, ES-VendorD, and ES-VendorE. We found that although the total target size varied significantly across platforms, all platforms showed broad coverage of disease-associated genes. Specifically, the analysis revealed a 6.3-fold difference in the total target size, ranging from the smallest at 16.13 Mb (ES-VendorD) to the largest at 101.75 Mb (ES-VendorE). As can be seen in Figure S2, the core region shared by all platforms was only 15.83 Mb. ES-VendorE possessed the largest (15.97 Mb) unique capture regions, suggesting its expanded coverage may extend beyond the core exome regions commonly targeted by other platforms. From a functional coverage perspective (Table S1), over 98% of the sequences targeted by each platform (except for the smallest panel, ES-VendorD) overlapped functional exon regions. Crucially, all platforms showed excellent coverage of disease-related genes. Even ES-VendorD, which had the smallest target size, covered 92.95% (4,693 genes) of the genes in the OMIM database, while ES-VendorC achieved the highest coverage at 97.94% (4,945 genes). This indicates that despite their different design focuses, these ES platforms are all capable of sufficiently covering the vast majority of known human disease -associated genes.

To account for differences in DP, we assessed the proportion of target bases achieving coverage thresholds of ≥10×, ≥20×, ≥30×, and ≥40× at matched DPs (Figure S3); performed downsampling analyses (Figure S4); and reassessed the proportion of target bases achieving coverage thresholds of ≥10×, ≥20×, ≥30×, and ≥40× at matched DPs. The downsampling curves also revealed platform differences after depth normalization. WGS and ES-VendorD reached high proportions of bases covered at ≥10× and ≥20× even at relatively low DPs, while ES-VendorB approached near-complete coverage as depth increased. In contrast, ES-VendorA and ES-VendorE showed lower plateau levels across multiple coverage thresholds, suggesting that their reduced coverage breadth could not be fully attributed to DP alone. These differences became more pronounced at higher coverage thresholds (≥30× and ≥40×), highlighting the influence of factors beyond depth. Second, we evaluated the coverage quality of individual target exons. Despite all ES platforms being designed to capture the same exon set, a substantial proportion of exons remained poorly covered. In this analysis, ES-VendorD exhibited the highest proportion of poorly covered exons (Figure 2A). Overall, WGS yielded a greater number of well-covered exons than any ES platform (Figure 2B). Among the ES platforms, ES-VendorE and ES-VendorB contained the largest numbers of well-covered exons, followed by ES-VendorC and ES-VendorA. To reduce the influence of capture design and probe efficiency, we further restricted the analysis to exons categorized as “full-captured” and evaluated the proportion of well-covered and poorly covered exons across matched downsampled depths. As shown in Figure 2C, ES-VendorB, ES-VendorC, and ES-VendorD each achieved full coverage (well-covered) for ≥96% of these fully captured exons. Among them, ES-VendorD stood out with a 99.93% coverage rate, demonstrating exceptional uniformity. Platform-specific differences persisted after depth normalization (Figure S3). ES-VendorD achieved the highest proportion of well-covered fully captured exons (99.81%) and approached saturation at higher DPs, followed by ES-VendorB (96.43%), whereas ES-VendorA (80.71%) reached lower coverage plateaus, suggesting limitations beyond DP alone. ES-VendorC (86.09%) and ES-VendorE (86.36%) showed intermediate performance. The quality statistics of gene coverage in NA12878 by TargetQC

To ensure sufficient coverage of genes associated with known human genetic diseases and reduce false-negatives (FNs) in variant detection, we evaluated coding region coverage using two thresholds: well-covered bases (≥30×) and poorly covered bases (≤20×). Genes were classified as well covered if ≥ 95% of their coding bases met the 30× threshold and poorly covered if ≥ 5% of their coding bases fell below 20×. These thresholds were chosen as clinically interpretable default parameters and can be adjusted by users according to laboratory-specific validation requirements. To assess the robustness of these classifications, we further examined the cumulative number of genes satisfying different coverage-percentage thresholds. As expected, the number of genes classified as well covered decreased as the required proportion of bases meeting the 30× threshold increased, whereas the number of genes classified as poorly covered increased as the permitted fraction of bases with <20× coverage decreased (Figure S5).

We then compared the number of well-covered genes across ES and WGS platforms (Figure 3A; Table S10). For all genes, WGS, which sequences the entire genome, achieved ≥30× coverage in 95% of coding regions for 87.46% of genes. Focusing on OMIM genes, all high-depth ES platforms outperformed WGS. Specifically, ES-VendorB and ES-VendorE achieved near-complete (∼100%) coverage of OMIM coding sequences (Figure 3B). Interestingly, despite its suboptimal exon-level coverage, ES-VendorA still reached 88.84% of OMIM genes meeting the well-covered standard. In contrast, most pseudogenes had >5% of exonic regions with coverage ≤20×, highlighting the challenge of uniform sequencing in these regions compared with functional genes.

After depth normalization, the proportion of well-covered genes increased and the proportion of poorly covered genes decreased with increasing DP across all platforms. Downsampling analysis showed persistent platform-specific differences after depth matching. For all genes, ES-VendorE and WGS approached the highest proportions of well-covered genes at approximately 40× depth, reaching 28.99% and 29.82%, respectively, outperforming the other ES platforms (which ranged from 1.83% to 18.66%) (Figures S6A and S6B). In the protein-coding gene set, ES-VendorE showed the fastest increase, reaching 97% well-covered genes at 40× depth and plateauing at 98.77%, while ES-VendorB reached 91.08% at 90× depth and plateaued at 94.42% (Figures S6C and S6D). For OMIM inherited protein-coding genes, ES-VendorE and ES-VendorB achieved the highest plateau levels (99.71% and 98.74%, respectively), whereas the remaining platforms plateaued between 74.10% and 93.67% (Figures S6E and S6F). We further analyzed poor ly covered disease genes across all platforms (Figure S7). An intersection analysis showed that 3,737 genes met the coverage standard across all platforms, whereas only six genes consistently failed: HLA-DRB1 (HGNC: 4948), MAF (HGNC: 6776), POU4F1 (HGNC: 9218), SORD (HGNC: 11184), TAF4 (HGNC: 11537), and UGT2B17 (HGNC: 12547). As shown in Figure S8, the CDS region of POU4F1 (chr13: 78,601,417–78,602,551) and TAF4 (chr20: 62,064,450–62,065,810) showed low coverage in one sample from ES-VendorA. Additionally, platform-specific low-coverage genes were identified, including 265 unique to ES-VendorD and 236 unique to ES-VendorC, indicating that some genes remain challenging to capture depending on the capture platform.

The quality statistics of variant calling in NA12878 by TargetQC

Next, we investigate the ability of these platforms to accurately identify single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions and deletions (indels) using the variant calling QC module of TargetQC. In general, indel detection performance was consistently lower than SNV detection performance, as reflected by lower recall, precision, and F1-scores (recall: p = 0.0002 < 0.001; precision: p = 0.0002 < 0.001; F1-score: p = 0.0000 < 0.001). When analyzing the variants found in the respective target regions, ES-VendorA and ES-VendorE showed the most significant differences in recall from the other protocols (ES-VendorA: 59.51%; ES-VendorE: 67.91%), with the highest risk of FNs. In contrast, ES-VendorB, ES-VendorC, ES-VendorD, and WGS exhibited the highest recall (above 95%), among which WGS stood out particularly, with recall of 99.78% for SNVs. Precision was ≥88% for all platforms (Table 2). After depth normalization, SNV recall and F1-score increased with DP and reached a plateau at approximately 20×–40× in high-performing platforms (Figure S9). WGS showed the highest overall performance, with SNV recall and F1-score approaching 100%. Among ES platforms, ES-VendorB and ES-VendorD maintained high SNV performance (recall: ES-VendorB: 97.91%; ES-VendorD: 99.45%), and ES-VendorA and ES-VendorE remained lower after depth normalization. Indel detection was more sensitive to DP and target-region design. Although indel recall and F1-score increased with depth, platform differences remained larger than those observed for SNVs. Furthermore, restricting analysis to OMIM coding regions showed that all platforms’ recall increased to over 95%, and precision was more than 92% (Table 3). Downsampling showed higher and more consistent performance across platforms (Figure S10). SNV recall rapidly plateaued at 96%–0.99% with F1-score 94%–96%. Application of TargetQC in clinical samples

Finally, TargetQC supports evaluating individual clinical NGS samples through its target exon QC, target gene QC, and variant capture QC modules. To further validate the practicality of TargetQC in real-world clinical settings, we then respectively used it to reanalyze the sequencing quality of 665 randomly selected clinical samples generated using the five ES platforms described above. The results were generally consistent with those obtained from the NA12878. First, with regard to exon quality, ES-VendorB, ES-VendorC, and ES-VendorD had the most well-covered full-captured exons (p < 0.0001) (Figure 5A; Tables S2–S9; Figures S11 and S12). In terms of OMIM gene coverage, more than 85% of OMIM genes met the coverage criterion across all ES platforms, among which ES-VendorB, ES-VendorD, and ES-VendorE all reached more than 95% (Figure 5B; Tables S11–S14; Figures S13 and S14). Moreover, for variant detection, despite the lack of clinical sample variant calling benchmark, TargetQC still can evaluate the quality of known pathogenic variant loci. First, we calculated the percentage of known pathogenic variants with DP ≥ 30× and ≤20×. Furthermore, the BAF distribution was analyzed separately for both homozygous and heterozygous variants, along with the proportion of loci exhibiting BAF ≤0.05. Case study

To evaluate the clinical utility of TargetQC, we present a representative clinical case in which the framework identified low-confidence sequencing evidence for a clinically relevant variant located within the capture region of the ES platform (Figure S16). The patient was clinically suspected to have a liver-related disorder, while the initial ES-based clinical exome analysis was negative. Importantly, the sample passed routine global sequencing QC metrics, including a mean target depth of 177.92×, 98.19% of target bases covered at ≥20×, and 93.72% covered at ≥30×. Conventional QC tools, including Picard CollectHsMetrics14 and Qualimap,15 also did not indicate an overall sequencing-quality failure.

TargetQC assessment of liver-disease-associated genes identified inadequate coverage within JAG1. Specifically, TargetQC showed that JAG1 exon 1, where the subsequently confirmed variant was located, had low-confidence sequencing quality, with 68.93% of CDS regions covered at ≥30× and 22.22% covered at ≤20×. This result indicated that the negative ES result could not reliably exclude a pathogenic variant in JAG1. Guided by the TargetQC result and the patient’s phenotype, we performed Sanger sequencing of JAG1. Sanger sequencing of JAG1 detected the pathogenic variant locus NM_000214:exon1:c.3G>A, p.Met1Ile, a variant in a gene associated with autosomal dominant Alagille syndrome 1. Inspection of the alignment data showed that the variant position was covered by only 18 reads, with an allelic depth of 5 reference reads and 13 alternate reads and had, therefore, been filtered out by the standard analysis pipeline. These findings established a molecular diagnosis that had been missed in the initial exome analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we developed TargetQC, a clinically oriented quality assessment framework designed to evaluate sequencing performance at the exon, gene, and pathogenic-variant levels. By comparing five ES platforms and one WGS platform, we observed substantial variability in coverage and variant detection across clinically relevant regions despite generally acceptable global sequencing metrics. Several high-depth ES platforms achieved more complete coverage of OMIM disease genes than standard-depth WGS, whereas recurrent poorly covered regions were associated with distinct genomic features. Pathogenic variant detection also varied across platforms, underscoring that overall sequencing metrics alone do not adequately reflect clinical performance. These findings demonstrate the value of clinically focused quality assessment and highlight the importance of evaluating sequencing quality within disease-relevant genomic regions to support reliable genetic diagnosis.

The primary strength of TargetQC is organizing coverage and variant-calling metrics into a clinically interpretable and customizable framework. Conventional quality-control tools, including Picard,14 Qualimap,15 and many variant benchmarking workflows, provide valuable information regarding overall sequencing performance. TargetQC prioritizes user-defined disease genes, clinically relevant coding regions, and known pathogenic variants, integrating sequencing coverage assessment and variant detection performance into a unified framework (Tables S15 and S16). This design enables laboratories to evaluate not only whether a sequencing run satisfies general quality requirements but also whether genomic regions relevant to a specific clinical indication have been adequately interrogated.

Our exon-level analyses revealed substantial variability in coverage across platforms and target regions. Although several ES platforms achieved near-complete coverage of intended targets, a subset of designed exons consistently exhibited suboptimal coverage. These observations are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that capture design alone cannot guarantee uniform enrichment across all genomic regions. Factors such as GC-rich sequence composition, repetitive elements, and local genomic architecture continue to influence capture efficiency and sequencing performance. Conversely, some exons not explicitly targeted by capture probes, nonetheless, achieved adequate coverage, likely due to nonspecific enrichment in homologous genomic regions. Together, these findings demonstrate that both incomplete capture of intended targets and unintended enrichment of homologous regions should be considered when interpreting sequencing results at exon resolution.

One notable finding was that several high-depth ES platforms achieved more complete coverage of OMIM coding regions than standard-depth WGS. Although WGS provides more uniform genome-wide coverage and avoids capture-related biases, clinical WGS is typically performed at substantially lower average DPs to balance diagnostic yield, throughput, and cost. In contrast, targeted enrichment concentrates sequencing reads within coding regions, enabling deeper coverage of disease-associated genes. These results suggest that, for detecting small variants in certain disease-associated coding regions, well-designed and sufficiently deep ES may provide advantages over standard-depth WGS. However, this observation should not diminish the established strengths of WGS for detecting structural variants, non-coding variants, and other classes of genomic alterations beyond the scope of exome capture.

The recurrently poorly covered genes identified in this study further illustrate the diverse mechanisms underlying sequencing failure. We investigated six genes that consistently showed poor coverage across all evaluated platforms: HLA-DRB1, POU4F1, TAF4, MAF, SORD, and UGT2B17. For POU4F1, TAF4, and MAF, poorly covered regions were characterized by markedly elevated GC content and increased overlap with repetitive sequences (Table S17). The GC content reached 85.9%, 87.4%, and 93.2%, respectively, substantially exceeding that of the corresponding exon backgrounds. These observations suggest that localized GC-rich and repetitive sequence features remain important determinants of reduced capture efficiency and sequencing performance. In contrast, poor coverage in HLA-DRB1, SORD, and UGT2B17 appeared to be driven primarily by broader genomic complexity. Extensive overlap with paralogous sequences was observed across both poorly covered regions and exon backgrounds, indicating that sequence homology may impair unique read mapping and reliable coverage estimation. Consistent with this interpretation, SORD also exhibited reduced mappability, reflecting the well-recognized challenge of distinguishing this gene from highly homologous duplicated or pseudogene-related sequences. Similarly, the poorly covered regions in UGT2B17 spanned much of the coding sequence and occurred within a structurally complex genomic context characterized by extensive paralogous sequence overlap. Collectively, these findings suggest that recurrent coverage deficiencies arise through multiple mechanisms, including extreme GC content, repetitive elements, paralogous sequence homology, reduced mappability, and broader structural complexity. Importantly, the underlying causes are often gene-specific, highlighting the need for locus-level quality assessment rather than reliance on global sequencing metrics alone.

Variant detection analyses provided additional insights into sequencing performance. Across all platforms, indel detection remained less sensitive than SNV detection, consistent with previous benchmarking studies and the known challenges of read alignment and local haplotype reconstruction in complex genomic regions. Within clinically relevant coding regions, all evaluated platforms achieved high variant-calling accuracy, although differences in sensitivity remained apparent across platforms. Notably, platform-specific differences became more pronounced when analyses focused on known pathogenic variant sites. ES-VendorB and WGS showed the highest observed accuracy in this dataset, whereas other platforms exhibited a measurable risk of missing pathogenic variants. These findings emphasize that coverage metrics alone may not fully reflect diagnostic performance and that direct evaluation of clinically relevant variant sites can provide complementary information when selecting or validating sequencing strategies.

Clinical guidelines recommend minimum DPs and other global quality metrics for exome and WGS, providing essential benchmarks for routine quality control. However, satisfactory global performance does not necessarily ensure adequate coverage of all clinically relevant regions, as sequencing quality can vary substantially across genes, exons, and pathogenic loci. Clinicians are concerned with whether genes relevant to an individual patient’s phenotype have been sequenced with sufficient depth and quality to support reliable variant detection. This limitation is particularly important, where reliable assessment of disease-associated regions is critical for distinguishing true-negative findings from technical FNs. TargetQC addresses this challenge by systematically evaluating coverage and variant detection performance within clinically relevant genes and loci.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that global sequencing quality metrics alone may not adequately reflect performance within clinically relevant genomic regions. By directly linking sequencing quality to clinically actionable targets, TargetQC extends conventional QC from technical assessment toward clinically meaningful evaluation, supporting more informed genomic analyses and precision medicine applications. Its flexible and customizable framework further enables quantitative assessment of user-defined genes, loci, and genomic intervals across a broad range of research and clinical settings.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the primary objective was to demonstrate the application scenarios and practical utility of the TargetQC framework rather than to conduct a comprehensive benchmarking study of sequencing platforms. Variant-level analyses were performed using the well-characterized NA12878 reference sample, and the number of replicates for each library preparation protocol was limited. In addition, only a subset of currently available sequencing platforms was evaluated. As sequencing technologies continue to evolve, improvements in target capture designs, sequencing chemistries, and bioinformatics pipelines may influence future platform performance. Second, DP was not uniformly normalized across platforms. Consequently, observed differences in OMIM gene coverage and pathogenic variant detection may reflect both platform-specific characteristics and depth-related effects. Third, the current implementation of TargetQC focuses primarily on coverage assessment and the evaluation of SNVs and indels. Other clinically relevant variant classes, including copy-number variants, structural variants, repeat expansions, mitochondrial variants, and mobile element insertions, were not systematically assessed and warrant further investigation. Finally, while this study demonstrates the value of TargetQC for sequencing quality assessment, its impact on diagnostic efficiency, clinical decision-making, and patient outcomes remains to be evaluated through prospective studies in real-world clinical settings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human samples

Peripheral blood was collected from 665 individuals who underwent genetic test at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University with informed consent. The age of the participants was range from 7 days to 18 years and the study cohort comprised 375 males and 290 females. Based on a minimum of 40 samples per ES platform requirement, we randomly selected the sequencing batches from those conducted during periods of stable platform operation within the study period, while considering the sex distribution of the final cohort to avoid significant sex imbalance. All samples in the selected batches were included in the analysis: 100 with ES-VendorA (57 males and 43 females), 98 with ES-VendorB (67 males and 31 females), 44 with ES-VendorC (25 males and 19 females), 285 samples with ES-VendorD (155 males and 130 females) and 138 with ES-VendorE (71 males and 67 females). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood, fragmented, and processed for exome sequencing using corresponding capture kits. All samples were de-identified in this study. TargetQC does not use sex or gender as an algorithmic input variable, and users can define the target genes or genomic regions according to their specific applications, including the option to evaluate sex chromosomes separately when appropriate. Therefore, in the assessment of clinical samples by TargetQC, since males and females differ in X chromosome copy number, sex chromosomes were evaluated separately according to sex to account for sex-related differences in sequencing coverage. Autosomal regions were analyzed using the same criteria for male and female samples; chromosome X was evaluated separately in male and female samples; and chromosome Y was evaluated only in male samples.

Ethics considerations

Clinical samples analyzed in this study were obtained from individuals who underwent clinical genetic testing at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University during September 25, 2017 to June 1, 2024, and consent was obtained as part of clinical care. The original protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Approval No. 2015-130). The TargetQC study involved a retrospective secondary analysis of existing de-identified sequencing data generated from clinical genetic testing. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Approval No.2022-234, 2022.8.22–2024.7.31), which waived the requirement for additional informed consent.

Method details

Sample collection and sequencing

This study included five ES platforms and one WGS platform, each with its own strengths. Among them, INTEGRATE DNA TECHNOLOGIES’s xGen Exome Hyb Panel v219 (abbreviated as ES-VendorA) uses 415,115 5′-biotin-modified probes to specifically target the coding sequences (CDS) of 19,435 genes in the RefSeq 109 database. Berry Genomics’ NanoWES20 (abbreviated as ES-VendorB) could cover 56.66 Mb DNA, including whole exons and exon-intron boundaries of 21,019 nuclear genes and supports automated library preparation. Illumina’s TruSeq Exome Targeted Regions Manifest v1.221 (abbreviated as ES-VendorC) targets CDS from databases such as CCDS and ACMG, focusing on pathogenic variants in ClinVar. Meanwhile, Twist Human Core Exome22 (abbreviated as ES-VendorD) and Twist Exome 2.0 Plus Comprehensive Exome Spike-in23 (abbreviated as ES-VendorE) are two panels from Twist Bioscience. Both are designed to cover clinically relevant coding and non-coding regions, integrate updated annotations from databases such as GENCODE and ClinVar, and use optimized hybrid-capture technology to achieve high target-region coverage. Finally, FulGenome24 from Fulgent (abbreviated as WGS) has a core strength in its PCR-free library preparation process. It covers SNVs, CNVs, mitochondrial variants, and non-coding regions (such as ROH and repeat expansions).

For platform benchmarking, the reference sample NA1287825 was used. A total of 13 DNA libraries were prepared: three libraries each for ES-VendorA, ES-VendorB, ES-VendorC, ES-VendorC and ES-VendorE; two libraries for WGS.

For clinical performance evaluation, 665 de-identified clinical samples were retrospectively collected from the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University with informed consent. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood, fragmented, and processed for exome sequencing using the respe ctive kits: 100 with ES-VendorA, 98 with ES-VendorB, 44 with ES-VendorC, 285 samples with ES-VendorD and 138 with ES-VendorE. All samples were anonymized immediately after collection.

Libraries were prepared following the manufacturers’ protocols. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150 bp reads under standardized experimental conditions. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 reference genome26 (NCBI Assembly: GCA_000001405.15), excluding alternative scaffolds (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCA/000/001/405/GCA_000001405.15_GRCh38_no_alt_analysis_set.fna.gz). Alignment and variant calling were conducted using the DRAGEN v4.216 pipeline.

TargetQC framework

TargetQC is an automated quality control tool that quantitatively evaluates the coverage of clinically important genes and variant detection performance in short-read ES and WGS data. Its primary objective is to provide researchers with a framework for in-depth assessment of sequencing data, including target-region coverage, variant-calling reliability, and sequencing quality at known pathogenic variant sites.

An overview of the TargetQC design is shown in Figure 1. TargetQC comprises four core functional modules: Target Exon QC, Target Gene QC, Variant Calling QC, and Variant Capture QC. To perform these analyses, users are required to provide basic sequencing data files, including aligned BAM files and variant call format (VCF) files. The exon- and gene-level QC modules additionally require a target gene list and a gene annotation file. For ES analysis, a BED file specifying the capture regions is also required. The Variant Calling QC module requires a sample gVCF file, a known pathogenic variant list, and a gold-standard dataset. The Variant Capture QC module uses a known pathogenic variant set to evaluate sequencing quality at key genomic positions. The components and workflow of each module are described below.(1)The Target Exon QC module first calculates per-base coverage across target regions using mosdepth17 and summarizes the distribution of bases across different coverage thresholds. A key function of this module is to classify exons in target genes according to user-defined coverage thresholds. For ES data, the module evaluates exon quality at two levels—capture and coverage—by incorporating a probe region BED file. At the capture level, exons are categorized as “full-capture”, “partial-capture”, “near-capture” or “no-capture” depending on their overlap with probe regions. At the coverage level, exons are classified as “well-covered” “middle-covered” or “poor-covered” based on whether they meet the preset coverage thresholds. For WGS data, only the coverage-based classification is applied. The module outputs a classification label for each exon and summarizes the counts of exons in each category.(2)The Target Gene QC module evaluates coverage quality at the gene level. It calculates the proportion of bases within user-defined coverage ranges (e.g., ≥30X, 20–30X, ≤20X) across the entire coding region of protein-coding genes or all exon regions of non-coding genes. Based on user-defined criteria (for example, genes with ≥95% of bases covered at ≥30X are classified as " well-covered" while those with >5% of bases covered at ≤20X are classified as " poor-covered"), target genes are assigned to quality categories. The module further summarizes the number of well-, middle-, and poor-covered genes across different functional categories (such as protein-coding genes and pseudogenes), thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of gene-level coverage across functionally relevant genomic regions.(3)At the variant level, TargetQC offers two evaluation modes. For samples with a gold standard (such as reference materials), the Variant Calling QC module performs rigorous benchmarking using hap.py.18 It generates core performance metrics, including recall, precision, and F1-score, for homozygous and heterozygous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions and deletions (Indels). Additionally, it analyses sequencing depth (DP) and B-allele frequency (BAF) distributions for true positive (TP), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) variants, and outputs a list of missed known pathogenic variants to assess the accuracy of the variant calling pipeline.(4)The Variant Capture QC module is designed for routine clinical samples lacking a gold standard. It indirectly evaluates the reliability of key variant sites by examining DP and BAF in each sample, thereby providing supporting evidence for variant interpretation.

Performance evaluation

To validate the utility of TargetQC and systematically compare the performance of mainstream sequencing platforms, we designed and implemented a comprehensive evaluation scheme using the NA12878 reference sample and clinical samples.

For the NA12878 reference sample, we used TargetQC to compare the performance of representative ES platforms and WGS. GENCODE release v4727 (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/gencode/Gencode_human/release_47/gencode.v47.annotation.gtf.gz) was used as the gene annotation set. The analysis included 77,077 genes, including 20,050 protein-coding genes, with the canonical transcript selected as the representative sequence for each gene. In addition, 5,021 protein-coding genes with known phenotype associations in OMIM28 (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man; https://www.omim.org, downloaded December 31, 2024) were included as the target disease-associated gene set.

TargetQC uses 20× and 30× as clinically interpretable default coverage thresholds for exon- and gene-level assessment. The 20× threshold was used to flag regions with potentially insufficient coverage, whereas the 30× threshold was used to define well-covered regions with higher confidence for germline variant detection. These thresholds are user-adjustable and can be modified according to assay validation data, sequencing strategy, target design, assay type, and clinical reporting requirements. Based on these default settings, the exon- and gene-level QC modules generated the following metrics.(1)At the exon level, exons were classified as well-covered (all bases ≥30×), poorly covered (≥5 bp with coverage ≤20×), or middle-covered (all others). The analysis included: The distribution of bases across different coverage thresholds within the target regions (capture regions for ES; exon regions of target genes for WGS); The number of exons assigned to each classification category, allowing for a direct comparison of target capture efficiency. To assess the robustness of exon-level classification, threshold-sensitivity analysis was also performed by varying the depth cutoffs used to define well-covered and poorly covered exons while keeping the corresponding classification logic unchanged.(2)At the gene level, genes were classified as well-covered if ≥ 95% of their coding bases achieved ≥30× coverage. Genes were classified as poorly covered if ≥ 5% of their coding bases had coverage ≤20×. Genes that did not meet either criterion were classified as middle-covered. Metrics included: The percentage of each gene’s region achieving specific coverage thresholds (e.g., ≥30× or ≤20×). The cumulative number of genes meeting different percentage thresholds. The count of genes classified into well-, middle-, and poor-covered grades across different gene functional types, assessing the coverage completeness of each platform from a functional genomics perspective. The intersection of high- and low-quality genes for all platforms was counted. To evaluate whether gene-level classification was driven by a single arbitrary cutoff, threshold-sensitivity analysis was performed for all genes, protein-coding genes, and OMIM protein-coding genes with defined inheritance patterns by varying both the depth thresholds and the percentage criteria used to define well-covered and poor-covered genes.(3)For variant-site coverage and detection-quality assessment, we included 290,151 pathogenic variant sites from HGMD29 (The Human Gene Mutation Database; http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk, version December 31, 2024) and 300,343 pathogenic variant sites from the ClinVar30 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar, version December 31, 2024). For variant sites with inconsistent annotations between databases, the most clinically significant annotation was retained.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), as specified in the corresponding figure legends. All statistical tests were two-sided.

For analyses of the NA12878 reference sample, the biological sample size was N = 1. Independently generated library and sequencing datasets were treated as technical replicate datasets rather than biological replicates. Three technical replicate datasets were analyzed for each exome sequencing platform, and two technical replicate datasets were analyzed for whole-genome sequencing. Exon-level and gene-level percentages were compared between sequencing platforms using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied separately within each coverage or outcome category to control the false discovery rate across pairwise platform comparisons.

For the variant-level NA12878 analyses, DP distributions at true-positive and false-negative loci and BAF distributions at true-positive and false-positive loci were compared within each sequencing dataset using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Individual variant loci were the observational units in these analyses. p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure across sequencing-dataset comparisons within each figure panel.

For analyses of the clinical samples, the individual clinical sample was considered the biological unit. Exon-level, gene-level, and pathogenic-variant coverage percentages were calculated separately for each sample and compared between platforms using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. For comparisons of BAF quality measurements, the median BAF deviation was first calculated within each clinical sample for the relevant genotype category, and these sample-level median values were compared between platforms using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Samples without a qualifying variant site for a given genotype category were excluded only from the corresponding analysis. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied across pairwise platform comparisons within each outcome category or figure panel.

Matched SNV and indel performance metrics were compared using two-sided paired t-tests. Fisher’s exact test was used for analyses of 2 × 2 categorical contingency tables. Statistical significance was defined as a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p value < 0.05 when multiple comparisons were performed and as a two-sided p value < 0.05 for prespecified single comparisons.

Asterisks in the figures indicate adjusted p values as follows: ∗∗∗∗p_adj <0.0001, ∗∗∗p_adj <0.001, ∗∗p_adj <0.01, ∗p_adj <0.05, and ns, not significant. Exact test statistics, degrees of freedom where applicable, raw and adjusted p values, group-specific sample or variant-locus numbers, 95% confidence intervals where applicable, and effect-size estimates are reported in Data S1. Hedges’ g was reported for independent two-sample t-tests, paired Cohen’s dz for paired t-tests, and rank-based effect sizes for Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. p values smaller than the numerical precision of R were reported as p < 2.22 × 10−16 rather than as p = 0.
