Characterization of the role and fate of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets in middle ear regeneration
Authors: Kazuhisa Yamamoto, Tsunetaro Morino, Nobuki Fukuda, Yuki Takizawa, Shun Kikuchi, Yutaka Yamamoto, Masayuki Yamato, Hiromi Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid and functional regeneration of the middle ear mucosa after surgery is essential for maintaining postoperative aeration and improving long-term outcomes in middle ear diseases such as cholesteatoma. Although nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation has shown clinical efficacy, the in vivo behavior, fate of transplanted cells, and the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.MethodsA middle ear mucosal removal model was established in immunodeficient rats. EGFP-labeled nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets were transplanted onto the denuded bone surface of the middle ear cavity. The engraftment, persistence, and fate of transplanted cells were evaluated longitudinally using macroscopic fluorescence imaging, histological and immunohistochemical analyses, and in vivo micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). To identify an optimal cell source for middle ear mucosal regeneration, oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ASC) sheets were also transplanted and comparatively analyzed.ResultsNasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets rapidly engrafted onto the exposed middle ear bone surface and formed a continuous epithelial covering. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated preservation of middle ear aeration in the nasal cell sheet group, whereas progressive aeration loss and bone overgrowth were observed in the mucosal removal and ASC groups. Histological analyses revealed that most transplanted nasal epithelial cells were gradually replaced by host-derived middle ear mucosa, indicating a transient scaffold-like role rather than permanent epithelial replacement. In contrast, oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets showed epithelial stratification and keratinization, while ASC sheets induced early bone hyperplasia and middle ear cavity narrowing.ConclusionsNasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets suppress pathological wound healing responses, including reactive bone formation, by temporarily covering the exposed middle ear bone surface and promoting regeneration of host-derived middle ear mucosa. Among the tested cell sources, nasal mucosal epithelium was the most suitable for middle ear mucosal regeneration. These findings provide valuable insights into the behavior of transplanted cells and a scientific basis for clinical approaches aimed at improving postoperative outcomes in middle ear surgery.

Introduction

Middle ear cholesteatoma is a representative intractable middle ear disease characterized by progressive bone destruction and invasion of surrounding structures. Surgical removal remains the only definitive treatment option. The ideal postoperative condition of the middle ear is the restoration of a physiologically aerated cavity that closely resembles the native anatomical state. However, to achieve complete removal of the lesion, extensive resection of the middle ear mucosa is often required during surgery, which frequently results in impaired postoperative aeration of the middle ear cavity. This condition contributes to disease recurrence and poor postoperative hearing outcomes [[1], [2], [3]]. Therefore, the rapid and functional regeneration of the middle ear mucosa is crucial for improving long-term outcomes following middle ear surgery, including cholesteatoma surgery. Despite its clinical importance, no established therapeutic strategy exists to promote effective mucosal regeneration.

Against this background, we have developed a novel regenerative therapy using autologous cultured nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets as a cell source and have successfully translated this approach into a clinical application for refractory middle ear diseases [1,4]. Previous clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated that the transplantation of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets promotes postoperative aeration of the middle ear cavity and contributes to improved middle ear ventilation [5,6]. The nasal mucosal epithelium was selected as a cell source because it shares an embryological origin and physiological characteristics with the middle ear mucosa, as part of the respiratory epithelial lineage. Furthermore, nasal mucosal tissue can be harvested in a minimally invasive manner and has already been clinically applied in regenerative therapies for middle ear disease. Based on these properties, the nasal mucosal epithelium is considered a rational and clinically suitable cell source for middle ear mucosal regeneration. However, the engraftment pattern, long-term fate of transplanted cells, and detailed mechanisms underlying their therapeutic effects remain insufficiently elucidated.

In particular, it remains unclear whether transplanted epithelial cells persist long-term within the middle ear cavity or are eventually replaced by host-derived middle ear mucosa. Furthermore, it is unknown whether temporary coverage of the exposed middle ear bone surface by the transplanted cell sheet plays a key role in modulating the wound healing response. When the middle ear mucosa is lost and bone surfaces are exposed, bone-derived mesenchymal cells migrate into the cavity, leading to granulation tissue formation and reactive bone hyperplasia, which subsequently narrows the middle ear space [[5], [6], [7]]. Elucidating how cell sheet transplantation influences these pathological processes is essential for optimizing the regenerative strategies.

In the present study, we established a middle ear mucosal injury model using immunodeficient rats and transplanted GFP-labeled nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets into the middle ear cavity. This approach enables direct and longitudinal observation of the behavior and fate of transplanted cells in vivo. In addition, to determine the most appropriate cell source for middle ear mucosal regeneration, we conducted comparative analyses using nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets, oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets, and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell [ASC] sheets. This study aimed to elucidate the mechanism of action of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation and identify the optimal cell source for middle ear mucosal regeneration.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experimental procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tokyo Women's Medical University (approval number: AE21-117) and Jikei University School of Medicine (approval numbers: 2018-012 and 2022-027). Donor cells were obtained from SD-Tg(CAG-EGFP) rats (8 weeks old, male; SLC, Tokyo, Japan; hereafter referred to as EGFP rats), which ubiquitously express enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) to facilitate the tracking of transplanted cells. To prevent immunological rejection, nude rats (F344/NJcl-rnu/rnu) (8 weeks old, male; total n = 36; CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were used for the middle ear mucosal removal and transplantation models. The animals were assigned to one of four experimental groups: the Nasal group (transplantation of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets), the Oral group (transplantation of oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets), the ASC group (transplantation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell [ASC] sheets), and the Removal group (mucosal removal only as a control). For quantitative evaluation using micro-computed tomography (CT) at 56 days post-operation, the Nasal (n = 5), Removal (n = 4), and ASC (n = 4) groups were analyzed.

Fabrication of cell sheets

Nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets

Nasal mucosal tissues were harvested from EGFP rats after humane euthanasia. The tissues were subjected to primary explant culture in keratinocyte culture medium (KCM) supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) using standard culture dishes (Primaria Dish, 60-mm diameter; Corning, NY, USA). The KCM was composed of a basal mixture of Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) and DMEM with Ham's F-12 medium (Gibco) at a 1:1 ratio, supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Life Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA), 0.3 mM saxizon (Takeda yakuhin kogyo, Osaka, Japan), 140.0 mU/mL insulin (Humulin, Novo Nordisk, Bagsvaerd, Denmark), 2.0 nM triiodothyronine (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA), 0.2 μM epidermal growth factor (Higeta-Shoyu, Tokyo, Japan), 1.0 nM cholera toxin (Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan), 100 U/mL penicillin, 69 mM streptomycin (Wako Pure Chemicals), and 0.4 μg/mL of amphotericin B (Bristol-Myers Squibb, NY, USA). After 12 days of culture at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, the outgrown cells were harvested and cryopreserved at −80 °C using CELLBANKERⅠ (Zenoaq, Fukushima, Japan). To fabricate nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets, cryopreserved cells were thawed and seeded onto temperature-responsive culture dishes (CellSeed, Tokyo, Japan) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/cm2. After 7 days of subculture, intact nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets were harvested by reducing the incubation temperature to 20 °C for 30 min.

Oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets

The buccal mucosa was harvested from EGFP rats and treated with 1000 U/mL dispase (Godo Shusei, Tokyo, Japan) for 2 h at 37 °C to isolate the epithelial layer. The separated epithelium was dissociated into a single-cell suspension using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. The cells were then seeded onto temperature-responsive cell culture inserts (CellSeed) at a density of 4 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured in KCM for 7 days. Finally, intact oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets were harvested by reducing the incubation temperature to 20 °C for 30 min.

Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ASC) sheets

Inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissue was harvested from EGFP rat and enzymatically digested with 0.1% type II collagenase (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA). The isolated cells were cultured in DMEM with 20% FBS, and cells at passages 3–4 were used. To fabricate ASC sheets, 1.0 × 106 cells were seeded onto 35-mm temperature-responsive culture dishes and cultured for 2 days in a medium supplemented with 82 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Wako Pure Chemical Industries). Finally, intact ASC sheets were harvested by reducing the incubation temperature to 20 °C for 30 min.

Cell culture and microscopic observation

Throughout the culture period, cell morphology and confluence were regularly monitored using phase-contrast microscopy. All cell culture procedures were performed at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Viable cell count and colony-forming assay

To evaluate the proliferative potential of the cells within the fabricated cell sheets, viable cell count and colony-forming assays were performed as previously described [8]. Live cells harvested from the cell sheets were seeded onto NIH-3T3 cells treated with mitomycin C (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) to detect clonal growth of epithelial cells. After 12 days of culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to visualize colonies. Colony-forming efficiency (CFE) was calculated by dividing the number of observed colonies by the initial number of seeded cells.

Middle ear mucosal removal model and transplantation

Anesthesia was induced via intramuscular injection of a three-drug cocktail consisting of midazolam (Astellas, Tokyo, Japan), medetomidine (Zenoaq, Fukushima, Japan), and butorphanol (Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan). Following a retroauricular incision, the middle ear bulla was exposed under a surgical microscope with careful preservation of the facial nerve and surrounding blood vessels. A small bony window (less than 2 mm in diameter) was created in the middle ear bone using an 18-gauge needle. Through this window, the mucosal layer of the inferior portion of the middle ear cavity was meticulously removed using a copper needle and round knife. During the procedure, the integrity of the tympanic membrane, ossicles, Eustachian tube, and inner ear structures was maintained.

For the transplantation groups, each harvested cell sheet was trimmed to approximately 4–5 mm square. The sheet was then carefully inserted through the bony window and transplanted onto the denuded bone surface of the middle ear cavity. Following transplantation (or mucosal removal alone in the control group), the bony window was sealed with silicone putty (Exafine; GC, Tokyo, Japan), and the surgical wound was closed.

Micro-computed tomography (CT) analysis

To assess the temporal changes in middle ear aeration and the progression of new bone formation, in vivo micro-CT imaging was performed using the Stella Scan system (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) at 90 kV and 160 μA. Scans were conducted on postoperative days 3, 7, 14, 28, and 56. The acquired DICOM datasets were reconstructed to define a specific region of interest (ROI) centered on the mucosal removal and transplantation site. To ensure longitudinal consistency and reproducibility, the XYZ axes were aligned for each subject to establish the bilateral symmetry of the middle ear structures. The inner ear was used as a stable anatomical landmark to standardize the cross-sectional plane across all time points. Within this standardized plane, the middle ear aeration was quantified by measuring the cross-sectional area of the air-filled space (air density). The aeration ratio was calculated as the percentage of the treated middle ear area relative to the contralateral, non-operated control side, serving as an index of bone overgrowth-induced stenosis.

Macroscopic observation of transplanted cell sheets

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) and euthanized on postoperative days 3, 7, 14, 28, and 56. Middle ear specimens were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, and decalcified in 10% EDTA at 4 °C for 14 days. To examine the internal environment of the middle ear cavity and the engraftment of EGFP-expressing cell sheets, decalcified bullae were carefully sectioned at the site of the bony window using a scalpel. The macroscopic distribution of the transplanted sheets and their fluorescence were observed and captured using a fluorescence stereomicroscope [MVX10; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan].

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Following macroscopic observation, the specimens were embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm-thick longitudinal sections. For morphological assessment, sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining according to conventional protocols. For immunohistochemical analysis, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Antigen retrieval was performed using either proteinase K digestion (Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) or heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6; Dako) at 125 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with a blocking solution (S2023; Dako) for 30 min, followed by incubation with a protein blocking reagent for 1 h at room temperature to minimize non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-pancytokeratin (AE1/AE3; 1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab27988) or mouse monoclonal anti-vimentin (V9; 1:100; Dako; GA63061-2J). Detection was performed using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody system (EnVision; Dako) for 30 min and visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Brightfield images of H&E and immunohistochemically stained sections were captured using an Axio Cam camera and analyzed using the AxioVision software (version 4.8; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Fluorescence images were captured to evaluate the integration of EGFP-expressing cells at the cellular level. Cell nuclei were visualized using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) counterstaining. All histological and immunofluorescence images were analyzed using a BZ-X700 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Statistics

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of cell sheet transplantation on middle ear aeration at 56 days post-operation, statistical differences among the three experimental groups (Nasal, Removal, and ASC groups) were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For subsequent multiple comparisons between individual groups, Tukey's post-hoc test was applied. The normality of the data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to ANOVA. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical computations were performed using the JMP software package (version 13; SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan).

Macroscopic engraftment of nasal epithelial cell sheets in the middle ear cavity and longitudinal micro-CT observations

A nude rat model of middle ear mucosal removal and sheet transplantation was established, and EGFP-labeled nasal epithelial sheets were transplanted onto the denuded bone surface through a small bony window (Fig. 2A). Macroscopic fluorescence imaging demonstrated that, at the early postoperative stage (day 3, n = 4), EGFP-positive cell sheets were widely preserved over the transplantation site, with clearly detectable fluorescence signals. Although there was some interindividual variability in the extent of signal retention, approximately half of the initially transplanted area was generally maintained at this time point. By postoperative day 7, the EGFP-positive area had markedly decreased to less than 10% of the initial area. On days 14 and 28 (days 7–28, total n = 9), only limited island-like fluorescent signals were observed, indicating persistent but restricted retention of transplanted cells. At the mid-term time point of postoperative day 56 (n = 5), replacement by the surrounding middle ear mucosa progressed further. Discussion

In the present study, we utilized a rat middle ear mucosal injury model to comprehensively analyze the in vivo behavior and fate of transplanted nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets and clarify their specific regenerative effects on the exposed middle ear bone surface. Although previous clinical studies have demonstrated that nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation contributes to improved postoperative aeration of the middle ear cavity [1,4], the underlying mechanisms and long-term fate of transplanted cells have not been fully elucidated. The present findings provide important mechanistic insights into these unresolved issues.

One possible mechanism underlying this process is that the transplanted epithelial cell sheet rapidly covers the exposed bone surface, thereby suppressing mesenchymal cell invasion and pathological wound healing responses. Such an environment may facilitate the migration of host-derived epithelial cells from the surrounding mucosa. Furthermore, these host cells gradually replace the transplanted cells over time. Using GFP-labeled cell sheets, we confirmed that the nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets rapidly adhered to the exposed middle ear bone surface and formed a continuous epithelial covering. Following middle ear mucosal loss, exposed bone surfaces permit the migration of periosteum- or bone-derived mesenchymal cells, leading to granulation tissue formation and reactive bone hyperplasia [[5], [6], [7]]. In this study, these pathological changes were clearly suppressed at the sites covered by epithelial cell sheets. These findings suggest that early epithelialization of the exposed bone surface itself plays a crucial role in interrupting the initiation of pathological wound healing responses and preventing aberrant remodeling of the middle ear cavity.

Longitudinal observations further revealed that most transplanted nasal mucosal epithelial cells did not persist long-term but were gradually replaced by host-derived middle ear mucosa. This indicates that cell sheet transplantation does not function as a permanent epithelial replacement therapy; rather, it likely serves as a biocompatible temporary scaffold that provides a favorable regenerative microenvironment during the early postoperative phase. Similar phenomena, in which transplanted epithelial cells are eventually replaced by host tissue, have been reported in regenerative therapies of the esophagus [9]. The present results are consistent with these observations and support the concept that transient epithelial coverage is sufficient to modulate tissue repair. Although the disappearance of GFP fluorescence suggests replacement by host-derived tissue, GFP signal loss alone cannot definitively prove complete cellular replacement, as signal attenuation or cell death may also contribute to this phenomenon. However, the progressive reduction of the GFP signal in combination with histological findings supports the interpretation that host-derived mucosa gradually replaces the transplanted cells.

Interestingly, a small population of transplanted cells persisted within the middle ear cavity and exhibited differentiation toward a flattened respiratory epithelial–like phenotype lacking goblet and ciliated cells. This finding suggests that nasal mucosal epithelial cells possess a high degree of plasticity and can alter their phenotype in response to changes in the local microenvironment. Environment-dependent differentiation of respiratory epithelial cells has been described in studies of the nasal and airway epithelium [10], and our findings extend this concept to the context of middle ear mucosal regeneration.

Comparative analyses using different cell sources further demonstrated that epithelial lineage identity and histological and functional compatibility with the native middle ear mucosa are critical determinants of successful regeneration. Oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets have been widely applied in regenerative medicine, including stem cell–based treatment for limbal stem cell deficiency of the cornea and prevention of esophageal stricture following endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) [11,12]. Similarly, adipose tissue–derived regenerative therapies have been developed for various conditions, such as complex perianal fistulas associated with Crohn's disease, and ASC sheets have shown therapeutic efficacy in models of bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, acute kidney injury, and cerebral ischemia [[13], [14], [15]]. These cell sources are commonly used to enhance wound healing and tissue repair.

Although oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets partially suppressed bone hyperplasia in our model, they exhibited epithelial stratification and keratinization in the middle ear cavity. As the middle ear mucosa is responsible for transmucosal gas exchange, a thin, flattened epithelial architecture is required for optimal function. Stratification and keratinization of the oral mucosal epithelium may impair this function. Moreover, excessive stratification and keratinization could theoretically increase the risk of cholesteatoma formation.

In contrast, transplantation of ASC sheets resulted in the early induction of bone hyperplasia and eventual narrowing of the middle ear cavity. Mesenchymal stem cells are widely recognized to possess angiogenic and wound healing–promoting properties; however, they also have inherent differentiation potential toward osteogenic and chondrogenic lineages [16,17]. In the unique environment of the exposed middle ear bone, ASCs may have preferentially promoted osteogenesis rather than mucosal regeneration. In this study, although we did not directly confirm whether transplanted ASCs differentiated into osteogenic cells, the observed bone hyperplasia suggests that ASC-derived signals stimulated bone formation from the middle ear bone surface. Given that mesenchymal stem cells are of mesodermal origin and intrinsically predisposed to differentiate into bone, cartilage, and adipose tissue, their use for mucosal regeneration in the middle ear may not be optimal.

Collectively, among the cell sources examined, nasal mucosal epithelial cells were the most suitable for middle ear mucosal regeneration. Nasal mucosal epithelium originates from the respiratory epithelium and shares developmental and functional compatibility with the middle ear mucosa. Furthermore, the observed regenerative pattern —temporary coverage of the exposed bone to suppress pathological wound healing, followed by replacement with host-derived mucosa—represents a biologically rational and potentially safe mechanism of tissue repair. These findings are consistent with previously reported favorable clinical outcomes of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation [1,4].

The results of this study suggest that the critical period for therapeutic efficacy lies in the early postoperative phase, particularly within the first few weeks, during which rapid epithelialization of the exposed bone surface is essential for suppressing pathological wound healing. From a clinical perspective, early postoperative evaluation of middle ear aeration may be important for predicting subsequent middle ear environmental outcome.

This study has several limitations. The rat middle ear cavity is extremely small, which limits comprehensive functional assessments compared to larger animal models such as rabbits. Nevertheless, this model provides a unique advantage by allowing direct observation of cellular behavior on the exposed middle ear bone surfaces. Therefore, it offers valuable mechanistic insights that are difficult to obtain from clinical studies or large animal models. In clinical settings, such as cholesteatoma surgery, the middle ear environment is often associated with inflammation. Although the bone exposure model in nude rats used in this study does not fully replicate the chronic inflammatory conditions observed in cholesteatoma, it effectively mimics the “bone-exposed state” that induces reactive bone hyperplasia and allows for the isolated evaluation of the effect of epithelial coverage on bone healing response. Therefore, this model provides important mechanistic insights into the role of epithelialization in regulating the wound healing process in the middle ear.

Conclusion

The present study clarified both the therapeutic effects of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation and the fate of transplanted cells in a rat model of middle ear mucosal injury. Through comparative transplantation experiments using cell sheets derived from the oral mucosa and adipose tissue, our findings indicate that the nasal mucosal epithelium is the most appropriate cell source for middle ear mucosal regeneration.

Nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation suppressed reactive bone hyperplasia on the exposed middle ear bone surface while promoting the regeneration of host-derived middle ear mucosa. These findings provide important mechanistic evidence supporting the regenerative effects of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets and strengthen the scientific foundation for regenerative medicine strategies aimed at improving long-term outcomes after middle ear surgery.
