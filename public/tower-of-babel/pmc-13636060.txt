Re: Chujo et al.: Photoreceptor Density-Dependent Kinetics of Geographic Atrophy Progression
Authors: Sun Young Lee
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Chujo et al1 proposed a photoreceptor density-based framework for understanding the spatial kinetics of geographic atrophy (GA). Using theoretical cone and rod density maps derived from human histology, they demonstrated that GA enlargement follows a spatial gradient across the foveal, parafoveal, and perifoveal regions, with the slowest progression in the central 0- to 0.5-mm ring, maximal expansion within the parafoveal 1- to 3-mm rings, and attenuation in the outermost 3- to 5-mm region. They further showed that weighting lesion border displacement by local photoreceptor density reduced the apparent directional asymmetry of lesion expansion. These findings provide an elegant anatomy-informed framework and introduce photoreceptor density as a biologically informed anatomical parameter for interpreting regional GA progression.

These observations may also be interpreted within a broader framework of regional retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) support workload, extending the established concept that GA originates from dysfunction of the RPE–Bruch membrane–choriocapillaris complex. Because the RPE forms a single-cell epithelial monolayer that supports multiple photoreceptors, the physiological burden experienced by each RPE cell is likely shaped by several spatially organized anatomical determinants, including the number and identity of the photoreceptors it supports, together with regional variation in RPE cell density and morphology.2 Together, these factors determine the metabolic, retinoid recycling, phagocytic, transport, and homeostatic demands placed upon the RPE. These determinants also vary systematically from the fovea to the parafovea and perifovea, paralleling the regional pattern of GA expansion. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that the RPE itself exhibits region-specific structural and molecular specialization, suggesting that regional RPE biology may contribute to differential susceptibility across the macula.3 Thus, because photoreceptor density and other biological factors, including choriocapillaris and macular pigment density, exhibit overlapping spatial gradients across the macula, the independent contribution of photoreceptor density to regional GA progression remains to be determined.

The biological consequences of this workload are also unlikely to be equivalent for rods and cones. Although both photoreceptor classes depend critically on the RPE for outer-segment renewal, retinoid recycling, nutrient transport, lipid homeostasis, and waste removal, cones benefit from an additional Müller cell–mediated support system. Beyond structural and metabolic support, Müller cells participate in an auxiliary intraretinal visual cycle that complements the canonical RPE visual cycle for cones. Rods, in contrast, rely predominantly on the RPE for chromophore regeneration and consistently exhibit earlier functional impairment when RPE physiology becomes compromised. Delayed rod-mediated dark adaptation, extensively characterized by Curcio et al, is among the earliest functional manifestations of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and is widely interpreted as evidence of impaired RPE function preceding irreversible photoreceptor degeneration.4 Retinal pigment epithelium support workload may be particularly relevant within the cone–rod transition zone, where individual RPE cells simultaneously support mixed cone and rod populations, integrating the distinct metabolic and retinoid-processing demands of both photoreceptor classes. This perspective may also help reconcile the apparent paradox of the central fovea. Despite its exceptionally high cone density, foveal cones remain relatively resistant to early degeneration, and central visual acuity is often preserved until late-stage GA. Instead, regional vulnerability may reflect differences in the resilience of the outer retinal support unit, comprising photoreceptors, RPE, Müller cells, and the underlying choriocapillaris. The specialized organization of the fovea, including regional RPE specialization, Müller cell-mediated cone support, dense choriocapillaris perfusion, and high macular pigment density, likely provides greater physiological reserve than extrafoveal zones.

Finally, this work highlights an important opportunity to bridge clinical imaging with molecular retinal biology. Although single-cell transcriptomic atlases have transformed our understanding of retinal cell types and states, dissociation-based approaches inherently lose spatial context and, therefore, cannot explain why cells with similar molecular identities exhibit markedly different susceptibilities across the uniquely organized human macula, a specialized structure not faithfully recapitulated in rodent models. Rapid advances in spatial transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and multiplex molecular imaging now preserve tissue architecture while simultaneously enabling high-throughput, spatially resolved molecular profiling.5 Integrating these approaches with longitudinal clinical imaging and anatomy-based models such as that proposed by Chujo et al may reveal region-specific RPE programs, photoreceptor–RPE–choroid interactions, and molecular mechanisms underlying the spatial gradient of vulnerability across the human macula. More broadly, combining imaging-derived natural history with spatially resolved molecular profiling may establish a mechanistic framework for understanding macular aging and the pathogenesis of AMD beyond GA, while enabling the identification of region-specific molecular pathways that could inform more precisely targeted therapies.
