Factors associated with executive function in children with lymphoma undergoing chemotherapy: A cross-sectional study based on the theory of unpleasant symptoms
Authors: Xinyu Guo, Mengxue He, Jiwen Sun, Nanping Shen, Ying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the factors influencing executive function of pediatric lymphoma patients based on the theory of unpleasant symptoms, across three dimensions: physiological, psychological, and situational.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted involving 215 pairs of pediatric lymphoma patients and caregivers between October 2024 and October 2025. Patients completed the Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21, while caregivers provided demographic information and completed the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, the Feetham Family Functioning Survey and the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function. Descriptive statistics, univariate analysis, Pearson correlation analysis, and hierarchical multiple regression were used for statistical analyses.ResultsThe Global Executive Composite score was 106.78 ± 21.61. Higher levels of patients’ anxiety (β = 0.249, P = 0.015) and stress (β = 0.245, P = 0.014) were significantly associated with greater executive function impairment. Situational factors, including having fathers as primary caregivers (β = 0.121, P = 0.038), non-only child status (β = 0.129, P = 0.027), lower family income (β = −0.140, P = 0.047), passive caregiver coping style (β = 0.147, P = 0.020) and poorer family functioning (β = 0.185, P = 0.002) were also significant independent predictors. The final model explained 45.2% of the variance in executive function.ConclusionsExecutive function impairment of pediatric lymphoma patients was influenced by the interaction of physiological, psychological, and situational factors. These findings underscored the necessity for comprehensive interventions that not only treat the disease but also address psychological stress and provide guidance for families, to preserve children's cognitive function.

Introduction

Pediatric lymphoma, one of the most common malignant tumors in children, encompasses Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) two major categories.1 With continuous advances in pediatric oncology treatment and optimized supportive care, significant progress has been made in treatment efficacy, with 5-year survival rates now exceeding 85%.2, 3, 4 However, children and adolescents who achieve long-term survival after antineoplastic therapy may experience a higher proportion of treatment-related delayed complications compared to adult patients, with executive function impairment being one notable concern. Executive function represents a higher-order cognitive process involving the conscious regulation of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors.5 Definitions of executive function classification vary across disciplines. Pennington and Ozonoff proposed that executive function encompasses three dimensions: working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility.6 Conversely, Gioia suggested that it included multiple components, including inhibitory control, initiation, switching, planning, organization, self-monitoring, emotional regulation, and working memory.7 For children, executive function serves as the foundation for learning, social interaction, behavioral control, and emotional regulation, playing a critical role in overall development.8

Evidence from large-scale cohort studies has highlighted the substantial burden of executive function impairment in childhood lymphoma survivors. The U.S. Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) cohort9 reported that 5.4% of long-term childhood lymphoma survivors exhibited emotional regulation disorders, 4.4% demonstrated reduced organizational abilities, and 5.8% experienced working memory difficulties. Data from the St. Jude Lifetime (SJLIFE) cohort studies10,11 further revealed that two-thirds of childhood NHL survivors had at least mild executive function impairment, with 18% experiencing moderate to severe impairment, particularly in working memory and cognitive flexibility. One-third of childhood HL survivors demonstrated executive function impairment, notably in working memory. Collectively, these findings indicate that childhood lymphoma survivors perform markedly worse on executive function measures compared to the general population.

The consequences of executive function impairment extend across multiple domains of daily function. Impaired executive function not only affects children's daily activities and academic achievement,12 but also impacts their future social interaction abilities and emotional development.13 Declines in executive function directly reduce children's autonomy in daily life, thereby lowering their quality of life.10 Frequent medical interventions and sustained psychological stress make it difficult for pediatric lymphoma survivors to concentrate on learning, affecting long-term academic outcomes and career development.14 Furthermore, executive function impairment poses challenges for pediatric patients in expressing emotions, navigating interpersonal relationships, and coping with social pressures, hindering their social integration and self-confidence development.15

Despite the significant impacts of executive function impairment on the quality of life, academic performance, and social adaptation of pediatric lymphoma patients, research in this area remains relatively limited. Most existing studies focus on cognitive outcomes among survivors, generally conducting follow-up assessments after treatment completion rather than during active treatment, with follow-up intervals extending from six months to several years or beyond.16 However, evidence also suggests that executive function deficits emerge early during treatment. Children with lymphoma undergoing chemotherapy cycles 3 to 6 already exhibited significant deficits compared to healthy peers, particularly in attention control, memory, planning, and task switching.17 After approximately one year of ongoing treatment, parents of pediatric lymphoma patients reported concerns about their children's working memory and metacognitive abilities.18 Although some patients experience partial recovery of executive functions after treatment, many face persistent impairment that affect learning and daily living skills.10 Consequently, early assessments and interventions are crucial for improving the quality of life for these children.

Prior studies have identified multiple factors influencing executive function in pediatric cancer patients, including demographic characteristics such as age at initial diagnosis and gender19,20; disease-specific features such as tumor subtype and disease severity21,22; treatment-related factors including cranial radiotherapy14; individual psychological distress, primarily anxiety and depression23; and family-level factors such as parental education level, family income, family members’ coping styles and overall family function.24,25 While disease- and treatment-related factors affecting executive function have been extensively studied,26 the contributions of individual psychological factors and family or social environmental conditions have not received commensurate attention or in-depth investigation. Cancer diagnosis and treatment impose varying degrees of psychosocial burden on pediatric patients,27 and repeated hospitalizations with prolonged treatment further disrupt peer social interactions and reduce social engagement,28 all of which may adversely affect executive function.

Executive function impairment in pediatric lymphoma patients cannot be fully explained by neurotoxicity or disease burden alone. Evidence indicates that cognitive outcomes are influenced by biological conditions, emotional distress, and environmental factors.29 However, existing research predominantly focused on single physiological factors, without integrating psychological and contextual factors into a comprehensive analytical framework. This gap may hinder the identification of effective multidimensional intervention targets. To address this limitation, the present study applied the theory of unpleasant symptoms (TOUS) to examine factors associated with executive function impairment in pediatric lymphoma patients during chemotherapy.

TOUS was proposed by Lenz30 in 1995. The theory posits that symptom outcomes arise from the interaction of physiological, psychological, and situational factors. Physiological factors encompass objectively existing bodily functions and disease-related conditions; psychological factors cover individual emotional responses and mental states such as anxiety, depression, and stress; and situational factors include social contextual elements that influence symptom perception such as lifestyle, family environment, social support and healthcare context.31 Within the TOUS framework, executive function impairment can be conceptualized as a performance outcome resulting from the combined effects of biological vulnerability, emotional distress, and social-environmental factors.

Compared with traditional biomedical approaches that focus primarily on pathophysiological mechanisms, TOUS offers a more comprehensive and multidimensional perspective. It is particularly suited to pediatric oncology, where treatment-related neurotoxicity interacts with psychosocial stressors during sensitive periods of cognitive development. By integrating physiological, psychological, and situational domains, TOUS enables systematic examination of both medical and psychosocial determinants of executive function.

In this study, TOUS guided the selection and classification of variables into physiological, psychological, and situational domains. It structured the hierarchical regression analysis, with variables entered sequentially according to these domains. It also provided a theoretical basis for interpreting the findings by examining how factors from different domains were associated with executive function impairment (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Theoretical framework.Fig. 1

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the contributions of physiological, psychological, and situational factors to executive function, thereby providing new insights for the development of tailored psychological support and cognitive rehabilitation strategies.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey design was adopted, adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines32 to ensure methodological rigor and comprehensive reporting of observational research findings.

Participants and sampling

The study enrolled pediatric patients diagnosed with lymphoma who were hospitalized in the Department of Hematology and Oncology at a Shanghai Children's Medical Center between October 2024 and October 2025.

Inclusion criteria for pediatric patients were as follows: (1) newly diagnosed with lymphoma; (2) currently hospitalized for treatment and accompanied by a primary caregiver; and (3) aged 8–18 years, as patients aged ≥ 8 years are considered capable of self-reporting. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of neurological disorders affecting behavioral development, such as cerebral palsy, mental retardation, or Down syndrome; (2) recurrent lymphoma; and (3) unable to participate in the assessment due to clinical instability requiring emergency resuscitation or intensive care support.

Primary caregivers were eligible if they were adults primarily responsible for the children's daily care during hospitalization, had the most frequent contact with the children, and could understand and complete the questionnaires. Caregivers with limited literacy, cognitive impairment, or communication difficulties were excluded.

Sample size was estimated using Kendall's method33 for multiple linear regression, which recommends 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. Given that 20 independent variables were included and accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the required sample size ranged from 120 to 240. A total of 215 valid pediatric patient-caregiver dyads were included.

Instruments

Instrument selection was guided by the TOUS framework. Physiological variables were obtained from demographic data and medical records. Psychological factors were measured using the Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21. Situational factors were captured using demographic and social characteristics, as well as the Feetham Family Functioning Survey and the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, which assess socioeconomic status, caregiver characteristics, caregiver coping styles and family functioning. Executive function was assessed using the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function-Parent Version.

Performance-based neuropsychological tests are often considered the gold standards for measuring executive function. However, these tests require a lot of time, trained staff, and sustained patient concentration, which limit feasibility for children undergoing active chemotherapy. Fatigue, nausea, and psychological distress can affect test performance and increase children's assessment burden. The BRIEF was therefore selected because it assesses executive function as manifested in everyday life for its ecological validity. As TOUS emphasizes that symptom outcomes are embedded within contextual conditions, a caregiver-reported measure capturing real-life functioning is considered theoretically consistent and clinically appropriate, as it reflects children's functional behavior in naturalistic settings.(1)General demographic characteristics

Disease characteristics and treatment information

Disease- and treatment-related variables identified in the literature as potential influences on executive function were assessed, including lymphoma type and stage, disease severity, duration of the disease, and number of chemotherapy cycles completed. Disease severity was graded according to the Children's Oncology Group (COG) lymphoma risk stratification criteria. Relevant clinical information was extracted by the research team from the hospital's electronic medical records and nursing documentation.(3)Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

(3)Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

The SCSQ was developed by Chinese scholar Xie Yaning,34 and is widely used in Chinese population, with good reliability and validity. The scale consists of 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), comprising active coping (12 items) and passive coping (8 items). Subscale scores are calculated as the mean of corresponding items, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward that coping style. In this study, it was completed by the primary caregiver to reflect the caregiver's personal coping style. Previous study reported Cronbach's α values of 0.90 for the total scale, 0.89 for active coping, and 0.78 for passive coping.34 In this study, the Cronbach's α was 0.813 for active coping, 0.771 for passive coping, and 0.772 for the full scale.(4)Feetham Family Functioning Survey (FFFS)

(4)Feetham Family Functioning Survey (FFFS)

Feetham Family Functioning Survey (FFFS)

The Chinese Version of FFFS was employed to assess family functioning in families of children with chronic illness.35 The scale comprises 25 items, each containing three components: (a) Current Situation; (b) Desired Situation; and (c) Importance to You. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The current and desired situation components range from "hardly ever" to "extremely often", while the importance component ranges from "not important" to "extremely important". Following Porter's scoring method, three direct scores (a, b, and c) and one indirect score (d) are generated. The indirect score (d), calculated as the discrepancy between the current and desired situation scores (a - b), reflects dissatisfaction with family functioning. The total score ranges from 0 to 150, with higher scores indicating greater dissatisfaction with family functioning. The indirect (d) scores were used in all analyses. The scale demonstrates good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.87.35 In this study, its Cronbach's α was 0.817.(5)Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)

(5)Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)

Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 is a self-reported scale assessing three dimensions of negative emotional states: depression, anxiety, and stress.36 Each subscale contains 7 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), and subscale scores are obtained by multiplying the sum of item scores by 2. The score for each subscale ranges from 0 to 42. Higher scores indicate greater severity of the respective emotional state. The Chinese version of the DASS-21 has been validated for use in pediatric cancer patients aged 8–18 years, with a Cronbach's α of 0.916.37 In this study, its Cronbach's α was 0.910.(6)Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function - Parent Form (BRIEF)

(6)Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function - Parent Form (BRIEF)

Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function - Parent Form (BRIEF)

The BRIEF was originally developed by Gioia7 in 2000, and subsequently introduced to China by Qian Ying38 in the early 2000s. It has demonstrated good ecological validity and reliability within the Chinese cultural context and can assess children's executive function levels in daily life.38 The BRIEF Parent Form (5–18 years) is comprised of 86 items, including 72 clinical items which are organized into two broad indices: the Behavioral Regulation Index (BRI) and the Metacognitive Index (MI). The BRI encompasses three dimensions: Inhibit (10 items), Shift (8 items), and Emotional Control (10 items). The MI encompasses five dimensions: Initiate (8 items), Working Memory (8 items), Plan/Organize (10 items), Organization of Materials (6 items), and Monitor (8 items). Each item is rated on a 1–3 point scale (1 = never to 3 = often). The sum of all factor scores constitutes the Global Executive Composite (GEC) score, ranging from 72 to 216. Higher scores indicate greater executive function impairment. The scale demonstrates test-retest reliability ranging from 0.68 to 0.89 and internal consistency coefficients between 0.74 and 0.96.38 In this study, the Cronbach's α ranged from 0.744 to 0.962.

Data collection

Prior to data collection, our research team coordinated with relevant hospital departments to secure cooperation and support, facilitating participants' recruitment and data access. Eligible pediatric lymphoma patients and their primary caregivers were approached by research staff who received uniform training regarding instrument administration and ethical considerations. The study purpose, potential benefits, confidentiality assurances, and voluntary nature of participation were clearly explained to all participants. Informed consent was obtained from both children and their caregivers.

Researchers used standardized instructions to guide participants in completing each questionnaire. Children completed the DASS-21 independently, and primary caregivers completed the SCSQ, FFFS, BRIEF and demographic questionnaire. Completed forms were collected immediately and reviewed for missing responses, which were clarified and completed on-site if possible. Clinical data were retrieved and cross-verified by the researchers from electronic medical record system. A database was established using Excel 2017 to input and manage all data.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis and model building were performed using SPSS 24.0. Continuous data were described as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while categorical data were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

Independent samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA were performed, using the Global Executive Composite (GEC) as dependent variable, and physiological factors, psychological factors and situational factors as independent variables. Pearson's correlation analysis assessed associations between stress-anxiety-depression scores and family functioning score with GEC. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted to access the effects of physiological, psychological and situational factors on executive function. Variables that reached statistical significance in univariate or correlation analyses (P < 0.05) were entered into the regression model. Continuous data was confirmed to normal distribution by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (P > 0.05). Results are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 304 pediatric lymphoma patients were screened for eligibility, among whom 230 patients and their primary caregivers met the criteria and were voluntary to participate. Two hundred and fifteen patient-caregiver dyads were ultimately included in the final analysis, indicating a valid response rate of 93.5%. Fifteen were excluded due to missing responses or exhibiting obvious patterns in response (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Flow diagram of participant recruitment and inclusion.Fig. 2

The sample was comprised of 149 males (69.3%) and 66 females (30.7%). The mean age of the patients was 10.80 ± 2.55 years, with a median age of 11 years (range: 8–16 years). Patients were categorized into two developmental groups: younger children (< 10 years, 41.4%) and adolescents (≥ 10 years, 58.6%). Slightly over half were only children (53.5%), while 46.5% had siblings. The majority resided in city or suburban areas (69.3%), with 30.7% from rural areas. Regarding disease characteristics, 72.6% were diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), and 27.4% with Hodgkin lymphoma (HL). Disease Stage III was the most common (52.1%), followed by Stages I-II (25.6%) and IV (22.3%). Severe disease accounted for 68.8% of cases.

Both fathers and mothers most commonly had a college education or above (62.3% and 54.0%, respectively). Correlation analysis between depression, anxiety, stress, family functioning and executive function

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the associations between executive function (Global Executive Composite, GEC) and psychological factors (depression, anxiety, and stress) as well as one situational factor (family functioning). The results indicated that GEC scores were significantly positively correlated with depression (r = 0.372, P < 0.001), anxiety (r = 0.517, P < 0.001), and stress (r = 0.501, P < 0.001). Among the psychological variables, anxiety showed the strongest correlation with executive function impairment. Family functioning was also significantly positively correlated with GEC (r = 0.364, P < 0.001), suggesting that greater discrepancies between current and desired family functioning were associated with more severe executive function impairment.

In Model 1, physiological factors were found to significantly account for 8.5% of the variance in GEC scores (F = 4.901, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.085). However, none of the physiological variables were significant independent predictors. The inclusion of psychological factors (Model 2) and situational factors (Model 3) significantly increased the model's explanatory power, with R2 change values of 0.247 and 0.119, respectively (both P < 0.001). The final full model (Model 3) indicated that the combined variables explained 45.2% of the variance in executive function (F = 8.464, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.452).

The final model demonstrated higher anxiety scores (β = 0.249, P = 0.015) and higher stress scores (β = 0.245, P = 0.014) were independently associated with greater executive function impairment. Having the father as the primary caregiver (compared to the mother, β = 0.121, P = 0.038) and being a non-only child (β = 0.129, P = 0.027) were associated with poorer executive function. Higher monthly family income (β = −0.140, P = 0.047) served as a protective factor for executive function. Caregivers employing a passive coping style (β = 0.147, P = 0.020) and those with higher family functioning scores (β = 0.185, P = 0.002) were associated with more severe executive function impairment in children. Disease-related physiological factors including lymphoma type, stage, duration of the disease and treatment cycles, depression among psychological factors, and situational factors such as age of primary caregiver, parental education level, and residence no longer showed significant independent predictive effects (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Main findings

This study, guided by TOUS, examined the multidimensional factors influencing executive function in pediatric lymphoma patients undergoing chemotherapy. Executive function was significantly associated with patients' anxiety and stress levels, fathers as primary caregivers, non-only child status, family income, caregiver coping style and family functioning. These findings validate the applicability of TOUS in pediatric oncology research on cognitive symptoms and highlight the differential contributions of physiological, psychological, and situational factors.

Hierarchical modeling revealed a progressive pattern of explanatory power. When the model included only physiological factors such as disease type and stage, the proportion of variance explained in executive function was limited, confirming that biomedical factors alone cannot fully account for cognitive impairment. Subsequently, incorporating psychological factors such as anxiety, depression and stress maximized the model's explanatory power, indicating that emotional distress plays a crucial role in executive function impairment. Finally, adding situational factors such as family structure, caregiver coping style and family functioning to the model further enhanced its explanatory power. These results indicate that executive function impairment in pediatric lymphoma patients is shaped by the interaction of physiological, psychological, and situational factors, as conceptualized by TOUS.

Our findings demonstrated a significant association between stress and impaired executive function, aligning with existing neurobiological evidence. Prolonged stress can dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to increased cortisol levels that adversely affect the prefrontal cortex, a critical brain region for executive function.15 This finding corroborates Godoy's study,39 which reported a negative correlation between stress and executive function. In pediatric oncology, the inherent long-term uncertainty of chemotherapy, complex treatment process, and physical discomfort collectively create a persistent stress environment for children. Children must continuously mobilize psychological resources to adapt to the hospital environment and comply with treatment, diverting cognitive resources away from complex thinking, planning, and self-regulation, thereby impairing executive function.

Compared to stress, anxiety showed a slightly weaker but still significant independent effect. Anxiety primarily involves excessive worry and fear about future threats, such as worsening symptoms, poor prognosis, cancer recurrence or social disconnection. This supports the "Scar Theories", which posits that anxiety itself exerts a lasting negative impact on executive function.40 Various anxieties experienced by patients may consume limited cognitive resources: repetitive worries occupy working memory capacity; and avoidance of cognitive activities learning or playing due to negative mood reduces engagement in planning, memory, and flexible thinking. Notably, depression did not show a significant independent effect on executive function performance, differing from Campbell's findings.41 This discrepancy may reflect the early to middle treatment stage of patients, during which depression may not yet have accumulated sufficiently to produce detectable structural or functional changes in executive function systems.

A key finding of this study is the critical role of situational factors. Children whose primary caregivers employing passive coping style exhibited more severe executive function impairment, consistent with the stress-buffering model.42 As primary caregivers play a central role in illness management, their coping style shapes the emotional atmosphere at home, which in turn influences children's development.43 Active coping is often characterized by efforts to directly manage and resolve stressors, such as seeking information, planning, and engaging in problem-solving activities.44 Adopting active coping style enables caregivers effectively handle disease-related stress, thereby creating a secure context that allows children to explore new situations and adapt to change — processes that support the development and expression of executive function.45 Conversely, passive coping involves avoidance or disengagement from stressors, such as avoiding illness-related discussions, postponing necessary decisions, and neglecting to seek information or support.44 These responses tend to perpetuate high-tension family dynamics, depleting children's psychological resources for cognitive flexibility. For caregivers of children with cancer, active coping is generally preferred, as it enables caregivers to take an active role in the child's care, seek necessary resources, and maintain a sense of control, all of which are essential for managing the demands of pediatric cancer treatment.46 In contrast, passive coping contributes to increased psychological distress, burnout, and reduced involvement in caregiving tasks, potentially exacerbating the challenges faced by both caregivers and patients.46

Another equally important finding is that higher scores on the Family Functioning Survey, indicating greater dissatisfaction with family functioning, were associated with more pronounced executive function impairment. This highlights the foundational role of family system health. According to family system theory, poor family functioning may indicate internal issues such as communication breakdown, role confusion, inadequate emotional support, or conflicting rules.47 Such an ecosystem fails to provide a stable and supportive environment for children undergoing critical brain development while coping with serious illness.

Additionally, non-only children exhibited significantly poorer executive function compared to only children, a finding that diverges from Yu and Yan's study,48 which suggested that having siblings may enhance social skills and certain cognitive abilities. However, McHale49 emphasized the complex relationship between sibling count and cognitive outcomes, noting that such relationship may be moderated by family resource allocation, parental attention distribution, and levels of family conflict. Caring for a child with cancer is a round-the-clock, emotionally demanding endeavor.50 In single-child families, parents, especially the primary caregiver, can devote all their time, attention, and emotional resources to that child. In muti-child families, parents must distribute resources, time, and energy across multiple children while managing the illness.51 Consequently, the sick child may receive less focused support, limiting opportunities for executive function training. Furthermore, most pediatric patients undergo long-term treatment away from home, leaving siblings typically in the care of other family members. This disrupts usual sibling interactions, and healthy siblings may feel neglected,52 potentially triggering family anxiety or competitive conflicts. These dynamics negatively impact the sick child's emotional regulation and task persistence.53

Our study identified that having the father as the primary caregiver, compared to the mother, constituted an independent risk factor for impaired executive function, potentially reflecting gender differences in parenting styles. Qian's study54 indicated that fathers engage in executive function parenting behaviors less frequently than mothers in daily routines, limiting children's opportunities to practice cognitive skills. When communicating about children's illness, fathers also tend to emphasize problem-solving over emotional support,55 which may hinder children's emotional regulation. Within the Chinese cultural context, the primary responsibility for childcare typically falls on mothers, so fathers assuming the role of primary caregiver may signal an atypical family situation, potentially indicating maternal absence due to health, emotional, or work-related reasons, or strained parental relationships.56 Such shifts in family structure can create stress and instability, and coupled with the challenges fathers may face in providing detailed daily care, these factors may negatively affect children's executive function.

In contrast, although some children were primarily cared for by grandparents, we did not observe significant differences in executive function compared to those cared for by mothers. Previous research indicates that when grandparents assume primary caregiving roles and provide stable, consistent, and supportive care, they can support children's development and reduce the impact of family stress or limited resources.57

In this study, higher family income was associated with better executive function, consistent with Thomas and Coecke's finding that higher socioeconomic status reflected greater family capacity to access nutrition support, educational resources, and healthcare, thereby supporting cognitive training and rehabilitation.58

Our findings highlighted that executive function impairment in pediatric lymphoma patients is jointly shaped by physiological, psychological, and situational factors, rather than isolated biomedical variables. While traditional medical variables alone limited predictive power in this study, psychological factors, particularly stress and anxiety, and family-related factors, including caregiver type, only-child status, family income, caregiver coping style and family functioning, play critical roles. By applying the TOUS, this study provides reference for understanding and addressing cognitive vulnerabilities in children facing serious illness.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Given these findings, nursing practice requires a multi-level intervention system to protect executive function in pediatric patients undergoing chemotherapy. First, during hospitalization, nurses should routinely screen children for psychological distress and executive function problems using brief and feasible instruments. Emotional symptoms can be assessed with tools such as the Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales-21 or other validated short screening scales, while executive function difficulties can be identified using caregiver-reported instruments such as the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function or brief behavioral checklists. For children exhibiting significant emotional distress, interventions including mindfulness exercises, guided breathing techniques, and structured psychological education programs may reduce emotional distress.59,60 Concurrently, simple executive function training can be incorporated into ward-based activity programs, including memory card games to strengthen working memory, rule-switching games to train cognitive flexibility, and structured planning tasks such as organizing daily schedules or step-by-step task completion exercises to improve planning and inhibitory control. Second, nursing care should extend to the family system. Families can be guided to establish stable routines and engage in structured parent–child activities that promote problem-solving and self-regulation.61 For high-risk families characterized by passive coping styles or poor family functioning, nurses should provide targeted guidance to clarify caregiving priorities, promote adaptive coping strategies, and reduce treatment-related stress. Additional support may be necessary for families in which fathers serve as primary caregivers to help them improve caregiving skills and emotional communication. Finally, multidisciplinary collaboration among nurses, physicians, psychologists, social workers, and educators is essential to provide comprehensive support for the children's cognitive rehabilitation.

Future nursing research should conduct longitudinal studies in pediatric lymphoma patients to examine changes in executive function from diagnosis through survivorship and identify optimal intervention windows. Intervention studies should evaluate the effectiveness of nursing-led programs that integrate disease management, psychological support, and family-centered care. In addition, implementation research should examine whether early multidimensional intervention can reduce long-term cognitive impairment in pediatric lymphoma survivors and assess the feasibility within routine clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design can reveal associations between variables but cannot establish causality. For instance, while we observed a strong association between stress and impaired executive function, the directionality of this relationship remains unclear and should be examined in prospective longitudinal studies. Second, this study is a single-center investigation, and the sample may not represent patients from diverse healthcare settings, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future study should involve pediatric patients from different regions and healthcare facilities of varying levels. Additionally, executive function was assessed using parent-reported measures, which efficiently capture daily behavioral manifestations but may be influenced by subjective bias. Future studies can strengthen validity by incorporating objective neuropsychological tests and physiological or pathological reports such as electroencephalograms to complement caregiver-reported assessments. Finally, despite the regression model incorporating multiple important variables, unmeasured confounding factors may still exist, including the children's baseline cognitive level prior to treatment and the neurocognitive side effects of specific chemotherapy agents. Their potential impacts should be considered in future research.

Conclusions

This study systematically examined factors influencing executive function in pediatric lymphoma patients undergoing chemotherapy, grounded in the theory of unpleasant symptoms. Psychological factors, including anxiety and stress, together with situational factors, including family structure (father as the primary caregiver and non-only child status), lower family income, caregiver's passive coping style and poorer family functioning, emerged as significant predictors. Conversely, the independent effects of traditional medical factors diminished in the comprehensive model. These results validated the applicability of the theoretical framework in this population, underscoring that executive function impairment results from complex interaction among physiological, psychological, and situational factors. The family system, in particular, is a critical target for intervention. These findings provide theoretical guidance for clinical nursing, advocate for a shift from disease management alone toward psychological support and family-centered care for pediatric patients. Implementing comprehensive interventions, including early screening, family empowerment, and multidisciplinary collaboration, can effectively protect children's neurocognitive development and improve their long-term quality of life. Future research should validate causal relationships through longitudinal designs and develop evidence-based, family-centered intervention approaches.
