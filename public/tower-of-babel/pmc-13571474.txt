A systematic review of Leptospira and leptospirosis in wildlife: A global perspective
Authors: Diego Guzmán, Giuliana Pazmiño-Zurita, Sebastian Melo, Talima Pearson, Verónica Barragán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There are many different pathogenic species of Leptospira that circulate among a wide range of wild and domestic animals. Certain species and serovars can cause disease in some hosts but not in others. Although leptospirosis has been extensively studied in humans and domestic animals, with its epidemiology and economic impact being well characterized, much less is known about its ecological dynamics in wildlife. The host-pathogen associations, transmission pathways, and disease outcomes in wild animal populations remain poorly understood. This review summarizes the current evidence on Leptospira and leptospirosis in wildlife, identifying methodological biases and highlighting key knowledge gaps. It also compiles information on wild animal species in which exposure to the pathogen has been documented.A systematic search of the scientific literature published between 2000 and 2025 was performed across multiple databases in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Following screening and an assessment of eligibility, data were extracted from 389 peer-reviewed articles reporting exposure to, infection with, or detection of the pathogen in wildlife species worldwide. An analysis of study locations and publication trends was also performed.The reviewed studies revealed substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic methods, sampling effort, and geographic coverage, as well as a wide taxonomic diversity of wildlife species exposed to or infected with Leptospira. Clinical cases were most frequently reported in the orders Carnivora and Rodentia, with mortality documented in marine mammals, large felids and primates, particularly in untreated cases. Analysis of carriage and exposure studies revealed a strong sampling bias toward mammals compared to birds and reptiles. Molecular detection was reported in 155 articles, with lipL32 being the most commonly used marker. Sequencing of amplicons enabled identification of Leptospira at the level of the species. Meanwhile, serological testing predominantly relied on MAT reporting.

Introduction

Leptospirosis is a globally distributed zoonosis caused by pathogenic species of the genus Leptospira, affecting humans, domestic animals, and wildlife across a wide range of ecological contexts [1], [2]. Although its epidemiology has been extensively studied in humans and livestock [3], [4], [5], studies of its occurrence and dynamics in wildlife remain strikingly scarce and fragmented. Understanding leptospirosis in wild animals is crucial as these animals can function as reservoirs or incidental hosts that harbor and shed the pathogen into the environment, shape transmission dynamics at the human–animal–environment interface, and possibly act as sentinels or predictors of disease [6], [7], [8].

Research on leptospirosis in wildlife faces profound challenges, stemming from the logistical difficulties of obtaining biological samples and the ethical and legal restrictions on capturing animals in their natural habitats. These constraints have limited the feasibility of large-scale surveys and long-term epidemiological studies. Nevertheless, the few investigations conducted to date have provided valuable ecological insights. Evidence of exposure and infection has been documented across diverse ecosystems, from tropical forests and páramo landscapes to savannas, mountain ranges, and marine environments, highlighting the broad ecological range for interactions of pathogenic Leptospira with wildlife [9], [10].

Beyond these practical limitations, climate change and anthropogenic modifications affecting our planet increase the importance of understanding the ecoepidemiology of Leptospira in wildlife. These changes may alter the persistence of Leptospira, host distribution, and interactions among wildlife, domestic animals, and humans, potentially favoring the emergence of new hotspots and interspecific transmission events [11]. Given the limited data on leptospirosis in wild animals, this review seeks to compile and critically assess data from diverse studies to highlight patterns and knowledge gaps that may help guide future scientific inquiry.

Material and methods

We conducted a systematic review of the literature on leptospirosis in wildlife, following established guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility. The process included a comprehensive database search, rigorous screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and standardized data extraction for subsequent analyses (Fig. S1).

Research strategy and databases

This study followed the Preferred Reporting of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The search for articles was conducted on 31 August 2025 using four international databases: PubMed (n = 725), Scielo (n = 43), Scopus (n = 637), and Web of Science (n = 659). The keywords used in the search query of every database were (Leptospir* AND (“wild animals” OR wildlife)). Studies published between January 2000 and 25 August 2025 were considered eligible for screening. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described below.

Article selection and screening

Duplicate articles were manually removed. Non-original scientific articles, such as reviews, book chapters, posters, and conference abstracts were also excluded. Titles unrelated to leptospirosis in wild animals, articles not published in English, Spanish, or Portuguese, and experimental studies that did not focus on Leptospira or leptospirosis in wildlife were excluded (Fig. S1). Screening was conducted independently by two reviewers (GP and SM) with any uncertainties resolved by a third reviewer (DG).

After screening article titles, abstracts and subsequently full-text articles were screened and excluded based on the following criteria: 1) articles not related to leptospirosis, 2) studies that focused solely on domestic animals, and 3) studies not about natural infection in wild animals or wildlife. A fourth researcher (VB) resolved any discordant decisions. Papers with unavailable abstracts were read fully by the reviewers (DG, GP, and SM).

Data extraction, synthesis and analysis

Reviewers independently extracted relevant data using a standardized data extraction form to gather the following information: 1) Authors, 2) Title, 3) Year of publication, 4) Type of study (surveillance or diagnostic), 5) Continent of study, 6) Country, 7) Specific site, 8) Year of study, 9) Study designation (Wildlife: species of animals that live in their natural state, with minimal direct human intervention in their habitat, such as, national parks, conservation areas, caves, mountains, bays, coastal zones, and nature reserves; Rural: a geographic area characterized by low population density, a predominantly agricultural, natural, or pastoral environment, and close proximity to wildlife; Captive: species of animals kept in confinement or enclosed in some type of facility by humans, such as, zoos, aquaria, rescue and rehabilitation centers, research facilities, and animal farms; Peri-urban: areas located on the outskirts of a main city but still significantly connected to that city), 10) Animal species, 11) Animal class, 12) Animal Order, 13) Animal clinical signs, 14) Type of sample collected, 15) Tissue lesions, 16) Serological technique used for diagnosis, 17) Serogroups identified (only the serogroup with the highest titers was considered), 18) Isolation of Leptospira by culture (yes/no), 19) Molecular technique used for pathogen detection (PCR/qPCR), 20) Leptospira species identified, and 21) Co-infection of Leptospira with another pathogen). Information regarding peridomestic rodent species commonly found in urban environments (synanthropic), such as Rattus spp., Mastomys natalensis, and Mus musculus domesticus, were excluded from this review, as these taxa are closely associated with human habitats and thus not considered to be wildlife for this review, this information was confirmed using the Animal Diversity Web (ADW) database (https://animaldiversity.org). Taxonomic nomenclature was standardized following currently accepted species names. Historical synonyms were unified, and species complexes were retained when original studies did not allow for finer taxonomic resolution.

Extracted information was organized into four components: 1) clinical manifestations and outcomes in wildlife leptospirosis, 2) carriage and evidence of exposure to pathogenic Leptospira, 3) techniques used for detecting and diagnosing leptospirosis, and 4) analysis of study location and publication output on Leptospira/leptospirosis in wildlife.

Information on Clinical manifestations and outcomes in wildlife leptospirosis was extracted from records of animals that we classified as diagnosed with leptospirosis. This required at least one of the following criteria to be met: (i) clinical signs consistent with leptospirosis confirmed by at least one diagnostic test (molecular, serological, or culture), or (ii) compatible tissue lesions confirmed by a diagnostic test (molecular, serological, or culture). When Microscopic Agglutination Test (MAT) data were available, only the serogroup with the highest antibody titer was recorded.

Carriage and evidence of past exposure to pathogenic Leptospira in wildlife were assessed specifically in animals without clinical signs of leptospirosis. This was done (i) through studies using serological methods, primarily the microscopic agglutination test (MAT) [12], [13], to identify evidence of prior exposure, and (ii) through studies employing molecular or culture-based methods to detect urinary shedding of Leptospira, indicative of a carriage state. From the serological studies, only articles that explicitly reported MAT results on samples collected from wild animals with no signs of disease were included. To ensure consistency and comparability and to buffer against cross reactivity of similar serogroups, only the serogroup with the highest antibody titer per sample was recorded for analysis. As our primary interests were to understand the wildlife species exposed to Leptospira as well as the serogroups to which the animals have been exposed, we focused on studies providing clear serogroup information. Serological outcomes obtained from assays with lower sensitivity or specificity, such as ELISA (IgM), were excluded to minimize variability and reduce potential confusion based on cross-reactivity with other pathogens [12]. From studies that used molecular or culture-based methods to detect and identify pathogenic strains from wildlife, the compiled information included wild animals without clinical signs of leptospirosis. Methods of species designation included PCR/qPCR followed by DNA sequencing as well as culture isolation followed by molecular or serological and molecular methods. Because some studies did not specify whether multiple samples originated from the same or different individuals, all tables and figures presented in this review refer exclusively to the number of samples analyzed within each taxonomic group and not the number of individual animals that may have been sampled. Exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) 95% confidence intervals were calculated for MAT and PCR positivity, to account for unequal sample sizes and small sample numbers in certain taxa, using the binom package in RStudio software (v. 2024.09.0 + 375), and results were summarized and exported using automated scripts. The compiled data was subsequently used to generate bar graphs and scatterplots illustrating the number of positive and negative samples per taxonomic group. The graphs were made in GraphPad Prism 10 (v. 10.5.0, Boston, Massachussets USA) and in RStudio software using the ggplot2 library (v. 2024.09.0 + 375). Additionally, all the compiled data were organized at the species and higher taxonomic levels as information regarding sub-species designations was inconsistent.

To describe the techniques used for detecting and diagnosing Leptospira spp., all studies were included and classified according to their diagnostic approach: molecular, serological, bacteriological, or histopathological. Molecular studies were subdivided by technique (conventional PCR, real-time PCR, MLST, and gene sequencing). For each molecular assay, we recorded the target gene, the first author who originally published the primers cited in the study, and the type of sample analyzed. Articles that did not cite the diagnostic assay were excluded from this category. Serological and histopathological studies were similarly classified according to the specific assays employed.

The analysis of study locations and publication output was conducted to identify where most research on Leptospira/leptospirosis in wildlife has been carried out and to contrast these patterns with each country's Gross National Income (GNI) per capita and potential Leptospira animal carrier species richness. This allowed us to analyze where studies on leptospirosis in wildlife are particularly lacking, especially considering level of development and natural richness. Vertebrate species counts (mammals, birds, amphibians, and reptiles) were obtained from the “Map of Life” platform (https://mol.org/dashboard/home). Species richness density was calculated as the total number of vertebrate species per country divided by land area (km2) and standardized to “species richness per 10,000 km2”. GNI per capita, expressed in current US dollars, was retrieved from the World Bank data portal via Research4Life eligibility criteria (https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GNP.PCAP.CD). Geospatial visualizations were produced using QGIS (v. 3.34.13; QGIS Development Team, 2024), and the relationship between GNI per capita and number of publications per country was assessed using Spearman's correlation coefficient in R Studio software (v. 2024.09.0 + 375).

Results

Clinical manifestations and outcomes in wildlife leptospirosis

Our analysis provides a comprehensive overview of documented host species, clinical signs, pathological alterations, pathogen serogroups and species associated with disease, and health outcomes connected with leptospirosis in wild animals. Findings underscore the wide taxonomic diversity of affected hosts, the spectrum of lesions and serogroups involved, and the variability in disease progression, ranging from asymptomatic carriers (see also section on carriage and exposure) to severe multisystemic disease and fatalities.

According to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the methodology, 29 out of 389 articles provided relevant information on this category of clinical manifestations and outcomes in wildlife. Eleven taxonomic orders, encompassing 24 families and 35 species of wildlife, have been reported with cases of leptospirosis (Table S1). Carnivora contained the highest diversity of affected families (n = 7 families), followed by Rodentia (n = 4), Primates (n = 4), Artiodactyla (n = 2), and Squamata (n = 2). The majority of investigations were conducted in America and Europe (Table S1).

The most frequently observed clinical signs in wild animals with leptospirosis were lethargy, anorexia, dehydration, jaundice, and fever. Neurological symptoms and respiratory distress were occasionally documented, emphasizing the systemic potential of infection. Pathological examinations of tissues show that the predominant renal lesions were interstitial nephritis (chronic, acute, or lymphoplasmacytic), tubular necrosis (ranging from mild to severe), and interstitial fibrosis in some instances. The serogroups most frequently identified in animals with disease had cut-off MAT titers ranging from ≥1:50 to ≥1:600 and included Icterohaemorrhagiae, Pomona, Bratislava, Grippotyphosa, Sejroe, Ballum, Australis, and Canicola. It is noteworthy that Icterohaemorrhagiae was more frequently detected in Carnivora, Rodentia, and Lagomorpha, indicative of a robust association with mammalian reservoirs. The presence of Pomona was primarily detected in cetaceans, artiodactyls, and pinnipeds. Canicola is present in Artiodactyla and Lagomorpha, while Grippotyphosa in Perissodactyla and marine mammals (Table 1). The pathogenic Leptospira species identified were L. interrogans, L. kirschneri, and L. borgpetersenii. L. interrogans was the most common species, affecting various mammal species in the orders Carnivora, Primates, Rodentia, and Pilosa. L. kirschneri was detected most frequently in cetaceans, followed by rodents and carnivores. L. borgpetersenii was recorded in raccoon dogs (Table 1).

Mortality was reported in marine mammals, large felids, and primates, particularly in cases without timely treatment. Fatal outcomes were documented in several species, including the Northern sea otter (Enhydra lutris kenyoni, Mustelidae), California sea lion (Zalophus californianus, Otariidae), Patagonian mara (Dolichotis patagonum, Caviidae), cougar or mountain lion (Puma concolor, Felidae), raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides, Canidae), red fox (Vulpes vulpes, Canidae), Eastern black rhinoceros (Diceros bicornis michaeli, Rhinocerotidae), and Wied's marmoset (Callithrix kuhlii, Callitrichidae).

Positive treatment outcomes were documented in the Southern lesser kudu (Tragelaphus imberbis australis, Bovidae), Sumatran tiger (Panthera tigris sumatrae, Felidae), and Andean fox (Lycalopex culpaeus, Canidae), following antimicrobial therapy with doxycycline, penicillin, or ceftiofur combined with supportive care. Asymptomatic infections were reported in many wildlife species (see section on carriage and exposure), but in coypu (Myocastor coypus, Echimyidae) and big hairy armadillo (Chaetophractus villosus, Dasypodidae), renal lesions consistent with subclinical leptospiral infection were present, despite being reported with no clinical signs of infection.

Carriage and evidence of exposure to Pathogenic Leptospira in Wildlife

The compiled data about exposure to and detection of pathogenic Leptospira DNA in wild animals was grouped by animal class (Aves, Mammalia, and Reptilia) and further categorized by order, family, genus, and species (Table S2). In general terms, Mammalia exhibited the greatest taxonomic diversity of non sick animals identified to be exposed or to carry the bacteria, encompassing 18 orders, 74 families, 306 genera, and 501 species, with a total of 89,414 samples analyzed. In contrast, studies of class Aves only focused on 10 orders, 12 families, and 17 different species, with a total of 928 samples analyzed; while publications about reptiles showed information of just 3 orders, 8 families and 15 species with a total of 541 analyzed samples (Table S2). Information from serological testing is indicative only of past exposure to Leptospira.

MAT yielded the highest positivity rate in reptiles (58.7%, 209/356, CI. 53.4%-63.9%), followed by mammals (20.8%, 12,306/59,023, CI. 20.5%-21.2%), while birds had a markedly lower positivity (1.3%, 12/928, CI. 0.7%-2.2%). Results from PCR, indicative of current carriage, show a positivity of 13.0% in mammals (3945/30,391, CI. 12.6%-13.4%), 18.4% in reptiles (34/185, CI. 13.1%-24.7%), and zero samples tested by PCR in class Aves. The significantly smaller sampling of reptiles and birds was reflected in the number of papers published regarding these orders, with only 20 and 6 papers respectively, in contrast to mammals with more than 200 papers.

Evidence of exposure to different serogroups varied by animal class. Birds showed antibodies against Tarassovi, Icterohaemorrhagiae, and Grippotyphosa, ranked from highest to lowest according to the number of articles describing them. Reptiles were exposed to Icterohaemorrhagiae, Sejroe, Autumnalis, and rarely (in single publications), Australis, Andamana, Panama, Celledoni, Canicola, Pomona, Djasiman, Cynopteri, Grippotyphosa, Pyrogenes, Javanica and Hebdomadis. Mammals exhibited the broadest spectrum of antibodies, with MAT detecting exposure to all the previously mentioned serogroups, plus Bataviae, Ballum, Iquitos, Shermani, Mini, and in single publications, Fluminense, Sarawak, Semaranga, Sarmin and Brasiliensis (Table S2). However, from the 12,527 MAT-positive samples across all the animal classes, the specific serogroup was identified in a very small percentage of positive samples as most of them showed cross-reactions (positive results for >1 serogroup). As a result, from the 178 papers reporting MAT-positive samples, less than 115 identified specific Leptospira serogroups.

Detection of Leptospira DNA was achieved through molecular detection and, in certain animal classes, species-level identification by amplicon sequencing. In reptiles, L. interrogans DNA was detected in the 2 studies that had positive samples. From the total of 107 papers reporting PCR-positive samples in mammals, less than 67 papers sequenced those amplicons to identify the Leptospira species carried by the animal. In mammals, multiple Leptospira species have been identified: L. interrogans in 47 publications, L. borgpetersenii in 20 papers, L. kirschneri in 15 papers, L. noguchii in 6 papers, L. santarosai in 4 papers, and other species such as L. alexanderi, L. fanei, L. wolfii, L. mayotensis and L. alstonii reported in only a few publications (Table S2). For the class Aves, there were no reported samples tested by PCR.

Class Aves

Evidence of exposure to pathogenic Leptospira in wild birds was reported only in 5 of the 12 avian families studied (See Fig. 1): Ardeidae was the family with the highest number of sampled individuals with 0.7% of positive samples (3/436, CI. 0.1%-2.0%), but consists only of samples from Western Cattle Egret (Bubulcus ibis). The other avian seropositive families included Threskiornithidae with 23.1% (3/13, CI. 5.0%-53.8%) samples of Black-faced Ibis (Theristicus melanopis), Psittacidae with 33.3% (3/9, CI. 7.5%-70.1%) samples of Choroy (Enicognathus leptorhynchus), Spheniscidae with 1.5% (2/135, CI. 0.2%-5.3% of positive Magellanic penguin (Spheniscus magellanicus) samples and Phasianidae with 100% (1/1, CI. 2.5%-100%) of positive samples of Grey Partridge (Perdix perdix) (Table S3). The highest MAT positivity was observed in the Emydidae family of Testudines (34/34, 100%, CI. 89.7%-100%), and Crocodylidae in the order Crocodilia with 89.6% (43/48, CI. 77.3%-96.5%). Blanding's turtle (Emydoidea blandingii) was the only species of Emydidae sampled and accouts for the 34/34 (100%, CI. 89.7%-100%) MAT-positive samples. In the case of Crocodylidae, 100% (14/14, CI. 76.8%-100%) samples from Morelet's crocodile (Crocodylus moreletii) and 85.3% (29/34, CI. 68.9%-95%) samples of American crocodile (Crocodylus acutus) resulted in MAT-positive results. For all samples, multiple cross reactions between serogroups were reported. Notably, Boidae, a family in the order Squamata, also showed a high positivity rate with 70% (42/60, CI. 56.8%-81.2%) MAT-positive samples. In this family results from the boa constrictor stand out with 74% (37/50, CI. 59.7%-85.4%) of samples being seropositive. In contrast, Iguanidae, another family from the order Squamata with only samples from the green iguana (Iguana iguana), did not exhibit any MAT positivity (0/29, CI. 0%-11.9%). There were also reports from families with a very small sample number such as Teiidae with 4/13 MAT-positive (30.8%, CI. 9.1%-61.4%) from black and white tegu (Salvator merianae) and Cheloniidae with only 1 sample which was MAT-negative from green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas) (0%, CI. 0%-97.5%).

Regarding the serogroups reported, Canicola, Cynopteri and Pyrogenes were exclusively presented in Alligatoridae; Australis, Grippotyphosa and Pomona just described in Boidae; and Djasiman, Javanica, Hebdomadis and Andamana exclusive to Viperidae. In samples from Emydidae, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Sejroe, and Autumnalis serogroups were reported. In Boidae, serogroups include Australis, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Pomona, Sejroe, Autumnalis, Grippotyphosa, Panama and Celledoni. Other serogroups such as Panama and Celledoni were most commonly found in the order Squamata, while serogroups Icterohaemorrhagiae, Sejroe and Autumnalis were shared by different families across orders (Table S2).

For reptilian families only 185 PCR-tested samples have been reported in 5 studies, with low PCR-positivity rates for leptospira. In the Crocodilia order, there was only one species tested by PCR: broad-snouted caiman (Caiman latirostris) with 0% (0/20, CI. 0%-16.8%) positive samples. Regarding the order Squamata, Boa constrictor had 15.2% (7/46, CI. 6.3%-28.9%) of positive samples with L. interrogans described. Finally, in the order Testudines, Blanding's turtle (Emydoidea blandingii) showed a relatively high positivity of 73.5% (25/34, CI. 55.6%-87.1%) samples; whereas no PCR-positive cases were reported in the pond slider (Trachemys scripta): a species from Emydoidea family with 0/83 (0%, CI. 0%-4.4%) positive samples (Table S3). It is important to add that only a few species of reptiles were tested with both PCR and MAT: broad-snouted caiman (Caiman latirostris), Boa constrictor, Brazilian lancehead (Bothrops moojeni) and Blanding's turtle (Emydoidea blandingii); the 11 remaining reptile species sampled only had samples tested either by MAT or PCR. In addition, there were species such as jararaca (Bothrops jararaca), whitetail lancehead (Bothrops leucurus), Brazilian lancehead (Bothrops moojeni), and green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas) which had less than 10 samples analyzed either by MAT, PCR or both.

Class Mammalia

As noted in Fig. 3, out of the 18 orders sampled, there were only 8 with at least 1000 total samples. Artiodactyla has been most sampled (Fig. 3A), with 33,507 total samples from 4 animal families: Bovidae, Cervidae, Suidae and Tayassuidae; with an overall MAT-positivity rate of 13.3% (3887/29,297, CI. 12.9%-13.7%) and PCR-positivity rate of 6.3% (264/4210, CI. 5.6%-7.1%) (Table S2). Within this order, Suidae was the most sampled family with a total of 17,703 samples, from which all 13.7% of MAT-positive (2023/14,810, CI. 13.1%-14.2%) and 8.1% PCR-positive (235/2893, CI. 7.2%-9.2%) samples were reported exclusively in wild boars (Sus scrofa). Cervidae was the second most-sampled family with a total of 13,516 samples, of which more than half were from mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) and red deer (Cervus elaphus) samples with low positivity. In contrast, white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus) had high MAT-positivity (667/1290, 51.7%, CI. 48.9%-54.5%). The families of Bovidae and Tayasuidae have had the smallest sampling effort of Artiodactyla. While Bovidae had 2058 MAT-tested and 100 PCR-tested samples, Tayassuidae included only 130 MAT-tested samples. It is noteworthy that the positivity detected between species from the same family, and even from the same genus, was very heterogeneous (Table S3). Australis, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Pomona, Grippotyphosa, Sejroe and Canicola were the most frequently described serogroups in Suidae and Cervidae. Panama and Cynopteri were exclusively from Cervidae. Bataviae, Semaranga, and Andamana were only described in Suidae. The Rodentia order included a total of 23,090 samples from 156 species across 18 families (Fig. 3L), with an overall MAT-positivity of 15.9% (1758/11,031, CI. 15.3%-16.6%) and PCR-positivity of 11.0% (1326/12,059, CI. 10.4%-11.6%) (Table S2). Cricetidae and Muridae showed the highest sampling efforts with both MAT and PCR. On one hand, both families had at least 4400 MAT-tested samples each; however, most of the samples came from only 3 species: common vole (Microtus arvalis) in Cricetidae and striped field mouse (Apodemus agrarius), and wood mouse (Apodemus sylvaticus) in Muridae with the following respective seropositivity: 16.0% (419/2622, CI. 14.6%-17.4%), 7.3% (147/2003, CI. 6.2%-8.6%) and 5.6% (78/1383, CI. 4.5%-7.0%). In this order, there is also relatively plentiful data on seropositivity for coypu (Myocastor coypus), a species from Myocastoridae, with 36.3% (308/848, CI. 33.1%-39.7%).

Regarding PCR-positivity, Muridae had the highest sampling effort with 10.5% (680/6469, CI. 9.8%-11.3%) positive samples: half of those positive samples came from striped field mouse (Apodemus agrarius) and yellow-necked mouse (Apodemus flavicollis with respective positivity rates of 11.7% (308/2628, CI. 10.5%-13.0%) and 10.6% (118/1117, CI. 8.9%-12.5%); but also, species such as bank vole (Myodes glareolus) from Cricetidae provided useful information about PCR-testing with a total of 99/1403 positive samples (7.1%, CI. 5.8%-8.6%).

The most frequently reported serogroups across various species were Icterohaemorrhagiae, Australis, Pomona, Sejroe, Canicola and Grippotyphosa; however, Iquitos and Celledoni were described only in Sciuridae, Ballum was only found in Chinchillidae, and Autumnalis only in Cricetidae. Reported Leptospira species included L. interrogans in various rodent families, L. kirschneri and L. borgpetersenii solely in Cricetidae and Muridae, L. santarosai in Caviidae and Cricetidae, and L. noguchi in Cricetidae and Echimyidae (Table S2).

The order Carnivora is depicted in Fig. 3B, with 20,489 tested samples from 89 species across 13 animal families, with an overall MAT-positivity rate of 37.9% (5293/13,967, CI. 37.1%-38.7%) and PCR-positivity rate of 19.7% (1282/6522, CI. 18.7%-20.6%) (Table S2). The most sampled families were Canidae and Procyonidae, followed by Mustelidae and Otariidae. For Canidae, there were 28.1% MAT-positive samples (1729/6148, CI. 27.0%-29.3%) from which, most of the MAT-positive samples came from Canis latrans (682/1452, 47.0%, CI. 44.4%-49.6%) and Vulpes vulpes (785/2996, 26.2%, CI. 24.6%-27.8%), commonly known as coyote and red fox respectively. DNA overall detection rate in Canidae was 7.8% (109/1392, CI. 6.5%-9.4%) with a similar tendency of high PCR-positive samples among Vulpes and Canis: V. vulpes (57/752, 7.6%, CI. 5.8%-9.7%) and C. lupus (19/311, 6.1%, 3.7%-9.4%). In the Procyonidae family, racoon (Procyon lotor) had the highest sampling effort with a total of 4013 samples with 70.5% of MAT-positive (2096/2971, CI. 68.9%-72.2%) and 11.0% PCR-positivity (115/1042, CI. 9.2%-13.1%) (Table S3). From Mustelidae family, European mink (Mustela lutreola) stands out with the highest seropositivity (81/133, 60.9%, CI. 52.1%-69.2%), while the common polecat (Mustela putorius) had a high DNA detection rate of 60.3% (91/151, CI. 52.0%-68.1%). Finally, in the Otariidae family, California sea lion (Zalophus californianus) was the most sampled species with 64.2% MAT-positive samples (459/715, CI. 60.6%-67.7%) and 46.6% PCR-positive samples (422/905, CI. 43.3%-49.9%) (Table S3). The most reported Leptospira species in Carnivora were L. interrogans, L. borgpetersenii and L. kirschneri, with L. wolfii, L. santarosai and L. noguchii documented much less frequently. The most common serogroups were Icterohaemorrhagiae, Australis, Grippotyphosa, Pomona, Sejroe and Canicola (Table S2).

From the order Chiroptera (Fig. 3D) a total of 3824 samples from 102 species across 9 animal families were sampled with 22.1% of MAT-positive (127/574, CI. 18.8%-25.7%) and 15.5% (503/3250, CI. 14.2%-16.8%) of PCR-positive samples (Table S2). MAT was only described in 4 families: Hipposideridae, Pteropodidae, Rhinolophidae and Vespertilionidae; from which Pteropodidae showed the highest sampling effort and relatively high MAT-positivity (113/375, 30.1%, CI. 25.5%-35.1%). In this case, most of the MAT-positive samples came from black fruit bats (Pteropus alecto) and spectacled fruit bats (Pteropus conspicillatus). Australis, Cynopteri, Sejroe, and Autumnalis were the most common serogroups described in Pteropodidae. Leptospira DNA detection among the most commonly sampled families yielded the following positivity results: Phyllostomidae (145/1030, 14.1%, CI. 12.0%-16.4%), Pteropodidae (170/916, 18.6%, CI. 16.1%-21.2%) and Vespertilionidae (110/903, 12.2%, CI. 10.1%-14.5%). Some species including black fruit bat (Pteropus alecto) and insular flying fox (Pteropus tonganus) showed considerable leptospira PCR-positivity: 23.7% (32/135, CI. 16.8%-31.8%) and 51.8% (29/56, CI. 38.0%-65.3%) respectively. Among Chiroptera, L. interrogans, L. borgpetersenni, L. kirscheri, L. noguchii and L. alexanderi were detected in various families; while L. santarosai and L. mayotensis were only described in Mormoopidae, and L. fanei only doccumented in Phyllostomidae (Table S2).

For the order Eulipotyphyla (Fig. 3G), a total of 2712 samples from 26 species across 3 families have been characterized: the families Erinaceidae, Soricidae and Talpidae show an overall seropositivity of 10.8% (90/835, CI. 8.8%-13.1%) and PCR-positive rate of 13.9% (260/1877, CI. 12.3%-15.5%) (Table S2). Soricidae was the most sampled family, closely followed by Erinaceidae, with both MAT and PCR, resulting in 6.3% of seropositivity (32/504, CI. 4.4%-8.9%) and 8.9% PCR-positivity (78/878, CI. 7.1%-11.0%). However, seropositivity data were based primarily from the common shrew (Sorex araneus) (Table S3). Erinaceidae had 17.6% (58/330, CI. 13.6%-22.1%) MAT-positive and 22.4% (177/790, CI. 19.5%-25.5%) PCR-positive samples. In this case, the MAT and PCR data came mostly from samples of the common hedgehog (Erinaceus europaeus) (Table S3). The only serogroups identified were Australis, Pomona and Shermani from samples from Soricidae. L. borgpetersenii, L. interrogans and L. kirschneri were found in both Soricidae and Erinaceidae. Finally, it is important to note that L. alstonii was found in samples from greater white-toothed shrew (Crocidura russula), a species of Soricidae family. This is noteworthy as we found no other record of L. alstonii associated with any other wildlife species for this review.

For Primates (Fig. 3K), there were a total of 2314 samples from 21 species across 5 families: Atelidae, Callitrichidae, Cebidae, Cercopithecidae and Hominidae. This order had 36.6% (591/1615, CI. 34.2%-39.0%) and 23.2% (162/699, CI. 20.1%-26.5%) positivity by MAT and PCR, respectively (Table S2). These families, except for Hominidae with no samples MAT-tested, had relatively similar sampling efforts for MAT-testing. However, the Callitrichidae family had the highest seropositivity with 57.2% (399/697, CI. 53.5%-61.0%); from which, most of the positive samples came from golden-headed lion tamarins (Leontopithecus chrysomelas) with 62.4% (370/593, CI. 58.4%-66.3%) seropositivity. For PCR-testing, Cercopithecidae had the most samples tested with a detection rate of 25.0% (122/488, CI. 21.2%-29.1%); however, all positive samples came from rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) which had 26.6% of PCR-positive samples (122/458, CI. 22.6%-30.9%). Reported serogroups across different families included Icterohaemorrhagiae, Bataviae, Sejroe, Shermani, Javanica and Hebdomadis. Iquitos was only described in Callitrichidae; while Australis, Panama and Fluminense were only in documented in Atelidae. Pomona, Mini, Autumnalis, Grippotyphosa, Pyrogenes and Sarmin were found in Cebidae and; Ballum and Sarawak serovars were only described in Cercopithecidae. Identified Leptospira species were L. interrogans and L. borgpetersenii in Cebidae (Table S2). The Sarawak serovar was only described in a single study from Malasia as it is endemic in that country [41].

The Lagomorpha order described in Fig. 3H had 1251 total samples from 7 species, with an overall MAT-positivity of 6.7% (37/552, CI. 4.8%-9.1%) that came from only 2 families: Ochotonidae had only 2 samples tested, both of which were MAT-positive (100%, CI. 15.8%-100%) while in Leporidae, 6.4% (35/550, CI. 4.5%-8.7%) were MAT-positive (Table S2). Regarding PCR testing, the order Lagomorpha showed an overall positivity of 1.0% (7/699, CI. 0.4%-2.1%) which came exclusively from Leporidae. Specifically most of the MAT information came from samples of brown hare (Lepus europaeus), with a seropositivity of 6.4% (35/550, CI. 4.5%-8.7%). PCR results were obtained primarly from brown hare (Lepus europaeus) but also included coney (Oryctolagus cuniculus), with both species having been well sampled with low positive rates of 1.4% (5/370, CI. 0.4%-3.1%) and 0.3% (1/317, CI. 0%-1.7%) respectively (Table S3). Australis and Ballum were the only serogroups described from this order, and L. interrogans the only species detected (Table S2).

From the order Didelphimorphia, there were 1107 samples analyzed corresponding to 25 species exclusively from the Didelphidae family. 13.4% (77/574, CI. 10.7%-16.5%) were MAT-positive and 12.4% (66/533, CI. 9.7%-15.5%) were PCR-positive samples (Table S2). White-eared opossum (Didelphis albiventris) and North American opossum (Didelphis virginiana) were the most sampled species for both MAT and PCR. Their seropositivity was 1.3% (3/235, CI. 0.3%-3.7%) and 22.3% (68/305, CI. 17.7%-27.4%) respectively. The PCR detection rate of Leptospira was 4.2% (11/263, CI. 2.1%-7.4%) and 1.5% (2/137, CI. 0.2%-5.2%) respectively. In this order, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Canicola, and Grippotyphosa were the most commonly described serogroups, while Pomona, Autumnalis and Panama were only mentioned in one study. L. interrogans and L. santarosai were the species most described while L. borgpetersenni and L. noguchii were Leptospira species detected in fewer studies (Table S3).

Here are some final interesting observations shown in Table S3. On one hand, the information obtained from Cetaceae, an infra-order of Artiodactyla separated due to their adaptation to aquatic life, was assessed exclusively by PCR and Delphinidae was the most sampled family (19/127, 15%, CI. 9.3%-22.4%) (Table S2). On the contrary, there were some orders such as Diprotodontia, Perissodactyla and Monotremata which only were tested by MAT; the latter being interesting as its considerate seropositivity came only from samples of platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus): 41.0% (256/624, CI. 37.1%-45.0%), identifying Sejroe. On the other hand, Pilosa, Scandentia, Dasyuromorphia, Cingulata and Sirenia were the least sampled animal orders, having less than 200 total samples each; even when they encompass some interesting species such as Tasmanian devil (Sarcophilus harrisii), giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla), Brazilian three-banded armadillo (Tolypeutes tricinctus), Amazonian manatee (Trichechus inunguis), which are currently threatened (Table S2). Another interesting observation was that in some orders, mainly Chiroptera and Rodentia, the correct identification of animal species was difficult, leading to taxonomic classifications of animal samples down to the genus level; and thus, it complicated the correct analysis of their role in the Leptospira cycle. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that only 64 species, out of a total of 506 mammalian species, had more than 200 samples; which means, most of the information analyzed came from just 12% of species, showing clear bias in sampling.

Techniques for detecting and diagnosing Leptospira spp.

Molecular techniques

In our search for relevant leptospirosis research for this review, 155 of 389 articles contained details of molecular techniques for detecting leptospirosis in wildlife (Table S4). Among the genes most frequently targeted for the molecular detection of Leptospira, lipL32 was the predominant marker, used in 51.6% (80/155 articles), reflecting the belief that it is highly conserved among pathogenic Leptospira species but absent in non-pathogenic species of Leptospira. The 16S rRNA (rrs) gene was also widely used (34.8%, 54/155 articles), followed by the secY gene (19.4%, 30/155 articles). In addition, 23 other genes were employed in a limited number of publications, including flaB, gyrB, glmU, hap1, lipL41, lfb1, as well as insertion sequences (IS1500, IS1533) and the I-rep gene. Molecular typing techniques such as MLST (MultiLocus Sequence Typing) (16.8%, 26/155 articles), MLVA (MultiLocus Variable-number tandem repeat Analysis) (7.1%, 11/155 articles), MST (Multispacer Sequence Typing) (1.9%, 3/155 articles), and PFGE (Pulsed-Field Gel Electrophoresis) (1.3%, 2/155 articles) have been used to characterize the genetic diversity of Leptospira in wildlife populations (Table 2). Newer higher-resolution typing methods such as highly multiplexed amplicon sequencing [42], sequencing after DNA capture and enrichment [43], and whole genome sequencing from culture have not been In some studies, more than one assay was used; for this reason, the number of references is higher than the total number of articles analyzed.

For these target genes, multiple primer protocols have been employed. Among the most frequently used, the lipL32 qPCR assay developed by Stoddard et al. [44] was cited in 34 studies. For conventional PCR, the assays of Stoddard et al. [44] and Levett et al. [45] were referenced 20 and 9 times, respectively. For the secY gene, the most frequently cited primers were those of Gravekamp et al. [46], Ahmed et al. [47], and Grillová et al. [43], with 9, 5, and 5 citations, respectively. In the case of PCRs targeting the 16S rRNA gene, the assays of Mérien et al. [48] and Gravekamp et al. [46] were the most referenced, with 23 and 9 citations, respectively, whereas for qPCR assay of Smythe et al. [49] was cited 6 times.

The MLST the schemes proposed by Boonsilp et al. [50] cited by 16 articles and Ahmed et al. [47] by 7 articles, were the most used. Serology and other methods

The most used serological technique was the Microscopic Agglutination Test (MAT), which is considered the gold standard for detecting leptospirosis. This technique was reported in 72.8% (283/389) scientific articles and was primarily applied to serum samples, though in some cases it was applied to renal exudates (Table 3). Only five studies performed paired MAT testing for higher diagnostic accuracy and differentiating between active/recent and past exposure to Leptospira. Other serological methods have also been used for the detection of antibodies, albeit less frequently. These include the fluorescent antibody technique (FAT), indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), ELISA, indirect hemagglutination assay (IHA), microagglutination immunoassay (MIA), immunoperoxidase assay (IPA), as well as rapid tests such as the Lepto Latex Test and the SNAP Lepto Test. Culture was used in 11.8% (46/389) studies, with sample types including blood, kidney, urine, and various combinations of organs. A smaller number of studies applied histopathological assays (6.9%, 27/389). The Warthin-Starry (WS) silver stain was used on kidney tissue and pooled samples. Other techniques included immunoperoxidase staining (kidney), Steiner's silver stain (liver), and silver staining (kidney and urine pool). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used in 5.1% (20/389) studies with samples from the kidney, urine, liver, and various combinations of organs, reflecting its versatility in detecting Leptospira antigens Analysis of study locations and publication output

The analysis of scientific publications on leptospirosis in wildlife in relation to countries´ gross national income (GNI) per capita revealed a moderately positive and statistically significant correlation (Spearman's rs = 0.42, p < 0.001, n = 71 countries). Countries with higher GNI per capita generally had more scientific productivity in this field. The highest number of published articles was observed from 2000 to 2025 with 70 studies from the United States and 81 articles conducted in Brazil (Fig. 4A). These study locations were followed by Italy (26 articles), Argentina (18 articles), France (14 articles), Chile (13 articles), and Spain (13 articles). Publications on leptospirosis in wild animals exhibit a marked concentration within the tropical and subtropical regions (Fig. 4C). In contrast, despite favorable climatic conditions for leptospirosis, most African countries within tropical and subtropical zones and with low to medium-to-high species richness, have been the site of only a limited number of studies.

In temperate zones where species richness is generally lower, there have been fewer studies. There are typically between 1 and 5 studies conducted in most European nations. However, studies in Italy, France, Spain, and Canada were more common. Finally, only a single study has been conducted in the polar zone, represented by Antarctica.

Discussion

Wildlife likely plays a critical role in the epidemiology of leptospirosis, acting as natural reservoirs to maintain the circulation of pathogenic Leptospira strains within ecosystems. Despite this important role, the ecological and transmission dynamics involving wildlife remain largely underexplored. Our systematic literature search (covering the years 2000–2025) identified 389 relevant articles. Most studies were narrowly focused on specific animal species, geographic regions, or isolated outbreak events, significantly limiting broader epidemiological interpretation. Existing studies therefore reveal fragmented, limited, and geographically uneven understanding of leptospirosis in natural ecosystems.

For this review, very few studies definitively diagnosed Leptospira infection in wildlife. We classified research as diagnostic only if it met the following criteria: evidence of clinical signs or tissue lesions and accompanied by a confirmatory test (MAT, PCR, or culture). This does not necessarily indicate that such infections are rare in wild animals, rather, it reflects the lack of systematic investigations in this field. Substantial logistical challenges are presented by wildlife sampling, given regulatory requirements and permitting processes for animal capture, particularly when dealing with threatened or endangered species [406].

Differentiating between active and past infections is important for understanding the burden of disease in wildlife. Active infections are best confirmed with paired MAT testing, as a rise in antibody titers against a specific serovar is indicatve of the causative agent and an active infection [407]. However, only a very limited number of studies employed such paired serological testing. Detecting pathogen DNA is also difficult, and we observed considerable heterogeneity in the primers used for molecular detection of Leptospira, targeting different genes across studies. This methodological variability hinders comparability, reduces reproducibility, and limits progress toward standardized diagnostic approaches. In many cases, primers were designed for specific species, restricting the ability to detect a wider range of Leptospira diversity. Conversely, several studies target genes specific to Leptospira in the pathogenic clades, such as lipL32 while others like secY will detect pathogenic as well as non-pathogenic leptospires [408], [409], [410]. Detection is also made more difficult as some animals may be asymptomatic, yet serve as a host or reservoir [411]. In this context, diagnostic information in wildlife is limited because current World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) guidelines do not clearly define how to classify a suspected or confirmed case in animals without signs of infection [412]. This gap may introduce bias into clinical decision-making, such as whether to administer antibiotic treatment, and into conservation management, especially when considering the release of potentially positive animals back into their natural habitat.

Conducting Leptospira surveillance in wildlife habitats provides valuable insights into exposure patterns and the circulation of the pathogen within ecosystems. However, implementing disease management programs in wildlife is challenging and costly due to the high biodiversity in natural ecosystems [413]. This complexity also makes it difficult to accurately sample wildlife and thus identify circulating genotypes and determine which species act as maintenance hosts or actively shed the pathogen through urine. Additionally, exposure risk is likely to be highly variable given behavioral diversity associated with interactions involving water, humid environments, soil, diet, or territorial behavior [411], [414]. The most commonly used method to assess exposure is the MAT. However, its application in wildlife has important limitations, particularly the lack of characterized local Leptospira strains from antigen panels, which may result in false-positive identification of infecting serogroups.

In contrast to mammals, birds and reptiles are not currently considered epidemiologically significant reservoirs even though they may be exposed to Leptospira. Birds and reptiles are thought to be exposed to Leptospira through contact with contaminated environments or food, however evidence demonstrating active shedding in reptiles remains limited and inconclusive and completely absent in birds [415]. Nevertheless, surveillance of these vertebrate classes is valuable because it may provide insight into the environmental circulation of the pathogen and reveal underexplored reservoirs and transmission pathways.

Leptospirosis is estimated to cause approximately 1.03 million cases and 58,000 deaths in humans annually, corresponding to a case fatality rate of about 6% [416]. Infection is also widespread in domestic animals. For example, a global seroprevalence of ∼21.95% has been reported in pigs [417], and the rate of asymptomatic shedding in dogs ranges from 0.2% to 94.7% [418], [419], [420], depending on the geographic region and diagnostic method. However, comparable epidemiological indicators, such as standardized estimates of prevalence, shedding rates, and disease outcomes, are largely unavailable for wildlife species. This highlights a critical knowledge gap in our understanding of Leptospira transmission ecology at the human-domestic/silvatic animal-environment interface.

The genera Sus, Canis, Vulpes, Procyon, Zalophus, Artibeus, and Eidolon include globally distributed species with opportunistic synanthropic behavior. [300], [421], [422], [423]. This proximity to both humans and other wildlife places these animals in positions to act as transmission bridges between wild and urban environments. Leptospiral DNA was detected in the urine of several wildlife species, indicating active shedding and the likelihood of transmission. A high genetic diversity of Leptospira was observed, and a total of 10 pathogenic species were identified: L. interrogans, L. santarosai, L. borgpetersenii, L. kirschneri, L. noguchi, L. alexanderi, L. fanei, L. wolfii, L. mayottensis, and L. alstonii, (Table S2).These species are commonly associated with human outbreaks and domestic animal infections, underscoring the significant diversity of pathogenic Leptospira in natural ecosystems and the possible involvement of wildlife in transmission chains and environmental maintenance [424], [425], [426]. The detection of Leptospira in marine mammals expands the ecological context of leptospirosis beyond terrestrial/fresh water transmission cycles. Coastal and estuarine species may be exposed through freshwater runoff and overlap with synanthropic animals, although the importance of these routes remains unclear [29], [129], [164]. However, the extent of involvement and exact roles of wildlife remains poorly understood. For instance, in rural areas of Ecuador and Venezuela, multiple Leptospira species have been documented in humans and domestic animals, yet there is no corresponding information regarding wildlife [427], [428].The lack of studies surveilling wildlife, domestic animals, and humans limits our understanding of broader ecological transmission dynamics.

Wildlife-rich natural ecosystems are distributed globally, however there is a bias in study locations with most research being conducted in high-income countries. Our analysis of scientific output revealed a significant positive correlation (Spearman's rs = 0.42, p < 0.001, n = 71 countries), between high, middle, and low-income countries and quantity of research studies. This pattern suggests that regions with high biodiversity and potentially complex leptospirosis transmission and maintenance patterns are underexplored and underrepresented in the scientific literature. Research on leptospirosis has primarily focused on understanding epidemiological cycles in urban and rural areas with little attention to the role of wildlife which interacts with both these environments and may be critical for long-term pathogen persistence [429]. Similarly, a higher number of studies have been conducted in tropical and subtropical regions. Ecological conditions such as warm and humid climates are thought to favor the prevalence, persistence and transmission of Leptospira, however the impact of these conditions on wildlife can be better tested with the inclusion of studies in other climatic zones.

Conclusions

Wildlife is likely to play a critical role in the cycling, maintenance, and evolution of pathogenic Leptospira. This review revealed that a high diversity of wildlife species can act as host, however, there is little information on the extent to which birds and reptiles contribute to the leptospirosis transmission cycle. In addition, a high diversity of pathogen genotypes are circulating among wildlife hosts in different countries. Unfortunately, there is an overall lack of studies of Leptopsira in wildlife, and the ones that exist are concentrated in higher-income countries and in tropical and subtropical regions. Research on wildlife leptospirosis faces many obstacles, including challenges in obtaining sampling permits, planning and conducting sampling across a wide variety of host species, and the high genomic diversity of Leptospira, which hinders detection. The difficulty of culturing Leptospira results in MAT panels that are not representative of local diversity, thereby increasing the risk of inaccurate serological tests. Finally, it is clear that much additional research is needed to understand the roles and importance of different wildlife species in the maintenance and evolution of pathogenic Leptospira and transmission among wildlife, domestic animals, and humans across a variety of environmental settings. Addressing these gaps will require expanded surveillance in wildlife, particularly in underrepresented regions, the development of diagnostic and survilance tools that better capture local Leptospira diversity, and integrative approaches that link wildlife, domestic animals, humans, and environmental factors within a One Health framework based on coordinated surveillance and information sharing across these sectors.
