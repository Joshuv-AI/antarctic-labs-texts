Association between C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose related indices and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome
Authors: Wenguang Lai, Yang Zhou, Louyi Xiao, Tingting Zhang, Wenbiao He, Junhao He, Wenjun Gu, Yucui Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) has been proposed as a biomarker for insulin resistance and inflammation, which contribute to the development of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. Waist- circumference-based anthropometric indices have been extensively studied as measures of abdominal obesity, which is strongly associated with mortality risk. This study evaluates the association with mortality of CTI and its combination with several waist circumference based anthropometric indices across stages of CKM.MethodsA total of 10,941 participants were included from NHANES database. CTI and its derivatives (CTI-waist-to-height ratio [CTI-WHtR], CTI-weight-adjusted waist index [CTI-WWI], and CTI-a body shape index [CTI-ABSI]) were calculated. Study outcomes were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Cox regression model and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were used to assess the association between the CTI-related indices and outcomes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to show their predictive ability for outcomes. Subgroup analyses were conducted to verify the robustness.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 12.83 years, 20.3% of patients (n = 2223) died, including 6.3% (n = 693) from cardiovascular causes. After adjustment, higher CTI-related indices were associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality (CTI: HR = 1.21, 95%CI 1.14–1.28; CTI-WHtR: HR = 1.14, 95%CI 1.08–1.21; CTI-WWI: HR = 1.25, 95%CI 1.18–1.33; CTI-ABSI: HR = 1.29, 95%CI 1.22–1.37) and cardiovascular mortality (CTI: sHR = 1.31, 95%CI 1.18–1.45; CTI-WHtR: sHR = 1.29, 95%CI 1.16–1.42; CTI-WWI: sHR = 1.39, 95%CI 1.25–1.55; CTI-ABSI: sHR = 1.40, 95%CI 1.26–1.55). RCS analysis showed that CTI was nonlinearly associated with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, whereas CTI-WWI and CTI-ABSI showed linear associations with both outcomes; CTI-WHtR was nonlinearly associated with all-cause mortality but linearly with cardiovascular mortality. ROC analyses revealed that CTI-ABSI had the highest predictive efficacy for all-cause mortality (AUC = 0.711) and cardiovascular mortality (AUC = 0.690). Notably, subgroup analyses revealed that the association between CTI-related indices and mortality was more pronounced in early CKM stages (stages 0–2) than in advanced stages (stages 3–4).ConclusionHigher CTI-related indices were significantly associated with a higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome. Among these indices, CTI-ABSI exhibits superior predictive performance, suggesting its potential as practical prognostic biomarkers for CKM patients.Graphical abstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03228-7.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a systemic disorder driven by pathophysiological interactions among cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic system, ultimately resulting in multiorgan dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [1]. To characterize the progression of CKM syndrome, the American Heart Association (AHA) established a staging system classifying it from stage 0 to stage 4 [2]. Recent studies have indicated that the prevalence of CKM stage 1 or higher reached 90% in US adults between 2011 and 2020, with 15% in advanced stages (3 or 4), leading to a significant public health burden [3, 4].

Insulin resistance (IR) is a core mechanism in the development and progression of CKM syndrome, which triggers abnormal lipid metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerotic damage, ultimately increasing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events and mortality [5, 6]. C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI), as a novel surrogate marker for IR, has been proven to be associated with increased mortality in populations with older adults, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease [7–9]. In addition, to enhance the predictive value of such metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers, previous studies have integrated anthropometric indicators into risk assessment frameworks, such as combining the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index with obesity-related metrics to improve cardiovascular risk prediction [10–12]. Similarly, some studies have incorporated the anthropometric parameters into CTI index to reflect the complex interactions between metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and body composition, including CTI-waist-to-height ratio (CTI-WHtR), which reflects the combined effect of IR, inflammation, and central adiposity; CTI-weight-adjusted waist index (CTI-WWI), which links metabolic-inflammatory status to waist circumference (WC) adjusted for body weight; and CTI-a body shape index (CTI-ABSI), a novel composite index that normalizes WC by body mass index (BMI) and height, focusing more on abdominal obesity while controlling for the confounding effects of overall weight and height [13].

Previous research has primarily focused on combining the TyG index with obesity-related indicators, while limited studies have systematically evaluated the association between CTI and its related indices (including CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI) and mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome [14–16]. Furthermore, the relative predictive performance of these three CTI-derived indices compared to the original CTI index in CKM syndrome remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between CTI and its related indices with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome. In addition, we also compare the predictive value of these CTI-related indices with the original CTI index, and identify the optimal indicator for mortality risk assessment in this population.

Methods

Study design and population

Data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, which is managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Centers for Health Statistics in the US. The study program follows the guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for reporting observational studies. NHANES protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the NCHS, and informed written consents were obtained from all the participants.

A total of 62,160 participants were covered in the NHANES database 1999–2010. Participants with age ≥ 20 years were involved in the study. Individuals meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) within pregnancy; (2) without follow-up information; (3) missing fasting examination data; (4) missing data on CTI and CTI-related indices; (5) missing data to determine CKM stage. Data collection

Information regarding demographic information, physical and laboratory examination, medical history, and medicine were collected from NHANES database. Demographic information included age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status, family income-poverty ratio (PIR), smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Physical and laboratory examination indices included BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), WC, total cholesterol, triglycerides (TG), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), glycohemoglobin (HbA1c), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albumin, and C-reactive protein. Medical history included cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension (HT), and diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic syndrome (MetS).

Outcomes and exposure definitions

The primary outcomes were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Mortality data were sourced from the National Death Index (NDI) database. The all-cause mortality was identified based on the International Classification of Diseases codes. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as death attributable to cardiovascular diseases (codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51) as well as cerebrovascular diseases (codes I60-I69).

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of study individuals were categorized into 4 groups according to CTI quartiles. Mean (± standard deviation [SD]) was used to report the continuous variables with normal distribution, and median (25th-75th percentile) was used to present the continuous variables with non-normal distribution. For categorical variables, number (percentages) was used to summarize. Differences among groups were evaluated with ANOVA analysis or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous data and chi-square tests for categorical data. In addition, the baseline characteristics were compared between included and excluded participants after excluding individuals under 20 years old (Table S1).

Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves and Log rank test were used to analyze cumulative hazard of all-cause mortality, and cumulative incidence function (CIF) and Gray’s (1988) test was used to calculate cardiovascular mortality. To evaluate the association between CTI-related indices and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and Fine-Gray competing risks models were conducted to estimate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) with 95% CIs, respectively. In the multivariate regression model, the selection of covariates was based on prior literature and clinical relevance. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. For covariates that violated the assumption, we used stratification (strata) or time‑dependent coefficients (tt). Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age and sex; model 3 adjusted for model 2 plus ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR level, smoking, drinking, CKM stages, eGFR, HDLC, and albumin. All analyses were performed using the multiple imputation method. Missing data were minimal (< 10% for all variables). Multiple imputation was performed using the MICE algorithm with predictive mean matching, generating 5 imputed datasets. Each exposure variable was imputed in a separate model that included that exposure, the outcome, and all covariates. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted to investigate the nonlinear association between CTI-related indices and mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curves (AUC) were used to show the predictive power of CTI-related indices on all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. DeLong’s test was performed to compare the predictive performance of CTI, CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI. Time-dependent ROC analyses were also conducted to predict all-cause and cardiovascular mortality during 1, 3, 5, 10-year follow up. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the effects of CTI-related indices (continuous) on the all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in several subgroups, including age (≥ 60 vs. < 60 years), sex (males vs. females), BMI (≥ 28 vs. < 28 kg/m2), smoking status (current vs. former vs. never smoking), drinking status (current vs. former vs. never drinking), and CKM stages (non-advanced vs. advanced stages). Furthermore, we performed complex sample weighting analysis using NHANES-recommended fasting subsample weights (implemented with the ‘survey’ package in R) [21]. Weighted baseline characteristics and weighted regression model were conducted to verify the main results (Tables S2 and S3). The statistical analysis was performed by R software (version 4.4.1). Two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Predictive performance of CTI-related indices in the outcomes

The ROC curve analysis showed that CTI-ABSI had the highest predictive value for all-cause mortality (AUC = 0.711, 95%CI: 0.700–0.723), followed by CTI-WWI (AUC = 0.690, 95%CI: 0.678–0.702), CTI (AUC = 0.630, 95%CI: 0.618–0.643), and CTI-WHtR (AUC = 0.620, 95%CI: 0.608–0.633). Regarding cardiovascular mortality, CTI-ABSI still had the highest predictive value (AUC = 0.690, 95%: CI 0.671–0.709), followed by CTI-WWI (AUC = 0.678, 95%: CI 0.659–0.697), CTI-WHtR (AUC = 0.626, 95%: CI 0.606–0.645), and CTI (AUC = 0.614, 95%: CI 0.593–0.635) (Fig. 4 and Table S4). Sensitivity analysis

In subgroup analysis, each 1-SD increase in CTI, CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI levels was still associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, which was consistent with the main results. Significant interactions were detected between all indices and age and CKM stages (all P < 0.05). Additional interactions were observed for CTI-WHtR and CTI-WWI with BMI and drinking status, and for CTI-ABSI with sex (all P < 0.05) (Figs. S2–S5). After correcting for violations of the proportional hazard assumption using stratification and time‑dependent coefficients, the hazard ratios for all CTI‑related indices remained essentially unchanged from the primary analyses (Table S5), supporting the robustness of our main findings.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the association between CTI and its related indices (CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI) with the all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in CKM syndrome. Our findings demonstrated that elevated level of CTI-related indices were significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. RCS analysis showed a J-shaped association between CTI-related indices and the outcomes. In addition, ROC analysis showed that CTI-ABSI index had the highest predictive value for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared with other indices in CKM syndrome. Furthermore, subgroup analysis indicated that the increasing of CTI-related indices was still related to a higher risk of mortality, which was consistent with the main result.

Previous studies indicated that IR and TyG-related indices were significantly associated with the poor prognosis and cardiovascular disease progression in CKM syndrome [22–24]. Meanwhile, CRP, as an inflammatory marker, was widely applied in clinical screening and risk assessment of cardiovascular diseases [25]. Existing studies have confirmed that the combined use of CRP and TyG index can achieve more effective stratified assessment of cardiovascular disease risk. Ou et al. demonstrated that CTI index was associated with CVD and mortality risk in CKM syndrome stages 0–3 [26]. Similarly, Xu et al. reported that CTI index was positively associated with the risk of stroke in CKM syndrome stages 0–3 [27]. Our results were consistent with the previous study. In addition, many studies have adopted the combined application of the TyG index and traditional obesity-related parameters, which can provide a better assessment of cardiovascular risk compared with the TyG index alone [28–30]. Regarding the CTI index, Zhou et al. found that the predictive efficacy of CTI derivatives was superior to that of the single CTI indicator, among which CTI-WHtR had the strongest association with the risk of stroke [13]. In our study, we compared the mortality risk of CTI and CTI-related indicators (CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI) in CKM syndrome, and found that CTI-ABSI had the strongest association with the risk of all-cause mortality, and CTI-WWI showed the most significant correlation with the risk of cardiovascular mortality.

Potential biological mechanisms may explain the observed associations between CTI-related indices and mortality in CKM syndrome. IR and chronic inflammation have been reported to be important mechanisms in the development and progression of CKM syndrome, and CTI index reflects both conditions simultaneously [5, 6]. The synergistic effect of combining the CTI index with obesity-related indices may promote the progression of CKM syndrome through multiple pathophysiological pathways. On the one hand, IR leads to a long-term elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress markers, and impairs insulin signal transduction through the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, reducing nitric oxide bioavailability and inducing endothelial dysfunction [31, 32]. Meanwhile, IR enhances mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway activity, which increases vasoconstriction and pro-inflammatory responses, aggravating endothelial dysfunction, myocardial stiffness, and renal injury [33, 34]. In addition, IR can also lead to hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, which are common in patients with CKM syndrome, manifesting as elevated glycosylated acid and decreased HDLC concentrations, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, and accelerating the progression of vascular remodeling and atherosclerosis [35, 36]. On the other hand, integrating CTI with obesity-related indices captures the synergistic damage from ectopic adipose tissue deposition. Obesity triggers the release of free fatty acids and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), which further worsens insulin signaling, induce local inflammation, oxidative stress and β-cell dysfunction, which exacerbates cardiovascular and metabolic disorders and ultimately promote atherosclerosis, cardiac remodeling and renal fibrosis [37, 38] . Given that WHtR, WWI, and ABSI are all derived from waist circumference, their high intercorrelation may explain the broadly similar mortality associations observed across the three CTI-derived indices. Thus, the CTI combined with obesity indices may better characterize the vicious cycle among metabolic disorder, cardiovascular injury, and renal dysfunction in CKM populations.

Notably, subgroup analysis further verified the robustness of the positive associations between CTI-related indices and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, while revealing obvious population heterogeneity. Stratified by age, the associations of CTI, CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI and CTI-ABSI with mortality outcomes were significantly stronger in participants aged < 60 years than in those aged ≥ 60 years, suggesting that these indices have superior early warning value for young and middle-aged CKM syndrome patients. Stratified by CKM stages, the positive correlations between all CTI-related indices and mortality risk were more prominent in non-advanced stages (CKM stages 0–2) than in advanced stages (CKM stages 3–4). The stronger association in early CKM stages may reflect that in the absence of established cardiovascular or kidney disease, metabolic inflammation (captured by CTI) plays a more dominant role in mortality risk. In advanced CKM stages, competing risks and irreversible organ damage may attenuate the relative contribution of CTI. Although event numbers precluded a separate analysis of CKM stage 0, we hypothesize that CTI‑related indices might be most useful for mortality risk identification at the earliest stage of the CKM continuum (stage 0), where metabolic inflammation has not yet led to irreversible target organ damage. This hypothesis warrants further investigation in larger prospective cohorts. In addition, significant interaction effects were observed in some subgroups: CTI-WHtR and CTI-WWI showed interactions with BMI and drinking status, while CTI-ABSI presented an interaction with sex. Nevertheless, all CTI-related indices remained stably associated with elevated mortality risk across different sex, BMI, smoking and drinking subgroups. Overall, the subgroup findings were highly consistent with the main results, suggesting that CTI and its obesity-related derivatives are universal prognostic biomarkers for CKM syndrome patients, with more prominent advantages in young and non-advanced populations.

The present study has several notable strengths that support the reliability of our results. To our knowledge, this study was the first to evaluate the relationship between CTI-related indices and mortality in CKM syndrome, and results showed that CTI-related indices were independently associated with mortality in CKM syndrome. The ROC curve analysis revealed that CTI-related indices had moderate prognostic performance for mortality, among which CTI-ABSI exhibited the highest predictive efficacy. These findings indicate that CTI and its obesity-related derivatives (CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, CTI-ABSI), as convenient and non-invasive biomarkers, may have the potential to assist clinicians in the early screening and risk stratification of populations with CKM syndrome. Notably, these indices integrate inflammation, metabolism, and adiposity into a composite marker and may provide incremental prognostic information beyond conventional clinical predictors, including CRP, TyG index, or routine laboratory indicators. Further prospective studies are needed to validate our findings and clarify the prognostic value of CTI-related indices for mortality risk stratification in CKM syndrome.

Limitation

Several limitations existed in this study. Firstly, the NHANES dataset is nationally representative of the U.S. population, the sample may not fully capture the diversity of other racial or geographical groups, thereby limiting its broader applicability. Secondly, the calculation of CTI-related indices was obtained from baseline assessments, without accounting for the dynamic changes throughout the follow-up period. In addition, although we have adjusted several potential confounders in our analysis, residual confounding factors may still exist. For instance, the elevation of CRP levels may be affected by subclinical infection, inflammatory diseases, malignant tumors, and frailty. Meanwhile, medication use (such as statins, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors), dietary habits, physical activity, renal treatment, and healthcare accessibility may all influence the results of our study. Future research should adopt prospective designs and interventional measures to clarify the role of CTI-related indices in the prognosis of CKM syndrome.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that the elevated CTI-related indices (CTI, CTI-WHtR, CTI-WWI, and CTI-ABSI) are significantly associated with the increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome. These indices demonstrate moderate prognostic performance for mortality risk stratification, among which CTI-ABSI exhibits highest predictive performance for mortality.
