Abstract

This strictly extends the Beta ⁡ ( α , β ) \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) case. Let η \eta denote the common law of the i.i.d. initial number of particles { η x } x ∈ ℤ \{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}} . Using a percolation comparison and sharp one-particle displacement tails, we obtain a universal threshold at β = 1 2 \beta=\tfrac{1}{2} . If β < 1 2 \beta<\tfrac{1}{2} and ℙ ⁡ ( η = 0 ) < 1 \mathbb{P}(\eta=0)<1 , survival has positive probability. Introduction

Interacting particle systems that combine random motion with local activation rules have provided deep insights into the probabilistic mechanisms underlying epidemic propagation, rumor dynamics, and branching structures in random environments. Among these, the
frog model
, introduced in the late 1990s and subsequently developed by Alves, Machado and Popov
[
1
]
, has become one of the most studied frameworks. The dynamics are straightforward: particles sit on the vertices of a connected graph; those at the root begin active while all others are inactive. Active particles perform independent simple symmetric random walks, and any inactive particle encountered is immediately activated, itself beginning a random walk and perpetuating the mechanism. The central question is whether the system
survives
, meaning that active particles persist indefinitely, or whether it eventually
dies out
. This binary outcome masks a remarkable richness of behavior, shaped by the geometry of the graph, the distribution of initial particles, and the stochastic laws governing activation and death.

The model exhibits contrasting behaviors across different settings. On trees and higher-dimensional lattices, it has been shown that the frog model undergoes genuine phase transitions, with survival depending on critical parameters that can often be computed or bounded
[
6
,
9
,
7
]
. On
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
, phase transitions occur depending on the number of initial particles per site
[
1
]
. On homogeneous trees, upper and lower bounds for the critical probability have been refined in a sequence of works
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. These results established the frog model as a fertile ground for investigating survival phenomena analogous to those of percolation and branching processes. In sharp contrast, on
\mathbb{Z}
with geometric lifetimes governed by a
fixed
parameter
p
, extinction occurs almost surely under mild assumptions, and no non-trivial survival is possible. This rigidity has motivated the study of variations designed to recover survival, including models with drifted random walks or spatially varying survival parameters
[
3
,
8
]
.

A breakthrough in this direction was recently provided by Carvalho and Machado
[
5
]
, who considered a version of the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
with a
random survival parameter
. In their formulation, each particle has a survival probability
\pi
drawn independently from a distribution on
(0,1)
, and its lifetime is geometric with parameter determined by this realization. Focusing on the Beta family
\pi\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
, they proved a sharp threshold at
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
: extinction for
\beta>1/2
and survival with positive probability for
\beta<1/2
, with the behavior at the boundary case
\beta=1/2
depending on
\alpha
and on the initial configuration.

The present work extends this phenomenon to a broad and natural class of survival distributions whose densities satisfy, near the right endpoint,

f_{\pi}(u)\ \sim\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L\!\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\qquad(u\uparrow 1),

(1)

where
\beta>0
and
L
is slowly varying at
\infty
. This class contains the Beta family as a special case (
L\equiv\text{constant}
) and also accommodates a wide range of heterogeneous right tails. We show that the extinction–survival dichotomy depends only on the exponent
\beta
: if
\beta>\tfrac{1}{2}
the process dies out, while if
\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}
it survives with positive probability. In the critical regime
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
, the outcome hinges on the quantities
\liminf_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2})
and
\limsup_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2})
, and on the initial configuration.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduce some definitions for the model and states the main results, whose proofs are presented in Section
3
. Section
4
is devoted to examples: Section
4.1
presents concrete families of survival–parameter laws satisfying condition (
1
), together with their parameter
\beta
and slowly varying factor
L
; Section
4.2
provides an example of a case that falls in the inconclusive regime of the main theorem, where we can still prove results regarding survival; Section
4.3
gives an extensive list of distributions for odds satisfying (
3
).

2
Definitions and main results

This section introduces the formal setup of the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
with particle-wise random survival parameters. We specify the initial configuration, the independent random walks, and the geometric lifetimes determined by a common random variable
\pi\in(0,1)
. The analysis focuses on survival-parameter laws whose densities satisfy condition (
1
),
where
\beta>0
and
L
is slowly varying at infinity. This class strictly extends the Beta family and captures a broad range of regularly varying right tails.

We then introduce the notions of survival and extinction of the system. The main result establishes a sharp phase transition governed solely by the exponent
\beta
. When
\beta>1/2
, the process becomes extinct almost surely under mild moment assumptions on the initial particle counts, whereas for
\beta<1/2
the system survives with positive probability whenever the initial configuration is not identically zero. The critical case
\beta=1/2
requires a refined analysis involving the limits of
L(n^{2})
and the expectation of the initial distribution. These definitions and asymptotic conditions form the basis for the proofs presented in Section 3.

2.1
Model and notation

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We say that a function
L:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
is
regularly varying
with index
\rho
at infinity if

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{L(\lambda x)}{L(x)}=\lambda^{\rho},\qquad\text{for every }\lambda>0;

moreover,
L
is called
slowly varying
at infinity if
\rho=0
. We say that
f(x)\sim g(x)
as
x\to\infty
when
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
; the notation is used analogously for other limits, such as
x\downarrow 0
or
x\uparrow 1
.

We now define the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
in a formal way. For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\eta_{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the initial number of particles at
x
. Particles are indexed by
(x,i)
with
1\leq i\leq\eta_{x}
.
The families

\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad\{\pi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}

are mutually independent, where
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{\pi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. sequences, and
\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x,i}
is a family of independent simple symmetric random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
with
S^{x,i}_{0}=x
; the variables
\pi_{x,i}\in(0,1)
have common law
\pi
(with density
f_{\pi}
when
\pi
is absolutely continuous).

Given
\pi_{x,i}=p
, the lifetime
L_{x,i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is geometric with tail

\mathbb{P}(L_{x,i}\geq k\mid\pi_{x,i}=p)=p^{k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Conditional on
\{\pi_{x,i}\}
, the lifetimes
\{L_{x,i}\}
are independent of
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
and of the walks

\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}
(and of each other).

At time
0
all particles at the origin are
active
and all others are
inactive
. Each active particle evolves as follows: before each step it dies with probability
1-\pi_{x,i}
; if it survives, it takes one step of its simple symmetric random walk. Whenever an active particle visits a site, all particles
present there become active and evolve independently by the same rules. We denote this system by
\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)
, where
\pi
is the common law of the survival parameters and
\eta
is the law of the initial number of particles per vertex.

2.2
Survival and extinction

This section formalizes the notions of survival and extinction for the frog model on
\mathbb{Z}
. We work under the framework introduced in Section 2.1, where particle lifetimes are determined by a survival parameter with density exhibiting the asymptotic form
(1-u)^{\beta-1}L((1-u)^{-1})
as
u\uparrow 1
.

The main theorem presented here establishes a sharp dichotomy governed by the exponent
\beta
. When
\mathbb{E}[\eta]<\infty
and
\beta>1/2
, extinction occurs almost surely. In contrast, if
\beta<1/2
and the initial configuration is not almost surely empty, the process survives with positive probability. The critical case
\beta=1/2
requires additional conditions on the slowly varying function
L(n^{2})
and the expected number of initial particles.

Definition 1
.

A realization of
\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)

survives
if at all times there exists at least one active particle; otherwise it
dies out
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the survival-parameter law
\pi
has density satisfying
condition

f_{\pi}(u)\ \sim\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L\!\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\qquad(u\uparrow 1),

(2)

with
\beta>0
and
L
slowly varying at
\infty
. Then:

(i) If
E(\eta)<\infty
and either

•

\beta>\tfrac{1}{2}
,  or

•

\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\displaystyle 8\;\limsup_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2})\ <\ \frac{1}{E(\eta)}
,

then

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}\big]=0.

(ii) If
\mathbb{P}(\eta=0)<1
and either

•

\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}
, or

•

\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\;\liminf_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2})\ >\ \frac{1}{E(\eta)}

(with
1/E(\eta):=0
when
E(\eta)=\infty
)
,

then

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\text{ survives}\big]>0

Remark 3
.

In particular,
\pi\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
satisfies condition (
2
), with
L\equiv 1/B(\alpha,\beta)
. When
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
, item
(i)
becomes
B(\alpha,\tfrac{1}{2})>8\,E(\eta)
and item
(ii)
becomes
B(\alpha,\tfrac{1}{2})<\sqrt{2}\,E(\eta)
. This improves the results in
[
5
]
.

Remark 4
.

The following consequences are immediate from the proof of Theorem
2
.
Let
U\in(0,1)
,
\beta>0
, and
L
be a slowly varying function.

•

If
f_{\pi}(u)\ \leq\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{1-u}\Big)\quad\text{for all }u\in[U,1),
then Theorem
2
(i) holds.

•

If
f_{\pi}(u)\ \geq\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{1-u}\Big)\quad\text{for all }u\in[U,1),
then Theorem
2
(ii) holds.

2.3
Heavy-tailed odds

Recall that
\pi
can be interpreted as the (random) probability that a particle survives at any given instant of time. In this section, we shift our focus from probabilities to odds by setting

X:=\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}\in(0,\infty),

and concentrate on cases where
X
is heavy-tailed. The inverse relation is then given by

\pi=\frac{X}{1+X}\in(0,1).

Proposition 5
.

Let X be a non-negative random variable with density
f_{X}(t)
such that

f_{X}(t)\ \sim\,t^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(t)\qquad(t\rightarrow\infty)

(3)

for some constants
\beta>0
and a function
L_{X}
slowly varying at infinity.
Then, the random variable
\pi:=\frac{X}{1+X}
has a density and

f_{\pi}(u)\ \sim(1-u)^{\beta-1}L_{X}\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\qquad(u\uparrow 1).

Therefore, Proposition
5
complements Theorem
2
by providing results on survival or extinction through the odds distribution. Moreover, one can also note that condition (
3
) is equivalent to

f_{X}
being regularly varying with index
\rho=-(\beta+1)
at infinity.

We now state a transfer principle showing that regularly varying tails are stable under multiplication by an independent factor possessing a moment slightly above the tail index. Specifically, if

f_{X}(t)\sim t^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(t)
as
t\to\infty
and
Y\geq 0
is absolutely continuous and independent of
X
with

\mathbb{E}[Y^{\beta+\varepsilon}]<\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then the product
Z:=XY
retains the same tail index
\beta
and carries over the slowly varying factor
L_{X}
. The precise statement is as follows.

Proposition 6
.

Let
X
have density
f_{X}
satisfying

f_{X}(t)\ \sim t^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(t)\qquad(t\to\infty),

(4)

for some
\beta>0
and
L_{X}
slowly varying at
\infty
.
Let
Y\geq 0
be absolutely continuous and
independent
of
X
and suppose there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{E}[Y^{\beta+\varepsilon}]<\infty
.
Define
Z:=XY
. Then, as
z\to\infty
,

f_{Z}(z)\ \sim z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{Z}(z),

(5)

with slowly varying factor

L_{Z}(z):=\mathbb{E}[Y^{\beta}]\,L_{X}(z)

3
Proofs

This chapter contains the proofs of the main results stated in Section 2. The analysis relies on sharp asymptotic estimates for the maximal displacement of a single particle during its lifetime, together with a percolation-style comparison that translates these estimates into survival or extinction of the full system.

3.1
Survival and extinction

For a particle
i
initially at
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the maximal one–sided and two–sided displacements during its lifetime:

D^{\rightarrow}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq L_{z,i}}\big(S^{z,i}_{n}-z\big),\qquad D^{\leftarrow}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq L_{z,i}}\big(z-S^{z,i}_{n}\big),\,

D^{*}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq L_{z,i}}\big(|S^{z,i}_{n}-z|\big)=D^{\rightarrow}_{z,i}\vee D^{\leftarrow}_{z,i}.

By homogeneity, write
D^{\rightarrow}
and
D^{*}
for generic copies. A key identity (see
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
below) is the exact conditional tail

\mathbb{P}\!\big(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n\mid\pi=p\big)=\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-p^{2}}}{p}\Big)^{\!n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

(6)

and, by symmetry,

\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)=\mathbb{P}(D^{\leftarrow}\geq n)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(D^{*}\geq n),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(7)

We rely on a percolation comparison that translates survival/extinction into sharp asymptotics for
D^{\rightarrow}
and
D^{*}
. We will use Proposition 1.2 in
[
5
]
, which states that

If
E(\eta)<\infty
and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{*}\geq n)\leq 2\limsup_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\ <\ \frac{1}{2E(\eta)},

then
\mathbb{P}\!\big[\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\ \mathrm{survives}\big]=0
.
Conversely, if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\ >\ \frac{1}{E(\eta)}\quad\textup{(with $1/E(\eta):=0$ when $E(\eta)=\infty$)},

then
\mathbb{P}\!\big[\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta)\ \mathrm{survives}\big]>0
.

By (
6
) and integrating over
\pi
, the decisive quantity becomes

n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\ =\ \int_{0}^{1}n\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\Big)^{\!n}f_{\pi}(x)\,dx,

(8)

whenever
\pi
has density
f_{\pi}
; for upper bounds we also use

I_{n}:=\int_{0}^{1}n\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\Big)^{\!n}f_{\pi}(x)\,dx,

(9)

exploiting
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\geq\sqrt{1-x}
, and for lower bounds

J_{n}:=\int_{0}^{1}n\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\Big)^{\!n}f_{\pi}(x)\,dx.

(10)

So, to conclude Theorem
2
(i), it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\beta>\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad I_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\quad\text{hence }\limsup_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{*}\geq n)=0;

(11)

\displaystyle\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}I_{n}\ \leq\ 2\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2}),

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{thus }\limsup_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{*}\geq n)\leq 4\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2}).

Likewise, we prove Theorem
2
(ii) by showing that

\displaystyle\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}=\infty,\quad\text{hence }\liminf_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)=\infty;

(13)

\displaystyle\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}J_{n}\ \geq\ \sqrt{2}\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2}),

(14)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{thus }\liminf_{n\to\infty}n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\geq\sqrt{2}\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2}).

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Assume that for some
\beta>0
,

f_{\pi}(u)\ \sim\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}L\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\qquad(u\uparrow 1).

(15)

Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
such that for all
u\in(1-\eta_{\varepsilon},1)
,

(1-\varepsilon)\,(1-u)^{\beta-1}L\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\ \leq\ f_{\pi}(u)\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)\,(1-u)^{\beta-1}L\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big).

(16)

For

I_{n}\ :=\ \int_{0}^{1}n\Big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}\Big)^{\!n}f_{\pi}(x)\,dx.

Note that

\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x}}{x}=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1-x}},

hence, defining

I=\int_{0}^{1}K_{n}(x)\,f_{\pi}(x)\,dx

I_{n}=A_{n}+B_{n},\qquad A_{n}:=\int_{0}^{1-\eta_{\varepsilon}}K_{n}(x)f_{\pi}(x)\,dx,\quad B_{n}:=\int_{1-\eta_{\varepsilon}}^{1}K_{n}(x)f_{\pi}(x)\,dx.

(17)

For

(18)

Therefore,
0\leq A_{n}\leq n\,\rho^{n}
, which tends to

.

Now, for
x\in(1-\eta_{\varepsilon},1)
, set

(i.e.,

,

), then let

(so

).
Without any approximation,

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}}2n\,s\,(1+s)^{-n}\,f_{\pi}(1-s^{2})\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\int_{0}^{n\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}}t\Big(1+\frac{t}{n}\Big)^{-n}\,f_{\pi}\!\Big(1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\,dt.

\Big(1+\frac{t}{n}\Big)^{-n}\ \leq\ e^{-t},

\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)

\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}\leq 1/\sqrt{X(A,\varepsilon)}

\eta_{\varepsilon}
by
\hat{\eta}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1/X(A,\varepsilon))

\eta_{\varepsilon}

[0,n\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}]\subset[0,n/\sqrt{X(A,\varepsilon)}]

37
) from Lemma

, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq B_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\int_{0}^{n\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}}t\Big(1+\frac{t}{n}\Big)^{-n}\,(1+\varepsilon)\Big(\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)^{\beta-1}L\!\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{t^{2}}\Big)\,dt

(19)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\varepsilon)\,n^{1-2\beta}\int_{0}^{n\sqrt{\eta_{\varepsilon}}}t^{2\beta-1}\Big(1+\frac{t}{n}\Big)^{-n}A\,\max\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\,L(n^{2})\,dt

) hold, and item (i) follows.

Proof of Theorem
2
(ii):

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
R_{n}(x):=n\big(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\big)^{n}
, and

J_{n}\ :=\ \int_{0}^{1}R_{n}(x)\,f_{\pi}(x)\,dx.

For
x\in(0,1)
, set
s=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
(so
x=\sqrt{1-s^{2}}
and
dx=-\frac{s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\,ds
).
Then

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}n\Big(\frac{1-s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\Big)^{n}\frac{s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\,f_{\pi}\big(\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\big)\,ds.

Making the scaling
t=ns
(so
ds=dt/n
and
t\in(0,n)
) leads to

J_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{n}t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ f_{\pi}\!\biggl(\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}\biggr)\,dt.

(21)

We now seek suitable bounds for each factor in the integrand. For
s\in(0,1)
, define

r(s):=\frac{1-s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\in(0,1).

By Lemma
10
(iii)
\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\frac{-\log r(s)}{s}=1
. By the definition of limit, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)\subset(0,1/2]
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon,1}\in(0,1)
such that
\bigl|\frac{-\log r(s)}{s}-1\bigr|<\varepsilon
for all
s\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon,1}]
. Hence

(1-\varepsilon)\,s\ \leq\ -\log r(s)\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)\,s,\qquad s\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon,1}].

Exponentiating yields
e^{-(1+\varepsilon)s}\leq r(s)\leq e^{-(1-\varepsilon)s}
for all
s\in[0,\delta_{\varepsilon,1}]
. Taking again
s=t/n
, we deduce

e^{-(1+\varepsilon)t}\ \leq\ \Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}}}\Biggr)^{\!n}=r\!\Bigl(\frac{t}{n}\Bigr)^{\!n}\ \leq\ e^{-(1-\varepsilon)t},\qquad t\in(0,n\delta_{\varepsilon,1}].

(22)

Moreover, for any fixed
t>0
, the bounds in (
22
) can be made arbitrarily close to
e^{-t}
, and choosing
n
large enough so that
t<n\delta_{\varepsilon,1}
ensures that the inequality holds. Consequently, we have the pointwise convergence

\lim_{n\to\infty}r\!\Bigl(\frac{t}{n}\Bigr)^{\!n}=e^{-t}.

(23)

Next, we bound
f_{\pi}(\sqrt{1-s^{2}})
for
s
near
0
. Note that by Lemma
10
(ii), we have, as
s\downarrow 0
,

1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}=\frac{s^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(s^{4})=\frac{s^{2}}{2}(1+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})).

Applying Lemma
10
(i) gives
(1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}})^{\beta-1}=2^{\,1-\beta}s^{2\beta-2}(1+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}))
as
s\downarrow 0
. Therefore, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon,2}\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon,1}]
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\,2^{\,1-\beta}\,s^{2\beta-2}\ \leq\ \bigl(1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\bigr)^{\beta-1},\qquad s\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon,2}].

Combining this with equations (
36
) and (
16
) in which
u=\sqrt{1-s^{2}}
, we conclude that there exists
\delta^{*}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon,2}]
such that

(1-\varepsilon)^{3}\,2^{\,1-\beta}\,s^{2\beta-2}L\Big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\Big)\ \leq\ f_{\pi}\big(\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\big),\qquad s\in(0,\delta^{*}_{\varepsilon}].

(24)

Now define

D_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{n\delta^{*}_{\varepsilon}}t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ f_{\pi}\!\biggl(\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}\biggr)\,dt\

(25)

and note that
D_{n}\leq J_{n}
.

Combining (
25
) with (
24
) and applying the Potter’s bound (
37
) from Lemma
11
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\ n^{1-2\beta}\int_{0}^{T}t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ (1-\varepsilon)^{2}\,2^{\,1-\beta}t^{2\beta-2}L\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}}{t^{2}}\Bigr)\,dt

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}A^{-1}2^{\,1-\beta}\,\ n^{1-2\beta}L(n^{2})\int_{0}^{T}t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\,t^{2\beta-2}\,\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}dt

for any fixed
T>0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
,
A>1
, and sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
T\leq n\delta^{*}_{\varepsilon}
.

Additionally, for
t\in[0,T]
,

1\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{T^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ 1,

and by (
22
) there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, if
n\geq N
,

t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ t^{2\beta-2}\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\ \leq\ 2e^{-(1-\varepsilon)t}t^{2\beta-1}\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\},\qquad t\in[0,T].

Moreover,
2e^{-(1-\varepsilon)t}t^{2\beta-1}\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}
is integrable on
[0,T]
, and by (
23
) the hypotheses of the Dominated Convergence Theorem are satisfied. Therefore,

\int_{0}^{T}t\,\Biggl(\frac{1-\frac{t}{n}}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\Biggr)^{\!n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\,1-\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\,}}\ t^{2\beta-2}\,\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}dt\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \int_{0}^{T}e^{-t}t^{2\beta-1}\,\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\,dt.

Then,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\ \frac{D_{n}}{n^{\,1-2\beta}L(n^{2})}\ \geq\ (1-\varepsilon)^{2}A^{-1}\,2^{\,1-\beta}\int_{0}^{T}t^{2\beta-1}e^{-t}\,\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\,dt.

(26)

Note that (
26
) holds for every
T>0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta)
, and
A>1
. Additionally,
\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\leq t^{-2\varepsilon}
and
t^{2(\beta-\varepsilon)-1}e^{-t}
is integrable on
[0,T]
. Then, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem, letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
we obtain that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\ \frac{D_{n}}{n^{\,1-2\beta}L(n^{2})}\ \geq\ A^{-1}\,2^{\,1-\beta}\int_{0}^{T}t^{2\beta-1}e^{-t}\,dt.

Finally, letting
T\to\infty
and
A\downarrow 1
yields

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\ \frac{D_{n}}{n^{\,1-2\beta}L(n^{2})}\ \geq\ 2^{1-\beta}\Gamma(2\beta).

Therefore,

\begin{cases}\liminf_{n\to\infty}D_{n}=\infty,&\text{if }\beta<\tfrac{1}{2},\\[4.0pt]
\liminf_{n\to\infty}D_{n}\geq\sqrt{2}\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}L(n^{2}),&\text{if }\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(27)

Since
J_{n}\geq D_{n}
, (
13
) and (
14
) hold, and item (ii) follows.

∎

3.2
Results regarding odds

This section develops auxiliary results concerning the odds variable which provides an alternative parametrization of the survival law.

The proofs rely on change-of-variables arguments and on uniform convergence theorems for regularly varying functions. These results ensure that heavy-tailed behavior is stable under natural transformations and confirm that the exponent
\beta
continues to govern the asymptotics relevant to the frog model.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Let
\pi:=\frac{X}{1+X}
. By the change-of-variables theorem,
\pi
admits a density given by
f_{\pi}(u):=f_{X}\!\big(u/(1-u)\big)/(1-u)^{2}
. For
t=\frac{u}{1-u}
we have
(1-u)=1/(1+t)
and

\frac{f_{\pi}(u)}{(1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L_{X}\!\big(\tfrac{1}{1-u}\big)}=\frac{f_{X}(t)\,(t+1)^{\beta+1}}{L_{X}(t+1)}=\bigg(\frac{f_{X}(t)\,t^{\beta+1}}{L_{X}(t)}\bigg)\,\frac{L_{X}(t)}{L_{X}(t+1)}\,\big(1+1/t\big)^{\beta+1}.

(28)

Since
L_{X}
is slowly varying at infinity, we have
L_{X}(t)\sim L_{X}(t+1)
, and therefore, by (
3
), the expression in (
28
) converges to
1
as
t\uparrow\infty
. This completes the proof, since
u\uparrow 1
as
t\uparrow\infty
.

∎

Proof of Proposition
6
.

Let
Z:=XY
. By the change–of–variables theorem, consider

T:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\qquad T(x,y)=(z,y)\ \text{ with }\ z=xy.

For
y>0
the inverse map is
T^{-1}(z,y)=(z/y,y)
, and the Jacobian determinant is

\det\tfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(z,y)}=\frac{1}{y}.

If
X
and
Y
are independent with densities
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
, then the joint density of
(Z,Y)
is

f_{Z,Y}(z,y)=\frac{1}{y}\,f_{X}\!\Bigl(\frac{z}{y}\Bigr)\,f_{Y}(y),\qquad y>0.

Integrating out
y>0
yields the density of
Z
:

f_{Z}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\,f_{X}\!\Bigl(\frac{z}{y}\Bigr)\,f_{Y}(y)\,dy,\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}.

Define the rescaled integrand

H_{z}(y)\ :=\ \frac{1}{y}\,\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)},\qquad y>0,

(29)

so that

\frac{f_{Z}(z)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)}\ =\ \int_{0}^{\infty}H_{z}(y)\,f_{Y}(y)\,dy\ =\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[H_{z}(Y)\right].

By (
4
), for each fixed
y>0
,

\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)}=\underbrace{\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{(z/y)^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z/y)}}_{\longrightarrow\ 1}\cdot y^{\beta+1}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{L_{X}(z/y)}{L_{X}(z)}}_{\longrightarrow\ 1}\qquad(z\to\infty).

Hence, by (
29
),

H_{z}(y)\ \xrightarrow[z\to\infty]{}y^{\beta}\qquad\text{for every }y>0.

(30)

We use Potter’s bounds for regularly varying functions (see
[
4
, Thm. 1.5.6]
): for every
\delta\in(0,1)
and
C>1
there exist a constant
t_{\delta,C}
such that

\frac{f_{X}(tu)}{f_{X}(t)}\ \leq C\,u^{-(\beta+1)+\delta},\,u\geq 1,\qquad t\geq t_{\delta,C}.

(31)

Moreover, by (
4
), there exists
t_{1}
such that
f_{X}(t)\leq 2\,t^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(t)
for all
t\geq t_{1}
.
Fix
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
and take
z\geq z_{0}:=\max\{t_{\delta,C},t_{1}\}
. With
t=z
and
u=1/y
we obtain

\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)}=\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{f_{X}(z)}\cdot\frac{f_{X}(z)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(z)}\ \leq\ 2\,C\,y^{\beta+1+\delta},\,\,0<y\leq 1,\\

(32)

Moreover, let
g(z):=f(z)z^{\beta+1+\delta}
; it is easy to show that
g
is regularly varying with index
\delta
and the Uniform Convergence Theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 1.5.2]
) yields that for every
b>0

\sup_{\lambda\in(0,b]}\Bigg|\frac{g(\lambda z)}{g(z)}-\lambda^{\delta}\Bigg|\xrightarrow[z\to\infty]{}0.

In particular, taking
b=1
. Now, given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
z_{1}:=z_{1}(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
z\geq z_{1}
, then
\frac{g(\lambda z)}{g(z)\lambda^{\delta}}\leq\varepsilon\lambda^{-\delta}+1
for all
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Then, for
z\geq\max\{z_{1},t_{1}\}
and
y>1
, that is,
\frac{1}{y}\in(0,1]
, we have

\frac{f_{X}(z/y)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)}=\frac{f_{X}(z/y)\frac{1}{y^{\beta+1+\delta}}}{f_{X}(z)\frac{1}{y^{\delta}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{y^{\beta+1}}}\cdot\frac{f_{X}(z)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(z)}\ \leq\ 2\,y^{\beta+1}\,(\varepsilon y^{\delta}+1),\,\,y>1,\\

(33)

Dividing by
y
(cf. (
29
)) yields the
uniform
bound

0\ \leq\ H_{z}(y)\ \leq\ 2\,Cy^{\beta+\delta}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,1]}(y)+2\,y^{\beta}\,(\varepsilon y^{\delta}+1)\,\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(y),\quad z\geq z_{0}\vee z_{1}.

(34)

Let
G(y):=2\,Cy^{\beta+\delta}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,1]}(y)+2\,y^{\beta}\,(\varepsilon y^{\delta}+1)\,\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(y)
. Taking expectation with respect to
Y
, we have that
\mathbb{E}\!\left[G(Y)\right]<\infty
. Since
\delta<\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{E}[Y^{\beta+\varepsilon}]<\infty
, we have
\mathbb{E}[Y^{\beta+\delta}]<\infty
. Therefore, there exists an integrable random variable
G(Y)
such that
0\leq H_{z}(Y)\leq G(Y)
for all
z\geq z_{0}\vee z_{1}
. Then, the Dominated Convergence Theorem gives

\frac{f_{Z}(z)}{z^{-(\beta+1)}\,L_{X}(z)}=\mathbb{E}[H_{z}(Y)]\ \xrightarrow[z\to\infty]{}\ \mathbb{E}[\,Y^{\beta}].

Multiplying both sides by
z^{-(\beta+1)}L_{X}(z)
yields the asserted asymptotic (
5
).

∎

4
Examples

This chapter presents examples that illustrate the scope of the main theorem and clarify how the parameter
\beta
and the slowly varying function
L
arise in concrete settings. These cases demonstrate that the exponent
\beta
alone determines the phase transition, while
L
influences only the boundary behavior. The examples also show that a wide range of probabilistic models naturally fit into the framework developed in the previous sections.

4.1
Distributions satisfying the main condition

This section lists representative survival-parameter distributions whose densities satisfy condition (
2
).

The examples include elementary choices, such as power-law densities, as well as distributions obtained through transformations of Gamma variables. These explicit computations illustrate how diverse families of distributions fall within the scope of the general asymptotic condition used in Theorem 2.2.

Example 7
.

Let
\beta>0
and define, for
u\in(0,1)
,

f_{\pi}(u)=\frac{(1-u)^{\beta-1}}{\beta}.

Then,
f_{\pi}
satisfies condition (
2
), with the explicit choices

\beta\,\text{ as given},\qquad L((1-u)^{-1})\equiv\frac{1}{\beta}.

Example 8
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda_{1})
and
Y\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\beta,\lambda_{2})
be independent, with shapes
\alpha,\beta>0

and (rate) parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
. Define

\pi:=\frac{X}{X+Y}\in(0,1).

We shall prove that
\pi
has density, for
0<u<1
,

f_{\pi}(u)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}\lambda_{2}^{\beta}\,u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}}{\big(\lambda_{2}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})u\big)^{\alpha+\beta}}.

Therefore,

f_{\pi}(u)\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\,\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)\,\lambda_{1}^{\beta}}\,(1-u)^{\beta-1}\qquad(u\uparrow 1).

Thus,
f_{\pi}
fits condition (
2
), with the explicit choices

\beta\,\text{ as given},\qquad L((1-u)^{-1})\equiv\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\,\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)\,\lambda_{1}^{\beta}}.

Moreover, for
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, this reduces to the case where
\pi\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

Let

\pi=\frac{X}{X+Y}\in(0,1),\qquad V=X+Y\in(0,\infty).

By independence,

f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x}\,\frac{\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}y^{\beta-1}e^{-\lambda_{2}y}\,\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)^{2}}(x,y).

Consider the bijection
T:(0,\infty)^{2}\to(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
defined by

(u,v)=T(x,y):=\Big(\tfrac{x}{x+y},\,x+y\Big),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad(x,y)=(uv,\,(1-u)v).

The support maps as
(x,y)\in(0,\infty)^{2}\Longleftrightarrow(u,v)\in(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
.

The Jacobian determinant of the inverse map
(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)
equals

J(u,v)=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial x/\partial u&\partial x/\partial v\\[2.0pt]
\partial y/\partial u&\partial y/\partial v\end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix}v&u\\[2.0pt]
-\,v&1-u\end{pmatrix}=v.

For
u\in(0,1)
and
v>0
,

\displaystyle f_{\pi,V}(u,v)

\displaystyle=f_{X,Y}(uv,(1-u)v)\;|J(u,v)|

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}(uv)^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}uv}\,\frac{\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}\big((1-u)v\big)^{\beta-1}e^{-\lambda_{2}(1-u)v}\,|v|

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\;u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\;v^{\alpha+\beta-1}\;e^{-\{\lambda_{2}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})u\}\,v}.

Set

A(u):=\lambda_{2}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})u\quad\text{so that}\quad A(u)>0\ \text{for}\ u\in(0,1).

Integrating
f_{\pi,V}(u,v)
in
v
,

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{U,V}(u,v)\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{\alpha+\beta-1}e^{-A(u)v}\,dv.

Using the gamma integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{k-1}e^{-av}\,dv=\Gamma(k)a^{-k}
(valid for
a,k>0
) with
k=\alpha+\beta
and
a=A(u)
, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\;\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\,\big(A(u)\big)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\alpha}\lambda_{2}^{\beta}\,u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}}{\big(\lambda_{2}+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})u\big)^{\alpha+\beta}},\qquad 0<u<1,

which is the claimed density.

∎

Example 9
.

The generalized Beta distribution of the first kind has density

f_{\pi}(u;a,b,c)=\frac{c}{B(a,b)}\,u^{ac-1}\,(1-u^{c})^{b-1},\qquad 0<u<1,

with
a,b,c>0
. As

,
u^{ac-1}\to 1
and

, hence

f_{\pi}(u)\sim\frac{c^{\,b}}{B(a,b)}\,(1-u)^{b-1}.

Therefore,

satisfies condition (
2
), with

\beta=b,\qquad L((1-u)^{-1})\equiv\frac{c^{\,b}}{B(a,b)}.

Here we give an example where the conditions of Theorem

,

Lemma 10
.

(i)
Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\varphi(x)=(1+x)^{\gamma}
for
x>-1
.
Fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\gamma,\delta)>0
such that

\bigl|(1+x)^{\gamma}-\bigl(1+\gamma x\bigr)\bigr|\ \leq\ C\,x^{2}\qquad\text{for all }|x|\leq\delta.

Equivalently,

(1+x)^{\gamma}\ =\ 1+\gamma x+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})\qquad(x\to 0).

If
\theta=\theta(s)\to 0
as
s\to 0
, then

(1+\theta(s))^{\gamma}=1+\gamma\,\theta(s)+\mathcal{O}\!\big(\theta(s)^{2}\big).

In particular, if
\theta(s)=\mathcal{O}(s^{k})
for some
k>0
, then

(1+\theta(s))^{\gamma}=1+\gamma\,\theta(s)+\mathcal{O}(s^{2k}).

(ii) There exist
\delta\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that, for all
s
with
|s|\leq\delta
,

\Bigl|\,\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,s^{2}\,\Bigr|\ \leq\ C\,s^{4}.

Equivalently,

1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,s^{2}+\mathcal{O}(s^{4})\qquad(s\to 0).

(iii) Let
r(s):=\dfrac{1-s}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}
for
s\in(-1,1)
.
There exist
\delta\in(0,1)
and
K>0
such that, for all
|s|\leq\delta
,

\bigl|\log r(s)+s\bigr|\ \leq\ K\,|s|^{3}.

Equivalently,
\log r(s)=-s+\mathcal{O}(s^{3})
as
s\to 0
.

Proof.

Proof of (i): Let
x\in[-\delta,\delta]
we have
1+x\geq 1-\delta>0
, hence
\varphi
is
C^{2}
there with

\varphi^{\prime}(x)=\gamma(1+x)^{\gamma-1},\qquad\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\gamma(\gamma-1)(1+x)^{\gamma-2}.

By Taylor’s theorem with Lagrange remainder at
0
,

(1+x)^{\gamma}=\varphi(0)+\varphi^{\prime}(0)\,x+\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{x})}{2}\,x^{2}=1+\gamma x+\frac{\gamma(\gamma-1)}{2}\,(1+\xi_{x})^{\gamma-2}\,x^{2},

for some
\xi_{x}
between
0
and
x
. Since
(1+\xi)^{\gamma-2}
is continuous on
[-\delta,\delta]
,

M:=\sup_{|\xi|\leq\delta}\bigl|(1+\xi)^{\gamma-2}\bigr|<\infty,

and hence

\bigl|(1+x)^{\gamma}-(1+\gamma x)\bigr|\leq\frac{|\gamma(\gamma-1)|}{2}M\,x^{2}=:C(\gamma,\delta)\,x^{2}.

Proof of (ii): Consider
\varphi(x):=\sqrt{1-x}
on
x\in[0,1)
. Then

\varphi(0)=1,\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x)^{-1/2},\qquad\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{4}(1-x)^{-3/2}.

By Taylor’s theorem with Lagrange remainder at
0
, for any
x\in[0,\delta^{2}]
we have

\varphi(x)=\varphi(0)+\varphi^{\prime}(0)\,x+\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{x})}{2}\,x^{2}=1-\tfrac{1}{2}x+\frac{-\tfrac{1}{4}(1-\xi_{x})^{-3/2}}{2}\,x^{2}=1-\tfrac{1}{2}x-\tfrac{1}{8}(1-\xi_{x})^{-3/2}x^{2},

for some
\xi_{x}\in(0,x)
. Rearranging,

1-\sqrt{1-x}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,x+\tfrac{1}{8}(1-\xi_{x})^{-3/2}\,x^{2}.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
; then for all
x\in[0,\delta^{2}]
we have

(1-\xi_{x})^{-3/2}\leq(1-\delta^{2})^{-3/2}
. Hence,

\Bigl|\,\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-x}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}x\,\Bigr|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{8}(1-\delta^{2})^{-3/2}\,x^{2}\ =:C_{0}\,x^{2}.

Finally, set
x=s^{2}
to obtain, for
|s|\leq\delta
,

\Bigl|\,\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,s^{2}\,\Bigr|\ \leq\ C_{0}\,s^{4},

which proves the claim with
C=C_{0}
.

Proof of (iii): Write
\log r(s)=\phi(s)+\psi(s)
with

\phi(s):=\log(1-s),\qquad\psi(s):=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1-s^{2}).

On
[-\delta,\delta]\subset(-1,1)
,
\phi
is
C^{3}
with

\phi(0)=0,\quad\phi^{\prime}(s)=-(1-s)^{-1},\quad\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)=-(1-s)^{-2},\quad\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=-2(1-s)^{-3}.

Taylor’s theorem with Lagrange remainder at
0
yields

\phi(s)=\phi(0)+\phi^{\prime}(0)s+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)s^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{s})s^{3}=-s-\tfrac{1}{2}s^{2}+R_{\phi}(s),

for some
\xi_{s}
between
0
and
s
, with

|R_{\phi}(s)|=\frac{|\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi_{s})|}{6}\,|s|^{3}\leq\frac{2}{6}(1-\delta)^{-3}\,|s|^{3}=:C_{1}\,|s|^{3}.

Define
\chi(x):=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1-x)
on
[0,\delta^{2}]
. Then,
\chi
is
C^{3}
with

\chi(0)=0,\quad\chi^{\prime}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x)^{-1},\quad\chi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x)^{-2},\quad\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=(1-x)^{-3}.

Taylor at
0
gives

\chi(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x+\tfrac{1}{4}x^{2}+R_{\chi}(x),\qquad|R_{\chi}(x)|\leq\frac{\sup_{[0,\delta^{2}]}\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}}{6}\,x^{3}\leq\frac{(1-\delta^{2})^{-3}}{6}\,x^{3}.

Now take
x=s^{2}
. Then

\psi(s)=\chi(s^{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}s^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}s^{4}+R_{\chi}(s^{2}),\qquad|R_{\chi}(s^{2})|\leq C_{2}\,|s|^{6},\quad C_{2}:=\tfrac{1}{6}(1-\delta^{2})^{-3}.

Summing
\phi
and
\psi
,

\log r(s)=\bigl(-s-\tfrac{1}{2}s^{2}+R_{\phi}(s)\bigr)+\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}s^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}s^{4}+R_{\chi}(s^{2})\bigr)=-s+\bigl(R_{\phi}(s)+R_{\chi}(s^{2})+\tfrac{1}{4}s^{4}\bigr).

Hence

\bigl|\log r(s)+s\bigr|\ \leq\ |R_{\phi}(s)|+|R_{\chi}(s^{2})|+\tfrac{1}{4}|s|^{4}\ \leq\ C_{1}|s|^{3}+C_{2}|s|^{6}+\tfrac{1}{4}|s|^{4}\ \leq\ K\,|s|^{3},

for all
|s|\leq\delta
, with
K:=C_{1}+\tfrac{1}{4}\delta+C_{2}\delta^{3}
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
L
be slowly varying at
\infty
. Then, as
s\downarrow 0
,

\frac{L\!\Big(\dfrac{1}{\,1-\sqrt{\,1-s^{2}\,}}\Big)}{L\!\Big(\dfrac{1}{s^{2}}\Big)}\ \longrightarrow\ 1.

Proof.

For
s\in(0,1)
,

1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}=\frac{(1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}})(1+\sqrt{1-s^{2}})}{1+\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}=\frac{s^{2}}{\,1+\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\,}.

Hence

\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}{s^{2}}=\lambda_{s}\,x_{s},\qquad x_{s}:=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\xrightarrow[s\downarrow 0]{}\infty,\quad\lambda_{s}:=1+\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\in(1,2).

Since
L
is slowly varying at
\infty
, the Uniform Convergence Theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 1.2.1]
) yields that for every compact

K\subset(0,\infty)
,

\sup_{\lambda\in K}\Bigg|\frac{L(\lambda x)}{L(x)}-1\Bigg|\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}0.

Apply this with
K=[1,2]
,
x=x_{s}
and
\lambda=\lambda_{s}
to obtain:
for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
such that for all

s\in(0,\delta_{\varepsilon})
,

\left|\frac{L(\lambda_{s}x_{s})}{L(x_{s})}-1\right|=\left|\frac{L\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\,1-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\big)}{L\!\big(\tfrac{1}{s^{2}}\big)}-1\right|<\varepsilon.

This proves the claim. ∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
L
be slowly varying at
\infty
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
A>1
there exist

X=X(A,\varepsilon)\geq 1
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(A,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
t>0
with

n^{2}\geq X\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{n^{2}}{t^{2}}\geq X\ \ \big(\text{equivalently }0<t\leq n/\sqrt{X}\big),

A^{-1}\,\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\,L(n^{2})\;\leq\;L\!\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{t^{2}}\Big)\;\leq\;A\,\max\{t^{2\varepsilon},\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\}\,L(n^{2}).

(37)

In particular, if
t\in(0,1]
, one obtains the one–sided form

A^{-1}\,t^{2\varepsilon}\,L(n^{2})\;\leq\;L\!\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{t^{2}}\Big)\;\leq\;A\,t^{-2\varepsilon}\,L(n^{2})\qquad(n\ \text{sufficiently large}).

(38)

Moreover, for every compact interval
0<a\leq t\leq b<\infty
,

\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\Bigg|\frac{L\!\big(n^{2}/t^{2}\big)}{L(n^{2})}-1\Bigg|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(39)

that is, the convergence is uniform on compact sets of
t

By Potter’s bound for slowly varying functions (regularly varying of index

),
for every
\varepsilon>0
and every

there exists
X=X(A,\varepsilon)\geq 1
such that, for all

x,y\geq X
,

A^{-1}\,\min\!\Big\{\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)^{\varepsilon},\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)^{-\varepsilon}\Big\}\;\leq\;\frac{L(y)}{L(x)}\;\leq\;A\,\max\!\Big\{\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)^{\varepsilon},\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)^{-\varepsilon}\Big\}.

(40)

(See
[
4
, Thm. 1.5.6]
).

Apply (
40
) with
x=n^{2}
and

. The stated size
conditions ensure

37
).
If, in addition,

\min\{t^{2\varepsilon},t^{-2\varepsilon}\}=t^{2\varepsilon}

Lemma 14
.

Let

L(x):=(\log x)^{\sin(\log\log x)}=\exp\!\big((\log\log x)\,\sin(\log\log x)\big),\qquad x>e.

Then,
L
is a slowly varying function with

\liminf_{x\to\infty}L(x)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\limsup_{x\to\infty}L(x)=\infty.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda>0
and set
u(x):=\log\log x
and

\delta(x):=u(\lambda x)-u(x)=\log\!\left(\frac{\log(\lambda x)}{\log x}\right)=\log\!\left(1+\frac{\log\lambda}{\log x}\right).

Since
\frac{\log\lambda}{\log x}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
, there exists
x_{0}
such that for
x\geq x_{0}
,

|\delta(x)|\leq\frac{2|\log\lambda|}{\log x}\leq 1.

Write
\log L(x)=g(u(x))
with
g(u):=u\sin u
. By the mean value theorem,

\big|g(u(x)+\delta(x))-g(u(x))\big|=|g^{\prime}(\xi_{x})|\,|\delta(x)|,

for some
\xi_{x}
between
u(x)
and
u(x)+\delta(x)
, where

g^{\prime}(u)=\sin u+u\cos u,\qquad\text{so}\qquad|g^{\prime}(\xi_{x})|\leq 1+\xi_{x}\leq 2+u(x)\quad\text{for $x\geq x_{0}$}.

Hence, for
x\geq x_{0}
,

\left|\log\frac{L(\lambda x)}{L(x)}\right|=\big|g(u(\lambda x))-g(u(x))\big|\leq\big(2+\log\log x\big)\,\frac{2|\log\lambda|}{\log x}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}0.

Therefore,
\frac{L(\lambda x)}{L(x)}\to 1
, i.e.,
L
is slowly varying at infinity.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
set

t_{k}:=\frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi k,\quad s_{k}:=\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi k,\quad x_{k}:=\exp(\exp t_{k}),\quad y_{k}:=\exp(\exp s_{k}).

Then,
u(x_{k})=t_{k}
and
u(y_{k})=s_{k}
, hence

L(x_{k})=\exp\!\big(t_{k}\sin t_{k}\big)=\exp(t_{k})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\infty,\quad L(y_{k})=\exp\!\big(s_{k}\sin s_{k}\big)=\exp(-s_{k})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0,

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let

G_{n}\ :=\ n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)\,\log\!\Big(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)}\Big),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Assume there exist
0<A_{-}\leq A_{+}<\infty
such that

0<A_{-}\ \leq\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)}{L(n^{2})}\ \leq\ A_{+}\ <\infty,

(41)

where
L(x)
is as defined in Lemma
14
. Then:

\displaystyle\frac{A_{-}}{2}\ \leq\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}G_{n}.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
and set

\ell_{n}:=\log(n^{2})=2\log n,\qquad u_{n}:=\log\ell_{n}=\log(2\log n),\qquad t_{n}:=\sin u_{n}\in[-1,1].

Then,
L(n^{2})=e^{t_{n}u_{n}}
. Define

H_{n}\ :=\ -\log\!\Big(\frac{L(n^{2})}{n}\Big)\,L(n^{2})=e^{t_{n}u_{n}}\big(\log n-t_{n}u_{n}\big).

A direct identity gives

G_{n}=a_{n}\,H_{n}\ -\ a_{n}\,L(n^{2})\,\log a_{n},\qquad a_{n}:=\frac{n\,\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)}{L(n^{2})}.

(42)

From (
41
), for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{\varepsilon}
such that, for
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,

a_{n}\ \leq\ A_{+}+\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|\log a_{n}|\leq C_{\varepsilon},

(43)

for some finite
C_{\varepsilon}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

h_{n}(t):=e^{tu_{n}}\big(\log n-tu_{n}-\log a_{n}\big),\qquad t\in[-1,1].

Then

h^{\prime}_{n}(t)=u_{n}e^{tu_{n}}\,\big(\log n-tu_{n}-1-\log a_{n}\big).

Since
u_{n}=\log(2\log n)=o(\log n)
and
|\log a_{n}|\leq C_{\varepsilon}
, there is
N_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
such that, for
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
and
t\in[-1,1]
,

\log n-tu_{n}-1-\log a_{n}\ \geq\ \log n-u_{n}-1-C_{\varepsilon}\ >\ 0.

Thus,
t\mapsto h_{n}(t)
is strictly increasing on
[-1,1]
for all
n
large, and hence

h_{n}(t_{n})\ \geq\ h_{n}(-1)=e^{-u_{n}}\big(\log n+u_{n}-\log a_{n}\big).

(44)

Since
G_{n}=a_{n}h_{n}(t_{n})
by (
42
), (
44
) yields

G_{n}\ \geq\ a_{n}\,e^{-u_{n}}\big(\log n+u_{n}-\log a_{n}\big).

Using
e^{u_{n}}=2\log n
and
u_{n}/\log n\to 0
,

\frac{\log n+u_{n}}{e^{u_{n}}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{u_{n}}{2\log n}=\frac{1}{2}+o(1).

Moreover, from (
43
), we obtain

\frac{|a_{n}\,\log a_{n}|}{e^{u_{n}}}\ \leq\ \frac{(A_{+}+\varepsilon)\,C_{\varepsilon}}{2\log n}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Hence

\liminf_{n\to\infty}G_{n}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\ \geq\ \frac{A_{-}}{2}.
