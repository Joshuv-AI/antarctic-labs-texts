2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline and HEARTS 2.0: implications for the future evolution of integrated cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic care in the Americas
Authors: Donald J DiPette, Sean J Battle, Andres Rosende, Jeffrey Brettler, Marc G Jaffe, Paul K Whelton, Pedro Ordunez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease. In 2025, two major evidence-based frameworks evolved in parallel: HEARTS 2.0, an implementation-oriented clinical pathway for integrated cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) care through primary health care, and the updated American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Blood Pressure Guideline, one of the world's leading hypertension guidelines. Although developed for different intended applications, both drew on a common and rapidly expanding evidence base, making it timely to examine their areas of convergence and divergence. Using the four operational domains of the HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway, this article compares both approaches and examines their implications for the future evolution of integrated CKM care in the Americas. The analysis demonstrates substantial concordance across all domains, including accurate blood pressure measurement, risk-based care, simplified treatment protocols with early use of single-pill combinations, rapid treatment intensification, team-based care, and comprehensive CKM risk reduction. The few remaining differences should not be viewed as contradictions but as opportunities for future refinement. They primarily concern the diagnostic threshold for hypertension, the application of blood pressure targets below 130/80 mmHg, and emerging cardiometabolic therapies. These areas reflect ongoing debates and evolving evidence that may inform future updates of HEARTS 2.0, with adoption varying across countries according to context, feasibility, affordability, and implementation capacity. Overall, this comparison reinforces the scientific foundations of both frameworks and supports their interpretation as complementary and mutually reinforcing approaches for advancing the implementation of scalable, integrated CKM care in the Americas.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the Americas, with hypertension as its principal modifiable risk factor (1). To address this burden, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) launched HEARTS in the Americas, the largest regional adaptation of the World Health Organization (WHO) Global HEARTS Initiative (2). Focused on strengthening health system capacity in primary care settings, HEARTS provides country-specific guidance for the management of hypertension and overall CVD risk. Since 2018, the program has expanded to 33 countries and currently reaches approximately 50 million adults through primary health care systems (3).

HEARTS in the Americas emphasizes evidence-based, standardized care through the HEARTS Clinical Pathway (4). Its core pharmacological strategy is aligned with the 2021 WHO Guideline for the Pharmacological Treatment of Hypertension in Adults (5). Additionally, the program has been strongly influenced by the Kaiser Permanente model in California (6), which has shaped the design of HEARTS’ key drivers for hypertension control, including team-based care, systematic monitoring, and performance feedback (7). By providing a structured framework for implementation, HEARTS enables participating countries to strengthen clinical practices, improve patient outcomes, and maintain consistency across diverse health systems in the region (8).

Building on this foundation, HEARTS 2.0 expands beyond hypertension management toward integrated cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) care. Developed through a structured, evidence-based consensus process, HEARTS 2.0 does not create recommendations de novo. Rather than representing a new clinical guideline, HEARTS 2.0 compiles, harmonizes, and operationalizes recommendations from multiple high-quality guidelines and scientific statements into a simplified clinical pathway designed to facilitate implementation at scale (9–11).

The American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) developed and released an updated high blood pressure (BP) guideline in 2025 (12), while HEARTS 2.0 concurrently underwent its own evidence-based consensus update. Thus, both approaches evolved in parallel, drawing on a common and rapidly expanding evidence base, although with different objectives and intended applications. Unlike the AHA/ACC guideline, which is intended to inform clinical practice across the full spectrum of health care settings, HEARTS 2.0 was specifically designed to operationalize evidence-based recommendations within primary health care, where most hypertension and CKM care is delivered in the Americas. Accordingly, differences between both approaches frequently reflect differences in scope and intended use rather than disagreement in clinical recommendations (13).

Given the influence of AHA/ACC recommendations throughout Latin America and the Caribbean, and while there are far more similarities than differences, understanding where both approaches converge and where meaningful differences remain is timely and relevant. For clinicians, program implementers, researchers, scientific societies, and policymakers, areas of convergence reinforce the scientific foundations of HEARTS 2.0, whereas areas of divergence warrant closer examination as opportunities to anticipate future needs and guide implementation research, contextual adaptation, and innovation.

Accordingly, this article uses the four operational domains of the HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway—detection and diagnosis of hypertension, early CKM detection and risk assessment, treatment and complementary CKM therapy, and follow-up and preventive strategies—to examine where the AHA/ACC and HEARTS 2.0 reports converge, where they differ, and what those differences may imply for the future evolution of integrated CKM care in the Americas.

HEARTS 2.0: An Implementation-Oriented Clinical Pathway for Integrated CKM Care

The original HEARTS initiative focused primarily on hypertension control, whereas HEARTS 2.0 evolved to reflect the clinical reality that hypertension frequently coexists with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and other cardiovascular risk conditions. These conditions are linked through interconnected biological pathways that amplify cardiovascular risk and have been conceptualized as the CKM syndrome (14). HEARTS 2.0 preserves hypertension management as its clinical core while evolving the strategy toward integrated CKM care delivered through primary care.

The resulting HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway (13) is organized around four operational domains: (A) detection and diagnosis of hypertension; (B) early CKM detection and risk assessment; (C) treatment and complementary CKM therapy; and (D) follow-up and preventive strategies (Figure 1). These domains provide the framework for the present analysis. By following the structure of the pathway, areas of convergence with the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline can be highlighted, and areas of divergence and their implications can be examined in greater detail.

A. Detection and Diagnosis of Hypertension

Both the HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway (11) and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) strongly emphasize accurate BP measurement as the foundation for hypertension diagnosis and management. Both recommend standardized measurement techniques, exclusive use of automated clinically validated BP measurement devices, and expanded roles for nonclinicians in screening and cardiovascular risk assessment. In addition, HEARTS 2.0 explicitly incorporates targeted case-finding strategies to improve access among underserved populations.

The major difference between the two approaches lies in the threshold used to define hypertension. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline retains the classification introduced in 2017, defining Stage 1 hypertension as an average BP ≥130/80 mmHg and Stage 2 hypertension as ≥140/90 mmHg. In contrast, HEARTS 2.0 employs the WHO recommended threshold of ≥140/90 mmHg in most individuals, while recognizing a lower systolic BP of ≥130 mmHg in high-risk individuals such as those with existing CVD, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, or with an elevated cardiovascular risk by risk calculator.

This difference raises important questions: what is the added population benefit of lower thresholds, for whom, and under what implementation conditions? This issue is not unique to HEARTS 2.0 and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline but reflects a broader and ongoing debate across major hypertension guidelines regarding diagnostic thresholds, treatment targets, risk stratification, and implementation feasibility (15). Lower diagnostic thresholds may further reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, particularly among high-risk individuals, and may also provide benefits beyond CVD, including reduced risk of mild cognitive impairment and dementia (16). However, they would substantially increase the number of individuals eligible for diagnosis, treatment, and long-term follow-up and require careful consideration of the balance between benefits and potential treatment-related adverse effects. (17). At the same time, the caution regarding implementation should not become therapeutic inertia among individuals at clearly elevated cardiovascular risk, for whom earlier control and lower targets may provide substantial benefit (18).

Because each population care environment (country, region, health system, for instance) may differ in epidemiology, health system capacity, resources, and baseline hypertension control, moving toward lower thresholds should not be viewed as an all-or-none proposition. Some countries may be ready to adopt more intensive approaches, whereas others may benefit from phased or targeted implementation while continuing to expand hypertension control at scale.

B. Early CKM Detection and Risk Assessment

Both HEARTS 2.0 (11) and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) recognize that hypertension should not be managed in isolation. Both recommend early evaluation of concomitant CKM conditions, including diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and hypertension-mediated organ damage. Both also incorporate kidney assessment using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) and recommend electrocardiography to identify cardiac involvement.

A distinctive feature of HEARTS 2.0 is its emphasis on early CKM risk stratification (11). While the pathway incorporates the WHO cardiovascular risk charts to estimate absolute cardiovascular risk, including through the HEARTS in the Americas software application, it reinforces a practical implementation approach by automatically classifying individuals with established CVD, stroke, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, or high estimated cardiovascular risk as high risk. This classification directly guides BP targets and complementary CKM therapies while ensuring that laboratory testing and risk assessment do not delay timely initiation or intensification of antihypertensive treatment.

In addition, HEARTS 2.0 provides pragmatic laboratory thresholds and treatment targets for key CKM indicators—including glycated hemoglobin, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, eGFR, and uACR—to facilitate standardized implementation, monitoring, and treatment decisions in primary health care.

In contrast, the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) adopts the PREVENT risk equations, a contemporary risk prediction model that estimates 10- and 30-year risks of atherosclerotic CVD, heart failure, and total CVD using routinely available clinical variables. The guideline also recommends a broader baseline evaluation, including thyroid-stimulating hormone, serum electrolytes, calcium, and complete blood count, partly to support the detection of secondary hypertension. Together, these approaches raise important implementation questions: how comprehensive should CKM detection and risk assessment be in routine primary care, and which components should be universal, targeted, or phased? A pragmatic risk-based approach may facilitate implementation on a scale, whereas more extensive testing may provide additional diagnostic value in selected settings.

HEARTS 2.0 prioritizes a pragmatic strategy that facilitates rapid treatment decisions and large-scale implementation, whereas the AHA/ACC approach provides a more comprehensive framework for individualized risk assessment. Rather than competing models, these approaches may be viewed as complementary strategies that can be adapted to context.

C. Treatment and Complementary CKM Therapy

Pharmacologic Treatment and Complementary Therapy for High-Risk Individuals

Both HEARTS 2.0 (11) and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) emphasize prompt initiation of combined treatment, simplified treatment algorithms, rapid treatment intensification, and the use of single-pill combinations whenever possible. Both approaches also support treatment simplification through triple-combination therapy when BP remains uncontrolled (19).

A distinctive feature of HEARTS 2.0 is that BP treatment and complementary CKM therapies are integrated within the same pathway. Individuals identified as high risk receive lower BP targets (<130/80 mmHg) and complementary therapies according to their CKM profile, including statins, SGLT2 inhibitors, and aspirin for secondary prevention. This approach reflects a shift from isolated hypertension management to integrated risk reduction.

Differences between the two approaches are more quantitative than conceptual. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline recommends broader use of intensive BP targets and provides a more expansive discussion of therapies for obesity, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and emerging cardiometabolic therapies, including GLP-1 receptor agonists. It also provides more detailed guidance for clinical scenarios such as persistent albuminuria, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and subclinical hypertension-mediated organ damage. This greater level of detail primarily reflects the broader scope of the AHA/ACC guideline across multiple levels of care, including specialized settings, rather than deficiencies of the implementation-oriented HEARTS 2.0 pathway. These distinctions raise questions regarding the magnitude of benefit achievable with increasingly intensive and complex pharmacological strategies, particularly among high-risk individuals, and how these interventions can be incorporated into scalable models of care without introducing complexity that reduces implementation efficiencies.

Future updates of HEARTS 2.0 may consider the incorporation of obesity therapies, such as GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonists, as evidence and affordability and implementation feasibility evolve. HEARTS 2.0 already incorporates treatment escalation to triple single-pill combinations; therefore, future efforts should focus on expanding their availability and effective implementation across health systems. Importantly, the pace and extent of adoption of these therapies and treatment strategies will likely differ across settings according to disease burden, implementation capacity, and health system priorities.

Nonpharmacologic Treatment

Both HEARTS 2.0 (11) and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) strongly emphasize lifestyle interventions as essential components of CKM prevention. Recommendations include healthy dietary patterns, sodium reduction and increased potassium intake, weight control, regular physical activity, avoidance of harmful alcohol use, and smoking cessation. HEARTS 2.0 deliberately aligns these recommendations with existing WHO and PAHO evidence-based guidance on a healthy diet, physical activity, tobacco control, healthy body weight, and sodium reduction, rather than reproducing detailed lifestyle guidance within the clinical pathway itself.

Rather than being adjunctive measures, these interventions constitute the foundation of CKM prevention and treatment. Their implementation, however, remains challenging and often requires coordinated policies and multisectoral actions beyond the capacity of a health system. Continued efforts to improve adherence to healthy lifestyles, increase access to low-sodium/potassium-enriched salt substitutes, strengthen tobacco cessation services, and promote healthier food and physical environments represent important opportunities to further enhance the impact of HEARTS 2.0.

D. Follow-up and Preventive Strategies

Both HEARTS 2.0 (11) and the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline (12) recognize that achieving BP control is only the beginning of long-term CKM management. Both emphasize treatment adherence, continuity of care, team-based management, and periodic reassessment of cardiovascular risk factors.

A distinctive operational feature of HEARTS 2.0 is its recommendation for treatment intensification every two weeks until BP targets are achieved, thereby minimizing therapeutic inertia and cumulative exposure to uncontrolled hypertension. Once BP control has been achieved, the pathway recommends structured long-term follow-up every three months for higher-risk individuals and every six months for those at lower risk.

Beyond BP monitoring, the pathway incorporates telemedicine, longer medication refills, periodic monitoring of glucose metabolism, lipids, and kidney function, vaccination, and electrocardiographic screening for left ventricular hypertrophy and atrial fibrillation in high-risk individuals. It also prioritizes continuity of care for vulnerable populations and reinforces adherence to disease-specific management plans for diabetes, chronic kidney disease, CVD, and other chronic conditions.

Both approaches support the central role of nonphysician health care workers and recognize the growing use of digital technologies. However, HEARTS 2.0 explicitly encourages medication intensification by nonphysician health care workers and establishes optimal targets across the CKM continuum—including BP, LDL cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin, body weight, and albuminuria—thereby shifting the focus from isolated, physician-centered BP control to sustained risk reduction driven by team-based care.

A further difference in scope is that HEARTS 2.0 was developed for the adult population, whereas the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline also includes recommendations for children and adolescents. Future adaptations of the HEARTS approach could examine how its implementation principles might support CKM prevention earlier in the life course while recognizing that pediatric care would require population-specific diagnostic criteria, treatment recommendations, and delivery models.

Overall, while substantial concordance exists between the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline and HEARTS 2.0, some differences persist as opportunities to inform future refinements. The 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN Guideline (20) dedicated specifically to managing the CKM syndrome also merits examination, particularly regarding CKM staging, risk prediction, obesity management, kidney protection, cardiometabolic therapies, and coordinated care, and how these elements may inform future iterations of HEARTS 2.0.

Conclusion

Publication of the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline provides a timely opportunity to reflect on the evolution of integrated CKM care in the Americas. Despite their different objectives and intended applications, AHA/ACC report and HEARTS 2.0 evolved largely in parallel and demonstrated substantial concordance across the four domains of the HEARTS 2.0 Clinical Pathway, reinforcing the scientific foundations of both approaches.

Differences between the two reports are few and should not be viewed as contradictions but rather as opportunities to guide future refinement. Three areas appear particularly important for evaluation and potential incorporation into HEARTS 2.0: the population benefits of lowering the diagnostic threshold for hypertension to 130/80 mmHg; whether BP targets below 130/80 mmHg should be pursued universally or preferentially among individuals at higher cardiovascular risk; and the role of GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonists within integrated CKM pathways. Because the 2025 AHA/ACC BP Guideline and recent WHO recommendations on the use of GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonists were published after completion of the HEARTS 2.0 consensus process, these issues should be viewed not as omissions but as new evidence and guidance requiring consideration in future updates. Their incorporation is unlikely to follow identical pathways across all countries and health systems, underscoring the importance of contextual adaptation, implementation research, and continued learning.

Beyond specific recommendations, HEARTS 2.0 advances a broader operational proposition: that primary health care can serve as the preferred platform for delivering integrated CKM care, provided it is equipped with practical clinical pathways, essential medicines, diagnostics, team-based care, information systems, and structured follow-up. In this sense, HEARTS 2.0 expands the role of primary health care beyond risk-factor identification to encompass effective treatment, continuity of care, and measurable improvements in population health.
