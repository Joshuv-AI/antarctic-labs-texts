Prognostic value of peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity in hospitalized patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction: an multi-institutional study
Authors: Chieh-Yu Chang, Chun-Chi Chen, Ming-Lung Tsai, Ming-Jer Hsieh, Cheng-Hung Lee, Tien-Hsing Chen, Shao-Wei Chen, Shang-Hung Chang, Pao-Hsien Chu, I-Chang Hsieh, Ming-Shien Wen, Dong-Yi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) represents a significant clinical challenge with limited prognostic markers. The prognostic value of Doppler-derived peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRVmax) in HFpEF patients remains underexplored.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analysed 6,438 patients with HFpEF hospitalized for acute heart failure between 2007 and 2018. Patients were stratified into three groups based on baseline TRVmax values: ≤2.8 m/s, 2.9–3.4 m/s and >3.4 m/s. The primary outcome was a composite of heart failure rehospitalization and cardiovascular mortality. Association between TRVmax levels and the risks of outcome was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards model with adjustment for established risk factors.ResultsDuring the median follow-up of 2 years (interquartile range: 0.8–4 years), increasing incidence rates of the composite outcome were observed across the three groups (17.8, 21.2 and 23.9 per 100 person-years, respectively). Compared to the TRVmax ≤2.8 m/s group, significantly higher risks were observed in the TRVmax 2.9–3.4 m/s group (adjusted HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.003–1.22) and TRVmax >3.4 m/s group (adjusted HR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.09–1.39). Each 0.5 m/s increase in TRVmax was associated with a 7% increase in composite outcome risk (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10).ConclusionsTRVmax exceeding 2.8 m/s has prognostic value for heart failure rehospitalization and cardiovascular mortality in HFpEF patients, suggesting its potential utility as a risk stratification tool in this population.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex and life-threatening condition [1]. Unlike HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is characterized by impaired diastolic function, resulting in elevated filling pressure and pulmonary congestion [2]. Compared with HFrEF, HFpEF is typically associated with higher rates of HF-related rehospitalization and poorer health outcomes, with a 5-year survival rate below 50% [3,4]. Pulmonary hypertension (PH) develops in approximately 80% of patients with HFpEF and is associated with more severe symptoms and an increased risk of mortality [5,6]. Measuring pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) in patients with HFpEF facilitates the early detection of PH and deteriorating HF, thereby providing valuable insights for timely interventions and optimal titration in guideline-directed medical therapy [5–7].

Currently, Doppler echocardiography is recommended by many guidelines, specifically for the measurement of peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRVmax), as the primary diagnostic tool for determining the likelihood of PH in suspected cases [8,9]. Research indicated that an elevated tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient (TRPG) is an independent predictor of mortality in patients hospitalized for HFpEF [10]. Nevertheless, the prognostic value of the TRPG in identifying the risk of HF rehospitalization and cardiovascular (CV) mortality in this population remains unclear. Given the strong observed between TRVmax and the TRPG, further research is required to address the knowledge gap regarding whether a specific TRVmax threshold correlates with both CV mortality and HF hospitalization in patients with HFpEF. To address this gap, we explored the association between admission TRVmax values and subsequent clinical outcomes, including CV mortality and HF hospitalization, in patients with HFpEF.

Methods

Data source

To comprehensively explore this clinically relevant topic, we extracted data on Taiwanese patients from the Chang Gung Research Database (CGRD), an integrated electronic medical repository comprising extensive information from all emergency, inpatient, and outpatient visits across the network [11,12]. The Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMH) system comprises four tertiary academic medical centres and three teaching hospitals distributed across seven branches throughout Taiwan [13]. The CGRD offers extensive demographic characteristics, electronic medical records, prescription details, echocardiography reports, laboratory results, discharge summaries and nursing records. In this study, diseases were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification for entries before 1 October 2015, after which International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes were implemented (Supplemental Table 1). All the methods and procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations [14]. The coverage period for data from the CGRD was set between 2001 and 2019. All procedures involving human participants in this study were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards [15]. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Ethics Review Board of CGMH (approval no. 202300950B0) approved this study and waived the need of obtaining informed consent.

Study population

In this retrospective cohort study, data on patients who were admitted to a CGMH facility for acute HF between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2018, were extracted from the CGRD. Acute HF events were defined as those meeting the B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) criteria and requiring the use of diuretics during hospitalization. For patients with multiple admissions for HF during this period, only the first admission was regarded as the index HF hospitalization. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded from the study: missing key demographic information (i.e. age and sex), being under 20 years of age, missing left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) data during hospitalization, having a baseline LVEF of less than 50% and having a history of HF with mid-range ejection fraction (HFmrEF) or HFrEF. Patients with an HFpEF mimic such as amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, Fabry disease, hemochromatosis, severe mitral valve regurgitation due to mitral valve prolapse or chordal rupture, vegetation or rheumatic heart disease were also excluded [16]. Patients were excluded regardless of whether the diagnosis was made before admission or during the follow-up period. In addition, patients with in-hospital mortality were excluded from the analysis (Figure 1). The final study cohort comprised patients meeting the following HFpEF criteria: having a baseline LVEF of 50% or greater, receiving a diuretic during hospitalization and having a BNP level of >35 pg/mL (for patients with sinus rhythm) or 105 pg/mL (for patients with atrial fibrillation). The index date was defined as the date of discharge following the index HF hospitalization. The patients with HFpEF were divided into three groups depending on their baseline TRVmax value: TRVmax ≤ 2.8 m/s, TRVmax = 2.9–3.4 m/s, and TRVmax > 3.4 m/s based on recent PH guidelines [8,17].

Measurement of covariates

We examined various factors: demographic characteristics (i.e. age, sex, smoking history, body mass index and body surface area), physiological parameters (heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure) and 12 comorbidities (i.e. hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, peripheral arterial disease, liver cirrhosis, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and malignancy). We also explored factors such as coronary intervention history, baseline echocardiographic data (i.e. left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic volume, left ventricular end-systolic volume, left atrium diameter, interventricular septum thickness, left ventricular posterior wall thickness, left ventricular mass, left ventricular mass index, E/A ratio, peak E-wave velocity, peak A-wave velocity, relative wall thickness, tricuspid regurgitation peak gradient and TR Vmax), laboratory findings (e.g. BNP, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, sodium, potassium, haemoglobin, haematocrit, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, high-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), uric acid, neutrophil, platelet, lymphocyte, white blood cell, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet lymphocyte ratio), implantable cardioverter defibrillator use and concurrent medications. Smoking history data were extracted from the secondary nursing database of the CGRD. Data on body mass index, physiological parameters, laboratory results, echocardiographic findings and medications prescribed within the 3 months before the index date were obtained from the medical records of the CGRD. Comorbidities were identified with a minimum of two outpatient diagnoses or a single inpatient diagnosis before the index admission. To evaluate the severity of HF, the frequency of HF admission was quantified in the 3 years before the index admission [13].

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome of interest was a composite of HF hospitalization and CV mortality. An HF event was defined as hospitalization with a discharge diagnosis of HF, as validated in a previous claim-based study [18]. To enhance specificity, unscheduled HF hospitalizations were restricted to cases requiring at least a single treatment involving a diuretic, nitrate or inotropic agent [13]. Mortality data were systematically acquired from the Death Registry database, which is linked to the CGRD. Cases of CV mortality included those attributable to acute myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death, HF, stroke, CV procedure, CV haemorrhage and other related causes [19]. Baseline comorbidities and composite outcomes were identified using International Classification of Diseases codes (Supplemental Table 1). Patient follow-up began from the index date and extended to a maximum of 5 years. Follow-up continued until the occurrence of an HF hospitalization event or CV mortality within this time frame, up to the fifth year for those who remained event-free and at risk, or the last visit in our hospitals, or until the end of the database as of 31 December 2019, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients in the three study groups (TRVmax of ≤2.8, 2.9–3.4 and >3.4 m/s) were compared using a one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables, a chi-squared test for categorical variables, and a Kruskal-Wallis test for BNP because of its non-normality. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni’s correction. Risks of time-to-event outcomes, with the group with TRVmax ≤ 2.8 m/s as the reference group, were compared using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. Linear trends were analysed by including the three study groups as the continuous variables in a Cox model. Covariates were identified from previous studies [20–22]. These covariates included age, body mass index, heart rate, diastolic blood pressure, number of HF hospitalizations in the preceding 3 years, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, LVEF, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left atrium diameter, interventricular septum thickness, left ventricular posterior wall thickness, severity of mitral regurgitation, BNP, blood urea nitrogen and estimated glomerular filtration rate. They also included sodium, potassium, triglyceride, glycated haemoglobin and uric acid levels; neutrophil count and white blood cell count.

In an alternative Cox model, TRVmax was treated as a continuous variable and expressed in increments of 0.5 m/s. The covariates described above were incorporated simultaneously into a fully adjusted model; no sequential or partially adjusted models were constructed.

To examine the relationship (linear or nonlinear) between TRVmax and the risks of the primary composite outcome and HF admission, TRVmax was modelled as a restricted cubic spline (RCS) variable. Knots were placed at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the TRVmax distribution. To address missing values, all analyses, except for the subsequent subgroup analysis, were conducted using a complete imputed data set based on a single expectation-maximization algorithm. Patients with atrial fibrillation lack measurable peak E-wave, peak A-wave and E/A ratio; therefore, these three variables were excluded from imputation, and all remaining Table 1 and Table 2 parameters were used, with 4,639 of 6,438 patients (72.1%) having at least one missing value. Analyses of the association between TRVmax (with increments of 0.5 m/s) and the risk of the primary composite outcome were stratified by several baseline characteristics depending on their clinical relevance in the original cohort before missing imputation. The same covariates adjusted for in the primary analysis were accounted for in the RCS and subgroup analyses. One-way analysis of variance with linear contrasts was used to test the trend of haemodynamic parameters obtained from right heart catheterization across the study groups. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The RCS analysis was conducted using the ‘rms’ package of R software version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All other statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 6,438 patients with HFpEF were included in the study. Among these patients, the mean age at baseline was 75.3 ± 12.7 years, with 57.7% being women. In addition, the mean LVEF was 65.6% ± 9.2%, and the median BNP level was 637.8 pg/mL. The patients were divided into three groups depending on their TRVmax value: 3,674 patients (57.1%) with TRVmax ≤ 2.8 m/s, 1,780 patients (27.6%) with TRVmax = 2.9–3.4 m/s and 984 patients (15.3%) with TRVmax > 3.4 m/s. These three groups had mean TRVmax values of 2.2 ± 0.5, 3.1 ± 0.2 and 3.8 ± 0.4 m/s, respectively. Table 1 and Table 2 present the baseline characteristics and echocardiographic parameters of the patients. The prevalence of atrial fibrillation and moderate or severe mitral regurgitation was higher among those with TRVmax > 3.4 m/s than among the other patients. Those with TRVmax > 3.4 m/s also had a higher BNP level, greater LVEF, a greater left atrium diameter, a higher peak E-wave velocity and a higher E/A ratio.

TRVmax and the clinical outcomes

With a mean follow-up duration of 2.7 years, the primary composite outcome (HF rehospitalization and CV mortality) incidence was 14.3, 17.5 and 18.8 per 100 person-years for TRVmax ≤2.8 m/s, 2.9–3.4 m/s and >3.4 m/s groups, respectively. Risk was significantly higher with TRVmax 2.9–3.4 m/s (aHR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.003–1.22) and TRVmax >3.4 m/s (aHR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.09–1.39) versus TRVmax ≤2.8 m/s (Table 3, Figure 2A). HF rehospitalization incidence was 7.5, 9.7 and 10.2 per 100 person-years across respective groups, with significantly higher risk in TRVmax 2.9–3.4 m/s (aHR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.04–1.37) and TRVmax >3.4 m/s (aHR = 1.26, 95% CI = 1.07–1.48) (Table 3, Figure 2B). CV death incidence was 7.7, 9.4 and 9.9 per 100 person-years, with significantly higher risk only in the TRVmax >3.4 m/s group (aHR = 1.22, 95% CI = 1.05–1.43) compared to TRVmax ≤2.8 m/s (Table 3, Figure 2C).

Incremental risk analysis of TRVmax

An increase in TRVmax was associated with increased risks of HF rehospitalization (adjusted P trend = 0.002), CV mortality (adjusted P trend = 0.01), all-cause death (adjusted P trend = 0.002) and the primary composite outcome (adjusted P trend < 0.001). Each 0.5 m/s increase in TRVmax was associated with a 9% increase in the risk of HF rehospitalization (HR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.04–1.13), a 7% increase in the risk of CV mortality (HR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.03–1.11), a 3% increase in the risk of all-cause death (HR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01–1.06) and a 7% increase in the risk of the primary composite outcome (HR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.04–1.10; Table 3). For baseline TRVmax values exceeding 2.8 m/s, the risks of HF rehospitalization and the primary composite outcome were increased (Figure 3A and 3B). The RCS analysis revealed that the relationship between TRVmax and the risks of the outcomes was linear (P for nonlinearity = 0.156 for the primary composite outcome and 0.576 for HF rehospitalization). Association between TRVmax and PAP

We measured the PAP of 297 patients in the original cohort who underwent right heart catheterization. Subsequently, we stratified the haemodynamic parameters of right heart catheterization depending on the TRVmax value of each group (Supplemental Table 2). A significant positive correlation was discovered between TRVmax and mean PAP (P for trend < 0.001). Specifically, a higher TRVmax value was associated with higher mean PAP.

Discussions

Novel findings and clinical implications

This cohort study investigated how TRVmax relates to HF hospitalization and CV mortality in HFpEF patients following acute HF hospitalization, finding that TRVmax values >2.8 m/s at admission were associated with significantly higher risks of these outcomes. Each 0.5 m/s increase in TRVmax corresponded to a 7% higher risk of HF rehospitalization and CV mortality combined, with this risk increase remaining consistent across subgroups. To the best of our knowledge, compared with previous studies [10,23], our study included the largest cohort and had a similar follow-up duration but incorporated more modern considerations by evaluating clinical outcomes – including all-cause mortality, CV mortality and HF hospitalization – that have received increasing attention in recent research and guidelines. TRVmax values >3.4 m/s were linked to higher all-cause mortality compared to values ≤2.8 m/s (26.1% vs. 21.8% person-years, aHR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.06–1.28), with this 3.4 m/s threshold representing a TRPG of 46 mmHg and indicating high PH probability. This elevated risk remained significant after adjusting for multiple factors. Current guidelines indicate that TRVmax >2.8 m/s (TRPG of 31 mmHg) suggests intermediate PH probability and recommend further evaluation for unexplained dyspnoea [24]. These findings underscore the importance of enhanced surveillance and intervention in HFpEF patients with TRVmax >2.8 m/s, given its association with increased CV mortality and HF hospitalization risks. From a clinical management perspective, a TRVmax >2.8 m/s in HFpEF identifies a high-risk phenotype indicative of probable group 2 PH. This finding mandates a strategy focused on aggressive volume optimization using diuretics and SGLT2 inhibitors, while strictly avoiding PAH-specific vasodilators, which may be detrimental in isolated post-capillary disease. Should elevated velocities persist despite euvolemia, or if accompanied by echocardiographic signs of right ventricular uncoupling (such as a TAPSE/sPAP ratio <0.55), this threshold supports the indication for right heart catheterization to distinguish isolated post-capillary from combined pre- and post-capillary PH, as suggested by the recent guidelines.

In patients with HFpEF, chronic congestion is associated with pulmonary venous, capillary and arterial remodelling, precipitated by the backward transmission of elevated left heart filling pressure. This pathophysiological cascade results in a chronically high afterload on the right heart, manifesting as high PAP [25]. According to studies, pulmonary arterial systolic pressure is a strong predictor of mortality in patients with chronic HFpEF. PAP-guided management of HF, irrespective of ejection fraction, is associated with a reduction in the frequency of HF hospitalization [5–7]. While right heart catheterization is the gold standard for PAP measurement, its invasive nature poses risks. Technological improvements in echocardiographic PAP quantification now offer a safe, accessible alternative [23]. Traditional echocardiographic PAP measurement combines TRVmax-derived right ventricular pressure with estimated RAP. However, this method lacks precision due to uncertainties in RAP estimation based on inferior vena cava characteristics, particularly in acute HF patients [26,27]. This limitation sheds light on the importance of evaluating the clinical effect of the TRPG independently of RAP assessment. Our data showed that, in acute HFpEF patients, without RAP estimation, each 0.5 m/s increase in TRVmax corresponded to higher risks, with 3% for all-cause mortality, 9% for HF hospitalization and 7% increases in both CV mortality and the combined outcome. While previous studies like the Japanese registry showed TRPG’s association with mortality (2–4% higher risk per 1 mmHg increase) [10], our study newly demonstrated TRVmax’s significant relationship with HF hospitalization risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its large representative sample derived from multi-institutional medical data and comprehensive longitudinal follow-ups. In this study, we identified TRVmax as a valuable marker that can guide clinicians in risk stratification and optimal therapy selection for patients admitted with HFpEF. We discovered that once the value of TRVmax exceeded the threshold of 2.8 m/s, the risk of long-term CV mortality and HF hospitalization increased significantly. Other research indicated that patients with acute decompensated HF whose TRPG is reduced 6 months after their discharge are at lower risks of all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization compared with those whose is not, regardless of their initial TRPG level [28]. Therefore, monitoring dynamic changes in TRVmax after pharmacological interventions may help clinicians evaluate the efficacy of treatment because a change in TRVmax may reflect changes in clinical outcomes. This approach can provide valuable insights into disease progression and the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions in patients with HFpEF.

This study has several limitations. First, TRVmax is highly sensitive to cardiac output and volume. Among the limitations of continuous-wave Doppler echocardiography for the tricuspid regurgitation jet area is the potential for pressure overestimation in high-output conditions such as anaemia or thyrotoxicosis as well as in cases of increased pulmonary venous pressure due to mitral valvulopathy [29]. Consequently, right heart catheterization remains the gold standard for evaluating and managing PH in patients with HFpEF. Nevertheless, our analysis of a subcohort of 297 patients who underwent right heart catheterization revealed a significant positive correlation between TRVmax and mean PAP (P for trend < 0.001). Specifically, we found that a higher TRVmax was associated with higher mean PAP. This finding indicates that, despite its limitations, TRVmax may serve as a valuable noninvasive indicator of PAP in patients with HFpEF. Second, we adopted clinical echocardiogram reports from the CGRD, which lacked certain echocardiographic parameters such as tissue Doppler e’ velocity and left atrial volume because these parameters are not routinely measured at CGMH. This lack of parameters may have affected the assessment of diastolic function and left atrial remodelling in our study population. Third, the inter-reader reproducibility of TRVmax measurements was not formally assessed in this study.

Conclusions

To summarize, TRVmax has significant prognostic value in predicting CV outcomes in patients with HFpEF. Our findings indicate that TRVmax exceeding 2.8 m/s is significantly associated with an increased risk of CV mortality and HF hospitalization. These insights confirm the role of TRVmax as a key echocardiographic parameter in informing clinical decision-making and optimizing the management and prognosis of patients with HFpEF. Utilizing TRVmax as a noninvasive marker may facilitate risk stratification and guide therapeutic interventions in this patient population, potentially improving their long-term outcomes.
