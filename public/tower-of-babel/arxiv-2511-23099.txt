On Computational Aspects of Cores of Ordered Graphs
Michal Čertík, Andreas Emil Feldmann, Jaroslav Nešetřil, Paweł Rzążewski

Abstract
An ordered graph is a graph enhanced with a linear order on the vertex set. An ordered graph is a core if it does not have an order-preserving homomorphism to a proper subgraph. We say that H H is the core of G G if (i) H H is a core, (ii) H H is a subgraph of G G , and (iii) G G admits an order-preserving homomorphism to H H . We study complexity aspects of several problems related to the cores of ordered graphs. Interestingly, they exhibit a different behavior than their unordered counterparts. We show that the retraction problem, i.e., deciding whether a given graph admits an ordered-preserving homomorphism to its specific subgraph, can be solved in polynomial time. On the other hand, it is NP -hard to decide whether a given ordered graph is a core. In fact, we show that it is even NP -hard to distinguish graphs G G whose core is largest possible (i.e., if G G is a core) from those, whose core is the smallest possible, i.e., its size is equal to the ordered chromatic number of G G . The problem is even W[1] -hard with respect to the latter parameter.

3
Ordered Core

We start with the following statement, analogous to unordered digraphs (see
[
26
]
), providing an equivalent definition for ordered core and its uniqueness per each ordered graph.

Theorem 3.1

Let
G
be an ordered graph. Then an ordered graph
G
is a core if and only if there is no ordered homomorphism from
G
to a proper ordered subgraph of
G
. Every ordered graph is homomorphically equivalent to a unique ordered core.

Proof.

To prove the left direction, if an ordered graph
G
retracts to a proper ordered subgraph
H
then there is an ordered homomorphism
f:G\to H
. Conversely, if there is an ordered homomorphism
f:G\to H
, where
H
is a proper ordered subgraph of
G
, then let
H
be a proper ordered subgraph of
G
with fewest vertices to which
G
is homomorphic. Then there cannot be an ordered homomorphism of
H
to its proper subgraph, and therefore any of
H
to
H
must be an automorphism of
H
. Let now
f:G\to H
be an ordered homomorphism. Thus, the restriction
a
of
f
to
H
is an automorphism of
H
and therefore has an inverse automorphism
a^{-1}
. Then
a^{-1}\circ f
is an ordered retraction of
G
to
H
.

To prove the second part of the statement, we notice that if ordered subgraphs
H
and
H^{{}^{\prime}}
are retracts of
G
then there exist ordered homomorphisms
f:H\to H^{{}^{\prime}}
and
g:H^{{}^{\prime}}\to H
(from transitivity). If both
H
and
H^{{}^{\prime}}
are also cores, then
f\circ g
and
g\circ f
must be automorphisms (as shown above); hence
H
and
H^{{}^{\prime}}
must be isomorphic.
∎

In this article, we will therefore consider a computational problem that we will call
CORE
<
.

Problem 1
.

CORE
<

Input:

Ordered graph
G
.

Question:

Is there a non-surjective homomorphism

?

Note that, as in
[

, the cores are precisely the ordered graphs for which the answer is NO. This formulation has the advantage of giving a problem that belongs to the class

, since it is easy to verify in polynomial time that a proposed mapping is indeed a non-surjective ordered homomorphism (and it is a decision problem).

Let us start this section by a (standard) definition of

Formally, a hypergraph is an ordered pair

Then an

Let us now define an

We show

We note that this means that we cannot simplify the problem by breaking it apart into more smaller problems (or formulas) in this way. This promise variant of ONE-IN-THREE SAT is, of course, still

[

We define an ordered

We then note that dynamic triples are the only triples that can be mapped to a subgraph of

We then notice that since the ONE-IN-THREE SAT formula is connected, our hypergraph

For contradiction, suppose this is not the case. Then, there is at least one dynamic triple that does not map to one of the static triples. But then either this dynamic triple is not connected to other dynamic triples, in which case

Also, one easily notes that shared vertices of different dynamic triples must map to the same vertex. Therefore, the same variable in different formulas will map to the same value.

Equivalence of instances.

Suppose that we have a YES-instance of our ONE-IN-THREE SAT formula. Then for each dynamic triple in its corresponding clause in

We then note that by adding

CORE

Next, we will connect the second vertex of each variable gadget with second and third vertices in other variable gadgets within the same clause gadget, and the third vertex of each variable gadget with second and third vertices in other variable gadgets within the same clause gadget. Let us now define a set of ordered graphs

. We first define the functions

\displaystyle g(n)=n-\frac{n-1}{9},

\displaystyle h(n,m)=m-6\times\frac{n-1}{9}.

We see that with

Equivalence of Instances.

We now claim that there exists a solution to ONE-IN-THREE SAT with each variable in at least three clauses if and only if there is an ordered homomorphism

from

to one of the ordered subgraphs in
\mathcal{H}
. But before we get to the proof of this equivalence, we notice a couple of observations about the nature of

and the ordered graphs in
\mathcal{H}
.

The first observation is that each variable gadget in
G
can be mapped to three possible ordered graphs. One is identity, the second is

, and the third is

. We notice that only the first two will be an ordered homomorphism image that is a subgraph of

(since

would add an edge from the first to the third vertex).

Therefore, the only way to map

to its proper ordered subgraph with the restrictions of our problem is to map some of its variable gadgets to
P_{2}
. By mapping a variable gadget to
P_{2}
we decrease the number of vertices by one and also the number of variable edges by one. As a result, since we are decreasing the number of vertices by
c
(from the definition of

), we will also decrease the number of variable edges by exactly

.

Notice that the ordered graphs in
\mathcal{H}
are not on the
\chi(G)
vertices, because the
\chi(G)=c(3\times 3)+1-3c=5c-1
, since each variable gadget on four vertices can be mapped to an ordered graph on three vertices, and there are

. In our problem, we are mapping only the

variable gadgets to ordered graphs
P_{2}
, instead of the

variable gadgets.

Now we need to ensure that if we map a variable gadget in one clause to

, we will map that variable gadget to

also in all other clauses, where this variable appears. But this is ensured by the external edges connecting the same variables gadgets in different clauses.

Assume otherwise, or that a variable gadget in
G
is mapped to
P_{2}
in a clause gadget and the same variable gadget in the other clause gadget (connected by two external edges) is mapped by identity. But then the variable gadget mapped to
P_{2}
will have an additional edge coming from the second vertex, and the ordered homomorphic image will not be a subgraph of
G
. Now, if both variables are mapped by identity, the resulting graph is a subgraph, and if they are both mapped to

the resulting graph is also a subgraph. Therefore, the same variables always need to map the same way.

Here we note an important observation that by having each variable in at least three clauses, by decreasing the number of vertices by
c
we will decrease the number of external edges also by
c
, as we are mapping
c
variable gadgets to
P_{2}
and for each variable

occurring in

clauses, we have
2o_{x}
external edges formed by two cycles of size

which are mapped to each other. Thus, again, by decreasing the number of vertices by

c
.

Another point we will need to ensure is that only one variable gadget per each clause gadget in
G
will be mapped to
P_{2}
(from the definition of the ONE-IN-THREE SAT). But we notice that by mapping one variable gadget to
P_{2}
in a clause, we decrease the number of clause edges by four. By mapping two variable gadgets in the same clause to
P_{2}
, the second variable gadget mapped to
P_{2}
will decrease the number of clause edges by three. Lastly, by mapping three variable gadgets in the same clause to
P_{2}
, the last variable gadgets mapped to
P_{2}
will decrease the number of clause edges by two.

We notice that since the number of variable edges has already decreased by
c
and external edges by
c
too, we need to decrease the number of remaining clause edges by

(since

). Therefore, since we are decreasing the number of vertices by
c
, this is only possible if by mapping a variable gadget in
G
to
P_{2}
, we decrease the number of clause edges by four. But this is only possible if all the
c
variable gadgets in
G
mapped to
P_{2}
are in different clauses.

Now we should be ready to show our equivalence. Let us assume that there is a true ONE-IN-THREE SAT formula with each variable in at least three clauses. Constructing
G
as described above and for each true variable mapping its variable gadgets to
P_{2}
, we obtain our ordered retraction from

to

as defined in our
SLICE
<gh
problem.

Conversely, having an non-surjective ordered retraction

from

to

as defined above, we simply choose a true value for every variable gadget mapped by

to
P_{2}
and a false value for every variable gadget mapped by identity.

In order to prove that this ordered retraction

must satisfy

for all

we first see that because each variable gadget contains

and these

that are not mapped by identity are the variable gadgets that are mapped to

. This can happen only by mapping a third vertex to a second vertex of the variable gadget, or vice versa.

We see that mapping a third vertex to a second would not be an identity on

as this would connect the first and second vertex of the variable gadget and the first and second vertex are not connected in the variable gadget in

. We notice that by mapping the second vertex of the variable gadget in

to the third, we get the identity on

. But since the third vertex can be mapped to the second vertex within the variable gadget in

must satisfy

, and we get an ordered retraction

We also see that by construction, all the ordered graphs in

must be proper ordered subgraphs of

Our problem is in

This completes the proof.
∎

M^{C}_{i},i>4

Let us now show that all ordered matchings

are edge-collapsible.

Lemma 2

For every
i>3,i\in\mathbb{N}
,
M^{C}_{i}
is edge-collapsible.

Proof.

Let us prove the statement by induction on a number of edges

.

The base case for
i=4
holds by Lemma

.

Assume that the statement holds for
i
. By definition, the new edge

is added in a way that
\chi^{<}(M^{C}_{i+1})=2
, since
v
is the leftmost vertex and
w
is between
v_{i}\in M^{C}_{i}
and
v_{i+1}\in M^{C}_{i}
or
w
is placed after the rightmost vertex
v_{2i}\in M^{C}_{i}
. Therefore,
M^{C}_{i+1}
can map to
P_{2}\subset M^{C}_{i+1}
.

The remaining part is to show that
M^{C}_{i+1}
cannot map to any other ordered subgraph than
P_{2}
. We prove this by contradiction and assume that
M^{C}_{i+1}
is not edge-collapsible.

We observe that by adding the edge
e
to
M^{C}_{i}
, we cannot make the ordered subgraph
M^{C}_{i}
of
M^{C}_{i+1}

not
edge-collapsible. Therefore, the only way
M^{C}_{i+1}
could be not edge-collapsible is if the edge
e
mapped to some other edge in
M^{C}_{i}
, or the edge in
M^{C}_{i}
mapped to
e
. We notice that the only edge where
e
could possibly map (or the edge that could map to
e
) is the edge
v^{e}_{1}\in M^{C}_{i}
, since the vertex
v
has only the vertex
v_{1}\in M^{C}_{i}
next to it.

Let us denote a vertex
v_{1_{j}}\in M^{C}_{i}
, the vertex adjacent to
v_{1}\in M^{C}_{i}
. However, since by construction, the vertices
w
and
v_{1_{j}}
have at least one vertex
v_{w1_{j}}\in M^{C}_{i}
between them, at least the edges
v^{e}_{w1_{j}}
,
e
, and
v^{e}_{1_{j}}
must be mapped together to one of these three edges if this was ordered homomorphism to an ordered subgraph of
M^{C}_{i+1}
. But by the induction hypothesis, since
M^{C}_{i}
is edge-collapsible, mapping edges
v^{e}_{w1_{j}}
and
v^{e}_{1_{j}}
to one edge is not possible without mapping all other edges in
M^{C}_{i}
to just one edge, and we arrive to a contradiction.

We only add that the first step of the iteration when
i=5
,
M^{C}_{5}
is edge-callapsible, using the same arguments as above and Lemma
1
.
∎

Now we should be ready to prove the main statement.

Theorem 5.3

The following hardness results hold. The
CORE
{}_{<}^{\chi^{<}(G)}
problem

1.

is
NP
-hard,

2.

is
W[1]
-hard, when parameterized by
k
,

3.

cannot be solved in time
2^{o(|V(G)|)}
nor in time
n^{o(k)}
, unless the ETH fails.

Proof.

To prove the statement, we proceed with the reduction from the
Multicolored Clique

be the instance graph whose vertex set is partitioned into
k

V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k}
, each

has a clique, intersecting every set

.

By copying some vertices if necessary, without loss of generality, we may assume that for each

we have
|V_{i}|=l
, and that

. The problem
Multicolored Clique
is

-complete and
W[1]
-hard when parameterized by

(k\ell)^{o(k)}
, unless the ETH fails (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
,
[
17
]
and
[

]
).

We start by fixing an arbitrary total order for each
V_{i}=\{x^{i}_{1},\ldots,x^{i}_{l}\},i\in[k]
in

.

Let us now define an ordered graph
G
, based on the input instance graph

of the
Multicolored Clique

Definition 5.1

(Ordered Graph

from

to
CORE
{}_{<}^{\chi^{<}(G)}

.

For each
i\in[k]
, let

be a set of

C_{i}=\{v^{i}_{1},\ldots,v^{i}_{l}\},

which also determines the ordering of the vertices in
C_{i}
. The sets
C_{i}
are mutually disjoint.

Next, let
B_{i},i\in[k]
be a set of
k-1
vertices denoted as follows.

B_{i}=\{w^{i}_{1},w^{i}_{2},\ldots,w^{i}_{i-1},w^{i}_{i+1},\ldots,w^{i}_{k}\}

This also determines the order of the vertices in
B_{i}
, and all sets
B_{i},i\in[k]
are mutually disjoint.

We also define
D_{i},i\in[k]
, as a set of

D_{i}=\{d^{i}_{1},\ldots,d^{i}_{l},d^{i}_{l+1},\ldots,d^{i}_{l+k-1}\}

, and all the sets

Now we define a set of vertices

, where the vertices in
C_{i}
are followed by the vertices in
B_{i}
, as follows,

A_{i}=C_{i},B_{i}.

The sets

are mutually disjoint.

We order these sets of vertices in the following way, adding a vertex in the beginning, at the end, and in between sets
D_{i},i\in[k]
and
A_{i},i\in[k]
in the following way.

p_{1},D_{1},p_{2},D_{2},\ldots,p_{k},D_{k},p_{k+1},A_{1},p_{k+2},A_{2},\ldots,p_{2k},A_{k},p_{2k+1}

For fixed

, we then connect the vertex

with all vertices in

, and we connect the vertex
p_{i+1}
with all vertices in

. Also, for fixed

, we connect the vertex
p_{i}
with all the vertices in
C_{i}
, and connect the vertex

with all the vertices in

. We will call both sets of these edges

.

Then, for all
i\in[k]
and all

, we connect the vertices
w^{j}_{i}
and
w^{i}_{j}
. We will call these edges

.

We also add another set of edges in the following way. If
x^{i^{\prime}_{1}}_{j^{\prime}_{1}}
and

are adjacent in

and

in

. We will call these edges

.

We define the last set of edges by forming an ordered matching

defined above on the vertices

and

. We see that
|V(M^{C}_{l+k-1})|=|V(D_{i})|+|V(A_{i})|

.

This completes the definition of

. Note that

\chi^{<}(G)=2k+1+2k=4k+1
.

Equivalence of instances.

We claim that there exists a clique in
F
, intersecting every set
V_{i},i\in[k]
, if and only if there exists an ordered homomorphism
f
from
G
to an ordered subgraph

of

.

In the remainder of the proof, we may keep the same notation of vertices in

as we do in the ordered graph

without risk of confusion, since

is an ordered subgraph of

.

Suppose that
F
has a yes-instance of
Multicolored Clique
, that is,
for each
i\in[k]
there is
j_{i}
, such that
C_{F}=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}\{x^{i}_{j_{i}}\}
is a clique in

.

We define
f:V(G)\to V(H)
, where

, in the following way. Fix
i\in[k]
. We map the vertices in
A_{i}\in V(G)
to
v^{i}_{j_{i}}\in V(G)
. This is feasible since the vertices in
A_{i}
form an interval and
v^{i}_{j_{i}}
is a vertex in
A_{i}\in V(G)
.

We also map the vertices in
D_{i}\in V(G)
to the vertex

, which is adjacent by a collapsible edge with

. This is also feasible since the vertices in
D_{i}
form an interval and

is a vertex in

.

Then we map
p_{i}\in V(G)
to

by identity.

Let us show that

is an ordered homomorphism. Clearly, by the construction of

,

respects the ordering of vertices.

Let us show that it preserves the edges.
With fixed
i\in\{k+1,\ldots,2k+1\}
, we see that the path edges connecting
p_{i}
with the vertices in
A_{i}
in
G
will map to the edge connecting the vertices
p_{i}
and
v^{i}_{j_{i}}
in

, and the path edges connecting
p_{i+1}
with the vertices in

in

will map to the edge connecting
p_{i+1}
and
v^{i}_{j_{i}}
in

.

With fixed
i\in[k]
, the same will hold for the path edges connecting
p_{i}
with the vertices in
D_{i}
in

. They will map to the edge connecting the vertices
p_{i}
and
d^{i}_{j}
in
H
, and the paths that connect
p_{i+1}
with the vertices in
D_{i}
in

will map to the edge connecting
p_{i+1}
and
v^{i}_{j}
in
H
.

For complete edges, we see that since there is a complete edge between every pair
B_{i^{\prime}_{1}}
and

in
G
, if the vertices of each

map to a vertex

in

, then the complete edges in

will form a complete ordered graph

. To see this, we can assume for contradiction that there is no complete edge between vertices

and

in

. But there is a complete edge connecting
w^{i}_{j}
and
w^{j}_{i}
in

that mapped to an edge connecting
v^{i}_{j_{i}}
and
v^{j}_{j_{j}}
in

, a contradiction.

Now, since
C_{F}=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}\{x^{i}_{j_{i}}\}
is a clique in

,

, therefore, the mapping

will preserve the complete edges.

G[C_{G}]

is also a reason why the original edges will be preserved, since they will all map to a complete ordered graph

.

The last set of edges which preservation we will need to check are collapsible edges. But we showed in Lemma
2
, that
M^{C}_{l+k-1}
can map to a single edge, so since

is an edge in

.

We can see that

in

. Also, the directed ordered path

p_{1},d^{1}_{j_{v^{1}}},p_{2},d^{2}_{j_{v^{2}}},\ldots,p_{k},d^{k}_{j_{v^{k}}},p_{k+1},v^{1}_{j_{1}},\ldots,p_{2k},v^{k}_{j_{k}},p_{2k+1}.

As we reasoned above, for each

, the collapsing edges connecting

and

in

, are also present in

.
There are no other vertices or edges in

, and

.

Now consider an ordered homomorphism

.

Let us first show the following two lemmas, which will help us determine to which vertices in a proper ordered subgraph

Lemma 3

Let
G
be an ordered graph defined in Definition
5.1
, and

be an ordered homomorphism. Then for each
i\in[2k+1]
,

maps the vertex

in

to the vertex
p_{i}
in

.

Proof.

We see that for
i\in[k-1]

can map only to vertex
p_{i+k+1}
, since it is connected to vertex
v^{i+1}_{1}
, none of the vertices in
B_{i}
are connected to
v^{i+1}_{1}
(so if mapped to vertices in
B_{i}
, this mapping would not preserve edges incident to
p_{i+k+1}
), and
p_{i+k+1}
is also connected to
v^{i}_{l}
.

Since none of the vertices in
B_{k}
is connected to
v^{k}_{l}
, and

is connected to

in

.

The vertex

in

can only be mapped to the vertex

in

, since it is connected to neighboring vertices

on the right and

on the left.

We see that for
i\in[k]

can map only to vertex
p_{i}
, since it is connected to neighboring vertices

on the right and
d^{i}_{l+k-1}
on the left from

.
∎

Lemma 4

Let
G
be an ordered graph defined in Definition
5.1
, and
f:V(G)\to V(H),H\subset G
be an ordered homomorphism. Then for each
i\in[k]
,
f
maps all the vertices in
A_{i}
to a vertex
v^{i}_{j_{i}},j_{i}\in[l]
in

, and all the vertices in
D_{i}
to a vertex
d^{i}_{j^{\prime}_{i}},j^{\prime}_{i}\in[l+k-1]
in

, where
v^{i}_{j_{i}}
and
d^{i}_{j^{\prime}_{i}}
are connected by collapsing edge.

Proof.

We begin the proof by showing where the vertices in
G
can possibly map.

Lemma
3
shows us that all of the
2k+1
vertices
p_{i},i\in[2k+1]
in

will map by identity to vertices
p_{i}
in
H\subset G
.

Let us fix
i\in[k]
. We see that the vertices in

can only map to the vertices in

, since the vertex
p_{k+i+1}
is connected only to vertices in

and

, while the vertices in

are connected to other vertices in

, and vertex

is connected to vertex

by an edge, so vertices in

cannot map to

, since this mapping would not preserve the order of vertices.

We also see that vertex

in

can only map to the vertices in

, since other vertices in

could not preserve edges incident to

mapped to other vertices in

. Therefore the vertices in

can either map to the vertices in

, or they map to themselves by identity.

The vertices in

can only map to the vertices in

, since no other vertices are connected to

and

.

Also, vertices in

can only map to the vertices in

, since no other vertices are connected to

and

.

We therefore see that vertices in

and

are the only vertices in

that can map to other vertices in

, since by Lemma

must map by identity.

Next aspect we notice is that since

, at least one vertex in

will map to the subgraph of

But from Lemma

must map to just one vertex in

, and all the vertices in

must map to just one vertex in

, and from the reasoning above, more precisely all the vertices in

must map to just one vertex in

.

Let us now show that if all the vertices in one

, then also all vertices in other

.

We will show this by contradiction, assuming that all vertices in other

.

Note that vertices in other

to a vertex in

, are adjacent to a vertex in

in

that has degree one. However, if all the vertices in

will map to a single vertex

in

, the vertex

will have degree

. Therefore, in order for

to be an ordered subgraph of

, the vertices in

that are adjacent to vertices in

Since vertices in our

are attached to every

), all the vertices in every

.

But once again by Lemma

, for each fixed

, then also all vertices in

and all vertices in

and one vertex in

, respectively.

In order for

.

From Lemma

, for each

there is exactly one

Putting

, we claim that

is a clique in

.
For contradiction, suppose that

is not adjacent to

However, there is an edge between the vertices

, and

. And

Therefore, from the construction of

, we can define

, which will be a clique in

, intersecting every set

.

Since
|V(G)|=2k+1+2k(l+k-1)=2kl+2k^{2}+1

, we see that this is a polynomial reduction; and since again

∎

The following result then follows.

Corollary 3

CORE
<
is

-complete and

-hard when parameterized by the number of the vertices of the image ordered proper subgraph of the instance ordered graph.

-vertex instances cannot be solved in time

nor in time

, unless the ETH fails.

Proof.

All hardness statements follow directly from Theorem
5.3
.

The remaining point to show is that

is in

. But this is clear since the ordered homomorphism certificate can be verified in polynomial time.
∎

We note that the

We conclude this article by showing that the

{}_{<}^{k}

. Thus, these problems are in

Proof.

For
