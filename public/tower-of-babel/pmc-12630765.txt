Continuous and componentized facial palsy measurement alignment and clinical interpretable model
Authors: Xiudong Guan, Haixin Wang, Dainan Zhang, Chenglong Hou, Shunchang Ma, Jun Zhang, Xiaofeng Sun, Wenke Liu, Meiqing Lou, Yan Qu, Tianzhi Zhao, Dong Zhong, Jiayi Peng, Peiliang Li, Dan Xiao, Yuanwen Jiang, Peng Kang, Fei Yao, Xiaoxue Fu, Zhan Xue, Liangpeng Chen, Ziyang Li, Haolin Li, Muzhi Yu, Shikun Zhang, Ming Ni, Guijun Jia, Kuangyu Shi, Jinan Sun, Deling Li, Wang Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Facial palsy, affecting 1 in 60 individuals, requires precise assessment for effective treatment and follow-up. Current grading systems are inconsistent due to redundant subjective factors. Discrete assessment of the whole face fails to address eyelid and mouth movements innervated by separate nerves. Herein, we proposed modified House-Brackmann (H-B) criteria with continuous and componentized measurements for the eyelids and mouth. Using a dataset of 274 patients with vestibular schwannoma (VS), we developed a dense facial landmark alignment model. This model outperformed existing algorithms in facial palsy detection and produced clinically interpretable asymmetric coefficients of eyelids and mouth correlated with consensus H-B grades (r = 0.892 and 0.890, P < 0.001). Thresholds from ROC analysis transformed these coefficients into grades, comparable to consensus H-B grades. Validated in a separate post-VS multi-center cohort, this model showed high accuracy (eyelid Grade 6 not observed; analyses restricted accordingly) and potential for continuous monitoring of facial palsy, offering a more precise and comprehensive assessment tool. Introduction

Facial palsy is weakness or paralysis of the muscles of the face. It is an extremely common clinical manifestation of several conditions, such as Bell’s palsy, viral infection, trauma, surgery, and iatrogenic damage of the facial nerve, with a high prevalence of 120/100,000 in a national survey in South Korea1,2. The annual incidence of Bell’s palsy alone in the United Kingdom is 20/100,000, with 1 in 60 people affected during their lifetime3,4. Accurate and objective assessment of the severity of facial palsy is important in the clinic1, including quality assessment of relevant operations like vestibular schwannoma resection, choosing optimal treatment for facial palsy (observing, medications, different rehabilitation, neurorrhaphy)5,6, among others.

Although facial palsy presents a large disease burden for humans, there are problems with manually diagnosing the severity of facial palsy based on current grading systems7,8, such as inconsistent results due to redundant subjective factors9–11. There are two issues in explaining the problems of these clinical tools. Firstly, for the most utilized House-Brackmann (H-B) grading system12, grading decisions are subjective, therefore influencing inter-observer consistency8. Notably, there may be similarities between patients which cannot be differentiated by discrete values. In addition, patients undergo a continuous process of rehabilitation13, which cannot be coherently presented by discrete values. Therefore, for more consistent and clinically meaningful evaluation, asymmetric indicators for continuous evaluation rather than discrete grading would be better for grading facial palsy. Secondly, different degrees of severity in the eyelids and mouth in facial palsy patients are common in the clinic, which leads to difficulties regarding assessment criteria for the whole face. The reason for this is that the eyelids and mouth are innervated by different facial nerve branches. It is more reasonable to assess facial palsy by separately measuring the asymmetry of the eyelids and mouth. Given the above considerations, we suggest a modified H-B grading system with continuous parameters and componentized eyelid and mouth parameters.

How to further remove subjective human factors when analyzing facial asymmetry is worthy of study with artificial intelligence (AI). Firstly, considering interpretability and stability, accurate facial landmark detection is key in designing a framework using eyelid and mouth movement in facial palsy patients. These factors, such as variations in expressions, shapes, poses, and occlusions, could greatly affect the performance of landmark localization14. Other studies have tried to alleviate these problems from the perspective of structural characteristics, usage of geometric information, and correction of loss functions15,16. However, almost all prior studies were trained on normal facial datasets to assess the accuracy of landmark localization, and have downgraded performance for abnormal faces. Notably, the performances of existing methods in landmark detection in facial palsy patients have not been elucidated14,15. Secondly, most existing methods depend on end-to-end deep networks without interpretation ability. Fusing the human experience into the whole rationale and algorithm for quantitative measurement of the asymmetry would support transparent logic and clinical interpretability17. Therefore, developing and introducing the most accurate facial landmark detection model into facial palsy analysis makes sense both in computer vision and clinical fields.

In this study, we proposed a continuous and componentized facial palsy measurement based on a dense facial-landmark detection technique and clinical interpretable modelling (Fig. 1). First, we constructed a landmark dataset including both static images and dynamic videos as well as built the detection model training process including pre- and post-processing (Fig. 1a, b). We then captured videos of standard facial movements from patients with facial palsy after a vestibular schwannoma operation (Fig. 1c). After detecting dense facial landmarks (Fig. 1b), we were able to implement continuous and componentized facial palsy measurements based on clinical interpretable modeling (Fig. 1d). Four experienced neurosurgeons graded patients using the classical H-B grade and our modified H-B grade with the eyelids and mouth separated, which were analyzed for consistency, and then used the consensus as the dataset for assessing the performance of this automated algorithm (Fig. 1e). Since the thresholds between manual consensus grades were calculated, these continuous asymmetric coefficients can be transformed to grades and then compared with classical grading (Fig. 1f). Results

Modified House-Brackmann criteria improved inter-observer consistency

Of all H-B grades blindly and independently assessed by three neurosurgeons, 27.3% (75/274) were identical in the training dataset. Among the patients with different grades, 10.5% (21/199) of those differed by at least 2 grades. After the patients were measured according to the modified H-B grading criteria of merging neighboring grades, the percentage of inter-observer consistency increased to 48.2% (132/274) and 28.1% (77/274), respectively for eyelid and mouth H-B grades. The percentage of patients who differed by at least 2 grades decreased to 6.3% (9/142) and 9.6% (19/197) for eyelid and mouth H-B grades, respectively. The Fleiss kappa coefficient, an indicator of consistency between the three neurosurgeons, increased to 0.36 and 0.53 for mouth and eyelid H-B grades (respectively) from 0.31 for classic H-B grades (Fig. 2a). The kappa coefficients for H-B grades between each two neurosurgeons were 0.23, 0.37 and 0.33; however, those for eyelid and mouth H-B grades increased to 0.46 and 0.38 between neurosurgeons 2 and 3 (respectively) (Fig. 2b). Therefore, the modified H-B grading criteria with componentized analysis of the eyelids and mouth dramatically improved inter-observer consistency. Proposed dense facial-landmark detection model

The comparison model included recent state-of-the-art open-source algorithms, such as FAN-Face18, Awing19, and almost all publicly available facial-landmark detection commercial services developed by AI corporations, with open surveys, including Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, and Face + +. We found that state-of-the-art facial-landmark detection techniques sometimes failed in facial palsy measurements due to asymmetric face shapes. In addition, for medical applications, all existing open-source algorithms with sparse landmark points (68 landmarks on the whole face) are not always accurate.

Therefore, a facial palsy landmark detection dataset (FPLD), including 2,423,850 landmarks in 2486 facial palsy images, was constructed to train and test our detection model. To make a fair comparison, we considered the following two perspectives. On one hand, FAN-Face and Awing failed in some cases with null landmarks, demonstrating that these open-source algorithms may have difficulty in detecting landmarks in the FPLD. Therefore, we took the maximum value for the failed cases. We found that FAN-Face fails to detect the landmarks in about 1.63% of asymmetric face shapes, especially when patients suffer from severe facial palsy. On the other hand, the number of labeled landmarks differs between algorithms. Therefore, we set up a unified principle to compare the performance between dense and sparse landmarks. In addition, we exploited the common evaluation metrics of mean error, area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and failure error to estimate the performance of different landmark detection models.

Our proposed model trained on the FPLD outperformed both state-of-the-art open-source algorithms and closed-source commercial algorithms on most evaluation metrics (Table 1). Our proposed method outperformed the best open-source algorithm by 46.93% on mean error, 12.67% on AUC, and 14.77% on failure error. This algorithm also outperformed the best commercial tool by 10.34% on a mean error and 2.46% on AUC. The performance in failure error was almost identical to the state-of-the-art algorithms. Comprehensively, our dense facial-landmark detection model achieved excellent performance on irregular face shapes. To calculate the mouth asymmetric coefficients the first time, the most stable symmetric reference lines were set up from the midpoint of both sides of the inner canthus to the middle point of the lower half of the face contour (Fig. 3d). The timing diagram of the area of the upper, lower, and inner lids can be calculated using the left and right sides (Fig. 3e–h). The mouth asymmetric coefficients were obtained by automation. Automated grading is comparable to consensus grading

In order to provide an interpretable metric akin to classical H-B grading, we calculated grade thresholds for the eyelids and mouth asymmetry coefficients using ROC curve analysis. The AUC, threshold, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated (Table 2). For eyelid asymmetric coefficients, the thresholds for differentiating Grades 1–5 were as follows: 0.177, 0.252, 0.336, 0.447 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Because of the lack of Grade 6 cases with complete eyelid palsy (Supplementary Table 1), a threshold between Grade 5 and 6 could not be obtained. Thresholds for mouth grading were 0.108, 0.160, 0.208, 0.290, and 0.317 for Grades 1–6, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). Accordingly, eyelid-related analyses and interpretations are restricted to Grades 1–5, whereas mouth-related analyses cover Grades 1–6. All thresholds remained unchanged after excluding values outside the 5th–95th percentile range (see Statistical analysis), confirming that they were stable and subsequently applied for classification. With these thresholds, we designed modified H-B grading criteria. In order to test whether automated grading criteria based on asymmetric coefficient and threshold calculations were accurate, they were analyzed pairwise with manual consensus grades. We further visualized the relationship between automated and manual consensus grades with a heatmap. We expected the heat points to fall on the diagonal, showing a correlation between automated eyelid H-B grades and experienced consensus H-B grades. It was basically consistent with our expectations (Fig. 5c, d). Therefore, this proposal, including facial landmark detection, asymmetric coefficient analysis, and grade classification, reaches the consensus assessment levels to assess the severity of facial palsy.

Performance of the algorithm in a prospective separate validation dataset

To evaluate the performance of this algorithm and the asymmetric coefficient grading system, a prospective cohort of 274 facial palsy patients with the same inclusion criteria was enrolled in a separate validation dataset. Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis showed that the automated asymmetric coefficient and the manual consensus grades were significantly positively correlated for both eyelid and mouth (r = 0.857 and 0.875, respectively, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Based on the determined grading threshold systems, automated eyelid and mouth H-B grades were obtained in this cohort. The heatmap showed a similar trend to the training dataset (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Therefore, this method also achieved the same level of consensus grading in this separate validation dataset.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed novel modified H-B criteria and an algorithm for componentized measurements of facial palsy for the eyelids and mouth. Clinical research confirmed that this modification improves inter-observer consistency. This can be explained by inconsistent damage of different facial nerve branches innervating muscles for the eyelids and mouth. Although this explanation aligns with known neurophysiological principles, further studies are needed to validate this hypothesis. However, there is still a large percentage of patients with inconsistent measurements by doctors, which needs to be improved. While our modifications address some of these inconsistencies, they are not sufficient to completely resolve them. This issue is also reported in prior studies on facial palsy grading systems7,8.

Here, we elucidate the underlying principle of our entire design. We assumed that the degree of facial palsy is correlated with facial asymmetry. In contrast to existing AI systems, we constructed novel grading criteria and asymmetry indicators for more applicable targets9,19. These modified grading criteria facilitate continuous and componentized measurement of facial palsy affecting the eyelids and mouth. Specifically, we assessed the differences between the left and right sides of the mouth and eyelids to quantify asymmetric indicators. To better model the indicators, we developed a two-stage model that integrated a landmark detection model via deep learning with a clinical statistical model. The facial landmark detection networks and methods for analyzing asymmetry were specifically designed for patients, focusing on physiological characteristics. Then, clinical interpretable modeling was developed for continuous asymmetric coefficient assessment and for grading the severity of facial palsy. The automatically analyzed results from this whole set of algorithms were well-correlated with manual consensus grades by experienced neurosurgeons. The grading criteria based on the thresholds obtained in the training dataset rendered this algorithm comparable with H-B grading. Notably, the grading results from the asymmetry coefficients reached a consensus in both the training and multi-central validation datasets. This clinical interpretability makes this tool much easier for clinical translation20,21.

More specifically, this approach has the following four unique advantages. Firstly, we have designed novel modified H-B criteria. Since facial palsy induces a weakness or loss of facial expression through damage to the facial nerve, an assessment of the severity of facial palsy is important for diagnosis and relevant treatment7. Conventional judgements are extremely dependent on the clinician’s subjective experience, which brings problems of redundant subjective factors, inconsistent evaluation results, coarse granularity, and a complicated amount of work9–11. Hence, a quantitative measurement system would be valuable for physicians. In the past decade, several automated systems have been constructed for the grading of facial palsy9,14–16. However, the main limitation is the lack of research on reasonable criteria for automated systems. Almost all systems are measured by comparing with classical H-B criteria. However, the accuracy of H-B grading notwithstanding, the H-B grading among different physicians is inconsistent. In practice, H-B criteria are designed for human use, which is not necessarily suitable for automated systems. Therefore, more interpretable criteria are essential for automated systems. In this study, we proposed modified H-B criteria for the eyelids and mouth, which are more effective in practical application. Besides, sample sizes of previous studies ranged from 10 to 118 participants9,22–26. Most of the literature primarily relied on photographs, while a few studies utilized video data. Furthermore, the datasets were often single-center and even sourced from public databases. In contrast, our study includes a training set of 274 individuals and validates it using a multi-center validation set of 274 participants. All data were collected through videos. Therefore, our study is more objective and reliable compared to previous research.

Secondly, we designed the first dense facial-landmark detection model targeting irregular faces, which is of great significance both for computer vision and clinical medicine. Conventional algorithms, which rely on handcrafted local image features, are sensitive to variations, such as the Active Shape Model27, Active Appearance Model28–31, Constrained Local Model32,33, and Cascade Regression34–36. Based on the development of deep learning-based models16,37–39 and loss functions19,40, we constructed a detection model with dense landmarks. More importantly, we dropped the conventional 68 labeling points19,40 in open-source model designs and introduced dense facial landmark labels (975 points). Dense facial landmarks effectively decreased the measurement error by about 46.93% compared with 68 facial landmarks. Measuring facial asymmetry is obviously influenced by the detection of facial landmarks to a large extent. The reasons contributing to the error of asymmetry are two-fold: 1) the landmark localization accuracy, where better models provide a more accurate fitting ability of landmarks to decrease measurement error; and 2) the density of landmark distribution, where a denser landmark distribution is closer to the actual curve distribution of face shapes, which helps to better exploit facial information for asymmetry measurement. Our dense facial-landmark detection model outperforms existing open-source algorithms and commercial methods developed by AI corporations on our facial palsy datasets9,19. Since a dense landmark distribution is close to the actual distribution of face shapes, the model can better fit the actual distribution of facial shapes. Therefore, it can improve prediction accuracy and better divide the area into left and right parts, which is of great significance for the following step to construct an asymmetry measurement.

Thirdly, this method is clinically interpretable and predictive compared with existing facial palsy measurement systems7–9. End-to-end framework is often exploited by previous systems research to map medical images to final gradings. However, it is well-known that deep learning methods are still in the “black box” stage, which is to say that they have a lack of interpretable capability for practical clinical applications. Therefore, we proposed to integrate a specific and precise landmark detection model with clinical interpretable modeling. Several studies have explored automated systems before the development of deep learning methods for facial landmark detection41–44. Due to the insufficient handcrafted local image features mentioned earlier, they cannot capture deep information for detection. However, automated measurement of facial palsy depends on reasonable modeling of the severity. A distance ratio, which calculates the distance between two identified points on each side of the face, was exploited to measure asymmetry41. The distance between the superior border of the eyebrow and the lower eyelid was proposed as the asymmetry measurement45. Almost all systems only consider one-dimensional information, which is extremely vulnerable to instability in a landmark detection model and is insufficient to judge facial palsy gradings. More importantly, they fail to integrate the physical and clinical experience into consideration.

Furthermore, from clinical and statistical aspects, it is of great significance to provide continuous and temporal asymmetric assessments for facial palsy. In this study, the automated asymmetric coefficient represents not only the grading but also the similarity of different cases. Patients diagnosed as classic H-B Grade 3 may differ in severity, where one is closer to the threshold between Grades 2 and 3, but the other is closer to that between Grades 3 and 4 in this automated grading. In addition, patients classified into different H-B grades may be similar from the perspective of the automated asymmetric coefficients. This phenomenon was often seen in vestibular schwannoma patients with continuous rehabilitation of facial palsy after the operation. Therefore, this continuous and componentized automated algorithm for facial palsy measurement has the potential to follow patients, assess the quality of an operation, and monitor the effectiveness of treatment targeting facial palsy. In addition, this method was designed based on dynamic video data instead of static images. The dynamic data can record richer information, including temporal continuous measurements of mouth and eyelid movements, which is the most important part in measuring asymmetry to accurately find the opened and closed states to the maximum or minimum. However, capturing static images may ignore the more extreme states and cast doubt on the accuracy of the following steps.

This model also has limitations. In our single-center training cohort (Beijing Tiantan Hospital) and domestic multi-center validation, cases with complete eyelid paralysis (H-B Grade 6) were absent (Supplementary Table 1). From the perspective of H-B grading, our dataset shows a skewed and long-tailed distribution, where a few grades occupy most cases and other grades are rare. Consequently, the boundary between eyelid Grades 5 and 6 could not be derived; we therefore do not compute or report performance for eyelid Grade 6, and eyelid-related analyses and interpretations are restricted to Grades 1–5, whereas mouth-related analyses cover Grades 1–6. Future studies should prioritize inclusion of patients with very severe eyelid palsy and broader external cohorts to enable calibration of the eyelid Grade 5–6 threshold. The present evidence primarily reflects postoperative VS; applicability to Bell’s palsy, traumatic, infectious, and other etiologies awaits prospective international, multi-ethnic external validation and cross-site calibration.

In the future, we will further examine the construction of these automatically modified H-B criteria from the following three perspectives. Firstly, with the increase of data samples from local hospitals, we will be able to enrich rarer cases to improve the data imbalance. Therefore, patients between H-B Grades 5 and 6 can be divided more clearly. Secondly, in the future, the dense key-point prediction can go to a pixel-wise key-point prediction to form a closed curve, which will further decrease the measurement error for the whole system. Third, considering the dynamic process, based on existing proposed soft criteria, we will give an H-B grade sequence to track the clinical process of patients, which can be used to construct a quantitative measurement system for patient recovery. Finally, to better understand the grading process with human experience, we will also analyze the stability of neurosurgeons using the same dataset. Simply speaking, whether the same neurosurgeon will give the same grading for the same case in different periods will be studied. Ultimately, we have constructed a more reasonable and accurate clinical benchmark for facial palsy grading and a more robust facial-landmark detection model with improved computer vision.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

Data from the First Medical Centre, Chinese PLA General Hospital, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University, The Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University, and The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University was approved under the oversight of the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of each center. It was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900024638). Informed consents were obtained from all participants. All the identifiable photographs are fully consented to through written consent.

Patient enrolment and consensus assessment of standard facial palsy video

All enrolled adult patients with vestibular schwannoma in the training dataset underwent tumor resection in Beijing Tiantan Hospital. Those in the validation dataset were also postoperative vestibular schwannoma cases from seven hospitals in China (Supplementary Table 1). Exclusion criteria were pre-existing facial palsy, bilateral schwannoma, facial deformity, or inability to provide informed consent or cooperate with instructions. The high-definition videos taken of patients at 7 days post-operation were captured by trained research nurses using smartphones with cameras, which included face appearance at rest, the closing of the eyes, and mouth motion with grinning to the maximum extent (Fig. 1c). The App on the smartphone was designed for uploading the patients’ information and videos, as well as outputting results (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). During the first stage (March 2017 to December 2019), 274 patients with qualified videos were enrolled as the training dataset. A separate validation dataset was made with 274 patients prospectively enrolled from August 2020 to April 2021 in overall 7 hospitals. Three experienced neurosurgeons (with 3, 5, and 8 years of experience, respectively) blindly and independently assessed the facial palsy grades according to the classic H-B grading system12,46, which is a widely used system for grading peripheral facial palsy in clinics. Because of the inconsistent damage to different facial nerve branches, a novel modified H-B grading system was used for clinical assessment, with separate assessments for eye and mouth movements. Therefore, the respective grades of eyes and mouth (ExMy) were also assessed. The expert consensus of classic, eyelid, and mouth H-B grading was measured as the analysis reference for algorithm design. If the grades from the three neurosurgeons were the same, or if two results were the same and the third was within one grade difference, the consensus grade would be the majority. If all three results were different, or if two had the same result and the third was different by at least two grades, the fourth neurosurgeon with 12 years of experience was involved to reach a consensus.

Scope note: All training and validation data in this study involved postoperative VS–related peripheral facial palsy. The framework quantifies geometry and kinematics from standardized dynamic tasks using dense facial landmarks and is, in principle, etiology-agnostic; nevertheless, extension to non-VS etiologies warrants prospective external validation.

We quantitatively compared the consistency of the three H-B grades between different neurosurgeons with the kappa coefficient. The Fleiss kappa coefficients of the single mouth H-B grade or eyelid H-B grade with the classic H-B grade were calculated to demonstrate consistency. Additionally, we utilized image restoration skills to pre-process the data for high-quality images, while post-processing was utilized in our pipeline. We also constructed a Benchmark dataset for facial palsy landmark detection (FPLD) in AI.

Pre- and post-processing of facial palsy landmark detection (FPLD)

Automated clinical systems are difficult to put into practice. This is due to the reasonable combination of clinical interpretability and computer vision technique, and to the realistic application environment. To our best knowledge, AI systems encounter many undesired difficulties. In this section, we provide the full pipeline of our system (Fig. 1a–f) to solve the existing difficulty in practical application. First of all, patients may not cooperate with the acquisition protocol, and the devices used are only simple mobile phones, which can make the data blurry and may lead to low-quality images. Therefore, the images and videos captured by medical personnel cannot meet the standard of AI algorithms. To address these problems and strengthen practical applications, we utilized image restoration skills to pre-process the data for high-quality images. Image super-resolution is an important class of image processing techniques to enhance the resolution of images and videos in computer vision. Based on existing deep learning methods, we employed SRCNN47 to directly learn end-to-end mapping between the low- and high-resolution images. Besides the high restoration quality, this network could process images at high speed for practical application because the image patch extraction and aggregation are formulated as convolution layers. Thus, the following detection model was able to process high-quality images for better performance.

In addition, post-processing was utilized in our pipeline, because the original images may have differences in poses and face localization. For better analysis, we justified the images into a horizontal position. Based on the detection of facial landmarks, we utilized the centre points of the upper and lower lip to confirm whether the face was horizontal. According to the rotation angle, we implemented an affine transformation to justify the face. In this way, we comprehensively considered problems in practical application of automatic systems, which makes it promising to apply in the clinic.

Benchmark dataset

After systematically reviewing previous studies, we found that the main issue in the field of facial palsy landmark detection was a lack of a model trained on specific datasets. Therefore, we constructed the FPLD.

The FPLD was constructed to implement facial palsy landmark detection. In total, the FPLD included 2486 pictures and 10 videos with 3 subsets including a dataset with 600 high-definition facial palsy images (FPLD_HD) and 2 datasets with 971 and 913 pictures captured from 5 videos (FPLD_VA, FPLD_VB). In addition, the FPLD included facial palsy patients’ information with H-B Grades 1–6 scored by 4 doctors. Each video included the process of closing eyes, opening eyes, and gnashing teeth. Holistic facial landmarks were pre-marked using a commercial application (Face + +) and fine-tuned by 5 researchers artificially using semi-automated labelling tools for 6 months. There are 975 human-annotated landmarks in a facial palsy picture, and 2,423,850 fine-tuned landmarks in the whole dataset.

Statistical analysis

The kappa coefficients were used to measure the consistency of the eyelid, mouth, and classic H-B grades between any two observers, while the Fleiss kappa coefficients were calculated among the three observers. The eyelid, mouth, and comprehensive asymmetric coefficients were compared with the H-B grades by Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis. In the training dataset, the threshold values between the neighboring grades were obtained with the largest Youden’s index by ROC curve analysis48. In the clinical aspect, it is acceptable that the judged grade differs by 1 grade due to such diverse criteria with 6 grades. To calculate the thresholds between different patient grades, we grouped every two adjacent manual consensus grades together. To minimize the impact of outliers on the threshold determination, values below the 5th percentile and above the 95th percentile were considered outliers and excluded. The thresholds were then recalculated iteratively for each grade after removing these outlier values. For example, when calculating the threshold between grade 2 and grade 3, only the values within the middle 90% range were used, ensuring a more robust threshold estimation. Utilizing these thresholds, all continuous asymmetric coefficients were categorized into automated H-B grades, which were compared with the consensus grading with a heatmap to test the performance of this algorithm. P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
