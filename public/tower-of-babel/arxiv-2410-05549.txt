Highly Excited Electron Cyclotron for QCD Axion and Dark-Photon Detection
Xing Fan, Gerald Gabrielse, Peter W. Graham, Harikrishnan Ramani, Samuel S. Y. Wong, Yawen Xiao

Abstract
We propose using highly excited cyclotron states of a trapped electron to detect meV axion and dark photon dark matter, marking a significant improvement over our previous proposal and demonstration [Phys. Rev. Lett. 129, 261801]. When the axion mass matches the cyclotron frequency ω c \omega_{c} , the cyclotron state is resonantly excited, with a transition probability proportional to its initial quantum number, n c n_{c} . The sensitivity is enhanced by taking n c ∼ 10 6 ​ ( 0.1 ​ meV ω c ) 2 n_{c}\sim 10^{6}\left(\frac{0.1~\text{meV}}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{2} . By optimizing key experimental parameters, we minimize the required averaging time for cyclotron detection to t ave ∼ 10 − 6 t_{\text{ave}}\sim 10^{-6} seconds, permitting detection of such a highly excited state before its decay. An open-endcap trap design enables the external photon signal to be directed into the trap, rendering our background-free detector compatible with large focusing cavities, such as the BREAD proposal, while capitalizing on their strong magnetic fields. Furthermore, the axion conversion rate can be coherently enhanced by incorporating layers of dielectrics with alternating refractive indices within the cavity. Collectively, these optimizations enable us to probe the QCD axion parameter space from 0.1 meV to 2.3 meV (25–560 GHz), covering a substantial portion of the predicted post-inflationary QCD axion mass range. This sensitivity corresponds to probing the kinetic mixing parameter of the dark photon down to ϵ ≈ 2 × 10 − 16 \epsilon\approx 2\times 10^{-16} .

II
Electron Cyclotron as a Dark Matter Detector

II.1
Electron Cyclotron Detector

An electron suspended in a Penning trap was recently used to measure the electron magnetic moment
[
44
]
at an unusual precision, approaching 1 part in
10^{13}
, to test the most precise prediction of the Standard Model

[
50
,
6
]
. There is a long history of such measurements
[
99
,
61
,
60
,
44
]
. The key advance that enabled the great precision of the measurements was the realization of a “one-electron quantum cyclotron”
[
93
]
. The cyclotron motion of one suspended electron was cooled below 100 mK, whereupon the electron occupied only the ground state of this motion. Sensitive quantum non-demotion (QND) detection was then employed to detect driven, single quantum transitions to the first excited cyclotron state. This was the detector that we recently demonstrated could be used to detect meV dark photons with greatly improved sensitivity
[
45
]
.

In a Penning trap
[
17
]
, an electron is effectively confined in three dimensions using a combination of a spatially uniform magnetic field
\mathbf{B}=B_{0}\hat{z}
in the axial direction and a static quadrupole potential

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2}m_{e}\omega_{z}^{2}\left(z^{2}-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right).

(4)

In this expression we neglect small, higher order anharmonic terms (e.g.
x^{4}
,
y^{4}
,
z^{4}
) and the effects of trap electrode imperfections.
The magnetic field in a symmetric gauge is given by the vector potential
\mathbf{A}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}
.
In
V
, the single constant
m_{e}\omega_{z}^{2}
is written as the product of the electron mass and the square of an axial angular frequency, for convenience. The
B
-field produces radial cyclotron motion at a cyclotron frequency modified slightly by
V
. The potential
V(\mathbf{r})
traps the electron in the axial direction in a harmonic axial motion with axial frequency
\omega_{z}
.

For most of the measurements mentioned above, including the dark photon demonstration measurement, the potential was produced using cylindrical Penning trap electrodes
[
52
]
, with electrodes on the surface of a cylinder and on flat ends. For this proposal, an open-endcap electrode geometry
[
51
]
is considered instead, with trap electrodes that are coaxial cylinders. This choice makes it possible to couple the signal photons from the conversion cavity into the Penning trap within which the electron is centered.

Neglecting relativistic effects, the Hamiltonian for an electron in an ideal Penning trap is

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathbf{p}-e\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2m_{e}}+eV.

(5)

The axial motion separates naturally from the radial motion. The axial part of
H_{0}
is that of a simple harmonic oscillator with angular axial frequency
\omega_{z}=\frac{\sqrt{eV_{0}/m_{e}}}{D}
,

\displaystyle H_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{z}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{e}\omega_{z}^{2}z^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{z}\left(a^{{\dagger}}_{z}a_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)~,

(7)

where the effective trap dimension is given by
D^{2}=R_{\text{trap}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}d^{2}
, and
R_{\text{trap}}
and
d
are the radius and height of the cylindrical trap, respectively.
The second expression uses harmonic oscillator lowering and raising operators,
a_{z}
and
a_{z}^{\dagger}
, that are familiar functions of
z
and
p_{z}
.

The radial motions and Hamiltonian are more complicated because the vector and scalar potentials both depend upon
x
and
y
. However, Ref.
[
17
]
introduced two sets of harmonic oscillator raising and lowering operators that separate the radial Hamiltonian into uncoupled cyclotron and magnetron terms,

\displaystyle H_{r}=\hbar\omega_{c^{\prime}}\left(a^{{\dagger}}_{c}a_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\hbar\omega_{m}\left(a^{{\dagger}}_{m}a_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(8)

To an approximation that suffices for our purposes, the angular cyclotron frequency modified by the electrostatic quadrupole,
\omega_{c^{\prime}}\approx\omega_{c}
, where
\omega_{c}=eB_{0}/m_{e}
is the free-space cyclotron frequency. Also to an approximation that suffices for our purposes, the magnetron frequency is
\omega_{m}\approx\omega_{z}^{2}/(2\omega_{c})
.
Both the cyclotron lowering and raising operators (
a_{c}
and
a^{{\dagger}}_{c}
) and the magnetron lowering and raising operators (
a_{m}
and
a^{{\dagger}}_{m}
) are complicated but manageable functions of
x
,
y
,
p_{x}
, and
p_{y}
. The raising and lowering operators for each of the separated motions satisfy the same commutator relations as those for a harmonic oscillator. For example,
[a_{c},a^{{\dagger}}_{c}]=1
.

The energy eigenstates of
H_{0}=H_{z}+H_{r}
are direct products of oscillator eigenstates,
\ket{n_c,n_z,n_m}
.
The lowering and raising operators each operate on their respective oscillator states. For example,

\displaystyle a_{c}\ket{n_c,n_z,n_m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n_{c}}\ket{n_c-1,n_z,n_m}

(9)

\displaystyle a^{{\dagger}}_{c}\ket{n_c,n_z,n_m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n_{c}+1}\ket{n_c+1,n_z,n_m}~.

(10)

The energy eigenvalues are

\displaystyle E_{0}(n_{c},n_{z},n_{m})

\displaystyle=\left(n_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_{c}+\left(n_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_{z}-\left(n_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_{m}.

(11)

The angular cyclotron frequency
\omega_{c}
is much greater than the angular axial frequency
\omega_{z}
, which in turn is much greater than the magnetron frequency,
\omega_{m}
.
For this work, the small difference between the trap-modified cyclotron frequency and the free-space cyclotron frequency is neglected, and the magnetron is ignored.

Consider detecting one-quantum transitions from the cyclotron state
n_{c}
to
n_{c}+1
. The cyclotron energy and state are difficult to detect directly because the cyclotron frequency is in the mm microwave regime. The thousand times lower frequency of the axial oscillation can be much more easily measured. To couple this frequency to the cyclotron energy, a magnetic ring that encircles the cylindrical trap electrodes is added
[
108
,
43
]
to make a small magnetic gradient,
B_{2}z^{2}\hat{z}
, for an electron on the trap axis (with
x=0
and
y=0
). This adds

\displaystyle E_{cz}=\hbar\delta\left(n_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

(12)

to the energy eigenvalues, with

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{eB_{2}}{m_{e}^{2}\omega_{z}}~.

(13)

The axial frequency then effectively gains a small dependence on the quantum number
n_{c}
, and this effect is proportional to
\delta
. In effect, whenever the cyclotron state makes a transition from
n_{c}
to
n_{c}+1
, the axial frequency shifts accordingly

\displaystyle\omega_{z}\to\omega_{z}+\delta~.

(14)

This shift is large enough with current experimental methods so that the quantum state of the cyclotron motion can be unambiguously determined within a fraction of a second. This way of detecting the cyclotron energy as a shift of the axial frequency is a quantum nondemolition (QND) measurement, insofar as the addition to the Hamiltonian that produces
E_{cz}
commutes with
H_{0}
. The important consequence is that repeated measurements of the cyclotron energy do not change the cyclotron quantum state
[
43
]
.

The above description holds for small cyclotron quantum number
n_{c}\lesssim 10
. We will address how this is modified when introducing the technique of highly excited states in Section
III
.

The axial frequency is measured by Fourier transforming the oscillating image current generated in the wall of the trap due to the axial motion. By monitoring this axial frequency, the cyclotron quantum number
n_{c}
, and hence the quantum state, is continuously monitored. The “continuous” axial detection has an unavoidable averaging time
t_{\text{ave}}
that is needed to distinguish a cyclotron transition signal from Johnson noise. A measured axial frequency shift,
\Delta\omega_{z}
, could be caused by some combination of a cyclotron energy shift and noise in the detection electronics. For a given averaging time, we identify a
\Delta\omega_{z}
as a cyclotron excitation if
\Delta\omega_{z}
is larger than
\delta
by 5 standard deviations of the signal during this averaging time.

A dilution refrigerator keeps the trap electrodes at a temperature that we will assume is
T=10~\text{mK}\approx 10^{-3}~\text{meV}
so that black body photons do not have enough energy to excite the cyclotron motion, which has an energy threshold of
\hbar\omega_{c}=0.1\text{--}2.3
meV for our search. The theoretical black body excitation rate is

\Gamma_{\text{BB}}=n_{c}\gamma_{c,0}\frac{1}{\exp\left(\frac{\omega_{c}}{T}\right)-1}\approx n_{c}\times 10^{-51}~\text{s}^{-1},

(15)

where
\gamma_{c,0}={4\alpha\omega_{c}^{2}}/{(3m_{e})}\approx
0.3–100 s
-1
is the ground state cyclotron damping rate in the trap. Even for cyclotron states as high as
n_{c}\sim 10^{6}
that we will consider in this paper, the Boltzmann suppression makes the background rate negligible. In the demonstration dark photon measurement
[
45
]
, we observed no cyclotron excitations for 7 days for a temperature of 30 mK
[
45
]
. The measurement was thus background-free for 7 days.

II.2
Simultaneous Dark Photon and Axion Detection

The basic idea of these measurements
[
45
]
is to search for one-quantum cyclotron transitions caused by dark matter with mass
m
that is resonant with the cyclotron frequency
\omega_{c}
insofar as
mc^{2}=\hbar\omega_{c}
. A search for dark matter over the largest mass range is done by sweeping the magnetic field to vary the cyclotron frequency over the largest range. In the demonstration apparatus, the magnetic field could likely be reduced so the minimum frequency and energy for the search range is
\omega_{c}/(2\pi)=25~\rm{GHz}\approx 0.1~meV/(2\pi\hbar)
. The lowest possible limit depends upon the temperature (to keep the measurement free of background), the relative size of the trap and the cyclotron orbit, and keeping the cyclotron frequency well above the axial frequency.
In the demonstration apparatus, a maximum frequency
\omega_{c}/(2\pi)=180~\rm{GHz}\approx 0.75~meV/(2\pi\hbar)
would be accessible.
With a 20 T superconducting solenoid, the maximum could be increased to
\omega_{c}/(2\pi)=560~\rm{GHz}\approx 2.3~meV/(2\pi\hbar)
for the frequency/mass of the dark matter
1
1

1

For convenience, we will adopt natural units where
\hbar=c=1
throughout the remainder of this paper, except in numerical results where we explicitly show the dimensional factors. The conversion between mass and frequency will follow the conventions shown here. In particular, we will give numbers and label plots by mass in meV and frequency in GHz with this
2\pi\hbar
conversion factor between them. So, for example, a 2.3 meV mass corresponds to a 560 GHz frequency.
.

Introducing sensitivity to the axion, in addition to dark photons, requires converting the axion to a photon in a magnetic field away from the Penning trap of the quantum cyclotron. The field converts the axion to a photon polarized along the magnetic field direction. This polarization must be rotated so as to be perpendicular to the magnetic field within the trap in order to produce cyclotron excitation. The separated conversion and detection volumes have two additional advantages. (1) A larger and more efficient conversion “antenna” can be used (Section
IV.3
). (2) The conversion field, and hence the conversion efficiency, can be left fixed at the largest possible value while the field of the one-electron quantum cyclotron is swept over the widest possible range of possible axion masses.

II.3
Cyclotron Transition Rate

The rates at which dark photons and axions produce cyclotron excitations are summarized here, as is the constraint that comes from observing no excitation in a given observation time. The detailed derivation on which this is based is summarized in Appendix
A
.

A dark photon would excite a cyclotron state with quantum number
n_{c}
at a rate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{free}}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}e^{2}\pi(n_{c}+1)}{2m_{e}m_{A^{\prime}}}\frac{\rho_{\text{DM}}}{\Delta\omega}\langle\sin^{2}\theta\rangle~,

(16)

depending upon a frequency width,
\Delta\omega
. This width is approximately the larger of the cyclotron decay of the state via synchrotron radiation,
\Delta\omega_{c}
, and the anticipated width of the dark matter,
\Delta\omega_{A^{\prime}}=10^{-6}m_{A^{\prime}}
.
\langle\sin^{2}\theta\rangle=\frac{2}{3}
is the average dark photon polarization. This equation holds true only in free space; the boundary condition of the surrounding conductor gives rise to a cavity factor
\kappa^{2}
, to be discussed in Section
IV
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}=\kappa^{2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}e^{2}\pi(n_{c}+1)}{2m_{e}m_{A^{\prime}}}\frac{\rho_{\text{DM}}}{\Delta\omega}\langle\sin^{2}\theta\rangle~.

(17)

If no excitation is observed over an observation time of
t_{\text{obs}}
, the inequality

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}<-\frac{1}{\zeta t_{\text{obs}}}\log(1-CL)~.

(18)

expresses a constraint at confidence level
CL=0.9
, where
\zeta
is the detection efficiency.

For the axion, we have an analogous formula,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}=\kappa^{2}\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{2}}{m_{a}^{3}}B_{\text{ext}}^{2}\frac{e^{2}\pi(n_{c}+1)}{2m_{e}}\frac{\rho_{\text{DM}}}{\Delta\omega}~,

(19)

where
B_{\text{ext}}
is the external magnetic field that converts axions to photons. The same inequality pertains to the confidence limit.

III
Electron in a Highly Excited State

Our earlier proposal and demonstration was sensitive to any dark matter that was able to excite the cyclotron ground state of one trapped electron into its first excited state. QND detection revealed no background transitions at all for at least days. With no background, the sensitivity was high enough to realize a 75-times higher dark photon sensitivity at 148 GHz.

The cyclotron excitation rate in eq. (
17

III.1.5
Preparing a High
n_{c}
State

To prepare the highly excited state
\ket{\ncmax}
, we periodically apply a resonant external cyclotron drive to the trapped electron. As the wave function expands, the resulting frequency shift in the cyclotron mode is approximately 0.045 GHz, which remains insignificant compared to the axial bottle broadening of
\Delta\omega_{c}/2\pi=1.21
GHz (see eq. (
53
)). The required driving power is on the order of
10^{-10}
mW, which is far lower than the typical 10 mW output of commercially available sources.

Such a classical driving procedure typically produces coherent states rather than pure cyclotron eigenstates. However, the nearly continuous QND measurements of axial frequency rapidly collapse the coherent state into a definite cyclotron number state,
\ket{\nc}
. Mathematically, a coherent state is represented as a superposition of infinitely many energy eigenstates, parameterized by a complex number
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\ket{\alpha}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{n_{c}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n_{c}}}{\sqrt{n_{c}!}}\ket{n_c}~.

(47)

We employ a resonant drive to generate a coherent state with
|\alpha|^{2}=n_{c,\text{max}}
. The resulting distribution of cyclotron states follows a Poisson distribution with mean value
|\alpha|^{2}=n_{c,\text{max}}

[
55
]
:

\displaystyle|\innerproduct{\nc}{\alpha}|^{2}=\frac{|\alpha|^{2n_{c}}}{n_{c}!}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}~.

(48)

Thus, up to a minor quantum fluctuation, this approach yields the desired state,
\ket{\nc}
, where
n_{c}\approx n_{c,\text{max}}\pm\sqrt{n_{c,\text{max}}}
. Given the known initial value of
\omega_{z}
, along with the corresponding frequency shift
\delta
, we can precisely determine the specific cyclotron number of the system.

Since the observation time per frequency bin,
t_{\text{obs}}
(see next subsection), is significantly longer than a typical cyclotron lifetime,
\tau_{c}
, the cyclotron state will inevitably undergo numerous decays during the course of the experiment. Selection rules permit only the transitions
n_{c}\to n_{c}-1
, which manifest as a frequency shift
\omega_{z}\to\omega_{z}-\delta
. This decay-induced frequency shift is clearly distinguishable from the opposite shift
\omega_{z}\to\omega_{z}+\delta
that would result from a dark matter interaction (see Figure
1(b)
). Therefore, we can continue searching for a dark matter signal uninterrupted, even while observing multiple decays.

To prevent a significant reduction in sensitivity due to cyclotron decay, we periodically reinitialize the state to
\ket{\ncmax}
, a process that takes negligible time. By reapplying the preparation when
n_{c}
decreases to 90% of its initial value, the time interval between successive preparation pulses can be estimated using eq. (
20
) as:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\text{pulse}}=\sum_{n_{c}=0.9n_{c,\text{max}}}^{n_{c,\text{max}}}\tau_{c}(n_{c},\omega_{c})=0.34\text{ s}\left(\frac{24~\text{GHz}}{\omega_{c}/2\pi}\right)^{2}=0.34\text{ s}\left(\frac{0.1\text{ meV}}{m_{A^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}~.

(49)

The shortest required pulse time is approximately a millisecond, which is entirely feasible for implementation in our experimental setup.

III.2
Cyclotron Bandwidth and Scanning

The frequency width appearing in the transition rate formula eq. (
17

is the cyclotron quality factor, which depends on specific properties of the experimental apparatus.

It is crucial to scan the cyclotron frequency across bins of width

results in a quadratic loss of sensitivity in
D

[
23
]

As we elaborate below, the value of

does not impact the reach of our experiment, provided that the experiment remains background-free. This behavior also contrasts with that of a thermal-noise-limited experiment
[
23

When

17

III.3
More Electrons?

If we trap
n_{e}
electrons, the cyclotron transition rate scales linearly:
\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}\to n_{e}\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}
. Intuitively, this would suggest an improvement in sensitivity. This reasoning holds true in the low cyclotron number regime, and in our previous work
[
45
]
, a configuration with
n_{e}=10
electrons was proposed. However, as we demonstrate below, in the optimized regime explored here, trapping more than a single electron ceases to provide a sensitivity advantage.

We experimentally found that only the average axial image current of all
n_{e}
electrons can be measured, and the signal shift,
\delta
, scales as
1/n_{e}
. If a cyclotron transition occurs for one of the electrons, it becomes impossible to identify which specific electron underwent the transition. This introduces the risk that the signal could be canceled by a subsequent decay of another electron before a detection can be made. As a result, the effective cyclotron lifetime is reduced by a factor of
n_{e}
,
\tau_{c,\text{eff}}=\tau_{c}/n_{e}
.
This reduction also lowers the optimal cyclotron number by a factor of
n_{e}
(eq. (
42
)).
Since the transition rate is proportional to
n_{c}n_{e}
, these two effects cancel out, eliminating any net gain in sensitivity from trapping multiple electrons.

However, during the R&D phase of the experiment, achieving the optimal parameters may not be feasible in the initial iterations. Under these conditions, trapping multiple electrons could temporarily enhance sensitivity. Nevertheless, it is evident that increasing the number of trapped electrons does not contribute to the ultimate sensitivity in the fully optimized regime.

IV
Electron in a Cavity

The cyclotron transition rate, as given in eq. (
16
), only holds when the electron trap is in free space. In reality, the electron is surrounded by metallic conducting surfaces, which ground any electric fields parallel to their surfaces. This presence of conductors can significantly modify the expected transition rate.
Counterintuitively, an appropriately chosen geometry of conducting shield can even enhance our sensitivity to dark matter due to a “focusing"-like phenomenon. We begin by providing an intuitive physical explanation of this effect before proceeding with a detailed mathematical derivation.

The concept of using dish antennas to focus dark-photon- and axion-induced electromagnetic radiation was first introduced in Ref.
[
67
]
. The core idea is as follows: as discussed below, a metallic surface exposed to dark photon or axion dark matter generates Standard Model electromagnetic radiation normal to the surface. In the ray optics approximation, a spherical surface would emit rays that converge at the center, thereby concentrating all the radiated power at that focal point. This effect arises under the assumption that the Standard Model radiation is purely outgoing from the conductor’s surface. Such an assumption is valid when a good absorber is positioned at the center of the spherical surface.

In contrast, our setup involves a single electron located at the center of the cavity, which absorbs only a negligible fraction of the available energy. Therefore, both outgoing and incoming modes (or equivalently, standing wave modes) must be considered to accurately describe the focusing effect in an absorber-less cavity, as opposed to purely outgoing modes. We begin by providing an intuitive explanation based on ray optics in Section
IV.1
. This is followed by a rigorous derivation of the radiation pattern inside the cavity, without relying on the ray optics approximation, in Section
IV.2
, demonstrating that the focusing effect still persists.

IV.1
Ray Optics

We start by estimating the focusing effects with the ray optics intuition here and provide the rigorous justification in the next subsection. Consider an infinite conducting plate immersed in a dark photon dark matter (DPDM) background (everything below is the same for the axion as long as there is an external magnetic field). As long as the electric field sourced by dark photon has a parallel component

IV.1.2
Limit on Focuser Size

We cannot arbitrarily increase the size of the cavity to indefinitely enhance focusing. When the radius of the cavity
R
gets larger than the de Broglie wavelength (i.e. coherence length) of dark photon,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{coherence}}=\lambda_{\text{dB}}=\frac{2\pi}{m_{A^{\prime}}v}=12~\text{m}\left(\frac{0.1\text{ meV}}{m_{A^{\prime}}}\right)=12~\text{m}\left(\frac{24\text{ GHz}}{\omega_{c}/2\pi}\right)~,

(66)

the hitherto-ignored phase

in eq. (

[

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{max,sph}}^{2}\sim\left(\frac{2\pi}{v}\right)^{2}\approx 4\times 10^{7}~,

(67)

IV.2
Wave Optics

In this section, we derive the enhancement factor
\kappa
using first principles from wave optics, focusing on cylindrical and spherical trap cavities as dark photon converters. As mentioned, since the axion is a scalar with no intrinsic polarization, the electric field produced by its conversion aligns entirely with the direction of the applied magnetic field, rendering it undetectable by our cyclotron sensor, which is sensitive only to fields in the plane perpendicular to this direction. We will address the resolution to this problem using a more complicated focuser geometry in the next subsection.

The key quantity of interest is the observed electric field
7
7

7

The observed field is defined as the sum of the Standard Model electric field and the

\mathbf{E}^{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{E}+\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{\prime}

E^{\text{obs}}_{\text{cavity},x}(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\left|\frac{E^{\text{obs}}_{\text{cavity},x}(\mathbf{0})}{E^{\text{obs}}_{\text{free},x}}\right|^{2}~.

(68)

We begin by solving Maxwell’s equations for the electromagnetic fields inside the cavity. The dark photon induces an effective current

22

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\text{eff}}=-\epsilon m_{A^{\prime}}^{2}A^{\prime}\mathbf{\hat{A}^{\prime}}~,

(69)

[

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{-i\omega}{\omega_{n}^{2}-\omega^{2}}\frac{\int d^{3}x\mathbf{E}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\text{eff}}}{\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E_{n}(\mathbf{x})}|^{2}}~.

(71)

Here, the driving frequency is the dark photon mass

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n}\frac{c_{n}}{i\omega\epsilon A^{\prime}_{x}}E_{n,x}(\mathbf{0})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n}\frac{-i\omega}{\omega_{n}^{2}-\omega^{2}}\frac{\int d^{3}x\mathbf{E}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\left[-\epsilon\omega^{2}(A^{\prime}_{x}\hat{x}+A^{\prime}_{z}\hat{z})\right]}{\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E_{n}(\mathbf{x})}|^{2}}\frac{1}{i\omega\epsilon A^{\prime}_{x}}E_{n,x}(\mathbf{0})\right|^{2}~,

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}(1-\frac{2i}{Q_{n}})-\omega^{2}}\underbrace{\frac{\int d^{3}x\mathbf{E}_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\hat{x}}{\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E_{n}(\mathbf{x})}|^{2}}\mathbf{E}_{n}(\mathbf{0})\cdot\hat{x}}_{\equiv G_{n}}\right|^{2}~.

Roughly speaking, the factor

8

8

The addition of an imaginary part to the resonant frequencies

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}=\kappa^{2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}e^{2}\pi\sin^{2}\theta(n_{c}+1)}{2m_{e}m_{A^{\prime}}}\frac{\rho_{\text{DM}}}{\Delta\omega}~,

\displaystyle G_{p}^{\text{sphere}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{8}{3}j_{1}(u^{\prime}_{1p})\left[\frac{u^{\prime 5}_{1p}}{2+2u^{\prime 2}_{1p}-2u^{\prime 4}_{1p}+2(-1+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\cos(2 \up)+u^{\prime}_{1p}(-4+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\sin(2 \up)}\right]~.

IV.3
Open Trap with Large Conversion Cavity

Having established the validity of the geometric ray optics argument, we now apply it to our experiment. In our earlier work
[
45
]

The limitation arises because the trap must fit inside the magnetic bottle ring, which requires a small size to generate a large signal (see Section

). This size constraint conflicts with the need for a larger focusing area. Furthermore, this design is insensitive to axions since the signal photon has an axial polarization. Additionally, using the same magnetic field for both trapping the electron and axion conversion reduces sensitivity at lower frequencies due to the need for scanning the
B_{0}

We solve these issues by decoupling the trapping apparatus (radius

[
51
]

The signal can originate from a separate axion conversion region and be directed into the trap via a waveguide, effectively separating the detection and conversion regions.

For efficient axion conversion and focusing, we adopt a large cylindrical cavity design similar to that proposed by BREAD
[
85
,
79
]

R=1

The BREAD collaboration plans to use kinetic inductance detectors (KID) and transition-edge sensors (TES) as photosensors. In contrast, we propose using the electron Penning trap, which resolves the polarization issue by designing the waveguide to produce an electric field in the
xy

VI
Conclusion

We have introduced a novel method for detecting QCD axion and dark photon dark matter utilizing highly excited cyclotron states of a trapped electron, significantly improving our previous work
[
45
]
. By optimizing key experimental parameters, we have reduced the averaging time required to detect a cyclotron excitation to
t_{\text{ave}}=2.9\times 10^{-6}~\text{s}
, allowing us to observe the excitation of a cyclotron state with
n_{c}=1.2\times 10^{6}\left(\frac{0.1~\text{meV}}{m_{A^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}
before its decay. Since the transition probability scales with the initial quantum number
n_{c}
, this greatly enhances its sensitivity.

We propose an open-endcap Penning trap design that facilitates the coupling of external photon signals into the trap, leveraging the large focusing power and strong magnetic field of a large conversion cavity (e.g. the BREAD experiment
[
85
]
). Additionally, we propose filling the cavity with dielectric layers of alternating refractive indices to increase the conversion volume. This method is compatible with scanning by periodically switching the dielectric layers, conservatively assumed to be once a month.

These strategies, depicted schematically in Figure
1(a)
, enable the exploration of the QCD axion parameter space from 0.1 meV to 2.3 meV in a 1000-day-per-decade scan (Figure
2
), covering a significant portion of the predicted post-inflationary QCD axion mass range
m_{a}\in(0.04~\text{meV},0.18~\text{meV})

[
19
]
. The experiment also probes the kinetic mixing parameter of the dark photon down to
\epsilon\approx 2\times 10^{-16}
(Figure
3
).

To achieve the required averaging time, we need to lower the cyclotron quality factor to
Q_{c}=\frac{\omega_{c}}{\Delta\omega_{c}}=20\left(\frac{m_{A^{\prime}}}{0.1~\text{meV}}\right)
, which is much broader than the dark matter quality factor
Q_{\text{DM}}=10^{6}
. However, this does not diminish sensitivity because it allows for a longer observation time per frequency bin,
t_{\text{obs}}\approx 5.5
days. We have also shown that when the averaging time is minimized, trapping more than one electron does not improve sensitivity. Our design is advantageous because it is background-free, as demonstrated in Ref.
[
45
]
, and B-field compatible, allowing it to operate conveniently above the BREAD magnet.

Moreover, this paper has contributed to the literature by rigorously deriving the focusing effect and justifying the ray-optics approximation in the case of cylindrical and spherical cavity geometries without a perfect absorber. We have also worked out the consequence of a fixed dark photon polarization scenario for our experiment (Appendix
F
), which is representative of a class of experiments not addressed by Ref.
[
21
]
: a directionally sensitive quantum sensor (with discrete signals).

Our sensitivity remains well below the ultimate backreaction limit common to all dish-type experiments (Appendix
B
), so there is significant room for improvement. For instance, further lowering the averaging time could allow for an even higher cyclotron state
n_{c}
. This work thus opens up a “rapid measurement frontier" in the search for axions. In conclusion, this work provides a clear path to searching the well-motivated yet challenging meV QCD axion parameter space.

A.2
Rabi’s Formula and Selection Rule

Next, consider the cyclotron transition with the initial and final states,
\ket{i}=\ket{i_c, i_m, i_z}
and
\ket{f}=\ket{f_c, i_m, i_z}

\omega_{fi}=\omega_{c}|\Delta_{c}|
, where
\Delta_{c}\equiv f_{c}-i_{c}

m_{A^{\prime}}\approx\omega_{fi}

D\equiv m_{A^{\prime}}-\omega_{fi}
is small compared to
m_{A^{\prime}}

[

\mathscr{P}_{if}(t)=\frac{|W_{fi}|^{2}}{|W_{fi}|^{2}+D^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\sqrt{|W_{fi}|^{2}+D^{2}}\frac{t}{2}\right]~,

(116)

is the transition matrix element, with

defined in eq. (
115

It remains to compute the matrix element

\displaystyle W_{fi}=\frac{-\epsilon eA^{\prime}\sin\theta}{m_{e}}\bra{f}\left(p_{x}+e\frac{B_{0}}{2}y\right)\ket{i}~.

(117)

Writing

and

\displaystyle p_{x}+e\frac{B_{0}}{2}y\to i\sqrt{\frac{m_{e}\omega_{c}}{2}}(a^{{\dagger}}_{c}-a_{c})~,

(118)

the matrix element evaluates to

\displaystyle|W_{fi}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\epsilon eA^{\prime}\sin\theta\right)^{2}\frac{\omega_{c}}{2m_{e}}\left[(i_{c}+1)\delta_{f_{c},i_{c}+1}-i_{c}\delta_{f_{c},i_{c}-1}\right]~.

(119)

We see that to leading order in
\epsilon

|\Delta_{c}|=1
and
\omega_{fi}=\omega_{c}
. This implies we only need to consider one jump at a time. Since de-excitation is indistinguishable from radiative decay, we henceforth only consider the
n_{c}\to n_{c}+1
transition. We will also use the symbol

instead of

A.3
Memory-Loss Model of Frequency Width

The Rabi’s formula, eq. (
116

given by eq. (

\Delta\omega_{A^{\prime}}=10^{-6}m_{A^{\prime}}

\Delta\omega_{c}
. In particular, the monochromatic approximation only holds until a time at which the frequency width leads to a phase shift of
2\pi

t_{\text{coherence}}
. Since we are only sensitive to the overlap of the two sinusoids (the cyclotron and the dark photon), the coherence time is set by the larger of the two widths i.e. the smaller of the two independent coherence times:

\displaystyle t_{\text{coherence}}=\frac{2\pi}{\max\left(\Delta\omega_{A^{\prime}},\Delta\omega_{c}\right)}\equiv\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega}~.

(120)

We will model this frequency width by using Rabi’s formula only from
t=0
to
t=t_{\text{coherence}}
. If the averaging time, the duration over which measurements are averaged, is longer than the coherence time, we assume the sinusoid loses its memory of the phase and starts over discontinuously, but the probabilities add up between successive periods.

For averaging time longer than a coherence time, we can write it as

t_{\text{ave}}\approx N\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega}

(121)

where

N

(122)

The cyclotron transition rate is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{free}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{P}_{if}(t)}{dt}

(123)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{P}_{if}}{dN}\frac{dN}{dt}

(124)

\displaystyle=\frac{|W_{fi}|^{2}}{|W_{fi}|^{2}+D^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\sqrt{|W_{fi}|^{2}+D^{2}}\frac{\pi}{\Delta\omega}\right]\frac{\Delta\omega}{2\pi}~,

(125)

where the subscript emphasizes that this formula only applies in free space (i.e. ignoring the cavity).

A.5
Transition Probability

Write the cyclotron transition rate as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c,\text{cavity}}=\Gamma_{0}\sin^{2}\theta(t)~,

(137)

where we have isolated the time-dependent polarization angle,
\theta(t)
, which is rapidly oscillating with a dark photon coherence time
\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega_{A^{\prime}}}\sim 10^{-5}
s.

Next, let the transition probability from the cyclotron initial state to the excited state at time
t
be
P_{1}(t)
. With a decay rate
\gamma_{c}
, the probability satisfies the differential equation

\frac{dP_{1}}{dt}=\Gamma_{0}\sin^{2}\theta(t)-\gamma_{c}P_{1}~,

(138)

until the next averaging time when it is interrupted by measurement.

Since this polarization is random and unknown, and it is independent of the system, we can take its average value:

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta(t)\to\langle\sin^{2}\theta(t)\rangle~.

(139)

To find this average, first note that the area element on a unit sphere is proportional to
d(\cos\theta)
. For
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
to be pointing in a random direction in the unit sphere,
\cos\theta
must have a uniform probability density
P(\cos\theta)=\frac{1}{2}
. Then we can take the expected value under this distribution:

\displaystyle\langle\sin^{2}\theta\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta)\sin^{2}\theta P(\cos\theta)=\frac{2}{3}~.

(140)

Imposing the initial condition
P_{1}(0)=0
, the solution to the differential equation is

P_{1}(t)=\frac{(2/3)\Gamma_{0}}{\gamma_{c}}\left(1-\exp(-\gc t)\right)~.

(141)

This formula only applies within one averaging time, after which the probability gets reset to
P_{1}=0
. The observation time
t_{\text{obs}}
available at a given frequency bin satisfies
t_{\text{obs}}\gg t_{\text{ave}}
(see Table
1
). And since the measurement time
t_{\text{ave}}
must be shorter than the lifetime of the excited state for the detection to be possible, we take
\gamma_{c}t_{\text{ave}}\ll 1
. Then, the probability of detecting no transition in an observation time
t_{\text{obs}}
is given by the Poisson formula

\displaystyle P_{0}(t_{\text{obs}})

\displaystyle=(1-P_{1}(t_{\text{ave}}))^{t_{\text{obs}}/t_{\text{ave}}}

(142)

\displaystyle\approx\exp(-\Gammacavity\tobs)~.

(143)

Since it takes an averaging time
t_{\text{ave}}
to resolve a signal, and a detection threshold of
\Delta\omega_{z}>5\sigma
is chosen, an excitation that decays in a time less than
\frac{5\sigma}{\delta}t_{\text{ave}}
will not be detected, resulting in a detection efficiency
[

Appendix B
Backreaction: Fundamental Limit to Dish-Type Experiments

In this appendix, we point out that all dish-type experiments for axion or dark photon dark matter search, including our electron-trap proposal as well as other photosensors considered in BREAD

For dark photon dark matter, the focused signal-photon energy density is
\rho_{\text{signal}}=\kappa^{2}\epsilon^{2}\rho_{\text{DM}}

\lambda_{A^{\prime}}=\frac{2\pi}{m_{A^{\prime}}}
since the dark photon is converted to a photon

11

11

\sim 10^{-10}

\sim 10^{-6}
m).

is the ratio of the cavity area to an area of size the photon wavelength,

t_{\text{obs}}\approx 5.5

Putting these together, the backreaction limit for dark photon is

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\sim 10^{-18}\left(\frac{40}{N_{\text{switch}}}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1000~\text{days}}{t_{\text{total}}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{17.7~\text{meter}^{2}}{A_{\text{cavity}}}\right)^{1/2}~,

The calculation for axion is identical as long as we make the substitution

\displaystyle\epsilon\to\frac{g_{a\gamma\gamma}B_{\text{ext}}}{m_{a}}~.

(155)

The backreaction limit for axion is therefore

\displaystyle\approx\frac{m_{a}}{B_{\text{ext}}}\left(\frac{8\pi m_{a}}{t_{\text{obs}}\rho_{\text{DM}}A_{\text{cavity}}N_{l}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

Appendix C
Details of Achieving Large Cyclotron Number

This appendix collects various supporting arguments for how to achieve a large cyclotron number
n_{c}
.

C.1
Signal Formation Time

When the cyclotron state makes a transition
\ket{\nc}\to\ket{\nc+ 1}
, the axial frequency shifts
\omega_{z}\to\omega_{z}+\delta
immediately. However, there exists a minimum time
t_{\text{signal}}
for this change to be reflected in the signal. We will show that
t_{\text{signal}}=\frac{1}{\delta}
.

The electron’s classical axial motion
z(t)
is essentially a driven, damped harmonic oscillator. With a driving term
F_{0}\cos{\omega_d t}
of drive frequency
\omega_{d}
, the equation of motion for the electron axial mode can be written in the form

\displaystyle\ddot{z}+\gamma_{z}\dot{z}+\omega_{0}^{2}z=\frac{F_{0}}{m_{e}}\cos{\omega_d t},

(158)

where the initial natural frequency of the axial motion is the axial frequency
\omega_{0}=\omega_{z}
, and the drive frequency is chosen to match it
\omega_{d}=\omega_{0}
. In practice, the drive can be taken to have zero width.
\gamma_{z}
is the axial damping rate. We will consider the limit
\omega_{z}\gg\delta\gg\gamma_{z}
. To get a signal, we monitor the electron’s axial current, which is proportional to the rate of change
\dot{z}
of the electron’s axial position
z

[
17
]
.

Suppose a cyclotron transition occurs at some time
t=0
. We get the current signal:

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t<0)=\frac{F_{0}}{m_{e}}\omega_{d}(S_{ip}^{0}\cos{(\wdr t+\alpha)}-S_{Q}^{0}\sin{(\wdr t+ \alpha)})

(159)

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t>0)=\frac{F_{0}}{m_{e}}\omega_{d}(S_{ip}^{\prime}\cos{(\wdr t+\alpha)}-S_{Q}^{\prime}\sin{(\wdr t+ \alpha)})

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{\gamma_{z}}{2}t}(-A\sin{(\wdr+\delta) t}+B\cos{(\wdr+\delta) t}),

(160)

where
\alpha
is an unknown phase,
S_{ip}^{0}
and
S_{Q}^{0}
are the initial in-phase and quadrature amplitudes, and
S^{\prime}_{ip}
and
S^{\prime}_{Q}
are the corresponding amplitudes after the cyclotron jump, and
A
and
B
are some
\mathcal{O}(1)
constants determined by initial conditions
[
104
]
.

If we were directly measuring for changes in
S_{ip}
or
S_{Q}
, it takes a time
t\sim\frac{1}{\gamma_{z}}
for the transients to get damped. However, we can integrate the signal against another known sinusoid with the same phase
\sin{(\wdr t+ \alpha)}
over several periods of
\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{d}}
. After some uses of trig identities, it can be shown that the only surviving term is proportional to
\sin{(\delta t)}
. This means a frequency-shift signal can be measured after a time
t_{\text{signal}}\sim\frac{1}{\delta}
. In other words, since our signal is a small frequency shift, we can measure the beat at the corresponding time scale, which is much faster than the naive damping time.

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}(2\pi)\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)\frac{1}{u^{\prime}_{1p}/R}\int_{0}^{R}dr\left[\hat{J_{1}}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)+\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\hat{J_{1}}^{\prime}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right]~.

(188)

Integrating by parts, the radial integral can be evaluated trivially

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\mathbf{E}^{\text{TM}*}_{01p}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{z}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\omega}(2\pi)\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)\frac{1}{u^{\prime}_{1p}/R}\int_{0}^{R}dr\frac{d}{dr}\left[r\hat{J_{1}}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right]

(189)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}(2\pi)\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)R^{2}j_{1}(u^{\prime}_{1p})~.

(190)

Using the orthogonality of the Legendre and associated Legendre polynomials again, the denominator can be written as:

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E}^{\text{TM}}_{01p}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}

(191)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\left[|E^{\text{TM}}_{r,01p}|^{2}+|E^{\text{TM}}_{\theta,01p}|^{2}\right]

(192)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}(2\pi)\int_{0}^{R}dr~r^{2}\left[\left(\frac{2}{k^{\text{TM}}_{01p}r^{2}}\hat{J_{1}}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right)^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta)\left(P_{1}(\cos\theta)\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{r}\hat{J_{1}}^{\prime}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right)^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta)\left(P^{1}_{1}(\cos\theta)\right)^{2}\right]

(193)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}(2\pi)\left[\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\frac{4}{(u^{\prime}_{1p}/R)^{2}}\int_{0}^{R}dr\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(\hat{J_{1}}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)\int_{0}^{R}dr\left(\hat{J_{1}}^{\prime}\left(u^{\prime}_{1p}\frac{r}{R}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

(194)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}(2\pi)\left(\frac{R}{u^{\prime}_{1p}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)I(u^{\prime}_{1p})~,

(195)

where

\displaystyle I(u^{\prime}_{1p})=2\int_{0}^{u^{\prime}_{1p}}dx\left(j_{1}(x)\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{u^{\prime}_{1p}}dx\left(\hat{J_{1}}^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}~,

(196)

with the change of variables
x=u^{\prime}_{1p}r/R
. Using the analytic form of the spherical Bessel function

j_{1}(x)=-\frac{\cos(x)}{x}+\frac{\sin(x)}{x^{2}}~,

(197)

the integral can be easily evaluated as

\displaystyle I(u^{\prime}_{1p})

\displaystyle=\frac{2+2u^{\prime 2}_{1p}-2u^{\prime 4}_{1p}+2(-1+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\cos(2 \up)+u^{\prime}_{1p}(-4+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\sin(2 \up)}{-4u^{\prime 3}_{1p}}~.

(198)

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle G_{p}^{\text{sphere}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}j_{1}(u^{\prime}_{1p})\left[\frac{u^{\prime 2}_{1p}}{I(u^{\prime}_{1p})}\right]

(199)

\displaystyle=-\frac{8}{3}j_{1}(u^{\prime}_{1p})\left[\frac{u^{\prime 5}_{1p}}{2+2u^{\prime 2}_{1p}-2u^{\prime 4}_{1p}+2(-1+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\cos(2 \up)+u^{\prime}_{1p}(-4+u^{\prime 2}_{1p})\sin(2 \up)}\right]~,

(200)

and, using
\omega_{p}=k^{\text{TM}}_{01p}=u^{\prime}_{1p}/R
, we finally arrive at the cavity factor for the sphere

\kappa^{2}_{\text{sphere}}(\omega)=\left|\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega R)^{2}}{u^{\prime 2}_{1p}-(\omega R)^{2}}G^{\text{sphere}}_{p}\right|^{2}~.

(201)

D.2
Cylindrical Cavity

We will calculate
\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}
for a cylindrical cavity of radius
R
and height
d
. We orient the cylinder’s central axis to be parallel to the magnetic field direction
\hat{z}
, as was the case in the proof-of-principle experiment
[

\hat{x}
direction is taken to be the component of the dark photon field in the plane perpendicular to the magnetic field. We will use the cylindrical coordinates
(\rho,\phi,z)

\mathbf{0^{\prime}}\equiv\left(0,0,\frac{d}{2}\right)

Then we can start with the general equation eq. (

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{l}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2}}\frac{\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\mathbf{E}_{l}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}}{\int d^{3}x^{\prime}|\mathbf{E_{l}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}|^{2}}\mathbf{E}_{l}(\mathbf{0^{\prime}})\cdot\hat{x}\right|^{2}

(202)

\displaystyle\equiv\left|\sum_{l}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{l}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~,

(203)

where
\omega=m_{A^{\prime}}
is the driving frequency,
l
is a generic index running over all cavity modes, and we have omitted the quality factors. In cylindrical coordinates,

\displaystyle\hat{x}=\cos(\phi)\hat{\rho}-\sin(\phi)\hat{\phi}~.

(204)

The empty cavity modes of a cylindrical cavity can be divided into TE and TM modes (transverse electric and magnetic, respectively) with respect to the
z
-direction. There are three discrete indices
n=0,1,2,\dots
;
p=1,2,3,\dots
; and
q=0,1,2,\dots
, corresponding to the quantization of momentum in the
\phi
,
\rho
, and
z

[
63
]
:

\displaystyle E_{z,npq}^{\text{TM}}

\displaystyle=J_{n}\left(\frac{x_{np}}{R}\rho\right)\begin{Bmatrix}\sin(n\phi)\\
\cos(n\phi)\end{Bmatrix}\cos\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}z\right)

(205)

\displaystyle E_{\rho,npq}^{\text{TM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{\frac{x_{np}}{R}}\frac{q\pi}{d}J_{n}^{\prime}\left(\frac{x_{np}}{R}\rho\right)\begin{Bmatrix}\sin(n\phi)\\
\cos(n\phi)\end{Bmatrix}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d}z)

(206)

\displaystyle E_{\phi,npq}^{\text{TM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{(\frac{x_{np}}{R})^{2}}\frac{nq\pi}{d}\frac{J_{n}(\frac{x_{np}}{R}\rho)}{\rho}\begin{Bmatrix}\cos(n\phi)\\
-\sin(n\phi)\end{Bmatrix}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d}z)

(207)

\displaystyle E_{z,npq}^{\text{TE}}

\displaystyle=0

(208)

\displaystyle E_{\rho,npq}^{\text{TE}}

\displaystyle=i\frac{1}{(\frac{x_{np}^{\prime}}{R})^{2}}n\frac{J_{n}(\frac{x_{np}^{\prime}}{R}\rho)}{\rho}\begin{Bmatrix}\cos(n\phi)\\
-\sin(n\phi)\end{Bmatrix}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d}z)

(209)

\displaystyle E_{\phi,npq}^{\text{TE}}

\displaystyle=-i\frac{1}{\frac{x_{np}^{\prime}}{R}}J^{\prime}_{n}\left(\frac{x_{np}^{\prime}}{R}\rho\right)\begin{Bmatrix}\sin(n\phi)\\
\cos(n\phi)\end{Bmatrix}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d}z)~,

(210)

where
J_{n}
is the n-th Bessel function,
x_{np}
is the
p
-th zero of
J_{n}(x)
, and
x^{\prime}_{np}
is the
p
-th zero of the derivative
J^{\prime}_{n}(x)
. As we shall see, only the
\text{TE}_{1pq}
modes with odd

\kappa^{2}
. The resonant frequencies are

\displaystyle(\omega^{\text{TM}}_{npq})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x_{np}}{R}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}\right)^{2}

(211)

\displaystyle(\omega^{\text{TE}}_{npq})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x^{\prime}_{np}}{R}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}\right)^{2}~.

(212)

D.2.1
Only

In the numerator of eq. (
202
), we have

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\mathbf{E}_{l}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}~\propto\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\left(E_{\rho,npq}^{\text{TM}}\cos\phi-E_{\phi,npq}^{\text{TM}}\sin\phi\right)~.

(213)

Unless we choose the matching sinusoidal function with
n=1

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\sin\phi\sin(n\phi)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\cos\phi\cos(n\phi)=\pi\delta_{n1}~.

(214)

So only
n=1
modes contribute to
\kappa^{2}
.

D.2.2
Only TE Modes

Consider a term involving a TM
n=1
mode. The integral in the numerator of eq. (
202
) is proportional to

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\mathbf{E}_{l}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}~\propto

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}d\rho\rho\left[J^{\prime}_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\rho\right)+\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\right)^{-1}\frac{J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\rho\right)}{\rho}\right]

(215)

\displaystyle~\propto

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}d\rho\left[\rho\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\right)J^{\prime}_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\rho\right)+J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\rho\right)\right]

(216)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle~\left.\rho J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}}{R}\rho\right)\right|_{0}^{R}

(217)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0~,

(218)

where we used integration by parts in the third line, and in the last step, we used the property of the Bessel function
J_{1}(0)=0
and
J_{1}(x_{1p})=0
, by definition. Hence, the TM modes do not contribute. We will henceforth only use TE modes and drop the superscript in the resonant frequencies.

D.2.3
Final Expression of
\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}

We now compute

G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}=\frac{\int d^{3}x\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}*}_{1pq}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}}{\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}}_{1pq}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}}\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}}_{1pq}(\mathbf{0^{\prime}})\cdot\hat{x}~.

(219)

We first evaluate
\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}}_{1pq}(\mathbf{0}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}
, the electric field at the center of the cavity. Approaching the origin from the
\phi=0
direction, the
\phi
term vanishes, and the radial direction is equivalent to the
x
direction,
\hat{\rho}=\hat{x}
. So, the only relevant component is
E^{\text{TM}}_{\rho,1pq}
, given by eq. (
209
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}}_{1pq}(\mathbf{0^{\prime}})\cdot\hat{x}

\displaystyle=i\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-2}\lim_{\rho\to 0}\frac{J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\rho\right)}{\rho}\lim_{\phi\to 0}\cos(\phi)\lim_{z\to d/2}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d}z)

(220)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-1}\sin(\frac{q\pi}{2})~.

(221)

We see that only modes with odd
q
contribute.

The integral in the numerator is given by

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}*}_{1pq}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\cdot\hat{x}

\displaystyle=-i\pi\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-2}\int_{0}^{R}d\rho~\rho\left[\frac{J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\rho\right)}{\rho}+\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)J^{\prime}_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\rho\right)\right]\int_{0}^{d}dz~\sin(\frac{q\pi}{d} z)

(222)

\displaystyle=-i\pi\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-2}\left[\rho J_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\rho\right)\right]_{0}^{R}\frac{2d}{q\pi}

(223)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-2}\left(R\frac{2d}{q}\right)J_{1}\left(x_{1p}^{\prime}\right)~,

(224)

where we integrated parts in the second step. A similar calculation shows that the denominator is

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x|\mathbf{E}^{\text{TE}}_{1pq}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}=\left(\frac{x_{1p}^{\prime}}{R}\right)^{-4}\frac{1}{2}\left[x^{\prime 2}_{1p}J_{0}^{2}(x_{1p}^{\prime})+({x^{\prime 2}_{1p}}-2)J_{1}^{2}(x_{1p}^{\prime})\right](\pi)\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)~.

(225)

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4x_{1p}^{\prime}J_{1}\left(x_{1p}^{\prime}\right)}{\left[x^{\prime 2}_{1p}J_{0}^{2}(x_{1p}^{\prime})+({x^{\prime 2}_{1p}}-2)J_{1}^{2}(x_{1p}^{\prime})\right](q\pi)}

(226)

\displaystyle=\frac{4x^{\prime}_{1p}}{(x^{\prime 2}_{1p}-1)J_{1}(x_{1p}^{\prime})}\frac{1}{q\pi}\qquad(q\text{ odd})~,

(227)

where, in the second line, we used the identity
x^{\prime}_{1p}J_{0}(x^{\prime}_{1p})=J_{1}(x^{\prime}_{1p})
, which can be easily derived from differentiating the relation between the Bessel functions
\int dx~xJ_{0}(x)=xJ_{1}(x)
. The cavity factor is then

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}=\left|\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\text{ odd}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left(\frac{x^{\prime}_{1p}}{R}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}\right)^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~.

(228)

Appendix E
Linear Focusing of a Cylindrical Cavity

As derived in Appendix
D.2
, the cavity factor for a cylinder of radius
R
and height
d
is given by a sum over two discrete indices,
p
and odd integers
q
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\text{ odd}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{1pq}^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}

(229)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\text{ odd}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\left(\frac{x^{\prime}_{1p}}{R}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}\right)^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~,

(230)

where

\displaystyle G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}=\frac{4x^{\prime}_{1p}}{(x^{\prime 2}_{1p}-1)J_{1}(x_{1p}^{\prime})}\frac{1}{q\pi}\qquad(q\text{ odd})~,

(231)

J_{n}
is the n-th Bessel function, and
x^{\prime}_{np}
is the
p
-th zero of the derivative
J^{\prime}_{n}(x)
.

Now, let
\omega_{N}
be the
N
-th smallest resonant frequency among all
\omega_{1pq}
, i.e. we re-index the frequencies using a single number
N
. For a generic
\omega
, the sum is always dominated by the closest resonant mode
\omega_{N_{*}}
among all the
\omega_{N}
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\left|\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{N_{*}}^{2}-\omega^{2}}G_{N_{*}}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}

(232)

\displaystyle\approx\left|\frac{\omega^{2}}{2\omega(\omega_{N_{*}}-\omega)}G_{N_{*}}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~.

(233)

To understand the term
1/(\omega_{N_{*}}-\omega)
, consider the local density of resonance near a particular
\omega
. Note that, unlike the case of the sphere, which has one index, the density is not constant for the cylinder due to having two indices
p
and
q
. If the local density gets larger, the typical distance between
\omega
and
\omega_{N_{*}}
gets smaller. This quantity is of order the distance between neighbouring resonant frequencies:

\displaystyle\omega_{N_{*}}-\omega\lesssim\omega_{N_{*}}-\omega_{N_{*}-1}~.

(234)

This, in turn, is of order the “inverse local density of resonance":

\displaystyle\omega_{N_{*}}-\omega_{N_{*}-1}\sim\frac{\delta\omega}{\delta N}~,

(235)

where there are
\delta N
resonant frequencies between
\omega
and
\omega+\delta\omega
.

Next, we argue that
\delta N
is proportional to
\omega\hskip 1.99997pt\delta\omega
for a two-index family of resonant frequencies. First, consider the following relation between the frequencies:

\displaystyle\omega_{N}^{2}=\omega_{1pq}^{2}=\left(\frac{x^{\prime}_{1p}}{R}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q\pi}{d}\right)^{2}\equiv\omega_{p}^{2}+\omega_{q}^{2}~.

(236)

Notice that this describes a circle of radius
\omega_{N}
in
\omega_{p}\omega_{q}
-space.
N
, the number of resonant frequencies less than
\omega_{N}
, is proportional to (one quarter of) the area of the circle. If we increase the radius of the circle by
\delta\omega
, the additional ring has an area
2\pi\omega_{N}\delta\omega
, which is proportional to
\delta N
, as shown in Figure
7
a. So we have the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta N}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\omega_{N}\delta\omega}{\pi\omega_{N}^{2}}

(237)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\omega}{\delta N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{N}}{2N}~.

(238)

Substituting into eq. (
235
) and eq. (
233
), we get

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\left|NG_{N_{*}}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~,

(239)

justifying the aforementioned name.

To find the value of
N
, we need to go to
pq
-space. By eq. (
236
), we have an ellipse in
pq
-space, with semi-major and semi-minor axes
\omega_{N}R/\pi
and
\omega_{N}d/\pi
:

\displaystyle 1=\left(\frac{p}{\omega_{N}R/\pi}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q}{\omega_{N}d/\pi}\right)^{2}~,

(240)

where we used
x^{\prime}_{1p}\approx\pi p
for large
p
.

Now, consider the integer lattice of
pq
-space. Every point in this lattice with positive
p
and positive odd
q
corresponds to a unique resonant frequency, as shown in Fig.
7
(b). So, the area of the ellipse is
8N
:

\displaystyle 8N=\pi\left(\frac{\omega_{N}R}{\pi}\right)\left(\frac{\omega_{N}d}{\pi}\right)~.

(241)

Figure 7
:
(a) The shaded area of the ring is proportional to the incremental number of resonant frequencies
\delta N
. (b) The shaded area of the ellipse is approximately equal to the number of resonant frequencies
N
.

Using
\omega_{N}\approx\omega
, we now have

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\left|\frac{\omega^{2}Rd}{8\pi}G_{p_{*}q_{*}}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|^{2}~.

(242)

Finally, for large
p
, we have the scaling

\displaystyle\left|G_{pq}^{\text{cylinder}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{4x^{\prime}_{1p}}{(x^{\prime 2}_{1p}-1)J_{1}(x_{1p}^{\prime})}\frac{1}{q\pi}\right|\approx\left|\frac{4}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}_{1p}}\sqrt{2/\pi}}\frac{1}{q\pi}\right|~.

(243)

Using
\omega_{q}\approx\omega_{p}\approx\omega/\sqrt{2}
, this yields

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{cylinder}}^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\left|\sqrt{\frac{1}{8\pi}}\frac{\omega^{2}R}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}_{1p}}}\frac{d}{q\pi}\right|^{2}=\left|\sqrt{\frac{1}{8\pi}}\frac{\omega^{2}\sqrt{R}}{\sqrt{\omega_{p}}}\frac{1}{\omega_{q}}\right|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\omega R~,

(244)

verifying our prediction of linear focusing for a cylindrical cavity in Section
IV.1
.

Appendix F
Fixed Polarization Scenario

Several cosmological generation mechanisms of dark photon dark matter (DPDM) result in a fixed polarization scenario. This has a nontrivial interplay with the rotation of the Earth, modifying our dark photon limits.
[
21
]
systematically studied this effect on experiments, but that paper assumes continuous observables. Since our observable is discrete (quantum transitions), we need a different treatment.

F.1
Transition Probability

We first define a coordinate system with the center of Earth as the origin and the Earth rotation axis as
\hat{z}
(leaving
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
arbitrary for now). At the location of the experiment, define the unit vector pointing in the direction of the magnetic field of the Penning trap to be
\hat{B}(t)
, which is changing due to the rotation of the Earth. For our experiment, this happens to point straight up toward the zenith, making the calculation a bit simpler, although we will show that the result can be easily generalized.

Writing
\hat{B}(t)
in spherical polar coordinate, the polar angle
\theta
is fixed and is determined by the latitude of the location, while the azimuthal angle is rotating along with the Earth,
\varphi(t)=\omega_{\mathTerra}t+\varphi_{0}
. Here,
\omega_{\mathTerra}
is the angular velocity of Earth rotation, given by one cycle per sidereal day:
\omega_{\mathTerra}=\frac{2\pi}{23.93\text{ hour}}
. We can write this vector as

\hat{B}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta\cos\varphi(t)\\
\sin\theta\sin\varphi(t)\\
\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}~.

(245)

Next, let the unit vector along the direction of the fixed DPDM be
\hat{A}^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we can choose
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
such that its azimuthal angle
\varphi_{A}=0
. Then the unknown cosmological polarization of DPDM can be entirely parameterized by the polar angle
\theta_{A}
:

\hat{A}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta_{A}\\
0\\
\cos\theta_{A}\end{pmatrix}~.

(246)

Finally, define the angle between
\hat{B}(t)
and
\hat{A}^{\prime}
to be
\alpha(t)
. The quantity we are interested in is their dot product:

\displaystyle\cos\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{B}(t)

(247)

\displaystyle=\sin\theta\sin\theta_{A}\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0)+\cos\theta\cos\theta_{A}~.

(248)

It is easy to get
\theta
and
\varphi_{0}
for an arbitrary experimental orientation. Suppose the direction of the Penning trap magnetic field
\hat{B}
does not point to the Zenith, but instead has both a North-South tilt
\Delta\theta_{NS}
and an East-West tilt
\gamma_{EW}
. The North-South tilt can be accounted for by our previous formalism by modifying the definition of polar angle:
\theta\equiv\theta_{\text{location}}+\Delta\theta_{NS}
. Having a
\gamma_{EW}
amounts to shifting
\varphi\to\varphi+\Delta\varphi_{EW}
. This is degenerate with
\varphi_{0}
and hence makes no difference.

Now, consider the probability of observing no cyclotron excitation in the presence of DPDM, which follows a Poisson distribution. Consider the infinitesimal time interval between
t
and
t+dt
. Let the average number of excitations during this time be

d\lambda=\Gamma_{c}dt~,

(249)

where the cyclotron transition rate
\Gamma_{c}
is given by eq. (
75
). We will separate out its angular dependence by

\Gamma_{c}\equiv\Gamma_{0}\sin^{2}\alpha(t)=\Gamma_{0}\left[1-\cos^{2}\alpha(t)\right]~,

(250)

which is always possible for a cavity with azimuthal symmetry (see the discussion around eq. (
74
)). Notice that this quantity depends on
\cos(\tA)
and
\varphi_{0}
, which are both drawn from uniform distributions.

The conditional probability of observing no excitation during the time interval
dt
conditioned on a polarization angle
\theta_{A}
and initial azimuthal phase
\varphi_{0}
is:

dP_{0}(\cos\theta_{A},\varphi_{0})=e^{-d\lambda(\cos\theta_{A},\varphi_{0})}~,

(251)

where we have made explicit the dependence of
d\lambda
.
To convert the conditional probability to total probability, we marginalize over the distribution of the two uncorrelated conditions:

\displaystyle dP_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta_{A})P(\cos\theta_{A})\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{0}P(\varphi_{0})dP_{0}(\cos\theta_{A},\varphi_{0})

(252)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}dP_{0}(\cos\theta_{A},\varphi_{0})~.

(253)

Since we have no prior knowledge of the DPDM polarization angle and the initial azimuthal phase, we have taken both to be uniform distributions:
P(\cos\theta_{A})=\frac{1}{2}
and
P(\varphi_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}
. Notice that since the area element is proportional to
\sin(\tA)d\theta_{A}=d(\cos\theta_{A})
, this is the correct quantity to have a uniform distribution, not
\theta_{A}
itself.

Finally, the total probability of detecting nothing over the entire run time of the experiment at a given frequency bin from
t=0
to
t=t_{\text{obs}}
is the successive product of the instantaneous probabilities:

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle=\prod_{dt}dP_{0}

(254)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\prod_{dt}dP_{0}(\cos\theta_{A},\varphi_{0})

(255)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\prod_{dt}\exp[-d\lambda]

(256)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp[-\int d\lambda]

(257)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\Gamma_0 \int_0^{\tobs} dt \left[1- \cos^2\alpha(t) \right]}~.

(258)

Substituting eq. (
248
) and incorporating the probability of fast decay
\Gamma_{c}\to\zeta\Gamma_{c}
, where
\zeta
is the detection efficiency given by eq. (
144
), we can finally find the CL=90% confidence level limit by solving the following equation for
\Gamma_{0,\text{fixed}}
:

\displaystyle 1-CL=P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta)=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\zeta\Gf\int_0^{\tobs} dt \left[1- \left[ \sin\theta\sin\tA\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0) + \cos\theta\cos\tA\right]^2 \right]}~.

(259)

F.2
Long Time Limit

Although our current proposed parameters and the previous proof-of-principle experiment
[
45
]
both have observation times
t_{\text{obs}}
of several days, it is easy to adjust our quality factor
Q_{c}
to achieve a much shorter
t_{\text{obs}}
. Therefore, we will discuss both the long and short observation time limits (defined relative to a Sidereal day) and show that they, in fact, share the same result.

Eq. (
259
) tells us that the solution of
\Gamma_{0,\text{fixed}}
depends on
t_{\text{obs}}
via an integration limit. Consider the case that

\displaystyle T=t_{\text{obs}}\gg 1~\text{day}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{\mathTerra}}.

(260)

In this limit, we can write the time integral in terms of time average

\langle f(t)\rangle_{T}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}dtf(t)}{T}~.

(261)

Therefore, in the long time limit, the time integral of eq. (
259
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\left[1-\left[\sin\theta\sin\theta_{A}\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0)+\cos\theta\cos\theta_{A}\right]^{2}\right]\approx T\times(1-\langle\left[\sin\theta\sin\theta_{A}\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0)+\cos\theta\cos\theta_{A}\right]^{2}\rangle_{T})~.

(262)

Using
\langle\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0)\rangle_{T}=0
and
\langle\cos^{2}(\omega_{\mathTerra}t+\varphi_{0})\rangle_{T}=1/2
, we can then write the integral as

\displaystyle T\times(1-\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta_{A}-\cos^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta_{A})~.

(263)

Now we are able to rewrite eq. (
259
) and simplify it,

\displaystyle 1-CL=P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\zeta\Gf\tobs\times(1- \frac{1}{2} \sin^2 \theta\sin^2 \tA- \cos^2 \theta\cos^2 \tA)}

(264)

\displaystyle=\exp{-\zeta\Gf\tobs(1-\frac{1}{2}\sin^2 \theta)}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{2}\exp{\zeta\Gf\tobs(1-\frac{3}{2} \sin^2 \theta) x^2}~.

(265)

For comparison, we also recall the random polarization case from Appendix
A.5
:

\displaystyle 1-CL=P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}})=\exp{-\zeta\Gamma_{c,\text{random}}\tobs}=\exp{-\zeta\Gr\langle\sin^2 \theta_A \rangle\tobs}=\exp{-\frac{2}{3}\zeta\Gr\tobs}~.

(266)

F.2.1
Experiment in Chicago

The
\theta
in eq. (
264
) is a parameter that depends on the location of the experiment. In our previous work
[
46
]
, we did a multi-day proof-of-principle experiment in Chicago, which satisfies the long time limit requirement
t_{\text{obs}}\gg 1
day. Therefore, we can use eq. (
264
) to calculate
\Gamma_{0,\text{fixed}}
.

That experiment was located at Evanston, Illinois, which has a latitude of
42.05^{\circ}
N, corresponding to a polar angle of
\theta_{\text{Chicago}}=90^{\circ}-42.05^{\circ}=0.837
. The total run time was
t_{\text{obs}}=638870\text{ seconds}
. Substituting these two numbers, we numerically found a limit of

\Gamma_{0,\text{fixed}}<6.55\times 10^{-6}\text{ s}^{-1}~.

(267)

By comparison, the limit from eq. (
266
) is equivalent to

\Gamma_{0,\text{random}}<\frac{3}{2}\times 4.33\times 10^{-6}\text{ s}^{-1}=6.50\times 10^{-6}\text{ s}^{-1}~.

(268)

We see that the fixed polarization limit on
\epsilon
is only a little bit weaker than that of random polarization, and we will see below this is due to
\theta_{\text{Chicago}}=0.837
being not too far from the optimal angle
\theta_{*}=0.955
.

F.2.2
Optimal Angle

In Figure
8
, we plot the numerical result from eq. (
264
) and eq. (
266
), and it shows that the fixed polarization scenario, in general, sets a worse limit than the random polarization case. However, there is an optimal angle where they are equal. By comparing eq. (
264
) and eq. (
266
), it is obvious that this optimal polar angle occurs exactly at
\sin^{2}(\theta_{*})=\frac{2}{3}
, with
\theta_{*}=0.955
.

F.3
Short Time Limit

For short observation time where
t_{\text{obs}}\ll 1~\text{day}
, eq. (
259
) can be simplified as,

\displaystyle 1-CL=P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta,t_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\zeta\Gf\int_{t_0}^{t_0+\tobs} dt \left[1- \left[ \sin\theta\sin\tA\cos(\ws t + \varphi_0) + \cos\theta\cos\tA\right]^2 \right]}

(269)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\zeta\Gf\tobs\left[1- \left[ \sin\theta\sin\tA\cos(\ws t_0 + \varphi_0) + \cos\theta\cos\tA\right]^2 \right]}~.

(270)

Here,
t_{0}
represents the initial time of each observation. Since we are scanning in frequency continuously, different values of

t_{0}
can be regarded as representing an average value over the long observation period. Averaging
P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta,t_{0})
over one day gives the expected value of
P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta)
:

\displaystyle\langle P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta)\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d(\cos\theta_{A})}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{2\pi}\exp{-\zeta\Gf\tobs\langle\left[1- \left[ \sin\theta\sin\tA\cos(\ws t_0 + \varphi_0) + \cos\theta\cos\tA\right]^2 \right]\rangle_{T=1\text{day}}}~.

(271)

Comparing this expression of
\langle P_{0}(\zeta t_{\text{obs}},\theta)\rangle
with eq. (
262
), it turns out that we get the same answer for these two limits.
