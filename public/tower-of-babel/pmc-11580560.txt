Parkinson’s disease in the Lebanese population: knowledge and attitude scales’ validation and correlates
Authors: Soula Abramian, Samah Tawil, Marwan Akel, Chadia Haddad, Pascale Salameh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive degenerative neurological disorder that negatively affects the quality of life of affected individuals as well as their care givers. There is limited information in both epidemiological data and awareness level on PD in the Middle East. Considering the increasing prevalence of PD, the lack of awareness and negative attitudes toward PD pose a barrier in the provision of appropriate care for individuals with PD.ObjectiveThis study aimed to validate scales to explore the knowledge and attitude towards PD among the general population in Lebanon and identify the associated factors related to the knowledge and attitude assessment. This study’s results would guide awareness strategies in the country including public health strategies, campaigns and education sessions for the general population as well as targeted people.MethodologyAn online cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2023 in Lebanon. Snowball sampling was used to recruit 400 Lebanese adults living in Lebanon. The validity and reliability of the knowledge and attitude scales were assessed, followed by a thorough statistical analysis to explore the factors associated with these scales.ResultsThe knowledge and attitude scales generated by this study were valid and reliable; Cronbach’s alpha values for knowledge and attitude scales were 0.697 and 0.901, respectively. Using these scales, 91.8% demonstrated inappropriate knowledge and 93% showed positive attitude towards PD. Higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with female gender (Beta = 1.130), being a healthcare professional (Beta = 2.514), having heard about PD (Beta = 5.338), obtaining the source of information from electronic databases (Beta = 1.283), from pharmacist or physician (Beta = 1.212), from personal readings (Beta = 2.386), and having PD or knowing someone with PD; a friend or a family member (Beta = 1.179). However, being illiterate or having primary or secondary education (Beta = -0.874), having a higher household crowding index (Beta = -1.890), not knowing if contents from sources provide negative images about PD (Beta = − 1.908) and preferring not to say to which social class they belong (Beta = − 1.602) were significantly associated with lower knowledge scores. Believing that contents from sources provide negative images about PD (Beta = 8.246) was significantly associated with a higher attitude score, i.e., a more negative attitude towards PD.ConclusionThis study showed low levels of knowledge and an overall positive attitude towards PD in Lebanon. Factors associated with higher knowledge and attitude scores, including gender, education level, profession, knowing someone with the disease, household crowding index, social class, source of information and contents of such sources, reflect the need to consider those when implementing targeted corrective actions. Future studies can focus on educational campaigns to improve public awareness, reduce misconceptions and limit barriers to the early diagnosis and provision of optimal care to the affected patients.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Awareness, Knowledge, Attitude, General population

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic and progressive degenerative neurological disorder characterized by the decreased level of the neurotransmitter dopamine, due to the loss of dopaminergic neurons in a specific area of the brain called substantia nigra. Gradual loss of dopamine-containing neurons is a common aging feature; however, most people do not lose 70–80% of the dopaminergic neurons required to cause symptomatic PD [1].

It is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease, affecting approximately seven million people globally and one million people in the United States [2, 3]. Globally, the rates of disability and death due to Parkinson’s Disease are rising more rapidly than those for any other neurological disorder. Current estimates suggest that, in 2019, PD resulted in 5.8 million disability-adjusted life years and caused 329,000 deaths [4]. According to the latest WHO data published in 2020, PD deaths in Lebanon reached 226, representing 0.66% of total deaths. The age adjusted death rate, which is 3.63 per 100,000 of population, ranks Lebanon number 121 in the world [5].

Symptoms of PD generally develop slow over years. PD typically causes both motor and non-motor symptoms. The most recognizable symptoms are movement related, and include tremor at rest, bradykinesia, rigidity in arms and legs and postural instability. Non-motor symptoms, including autonomic dysfunction, neuropsychiatric problems, sensory troubles and sleep difficulties may be present as well [6].

PD is a clinical diagnosis, as such there is no specific test to diagnose the disease. There is no cure for PD; treatment aims to slow the progression of the disease, reduce symptoms, and improve quality of life. Initial treatment is typically with levodopa/carbidopa, monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAO-B inhibitors), or dopamine agonists. Other medication, such as anticholinergics can also be used. Diet, exercise, and certain forms of rehabilitation have shown some effectiveness at improving symptoms. When drugs are ineffective, surgery can be used by placing microelectrodes for deep brain stimulation [7].

Patients’ engagement in the healthcare process requires health awareness. A delayed treatment of PD may be associated with poorer outcomes, as it may be difficult to reverse the functional decline of PD patients. Despite advancements in treatment, diagnosis and care of PD patients, lack of adequate knowledge and associated beliefs and attitudes among the community might play an important role in the currently unexplained reasons behind diagnostic delays and in limiting access to timely treatment. Some aspects of PD may be stigmatizing because of their association with other more negatively viewed conditions. For example, speech and communication difficulties may be mistakenly associated with mental retardation. The association with ageing can be a cause of stigma. Poor awareness about the disease, negative public attitudes towards PD and stereotypes associated with it may trigger depression and social withdrawal in patients, patients’ families as well as care providers. Thus, understanding community knowledge and attitudes towards PD can help to inform care approaches that reduce stigma and promote help-seeking behavior [8].

There are several published studies from Asia, Africa, USA, and Australia on public awareness, beliefs, and attitude towards PD. A study done in South Korea to assess public awareness and knowledge about PD, concluded that participants between 40 and 59 years of age and those who had completed more than 12 years of education showed more awareness of PD compared to younger subjects who exhibited the least knowledge [9]. A study from Uganda found that 50.4% of participants did not know the body part related to PD. Nearly 1/3 of individuals believed that PD is a form of insanity and 17% believed that PD is contagious [10]. A study In Malaysia reported that 49.8% of the public believe that there is a cure for PD and 41.4% thought that it is usually familial [11]. A cross-sectional study conducted on Saudis showed that tremor was the most widely recognized symptom (86.10%), and weight loss was the most recognized non-motor symptom (24%). Motor symptoms were significantly better recognized (range 31.30-86.10%) than non-motor symptoms (range 4.10-24%) [12]. A survey was developed to assess public attitudes and beliefs about PD in Australia. The results showed that there is significant stigma associated with PD. The potential consequence of PD most concerning to people was being a burden (61%), followed by mental deterioration (59.5%) and being dependent on others (58.5%). Moreover, people with negative attitudes to PD were more likely to view the illness as stigmatized [8]. A cross-sectional study conducted to assess knowledge and attitudes about PD among older adults in Philadelphia found that one-third of community members had no to little knowledge about PD. In addition to this low level of awareness, the study was able to identify fear related to having PD and becoming a burden to their families as barriers to care [13].

Well-developed and validated questionnaires used in previous studies measured the quality of life in patients who already had PD. Such validated scales assessed how often PD patients experienced difficulties across different dimensions of mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well-being, stigma, social support, cognitions and communications Despite the presence of validated tools for PD patients, there are limited studies that have developed questionnaires about knowledge and attitude towards PD in the general population. In Malaysia, a knowledge of PD questionnaire (KPDQ) was developed to test recognition of PD symptoms among Malaysian public, PD patients and their caregivers [11]. Knowledge and attitude questionnaire was also used to assess public awareness and attitude towards PD in Australia [8]. However, the survey tool that was used in most of the previous studies about PD in the general population was not validated. It is crucial to have validated scales to tailor education towards specific populations that are being assessed using appropriate tools and measures.

Few studies concerning PD have been carried out in the Middle East, and to the best of our knowledge, there are no published studies that have evaluated community knowledge and attitude towards PD in Lebanon. Moreover, since knowledge and attitude aspects would be better assessed using a valid and reliable tool, such a tool would allow the detection of the facilitators and barriers to health-seeking behavior.

Thus, the aim of the study was to validate scales to assess knowledge and attitudes towards PD among the general population in Lebanon and identify the associated factors related to the knowledge and attitude assessment. This study’s results are expected to guide awareness strategies in the country towards the different aspects of PD.

Materials and methods

Study design

An observational cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2023 among 400 Lebanese adults (including non-healthcare and healthcare professionals). A snowball sampling technique was used to recruit participants from all Lebanese districts. None of the participants received incentive for their participation in the study, which was voluntary. All Lebanese adults aged 18 years and above, living in Lebanon and having an internet access were eligible to participate. Hence, participants who are below the age of 18 or who are not Lebanese or who are Lebanese bit live outside of Lebanon will be excluded from this study. Moreover, subjects who don’t have an internet access will not be able to participate in the study.

Ethical aspect

On July 25, 2023, INSPECT-LB Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol (2023REC-009-INSPECT-06-09). Written consent was obtained from each person at the beginning of the questionnaire.

Sample size calculation

In the absence of similar studies about PD in Lebanon, the CDC Epi-info 7 software was used to calculate the minimum sample size. In the absence of similar studies in Lebanon, the expected frequency for the dependent variables was kept at 50% to yield the largest sample size. Accordingly, a minimum of 384 participants was required to produce a 95% confidence interval, with an acceptable margin of error of 5% and a power of 80%. A sample of 400 participants was targeted to take into account possible missing values.

Questionnaire and variables

To reach a broader population from all Lebanese districts, an online questionnaire in Arabic, which is the native language of Lebanon, and English, which is also widely read, written and spoken in the country, was created on Google Forms (https://forms.gle/3hXQu2UnZwN5UFMy6). The link to the questionnaire was shared via WhatsApp and other platforms of social media. Participants were also encouraged to disseminate the survey to others using the snowball technique. At the beginning of the questionnaire, the purpose of the study was explained to all participants who were informed that their participation was voluntary and that their responses would remain anonymous and confidential. The questionnaire included three main sections; a sociodemographic category and other characteristics of the studied population, the source of information about PD, and a scale-based section related to the knowledge and attitude about PD.

Sociodemographic data and other characteristics

Age, gender, education level, region, marital status, household crowding index, occupation, monthly income, social class, being a healthcare professional and the connection of participants with PD were all covered in the sociodemographic part.

Source of information about PD

In this part of the questionnaire, participants were asked if they have ever heard about PD, what is the major source of their information about PD and whether the sources provided negative images about the disease.

Scale-based variables

After a thorough review of literature and in the absence of validated scales, questions related to the knowledge and attitude towards PD were retrieved from several previous articles and adjusted to fit the purpose of the study [8–11]. After the literature review, the authors’ panel had several rounds of discussion to decide on the final list of items to be included in the questionnaire. All panel members are public health specialists; one (PS) has a long expertise in clinical epidemiology and scale development, and the others are experienced in clinical pharmacy, including neurology. Using a Delphi technique, all items included in the questionnaire had more 90% agreement among the panel members”. The final set of questions were translated to Arabic by a certified translator, and back translated to English with no discrepancies found during the comparison of both versions by the researchers (who are all bilingual).

Knowledge about PD

Knowledge about PD

This section initially included 34 items; however, after factor analysis one item, i.e., question 21 (reduced level of dopamine in the brain) was removed. Hence, this section included 33 items related to definition of PD, clinical features of PD, causes of PD and treatment of PD. Answer options to all questions were Yes/No/I don’t know, except for questions 19 (part of the body related to PD), 20 (cause of PD), 28 (main symptom of PD) and 29 (problems experienced by people with PD). The total score was calculated by summing up all the correct answers to the knowledge questions. A higher score indicated better knowledge. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.697.

Attitude towards PD:

Attitude towards PD:

This section included 31 items that reflect the attitude of the general population towards PD.

Each statement had 5 answer options ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The total score was calculated by summing up all the negative answers to the attitude questions. A higher score indicated a more worrisome and negative attitude towards PD. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.901.

Statistical analysis

All variables were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).Before entry, all data were examined for accuracy. A descriptive analysis was performed using absolute frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations (SD) for quantitative measures.

Factor analysis with principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess the construct validity of the knowledge and attitude scales. For the knowledge scale, the Varimax rotation method was used, whereas for the attitude scale, the Promax rotation method was used. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was calculated to ensure the model’s adequacy. Factors with Eigenvalues greater than one were retained, and the scree plot method was used to determine the number of components to extract [14]. Moreover, Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the internal consistency of the studied scales: internal consistency values α ≥ 0.7 and ≥ 0.8 were considered acceptable and excellent, respectively [15].

The knowledge and attitude variables were deemed normally distributed, as verified by the visual inspection of the histogram, while the skewness and kurtosis were below 1.96. When applicable, the normality of the scales used was verified by the normality line of the regression and scatter plot of the residuals. Furthermore, knowledge score was dichotomized into good and inappropriate with a cut-off point of 75%, where good knowledge was reflected by a score of 75% and above and inappropriate knowledge by a percentage strictly below 75%. Attitude score was also dichotomized in to positive and negative with a cut-off point of 75% where negative attitude was reflected by a score of 75% and above and positive attitude by a percentage strictly below 75% [16–19].

In the bivariate analysis, the independent-sample t-test was used to compare the means of the knowledge and attitude scales between two groups, whereas ANOVA test was used to compare three or more means, followed by post hoc analysis to identify where the significant differences lay between variables. The Pearson correlation test was used to correlate continuous variables. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Following the bivariate analysis, two linear regressions using the enter method were performed, taking the knowledge and attitude scales as the dependent variables. In the first linear regression, the knowledge scale was taken as the dependent variable and sociodemographic characteristics as the independent variables. In the second linear regression, the attitude scale was taken as the dependent variable, and knowledge and sociodemographic characteristics as the independent variables. To decrease potential confounders, variables that showed a p-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate models.

Descriptive results

The mean scores of the knowledge and attitude were 17.00 ± 5.89, and 94.19 ± 16.78, respectively. Regarding the knowledge scale and considering the 75% cut-off point, the majority of the participants (91.8%) demonstrated inappropriate knowledge about PD. As for the attitude, most of the participants (93%) showed positive attitude toward PD.

As illustrated in Table 7 in Appendix 1, 76.5% of the participants were able to differentiate between PD and Alzheimer’s disease and 68% were able to recognize that PD is not a natural result of aging. The majority of the participants (82%) were able to identify the brain as the body part related to PD and nearly half of them (44%) reported that degeneration was responsible for the disease, while 42.3% did not know if the cause of PD is unknown. Almost all of the participants (91.5%) knew that PD is not contagious. Although 80.5% of the participants reported that PD is more common in older individuals and 53.8% reported that it can affect young adults, 48.8% and 42.3% were not sure if it is more common in women and whether it affects multiple members of the same family. In terms of symptoms, 74.8% of the participants recognized tremor as the main symptom of PD. Motor symptoms were better recognized than non-motor symptoms. In fact, tremor was the most recognized motor symptom (93.8%) followed by slowness of movement (81.8%), imbalance (79.8%) and lastly stiffness (67.3%). On the other hand, depression (58.3%), weight loss (52.3%) and memory problems (50.75%) were the most recognized non-motor symptoms. In terms of prognosis, 40.5% of participants did not know if PD progresses very rapidly and 42.5% believed that people with PD don’t have the same lifespan as people without PD. In terms of diagnosis and treatment, although 58.3% responded that they don’t know if there is a blood test that can diagnose PD, 63.2% were convinced that early diagnosis and timely treatment can reduce the disease progression. Moreover, more than half of the participants, 68.5% and 57.8% believed that medications and diet and exercise can improve the symptoms of PD but 56.5% were not sure if there are new treatments that can cure PD. Additionally, 54% of the participants did not know if there are any surgical options available for people with PD.

When asked about their attitude, 67% of participants disagreed that there is stigma or shame associated with PD. Less than one third of people felt uncomfortable to think about PD (31.3%) and became nervous while watching news about the disease (22.8%). Although almost half of the participants were afraid of PD because it a serious disease (49.15%) and were also afraid of losing family or friends because of PD (47.8%), only 9.1% of them thought that their risk of getting PD is high. While 74.5% of the participants agreed that they would become friends with someone who has young onset PD, only 13.8% were open to marrying someone with PD. The potential consequence of PD most concerning to people was physical deterioration (75.3%), followed by being a burden (73.6%), losing the ability to communicate effectively (69.8%), mental deterioration (68.1%), who would look after them (66.3%), being in pain (64.3%), losing independence (62.5%), not being able to work (53.1%) and becoming depressed (51.8%). Regarding social issues, 64.3% of people were worried about not being able to socialize and 52.8% were afraid of losing friendships and being lonely. Among the less worrisome social issues were what people think and whether they will accept them (31%), followed by being embarrassed to use a walker (24.6%). When questioned if they developed signs or symptoms of PD, 60.3% of the participants agreed that they might want to find answers on their own first and 67.6% agreed that they would tell their friends or members of their family. Moreover, most of the participants (80%) agreed that they would talk to their doctor immediately and 83.3% agreed to go to a healthcare professional and get educated on the disease. When asked about treatment, 65.6% of people were worried about finding the best treatment and 70% about the cost of medical treatment. Less worrisome treatment issues were the thought that PD treatment is not very effective (36.3%), followed by the feeling that doctors and hospitals do not have sufficient knowledge to handle PD (25.8%) and lastly that treatment is not worth the side effects of the medication (18.3%) (See Table 8 in Appendix 1).

Bivariate analysis

Considering the knowledge score as the dependent variable in the bivariate analysis showed that higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with female gender, being a healthcare professional, living in an urban area, knowing someone with PD and having a higher education level, i.e., Participants who were illiterate or had primary or secondary education had a lower knowledge score as compared to those with university and postgraduate degree and those with university degree had a lower knowledge score versus those with postgraduate degree. Higher knowledge scores were also significantly associated with higher social class, i.e.; People in the upper middle social class had a higher knowledge score when compared to people in the poor /low socioeconomic status social class. Furthermore, participants with very low and low monthly income had lower knowledge scores when compared to those with intermediate and high monthly income. Lastly, a higher crowding index was significantly associated with a lower knowledge score.

Considering the attitude scale as the dependent variable in the bivariate analysis showed that being a male was significantly associated with a more positive and less worrisome attitude towards PD (Table 4).

The association between the source of information about PD and the knowledge and attitude scales is presented in Table 5. The results showed that higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with having heard about PD and obtaining information from electronic databases, healthcare professionals mainly pharmacists and physicians, from personal readings and from conferences and brochures. Low knowledge score was significantly associated with those refusing to seek such information. Participants who didn’t believe that contents provided negative information had a significantly higher knowledge score as compared to those who did not know if these contents provided negative images about PD.

Discussion

This study evaluated the knowledge and attitude towards PD among the Lebanese population. It also validated a questionnaire assessing the PD knowledge and attitude. As far as we know, it is the first to perform such a validation in Lebanon. Studies that have well developed and validated questionnaires are mostly those that measure the quality of life in patients who have PD, such as the two best known scales; Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-39) and Parkinson’s Disease Quality of Life Questionnaire (PDQL) [20–22]. There are some studies that developed questionnaires for the general public. For instance, in Malaysia, a knowledge of PD Questionnaire (KPDQ) was developed and administered to members of the Malaysian general public, PD patients and caregivers to test recognition of PD symptoms and PD general knowledge [11]. Also, in Australia, attitude and knowledge questionnaires were used to assess public knowledge and attitude towards PD [7]. However, the scales that were used in the majority of the previous studies about PD in the general population were not validated. This could increase the risk of information bias and the conclusion regarding knowledge and attitude could be possibly affected by confounding in the absence of multivariate analysis. In our study, using Varimax and Promax rotation, the PCA showed good internal consistency (IC) for the knowledge scale (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.697) and excellent IC for the attitude scale (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.901).

It was noticeable that some of the knowledge questions were answered correctly by almost the majority of the participants such as the brain being the body part related to PD, PD being more common in the elderly, PD being non contagious and tremor being the main symptom of PD. Our observation that tremor was the best recognized symptom of PD is unsurprising because tremor is present in 80% of PD patients and it is a highly visible feature of the disease [23, 24]. As found in A.H. Tan et al.. study, non-motor symptoms of PD have been under-recognized. This may be partly due to the fact that non-motor symptoms may be non-specific and misreported to natural aging and comorbidities. Despite the fact that some of the knowledge statements were answered correctly, our study highlighted an overall very low knowledge and the presence of several misconceptions about PD causes, symptoms, diagnosis and treatment among the Lebanese population (91.8% had inappropriate knowledge according to our score calculations). There has been a similar study in the United States that also demonstrated a very low level of PD knowledge, where it was found that 33.3% of community members had no to little knowledge, and almost 46.8% of the community had some knowledge about PD [13]. There have also been several studies in other countries that have found low to moderate levels of knowledge about PD. One telephone survey conducted among Australian households asked similar questions related to PD with an average of 66% of items answered correctly [8]. This limited body of knowledge about PD can itself act as a barrier to healthcare. In a study of Alzheimer’s disease, a neurodegenerative disease that can be considered analogous to PD, lower knowledge was associated with being less likely to seek health care [25]. Similarly, low health literacy is associated with lower levels of knowledge, poorer use of healthcare services and worse health outcomes across multiple health conditions [26].

Our results showed that female gender, being a healthcare professional, having heard about PD, knowing someone with PD, and obtaining information from electronic databases, from pharmacists/physicians and from personal readings were predictive of better PD knowledge. However, low level of education, a higher crowding index, not knowing whether contents from sources provide negative images about PD and preferring not to say to which social class participants belong to were associated with lower knowledge. In our study, having PD or knowing someone with PD; a friend or a family member, was associated with a higher level of knowledge of PD. This finding was similar to that of A.H. Tan et al.. study and Moore & Knowles 2006 study, which also showed an association between knowing someone with PD and higher level of knowledge of PD. In contrast, a study in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia showed that caring for someone with PD was not associated with a higher level of knowledge of PD [12]. As A.H. Tan et al.., our study demonstrated association between healthcare workers and a higher level of knowledge of PD. Also, similar to A.H. Tan et al., the sociodemographic characteristic of the community also appears to be an influential predictor of PD knowledge. As a matter of fact, females had better knowledge. It is not well understood why females had a better understanding of the disease. It could be that females are generally more concerned about their health and that of their family, and thus, they tend to acquire more information about the disease. Having heard about PD and the source of information about PD appear to be important predictors of knowledge about the disease. A better score was significantly associated with getting information on PD from pharmacists/physicians, from personal readings and from electronic databases. The role of healthcare practitioners in providing educational information to the community and the role of electronic databases in promoting the health information system is well established. Digital data sources, including websites, were shown to be essential for gathering information in the area of several diseases and early warning systems [27].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies from other countries explored the impact of the household crowding index on knowledge about PD. In our study, a higher household index was negatively associated with a lower PD knowledge. A lower score was also linked to participants who were unwilling to disclose their social class. Lebanon is a developing lower-middle-income country that has been facing drastic socioeconomic crisis over the past few years. This unprecedented crisis has been linked to higher levels of poverty and catastrophic sociodemographic and medical consequences. Considering our results, there might be an inequity in health information due to the limited access and opportunity associated with lower socioeconomic status. Moreover, a lower perceived knowledge score was noted in participants with no to low education levels (illiterate, primary or secondary versus university), as published else-where [9]. This could be explained by the fact that people with lower levels of education tend to experience difficulties seeking new information and are less aware of their surroundings [28].

Interestingly, our study reported that most of the participants showed positive attitude towards PD (93%). A high percentage of participants did not believe that there is stigma associated with PD, in contrast to previous studies that raised concerns after finding out that nearly half the respondents saw a stigma attached to this disease [8]. Participants were mostly positive about wanting to find answers about the disease and were willing to tell their friends, family members and doctors in case they developed signs and symptoms of the disease. Keeping an illness hidden from others can be a sign of stigma. It is in fact relieving that our study showed minimal stigma since stigma beliefs can lead to depression as a result of discrimination, poor level of community support, fewer work and leisure opportunities which will ultimately make life more difficult for those with the disease and their care givers.

Worries about possible effects of PD were mostly physical deterioration, being a burden, problems with communication and mental deterioration. Furthermore, and similar to other studies conducted in Uganda and Australia, more than half of the respondents were afraid of losing their independence and ability to work [8, 10]. The physical, psychological, economic and social suffering caused by PD makes individuals dependent on their family for support. Perhaps, the reason why PD is so widely believed disabling by the community is that PD that is recognized tends to be later-stage illness, especially in stigmatizing communities in which PD patients might try to conceal their PD from others in the early stages when symptoms are less noticeable [29]. Such fearful beliefs do not acknowledge the common course of this disease, especially in young onset PD, in which diagnosis may be slow in coming and the sufferer may live with the illness for many years, with a quite gradual loss of function. This means that a person with PD is able to work for many years as changes to social and physical life need occur gradually. Long-term social and community support that does not label them as “too ill” to be involved and public education on the “can do” aspects of PD patients may be of value here to ensure that their years with the disease will be as fulfilling as possible [8].

Another aspect concerning to the participants was the issue of finding the best treatment as more than half of the participants were worried about PD therapy and the cost of the treatment. This fear might partly be related to the overall decline in the healthcare system, medical supply and insurance plans in Lebanon, especially in the last couple of years after the country went through one of the worst social and economic crises in the history of Lebanon. As such this fear may be non-specific to PD and may apply to finding proper care to all kinds of diseases.

Regarding predictors of negative attitude, our study showed that people who believed that contents from sources provided negative images about PD were more likely to have negative attitude towards PD. More often than not, the media portrays PD in a negative light, and this, in turn, affects the way we view the illness, even as healthcare professionals. Often, the information is inaccurate and misleading which creates a false picture of what PD really is. People are fed stereotypes which are usually based on misunderstandings that lead to a stigma and a fearful effect towards PD. No other association was found between attitude and other variables. However, a previous finding in Australia showed that negative attitudes were more common among participants who knew less about PD. Better public understanding about PD could help in demystifying the disease. Knowing that there are ways to manage the disease symptoms, especially in the early stages, would reduce fear of the disease, and lower the impact on those diagnosed and their families and would reduce stigma [8]. Interestingly, our study results were inconsistent with this previous study, as no significant association was found between knowledge and attitude. Moreover, in our study, age did not appear to have any significant impact on both attitude and knowledge, unlike previous studies in several other countries, such as South Korea which found out that younger subjects exhibited the least knowledge compared to respondents between 40 and 59 years of age and those above 60 years of age [9]. Additionally, the results of the study among Australians demonstrated that negative attitudes towards PD were more common in younger age groups [8].

Implications

Future research should concentrate on developing informational and educational programs aimed at increasing public awareness and individual knowledge about Parkinson’s Disease (PD), including its symptoms and treatments. By addressing societal and cultural misconceptions, these initiatives could encourage more people with PD to seek help earlier. This, in turn, may lead to reduced diagnostic delays and treatment disparities, ultimately enhancing the quality of life for both PD patients and their caregivers.

Limitations and strengths

Our study has several limitations. While the cross-sectional design of the study is practical for capturing a snapshot and rapidly identifying gaps in the disease knowledge and attitude, it does not provide temporality and does not allow for causal interference. Although the sample size was sufficient for statistical analyses to be carried out, the results could have been more representative with a larger sample.

Moreover, a snowball technique (online questionnaire) was used to recruit participants and collect data. This is a non-random data collection technique that might have eliminated people with inadequate digital literacy and might have resulted in a possible selection bias. However, the likelihood of this bias is minimal as the questionnaire was both in Arabic and English, and most of the people living in Lebanon have smartphones and are familiar with online access.

Furthermore, the results of the study might not be representative of the entire Lebanese population, especially that the sample had an unequal gender distribution, where the majority of subjects were females, employed, mostly located in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, lived in urban areas, and had a high level of education.

Despite all these limitations, our study is still among the very few in the region to have validated the knowledge and attitude questionnaire about PD for content and construct validity and reliability, consolidating the validity of the results and decreasing information bias. In addition to this, the data were collected from districts all over Lebanon, which allowed us to assess the knowledge and attitude of the entire Lebanese community.

Conclusions

In this study, we were able to develop and validate scales related to knowledge and attitude of the general population towards Parkinson disease. This study also revealed low levels of knowledge and high levels of positive attitude towards PD in Lebanon. Several factors were shown to be associated with higher knowledge and attitude scores, including gender, education level, profession, knowing someone with the disease, household crowding index, social class, source of information and contents of these sources, reflecting the need to consider those when implementing targeted corrective actions. Although participants displayed an overall positive attitude towards individuals with PD, the lack of knowledge of PD often made it difficult for participants to have a clear opinion on some of the statements as indicated by them selecting the “neutral” option for these specific statements. Future studies can focus on the development of informational and educational programs and campaigns to improve public awareness and individual knowledge about PD symptoms and treatment and reduce societal and cultural misconceptions about different aspects of PD. Educational sessions can include routine weekly or monthly conferences, seminars and webinars all over Lebanon in association with different health authorities such as the ministry of health in Lebanon or the ministry of education or the order of pharmacists of Lebanon and in association with different PD foundations in Lebanon and all over the world. PD educational material can be incorporated among schools and universities. Posters and flyers with information about PD can be distributed during PD or other neurological disorder conferences. Campaigns can also be organized specially during World Parkinson’s Day to raise awareness of everything that impacts people with Parkinson’s and those who care for and work with them.This may ultimately result in a greater help-seeking behavior among PD patients, which in turn may reduce diagnostic delays and treatment disparities leading to improved quality of life of PD patients and their care givers.
