The association between social drivers of health, HIV status and physical activity with perceived stress in U.S. women with or without HIV
Authors: Deborah Konkle-Parker, Katherine Hall, Jung Hye Sung, Masoumeh Karimi, Jenni M. Wise, Andrew Edmonds, Anjali Sharma, Sheri D Weiser, Ilene Brill, Maria L. Alcaide, Marlene Camacho-Rivera, Aruna Chandran, Mardge Cohen, Amanda Blair Spence, Gina Wingood, Melissa Klamm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe physiological adaptation to chronic stress can have deleterious health effects. Although higher physical activity (PA) has been linked to lower stress, it is unknown whether social driver of health (SDOH) burden and HIV status impact the stress reduction effects of PA due to their unique contributions to stress.MethodsUsing a cross-sectional sample (n = 444) from the Women’s Interagency HIV Study during 2014–2019, multivariate linear regression modeling was used to estimate the effect of SDOH burden and PA on stress. SDOH burden was defined as the sum of individual-level indicators: inadequate housing, income, and education as well as unemployment, and food insecurity.ResultsUnadjusted, there was a significant relationship between SDOH burden, Heavy PA and Moderate PA with stress (rs = 0.22 [p < .001], -0.10 [p < .05], and − 0.11 [p < .05] respectively) After adjusting for age, HIV status and race, and including both SDOH and PA, only SDOH predicted stress (β = 0.20, 95% CI [0.6, 1.87]; p < .001). Among this sample, food insecurity was the strongest predictor of stress, reducing the effects of PA when included in the model (β = 0.24, 95% CI [2.38, 5.99]; p < .001).ConclusionsFindings indicated that, after adjusting for age, HIV status and race, SDOH burden reduced the association between PA and stress, and was a stronger predictor of stress in women living with HIV than those living without HIV. The impact of food insecurity appears to be a powerful driver of stress in both women living with and without HIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24919-y.

Background

Health disparities are evident between racial groups, as well as between people living in poverty and those who are more highly resourced [1–3]. Racial and ethnic minorities and those who are economically disadvantaged have higher rates of chronic diseases and lower life expectancies [1, 4–6]. It has been suggested that a chronically elevated stress response related to living with significant negative social drivers of health (SDOH), such as inadequate housing, income and education, is an explanation for these health disparities [7–11]. Some of the physiological impacts of chronic stressor exposure may include reduction in cognitive processes, development of new or worsening cardiovascular risks, alterations in gastrointestinal functions, and compromises in immune function [12]. Given the challenges of providing adequate housing and reducing poverty as well as other stressors associated with negative SDOH, it may be important to identify more immediate and effective interventions for individuals living in disadvantaged circumstances to reduce stress as a step toward reducing health disparities.

A substantial body of evidence also suggests that repeat exposures to stigma and discrimination across the lifespan reduce psychological well-being, impairing the body’s response to stress over time as measured by the allostatic load index, a biomarker of physiologic exhaustion due to chronic stress responses [13, 14]. Women living with HIV (WLWH) are at increased risk for exposure to structural and social DOH such as adverse childhood events, abuse, and poly-stigma, compared to men living with HIV and HIV-seronegative women [7, 15–18]. These chronic exposures to stressors place WLWH at increased risk for psychological distress, with studies documenting rates of chronic stress, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder among WLWH 4–5 times that of the general population [19, 20]. Culturally embedded stigma related to race, gender, socioeconomic status, and HIV status place WLWH at increased risk for experiencing unfair treatment and internalizing stress from their environment [18, 21, 22], therefore, there is a great need to study stress in women.

Physical activity (PA) interventions have been suggested as strategies to reduce stress. Animal and human studies have shown that PA can decrease or prevent stress at the biochemical and behavioral levels [23]. The evidence suggests that regular PA leads to stress resistance and resilience. Several studies have demonstrated that higher levels of PA correlate with lower levels of perceived and measured stress [24–28], though the results are not always consistent [25]. PA interventions have been found to reduce stress among those with chronic diseases as well. Kim and Joo [26] found that higher levels of PA were linked to the lowest stress levels among adults living with HIV.

However, there is scant literature providing information about whether PA interventions are effective in reducing stress in individuals with complex life circumstances. While PA may reduce stress in certain circumstances, it is unknown whether the stress specifically associated with negative SDOH such as poverty, poor education, food insecurity, unemployment, and inadequate housing, may be reduced by PA interventions.

SDOH burden, often measured as the Social Risk Score or Social Adaptability Index, has been used in other studies to explore associations with chronic diseases in children (asthma, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, obesity, biomarkers of inflammation) [29–31] and in adults (cardiovascular disease, medication nonadherence, gestational diabetes) [32–35]. There is a growing body of evidence that shows that the impact of having multiple social and structural risks have a synergistic effect on physiologic adaptation in addition to the effects of each of those factors individually.

The Socioecological Model is frequently used to understand the influence of environmental, interpersonal, and individual-level factors on health outcomes. The model helps to explain how individual-level factors are nested within the influence of environmental and interpersonal factors, and to identify the fundamental causes that drive outcomes of interest [36]. For example, structural and social factors (e.g., low education, low income, inadequate housing, food insecurity, unemployment) can be viewed as fundamental causes of the wear and tear caused by stress through their impact on interpersonal relationships (e.g., social support), psychological outcomes (e.g., stress and depression), and individual-level behaviors (e.g., physical activity, smoking, and substance use) [13, 37, 38].

Since the stress associated with negative SDOH is likely to be a strong component in the development of health disparities, it is important to establish whether SDOH burden influences the positive impact that PA may have on stress. This analysis examines the relationships between PA, SDOH burden, HIV status and stress among WLWH and women without HIV (WLWoH) to support the future development of PA interventions that are likely to be useful in different populations.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the Women’s Interagency HIV Study (WIHS). The WIHS, which began in 1993, was a multicenter, longitudinal, prospective cohort study to explore the impacts of HIV in women in the U.S [39–41]. , following almost 5,000 WLWH and demographically comparable WLWoH. The original WIHS sites were in major cities in the U.S. (Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago, New York City and Washington DC), and four Southern sites in Florida, North Carolina, Georgia, and Alabama/Mississippi (joint site) were added in 2013 to reflect the higher HIV incidence and prevalence in the southeastern US [39]. In 2019, WIHS combined with the similar and even more long-standing men’s study, the Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study (MACS), to become the MACS/WIHS Combined Cohort Study (MWCCS) [42]. In WIHS, participants attended semiannual study visits to provide interview, anthropometric, imaging, and laboratory data along with biological specimens. The study reported here includes data for 444 participants from all sites at their first WIHS visit during the period Oct 1, 2014 - Sept 30, 2019 where they had a Perceived Stress score (PSS-10) [43], Physical Activity Questionnaire (PAQ) [44], and demographic data in order to derive the SDOH burden score, all within a six-month period. Each participant provided informed consent for collection of data and publication from those data, without identifying information included.

Variables

The variable of interest was PA from the PAQ [44], a self-reported questionnaire that collects the frequency, duration, and pace of recreational activities or exercises, as well as household and yard activities. The PAQ is an 18-item self-reported questionnaire, asking about involvement in a specific physical activity for a certain length of time in any month in the past 12 months (e.g., “Did you jog or run in the past 12 months for at least one-hour total time in any month? ), and asking about the number of months that the participant did that activity. Activities explored included vigorous leisure or job-related activities and less vigorous activities, such as walking or playing golf. It also asks about leisure time behavior, watching television and light or major household chores, though those responses do not go into the scores used for this study. Intensity of each activity was predetermined in the scoring criteria as moderate or heavy activity, and a total score included the sum of the moderate and heavy activity. According to Jacobs, et al., “since the number of minutes of performance in any month is unknown and likely to vary substantially across activities, no interpretation as caloric expenditure per week is available, and the score is left unaltered in ‘Exercise Units’” [44–46] and are used as continuous variables.

An SDOH burden variable [33–35] was calculated by summing the following individual demographic variables (for each, 1 if present and 0 if absent): (1) unstable housing (including housing besides their own or their parent’s house, which included “staying in other’s house/apartment,” rooming/boarding/halfway house, shelter/welfare hotel, street, jail, drug/alcohol facility, or other), (2) low income (household income ≤ $12,000 per year), (3) low education (less than high school), (4) unemployment, and (5) any food insecurity from the 10-item US Household Food-Security Survey Module from the USDA, including marginal, low, and very low food security [47].

Stress was measured by the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). Those scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher perceived stress within the past month [43]. This will be referred to as “stress” henceforth, and used as a continuous variable. Sample questions include “In the last month, how often have you felt nervous and “stressed?” and “In the last month, how often have you felt difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them?”

The variables of absolute CD4 count (cells/mm3) and HIV viral load by polymerase chain reaction (viral copies/mL), with ≥ 200 copies as the level of detection, were used only to describe the HIV-prevalent population for this study.

Analyses controlled for age, race and HIV status, as these values could theoretically affect PA and/or SDOH and thus the relationship between the two. Age was used as a continuous variable, race as Black, White or Other, and HIV status as prevalent or negative.

Statistical analyses

We employed independent sample t-tests for continuous variables or chi-square tests for categorical variables to describe and compare socioeconomic and health characteristics of WLWH and WLWoH. For highly skewed data, we used a median. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the association between SDOH burden and stress as well as between PA and stress. Using stress as the dependent variable, multivariate linear regression was performed to assess how well the following factors predicted stress: (1) SDOH burden, (2) PA, (3) interaction between PA and SDOH burden, and (4) interaction between PA and HIV status. All models controlled for age, race, and HIV status as potential covariates. Due to minor departures from normal distribution of residuals, we tried bootstrapping the regression estimates and found very similar results. Given the differential impact of age on the association between PA and stress, we conducted an additional analysis including only participants aged 60 or younger. This is based on the assumption that moderate or heavy PA may not be common among older individuals, potentially affecting the relationship disproportionately. However, after removing these 31 participants, results remained largely unchanged, with a slight reduction in the model’s goodness of fit that might be caused by reduction in sample size for the model. Thus, we will report findings using the full participant sample, regardless of age. Additionally, we investigated how individual components of the SDOH burden score, combined with PA, predicted stress.

Results

Table 1 presents a comparison of sociodemographic and health characteristics among study participants, divided into WLWoH and WLWH. The total sample consisted of 444 participants with an average age of 50 years (SD = 7.5). The racial composition was primarily non-Hispanic Black (77%), followed by non-Hispanic White (11%), and other races (13%). Regarding physical activity, 26% of participants engaged in little to no activity, 49% in moderate activity, and 24.8% in moderate to vigorous activity (data not shown). The mean score on the Perceived Stress Scale was 13.97(SD = 8.5), indicating mild to moderate stress on average. Sixty-one participants (14%) reported unstable housing, 117 (32%) reported any food insecurity, 143 (32%) reported less than a high school education, 237 (54%) reported an annual household income < $12,000, and 301 (68%) reported current unemployment. Almost 40% (n = 141) of participants reported at least 3 SDOH burden indicators, with unemployment and low income being the most prevalent.

The average age was 50 years for both groups of WLWoH and WLWH, with no significant difference. Racially, the WLWH group had a significantly higher proportion of non-Hispanic Black individuals (76.9%) compared to WLWoH (45.7%). Physical activity levels and Perceived Stress Scale scores were similar across groups (p = .581 and p = .324, respectively). Regarding the Social Drivers of Health burden, there were no significant differences between the groups (p = .849). In the WLWH group, the average CD4 + cell count was 708 cells/mm³, and 73% had an undetectable HIV viral load, demonstrating effective management.

Among all participants, there was a positive unadjusted relationship between SDOH burden and perceived stress (rs = 0.22, p < .001). Moderate-intensity activity was significantly related to stress (rs = -0.11, p < .05) to a greater extent than Heavy-Intensity activity (rs = -0.10, p < .05). (Supplementary Table 1).

Using multivariate linear regression modeling, adjusting for age, race, and HIV status, SDOH burden significantly predicted stress (B = 1.14; β = 0.20, 95% CI [0.60,1.87]; p < .001), but Moderate PA or Heavy PA did not have a significant relationship (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals were used due to slight non-normality in residuals. SDOH was a significant predictor (B = 1.23, BCa 95% CI [0.65 ,1.89]), indicating that for each unit increase in SDOH, Perceived Stress Scale increases by 1.23 units holding other predictors constant.

Discussion

This study explored whether SDOH burden, individual SDOH factors, and HIV status affected the relationship between PA and stress in women with and without HIV. This information will help inform the understanding of mechanisms that cause stress, as well as the development of targeted PA interventions.

Among primarily middle-aged black women with moderate SDOH burdens, higher PA and lower SDOH burden were each associated with lower perceived stress, though when controlling for age, race and HIV status, the association with PA was diminished. When examining specific SDOH factors instead of overall SDOH burden, food insecurity explained almost all the SDOH burden variance associated with perceived stress.

From these data, WLWH and WLWoH did not differ in their reported levels of PA and SDOH burden (Table 1), but SDOH burden appeared to have a stronger relationship with stress in WLWH. These are important findings, indicating that women in different SDOH brackets and HIV status may respond differently to PA interventions to reduce stress. It appears that reduction in SDOH, and particularly food insecurity, is critical to reduce stress, particularly in WLWH. These analyses also indicate that attention to food insecurity will be the most powerful contribution to stress reduction in those women who are food insecure, more than PA interventions.

To our knowledge, our findings on the relationships between PA and SDOH burden with perceived stress are the first of their kind. This important contribution to the literature facilitates understanding of the mechanisms underlying stress among women, and will contribute to developing interventions to reduce stress in WLWH or WLWoH. This is important as women have been less typically represented in research, especially related to HIV [48].

Despite the importance of this contribution, there are limitations. Given the need to consider five levels of SDOH burden and both WLWH and WLWoH, the sample size may have been inadequate to demonstrate significant relationships between PA and stress.

The PSS-10 may not be suitable for measuring chronic stress, as the time period reflected in the scale is “over the past month.” A measure that asks about stress experienced over the past year may better measure chronic stress. In addition, this sample of women from the WIHS did not report particularly high levels of stress, although more than 40% reported at least three of the individual SDOH factors. Given this SDOH burden level, exploration into why the self-reported perceived stress did not seem to reflect this burden may be indicated, and potentially a measure that takes habituation to stress into account would be warranted. Although individuals may not self-report feeling stressed by these SDOH, there may be underlying physiological stress responses to not being able to afford needed items or inability to find or hold a job due to other constraints, for example. A study using a different stress measure or a different population of women who are less stable in their HIV management might have yielded different results. The same relationships may not be found in all WLWH, given that this sample had a mean age of 50, had mostly been living with HIV for years, and were in successful treatment. In addition, given that this is a cross-sectional study, causality in the relationships cannot be implied.

For SDOH burden, additional SDOH measures could have been included besides housing, poverty, low level of education, unemployment, and food insecurity, such as lack of health insurance, transportation deficits, rurality, and distance from a health care setting. In previous literature examining the effect of an individual’s Social Risk Score on health outcomes, factors such as social support and social strain [33], and living single [32, 35] were used in this score, though it did not include impactful factors such as housing, unemployment and food insecurity that were included in this study.

Another limitation is the roughly 93% of the variance in stress that is not explained by SDOH burden and physical activity, adjusting for age, race and HIV status. While there is statistical significance showing relationships between the variables, the relationships are weaker than expected. Further research is needed in identifying other phenomena that explain the variance in stress more completely, in order to explore interventions to reduce stress and its consequent health outcomes.

Future research is needed to explore the same relationships in men to see if they are similar. Additional work in exploring the effects of food insecurity in WLWH has already been done, and this study can be added to that collection [49–53], as well as continuing to explore its importance in all women in predicting stress. Exploring the contribution of other SDOH factors may give us a greater understanding of how specific SDOH work in driving stress responses. Additionally, a longitudinal analysis would help explore causal pathways between PA and stress in the presence of significant SDOH. In addition, intervention research is needed to focus on PA, food insecurity, and other measures to reduce chronic stress, therefore potentially decreasing the health disparities caused, in part, by chronic physiological stress response.

Conclusion

Our analyses revealed that the level of SDOH burden played an important role in predicting perceived stress in women living with or without HIV, to the point where PA was no longer a significant predictor when it was included in the model. This is especially the case in WLWH. Among the SDOHs that were explored, food insecurity emerged as the strongest driver of stress, indicating that it should be the first focus in interventions aimed at reducing stress.
