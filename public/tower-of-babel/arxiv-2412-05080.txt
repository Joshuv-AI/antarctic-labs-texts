Introduction

An irreducible projective variety
X
defined over a number field
K
is said to have potentially dense rational points, or sometimes simply to be “potentially dense (PD)”, if there is a finite extension
L
of
K
such that the set of
L
-points
X(L)
is Zariski-dense in
X
. For example,
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{K}
is potentially dense for any
K
; it follows that the unirational varieties are potentially dense.

It is also not difficult to prove that the abelian varieties are potentially dense (see for example Prop. 3.1 of
[
HT00
]
,
and also Prop. 2.11 of
[
ABR11
]
for an alternative, elementary and self-contained proof). On the contrary, by a celebrated theorem of
Faltings, curves of geometric genus at least two are not potentially dense. It is generally believed and fits into
Lang–Vojta’s conjectural framework that the smooth projective varieties of general type are not potentially dense, whereas the varieties with negative or trivial canonical bundle are. Campana conjectures that potentially dense varieties are exactly the special varieties in the sense of
[
Cam04
]
(the class of special varieties includes all rationally connected varieties and all varieties of zero Kodaira dimension). But these conjectures are difficult to verify even in dimension two.

For instance, we still do not know whether a general K3 surface is potentially dense. The potential density has been obtained by Bogomolov and Tschinkel for elliptic K3 surfaces, and also for K3 surfaces with infinite automorphism group (
[
BT00
]
). But no example of a potentially dense (or of a not potentially dense) K3 surface with Picard number one is available. Only one example of a family of simply-connected varieties with trivial canonical bundle, such that a sufficiently general member has Picard number one and is potentially dense, is known: such are the varieties of lines contained in cubics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
(
[
AV08
]
,
[
ABR11
]
). These varieties are deformations of the second punctual Hilbert scheme of certain K3 surfaces. This family is complete in the sense that it exhausts all smooth polarized deformations of its general member. So far, this example remains unique.

Potential density of the
k
-th punctual Hilbert scheme
S^{[k]}

of a K3 surface
S
has been studied by Hassett and Tschinkel in
[
HT00
]
. Roughly speaking,
they prove that
S^{[k]}
is potentially dense when it admits (birationally) an abelian fibration. In particular,
when
S\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{g}
is a K3 surface of genus
g
, then
S^{[g]}
is potentially dense (indeed it is birational to the bundle
of
\operatorname{Pic}^{g}
of its hyperplane sections).

Take a degree 6 K3 surface

Proposition 2.1
.

The surface
S
has
(-2)
-curves. This surface is not elliptic. The variety
X
is not rationally fibered in abelian threefolds.

Proof:

The class
(2,-1)^{\operatorname{T}}
is an example of a

(-2)

has
(-2)
-curves. If the surface

Proposition 2.3
.

There are two very ample classes
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
on the surface
S
with self-intersection 6.

Proof:

We repeat the argument of
[

]
. Denote by

and
h_{2}
the classes of line bundles corresponding to the classes
(1,0)^{\operatorname{T}}
and
(0,1)^{\operatorname{T}}

Now, we show that the class

and

It remains to show that classes

-curve class

]

Lemma 2.4
.

The classes
2h_{1}-h_{2}
and
2h_{2}-h_{1}
correspond to

-curves.

Proof:

It is enough to prove the statement for the class

. Since
(2h_{1}-h_{2})^{2}=-2

2h_{1}-h_{2}
or the class
-(2h_{1}-h_{2})
is effective. The class

is ample and
(2h_{1}-h_{2})\cdot h_{1}=4
, so the class

is effective and corresponds to some curve

is not irreducible or not reduced. Then we can write

Proposition 2.5
.

There exist K3 surfaces
S
defined over a number field, with
\rho(S)=2
and the intersection form

as in (

).

Proof:

Such a K3 surface over

is a general member of a component of the Noether-Lefschetz locus of the family of intersections of a quadric and a cubic in

Birational automorphism and its invariant divisors

The classes
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
define two embeddings
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
as an intersection of a quadric and a cubic. Two embeddings induce two Beauville involutions
\iota_{1},\iota_{2}:S^{[3]}\dashrightarrow S^{[3]}
, see
[
Bea82
]
. Recall that a Beauville involution of
S^{[3]}
sends a general triple of points to the residual triple in the intersection of
S
and the plane through the triple. Denote by
H_{1},H_{2}\in\operatorname{NS}(X)\simeq\operatorname{NS}(S)\oplus\mathbb{Z}E
the classes corresponding to
h_{1},h_{2}\in\operatorname{NS}(S)
respectively. The induced morphism
\iota_{k}:\operatorname{NS}(X)\to\operatorname{NS}(X)

(k\in\{1,2\})
is opposite to the reflection in the hyperplane
(H_{k}-E)^{\perp}
(
[
Deb84
]

\iota_{k}
is given by

\iota_{k}^{*}(L)=-L+2\frac{q(L,H_{k}-E)}{q(H_{k}-E,H_{k}-E)}(H_{k}-E)=-L+q(L,H_{k}-E)(H_{k}-E).

In the basis
\{H_{1},E,H_{2}\}
the operators
\iota_{1}^{*},\iota_{2}^{*}
are given by matrices

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}5&4&8\\
-6&-5&-8\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\
-8&-5&-6\\
8&4&5\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

The following construction is described in
[
Bea82
]
. Denote by
\tilde{\Pi}_{k}
the indeterminacy set of the involution
\iota_{k}
. The set
\tilde{\Pi}_{k}
consists of length 3 subschemes contained in a line. The image of
S
under the embedding given by
h_{k}
is defined by the intersection of the quadric
Q_{k}
and some cubic hypersurface. Any line intersecting
S
in at least 3 points is contained in
Q_{k}
, so the indeterminacy set is parametrized by the lines in
Q_{k}
, i.e.
\tilde{\Pi}_{k}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{3}
. The involution
\iota_{k}
is biregular on the set
X\setminus\tilde{\Pi}_{k}
. Now, denote by
\epsilon_{k}:\widehat{X}_{k}\to X
the blow-up of
X
in
\tilde{\Pi}_{k}
. The exceptional divisor
E_{k}
of this blow-up is isomorphic to the projectivization of the conormal bundle
\mathbb{P}\left(N^{*}_{X/\tilde{\Pi}_{k}}\right)
. Since
\Pi_{k}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{3}
and is lagrangian, conormal is the same as tangent, and

\mathbb{P}\left(N^{*}_{X/\tilde{\Pi}_{k}}\right)\simeq\big\{(x,H)\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\times\left(\mathbb{P}^{3}\right)^{\vee}\big\}.

The map
\iota_{k}
extends to a biregular involution of
\widehat{X}_{k}
which maps

Define a birational automorphism

f=\iota_{2}\circ\iota_{1}:X\dashrightarrow X.

Proposition 3.1
.

1) The action of
f^{*}
on
\operatorname{NS}(X)
in the basis
\{H_{1},E,H_{2}\}
is given by the matrix

M_{1}M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}27&12&16\\
-18&-7&-10\\
-8&-4&-5\end{pmatrix}.

2)
(f^{n})^{*}=\left(f^{*}\right)^{n}

Proof:

The first part of the proposition is the equality
(\iota_{2}\circ\iota_{1})^{*}=\iota_{1}^{*}\circ\iota_{2}^{*}
, which holds because birational automorphisms of varieties with trivial canonical bundle contract no hypersurfaces. The second part is true by the same reason. ∎

The eigenvalues of the operator

are
1,7-4\sqrt{3},7+4\sqrt{3}
with eigenvectors

2H_{1}-7E+2H_{2},\,(-2+\sqrt{3})H_{1}+(1-\sqrt{3})E+H_{2},\,(-2-\sqrt{3})H_{1}+(1+\sqrt{3})E+H_{2}

.

The map

is not induced by any birational automorphism of

The only invariant divisors on

In this section, we find the closure of the cone of effective curves
NE_{1}(X)
(viewed as a part of the real Néron-Severi group by means of
q
) and the ample cone
\operatorname{Amp}(X)
of
X
. Recall that by Kleiman’s criterion, the divisor
D
is ample if and only if it is strictly positive on the closure of
NE_{1}(X)
.

The main tool for the computation is the classification of extremal rays established in
[
HT10
]
and
[

]

Lemma 4.1
.

\overline{\operatorname{NE}_{1}(X)}=\big\langle E,H_{1}-\frac{3}{2}E,H_{2}-\frac{3}{2}E,2H_{1}-H_{2}-\frac{1}{2}E,2H_{2}-H_{1}-\frac{1}{2}E\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}.

Proof:

Let
R=xH_{1}+yH_{2}+zE
be a primitive element of
N_{1}(X)
.
By
[
HT10
]
and
[
HT16
]
the ray generated by
R
is an extremal ray of
\overline{\operatorname{NE}_{1}(X)}
if and only if it satisfies one of the following properties

•

q(R,R)=-2,q(R,H_{1})\geq 0
;

•

q(R,R)=-4,q(R,H_{1})\geq 0
, and
x,y
are both divisible by 2 but not by for 4;

•

q(R,R)=-4,q(R,H_{1})\geq 0
, and
x,y
are both divisible by 4;

•

q(R,R)=-12,q(R,H_{1})\geq 0
, and
x,y
are both divisible by 2;

•

q(R,R)=-36,q(R,H_{1})\geq 0
, and
x,y
are divisible by 4.

It can be easily checked that the classes given in the statement of this lemma satisfy one of the properties above.

Now, assume that
R
generates an extremal ray. If
q(R,H_{1})=0
, then
(x,y)=(4t,3t)
for some
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus
(R,R)=-42t^{2}-4z^{2}
which is possible only for
R=E
. Therefore, we may assume that
R
intersects positively with both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. Now, write

\big\langle E,H_{1}-\frac{3}{2}E,H_{2}-\frac{3}{2}E,2H_{1}-H_{2}-\frac{1}{2}E,2H_{2}-H_{1}-\frac{1}{2}E\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}=\\
=\big\{xH_{1}+yH_{2}+zE\,:\,x+2y\geq 0,2y+x\geq 0,3x+5y+2z\geq 0,3x+5y+2z\geq 0,\\
3x+3y+2z\geq 0\big\}.

Let
R=xH_{1}+yH_{2}+zE
be an extremal class.
We need to check that numbers
x,y,z
satisfy all five inequalities above. By symmetry, it is enough to prove inequalities
x+2y\geq 0,5x+3y+2z\geq 0
and
3x+3y+2z\geq 0
. Observe that
\frac{3}{2}q(R,R)=t^{2}-7y^{2}-4z^{2}
, where
t=3x+4y>0
.

1.

Suppose that
x+2y<0
. Thus,

and we get

\frac{3}{2}q(R,R)=t^{2}-7y^{2}-4z^{2}<-3y^{2}-4z^{2}.

Recall that

, so there are finitely many such

and it can be shown by brute force that in fact there are no such

.

2.

Suppose that

. Thus,

\frac{3}{2}q(R,R)=t^{2}-7y^{2}-6z^{2}<6y^{2}-4yz-2z^{2}\leq-2y^{2}-z^{2}.

By the same argument, it can be checked that there are no such
R
.

3.

Suppose that
3x+3y+2z<0
. Thus,
t<2z-y
and we get

\frac{3}{2}q(R,R)=t^{2}-7y^{2}-6z^{2}<6y^{2}+4yz-2z^{2}\leq-2y^{2}-z^{2}.

This case is analogous to the previous one. ∎

Remark 4.4
.

From this description of the ample cone one can deduce that
X
has only finitely many automorphisms.
Indeed the projectivization of the cone of classes of positive square in the real Néron-Severi group can be seen as a model of the hyperbolic space
\operatorname{H}
. The projectivized ample cone of
X
inside
\operatorname{H}
is rational polyhedral of finite volume. According to
[
AV16
]
, its quotient by
Aut(X)
is one of finitely many pieces (of strictly positive volume) of the quotient orbifold of

\operatorname{H}
by the monodromy group, hence
Aut(X)
must be finite.

This explains the necessity to work with a birational, rather than a biregular, automorphism, in spite of some technical difficulties. The following proposition shall be useful in dealing with them.

Proposition 4.5
.

The intersection of
