Blood glucose trajectory phenotypes are associated with in-hospital mortality in a multicenter ICU stroke cohort
Authors: Juan Wang, Hai-Bo Li, Wen-Juan Li, Man-Man Xu, Chun-Hua Hang, Peng-Lai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-traumatic stroke (NTS) is associated with high mortality, and dynamic glucose patterns may provide prognostic information beyond static glucose measurements. This study aimed to identify glucose trajectory phenotypes in ICU patients with NTS and evaluate their association with in-hospital mortality. This multicenter retrospective cohort study included ICU patients with NTS from MIMIC-IV, eICU, and NSICU. Glucose trajectories during the first 168 h after ICU admission were identified using latent class growth modeling. Associations with in-hospital mortality were assessed using Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier analysis, competing-risk analysis, and subgroup analyses. Incremental predictive value was evaluated using AUC, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and median improvement in predicted risk. Among 11,177 patients, four glucose trajectory phenotypes were identified: normoglycemia (NG, n = 5,515), stable mild hyperglycemia (SMH, n = 3,425), persistent hyperglycemia (PH, n = 2,080), and highly variable hyperglycemia (HVH, n = 157). Compared with NG, SMH, PH, and HVH were progressively associated with higher in-hospital mortality in the fully adjusted model, with HRs of 1.668 (95% CI: 1.469–1.894), 2.406 (95% CI: 2.083–2.779), and 5.766 (95% CI: 4.319–7.699), respectively. HVH remained associated with increased mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Adding glucose trajectories to baseline prediction models modestly improved AUC, IDI, NRI, and predicted risk classification across LASSO, Boruta, and BSS-BIC models. In ICU patients with NTS, glucose trajectory phenotypes were associated with different risks of in-hospital mortality, with HVH showing the strongest association. Glucose trajectories may provide additional prognostic information for risk stratification beyond static glucose measurements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56298-8.

Introduction

Non-traumatic stroke (NTS), including ischemic stroke and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), refers to cerebrovascular events caused by non-traumatic factors1. According to Global Burden of Disease data, stroke is the third leading cause of death globally, with ICH accounting for approximately 28.8% of all cases2. This condition places a substantial burden on global health, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources. Despite advancements in acute stroke treatment, mortality and long-term prognosis remain closely associated with glycemic status3.

Effective blood glucose management is critical for stroke patients. Hyperglycemia during the acute phase is strongly linked to stroke occurrence, progression, and long-term mortality4,5. Conversely, hypoglycemia can worsen stroke outcomes by exacerbating neuronal injury and impairing brain function, underscoring the importance of managing both extremes in stroke treatment6,7. Importantly, glycemic instability is increasingly viewed as part of a broader cardiometabolic and vascular risk continuum rather than an isolated glucose abnormality8,9. Traditional blood glucose management primarily relies on static measurements, such as blood glucose at admission10. However, these methods fail to account for dynamic fluctuations in blood glucose over time, which can significantly impact patient outcomes. Existing stroke prediction models mainly utilize single glucose measurements, overlooking dynamic glucose changes during hospitalization and their prognostic implications.

Advances in glucose monitoring technologies have brought increasing attention to concepts such as glucose variability (GV), stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in blood glucose management11,12. Although dynamic parameters like GV and SHR provide insights into a patient’s metabolic state and treatment response, they often fail to fully capture disease progression complexity and continuous blood glucose fluctuations over time. In contrast, glucose trajectories, defined as dynamic patterns of blood glucose changes over time, offer a novel approach that more accurately reflects a patient’s disease progression, metabolic shifts, and treatment responses. While existing studies have examined GV’s impact on stroke outcomes13,14, systematic analyses of glucose trajectories remain limited, particularly in ICU settings, where multi-center, large-scale data are lacking.

With advances in serial glucose measurement and critical care data integration, glucose trajectories may provide additional prognostic information for patients with NTS. This study aimed to identify glucose trajectory phenotypes in ICU patients with NTS and evaluate their association with in-hospital mortality using multicenter datasets.

Methods

Database source

This study used data from three major ICU databases: MIMIC-IV v3.1 (2008–2022), eICU CRD v2.0 (2014–2015), and NSICU (2024–2025). The MIMIC-IV database provides detailed ICU admission data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston15, while the eICU database includes data from hospitals across the United States16. Both databases are publicly available and de-identified. The NSICU dataset, sourced from the Neurosurgical ICU at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, includes clinical records of NTS patients.

Study population

Adult patients diagnosed with NTS upon ICU admission in the MIMIC-IV, eICU, and NSICU databases were eligible for inclusion. The selection process is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Patients were excluded if they had ICU LOS < 1 day, age < 18 years, missing outcome data, fewer than three glucose measurements during the index ICU stay, or brain injury due to other causes such as traumatic injury, tumors, or toxic encephalopathy. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU admission of the first hospitalization was retained, and subsequent ICU admissions were excluded.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using standardized protocols to improve consistency across the MIMIC-IV, eICU, and NSICU datasets. MIMIC-IV and eICU data were retrieved using database-specific extraction scripts, whereas NSICU data were extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system and harmonized using predefined variable definitions. Extracted variables included demographics, admission characteristics, stroke subtype, physiological parameters, laboratory tests, comorbidities, neurological severity scores, treatment and monitoring interventions, discharge disposition, length of stay, and in-hospital mortality. Baseline variables were extracted within the first 24 h after ICU admission unless otherwise specified. GLT was defined as insulin or other glucose-lowering medication administered within the first 24 h, and ICP-derived variables were summarized only among patients who underwent ICP monitoring. The complete variable list is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Missingness of baseline covariates was assessed before imputation and is reported in Supplementary Table 2. Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 10 imputed datasets, and estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Imputation diagnostics included comparisons of observed and imputed distributions, comparisons of observed and completed datasets, and trace plots across iterations, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 2–4.

Latent class growth modeling for glucose trajectory analysis

Latent Class Growth Modeling (LCGM) was used to identify distinct temporal patterns in glucose dynamics among critically ill patients, assuming heterogeneous glucose trajectories within the population17,18. Blood glucose measurements after ICU admission were summarized into 12-hour windows and analyzed over three observation periods: 72, 120, and 168 h. The 168-hour window was used as the primary analysis, whereas the 72- and 120-hour windows were used for sensitivity analyses. Time zero was defined as ICU admission, and the first valid glucose measurement after ICU admission was recorded as baseline glucose. Patients were not required to have glucose measurements in every 12-hour window, and trajectory models used available glucose data within each observation period. All glucose values were standardized to milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL), and physiologically implausible values (< 30 mg/dL or > 600 mg/dL) were treated as missing data. The distribution and temporal availability of valid glucose data are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Models specifying 2 to 6 latent classes were tested using polynomial functions to define trajectory patterns. Model selection considered statistical fit indices, including the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and log-likelihood (LL), as well as entropy, class proportions, average posterior probabilities, clinical interpretability, and reproducibility across cohorts. The primary trajectory model was fitted in the merged cohort, and database-specific models were used to assess reproducibility across cohorts.

Feature selection and model comparison

Feature selection was performed using three approaches: LASSO regression, Boruta, and BIC-based best subset selection (BSS-BIC)19–21. LASSO used L1 regularization for variable selection, Boruta used a random forest-based feature importance framework, and BSS-BIC selected the feature subset with the lowest BIC to balance model fit and complexity. Glucose trajectory phenotypes derived from LCGM were then added to each baseline model to evaluate their incremental predictive value. Three trajectory-enhanced models were constructed: LASSO + trajectory, Boruta + trajectory, and BSS-BIC + trajectory. Model performance was compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and median improvement in predicted risk in the merged cohort.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software and the Free Statistics platform. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons across glucose trajectory groups were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted using Tukey’s test or Bonferroni correction when applicable. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations of glucose trajectories and glucose-related metrics with in-hospital mortality. Follow-up started at ICU admission and ended at in-hospital death or hospital discharge; patients discharged alive were censored at discharge. Glucose trajectory phenotypes and each glucose-related metric were evaluated in separate models. Multivariable adjustment was based on prior evidence, clinical relevance, and change-in-estimate and collinearity diagnostics, with GLT and ICP monitoring included as clinically relevant covariates. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare survival across trajectory groups. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and time-stratified Cox models were performed when appropriate. Fine–Gray models were used to account for discharge alive as a competing event. E-values were calculated to assess robustness to potential unmeasured confounding.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to age, hypertension, diabetes, stroke subtype, and initial GCS severity, with interaction tests used to assess effect modification. Sensitivity analyses included database-specific analyses and alternative glucose observation windows.

Results

A total of 23,672 ICU patients with NTS were initially identified, including 10,037 from MIMIC-IV, 12,181 from eICU, and 1,454 from NSICU. After applying the exclusion criteria, the final cohort included 11,177 patients: 4,964 from MIMIC-IV, 4,808 from eICU, and 1,405 from NSICU (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Identification and classification of blood glucose patterns

LCGM was applied to glucose values summarized in 12-hour windows over the first 168 h after ICU admission. Models with 2 to 6 latent classes were compared using model fit and classification metrics (Supplementary Table 3). In the merged cohort, BIC decreased from the 2-class to the 4-class model (782754.30, 781654.65, and 780732.75), showed only a small additional decrease in the 5-class model (780691.63), and increased in the 6-class model (781450.52). The 4-class model had moderate entropy (0.65) and acceptable class-specific average posterior probabilities (0.75–0.89). It was therefore selected as the primary solution by balancing model fit, classification quality, class size, interpretability, and reproducibility across cohorts.

The four trajectory phenotypes in the merged cohort and their corresponding patterns across individual cohorts are shown in Fig. 1. They were labelled as normoglycemia (NG), stable mild hyperglycemia (SMH), persistent hyperglycemia (PH), and highly variable hyperglycemia (HVH) according to their relative glucose levels and temporal patterns. In the merged cohort, the NG, SMH, PH, and HVH groups included 5,515, 3,425, 2,080, and 157 patients, respectively. Similar trajectory patterns were observed in MIMIC-IV, eICU, and NSICU. Sensitivity analyses using shorter observation windows showed broadly consistent four-trajectory patterns over the first 72 and 120 h after ICU admission (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7).

Clinical characteristics of the four glucose trajectories

Clinical characteristics differed across the four glucose trajectory phenotypes (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 4). Compared with NG, the SMH, PH, and HVH groups were generally older and had higher BMI, heart rate, diabetes prevalence, hypertension prevalence, Charlson score, WBC, BUN, INR, and APS III score, as well as lower albumin levels. The HVH group showed the highest heart rate, BUN, WBC, INR, and APS III score, and the highest proportions of mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use, but lower proportions of craniotomy and embolization. Renal dysfunction was more prominent in PH and HVH, as reflected by higher creatinine levels and higher proportions of renal disease and dialysis, whereas mannitol use was more common in hyperglycemic trajectory groups.

Association between glucose trajectories and in-hospital mortality

Covariates for multivariable Cox models were selected based on prior evidence, clinical relevance, and change-in-estimate and collinearity diagnostics (Supplementary Table 5). The proportional hazards assumption for Cox models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals (Supplementary Fig. 9). In the fully adjusted Cox model, glucose trajectory phenotypes were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in the merged cohort (Table 2). Compared with NG, the SMH, PH, and HVH groups showed progressively higher mortality risk, with HRs of 1.668 (95% CI: 1.469–1.894), 2.406 (95% CI: 2.083–2.779), and 5.766 (95% CI: 4.319–7.699), respectively. In separate models, baseline glucose, mean glucose, and CV of glucose were also associated with mortality, with HRs of 1.003 (95% CI: 1.002–1.003), 1.008 (95% CI: 1.007–1.009), and 2.022 (95% CI: 1.308–3.125), respectively. E-values suggested that the observed trajectory associations, particularly for HVH, were relatively robust to potential unmeasured confounding (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 10).

Supplementary Fig. 8 shows differences in glucose metrics, GCS scores, ICP metrics, and discharge disposition across trajectory groups. Glucose metrics were higher in hyperglycemic trajectory groups, particularly in PH and HVH. GCS at discharge and mean GCS were lower in hyperglycemic trajectory groups, whereas ICP metrics did not differ significantly among patients with ICP monitoring. Discharge disposition also varied across groups, with higher proportions of acute/intensive care discharge in PH and HVH and a higher proportion of home care/recovery in NG.

Kaplan–Meier curves showed clear survival separation across trajectory groups in the merged cohort and individual cohorts, with NG showing the highest survival probability and HVH the lowest (Fig. 2). Database-specific analyses showed similar risk gradients in MIMIC-IV and eICU; in NSICU, HVH remained significantly associated with mortality after full adjustment, whereas SMH and PH were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 6). Additional analyses using Fine–Gray competing-risk models and time-stratified Cox models showed broadly consistent results, with stronger early associations for HVH (Supplementary Table 7, Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Tables 8, and Supplementary Fig. 12).

Glucose trajectories and mortality stratified by diabetes status

Subgroup analyses showed a significant interaction by diabetes status (P for interaction = 0.014), whereas interactions for age, hypertension, stroke subtype, and initial GCS severity were not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 13). LCGM was therefore refitted separately in patients with and without diabetes to examine diabetes-specific glucose trajectory patterns. The four trajectory phenotypes within each diabetes stratum are shown in Fig. 3, and the corresponding model fit indices are provided in Supplementary Table 9. The four-class structure remained clinically consistent with the primary analysis. PH and HVH accounted for larger proportions of patients with diabetes than those without diabetes (38.80% vs. 10.39% for PH; 2.04% vs. 0.99% for HVH).

In diabetes-stratified Cox analyses based on these diabetes-specific trajectory phenotypes, the association between glucose trajectories and in-hospital mortality was stronger in patients without diabetes (Supplementary Table 10). Among patients with diabetes, PH and HVH remained significantly associated with mortality in the fully adjusted model, with HRs of 1.390 (95% CI: 1.046–1.849) and 4.814 (95% CI: 2.813–8.236), respectively, whereas SMH was not statistically significant. Among patients without diabetes, SMH, PH, and HVH were all significantly associated with mortality, with fully adjusted HRs of 1.735 (95% CI: 1.508–1.996), 2.710 (95% CI: 2.301–3.191), and 5.991 (95% CI: 4.257–8.432), respectively.

Incremental predictive value of glucose trajectories

Feature selection for mortality prediction was performed using three strategies: LASSO, Boruta, and BSS-BIC, with the corresponding selection procedures and model comparisons shown in Supplementary Figs. 14–16. Glucose trajectory phenotypes were then added to each baseline model to evaluate their incremental predictive value. Compared with the corresponding baseline models, the trajectory-enhanced models showed modest but statistically significant increases in AUC, from 0.799 to 0.811 for LASSO, from 0.812 to 0.822 for Boruta, and from 0.808 to 0.820 for BSS-BIC. Consistent improvements were also observed for IDI, continuous NRI, and median improvement in predicted risk across the three modeling strategies, with all comparisons reaching statistical significance (all P < 0.001; Table 3).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of ICU patients with NTS, we identified four glucose trajectory phenotypes: NG, SMH, PH, and HVH. These trajectories were progressively associated with in-hospital mortality, with HVH showing the highest risk. Diabetes status modified trajectory distribution and risk estimates, although HVH remained associated with increased mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Adding trajectory phenotypes to baseline mortality prediction models provided modest but statistically significant incremental value. Overall, longitudinal glucose patterns may offer prognostic information beyond static glucose measurements in critically ill patients with NTS. The moderate entropy likely reflects partial overlap among adjacent glycemic phenotypes, but posterior probabilities, cross-cohort reproducibility, and consistent risk gradients supported clinically meaningful classification.

The association between hyperglycemia and adverse stroke outcomes has been well documented. Prior studies have shown that elevated admission glucose is associated with increased mortality, poorer functional recovery after endovascular treatment, and higher risk of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage after thrombolysis4,10. Our finding that PH was associated with higher in-hospital mortality is consistent with this evidence and extends it by indicating that sustained hyperglycemia over time, rather than admission glucose alone, carries prognostic information in ICU patients with NTS. In contrast, we did not identify a distinct hypoglycemic trajectory, and the prognostic role of hypoglycemia in critically ill stroke patients remains uncertain22,23.

Traditional glycemic markers, such as HbA1c and SHR, have limitations in capturing acute glucose dynamics during stroke. HbA1c reflects average glycemic exposure over weeks24,25, whereas SHR mainly represents a relative stress response at a specific time point26,27. Short-term glucose fluctuations and GV have been associated with clinical deterioration, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and adverse cardiovascular or stroke outcomes11,28–30.

. In our study, CV of glucose was also associated with in-hospital mortality, supporting the relevance of longitudinal glucose instability. However, GV still compresses complex temporal information into a single summary measure. Trajectory-based modeling captures the level, persistence, direction, and variability of glucose changes over time, thereby providing a more comprehensive description of glycemic instability during critical illness18,31.

The association of PH and HVH with increased mortality may be partly explained by metabolic and vascular mechanisms. Prolonged hyperglycemia and marked glucose fluctuations can increase reactive oxygen species production, impair nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, and promote vascular inflammation and oxidative injury32–34. These adverse glycemic patterns may also accelerate atherogenic processes through lipid oxidation and protein kinase C-dependent pathways35. In addition, pronounced glycemic instability may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and pathological vascular remodeling36. From a broader cardiometabolic and vascular perspective, these mechanisms are consistent with metabolic signaling disturbance and panvascular vulnerability associated with sustained metabolic stress37,38.

Diabetes status modified both the distribution and risk estimates of glucose trajectories. Patients with diabetes more frequently exhibited PH and HVH, consistent with greater baseline metabolic dysregulation. However, HVH remained associated with increased mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, suggesting that acute glycemic instability may provide prognostic information beyond diabetes status alone. The stronger risk gradient observed in non-diabetic patients may reflect the greater clinical significance of stress-related hyperglycemia or abrupt glucose dysregulation in individuals without known chronic hyperglycemia.

The incremental predictive value of glucose trajectories was modest but consistent across modeling strategies. Adding trajectory phenotypes to LASSO, Boruta, and BSS-BIC models improved AUC, IDI, continuous NRI, and median improvement in predicted risk. These findings suggest that trajectory information may provide additional prognostic information beyond conventional clinical variables and static glucose measurements. However, the observed improvements should be interpreted as incremental risk stratification rather than evidence that trajectory-guided management improves outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding may remain despite multivariable adjustment, including potential confounding related to GLT and other ICU treatments. Second, because trajectory modeling requires repeated glucose measurements, the study population may overrepresent patients with more intensive monitoring or greater illness severity; however, sensitivity analyses using alternative observation windows showed broadly consistent trajectory patterns. Third, because all cohorts included ICU patients, the findings may not be generalizable to non-ICU stroke populations; moreover, the smaller single-center NSICU cohort may limit external validity across healthcare systems, which requires further validation. Finally, long-term functional outcomes were unavailable, and the identified trajectories may depend on glucose sampling frequency and data granularity39.

Conclusion

In this multicenter cohort of ICU patients with NTS, we identified four glucose trajectory phenotypes associated with different risks of in-hospital mortality. Hyperglycemic trajectories were associated with higher mortality risk, particularly PH and HVH, with HVH showing the strongest association in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients. Glucose trajectories provided additional prognostic information beyond static glucose measurements and modestly improved mortality prediction, supporting their potential value for risk stratification in critically ill patients with NTS.
