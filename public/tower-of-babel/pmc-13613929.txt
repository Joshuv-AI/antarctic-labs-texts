Occurrence Patterns and Controlling Mechanisms of In Situ Gas Content in Varied Marine Siliceous Shale Subfacies: A Case Study of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang Syncline, Northern Guizhou, China
Authors: Ming Xie, Shaobin Guo, Jiliang Yu, Deqiang Sun, Hailong Li, Gangquan Li, Xinbing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Significant discrepancies in in situ gas content and occurrence characteristics exist among different siliceous shale subfacies of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation, yet the underlying controlling mechanisms remain poorly understood, particularly in normal-pressure shale gas reservoirs. This study systematically investigated the gas-bearing heterogeneity and its dominant controls in the Anchang syncline, a representative normal-pressure block in northern Guizhou. A suite of laboratory analyses, including TOC determination, XRD mineralogy, FE-SEM, low-temperature N2 adsorption, and high-pressure methane adsorption, were employed. The results indicate that siliceous shales are divided into mixed-siliceous shale facies (S-2) and clay-bearing siliceous shale facies (S-4). S-2 is dominated by organic matter pores, exhibiting a higher total pore volume and specific surface area. In contrast, S-4 features abundant clay-related inorganic pores with underdeveloped organic pores. S-2 demonstrates superior methane adsorption capacity, with an average maximum absolute adsorption capacity of 3.676 cm3/g compared to 3.456 cm3/g for S-4. The TOC content and hydrophilic clay mineral proportion are the primary controls on adsorption capacity. SDR model results reveal that S-2 has a higher average in situ total gas content of 4.293 cm3/g with an adsorbed gas ratio of 79.27%, while S-4 averages 3.622 cm3/g with an adsorbed gas ratio of 87.47% and limited free gas. Gas-bearing properties are synergistically controlled by TOC, pore structure, mineral composition, and water saturation. Crucially, the higher water saturation of S-4, driven by its clay-rich composition, allows water to easily invade pores under normal-pressure conditions, weakening capillary sealing and causing a far more severe free gas loss than in S-2. Integrated analysis reveals that S-2 displays superior gas-bearing properties and more favorable occurrence conditions, positioning it as the prime exploration target in the Anchang syncline.

Introduction

Shale gas represents an important clean unconventional natural gas resource, playing a critical role in ensuring China’s energy security and achieving the “dual carbon” goals. , The upper Ordovician Wufeng Formation–lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation marine shale in the Upper Yangtze region is the primary target for shale gas exploration in China. This formation features favorable conditions, such as high organic matter abundance and well-developed reservoir spaces, providing a solid material foundation for large-scale shale gas development. − Through years of exploration practice and technological breakthroughs, multiple large-scale shale gas fields such as Fuling, Changning, and Weiyuan have been established in the Sichuan Basin and its periphery, with cumulative production continuing to grow steadily, fully confirming the huge resource potential and industrial development value of this layer. ,

In situ gas content and gas occurrence state are core parameters for evaluating shale gas resource potential and recoverability, directly determining the economic viability of shale gas reservoirs and the optimization of development strategies. − Shale gas occurs primarily in reservoirs as adsorbed gas and free gas. Adsorbed gas mainly attaches to the surfaces of organic matter and clay minerals, while free gas is stored in pore and fracture spaces. The proportions and distribution patterns of these two forms collectively control the recoverable potential of shale gas. , Significant variations in gas occurrence proportions exist across regions: in North American shale gas reservoirs, adsorbed gas accounts for 20–85%, whereas in the Sichuan Basin, this proportion ranges from 27.1% to 47.8%. , Methane adsorption experiments serve as an effective method for determining the adsorbed gas content in shale. − However, the adsorption capacity measured in these experiments is typically excess adsorption, consistent with the Gibbs excess adsorption behavior. Therefore, accurately characterizing methane adsorption features in shale is a key prerequisite for a precise evaluation of shale gas resource potential. Furthermore, the proportion of adsorbed versus free gas is not only controlled by intrinsic factors such as shale composition and pore structure but also closely related to reservoir conditions, including formation pressure and temperature. These distribution patterns directly influence desorption efficiency and seepage characteristics during production, ultimately affecting well productivity and the recovery factor. , However, existing studies mostly focus on estimating the total gas content, with insufficient attention paid to the differentiation mechanisms of adsorbed gas and free gas under different lithofacies conditions.

Scholars both domestically and internationally have conducted extensive research on the gas-bearing properties of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale, generally concluding that siliceous shale facies represent the most prospective high-quality lithofacies type for exploration in this interval. , Siliceous shale facies primarily form in deep-water, low-energy, anoxic-reducing depositional environments. The enriched biogenic siliceous minerals provide a rigid supporting framework for the shale, effectively resisting pore space destruction by later compaction. This preserves nanoscale pores and various secondary pores formed during the thermal evolution of organic matter, providing sufficient space for methane adsorption and free storage. ,− However, existing studies often treat siliceous shale facies as a relatively homogeneous reservoir unit, overlooking significant internal variations in mineral composition, pore structure, and water-bearing characteristics. In fact, variations in clay mineral content within siliceous shale can significantly influence gas occurrence modes by altering pore structure and water distribution. Recent imbibition experiments on deep Longmaxi Formation shales have demonstrated that water–rock interactions, strongly influenced by clay mineral type and salinity, significantly affect pore-water distribution and retention within the shale matrix. These interactions can lead to elevated water saturation that occupies effective pore space and thereby suppresses free gas storage. However, current research on the gas-bearing properties of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale mainly focuses on the main area of the Sichuan Basin, with insufficient attention to structurally complex marginal areas dominated by normal-pressure shale gas reservoirs. In these areas, free gas preservation conditions differ markedly from those in overpressured zones, necessitating further investigation into gas occurrence patterns.

The Anchang Syncline in northern Guizhou is located in the Wuling Depression on the southeastern margin of the Upper Yangtze Platform and is one of the key areas for marine shale gas exploration in southern China. The Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale in this area is completely developed with a relatively large thickness and has advantages such as high organic matter abundance and moderate thermal evolution degree, further highlighting its shale gas exploration potential. , In recent years, related studies in this area have gradually extended from basic geological characteristics to the evaluation of shale gas-bearing properties. Previous studies evaluated the gas-bearing intervals and indicated that the carbonaceous shale of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation first member possesses relatively high gas content, and the second sublayer of the Long 1 Member was considered the primary production interval. , Additionally, Wu et al. (2024) investigated the gas-bearing characteristics of the Wufeng–Longmaxi shale in the Anchang Syncline and pointed out that the total gas content increased with burial depth, peaked at the bottom of the Longmaxi Formation, and then decreased into the Wufeng Formation. However, studies on the fine division of shale facies and differences in gas-bearing properties remain relatively weak. Therefore, shale samples from the Wufeng Formation to the Long 1 Member, collected from multiple wells in the Anchang Syncline, northern Guizhou, were selected for this study. Through integrated geochemical analysis, scanning electron microscopy observations, pore structure characterization, high-pressure methane adsorption experiments, and in situ gas content calculations, a refined subdivision of siliceous shale facies was conducted; pore structure characteristics, methane adsorption capacity, and in situ gas occurrence features were systematically compared across the different siliceous shale subfacies, and the underlying controlling mechanisms were thoroughly investigated. The objective is to establish a geological foundation for sweet-spot evaluation and target optimization in normal-pressure marine shale gas reservoirs.

Geological Settings

The Anchang Syncline is located in Zheng’an County, northern Guizhou Province (Figure a). Structurally, it belongs to the Wuling Depression at the southeastern margin of the Upper Yangtze Platform and lies within the transitional zone bounded by the Central Sichuan Paleohigh, Central Guizhou Uplift, and Xuefeng Mountain Paleohigh. It represents a typical residual synclinal structural unit in the periphery of the Sichuan Basin. − The syncline exhibits an overall asymmetric “narrow-and-steep” morphology, with a gentle western limb and a steep eastern limb. Its axial trace displays a subtle “S”-shaped pattern, extending approximately 25 km in length, with the axial surface dipping northwestward (Figure b). , The total area is about 225 km2. The regional tectonic evolution involved multiple phases of modification, including the Xuefeng, Caledonian, Hercynian, Indosinian, Yanshanian, and Himalayan orogenies. The late Yanshanian to Himalayan compressional tectonics primarily shaped the present-day structural configuration of the syncline. ,− Two sets of faults mainly develop in the area, trending NNE and NNW. Most of the faults do not directly reach the surface, and only locally cut through the gas-bearing target layers (Figure c). The overall structure is in a compressive stress environment. ,

(a) Geographic location map of the Anchang Syncline in northern Guizhou, China; (b) Structural map of the Anchang Syncline; (c) Burial depth contour map of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang Syncline; (d) Composite stratigraphic column of Well AY1-6 in the Anchang Syncline.

The stratigraphy in the study area is relatively well-developed, with the Proterozoic, Paleozoic, and Mesozoic strata mainly developed from bottom to top. The target interval for shale gas exploration comprises the Upper Ordovician Wufeng Formation to the Lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation. The Wufeng Formation was deposited in a deep-water shelf environment, dominated by gray carbonaceous shale and siliceous shale, with minor thin bentonite interbeds. Its thickness ranges from approximately 5 to 10 m, gradually increasing northward, with the uppermost Guanyinqiao Member consisting of gray calcareous mudstone and carbonaceous shale, typically less than 30 cm thick. The Longmaxi Formation reflects a transitional depositional environment from an early deep-water shelf to a late shallow water shelf. Lithologically, it grades upward from gray-black carbonaceous shale to gray calcareous mudstone and silty mudstone, with the basal section enriched in graptolite fossils and pyrite nodules. The total thickness is about 30–40 m. The Longmaxi Formation can be further subdivided into the Long-1 and Long-2 Members, comprising five submembers in total, with the Long-1 Member serving as the primary target interval (Figure d).

The burial depth of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shales in the study area ranges from 1200 to 4200 m, with measured formation pressure coefficients of approximately 0.99, classifying it as a typical normal-pressure shale gas reservoir. ,

Samples and Methods

Samples

A total of 14 shale samples from the Upper Ordovician Wufeng Formation-Lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation were collected from wells AD2, AD3, and AD4 in the Anchang Syncline, northern Guizhou. The three wells are distributed on both limbs of the syncline: AD3 is located on the western limb, whereas AD2 and AD4 are situated on the eastern limb (Figure c). The straight-line distances among the three wells are 7.71 km (AD3–AD4), 4.63 km (AD4–AD2), and 10.70 km (AD3–AD2), forming a triangular coverage area of approximately 15.75 km2. The samples were collected from the Wufeng Formation to the first member of the Longmaxi Formation. The burial depth range of well AD2 is 1346.22–1361.77 m, that of well AD3 is 1243.83–1258.96 m, and that of well AD4 is 1325.96–1333.51 m. Vertically, the 14 samples span the entire target interval from the Wufeng Formation to the top of the Long 1 Member. Specifically, 4 samples were taken from the Wufeng Formation, 2 from S1l1 1, 3 from S1l1 2, 2 from S1l1 3, and 3 from S1l1 4, with individual depths ranging from 1243.83 to 1361.77 m. During the sampling process, full consideration was given to different submembers to comprehensively cover the vertical geological characteristics of the study interval.

Experiments

Geochemical Analysis

The TOC content was determined using a LECO CS230 carbon/sulfur analyzer. Approximately 2 g of each sample was ground to 200 mesh, treated with dilute hydrochloric acid to remove carbonate minerals, rinsed, and dried. The processed sample was then combusted in a high-temperature oxygen (pure O2) stream, converting total organic carbon to CO2, which was detected by an infrared detector to quantify the TOC content.

Thermal maturity was measured using a Leica DM4500P polarizing microscope combined with a CRAIC microspectrophotometer. Whole-rock samples were prepared into polished pellets (diameter 2.54 cm) by grinding and polishing, followed by surface drying and storage in a desiccator. The microspectrophotometer was calibrated prior to measurements. Bitumen reflectance (Rb) was determined, and equivalent vitrinite reflectance (Ro) was calculated using the empirical formula proposed by Feng and Chen (1988): Ro = 0.3364 + 0.6569 Rb (with a correlation coefficient r = 0.92). This conversion has been widely adopted in recent studies on the overmature Wufeng–Longmaxi marine shales in northern Guizhou and the adjacent southern Sichuan Basin, − confirming its regional applicability for the present samples.

Whole-rock mineralogy and clay mineral composition were analyzed using a D/max-2500PC automated powder X-ray diffractometer. To ensure experimental reproducibility and minimize measurement and operational errors, standardized and unified protocols were applied throughout the entire process of sample preparation, specimen mounting, and data acquisition. Shale samples were first oven-dried at <60 °C to constant weight and cooled to room temperature. Approximately 1–2 g of the dried sample was preliminarily crushed to <1 mm using a crusher or agate mortar, then further ground in a vibratory mill or agate mortar until all particles were <40 μm. A uniform particle size criterion was strictly followed to eliminate diffraction intensity deviations caused by particle size heterogeneity and preferred orientation, thereby guaranteeing the reproducibility of sample preparation. The aluminum sample holder was placed on a flat glass plate or clean paper to ensure tight contact at the base. Ground powder was uniformly loaded into the holder using a sample spoon and compacted vertically with moderate pressure to achieve a flat, randomly oriented surface without preferred orientation. Finally, the characteristic peak intensities of each mineral were recorded. Rather than using the less reproducible peak heights, the integrated intensities of diffraction peaks after baseline subtraction and background noise removal were uniformly adopted for quantification. For each mineral, multiple nonoverlapping characteristic peaks were selected for cross-checking and verification, and mineral contents were then calculated from these integrated intensities.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy

FE-SEM was employed to observe the occurrence and distribution of organic matter as well as the types of pores in the shale samples. Samples were first subjected to argon ion beam polishing using a Leica EM RES102 ion milling system, operated at a 5 kV accelerating voltage and a 2.2 mA beam current, for a polishing duration of 3 h. Subsequently, the polished cross-sections were sputter-coated with carbon in a high-vacuum environment using a Leica EM ACE600 coater to improve electrical conductivity and reduce charging effects. High-resolution imaging was then performed on the prepared samples using an FEI Apreo LoVac SEM instrument equipped with a field emission gun electron source.

Porosity Measurement

Porosity was determined using a PoroPDP-200 overburden porosity analyzer (Core Lab, USA). Cylindrical shale samples were first dried at 378 K for more than 8 h and weighed to obtain the dry mass. The bulk (apparent) volume was then measured. The sample was placed in a helium expansion chamber, where a known volume of gas was expanded into the core holder under controlled pressure, and the equilibrium pressure was recorded. This expansion process was repeated, and the grain (skeleton) volume of the shale was calculated according to Boyle’s law. Finally, porosity was computed from the difference between the bulk volume and the grain volume. It should be clarified that although helium, with a kinetic diameter of 0.26 nm, can penetrate most interconnected nanopores larger than 0.26 nm within the shale matrix, it cannot access isolated closed organic pores and blind intragranular pores that lack effective pore throats. Therefore, the measured parameter is more accurately defined as overburden-corrected effective connected matrix porosity rather than the absolute total porosity of the sample.

Water Saturation Measurement

Water saturation (S w) of the shale samples was determined using a combination of the oven-drying method and the alcohol saturation method. The original mass of each sample (m 1) was first measured. The sample was then dried to constant weight under near-vacuum conditions (378.15 K, <30 mmHg), and the dry mass (m 2) was recorded. The volume of water in the sample (V w) was calculated as 1 Vw=m1−m2ρw

Subsequently, the dried sample was fully saturated with ethanol, and the surface ethanol was carefully wiped off. The total mass after saturation (m 3) was measured, and the total pore volume (V p) was calculated as 2 Vp=m3−m2ρal

Finally, the water saturation (S w) was determined using the following equation: 3 Sw=VwVp×100% where S w represents the water saturation (%), ρw is the density of water (taken as 1 g/cm3), and ρal is the density of ethanol at room temperature (taken as 0.79 g/cm3). Notably, ethanol is nonreactive with clay minerals and avoids clay hydration swelling while effectively displacing free pore water in connected pores. Since clay interlayer-bound water is undetectable here, the obtained S w denotes saturation solely for the effective connected pore system.

Low-Temperature Nitrogen Adsorption

A low-temperature nitrogen adsorption experiment was performed on shale samples using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 surface area and porosity analyzer to characterize pore structure characteristics. Shale samples were first ground to 200 mesh powder and placed in sample tubes. Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated using a certified reference material. The samples were then degassed under vacuum at 378 K for 12 h to thoroughly remove adsorbed water and residual volatile components. All samples were degassed under the same conditions to ensure data comparability. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were subsequently measured at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K) over a relative pressure (P/P 0) range of 0.01–0.995, yielding complete adsorption and desorption branches. Specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Pore volume and pore size distribution were determined from the desorption branch data using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model. Pores were classified according to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC)-recommended classification: micropores (<2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (>50 nm). ,

High-Pressure Methane Adsorption

A high-pressure methane adsorption experiment was conducted using a Rubotherm Isosorp HP Static III magnetic suspension balance high-pressure gas isotherm adsorption instrument. Prior to the experiment, shale samples with particle sizes of 0.180–0.425 mm were selected, with a minimum mass of 20 g prepared for each test. The procedure began by sealing the sample basket in the high-pressure chamber under isothermal conditions. Nitrogen was used as the medium to calibrate the mass and volume of the sample basket based on balance readings at different pressures. Subsequently, a buoyancy test was performed using helium as the medium, collecting readings at multiple pressures to determine the actual mass and volume of the sample. Methane gas was then pressurized to approximately 1 MPa above the maximum experimental pressure to initiate the adsorption test. Equilibrium at each pressure point was considered achieved when the temperature fluctuation was ≤0.2 °C and either the equilibrium time was ≥2 h or the mass change was <50 μg within 10 min. These criteria were uniformly applied to all samples to ensure measurement consistency and data reliability. Based on the differences in formation temperatures of the research wells, the experimental temperature for samples from Well AD2 and Well AD3 is 46 °C, and that for Well AD4 is 55 °C.

The excess adsorption capacity and absolute adsorption capacity of methane are defined as follows: 4 nex=VL×PPL+P(1−ρgρa) 5 nads=nex1−ρgρa where n ex is the excess adsorption capacity (cm3/g), n ads is the absolute adsorption capacity (cm3/g), P is the pressure (MPa), V L is the Langmuir volume (m3/t), P L is the Langmuir pressure (MPa), ρa is the adsorbed phase density of methane (g/cm3), and ρg is the gas phase density of methane (g/cm3).

Various geological models have been used to describe the supercritical adsorption behavior of methane in shale. , Qian et al. (2023) evaluated five supercritical excess adsorption models for Wufeng–Longmaxi shale in southern Sichuan using root mean square error (RMSE) and identified the most suitable model based on fitting error comparison. In this study, three classic supercritical adsorption models, namely the supercritical Langmuir (S-L), conventional Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R), and supercritical Dubinin–Radushkevich (SDR) models, were applied to fit the methane adsorption experimental data of multiple shale samples. The fitting parameters, coefficients of determination (R 2), and RMSE of each model are summarized in Table S1. The statistical results demonstrate that the SDR model yields consistently higher R 2 values (all exceeding 0.99) and lower RMSE values than the S-L and conventional D-R models across all tested samples. Furthermore, the maximum absolute adsorption capacity derived from the SDR model can reasonably reflect the methane adsorption potential of shale micropores on the basis of micropore filling theory. Combined with its moderate number of adjustable parameters and robust theoretical foundation, the SDR model was ultimately selected to characterize supercritical methane adsorption in shale for subsequent analysis. The SDR model is expressed as 6 nex=n0×exp(−D×(ln(ρaρg)×R×T)2)×(1−ρgρa) where n 0 is the maximum absolute adsorption capacity (cm3/g), D is the pore structure parameter (mol2·kJ–2), R is the universal gas constant (8.314 × 10–3 kJ/(K·mol)), and T is the temperature (K).

In Situ Gas Content Calculation

The Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale in the Anchang Syncline exhibits a relatively high degree of thermal maturation. Consequently, the dissolved gas content is generally negligible and can be ignored during gas content calculations. The total in situ gas content is therefore considered to consist solely of free gas and adsorbed gas. 7 ntotal=nads+nfree

The absolute adsorbed gas content was calculated by combining Gibbs adsorption theory with the SDR model: 8 nads=n0×exp(−D×(ln(ρaρg)×R×T)2)

When calculating the amount of free gas, if the volume occupied by adsorbed gas is ignored, it will be assumed that all pore spaces can store free gas, leading to an overestimation of the amount of free gas. To improve accuracy, when estimating the amount of free gas, the volume occupied by adsorbed methane needs to be subtracted from the total pore volume. The free gas content is thus expressed as 9 nfree=⌀/100×(1−Sw/100)×ρg×Vmρbulk×M−nadsρa×ρg where n total is the total in situ gas content (cm3/g); n free is the free gas content (cm3/g); ⌀ is the porosity (%), S w is the water saturation (%), V m is the molar volume of gas at standard temperature and pressure (22.4 L/mol), ρbulk is the bulk density of the sample (g/cm3), and M is the molar mass of methane (16 g/mol).

Results

Mineral Compositions and Geochemistry Characteristics

The mineral composition of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale in the Anchang Syncline is mainly composed of quartz, clay minerals, and carbonate minerals (Figure a), with contents ranging from 35.51% to 67.28% (average 57.97%), 16.93% to 39.38% (average 24.81%), and 4.21% to 13.17% (average 7.86%), respectively. Feldspar and pyrite contents range from 3.99% to 16.30% (average of 7.63%) and 0% to 4.17% (average of 1.73%), respectively. The clay mineral components are mainly illite, illite/smectite mixed layer, and chlorite (Figure b), with relative contents of 46.97% to 73.23% (average 62.88%), 17.40% to 36.01% (average 26.73%), and 7.02% to 18.01% (average 9.74%), respectively.

(a) Whole-rock mineral composition of shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation; (b) Clay mineral composition of shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation; (c) Lithofacies classification of shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation.

Using quartz + feldspar (siliceous minerals), carbonate minerals, and clay minerals as ternary end-members, the shale can be classified into four major lithofacies categories: siliceous, calcareous, argillaceous, and mixed lithofacies associations. These four major categories can be further subdivided into 16 subfacies. , The Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale in the Anchang Syncline is predominantly composed of mixed siliceous shale facies (S-2) and clay-bearing siliceous shale facies (S-4) (Figure c).

Total organic carbon (TOC) contents of the shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation range from 3.02% to 6.16%, with an average of 4.70% (Figure a). The average TOC values for S-2 and S-4 are 4.89% and 4.43%, respectively. Equivalent vitrinite reflectance (Ro) values range from 2.30% to 2.71%, with an average of 2.47% (Figure b), indicating that the organic matter has reached the overmature stage.

Shale Reservoir Characteristics

Shale Pore Development Characteristics

FE-SEM observations reveal that pores in the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale of the Anchang syncline can be classified into three major types: organic matter pores, inorganic pores, and microfractures. Inorganic pores can be further subdivided into interparticle pores, intraparticle pores, and intercrystalline pores (Figure ). In S-2, organic matter pores dominate, while inorganic pores are also well-developed. Organic matter pores exhibit diverse morphologies, predominantly elliptical, circular, or honeycomb-like, and their pore sizes are concentrated in the range of 100–200 nm (Figure a). Interparticle pores are primarily residual spaces between organic matter and mineral grains (Figure b). Intraparticle pores are relatively small, generally on the order of tens of nanometers, and intercrystalline pores within pyrite crystals are also observed (Figure c). Microfractures are mainly diagenetic microfractures (Figure d), which form during diagenetic processes such as compaction or crystallization. They are mostly elongated, with lengths exceeding several micrometers, and significantly improve the connectivity of the shale pore system. In S-4, organic matter pores are poorly developed (Figure e). Interparticle pores are elongated and primarily occur between brittle mineral grains, with pore sizes generally ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers (Figure f). The pore size of the intragranular pores is concentrated in the range of several hundred nanometers. Although their development degree is relatively high, the pore connectivity is poor, which is unfavorable for effective gas seepage and contributes only limitedly to shale gas production (Figure g). Additionally, interparticle pores within clay minerals are frequently filled with organic matter (Figure h), while microfractures are commonly cemented by pyrite (Figure i).

Shale Pore Structure Characteristics

The results of the low-temperature nitrogen adsorption experiment show that the pore-specific surface area of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale samples ranges from 8.075 to 32.815 m2/g, with an average of 20.861 m2/g, and the pore volume ranges from 0.0164 to 0.1269 cm3/g, with an average of 0.0672 cm3/g (Table ). For S-2, the average pore-specific surface area is 22.713 m2/g, and the average pore volume is 0.0930 cm3/g; for S-4, the average pore-specific surface area is 19.010 m2/g, and the average pore volume is 0.0414 cm3/g. Overall, the pore volume is mainly provided by mesopores, which account for more than 70%.

Shale Porosity and Water Saturation

The porosity of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale ranges from 1.31% to 8.77%, with an average of 4.38%, and generally shows a gradually decreasing trend with increasing burial depth (Figure c). It is noteworthy that the thermal maturity of this shale interval is relatively uniform; therefore, pore development is primarily controlled by the combined effects of the organic and inorganic components. , The porosity of S-2 ranges from 2.47% to 8.77% (average 5.27%); the porosity of S-4 ranges from 1.31% to 3.95% (average 3.20%). Specifically, the porosity of S-2 has a positive correlation with both TOC content and clay mineral content (Figure a and c), with R 2 both being 0.36, but no obvious correlation with quartz content (Figure b). In contrast, the porosity of S-4 has a weak positive correlation with TOC content (Figure a), with R 2 being 0.33, and no obvious correlation with quartz and clay mineral contents (Figure b and c). This further indicates that the porosity of the S-2 samples is mainly contributed by the developed organic matter pores, with additional significant influence from microfractures associated with clay minerals (Figure d).

Cross plots of porosity versus total organic carbon (a), quartz (b), and clay mineral (c) contents of shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang Syncline.

Water saturation was calculated based on the measured water content, water volume, and total pore volume. The results show that the water saturation of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale ranges from 23.03% to 42.03%, with an average of 32.54% (Figure d). For the S-2 samples, water saturation ranges from 23.03% to 42.03% (average of 31.27%); for the S-4 samples, it ranges from 28.92% to 39.60% (average of 34.24%).

Methane Adsorption Characteristics

Figure presents the characteristics of methane adsorption isotherms of shale samples from different lithofacies of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang syncline. There is a significant difference between the excess adsorption capacity (n ex) and the absolute adsorption capacity (n ads). Discussion

Main Controlling Factors of Shale Adsorption Capacity

The methane adsorption capacity of shale is a core factor determining adsorbed gas content and is collectively influenced by TOC content, pore structure, mineral composition, and external conditions such as temperature and pressure. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TOC content is the dominant control on adsorption capacity in organic-rich shales, ,,, a conclusion clearly validated in the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale of the present study area. As shown in Figure a, the n 0 exhibits a strong positive correlation with TOC content, with R 2 values of 0.788 and 0.629 for S-2 and S-4, respectively. Higher TOC content leads to greater development of nanoscale organic pores formed by the cracking of organic matter during thermal evolution, significantly increasing specific surface area and providing more adsorption sites for methane molecules. , Furthermore, Figures b and c illustrate positive correlations between n 0 and both specific surface area and pore volume, confirming that the degree of organic pore development directly governs adsorption strength. Under supercritical conditions, especially in micropores and small mesopores with diameters less than 6 nm, the superposition effect of the adsorption potential of pore walls further enhances the interaction between methane molecules and pore surfaces, substantially increasing adsorption capacity. −

Relationships between n 0 and TOC content (a), total pore specific surface area (b), total pore volume (c), quartz content (d), and clay mineral content (e) in the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale of the Anchang Syncline.

To better distinguish the relative contributions of organic and inorganic matter, n 0 was normalized by TOC (n 0/TOC). The results show that the average value of S-2 is 0.755 cm3/(g·TOC) and that of S-4 is 0.797 cm3/(g·TOC), with little difference between the two. When TOC content approaches 0, the inorganic adsorption capacities of S-2 and S-4 are 1.350 cm3/g and 1.873 cm3/g, respectively, which are significantly higher than the inorganic adsorption capacity of the Longmaxi Formation shale in southeastern Chongqing (0.54 cm3/g). It should be noted that this extrapolation implicitly assumes two conditions. The first is a linear relationship between organic adsorption and TOC content. The second is a similar organic matter adsorption efficiency per unit TOC for the two subfacies. In the present case, the thermal maturity and kerogen type of S-2 and S-4 are comparable. Moreover, the measured n 0/TOC ratios differ by only 5.56%. This similarity supports the applicability of the assumption for a first-order estimate. This result indicates that inorganic minerals make a non-negligible contribution to adsorption capacity in the study area, particularly in samples with relatively low TOC, where inorganic adsorption may account for 30–50% of the total adsorbed gas.

The influence of mineral composition on adsorption capacity shows a clear differentiation between the two subfacies. In S-2, n 0 exhibits a weak positive correlation with quartz content, with an R 2 of 0.351 (Figure d), while it has basically no correlation with the clay mineral content (Figure e). This is mainly because quartz, as a rigid mineral, provides effective support for the shale framework, effectively alleviating damage to organic pores and secondary pores caused by compaction during deep burial, thereby indirectly ensuring the maintenance of the adsorption capacity. In contrast, for S-4, n 0 correlates negatively with quartz content, with an R 2 of 0.738 (Figure d), but strongly positively with clay mineral content, with an R 2 as high as 0.888 (Figure e). This reverse relationship arises because the TOC content of S-4 is generally low and the development of organic pores is limited. In this case, the interlayer micropores of clay minerals become important supplementary adsorption sites (Figure h), which, together with the limited organic pores, constitute the source of adsorption capacity. Moreover, an increased quartz content occupies the enrichment space of organic matter, restricting the thermal evolution process of organic matter and thus inhibiting organic pore development.

In addition, Ji et al. (2012) compared the adsorption capacities of different clay minerals through high-pressure methane adsorption experiments and found that illite has the lowest adsorption capacity (maximum 1.72 mL/g), significantly lower than montmorillonite (8.12 mL/g) and kaolinite (2.70 mL/g), and comparable to chlorite (1.81 mL/g). In the study area, clay minerals in both subfacies are dominated by illite. For S-2, pore structure parameters show no significant correlation with clay mineral content (Figure ), which is related to the high TOC content. The adsorption capacity of organic matter is much higher than that of clay minerals, thus masking the influence of the clay minerals. In S-4, however, pore structure parameters are strongly positively correlated with clay mineral content (R 2 > 0.6) (Figure ), indicating a relatively greater contribution of clay minerals to nanopore development, in agreement with SEM observations (Figure h).

Relationships between total pore specific surface area (a) and total pore volume (b) with clay mineral content in the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale of the Anchang Syncline.

Although this study did not conduct direct tests on the effect of temperature on shale adsorption capacity, published data from various geological settings (Figure ) consistently show that n 0 decreases significantly with increasing temperature (R 2 > 0.79), indicating a strong negative correlation. The slopes of the linear regression equations for each sample range from −0.015 to −0.011 cm3/(g·°C), suggesting that the degree of temperature-induced weakening on adsorption capacity is similar across different shale samples. The adsorbed phase density (ρa) also exhibits a strong negative correlation with temperature (R 2 > 0.85), primarily because elevated temperature increases the thermal kinetic energy of methane molecules, enlarging intermolecular distances and weakening adsorption forces between methane and pore surfaces. ,, This reflects a universal inhibitory effect of temperature on shale methane adsorption capacity.

In summary, the different mineral components interact through framework support and pore modification, which, together with the pore-generating effect of organic matter, coevolve to shape the reservoir pore system and ultimately determine the maximum adsorption capacity. S-2 possesses the dual advantages of high TOC and high quartz content. During deep burial and diagenesis, the abundant organic matter provides the material basis for organic pore generation, while the rigid quartz framework effectively resists compaction and cementation. These two factors synergistically promote the full development and long-term preservation of the organic matter pores. This coupled process substantially increases the specific surface area and creates abundant stable adsorption sites, providing core support for the high adsorption capacity of S-2. In contrast, S-4 is characterized by a high clay mineral content coupled with low TOC and low quartz content. The insufficient organic matter limits organic pore development, and the weak framework support due to the low quartz content fails to protect primary and secondary pores. Meanwhile, the abundant clay minerals dominate pore evolution, ultimately producing a pore system dominated by clay-associated inorganic pores with poorly developed organic pores. Consequently, S-4 is markedly inferior to S-2 in both the pore structure and adsorption-site availability.

Characteristics of In Situ Gas Content in Shale and Its Main Controlling Factors

In Situ Gas Content Characteristics of Different Siliceous Shale Subfacies

Previous studies indicate that the main burial depth range of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation shale in the Anchang Syncline, northern Guizhou, is 1000–3000 m, with a surface temperature of approximately 15 °C, a geothermal gradient of about 24 °C/km, and a shale gas reservoir pressure coefficient generally around 0.994. , Based on these geological parameters, the SDR model was applied to calculate the in situ gas content of the shale samples in the study area.

The results show that, for S-2, total gas content ranges from 3.290 to 5.570 cm3/g (average 4.293 cm3/g), adsorbed gas content ranges from 3.060 to 4.045 cm3/g (average 3.403 cm3/g), and free gas content ranges from 0.019 to 1.993 cm3/g (average 0.891 cm3/g) (Table ). The proportions of adsorbed gas and free gas are 79.27% and 20.73%, respectively. The adsorbed gas proportion is consistent with reported results from deep shale in the Weirong–Yongchuan and Luzhou areas. , For S-4, total gas content ranges from 3.148 to 4.556 cm3/g (average 3.622 cm3/g), adsorbed gas content ranges from 2.282 to 4.322 cm3/g (average 3.168 cm3/g), and free gas content ranges from 0.184 to 0.866 cm3/g (average 0.455 cm3/g). The proportions of adsorbed gas and free gas are 87.47% and 12.53%, respectively. Compared with S-2, S-4 exhibits a higher adsorbed gas proportion, a lower free gas proportion, and a lower average total gas content, highlighting the significant regulatory role of lithofacies differences on gas content and phase partitioning.

In S-2, quartz dominates as a rigid mineral, forming a supportive framework that effectively resists compaction during deep burial and minimizes destruction of primary and secondary pores. , Concurrently, a high TOC content promotes the extensive development of nanoscale organic matter pores during thermal maturation. , These pores not only provide abundant adsorption sites but also, due to their hydrophobic surfaces, are less likely to be occupied by bound water, thereby creating favorable conditions for free gas storage. This is particularly pronounced in medium- to high-maturity shales.

In contrast, S-4 contains a significantly higher clay mineral content than S-2. The strong hydrophilicity of clay minerals facilitates the formation of stable adsorbed water films on their surfaces, and interlayer pores are readily filled with capillary water, , resulting in the loss of an effective storage capacity in a large proportion of inorganic pores. Moreover, clay minerals readily form stable organoclay complexes with organic matter, confining organic matter in banded or aggregated forms within clay interlayers and thereby suppressing the development of organic pores. , Consequently, this facies shows a markedly greater dependence on adsorption sites provided by clay minerals.

Main Controlling Factors of Free Gas Content in Different Siliceous Shale Subfacies

The free gas content directly influences the recoverability of shale gas and is governed by the coupled effects of porosity, water saturation, and pressure conditions. As shown in Figure a, the free gas content of S-2 exhibits a strong positive correlation with porosity (R 2 = 0.949), whereas the correlation of S-4 is much weaker (R 2 = 0.207). This difference arises because S-2 is dominated by well-developed organic matter pores, which contribute more than 40% of the total porosity and exhibit good connectivity, thereby providing an effective storage space for free gas. ,, In contrast, S-4 is dominated by inorganic pores associated with the clay minerals. These pores are predominantly slit-like, poorly connected, and readily filled with bound water, resulting in reduced effective porosity. ,

Relationships between free gas content of shale samples from the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang Syncline and porosity (a), and water saturation (b); relationships between water saturation and quartz content (c) and clay mineral content (d).

Furthermore, water saturation significantly suppresses free gas content, as water molecules occupy effective pore space and compete for adsorption sites, with this inhibitory effect showing marked differences between the two lithofacies (Figure b). In S-2, the free gas content displays only a weak negative correlation with the water saturation value. Its water saturation value is positively correlated with quartz content and weakly negatively correlated with clay minerals (Figure c and d). Quartz, as an inert and weakly hydrophilic mineral, results in pore water occurring primarily in a free state, exerting limited occupation of effective pore space. , The relatively low clay mineral content further diminishes the adsorption and binding effects of water molecules, indirectly maintaining the stability of free gas content. However, the free gas content in S-4 has a significant negative correlation with water saturation value (R 2 = 0.494), and its water saturation value is strongly positively correlated with clay minerals (R 2 = 0.935) and negatively correlated with quartz content (R 2 = 0.756) (Figure c and d). On the one hand, hydroxyl groups on clay mineral surfaces form strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules, enabling water to preferentially occupy high-energy adsorption sites and thereby reducing methane adsorption capacity. , On the other hand, increasing the clay mineral content directly elevates water saturation; under high water saturation conditions, the occupation of effective pore space by water, combined with the effects of clay minerals, further intensifies the suppression of free gas. Additionally, under near-hydrostatic pressure conditions, the hydration swelling of clay minerals (particularly illite/smectite mixed layers) is more pronounced. This further blocks pore throats, reduces the storage and migration capacity of free gas, and exacerbates the disadvantage in the gas-bearing properties.

It is noteworthy that the reservoir pressure coefficient in the Anchang Syncline averages 0.994, indicating overall near-hydrostatic normal-pressure conditions. This normal-pressure state is primarily attributed to intense tectonic uplift and denudation during the Late Yanshanian–Himalayan orogenies, which triggered partial dissipation of the overpressure originally accumulated during deep burial and hydrocarbon generation. , In contrast, deep shale sequences in the southern Sichuan Basin retain remarkable overpressure with pressure coefficients ranging from 1.22 to 1.61, benefiting from favorable structural preservation and thicker regional cap seals. Overpressure environments typically enhance free gas preservation through pore protection, inhibition of gas diffusion, and increased gas solubility. , Under hydrostatic-pressure conditions, however, free gas in shale is more prone to diffusive loss, representing an important geological background for the overall lower free gas content observed in the Anchang Syncline.

Crucially, this normal-pressure setting does not affect the two siliceous subfacies uniformly. For S-2, the well-developed organic pore network with good connectivity and relatively low water saturation can still retain a substantial proportion of free gas even under low driving forces. In contrast, S-4, dominated by clay-associated inorganic pores with poor connectivity and elevated water saturation, suffers more severe free gas loss because weak capillary sealing and the water-locking effect are insufficient to resist gas escape at a near-hydrostatic pressure. Therefore, the regional normal-pressure conditions amplify the gas-bearing contrast between S-2 and S-4, making S-4 particularly disadvantaged in terms of free gas retention.

Occurrence Patterns of In Situ Gas Content in Different Siliceous Shale Subfacies

As discussed above, the adsorbed gas content is primarily controlled by organic matter abundance, while the free gas content is governed by porosity and water saturation, with water competition exerting a significant inhibitory effect. Based on the characteristics of mineral composition, organic carbon content, pore structure, and water saturation of the two subfacies, the occurrence modes of in situ gas in the two types of siliceous shale subfacies are summarized.

In S-2, the rock framework is predominantly composed of elliptical to subrounded quartz grains with quartz content averaging over 60%. This forms a stable rigid support network that effectively resists mechanical compaction and chemical pressure solution during later burial, thereby preserving a large proportion of primary organic matter pores and secondary pores (Figure a). , Organic matter is typically distributed as isolated patches or lumps, hosting abundant honeycomb-like and elliptical organic pores with diameters mainly concentrated in the 100–200 nm range. These pores provide a high specific surface area and are dominated by hydrophobic functional groups on their surfaces (Figure c). Consequently, in S-2, adsorbed gas is primarily stored within micropores and small mesopores of the organic matter, whereas free gas preferentially occupies better connected mesopores and macropores. Because of the relatively weak hydrophilicity of quartz and carbonate minerals, bound water mainly exists as free or capillary water and is less likely to form continuous thick water films on organic pore surfaces, resulting in weaker competition from water molecules for methane adsorption sites. Additionally, the relatively high connectivity of organic matter pores reduces the gas diffusion resistance, enabling a portion of free gas to remain stably stored within the effective pore network.

In S-4, the content of elongated clay minerals increases significantly, and they are arranged in an oriented manner, while quartz grains remain an important component, and carbonate mineral content decreases. Organic matter is commonly distributed in banded or dispersed forms between clay mineral aggregates (Figure b). The oriented arrangement of clay minerals enhances bedding plane development on the one hand and causes tight stacking of clay platelets on the other hand, leading to the development of abundant interlayer mesopores (with pore sizes mainly ranging from 10 to 50 nm). However, illite surfaces are rich in hydroxyl groups and exhibit strong hydrophilicity, readily forming stable adsorbed water films 2–5 molecular layers thick on clay platelet surfaces. , Water molecules preferentially occupy high-energy adsorption sites on clay mineral surfaces through competitive adsorption, significantly reducing methane adsorption capacity (Figure d); meanwhile, capillary condensation causes water to form concave liquid surfaces in slit-like inorganic pores, generating high capillary pressure that severely compresses the effective storage space for free gas. Furthermore, widespread organoclay complexes between clay minerals and organic matter encapsulate or confine portions of organic matter within clay interlayers, hindering the further development of organic matter pores and resulting in a markedly reduced contribution of organic matter pores in S-4.

Geological Significance

Siliceous shale facies, as a high-quality lithofacies type with great exploration potential in marine shale gas reservoirs, is closely related to the special sedimentary environment of deep-water, low-energy, and anoxic-reducing conditions. Its core characteristics are the enrichment of biogenic siliceous minerals, high organic matter content, and a well-developed pore structure. , Drilling engineering practices in recent years have shown that the Wufeng Formation to the first member of the Longmaxi Formation in the study area has stable roof and floor conditions, with limited structural disruption to the reservoir from regional tectonism, thereby providing a favorable geological foundation for shale gas preservation.

By combining shale gas occurrence patterns with predictive models of adsorbed and free gas contents, the gas-bearing properties of the two siliceous shale subfacies were systematically compared. The results indicate that S-2 has an average total gas content of 4.293 cm3/g, which is higher than the 3.622 cm3/g of S-4. Moreover, compared with S-2, S-4 not only has a lower average total gas content but also exhibits a lower proportion of free gases. The fundamental reason for these differences lies in the higher organic matter abundance in S-2, which provides abundant carriers for the adsorbed gas enrichment. Additionally, the higher content of rigid minerals such as quartz effectively resists compaction during later burial, promoting the preservation and development of organic matter pores and an effective storage space. The weaker water-locking effect of this lithofacies further optimizes the gas-bearing efficiency. However, because of its higher clay mineral content, S-4 is prone to directional alignment of clay minerals, leading to pore blockage, while the resulting elevated water saturation exacerbates the water-locking effect and thereby restricts free gas storage.

Therefore, for shale gas exploration in the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation in the Anchang syncline, priority should be given to targeting S-2. Detailed characterization of its distribution range, thickness variation, and lateral continuity is recommended to improve the exploration success rates and the scale. Although S-4 shows a slightly inferior gas-bearing performance, it still possesses exploration potential. Targeted measures, such as selecting low-damage water-based fracturing fluids and adopting staged multicluster fracturing technologies, can disrupt the oriented arrangement structure of clay minerals, reduce reservoir water saturation, and improve reservoir seepage conditions, thereby achieving the effective release of its shale gas resource potential.

Conclusions

(1)The siliceous shale facies of the Wufeng–Longmaxi Formation can be subdivided into mixed siliceous shale facies (S-2) and clay-bearing siliceous shale facies (S-4). S-2 is dominated by organic matter pores, exhibiting overall higher pore volume and specific surface area compared to S-4. This lithofacies demonstrates stronger methane adsorption capacity, with a higher average maximum absolute adsorption capacity than S-4, indicating that the degree of organic pore development is a key factor controlling adsorption performance.(2)On the basis of the SDR model calculations, the average in situ gas content of S-2 is 4.293 cm3/g, higher than that of S-4 at 3.622 cm3/g. Both types of shale are dominated by adsorbed gas, but the proportion of free gas in S-2 is relatively higher.(3)The in situ gas content of shale is not controlled by any single factor but by the synergistic coupling of TOC content, pore structure, mineral composition, and water saturation. The TOC content primarily dictates the upper limit of methane adsorption capacity. Pore structure and mineral composition influence gas preservation by regulating the availability of adsorption sites and the effective pore space for the free gas. Crucially, under normal-pressure conditions, water saturation exerts a particularly strong inhibitory effect on free gas retention, as an elevated water content occupies effective pore space and weakens capillary sealing, a mechanism that is especially pronounced in S-4.(4)S-2 is the preferred lithofacies for shale gas exploration and development in the Anchang syncline owing to its superior gas-bearing properties. For analogous normal-pressure residual synclines, clay-mineral-controlled water saturation represents a first-order control on free gas retention, and it is therefore recommended that water-saturation assessment be incorporated as a key parameter in reservoir evaluation alongside fine subfacies characterization. Future research should prioritize the establishment of a multiparameter integrated sweet-spot evaluation index system and the development of effective water-locking mitigation techniques for S-4.
