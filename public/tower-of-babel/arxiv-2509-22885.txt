Computing k k -mers in Graphs
Jarno N. Alanko, Maximo Perez-Lopez

Abstract
We initiate the study of computational problems on k k -mers (strings of length k k ) in labeled graphs . As a starting point, we consider the problem of counting the number of distinct k k -mers found on the walks of a graph. We establish that this is # \# P-hard, even on connected deterministic DAGs. However, in the class of deterministic Wheeler graphs (Gagie, Manzini, and Sirén, TCS 2017), we show that distinct k k -mers of such a graph W W can be counted using O ⁡ ( | W | ​ k ) O(|W|k) or O ⁡ ( n 4 ​ log ⁡ k ) O(n^{4}\log k) arithmetic operations, where n n is the number of vertices of the graph, and | W | |W| is n n plus the number of edges. The latter result uses a new generalization of the technique of prefix doubling to Wheeler graphs. To generalize our results beyond Wheeler graphs, we discuss ways to transform a graph into a Wheeler graph in a manner that preserves the k k -mers. As an application of our k k -mer counting algorithms, we construct a representation of the de Bruijn graph ( dBg ) of the k k -mers in time O ⁡ ( | d ​ B ​ g | + | W | ​ k ) O(|dBg|+|W|k) . Given that the Wheeler graph can be exponentially smaller than the de Bruijn graph, for large k k this provides a theoretical improvement over previous de Bruijn graph construction methods from graphs, which must spend Ω ⁡ ( k ) \Omega(k) time per k k -mer in the graph. Our representation occupies O ⁡ ( | d ​ B ​ g | + | W | ​ k ​ log ⁡ ( max 1 ≤ ℓ ≤ k ⁡ ( n ℓ ) ) ) O(|dBg|+|W|k\log(\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k}(n_{\ell}))) bits of space, where n ℓ n_{\ell} is the number of distinct ℓ \ell -mers in the Wheeler graph.

Introduction

In recent years, a sizeable body of literature in the field of computational biology has emerged on storing and indexing sets of
k
-mers (strings of length
k
). Typically, these
k
-mers are all the overlapping windows of length
k
in some genome sequence. In strings, the problem of counting the number of distinct
k
-mers is easily solvable in linear time using a combination of suffix and LCP arrays
[
16
,
15
]
, which are computable in
O(n)
time for polynomial alphabets
[
14
]
. There exist highly optimized practical tools for counting short
k
-mers on strings and encoding them efficiently by exploiting this overlap structure
[
17
]
.

However, a significant effort is underway to move from linear reference genomes into a more rich graph-based reference genome that encodes all variation in a species. Very little attention has been paid to the problem of counting
k
-mers in more general structures such as trees, DAGs and general graphs, some of which can encode an
exponential
number of distinct
k
-mers. There is some practical work toward this direction in the field of pangenomics
[
19
]
, but the theoretical limits and possibilities remain largely unexplored. In this paper, we take the first steps towards mapping this uncharted territory.

First, we show that it is #P-hard to compute the number of distinct
k
-mers in directed labeled graphs, even if we restrict ourselves to connected deterministic graphs without directed cycles. However, we find that in the special case of deterministic
Wheeler graphs

[
11
]
, we can compute the number of
k
-mers in
O(|W|k)
time with a simple dynamic programming approach. Pushing further, we study what it takes to improve the time complexity with respect to
k
. Here, the problem turns out to be significantly more complicated than on strings, but we manage an
O(n^{4}\log k)
algorithm by generalizing the technique of
prefix doubling

[
16
]
to Wheeler graphs. We believe there is room to improve the
O(n^{4})
factor. These results pave the way toward counting
k
-mers in arbitrary graphs without explicit enumeration, by reducing them to Wheeler graphs that preserve the
k
-mer set.

As an application of
k
-mer counting, we present an algorithm that builds the de Bruijn graph (dBg) of the
k
-mers of a Wheeler graph
W
in
O(|dBg|+|W|k)
time, where
|G|
denotes the total number of vertices and edges of a graph
G
. Crucially, the multiplicative factor of
k
is on the size of the Wheeler graph, rather than the size of the potentially exponential de Bruijn graph. Previous algorithms to build de Bruijn graphs from graphs or sets of
k
-mers spend
\Omega(k)
time per
k
-mer inserted in the graph, which inevitably incurs a cost of
O(k|dBg|)
. In the bioinformatics literature, we find the GCSA2 construction algorithm
[
19
]
, which builds the de Bruijn graph of a graph by building all distinct
k
-mers of the graph by prefix doubling. Other de Bruijn graph construction algorithms, such as dynamic versions of the data structure by Bowe et al.
[
5
,
4
]
, do not take a graph as input, and only allow the insertion of an explicit
k
-mer at a time, at a worst-case cost of
\Omega(k)
per insertion. This application showcases how our theoretical results translate into practical gains for graph algorithms.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we lay down some basic notation and concepts. In Section
3
, we discuss the computational complexity of the problem, and prove the problem to be
\#
P-hard. In Section
4
, we solve the problem on Wheeler graphs in time
O(|W|k)
or
O(n^{4}\log k)
. In Section
5
, we discuss ways of turning an arbitrary graph into a Wheeler graph with the same
k
-mers in order to apply algorithms in Section
4
. Section
6
describes the de Bruijn graph construction algorithm. Section
7
concludes the paper with some closing remarks.

2
Notation and preliminaries

We consider directed graphs with single character labels on the edges. Formally, we consider graphs
G
formed by a vertex set
V(G)
and an edge set
E(G)\subseteq V\times V\times\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is an alphabet of symbols, that is totally ordered by a relation
\prec
. The indegree and outdegree of a node
v
are denoted with
\mathsf{indeg}(v)
and
\mathsf{outdeg}(v)
, respectively. A
k
-mer
\alpha
is a string of length
k
from
\Sigma^{k}
, indexed from
1
to
k
. We compare strings by extending
\prec
to
\Sigma^{*}
co-lexicographically, which is the right-to-left lexicographical order (we abbreviate this by
colex
order). We say that a labeled graph
G
contains a
k
-mer
\alpha
if there exists a walk
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in
G
such that
(v_{i-1},v_{i},\alpha[i])\in E(G)
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. In this case, we say that
\alpha

reaches

v_{k}
, and write
\alpha\leadsto v_{k}
. A Wheeler graph is a labeled directed graph with the following properties
[
11
]
:

Definition 1
.

A labeled directed graph
G
is a
Wheeler graph
if there is an ordering on the nodes such that nodes with no incoming edges precede those with positive indegree, and every pair of edges
(u,v,a),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime},a^{\prime})
of the state graph satisfies the following two properties:

•

W1:
a\prec a^{\prime}\Rightarrow v<v^{\prime}

•

W2:
(a=a^{\prime}\land u<u^{\prime})\Rightarrow v\leq v^{\prime}

We say that a graph is
deterministic
if no two outgoing edges from the same vertex have the same label. In deterministic Wheeler graphs, we have the following stronger property:

Lemma 2
.

(
[
7
]
) In deterministic Wheeler graphs, for any pair of edges,
(u,v,a),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime},a^{\prime})
we have
(a=a^{\prime})\Rightarrow(u<u^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow v<v^{\prime})
.
∎

There exists an algorithm to transform a non-deterministic Wheeler graph into a deterministic one with the same
k
-mers, that in the worst case is only a linear factor larger
[
2
]
. In contrast, in general graphs the deterministic version could be exponentially larger. However, the running time of the algorithm is
O(n^{3})
, and improving this upper bound is an open problem. Thus, we assume that our input are deterministic graphs throughout the paper.

In a labeled graph we can identify the incoming
infimum
and
supremum
strings to every vertex, denoted
\inf_{v}
and
\sup_{v}
for all
v\in V
. We use the definition of
[
3
]
, though note that they read the strings backwards, and use lexicographical order, while we read the strings forward and use colex order instead. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{2n}
be the colexicographically sorted infimum and supremum strings of the
n
vertices of a Wheeler graph. The
longest common suffix
array is defined as an array
\mathsf{LCS}[2..2n]
, where
\mathsf{LCS}[i]
is the longest common suffix of
\gamma_{i-1}
and
\gamma_{i}
. It was first defined in
[
7
]
, where it was also proven that in a Wheeler graph,
\inf_{v}\preceq\sup_{v}
, and
\sup_{u}\preceq\inf_{v}
for all
u<v
in Wheeler order. Observe that we can find the
\mathsf{LCS}
value between any infima and suprema in
O(1)
time, by building a range minimum query data structure
[
10
]
over the
\mathsf{LCS}
array, and performing the appropriate query.

We find it convenient to divide the
\mathsf{LCS}
array into two parts: one is the
external

\mathsf{LCS}
array, written
\mathsf{ELCS}
, that holds the
\mathsf{LCS}
between the supremum and infimum string of
v_{i-1}
and
v_{i}
. The other one is the
internal

\mathsf{LCS}
array, written
\mathsf{ILCS}
, that holds the
\mathsf{LCS}
value of the infimum and supremum of the same vertex. We compute them as
\mathsf{ELCS}(v_{i-1},v_{i})=\mathsf{LCS}[2i-1]
, and
\mathsf{ILCS}(v_{i})=\mathsf{LCS}[2i]
. In Section
4
we show how to use these arrays in the context of
k
-mers.

Model of computation
. Since the number of
k
-mers in a graph may be exponential, the time complexity of integer addition and multiplication of large integers can become relevant for the running time of our algorithms. We put this issue to the side by assuming a Word RAM model
[
12
]
with a word size large enough to hold the maximum numbers that we handle, which are proportional to the maximum number of
\ell
-mers in the graph, for any
1\leq\ell\leq k
.

3
Computational complexity of counting
k
-mers in graphs

Figure 1:
Reduction from the example DNF formula
F=(x_{1}\wedge x_{3})\vee(\neg x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\vee(\neg x_{1}\wedge\neg x_{3})
, to counting
3
-mers. Note how
d_{1}=1,d_{2}=0,d_{3}=1
and thus
\#SAT(F)=10-2^{1}-2^{0}-2^{1}=5
.

In this section we show that the
k
-mer counting problem on graphs is
\#
P-hard even when the graph is acyclic, connected (i.e., there exists a node from which every other node can be reached) and deterministic (i.e., no two edges with the same label have the same origin).

Let
\#SAT(F)
denote the number of satisfying assignments to a boolean formula
F
over
n
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in conjunctive normal form. Computing
\#SAT(F)
is the archetypical
\#P
-complete problem
[
20
]
. It is also well known that the problem is
\#P
-complete in disjunctive normal form since we can write the number of solutions to a CNF formula
F
as
2^{n}-\#SAT(\neg F)
, where
\neg F
can be written in disjunctive normal form of the same size as
F
by de Morgan’s laws.

Theorem 3
.

Counting the number of distinct
k
-mers on walks on an acyclic deterministic connected graph
G
is
\#
P-hard.

Proof.

Reduction from counting satisfying assignments to a DNF formula. Let
n
be the number of distinct variables in the formula. For each clause, we introduce a graph gadget. The gadget for clause
C_{j}
is as follows: add one node
v_{i}
for each
i=0,\ldots,n
, and edges
(v_{i-1},v_{i},c)
, for all variables
x_{i}
that appear in
C_{j}
, such that the label
c
is 1 if
x_{i}
appears as positive in
C_{j}
, otherwise 0. For variables
x_{i}
that do not appear in
C_{j}
, we add both edges
(v_{i-1},v_{i},0)
and
(v_{i-1},v_{i},1)
. Now each distinct
n
-mer in this gadget corresponds to a satisfying truth assignment to the clause, such that the
i
-th character of the
n
-mer is 1 iff variable
x_{i}
is set to true. Let
G
be the union of all the gadgets for all clauses in the input.
Now, the number of distinct
n
-mers in
G
is equal to the number of satisfying truth assignments. To make the graph connected, we add a new source node that has an edge to the sources of each of the gadgets, with a unique label
\#_{j}
for each gadget
C_{j}
. See Figure
1
. Let us denote the modified graph with
G_{\#}
. The addition of the new source node adds
2^{d_{j}}
distinct
n
-mers ending in the gadget for
C_{j}
, where
d_{j}
is the number of nodes with two outgoing edges among the first
(n-1)
nodes of the gadget. Thus, if the number of
n
-mers in
G_{\#}
is
N
, and the number of clauses is
m
, then the number of satisfying assignments to the DNF formula is
N-\sum_{j=1}^{m}2^{d_{j}}
.
∎

It is
\#P
-hard even to count the number of
k
-mers arriving at a single node:

Corollary 4
.

Counting the number of
k
-mers arriving at a single node
v
in a graph
G
is
\#
P-hard.

Proof.

If there was an algorithm to count the
k
-mers coming into a single node
v
, then we could add a new sink state to any graph, with edges labeled with a unique symbol
\#
pointing to the sink: now the number of
(k+1)
-mers ending at the sink equals the number of
k
-mers in the original graph.
∎

Theorem
3
also implies that counting factors of length
k
in a regular language defined by a DFA is
\#
P-hard:

Corollary 5
.

Counting factors of length
k
in the language of a DFA is
\#
P-hard.

Proof.

We can turn a connected deterministic graph
G
into a DFA by picking a node that can reach all states as the initial state, and making every state accepting. Now counting factors of length
k
in the language of the automaton counts
k
-mers in the graph.
∎

4
Algorithms for
k
-mer counting in deterministic Wheeler graphs

In this section, we develop two polynomial-time algorithms for counting
k
-mers on deterministic Wheeler graphs. We abbreviate those by
DWGs
. Let
W
be a DWG, with
|V(W)|=n
,
|E(W)|=m
and
|W|=n+m
. The first algorithm has time complexity
O(|W|k)
. For the second algorithm, we reduce the dependency on
k
, but we end up with a time complexity of
O(n^{4}\log k)
.

These algorithms require, as an internal step, to obtain the LCS array up to a length of
k
, i.e.
\min\{LCS(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}),k\}
for all values in the array. In the case of the
O(n^{4}\log k)
algorithm, one version of the algorithm from
[
3
]
runs in
O(m+n^{2}+n\log\sigma)
time, which is within the bounds if we allow a small dependency on the alphabet size; so we choose to compute the full LCS first. In the case of the
O(|W|k)
algorithm, we note that the
O(m+n\log\sigma)
version from
[
3
]
only works for Wheeler
semi-DFAs
, which require having exactly one source, whereas here we do not make that assumption. The other algorithm from
[
3
]
spends
O(m\log n+n\log\sigma)
time. Here we present an alternative tailored to our algorithm: during the
k
-mer counting algorithm we can build at the same time the LCS array, with a total cost of
O(|W|k)
and the values capped to
k
, which is enough for our purposes.

4.1
Counting
k
-mers in
O(|W|k)
time

Let
S_{v}^{k}
be the set of labels on incoming walks of length
k
to vertex
v
. The algorithm relies on two properties of deterministic Wheeler graphs. First, the incoming edges to a node all have the same label (a direct consequence of the first condition in Definition
1
), a property called
input-consistency
. We denote with
\lambda(v)
the label on the incoming edges to
v
, setting
\lambda(s)=\varepsilon
for any source
s
. Second, we have the following well-known property on the colexicographic order of incoming
k
-mers, which we prove here for completeness.

Lemma 6
.

For two nodes
u,v
in a DWG, if
u<v
, then for any two
k
-mers
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha\leadsto u
and
\beta\leadsto v
, we have
\alpha\preceq\beta

Proof.

If
\alpha=\beta
, the claim is trivially true. Otherwise, let
i
be the largest index such that
\alpha[i]\neq\beta[i]
.
Let
\gamma=\alpha[i+1..k]=\beta[i+1..k]
.
There exists a path
u_{1},\ldots u_{\ell}
labeled with
\gamma
such that
u_{\ell}=u
, and a path
v_{1},\ldots v_{\ell}
labeled with
\gamma
such that
v_{\ell}=v
. We also have
u_{j}\neq v_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, or otherwise the graph is not deterministic, so we can iterate the second condition in Lemma
2
for pairs of edges
(u_{j},u_{j+1},c)
,
(v_{j},v_{j+1},c)
to obtain
u_{1}<v_{1}\Leftrightarrow u<v
, so we have
u_{1}<v_{1}
. Since
\lambda(u_{1})=\alpha[i]\neq\beta[i]=\lambda(v_{1})
, the first condition of Lemma
2
applies to incoming edges to
u_{1}
and
v_{1}
, and we have
\alpha[i]\prec\beta[i]\Leftrightarrow u_{1}<v_{1}
, so
\alpha[i]\prec\beta[i]
which implies
\alpha\prec\beta
.
∎

Corollary 7
.

If two different nodes
u<v
of the DWG share a
k
-mer, then they share
\max_{u}^{k}=\min_{v}^{k}
.
∎

If the DWG has sources, some vertices may not have incoming
k
-mers, but they always have an
infimum
and a
supremum
string, as defined in
[
3
]
. We also prove that, if two vertices
u<v
share a
k
-mer, then
\max^{k}_{u}
and
\min^{k}_{v}
are suffixes of
\sup_{u}
and
\inf_{v}
, respectively. If they do not share a
k
-mer, that may not hold in general. This makes it possible to check for shared
k
-mers using the original LCS array.

Lemma 8
.

Two different nodes
u<v
of a DWG share a
k
-mer if and only if
\mathsf{LCS}(\sup_{u},\inf_{v})\geq k
. In that case,
\max^{k}_{u}
is a suffix of
\sup_{u}
, and
\min^{k}_{v}
is a suffix of
\inf_{v}
.

Proof.

The lemma follows from two facts: 1)
u
and
v
can only share a
k
-mer if
|\sup_{u}|\geq k
and
|\inf_{v}|\geq k
, and 2)
|\sup_{u}|\geq k
implies that
\max^{k}_{u}
is a suffix of
\sup_{u}
, and
|\inf_{u}|\geq k
implies that
\min^{k}_{u}
is a suffix of
\inf_{u}
. Thus, if
\mathsf{LCS}(\sup_{u},\inf_{v})\geq k
, then
|\sup_{u}|\geq k,|\inf_{v}|\geq k
, and 2) implies
\max^{k}_{u}=\min^{k}_{v}
. Otherwise, if
\mathsf{LCS}(\sup_{u},\inf_{v})<k
, either a)
|\sup_{u}|<k\vee|\inf_{v}|<k
, and 1) does not hold, so the
k
-mers cannot be shared; or
|\sup_{u}|\geq k\wedge|\inf_{v}|\geq k
, but then by 2)
\max^{k}_{u}
and
\min^{k}_{v}
are suffixes of
\sup_{u}
and
\inf_{v}
, and then their
LCS
is less than
k
. We prove facts 1) and 2) in appendix
A
.
∎

Now, we show a recurrence to build the sets
S_{v}^{\ell}
for all
\ell=1,\ldots,k
. Let
\textrm{pred}(v)
be the set of nodes that have an outgoing edge to
v
. Then, for a non-source vertex, we have:

S_{v}^{\ell}=\bigcup_{u\in\textrm{pred}(v)}\{\alpha\cdot\lambda(v)\mid\alpha\in S_{u}^{\ell-1}\}

(1)

with the base case
S_{v}^{0}=\{\varepsilon\}
for all
v\in V
, where
\varepsilon
denotes the empty string. For sources,
S_{v}^{\ell}=\emptyset
for
\ell>0
.

However, at this point, we are not interested in enumerating the distinct
\ell
-mers, only counting them. Let
C_{\ell}(v)=|S_{v}^{\ell}|
. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}
be the in-neighbours of
v
sorted in the Wheeler order. For
\ell=0
, we have
C_{\ell}(v)=1
for all
v
. Then, for
\ell>0
, sources have
C_{\ell}(s)=0
; and for non-source vertices, the size of the union in Equation
1
can be written as:

C_{\ell}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}C_{u_{i}}^{\ell-1}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}|S_{u_{i-1}}^{\ell-1}\cap S_{u_{i}}^{\ell-1}|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}C_{u_{i}}^{\ell-1}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}[\max S_{u_{i-1}}^{\ell-1}=\min S_{u_{i}}^{\ell-1}],

where
[P]
is the Iverson bracket that evaluates to 1 if the predicate
P
is true, otherwise to 0. The last equality holds thanks to Corollary
7
. Now the only difficult part in computing the recurrence is determining whether
\max S_{u_{i-1}}^{\ell-1}=\min S_{u_{i}}^{\ell-1}
. As discussed before, if we have the LCS array available, by Lemma
8
, we can directly check it in the array. Otherwise, there exists a quickly computable recurrence for that as well.
Let
\ell\geq 1
. For non-source nodes
u,v
, let
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(u,v)=[\max S_{u}^{\ell}=\min S_{v}^{\ell}]
, and let
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(u,v)=0
if
u
or
v
are sources. Furthermore, for non-source
v
define

\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}(v)=[|\text{inf}_{v}|\geq\ell]\cdot[|\text{sup}_{v}|\geq\ell]\cdot[\min S^{\ell}_{v}=\max S^{\ell}_{v}],

and
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}(s)=0
for a source
s
. We have that
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(u,v)=1
iff
\lambda(u)=\lambda(v)
and
\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(\max\textrm{pred}(u),\min\textrm{pred}(v))=1
, with the base case
\mathsf{share}_{0}(u,v)=1
for all
u,v
. Similarly, we also have that
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}(v)=1
iff
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell-1}(\min\textrm{pred}(v))=1
,
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell-1}(\max\textrm{pred}(v))=1
and
\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(\min\textrm{pred}(v),\max\textrm{pred}(v))=1
, with the base case
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq 0}(v)=1
for all
v
. To compute these without tabulating all
O(|V|^{2})
input pairs
u,v
to
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}
, we exploit the formula

\displaystyle\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(u,v)=\min\{\min_{u<w\leq v}\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(w-1,w),\min_{u<w<v}\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}(w)\}.

With this, it suffices to compute
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}
only for adjacent inputs
(v_{i-1},v)
, and build a range minimum data structure
[
10
]
over both the
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}(v)
and
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(v_{i-1},v_{i})
arrays, to be able to resolve
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(u,v)
in constant time for any input pair. Proceeding level by level
\ell=1,2,\ldots,k
, we obtain:

Theorem 9
.

We can compute the number of distinct
k
-mers in the state graph
(V,E)
of a DWG in total time
O((|V|+|E|)k)=O(|W|k)
. ∎

Note that during the procedure to compute
\mathsf{share}_{\ell}
and
\mathsf{ILCS}_{\geq\ell}
for all
\ell
, we can also infer the values of the
\mathsf{ILCS}
and
\mathsf{ELCS}
array up to
k
, and keep all needed information in
O(n)
space.

4.2
Counting
k
-mers in
O(n^{4}\log k)
time in deterministic Wheeler graphs

In this section, we develop an algorithm that improves the dependency on
k
from
O(k)
to
O(\log k)
by using a prefix doubling technique. Implementing prefix doubling on a Wheeler graph turns out to be significantly more complicated than prefix doubling on strings, so the time complexity on
n
grows from
O(n)
to
O(n^{4})
compared to Section
4.1
. We suspect the factor
O(n^{4})
is probably not tight, but we leave any improvement as future work.

Let
C_{\ell}(u,v)
be the number of distinct
\ell
-mers from
u
to
v
. Let
P_{\ell}(u,v)
the set of distinct
\ell
-mers on paths of length
\ell
starting from
u
and ending at
v
. Let
T_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)=|\bigcup_{u\in[v_{i},\ldots v_{j}]}P_{\ell}(u,v)|
. We call an
\ell
-mer in the input graph
white
if all its occurrences arrive at the same node. Otherwise the
\ell
-mer is called
black
. We will count the
\ell
-mers in
T_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)
by counting the white and black
\ell
-mers separately. We denote the number of white
\ell
-mers with
T^{W}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)
.

To develop a recursive formula for
T_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)
, we count separately the
\ell
-mers whose first half is a white
(\ell/2)
-mer, and those whose first half is a black
(\ell/2)
-mer. Those whose first half is white can be counted with:

\mathsf{white\_start}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)=\sum_{w\in V(W)}T^{W}_{\ell/2}([v_{i},\ldots,v_{j}],w)C_{\ell/2}(w,v)

(2)

This avoids double counting because the first halves are unique. Those whose first half is black is computed as follows. Let
B_{\ell}
be the set of intervals of all black
\ell
-mers. Let
R_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}])
be the set of nodes reachable from
[v_{i},\ldots v_{j}]
with paths of length exactly
\ell
.
Let
D^{\inf}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)
be 1 if the smallest incoming
\ell
-mer to
v
is a suffix of
\inf_{v}
, and it can be reached from any of the nodes
[v_{i},\ldots v_{j}]
. Similarly, let
D^{\sup}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)
be 1 if the largest incoming
\ell
-mer to
v
is a suffix of
\sup_{v}
, and it can be reached from any of the nodes
[v_{i},\ldots v_{j}]
. Given a reachability set
S=R_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}])
, and an interval
I=[u_{1},\ldots u_{m}]\in B_{\ell}
, we define a special kind of an intersection
S\triangle I
that sometimes excludes endpoints of
I
. That is,
S\triangle I=S\cap I\setminus Q
, where
Q
contains
u_{1}
if
D^{\sup}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],u_{1})
is 0, and
u_{m}
if
D^{\inf}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],u_{m})
is 0.
Then the
\ell
-mers from
[v_{i},\ldots v_{j}]
to
v
whose first half is black is given by:

\mathsf{black\_start}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)=\sum_{I\in B_{\ell/2}}T_{\ell/2}(R_{\ell/2}([v_{i},...v_{j}])\triangle I,v)

(3)

This avoids double counting because each distinct black first half corresponds to a distinct term in the sum. We iterate the intervals in
B_{\ell}
in increasing order of their start point, maintaining the smallest and the largest node in the reachability set
R_{\ell/2}([v_{i},...v_{j}])
that intersects with the current interval
I
(this intersection is also an interval by path coherence). Combining the two, we have:

T_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)=\mathsf{white\_start}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)+\mathsf{black\_start}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)

(4)

We now show how to count the number of
\ell
-mers from
any node
into
v
given
T_{\ell/2}
and
C_{\ell/2}
. We again decompose the problem by whether the first half is black or white:

C_{\ell}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}T^{W}_{\ell/2}([v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}],w_{i})C_{\ell/2}(w_{i},v)+\sum_{I\in B_{\ell}}T_{\ell/2}(I,v)

(5)

where the left sum is the
\ell
-mers with a white first half and the right sum is the
\ell
-mers with a black first half.

4.2.1
Algorithm and details

We start by computing the LCS information to be able to answer queries for

(Section

(Section

for powers of two

Computing

and

We describe a recurrence to compute for all pairs of nodes

for range maximum queries for ranges of

To compute

. Otherwise, let

. Then,

D^{\inf}_{\ell}(u,v)=D^{\inf}_{\ell/2}(u,w_{\inf})\vee[\mathsf{ILCS}(w_{inf})\geq\ell/2]\cdot\Big(\bigvee_{w\in M_{\ell/2}\setminus\{w_{\inf}\}}\mathsf{share}_{\ell/2}(w_{\inf},w)\cdot D^{\inf}_{\ell/2}(u,w)\Big).

As a base case,
D^{\inf}_{1}(u,v)=[(u,v)\in E(W)]
. The formula scans the nodes in

that have the same incoming

-mer as the smallest of

, to see if in any case, this

-mer starts from

. To compute

by

.

Computing

The intervals of black

where

Computing

Given the value of

\displaystyle T^{W}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)

\displaystyle=T_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)

\displaystyle-\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(v,v-1)\cdot D^{\inf}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)

\displaystyle-\mathsf{share}_{\ell}(v,v+1)\cdot D^{\sup}_{\ell}([v_{i},\ldots v_{j}],v)\cdot[\mathsf{ILCS}(v)<\ell]

Computing

Let

be the reachability matrix with
\ell

is 1 if there is a walk of length

from

for constant-time boolean range-or queries by using a bit vector rank structure

, keeping the space at

4.2.2
Running time and space

The running time is determined by the time to compute the values of
T_{\ell}
. On each of the
\log k
levels of the recursion, there are
O(n^{3})
inputs to

(
O(n^{2})

and
O(n)

time to compute, for a total time of

for the whole algorithm. The space is determined by the total size of all

. Since the data structures for a certain level

only depend on the ones at level

5
k
-mer preserving graph transformations to Wheeler graphs

The algorithms in Section
4
are of limited usability since they apply only to deterministic Wheeler graphs. In this Section we discuss how to transform some more general graphs into deterministic Wheeler graphs.

An
layered DAG
with
k
layers is a directed acyclic graph that can be partitioned into
k
disjoint subsets of vertices
V_{0},\ldots V_{k-1}
such that edges exists only from vertices in
V_{i}
into
V_{i+1}
for
i=0,\ldots,k-2
. For any graph, we can build the
k
times unfolded DAG, which preserves the
k
-mers in the graph:

Definition 10
.

The
k
times unfolded DAG of a graph
G
is a layered DAG
G_{k}
with
k+1
layers such that the vertices on each layer
V_{i}
are a copy of the vertices of
G
, and the edge set is defined so that for every edge
(u,v)
in
G
there are edges from the copy of
u
in layer
i
to the copy of
v
in layer
i+1
for all
i=0,\ldots k-1
.

We can turn this into a deterministic DFA by introducing a new initial state with epsilon-transitions into the first layer, and determinizing with the classic subset construction. Now, since every
k
-mer corresponds to a distinct path from the initial state to the final layer, we can count
k
-mers by counting the distinct paths to each node on the final layer of the DAG, which is easily done with a dynamic programming algorithm that processes the nodes in topological order.

However, this does not exploit overlaps between
k
-mers. For deterministic acyclic graphs that have a source node that can reach all nodes, we may use the Wheelerization algorithm of Alanko et al.
[
2
]
that computes the smallest equivalent Wheeler DFA in
O(n\log n)
time in the size of the output. More generally, if the input is the state graph
G
of any DFA recognizing a Wheeler language, the minimum equivalent Wheeler DFA can be constructed in
O(|G|^{3}n\log n)
time, where
n
is the size of the output
[
9
]
.

Another option is to build the de Bruijn graph of the
k
-mers, which is always Wheeler
[
11
]
. Unfortunately, this does not help much with counting the
k
-mers because building the de Bruijn graph takes time proportional to the number of
k
-mers because there is one node per
k
-mer. That is, building the de Bruijn graph amounts to a brute force enumeration of all
k
-mers. However, the de Bruijn graph is not necessarily the smallest graph for a given set of
k
-mers: For example, the Wheeler DFA minimization algorithm of Alanko et al.
[
1
]
, run on the de Bruijn graph, can reduce the size of the graph while maintaining Wheelerness. Building this compressed graph directly without first building the de Bruijn graph would be useful, but seems to be nontrivial. We leave this open for future work.

as the concatenation of unary representations of the numbers, that is,

. Encoded this way, the total space is

6.3
Computing
F
,
L
and
K

We aim to compute all
F_{\ell}
,
L_{\ell}
and
K
in a combined time of
O(|W|k+|dBg|)
. We can think of
K(v)
as a special case of an array
K_{\ell}(v)
, that counts the number of left-extensions of the inner
\ell
-mers of
v
.
The key to understanding the algorithm is to see that the values in the arrays
F_{\ell}
,
L_{\ell}
and
K_{\ell}
encode the counts of
distinct left-extensions
of each of the distinct
(\ell-1)
-mers reaching a given node
v
, which are inherited from the left-extensions of
(\ell-2)
-mers of in-neighbors of
v
. This means that the values of
F_{\ell}
,
L_{\ell}
and
K_{\ell}
can be deduced inductively from the values of
F_{\ell-1}
,
L_{\ell-1}
and
K_{\ell-1}
. However, we can not compute
K_{\ell}
for all
\ell<k
within our time complexity contraints. The algorithm will thus compute and store
F_{\ell}
and
L_{\ell}
for all
2\leq\ell\leq k
explicitly, and build a data structure to infer values of
K_{\ell}
with
\ell=k
from the
F
and
L
values of ancestors of the node being processed.

We compute
F_{\ell}
and
L_{\ell}
following a recursive formula. The base case, for
\ell=2
, is
F_{2}(v)=L_{2}(v)=C_{2}(v)
by input consistency: all the
2
-mers of a vertex
v
share the only incoming
1
-mer of
v
as a suffix. For
\ell>2
and any
v
, let
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{d}
be the in-neighbours of
v
. If
v
is a source,
F_{\ell}(v)=L_{\ell}(v)=0
. Otherwise:

F_{\ell}(v)=F_{\ell-1}(u_{1})+[\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{1})\geq\ell-2]\cdot\sum_{j=2}^{d}\mathsf{share}_{\ell-2}(u_{1},u_{j})\cdot\Big(F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})-\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(u_{j},u_{j-1})\Big),

L_{\ell}(v)=L_{\ell-1}(u_{d})+[\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{d})\geq\ell-2]\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\mathsf{share}_{\ell-2}(u_{j},u_{d})\cdot\Big(L_{\ell-1}(u_{j})-\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(u_{j},u_{j+1})\Big).

The formula for
F_{\ell}(v)
can be found by observing that, if
\mu_{\ell-1}(v)
is defined, there is at least one path spelling it, that passes through
u_{1}
, in fact, in that case the prefix of length
(\ell-2)
of
\mu_{\ell-1}(v)
is
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{1})
. Furthermore, each distinct
(\ell-1)
-mer reaching
u_{1}
and having
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{1})
as a suffix gives rise to an
\ell
-mer of
v
having
\mu_{\ell-1}(v)
as a suffix. Thus, we add
F_{\ell-1}(u_{1})
to the total. It could happen that
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{1})=\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{j})
for some
2\leq j\leq d
, if
\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{1})\geq\ell-2
, and
\mathsf{share}_{\ell-2}(u_{1},u_{j})=1
. In that case,
(\ell-1)
-mers reaching
u_{j}
and sharing
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{j})
also give rise to
\ell
-mers of
v
that we must count. Thus, if this happens we add
F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})
to
F_{\ell}(v)
, with care of avoiding double counting of the possible shared
(\ell-1)
-mer between
u_{j}
and
u_{j-1}
(note that the
u_{j}
we must add form an interval). The formula for
L_{\ell}(v)
is found in a similar way. Clearly, we can compute
F_{\ell}(v)
and
L_{\ell}(v)
in time proportional to the in-degree of
v
, so computing
F_{\ell}
and
L_{\ell}(v)
for all
v
and all
2\leq\ell\leq k
takes
O((n+m)k)=O(|W|k)
time.

Computing
K(v)
is more involved, and we aim to compute
K(v)
for all non-sink and non-source
v
in a combined time of
O(|W|k+|dBg|)
. To explain the logic of the algorithm, consider a hypothetical function
Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha)
that given a vertex
v
and an inner
(\ell-1)
-mer
\alpha
of
v
, computes the number of
\ell
-mers reaching
v
, that have
\alpha
as a suffix. Then, computing
K(v)
amounts to computing
Q_{k}(v,\alpha_{i})
for all inner
(k-1)
-mers
\alpha_{i}
of
v
. We present a recursive formula for
Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha)
, that assumes that we know the in-neighbours of
v
that
\alpha[1..\ell-2]
reaches, named
u_{a},\ldots,u_{b}
. The idea hinges on the fact that if
\alpha[1..\ell-2]
is an external
(\ell-2)
-mer of one or more in-neighbours, we can obtain
Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha)
from the values of
F_{\ell-1}
and
L_{\ell-1}
of the in-neighbours that
\alpha[1..\ell-2]
reaches. Otherwise, if it is an inner
(\ell-2)
-mer of an in-neighbour, then that is the only vertex that the
(\ell-2)
-mer reaches, and we can recurse on that vertex with
\ell\leftarrow\ell-1
:

Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha)=\begin{cases}F_{\ell-1}(u_{a})\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{if }a=b\text{ and }\alpha[1..i-2]=\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{a}),\\
L_{\ell-1}(u_{a})\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{if }a=b\text{ and }\alpha[1..i-2]=\phi_{\ell-2}(u_{a}),\\
Q_{\ell-1}(u_{a},\alpha[1..\ell-2])\quad\text{if }a=b\text{ and }\alpha[1..i-2]\text{ is an inner }(l-2)\text{-mer of }u_{a},\\
L_{\ell-1}(u_{a})+\sum_{j=a+1}^{b}(F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})-\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(u_{j-1},u_{j}))\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that we define
Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha)
only if
\alpha
is an inner
(\ell-1)
-mer. If
\ell=2
, there does not exist an inner
1
-mer of
v
because of input consistency; therefore any call to
Q_{3}(v,\alpha)
will be resolved without recursing further (we do not recurse if
a\neq b
, and if
a=b
,
\alpha[1..\ell-2]=\alpha[1]
will be equal to
\mu_{1}(u_{a})
).

This recurrence would be too expensive to implement directly, for we cannot afford to build all the inner
(k-1)
-mers of
v
, neither search to find the right in-neighbours for each
(k-1)
-mer. Instead, our strategy is to scan the in-neighbours of
v
, left-to-right, and output the values of all the
(k-2)
-mers that reach the in-neighbours (making sure to avoid duplicates, and to avoid reporting the values for the external
(k-1)
-mers of
v
). This way, we ensure getting all the values of
K(v)
. If there are inner
(k-2)
-mers reaching an in-neighbour, we recurse on it before continuing to report the next values, to keep the reported values in order. More formally, if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{t}
are all the inner
\ell
-mers of
v
in colex order, define
Q_{\ell}(v)=(Q_{\ell}(v,\alpha_{i}):\alpha_{i}\leadsto v)_{i=1}^{t}
. In the following subsection, we show the algorithm to compute
Q_{\ell}(v)
.

6.3.1
Basic computation of
K(v)

To compute
K(v)=Q_{k}(v)
efficiently and in order, we define first a new data structure. For all
1\leq\ell\leq k
, and all vertices
v\in V(W)
with in-neighbours
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}
, we define the pointers
\mathsf{next}_{\ell}(v,u_{j})
, for all
1\leq j\leq d
, as the smallest
p>j
, such that there exists an
\ell
-mer reaching the in-neighbour
u_{p}
, that does not reach
u_{j}
. If no such in-neighbour exists, we define
\mathsf{next}_{\ell}(v,u_{j})
as
n+1
. We can compute the
\mathsf{next}_{\ell}
pointers from the left and right intervals in
O(|W|k)
time. We give the details in appendix
B
.

Now we describe the algorithm to compute
Q_{\ell}(v)
. We scan the in-neighbours of
v
with a pointer, starting at
j=1
. Every time we start scanning a new in-neighbour, we might want to avoid counting
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{j})
, because either a)
j=1
and
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{1})\lambda(v)=\mu_{\ell-1}(v)
is not an inner
k
-mer of
v
, or b)
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{j})=\phi_{\ell-2}(u_{j-1})
, and it has been counted already. We keep track of this with a variable
\mathsf{compute\_left}
, that is initially set to false, to avoid counting
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{1})
. Furthermore, before computing a value, we also check that it doesn’t correspond to the number of left extensions of
\phi_{\ell-1}(v)
, in which case we stop. We find two main cases:

1.

\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{j})\geq\ell-2
. We find the last in-neighbour
u_{b}
included in
\mathsf{left\_interval}_{\ell-2}(u_{j})
(see below how). If
b=d
and
\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{d})\geq\ell-2
, then the
(\ell-2)
-mer of
u_{j}
is the largest
(\ell-2)
-mer with an edge to
v
, so it will lead to
\phi_{\ell-1}(v)
, thus we are done with processing
v
. Otherwise, if
\mathsf{compute\_left}
is true, then we need to report a value. If
u_{b}=u_{j}
, then we report
F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})
, and we set
\mathsf{compute\_left}
to true. Otherwise, if
u_{b}>u_{j}
, then we report
F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})+\sum_{j<p\leq b}(F_{\ell-1}(u_{p})-\mathsf{share}_{\ell-1}(u_{p}))
.

In this case,
F_{\ell-1}(u_{j})=L_{\ell-1}(u_{j})
.
We set
\mathsf{compute\_left}

, otherwise to true.

2.

\mathsf{ILCS}(u_{j})<\ell-2
. In this case,
\mu_{\ell-2}(u_{j})\neq\phi_{\ell-2}(u_{j})
, unless they are both undefined. We perform the following three steps in order:

(a)

If
\mathsf{left\_interval}_{\ell-2}(u_{j})\neq\emptyset

.

(b)

If

. In general, a vertex

(C_{\ell}(v)-[\mathsf{left\_interval}_{\ell}(v)\neq\emptyset]-[\mathsf{ILCS}(v)<\ell]\cdot[\mathsf{right\_interval}_{\ell}(v)\neq\emptyset])>0.

(c)

Process the right interval. Here, we find three different cases:

i.

\mathsf{right\_interval}_{\ell-2}(u_{j})=\emptyset

\mathsf{right\_interval}_{\ell-2}(u_{j})\neq\emptyset

\mathsf{right\_interval}_{\ell-2}(u_{2})\neq\emptyset

.

6.3.2
Complexity

To analyse the running time of the algorithm, we observe that between a recursive call and the reporting of a value, we spend constant time. This holds thanks to the
\mathsf{next}_{\ell-2}
pointers, that allow us to jump to the next in-neighbour with a new
(\ell-2)
-mer to report. When reporting a value, we might spend time proportional to the value itself to compute it (if the sum formula is used). But every time we recurse, we might recurse
k
times before we reach a base case and output a new value, and this is inefficient. As presented, the algorithm for computing
Q_{k}(v)
spends time
O(|K(v)|k+\sum_{i=1}^{|K(v)|}K(v)[i])
per non-sink vertex
v
, which amounts to
O(|dBg|+\sum_{v\in V}|K(v)|k)
per Lemma
11
.

The only inefficiency that we suffer, at this point, is that each recursive call does not necessarily output one new value of
K(v)
, and we may have to recurse up to
O(k)
times before we output the next value. However, observe that the values that are reported when recursing on the same vertex are always the same. This makes it possible to apply memoization to solve the issue.

Memoization.

We add to all the vertices of
W_{\ell-1}
,
u^{\ell}
, a placeholder for two pointers
p
and
q
. At the start of the algorithm, these pointers are empty. Crucially, we keep and reuse the pointers between computations of
K(v_{i})
for different
v_{i}
. Whenever we recurse on a vertex
u^{\ell}
for the first time, we set
p
to the next position in the current list
K(v_{i})
, where the next value will be reported. When the recursion returns,
q
is set to point to the last filled position in
K(v_{i})
. Next time we recurse on
u^{\ell}
, whether in the computation of
K(v_{i})
or another
K(v_{j})
, instead of running the algorithm, we report all values between
p
and
q
on
K(v_{i})
. Clearly, there are at most
O(nk)
recursions during the computation of all
K(v)
. The theorem follows:

Theorem 13
.

Given a Wheeler graph
W
, we can compute the arrays
F
,
L
and
K
of the
k
-mers of
W
in time and space
O(|W|k+|dBg|)
.

7
Conclusion

This work, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to study the problem of
k
-mer counting on graphs. We have introduced the problem and established both hardness results and tractable cases. While the problem turns out to be
\#
P-hard, our results show that the problem is in P in the class of Wheeler graphs, and by extension on any class of graphs that can be transformed into a Wheeler graph of polynomial size, without changing the
k
-mers. As the first application for our
k
-mer counting algorithm, we have shown how to simulate the de Bruijn graph of the
k
-mers of a Wheeler graph, without having to construct
k
-mers explicitly, improving on the state-of-the-art in this respect
[
19
]
.

The tractability of the problem on Wheeler graphs raises several natural open questions. First, can we find the smallest Wheeler graph that preserves the
k
-mers of a given input graph? Second, which kinds of graphs admit polynomial-size Wheelerizations with the same
k
-mer set? Third, what is the best achievable polynomial degree of
n
for algorithms running in
O(poly(n)\log k)
time on Wheeler graphs? This work thus marks a first step toward a broader theory of
k
-mer counting in graphs.
