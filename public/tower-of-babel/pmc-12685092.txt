Myeloid GPSM1 regulates atherosclerosis progression by governing monocyte and macrophage activation and chemotaxis
Authors: Yuemei Zhang, Yuxin Cao, Yongxin Sun, Chenxia Zhou, Xiaorui Lyu, Daixi Wang, Jiangfei Zheng, Yuxin Shu, Lijun Yao, Rong Zhang, Jingyong Li, Feng Jiang, Xiang Cheng, Jing Yan, Cheng Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOur study explores the crucial role of G-protein signaling modulator 1 (GPSM1) in atherosclerosis, uncovering that its upregulation accelerates plaque formation while its ablation ameliorates disease progression. Furthermore, our investigation reveals that GPSM1 aggravates atherosclerosis through mechanisms involving peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22), facilitating monocyte and macrophage chemotaxis. Notably, blocking PMP22 or using a small-molecule inhibitor of GPSM1 reduces atherosclerosis in mice. These findings provide a promising basis for therapeutic strategies targeting GPSM1, ultimately benefiting patients with atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease and the leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide (1, 2). Its progression is driven by the sustained recruitment and activation of monocytes and macrophages. Initially, damaged endothelium releases adhesion molecules like VCAM-1 to recruit monocytes into the subintimal space, where they take up oxLDL and become foam cells (3, 4). These cells secrete proinflammatory factors and chemokines such as C-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 2 (CCL2; also known as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, MCP-1), leading to further activated monocyte infiltration and vessel damage (5, 6).

These monocytes and macrophages can be activated by diverse stimuli (e.g., cytokines, LPS, metabolic stress), leading to a hyperinflammatory state with enhanced chemotaxis and adhesion. This process involves low-dose inflammatory signals preactivating cells via TLR/NF-κB and MAPK pathways, as well as epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming (e.g., histone modifications, mTOR-HIF1α pathway) (7–11). Consequently, elucidating the regulators of monocyte/macrophage activation and migration remains a promising therapeutic strategy.

G-protein signaling modulator 1 (GPSM1) functions as a guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitor that stabilizes the GDP-bound conformation of Gαi (12–14) and is widely expressed in immune cells (15, 16). Our previous work demonstrated that GPSM1 deficiency ameliorated obesity-related metabolic disorders by suppressing adipose tissue inflammation (17). However, its role in atherosclerosis remains unclear. Considering that atherosclerosis is an inflammatory disease and that GPSM1 is required for the migration of lymphocytes and dendritic cells in vitro (16), we hypothesize that GPSM1 may regulate atherosclerosis progression by controlling monocyte and macrophage chemotaxis.

Here, our data show that myeloid-specific GPSM1-knockout mice on an Apoe−/− background (GPSM1LKOApoe−/−) or AAV-PCSK9-injected GPSM1LKO mice exhibit attenuated progression of atherosclerotic lesions, reduced necrotic core formation, and suppressed inflammation under a western diet (WD). By contrast, GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice with myeloid-specific GPSM1 overexpression are prone to atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, myeloid GPSM1 ablation inhibits monocyte and macrophage chemotaxis and adhesion to the aortic wall through downregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway, and the cAMP/PKA/KLF4/PMP22 axis functions as a downstream mediator for GPSM1 in regulating MAPK signaling. Notably, silencing PMP22 using siRNA-loaded liposomes protects GPSM1TG mice from atherosclerosis. Additionally, a small-molecule inhibitor of GPSM1 function that we identified could effectively postpone the development of atherosclerosis and might be a promising candidate for therapeutic intervention against atherosclerosis.

Results

Macrophage GPSM1 Expression Is Upregulated in Severe Atherosclerotic Plaque.

To elucidate the significance of GPSM1 in atherosclerosis, we first examined its expression in atherosclerotic lesions of Apoe−/− mice subjected to varying durations of a WD. Our findings revealed a 69.3% upregulation in GPSM1 protein expression in the aortas of Apoe−/− mice fed a WD for 12 wk, compared to those fed a WD for 6 wk (Fig. 1 A and B). Furthermore, we performed immunofluorescence staining to analyze GPSM1 expression in atherosclerotic lesions. Notably, GPSM1 expression was significantly elevated in CD68+ macrophages within the aortic root of Apoe−/− mice fed a WD for 12 wk, compared to those fed for 6 wk (Fig. 1 C and D). Moreover, to emphasize the clinical relevance of our findings, we extended our investigation to examine GPSM1 expression in human mild and severe atherosclerotic lesions. We observed higher GPSM1 expression in CD68+ macrophages of severe lesions within the human aortic wall (Fig. 1 E and F). Collectively, these results suggest that atherosclerosis leads to increased levels of GPSM1 in macrophages, indicating that GPSM1 may be involved in atherogenesis.

Myeloid-Specific GPSM1 Deletion Protects Against Atherosclerosis.

To investigate myeloid GPSM1’s role in the development of atherosclerosis, we generated Apoe and myeloid-specific GPSM1 double knockout mice (GPSM1LKOApoe−/−) by crossing GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice with Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice. The GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre; Apoe−/− mice were used as control mice. The macrophage-specific deletion of GPSM1 in GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice was confirmed by Western blot, while other tissues were not affected (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). In our study, male mice aged 8 wk were fed a WD for 16 wk. WD induced severe atherosclerotic lesions in both the enface view and cross-sections of the aortic roots in GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice. However, myeloid GPSM1 deficiency inhibited lesion formation in both the enface view (decreased by 40.3% vs. GPSM1fl/flApoe−/−, 40.5% vs. Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/−) and aortic root (decreased by 22.7% vs. GPSM1fl/flApoe−/−, 22.6% vs. Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/−) in GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice (Fig. 2 A–C). Furthermore, the necrotic core area in the aortic root of GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice was reduced by 48.9% vs. GPSM1fl/flApoe−/−, 51.3% vs. Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− (Fig. 2 B and D), while the collagen content was increased by 13.0% vs. GPSM1fl/flApoe−/−, 14.4% vs. Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (Fig. 2 B and E). As macrophages are considered the predominant cells producing proinflammatory cytokines during atherosclerosis development, GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice exhibited fewer macrophages, as indicated by CD68 staining of aortic root sections, suggesting that myeloid GPSM1 loss inhibited the infiltration ability of monocytes (Fig. 2F). Consistent with the decreased macrophage content in atherosclerotic plaques, the expression of the proinflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NOS2) and chemokines (CCL2), as well as endothelial-derived adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, E-SELECTIN) that contribute to leukocyte homing, was lower in the aortas of GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice compared to GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (Fig. 2G). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in body weight, serum cholesterol levels, or other indicators of lipid metabolism between the groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

Furthermore, to verify the effects of GPSM1 on the induction of atherosclerosis, we generated another atherosclerosis model by intravenously injecting GPSM1fl/fl and GPSM1LKO male mice with an adeno-associated virus overexpressing proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (AAV-c). After 16 wk of WD feeding, myeloid GPSM1 ablation mice receiving AAV-PCSK9 injection exhibited similar antiatherosclerotic and anti-inflammatory phenotypes as those with an Apoe−/− background (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Taken together, these results reveal the essential role of myeloid GPSM1 deficiency in protecting against atherosclerosis.

As sex differences in atherogenesis are important in cardiovascular analysis, we performed the atherosclerosis analysis in female mice after 16 wk of WD. Oil Red O staining of the entire aorta revealed a 33.5% reduction in atherosclerotic lesion area in female GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice compared with GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Cross-sectional analysis of the aortic roots further confirmed that the loss of GPSM1 in myeloid cells reduced the lesion areas (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Analysis of plaque morphology demonstrated a significant attenuation of necrotic core areas and increase of collagen content in myeloid-specific GPSM1-deficient mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). Furthermore, female GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice showed a significant decrease in the infiltration of CD68+ macrophages into atherosclerotic lesions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Therefore, these results are in line with our previous observation in male mice that the absence of GPSM1 in myeloid cells decelerates plaque progression and enhances the stability of atherosclerotic lesions.

Myeloid-Specific GPSM1 Overexpression Exacerbates Atherosclerosis.

Then myeloid-specific GPSM1 transgenic (GPSM1TGApoe−/−) mice were generated by breeding GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− mice with Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). The GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice were used as controls. The expression of GPSM1 was significantly upregulated in macrophages of GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). After 16 wk of WD feeding, Oil Red O staining revealed that myeloid GPSM1 overexpression exacerbated atherosclerotic lesion areas both across the entire aorta (showing a 41.3% and 34.9% increase compared with GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice, respectively) and within the aortic root (with a 32.6% and 31.9% increase vs. GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice, respectively) (Fig. 3 A–C). Moreover, the necrotic core area in GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice exhibited 35.7% and 40.3% increases, indicating a higher vulnerability to lesion rupture compared to GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− mice and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (Fig. 3 B and D). Masson’s staining showed an 18.5% and 19.5% reduction in collagen content in GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice compared to GPSM1ki/ki mice and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (Fig. 3 B and E). Given that macrophage infiltration is a hallmark of atherosclerosis, we examined the aortic root and found that myeloid GPSM1 overexpression significantly increased CD68+ macrophage content (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the elevated macrophage numbers in atherosclerotic plaques, the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and endothelial-derived adhesion molecules was significantly upregulated in the aortas of GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice compared to GPSM1ki/ki mice and Lyz2-Cre;Apoe−/− mice (Fig. 3G). Also, no significant differences were observed in body weight, serum cholesterol levels, or other indicators of lipid metabolism between the groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Furthermore, WD-fed female GPSM1 overexpression mice exhibited phenotypic changes similar to those of male mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Collectively, these data demonstrate that macrophage-specific GPSM1 overexpression renders mice more susceptible to atherosclerosis.

Myeloid GPSM1 Deficiency Inhibits Monocyte Activation and Monocyte-Derived Macrophage Recruitment, Chemotaxis, and Adhesion.

It is known that sustained hypercholesterolemia induces the expression of endothelial adhesion molecules (VCAM-1 and ICAM-1), which promote monocyte activation and dysfunction. For instance, circulating monocytes convert into a hyperchemotactic phenotype and subsequently differentiate into proinflammatory, proatherogenic macrophages that home to and accumulate in aortic plaques (18, 19). Our findings indicated that GPSM1 deficiency attenuated monocyte activation, leading to a less inflammatory phenotype, as evidenced by reduced mRNA levels of Tnf-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and Ccl2 in blood monocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In addition, Western blot analysis revealed that CCL2 stimulation, but not oxLDL treatment, significantly upregulated GPSM1 protein levels in monocytes (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B), suggesting that GPSM1 plays an important role in CCL2-induced monocyte activation and macrophage chemotaxis.

To further investigate monocyte and macrophage chemotaxis activity in vivo, we conducted a monocyte transfer experiment. Monocytes isolated from the peripheral blood of GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− or GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice were fluorescently labeled with DiI and intravenously injected into Apoe−/− mice with established atherosclerotic plaques (WD for 6 wk). Sixty hours postinjection, we harvested the hearts and aortas to analyze labeled macrophages either adherent to or within the atherosclerotic plaques (Fig. 4B). Aortic root sections analysis revealed significantly reduced recruitment of GPSM1LKOApoe−/−-derived monocytes to atherosclerotic plaques compared to controls (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that GPSM1 deficiency suppressed fluorescent monocyte chemotaxis to the aortas (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Additionally, we validated these findings using monocytes from GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− and GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice. Recipient Apoe−/− mice receiving GPSM1TGApoe−/−-derived monocytes exhibited enhanced monocyte accumulation in both aortic roots and aortas (Fig. 4 E and F). Furthermore, another model that matrigel plugs loaded with the monocyte chemokine CCL2 were implanted into the flanks of mice to assess the chemotaxis ability of monocyte-derived macrophages (20). After 72 h, the plugs were removed and enzymatically dissolved. Myeloid GPSM1 loss resulted in a 76.9% decrease in macrophage recruitment to the plugs (Fig. 4G), confirming that GPSM1 was crucial for responsiveness of blood monocytes to chemokines. Importantly, myeloid GPSM1 deficiency did not alter blood monocyte, lymphocyte, or neutrophil counts nor monocyte subset distribution under a WD, ruling out the effects of GPSM1 deletion on hematopoiesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D–F).

Next, we aimed to validate the inducible effect of GPSM1 on macrophage chemotaxis in vitro. CCL2 is well recognized as the key chemokine regulating the migration and infiltration of macrophages and proinflammatory responses. Therefore, we isolated blood monocytes from GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice, and these monocytes exhibited suppressed chemotaxis activity in response to CCL2 compared to those from GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice (Fig. 4H). Then we generated GPSM1 knockout human THP-1 cells as described previously (17) and established an in vitro macrophage migration assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). As oxLDL is a strong stimulator of inflammation and atherosclerosis(21), we pretreated THP-1 cells with oxLDL for 24 h to better mimic pathophysiological conditions during atherogenesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). We found that THP-1 cells lacking GPSM1 displayed significantly reduced chemotactic activity in response to CCL2, which was consistent with the in vivo recruitment assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). Consistently, an in vitro scratch assay demonstrated that GPSM1-depleted macrophages had poorer migration ability compared to wild-type macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D).

As the transmigration of monocytes to the subendothelial space involves not only chemotaxis but also monocyte-endothelial adhesion(22), we investigated the role of GPSM1 in monocyte adhesion using human blood monocytes and human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) for adhesion assays. Monocytes were transfected with lentiviral short hairpin RNA specific for GPSM1 (sh-GPSM1) or a negative control shRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). The result showed that GPSM1 deficiency significantly decreased the number of monocytes adhered to HAECs, indicating that GPSM1 regulates both chemotactic and adhesive functions (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, GPSM1 abrogation reduced the expression of adhesion molecule VCAM - 1 on macrophages, which may contribute to the decreased macrophage adhesion ability (Fig. 4J). In summary, these data suggest that GPSM1 deficiency inhibits monocyte-endothelial cell adhesion. Collectively, the results suggest that myeloid GPSM1 loss protects against atherosclerosis, which might be due to decreased monocyte-endothelial cell adhesion and monocyte-derived macrophage recruitment into atherosclerotic plaques.

As myeloid-restricted gene deletion has been reported to affect neutrophils, we evaluated the role of GPSM1 in neutrophils (23, 24). We observed that GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice and GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice, when fed a WD, exhibited comparable levels of neutrophil-related inflammatory markers (25, 26), such as S100a8, IL-6, Mmp9, Catalase, and Cxcl1, in neutrophils sorted from bone marrow (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). In addition, in vitro neutrophil function assays revealed similar migration abilities between GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice and GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and C). Similarly, GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice and GPSM1ki/kiApoe−/− mice showed comparable neutrophil inflammatory profiles and migration activities, indicating no significant phenotypic differences in neutrophils (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D and E). Taken together, GPSM1 deficiency did not affect neutrophil functions, indicating that monocytes and macrophages, rather than neutrophils, mediate the atherosclerotic protective phenotype observed in GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice.

GPSM1 Governs Monocyte and Macrophage Chemotaxis Via the MAPK Pathway In Vitro.

To understand how GPSM1 affects macrophage chemotaxis, we performed RNA-Seq transcriptomic analysis on GPSM1fl/fl versus GPSM1LKO BMDMs upon CCL2 stimulation. The expression of 298 genes was significantly downregulated, while 291 genes were upregulated in GPSM1LKO BMDMs compared to GPSM1fl/fl BMDMs (FDR < 0.05) (Figs. 5A and 6A). Notably, the analysis of enriched KEGG pathways showed that GPSM1 loss significantly downregulated the MAPK signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 5 B and C). Since the MAPK pathway is a well-known signaling cascade downstream of CCL2 and has been shown to activate macrophage recruitment (27, 28), we explored whether GPSM1 modulated the chemotaxis through the MAPK pathway. By culturing BMDMs, we found that phosphorylation of p38 and ERK increased in a time-dependent manner under CCL2 stimulation as expected. However, GPSM1 loss in macrophages inhibited CCL2-mediated activation of p38 and ERK (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Consistent with this, the activation of p38 and ERK was obviously promoted in GPSM1-overexpressed BMDMs (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Similarly, the inhibitory effect of GPSM1 deficiency on the phosphorylation of p38 and ERK upon CCL2 stimulation was also observed in HMDMs and THP-1 cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D).

Previous studies have established distinct roles for MAPK signaling cascades in macrophage function (9, 22, 29–32): p38 MAPK primarily regulates chemotaxis by mediating cytoskeletal reorganization and directional migration in response to chemokines like CCL2 (33–35), while ERK MAPK controls adhesion through modulation of adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 (36). To further explore these mechanisms, we isolated monocytes from GPSM1TGApoe−/− mice and treated them with the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580, which completely abrogated the enhanced chemotactic response induced by GPSM1 overexpression (Fig. 5F). Consistently, the same trend of the inhibitory effect of GPSM1 deletion on chemotaxis was observed in THP-1 cell lines, confirming that GPSM1 regulates macrophage chemotaxis via the p38 MAPK pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Additionally, GPSM1 deficiency reduced CCL2-induced ERK phosphorylation and VCAM-1 expression in macrophages (Figs. 4J and 5D). Treating GPSM1-overexpressing macrophages with the ERK inhibitor U0126 abolished GPSM1-mediated upregulation of VCAM-1, demonstrating that GPSM1 modulates macrophage adhesion through the ERK-VCAM-1 signaling axis (Fig. 5G). Together, these results establish GPSM1 as a critical regulator of macrophage chemotaxis and adhesion through distinct MAPK-dependent mechanisms.

Moreover, the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as Tnf-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 was significantly enhanced under CCL2 stimulation, whereas GPSM1 abrogation abolished this effect, indicating that GPSM1 deletion attenuated inflammation induced by CCL2 in macrophages (Fig. 5H). In conclusion, our cell experiments demonstrate that GPSM1 enhances the ability of macrophage chemotaxis and adhesion and promotes cellular inflammation by upregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway.

PMP22 Contributes to the GPSM1-Mediated Promotion of MAPK Signaling and Macrophage Chemotaxis.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying GPSM1-mediated MAPK signaling in promoting macrophage chemotaxis, we analyzed DEGs from the previously mentioned RNA-Seq data. Among them, we identified a significant downregulation of peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22) in BMDMs of GPSM1LKO mice (Fig. 6A). The reduced expression of PMP22 in the macrophages was validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6B). Consistently, compared with the control mice, PMP22 expression was markedly decreased in the aortas of GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice and GPSM1LKO mice fed a WD in vivo (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Further isolation of monocytes from GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice revealed a significant reduction in PMP22 expression (Fig. 6D). Immunofluorescence staining of atherosclerotic lesions demonstrated lower PMP22 expression in CD68+ macrophages from the aortic roots of GPSM1LKOApoe−/− mice compared to GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− controls (Fig. 6E). These results confirm that GPSM1 deficiency downregulates PMP22 expression in monocytes and macrophages.

PMP22, a glycoprotein strongly associated with peripheral nerve disorders, has been reported to decrease CCL2 and Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 1 (CXCL1) gene expression in Schwann cells, and its absence impairs the migration and adhesion abilities of Schwann cells and fibroblasts (37–40). Additionally, transgenic mice overexpressing PMP22 exhibit activation of the ERK signaling cascade in peripheral nerves (41). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that PMP22 might serve as a downstream target of GPSM1 in regulating macrophage chemotaxis. To test this hypothesis, monocytes isolated from GPSM1LKOApoe−/− and GPSM1fl/flApoe−/− mice were overexpressed with PMP22 via adenovirus. The results showed that PMP22 overexpression restored the impaired chemotaxis toward CCL2 caused by GPSM1 deficiency (Fig. 6F). A similar trend was observed in GPSM1-KO THP-1 cells overexpressing PMP22 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 B and C). Consistent with this, the scratch assay revealed that the reduced migration rate of GPSM1-deficient macrophages was significantly reversed by PMP22 overexpression (Fig. 6 G and H). Regarding downstream signaling, adenovirus-mediated PMP22 overexpression partially rescued the inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway in both GPSM1-KO BMDMs and THP-1 cells, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of p38 and ERK (Fig. 6I and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F). Furthermore, lentiviral shRNA targeting PMP22 was used to knock down its expression in BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G). As expected, PMP22 knockdown abolished the upregulation of p38/ERK MAPK signaling induced by GPSM1 overexpression (Fig. 6J and SI Appendix, Fig. S10H). Together, our data suggest that the effects of GPSM1 on MAPK signaling-mediated macrophage chemotaxis may be, at least in part, attributed to the induction of PMP22.

Next, we explored the role of PMP22 in GPSM1-mediated atherogenesis in vivo. To investigate this, we suppressed PMP22 expression in macrophages of GPSM1-overexpressing mice using siRNA-loaded liposomes. As previously described, these liposomes are efficiently taken up by macrophages (42, 43). To verify the silencing efficacy of the liposomes in vivo, we isolated monocytes from treated mice and confirmed that PMP22 expression was significantly reduced in the si-PMP22-loaded liposome group compared to the control siRNA (si-CON) group (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, 8-wk-old male GPSM1TG and GPSM1ki/ki mice were injected with AAV-PCSK9 and fed a WD for 16 wk. During this period, mice received weekly intravenous injections of either si-PMP22 or si-CON liposomes (Fig. 7B). Consistent with our previously reported data, the plaque area in the en face aorta and aortic root of GPSM1TG mice treated with si-CON was significantly larger than that in GPSM1ki/ki mice treated with si-CON (Fig. 7 C and D). Notably, si-PMP22 treatment in GPSM1TG mice reduced the plaque burden by 28.2% in the en face aorta and by 37.6% in the aortic root compared to si-CON treatment, demonstrating that PMP22 knockdown attenuates GPSM1-driven atherosclerotic plaque formation (Fig. 7 C–E). Moreover, si-PMP22 administration decreased the necrotic area and increased collagen content in plaques of GPSM1TG mice (Fig. 7 F and G). Additionally, CD68+ macrophage infiltration in plaques was significantly reduced by si-PMP22 treatment (Fig. 7 H and I).

GPSM1 Regulates PMP22 Expression Through the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 Axis.

To further investigate the molecular link by which GPSM1 loss downregulates PMP22 expression in macrophages, we employed JASPAR to predict potential transcription factors regulating PMP22 expression. Among the candidate factors, we focused on Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4). Given our previous finding that GPSM1 modulates cAMP/PKA signaling in LPS-stimulated macrophages (17), coupled with reports of cAMP/PKA regulating KLF4 in granulosa and dendritic cells (44, 45), we hypothesized that GPSM1 might similarly regulate KLF4 through cAMP/PKA signaling in CCL2-stimulated macrophages. As expected, GPSM1 deficiency in BMDMs significantly increased intracellular cAMP levels and PKA phosphorylation while downregulating KLF4 protein expression compared to controls (Fig. 8 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Notably, these effects were abolished by treatment with either the PKA inhibitor H-89 or the cAMP antagonist Rp-cAMPS (Fig. 8 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B), suggesting the involvement of the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 axis in GPSM1-mediated regulation of macrophage chemotaxis. Conversely, GPSM1 overexpression upregulated KLF4 protein abundance (Fig. 8D and SI Appendix, Fig. S12C). Next, using the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, we confirmed the direct binding of KLF4 to the PMP22 promoter in BMDMs (Fig. 8E). This transcriptional regulation was further supported by luciferase assays demonstrating that KLF4 promotes PMP22 transcription, supporting the notion that PMP22 is a transcriptional target of KLF4 (Fig. 8F). Importantly, treatment with the KLF4 inducer APTO-253 significantly increased PMP22 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S12D), providing additional evidence that GPSM1 regulates PMP22 through a cAMP/PKA/KLF4-dependent mechanism. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that GPSM1 modulates PMP22 expression via the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 signaling pathway (Fig. 8G).

Inhibiting GPSM1 Suppresses Atherosclerosis formation.

As we previously identified the small-molecule compound AN-465/42243987 as capable of counteracting the proinflammatory function of GPSM1 in macrophages (17), here, we validated whether the GPSM1 inhibitor could treat atherosclerosis formation. Apoe−/− mice that had been fed a WD for 6 wk were injected with AN-465/42243987 or vehicle control twice weekly for 6 wk to determine the effect of AN-465/42243987 on atherosclerotic lesion development (Fig. 9A). We found that AN-465/42243987 treatment significantly reduced atherosclerotic plaque area in both en face of the aorta and the aortic root compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 9 B and C). The percentage of the necrotic core of the aortic root was significantly lower in the AN-465/42243987 group than in the vehicle group (Fig. 9D). Consistent with improved plaque stability, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed increased collagen content in lesions from the treatment group (Fig. 9E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of CD68 in the aortic root lesion demonstrated that the AN-465/42243987 group showed less infiltration of CD68+ macrophages in plaque compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 9F). In addition to the observations described above, AN-465/42243987 treatment did not cause any obvious side effects on Apoe−/− mice and there was no significant difference in either the body weight or blood lipid profile between the two groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and B). Overall, these data indicate that the small-molecule compound AN-465/42243987 could attenuate atherosclerosis progression by reducing macrophage infiltration and enhancing plaque stability, suggesting its potential therapeutic effect on atherosclerosis.

Discussion

Here, our study investigates the role of GPSM1, a guanine nucleotide dissociation inhibitor, in atherosclerosis and demonstrates its significant impact on monocyte activation and macrophage chemotaxis. Through loss- and gain-of-function murine models and in vitro assays, we show that myeloid-specific GPSM1 deficiency reduces atherosclerotic plaque formation, yielding more stable plaques with decreased necrotic cores, increased collagen, and reduced macrophage infiltration, while GPSM1 overexpression exacerbates disease progression with heightened inflammation. Mechanistically, GPSM1 deficiency disrupts monocyte chemotaxis by impairing the MAPK and PMP22 pathways, with PMP22 blockade via siRNA mitigating GPSM1’s proatherogenic effects. Our study further identifies a cAMP/PKA/KLF4/PMP22 axis through which GPSM1 drives macrophage migration and activation using both murine and human cells. These findings highlight GPSM1 as a key regulator of atherosclerosis and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target, with small-molecule inhibitors of GPSM1 blockade representing potential strategies for treatment (Fig. 8G).

The chemokine CCL2 is a well-established mediator in atherosclerosis due to its potent chemotactic effects on monocytes/macrophages, and blockade of the CCL2–CCR2 axis significantly reduces atherosclerotic plaque formation by inhibiting monocyte recruitment (46, 47). Our work extends these findings by showing that CCL2 stimulation (rather than oxLDL) specifically upregulates GPSM1 expression in monocytes, thereby providing a molecular link between CCL2 and GPSM1 in atherogenesis. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that GPSM1 deficiency impairs monocyte and macrophage migratory responses and dampens proinflammatory activation, thereby disrupting the CCL2-initiated signaling cascade that sustains monocyte recruitment and activation within atherosclerotic plaques.

MAPK signaling pathways, particularly p38 and ERK, mediate CCL2-induced responses in monocytes and macrophages, which are essential for chemotaxis toward inflammatory sites and vascular adhesion. In this study, we elucidate the dual regulatory role of GPSM1 in macrophage chemotaxis and adhesion through distinct MAPK signaling pathways—p38 MAPK for chemotaxis and ERK MAPK for VCAM-1-mediated adhesion. Our findings reveal GPSM1 as an upstream regulator that orchestrates these processes. The result that GPSM1 regulates chemotaxis through p38 MAPK activation aligns with previous work by Ashida et al., who identified p38 MAPK and Rho as crucial mediators of CCL2-induced chemotaxis, with Rho kinase acting upstream of p38 MAPK in this signaling cascade (22). Our pharmacological inhibition experiments using the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 further confirm the essential role of p38 in GPSM1-mediated chemotaxis. Additionally, our observation that GPSM1 modulates adhesion through ERK-VCAM-1 signaling agrees with established ERK’s involvement in VCAM-1 regulation (36).

Furthermore, our data demonstrate GPSM1 as a key upstream regulator of p38/ERK MAPK signaling in macrophage chemotaxis and adhesion, with PMP22 serving as the downstream effector of GPSM1. PMP22, a glycoprotein predominantly expressed in Schwann cells, is essential for peripheral nerve myelination, cell adhesion, and cytoskeletal organization (37, 38, 48). As a key structural component of compact myelin, PMP22 interacts with P0 protein and integrin β4 to maintain myelin stability and mediate Schwann cell-extracellular matrix interactions (49). Additionally, PMP22 regulates cholesterol metabolism, proteasome activity, and cell migration; its deficiency impairs F-actin organization and reduces cell motility (37, 50, 51). Notably, PMP22 modulates chemokine signaling by increasing CCL2 and CXCL1 expression in Schwann cells while activating ERK signaling cascades in peripheral nerves (40, 41). Building on PMP22’s roles in migration and chemokine regulation, we identify it as a downstream effector of GPSM1 in macrophages. Overexpression of PMP22 rescues impaired p38/ERK phosphorylation in GPSM1-deficient macrophages, consistent with reports that PMP22 loss decreases migration and adhesion in Schwann cells and fibroblasts while PMP22 overexpression activates ERK signaling in peripheral nerves (37, 41). Our results show that PMP22 is a mediator of GPSM1-driven macrophage recruitment and atherogenesis. In vivo, macrophage-targeted PMP22 silencing attenuates plaque formation and improves plaque stability in GPSM1-overexpressing mice, directly validating the GPSM1–PMP22 axis as a crucial pathway in atherosclerosis. However, the mechanisms of PMP22-mediated MAPK regulation require further investigation. Nevertheless, our study identifies the GPSM1–PMP22 axis as a promising therapeutic target for macrophage-driven atherosclerosis.

Moreover, this study reveals that the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 axis mediates GPSM1-dependent regulation of PMP22. Based on our finding that GPSM1 modulates cAMP/PKA signaling as a Gαi3 accessory protein (17), we demonstrate that CCL2 stimulation activates this pathway in macrophages in a manner similar to LPS response. Specifically, GPSM1 deletion elevates intracellular cAMP levels and enhances PKA phosphorylation upon CCL2 stimulation. Using pharmacological inhibition of PKA, ChIP, and luciferase assays further show that PKA suppresses KLF4 expression, while KLF4 directly promotes PMP22 transcription in macrophages. Together, these findings establish that the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 axis is the central pathway through which GPSM1 governs PMP22 expression to modulate monocyte/macrophage chemotaxis.

It should be noted that the Lyz2-Cre mice used in this study were heterozygous (52, 53). This approach was adopted to minimize the well-documented risks associated with homozygous Cre expression, including increased cellular toxicity, off-target recombination, and potential confounding phenotypic alterations independent of the target gene deletion (52–55). The inclusion of Cre-only control animals in our experiments was designed to account for any possible effects arising from the heterozygous Cre expression itself (56).

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, given that macrophage lipid metabolism dysfunction (particularly foam cell formation) plays a central role in atherogenesis, the precise effects of GPSM1 on lipid uptake, synthesis, and cholesterol efflux warrant further investigation. Second, although the Lyz2-Cre system enabled myeloid-specific manipulation of GPSM1 expression, it is important to note that Lyz2 is also expressed in microglia and Kupffer cells beyond circulating monocytes and plaque macrophages. This expression pattern suggests that GPSM1 in these cell types may exert distinct biological effects and could potentially contribute to systemic metabolic regulation. The specific mechanisms by which GPSM1 in these different cell populations influences atherosclerosis progression remain to be fully elucidated. Third, although GPSM1-overexpressing mice exhibited increased susceptibility to atherosclerosis, the overexpression of GPSM1 (~25-fold) in BMDMs indeed represented a supraphysiological dosage. A primary concern is that the nonphysiological expression pattern fails to recapitulate the precise regulation of the endogenous gene, thus triggering compensatory mechanisms that can mask the true phenotype. We hypothesize that the supraphysiological expression of GPSM1 in the monocyte-macrophage cell lineage is attributable to the CAG promoter used in our overexpression construct; this strong constitutive CAG promoter is designed to overcome transcriptional silencing and drive high-level expression, particularly in less differentiated cells such as monocytes and myeloid progenitors (57–59). Therefore, inducible systems capable of achieving physiological-level expression are needed to more accurately define the pathophysiological role of GPSM1.

In summary, we identify GPSM1 as a critical regulator of atherosclerosis progression through its control of monocyte activation, macrophage recruitment, and adhesion. We delineate a signaling axis in which GPSM1 orchestrates these processes via the cAMP/PKA/KLF4 axis to regulate PMP22 and the downstream MAPK pathway. Together, these findings establish the GPSM1–PMP22 axis as a critical driver of atherosclerotic plaque development and highlight its therapeutic potential for atherosclerosis management.

Materials and Methods

We generated myeloid-specific GPSM1 knockout (GPSM1LKO) and overexpression (GPSM1TG) mice on Apoe−/− backgrounds through Cre-loxP breeding strategies. Atherosclerosis was induced by WD feeding. Histological analyses (Oil Red O, EVG, H&E, Masson’s staining, and immunofluorescence) were used to quantify plaque burden and macrophage recruitment. In vitro, CCL2-stimulated primary cells were used to explore GPSM1-mediated signaling pathways.

The study protocol involving human participants was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. The collection of human aortic tissues was approved under authorization no. 2024-KY-153(K), and the use of human umbilical cord blood was approved under authorization no. 2024-214. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.
