Rabi Oscillations Modulated Noise Squeezing in Active Quantum Dot Ensembles
Ori Gabai, Amnon Willinger, Igor Khanonkin, Vitalii Sichkovskyi, Johann Peter Reithmaier, Gadi Eisenstein

Abstract
Generation of squeezed light is usually implemented in nonlinear χ ( 2 ) \chi^{(2)} or χ ( 3 ) \chi^{(3)} materials. Semiconductor lasers and optical amplifiers (SOAs) also offer nonlinearities but they differ from passive elements in that they add amplified spontaneous emission noise (ASE). In a semiconductor laser, squeezing to below the shot noise limit has been demonstrated. An SOA contains no cavity and it adds significant noise. Gain saturation can lead, in principle, to squeezing of the photon number quadrature to below the shot noise level but often the noise is reduced only to below the ASE level of a linear amplifier. At the same time, the noise in the phase quadrature increases according to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Short resonant pulses interacting with a quantum dot SOA induce coherent effects such as Rabi oscillations. Here, we demonstrate, for the first time, that Rabi oscillations cause cyclical noise squeezing which varies periodically with the excitation pulse area. The noise in the present experiments does not reach the quantum limit so we term this condition quasi-squeezing. It occurs during the portions of the Rabi cycle when the quantum dots provide gain and repeats with every fourfold increase of the pulse excitation energy which amounts to a 2 π \pi increase in pulse area. In all other cases, the noise exhibits the properties of a coherent state.

Strong nonlinearities are also available in optically active media, such as lasers and semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs). In the context of light squeezing, active media differ from passive materials in that they add spontaneous emission noise. The nonlinearity in an active device, and its consequent ability to squeeze the noise, stems from saturation which is determined, in turn, by the dynamical properties. In solid state and doped fiber devices, dynamics are slow and saturation is governed by average powers. In contrast, the dynamics of semiconductor devices are fast, and this has a major impact on the noise properties under saturated conditions
bilenca2004noise
;
berg2004saturation
;
bogoni2004modeling
.

Squeezing in a DC driven semiconductor laser to below the quantum limit was demonstrated by Y. Yamamoto’s group several decades ago
yamamoto1992photon
;
machida1987observation
. Similar squeezing levels were achieved in A. Yariv’s group by applying a weak dispersive feedback to the laser
kitching1995room
. A model for squeezing in semiconductor lasers was reported in
vey1997semiclassical
.
The noise of a saturated SOA was shown both theoretically and experimentally to exhibit a spectral hole in the vicinity of the saturating signal
shtaif1996noise
;
bilenca2004noise
;
capua2008direct
. Clear narrowing of the the statistical distribution of the power spectral density in a saturated SOA was demonstrated experimentally for continuous waves (CW)
shtaif2002experimental
as well as pulse
bilenca2005statistical
excitations, and confirmed by a simulation
bilenca2005fokker
.

Short pulse propagation in an SOA induces several nonlinear coherent interactions
eisenstein2019coherent
. Once the pulses are sufficiently intense and their duration is short in comparison to the semiconductor coherence time (which is roughly 1 ps at room temperature
khanonkin2021room
), they induce coherent interactions such as Rabi oscillations, which were demonstrated experimentally mainly in quantum-dot (QD) SOAs
eisenstein2019coherent
;
capua2014rabi
;
borri2002coherent
;
kolarczik2013quantum
, but also in a quantum cascade laser
choi2010ultrafast
, and were modeled for quantum well SOAs
zhang2004rabi
. This means that during the pulse, the SOA oscillates between gain and absorption. This leaves, in turn, a direct imprint on the temporal profiles of the amplitude and phase of the SOA output pulse. The carrier density in the SOA also oscillates and this affects the amplified spontaneous emission noise accompanying the pulse.

We demonstrate here, for the first time, a direct interplay between Rabi oscillations and noise squeezing in a QD SOA operating at 1550 nm and at room temperature. In the present experiments, gain saturation does not squeeze the noise below the shot noise limit but rather reduces it, in the photon number quadrature, to below its value under linear gain conditions. We term this condition quasi-squeezing. At the same time, the noise in the other quadrature is correspondingly increased.

To induce Rabi oscillations, we excite the QD SOA with 100 fs pulses and characterize the noise of the resulting output pulses using homodyne detection, where a replica of the excitation pulse acts as the local oscillator (LO). Noise squeezing is found to occur only under conditions of gain saturation and it is cyclical with the input pulse excitation energy. Namely, squeezed noise is observed when the excitation pulse energies increase by factors of four which amounts to a 2
\pi
increase in pulse area. For other pulse excitation energies, the QD SOA output exhibits a coherent state. This periodic behavior extends over a very wide range of excitation pulse energies.

Note that the absolute values of the pulse area cannot be determined precisely, since a QD SOA is an inhomogeneously broadened, distributed gain medium. The pulse area
\Theta(z)
is defined as
\Theta(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mu}{\hbar}E(z,t)\,dt,

where
\mu
is the dipole matrix element and
E(z,t)
is the envelope of the electric field at position
z
. According to the McCall–Hahn area theorem
MccallSelf1969
, which was originally derived for resonant two-level systems under the assumptions of negligible dephasing, no inhomogeneous broadening, and no nonlinear gain dynamics, the pulse area in an amplifying medium evolves as
\frac{d\Theta}{dz}=\frac{g}{2}\sin\Theta,

where
g
is the gain coefficient. In a QD amplifier, these assumptions are only qualitatively satisfied: the ensemble exhibits inhomogeneous broadening, nonlinearity due to gain saturation, and complex carrier dynamics beyond the two-level approximation. While the exact initial pulse area cannot be determined accurately, changes in pulse area of
2\pi
are nevertheless exact. Quasi-squeezing occurs only when the system is in the gain regime during the Rabi cycle, while in every other case, the amplifier output is a coherent state whose Wigner function size is similar to that of the amplified spontaneous emission noise.

The cyclical nature of the quasi squeezing is supported by a simple phenomenological calculations which analyzes the spontaneous emission of an ideal two level system driven by a short excitation pulse
E(t)
, whose area is defined as
\Theta=\frac{\mu}{\hbar}\int E(t)\,dt
. The pulse induces Rabi oscillations at a frequency
\Omega(t)=\mu E(t)/\hbar
, where
\mu
is the transition dipole moment and
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant. In the rotating frame of the excitation pulse, the instantaneous excited-state population evolves as:

\rho_{ee}(t)=\sin^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\Omega(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}\right).

(1)

Spontaneous emission noise is incorporated by adding Gaussian noise whose variance is proportional to the excited-state population. The output electric field becomes:

\tilde{E}(t)=E(t)+\delta E_{\text{amp}}(t)+i\,\delta E_{\text{phase}}(t).

(2)

Both noise terms,
\delta E_{\text{amp}}(t)
and
\delta E_{\text{phase}}(t)
take on the form of a normal distribution

\delta E_{\text{amp}},\ \delta E_{\text{phase}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,~\sqrt{\gamma_{\text{sp}}\cdot\rho_{ee}(t)}\right),

(3)

where
\gamma_{\text{sp}}
is the spontaneous emission rate and the two noise terms are related to each other via the Henry’s linewidth enhancement parameter
henry2003theory
.

To estimate the resulting quantum state, we compute the average field amplitude and phase from the noisy output:

\alpha=|\langle\tilde{E}\rangle|\,e^{i\arg(\langle\tilde{E}\rangle)}
. This complex amplitude
\alpha
is scaled and used to construct a coherent density matrix
\rho=|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
. The Wigner function is then calculated directly from this density matrix using the standard QuTiP routine:
W(x,p)=W_{\rho}(x,p)
,
where
W_{\rho}
denotes the Wigner function of state
\rho
.

3
Conclussion

In this work, we demonstrated that a resonant ultra-short pulse excitation of a QD SOA induces cyclical quasi-squeezing, driven by coherent Rabi oscillations. Specifically, we observed that the output photon number quadrature noise is periodically reduced below the ASE level of a linear amplifier, while the conjugate phase quadrature noise increases correspondingly. This quasi-squeezing effect repeats with every fourfold increase in the excitation pulse energy, equivalent to a 2
\pi
increment in pulse area.

The underlying mechanism arises from the periodic evolution between gain and absorption in the QD SOA during Rabi oscillations. The noise is squeezed only during portions of the Rabi cycle when the QDs provide gain; otherwise, the output exhibits the noise properties of a coherent state. This behavior is in qualitative agreement with predictions based on the McCall–Hahn area theorem, despite the inhomogeneous broadening and complex carrier dynamics in QD SOAs.

While in the present experiments the noise does not reduce to below the quantum shot noise limit, it represents a clear manipulation of amplifier-added noise via coherent light–matter interactions. This demonstrates a fundamentally new regime of amplifier operation that could enable engineered noise profiles in active photonic devices. Potential applications include tailored noise suppression in optical amplifiers for quantum communication systems, or enhanced sensitivity in measurement schemes where reduced amplitude noise, is crucial.

Future research should explore extending this effect to other material systems, for example perovskite based amplifiers, and investigate whether cavity enhancement or tailored feedback could ensure squeezing below the shot noise limit. Additionally, incorporating pulse shaping techniques to precisely control the temporal dynamics of the Rabi oscillations
karni2016coherent
may open new avenues for deterministic quantum noise engineering on chip-scale photonic platforms.

4
Methods

4.1
Experimental setup

The experimental setup consists of a femtosecond fiber-based laser (Toptica FemtoFiber pro) operating at a center wavelength of 1550 nm, delivering pulses with a duration of approximately 100 fs. The optical path length was controlled using a motorized optical delay line (Physik Instrumente, model V-508, step resolution of 2 nm) to achieve precise temporal scanning. The output beams were combined and directed to a balanced photodetector (Femto, model HBPR-100M-60K-IN-FST) for detection of the interference signal. The resulting photocurrent was recorded using an oscilloscope (Keysight, model EXR404A, with 4 GHz bandwidth) for subsequent analysis. The delay line was moving periodically at rate of 5 Hz and an amplitude of 2
\mu
m and correspondingly produced a position signal that was sampled by the oscilloscope in sync with the signal from the balanced detector.

4.2
QD SOA fabrication

The active region of the gain medium we used comprised six layers of high-density
6\times 10^{10}
cm
-2
InAs QDs grown by molecular beam epitaxy in the Stranski - Krastanow mode. The optical amplifier is fabricated as a 2
~\mu\text{m}
wide, 1.5 mm long ridge waveguide whose end facets were anti-reflection coated. The QDs exhibit a narrow photo-luminescence linewidth, measured at 10 K, of 17 meV for a single layer and 26 meV for the six-layer stack. This linewidth represents a record uniformity compared to all reported QDs in any material system. Details of the device fabrication and characterization are given in
banyoudeh2015high
. The emission power of the measured electro-luminescence spectra increases with applied bias but the spectral shape remains unchanged
khanonkin2017ultra
.

4.3
Wigner function analysis

Histograms of the current at the balanced detector output, measured with a real time oscilloscope, is constructed from one thousand, periodic scans of the delay line with an amplitude of 2
\mu
m. Wigner functions are obtained from those histograms using the inverse Radon transform. This transform is intended for continuous waves (CW) or narrow-spectrum signal. The spectral width of the 100 fs excitation pulses is roughly 3% of the optical frequency and hence, the assumption of narrow band signal is valid.
