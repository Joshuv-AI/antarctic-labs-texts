Non–ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction in a Patient With Dextrocardia
Authors: Brandon Sánchez, Pedro Contreras, Pamela González, Luis Domínguez, Iván Goveo, José López Luciano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDextrocardia with situs inversus totalis is a rare congenital anomaly that complicates diagnosis and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for acute coronary syndromes due to mirror-image anatomy.Case SummaryA 50-year-old man with situs inversus totalis presented with severe angina, syncope, and markedly elevated troponin (non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction). Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed right axis deviation and atypical R-wave progression. Lead reversal confirmed ischemic changes. Coronary angiography revealed complete right coronary artery occlusion and multivessel disease. The right coronary artery was successfully revascularized, achieving TIMI flow grade 3.DiscussionThis case illustrates the rare combination of acute coronary syndrome in a patient with situs inversus. It reinforces that standard myocardial infarction management protocols must be applied with necessary, specific anatomical adaptations in both ECG interpretation and PCI technique.Take-Home MessageAccurate ECG interpretation in dextrocardia requires lead reversal, and successful PCI in situs inversus depends on adaptation of catheter selection and fluoroscopic imaging.Key words: acute coronary syndrome, dextrocardia, lead reversal, NSTEMI, percutaneous coronary intervention, situs inversus totalis

History of Presentation

A 50-year-old man with situs inversus and dextrocardia presented with acute retrosternal stabbing chest pain (9/10) of 2 days’ duration without associated symptoms. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable with sinus bradycardia (56 beats/min). Physical examination revealed a right-sided apical impulse, normal heart sounds without murmurs, clear lung fields, and no signs of heart failure. Based on the clinical presentation and troponin elevation, non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) was diagnosed, and the patient was referred for coronary angiography with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

Past Medical History

The patient had situs inversus totalis and type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with metformin 500 mg daily. He reported a 20 pack-year smoking history and occasional alcohol use and denied illicit drug use. Family history was notable for maternal hypertension and diabetes mellitus in 1 sibling. There was no history of prior surgery, transfusion, or drug allergies.

Differential Diagnosis

The presentation was most consistent with acute coronary syndrome given the presence of severe chest pain, syncope, and marked troponin elevation. In the setting of dextrocardia, diagnostic considerations included accurate ischemic localization and exclusion of alternative causes of chest pain and syncope, such as aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, arrhythmia, and inadvertent limb-lead misplacement.

Investigations

Chest radiography

Posteroanterior chest radiography confirmed dextrocardia with complete mirror-image transposition of thoracic and abdominal viscera, including a right-sided cardiac silhouette, right-sided aortic arch, and right-sided gastric bubble (Figure 1).

This posteroanterior chest x-ray confirms the diagnosis of dextrocardia with situs inversus totalis. A standard chest x-ray was performed on admission. The cardiac silhouette is located on the right side of the chest. Note the right-sided aortic arch (arow) and the gastric bubble (G) located in the right upper quadrant, confirming the complete transposition of thoracic and abdominal viscera (situs inversus totalis). Recognition of this anatomy is crucial for interpreting subsequent diagnostic tests and guiding cardiac catheterization.

Electrocardiogram

12-lead standard electrocardiogram

Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus bradycardia (56 beats/min), rightward axis deviation with an upright QRS complex in aVR, ST-segment depression in V1, inverted T waves in V1-V2, flattened T waves in V3, V5, and V6, and abnormal precordial R-wave progression (Figure 2A).

ECG Findings in Non–ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction With Dextrocardia

(A) Standard electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrates a positive QRS complex in lead aVR, extreme right-axis deviation, and abnormal precordial R-wave progression. (B) ECG recorded with limb-lead reversal and right-sided precordial lead placement demonstrates persistent right-axis deviation, more typical R-wave progression, and ST-segment depression in right-sided precordial leads (V2R and V6R). Appropriate lead reorientation is essential for accurate ischemia assessment in patients with dextrocardia.

Right-sided ECG

After recognition of dextrocardia, a modified ECG acquisition protocol was implemented, consisting of right–left arm limb lead reversal and right-sided precordial lead placement (V1R–V6R) (Figure 2B). This configuration demonstrated persistent right axis deviation and a positive QRS complex in aVR (anatomically expected), improved R-wave progression across right-sided precordial leads, incomplete right bundle branch block, and subtle ST-segment depression in V2R and V6R.

Coronary angiography

Troponin levels were markedly elevated at 14,485.5 pg/mL (reference range: 0-19 pg/mL).

Management (Medical Therapy and Interventions)

The patient was diagnosed with acute coronary syndrome with NSTEMI and admitted for urgent invasive management. Dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated with aspirin 325 mg and a clopidogrel loading dose of 300 mg, and a standard radial artery cocktail (nitroglycerin 100 μg, heparin 5,000 IU, and lidocaine 50 mg) was administered.

Coronary angiography revealed multivessel coronary artery disease with a totally occluded RCA identified as the infarct-related artery. A revascularization strategy targeting the culprit lesion only was therefore selected. Coronary angiography and PCI were performed using standard fluoroscopic projections adapted to mirror-image anatomy. Catheter manipulation relied on conventional torque and engagement principles, applied in the opposite rotational direction as dictated by the inverted coronary orientation, allowing stable ostial cannulation and successful device delivery. Selective engagement of the RCA was achieved using a 6F VISTA AR2 guiding catheter (Cordis), whose enhanced backup support facilitated stable ostial alignment in mirror-image anatomy, with anticoagulation provided by 9,000 IU of intracoronary heparin. The lesion was crossed with a Whisper Extra Support guidewire (Abbott) and predilated using a 2.0 × 12 mm balloon. Three drug-eluting stents (2.25 × 26 mm, 2.5 × 22 mm, and 3.5 × 32 mm) were deployed sequentially from distal to proximal segments, followed by postdilation with a 4.0 × 13 mm noncompliant balloon, restoring normal distal flow.

Despite the presence of additional significant lesions, staged revascularization was planned. This approach was guided by the ischemic burden and acute total occlusion of the culprit RCA, prioritizing prompt reperfusion of the infarct-related artery.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Final angiographic assessment demonstrated TIMI flow grade 3 with myocardial blush grade 3 in the right coronary artery (Figure 3). The patient remained hemodynamically stable with complete resolution of symptoms following the procedure. Postprocedure vital signs were within normal limits, including blood pressure of 140/80 mm Hg, heart rate of 66 beats/min, respiratory rate of 11 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. Given the presence of residual severe coronary artery disease, staged PCI was recommended, with planned placement of drug-eluting stents in the left anterior descending artery and diagonal branch. The patient was advised to maintain strict adherence to prescribed medical therapy, pursue aggressive control of cardiovascular risk factors, and implement lifestyle modifications in accordance with medical guidance.

Discussion

Dextrocardia with situs inversus totalis is a rare congenital anomaly in which the heart and internal organs are arranged as mirror images, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 10,000 births.1,2 Although these patients usually have a structurally normal heart and a prevalence of coronary artery disease similar to that of the general population, their atypical anatomy makes the diagnosis and management of acute myocardial infarction more challenging.3 Performing PCI in this setting poses an additional challenge for the interventional cardiologist, as the coronary arteries are inverted and require adjustments in angiographic interpretation and technique. International guidelines, such as the most recent 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Coronary Syndromes,4 do not include specific recommendations for patients with dextrocardia; therefore, the standard infarction management protocol is applied, with the necessary adaptations in angiography and PCI.

Patients with dextrocardia share the same risk factors of atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction as the general population, but in patients with dextrocardia, diagnosis and treatment might be delayed because of the atypical ECG findings.5 As a result, medical professionals must understand the common findings of dextrocardia and incorporate them into their differential diagnosis. Thoracic pain may be described on the right side of the chest with no radiation to the left arm, although this may not always be the case.6

In mirror-image dextrocardia, the cardiac apex lies in the right hemithorax, with a rightward base-to-apex axis that shifts the net depolarization vector anteriorly and rightward. On standard 12-lead ECG, this results in marked right axis deviation, an upright QRS in aVR, complete inversion of all waveforms in lead I, and absent normal R-wave progression with persistent S-wave dominance across the precordial leads. Limb lead reversal (exchange of left and right arm electrodes) restores a physiologically coherent frontal plane, allowing differentiation of true mirror-image anatomy from lead misplacement; in this configuration, persistent right axis deviation and a positive QRS in aVR are anatomically expected.7 Right-sided precordial lead placement (V1R-V6R) further aligns ECG with cardiac anatomy, demonstrating appropriate R-wave progression consistent with a rightward-positioned left ventricle. In our patient, when ECG limb leads were reversed and precordial leads were placed on the right side of the chest, right-sided deviation was still apparent, R-wave progression became more typical, and some ST-segment depression was seen in precordial leads, a sign of ischemia (Figure 2). Subtle ST-segment depression was observed in noncontiguous leads V2R and V6R only—insufficient for specific coronary territory classification (requires ≥2 contiguous leads), but anatomically localizing to the anteroseptal transition (V2R) and lateral wall (V6R) of the rightward-positioned left ventricle. The persistence after optimized lead placement, improved R-wave progression across V1R-V6R, and clinical/troponin correlation confirm true myocardial ischemia rather than artifact, supporting NSTEMI diagnosis in dextrocardia.

Some authors advocate a double-inversion fluoroscopic technique to approximate standard levocardia views during PCI in dextrocardia. However, this case demonstrates that successful revascularization can be achieved using standard fluoroscopic imaging with appropriate adaptation to mirror-image anatomy.8,9

Dextrocardia with situs inversus remains a rare but important clinical entity that requires heightened diagnostic awareness and technical expertise. Recognition of atypical ECG patterns and implementation of appropriate lead corrections are fundamental skills that enable clinicians to deliver optimal care. This case underscores the importance of familiarity with anatomical variants and their impact on both diagnosis and intervention, ensuring that patients with dextrocardia receive the same quality of care as patients with normal cardiac anatomy.

Conclusions

Diagnosing NSTEMI in a patient with dextrocardia and situs inversus totalis requires a high index of suspicion. Successful revascularization with PCI necessitates modification of the interventional technique, such as specialized catheter selection to overcome the challenges posed by mirror-image coronary anatomy.
