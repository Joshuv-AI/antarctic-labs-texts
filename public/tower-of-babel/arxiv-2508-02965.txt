Novel measures and estimators of income inequality
Roberto Vila 1 and Helton Saulo 1,2 1 Department of Statistics, University of Brasilia, Brasilia, Brazil 2 Department of Economics, Federal University of Pelotas, Pelotas, Brazil

Abstract
In this paper, we propose new income inequality measures that approximate the Gini coefficient and analyze the asymptotic properties of their estimators, including strong consistency and limiting distribution. Generalizations to the measures and estimators are developed. Simulation studies assess finite-sample performance, and an empirical example demonstrates practical relevance.

1
Introduction

Understanding and quantifying inequality in distributions of economic or social variables is a central concern across multiple disciplines, from economics
Yao, 1999
and environment studies
Sun et al., 2010
to health sciences
(
Kharazmi et al., 2023
)
and ecology
(
Damgaard and Weiner, 2000
)
. Among the array of summary metrics, the Gini coefficient
(
Gini, 1936
)
, the normalized average absolute difference between two randomly chosen observations, has become the benchmark due to its clear interpretation and straightforward estimation
(
Deltas, 2003
)
. It is routinely employed by institutions such as the World Bank to track income and wealth disparities worldwide
(
Baydil et al., 2025
)
.

Despite its widespread use, the classical Gini coefficient treats all pairwise gaps equally, which can mask nuanced patterns of dispersion. In this paper, we introduce two novel measures of inequality, denoted
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
, that admit a tunable parameter to control sensitivity to tail‐inequality. The index
G_{p}
, based on a logarithmic kernel as function of
p>1
, and the index
H_{q}
, derived from generalized sums as function of
q>0
, both converge to the classical Gini as
p,q\to\infty
. We propose
U
-statistic–based plug-in estimators for
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
and establish their strong consistency and asymptotic normality under mild moment conditions using standard results
(
Hoeffding, 1948
)
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
defines the new measures. Section
3
presents the corresponding sample estimators. In Section
4
, we prove strong consistency, and Section
5
derives the asymptotic distributions. Section
7
reports Monte Carlo results on finite-sample performance, and Section
8
illustrates an empirical application to GDP per capita data. Finally, in Section
9
, we provide some concluding remarks.

2
Income inequality measures

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two independent copies of a non-negative random variable
X
with a positive mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}(X)>0
. We define the following income inequality measures for
X
:

\displaystyle G_{p}\equiv G_{p}(X)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+p^{X_{2}-X_{1}}\right)+\log\left(1+p^{X_{1}-X_{2}}\right)\right]-2\log(2)}}{\displaystyle 2\log(p)\mu},\quad p>1,

(1)

and

\displaystyle H_{q}\equiv H_{q}(X)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[\left({X_{1}^{q}+X_{2}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}-\left({X_{1}^{-q}+X_{2}^{-q}\over 2}\right)^{-1/q}\right]}}{\displaystyle 2\mu},\quad q>0.

(2)

It is worth noting that as
p
and
q
both increase, the above measures approach the classical definition of the Gini coefficient
(
Gini, 1936
)
, denoted by
G
. That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}G_{p}=\lim_{q\to\infty}H_{q}={\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}-\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\right]\over 2\mu}={\mathbb{E}|X_{1}-X_{2}|\over 2\mu}=G.

Furthermore,
\lim_{p\to 1^{+}}G_{p}=\lim_{q\to 0^{+}}H_{q}=0.

Remark 2.1
.

The following relationships are noteworthy:

\displaystyle\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant\log\left[\left(\dfrac{p^{X_{1}}+p^{X_{2}}}{2}\right)^{1/\log(p)}\right],\left({X_{1}^{q}+X_{2}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}\leqslant\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}

and

\displaystyle-\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant\log\left[\left(\dfrac{p^{-X_{1}}+p^{-X_{2}}}{2}\right)^{1/\log(p)}\right]\leqslant-\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}.

Hence,

\left|\dfrac{\displaystyle{\log\left(1+p^{X_{2}-X_{1}}\right)+\log\left(1+p^{X_{1}-X_{2}}\right)-2\log(2)}}{\displaystyle\log(p)}\right|\\[5.69046pt]
=\left|\log\left[\left(\dfrac{p^{X_{1}}+p^{X_{2}}}{2}\right)^{1/\log(p)}\right]+\log\left[\left(\dfrac{p^{-X_{1}}+p^{-X_{2}}}{2}\right)^{1/\log(p)}\right]\right|\\[5.69046pt]
\leqslant\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}-\min\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}

and

\left|\left({X_{1}^{q}+X_{2}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}-\left({X_{1}^{-q}+X_{2}^{-q}\over 2}\right)^{-1/q}\right|\leqslant\left({X_{1}^{q}+X_{2}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}+\left({X_{1}^{-q}+X_{2}^{-q}\over 2}\right)^{-1/q}\leqslant 2\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}.

3
Income inequality estimators

The income inequality estimators of indices
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
, defined in Section
2
, are defined as follows (for
p>1
and
q>0
):

\displaystyle\widehat{G_{p}}={1\over n-1}\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\left[{\log\left(1+p^{X_{j}-X_{i}}\right)+\log\left(1+p^{X_{i}-X_{j}}\right)-2\log(2)}\right]}{\displaystyle\log(p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}

(3)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{H_{q}}={1\over n-1}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\left[\left({X_{i}^{q}+X_{j}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}-\left({X_{i}^{-q}+X_{j}^{-q}\over 2}\right)^{-1/q}\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},

(4)

respectively,
where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are iid observations from the population
X
.

Remark 3.1
.

Setting
p\to\infty
and
q\to\infty
in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively, we get the estimator of the Gini coefficient, denoted by
\widehat{G}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\widehat{G_{p}}=\lim_{q\to\infty}\widehat{H_{q}}

\displaystyle={1\over n-1}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\left[\max\{X_{i},X_{j}\}-\min\{X_{i},X_{j}\}\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}

\displaystyle={1\over n-1}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}=\widehat{G},

which initially was proposed by
Deltas, 2003
.

), based on a gamma sample. From this figure, we observe that the

4
Strong consistency

Note that
\widehat{G_{p}}
in (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{G_{p}}=\dfrac{U_{n}}{2\overline{X}},

where
\overline{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
is the sample mean,

\displaystyle U_{n}\equiv\displaystyle{\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}^{-1}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}g(X_{i},X_{j})

(5)

is a
U
-statistic
(
Hoeffding, 1948
)
and

\displaystyle g(X_{i},X_{j})\equiv{{\log\left(1+p^{X_{j}-X_{i}}\right)+\log\left(1+p^{X_{i}-X_{j}}\right)-2\log(2)\over\log(p)}}.

(6)

If
\mathbb{E}\left|g(X_{1},X_{2})\right|<\infty
, then, by strong law of large numbers for
U
-Statistics
(
Lee, 1990
;
Henze, 2024
)
,

\displaystyle U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right],\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

with
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
meaning almost sure convergence.
Since
\overline{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu
, it follows from the properties of almost sure convergence that

\displaystyle\widehat{G_{p}}=\dfrac{U_{n}}{2\overline{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right]}}{\displaystyle 2\mu}=G_{p},

where
G_{p}
is as defined in (
1
).

By proceeding with the same steps outlined above to verify the consistency of the estimator
\widehat{G_{p}}
, under the condition
\mathbb{E}\left|h(X_{1},X_{2})\right|<\infty
, we obtain, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\widehat{H_{q}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[h(X_{1},X_{2})\right]}}{\displaystyle 2\mu}=H_{q},

where
H_{q}
is as in (
2
) and

\displaystyle h(X_{1},X_{2})\equiv\left({X_{1}^{q}+X_{2}^{q}\over 2}\right)^{1/q}-\left({X_{1}^{-q}+X_{2}^{-q}\over 2}\right)^{-1/q}.

Remark 4.1
.

From Remark
2.1
we have
\left|g(X_{1},X_{2})\right|\leqslant\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
and
\left|h(X_{1},X_{2})\right|\leqslant 2\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
. Then, a sufficient condition for
\mathbb{E}\left|g(X_{1},X_{2})\right|<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left|h(X_{1},X_{2})\right|<\infty
is that

\mathbb{E}[\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}]<\infty.

But since
\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant X_{1}+X_{2}
, it is sufficient that both random variables,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, have finite expectations.

5
Asymptotic distribution

It is well-known that
(
Hoeffding, 1948
, Theorem 7.3 of)
, if
\mathbb{E}\left[g^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]<\infty
and

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]<\infty

then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\sqrt{n}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}U_{n}\\[5.69046pt]
\overline{X}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right]\\[5.69046pt]
\mu\end{pmatrix}\right\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N_{2}\left(\bf 0\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\[5.69046pt]
0\end{pmatrix},\bf\Sigma\equiv\begin{pmatrix}4\xi^{(1)}_{g}&2\xi^{(1,2)}_{g}\\[5.69046pt]
2\xi^{(1,2)}_{g}&\xi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\right),

where

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution,
U_{n}
is the
U
-statistics defined in (
5
),
g(X_{1},X_{2})
is as in (
6
) and
\bf\Sigma
is the covariance matrix whose elements are defined according to the following quantities:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}&\xi^{(1)}_{g}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X_{2}}^{2}[g(X_{1},X_{2})]\right\}-\mathbb{E}^{2}[g(X_{1},X_{2})],\\[5.69046pt]
&\xi^{(2)}\equiv\text{Var}(X_{1}),\\[5.69046pt]
&\xi^{(1,2)}_{g}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}\left\{X_{1}\mathbb{E}_{X_{2}}[g(X_{1},X_{2})]\right\}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\mathbb{E}[g(X_{1},X_{2})].\end{array}

In the above,
\mathbb{E}_{X_{2}}[g(X_{1},X_{2})]
indicates that the expectation is computed over the distribution of
X_{2}
, treating
X_{1}
as fixed.

For a given function
\vartheta
with continuous first partial derivatives and a specific value of
(\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right],\mu)^{\top}

for which
{\bf A}{\bf\Sigma}{\bf A}^{\top}>0
,
the multivariate delta method provides

\displaystyle{\sqrt{n}\left\{\vartheta\begin{pmatrix}U_{n}\\[5.69046pt]
\overline{X}\end{pmatrix}-\vartheta\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right]\\[5.69046pt]
\mu\end{pmatrix}\right\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(0,{\bf A}{\bf\Sigma}{\bf A}^{\top}\right),

(10)

where
{\bf A}
is a
1\times 2
matrix defined by

\displaystyle{\bf A}\equiv\left({\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial x}\quad{\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial y}\right)\Bigg|_{x=\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right],y=\mu}

and

\displaystyle{\bf A}{\bf\Sigma}{\bf A}^{\top}=\left[4\left({\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial x}\right)^{2}\xi^{(1)}_{g}+4\,{\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial x}\,{\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial y}\,\xi^{(1,2)}_{g}+\left({\partial\vartheta(x,y)\over\partial x}\right)^{2}\xi^{(2)}\right]\Bigg|_{x=\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right],y=\mu}.

By taking
\vartheta(x,y)=x/(2y)
in (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{G_{p}}-G_{p})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left\{\dfrac{U_{n}}{2\overline{X}}-\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right]}{\displaystyle 2\mu}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(0,\left[{1\over y^{2}}\,\xi^{(1)}_{g}-{x\over y^{3}}\,\xi^{(1,2)}_{g}+{x^{2}\over 4y^{4}}\,\xi^{(2)}\right]\bigg|_{x=\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{1},X_{2})\right],y=\mu}\right).

(11)

Similarly, under the conditions
\mathbb{E}\left[h^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]<\infty
and

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]<\infty
, we obtain that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{H_{q}}-H_{q})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(0,\left[{1\over y^{2}}\,\xi^{(1)}_{h}-{x\over y^{3}}\,\xi^{(1,2)}_{h}+{x^{2}\over 4y^{4}}\,\xi^{(2)}\right]\bigg|_{x=\mathbb{E}\left[h(X_{1},X_{2})\right],y=\mu}\right),

(12)

where
\xi^{(1)}_{h},\xi^{(1,2)}_{h}
and
\xi^{(2)}
are constructed in analogy with Equation (
5
).

Remark 5.1
.

The significance of the convergence results (
11
) and (
12
) lies in its applicability to constructing confidence intervals and performing hypothesis tests in the context of large samples.

Remark 5.2
.

Since
g(X_{1},X_{2})
and
h(X_{1},X_{2})
are non negative random variables, and
g(X_{1},X_{2})\leqslant\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
and
h(X_{1},X_{2})\leqslant 2\max\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
(see Remark
2.1
), we have
\mathbb{E}\left[g^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]\leqslant\max^{2}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant(X_{1}+X_{2})^{2}\leqslant 2(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})
and
\mathbb{E}\left[h^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]\leqslant 4\max^{2}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\leqslant 4(X_{1}+X_{2})^{2}\leqslant 8(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})
. Then, a sufficient condition for
\mathbb{E}\left[g^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[h^{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right]<\infty
is that both random variables,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, have finite second-order moments.
Moreover, assuming that
X_{1}
possess finite second moments, Lyapunov’s inequality ensures that condition
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1}^{2}\right]<\infty
is fulfilled.

6
Generalizations

The income inequality measures and corresponding estimators defined in Sections
2
and
3
can be generalized in the following sense:
Consider
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
as independent and identically distributed (iid) random variables, each following the same distribution as a non-negative random variable
X
with a positive mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}(X)>0
. Given
m\geqslant 2
, we define the following income inequality measures for
X
:

\displaystyle G_{m,p}\equiv G_{m,p}(X)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{m}p^{X_{j}-X_{1}}\right)+\log\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{m}p^{X_{1}-X_{j}}\right)\right]-2\log(m)}}{\displaystyle m\log(p)\mu},\quad p>1,

and

\displaystyle H_{m,q}\equiv H_{m,q}(X)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[\left({1\over m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{q}\right)^{1/q}-\left({1\over m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{-q}\right)^{-1/q}\right]}}{\displaystyle m\mu},\quad q>0.

Note that as
p
and
q
both increase, the above measures approach the
m
th Gini index (
IG_{m}
)
(
Gavilan-Ruiz et al., 2024
;
Vila and Saulo, 2025
)
, that is:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}G_{m,p}=\lim_{q\to\infty}H_{m,q}={\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}-\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}\right]\over m\mu}=IG_{m}.

Furthermore,
\lim_{p\to 1^{+}}G_{m,p}=\lim_{q\to 0^{+}}H_{m,q}=0.

Consequently, when
m=2
,
p\to\infty
and
q\to\infty
the measures
G_{m,p}
and
H_{m,q}
approach with the definition of the Gini coefficient
(
Gini, 1936
)
, denoted by
G
. That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}G_{2,p}=\lim_{q\to\infty}H_{2,q}=IG_{2}={\mathbb{E}|X_{1}-X_{2}|\over 2\mu}=G.

The income inequality estimators of indices
G_{m,p}
and
H_{m,q}
are defined as follows (for
m\leqslant n
,
p>1
and
q>0
):

\widehat{G_{m,p}}={(m-1)!\over(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-m+1)}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n}\left[{\log\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{m}p^{X_{i_{j}}-X_{i_{1}}}\right)+\log\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{m}p^{X_{i_{1}}-X_{i_{j}}}\right)-2\log(m)}\right]}{\displaystyle\log(p)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}

(13)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{H_{m,q}}={(m-1)!\over(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-m+1)}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n}\left[\left({1\over m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{i_{j}}^{q}\right)^{1/q}-\left({1\over m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{i_{j}}^{-q}\right)^{-1/q}\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},

(14)

respectively,
where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are iid observations from the population
X
.

Remark 6.1
.

It is clear that when
m=2
the measures
G_{m,p}
and
H_{m,q}
reduce to the measures
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
defined in Section
2
. Furthermore, in this case,
\widehat{G_{m,p}}=\widehat{G_{p}}
and
\widehat{H_{m,q}}=\widehat{H_{q}}
, where
\widehat{G_{p}}
and
\widehat{H_{q}}
are stated in Section
3
.

Remark 6.2
.

Following the approach outlined in Sections
4
and
5
, the strong consistency and asymptotic distribution of the estimators
\widehat{G_{m,p}}
and
\widehat{H_{m,q}}
can be established.

Remark 6.3
.

Setting
p\to\infty
and
q\to\infty
in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively, we get the estimator of the
m
th Gini index, denoted by
\widehat{IG}_{m}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\widehat{G_{m,p}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to\infty}\widehat{H_{m,q}}

\displaystyle={(m-1)!\over(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-m+1)}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leqslant n}\left[\max\{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{m}}\}-\min\{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{m}}\}\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{IG}_{m},

which originally was proposed by
Vila and Saulo, 2025
.

Now, setting
m=2
in the above formula, we obtain the estimator of the Gini coefficient, denoted by
\widehat{G}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\widehat{G_{2,p}}=\lim_{q\to\infty}\widehat{H_{2,q}}=\widehat{IG}_{2}={1\over n-1}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}=\widehat{G}.

7
Simulation study

This section presents a Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the estimators defined in equations (3) and (4), namely
\widehat{G}_{p}
and
\widehat{H}_{q}
. The objective is to assess their mean absolute relative error (MARE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) under different sample sizes and values of the parameter
p=q
. The MARE and RMSE of an estimator
\widehat{E}
for a parameter
E
were computed as follows:

\widehat{\text{MARE}}(\widehat{E})=\frac{1}{N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}\left|\frac{\widehat{E}^{(k)}-E_{\text{true}}}{E_{\text{true}}}\right|,

and

\widehat{\text{RMSE}}(\widehat{E})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}\big(\widehat{E}^{(k)}-E_{\text{true}}\big)^{2}},

where
E_{\text{true}}\in\{G_{p},H_{q}\}
denotes the corresponding true value of the inequality measure, and
N_{\text{sim}}
is the number of Monte Carlo replications.

The data were generated from a gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha=1.5
and scale parameter
\theta=1
, so that the mean is
\mu=\alpha\theta=1.5
. The simulation considered the following sample sizes:
n\in\{30,50,100,200,500\}
, and values of
p=q\in\{1.1,2,5,10,50\}
. For each combination of
(n,p)
, we generated
500
independent samples. The true values
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
, defined in Equations (
1
) and (
2
), were approximated via large-sample Monte Carlo averages with
10^{6}
observations.

The Monte Carlo simulation results are presented in Figures
4
–
7
. Particularly, Figures
4
and
5
display the MARE of the estimators
\widehat{G}_{p}
and
\widehat{H}_{q}
, respectively, as functions of the sample size
n
, for different values of
p
, whereas Figures
6
and
7
show the RMSE behavior for the same configurations. From these figures, we observe that, as expected, the MARE and RMSE tend to decrease as the sample size
n
increases. We also observe that larger values of
p
tend to yield smaller (larger) MARE (RMSE).

.

Application

In this section, we illustrate the proposed income inequality measures using a data set on gross domestic product (GDP) per capita for all countries and territories in the Americas in 2023. The raw data (in international dollars at 2021 prices) were downloaded from Our World in Data
https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/gdp-per-capita-worldbank
and converted into units of USD
\times 10^{3}

Figure
8
displays diagnostic plots based on the gamma distribution. From this figure, we observe that the gamma model can be a good choice for these data. To further evaluate the adequacy of the gamma model, we performed two goodness‐of‐fit tests: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test and the Cramér-von Mises (CvM) test. The respective
p

0.9151
and
0.9797
, indicating no evidence to reject the gamma model. Hence, these results provide strong support for the adequacy of the gamma distribution assumption.

9
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have introduced two flexible inequality measures,
G_{p}
and
H_{q}
, which generalize the classical Gini coefficient. By deriving closed‐form U‐statistic estimators for each index, we established strong consistency and asymptotic normality under mild moment conditions. We carried out a Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate the performance of the proposed estimators
\widehat{G}_{p}
and
\widehat{H}_{q}
, and the results have suggested that both the mean absolute relative error and root mean squared error tend to decrease as the sample size increases, as expected. An empirical illustration using GDP per capita in the Americas demonstrated how practitioners can select
p
or
q
to emphasize regulates the influence of disparities between observations. From a policy perspective, these indices may help to improve inequality analysis, as different weights can be attributed to disparities between observations.
