Geological and physicochemical characterization of Kobe Sorsa graphite deposit, Ethiopia
Authors: Bethelihem Tesfaye Emmiru, Samuel Getnet Tsegaye, Prabodh Ranjan Sahoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Kobe Sorsa graphite deposit is situated within the Adola Belt of southern Ethiopia. It composed of Graphitic phyllites and graphite schist with considerable economic potential. This study aims to characterize the Kobe Sorsa graphite for potential industrial applications through an integrated approach such as geochemical, mineralogical, petrography, sieve and carbon content analysis. Graphitic carbon content ranges from 5.37% to 22.08%, with an average concentration of 14.96%. According Petrographic and X-ray diffraction (XRD) investigation, graphite consists of medium- to coarse-grained, well-ordered flakes with an average size of ~ 300 μm, which reflect a high-quality crystalline structure. The REE and multi-element patterns of the Kobe Sorsa graphite-bearing rocks suggest a metasedimentary protolith formed from felsic upper continental crust, characterized by slight HREE enrichment, negligible Eu anomalies, and negative Nb–Ta–Ti anomalies consistent with passive-margin sedimentary sources. The enrichment of HREE’s reflects partitioning into garnet and staurolite-bearing assemblages during metamorphism, whereas the smooth, unfractionated patterns imply closed-system behaviour for REEs during metamorphic recrystallization. The AKF ternary diagram indicates stable mineral assemblages: muscovite-phengite, chlorite, K-feldspar, and pyrophyllite + quartz, suggesting metamorphic conditions within greenschist facies metamorphism. A binary plot of crystallite size (Lc(002)) versus interplanar spacing (d(002)) shows that the Kobe Sorsa graphite samples fall within the well-crystallized graphite field with Lc(002) values range from 530 Å to 600.2 Å, while the degree of graphitization (DG) values vary between 265.7% and 279.1%, confirming a highly ordered graphitic structure. Introduction

Graphite is a crystalline form of carbon composed of stacked layer of carbon atoms held together by strong covalent bond within layers and weak Van der Waals force between them, resulting in anisotropic properties1. It typically form through process such as precipitation from mantle-derived C-O-H fluids, reduction of carbonates, or metamorphism of biogenic carbonaceous material2–4. Graphite occurs in three principal forms: amorphous, flake, and vein, each with distinct physical and economic features5,6. Its global importance continues to grow, particularly due to its role as a precursor for graphene in modern technological applications2,7.

Graphite possesses a unique combination of non-metallic and metallic characteristics, including high thermal and electrical conductivity, lubricity, refractoriness, and chemical inertness8,9. These features makes it essential in wide range of industries, from traditional uses such as crucible, lubricants, brake linings, and refractories to advanced applications including lithium-ion batteries, reactors, semi-conductors, and graphene-based electronics10–12. Additionally, recent innovations highlight the potential sustainable graphite production from agriculture waste, which can enhance supply chain resilience while reducing environmental impact13.

Globally, graphite is mined in countries like China (the leading producer), India, and Brazil6,14,15. Other significant contributors include Russia, Mozambique and Canada. While countries such as North Korea, Turkey, and Mexico have reported production decline, Madagascar, Norway, and Sri Lanka maintained steady output16. Sri Lanka is well-known for its high-purity graphite vein graphite, whereas Mexico produces large amounts of amorphous and flake graphite6,17. African also hosts considerable graphite reserves, particularly in Angola, Ethiopia, Namibia, South Africa, Tanzania, and Uganda18.

In Ethiopia, graphite occurrences are associated within the Precambrian crystalline basement rocks and are found in various part of the country. Major deposit found in Southern Ethiopia (Adola belt, at localities such as Kobe Sorsa, Bekeka, Kenticha, and Chembi), Eastern Ethiopia (Hararghe), Western Ethiopia (Wollega and Asosa), and Northern Ethiopia (Tigray)19. Despite these resources, Ethiopia relies exclusively on imports to meet its domestic graphite demand, and production remains low20. Most previous studies have been limited to preliminary reconnaissance surveys with limited geochemical or mineralogical characterization, particularly in the Adola region. The single available study of the Kobe Sorsa deposit focuses entirely on carbon content, limited critical factors such as geology, mineralogy, particle size, graphitic carbon content, and geochemical characteristics.

This study aimed to fill a critical gap by conducting an integrated geological and physicochemical characterization of the Kobe Sorsa graphite deposit. The study focuses on assessing carbon content, flake size and shape, related mineral assemblages, and major and trace element geochemistry to determine the ore’s quality, origin, and the suitability for industrial applications, particularly in high-tech fields where graphite demand is growing.

Regional geology

The Neoproterozoic rock of southern Ethiopia form part of the Mozambique Belt and the Arabian–Nubian Shield, which, together, form the East African Orogen21–23. The Adola Belt is a key component of the Neoproterozoic structure in southern Ethiopia representing a significant low-grade volcano-sedimentary belt within the Pan-African orogeny (Fig. 1a)24. It is the northern extension of the Mozambique orogenic belt which forms a portion of the late Proterozoic intracratonic trough initiated on the ancient consolidated basement within Mozambique25. This geological setting is crucial for understanding the formation and distribution of mineral deposits in the region26–29. The belt covers an area of approximately 5000 km2 in southern Ethiopia, extending across the Oromia regional state, particularly within the Guji and Borana zones30,31.

The Adola Belt comprises the Adola granite–gneiss complex (GGC) and two ophiolitic fold-and-thrust belts (OFTB) : the Megado volcano-sedimentary belt in the west and the Kenticha ultramafic belt in the east22,29. These three litho-tectonic units are juxtaposed along north–south trending thrust and shear zones29. The Kenticha OFTB is characterized predominantly by ultramafic rocks, while the Megado Belt features a mix of mafic and ultramafic rocks29,30.The Adola GGC comprises amphibolite facies quartzo-feldspathic gneisses and deformed metagranitoids, whereas the Kenticha OFTB includes greenschist to lower amphibolite facies metasediments and metamorphosed mafic-ultramafic rocks32,33. The metamorphic conditions varied between 600 and 650 °C and 6–7 kbar in the Adola GGC and 520–580 °C and 4–5 kbar in the Kenticha OFTB, indicating a range of pressure and temperature conditions during metamorphism33,34. These conditions are consistent with a collisional tectonic setting, followed by east-directed thrusting and right-lateral strike-slip movements between 610 and 554 Ma30,34,35.

The occurrence of graphite in the Adola Belt is strongly linked to the region’s geological and tectonic evolution. Graphite-bearing lithologies, such as schists and quartzites, are distributed across specific localities within the belt, including Kobe Sorsa, Hidi Eda, Kucho, Melka Gonji, Kenticha, and Kibre Mengist. These deposits formed under specific metamorphic conditions that favored both the generation and preservation of high-quality graphite36. This is consistent with the broader metamorphic and tectonic framework of the East African Orogen, emphasizing the Adola Belt’s potential as a significant source of graphite. The presence of graphite in this context also highlights the belt’s industrial importance, as the mineral’s quality and distribution are inherently tied to the region’s geodynamic history.

Materials and methods

Eleven graphite-bearing rock samples were collected from Kobe Sorsa graphite deposit. Petrographic analyses were conducted on eight thin sections at the Department of Geology, Addis Ababa Science and Technology University, using reflected and transmitted light microscopy. Elemental compositions were determined using WD-XRF (Bruker S8 Tiger), ICP-AES, and ICP-MS, with certified reference materials (ME-MS81, ME-ICP06, OA-GRA05, etc.) ensuring data quality and accuracy of detection limits. XRD analysis (PANalytical X’Pert Pro, Cu Kα radiation) was performed over a 2θ range of 10°–80° to identify mineral phases. FT-IR spectroscopy (Nicolet IS50, 4000–500 cm⁻¹, 16 cm⁻¹ resolution) was used to identify functional groups. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) determined ash, moisture, and graphite carbon contents by calcining approximately 1 g of each sample at 950 ± 5 °C for 1.5 h. For particle size distribution analysis, ten representative graphite-bearing samples were first oven-dried at 105 °C to remove moisture content. The dried samples were then homogenized using the coning and quartering method to obtain approximately 100 g of each for analysis. Each subsample was subjected to dry sieving using a set of stainless-steel mesh sieves with aperture sizes ranging from + 590 μm to < 74 μm, corresponding to mesh sizes − 30 to −200. The sieving process was performed using a mechanical sieve shaker for 10 to 15 min to ensure thorough separation of flake sizes. After sieving, the retained material on each mesh was collected and weighed using a digital electronic balance. The flake size distribution was classified into five categories based on industry standards: extra-large flakes (+ 297 to + 590 μm), large flakes (+ 177 to + 297 μm), medium flakes (+ 149 to + 177 μm), small flakes (+ 74 to + 149 μm), and fine flakes (< 74 μm).

Results

Geology and petrography

The study area is located around 5 km northeast of Adola town within Adola Belt (Fig. 1a) and primarily consists of graphite schist and graphitic phyllite (Fig. 1b). The graphitic schist is dark-gray to pale-gray, displaying a characteristic silky sheen on schistosity surfaces and signs of weathering (Fig. 2a and b). It has extensive and tabular features and continuous layers with clear transitions to graphitic phyllites (Fig. 2c). The field observations reveal that the graphite mineralization which extends over hundreds of meters with a width of approximately one meter (Fig. 1b).

The graphitic phyllites are characterized by multiple graphitic bands, strong foliation, and occasional pyrite coatings, giving the phyllites a shiny surface. These graphitic phyllite units strike North–South and dip 45° to the west, with well-developed foliations that are parallel to the primary layering and consistent with the regional deformation fabric. This exposure is cut by prominent shear zones, which are indicated by yellow arrows (Fig. 2d). These shear zones cross-cut the foliation and reflect a secondary deformation event that further reworked the graphite-rich rocks (Fig. 2d). These rock units are generally dark gray to black and greasy, with the dominant minerals including quartz, feldspar, and graphite.

Graphite occurs as medium to coarse-grained flakes (average long axis ~ 300 μm, short axis ~ 50 μm) aligned along foliation planes (Fig. 3a, b, and c). The quartz grains display a granoblastic texture, with some showing undulose extinction (Fig. 3b). This mineral alignment and texture create the rock’s distinctive foliated structure. Particle size distribution

The particle size distribution of ten graphite ore samples from the Kobe Sorsa deposit was quantitatively assessed using the Krumbein phi (φ) scale. The results show that approximately 70% of the graphite falls within the φ = 2.62 class, corresponding to a midpoint particle size of ~ 163 μm, making it the dominant size fraction in the deposit. The graphite flakes in all samples predominantly range between φ = 1.17 and φ = 3.76, which corresponds to a particle size interval of approximately + 297 to −74 μm (Fig. 4). Table 4 presents the average particle size fractions derived from U.S. standard mesh sieve analysis, converted to the Krumbein phi (φ) scale for consistency and comparability. The distribution highlights a concentration of graphite flakes in the intermediate size classes, with smaller proportions in both the coarse and fine fractions.

The Kobe Sorsa graphite sample displays a distribution of particle sizes across four categories based on sieve analysis and converted into the Krumbein phi (φ) scale (Fig. 4). This distribution highlights the predominance of medium flakes, with notable contributions from both coarser and finer fractions.

Trace element geochemistry

Trace element analysis of Adola graphite ore reveals high large-ion lithophile element content, with Rb, Sr, and Ba ranging from 32 to 118.0, 28.1–164, and 215–2140 ppm, respectively, and average values of 63.48, 108.35, and 835.13 ppm. Rb/Sr varies from 0.12 to 3.54, while Sr/Ba ranges from 0.025 to 0.76. High field strength elements like Hf (3.4–5.7 ppm), Th (5.3–12.55 ppm), Ta (0.2–0.6 ppm), and Nb (7.9–14.2 ppm) show a small range in content, with Th/U varying from 0.13 to 2.38 (Table 6).

The North American Shale Composite (NASC)-normalized rare earth element (REE) patterns of the graphite-bearing metasedimentary rocks (Fig. 6a) shows moderate fractionation, with general enrichment of LREE relative to HREE, and smooth patterns that are slightly inclined towards the HREE side, with a weak negative Eu anomaly (Eu/Eu* < 1), which testifies to minor plagioclase fractionation during metamorphism. The total REE contents vary moderately among samples, indicating compositional heterogeneity of the protolith. In the NASC-normalized multi-element diagrams (Fig. 6b), samples exhibit enrichment of Rb, Ba, and U and depletion in Th, Nb, Ta, K, and Ti.

Discussion

Genesis and protolith of graphite host rock

The genesis and evolution of the host rock are inferred from the geochemical signatures of major, trace, and rare earth elements (REEs). Geochemical data indicate a silicate-rich origin, with SiO₂ (47.2–66.0 wt %), Al₂O₃ (8.20–15.80 wt %) and K2O (0.47% to 5.77% wt%) concentrations consistent with a shale or mudstone protolith44,45. High SiO₂ in samples like BT-01-B (64.6 wt %) suggests the original sediment was deposited in a siliceous, fine-grained marine or lacustrine setting46. The REE and multi-element diagrams of the Kobe Sorsa graphite-bearing rocks, display slight HREE enrichment over LREE in the REE patterns (Fig. 6a) produces a (La/Yb) _N < 1, which is characteristic of a relatively flat to slightly HREE-enriched profile suggestive of a metasedimentary source that has been metamorphosed under medium-grade and experienced some degree of recrystallization. The lack of significant Eu anomalies means that feldspar had only minor fractionation followed by subsequent alteration post-deposition, leaving the protolith’s sedimentary geochemical signature intact.

The multi-element (Fig. 6b) diagrams show strong enrichment in Rb, Ba, and U and strong depletion in Th, Nb, Ta, K, and Ti which are characteristic of upper continental crustal material derived from the erosion of felsic sources and subsequent sedimentary deposition in a continental or passive-margin setting. Elevated Zr and Hf values are consistent with detrital, resistant minerals such a zircon indicative of its derivation from a felsic igneous source. These observations are consistent with models proposed by47 who explained that metapelites sourced from the upper continental crust show HREE enrichment, LREE depletion, and strong negative Nb–Ta anomalies typical of crustal sources. This interpretation is supported by The AKF ternary diagram reveals stable mineral assemblages for K₂O-poor pelitic metamorphic rocks the samples lie in the compositional domains dominated by muscovite-phengite, chlorite, K-feldspar, and pyrophyllite + quartz. The mineral chemistry indicates sedimentary protolith derived from alumina-rich fine sediments, such as shale/mudstone. The geochemical characteristics further suggest deposition in continental or passive marginal setting, where clay-rich sediment accumulated. The coexistence of graphite within these rock along with its close association with phyllosilicate minerals, reinforces the interpretation that the parent rock was carbonaceous pelitic sediment implies that the parent sediments were rich in organic materials, which were present during the sedimentation process48.

The structural and textural attributes of graphite further confirm this metamorphic progression. The calculated crystallite dimensions (Lc(002)) range from 530Å to 600.2Å, while the degree of graphitization (DG) spans from 265.7% to 279.1%, reflecting a highly ordered crystalline framework (Table 2; Supplementary Fig. S3). These measurements imply that the graphite underwent adequate thermal conditions to attain advanced structural organization. This transformation is congruent with the gradual alteration of carbonaceous matter during metamorphism, as described by Okuyama et al. (1980), who recorded an increase in graphitization with ascending metamorphic grade, and by Feret, (1998), who observed the retention of organic material in low-grade metasediments. Consequently, the highly crystalline quality of the graphite from Kobe Sorsa signifies both the organic provenance of the carbon and the regional metamorphic processes that enabled its conversion into well-ordered graphite49–51. The original reducing conditions of the depositional environment, essential for the preservation of organic carbon, are supported by the absence of a Cerium (Ce) anomaly (Fig. 6a). This is a key indicator that the environment was not oxidizing, as Cerium would have been removed from the sediments under such conditions52. Collectively, these features show the graphite-bearing host rocks of the Kobe Sorsa area were likely derived from initial pelitic to semi-pelitic sediments of felsic origin and were subsequently to regional metamorphism that produced graphite from recrystallization of carbonaceous material.

Metamorphic history of Kobe Sorsa graphite occurrence

The Adola Fold and Thrust Belt in southern Ethiopia represents a late-Precambrian volcanic-sedimentary and ophiolite sequence with complex deformation and metamorphism22,29,30. Structural analysis shows two thrusting and three folding sequences, with metamorphism reaching greenschist to medium amphibolite facies22,24,25,30,53. The metamorphic features of the Kobe Sorsa graphite-bearing rocks exhibit a close correspondence with the regional metamorphic framework of the Kenticha terrane within the Adola Belt, part of the Neoproterozoic East African Orogen. Petrographic analysis of the graphite-bearing rocks reveals that the dominant minerals in the studied sample include quartz, muscovite, plagioclase, chlorite, and fine- to medium-grained graphite. The parallel alignment of graphite crystals with rock foliation indicates syn-metamorphic deformation during greenschist facies.

Mineral assemblages observed in the AKF ternary diagram (Fig. 5) suggest that the rocks underwent low-grade metamorphism, consistent with the greenschist facies chlorite zone. Stability fields dominated by muscovite–phengite, chlorite, K-feldspar, and pyrophyllite (+ quartz) reflect metamorphic conditions of moderate pressure and relatively low temperature54. Incidentally, low-greenschist facies rock containing graphite commonly occur within classic chlorite zone of the Scottish Barrovian sequence, supporting a similar metamorphic grade and environment of formation55.The stable coexistence of muscovite and chlorite indicates equilibration under hydrous conditions, while the presence of phengite suggests a slightly elevated pressure regime during metamorphism56,57. These conditions are consistent with burial or regional metamorphism associated with crustal thickening rather than contact metamorphism. Recent studies on metasedimentary sequence show that phengite composition evolve toward higher Fe-Mg with progressive burial conditions, while chemistry of chlorite provides a reliable record of metamorphic temperature in low- to medium-grade-terrains58. These textures and mineralogical relationships are consistent with graphite crystallization during regional metamorphism of an alumina-rich, organic-bearing sedimentary precursor.

Complementary geochemical evidence from the REE patterns reveals moderately fractionated LREE–HREE trends with slight HREE enrichment and subdued Eu anomalies, suggesting equilibrium under high-grade metamorphic conditions and limited post-metamorphic alteration. The enrichment of HREE’s reflects partitioning into garnet- and staurolite-bearing assemblages during metamorphism, whereas the smooth, unfractionated patterns imply closed-system behaviour for REEs during metamorphic recrystallization43,47. These mineralogical and geochemical characteristics indicate that the Kobe Sorsa graphite-bearing rocks evolved from Al-rich pelitic protoliths subjected to greenschist-facies regional metamorphism associated with the Pan-African tectonothermal reworking of the Adola Belt30,36. During this process, graphite likely formed from metamorphic transformation of organic carbon in sedimentary precursors under elevated temperature and pressure conditions typical of the belt’s thermal peak59.

Quality of graphite occurrence for industrial applications

The economic viability of graphite-bearing rocks is primarily influenced by their graphitic carbon content and flake size, which directly determine their market value and industrial applicability60. The Adola graphite occurrences exhibit graphitic carbon concentrations ranging from 5.37 wt% to 22.08 wt%, with an average of 14.96 wt%, classifying them as low- to medium-grade deposits. Typically, graphite with > 20 wt% carbon is considered high-grade and is highly desirable for advanced applications such as lithium-ion batteries and high-performance lubricants9. Medium-grade graphite, with 10–20 wt% carbon, is suitable for a broad range of industrial uses, including refractories and some battery types, while low-grade graphite (< 10 wt%) is less economically attractive for high-value applications61.

Flake size distribution in the Kobe Sorsa deposit is dominated by medium flakes (65.19–76.5%), followed by large flakes (8.4–20.5%). Small (7.1–12.3%) and fine flakes (1.9–7.4%) represent lesser proportions, while extra-large flakes are rare (0.8–1.2%). This distribution significant economic implications: large flakes (≥ + 177 μm) command premium prices due to demand in expandible graphite, graphene and specialty refractory62, while medium flake (149–177 μm) are widely used in refractories, foundry molds, and certain battery applications, offering balance of market demand and processing efficiency. Smaller flake (< 149 μm) are less valuable but still serve important roles in in lubricants, brake linings, and coatings6. The scarcity of extra-large flakes restricts their potential in niche applications, such as high-end crucibles63. Consequently, the predominance of medium flake with notable proportion of large flakes positions the deposit favorable for a diverse range of end uses and provides flexibility in targeting different market sector depending on the price trends. Importantly, the absence of free, liberated graphite flakes across all size ranges implies a need for size reduction and liberation to optimize recovery62–64.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicates that the graphite in the Adola samples is highly crystalline, consisting largely of well-oriented flakes (see Supplementary Fig. S3 online). Minor impurity phases, such as calcite, quartz, mica, and feldspar were also detected. The measured interlayer spacings (3.3540–3.470Å) closely approximate that of ideal hexagonal graphite, supporting the presence of well-ordered graphite structures65. The degree of graphitization, estimated using the d(002) spacing, ranges between 0.9884 and 1.0, further indicating a highly crystalline and mature graphite structure66–68.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy further supports this interpretation. Absorption bands at 909.24 cm⁻¹ and 1028.39 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O stretching, suggesting some surface oxidation. The band at 776.78 cm⁻¹ indicates the presence of C–H skeletal vibrations, suggesting partial retention of unoxidized graphite structures69. These observations imply that while some oxidation has occurred, the graphite largely retains its structural integrity, which is advantageous for processing and end-use performance. Geochemical analysis of the gangue minerals reveals a composition dominated by quartz, plagioclase, alkali feldspar, muscovite, and biotite. Although the overall carbon content categorizes the ore as low to medium grade, its favorable flake size distribution and high degree of graphitization enhance its economic attractiveness. The deposit holds significant potential for diverse applications across the battery, metallurgy, and thermal management sectors, particularly considering global shortages in high-grade graphite supply.

Conclusion

The Kobe Sorsa graphite deposits present a complex yet promising profile with significant potential for various industrial applications. The geological setting, predominantly consisting of graphitic quartzite and subordinate graphite schist, supports the presence of valuable graphite mineralization. This study highlights key insights into the Kobe Sorsa graphite deposits but is limited by the lack of subsurface data, which guides further exploration to define the extent of mineralization. Future work should focus on improving the beneficiation process to enhance the recovery of medium to large graphite flakes, exploring advanced techniques like flotation and magnetic separation. Additionally, further morphological studies and geochemical analysis are needed to assess graphite quality and the impact of trace element variations on purity. Optimizing recovery for fine flakes with specialized industrial uses is also recommended.
