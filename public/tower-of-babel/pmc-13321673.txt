Predictive value of carotid artery Doppler ultrasound for resuscitation outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest
Authors: Shuaishuai Zhou, Sa Wang, Shurong Xu, Jue Fang, Rui Zhang, Danping Yan, Yajie Liu, Meiling Wang, Yuwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCarotid artery Doppler ultrasound, as a non-invasive monitoring method, offers higher clinical utility during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) compared to invasive techniques. This study aimed to investigate the application of real-time carotid Doppler ultrasound in CPR patients and analyze the predictive efficacy of waveform-derived parameters for return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).Methods This prospective observational study consecutively enrolled adult patients who underwent CPR in the emergency department of a tertiary hospital in Zhejiang Province between March 2024 and March 2025. ROSC was defined as the first documented confirmation at a scheduled pulse/rhythm check, independent of Doppler findings. Patients were divided into ROSC and non-ROSC groups based on whether ROSC was obtained. Baseline characteristics and carotid Doppler parameters were analyzed to evaluate their prognostic value. Univariate analysis compared baseline characteristics, CPR data, and carotid Doppler parameters between groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to assess the predictive value of statistically significant indicators for ROSC.ResultsAmong 51 patients (23 in the ROSC group, 28 in the non-ROSC group). Significant differences were observed in carotid blood flow parameters between groups, including VTI、Vmax、Vmin、TAV、MD*、RI、dv.MAX (all P < 0.05). ROC analysis showed AUC values of 0.811 (VTI), 0.758 (Vmax), 0.727 (Vmin), 0.751 (cMD*) and 0.72 (dv.MAX), with optimal cutoffs of 18.8 87.1,19, 2318 and 8.75, respectively. Because ROSC was adjudicated at scheduled pulse/rhythm checks, the prespecified 60-second Doppler window before documented ROSC may have partially overlapped with spontaneous rather than purely compression-generated flow.ConclusionCarotid Doppler parameters (VTI、Vmax、Vmin、TAV、MD*、RI、dv.MAX) significantly differ between ROSC and non-ROSC groups. These parameters may provide objective hemodynamic insights during CPR. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the Doppler window preceding documented ROSC may have partially included ROSC-transition flow; therefore, the results are exploratory and require prospective multicenter validation.Keywords: Carotid artery blood flow ultrasound, CPR, ROSC, Predictive performance

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) is a major global health challenge with a high incidence and poor prognosis, ranking as the leading cause of death among adults. Despite continuous advancements in CPR techniques, equipment, and pharmacological interventions, the rate of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in patients experiencing cardiopulmonary arrest remains low. In China, outcomes remain poor: in the nationwide BASIC-OHCA registry, survival to hospital discharge or 30 days among EMS-treated OHCA patients was approximately 1.2% [1]. National data have also reported an IHCA survival-to-discharge rate of ~ 9.4% in China [2]. By comparison, contemporary registry reports suggest OHCA survival to discharge averages around 7.5% in Europe and 10.2% in the United States, while IHCA survival to discharge in European registry cohorts is approximately 27.3 ~ 62%, highlighting substantial international variation [3]. Therefore, improving the success rate of CPR and establishing an early prognosis assessment remain urgent challenges in emergency and critical care medicine. Traditional CPR relies primarily on basic and advanced life support measures, including mechanical chest compressions, defibrillation, airway management, and pharmacotherapy [4]. However, the efficacy of these interventions is influenced by multiple factors, such as the patient’s pre-arrest pathophysiological state, the timeliness of intervention, and individual variability during resuscitation. In this context, identifying early indicators of ROSC is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and determining whether resuscitation efforts should be continued. Currently, the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines recommend rhythm assessment every two minutes to promptly detect ROSC. However, this approach may inadvertently prolong resuscitation time, and the accuracy of manual pulse palpation remains questionable [5]. In recent years, bedside ultrasound has gained traction in emergency medicine due to its non-invasive and repeatable nature. Studies have demonstrated that monitoring carotid artery Doppler flow during CPR can effectively confirm ROSC, but most of this research has been conducted in animal models, with a predominant focus on a single parameter—blood flow velocity. There remains a lack of systematic investigation into waveform characteristics such as pulsatility and acceleration time [6, 7]. Furthermore, different studies have not yet established a standardized protocol for the timing of ultrasound monitoring (such as blood flow variations during the compression cycle) and the threshold values of key parameters, limiting its clinical applicability. Therefore, we prospectively collected real-time carotid Doppler ultrasound data during CPR and evaluated multiple velocity- and waveform-derived parameters for their association with ROSC. By predefining candidate indicators and analytic thresholds in the study protocol, we aimed to support a hemodynamics-informed approach to CPR monitoring that may aid real-time resuscitation decision-making and early recognition of ROSC.

Methods

Study population

A prospective study was conducted, enrolling patients with cardiac arrest admitted to a tertiary hospital from March 2024 to March 2025. Consecutive enrollment was implemented according to a pre-specified protocol. All patients received standard Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) measures upon admission. Based on whether ROSC was achieved, patients were categorized into the ROSC group and the non-ROSC group. The determination of ROSC was based on standard ACLS criteria, including palpable pulse, electrocardiographic rhythm changes, and clinical manifestations, and was adjudicated independently of Doppler parameters. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Patients who experienced in-hospital or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest; (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) Patients who underwent carotid blood flow monitoring during emergency CPR; (4) Intact cervical skin without damage. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Pregnant women; (2) Patients with carotid artery stenosis or carotid arteriosclerosis; (3) Inadequate Doppler signal quality despite standardized probe placement; (4) Missing clinical data. Screening and enrollment were performed in real time by trained research personnel, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was conducted in accordance with medical ethical standards and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the tertiary hospital. Written informed consent was waived per emergency research regulations; patient privacy and data security were strictly protected.

Carotid doppler measurement

In this study, the CADFlow™ UCM Doppler ultrasound system (Suzhou Shenzhi Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) was employed for data acquisition. This device is a portable, dual-crystal, continuous-wave Doppler system specifically designed for hands-free carotid artery monitoring. It can automatically acquire signals, display real-time waveforms, and compute blood flow velocity parameters through integrated software, requiring no manual adjustments or angle corrections. The operational procedure followed a standardized, prospectively specified Standard Operating Procedure (SOP): measurements were performed with patients in a supine position, preferably on the right side; if unavailable, the left side was used. The neck remained aligned with the trunk, extended and relaxed to prevent prominence of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. The specialized butterfly-shaped membrane was adhered approximately two finger-widths lateral to the laryngeal prominence, adjacent to the sternocleidomastoid muscle. The sensor probe was then positioned on this membrane, with ultrasound coupling gel filling the probe groove completely. The arrow on the probe was aligned with the arterial blood flow direction, while its long axis was parallel to the thyroid cartilage and perpendicular to the common carotid artery. Once correctly fixed and connected to the CADFlow™ UCM Doppler system, the probe required no further operator intervention, significantly reducing variability. It is noteworthy that Doppler probe placement did not require the presence of a palpable pulse. In all cases, the probe was placed during ongoing chest compressions, regardless of whether spontaneous circulation was present. This system enabled detection of carotid blood flow even in the absence of ROSC, facilitating continuous monitoring throughout cardiac arrest. Detailed sensor probe location is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

General demographic and clinical variables were collected prospectively at presentation, including age, gender, type of cardiac arrest (in-hospital or out-of-hospital), and length of hospital stay. Carotid Doppler waveforms were recorded continuously from CPR initiation until resuscitation termination or sustained ROSC. The ROSC time-point (T0) was defined as the first documented ROSC confirmation at a scheduled pulse/rhythm check rather than the exact physiologic onset of spontaneous circulation. Accordingly, true ROSC may have preceded documentation by up to approximately 120 s under standard ACLS rhythm-check intervals. For ROSC patients, Doppler parameters were extracted from a prespecified 60-second window immediately preceding documented T0 (T0 − 60 s to T0). For non-ROSC patients, T0 was defined as the time of resuscitation termination, and parameters were extracted from the final 60 s before termination. Extraction windows were restricted to periods of ongoing chest compressions and excluded brief interruptions for pulse/rhythm checks. Therefore, in some ROSC patients, the analyzed Doppler window may have partially overlapped with spontaneous rather than purely compression-generated flow near the ROSC transition. When multiple ROSC episodes occurred, the first ROSC event that led to sustained clinical management/transfer was used as the reference. Collected Carotid Blood Flow Parameters (10 items): ①Velocity-Time Integral (VTI): Represents the carotid blood flow distance per cardiac cycle. ②Peak Systolic Velocity (Vmax): The maximum blood flow velocity during systole. ③Minimum Diastolic Velocity (Vmin): Reflects the lowest blood flow velocity during diastole, indicating diastolic flow and forward flow status. ④HR: Number of heartbeats per minute. ⑤Time-Averaged Velocity (TAV): Overall status of carotid blood flow. ⑥Corrected Carotid Flow Time (ccFT): Reflects cardiac preload and assists in evaluating the patient’s volume status. ⑦Minute Distance (MD): Flow volume per unit area per minute.⑧Pulsatility Index (PI): Indicates the pulsatility of blood flow. ⑨Resistance Index (RI): Reflects vascular resistance in blood flow. ⑩Maximum Velocity Change Rate (dv.MAX): Peak acceleration of carotid blood flow during systole, indicating myocardial contractility. Placement and operation of the device were conducted exclusively by trained research personnel, rather than clinical resuscitation team members. Doppler data were automatically time-stamped by the device and logged in real time; key clinical time points (e.g., pulse checks) were concurrently annotated by the research team to enhance temporal alignment. The clinical resuscitation team was blinded to Doppler readouts, which were analyzed offline according to a prespecified plan. For group comparisons, the ROSC group used parameters immediately prior to documented ROSC; the non-ROSC group used parameters from the final minute before resuscitation termination, ensuring temporal consistency. Assessment for carotid stenosis/atherosclerosis followed the prospective protocol: for ROSC patients, evaluation was based on early post-ROSC carotid imaging; for non-ROSC patients, prior imaging or documented medical history was used. No carotid imaging was performed during active resuscitation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range (P25, P75). Categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage. For comparisons between groups: Independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables. Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. To further evaluate the discriminative ability of each parameter for ROSC, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the optimal cutoff values were determined based on the maximum Youden index. All analyses followed a prospectively prespecified statistical plan. In addition to ROC analysis, univariable logistic regression models were fitted for each candidate predictor to estimate effect sizes, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Because this was an exploratory pilot study, no formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. Finally, to assess whether Doppler-derived parameters remained independently associated with ROSC after accounting for baseline imbalance, we conducted an exploratory multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for cardiac arrest location (OHCA vs. IHCA). To reduce the risk of overfitting given the limited number of ROSC events: only one Doppler-derived parameter was included per model along with arrest location. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were reported.

In contrast, age, HR, ccFT, and PI were not statistically significant discriminators of ROSC (Table 3).

Discussion

In this prospective study of 51 patients with cardiac arrest, we evaluated whether carotid Doppler velocity- and waveform-derived parameters recorded during CPR were associated with ROSC. Several carotid Doppler parameters (VTI, Vmax, Vmin, TAV, MD*, RI, and dv.MAX) differed significantly between the ROSC and non-ROSC groups, suggesting that continuous carotid Doppler monitoring may provide objective hemodynamic information during resuscitation and support earlier recognition of ROSC and real-time optimization of compressions.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on animal models or single hemodynamic velocity parameters such as peak systolic velocity (Vmax) [8]. The results of this study demonstrate significant correlations between carotid blood flow parameters and the success rate of ROSC, supporting the utility of these seven parameters as critical physiological indicators reflecting the quality of CPR during resuscitation efforts. Through ROC curve analysis, the study further validated the sensitivity and specificity of VTI, Vmax, Vmin, cMD* and dv.MAX in predicting ROSC rates. Among these, VTI exhibited the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.811), suggesting its potential as a real-time monitoring tool to assist clinical decision-making. This finding suggests that when the forward blood flow distance driven by each individual chest compression reaches a certain threshold (approximately 18.8 cm), the probability of achieving ROSC increases significantly. In comparison, the area under the ROC curves (AUC) for Vmax and Vmin were 0.758 and 0.727, respectively, indicating good discriminative ability. The optimal cutoff value for Vmax was around 87.1 cm/s, indicating that higher peak systolic velocities are associated with ROSC. In the prospective study by Koch et al. [9], mean Vmin during CPR was close to 0 cm/s overall, whereas patients who achieved ROSC tended to exhibit higher trough velocities (approximately 28.6 cm/s), although statistical significance was limited by sample size. In our cohort, the ROC-derived Vmin cutoff (~ 19 cm/s) appears higher than many prior reports and should be interpreted cautiously. This difference likely reflects both methodological and physiologic factors: (1) Vmin in our system is obtained via automated envelope tracking and represents the trough between compression-generated pulses rather than native cardiac-cycle diastole, such that device-specific smoothing may elevate baseline estimates; (2) extraction immediately preceding ROSC may include ROSC-transition beats or intermittent native contractions (“micro-ROSC”), increasing trough velocities despite ongoing compressions; and (3) consistent use of real-time compression feedback and protocolized resuscitation may generate measurable forward flow even during trough phases in selected patients. Accordingly, VTI remained the most robust and clinically interpretable primary signal, whereas Vmin should be viewed as a supportive waveform feature requiring validation against raw Doppler traces and synchronized CPR-quality or invasive hemodynamic reference measures.

Effect-size analyses further strengthened these observations. In univariable logistic regression (Table 4), VTI showed the most consistent association with ROSC, with each 1-cm increase corresponding to higher odds of ROSC. Vmin, dv.MAX, Vmax, TAV and MD* were also positively associated with ROSC. RI showed an inverse association with ROSC, although interpretation is constrained by scaling and physiologic interdependence with velocity measures. To address potential confounding by arrest location, we performed an exploratory multivariable model including VTI and cardiac arrest location (OHCA vs. IHCA). In this adjusted model (Table 5), VTI remained independently associated with ROSC, whereas arrest location was not statistically significant after adjustment. Because of the limited number of outcome events, we intentionally restricted model complexity and did not include additional covariates (e.g., initial rhythm) to reduce overfitting risk; therefore, adjusted findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Because arrest location differed between groups and is a clinically important determinant of outcome, we also performed sensitivity analyses within IHCA-only and OHCA-only cohorts. In IHCA patients (Table 6), VTI remained significantly higher in the ROSC group than in the non-ROSC group, while other Doppler parameters did not reach statistical significance. In OHCA patients (Table 7), VTI again differed significantly, and MD* and dv.MAX were also higher in the ROSC group. These stratified findings support that the association between VTI and ROSC is not solely attributable to imbalance in arrest location and suggest that additional waveform-derived indices (e.g., MD* and dv.MAX) may provide complementary information in selected contexts, particularly in OHCA. Together, these subgroup findings support VTI as a consistent marker across arrest settings and suggest that waveform-derived indices such as MD* and dv.MAX may offer complementary information in selected contexts, particularly in OHCA.

Differences in Doppler ultrasound parameters observed in this study can be explained by the underlying hemodynamic mechanisms during CPR. Because flow per cycle is proportional to the VTI multiplied by vessel cross-sectional area, a higher carotid VTI during chest compressions reflects greater forward carotid blood flow (i.e., higher carotid pulse volume), serving as a pragmatic surrogate of systemic perfusion and cerebral-directed flow during CPR [10]. Consequently, when VTI exceeds the study-identified threshold, it may indicate that the stroke volume generated by compressions is sufficient to maintain minimal organ perfusion pressures, thereby enhancing myocardial and cerebral perfusion and increasing the likelihood of ROSC. Consistent with this, previous studies have highlighted that high-quality CPR should aim for adequate circulation, including maintaining coronary perfusion pressure above 20 mmHg, as well as higher systolic and diastolic blood pressures [9]. The VTI threshold identified in this study may help frame future investigations of quantitative hemodynamic targets during CPR. However, because the present data are derived from a small single-center cohort, this value should not yet be interpreted as a definitive clinical target and instead requires validation in larger prospective studies. Similarly, higher Vmax values likely reflect better circulatory tone and higher cerebral and coronary perfusion pressures, which are favorable conditions for achieving ROSC. Conversely, persistently low Vmax may reflect inadequate compression-generated flow, reduced vascular tone, or insufficient intravascular volume. These interpretations remain physiologic hypotheses in the present study and should not be used alone to guide escalation decisions without further validation. Our findings confirm significant differences in Vmin between groups, indicating that maintaining a certain level of forward blood flow during diastole is crucial for successful resuscitation. Physiologically, when aortic pressures rapidly decline or reverse flow occurs upon relaxation of compressions, coronary and cerebral perfusion is severely compromised, decreasing the likelihood of ROSC [10]. Mechanistically, high-quality compressions with complete chest recoil (avoiding leaning) help preserve diastolic aortic pressure and thus coronary perfusion pressure during the decompression phase of CPR. In Doppler terms, more preserved trough/diastolic velocity (higher Vmin/EDV) and a lower resistive index (RI) may reflect relatively sustained forward flow between compressions, a physiologic condition that is favorable for myocardial perfusion and ROSC [11]. RI essentially reflects the continuity and resistance of pulsatile blood flow: RI = (Vmax−Vmin) / Vmax, with values approaching 1 indicating minimal diastolic flow (high resistance state). In this study, lower RI values in the ROSC group imply more physiologic, continuous blood flow patterns, whereas RI values close to 1 in the non-ROSC group suggest minimal or reversed diastolic flow. Thus, a lower RI during CPR may reflect more favorable diastolic perfusion and may be associated with a greater likelihood of ROSC. This finding aligns with current CPR guidelines that emphasize minimizing interruptions in chest compressions and maintaining adequate coronary perfusion pressures.

Current clinical monitoring modalities for resuscitation, including manual palpation of carotid pulses, electrocardiography (ECG), and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) monitoring, exhibit limitations in detecting ROSC. First, carotid pulse palpation relies heavily on clinicians’ experience and technique, which may be compromised by patient body habitus, hemodynamic status, or chaotic resuscitation environments, leading to inaccuracies or delays [12]. Second, intermittent pulse assessments fail to capture dynamic changes during CPR and are time-consuming. Although EtCO2 monitoring reflects pulmonary gas exchange, it lags behind changes in blood flow [13]. In contrast, real-time carotid Doppler ultrasound, as introduced in this study, provides continuous, non-invasive, and objective hemodynamic information during CPR. Even in low-flow states, it may help characterize blood flow dynamics while minimizing interruptions in chest compressions, which is consistent with prior work [14]. Carotid Doppler ultrasound may therefore have potential as an adjunctive monitoring tool for individualized hemodynamic assessment during resuscitation. However, the present study was not designed to evaluate Doppler-guided treatment adjustment, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence for a specific clinical management algorithm. Some studies have proposed the concept of “flow-guided CPR,” adjusting compression and pharmacological interventions based on real-time circulatory indices [15]. Future studies should determine whether carotid Doppler-derived parameters, particularly VTI, can be incorporated into standardized resuscitation monitoring frameworks as adjunctive real-time hemodynamic markers during CPR. At present, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and any flow-guided implementation strategy will require prospective multicenter validation before clinical adoption.

Limitations

This single-center prospective observational study had a modest sample size (n = 51), which limited statistical power and constrained robust multivariable adjustment. In the absence of a formal a priori sample size calculation, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Although an exploratory multivariable model was performed, the limited number of ROSC events restricted the number of covariates that could be included reliably; consequently, adjustment was limited to cardiac arrest location (OHCA vs. IHCA), and the adjusted results should likewise be interpreted with caution.

A further important limitation concerns the ROSC adjudication window. ROSC was defined as the first documented confirmation at a scheduled pulse/rhythm check rather than the exact physiologic onset of spontaneous circulation. Given standard 2-minute rhythm-check intervals, true ROSC may have preceded documentation by up to approximately 120 s. Accordingly, the prespecified 60-second Doppler extraction window immediately preceding documented ROSC may have partially overlapped with spontaneous rather than purely compression-generated flow, potentially inflating Doppler parameters in the ROSC group and contributing to the observed discriminative performance of some Doppler-derived indices.

In addition, carotid Doppler measurements obtained during CPR are inherently influenced by compression mechanics, patient vascular characteristics, and device-related signal processing, all of which may affect waveform stability and derived parameters. Although we sought to reduce bias through standardized sensor placement, continuous hands-free acquisition, prespecified extraction rules, and blinded offline analysis, invasive hemodynamic reference measures and objective CPR-quality data were unavailable, limiting mechanistic interpretation and full adjustment for residual confounding. Exclusion of patients with significant carotid disease may also restrict external validity, particularly in older or vasculopathic populations. Furthermore, initial rhythm and no-flow/low-flow intervals were not systematically recorded, precluding adjustment for these potentially important confounders. Larger multicenter studies with synchronized waveform adjudication and more precise temporal determination of ROSC are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, prospective, real-time carotid Doppler ultrasound monitoring offers a novel tool to improve resuscitation quality and predict patient ROSC during CPR. Future studies should confirm the stability and reliability of carotid hemodynamic parameters in ROSC prediction through multicenter, large-sample prospective cohorts. By combining these parameters with other clinical indicators and optimizing technical integration and workflows, CPR management may evolve from an “experience-driven” to a “data-driven” paradigm, laying the foundation for a precision-oriented resuscitation evaluation system in cardiac arrest.
