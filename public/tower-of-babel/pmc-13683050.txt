CircLAMA5 traps Sam68 in the cytoplasm to regulate alternative splicing and drive sunitinib resistance in ccRCC
Authors: Zhehao Xu, Haohua Lu, Zeyi Lu, Ruyue Wang, Ziwei Zhu, Yiming Ding, Yang Li, Yi Lu, Fan Li, Wenqin Luo, Yudong Lin, Xudong Mao, Chenshuo Xu, Yi Chen, Zhen Yan, Guanghua Huo, Zhenwei Zhou, Min Xu, Lifeng Ding, Gonghui Li, Liqun Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance remains a major challenge in the treatment of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), yet the underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. Here, we identify circular RNA circLAMA5 (hsa_circ_0061093) as a critical driver of sunitinib resistance in ccRCC. CircLAMA5 is significantly upregulated in sunitinib‑resistant cell lines and cell‑derived xenografts (CDX-R). Functional studies demonstrate that circLAMA5 enhances ccRCC cell survival, colony formation, and apoptosis evasion under sunitinib treatment both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, circLAMA5 binds to the C-terminal region of Sam68 and promotes its cytoplasmic retention, at least in part by impairing the interaction between Sam68 and the nuclear import receptor TNPO1. This reduces nuclear Sam68 availability and suppresses its regulatory role in the alternative splicing of Bcl-x pre-mRNA, leading to a decreased pro‑apoptotic Bcl‑x(s)/anti‑apoptotic Bcl‑x(L) ratio and ultimately conferring sunitinib resistance. Importantly, we developed a biomimetic nanoparticle system coated with ccRCC cell membranes (RCCM@NPs) for targeted delivery of sh‑circLAMA5, effectively restoring sunitinib sensitivity in resistant tumors in vivo. Our findings reveal circLAMA5 as a key regulator of sunitinib resistance via the Sam68‑Bcl‑x splicing axis and propose a promising targeted nanotherapeutic strategy for overcoming resistance in advanced ccRCC.

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent histological subtype of renal cancer and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality [1]. VEGF/VEGFR-driven angiogenesis is a defining biological feature of ccRCC, making VEGFR-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) a long-standing therapeutic backbone. Sunitinib, in particular, has served as a prototypical VEGFR-TKI and a widely used model to study response and resistance in ccRCC [2–4]. Despite meaningful clinical benefit in subsets of patients, intrinsic and acquired resistance to sunitinib is common and ultimately limits durable efficacy [2–6]. Mechanistically, resistance is multifactorial, involving bypass signaling activation, inadequate intratumoral drug accumulation and sequestration, tumor microenvironment remodeling, as well as metabolic and epigenetic adaptations under chronic drug pressure [2–6]. These observations collectively highlight a critical and unmet need to identify upstream, drug-inducible regulators that serve as master coordinators, linking sustained TKI exposure to the establishment of stable tumor-intrinsic survival programs.

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed transcripts generated by back-splicing and are often highly stable and context-specific, positioning them as durable regulators in cancer biology [7–9]. Beyond the canonical competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) paradigm, circRNAs can directly bind RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), act as molecular scaffolds/decoys, modulate protein activity and subcellular localization, and influence transcription and splicing programs [7–11]. In renal cancer, circRNA dysregulation correlates with tumor progression and treatment response and is under active investigation as a source of biomarkers and therapeutic targets [12].

Importantly, multiple circRNAs have been reported to be substantially associated with sunitinib resistance in ccRCC. CircSNX6 promotes resistance via a miR-1184/GPCPD1/lysophosphatidic acid axis [13], and circME1 enhances aerobic glycolysis and resistance development via cis-regulation of ME1 [14]. CircPTPN12 drives progression and resistance via an hnRNPM/IL-6/STAT3 pathway [15], while circRARS cooperates with IGF2BP3 to facilitate m6A-dependent post-transcriptional regulation and lipid metabolic remodeling [16]. These findings emphasize the crucial regulatory role played by circRNAs in mediating sunitinib resistance in ccRCC. However, the potential that sunitinib-induced circRNAs exert drug-resistance effects through other regulatory mechanisms warrants further investigation.

In parallel, alternative splicing (AS) is increasingly recognized as a major driver of cancer plasticity and drug resistance by reshaping isoform composition without requiring new transcription [17–19]. RBPs execute splicing decisions in signal- and context-dependent manners, enabling rapid phenotypic adaptation under therapy stress [17–19]. Notably, Sam68 (KHDRBS1) is a signal-responsive STAR family RBP with a well-established central role in regulating BCL2L1 (Bcl-x) alternative splicing, thereby controlling the delicate balance between anti-apoptotic Bcl-x(L) and pro-apoptotic Bcl-x(s) isoforms [20]. Critically, Sam68 has direct relevance to ccRCC drug response, as it has been linked to sunitinib-induced apoptosis and therapeutic sensitivity in renal cancer models [21]. These findings point to a compelling yet unexplored model of resistance: whether specific drug-responsive circRNAs may reprogram sunitinib response by controlling Sam68 function and Sam68-dependent AS programs, thereby shifting apoptosis-relevant isoform balances under TKI stress.

In this study, we identify a sunitinib-induced, resistance-associated circRNA (circLAMA5) that promotes ccRCC survival under sunitinib treatment. We demonstrate that circLAMA5 directly binds Sam68 and limits its nuclear accumulation, thereby reducing nuclear Sam68 availability. This sequestration reprograms Sam68-dependent alternative splicing, most notably suppressing the Bcl-x(s)/Bcl-x(L) ratio, which blunts sunitinib-triggered apoptosis and confers a robust drug-resistant phenotype. To translate this mechanism, we engineer a renal cancer cell membrane-coated nanoparticle platform for targeted delivery of sh-circLAMA5, enabling effective circLAMA5 silencing in vivo and resensitization of resistant tumors to sunitinib.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

ccRCC cell lines 786-O, SW839, OS-RC-2, Caki-1, and A498, together with the normal human renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2, were used in this study. The 786-O, OS-RC-2, Caki-1, and HK-2 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, A498 cells were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA), and SW839 cells were kindly provided by Prof. Chawnshang Chang (University of Rochester). 786-O, OS-RC-2, and Caki-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640, SW839 cells in DMEM, A498 cells in MEM, and HK-2 cells in DMEM/F12. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; CellMax, Beijing, China), and cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

To establish sunitinib-resistant sublines, parental 786-O and SW839 cells were exposed stepwise to increasing concentrations of sunitinib (Selleck, Houston, Texas, USA; S7781) until 14 μM and 16 μM, respectively. Resistant sublines (786-OR and SW839R) were maintained in complete medium containing 10 μM sunitinib for >20 passages before use.

Plasmid and siRNA transfections were performed using jetPRIME (Polyplus, Illkirch, France; 101000046). siRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and plasmids were constructed by CORUES (Nanjing, China). Sequences are provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (SRRSH202202329).

For subcutaneous CDX models, 1 × 107 786-O cells were mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA; 354234) and injected into the flanks of 4–6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. When tumors reached ~200 mm3, mice received intratumoral injections of si-circLAMA5 or negative control siRNA (NC) (2 nmol per injection in PBS, 50 μL per tumor, twice per week), both siRNAs were modified by 5’ Cholesterol and 2’ OMe to prolong each half-life to about 96 h. In parallel, mice received vehicle or sunitinib (40 mg/kg/day) by oral gavage for 4 weeks. Sunitinib was suspended in 1% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; HY-Y0703) solution containing 0.1% Tween-80.

To generate resistant xenografts, sunitinib-treated tumors with sustained growth were excised, cut into ~1 mm3 fragments, and re-implanted into new mice for additional treatment cycles. This process was repeated for three serial passages. Third-generation sunitinib-treated tumors were defined as CDX-R, and vehicle-passaged tumors were defined as CDX-S.

For subsequent experiments, CDX-R fragments (~1 mm3) were transplanted subcutaneously. Tumor size was measured every 3 days and calculated as volume = (length × width2)/2. Tumors were harvested and weighed at the indicated endpoints.

The number of animals per group was determined based on prior studies using similar in vivo models, ensuring sufficient biological replicates for robust and consistent phenotype assessment. Animals were randomly assigned to control and experimental groups to minimize selection bias. Blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the experimental procedures; however, outcome measurements were conducted according to predefined quantitative criteria to mitigate potential bias.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (CWbio, Taizhou, China; CW0580S). cDNA was synthesized using HiFiScript gDNA Removal RT Master Mix (CWbio, CW2020M). qRT-PCR was performed using 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (CWbio, CW0957). Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 103 cells per well). Drug treatment was initiated 24 h after seeding. At the indicated time points, CCK-8 reagent (GLPBIO, Montclair, California, USA; GK10001) was added and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 103 cells per well) and treated as indicated. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.3% crystal violet. Images were acquired using a GelDoc XR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA).

Annexin V apoptosis detection

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/PI kit (Liankebio, Hangzhou, China; AP107-100). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, incubated for 12 h, and treated as indicated for 48 h. Cells were collected, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL binding buffer. Samples were stained with Annexin V-APC and PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark and analyzed by flow cytometry using CytoFLEX LS (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA).

Western blotting

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer. Proteins were denatured at 95 °C for 20 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.2 μm; Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA; 1620177). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies. Signals were detected using ECL reagents (FDbio, Hangzhou, China; FD8000/FD8030). Antibodies are listed in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP kit (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA; MAGNARIP01). Briefly, 2 × 107 cells were lysed in RIP lysis buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibody or IgG control and magnetic protein A/G beads. Beads were washed five times, complexes were digested with proteinase K, and immunoprecipitated RNA was purified for downstream qRT-PCR.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and DAPI staining. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Microarray analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 786-O and 786-OR cells using TRIzol, purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit and RNase-Free DNase Set, and quality-checked using a NanoDrop ND-2000 and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Labeling and hybridization were performed using the SBC Human (4 × 180 K) ceRNA array V1.0, and arrays were scanned with an Agilent G5761A scanner (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). Raw data were extracted using Agilent Feature Extraction software, quantile-normalized, and log2-transformed using the limma package in R. Differential expression was analyzed using limma, with P values adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. CircRNAs with |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH was performed using an RNA FISH kit (GenePharma, F40111). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100. Samples were prehybridized at 37 °C for 30 min and hybridized with a Cy3-labeled probe targeting circLAMA5 (5′-GTAGTTCCAGGAAGAAGCGTTGCCTGT-3′) at 37 °C overnight. After hybridization, cells were washed with 4×, 2×, and 1× SSC at 42 °C (5 min each), counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Validation of circLAMA5 circularity

Sanger sequencing. The back-splicing junction of circLAMA5 (hsa_circ_0061093) was amplified from cDNA using divergent primers (Table S2, Supporting Information). PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing (TsingKe Biotechnology, China). Junction sequences were aligned to circBase.

Divergent and convergent PCR. Convergent and divergent primers were used to amplify circLAMA5 from cDNA and genomic DNA (gDNA). Products were resolved on 2% agarose gels. Divergent primers amplified circLAMA5 from cDNA but not from gDNA, whereas convergent primers amplified linear LAMA5 from both templates.

RNase R digestion. Total RNA (2 μg) was incubated with or without RNase R (3 U/μg; Epicenter) at 37 °C for 15 min and analyzed by qRT-PCR to quantify circLAMA5 and linear LAMA5.

Actinomycin D assay. Cells were treated with actinomycin D (2 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) and collected at 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h. Remaining circLAMA5 and linear LAMA5 levels were quantified by qRT-PCR and normalized to 0 h.

MS2-TRAP pull down and mass spectrometry

MS2-based RNA affinity purification (MS2-TRAP) was performed to identify circLAMA5-binding proteins. Cells stably expressing MS2-tagged circLAMA5 or an MS2-empty control were lysed in RIP lysis buffer (Magna RIP kit; Millipore, MAGNARIP01) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and RNase inhibitor. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation and incubated with recombinant FLAG-MS2 coat protein at 4 °C with rotation. The complexes were then incubated with an anti-FLAG antibody and captured using protein A/G magnetic beads. Beads were washed five times with RIP wash buffer, and bound complexes were eluted. Enrichment of circLAMA5 in the pull down was confirmed by qRT-PCR using divergent primers. Eluted proteins were analyzed by LC-MS/MS and immunoblotting. Proteins enriched in the MS2-circLAMA5 pull down relative to the MS2-empty control were considered candidate interactors.

RNA truncation pull down assay

To map the Sam68-binding region of circLAMA5, circLAMA5 was divided into serial RNA fragments of approximately 120 nt with 30-nt overlaps. An additional fragment containing the back-splice junction site was also designed. The sequences and positions of these RNA fragments are listed in Table S5 (Supporting Information). The corresponding biotin-labeled RNA fragments were synthesized by Tsingke (China) and used for RNA pull down assays. After incubation with cell lysates, RNA-protein complexes were captured using streptavidin beads and analyzed by western blotting using an anti-Sam68 antibody.

Molecular docking prediction

The potential interaction between circLAMA5 and Sam68 was predicted using HADDOCK. The Sam68 structure was predicted by AlphaFold 3.0, and the circLAMA5 RNA structure was predicted using RNAfold. Docking results were visualized to infer candidate interfaces.

Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractionation

Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were prepared for RNA and protein analyses using commercial kits. For RNA fractionation, cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA was isolated using the Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, Canada) following the manufacturer’s protocol; GAPDH and U6 were used as cytoplasmic and nuclear controls, respectively (primers in Table S2, Supporting Information). For protein fractionation, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, Haimen, China; P0028) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sam68 distribution was assessed by immunoblotting, with GAPDH and histone H3 as cytoplasmic and nuclear markers, respectively.

RT-PCR analysis of Bcl-x alternative splicing

Bcl-x splicing was analyzed using primers flanking the alternative region to amplify Bcl-x(L) (~700 bp) and Bcl-x(s) (~500 bp) (Table S2, Supporting Information). PCR was performed in 25 μL reactions containing 1 μL cDNA, 12.5 μL 2× Taq Master Mix (CWbio, CW0682), and 0.5 μM primers. Cycling conditions were 94 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by 72 °C for 5 min. Products were resolved on 2% agarose gels containing GelRed (Biosharp, Beijing, China; BS354A) at 100 V for 40 min and imaged using a GelDoc XR system (Bio-Rad).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in 1% NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were incubated with anti-Sam68 antibody or IgG control overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein G agarose beads (Cytiva, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA; 17-0618-01) for 4 h at 4 °C. After washing, the immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted and analyzed by western blotting using antibodies against TNPO1 and Sam68.

Preparation of PEI/pDNA polyplexes

Polyethylenimine (PEI, MW 25,000) (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in HEPES buffer. PEI and plasmid DNA were mixed at the indicated N/P ratio, vortexed for 10 s, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to form polyplexes.

Preparation of RCCM@NPs

Cell membranes were isolated from 786-O cells using a membrane/cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime). Cells were washed with cold PBS, resuspended in buffer A, and disrupted by three freeze-thaw cycles. Samples were centrifuged at 700 × g for 10 min (4 °C) to remove debris, and membranes were collected by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min (4 °C). Membranes were extruded through 400 nm polycarbonate membranes (Whatman, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA) using an Avanti mini-extruder to obtain RCCM vesicles. RCCM vesicles were coated onto PEI/pDNA polyplexes by sonication or co-extrusion through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane to generate RCCM@NPs. Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with RCCM@PEI at the indicated concentrations. After 24 h, cell viability was measured by CCK-8.

In vivo tumor targeting ability

For subcutaneous implantation, 2 × 106 786-O cells in 50 μL PBS/Matrigel (1:1) were injected into the flanks of 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. After 4 weeks, mice received tail-vein injection of free indocyanine green (ICG) or ICG-labeled RCCM@NPs. Fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using IVIS (Ex 710 nm/Em 785 nm).

Biocompatibility evaluation

Healthy mice received six intravenous injections of RCCM@NPs or saline over 21 days. At day 21, serum ALT, AST, BUN, and creatinine were measured. Major organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and analyzed by H&E staining.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of RCCM@NPs

CDX-R tumor fragments (~1 mm3) were transplanted subcutaneously. After 7 days, mice were randomized into the indicated treatment groups. For the primary therapeutic study, mice were intravenously injected with RCCM vesicles or RCCM@NPs (sh-circLAMA5 plasmid, 0.5 mg/kg) every 3 days for six injections and received daily oral gavage of vehicle or sunitinib (40 mg/kg/day). For the additional control experiment, CDX-R tumor-bearing mice were treated with saline, RCCM vesicles, RCCM@PEI, NPs, or RCCM@NPs, all combined with sunitinib treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. GraphPad Prism 9 was used for plotting and analysis. Two-group comparisons were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, as specified in the figure legends. Multiple pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm-Šídák method. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To verify the resistance of the third‑generation CDX-R, we implanted both CDX‑R tumors and sensitive cell‑derived xenograft (CDX‑S) tumors into nude mice. Circular RNA circLAMA5 is upregulated in sunitinib resistant ccRCC tissues and cell lines

To explore whether circRNAs contribute to sunitinib resistance in ccRCC, we performed a circRNA microarray comparing sunitinib-resistant 786-OR cells with parental 786-O cells (Fig. 2A). To prioritize candidate circRNAs, we first selected significantly dysregulated circRNAs (|log2FC| > 1, adj. p value < 0.05) and excluded those >5000 nt to facilitate subsequent primer design and functional studies. The remaining candidates were then prioritized chiefly by |log2FC| in descending order and secondarily by adj. P value in ascending order. From the top of this ranked list, specific back-splice junction (BSJ)-spanning qRT-PCR primers were designed. This yielded 42 upregulated and 70 downregulated circRNAs for initial qRT-PCR screening, which identified 36 circRNAs yielding specific amplification products with reliable melting curves. Among these, eight circRNAs with adequate expression abundance and the greatest expression fold changes were selected for presentation in Fig. 2B, where hsa_circ_0061093 and hsa_circ_0067689 were significantly upregulated, whereas hsa_circ_0039407 was significantly downregulated in 786-OR cells. In addition, we checked the expression of these circRNAs in our CDX-R tumors, only hsa_circ_0061093 was highly expressed in the CDX-R model (Fig. 2C). Moreover, hsa_circ_0061093 was also upregulated in 786-OR and SW839R cells compared to 786-O and SW839 parental cells (Fig. 2D). Hsa_circ_0061093 is formed by back-splicing exons 25–28 of the LAMA5 gene, resulting in a mature sequence length of 531 nt (Fig. 2F), which is confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 2G), leading us to name it circLAMA5. We further designed convergent and divergent primers to verify the circular characteristics of circLAMA5. As expected, circLAMA5 was amplified only from cDNA by different primers and was not amplified from gDNA (Fig. 2H). Its circular structure was further verified by its resistance to RNase R digestion (Fig. 2I). Actinomycin D treatment showed circLAMA5 was more stable than linear LAMA5 (Fig. 2J). Collectively, our screening results revealed that circLAMA5, as a circular RNA, was upregulated in sunitinib resistant cells and CDX-R model, may play a biological role in the sunitinib resistance of ccRCC.

CircLAMA5 regulates ccRCC response to sunitinib in vitro and in vivo

To examine the role of circLAMA5 in the development of sunitinib resistance, we designed specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting the back‑splicing junction sites of circLAMA5. qRT‑PCR analysis confirmed that these siRNAs selectively reduced circLAMA5 expression without notably affecting LAMA5 mRNA levels (Fig. S1A, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, we overexpressed circLAMA5 in 786‑O and SW839 cell lines using plasmids, and overexpression efficiency was verified (Fig. S1B, Supporting Information).

We next asked whether circLAMA5 influences the viability of ccRCC cells with or without sunitinib treatment. CCK‑8 assays showed that downregulating circLAMA5 in 786‑OR and SW839R cells had little effect on cell survival in the absence of sunitinib (Fig. S1C, Supporting Information). However, under treatment with 4 μM sunitinib, circLAMA5 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability (Fig. 3A). Moreover, the IC50 values of 786‑OR and SW839R cells decreased markedly after circLAMA5 knockdown (Fig. 3B). Conversely, overexpression of circLAMA5 in 786‑O and SW839 cells clearly increased cell viability under 2 μM sunitinib and raised the IC50 (Fig. 3C, D), whereas no obvious change was observed without drug treatment (Fig. S1D, Supporting Information). Colony formation assays further demonstrated that under sunitinib treatment, circLAMA5 knockdown significantly impaired the colony forming ability of resistant cells, while its overexpression enhanced it (Fig. 3E–H), underscoring the crucial role of circLAMA5 in sunitinib resistance. As sunitinib‑induced cell death is primarily apoptotic [23], and our prior work showed reduced apoptosis in resistant cells, we examined apoptotic rates. To validate our hypothesis, an MS2-tagged RNA affinity purification (MS2-TRAP) assay was performed to examine whether circLAMA5 binds proteins (Fig. 4C). We generated 786-O cells overexpression MS2 tagged circLAMA5, which allowed capture of circLAMA5-binding proteins. We first verified that the MS2-circLAMA5 plasmid was overexpressed and properly cyclized in cells via qRT-PCR assay and by Sanger sequencing (Fig. S2C, Supporting Information), and then validated that circLAMA5 was highly enriched through bead-mediated capture (Fig. S2D, Supporting Information). LC-MS/MS identified 26 proteins that were uniquely enriched in the circLAMA5-MS2 group (Table S4, Supporting Information). Among them, KHDRBS1 (Sam68) had the highest score of 283 (Fig. 4D), and its identification was further supported by a representative MS/MS spectrum of a Sam68-specific peptide (Fig. S2E, Supporting Information). MS2 pull down followed by western blot confirmed the direct binding of circLAMA5 and Sam68 (Fig. 4E). Sam68-RIP assay also indicated the interaction of Sam68 and circLAMA5 (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, FISH and immunofluorescence assays exhibited that they were colocalized in the cytoplasm, indicating their interaction (Fig. 4G).

To further elucidate the biological relevance and mechanistic basis of the circLAMA5-Sam68 interaction, we identified the Sam68 region required for circLAMA5 binding. Sam68 is composed of an N-terminal domain, a KH domain, as well as a C-terminal domain (Fig. 4H) [25]. It has been reported that the KH domain mediates the sequence-specific RNA-binding activity of Sam68 [26]. Thus, we designed full-length and truncated Sam68 plasmids with an HA tag. MS2 pull down assay revealed that deletion of the C-terminal domain of Sam68 abrogated its binding to circLAMA5, indicating that instead of KH domain, circLAMA5 specifically interacts with the C-terminal domain of Sam68 (Fig. 4I). This interaction pattern was further confirmed by RIP assay (Fig. 4J).

We then sought to identify the Sam68-binding region within circLAMA5. CircLAMA5 was divided into serial RNA fragments of approximately 120 nt with 30-nt overlaps, and an additional fragment containing the back-splice junction site (FJ) was included (Table S5, Supporting Information). RNA pull down followed by western blot analysis showed that Sam68 was mainly enriched by specific circLAMA5 fragments (Fig. 4K), suggesting that these regions are responsible for the interaction between circLAMA5 and Sam68. The putative binding mode between circLAMA5 and Sam68 was illustrated by HADDOCK as shown in Fig. S2F, Supporting Information [27]. Altogether, these data reveal that circLAMA5 specifically interacts with Sam68 through the C-terminal domain of Sam68, and that defined regions within circLAMA5 contribute to this interaction.

CircLAMA5 regulates Sam68-mediated alternative splicing via affecting its subcellular distribution

To investigate how circLAMA5 binding might regulate Sam68, we first examined whether circLAMA5 affects Sam68 protein expression. Western blot analysis showed that Sam68 protein levels were comparable between sensitive and resistant cells and were not significantly affected by circLAMA5 (Fig. S4A, B, Supporting Information). Sam68 contains a non-conventional nuclear localization signal (NLS) in its C-terminal region that is required for its nuclear localization [28, 29]. Given that circLAMA5 was demonstrated to bind the C-terminal domain of Sam68, we next investigated whether circLAMA5 affects the subcellular distribution of Sam68. Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assay demonstrated that in sunitinib-resistant cells, Sam68 protein was more abundant in the cytoplasm. Knockdown of circLAMA5 significantly reduced cytoplasmic Sam68 levels (Fig. 6A). Conversely, circLAMA5 overexpression enhanced the cytoplasmic distribution of Sam68 while decreasing its nuclear localization (Fig. S4C, Supporting Information). Moreover, circLAMA5 silencing reduced cytoplasmic Sam68 and increased nuclear Sam68 in sunitinib-treated CDX-R tumors (Fig. 6B). As previously reported, Sam68 acts as a signal‑dependent regulator of alternative splicing [30], and its nuclear localization is required for modulating Bcl‑x pre‑mRNA splicing to generate the pro‑apoptotic isoform Bcl‑x(s) [20]. We confirmed the association between Sam68 and Bcl-x pre-mRNA in ccRCC cells by RIP-qRT-PCR (Fig. S4D, Supporting Information). Indeed, Sam68 knockdown reduced the Bcl-x(s)/Bcl-x(L) ratio in parental cells (Fig. 6D), while its overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 6E), consistent with previous findings. Furthermore, a decreased Bcl‑x(s)/Bcl‑x(L) ratio was observed in sunitinib‑resistant ccRCC cells (Fig. 6F). We therefore hypothesized that circLAMA5 retains Sam68 in the cytoplasm, thereby impairing Sam68‑mediated Bcl‑x alternative splicing. Subsequent RT-PCR analysis revealed that circLAMA5 knockdown increased the Bcl-x(s)/Bcl-x(L) ratio (Fig. 6G), whereas circLAMA5 overexpression reduced this ratio in parental cells (Fig. 6H). Importantly, rescue experiments showed that Sam68 overexpression reversed the circLAMA5 induced reduction in the Bcl‑x(s)/Bcl‑x(L) ratio (Fig. 6I). Consistent with the in vitro results, circLAMA5 silencing increased the Bcl-x(s)/Bcl-x(L) ratio in CDX-R tumors (Fig. 6J). qRT-PCR analysis revealed similar results (Fig. S4E–K, Supporting Information). Moreover, circLAMA5 knockdown increased the levels of cleaved PARP and cleaved Caspase-3 in these CDX-R tumors, further supporting the association between circLAMA5 inhibition and apoptosis activation in vivo (Fig. S4L, Supporting Information).

To further clarify how circLAMA5 regulates Sam68 localization, we examined whether the NLS-containing region of Sam68 is required for circLAMA5 binding. MS2-TRAP and RIP assays showed that deletion of the NLS did not abolish the interaction between Sam68 and circLAMA5 (Fig. S4M, N, Supporting Information). These results suggest that circLAMA5 binding to Sam68 is not directly dependent on the NLS-containing sequence itself. Since circLAMA5 markedly altered Sam68 subcellular distribution, we next asked whether circLAMA5 affects Sam68 distribution by promoting nuclear export or impairing nuclear import. Leptomycin B treatment did not reverse the circLAMA5-induced cytoplasmic accumulation of Sam68 (Fig. S4O, Supporting Information), suggesting that CRM1-dependent nuclear export is unlikely to be the major mechanism. Given the role of TNPO1 in PY-NLS-dependent nuclear import [31], we then examined the interaction between Sam68 and TNPO1 and its regulation by circLAMA5. Co-immunoprecipitation assay showed that Sam68 interacted with TNPO1, whereas circLAMA5 overexpression weakened this interaction (Fig. 6K; Fig. S4P, Supporting Information). After confirming the efficiency of si-TNPO1 (Fig. S4Q, Supporting Information), nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation showed that TNPO1 depletion increased cytoplasmic Sam68. CircLAMA5 knockdown failed to restore nuclear Sam68, indicating that circLAMA5 regulates Sam68 localization in a TNPO1-dependent manner (Fig. S4R, Supporting Information), suggesting that circLAMA5 promotes cytoplasmic retention of Sam68, at least in part, by interfering with TNPO1-associated nuclear import.

Together, these results demonstrate that circLAMA5 promotes sunitinib resistance in clear cell renal cell carcinoma by sequestering Sam68 in the cytoplasm and modulating its alternative splicing function.

PEI condenses DNA into nanocomplexes to facilitate cellular uptake [37]. This biomimetic drug delivery system consists of two components: an inner cationic polymer core (PEI complexed with the gene) and an outer functional coating of ccRCC cell membrane for tumor targeting. Specifically, PEI and negatively charged sh-circLAMA5 plasmid self-assemble through electrostatic interactions to form the PEI/sh-circLAMA5 core nanoparticles. Meanwhile, ccRCC cell membrane vesicles (RCCM) were prepared by sonication or extrusion. The RCCM vesicles were then coated onto the surface of the PEI/sh-circLAMA5 core via sonication/extrusion to obtain the final biomimetic nanomedicine, termed RCCM@NPs. An N/P ratio of 8 was selected for subsequent experiments. The resulting RCCM@NPs exhibited an average diameter of approximately 161 nm and a zeta potential of −24.07 mV, similar to that of RCCM vesicles; transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further indicated successful surface coating with cell membrane (Fig. 7B; Fig. S5A, B, Supporting Information). Excellent biocompatibility was essential, and in vitro experiments confirmed minimal cytotoxicity of RCCM@PEI (Fig. 7C).

To evaluate targeting efficacy in vivo, 786-O cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Indocyanine green (ICG)-labeled RCCM@NPs were administered via tail vein injection, and real-time fluorescence imaging was used to monitor their distribution and tumor accumulation. Significant enrichment at the tumor site was observed within 48 h, and the accumulation efficiency in the RCCM@NPs group remained markedly higher than that of the control group even after 96 h (Fig. 7D, E), demonstrating excellent tumor-targeting specificity of the nanoparticles in mice. Furthermore, blood biochemical parameters and histopathological images of major organs indicated that RCCM@NPs caused negligible hepatic and renal toxicity compared to the PBS control, with no obvious signs of tissue damage or inflammation observed, further confirming the favorable biocompatibility of RCCM@NPs in vivo (Fig. 7F, G).

To evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in vivo, CDX-R tumor fragments were transplanted subcutaneously into nude mice. We first compared different carrier formulations under sunitinib treatment to clarify the contribution of RCCM coating and exclude potential carrier-related effects. All groups received sunitinib treatment and were intravenously treated with saline, RCCM vesicles, RCCM@PEI, NPs, or RCCM@NPs. Compared with saline, RCCM vesicles and RCCM@PEI did not show obvious antitumor effects, whereas NPs exhibited therapeutic efficacy. Notably, RCCM@NPs further enhanced tumor suppression compared with NPs, supporting the advantage of RCCM coating in improving therapeutic performance (Fig. S5C–E, Supporting Information). We next evaluated whether RCCM@NPs could reverse sunitinib resistance in the CDX-R model. Mice were intravenously administered RCCM vesicles or RCCM@NPs, combined with vehicle or sunitinib treatment. The results showed that treatment with RCCM@NPs significantly enhanced the sensitivity of sunitinib-resistant tumors to sunitinib (Fig. 7H–J).

Discussion

Our study establishes a circRNA-RBP-splicing mechanism that promotes VEGFR-TKI resistance in ccRCC and provides a delivery-enabled strategy to therapeutically reverse it. The core discovery is that circLAMA5 is highly expressed in sunitinib-resistant ccRCC, and it promotes resistance by dampening sunitinib-induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, circLAMA5 binds the C-terminal region of Sam68 and retains it in the cytoplasm, thereby suppressing Sam68-dependent alternative splicing of Bcl-x toward the pro-apoptotic Bcl-x(s) isoform, blunting sunitinib-triggered apoptosis, and ultimately enabling resistant outgrowth under continued drug exposure. These findings provide a concrete post-transcriptional checkpoint that complements established multilayer resistance frameworks (bypass signaling, drug sequestration, microenvironment and metabolic/epigenetic adaptations) observed during chronic sunitinib treatment [2–6].

Several circRNAs have been reported to participate in sunitinib resistance in ccRCC, but their mechanistic modes of action are distinct from the mechanism identified in the present study. CircSNX6 promotes sunitinib resistance through a miR-1184/GPCPD1/lysophosphatidic acid axis, representing a classical circRNA-miRNA regulatory model linked to lipid signaling [13]. CircME1 enhances resistance by cis-regulating its host gene ME1 and promoting aerobic glycolysis, highlighting a metabolism-centered mechanism [14]. CircPTPN12 drives renal cancer progression and sunitinib resistance through an hnRNPM/IL-6/STAT3 pathway, thereby connecting circRNA-RBP interaction to inflammatory signaling [15]. CircRARS cooperates with IGF2BP3 to facilitate m6A-dependent post-transcriptional regulation and lipid metabolic remodeling [16]. Together, these studies have established the importance of circRNAs in shaping metabolic, inflammatory, and post-transcriptional programs during sunitinib resistance. In contrast, our study reveals a different layer of circRNA function: circLAMA5 does not primarily act through an AGO2-dependent miRNA-sponge mechanism or a coding mechanism, but instead directly interacts with Sam68 to regulate the subcellular availability of a splicing regulator. By linking circRNA-mediated protein localization to Sam68-dependent Bcl-x alternative splicing, our work expands the known circRNA resistance mechanisms in ccRCC from miRNA regulation, host-gene regulation, and RBP-assisted RNA regulation to the spatial control of RBP function and apoptosis-related splicing.

A major conceptual innovation lies in how circLAMA5 regulates Sam68 nuclear localization. Sam68 is a STAR family RBP whose KH/STAR region mediates RNA binding and oligomerization, while its C-terminal determinants control nuclear localization and nuclear function, enabling regulation of splicing programs [38]. Importantly, a classic study identified a non-canonical NLS within the C-terminal region of Sam68, showing that this region is required for nuclear localization [39]. Consistently, prior work showed that Sam68 can shift between nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments in a context-dependent manner, indicating that its subcellular distribution is dynamic and functionally linked to its nuclear splicing activity [29]. Most mechanistic studies to date have focused on post-translational modifications (PTMs) as the major route to modulate Sam68 activity and/or localization-for example, Cdk1-mediated threonine phosphorylation reduces RNA binding, modulates localization, and attenuates Sam68 splicing activity [40]. In addition, the seminal Bcl-x splicing study demonstrated that tyrosine phosphorylation (e.g., by Fyn) can invert Sam68’s splicing output, underscoring how signaling inputs remodel Sam68 function [20]. In contrast to these PTM-centered models, our work introduces a new regulatory paradigm: a circRNA directly targets the Sam68 C-terminal region, which contains the non-conventional NLS, and thereby affects its nuclear access. Notably, the circLAMA5-Sam68 interaction is highly specific: circLAMA5 does not primarily engage the KH RNA-binding domain and does not need to change total Sam68 expression to exert its effect; instead, it reprograms Sam68 function via spatial restriction, creating a functional “nuclear Sam68 deficiency” while leaving total protein levels comparatively intact. This represents, to our knowledge, the first demonstration that circLAMA5 regulates Sam68 nuclear localization by impairing nuclear import factor binding, expanding the non-canonical repertoire of circRNA action from protein binding in general to protein trafficking control as a mechanism of drug resistance.

Downstream, our data implicate the Sam68-Bcl-x alternative splicing axis as a key mechanistic driver of apoptosis escape and sunitinib resistance. The balance between Bcl-x isoforms is a canonical apoptotic rheostat: anti-apoptotic Bcl-x(L) promotes survival, whereas Bcl-x(s) promotes mitochondrial apoptosis, and dysregulation of this balance contributes to therapy tolerance [41, 42]. Paronetto et al. established that Sam68 binds Bcl-x transcripts and promotes splicing toward Bcl-x(s), whereas loss of Sam68 activity favors accumulation of Bcl-x(L), directly linking Sam68 nuclear function to apoptotic competence [20]. A complementary mechanism was later reported in which FBI-1 can inhibit Sam68-mediated Bcl-x splicing and shift output toward anti-apoptotic Bcl-x(L), emphasizing that Sam68-dependent Bcl-x splicing is tunable by interacting regulators [43]. In our context, circLAMA5-mediated cytoplasmic sequestration of Sam68 suppresses the pro-apoptotic Bcl-x(s) splicing output, lowering the Bcl-x(s)/Bcl-x(L) ratio and enabling survival under sunitinib treatment. This is consistent with broader evidence that alternative splicing programs can act as “final common pathways” for drug resistance [17]. Importantly, apoptosis escape is clinically relevant in ccRCC and is increasingly viewed as a rational vulnerability, although effective translation requires precise biomarkers and delivery strategies [44]. Moreover, sunitinib has documented direct tumor-cell pro-apoptotic effects (in addition to anti-angiogenesis), reinforcing that tumor-intrinsic apoptotic wiring can meaningfully determine treatment response and failure [45].

A second major innovation is our translational implementation using a biomimetic nanodelivery system. Cell membrane coating nanotechnology can endow nanoparticle cores with complex membrane-derived functionalities, improving immune evasion, circulation stability, and tumor homing [32]. Cancer cell membrane-coated systems, in particular, can provide homotypic targeting and improved tumor accumulation compared with uncoated nanoparticles [46]. Authoritative reviews further outline rational design and translational considerations for membrane-coated nanoparticles, including quality control and manufacturability [47, 48]. In addition, coating integrity is not a cosmetic attribute: a key study demonstrated that coating integrity strongly influences internalization mechanisms and biological behavior, directly affecting reproducibility and efficacy [49]. In this framework, RCCM@NPs offer practical advantages for circRNA targeting-homologous tumor targeting, favorable biocompatibility, and modular nucleic acid loading-providing a feasible route to silence circLAMA5 in vivo and resensitize tumors to sunitinib. At the core level, PEI is a well-established nucleic acid condensing material with classic evidence supporting its utility for gene/oligonucleotide delivery [36], and robust methods for PEI-mediated transient transfection are widely used [50]. Nevertheless, broader nanomedicine translation experience highlights the importance of long-term safety, batch reproducibility, and scalable manufacturing as key barriers that must be addressed during preclinical development [51, 52].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although circLAMA5 was consistently upregulated and functionally validated in sunitinib-resistant cell lines and CDX-R models, further validation in larger clinical cohorts, especially paired pretreatment and post-resistance ccRCC specimens, will be necessary to establish its clinical relevance and predictive value. Second, although our data support a model in which circLAMA5 impairs Sam68 nuclear accumulation by interfering with TNPO1-associated nuclear import, the precise manner by which circLAMA5 binding to the Sam68 C-terminal region affects the accessibility or recognition of the NLS-containing region by TNPO1 remains to be fully resolved. In addition, although our mechanistic data indicate that circLAMA5 regulates Sam68 mainly by reducing its nuclear availability rather than altering total Sam68 abundance, our functional validation relied primarily on Sam68 knockdown or overexpression. Future approaches that selectively manipulate nuclear Sam68 without changing total Sam68 expression would further strengthen the causal link between Sam68 nuclear depletion and sunitinib resistance. Third, while our data identify Bcl-x alternative splicing as a key downstream event, Sam68 is a broad splicing regulator, and additional Sam68-dependent splicing targets may also contribute to sunitinib resistance. Transcriptome-wide splicing analysis may reveal additional resistance-relevant splicing programs that cooperate with apoptosis escape [17–19]. Finally, although RCCM@NPs showed encouraging tumor targeting, biocompatibility, and therapeutic efficacy in preclinical models, their long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, batch reproducibility, and efficacy in orthotopic or patient-derived models remain to be systematically evaluated before clinical translation [51, 52].
