Unravelling the Downhill Spiral: A Tale of Alcohol, Varices, and a Hidden Stenosis
Authors: Edris Adel, Urwah Ahmad, Ramya Vasireddy, Jasmine Barrow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Downhill esophageal varices (EVs) were first described in 1964 by Felson and Lessure. They are rare with an incidence rate of 0.5 % in the general population. They usually develop due to an increase in pressure and/or obstruction of the superior vena cava (SVC) and rarely present with hematemesis. SVC syndrome, surgical ligation of the SVC, Castleman syndrome, pulmonary hypertension, and hypercontractile esophageal motility disorders can cause this. We report a case of downhill EVs in an older male with alcohol use disorder with incidental finding of SVC stenosis secondary to an old, unused chemotherapy port.

Introduction

EVs are commonly associated with liver disease and portal hypertension.1 EVs can be further classified based on the direction of venous flow into “uphill”, as well as “downhill” EVs.2 Compared to uphill EVs, which account for ~50 % of EVs and are mainly caused by portal venous hypertension, downhill varices account for only 0.4–11 % of esophageal varices, and less than 0.1 % of these patients present with hematemesis.2 The pathophysiology of downhill varices involves an increase in pressure, or obstruction of the SVC in the upper third of the esophagus.3

Several causes that lead to SVC syndrome, including thrombosis of the superior vena cava, fibrosing mediastinitis, dialysis catheter use, chemotherapy port implantation, tumours of the upper mediastinum, pulmonary hypertension, Castleman syndrome and hypercontractile esophageal motility disorders can lead to downhill varices. 3 Herein, we discuss a case of downhill varices uncovered in a 71-year-old patient with alcohol use disorder and incidental finding of SVC stenosis secondary to an unused in situ chemotherapy port.

Objective

There has been no previous association between chronic alcoholism and downhill EVs, which posed a diagnostic challenge. Additionally, due to its rarity, there are no current guidelines when it comes to its acute and long-term management. This paper will highlight the importance of recognizing pathognomonic signs of downhill EVs, the acute and long-term treatment of choice and possible association with alcohol intake.

Case report

A 71-year-old man with alcohol and tobacco use disorders and history of colon cancer status post partial colectomy with ostomy presented to the hospital with concern for hematemesis following heavy alcohol intake. In the emergency department, patient was afebrile and normotensive. Laboratory diagnostics revealed an elevated lactate and initial hemoglobin of 9 mg/dl, which later down trended to 7.7 mg/dl. Comprehensive metabolic panel and a right upper quadrant ultrasound were unrevealing and did not show any signs of cirrhosis or liver masses.

An esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed grade III large (>5 mm) downhill EVs, for which seven bands were applied (Fig. 1).

A biopsy showed mild chronic inflammation without H. pylori infection. This EGD finding prompted further imaging with computed tomography scan of the chest which revealed a left subclavian Port-a-Cath with its tip in the SVC, along with focal narrowing of the SVC and multiple chest wall collaterals. Patient’s chemotherapy Port-a-Cath had been in place for over 15 years and was no longer in use. Consequently, interventional radiology performed Port-a-Cath removal, and an SVC venogram with balloon dilation of the SVC was performed. Patient’s varices were attributed to SVC stenosis from the long-standing chemo port (Fig. 2). He was subsequently discharged home on daily proton pump inhibitor therapy.

However, the patient returned three months later with concern for ongoing hematemesis and melena. He was hemodynamically stable, and hemoglobin was 10 mg/dl with elevated blood urea nitrogen of 46 and positive fecal occult blood test. Computed tomography angiography of the abdomen and pelvis revealed active bleeding in the distal esophagus extending into the gastric lumen. Patient was started on intravenous pantoprazole and octreotide. The patient underwent an EGD that showed grade I downhill EVs and new portal hypertensive gastropathy. A biopsy was taken, but no banding was provided. Later that day, the patient had worsening hematemesis and became hypotensive with hemoglobin dropping to 5.7 mg/dl and lactic acid rising to 6.4 mmol/l. He received a liter bolus of lactated ringer solution, 3 units of packed RBCs, and was started on vasopressors. A repeat EGD revealed low-grade downhill EVs. One band was applied with incomplete eradication.

Given the known severe SVC stenosis and the presence of prominent chest wall collaterals, interventional radiology performed an SVC venogram followed by stent placement and venoplasty. Patient had complete resolution of hematemesis and was discharged on aspirin 81 mg for three months post venoplasty to prevent thrombosis of the stent, which could cause recurrence of the downhill EVs. Although aspirin carries a risk of exacerbating variceal bleeding, it was considered that addressing the underlying cause of the esophageal varices, namely, the superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction through venoplasty and stenting would reduce this risk. The use of aspirin was intended to prevent restenosis of the SVC and, consequently, recurrence of the varices. In this context, we determined that the potential benefits of aspirin outweighed the associated risks.

Discussion

Downhill EVs is an uncommon cause of gastrointestinal bleeding accounting for 0.1 % of all variceal bleeding.4 While SVC obstruction has been predominantly reported with malignancy in 60 % of cases, it only accounts for 14 % of bleeding downhill EVs.1 Instead, bleeding downhill EVs has been predominantly caused by benign processes causing SVC obstruction, such as in our patient who had SVC stenosis due to a left subclavian Port-a-Cath that was unused for 15 years.

Downhill EVs can be further categorized into type I–III depending on the degree of venous strand length and esophageal lumen burden.3 The location of SVC obstruction in relation to the azygous vein directly dictates the esophageal lumen burden.1 If the obstruction is above the azygos vein, varices tend to form in the upper third of the esophagus (Fig. 3). Conversely, if the obstruction involves the azygous vein system, then the entire esophageal tract can be affected.5 During our patient’s first presentation, the endoscopic findings consisted of four columns of grade III and large (>5) esophageal varices present throughout the entire esophageal tract, suggestive of the obstruction being at or below the level of the azygous vein.

Chronic alcohol use can lead to steatohepatitis, and eventually liver cirrhosis complicated by portal hypertension. The challenge of this case was that our patient had a history of chronic alcohol use and initially presented after an episode of binge drinking. However, the presence of downhill, rather than uphill EVs prompted further evaluation with computed tomography of the chest with contrast, which showed focal severe stenosis along the SVC adjacent to the site of the central venous catheter. Additionally, no features of cirrhosis were found on abdominal imaging which showed normal morphology and contour of the liver with normal echogenicity. Laboratory diagnostics were unremarkable with no signs of hypocoagulability, and patient did not exhibit clinical signs of cirrhosis including jaundice, ascites or hypertension. As a result, the mild portal hypertensive gastropathy found during the second presentation was likely secondary to SVC stenosis as well. However, this needs to be explored further to determine if there is any long-term association between downhill EVs and portal hypertension.

Based on the differences in pathophysiology of bleeding uphill and downhill EVs, the use of octreotide and proton pump inhibitors, which remain mainstay in treatment of portal hypertension induced bleeding uphill EVs would have little to no benefit in downhill EVs. The splanchnic circulation is unaffected in SVC stenosis as there is no interaction with the portal system.1 Proton pump inhibitors can be used in certain situations, such as presence of gastric erosions, and mild portal hypertensive gastropathy such as in our patient. It would provide no medical benefit in isolated downhill EV bleeds with normal gastric surfaces as proximal esophageal varices would not be exposed to gastric acid content.6

The mainstay of treatment to halt bleeding in downhill EVs in the acute setting includes esophageal variceal band ligation, but ultimately treatment should be aimed at the underlying cause of SVC obstruction to prevent bleeding recurrence.1 Treatment options for SVC obstruction can include thrombolysis, thrombectomy, chemoradiation therapy as well as venoplasty and stenting. In our case, removal of the Port-a-Cath along with serial balloon dilatation of the SVC was insufficient as the patient was re admitted with hematemesis and had to undergo definitive SVC venoplasty with stent placement for complete resolution.

Downhill EVs present a diagnostic challenge, and their presence should prompt immediate work up with imaging of the chest to determine underlying SVC pathology. A multi modal coordinated approach is essential for management. Currently, there are no guidelines for management and most treatments are based on case reports.9

Our case highlights that when it comes to treatment options, in the acute setting, variceal band ligation should be prioritized to halt the bleeding, followed by treatment of the SVC obstruction. In the case of SVC thrombosis, thrombolysis, venoplasty and stenting has been reported to produce favourable outcomes.9 If obstruction is caused by a tumor, the use of steroids, chemo-and radiation therapy, as well as tumor resection, have shown to relieve SVC obstruction.9 Additionally, the presence of mild portal hypertensive gastropathy in the absence of any liver cirrhosis and unremarkable liver enzymes raises the question whether there is any association between SVC stenosis of any cause and potential development of portal hypertension. Further investigation is required on whether portal hypertensive gastropathy could be an early sequela of cirrhosis.

In summary, there has been no previous association between chronic alcoholism and downhill EVs, which posed a diagnostic challenge. Additionally, due to its rarity, there are no current guidelines when it comes to its acute and long-term management. This paper highlights the importance of recognizing pathognomonic signs of downhill EVs, the acute and long-term treatment of choice and possible association with alcohol intake.
