LY86-AS1 predicts poor prognosis and inhibits progression in non-small cell lung cancer by targeting the miR-132-3p/RB1CC1 axis
Authors: Yang Liu, Jian Pan, Yuejie Dai, Shanwu Man, Jun Li, Yin Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancer cases. Despite significant advances in surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted and immunotherapy, the overall five-year survival of NSCLC patients is still dismally low. The objective of this investigation was to evaluate the potential of LY86-AS1 as a prognostic biomarker in NSCLC and to elucidate its associated molecular regulatory network.MethodsExpression levels of LY86-AS1, miR-132-3p, and RB1CC1 were detected in NSCLC tissues and cell lines using RT-qPCR. Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for roughly 85% of the total incidence of lung cancer [1]. Despite considerable advances in therapeutic strategies, which include targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the general prognosis of patients diagnosed with NSCLC is still not good due to late diagnosis, high metastatic rates, and acquired drug resistance [2, 3]. As a result, there is an urgent, critical need in NSCLC research to identify novel, reliable molecular biomarkers for early detection, accurate prognosis prediction, and development of new therapeutic targets.

Currently, 18 F-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18 F-FDG PET/CT) is a recommended clinical tool for lymph node metastasis (LNM) detection, and its derived metabolic parameters such as maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and mean standardized uptake value (SUVmean) have been confirmed to be effective in predicting preoperative LNM in resectable NSCLC [4]. Many natural compounds exert anti-tumor effects by targeting classic oncogenic signaling pathways in NSCLC. For instance, allicin, a natural active component from garlic, has been proven to inhibit NSCLC cell proliferation and migration by targeting the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway, a core oncogenic pathway in NSCLC [5]. In addition to natural product intervention, exploring novel molecular regulatory axes is another important direction for NSCLC targeted therapy. Long non-coding RNA (lncRNAs) exert their functions through intricate mechanisms such as chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional processing, and sponging microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby influencing fundamental cancer hallmarks like cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis [6]. Numerous lncRNAs, such as HOTAIR [7], MALAT1 [8], and XIST [9], have been extensively documented as key drivers or suppressors in NSCLC pathogenesis. The lncRNA LY86-AS1 has recently emerged as a potential player in human diseases. Indeed, LY86-AS1 proved discriminative for diagnosing between plasma cell leukemia and multiple myeloma [10]. Besides, downregulation of LY86-AS1 has been associated with T2DM due to its potential role as a diagnostic marker [11]. Tao Yi et al. through bioinformatics analysis of LUAD datasets, found that LY86-AS1 is downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common pathological subtype of NSCLC, and predicted that this downregulation may affect the clinical outcomes of LUAD patients [12]. In line with this, Sheng Yu et al. identified LY86-AS1 as a cuproptosis-related lncRNA and proposed it as a potential prognostic factor for LUAD patients via bioinformatic screening [13]. While these bioinformatic studies provided preliminary clues for the role of LY86-AS1 in lung cancer, this study further verifies and expands these findings by using clinical NSCLC tissue samples and in vitro cell experiments, and the expression pattern and regulatory mechanisms of LY86-AS1 have not been well established in the entire NSCLC population encompassing all pathological subtypes.

The oncogenic potential of miR-132 is manifested through its promotion of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [14]. Exosomal miR-132-3p from lung cancer activates normal human lung fibroblasts, contributing to the pathogenesis of interstitial lung disease [15]. Overexpression of miR-132-3p was indeed found in NSCLC [16, 17]. On another front, RB1CC1 plays a tumor-suppressive role in prostate cancer [18] and is frequently mutated in breast cancer, acting as a prototypical tumor suppressor [19]. In lung cancer, the nuclear localization of RB1CC1 is linked to lipid peroxidation, and its associated signaling enhances tumor cell sensitivity to ferroptosis, implying that RB1CC1-targeted therapies may be advantageous [20]. Despite these individual findings, the relationship between LY86-AS1, miR-132-3p, and RB1CC1 and their regulatory function within NSCLC requires further investigation.

The objective of this investigation was to evaluate the potential of LY86-AS1 as a prognostic biomarker in NSCLC and to elucidate its associated molecular regulatory network, which may contribute to new theoretical insights for refining treatment strategies against NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University Affiliated Central Hospital, and all methodological procedures were executed in conformity with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was secured from each participant before any study-related procedures began.

This study included 128 patients with NSCLC. Tumor tissue and adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples were collected from the patients, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for future use. All patients were followed up for 5 years, and their survival status was recorded.

Cell culture and transfection

The NSCLC cell lines HCC827, A549, NCI-H1299, CALU-3, and the Human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B were obtained from ATCC. Each cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, USA). The cell cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

miR-132-3p mimic, inhibitor, and associated negative controls were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). The pcDNA3.1 vectors and empty pcDNA3.1 (Invitrogen, USA) were used to construct the overexpression vectors of LY86-AS1 and RB1CC1 (oe-LY86-AS1, oe-RB1CC1), and the negative control (oe-NC). Cell transfection was carried out with the Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) on cells in the logarithmic growth phase, following the supplier’s protocol.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells and tissue specimens with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using either the TaqMan miRNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was then carried out with the SYBR PrimeScript RT-PCR kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) on an ABI 7500 PCR System. GAPDH served as the endogenous control for LY86-AS1 and RB1CC1, whereas U6 was used for miR-132-3p. Data analysis was conducted utilizing the 2−ΔΔCt method. The specific primer sequences for target genes and internal references are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The PCR amplification conditions were optimized for each primer, and the specificity and amplification efficiency of primers were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and standard curve analysis, respectively. All primers showed a single specific band on electrophoresis, with amplification efficiency ranging from 90% to 110%, meeting the requirements of qPCR experiments.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; DOJINDO), following the supplier’s protocol. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 3 × 10³ cells per well, with 3 technical replicates set for each experimental group in a single experiment. Following incubation periods of 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 was introduced into each well. Absorbance measurements were then taken at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). All CCK-8 assays were independently repeated three times to ensure the reproducibility of the experimental results.

Transwell assay

For the cell invasion assay, cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into Transwell chambers pre-coated with Matrigel. Each upper chamber received 5 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL suspension, whereas the lower well was filled with 500 µL of complete medium. After incubation, non-invasive cells on the upper side of the membrane were carefully removed with cotton swabs. The invaded cells that translocated to the underside of the membrane were then fixed, stained with crystal violet, and examined microscopically. Five random visual fields were selected from each Transwell chamber, and the number of transmembrane cells was counted under a light microscope; the average number of cells in the five fields was used as the quantitative indicator of cell invasion ability. For the migration assay, the same protocol was followed, except that the chambers were not coated with Matrigel.

Dual-luciferase assay

The luciferase reporter plasmids containing wild-type (wt-LY86-AS1/RB1CC1) binding sequences (the binding sequences of LY86-AS1 and miR-132-3p were predicted by lncRNASNP2 database, and those of RB1CC1 and miR-132-3p were predicted by starBase database) or mutant (mut-LY86-AS1/RB1CC1) binding sequences (mutation sites were designed based on the above predicted binding sequences and constructed via site-directed mutagenesis technology) in LY86-AS1 or RB1CC1 were synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. and subsequently cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Promega Corporation). These plasmids were then co-transfected into NSCLC cells cultured in 24-well plates along with miR-132-3p mimic, mimic NC, miR-132-3p inhibitor, or inhibitor NC, assisted by Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Luciferase activity was quantified 48 h after transfection using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega Corporation, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted with SPSS 26.0 or GraphPad Prism 9.0. Continuous variables were compared between two groups using the t-test, while comparisons across multiple groups were performed via one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Categorical variables were assessed with the chi-square test. Overall survival was illustrated using Kaplan-Meier curves, and group differences in survival were evaluated with the Log-rank test. Independent prognostic factors were identified by multivariate Cox regression analysis. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The expression of LY86-AS1 in NSCLC patients and its relationship with prognosis

LY86-AS1 was significantly downregulated in NSCLC tumor tissues and all tested NSCLC cell lines (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Patients with low LY86-AS1 expression (stratified by median value) had poorer 5-year overall survival (Log rank P = 0.023, Fig. 1B). Low expression levels of LY86-AS1 were correlated strongly with larger tumor size (P = 0.048), LNM (P = 0.019), and higher TNM stage (P = 0.017) (Table 1). LY86-AS1 (HR = 0.370, 95% CI = 0.159–0.857, P = 0.020) was identified as an independent prognostic factor for NSCLC, also including LNM (HR = 2.149, 95% CI = 1.043–4.427, P = 0.038) and TNM stage (HR = 3.285, 95% CI = 1.112–9.705, P = 0.031), based on multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2). Notably, a prominent decrease in LY86-AS1 expression was detected across NSCLC cell lines (P < 0.001, Fig. 1C), particularly in NCI-H1299 and A549, which were consequently selected for subsequent experiments.

Overexpression of LY86-AS1 inhibited the function of NSCLC cells

We upregulated LY86-AS1 expression in NCI-H1299 and A549 cells through transfection with an oe-LY86-AS1 construct. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed efficient transfection, showing significantly increased LY86-AS1 levels in treated cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 2A and B). Functional assays revealed that enhanced LY86-AS1 expression suppressed cell proliferation (P < 0.001, Fig. 2C and D) and impaired both invasion and migration abilities (P < 0.001, Fig. 2E-H).

miR‑132‑3p was directly targeted by LY86-AS1

Bioinformatic analysis using the lncRNASNP2 database predicted potential binding sites between LY86-AS1 and miR-132-3p (Fig. 3A), a prediction subsequently confirmed by dual-luciferase reporter assays. In both NCI-H1299 and A549 cells, miR-132-3p mimic reduced wt-LY86-AS1 luciferase activity (P < 0.01), while its inhibitor increased the activity (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B and C). miR-132-3p was upregulated in NSCLC tissues and cell lines (P < 0.001, Fig. 3D), exhibiting a significant inverse correlation with LY86-AS1 expression levels (r=-0.783, P < 0.001, Fig. 3E). Consistent with this, elevated miR-132-3p levels were also observed in cells compared to normal controls (P < 0.01, Fig. 3F). LY86-AS1 overexpression significantly downregulated miR-132-3p (P < 0.001), and this effect was reversed by miR-132-3p mimic (P < 0.01, Fig. 3G and H).

RB1CC1 was one of the target genes of miR-132-3p

Potential interaction sites between RB1CC1 and miR-132-3p were forecasted using the starBase database (Fig. 4A). According to dual-luciferase reporter assays, luciferase activity associated with wt-RB1CC1 was significantly repressed by miR-132-3p mimics in NCI-H1299 and A549 cells (P < 0.01). Transfections with miR-132-3p inhibitors robustly potentiated this activity (P < 0.001, Fig. 4B and C). RB1CC1 was downregulated in NSCLC tissues and cell lines (P < 0.001, Fig. 4D), which correlated negatively with miR-132-3p expression (r=-0.751, P < 0.001, Fig. 4E), and its expression was negatively correlated with miR-132-3p in NSCLC tissues (P < 0.01, Fig. 4F).

miR-132-3p reversed the tumor suppressor effect of LY86-AS1 by targeting RB1CC1

miR-132-3p mimic significantly downregulated RB1CC1 expression in NSCLC cells (P < 0.001). This effect was reversed upon introduction of an RB1CC1 overexpression construct (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A and B). Notably, miR-132-3p overexpression abrogated the LY86-AS1-mediated inhibition of cell proliferation (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C and D), migration (P < 0.001, Fig. 5E and F), and invasion (P < 0.001, Fig. 5G and H). In contrast, enforced expression of RB1CC1 considerably impaired these oncogenic cellular capabilities (P < 0.01, Fig. 5C-H).

Discussion

Despite significant advances in the field of oncology, the outcome for patients suffering from NSCLC is quite dismal [21]. We found LY86-AS1 is downregulated in patients with NSCLC, and its low expression levels may be associated with poor outcomes in patients. Mechanistically, LY86-AS1 exerts its tumor-suppressive roles by sponging miR-132-3p to upregulate RB1CC1 expression, thereby inhibiting function of NSCLC cells.

Some lncRNAs have been related to NSCLC progression. For instance, EGFN1-AS1 is upregulated in NSCLC, closely related to overall survival of patients [22]. AFAP1-AS1 is upregulated in NSCLC tissue and may be a prognostic factor for NSCLC [23]. On the contrary, LINC8087 is downregulated in NSCLC. The low expression of this lncRNA is significantly related to lower survival [24]. Previous studies have focused on the expression and clinical significance of LY86-AS1 in LUAD, a major subtype of NSCLC. Tao Yi et al. reported that LY86-AS1 is significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues, and its low expression is associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes of patients; Sheng Yu et al. also identified LY86-AS1 as a potential cuproptosis-related prognostic lncRNA in LUAD, suggesting its predictive value for patient prognosis and immunotherapy response [12, 13]. In line with these LUAD-focused studies, our research further confirmed that LY86-AS1 is underexpressed in the overall NSCLC population (including both LUAD and squamous cell carcinoma), and low LY86-AS1 expression is an independent poor prognostic factor correlated with LNM and advanced TNM stage. This finding extends the clinical significance of LY86-AS1 from a single LUAD subtype to the entire NSCLC cohort, verifying the universal prognostic value of LY86-AS1 in NSCLC and providing a more comprehensive theoretical basis for its application as a pan-NSCLC prognostic biomarker.

The previous LUAD research was based on bioinformatics analysis without in vitro functional experiments to verify the biological role of LY86-AS1; while our study included both LUAD and squamous cell carcinoma samples, but did not conduct subgroup analysis to explore the potential differences in the expression level, prognostic value and regulatory network of LY86-AS1 between different NSCLC subtypes. This may be due to the limited sample size of our study, and future research with a larger sample size is needed to carry out subtype-specific analysis, which will help to further refine the clinical application of LY86-AS1 in different NSCLC subtypes.

Strongly negatively correlating with key drivers of cancer progression, such as LNM and advanced TNM stage, and being identified as an independent factor against poor prognosis, LY86-AS1 could have a regulatory function in suppressing tumor progression. Thus, in our cellular experiments, we demonstrate that LY86-AS1 overexpression inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities in NSCLC cells, findings that further support that LY86-AS1 may impart its tumor-suppressive function by inhibiting these cellular activities.

Another of the key points in our research was to clarify the endogenous competing RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network, which included LY86-AS1 and miR-132-3p. The ceRNA hypothesis, one of the most important regulatory mechanisms in cancer biology, indicates that lncRNAs behave as molecular sponges for miRNAs and could also take part in NSCLC regulation [25]. This kind of regulation has indeed been reported before in other NSCLC studies. For instance, miR-16-5p inhibitor was able to reverse the effect of XIST knockdown on the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells [26]. In our study, miR-132-3p was upregulated in NSCLC in this study, which is consistent with previous studies [27]. In addition, LY86-AS1 overexpression decreased miR-132-3p levels, and a miR-132-3p mimic reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of LY86-AS1. Thus, the tumor-suppressive effects of LY86-AS1 can be largely explained by inhibiting this oncogenic miRNA and derepressing its downstream tumor-suppressive targets.

Notably, the LY86-AS1/miR-132-3p/RB1CC1 ceRNA axis identified in our study exhibits both conserved characteristics and unique regulatory features compared with the well-documented lncRNA-ceRNA axes in NSCLC, such as MALAT1- and XIST-related axes. XIST has been reported to act as a ceRNA to sponge various miRNAs (e.g., miR-16-5p, miR-137) in NSCLC, thereby regulating target genes to modulate cell proliferation, EMT and radio/chemoresistance [26, 28]; MALAT1, as an oncogenic lncRNA, forms ceRNA axes with miR-197-3p, miR-1914-3p and other miRNAs to promote NSCLC cell metastasis and reduce chemosensitivity [29, 30]. Similar to these classic axes, our study further verifies the universality of the ceRNA regulatory mechanism in NSCLC, in which lncRNAs exert biological functions by sponging specific miRNAs to regulate downstream target gene expression. However, distinct from the oncogenic roles of MALAT1 and XIST in most NSCLC-related ceRNA axes, LY86-AS1 acts as a tumor-suppressive lncRNA in the ceRNA network, which enriches the diversity of lncRNA functions in NSCLC ceRNA regulation.

To confirm our hypothesis, we identified RB1CC1 as one of the important target genes of miR-132-3p. Being a tumor suppressor, RB1CC1 is involved in the modulation of autophagy and several signaling pathways [31]. Its expression in NSCLC tumor tissues was found to be downregulated and inversely correlated with the level of miR-132-3p. The antitumor effect of LY86-AS1 overexpression was reversed by cotransfection with a miR-132-3p mimic, whose action was counteracted by RB1CC1. This result suggested that LY86-AS1 exerts its tumor-suppressive effect by downregulating miR-132-3p and subsequently elevating the expression level of its target gene, RB1CC1.

However, notable differences exist between our study and the existing LUAD research in the regulatory network of LY86-AS1. Previous LUAD studies only identified LY86-AS1 as a potential prognostic lncRNA through bioinformatics analysis of public databases, but did not explore its specific molecular regulatory mechanism and downstream target molecules involved in tumor progression. In contrast, our study is the first to elucidate the functional regulatory axis of LY86-AS1 in NSCLC: LY86-AS1 acts as a ceRNA to sponge miR-132-3p, thereby upregulating the expression of the tumor suppressor gene RB1CC1 and further inhibiting the proliferation, migration and invasion of NSCLC cells. This finding fills the gap in the research on the molecular mechanism of LY86-AS1 in NSCLC, and clarifies the specific downstream regulatory pathway of LY86-AS1 in tumorigenesis and development, which is the core innovation of our study compared with the existing LUAD-related research.

The mechanism of lncRNAs in NSCLC is highly complicated, and they are very diverse, involving many signaling pathways and various biological processes. For instance, XIST inhibits proliferation and EMT in NSCLC through the Notch-1 pathway [28]. The SOX2-OT/miR-30d-5p/PDK1 axis regulates the PD-L1 checkpoint through the mTOR signaling pathway, thus promoting NSCLC progression and immune evasion [32]. MIR503HG suppresses NSCLC cell metastasis in an NF-κB/NLRP3-dependent manner [27]. Although the functional mechanisms of such lncRNAs are relatively well described above, the specific regulatory network of LY86-AS1 in NSCLC is still unclear. To date, some preliminary data have shown that LY86-AS1 might have a tumor-suppressive role by sponging miR-132-3p via the ceRNA mechanism and then modulating RB1CC1 expression. However, it will be validated in further functional assays and animal models whether it participates in critical processes such as EMT, chemoresistance, metabolic reprogramming, or immune escape.

Malignant progression of NSCLC is driven by complex and interwoven molecular regulatory networks, and different types of regulatory pathways form positive feedback or ceRNA axes to modulate tumor cell biological behaviors. The FOXM1/NSUN2 axis forms a typical transcription-epigenetic positive feedback loop to accelerate NSCLC development by regulating ferroptosis and tumor microenvironment [33], while our study revealed a novel lncRNA-mediated ceRNA axis (LY86-AS1/miR-132-3p/RB1CC1) that exerts tumor-suppressive effects by upregulating the ferroptosis-sensitizing gene RB1CC1. These findings suggest that NSCLC harbors diverse molecular regulatory patterns, and targeting different types of key regulatory axes (including positive feedback loops and ceRNA axes) may provide multiple potential directions for clinical targeted therapy of NSCLC.

This study has several limitations that warrant further discussion. First, the sample size of NSCLC patients enrolled in this study is relatively small (n = 128) and derived from a single center, which may affect the extrapolation and external validity of the results, and with no stratified analysis conducted for LUAD and squamous cell carcinoma, the two main pathological subtypes of NSCLC. This deficiency not only reduces the statistical power of the correlation analysis between LY86-AS1 expression and clinicopathological features but also fails to clarify the subtype-specific expression patterns and regulatory characteristics of the LY86-AS1/miR-132-3p/RB1CC1 axis, thus limiting the generalizability of our findings to the entire NSCLC population. Additionally, our research lacks in vivo animal models (e.g., tumor xenograft models in nude mice) to validate the tumor-suppressive function and therapeutic potential of the LY86-AS1/miR-132-3p/RB1CC1 axis in the intact tumor microenvironment; the current conclusions are solely based on in vitro cell experiments and clinical tissue correlation analyses, lacking direct in vivo evidence to support the translational application of this axis as a therapeutic target. Moreover, our mechanistic research is relatively superficial, with no in-depth exploration of the downstream signaling pathways of RB1CC1 and its crosstalk with other tumor-related biological processes (e.g., ferroptosis, autophagy, epithelial-mesenchymal transition) in NSCLC. Future research should expand the multi-center clinical sample size and perform subtype-stratified analysis to improve the external validity of the conclusions; construct LY86-AS1 overexpression mouse xenograft models to verify the in vivo regulatory effect and therapeutic efficacy of the LY86-AS1/miR-132-3p/RB1CC1 axis; and conduct multi-layered molecular mechanism studies to explore the downstream regulatory network of RB1CC1, and further exploring the association between LY86-AS1 expression and the efficacy of clinical chemotherapy and immunotherapy in NSCLC patients.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LY86-AS1 is significantly downregulated in NSCLC patients, and LY86-AS1 upregulates RB1CC1 expression by sponging miR-132-3p, thereby inhibiting the malignant progression of NSCLC. This lncRNA may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for NSCLC.
