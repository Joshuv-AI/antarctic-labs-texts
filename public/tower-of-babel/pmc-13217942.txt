PHTLS training increases knowledge but paradoxically decreases self-efficacy in emergency healthcare professionals: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Pınar Çevikbaş, Kurtuluş Açıksarı, Cihad Ünsal Karahaliloğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrauma remains a leading cause of death globally, with prehospital care quality critically influencing outcomes. While the Prehospital Trauma Life Support (PHTLS) course improves trauma management knowledge among emergency healthcare professionals, the relationship between knowledge acquisition and self-efficacy remains underexplored, though clinical confidence is as important as theoretical knowledge for effective trauma care.This study evaluated PHTLS course effectiveness on trauma management knowledge and self-efficacy of emergency healthcare professionals, investigating whether PHTLS training, while improving objective knowledge, is associated with decreased self-perceived competence, in a pattern compatible with a Dunning-Kruger-like effect whereby training reveals the complexity of trauma care.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at a trauma center in Istanbul, Turkey, in 2022, including 98 healthcare professionals (physicians, paramedics, nurses, emergency medicine technicians [EMT]) divided into PHTLS-trained (n = 48) and non-trained control (n = 50) groups. Data were collected using a sociodemographic questionnaire and a 50-item multiple-choice knowledge test from the PHTLS curriculum (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82). Self-perceived competence (as a proxy for self-efficacy) was assessed with a singledirect yes/no question. Statistical analyses included Mann-Whitney U, chi-square tests, and logistic regression. Subgroup analyses by profession and effect size (Cohen’s d) were calculated.ResultsParadoxically, self-perceived competence was significantly lower in the trained group. The PHTLS-trained group demonstrated significantly higher mean knowledge scores than the non-trained group (70.46 ± 14.68 vs. 55.28 ± 11.44; p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.15). Consequently, 43.8% of the trained group met the PHTLS certification threshold of ≥ 76 points, compared to only 4% of the non-trained group (p < 0.001). Paradoxically, self-perceived competence (self-efficacy proxy) was significantly lower in the trained group (25%) than the non-trained group (62%; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed physicians in the non-trained group had disproportionately high self-efficacy despite lower knowledge scores compared to trained paramedics. Logistic regression confirmed PHTLS training as the strongest independent predictor of passing (OR: 3.2, 95% CI: 1.8–5.6, p < 0.001).ConclusionsPHTLS training effectively increases theoretical trauma management knowledge but is paradoxically associated with lower self-perceived competence, a pattern consistent with increased awareness of limitations and the theory-practice gap following training. This suggests theoretical training alone is insufficient. Standardized training programs must be combined with high-fidelity, simulation-based practical skills training to build both competence and confidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09149-z.Keywords: Emergency medical services, Emergency medicine, Health personnel, Prehospital trauma life support, Self-efficacy, Dunning-Kruger effect, Education, Competency-based education, Clinical competence, Wounds and injuries

Introduction

Trauma is a global public health crisis, representing a leading cause of death and disability, particularly among the young and economically productive population [1, 2]. In Turkey, trauma constitutes a significant burden, accounting for 3.3% of all deaths in 2022 and ranking as the primary cause of mortality for individuals aged 14–45 [3]. The majority of preventable trauma-related deaths occur in the prehospital setting, making the quality of initial care a key determinant of patient outcomes [2, 4]. The “golden hour” - the first 60 min following injury - is a foundational concept in trauma care, emphasizing that timely and effective interventions during this window maximize the chances of survival [4, 5]. Consequently, enhancing the competence of prehospital and emergency healthcare professionals is central to any effective trauma system.

Standardized training programs are essential for equipping these professionals with the necessary skills. The Prehospital Trauma Life Support (PHTLS) course, developed by the National Association of Emergency Medical Technicians (NAEMT) in cooperation with the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma, is the global gold standard for prehospital trauma education [6]. Based on the principles of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS), PHTLS provides a systematic framework for assessing and managing critically injured patients, with studies demonstrating its positive impact on knowledge, skill performance, and patient outcomes [7–11].

However, the main goal of medical education is not just to impart knowledge, but to cultivate competence, which is a complex interplay of knowledge, skills, and the confidence to apply them effectively under pressure. This confidence is closely related to the concept of self-efficacy, defined by Bandura as an individual’s belief in their own capacity to execute the behaviors necessary to produce specific performance attainments [12]. In medicine, higher self-efficacy is linked to better clinical performance, greater persistence in challenging situations, and reduced burnout [13, 14]. It is, therefore, an important outcome for any training program. Given our pragmatic, single-item measure, in this study the outcome is referred to as self-perceived competence (a proxy for self-efficacy) to avoid overstating measurement precision and to remain conceptually aligned with distinctions between perceived competence and self-efficacy [15].

Intriguingly, the relationship between knowledge and self-perception is not always linear. The Dunning-Kruger effect, a well-documented cognitive bias, posits that individuals with low ability at a task tend to overestimate their ability [16]. As they gain more expertise, they often become more aware of the complexities and nuances they previously overlooked, leading to a temporary, and more realistic, dip in confidence. This phenomenon, where a little knowledge can be a dangerous (or at least, overconfident) thing, has been observed across various domains, including medical education [17, 18]. It raises an important question: Does standardized training like PHTLS, by revealing the true scope and difficulty of trauma care, paradoxically decrease self-efficacy even as it increases knowledge?

While the effectiveness of PHTLS in improving knowledge is established, its impact on the self-efficacy of emergency professionals remains underexplored, particularly the potential for a paradoxical decrease in confidence. This study, therefore, aimed not only to confirm the knowledge benefits of PHTLS training among a cohort of Turkish emergency healthcare professionals but also to investigate its concurrent impact on their self-efficacy. The study hypothesized that while PHTLS-trained individuals would demonstrate superior knowledge, they might paradoxically report lower self-efficacy, highlighting an important gap between theoretical knowledge and practical confidence that can only be bridged by hands-on, simulation-based training.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional, descriptive study was conducted between August and October 2022 at the Adult Emergency Medicine Clinic of Goztepe Prof. Dr. Süleyman Yalçın City Hospital, a high-volume tertiary trauma center in Istanbul, Turkey. The study received ethical approval from the Istanbul Medeniyet University Ethics Committee on August 17, 2022 (Decision No: 2022/0486) and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants and sample size

The study population consisted of healthcare professionals working in the emergency department, including physicians (residents and specialists), paramedics, nurses, and emergency medical technicians (EMTs). Participants were divided into two groups:

1-PHTLS-Trained Group (n = 48): Healthcare professionals who had successfully completed an official NAEMT-certified PHTLS course within the previous year.

1-PHTLS-Trained Group (n = 48): Healthcare professionals who had successfully completed an official NAEMT-certified PHTLS course within the previous year.

2-Non-Trained Group (n = 50): Healthcare professionals with similar job descriptions and experience levels who had not yet received PHTLS training but were scheduled to attend an upcoming course.

2-Non-Trained Group (n = 50): Healthcare professionals with similar job descriptions and experience levels who had not yet received PHTLS training but were scheduled to attend an upcoming course.

Inclusion criteria required active involvement in emergency or prehospital care. Exclusion criteria included incomplete survey responses or refusal to provide informed consent. Of the 100 eligible participants initially approached, 98 were included in the final analysis, with 2 excluded due to incomplete responses (Fig. 1).

The sample size was determined a priori using a power analysis. To detect a 10-point difference in mean test scores between the groups with 80% power and a 5% alpha level, a minimum of 45 participants per group was required. The study targeted 50 participants per group to account for potential attrition.

Data collection and variables

Data were collected face-to-face by two trained researchers. A standardized protocol was used to minimize bias. The data collection instrument consisted of two parts:

Sociodemographic and Contextual Questionnaire: A 10-item questionnaire collected data on age, gender, profession, years of professional experience, and monthly trauma patient caseload. It also included a single-item, dichotomous (“Yes/No”) question to assess self-efficacy: “Do you feel competent in your approach to a trauma patient?“. This questionnaire was specifically developed for the present study to capture key demographic and contextual factors relevant to trauma care, and it has not been previously published as a standalone instrument. An English-language version of the full questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. As the knowledge scores were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison between the two groups. Categorical variables, such as proficiency pass rates and self-efficacy responses, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

The effect size for the difference in knowledge scores was calculated using Cohen’s d. A logistic regression model was developed to identify independent predictors of passing the knowledge test, controlling for potential confounders such as age and years of experience. Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare knowledge and self-perceived competence outcomes between different professions (physicians vs. paramedics/nurses) within both the trained and non-trained groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Baseline imbalances in age and profession were examined through prespecified subgroup comparisons and multivariable logistic regression. Effect sizes were reported with 95% confidence intervals (Cohen’s d and bias-corrected Hedges’ g) using pooled SD.

This knowledge gap was further reflected in the pass rates. A total of 21 participants (43.8%) in the trained group achieved the official PHTLS certification score of ≥ 76, whereas only 2 participants (4.0%) in the non-trained group reached this threshold (p < 0.001).

In stark contrast to the knowledge outcomes, self-perceived competence showed an inverse relationship. Only 25% (n = 12) of the PHTLS-trained participants reported feeling competent in managing trauma patients, compared to 62% (n = 31) of the non-trained participants. This paradoxical finding was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

To quantify the magnitude of this association, the odds ratio was calculated from the 2 × 2 contingency table. Participants in the PHTLS-trained group had significantly lower odds of reporting self-perceived competence compared with their non-trained counterparts (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.086–0.487, p < 0.001). The phi coefficient for the training × self-perceived competence association was − 0.373, indicating a large effect size. These findings confirm that the inverse relationship between training status and self-perceived competence is not only statistically significant but also of substantial practical magnitude.

Among physicians, the non-trained group (primarily residents) demonstrated high self-reported competence (58.3%) despite achieving relatively low knowledge scores (56.1 ± 10.2). In contrast, trained physicians showed improved knowledge scores (68.2 ± 12.4) but lower self-confidence (28.0%).

However, trained paramedics achieved the highest knowledge scores of all groups (72.5 ± 15.8) while reporting the lowest self-confidence (17.6%). This pattern is compatible with descriptions of the Dunning-Kruger effect, in which increased competence can be accompanied by decreased confidence as individuals become more aware of the complexity of their field.

Knowledge–confidence relationship across subgroups

To further examine the directional relationship between knowledge level and self-perceived competence across professional strata, mean group knowledge scores were plotted against self-perceived competence rates for all available profession × training strata (Fig. 3). The six data points (comprising overall trained and non-trained groups and four profession-specific subgroups) revealed a strongly negative monotonic relationship, with Spearman ρ = −1.00 (p < 0.001). Regression analysis

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of passing the knowledge test. After adjusting for age, years of experience, and profession, PHTLS training remained the strongest independent predictor of success (Odds Ratio: 3.2, 95% Confidence Interval: 1.8–5.6, p < 0.001). This suggests that the knowledge gain is more likely related to the training itself than to measured demographic variables, although residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded.

Discussion

This study supports the view that the PHTLS course is highly effective in enhancing the theoretical knowledge of emergency healthcare professionals in trauma management. The trained group not only achieved significantly higher test scores, with a large effect size, but was also more than ten times as likely to meet the official certification standard. However, the study’s most significant and paradoxical finding is the inverse relationship between this increased knowledge and self-perceived competence. Professionals in the PHTLS-trained group were significantly less likely to report feeling competent than their non-trained colleagues, suggesting an association between training status and self-perceived competence. This finding is consistent with a Dunning–Kruger-like pattern, whereby initial training may move participants from a state of ‘unconscious incompetence’ to ‘conscious incompetence,’ making them more aware of the complexities and their own limitations in trauma care. It should be noted that the present study design does not permit direct measurement of metacognitive calibration; the Dunning–Kruger framework is therefore proposed here as a plausible interpretive lens rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Additionally, the superior knowledge scores in the PHTLS-trained group are consistent with a large body of literature demonstrating the educational efficacy of standardized trauma courses like PHTLS and ATLS [7, 9, 10]. These findings, with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.15), add further support to this consensus, suggesting that the curriculum successfully imparts critical theoretical knowledge.

The novel contribution of the present study lies in the exploration of the self-efficacy paradox. While some studies have reported increased confidence after trauma training [20], these results align with a more nuanced view of medical education. The Dunning-Kruger effect, where novices overestimate their abilities, provides a compelling theoretical framework for these findings [16]. The non-trained group, with less awareness of the specific PHTLS protocols and the subtleties of trauma pathophysiology, may have possessed an inflated sense of their capabilities. The PHTLS course, by presenting a comprehensive and evidence-based system of care, may have been associated with greater exposure to the vastness of the field and the paramount importance of precise, sequential interventions. This increased awareness of the gap between theoretical knowledge and the demands of real-world clinical practice may have contributed to a more calibrated self-appraisal of competence in the trained group.

The subgroup analysis provides further support for this interpretation. The highest level of overconfidence was observed in non-trained resident physicians, who reported high self-efficacy despite having knowledge scores comparable to non-trained paramedics. Conversely, trained paramedics, despite achieving the highest knowledge scores, reported the lowest self-efficacy. This pattern suggests that the training may have contributed to recalibrating the self-perception of this highly skilled but non-physician group, potentially making them more aware of their role and limitations within the broader trauma team.

The quantitative relationship between knowledge and self-perceived competence across all available profession × training subgroups further supports the observed paradox. Plotting mean knowledge scores against self-perceived competence rates across the six strata yielded a Spearman ρ of − 1.00 (p < 0.001), indicating a perfectly monotonic inverse relationship between knowledge level and reported competence across groups (Fig. 3). While this analysis is based on group-level means rather than individual-level data, a limitation that precludes causal inference; the consistency of the pattern across professional roles strengthens the ecological validity of the association. Future studies employing pre-post designs with individual-level data would allow formal mediation analyses and individual-level knowledge–confidence calibration curves.

Regarding potential confounding by demographic variables, the logistic regression model confirmed that PHTLS training remained the strongest independent predictor of knowledge test performance after adjusting for age, years of experience, and profession (OR: 3.2, 95% CI: 1.8–5.6). For self-perceived competence specifically, the strong bivariate association (OR = 0.20, phi = − 0.373) was corroborated across all profession-specific subgroups, with the inverse knowledge–confidence pattern observed consistently in both physician and paramedic strata. Although a formal multivariable logistic regression model for self-perceived competence, simultaneously adjusting for age, profession, and monthly caseload at the individual level, was not feasible with the available data structure, the consistency of the subgroup-level pattern reduces the likelihood that the observed association is attributable solely to profession or experience differences.

This gap between knowing what to do (theoretical knowledge) and feeling confident that one can do it (self-efficacy and practical skill) is the central challenge that modern medical education must address. Consistent with the measurement approach adopted in this study, confidence outcomes are interpreted as self-perceived competence (proxy for self-efficacy). Theoretical instruction and multiple-choice tests, while valuable for assessing cognitive knowledge, are insufficient for building true clinical competence and the confidence that accompanies it. The anxiety and uncertainty expressed by the trained group are not a failure of the PHTLS course but rather a clear signal of the need for the next step in their education: practical application. This is where high-fidelity simulation-based training becomes essential. Simulation allows learners to apply their theoretical knowledge in a safe, controlled, and realistic environment. It provides the opportunity to practice procedural skills, make critical decisions under pressure, and engage in effective teamwork, all of which are essential for building both skill and self-efficacy [21, 22]. The lower self-efficacy in the trained group should be interpreted as a readiness and a demand for such practical, hands-on training.

These findings converge with reports that ATLS/PHTLS-type courses reliably increase knowledge, while effects on confidence depend on the inclusion and timing of simulation-based practice. Studies using multi-item self-efficacy instruments after scenario-based simulations tend to show modest confidence gains, whereas theory-only interventions yield mixed or transient changes in perceived competence. These discrepancies likely reflect instrument specificity (self-efficacy vs. perceived competence), exposure to realistic scenario complexity, and learner seniority, which jointly shape metacognitive calibration [17]. For instance, high-fidelity simulation has been associated with greater gains in self-efficacy among medical trainees compared with theory-only exposure [23]. In prehospital trauma contexts, simulation training increased providers’ confidence in performing life-saving procedures [24]. Reviews and scoping analyses similarly report improvements in knowledge, teamwork, and non-technical skills with simulation-based team training, while calling for standardized outcome reporting [25, 26].

The observed pattern - substantial knowledge gains but only modest improvements in perceived competence following theory-focused training -- mirrors prior reports suggesting that simulation exposure is pivotal for consolidating confidence and self-efficacy [23, 24].

These findings are consistent with evidence from diverse international settings, where standardized trauma training programs such as PHTLS have been associated with improvements in prehospital care delivery and patient outcomes [22, 27–29].

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inference; this study can only conclude that PHTLS training status is associated with differences in self-perceived competence, not that training caused these differences. A longitudinal, pre-post design (measuring knowledge and self-perceived competence in the same individuals before and after training) would provide substantially stronger evidence for directionality. The non-trained group’s higher baseline confidence may reflect genuine pre-existing differences rather than a consequence of training.

A related concern involves the measurement of self-perceived competence, which relied on a single dichotomous (‘Yes/No’) item. Single-item measures do not capture the full multidimensional construct of self-efficacy as conceptualized by Bandura [12]. The outcome is therefore interpreted strictly as self-perceived competence (a pragmatic proxy) rather than self-efficacy proper. The binary format likely introduced ceiling and floor effects that compressed variance. Future investigations should employ validated multi-item instruments, such as the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) or trauma-specific tools, to obtain more nuanced assessments.

Although subgroup analyses and a logistic regression model were performed to address potential confounding by age and professional role, a formal multivariable model with self-perceived competence as the primary outcome, adjusting simultaneously for age, profession, and monthly caseload, was not feasible with the available data structure. This represents a methodological gap that warrants attention in future studies with individual-level datasets and larger, more balanced samples across professional strata.

The study was conducted at a single, high-volume tertiary trauma center in Istanbul, Turkey, which limits the generalizability of findings to other healthcare settings, staffing models, and training systems. The observed age and professional role imbalances between groups further constrain external validity, despite the adjustment analyses performed. Multicenter replication with stratified sampling across professional groups and training exposures is warranted.

The knowledge-confidence calibration plot (Fig. 3) illustrates a consistent negative monotonic relationship across profession × training subgroups (Spearman ρ = −1.00); however, this analysis is based on group-level means rather than individual-level data points, which limits the precision of the correlation estimate. A Dunning-Kruger calibration analysis in the strict methodological sense would require individual knowledge quartile assignments paired with individual self-efficacy scores, an approach that was not achievable in the present study and remains an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

PHTLS training is highly effective at increasing the theoretical knowledge of trauma management among emergency healthcare professionals. However, this acquisition of knowledge is paradoxically associated with a notable decrease in self-perceived competence. This finding highlights an important gap between theory and practice, suggesting that while theoretical training makes professionals aware of what they do not know (conscious incompetence), it is insufficient for building the confidence required for effective clinical practice. To address this gap, standard trauma education should be strengthened with regular, high-quality, simulation-based training. Such programs are necessary to translate theoretical knowledge into practical skills and to build the self-efficacy needed for optimal performance in high-stakes trauma situations.
