Eyring–Kramers law for the hyperbolic ϕ 4 \phi^{4} model
Nikolay Barashkov, Petri Laarne

Abstract
We study the expected transition frequency between the two metastable states of a stochastic wave equation with double-well potential. By transition state theory, the frequency factorizes into two components: one depends only on the invariant measure, given by the ϕ d 4 \phi^{4}_{d} quantum field theory, and the other takes the dynamics into account. We compute the first component with the variational approach to stochastic quantization when d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 . For the two-dimensional equation with random data but no stochastic forcing, we also compute the transmission coefficient. Keywords: Metastability, Eyring–Kramers law, Transition state theory, ϕ 4 \phi^{4} measure, Nonlinear wave equation, Boué–Dupuis formula MSC (2020): 60H30, 81S20 (Primary); 35L71, 81T08 (Secondary)

Introduction

Consider the
stochastic damped nonlinear wave equation

\partial_{tt}u(x,t)+\gamma\partial_{t}u(x,t)-(m^{2}+\Delta)u(x,t)=-u(x,t)^{3}+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\xi(x,t),

(SdNLW)

where
\xi
is the space-time white noise,

\beta>0
is the inverse temperature,
\gamma>0
is a damping parameter,
and
m^{2}>0
is a mass term.
The
-u^{3}+m^{2}u
terms mean that there are symmetric potential wells at
\pm m
.

Since there is stochastic forcing,
a solution
u
will stay most of the time near either constant function
\pm m
,
but will occasionally jump between the two wells.
The question is: how often will such a jump happen?

This question was originally posed in the context of chemical reaction rates
and for the Langevin dynamics,
which are the finite-dimensional analogue of (
SdNLW
).
The
Eyring–Kramers law
,
named after
[
21
,
36
]
,
states that the expected transition time
is proportional to
\exp(\beta h)
,
where
h
is the height of the potential barrier (see
Figure

1.1
).
Our concern is to understand this relation in the low-temperature regime for this particular PDE.

As
\gamma
approaches either
0
or
\infty
, (
SdNLW
) gives different equations in the limit.
In the overdamped limit
\gamma\to\infty
, one obtains after a suitable rescaling of time
the
stochastic heat equation

\partial_{t}u(x,t)-(m^{2}+\Delta)u(x,t)=-u(x,t)^{3}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\xi(x,t),

(SHE)

which is the parabolic counterpart of (
SdNLW
).
Recently this equation has been of interest in constructive quantum field theory,
as the
\phi^{4}
measure can recovered as the invariant measure of (
SHE
).
When the mass term has negative sign as in here,
this equation is also called the
stochastic Allen–Cahn equation
.
Metastable behaviour for overdamped Langevin dynamics has been extensively studied in the literature;
see Section
1.1
for an overview.

Conversely taking
\gamma\to 0
one obtains the
nonlinear wave equation

\partial_{tt}u(x,t)-(m^{2}+\Delta)u(x,t)=-u(x,t)^{3}.

(NLW)

This equation lacks both the stochastic forcing and the
\partial_{t}u
damping term.
Yet if the initial data is sampled from the invariant measure of (
SdNLW
),
the measure is again invariant under the dynamics and
one can still ask the same question about metastability.
In comparison to the overdamped case, the Langevin dynamics corresponding to (
SdNLW
)
and its underdamped limit (
NLW
)
have received little attention, especially in the PDE setting.
Our aim in this article is to make a step in that direction.

1.1
Previous literature

The Eyring–Kramers law has its roots in the work of van ’t Hoff and Arrhenius in the 1880s.
Some of the early history of the Eyring–Kramers law in chemistry and physics

Mathematical study of metastability of diffusion processes

Much of the recent progress in the field has built on

Transition state theory only depends on the invariant measure,

However, TST overcounts the number of transitions:

The flows of (

To the knowledge of the present authors,
dynamical corrections for neither model have not been explicitly derived before.

In two dimensions, the solution theory of our equations gets more challenging.
Due to the irregularity of the space-time white noise,
the solutions are almost surely distribution-valued.
It becomes necessary to renormalize the

nonlinearity by Wick ordering,
denoted
\mathinner{:\!u^{3}\!:}
.
The system is truncated to wavenumbers of magnitude at most
N

which lets us replace the nonlinearity by

\proj_{N}\mathinner{:\!u_{N}^{3}\!:}=\proj_{N}(u_{N}^{3})-C_{N}u_{N}.

The divergent renormalization constant
C_{N}
is given in
Definition

2.18
.
We defer further details to
Section

2.4
.

Berglund, Di Gesù, and Weber were able to compute the expected transition time
for truncated, renormalized (

) in two dimensions:

Theorem 1.5

(
[
10

)
.

Given
0<\delta<1
and
\varepsilon>0

\mathcal{B}_{\pm}\coloneqq\Big\{u\in H^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\colon{\left|\pm 1-\hat{u}(0)\right|}<\delta,\left\|\proj_{\perp}u\right\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\lesssim\beta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)\Big\}.

There exists a sequence
(\mu_{N})
of probability measures concentrated on
\partial\mathcal{B}_{+}

such that the expected hitting time of
\mathcal{B}_{-}

by the (
SHE
) flow is

\bigg[2\pi\sqrt{\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{|C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}-1|}{C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}+2}}\exp(3C_{N}L^{2}/2)\bigg]\exp(-\beta L^{2}/4)(1+r(\beta)),

where the error term satisfies
-\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1})\leq r(\beta)\leq\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1/2})
.
This estimate holds uniformly in a subsequence
N\to\infty
.
The prefactor in brackets is positive and bounded.

.

Although the result is stated for a flow started from
\partial\mathcal{B}_{+}
,
the details of the initial distribution are expected not to matter very much.
As
\beta\to\infty
, the time spent within a potential well should be large enough
for mixing to occur.

Remark 1.7
.

The product over
{\left|k\right|}\leq N
vanishes as
N\to\infty

but this is balanced by the divergent exponential renormalization term.
See

The renormalization constant
C_{N}
depends on the mass of the field.

a finite shift

\exp(3\theta_{N}L^{2}/2)

In this article, we state all results with respect to the constant

1.3
Further directions

Our results leave two interesting questions:

•

What is the transmission coefficient of (
NLW
) in three dimensions?
The
\phi^{4}_{3}
measure is not absolutely continuous with respect to a Gaussian measure,
whereas
\phi^{4}_{2}
is.
The additional renormalization also changes the dynamics.
We expect that adapting the techniques developed in
[
18
]

one should be able to show that the dynamical correction is again close to
1
,
but this is certainly technically difficult.

•

What is the transmission coefficient of (
SdNLW
), in any dimension?
The stochastic forcing should increase the likelihood of the flow “turning back” near the saddle,
and we would expect the transition coefficient to approach a value strictly between
0
and
1
.

The last question is especially interesting,
as (
SdNLW
) can be seen to “interpolate” between (
NLW
) and (
SHE
) dynamics
as discussed in the beginning of this section.
Better understanding of the stochastic case could thus lead to a more unified metastability
theory of the parabolic and hyperbolic equations.
A heuristic result is discussed in
[
11
, Remark 5.2]
.

Finally, let us remark on convergence as
N\to\infty
.
Our estimates for the transition frequency and transmission coefficient are uniform in
N
,
and
Section

2.1
holds also for the limiting flow.
However, we refrain here from stating a precise convergence result for the random variable
\mathcal{N}(T)
.
This topic is discussed in
[
10
, Remark 2.7]
,
where convergence in probability is sketched.

The one-dimensional
Theorem

1.2
does hold for the limiting flow,
thanks to higher moment bounds for hitting times and thus uniform integrability

[
9
, Section 3.3]
.
Such a moment bound depends on the particular PDE dynamics.
The proof in
[
9
]
depends heavily on the Markov property of the flow.

2.1
Expected transition frequency

The prefactor in the Eyring–Kramers law is usually written as an expression involving
eigenvalues of
-\Delta+U^{\prime\prime}
evaluated at the local maxima and minima of the potential
U

(see e.g. the introductory sections of
[
9
,
43
]
).
In this article, we simplify the notation by always writing these eigenvalues explicitly,
under the assumption
m^{2}=1
.
The derivation of the prefactor as a Gaussian normalization constant is done in
Section

3
.

In this subsection, we discuss the abstract
Theorem

1.3

and the definition of the transmission coefficient.
The following proof is adapted from
[
43
, Section 4.1]
.
For illustrative purposes, we work here directly with Dirac deltas
\delta_{0}

and not any regularized objects.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

The majority of proof holds for an arbitrary hyperbolic PDE that has an invariant measure,
and state space partitioned into two generic open sets and their boundary.
The sets are parametrized by a continuous function
q
so that
\mathcal{D}_{+}\coloneqq\{q>0\}
and

\mathcal{D}_{-}\coloneqq\{q<0\}
.

Let
u(t)
evolve according to the flow of the PDE.
The number of
\mathcal{D}_{-}\to\mathcal{D}_{+}
crossings in the time interval
{[{0},{T}]}
is

\int_{0}^{T}\max\{0,\partial_{t}\Gamma(q(u(t)))\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(2.1)

where
\Gamma
is the Heaviside step function.
The maximum function is used to count only the positive-direction crossings.
We can expand the derivative with the chain rule:

\int_{0}^{T}\max\{0,\nabla q(u(t))\cdot\partial_{t}u(t)\}\delta_{0}(q(u(t)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(2.2)

Now if we assume the initial data to be sampled from the relevant invariant measure
\mu
,
the distribution of
(u,v)=(u,\partial_{t}u)
is also
\mu
.
Integrating over
\mu
and dividing by
T
, we find that the expected crossing frequency is

\int\max\{0,\nabla q(u)\cdot v\}\delta_{0}(q(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u,v).

(2.3)

We now put
q(u)\coloneqq\hat{u}(0)
into this formula
and expand the definition of the product measure to get

\frac{\E_{\beta}\max\{0,\hat{v}(0)\}\int\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta)}.

(2.4)

By symmetry of the potential,
we multiply by two in order to account for
\mathcal{D}_{+}\to\mathcal{D}_{-}
crossings as well.

For both (
NLW
) and (
SdNLW
),
the distribution of
v=\partial_{t}u
is white noise by the product measure.
The white-noise part has the one-dimensional Gaussian expectation

\E_{\beta}\max\{0,\hat{v}(0)\}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\beta L^{d}}},

and this yields the stated formula.
∎

If we identify a Schwartz distribution on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with its Fourier coefficients,
we see that the set
\{u\colon\hat{u}(0)=0\}

is a hypersurface of codimension
1
.
In the following, we will call the integral over this surface the
saddle integral
,
as it corresponds to the saddle point of the potential.

Remark 2.1
.

The parabolic results are about the expected hitting time of
\mathcal{D}_{+}
,
whereas we derived the expectation of the inverse quantity.
The inverse of the expected frequency only provides a lower bound
for the expected transition time by Jensen’s inequality.

However, the difference is expected to be vanishing in the
\beta\to\infty
limit,
since the flow spends most of its time inside the potential wells.
This makes it approach a two-state continuous-time Markov process.

This derivation depended in no way on the specifics of the flow,
only the invariant measure.
This implies that the number of
\{\hat{u}(0)=0\}
crossings
is the same for both (
NLW
) and (
SdNLW
).
Their difference is in the crossing frequency between the smaller subsets

\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}
defined in
Theorem

1.9
.

Since (
NLW
) is a Hamiltonian equation with no external forcing,
its past and future trajectory is completely determined by the current state space position.
If we consider a
\mathcal{D}_{-}\to\mathcal{D}_{+}
crossing,
the hypersurface
\{\hat{u}(0)=0\}
is partitioned into two:
those trajectories that are also part of a
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}
crossing,
and those that return to
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}
without entering
\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}
.

This idea can be formalized as follows.
If
\hat{u}(0,t)=0
, then we define the four hitting times

\begin{split}\tau_{t}^{+}\coloneqq\inf_{s>t}\{\hat{u}(0,s)\geq 1-\delta\},\quad&\quad\tau_{t}^{-}\coloneqq\sup_{s<t}\{\hat{u}(0,s)\leq-1+\delta\},\\
\sigma_{t}^{+}\coloneqq\inf_{s>t}\{\hat{u}(0,s)\leq 0\},\quad&\quad\sigma_{t}^{-}\coloneqq\sup_{s<t}\{\hat{u}(0,s)\geq 0\}.\end{split}

(2.5)

Thus
\tau_{t}^{+}<\sigma_{t}^{+}
corresponds to the flow going to
\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}

without turning back to the saddle,
and
\tau_{t}^{-}>\sigma_{t}^{-}
to the flow having not already hit the saddle after departing from
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}
.
Therefore a
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}
crossing at time
t
can be written as the event

E_{t}\coloneqq\{\hat{u}(0,t)=0\}\cap\{\tau_{t}^{+}<\sigma_{t}^{+}\}\cap\{\tau_{t}^{-}>\sigma_{t}^{-}\}.

(2.6)

We can then modify (
2.1
) as

\int_{0}^{T}\max\{0,\partial_{t}\Gamma(q(u(t)))\}\mathds{1}(E_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(2.7)

The rest of the derivation proceeds as before,
meaning that the
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\prime}
transition frequency is

\frac{\E_{\beta}\max\{0,\hat{v}(0)\}\int\mathds{1}(E_{t})\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta)}.

(2.8)

Observe that we may use conditional probability to write

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\int\mathds{1}(E_{t})\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u).\\
&=\Prob_{\perp}(E_{t})\int\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u).\end{split}

(2.9)

where the probability
\Prob_{\perp}
is with respect to the measure given by

\frac{\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)}{\int\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)},

that is, the
\phi^{4}
field conditioned to be at the saddle surface.
Finally, the reversibility of (
NLW
) implies that it suffices to look in one direction of time:

\begin{split}\Prob_{\perp}(E_{t})&=\Prob_{\perp}\!\big(\{\tau_{t}^{+}<\sigma_{t}^{+}\}\cap\{\tau_{t}^{-}>\sigma_{t}^{-}\}\big)\\
&=1-\Prob_{\perp}\!\big(\{\tau_{t}^{+}\geq\sigma_{t}^{+}\}\cup\{\tau_{t}^{-}\leq\sigma_{t}^{-}\}\big)\\
&\geq 2\Prob_{\perp}(\tau_{t}^{+}<\sigma_{t}^{+})-1.\end{split}

(2.10)

By the stationarity of the flow
\Prob_{\perp}(E_{t})
does not depend on
t
.
This leads us to the following conclusion:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{N}(T)
be defined as in
Theorem

1.9
.
Then

\E\frac{\mathcal{N}(T)}{T}=\frac{\E_{\beta}\max\{0,\hat{v}(0)\}\int\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta)}\times\Prob_{\perp}(E_{t}),

where

2\Prob_{\perp}(\tau_{t}^{+}<\sigma_{t}^{+})-1\leq\Prob_{\perp}(E_{t})\leq 1.

Here
\Prob_{\perp}
is probability with respect to initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
so that

•

u_{0}
is sampled from the
\phi^{4}
measure conditioned on
\hat{u}_{0}(0)=0
, and

•

v_{0}
is sampled from the white noise measure conditioned on
\hat{v}_{0}(0)>0
.

We will call
\Prob_{\perp}(\tau^{+}_{t}<\sigma_{t}^{+})
the dynamical correction.
It is estimated in
Section

4
.
The stopping time
\tau^{+}_{t}
does not take the size of the oscillatory modes into account,
but as a byproduct of the proof we find that

\left\|\proj_{\perp}u(\tau^{+}_{t})\right\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\lesssim\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}

(2.11)

with high probability,
as required by the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{+}
.

The same principle of hitting is expected to apply to (
SdNLW
),
however in (
2.7
) one sees a probability instead of a characteristic function,
since the dynamics is no longer completely determined by the initial data.
Computing this probability is expected to give rise to a different dynamical correction
leading to a modification of the Eyring–Kramers formula.
We consider this to be an interesting open problem.

2.2
Besov spaces

Throughout this article, we consider functions and distributions as elements of Besov spaces.
These spaces somewhat generalize Sobolev spaces.
Below we recall the definition and basic properties.
For more information, see e.g. the textbook
[
3
]
,

[
28
, Appendix A]
,
or
[
41
, Section 3]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\chi
be a smooth function that equals
1
in
B(0,2)\setminus B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
,
and is supported in
B(0,8/3)\setminus B(0,3/4)
.
The
Littlewood–Paley blocks

\Delta_{k}
,
k\geq-1
, are defined as Fourier multipliers:

•

for
k\geq 0
, they are defined by symbol
\xi\mapsto\chi(2^{-k}{\left|\xi\right|})
,
that is, they restrict frequencies to dyadic annuli; and

•

\Delta_{-1}
has symbol
\xi\mapsto 1-\sum_{k\geq 0}\chi(2^{-k}{\left|\xi\right|})
,
making it a smooth indicator of the unit ball in frequency space.

Here
\chi
is chosen so that the symbols form a partition of unity.

Definition 2.4
.

The (nonhomogeneous)
Besov space

B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

is the completion of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with respect to the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\coloneqq\left\|2^{ks}\left\|\Delta_{k}f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right\|_{\ell^{r}(k\geq-1)},\quad 1\leq p,r\leq\infty,\;s\in\mathbb{R}.

We abbreviate
H^{s}\coloneqq B^{s}_{2,2}
and
\mathcal{C}^{s}\coloneqq B^{s}_{\infty,\infty}
.

Remark 2.5
.

As a particular case we get from Plancherel’s identity

\left\|f\right\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}^{2s}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}.

The decomposition of functions/distributions into dyadic frequency blocks
permits a useful decomposition of products.
Paraproducts capture low–high-frequency interactions,
whereas the resonant product captures interactions between similar frequency ranges.
Paraproducts are heavily used in the analysis of the three-dimensional
\phi^{4}
model.

Definition 2.6
.

We can decompose

fg=f\prec g+f\odot g+f\succ g,

where the
paraproduct
is defined as

f\prec g\coloneqq\sum_{k<\ell-1}(\Delta_{k}f)(\Delta_{\ell}g),

and the
resonant product
as

f\odot g\coloneqq\sum_{{\left|k-\ell\right|}\leq 1}(\Delta_{k}f)(\Delta_{\ell}g).

An important consequence of the definitions is that
paraproducts are Fourier-supported away from the origin.
The mean of the product
fg
is completely contained in the resonant term
f\odot g
.
Moreover, paraproducts and resonant products
are “almost adjoint” operators.
This is stated precisely in
Lemma

5.16
,
closer to where the result is used.

The usefulness of Besov spaces lies in the following multiplication inequalities.
A distribution and a function can be multiplied together if their differentiabilities
(parameter
s
in the norm) sum to a positive value.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
1/p=1/p_{1}+1/p_{2}
.
Then we have the paraproduct estimate

\left\|f\prec g\right\|_{B^{min(0,\alpha)+\beta}_{p,r}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p_{1},\infty}}\left\|g\right\|_{B^{\beta}_{p_{2},r}}.

Note that the low-frequency term can only decrease the smoothness of the product.
On the other hand, for
\alpha+\beta>0
we have the resonant bound

\left\|f\odot g\right\|_{B^{\alpha+\beta}_{p,r}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p_{1},\infty}}\left\|g\right\|_{B^{\beta}_{p_{2},r}}.

Together these imply that for
\alpha+\beta>0
we have

\left\|fg\right\|_{B^{\min(\alpha,\beta)}_{p,r}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p_{1},r}}\left\|g\right\|_{B^{\beta}_{p_{2},r}}.

There is also a special case for duality, where
s\in\mathbb{R}
:

\left\|fg\right\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{-s}_{p,r}}\left\|g\right\|_{B^{s}_{p^{\prime},r^{\prime}}},\quad 1=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}.

An especially useful variant of the multiplication estimate
is proved in
[
27
, Lemma A.7]
:

Theorem 2.8
.

For
s,\varepsilon>0
we have

\|f^{2}\|_{B^{s}_{1,1}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s+\varepsilon}_{2,2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\|f^{3}\|_{B^{s}_{1,1}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s+\varepsilon}_{2,2}}.

Besov spaces can be defined via interpolation of Sobolev spaces,
and there are several interpolation results between Besov spaces.
We use the following one to estimate some Besov norms in terms of
L^{4}
and
H^{1}
norms.

Theorem 2.9
.

Fix
\theta\in(0,1)
such that
s=\theta\alpha+(1-\theta)\beta
and

\frac{1}{p}=\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}+\frac{1-\theta}{p_{2}},\quad\frac{1}{r}=\frac{\theta}{r_{1}}+\frac{1-\theta}{r_{2}}.

Then we can interpolate

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p_{1},r_{1}}}^{\theta}\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\beta}_{p_{2},r_{2}}}^{1-\theta}.

Another important property is the ability to trade smoothness for integrability.
This leads to the following embedding results.
Note that the “exchange rate” between the
p
and
s
parameters
depends on the dimension of the underlying space.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
1\leq q<p\leq\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s^{\prime}}_{q,r}},\quad\text{where }s^{\prime}\geq s+d\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}\right).

There are also the following relations between Besov and usual Sobolev norms
(where
k\in\mathbb{N}
, including
k=0
corresponding to
L^{p}
space):

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{k}_{p,\infty}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{W^{k,p}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{k}_{p,1}}.

2.3
Variational formulation

We will be computing expectations of form
\E e^{-V}
.
It is useful to rephrase them as stochastic control problems over
V

without the exponential.

This variational approach is due to Üstünel
[
50
]
,
who in turn extended previous work of Boué and Dupuis
[
14
]
.
Barashkov and Gubinelli used this technique to construct the
\phi^{4}
measure

[
5
]
.
(See also e.g.
[
17
,
19
]

for other applications of the same underlying argument.)

We begin by defining our stochastic objects precisely.
As we discuss in
Section

2.4
,
it is necessary to consider regularized versions of these objects.
In the variational formalism the regularization is controlled by a continuous parameter
T\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
1
1

1

This parameter is sometimes referred to as “time”,
but is not to be confused with the dynamics of the wave equation.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
(B^{k}_{t})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be standard complex Brownian motions,
independent except for
B^{-k}_{t}=\overline{B^{k}_{t}}
.
For
k=0
we take
B^{0}_{t}
to be a real-valued Brownian motion.
We then define a Gaussian process

B_{t}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{L}ik\cdot x\right)B^{k}_{t}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the filtration corresponding to this process.
We denote by
\mathbb{H}_{a}
the space of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-progressively measurable processes
that are
\Prob
-almost surely in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d};\;\mathbb{R})
.
Elements of
\mathbb{H}_{a}
are called
drifts
.

We are going to define the Gaussian free field as the limit of regularized processes
where
B_{t}
is Fourier-multiplied with the covariance
(\pm m^{2}-\Delta)^{-1}
.
We first define a family of auxiliary functions
that are used for regularization.

Definition 2.12
.

Let us fix a non-increasing function
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\;\mathbb{R}_{+})

with
\rho(s)=1
for
s\leq 1
and
\rho(s)=0
for
s\geq 2
.
We then define

\rho_{t}(k)\coloneqq\rho\left(\frac{2{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{t}(k)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{t}(k)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The definition of
\sigma_{t}
is chosen so that

\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{t}(k)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\rho_{T}(k)^{2}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}1.

(2.12)

This means that the following definition would yield a Gaussian process that
has
(m^{2}-\Delta)^{-1}
as its limiting covariance when
T\to\infty
.
Up to any finite
T
, the process is continuous in
t
and smooth in space.

Remark 2.13
.

Here we deviate slightly from the argument of
[
5
]
.
The invariance of the
\phi^{4}
measure follows immediately from Liouville’s theorem
if the measure and dynamics are truncated to finitely many Fourier modes.
This is incompatible with the continuous parametrization.

This issue prompted Bringmann to use a slightly different definition in
[
17
]
.
We instead truncate the dynamics to wavenumbers of magnitude at most
N
,
and stop the stochastic control evolution once
T\geq N
.
This is possible since we never pass
N\to\infty
,
as long as our estimates are uniform in
N
.

Definition 2.14
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\sigma_{t}
be as above,
and let the mass associated with the Gaussian free field be
\pm m^{2}
.
We define the operator
J_{t}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
by its Fourier transform

\widehat{J_{t}[g]}(k)\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{t}(k)\mathds{1}_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\mathds{1}_{\pm m^{2}+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}>0}}{\sqrt{\pm m^{2}+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}}}\hat{g}(k),

where
C_{L}=(2\pi/L)^{d}
,
and then the operator
I_{T}\colon\mathbb{H}_{a}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
by

I_{T}[f]\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}J_{t}f_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

In particular, we define the
N
-truncated Gaussian free field
as
\phi_{N}\coloneqq I_{N}[B_{t}]
,
which equals
I_{T}[B_{t}]
for all
T\geq N
.

Let us note that
t\mapsto J_{t}[B_{t}]
and
t\mapsto I_{t}[B_{t}]
are both martingales.
We use this property together with the Itô formula.
Additionally, the following lemma formalizes the smoothing behaviour of
J_{t}
.

Lemma 2.15
.

For any
s,r\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in H^{r}
we have

\left\|J_{t}[g]\right\|_{H^{r}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+2s}}\left\|g\right\|_{H^{r-1+s}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let us write

\left\|J_{t}[g]\right\|_{H^{r}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{(1+{\left|k\right|}^{2})^{r}}{\pm m^{2}+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}}{\left|\rho_{t}(k)\rho^{\prime}(2{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}/{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle})\frac{{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{2}}\right|}\hat{g}(k)^{2}.

(2.13)

(If the mass term is
-1
, we sum over non-zero
k
only.)
Both
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are bounded by smoothness.
As
\sigma_{t}
is supported in an annulus proportional to
t
,
we also have
m^{2}+C{\left|k\right|}^{2}\simeq{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}^{2}\simeq{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{2}
.
Thus we can move out
{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{-2}

and introduce
{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}^{2s}/{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{2s}\simeq 1
to the above:

\frac{1}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+2s}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\left\langle{k}\right\rangle}^{2r-2+2s}\hat{g}(k)^{2},

(2.14)

which is exactly as required.
∎

We are now ready to present the
Boué–Dupuis formula
.
The fourth-order polynomial potential we study satisfies the tameness condition
by boundedness from below and hypercontractivity of the Gaussian measure
(see e.g.
[
42
, Proposition 3.3]
).

Definition 2.16
.

We call a measurable functional
V\colon C_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

tame
if

\E{\left|V(\phi_{T})\right|}^{p}+\E\exp(-qV(\phi_{T}))<\infty\quad\text{for some }\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

Theorem 2.17

(
[
50
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Fix
0<T<\infty

and let
V
be as above.
Then

-\log\E e^{-V(\phi_{T})}=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{H}_{a}}\E\left[V(\phi_{T}+I_{T}[v])+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

2.4
Wick renormalization

Let us now turn to the necessity of regularization and renormalization.
As was mentioned in the introduction,
the Gaussian free field
\phi
is not well-defined in two or more dimensions.
This is due to the divergence of

\E\phi^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}[\beta^{-1}(\pm m^{2}-\Delta)^{-1}]=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{1}{\beta(\pm m^{2}+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}

(2.15)

when
d\geq 2
.
However, it turns out that by subtracting this divergence,
we can get a well-defined stochastic object.
(The case of negative mass is only formal at this point,
but makes sense once coupled with the quartic potential.)

Definition 2.18
.

Let us define

C_{T,\beta,+}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
{\left|k\right|}\leq N\end{subarray}}\frac{\rho_{T}(k)^{2}}{\beta(2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})},\quad C_{T,\beta,-}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\\
{\left|k\right|}\leq N\end{subarray}}\frac{\rho_{T}(k)^{2}}{\beta(-1+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}.

Note that the other truncation parameter
N
is implicit in this definition.
We will rescale the Gaussian field to
\beta=1
below, and will omit
\beta
from the notation.
Moreover, we write
C_{T}
when the mass is clear from the context.
The Wick powers of
\phi_{N}
are then defined via Hermite polynomials;
the first four are

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}\!:}

\displaystyle=\phi_{T},

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{2}\!:}

\displaystyle=\phi_{T}^{2}-C_{T},

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{3}\!:}

\displaystyle=\phi_{T}^{3}-3C_{T}\phi_{T},

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{4}\!:}

\displaystyle=\phi_{T}^{4}-6C_{T}\phi_{T}^{2}+C_{T}^{2}.

Remark 2.19
.

Let us emphasize again that when
T\geq N
, then

C_{T}=C_{N}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
{\left|k\right|}\leq N\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\pm m^{2}+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}}.

This is the renormalization constant for GFF truncated sharply to wavenumbers at most size
N
.
For
T<N
the constant
C_{T}
is adapted to the continuous variational formulation.

Let us then collect some important properties of Wick powers.
See e.g.
[
10
, Appendix A]

and
[
20
, Section 3]
for more details.

Theorem 2.20
.

For any
j=1,2,\ldots
and
T\geq 0
, the Wick power
\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{j}\!:}
has zero expectation
under the Gaussian measure of the corresponding mass.

Theorem 2.21
.

Let
\mathinner{:\!\phi^{j}\!:}^{\prime}
be the Wick powers with respect to a different renormalization constant
C^{\prime}_{T}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}\!:}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}\!:},

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{2}\!:}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{2}\!:}-(C^{\prime}_{T}-C_{T}),

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{3}\!:}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{3}\!:}-3(C^{\prime}_{T}-C_{T})\phi_{T},

\displaystyle\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{4}\!:}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{4}\!:}-6(C^{\prime}_{T}-C_{T})\mathinner{:\!\phi_{T}^{2}\!:}+3(C^{\prime}_{T}-C_{T})^{2}.

Theorem 2.22
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we have the usual binomial formula

\mathinner{:\!(\phi+x)^{j}\!:}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}\mathinner{:\!\phi^{k}\!:}\,x^{j-k}.

We then have the following moment bounds for the Gaussian free field in suitable Besov spaces.
The proof of the two-dimensional case can be found in

Theorem 2.23
.

The Wick powers of Gaussian free field on

3
TST in two dimensions

We now move on to the proof of
Theorem

1.8
.
In this section we consider the two-dimensional case where the technical details are easier.

To recall
Theorem

1.3
, the expected transition frequency
between the two potential wells can be computed from

\frac{2\int\delta_{0}(\hat{u}(0))\exp(-\beta H(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta L^{d}}\mathcal{Z}(\beta)}.

(3.1)

We must compute the partition function
\mathcal{Z}(\beta)

and the “saddle integral” in the numerator.
We first derive the exact prefactor of the Eyring–Kramers law in this subsection,
and then estimate the normalized integrals with the variational method.

The Gibbs measure of the truncated
\phi^{4}_{2}
model can be written as

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\!\!\proj_{N}\mathinner{:\!u(x)^{4}\!:}_{\beta,-}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}-u(x)^{2}+{\left|\nabla u(x)\right|}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{u}(k).

(3.2)

In the limit
N\to\infty
, the product Lebesgue measure ceases to make sense.
However, the second-order term within the exponential defines a Gaussian density
with covariance
\beta^{-1}(-1-\Delta)^{-1}
.
It is well-known in the theory of stochastic quantization
that limits of measures like (
3.2
) are well-defined
and absolutely continuous with respect to the Gaussian measure
(
[
20
]
, see also
[
41
, Section 6]
).

However, the above explanation is not strictly true.
A Gaussian free field cannot have covariance
\beta^{-1}(-1-\Delta)^{-1}

since the zero Fourier mode would have negative variance.
The other Fourier modes are well-defined if the domain size
L
is strictly smaller than
2\pi
.

The dominant fourth-order term fixes this issue.
As done in
[
10
]
and other literature,
we can re-centre the field around a potential minimum.
By symmetry we can take the constant function
+1
as the minimum
and compute
\mathcal{Z}_{N}/2
by integrating over the half-space where
\hat{u}(0)>0
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
can be written as

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}{2}=\sqrt{\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{2\pi}{\beta L^{2}(2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}}\exp\left(\!\frac{3C_{N}L^{2}}{2}+\frac{\beta L^{2}}{4}\right)\int_{\mathcal{D}_{+}}\!\!\exp\left(-V_{\beta}(\varphi)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{N}(\varphi),

where

•

\displaystyle{V_{\beta}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{4}\!:_{-}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{3}\!:_{-}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}
,

•

\rho_{N}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi)
is a Gaussian measure with covariance
(2-\Delta)^{-1}
,
truncated to wavenumbers of magnitude at most
N
,

•

C_{N}
is the Wick renormalization constant with negative mass, and

•

\mathcal{D}_{+}\coloneqq\{\hat{\varphi}(0)>-\sqrt{\beta}\}
.

Proof.

If we denote
u=\varphi+1
, then the fourth-order term becomes

\frac{1}{4}\mathinner{:\!(\varphi+1)^{4}\!:}=\frac{1}{4}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{4}\!:}+\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{3}\!:}+\frac{3}{2}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{2}\!:}+\varphi+\frac{1}{4}.

(3.3)

The second-order term is changed to

\frac{1}{2}\left[-u^{2}+{\left|\nabla u\right|}^{2}\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}-\varphi-\frac{1}{2}+{\left|\nabla\varphi\right|}^{2}.

(3.4)

The newly introduced first-order terms cancel out,
and we may move
3\varphi^{2}/2

from (
3.3
) to (
3.4
).
This changes the covariance to
\beta^{-1}(2-\Delta)^{-1}
.

There are also two constant terms,
\beta L^{2}/4
and
the
3\beta L^{2}C_{N,\beta}/2
coming from renormalization.
The latter simplifies as
3L^{2}C_{N}/2
.
We move these out into the exponential prefactor.

We then normalize the Gaussian measure,
and denote the normalized measure by
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{N,\beta}(\varphi)
.
The partition function is therefore equal to

\mathcal{Z}_{N}=2\sqrt{\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{2\pi}{\beta L^{d}(2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}}\exp\left(\frac{3C_{N}L^{2}}{2}+\frac{\beta L^{2}}{4}\right)\\
\int_{\{\hat{\varphi}(0)>-1\}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\proj_{N}[\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{4}\!:_{-}}+4\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{3}\!:_{-}}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{N,\beta}(\varphi).

(3.5)

We can drop the projection operator
\proj_{N}
,
since it does not affect the value of the integral over whole
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

We finally simplify the notation by rescaling
\varphi
by
\sqrt{\beta}
;
this cancels the
\beta^{-1}
from the covariance and leaves us with the claim.
From now on we drop
\beta=1
from the notation for the Gaussian measure
\rho_{N}
.
∎

There is now a mismatch between the random field and the Wick ordering:
we need to change the renormalization
from
(-1-\Delta)^{-1}
covariance to
(2-\Delta)^{-1}
covariance.
This is done with
Theorem

2.21

and it introduces some second-order correction terms.
These terms are easily estimated as part of the stochastic quantization argument.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
\beta
large enough, we have

\mathcal{Z}_{N}=2\sqrt{\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{2\pi}{\beta L^{d}(2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}}\exp\left(\frac{3C_{N}L^{2}}{2}+\frac{\beta L^{2}}{4}\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1})).

Proof.

We only need to show that the integral in

3.1
approaches
1
sufficiently fast.
First, we split the integration region
\mathcal{D}_{+}
into two parts:
a slightly smaller “good” set

\mathcal{B}_{+}\coloneqq\left\{\hat{\varphi}(0)>(-1+\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\right\},

(3.6)

and the remaining region

\mathcal{R}_{+}\coloneqq\left\{-\sqrt{\beta}<\hat{\varphi}(0)<(-1+\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\right\}.

(3.7)

Here

is a parameter to be fixed in (
3.34

it will be close to but strictly smaller than
1
.
The integral over

corresponds to a (half-)neighbourhood of the saddle.
We show it to be negligible in

.

The variational formula in
Theorem

2.17
is defined for integrals over the whole space.
To go back to the full space, we can write

\int_{z}^{\infty}F(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\kappa\llbracket x-z\rrbracket^{2})F(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(3.8)

where

denotes the negative part of

If we fix

the estimation error will be

\int_{-\infty}^{z}\exp(-\kappa\llbracket x-z\rrbracket^{2})F(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.9)

In our concrete case, the approximating integral is thus

\int\exp(-\kappa\llbracket\hat{\varphi}(0)+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket^{2})\exp\left(-V_{\beta}(\varphi)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi),

(3.10)

which is shown to be finite in

The error is

\int_{\{\hat{\varphi}(0)<(-1+\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\}}\exp(-\kappa\llbracket\hat{\varphi}(0)+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket^{2})\exp\left(-V_{\beta}(\varphi)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi).

We can use Hölder to upper-bound the error by

\Prob\!\left(\hat{\varphi}(0)<(-1+\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\right)^{1-1/q}\\
\bigg[\int\exp(-q\kappa\llbracket\hat{\varphi}(0)+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket^{2})\exp\left(-qV_{\beta}(\varphi)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi)\bigg]^{1/q}.

(3.12)

The term in brackets is (
3.10
) except for the additional

For

3.1
still works.
The prefactor is a one-dimensional Gaussian probability that has exponential Markov bounds;
therefore it is much smaller than the required order

.
∎

We also need to compute the integral over the hypersurface where
\hat{u}(0)=0
.
Since the zero mode was the only problematic part of the covariance (as long as

),
we will not need to repeat the re-centering trick.
Using relevant parts from

Theorem 3.3
.

The saddle integral

\mathcal{I}_{N}=\int_{\{\hat{u}(0)=0\}}\hskip-10.00002pt\exp\!\left[-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!u(x)^{4}\!:}_{-}+\frac{1}{2}u(x)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}{\left|\nabla u(x)\right|}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{u}(k)

\mathcal{I}_{N}=\sqrt{\prod_{0<{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{2\pi}{\beta L^{d}(C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}-1)}}(1+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1})).

Proof.

Since there is no re-centring, the prefactor comes from

As in

, we can write the variational formulation of the saddle integral as

-\log\mathcal{I}_{N}=\inf_{v}\E_{u}\left[\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(u+I_{N}[v])^{4}\!:_{-}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

(3.13)

The lower bound follows by the quartic terms in

,

We note that the operator

depends on the mass

Lemma 3.4

We have the uniform-in-

\sqrt{\prod_{0<{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}+2}{C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}-1}}\exp(-3C_{N}L^{d}/2)\simeq 1.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{3}\!:_{+}}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\|\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{3}\!:_{+}}\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}\|\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{3}\!:_{+}}\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(3.26)

The first term has finite expectation by
Theorem

2.23
.
The introduction of
\rho
is even not necessary here, as it would suffice to take
\beta
large.

In the second mixed term, we apply
Theorem

2.8

to use both the
H^{1}
and
L^{4}
controlling terms:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:_{+}}I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\|\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:_{+}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\|I_{N}[v]^{2}\|_{B^{\varepsilon}_{1,1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\|\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:_{+}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{2\varepsilon}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}\|\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:_{+}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}^{4}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Again, the first term has finite expectation.
Finally, we use
Theorem

2.8
to estimate the third term:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\phi_{N}I_{N}[v]^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\|I_{N}[v]^{3}\|_{B^{\varepsilon}_{1,1}}\\
&\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{2\varepsilon}}.\end{split}

(3.28)

Now we use a crude application of
Theorem

2.9
to estimate

\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{2\varepsilon}}\lesssim\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{B^{1/2}_{8/3,\infty}}\lesssim\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{B^{0}_{4,\infty}}^{1/2}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{B^{1}_{2,\infty}}^{1/2},

(3.29)

which lets us bound

\begin{split}\eqref{eq:2d partition pi3}&\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{5/2}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{1/2}\\
&\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}^{8}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.\end{split}

(3.30)

As the stochastic term has finite expectation,
this completes the estimation of fourth-order terms.

Third-order terms

With the fourth-order terms we could have
\beta^{-1}
in front of all controlling terms,
including
\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}
.
Here we only have
\beta^{-1/2}
at our disposal.
We can modify the argument of (
3.26
) to estimate

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:_{+}}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}^{2}\right\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}^{2}+\rho\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(3.31)

We can similarly distribute the
\beta
in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\phi_{N}I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{B^{-\varepsilon}_{4,4}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{B^{\varepsilon}_{8/3,8/3}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{B^{-\varepsilon}_{4,4}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}\left\|\phi_{N}\right\|_{B^{-\varepsilon}_{4,4}}^{4}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\rho\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

(3.32)

The only problematic term is the
I_{N}[v]^{3}
term.
Here we need to use the mass of the translated field.
We split
I_{N}[v]
into its mean
I_{N}^{\circ}[v]
and the remaining oscillatory part
I_{N}^{\perp}[v]
.
We can then apply the following Poincaré inequality:

Lemma 3.9
.

On a periodic domain
[{0},{L}]^{d}
, we have

\left\|I_{N}^{\perp}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2+(2\pi/L)^{2}}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

As in
Lemma

3.7
, we can estimate the norm as

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}}\left[\int_{0}^{N}\sigma_{t}(k)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\left[\int_{0}^{N}v_{t}(k)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

Since
{\left|k\right|}\geq 1
, we can bound the prefactor with
1/(2+C_{L})
.
∎

We can use Young’s inequality (now with explicit constants) and
Lemma

3.9
to estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[v]^{2}I_{N}^{\perp}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{4\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}\left\|I_{N}^{\perp}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{4\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{1}{(2+(2\pi/L)^{2})\rho^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.33)

Everywhere above, we could choose
\rho
to be arbitrarily small,
but here
\rho^{\prime}
needs to satisfy both
\rho^{\prime}<1
and
(2+(2\pi/L)^{2})\rho^{\prime}>2

in order to match (
3.24
).
This choice is always possible, even without any assumptions on
L
.

Finally, we write the remaining part of the
I_{N}[v]^{3}
term as

\displaystyle\E\frac{I_{N}^{\circ}[v]}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\E\frac{\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)+I_{N}^{\circ}[v]+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-(1-\delta^{\prime})\E\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.34)

(Recall that
\E\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)=0
.)
We fix
\delta^{\prime}
so that

(1-\delta^{\prime})\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.35)

The remaining two terms can be bounded from below as

\displaystyle\E\frac{-\llbracket\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)+I_{N}^{\circ}[v]+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\;

\displaystyle-C_{\rho}\E\llbracket\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)+I_{N}^{\circ}[v]+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket^{2}-\frac{\rho}{\beta}\E\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}.

(3.36)

We now fix
\kappa=C_{\rho}
in (
3.10
).
Therefore the first term of (
3.36
) is cancelled by a matching positive term.

Change of Wick ordering

Let us then still check the correction terms stemming from changing the Wick ordering.
We now use
Theorem

2.21
to replace the Wick ordering by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!\bullet^{4}\!:_{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!\bullet^{4}\!:_{+}}-\frac{6(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!\bullet^{2}\!:_{+}}+\frac{3(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})^{2}}{4\beta},

(3.37)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\mathinner{:\!\bullet^{3}\!:_{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\mathinner{:\!\bullet^{3}\!:_{+}}-\frac{3(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})}{\sqrt{\beta}}\bullet.

(3.38)

Here the difference of renormalization constants satisfies

\sup_{N}|C_{N}^{+}-C_{N}^{-}|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}{\left|\frac{1}{C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}+2}-\frac{1}{C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2}-1}\right|}<\infty.

(3.39)

As we substitute
\bullet=\phi+I_{N}[v]
,
the raw Wick powers
\phi
and
\mathinner{:\!\phi^{2}\!:_{+}}
vanish under the expectation.
The cross term of (
3.37
) is bounded by

\frac{3(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})}{\beta}\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\phi I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta^{2}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}^{2}+\rho\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

(3.40)

and the other term in (
3.37
) follows similarly:

\frac{3(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})}{2\beta}\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}+\frac{\rho}{\beta}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\rho\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.41)

Then the final term in (
3.38
) is estimated as

\frac{3(C_{N}^{-}-C_{N}^{+})}{\sqrt{\beta}}\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta}+\rho\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.42)

In summary, all of these correction terms could be controlled within the previous framework.

3.2
Concentration of measure

To finish the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, we want to show that the integral over

\mathcal{R}_{-}\cup\mathcal{R}_{+}\coloneqq\left\{-\delta^{\prime}\sqrt{\beta}<\hat{u}(0)<\delta^{\prime}\sqrt{\beta}\right\}

(3.43)

is negligible in the computation of
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.
In the following, we denote by
\mathcal{Z}^{\flat}_{N}
the integral over
\mathcal{B}_{+}
computed in
Section

3.1

and by
\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}_{N}
the integral over
\mathcal{R}_{-}\cup\mathcal{R}_{+}
.
The precise result we will prove in this section is:

Theorem 3.10
.

For
\beta
sufficiently large, we have

\mathcal{Z}_{N}\leq 2(1+C/\beta)\mathcal{Z}^{\flat}_{N}
.

Since
\mathcal{Z}^{\flat}_{N}
is finite, it will be sufficient to show

\frac{\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}_{N}}{\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}_{N}+2\mathcal{Z}^{\flat}_{N}}<\exp(-C\beta)\quad\text{for some }C>0.

(3.44)

This formulation is useful since the denominator corresponds to the full partition function.
It is equivalent to showing that

-\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\log\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}_{N}-\log\mathcal{Z}_{N}\right]>C,

(3.45)

which is set up for the variational formula.

Instead of doing the translation
u=1+\varphi
,
we will only add and subtract a quadratic term; that is, we write

\mathcal{Z}^{\sharp}_{N}=\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\pm}}\!\!\exp(-W_{\beta}(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(u)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\pm}}\!\!\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{1}{4\beta}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{4}\!:}-\frac{3}{2}\mathinner{:\!\varphi^{2}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi).

(3.46)

With this choice, the Gaussian measure
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho
is again of covariance
(2-\Delta)^{-1}
.

We again need to move to the full integration region like in
Theorem

3.2
.
We estimate

\int_{\mathcal{R}_{+}}\exp(-W_{\beta}(\varphi))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi)\leq\int\exp(-\beta\kappa h(\hat{\varphi}(0)/\sqrt{\beta}))\exp(-W_{\beta}(\varphi))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi),

(3.47)

where
h
is a smoothed indicator of
\mathbb{R}\setminus{[{-\delta^{\prime}},{\delta^{\prime}}]}

and
\kappa>0
will be fixed in the end.
By this and the Boué–Dupuis formula, the left-hand side of (
3.45
)
is then bounded from above by

\inf_{v}\E\left[\kappa h\bigg((\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)+\frac{\widehat{I_{N}[w]}(0))}{\sqrt{\beta}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{\beta}W_{\beta}(\phi_{N}+I_{N}[v])+\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
-\inf_{w}\E\left[\frac{1}{\beta}W_{\beta}(\phi_{N}+I_{N}[w])+\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

(3.48)

We need to show that the function
h
pushes the two infima sufficiently far apart from each other.
Before estimating (
3.48
),
let us perform one more simplification.
By the definition of the potential, we have

\frac{1}{\beta}W_{\beta}(\phi_{N}+I_{N}[v])=W_{1}\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+\frac{I_{N}[v]}{\sqrt{\beta}}\right).

(3.49)

We can therefore rename
v/\sqrt{\beta}
to
v
in both infima.
We are hence left with

\inf_{v}\E\left[\kappa h\bigg(\frac{\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)}{\sqrt{\beta}}+\widehat{I_{N}[v]}(0)\bigg)+W_{1}\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+I_{N}[v]\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
-\inf_{w}\E\left[W_{1}\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+I_{N}[w]\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

(3.50)

This formula suggests that passing
\beta\to\infty
is going to lead to a deterministic limit.
Let us then prove this.
First, we collect an application of the triangle inequality:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an arbitrary set, and
F_{\beta},F
some maps
\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
.
If

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{G}}{\left|F_{\beta}(v)-F(v)\right|}=0,

then we also have

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}{\left|\inf_{v\in\mathcal{G}}F_{\beta}(v)-\inf_{w\in\mathcal{G}}F(w)\right|}=0.

Proof.

It suffices to note that

\inf_{v\in\mathcal{G}}F_{\beta}(v)=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{G}}\big[F_{\beta}(v)-F(v)+F(v)\big]\leq\sup_{v\in\mathcal{G}}\big[F_{\beta}(v)-F(v)\big]+\inf_{v\in\mathcal{G}}F(v).

The other required inequality follows by symmetry.
∎

We use this lemma as follows:
In the second bracket of (
3.50
) we set

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E W_{1}\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+I_{N}[w]\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(3.51)

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[w]^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{N}[w]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.52)

In the first bracket of (
3.50
),
we additionally include
h
in a similar way.
Therefore all the randomness is now contained in the definition of
F_{\beta}
.
If we can estimate the supremum of
{\left|F_{\beta}-F\right|}
, then the error in replacing
\inf F_{\beta}
by
\inf F

can be bounded by any
\varepsilon>0
once
\beta
is large enough.

For this, we need to be able to commute the supremum and the limit.
The following two results give sufficient uniform convergence bounds.

Lemma 3.12
.

There exists
K>0
such that the minimizers
of both infima in (
3.50
) are contained in

\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\left\{v\in\mathbb{H}_{a}\colon\E\left[\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{4}^{4}+\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]<K\text{ for all }N\geq 0\right\}.

This holds for all
\beta
large enough.
The universial quantifier also applies to the implicit dependency of
I_{t}
on
N
.

Proof.

The argument in
Section

3.1
lets us bound the quartic term of
W_{1}
as

\E\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+I_{N}[v]\right)^{4}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq-\frac{C_{\rho}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{1-\rho}{4\beta^{2}}\E\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{4}^{4}-\frac{\rho}{2\beta}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.53)

In the quadratic term, the Wick power
\mathinner{:\!\phi_{N}^{2}\!:}
again contributes nothing to the expectation.
We can use a straightforward Young estimate like

\E\frac{3}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}2\phi I_{N}[v]+I_{N}[v]^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{\rho}+\frac{\rho}{\beta^{2}}\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{4}^{4}

(3.54)

for the remaining two terms.
Therefore we have

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{\geq}}\E\left[\frac{1}{\beta}V_{\beta}(\phi_{N}+I_{N}[v])+\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&\geq-\frac{C_{1}}{\beta^{2}}-C_{2}+\frac{1-\rho}{4\beta^{2}}\E\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{4}^{4}+\frac{1-\rho}{2\beta}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(3.55)

In the version that contains
h
,
we also include
\kappa\left\|h\right\|_{\infty}
in
C_{2}
.
The left-hand side of (
3.55
)
can be bounded from above by
0
by plugging in
v=0
as before.
As we then rewrite
w=v/\sqrt{2\beta}
, we get

\E\left[\left\|I_{N}[w]\right\|_{4}^{4}+\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}/\beta^{2}+C_{2}}{1-\rho},

(3.56)

which is exactly as stated in the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

In the set
\mathcal{G}
defined above we have

{\left|\E h\bigg(\frac{\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)}{\sqrt{\beta}}+\widehat{I_{N}[v]}(0)\bigg)-h\left(\widehat{I_{N}[v]}(0)\right)\right|}<\frac{C}{\sqrt{\beta}},

and

{\left|\E W_{1}\!\left(\frac{\phi_{N}}{\sqrt{\beta}}+I_{N}[v]\right)-W_{1}\left(I_{N}[v]\right)\right|}<\frac{C}{\sqrt{\beta}}.

Both of these estimates are uniform in
v\in\mathcal{G}
and
N>0
.

Proof.

Since
h
is Lipschitz-continuous, the left-hand side of the first claim is bounded by

C\E{\left|\frac{\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)}{\sqrt{\beta}}\right|}.

Since
\hat{\phi}_{N}(0)
is an ordinary Gaussian random variable,
the expectation is finite and the quotient vanishes with correct rate.

In the second claim, we expand the potentials.
All purely
I_{N}[v]
-dependent terms are cancelled.
The remaining mixed terms can be estimated as in
Section

3.1
.
Any expectations of
\phi_{N}
and its Wick powers are bounded as before;

\left\|I_{N}[v]\right\|_{4}^{4}
and the integral of
\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}
are bounded by the definition of
\mathcal{G}
;
and each term has a prefactor of
\beta^{-1/2}
or better.
∎

Now we can apply
Lemma

3.11

to replace the stochastic functionals with their deterministic limits.
The error
\varepsilon>0
can be chosen freely,
at the expense of large enough
\beta
.
The variational problem (
3.50
) thus has a lower bound

\inf_{v}\left[\kappa h(\widehat{I_{N}[v]}(0))+W_{1}(I_{N}[v])+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
-\inf_{w}\left[W_{1}(I_{N}[w])+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]-\varepsilon.

(3.57)

Since we assume
L<2\pi
, only constant functions minimize the potential
W_{1}
.
Thus by suitably large
\kappa
we should be able to force
v
to be near the saddle
whereas
w
can lie in a potential well.
Let us still prove this rigorously.

Lemma 3.14
.

Both infima in (
3.57
) can be taken over constant functions.

Proof.

Since
h
already only depends on the zero mode, we only need to look at the second infimum.
Let
w=w_{0}+w_{\perp}
, where
w_{0}
is the average of
w
.
Then the bracket becomes (abbreviating
I_{0}=I_{N}[w_{0}]
and
I_{\perp}=I_{N}[w_{\perp}]
)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{1}{4}I_{0}^{4}+I_{0}^{3}I_{\perp}+\frac{3}{2}I_{0}^{2}I_{\perp}^{2}+I_{0}I_{\perp}^{3}+\frac{1}{4}I_{\perp}^{4}-\frac{3}{2}I_{0}^{2}-3I_{0}I_{\perp}-\frac{3}{2}I_{\perp}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{0,t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{\perp,t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.58)

We need to show that the above expression is larger than the equivalent where
I_{\perp}=0
:

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{1}{4}I_{0}^{4}-\frac{3}{2}I_{0}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{0,t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.59)

The terms with a single power of
I_{\perp}
vanish in the integration,
so we only need to control
I_{0}I_{\perp}^{3}
and
-3I_{\perp}^{2}/2

with the three positive terms that depend on
I_{\perp}
.

By the Poincaré-type
Lemma

3.9

and the assumption
L<2\pi
we have

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{\geq}}-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}I_{\perp}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{\perp,t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{2(2+(2\pi/L)^{2})}\right)\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{\perp,t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.60)

On the other hand we can use Young’s inequality to estimate

I_{0}I_{\perp}I_{\perp}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}I_{0}^{2}I_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}I_{\perp}^{4}.

(3.61)

This implies (by choosing
\lambda=3
)

I_{0}I_{\perp}I_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{3}{2}I_{0}^{2}I_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}I_{\perp}^{4}\geq\frac{1}{12}I_{\perp}^{4}\geq 0.

(3.62)

Thus any oscillatory perturbation to a constant function
is only going to grow the expression.
∎

Now (
3.57
) is reduced to

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{\eta:I_{N}(\eta)=1}\left[\kappa h(x)+\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{3|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
-\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{\eta:I_{N}(\eta)=1}\left[\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|y^{4}}{4}-\frac{3|\mathbb{T}^{2}|y^{2}}{2}+\frac{y^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]-\varepsilon,

(3.63)

We set
\Xi\coloneqq|\mathbb{T}^{2}|^{-1}\inf_{\eta:I_{N}(\eta)=1}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
.
Note that

\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=2|\mathbb{T}^{2}|\text{ for the choice }\widehat{\eta_{t}}(k)=\sqrt{2}\sigma_{t}(0)\mathds{1}_{k=0},

(3.64)

so
\Xi\leq 2
.
We are now left to show that

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{>}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left[\kappa h(x)+\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}\right]\\
&>\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left[\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|y^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|y^{2}}{2}\right].\end{split}

(3.65)

Notice that the infimum in the second line is attained (only) at
y=\pm\sqrt{3-\Xi}
,
and is given by
-|\mathbb{T}^{2}|(3-\Xi)^{2}/4
. This shows that

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}

\displaystyle\inf_{x[-\delta,\delta]}\left[\kappa h(x)+\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}\right]

(3.66)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x[-\delta,\delta]}\left[\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}\right]

(3.67)

\displaystyle>\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left[\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}\right].

(3.68)

On the other hand, since
h>0
on
[-\delta,\delta]^{c}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\inf_{x[-\delta,\delta]^{c}}\left[\kappa h(x)+\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{4}}{4}-\frac{(3-\Xi)|\mathbb{T}^{2}|x^{2}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x[-\delta,\delta]^{c}}\left[\kappa h(x)-|\mathbb{T}^{2}|\frac{(3-\Xi)^{2}}{4}\right]

\displaystyle>-\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{2}|(3-\Xi)^{2}}{4}.

This shows that (
3.57
) is strictly positive
once
\varepsilon
is small enough,
and thus (
3.45
) is bounded as required.

4
Dynamical correction

In this section, we show that transition state theory does not overestimate
the number of transitions by (
NLW
) flow by too much:
If the solution manages to “climb” to the saddle,
it will have sufficient “inertia” to fall into the opposite well without turning back.
However, we are only able to do this in up to two dimensions,
and not at all for (
SdNLW
).

Remark 4.1
.

Throughout this section,
we translate time so that the saddle is hit at
t=0
.
By symmetry, we will also only consider transitions from
\{\hat{u}(0)<-1+\delta\}

to
\{\hat{u}(0)>1-\delta\}
.

Let us first motivate the following analysis by looking at the ODE

\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}u(t)-u(t)&=-u(t)^{3},\\
\hfill\partial_{t}u(0)&=Q>0,\\
\hfill u(0)&=0.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Integrating the equation twice in time, we find that the solution satisfies

u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}[u(r)-u(r)^{3}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+Qt.

(4.2)

In the region
0\leq u(t)\leq\delta\ll 1
, we can linearize this as

u(t)\sim\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}u(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+Qt,

(4.3)

which yields
u(t)\sim Q\sinh(t)
.
Intuitively, the behaviour of
u
is dominated by the initial velocity
Q
on the saddle,
followed by a rapid descent into the potential well.
Solving for
\tau
in
u(\tau)=\delta
yields
\tau\sim\log(Q^{-1})
.

4.1
The zero mode

If we project the two-dimensional, non-stochastic equation (
NLW
) to the zero mode,
we find that
w\coloneqq\proj_{0}u
solves

\partial_{tt}w(t)-w(t)=-w(t)^{3}-3w(t)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathinner{:\!(\proj_{\perp}u)^{2}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathinner{:\!(\proj_{\perp}u)^{3}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(4.4)

Importantly, the first power of the oscillatory part
\proj_{\perp}u
does not influence
w
.
If
\mathinner{:\!(\proj_{\perp}u)^{k}\!:}
is of order
\beta^{-k/2}

and the initial velocity
Q\coloneqq\partial_{t}w(0)
of order
\beta^{-1/2}
,
then (
4.4
) can be regarded as a small perturbation of the zero-dimensional equation
(
4.1
).

By these heuristics, we can say that a transition
has vanishingly small probability of turning back after crossing the saddle:

Theorem 4.2
.

We fix
\delta>0
small enough and define the hitting times

\tau_{+}\coloneqq\inf_{t>0}\{w(t)\geq 1-\delta\},\quad\sigma_{+}\coloneqq\inf_{t>0}\{w(t)\leq 0\}

as in
Lemma

2.2
.
Consider the flow of (
NLW
) in two spatial dimensions,
with initial data as in
Lemma

2.2
.
Then
\Prob_{\perp}(\tau_{+}<\sigma_{+})\geq 1-C\beta^{-1/4+3\varepsilon}
,

Remark 4.3
.

Since the stochastic forcing of (
SdNLW
) is of order
\beta^{-1/2}
,
the same analysis does not apply to that equation.
We expect its analysis to require more involved probabilistic techniques.

Similarly, (
NLW
) in three dimensions is more complicated to study,
since the
\phi^{4}_{3}
measure is not absolutely continuous with respect to the GFF,
and the dynamics is more complicated due to the additional renormalization.

The proof has two main steps:
First, we assume the oscillatory part to be small,
and show that the hitting times then satisfy
\tau_{+}<\sigma_{+}
.
After that, we show the assumption to hold with large probability.

We decompose the solution as

u=w+Z+\psi,

(4.5)

where
w
is the zero mode governed by (
4.4
),

Z
solves the linear wave equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}Z(x,t)-(1+\Delta)Z(x,t)&=0,\\
\hfill\Law(Z(0))&=\proj_{\perp}(\phi^{4}_{2}(\beta)),\\
\hfill\Law(\partial_{t}Z(0))&=\proj_{\perp}(\beta^{-1/2}\text{ white noise}),\end{cases}

(4.6)

and the nonlinear oscillatory part
\psi
is left to solve

\begin{cases}\partial_{tt}\psi(x,t)-(1+\Delta)\psi(x,t)&=-\proj_{\perp}\mathinner{:\!(Z+w+\psi)(x,t)^{3}\!:},\\
\hfill\psi(0)&=0,\\
\hfill\partial_{t}\psi(0)&=0.\end{cases}

(4.7)

Note that both
\psi
and
Z
are purely oscillatory.

Assumption 4.4
.

Let
T=2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)
.
For
k=1,2,3
we have the bound

\left[\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]^{1/2}\leq\beta^{-k/2+k\varepsilon},

(4.8)

and

Q\geq\beta^{-3/4+3\varepsilon}.

(4.9)

Assumption 4.5
.

With
T
as above, we assume that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\beta^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}.

The choice of
T=2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)
as the upper integration bound
stems from the following
a priori
estimate for the stopping time.
This estimate also implies that
w
is positive throughout,
meaning that
\tau_{+}<\sigma_{+}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
4.4
and
4.5
hold and
\beta
is large enough,
then the hitting time satisfies
\tau\leq 2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)
.
Moreover,
w^{\prime}(t)>0
and

0<w(t)<\frac{3Q}{2}\sinh(t)

for all
0<t\leq\tau
.

Proof.

Integrating (
4.4
) twice in time,
we find that the counterpart to (
4.2
) is

w(t)=Qt+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}[w(r)-w(r)^{3}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\left[-3w(r)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{2}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.10)

By
Assumption

4.4
the second line can be bounded with

C\int_{0}^{t}(T^{1/2}+T)\left[\beta^{-1+2\varepsilon}+\beta^{-3/2+3\varepsilon}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}t.

(4.11)

Now when
\beta
is large enough, we have
C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}\leq Q/2
.
Therefore we can estimate

\eqref{eq:corr zero mode full}\geq\frac{Qt}{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}(1-(1-\delta)^{2})w(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.12)

This integral equation implies that in the
0\leq w(t)\leq 1-\delta
regime,

w(t)\geq\frac{Q}{2\sqrt{2\delta-\delta^{2}}}\sinh(\sqrt{2\delta-\delta^{2}}t).

(4.13)

This shows that
w(t)
stays positive until the hitting time.
From the equation we can also solve an upper bound for
\tau
,
assuming
\beta
large enough:

\begin{split}\tau&\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\delta-\delta^{2}}}\arcsinh\left(\frac{2\delta\sqrt{2\delta-\delta^{2}}}{Q}\right)\\
&\leq 2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta).\end{split}

(4.14)

Here we crudely bounded
\arcsinh(x)\leq\log(3x)\leq 2\log(x)
for
x
large.

If we integrate (
4.4
) only once in time,
the same argument shows that
w^{\prime}(t)>0
in the domain.
Finally, the upper bound for
w(t)
follows by noting that
w(r)-w(r)^{3}\leq w(r)

and modifying (
4.12
) accordingly.
∎

We now use this estimate to bootstrap a better estimate for the behaviour near the saddle.
We introduce an auxiliary hitting time:

Definition 4.7
.

With
\delta>0
as in
Theorem

4.2
, we define the hitting time

\theta_{+}\coloneqq\inf_{t>0}\{w(t)\geq\delta\}.

The rationale for this auxiliary object is that
\theta_{+}
will be of order
\mathcal{O}(\log\beta)
,
whereas
\tau_{+}-\theta_{+}
will be of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The next theorem shows that

\theta_{+}
is only an additive perturbation away from the zero-dimensional hitting time
sketched in (
4.1
).
The difference to
\tau_{+}
is then shown in
Lemma

4.10
.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
4.4
and
4.5
hold and
\beta
is large enough, then

\theta_{+}\leq\arcsinh((1-\delta)Q^{-1})+2\delta^{2}.

Proof.

We can write the evolution of
w
as the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}w(t)&=p(t),\\
\partial_{t}p(t)&=-V^{\prime}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(4.15)

where

V(t)=\frac{1}{4}w(t)^{4}-\frac{1}{2}w(t)^{2}\\
-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\left[-3w(s)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{2}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}_{G(t)}.

(4.16)

Observe that by monotonicity of
w
,
we can bound
{\left|G(t)\right|}
from above by

\mathcal{G}(w)=w(T^{1/2}\|\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{2}\!:}\|_{L^{2}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})})+T^{1/2}\|\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}\|_{L^{2}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

(4.17)

Now let
\bar{w}
solve the Hamiltonian system

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bar{w}(t)&=\bar{p}(t),\\
\partial_{t}\bar{p}(t)&=-\bar{V}(w(t)),\end{aligned}\right.

(4.18)

where

\bar{V}(\bar{w}(t))=-\frac{1}{4}\bar{w}(t)^{4}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{w}(t)^{2}-\mathcal{G}(\bar{w}).

Here
\mathcal{G}
depends on
t
only though
\bar{w}(t)
.
Let
\bar{\theta}_{+}
be the first time that
\bar{w}\geq\delta
.
Observe that
\bar{w}\leq w
which implies
\bar{\theta}_{+}\geq\theta_{+}
.
The conservation of energy
p^{2}/2+\bar{V}
and the initial conditions
p(0)=Q
,
\bar{V}(0)=0
then imply that

\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}=\frac{1}{2}p(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{w}(t)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\bar{w}(t)^{4}-\mathcal{G},

(4.19)

which in turn implies

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{w}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=\bar{p}(t)=\sqrt{Q^{2}+\bar{w}(t)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{w}(t)^{4}+\mathcal{G}}.

(4.20)

We use this to write
\bar{\theta}_{+}
via the line integral

\bar{\theta}_{+}=\int_{0}^{\bar{\theta}_{+}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{w}}{\sqrt{Q^{2}+\bar{w}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{w}^{4}-\mathcal{G}}},

(4.21)

We can then rescale the variable (
\bar{w}=Qz
) to get

\theta_{+}\leq\bar{\theta}_{+}\leq\int_{0}^{\delta Q^{-1}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}-\tfrac{Q^{2}}{2}z^{4}-Q^{-2}\mathcal{G}(z)}}.

(4.22)

We Taylor-expand the function
t\mapsto(1+z^{2}+t)^{-1/2}
around
t=0
.
The zeroth-order term gives

\theta_{+}\approx\int_{0}^{\delta Q^{-1}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}=\arcsinh(\delta Q^{-1}).

(4.23)

4.4
and
4.5
imply that
Q^{-2}\mathcal{G}
is bounded by

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{\leq}}Q^{-1}zT^{1/2}\|\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{2}\!:}\|_{L^{2}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+Q^{-2}T^{1/2}\|\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}\|_{L^{2}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\\
&\leq\beta^{-1/4}z+\beta^{-\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(4.24)

Here we took
\beta^{-\varepsilon}
to be small enough to cancel
T^{1/2}=\sqrt{2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)}
.
The remaining terms of the Taylor expansion are thus bounded by

\begin{split}&\mathrel{\phantom{\leq}}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{k!\,2^{k}}\int_{0}^{\delta Q^{-1}}\frac{(\tfrac{Q^{2}}{2}z^{4}+\beta^{-1/4}z+\beta^{-\varepsilon})^{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{(1+z^{2})^{k+1/2}}\\
&\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{k!\,2^{k}}\int_{0}^{\delta Q^{-1}}\frac{(Q^{2k}z^{4k}+2^{k}\beta^{-k/4}z^{k}+2^{k}\beta^{-\varepsilon k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\max\{1,z^{2k+1}\}}.\end{split}

(4.25)

As we estimate each term of the integral, we finally get an upper error bound

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{k!\,2^{k}}\left[\delta^{2k}+\beta^{-\varepsilon k}\right]\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\delta^{2k}\leq 2\delta^{2},

(4.26)

which holds when
\beta
is large enough.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Under
4.4
and
4.5
, we also have

\int_{0}^{\theta_{+}}w(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq 2(\delta+\delta^{3}).

Proof.

By
Lemmas

4.6
and
4.8
, we can now estimate

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\theta_{+}}w(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t&\leq\int_{0}^{\arcsinh(\delta Q^{-1})+2\delta^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{3Q}{2}\sinh(t),\delta\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{3Q}{2}\int_{0}^{\arcsinh(\delta Q^{-1})}\sinh(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{2\delta^{2}}\delta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{3Q}{2}\sinh(\arcsinh(\delta Q^{-1}))+2\delta^{3}.\qed\end{split}

(4.27)

Lemma 4.10
.

Under
4.4
and
4.5
and
\beta
large,
there exists
C>0
independent of
\beta
such that

\tau_{+}\leq\theta_{+}+C.

Proof.

We can start from (
4.10
) and estimate it from below by

\begin{split}w(t)&=\int_{\theta_{+}}^{t}\int_{\theta_{+}}^{s}[w(r)-w(r)^{3}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\left[-3w(r)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{2}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathinner{:\!(Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&\geq\int_{\theta_{+}}^{t}\int_{\theta_{+}}^{s}w(r)[1-w(r)][1+w(r)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}t\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(t-\theta_{+})^{2}-\frac{\delta}{2},\end{split}

(4.28)

where in the last line we used that
\beta
is sufficiently large.
So for
w(t)\leq 1-\delta
to be satisfied
we must have
\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(t-\theta_{+})^{2}\leq 1-\delta/2
,
which yields
\tau_{+}\leq\theta_{+}+\sqrt{\frac{2-\delta}{\delta^{2}}}
.
∎

4.2
The oscillatory part

Let us then consider the second part of the proof.
We show that
Assumption

4.4
implies
Assumption

4.5
,
and that
Assumption

4.4
holds with large probability.
For the first claim, we use a continuity argument.
We again split the transition into
\mathcal{O}(\log\beta)
and
\mathcal{O}(1)
parts
represented by
\theta_{+}
and
\tau_{+}
.

The existence of
u
, and consequently of
\psi
, follows from an existing result:

Theorem 4.11
.

(
NLW
) has a mild solution
u\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\;H^{-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))

for almost all initial data sampled from the
(\phi^{4}_{2}\times\text{white noise})
measure.

Proof.

Although Oh and Thomann state this result
[
44
, Theorem 1.5]

only in the case of positive mass,
the local well-posedness theory is straightforward to modify:
One substitutes

\begin{split}\partial_{tt}u(x,t)-(m^{2}+\Delta)u(x,t)&=-u(x,t)^{3}\\
&\Downarrow\\
\partial_{tt}u(x,t)+(1-\Delta)u(x,t)&=-u(x,t)^{3}+(m^{2}+1)u(x,t),\end{split}

(4.29)

and modifies the fixpoint argument accordingly.
Existence of the invariant measure follows from
Section

3.1
in the present article,
and the globalization argument then proceeds as is.
(See also
[
7
, Section 4]
for more details
on the local solution theory.)
∎

Since this result is global in time,
it does not matter that we only consider solutions starting from the time they hit the saddle.
By writing the equation in Duhamel form,
we see that the nonlinear term
\psi
satisfies a fixpoint condition
\psi=F[\psi]
.
We can then find a quantitative bound for the solution.
We first derive the bound up to time
\theta_{+}
:

Lemma 4.12
.

Assume that
Assumption

4.4
holds and that
\beta
is large enough.
For any
T^{\ast}\leq 2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)
, the operator

F[\psi](t)\coloneqq-\int_{0}^{\min(t,\theta_{+})}\frac{\sin({\left\langle{\nabla}\right\rangle}_{-}(t-s))}{{\left\langle{\nabla}\right\rangle}_{-}}\proj_{\perp}\mathinner{:\!(w+Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

maps
B(0,\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
into
B(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon})

in the space
C({[0,T^{\ast}]};\;H^{1-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
.

Proof.

Minkowski’s integral inequality
and
H^{-\varepsilon}\to H^{1-\varepsilon}
boundedness of the operator

\proj_{\perp}\sin({\left\langle{\nabla}\right\rangle}_{-}(t-s))/{\left\langle{\nabla}\right\rangle}_{-}
imply that

\left\|F[\psi](t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\min(t,\theta_{+})}\left\|\proj_{\perp}\mathinner{:\!(w+Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}(s)\right\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.30)

Let us then expand the trinomial.
Since
\proj_{\perp}w(s)^{3}
vanishes, we have nine terms to estimate:

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{+}}C\int_{0}^{t}\left[\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{3}\!:}(s)\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}+w(s)\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{2}\!:}(s)\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(4.31)

\displaystyle\mathrel{+}C\int_{0}^{t}\big[\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{2}\!:}(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}+w(s)\|Z(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\|Z(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon}}\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}^{2}\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(4.32)

\displaystyle\mathrel{+}C\int_{0}^{t}\left[w(s)\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}^{2}+\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}^{3}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(4.33)

\displaystyle\mathrel{+}C\int_{0}^{\min(t,\theta_{+})}\left[w(s)^{2}\|Z(s)\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}+w(s)^{2}\|\psi(s)\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.34)

The first line (
4.31
) is bounded by
C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}

thanks to
Assumption

4.4
.
Using also the assumption
\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1-\varepsilon}}<\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
,
we see that
\eqref{eq:corr bounds 2}\leq C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}
.
The
L^{\infty}
bound also gives that
\eqref{eq:corr bounds 3}\leq C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}
.

The last line is the dominating one, since here we get

\eqref{eq:corr bounds 4}\leq C\int_{0}^{\min(t,\theta_{+})}w(s)^{2}\|Z(s)\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+C\delta\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1-\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{t}w(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.35)

By
Assumption

4.4
and
Lemma

4.9
we bound this by

C\delta\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
.
By choosing
\delta
small enough and
\beta
large enough,
the sum of (
4.31
)–(
4.34
)
can be bounded by
\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}/2
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

Lemma

4.12
could also be viewed as the boundedness part of a fixpoint argument for
\psi
.
It is uncertain whether contractivity up to
\mathcal{O}(\log\beta)
horizon times could be proved,
since one would need better control of
w
as a function of
\psi
.
Because of this, we use the
a priori
result

4.11
instead.

Since
\psi=u-Z-w
is continuous in time,

Lemma

4.12
implies that
\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}

cannot violate
Assumption

4.5
until after
w
has already passed
\delta
.
We now extend this up to the time
\tau_{+}
where
w
hits
1-\delta
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Assume that
Assumption

4.4
holds,
and that
\psi
solves (
4.7
) with

\left\|\psi(\theta_{+})\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
.
Then for any

we have

\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\delta}}\leq\beta^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}.

(4.36)

Proof.

We again repeat a continuity argument.
Assume that
\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\beta^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}

for all
\theta_{+}\leq t\leq t_{1}
, where
t_{1}\leq\tau_{+}
.
We can then again use the Duhamel formulation to estimate

\begin{split}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}&=\eqref{eq:corr fixpoint 1}+\int_{\theta_{+}}^{t}\left\|\proj_{\perp}\mathinner{:\!(w+Z+\psi)^{3}\!:}(s)\right\|_{H^{-\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C\beta^{-1+3\varepsilon}+C\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}+C\int_{\theta_{+}}^{t}\left\|\psi(s)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}+C\int_{\theta_{+}}^{t}\left\|\psi(s)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(4.37)

Here we again used the rough estimate
Lemma

4.6
and
\delta\leq 1
.
Now Grönwall’s inequality gives

\sup_{\theta_{+}\leq t\leq t_{1}}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|_{H^{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\exp(C(\tau_{+}-\theta_{+}))\beta^{-1/2+\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1/2+2\varepsilon},

for
\beta
large enough.
Thus the set where (
4.36
) is satisfied is open by continuity of
\psi
.
It is clearly closed, also by continuity of
\psi
.
Since it contains
t=\theta_{+}
it is the whole interval
[\theta_{+},\tau_{+}]
.
∎

The estimate for
\psi
still depends on a growth bound for the linear part
Z

stated in
Assumption

4.4
.
Its validity follows from the following stochastic estimate.

Theorem 4.15
.

The solution
Z
to the linear wave equation (
4.6
) satisfies

\E\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{L^{p}([0,T];\;B^{-\varepsilon}_{p,p})}^{p}\leq C_{p,\varepsilon}T\beta^{-kp/2}

for
k=1,2,3
, all
T>0
, and sufficiently large

.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
7
, Section 4.1]
.
The idea is that
Z
can be decomposed into a stationary Gaussian free field part
and a more regular part bounded uniformly in time.
(In the cited reference, the presence of weights makes the latter bound also depend on

.)
∎

Corollary 4.16
.

Assumption

4.4
is satisfied with probability at least
1-C\beta^{-1/4+3\varepsilon}
.
Consequently,
Theorem

4.2
holds.

Proof.

For the moments of
Z
, we use Markov’s inequality, Besov embedding,
and
Theorem

4.15
to estimate

\displaystyle\Prob\!\left(\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\;\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon})}>\beta^{-k/2+k\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\E\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\;\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon})}^{p}}{\beta^{-kp/2+kp\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T^{1/2-1/p}\E\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{L^{p}([0,T];\;B^{-\varepsilon/2}_{p,p})}^{p}}{\beta^{-kp/2+kp\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{T^{1/2}}{\beta^{kp\varepsilon}}.

(4.38)

Since we set
T=2\delta^{-1/2}\log(\beta)
, we can choose

large enough (depending on
\varepsilon
) so that

\Prob\!\left(\max_{k=1,2,3}\|\mathinner{:\!Z^{k}\!:}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\;\mathcal{C}^{-\varepsilon})}\leq\beta^{-k/2+k\varepsilon}\right)\leq 1-C/\beta.

(4.39)

On the other hand, for the assumption on

we need to use Gaussian asymptotics near

.
Since
\Law(Q)=\Law(\beta^{-1/2}X\mid X>0)
for standard normal variable

,
we have

\displaystyle\Prob(Q\geq\beta^{-3/4+3\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=1-\Prob({\left|X\right|}\leq\beta^{-1/4+3\varepsilon})

(4.40)

\displaystyle\geq 1-C\beta^{-1/4+3\varepsilon}.\qed

.

We have not strived to achieve optimal convergence rate in this section.
The bound on

is the limiting term in the preceding proof,

The argument in

5
TST in three dimensions

In three dimensions the
\phi^{4}
model requires further renormalization.
The truncated and renormalized quartic potential is

\proj_{N}\mathinner{:\!u_{N}^{4}\!:}-\gamma_{N}\proj_{N}\mathinner{:\!u_{N}^{2}\!:}-\delta_{N},

(5.1)

where
\gamma_{N}
and
\delta_{N}
diverge as
N\to\infty
.
Note that these counterterms are in addition to, not in lieu of, the Wick ordering.
Since
\delta_{N}
is a constant, it does not affect the dynamics of (
NLW
),
but it simplifies working with the measure.

While our overall goal is to compute the exactly same quotient (
3.1
)
as in two dimensions,
three issues complicate the details:

•

The Gaussian free field has only
-1/2-\varepsilon
derivatives of regularity;

•

Further Wick powers of the Gaussian free field have even worse regularity;

•

The additional counterterms yield more terms to estimate as we substitute
u=\varphi+1

and as we change the mass of the Wick ordering.

The first two points have been addressed in the literature on stochastic quantization
of the
\phi^{4}_{3}
measure;
in the following, we reuse as much of Barashkov and Gubinelli’s article
[
5
]

as possible.

Let us first establish the form of the partition function.
The following lemma is the direct analogue of
Lemma

3.1
.
The bracketed term in
V_{\beta}
matches the Hamiltonian studied in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The partition function can now be written as

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}{2}=\sqrt{\prod_{{\left|k\right|}\leq N}\frac{2\pi}{\beta L^{3}(2+C_{L}{\left|k\right|}^{2})}}\exp\left(\!-\frac{3C_{N}L^{3}}{2}+\frac{\beta L^{3}}{4}\right)\int_{\mathcal{D}_{+}}\!\!\exp\left(-V_{\beta}(\varphi)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\varphi),

where the potential is

V_{\beta}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big[\lambda\mathinner{:\!\varphi_{N}^{4}\!:_{-}}-\lambda^{2}\gamma_{N,-}\mathinner{:\!\varphi_{N}^{2}\!:_{-}}-\delta_{N,-}\big]\\
+2\sqrt{\lambda}\mathinner{:\!\varphi_{N}^{3}\!:_{-}}-\lambda^{3/2}\gamma_{N,-}\varphi-\frac{\lambda}{4}\gamma_{N,-}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Here

is chosen to match the notation of
[

]

Proof.

We substitute

into the Hamiltonian

H(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\mathinner{:\!u_{N}^{4}\!:}-\lambda^{2}\gamma_{N}\mathinner{:\!u_{N}^{2}\!:}-\frac{\delta_{N}}{\beta}.

(5.2)

The modification of the

and Gaussian terms proceeds

As we then perform the rescaling by

.

We fix the divergent constants as

\displaystyle\gamma_{N,\bullet}=\E_{\bullet}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{0}^{N}(J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\text{ and}

Proof.

We again do an approximation where we first restrict to a slightly smaller “good” set

compare with

.

To show that the remaining “bad” region

The proof translates to three dimensions verbatim.

Quartic terms

The
\mathbb{W}_{N}^{3}I_{N}[v]
term.

By the Itô formula we can write

\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{W}_{N}^{3}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\E\int_{0}^{N}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}\partial_{t}(I_{t}[v])\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\E\int_{0}^{N}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}I_{t}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5.5)

The latter integral is a martingale, and thus vanishes in expectation.
In the first integral we note that
\partial_{t}I_{t}[v]=J_{t}v_{t}

and then use self-adjointness of
J_{t}
.
These observations give us

\lambda\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{W}_{N}^{3}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\lambda\E\int_{0}^{N}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3})v_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.6)

Let us now update our ansatz as

v_{t}=-\lambda J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}+w_{t}.

(5.7)

This means that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}(J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3})^{2}-\lambda(J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3})w_{t}+w_{t}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5.8)

Combining (
5.6
) and (
5.8
) gives

\displaystyle\lambda\E\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{W}_{N}^{3}I_{N}[v]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}}\E\int_{0}^{N}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5.9)

The first term is purely stochastic and is cancelled by the first term of
\delta_{N}

The operator

Lemma 5.16
.

Assume that
\alpha+\gamma<0

\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0

{\left|R(f,g,h)\right|}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{B^{\alpha}_{p_{1},r_{1}}}\left\|g\right\|_{B^{\beta}_{p_{2},\infty}}\left\|h\right\|_{B^{\gamma}_{p_{3},r_{2}}}.

Proof.

[
26

,
with minor modification to use arbitrary

Conclusion

All together, we have now showed that

\eqref{eq:3d variational everything}\geq-\lambda C_{\rho}+(1-\rho)\E\left[\lambda\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}I_{N}[v]^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

As in

\int_{\mathcal{B}_{+}}\exp(-q\kappa\llbracket\hat{\phi}(0)+(1-\delta^{\prime})\sqrt{\beta}\rrbracket^{2})\exp(-qV_{\beta}(\phi))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\phi)\leq\exp\left(\frac{C_{\rho}}{4\beta}\right),

(5.48)

and this finishes the upper bound of
Theorem

5.2
.

5.2
Lower bound for partition function

As a counterpart to (
5.47
), an upper bound for the variational problem is

\eqref{eq:3d variational everything}\leq\lambda C_{\rho}+(1+\rho)\E\left[\lambda\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}I_{N}[v]^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right].

(5.49)

We need to choose a drift
v
such that this expression is finite and of order
\lambda
.
The following argument is based on
[
5
, Section 5]
,
but somewhat simplified since our bounds may freely depend on domain size.
Another minor modification is due to the
J_{t}\mathbb{W}^{2}_{t}
term coming from (
5.39
).

As part of the previous section, we have decomposed the drift as

v_{t}=-\lambda J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}-\lambda J_{t}(\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{N}[v])-\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{2}J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}+g_{t}.

(5.50)

We will first introduce one more regularizing operator.
Its usefulness is due to the following two lemmas
that let us control the ansatz.

Definition 5.20
.

Let
\chi
be a smoothed indicator of
B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
We define
\mathcal{X}_{t}
to be a Fourier multiplier with symbol

\xi\mapsto\chi\!\left(\frac{{\left|\xi\right|}}{(1+\left\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}})^{1/\varepsilon}}\right).

That is,
\mathcal{X}_{t}
restricts frequencies to a ball
whose radius rapidly increases with the norm of
\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}
.

Lemma 5.21
.

We have

\left\|(1-\mathcal{X}_{t})\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-3\varepsilon}}\lesssim 1

uniformly in
t
.

Proof.

Let us recall that if
f
is Fourier-supported outside
B(0,R)
and
\delta>0
,
then Bernstein’s inequality states that

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}\lesssim R^{-\delta}\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s+\delta}_{p,r}}.

(5.51)

We also use the uniform boundedness of projection operators (on
L^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
)
together with Besov embedding to see that
1-\mathcal{X}_{t}
is bounded
from
\mathcal{C}^{-1-2\varepsilon}
to
\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}
.
This gives us the bound

\left\|(1-\mathcal{X}_{t})\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-3\varepsilon}}\lesssim\frac{\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}}}{1+\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}}}.

(5.52)

Thus we are left with a quotient bounded by
1
.
∎

Lemma 5.22
.

We have

\left\|\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1+\varepsilon}}\lesssim 1+\left\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}}^{2}.

Proof.

A converse version of Bernstein’s inequality states that
if
f
is Fourier-supported inside
B(0,R)
, then

\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s+\delta}_{p,r}}\lesssim R^{\delta}\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s}_{p,r}}.

(5.53)

In our case this yields

\left\|\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1+\varepsilon}}\lesssim(1+\left\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}})\left\|\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}}.

(5.54)

We again used uniform boundedness of
\mathcal{X}_{t}
.
∎

Let us then define
\check{v}
as the solution to the equation

\check{v}_{t}=-\lambda J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}-\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{2}J_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}-\lambda J_{t}((1-\mathcal{X}_{t})\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{t}[\check{v}]).

(5.55)

For all

,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and almost all realizations of the random field,
a solution exists in

by Banach’s fixed-point theorem,
at least assuming
\lambda
small enough.
The proof is a modification of the following lemma,
which gives an
L^{4}
estimate:

Lemma 5.23
.

The
L^{4}
norm of this drift satisfies uniformly in
N
the estimate

\E\left\|I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}\lesssim\lambda^{2}.

Proof.

By Minkowski’s integral inequality, we may write

\left\|I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{B^{1/2-2\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\leq\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\lambda J_{t}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}+\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{2}J_{t}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{B^{1/2-2\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
+\lambda\int_{0}^{N}\left\|J_{t}^{2}((1-\mathcal{X}_{t})\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{t}[\check{v}])\right\|_{B^{1/2-2\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5.56)

By
Lemma

2.15
the right-hand side is bounded by

\lambda^{1/2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|J_{t}(\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}+\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2})\right\|_{B^{-1/2-\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1/2+\varepsilon}}\\
+\lambda\int_{0}^{N}\left\|(1-\mathcal{X}_{t})\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{t}[\check{v}]\right\|_{B^{-3/2-\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+\varepsilon}}.

(5.57)

The second term is bounded by

\lambda\int_{0}^{N}\left\|I_{t}[\check{v}]\right\|_{B^{1/2-2\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+\varepsilon}}

(5.58)

by
Lemma

5.21
,
and then Grönwall’s inequality gives the bound

\left\|I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{B^{1/2-2\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\lesssim\lambda^{1/2}\int_{0}^{N}\left\|J_{t}(\mathbb{W}_{t}^{3}+\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2})\right\|_{B^{-1/2-\varepsilon}_{p,p}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1/2+\varepsilon}}.

(5.59)

The fourth power of this expression has finite expectation due to
the end of
[
5
, Lemma 6]
,
and the result then follows from
L^{p}
embedding (
Theorem

2.10
).
∎

As we plug
\check{v}
into (

the remainder is

\check{g}_{t}=\lambda J_{t}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{N}[\check{v}]).

Its

Lemma 5.24
.

We have the uniform-in-

\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\check{g}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\lambda^{3}.

Proof.

Let us first use
Lemma

2.15
to estimate

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|J_{t}(\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{N}[\check{v}])\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\;

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\succ\Theta_{t}I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{H^{-1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+2\varepsilon}}.

(5.61)

By a paraproduct estimate, this is bounded by

\lambda^{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left\|\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1+\varepsilon}}^{2}\left\|I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+2\varepsilon}},

(5.62)

and by

\lambda^{2}\E\int_{0}^{N}\left[\lambda(1+\left\|\mathcal{X}_{t}\mathbb{W}_{t}^{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{-1-\varepsilon}}^{2})^{4}+\lambda^{-1}\left\|I_{N}[\check{v}]\right\|_{L^{2}}^{4}\right]\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{{\left\langle{t}\right\rangle}^{1+2\varepsilon}}.

(5.63)

Now

5
, Lemma 6]
,
whereas

∎

These estimates show that our choice of drift
