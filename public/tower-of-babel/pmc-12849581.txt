The applicability of the Migratest® kit for evaluating the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes in lambs supplemented with a multi-strain probiotic formulation (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium)
Authors: Roman Wójcik, Dawid Tobolski, Grzegorz Zwierzchowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes in lambs supplemented with a multi-strain probiotic formulation (containing three Lactobacillus strains: Lactobacillus plantarum AMT14, Lactobacillus plantarum AMT4, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus AMT15, and one Bifidobacterium animalis AMT30 strain) was evaluated with the use of the Migratest® kit.ResultsThe study found that lambs fed a probiotic supplement had significantly enhanced white blood cell migration to areas of infection. This was measured using the Migratest® kit, which assesses L-selectin expression. Lower L-selectin levels were linked to improved chemotactic activity. Probiotic supplementation resulted in decreased L-selectin expression in lambs compared to the control group, suggesting a potential mechanism for the observed immune benefits.ConclusionThe study found that dietary probiotics can boost the immune system of lambs by increasing the ability of their white blood cells to migrate to sites of infection. Using the Migratest® kit, researchers were able to accurately measure this improvement. This discovery suggests that tested a multi-strain probiotic formulation may have positive effects on the immune systems of other animals as well and warrants further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.Keywords: Lambs, Chemotaxis, Migratest®, Granulocytes, Probiotic

Background

Phagocytosis is the key mechanisms of non-specific immunity which constitutes the first line of defence in the immune response. This process involves two types of phagocytic cells that can identify and eliminate foreign and infected cells. The first type of cells are professional phagocytes, including polymorphonuclear neutrophils, monocytes, monocyte-derived macrophages, and tissue-resident macrophages, that rapidly migrate and accumulate in large quantities at the site of inflammation to protect the organism against invading pathogens. The second type of cells are non-professional phagocytes, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and dendritic cells, that have more limited capabilities, are less effective, and eliminate pathogens at a slower rate than professional phagocytes [1]. Phagocytosis consists of four main stages: chemotaxis and migration, adherence, absorption, and intracellular digestion. As a result, the ingested particle is destroyed, and immune mechanisms are activated [2]. When a phagocyte ingests bacteria or viruses, an epitope, i.e. a portion of a foreign protein, is presented on the surface of an antigen that is recognised by the immune system [3]. Phagocytosis is initiated by chemotaxis, i.e. the directed movement of immunocompetent cells induced by exogenous and endogenous chemotaxins (such as bacterial fMLP, IL-8, leukotriene B4 (LTB4), complement components C5a and C3a), and the presence of receptors for various ligands, including chemotactic factors and adhesion molecules, on the surface of these cells [4, 5]. In addition, during chemotaxis, polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs) secrete serine collagenase and protease enzymes by exocytosis of primary and secondary granules, mainly via secretory vesicles, and these enzymes activate and facilitate the migration of PMNs [6]. Chemotaxis occurs not only during inflammatory or immune responses, but also during the migration of hematopoietic stem cells and lymphocyte recirculation between the blood and lymphoid organs.

Phagocytosis can be analysed with the use of various methods that examine a single or several stages of the process. The most popular analytical techniques rely on light microscopy, spectrophotometry, fluorescence microscopy, and flow cytometry. These methods differ in specificity, accuracy of quantitation, and applicability [7–10]. Microscopic methods are time-consuming, and they are used to examine small cell populations, which compromises reliable assessments of phagocytosis. In the spectrophotometric approach, some chemical substances in samples and reagents can affect light absorbance. In turn, flow cytometry is an increasingly popular method due to its high sensitivity, reliability, reproducibility, and the ability to scan a large number of cells within a short time. Flow cytometry techniques have evolved rapidly in recent years, becoming important analytical tools in both human and veterinary medicine despite certain limitations associated with accurate quantification of internalised particles due to lower spatial resolution, compared with microscopic methods [11, 12]. Flow cytometry is also used to evaluate the activity of phagocytes in different stages of phagocytosis. Several commercial test kits have been developed for this purpose, including the Phagoburst® test kit which analyses oxidative burst activity in phagocytes, the Phagotest® kit which assesses phagocytes’ ability to engulf bacteria, and the Migratest® kit which evaluates the chemotactic activity of phagocytes. These tests have been designed for use in human medicine, but they have been successfully applied in the authors’ previous studies of rats [13], lambs [14], young goats [15, 16], and calves [17, 18]. An analysis of the literature revealed that the only animal study involving the Migratest® kit was conducted on piglets [19], and the only study where this test kit was applied in lambs was performed by the authors [14].

The aim of the present study was to determine the applicability of the Migratest® kit for evaluating the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes in lambs supplemented with a multi-strain probiotic formulation (three Lactobacillus strains: Lactobacillus plantarum AMT14, Lactobacillus plantarum AMT4, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus AMT15, and one Bifidobacterium animalis AMT30 strain) relative to the non-supplemented control group.

Discussion

The present study was undertaken to evaluate the effect of dietary supplementation with a multi-strain probiotic formulation (containing three Lactobacillus strains: Lactobacillus plantarum AMT14, Lactobacillus plantarum AMT4, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus AMT15, and one Bifidobacterium animalis AMT30 strain) on chemotaxis, one of the stages of phagocytosis of peripheral blood granulocytes in lambs.

The chemotactic activity of granulocytes is an important indicator in analyses of immune responses, and it can provide valuable information about the efficacy of probiotic supplements in lambs and the applicability of the Migratest® kit in such analyses. A review of the literature revealed that the influence of probiotics on this parameter of the innate immune response has not been previously investigated in lambs, especially with the use of the Migratest® kit. Humoral and cell-mediated innate immune responses constitute the first line of defence and play a key role in protecting the organism against various pathogens. Chemotaxis is a stage of phagocytosis and an important element of the cell-mediated innate immune response that effectively eliminates pathogens and decreases the incidence of infectious diseases. This process plays a particularly important role in young animals which are more susceptible to negative stimuli and external factors, including bacteria, than adult individuals. Lambs face numerous challenges in early life, which may compromise their growth, feed intake, metabolism, and health. In most cases, these adverse events are highly correlated with immune deficiencies (physiological) resulting from incompletely developed or impaired immune mechanisms [20, 21].

Probiotics are dietary supplements that deliver health-promoting effects and contribute to gut homeostasis. Probiotics modulate the activity of gut microbiota via three mechanisms: by stimulating innate and adaptive immune responses, increasing the bioavailability of nutrients and usable substrates, and decreasing food intolerance [22–26]. Probiotic bacteria have immunomodulatory properties because they communicate and interact with various immune cells, including dendritic cells, lymphocytes, epithelial cells, monocytes, and macrophages [27]. Probiotics appear to be most effective in activating dendritic cells that stimulate and direct the appropriate immune response. Dendritic cells can induce inflammatory responses that involve subpopulations of naive T cells (Th1, Th2, and Th 17) or can inhibit these responses via regulatory T cells (CD4+, CD25+, FOXP3+) produced from the same precursors (Th0 cells), and via interleukin 10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine produced by regulatory T cells [28]. In addition, probiotics can inhibit the inflammatory response by decreasing the expression of TLR on the surface of cells. This is achieved through the secretion of metabolites that inhibit TNF-α and through the inhibition of the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway in enterocytes [29]. According to Maldonado-Galdeano et al. [26], changes in the gut microbiome can modify/switch the immune response from T2 (humoral) to Th1 (cell-mediated), while maintaining the gut immune system’s ability to sustain homeostasis. Probiotics can also indirectly modulate the immune response by stimulating selected subpopulations of immunocompetent cells to produce cytokines [30–33]. The direction of the immune response is determined by the type of applied probiotic because various probiotic strains can stimulate the secretion of different cytokines [26, 34–36]. It should also be noted that the results of a metadata analysis conducted by Rolfe [37] suggest that probiotic strains can exert varied effects on immune functions. Therefore, further research is needed to identify and select the strains of probiotic bacteria that may provide the greatest health benefits to lambs.

The mechanism that stimulates the phagocytic and bactericidal activity of immunocompetent cells in lambs cannot be identified based on the results of this study. However, according to Blum et al. [38], probiotics enhance immunity by increasing the number and activity of immune cells, including granulocytes. Granulocytes, in particular neutrophils, play a key role in the inflammatory response, and their chemotactic activity is a robust indicator of immune system efficiency and function. Granulocytes migrate to the site of infection in response to chemical signals, and they effectively protect the organism against pathogens. Their migration is dependent on a variety of chemoattractants originating from the injured tissue, pathogenic stimuli, other immune cells, or tumours. These chemoattractants can be broadly classified into four groups: chemokines, N-formylated peptides, complement anaphylatoxins, and lipids, which interact through G-coupled protein receptors (GPCRs) [39].

The Migratest® kit is a modern diagnostic tool which supported accurate and reproducible measurements of chemotaxis, namely the ability of peripheral blood granulocytes to migrate in response to chemotactic stimuli. The results of the conducted analyses revealed a significant increase in the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes, expressed by the chemotactic index and the percentage of migrating cells, in the group of experimental lambs supplemented with the multi-strain probiotic formulation relative to the non-supplemented control group. A previous study conducted by the authors on calves [15] demonstrated that the chemotactic index is the most accurate parameter describing the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes.

The results of this experiment cannot be confronted with the findings of other authors because no such studies have been conducted on lambs to date. Other researchers [40–45] found that probiotics exert positive effects on phagocytosis, but specific stages of the process were not indicated, or stages other than chemotaxis were examined. In addition, most of these studies were conducted on humans or, to a limited extent, on various species of animals, but not sheep. The present results corroborate the findings of Lebeer et al. [40] who found that the mobility and phagocytic activity of granulocytes increased in humans whose diets were supplemented with probiotic bacteria of the genus Lactobacillus. Roessler et al. [42] also demonstrated that probiotic lactic acid bacteria can increase the phagocytic activity of granulocytes as well as monocytes in healthy humans. In turn, Park et al. [46] reported an increase in the phagocytic activity of monocytes and macrophages in mice receiving the Lactobacillus fermentum JDFM216 probiotic strain with drinking water. Studies conducted on animal models also revealed that orally administered probiotics confer effective protection against Salmonella typhimurium infections by promoting macrophage activation in the peritoneum and the spleen [47]. Brodzki et al. [48]confirmed that the supplementation of dairy cows’ diets with Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus plantarum, and Rhodopseudomonas palustris strains positively influenced phagocytosis by increasing the percentage and effectiveness of phagocytic granulocytes and monocytes in peripheral blood, and increasing the percentage of granulocytes exhibiting oxidative burst, relative to the non-supplemented control group.

In contrast, Sustrova et al. [49] reported an increase in the apoptotic activity of porcine and human neutrophils cultured in vitro with the addition of Bifidobacterium bifidum, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, and Enterococcus faecium strains relative to cultures without these probiotics. In turn, Saxami et al. [50] observed a significant decrease in the growth and proliferation of Caco-2 cells under the influence of Lactobacillus pentosus B281 and Lactobacillus plantarum B282. In a mouse model, these strains promoted rapid recruitment of leukocytes, mainly neutrophils [50]. These results clearly indicate that the activity of probiotic strains is species-specific and that further detailed research is needed to analyse the unique functions of each strain.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study confirmed that dietary probiotic supplements can positively influence the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes in lambs. The Migratest® kit is a reliable diagnostic tool for evaluating this activity, and these observations inspire further research into the mechanisms by which probiotics affect the immune system in various animal species.

Methods

Experimental design

The study was carried out on a flock of Kamieniec sheep at a farm in Komalwy, located in the Warmia and Mazury region of Poland. Sixteen young rams, offspring of 3-year-old ewes, were selected for the experiment. The lambs were divided into two groups of eight: a control group (Group C) and an experimental group (Group E), using the analogue method based on their body weights at 10 days old. Both groups had similar body weights. At the conclusion of the study, when the lambs were 40 days old, they were returned to the main flock. The lambs were housed in two separate pens, with free movement and access to water without antibiotics throughout the experiment.

In both groups, the ewes and lambs were fed the same diet based on the feeding system used at the farm. The ewes had unlimited access to a total mixed ration (TMR), which consisted of 64% grass silage, 32% maize silage, 3.5% concentrate, and 0.5% Milafos L mineral and vitamin premix. The concentrate feed was made from a mix of ground oats (50%), ground wheat (30%), ground maize (10%), and ground soybeans (10%). The animals also had unrestricted access to Multi-Lisal Se mineral licks. Lambs were naturally fed colostrum within the first hour of life, and for the first 10 days, their diet consisted only of the mother’s milk. From 11 days old, the lambs were given ad libitum access to concentrate feed. Group C lambs were fed a basic diet, while Group E lambs received the same diet with added probiotics, as described below.

Starting at 11 days of age (defined as experimental day 0), the diet of Group E lambs was supplemented with a multi-strain probiotic. This probiotic formulation included four bacterial strains: three Lactobacillus strains (Lactobacillus plantarum AMT14, Lactobacillus plantarum AMT4, and Lactobacillus rhamnosus AMT15) and one strain of Bifidobacterium animalis AMT30 (Nature Science, Stawiguda, Poland), with a viable count of 1.0 × 10^9 CFU/g of each strain. The probiotic was administered orally to each lamb daily in an aqueous solution according to this schedule: from days 11 to 20 (experimental days 0–10), each lamb received 10 mL of the solution containing 1 g of the probiotic; from days 21 to 30 (experimental days 11–20), 10 mL with 2 g of the probiotic; and from days 31 to 40 (experimental days 21–30), 10 mL with 3 g of the probiotic.

Sample collection

Blood samples, approximately 10 mL each, were collected from the jugular vein at three points during the study: at the start of the experiment before probiotic supplementation (experimental day 0), and again on experimental days 15 and 30. These samples were used to assess the chemotactic activity of peripheral blood granulocytes via flow cytometry using the Migratest® kit. All blood collections took place prior to the morning feeding.

Determination of the chemotactic activity of blood granulocytes in lambs with the migratest® kit (Glycotope Biotechnology GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany)

The assay was carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions. Leukocyte-rich plasma (LRP) was obtained from heparinized whole blood through spontaneous sedimentation and used in the experiment. LRP from each sample was placed in two cell culture inserts with 3.0 μm pores. One insert was transferred to a well containing the chemoattractant N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLP), while the other insert was placed in a buffer solution without the chemotactic peptide, serving as a negative control. Chemotaxis was allowed to occur for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were then stained for 10 min with an antibody reagent (FITC-labeled anti-CD62L) containing counting beads. Before flow cytometry analysis, a vital DNA dye was added for 5 min on ice. The samples were analyzed by flow cytometry to quantify the migrated granulocytes and to assess L-selectin shedding from activated cells. Results were expressed as the chemotactic index, calculated by dividing the number of cells that migrated towards fMLP by those that migrated in its absence [51].

FACS acquisition and analysis

Flow cytometry was conducted using a FACSCanto II cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data acquisition was done through FACSDiva software version 6.1.3 (BD Biosciences, USA), and the analysis was performed using FlowJo 10 software (Tree Star, USA). The photomultiplier tube (PMT) was initialized with cytometry setup and tracking beads (CST; BD Biosciences, USA). Granulocytes and counting beads were identified in the PerCP-to-SSC scatter plot and visualized in the FSC-to-SSC scatter plot. Data collection continued until exactly 2,000 events were captured in the counting beads region. The number of granulocytes was then determined by comparing the count from the control sample to the positive control sample stimulated with fMLP. Simultaneously, the reduction in L-selectin expression was measured. The downregulation of this cell adhesion molecule directly correlates with granulocyte activation in response to chemotactic factors. Granulocyte activation can also be tracked by observing changes in cell shape, which are detected as shifts in forward scatter signals during flow cytometry. (Fig. 6).

Statistical analysis

Data were organized in Excel spreadsheets for statistical analysis. To determine if parametric methods were appropriate, the normality of data distributions and the homogeneity of variances across all study groups were first evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and the Levene test for variance homogeneity. Parametric tests were applied when the p-values indicated no significant deviations from normality or homogeneity. When these assumptions were met, parametric analyses were conducted. The numerical data are presented as arithmetic means ± standard deviation (SD). To identify significant differences, two-way ANOVA for orthogonal designs was used. Post-hoc analysis was performed using Dunnett’s test to evaluate changes over time in group E, comparing baseline (day 0) with results from days 15 and 30. The significance thresholds were set at: A p < 0.05; B p < 0.01; C p < 0.001; D p < 0.0001. Tukey’s test was also applied to compare group E with group C at each time point, with results reported to three decimal places. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 7 software.
