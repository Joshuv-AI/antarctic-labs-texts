Abstract
. We establish a right-exactness theorem for the cross-effects of bifunctors, and consequently for cosmash products, in Janelidze–Márki–Tholen semi-abelian categories. This result motivates an intrinsic definition of a bilinear product, a tensor-like operation on objects of a category, constructed in terms of limits and colimits. Given two objects in the category, their bilinear product is the abelian object obtained as the cosmash product in the category of two-nilpotent objects of the reflections of these objects. In many concrete cases, this operation, applied to a pair of abelian objects, captures a classical tensor product. We explain this by proving a recognition theorem, which states that any symmetric, bi-cocontinuous bifunctor on an abelian variety of algebras can be recovered as the bilinear product within a suitable semi-abelian variety, namely of algebras over a certain 2 2 -nilpotent operad. In other words, the extra structure carried by such a bifunctor on the abelian variety (for instance, a tensor product, known in the literature) is encoded by means of a surrounding semi-abelian variety whose abelian core is the original variety. We illustrate the construction with several examples, develop its basic properties, and compare it to the semi-abelian analogue of the Brown–Loday non-abelian tensor product. As an application, we present a categorical version of Ganea’s six-term exact homology sequence. Finally, we characterise abelian extensions via internal action cores, yielding explicit descriptions of cosmash products and bilinear products in certain categories of representations.

Introduction

In a pointed variety of universal algebras
\mathbb{V}
, let the expression
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
denote a term with distinct variables
x_{1}
, …,
x_{m}
. Let
0
denote the unique constant in the theory of
\mathbb{V}
. In particular, this implies that
t(0,\dots,0)=0
. We say that

is a

if
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0
whenever

, for any
1\leq i\leq m
. For instance, in the theory of groups (where the constant is denoted
1
), we may take
t(x,y)\coloneq[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
because
t(x,1)=1=t(1,y)
. The term
u(x,y,x)\coloneq[x,[y,z]]
is another commutator term. It is well known that in the variety of groups, any commutator term can be written as a product of such basic commutator terms; and in fact, reciprocally, many of the “usual” commutators naturally occurring in pointed varieties of universal algebras may indeed be characterised by means of abstract commutator terms in the above sense. In the case of commutative associative algebras, for instance,
v(x,y)\coloneq xy
is a commutator term, because
0y=0=0x
. Likewise, so is
w(x,y,z)\coloneq[x,[y,z]]
in the case of Lie algebras. Commutator terms generate commutator objects: for example, the
commutator subgroup

[X,X]\coloneq\langle[x,y]\mid\text{$x$, $y\in X$}\rangle
of a group
X
vanishes if and only if
X
is an abelian group.

What interests us here is the remarkable observation (first examined in
[
Mac60
]
) that, in the seemingly quite remote (abelian!) situation of a tensor product
A\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}B
of two abelian groups, the pure tensors

behave precisely like this. Not only do we have that
a\otimes 0=0=0\otimes b
for all

and

A\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}B
may be recovered as a commutator object
in the variety of nil-
2
groups
.

The aim of this article is to explore the idea of viewing tensor products as commutators from a categorical perspective, using it to prove some fundamental general results in homological algebra involving tensors, which so far have remained out of reach of categorical algebra—while at the same time sketching the connections with abstract polynomial functor calculus. As we shall see, a general version of the nilpotency condition that occurs in the case of groups is what extends the mere preservation of zeroes in both variables of the product to
bilinearity
. A key tool here is the concept of a
cosmash product
.

Cosmash products

In the article
[
CJ03
]

are studied in a general, categorical setting, Carboni and Janelidze remark that for algebraic objects, the dual concept is in fact far more interesting. Indeed, unlike in topology, smash products in algebraic categories tend to be trivial, while cosmash products turn out to have many uses, the limits of which are still unknown today. It is already well established that the cosmash product of two objects in a semi-abelian category

in order to capture aspects of Higgins’s approach to commutator theory

This naturally leads to the question of how to capture other tensor products occurring “in nature” as a cosmash product. Assuming, for instance, that the classical tensor product of modules over a commutative ring is a cosmash product, then what we must ask ourselves is:
Where?
or, more precisely:
In which category?
Answering this question is one of our two main goals. In particular, motivated by the fundamental example of the tensor product of abelian groups, we obtain our first main result: a recognition theorem (Theorem

Our second main goal, to which we devote a large portion of the text, is to answer the converse question: given a (semi-abelian) category
\mathbb{X}

intrinsic symmetric bilinear product
playing the multiple roles for

Given some known tensor product in an abelian category, in which category may it be seen as a cosmash product?

Bilinear cosmash products and two-nilpotent categories

Recall that the tensor product of modules over a commutative ring

Limitations

Of course, not all tensor products “in nature” are readily captured as cosmash products in some two-nilpotent semi-abelian category
\mathbb{X}
. An important limitation of our approach is that a bilinear cosmash product of objects in
\mathbb{X}
will automatically be an abelian object in
\mathbb{X}
, which is a requirement that seems hard to satisfy in certain cases. One “bad” example is the tensor product of associative algebras, which is supposed to be again an associative algebra—but certainly not an abelian one, which means that it has a trivial multiplication. We do not know in which way this example fits the theory, or whether it fits the theory at all.

In fact, our original motivating example taken from
[
CJ03
]
, the category of commutative rings, does not follow the pattern we just sketched: here the cosmash product in the category itself is already the tensor product in
\mathsf{CRng}
. This example is further developed in
[
RVdLV23
]
.

Another such example is the Brown–Loday
non-abelian tensor product

[
BL87
]
of two groups mutually and compatibly acting on each other, where again it is not obvious which category to choose, or whether such a category can be found at all. However, in this case, one connection between the two tensor products is clear, because the bilinear product of two abelian groups is the non-abelian tensor product of these groups, when viewed as acting trivially on each other. As proved in
[
dMVdL20
, Theorem 7.4]
, this result stays valid for the bilinear product in any
algebraically coherent

[
CGVdL15
]
semi-abelian category: given two abelian objects acting trivially on each other, their non-abelian tensor product as introduced in
[
dMVdL20
]
is precisely the bilinear product. In such categories, the bilinear product may be viewed as a special case of the non-abelian tensor product.

Finally, other examples are just complicated: for instance, finding a precise formula for the bilinear product of internal actions in a semi-abelian category is difficult, at least in a category which is not
locally algebraically cartesian closed
—cf. Section
11
. This will be a subject of future work.

2.
Two-nilpotency and the bilinear product

A
Birkhoff subcategory

[
JK94
, Section 1.2]
of a semi-abelian category is a full reflective subcategory closed under subobjects and regular quotient objects.

In what follows, we consider certain Birkhoff subcategories any semi-abelian category has, each determined by objects satisfying a commutator condition.

2.1.
Abelian objects.

An object
A
of a semi-abelian category
\mathbb{X}
is
abelian
when it admits a (necessarily unique) structure of internal abelian group. It is well known that this happens precisely when
[A,A]=0
, or equivalently, when the codiagonal morphism
\nabla_{A}=\langle 1_{A},1_{A}\rangle\colon A+A\to A
factors through the regular epimorphism
\textstyle{r_{A,A}\colon A+A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A\times A}
(where the resulting map
A\times A\to A
is the internal abelian group structure), cf. Subsection
1.1
.

The (abelian) Birkhoff subcategory of
\mathbb{X}
determined by the abelian objects is denoted
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
and called the
abelian core
of
\mathbb{X}
. The reflector
\ab\colon{\mathbb{X}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})}
is determined by
\ab(X)=X/[X,X]
. Note that all objects of
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
are indeed abelian, and that
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
is the largest Birkhoff subcategory with this property.

2.2.
Two-nilpotent objects.

Likewise, an object
U
is called
two-nilpotent
or
nilpotent of class
2
when the ternary commutator
[U,U,U]
vanishes. This determines a Birkhoff subcategory of
\mathbb{X}
denoted
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
and called the
two-nilpotent core
of
\mathbb{X}
. The reflector
\mathrm{Nil}_{2}\colon{\mathbb{X}\to\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})}
is given by
\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(X)=X/[X,X,X]
.

2.3.
Two-nilpotent categories.

A semi-abelian category is called
two-nilpotent
when all ternary cosmash products in it vanish. This happens precisely when its objects are two-nilpotent, because for any three given objects
U
,
V
and
W
, the object
U\diamond V\diamond W
is the commutator
[\iota_{1}(U),\iota_{2}(V),\iota_{3}(W)]\mathrel{\lhd}U+V+W
of
U
,
V
and
W
, smaller than the commutator
[U+V+W,\;U+V+W,\;U+V+W]
, which is trivial when
U+V+W
is two-nilpotent. As a consequence, the two-nilpotent core
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
of any semi-abelian category
\mathbb{X}
is indeed two-nilpotent. The converse is immediate from the definition. Similarly to what happens for
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
, since
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
includes all two-nilpotent objects of
\mathbb{X}
, it is the largest two-nilpotent Birkhoff subcategory of
\mathbb{X}
.

2.4.
Products and coproducts

Since the right adjoint inclusions preserve all limits, the product in
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
or in
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
is given by the product in
\mathbb{X}
. For coproducts, however, this is not the case. In
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
the coproduct is the product
A\times B
which we denote
A\oplus B
, the biproduct of
A
and
B
. And in order to avoid confusion with the coproduct
X+Y
in
\mathbb{X}
, we write
U+_{2}V
for the sum in
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
. Always
\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(X)+_{2}\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(Y)
so that when
A
and
B
are abelian (or two-nilpotent),
A+_{2}B=\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(A+B)
.

2.9.
The bilinear product on a semi-abelian category.

On a semi-abelian category
\mathbb{X}
, the

is the extension of the cosmash product in

\otimes_{\mathbb{X}}\colon\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}\colon(X,Y)\mapsto X\otimes_{\mathbb{X}}Y\coloneq\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(X)\diamond_{2}\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(Y)\text{.}

This bifunctor

\mathsf{Nil}_{2}\colon{\mathbb{X}\to\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{X})}
preserves binary sums.

When the category
\mathbb{X}

2.10.

It is immediately clear that bilinear products are symmetric—there exists a natural

\textstyle{\tau=\tau_{X,Y}\colon X\otimes Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

We shall further see that

\mathbb{X}

3.1.

5.
Examples of bilinear products

5.1.
Groups and the tensor product of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.

In the category of groups,

X\diamond Y=\langle[x,y]\mid\text{$x\in X$, $y\in Y$}\rangle

where
[x,y]\coloneq xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
, and
X\diamond Y\diamond Z
contains all

for

,

and

. A group is

[
Mac60
]
and
[
HV11
, Proposition 2.3]
that for any two groups

and

coincides with

\mathbb{Z}
-modules of their abelianisations. Following Section
2.4
, here a generator

of
\ab(X)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\ab(Y)
corresponds to the equivalence class of
[x,y]
in the quotient
X+_{2}Y=\mathrm{Nil}_{2}(X+Y)
of
X+Y
. As explained in
[
dMVdL20
]
, this result also follows from its being a special case of the Brown–Loday non-abelian tensor product
[
BL87
]
.

Note that the bilinear product in
\mathsf{Gp}
is associative. However, since non-abelian groups exist, the bilinear product does in general not have a unit. Of course, when

is abelian, we

have that

. For us this is an argument in favour of the idea that the bilinear product should be considered as an operation on the abelian core. It is also worth noticing that the functor

is precisely the abelianisation functor, which follows from the fact that in

has

as a unit. As mentioned before, this remains true in other cases where a unit exists for the bilinear product viewed as a product on
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})

For
A

5.3.
Commutative associative algebras: the tensor product of

Given a unitary commutative ring

[

]
where this is explained.

Then what is the bilinear product in

and

Here, the symmetry isomorphism

as in Subsection
5.1
, the induced symmetry isomorphisms are different, which proves that the shape of the symmetry isomorphism depends on the surrounding semi-abelian category in which the bilinear product is computed.

5.4.
Non-commutative algebras.

The case of non-commutative algebras is different, and provides an example of a situation where we do
not
find the ordinary tensor product of
R
-modules out of a construction in a bigger category of which
\mathsf{Mod}_{R}
forms the abelian core. This illustrates how the bilinear product depends on the surrounding category.

In the category
\mathsf{Alg}_{R}
of non-unitary non-commutative associative algebras over a commutative unitary ring
R
, sums are computed as follows
[
Ber65
]
. Given algebras
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
we write
(A_{1},A_{2})_{n}
for the alternating tensor product over
R
of length
n
: so
(A_{1},A_{2})_{0}=R
,
(A_{1},A_{2})_{1}=A_{1}
,
(A_{1},A_{2})_{2}=A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}
,
(A_{1},A_{2})_{3}={A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}\otimes_{R}A_{1}}
, etc. Then the coproduct of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
is

A_{1}+A_{2}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}\bigl((A_{1},A_{2})_{n}\oplus(A_{2},A_{1})_{n}\bigr)

with multiplication
(A_{i_{1}},A_{i_{2}})_{k}\otimes(A_{j_{1}},A_{j_{2}})_{l}\to(A_{i_{1}},A_{i_{2}})_{m}
induced either by the multiplication on
A_{j_{1}}
(if the last term in the tensor product
(A_{i_{1}},A_{i_{2}})_{k}
is
A_{j_{1}}
; then we take
m=k+l-1
) or by identities (if not; then we take
m=k+l
).

It follows that for three given algebras
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
, their cosmash product
A_{1}\diamond A_{2}\diamond A_{3}
is a direct sum

\bigoplus_{n\geq 3}(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})_{n}

where
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})_{n}
sums all possible tensor products of length
n
in
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
such that any two adjacent factors are different. So, for instance, the products
{A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}\otimes_{R}A_{3}\otimes_{R}A_{2}}
and
A_{3}\otimes_{R}A_{2}\otimes_{R}A_{1}\otimes A_{2}
are terms in
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})_{4}
, but
A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}\otimes_{R}A_{3}\otimes_{R}A_{3}
and
A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}\otimes_{R}A_{1}\otimes_{R}A_{2}
are not.

When computing the bilinear product
X\otimes Y
, we now have to divide the images of
X\diamond X\diamond Y
and
X\diamond Y\diamond Y
out of
X\diamond Y=\bigoplus_{n\geq 2}\bigl((X,Y)_{n}\oplus(Y,X)_{n}\bigr)
. It is clear that this quotient simply makes the terms of length three and higher vanish, so

\displaystyle X\otimes Y

\displaystyle\cong\bigl(\ab(X)\otimes_{R}\ab(Y)\bigr)\oplus\bigl(\ab(Y)\otimes_{R}\ab(X)\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\bigl(\ab(X)\otimes_{R}\ab(Y)\bigr)\text{.}

In this example, the symmetry isomorphism
A\otimes B\to B\otimes A
sends
(a\otimes b)\oplus(b^{\prime}\otimes a^{\prime})
to
(b^{\prime}\otimes a^{\prime})\oplus(a\otimes b)
.

When
R
is a field, we find an example showing that a bilinear product need not be associative, nor have a unit. On the one hand, it suffices to compare the products
X\otimes(Y\otimes Z)
and
(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z
; while on the other, we note that if
I
is a unit, then

R=I\otimes R=(I\otimes_{R}R)\oplus(R\otimes_{R}I)=I\oplus I\text{,}

which gives a contradiction with the fact that
R
is one-dimensional as a vector space over itself.

5.5.
Lie algebras and the tensor product of
R
-modules.

Lie algebras over
R
behave quite similarly to commutative associative algebras—here also we regain the tensor product of
R
-modules when restricting the tensor to abelian objects. This may be explained by the fact that Lie brackets are antisymmetric. The two-nilpotent core of
\mathsf{Lie}_{R}
consists of algebras
(X,[-,-])
where
[x,[y,z]]=0
and
[x,y]=-[y,x]
for all
x
,
y
,
z\in X
. Hence the coproduct
X+_{2}Y
in
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathsf{Lie}_{R})
of two abelian Lie algebras
A
and
B
is
A\oplus B\oplus(A\otimes_{R}B)
, so that
A\diamond B
is
A\otimes_{R}B
, and
X\otimes Y\cong\ab(X)\otimes_{R}\ab(Y)
for all
X
and
Y
in
\mathsf{Lie}_{R}
. Here the symmetry isomorphisms are as in Subsection
5.3
.

5.6.
Leibniz algebras.

Similarly to the difference between commutative and non-commutative algebras, we may now compare the Lie algebra with the Leibniz algebra tensor product. Leibniz algebras being “non-antisymmetric Lie algebras”, we may expect a similar result. Recall from
[
Lod93
]
that for a field
R
, an object
X
in
\mathsf{Leib}_{R}
is an
R
-vector space with a bilinear bracket
[-,-]\colon X\times X\to X
that satisfies the identity

[x,[y,z]]=[[x,y],z]-[[x,z],y]

for
x
,
y
,
z\in X
. And indeed, using that the coproduct
A+_{2}B
in
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathsf{Leib}_{R})
of two abelian Leibniz algebras
A
and
B
is
A\oplus B\oplus(A\otimes_{R}B)\oplus(B\otimes_{R}A)
, in accordance with
[
CP00
]
, we obtain the isomorphism

X\otimes Y\cong\bigl(\ab(X)\otimes_{R}\ab(Y)\bigr)\oplus\bigl(\ab(Y)\otimes_{R}\ab(X)\bigr)\text{.}

The symmetry isomorphisms are as in Subsection
5.4
.

5.7.
Heyting semilattices.

The semi-abelian category
\mathsf{HSLat}
of Heyting semilattices is arithmetical
[
Ped95
,
BB04
,
Joh04
]
, which implies that the only abelian object in
\mathsf{HSLat}
is the zero semilattice. As a consequence, all bilinear products in
\mathsf{HSLat}
are trivial. For the same reason, bilinear products vanish in the dual of the category of pointed objects in any topos
[
Bou04b
]
, in the categories of
boolean
and
von Neumann regular
rings
[
BB04
]
and in the category of
C^{*}
-algebras
[
GR04
]
.

5.8.
Abelian categories.

On the other end of the spectrum we have the context of abelian categories. Here also all bilinear products vanish, because all cosmash products are trivial. To obtain the bilinear product of modules over a commutative ring
R
, those modules may be considered as commutative algebras over
R
(with a trivial multiplication
x\cdot y=0
) as in
5.3
.

5.9.
Sheaves of abelian groups.

The usual tensor product of sheaves of abelian groups (for a certain topology
\mathscr{T}
) coincides with the bilinear product in the semi-abelian category
\mathsf{Sh}_{\mathscr{T}}(\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathsf{Gp}))
of sheaves of two-nilpotent groups. Indeed, the tensor product of two sheaves is the sheafification of their tensor product as presheaves, which is taken pointwise. Following
5.1
, these pointwise tensor products may be computed in
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathsf{Gp})
. Now it suffices to note that sheafification is an exact functor. Note that here the symmetry isomorphisms are pointwise as in Subsection
5.1
.

5.10.
Beck modules

In Section
11
, we study the bilinear product in categories of internal actions, which leads to a tensor product of Beck modules
[
Bec03
,
BB69
]
. We recover the known tensor products of representations of groups and Lie algebras as special cases.

5.11.

5.12.
Internal precrossed modules

The example of internal precrossed modules in a semi-abelian category is already implicit in the above: now we do not need to reflect to internal groupoids, so the bilinear product of two abelian precrossed modules
(G,A,\partial\colon{A\to G})
and
(H,B,\delta\colon{B\to H})
simply is the normalisation of the reflexive graph (
B
), which is the abelian (pre)crossed module

(G\otimes H,\;(G\otimes B)\oplus(A\otimes H)\oplus(A\otimes B),\;\langle 1_{G}\otimes\delta,\partial\otimes 1_{H},\partial\otimes\delta\rangle)\text{.}

This agrees with the definition proposed in
[
AL06
]
in the case of crossed modules of groups.

The difference between
5.12
and
5.11
provides another example showing that the bilinear product of objects in an abelian category may depend on the surrounding semi-abelian category in which it is computed—note that an abelian precrossed module is automatically an abelian crossed module, so that the two abelian cores coincide.

Also in this case, the symmetry isomorphism is induced from the base category.

7.
Varieties of algebras, in general

In this section we prove some general results on bilinear products in semi-abelian varieties of algebras. We work towards the Recognition Theorem
7.6
which characterises any symmetric bi-cocontinous bifunctor on an abelian variety as the bilinear product of a variety of algebras over an operad induced by the given bifunctor.

7.1.
Properties of tensoring with a fixed object of a variety

We resume the discussion at the end of Section
2
in the case of a variety of algebras
\mathbb{V}
. Then the functors
X\otimes(-)\colon\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
do not just preserve all finite sums, but are actually cocontinuous:

Proposition 7.2
.

If
\mathbb{V}
is a semi-abelian variety of algebras and
X\in\mathrm{Ob}{(\mathbb{V})}
then the functor
X\otimes(-)\colon{\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})}
preserves all colimits.

Proof.

In any variety of algebras, filtered colimits commute with finite limits (see, for instance,
[
Bor94
]
) so
X\otimes(-)
preserves filtered colimits (because so do the abelianisation functor and the functors
\ab(X)+(-)
and
\ab(X)\times(-)
in the category
\mathsf{Nil}_{2}(\mathbb{V})
) and, hence, arbitrary sums, because they are filtered colimits of finite sums, which are preserved by the argument above. It also preserves all coequalisers, since
X\otimes(-)
factors through the abelianisation functor
\ab\colon{\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})}
, coequalisers in
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
are calculated as the cokernel of a difference, and the functor
{X\otimes(-)}\colon{\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})}
is right exact by Proposition
4.12
.
∎

Thus in view of the symmetry of the tensor product (Subsection
2.10
) it is bi-cocontinous.

Combining this with the Special Adjoint Functor Theorem, we see that the functors of the form
X\otimes(-)\colon{\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})}
are always left adjoints. This follows from the Special Adjoint Functor Theorem combined with
[
AR94
, Theorem 1.58]
which explains that any variety of algebras is well-co-powered. Note that as a generating set, we may use the free object on a single generator. Thus we proved:

Proposition 7.3
.

For any object
X
of a semi-abelian variety
\mathbb{V}
, the functor

X\otimes(-)\colon{\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})}

is a left adjoint. ∎

In case the bilinear product
\otimes
admits a unit
I
when restricted to
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
, then the functor
I\otimes(-)\colon\mathbb{V}\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
is naturally isomorphic to abelianisation. Note that such a unit need not exist in general: see, for instance, the example of associative non-commutative algebras in Subsection
5.4
. However, when a unit does exist, we find a closed symmetric monoidal structure on
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
, as soon as the product is associative:

Proposition 7.4
.

If
\mathbb{V}
is a semi-abelian variety such that the bilinear product
\otimes
is associative and admits a unit
I
when restricted to
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V})
, then
(\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{V}),\otimes,I)
is a symmetric monoidal closed category. ∎

7.5.
Realisability of bilinear bifunctors on abelian varieties as cosmash products

We are now ready to give a necessary and sufficient condition for a bilinear (that is, biadditive) bifunctor on an abelian variety

A first simplification occurs when

For the forgetful functor

[

—when

is a variety of universal algebras. Examples of semi-abelian (LACC) categories are relatively scarce, though. The only ones currently known in the literature are: essentially affine categories (which include all additive categories)
[

; internal groups in a cartesian closed category with pullbacks (which include the examples of classical groups, crossed modules and cocommutative Hopf algebras)
[
BG12
,
GMVdL18
]
; and internal Lie algebras in an additive cocomplete symmetric closed monoidal category (including classical Lie algebras)
[

]
. On the other hand, almost any other known semi-abelian category is known

to be (LACC): see, for instance
[

]
where the context of varieties of non-associative algebras over a field is treated in detail. There it is shown that over a field of characteristic zero, the variety of Lie algebras is the only non-abelian example.

Example 11.2

(Lie algebra representations)
.

We view a representation
(A,\xi)
of an
R
-Lie algebra
X
as a morphism
\xi\colon X\to\gl(A)
where

denotes the Lie algebra of linear endomorphisms of the

X

Recall that a general
X

We view the bilinear product of the representations

Upon taking the kernel of

Let us verify that it does indeed satisfy the universal property of a coproduct of

and

are equivariant with respect to the given

-actions. Now given an

-action
(C,\theta)
and equivariant Lie algebra morphisms

is equivariant with respect to

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle\bigl((\xi+_{2}\zeta)(x)(a,b,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\langle f,g\rangle\Bigl(\xi(x)(a),\zeta(x)(b),\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\bigl(\xi(x)(a_{i})\otimes b_{i}+a_{i}\otimes\zeta(x)(b_{i})\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=f(\xi(x)(a))+g(\zeta(x)(b))+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\bigl([f(\xi(x)(a_{i})),g(b_{i})]+[f(a_{i}),g(\zeta(x)(b_{i}))]\bigr)

where the fourth equality follows because

12.
Further questions and remarks

We end this article with a few questions which remain open for now, and which in our opinion might lead to interesting further investigations.

12.1.
Bilinear products of representations

In Section
11
we explained that if
\mathbb{X}
is a locally algebraically cartesian closed semi-abelian category, then for any object
G
of
\mathbb{X}
, the bilinear product of two
G
-representations is the bilinear product of the underlying objects, equipped with an appropriate
G
-action. The general situation, for arbitrary semi-abelian categories, appears to be much more complicated. It would seem interesting to obtain an explicit description of how to tensor two representations even in a category which is not (LACC).

On the other hand, an explicit description of the bilinear product of representations of cocommutative Hopf algebras is within reach, because the category of cocommutative Hopf algebras over a field is semi-abelian
[
GKV16
,
GSV19
]
and locally algebraically cartesian closed as explained above.

12.2.
Tensor products of topological vector spaces

We defined the bilinear product in the context of a semi-abelian category, but this definition works as well for homological categories
[
BB04
]
with finite sums. Hence we may ask the question, what it amounts to in the case of topological abelian groups or topological vector spaces. Is it a known tensor product? Note that only the nature of the topology is an issue, since algebraically these bilinear products are the classical tensor products.

12.3.
When is the functor
X\otimes(-)
idempotent?

By Proposition
7.3
, for any object
X
of a semi-abelian variety
\mathbb{X}
, the functor
X\otimes(-)\colon{\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})\to\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})}
is a left adjoint. We may wonder when is it idempotent, so that it defines a reflector to an appropriate subcategory of
\mathsf{Ab}(\mathbb{X})
. A study of the properties of this subcategory—for instance, when is it Birkhoff?—may eventually lead to a description of the derived functors of the functor
X\otimes(-)
in terms of higher Hopf formulae following
[
EGVdL08
,
EG15
]
.

12.4.
When is the bilinear product monoidal?

In this article, the main property we want a product on a category to satisfy is bilinearity. Associativity and existence of a unit are secondary here, and do indeed not need to hold in general, as we have seen in Section
5
. However, it would seem interesting to have a precise characterisation of those semi-abelian categories where the bilinear product defines a symmetric monoidal (closed) structure on the abelian core—cf. Proposition
7.4
.
