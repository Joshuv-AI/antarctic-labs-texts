KVASIR: A backscattering neutron spectrometer for hard condensed matter at ESS Preprint: APS/123-QED
Amalie F. Davidsen, Kristine M. L. Krighaar, Pascale P. Deen, Kim Lefmann

Abstract
We present the instrument concept for KVASIR, a backscattering indirect time-of-flight neutron spectrometer for the European Spallation Source (ESS). KVASIR will probe low lying excitations of single crystal hard condensed matter that many advanced technologies rely upon. The instrument is simultaneously optimized for high resolution of energy- and momentum transfer, δ \delta E = 4 ​ μ 4\ \mu eV and δ ​ 𝐪 \delta\bf q = 0.03 0.03 Å -1 (FWHM), respectively, while ensuring perturbation by high magnetic fields. Detailed ray tracing simulations, considering the true geometry and aberrations of the analyser configuration, achieve δ \delta E = 5.7 ​ μ 5.7\ \mu eV and δ ​ 𝐪 \delta\bf q = 0.03 0.03 Å -1 . The proposed concept has room for polarisation analysis and extreme sample environments. Inelastic neutron scattering studies of single crystal hard condensed matter, with a particular focus on quantum, at yet unseen spatial and dynamic precision under extreme conditions are hereby enabled.

II
Analytical considerations

Prior to simulating the details of the instrument with ray tracing techniques, we provide some analytic considerations. Primary and secondary resolutions in energy and momentum transfer are added in quadrature to determine the final resolutions. All resolution values in this paper are written as FWHM (given for any line shape as
2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the standard deviation).

II.1
Analytical energy resolution

The resolution of the primary spectrometer is determined by the opening time of the pulse shaping choppers derived from the chopper blade parameters
Vickery and Deen 2014
, the incident energy and wavelength,
E_{\rm i}
and
\lambda_{\rm i}
, and the pulse shaping chopper to sample distance,
L_{\rm i}
. The contribution from the variation in flight path length is neglected for this analytical calculation. We assume an opening time of 100
\mu
s, as a high resolution mode, with a pulse shaping chopper to sample distance of
L_{\rm i}
= 155.5 m, and a final analyser energy
E_{\rm f}
= 1.95 meV.
The time distribution shaped by the pulse shaping choppers, which consist of two co-rotating blades, is given by the parameters
\delta t
and
\Delta t
that define the time distribution derived from

\displaystyle\Delta t=\frac{\phi}{4\pi\nu},\ \ \ \delta t=\frac{1}{2\pi\nu}\frac{W/2}{R-H},

(1)

with
W=7.0
cm the width of the chopper blade,
R
= 35 cm is the chopper radius,
H
= 5.4 cm is the height of the beam and
\nu
= 196 Hz in the high resolution modes. The chopper opening angle
\phi
is the opening angle of the chopper(
170^{\circ}
) minus the angle corresponding to the phase difference between the choppers to reach a variable incident opening time. The chopper dimensions and time distribution is presented in the appendix figure
12
. From this time distribution, the primary energy resolution is derived
Holm 2014
:

\displaystyle\delta_{E_{\rm i}}=\frac{E_{\rm i}}{\alpha L_{\rm i}\lambda_{\rm i}}\left(\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{6}-\frac{(\Delta t-\delta t)^{2}}{6}\right)2\sqrt{2\ln 2},

(2)

where
\alpha=m_{\rm n}
/
h
. This results in an analytical energy resolution for the primary spectrometer of
1.35\ \mu
eV for the high resolution mode.

The energy resolution of the secondary spectrometer is given by the final energy reflected in the analysers (
E_{\rm f}
), the Bragg angle of the analysers(
\theta_{\rm A}
) and the interatomic distance for the [0 0 2] PG lattice planes (
d
) such that:

\displaystyle\delta_{E_{\rm f}}=2E_{\rm f}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta_{d}}{d}\right)^{2}+(\cot\theta_{\rm A}\delta_{\theta_{\rm A}})^{2}}\

\displaystyle\lim_{\theta_{\rm A}\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{2}}\delta_{E_{\rm f}}\rightarrow 2E_{\rm f}\frac{\delta_{d}}{d}

(3)

As displayed in the above equation, the energy resolution is reduced as the second term becomes negligible when
2\theta_{\rm A}\rightarrow 180^{\circ}
. For current suppliers of PG,
\delta_{d}/d\approx 10^{-3}
is available. The analytical energy resolution of the secondary spectrometer is thus 3.9
\mu
eV.

The resultant energy resolution of the complete spectrometer is therefore 4.13
\mu
eV at the focal point on the detectors. The true geometry of the analyser configuration and the aberrations of the configuration will contribute to the true energy resolution, which we consider in the ray-tracing simulations below.

II.2
Analytical
\bf q
-resolution

The
\bf q
-resolution can be determined analytically by error propagation of the divergence of the neutron beam of the primary spectrometer and the details of the secondary spectrometer considered in quadrature:

\delta_{\theta}=\sqrt{\delta_{det}^{2}+\delta_{div}^{2}}

(4)

using the energy-momentum relationship:

{\bf q}^{2}={\bf k_{\rm i}}^{2}+{\bf k_{\rm f}}^{2}-2{k_{\rm i}}{k_{\rm f}}cos(\theta),

(5)

and the relationship between energy resolution and the analyser scattering angle of the secondary spectrometer, equation
3
. In equation
5
,
{\bf k}_{\rm i}
and
{\bf k}_{\rm f}
are the initial and final wavevector and
\theta
is the scattering angle. We consider an incident beam divergence of
\pm 1.5^{\circ}
, both horizontally and vertically resulting in a
\delta_{div}
= 0.026 Å
-1
. We consider 1 inch wide detector tubes. We do not take into account sample-analyser-detector distance and the smearing in scattering angle by the mosaicity of the HOPG analyser crystals.
The resultant analytical resolution in wavevector transfer is 0.031 Å
-1
.

The analyser configurations that have been investigated are: (1) constant

\displaystyle t_{\rm ToF}(\epsilon,l)=t_{\rm i}+t_{\rm f}=\frac{L_{\rm i}}{v_{\rm i}}+\frac{L_{\rm f}(\epsilon,l)}{v_{\rm f}(\epsilon,l)}

The energy transfer for an indirect time of flight spectrometer is given by:

\displaystyle\hbar\omega=E_{\rm i}-E_{\rm f}=\frac{m_{\rm n}}{2}\left(\frac{L^{2}_{\rm i}}{(t_{\rm ToF}-t_{\rm f})^{2}}\right)-E_{\rm f}

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{\rm n}}{2}\frac{L_{i}^{2}}{\left(t_{\rm ToF}(\epsilon,l)-\sqrt{\frac{L_{\rm f}^{2}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})m_{\rm n}}{2E_{\rm f}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})}}\right)}-E_{\rm f}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})

(9)

E_{\rm f}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})
is the energy reflected in each analyser as a function of the out of plane scattering angle from the sample(
\epsilon_{\rm opt}
=
0^{\circ}
) and the corresponding optimized radius from the sample position(
l_{\rm opt}
). The optimization of
l_{\rm opt}

L_{\rm f}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})
is the secondary path length calculated from the same variables as
E_{\rm f}(\epsilon_{\rm opt},l_{\rm opt})
. For configuration (1) and (2) the focusing is implemented analytically. For configuration (3), equation
7

9
numerically for
\hbar\omega=0

l

\epsilon

Configuration 1: Many backscattering instruments today use symmetric, or close to symmetric, Rowland geometry
Johan 1931
analyser positions since this geometry minimises analyser aberrations and enhances energy resolution. In the Rowland geometry the analyser and detectors are placed on the Rowland circle so that all rays satisfy the Bragg condition at the same energy. The analysers follow the tangent of the circle which has twice the radius of curvature as the circle containing sample, analyser and detector, named the Rowland circle, see figure
8
(a). This geometry can be obtained analytically, by finding the focal points of a circular lens using Fermat’s principle. However, using near-symmetrical Rowland geometry in backscattering limits the
q
-resolution, see figure
8
(b). For the optimized configuration in KVASIR, the distances
d_{SA}
and
d_{AD}
are rather different, leading to a highly asymmetrical Rowland geometry, see the red line in figure
6
. This causes a spread in the secondary path length,
L_{\rm f}
. However, when processing the data, one needs to use an average value of
L_{\rm f}
, since the true neutron path is unknown. In turn, this broadens the energy resolution of the secondary spectrometer beyond our target resolution
Davidsen 2025
.

Configuration 2:
In this configuration, the secondary flight path is held constant, corresponding to analysers being placed on an ellipse with focal points at the sample and detector positions (the blue line on figure
6
). While
L_{\rm f}
is now well determined, the geometry leads to a spread in analyser scattering angle,
\theta_{A}
. Therefore
\delta E_{\rm f}
significantly increases the energy resolution, again beyond the target resolutions.
Configuration 3:
By adding a flight path correction to the ellipse with constant
L_{\rm f}
for each analyser position (pink line on figure
6
), the ellipse can be corrected for the time of flight of each neutron to match the scattering angle of the analyser. This will result in a constant
t_{\rm f}=\sqrt{L_{\rm f}^{2}m_{n}/(2E_{\rm f})}
. This narrows the energy resolution compared to configuration 2.
Configuration 4:
The analyser configuration that results in an optimised energy resolution is the asymmetric Rowland geometry marked by the purple line in figure
6
. The configuration with constant
\hbar\omega
provides the narrowest energy resolution at the focal point of the detector.

III.5
Prismatic concept

In addition to the optimisations performed for configuration 4, we also employ the prismatic analyser concept. This means that we minimise effects due to the broad mosaicity of the PG analyser crystals by having the spread in 2
\theta_{A}
limited by sample height, analyser height and detector pixel size. In addition, the analyser mosaicity will spread different energies onto different pixels in the detector. By taking into account the concurrent deviation in E
f
, each pixel on the
3
He detector can be tof converted individually.

IV.1
Benchmarking

We have determined the neutron flux profile of the ESS backscattering instrument, MIRACLES, using McStas simulations, to benchhmark KVASIR. We compare the intensities at the detector position for an equivalent analyser coverage. The neutron flux at the detector position is simulated to be
I_{\rm MIRACLES}
= 7.38(8)10
3
n/s, for an energy resolution similar to that obtained for KVASIR, MIRACLES:
\delta
E = 5.20
\mu
eV, compared to KVASIR
\delta
E = 5.70
\mu
eV. It should be noted that MIRACLES is optimised for higher energy resolution than KVASIR and we only use this energy resolution for benchmarking purposes. The neutron flux at the detector position for KVASIR is
I_{\rm KVASIR}
=1.050(7)10
5
n/s. The detected flux for KVASIR is therefore more than an order of magnitude greater:
I_{\rm KVASIR}/I_{\rm MIRACLES}=14.2\pm 0.2
. The increased count rate is a direct consequence of the use of HOPG analyser crystals with higher reflectivity and broader acceptance in
E_{\rm f}
, the use of the prismatic concept and the matching of primary and secondary spectrometer resolutions.

V
Discussion

We have considered a backscattering spectrometer optimised for hard condensed matter at ESS. We use analytical and ray tracing simulation methods to determine the accessible parameter space for an indirect geometry instrument employing HOPG with the prismatic analyser concept.

We determine from simulations that KVASIR can deliver an energy resolution of 5.72
\mu
eV and a
q
-resolution of
\approx
0.03 Å
-1
with sufficient space for the required sample environment. While the
q
-resolution meets the design criteria of the instrument quite well, the energy resolution is somewhat broader than the criteria of 4
\mu
eV.

However, this study shows that significant further improvements are possible. The primary spectrometer has not been optimised and further gains could be made by studying the effect of variable incident divergence, possibly via the use of divergence slits, to optimise the
q
-resolution. This could further increase the neutron count rate which already, with this unoptimised primary spectrometer, is an order of magnitude greater than expected for MIRACLES.

Further improvements include increasing the analyser angle closer to a true backscattering configuration, to sharpen the energy resolution. This would, however, limit the accessible vertical solid angle and therefore the count rate. One can accommodate this by either moving the detectors slightly closer to the sample position or shortening the detectors. Moving the detectors closer to the sample would widen the
q
-resolution slightly, to around
\delta q
= 0.04 Å
-1
.

We have not yet investigated opportunities that could be provided by employing different analyser shapes. Rectangular analysers have been used in all simulations, however it has been shown that elastically straining the analyser crystals, made possible by using hexagonal shaped analyser as shown with Si analysers on BASIS, could deliver an improved energy resolution
Mamontov and Herwig 2011
;
Osti et al. 2020
.

We are also aware that it has been very difficult in the past few years to provide a suitable supply of HOPG with the correct mosaicity. Suppliers tend to provide highly variable mosaicities ranging from from
0.5^{\circ}
to
1.5^{\circ}
. Although broad mosaicities can be accommodated when using the prismatic concept, a detailed study on the effect of variable mosaicities for the KVASIR concept must be performed in the future.

VI
Conclusion

Finally, in this study we have provided a preliminary optimisation of the secondary spectrometer of a neutron backscattering spectrometer, for ESS, with enhanced
\bf q
- and energy resolution, in addition to suitable sample space. The instrument, KVASIR, opens up a new parameter space for the study of low-lying dynamics in functional and quantum materials much needed to explore and develop advanced technologies.
