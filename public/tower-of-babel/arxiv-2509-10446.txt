DeepDive: Advancing Deep Search Agents with Knowledge Graphs and Multi-Turn RL
Rui Lu, Zhenyu Hou, Zihan Wang, Hanchen Zhang, Xiao Liu, Yujiang Li, Shi Feng, Jie Tang, Yuxiao Dong

Abstract
Augmenting large language models (LLMs) with browsing tools substantially improves their potential as deep search agents to solve complex, real-world tasks. Yet, open LLMs still perform poorly in such settings due to limited long-horizon reasoning capacity with browsing tools and the lack of sufficiently difficult supervised data. To address these challenges, we present DeepDive to advance deep search agents. First, we propose a strategy to automatically synthesize complex, difficult, and hard-to-find questions from open knowledge graphs. Second, we apply end-to-end multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance LLMs’ long-horizon reasoning with deep search. Experiments show that DeepDive-32B achieves a new open-source competitive result on BrowseComp, outperforming WebSailor, DeepSeek-R1-Browse, and Search-o1. We demonstrate that multi-turn RL training improves deep search ability and significantly contributes to the performance improvements across multiple benchmarks. We observe that DeepDive enables test-time scaling of tool calls and parallel sampling. All datasets, models, and code are publicly available at https://github.com/THUDM/DeepDive .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs)—trained with reinforcement learning (RL) using verifiable rewards—have demonstrated strong performance in complex reasoning tasks, such as mathematics and coding competitions
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
OpenAI 2024b
;
OpenAI 2025
;
Grok 2025
)
.
As real-world tasks become increasingly complex, integrating external tools like browsing expands an LLM’s knowledge beyond its training corpus.
This shift requires the LLM to execute as an autonomous
agent
capable of handing complex tasks.

Notably, deep search agents are expected to reason over and search from hundreds of online sources to locate complex, hard-to-find information, such as answering the questions in BrowseComp
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
.
However, open models fall far behind proprietary LLMs such as OpenAI DeepResearch as deep search agents
(
Li et al. 2025b
;
Song et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025c
;
Wu et al. 2025a
)
.
We attribute this gap to the shortage of hard-to-find data and the absence of multi-turn RL training.

First, data-wise, most existing QA datasets usually feature relatively simple questions that do not reflect true “hard-to-find” cases.
For example, questions in HotpotQA
(
Yang et al. 2018
)
can often be solved by searching for a few clear entities.
In contrast, deep search questions such as those in BrowseComp usually involve multiple blurry entities, requiring long-horizon reasoning and deep search to reach the correct answer.
Second, training-wise, how to effectively combine long-horizon reasoning with deep search tool use remains an open question.
Even strong reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1
(
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
make only shallow tool calls and often suffer from hallucinations (see Figure

In addition, existing search/browsing agents that integrate browsing tools are primarily designed to address direct search tasks.
For example, systems like R1-Searcher
(
Song et al. 2025
)

(
Chen et al. 2025
)

(

(

(

)
, and Musique
(

To address these challenges, we present DeepDive to advance deep search agents.
First, we introduce a strategy to automatically synthesize hard-to-find questions from open knowledge graphs (KGs).
Second, we apply end-to-end multi-turn RL to enhance LLMs’ long-horizon reasoning with deep search.

On the data side, we address the lack of difficulty in QA datasets by automatically constructing a deep search QA dataset from KGs, as they naturally support multi-hop connections and each entity has different attributes.
By deliberately blurring some attributes of each entity during question construction, we create a form of “blurry entity”.
We then perform random walks on the KG to extract long, multi-hop paths and use LLMs to further obfuscate key cues, making the QA pairs more challenging.
This data synthesis process produces data that effectively stimulates LLMs’ long-horizon reasoning and deep search abilities.

On the training side, we adopt end-to-end multi-turn RL training to integrate reasoning with search tool use.
We employ the multi-turn GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
algorithm for end-to-end RL, where the LLM interacts with a web environment and receives rewards based on the final answer in the constructed QA dataset.
Experiments show that the RL-trained model increases its tool use more effectively than baselines during inference, demonstrating test-time scaling of tool calls for improved long-horizon reasoning and deep search (see Figure

The DeepDive method is trained on two open models: GLM-Z1-9B-0414
(

Team 2025b

The constructed data consists of 3,090 high-quality deep search QAs derived from KGs.
Built on this, DeepDive-32B reaches an accuracy of 14.8% on BrowseComp, surpassing all existing open systems—WebSailor, Search-o1, and DeepSeek-R1-Browse—and achieving a new open-source competitive result (see Figure

Experiments demonstrate that the performance of the DeepDive models benefits significantly from the proposed end-to-end multi-turn RL training (see Figure

We further validate across multiple challenging deep search QA benchmarks and analyze test-time scaling for tool calls and parallel sampling.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose an automated method to synthesize deep search QA pairs from open KGs.

•

We introduce DeepDive, an end-to-end multi-turn RL framework that enables the model to effectively combine internal reasoning with external web search.

•

Built on open models, DeepDive-32B achieves 14.8% accuracy on BrowseComp—an open competitive model, revealing test-time scaling of tool calls and parallel sampling in deep search.

Related Work

Language Model Reasoning.

Recent research has shown that large language models (LLMs) can improve performance on math and code tasks by generating explicit reasoning traces during inference.
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Team 2025b
)

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful training paradigm for reasoning, with methods such as self-correction using reward signals to refine internal reasoning
(
Kumar et al. 2024
)
. Proprietary models such as OpenAI’s o1 series demonstrate that increasing the length of the reasoning inference time alone can produce notable improvements in math, code, and scientific benchmarks. Building on this, DeepSeek-AI introduced DeepSeek-R1, an open-source LLM trained using large-scale RL with a novel Grouped PPO algorithm to directly optimize reasoning accuracy
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
. These models reason entirely through their own token generation without environmental feedback, producing verifiable step-by-step solutions with self-checking mechanisms. This research confirms that well-designed RL training can equip LLMs with sophisticated autonomous problem-solving abilities.

Tool-Augmented Agents for Long-Horizon Reasoning.

Retrieval-grounded systems such as WebGPT
Nakano et al. 2022
and Self-Ask
Press et al. 2022a
first demonstrated that LLMs could issue search queries and integrate external documents during answer generation, improving factuality.
ReAct
Yao et al. 2023
unified chain-of-thought planning with explicit API calls in an interleaved loop, enabling dynamic decision making over multiple steps.
Toolformer
Schick et al. 2023
showed that models can self-supervise the selection and timing of tool invocations, yielding substantial accuracy gains on knowledge-intensive tasks.
Recent RL-based agents like SWiRL
Goldie et al. 2025
leverage synthetic trajectory generation and reward signals to plan multi-hop searches and tool interactions, achieving state-of-the-art on HotpotQA and MuSiQue.
AutoCoA
Zhang et al. 2025
and ReSearch
Chen et al. 2025
internalize decision policies for dynamic query composition without manual prompt engineering.
Distilled variants such as SimpleDeepSearcher
Tan et al. 2025
demonstrate that smaller models trained on expert tool-use trajectories can rival larger RL-trained agents.
Systems like Search-o1
Li et al. 2025b
, WebThinker
Li et al. 2025c
, WebShaper
Tao et al. 2025b
and Search-R1
Jin et al. 2025
further combine planning, memory, and API orchestration to excel on multi-hop QA and long-form research benchmarks (e.g. WebWalkerQA
(
Wu et al. 2025b
)
, GAIA).
Emerging open frameworks (Open Deep Research
Hugging Face 2025
) and end-to-end RL agents (DeepResearcher
Zheng et al. 2025
) extend these capabilities to real-world web navigation, noisy result handling.

The DeepDive Method

We present DeepDive to advance the long-horizon information-seeking ability of deep search agents.
In DeepDive, we introduce two techniques, targeting the data construction and RL stages, respectively.
To generate a large-scale corpus of challenging, deep-search QA pairs, we develop an automated and controllable data synthesis method with knowledge graphs (KGs) (Cf Figure
3
).
To enhance the agent’s capabilities for long-horizon reasoning and browsing, we leverage the constructed data to perform end-to-end multi-turn RL training (Cf Figure
5
).

To mimic human web navigation, we establish an interaction framework that serves as the learning environment for the deep search agent.
In this framework, each of the agent’s decisions follows an iterative cycle of reasoning, tool call, and observation (see Figure
3
).

Formally, at step
t
, the agent generates a chain-of-thought
c_{t}
, executes a browsing action
a_{t}
, and observes the web content
o_{t}
.
This process repeats until the agent determines it has collected sufficient information and executes a terminating action
a_{\text{eos}}
to give the final answer.
The entire task execution can be represented as a trajectory
\mathcal{T}
:

\mathcal{T}=\left[q,\left(c_{1},a_{1},o_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(c_{m},a_{m},o_{m}\right),c_{\mathrm{ans}},a_{\mathrm{eos}}\right],\quad m\leq n

(1)

The action
a_{t}
is drawn from a browsing action space with three core operations:
search
,
click
, and
open
.
A
search
action to retrieve web page summaries with given keywords,
a
click
action to access specific pages from search results,
and an
open
action to access specified URLs directly.
Notably, both the
click
and
open
actions support retrieving multiple pages in a single call, thereby improving the efficiency of information gathering.

3.1
Automated Data Synthesis from Knowledge Graphs

Building deep search agents requires training data that goes beyond conventional multi-hop QA.
While datasets like HotpotQA involve predictable reasoning steps, true deep search agents should act like human researchers who iteratively search, filter, and synthesize scattered evidence from the web.
This thus calls for complex, difficult, and hard-to-find questions that even domain experts need hours to search and solve.
Such complex training data is critical for developing agents to handle real-world tasks where information is scattered, conflicting, and hard to locate.

However, the specific training data required to cultivate this skill is naturally scarce on the internet.
With manual annotation being prohibitively expensive and difficult to scale, synthetic data generation emerges as the most efficient and scalable solution.

Knowledge Graphs with Hard-to-Find Information.

Naturally, knowledge graphs (KGs) provide a structured and semantically-rich environment for multi-hop reasoning, making them particularly well-suited for generating supervision data for training deep search agents.
First,
verifiability
: KGs encode factual entity-relation triples that are inherently traceable and objective, ensuring answer correctness and significantly improving data reliability compared to fully model-generated QA pairs.
Second,
multi-hop structure
: KGs allow us to explicitly control reasoning depth by performing random walks of varying lengths, enabling the generation of questions requiring multiple inference steps.
Third,
reasoning controllability
: each entity node contains multiple attributes that can be selectively obscured (such as dates, names, or locations), thereby increasing ambiguity and preventing models from exploiting shortcut solutions.
Building data from KGs can force models to iteratively reason, search, validate, and reflect before arriving at accurate answers.
In light of these advantages, we propose an automated KG-based method to generate scalable, high-quality, and reasoning-intensive QA pairs.

Automated Data Synthesis from KGs.

The main idea is to generate complex reasoning paths from KGs.
Formally, a knowledge graph is defined as a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of entities (nodes), and
E\subseteq V\times V
is the set of directed edges representing the known relationships between them
(
Ji et al. 2021
)
.
Each node
v_{i}\in V
is associated with a set of attributes
A(v)=\left[a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1},\cdots,a_{i}^{t}\right]
.

To create questions that require deep reasoning and browsing, we generate paths by taking a random walk through the graph.
Starting from an initial node
v_{0}
, we navigate through the graph for
k
steps to form a path
P=\left[v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\right]
, where each step (
v_{i},v_{i+1}
) is a valid edge in the graph.
We choose a longer path length (e.g.,
k>5
) to increase the potential reasoning complexity.
However, questions generated solely based on the node sequence
P
tend to be too simple, similar to those in HotpotQA, as their answers can be found by direct search.

To further increase the complexity and ambiguity of the questions, we enrich and obfuscate the path by incorporating node attributes.
Specifically, we combine each node
v_{i}
in the path with its corresponding attributes to form an attribute-rich path
P_{A}
:

P_{A}=\left[\left(v_{0},\left[a_{0}^{0},a_{0}^{1},\ldots\right]\right),\left(v_{1},\left[a_{1}^{0},a_{1}^{1},\ldots\right]\right),\ldots,\left(v_{k},\left[a_{k}^{0},a_{k}^{1},\ldots\right]\right)\right]

(2)

Subsequently, we select an attribute
a_{k}^{i}
from the terminal node of the path,
v_{k}
, as the ground-truth answer.
An LLM is then employed to obfuscate the information along the entire attribute-rich path
P_{A}
.
This process involves techniques such as generalizing specific dates into ranges.
The final output is a pair of challenging questions and answers
(q,a_{k}^{i})
, generated as follows:

(q,a_{k}^{i})=\text{LLM-obscure}(P_{A})

(3)

Improving Path Quality and Complexity.

In a graph random walk, each step directly impacts the quality of the final path, which in turn determines the complexity and logical soundness of the generated QA pair.
To improve path quality, we apply two constraints to the random walk process.

First, we filter candidate nodes by setting an appropriate out-degree range
\left[d_{\min},d_{\max}\right]
.
If a node’s out-degree is excessively high, it tends to be overly popular, making answers too predictable for the model.
Conversely, nodes with low out-degree may hinder effective path expansion.
Thus, the candidate set of nodes for the next step
\mathcal{N}\left(v_{i}\right)
is defined as:

\mathcal{N}\left(v_{i}\right)=\left\{u\mid\left(v_{i},u\right)\in E\wedge d_{\min}\leq d(u)\leq d_{\max}\right\}

(4)

Second, to ensure logical consistency of the path, we leverage an LLM to choose the next node.
Given the current path context
P_{i}=\left[v_{0},\ldots,v_{i}\right],i<k
, the LLM evaluates all candidates in
\mathcal{N}\left(v_{i}\right)
and selects the node most strongly related to the existing path as
v_{i+1}
. This selection process is defined as:

v_{i+1}=\text{LLM-select}\left(P_{i},\mathcal{N}\left(v_{i}\right)\right)

(5)

Together, these constraints guide the random walk to produce reasoning paths that are both complex and coherent, synthesizing high-quality QA pairs.

To further increase question difficulty, we engineer an automated difficulty filter.
We use a frontier model (e.g., GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024a
)
) with basic search via function calls to attempt each question four times.
If the frontier model solves a question in any attempt, it is discarded as too easy.
Only questions that the frontier model fails in all four attempts are kept in the final dataset.
This ensures the training data contains very challenging tasks that require complex reasoning and advanced web browsing, rather than simple information lookups.

3.2
End-to-End Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning

Given the challenging QA dataset, we use end-to-end multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) to train the agent for deep search.
Built on the web interaction environment, DeepDive applies a strict reward mechanism that guides the agent on how to search, what to search for step by step, and when to terminate searching.
This RL training enhances the agent’s ability for deep, long-horizon search.

Multi-Turn RL.

End-to-end multi-turn RL trains the model to better use iterative reasoning and sequential tool calls.
Unlike single-turn RL, where the model outputs a single response per question, multi-turn RL lets the agent perform multiple reasoning and tool-use steps before arriving at a final answer.
We employ the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
to train the deep search agent.
For each question
q
, we sample the tool calling trajectories
G
from the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
.
For each trajectory
\mathcal{T}
, we then calculate a normalized advantage
A_{i}
, as follows:

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}\left(\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{G}\right)}{\operatorname{std}\left(\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{G}\right)}

(6)

The policy parameters
\theta
are updated to maximize a clipped objective function with a KL penalty:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left[\min\left(\rho_{i}A_{i},\operatorname{clip}\left(\rho_{i},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A_{i}\right)-\beta\mathrm{KL}\left(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)\right]

(7)

This objective uses the importance ratio
\rho_{i}=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old }}}\left(\mathcal{T}\right)}
, where
\epsilon
controls the clipping range and
\beta
weights the penalty for diverging from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
.

Strict Rewards.

A key challenge in web-browsing tasks lies in their inherently complex, multi-step nature—as formalized in Eq.
1
, a complete trajectory
\mathcal{T}
typically requires multiple rounds of tool interaction before arriving at a final answer.
This complexity requires a carefully-designed reward signal that can effectively guide policy optimization while preventing reward hacking
(
OpenAI 2025
)
.

To address this challenge, we construct a strict binary reward function with two requirements: (i) the format of each step (including both chain-of-thought reasoning
c_{i}
and tool invocation
a_{i}
) must be strictly correct; and (ii) the final answer
a_{\text{eos}}
must match the ground-truth
a^{*}
exactly.
A trajectory receives a reward of +1 if and only if both conditions are satisfied simultaneously; otherwise, it receives a reward of 0.
This reward function is formalized as:

r(\mathcal{T})=\begin{cases}1,&\left(\forall\ i,\text{Format}\left(c_{i},a_{i}\right)\right)\wedge\text{Judge}\left(a_{\mathrm{eos}},a^{*}\right)\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

where
a_{i}
denotes the
i
-th function call,
a_{\text{eos}}
represents the final answer,
a^{*}
represents the ground-truth, and the
Judge
relies on LLM-as-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
to verify answer correctness.

To further improve rollout efficiency and ensure the strict validity of positive samples, we introduce an early exit mechanism: once the model produces a format error at any step, trajectory generation is terminated immediately and assigned a reward of
0
.
This guarantees that all positively rewarded trajectories are both error-free and fully reliable, thereby enhancing robustness in multi-turn tool usage.

In summary, DeepDive trains a deep search agent with multi-turn RL using QA pairs that are hard to find and solve but easy to verify.
Within a multi-turn web-browsing environment, the proposed method gradually builds the agent’s ability for multi-turn search and long-horizon reasoning.

Data Synthesis Details.

We build synthetic datasets from two public knowledge graphs, KILT
(
Petroni et al. 2020
)
and AMiner
(
Tang et al. 2012
)
.
First, we generate long-chain paths via random walking with parameters set to
k\in[5,9]
,
d=3,d_{\min}=4,d_{\max}=8
.
We then use Gemini-2.5-Pro
(
Team et al. 2023
)
, leveraging its superior long-context ability, to obscure entities and synthesize the QA pairs.
This process yields 3,250 deep search QA pairs, which are randomly split into 1,016 samples for Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and 2,234 for Reinforcement Learning (RL).

Training Details.

We integrate the Serper API
(
Serper
)
for search, where each search query yields the top-10 pages and the model may click or open only among these results.
The Jina API
(
Jina.ai 2025
)
supports click and open operations.
Following recent works that apply RL to large language models
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Hou et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
)
, our training process begins with a cold-start phase.
We employ Claude-4-Sonnet-Thinking
(
Anthropic 2025a
)
as a teacher model, which supports tool-calling capabilities, to interact with our browsing tools and solve the synthetic problems, thereby generating cold-start data.
Following multiple attempts and reject sampling, we obtain 858 high-quality SFT traces.

We choose two open models—GLM-Z1-9B-0414
(
GLM et al. 2024
)
and QwQ-32B—
(
Team 2025b
)
as our backbone models.
Each model is trained for 3 epochs with a global batch size of 32, a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, and a maximum context length of 104,800.

During RL, we first evaluate the SFT model’s performance using pass@8 on the remaining dataset to assess difficulty levels.
We select data with accuracy rates between 0.125 and 0.75 for RL training, 974 in total, to ensure optimal learning conditions.
The RL training is conducted using the open-source Slime
1
1

1

https://github.com/THUDM/slime/
framework, with a rollout size of 8, sample size for each prompt 16, global batch size 128, temperature set to 1.0, and a maximum of 50k context length permitted.
To promote exploration, we set the KL penalty coefficient to
\beta=0

(
Vassoyan et al. 2025
)
and employ a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
.

Evaluation.

For datasets and models with previously-reported scores, we directly adopt the results from their respective papers.
For all other evaluations, we follow the LLM-Judge framework
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, employing Llama-3.1-70B
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
to assess whether a model’s final output matches the ground truth answer.
To speed up evaluation during reinforcement learning (RL) training, each checkpoint is assessed on a fixed, randomly pre-sampled subset of BrowseComp-266, with a maximum of 75 turns.
Once training saturates, we evaluate later checkpoints on the full BrowseComp dataset (1,266 instances) with the turn limit raised to 128.
For other benchmarks whose total size is below 300, we evaluate on the entire dataset.
To reduce variance and improve robustness, every dataset is evaluated twice, and the average accuracy is reported as the final result.

Competitive among Open Deep Search Agents.

The DeepDive-32B model excels on four challenging deep search benchmarks. For BrowseComp and BrowseComp-ZH benchmarks, it ranks just behind OpenAI’s DeepResearch and far ahead of other open-source models or agents.
It also shows clear advantages on SEAL-0 and XBench-DeepSearch, indicating effective use of web-browsing for complex reasoning.
While most open-source models score under 10% on BrowseComp, DeepDive-32B achieved 14.8%, setting a new record for open-source models.
This also highlights the power of reinforcement learning (RL).
The 32B model with only SFT has already scored 9.5% on BrowseComp, beating some proprietary models such as GPT-4o and Claude-3.7-Sonnet, which access browsing via function call.
RL then enhances the model’s core ability to combine reasoning with search, resulting in stable performance growth over the SFT version on every benchmark.
Notably, these gains are less pronounced for the smaller 9B model, potentially because of its limited reasoning capacity or a tendency to overfit on synthetic data during its training.

Parallel Sampling and Tool Call Voting.

In addition to increasing computation via a larger tool call budget, parallel sampling is another effective strategy that scales computation by generating multiple independent reasoning trajectories for each question. To evaluate this, we fix the model to DeepDive-32B and perform 8 independent trajectories per question on a randomly selected subset, BrowseComp-266, to enable efficient evaluation.
Each question is independently solved multiple times, and we explore different strategies for selecting the final answer among the sampled outputs.
A basic approach is majority voting, where the answer that appears most frequently among the 8 samples is selected.
However, upon analyzing the distribution of tool calls across samples, we observe a notable pattern: answers that require fewer tool calls before submission tend to be more accurate. This is likely because when the model struggles to find a correct answer, it often continues searching up to the 128-call limit, resulting in overly long trajectories typically associated with incorrect predictions.

Motivated by this observation, we adopt a simple yet effective selection strategy: choosing the answer that requires the fewest tool calls among the 8 sampled trajectories. As shown in Figure
9
, while majority voting improves the score from 12.0 to 18.8, our method brings an even larger improvement, boosting the score from 12.0 to 24.8. This suggests that, assuming each round of model reasoning and tool invocation takes approximately the same amount of time, simply selecting the earliest-submitted answer (which typically corresponds to the one with the fewest tool calls) in an 8-parallel sampling setup can yield a substantial performance gain, nearly doubling the final accuracy.

Figure 9
:
Comparison of answer selection strategies in 8-parallel sampling for DeepDive-32B on BrowseComp-266.
While majority voting provides improvement over single sampling, selecting the answer with minimal tool calls achieves greater performance gains, leveraging the observation that fewer tool calls typically correlate with higher accuracy.

Ablation Study

We evaluate the impact of our synthetic deep search QA data on model performance through ablation studies across two training stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL).
The evaluation covers four benchmarks: BrowseComp-266 (a subset for faster evaluation), BrowseComp-ZH, SEAL-0, and XBench-DeepSearch.
We focus on two key metrics: task accuracy (Acc) and the average number of tool calls (#Turn), with the latter indicating the model’s capability for long-horizon deep search.

As shown in Table
2
, the base model (QwQ-32B) delivers low accuracy and few tool calls, which makes it ineffective for deep search tasks.
While fine-tuning on HotpotQA yields moderate improvements on SEAL-0 and XBench-DeepSearch, suggesting some initial web-browsing ability, it provides only limited gains on more difficult benchmarks like BrowseComp-266.
Using our synthetic data for SFT yields far more substantial improvements, significantly enhancing the model’s performance across all four benchmarks. The model not only achieves notably higher accuracy but also shows a marked increase in average tool-call turns, indicating stronger long-horizon reasoning and browsing capabilities.

Building on the SFT experiments, we further investigate how training data in the RL stage impacts performance.
We fix the best SFT model (trained on our synthetic data) and conduct RL training using either HotpotQA or our synthetic data to compare the effects of different RL datasets.
The results indicate that while HotpotQA provides a slight performance boost, it does not meaningfully change the number of tool calls.
In contrast, applying RL with our data yields substantial additional improvements across all tasks, with particularly strong gains in both accuracy and the number of tool calls on BrowseComp-266 and BrowseComp-ZH.

In summary, our synthetic deep search data is critical in both the SFT and RL stages, as it not only improves the overall performance of the model but also effectively activates its long-horizon deep search capabilities.

Additional Study: Semi-Automated i.i.d. Deep Search QA Synthesis for RL

We perform an additional study to directly improve model performance on deep search benchmarks.
Straightforwardly, we can construct i.i.d. QA pairs with BrowseComp, whose questions are so challenging that expert annotators have to spend hours solving them, ensuring simple search strategies are ineffective.
However, reaching the depth and breadth of BrowseComp requires heavy human effort in research, annotation, and data curation.
To reduce annotation costs, we present a semi-automated framework.

i.i.d. Data Synthesis.

We adopt a semi-automated framework to reduce the burden on annotators, where each annotator is supported by the OpenAI o3 model
(
OpenAI 2025
)
equipped with search capabilities and follows a four-stage process.

First, based on the nine topical domains defined in BrowseComp, the annotator collaborates with the model to identify root domains that contain abundant factual and structured web content.
Second, the annotator explores various linked pages within each root domain using the model’s navigation and search features, and selects verifiable entities along with their associated attributes.
Third, the annotator conducts further targeted searches related to each selected entity and engages in multi-turn interactions with the model to construct new challenging multi-hop questions. These questions are carefully written to obscure key information while retaining verifiability.
Fourth, the annotator uses the model to attempt to answer the synthetic question. If the answer is incorrect or if multiple plausible answers exist, the sample is discarded.
The annotator also records the time taken by the model to arrive at an answer in order to identify questions that are more difficult and of higher quality. This workflow requires minimal prior knowledge from the annotator.

Through iterative model-guided discovery, question construction, and verification, annotators can efficiently produce complex, high-quality deep search QA pairs. The same procedure is applied to Chinese websites to enhance multilingual performance.
As a result, we obtain a total of 2,997 English and 275 Chinese challenging deep search QA pairs.

RL with i.i.d. Deep Search Data.

We follow the same pipeline as Section

Data Contamination Analysis.

To ensure that the performance improvements are not the result of data leakage, we follow the contamination analysis protocol introduced in LLaMA 2
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
and evaluate the Human-in-the-Loop dataset used for training.
For each evaluation sample, we tokenize the input (excluding special tokens) and extract all contiguous 10-token n-grams.
A token is considered contaminated if it appears in any n-gram also found in the training corpus.
The contamination rate for a sample is defined as the proportion of contaminated tokens. Based on these rates, we categorize each sample into four non-exclusive subsets: Clean (less than 20% contamination), Not Clean (20% or more), Not Dirty (less than 80%), and Dirty (80% or more).
As shown in Table
4
, more than 97% of the samples in the dataset are classified as Clean, and there are no samples in the Dirty category.
The results indicate that there is almost no test-data leakage in the constructed dataset for training.

6
Conclusion

We present DeepDive that aligns deep reasoning with multi-turn web search through automated deep search QA synthesis and end-to-end multi-turn reinforcement learning.
The data pipeline creates ambiguity-rich, multi-hop questions with hidden cues that match real long-horizon tasks.
After the RL stage, DeepDive-32B attains 14.8% accuracy on BrowseComp, sets a new open-source competitive result, and outperforms larger agents and several strong proprietary baselines.
Analyses show that complex supervision and multi-turn RL jointly ground tool use, that performance scales with tool-call budgets and parallel sampling, and that skills learned on hard problems transfer to simpler settings.
We release datasets, models, and code to support progress toward open deep search systems.

7
Limitation

Although the two challenging deep research QA data synthesis methods we proposed enable us to generate high-quality data with guaranteed difficulty and accuracy—helping DeepDive-32B achieve competitive results among open-source models—the upper limit of difficulty remains significantly lower than datasets like BrowseComp. This limitation indirectly contributes to DeepDive-32B substantially lower performance on BrowseComp compared to advanced models such as o3 with browsing.
Additionally, our training approach, which primarily targets difficult data, has led to an "over-search" phenomenon in DeepDive-32B. Determining optimal training steps and designing more appropriate reward mechanisms for the reinforcement learning stage represents an important area for future exploration.
