Comparative Analysis of Hypertension Stages Prevalence and Associated Factors in Bangladesh: Evidence From BDHS 2017–18 and 2022
Authors: Tonmoy Kumar Roy, Md Shoebuj Zaman, Ashikul Islam Pollob, Md Asadujjaman, Md Menhazul Abedin, Md Merajul Islam, Md Maniruzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study was designed to identify the changing patterns of the potential covariates that influence hypertension stages (SHT) over time. The study used two cross‐sectional datasets (BDHS 2017/18 and 2022), which had 12,189 and 13,843 respondents aged ≥18 years. The χ2analysis examined the association between the selected covariates and SHT as well as their influence on SHT using multinomial logistic regression (MLR) models (p < 0.05). Moreover, a SHAP‐based approach was used to quantify the contribution of each covariate to the predictive performance for SHT classification. By adjusting for age and gender, the MLR‐based model showed that seven significant covariates were found for SHT in both BDHS 2017/18 and 2022. Both MLR and SHAP‐based approaches identified education, BMI, wealth index, diabetes status, and division as the principal determinants of SHT for BDHS 2022 dataset. Education and BMI were a significant and consistent predictors of SHT in both survey years. Respondents with secondary education and obese had 1.4 times (AOR: 1.4, 95% CI: 1.1‐1.8) and 5.3 times (AOR: 5.3, 95% CI: 3.2‐8.7) higher odds of having controlled hypertension (CHT) and severe hypertension in 2022. The respondents from middle and rich households also had higher odds of mild hypertension in 2017/18, whereas only rich respondents were found to have CHT and mild hypertension in 2022. Marital status showed a changing association with SHT over time, with divorced respondents showing a significantly higher risk of developing severe hypertension in 2022. These findings highlight the need for geographically targeted and socioeconomically tailored public health interventions in Bangladesh.Keywords: prevalence, hypertension stages, covariates, comparative study, BDHS, multinomial logistic regression

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the leading risk factors for cardiovascular disease, which has doubled in prevalence worldwide during the past three decades [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), globally, about 1.28 billion people aged 30–79 years suffer from hypertension. It was noted that about two‐thirds of those individuals came from low and middle‐income countries [2]. The urgent need for research and intervention is highlighted by resource constraints for this global burden. Hypertension or chronically elevated blood pressure (BP) is a dangerous medical condition that increases the risk of kidney, heart, and brain problems [2, 3, 4, 5]. Hypertension prevalence draws great attention to regional differences due to dietary habits, lifestyle choices, and access to healthcare [6]. Hypertension is more common in low‐ and middle‐income countries because of delayed diagnosis and a lack of adequate medical resources [7, 8].

Based on the BP‐based measurements, the stages of hypertension (SHT) were four categories as: normal, mild, moderate, and severe [5, 9]. Between the normal and severe stages, the mean systolic and diastolic BP rise noticeably, which indicates the rising incidence of hypertension, particularly among the elderly. It is imperative to treat early‐stage of hypertension, which includes mild and moderate forms, because it can develop into severe hypertension, which increases the risks of cardiovascular problems [10]. SHT study helps physicians to diagnose the severity of hypertension, stage‐specific risks, and personalize treatment [11]. Early detection of hypertension helped the respondents to control it, which may decrease the risks of related diseases [9]. To reduce the health risks, effective management options place a strong emphasis on the necessity of both pharmaceutical therapies and lifestyle modifications, particularly for early SHT [12].

An analysis showed a 26.7% prevalence of hypertension for 46 low and middle‐income countries. The significant associated risk factors for hypertension were age, body mass index (BMI), and smoking [13]. Furthermore, a South‐Asian study reported that 19.8% prevalence of hypertension were found among Bangladeshi adults. There was a significant association between hypertension and socioeconomic status, residence types, and BMI [14]. For Bangladeshi urban adults, there is a high prevalence of 23.7% hypertension. Older age, smoking, and high waist circumference were also key risk factors [15].

However, there are fewer studies on SHT across regions and socioeconomic groups. So, there is no evidence that the relative importance of hypertension risk factors has changed across survey cycles in Bangladesh. To fill the gap, we conducted a comparative cross‐sectional analysis using two nationally representative Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) datasets (2017/18 and 2022). The study aims were as follows: (a) to estimate the prevalence of SHT across the survey years 2017/18 and 2022; (b) to identify sociodemographic and health‐related factors associated with SHT in each survey year; (c) to compare the associations of these covariates with hypertension stages that have changed over time.

Materials and Methods

Conceptual Framework of Proposed Methodology

The conceptual framework of the proposed methodology is presented in Figure 1. This study performed a comparative analysis of SHT prevalence between BDHS 2017/18 and BDHS 2022. Firstly, we used a χ2 analysis to find the association between selected covariates and SHT. Following that, the important covariates were selected for SHT from two ways: multinomial logistic regression (MLR) analysis and discriminative‐based SHAP approach. In MLR analysis, we employed both unadjusted and adjusted models to determine the optimal risk covariates of SHT using the OR & AOR and p‐value (<0.05). Whereas, respondents age and gender were adjusted in MLR model. Subsequently, the discriminative‐based SHAP approach was adopted to understand the contribution of each factor and compared its findings with those of MLR analysis. Finally, a comparative study was performed to show the changing patterns of the selected optimal risk covariates for SHT over time.

Conceptual framework of the proposed study.

Study Design

The BDHS 2017/18 survey was carried out from October 2017 to March 2018, whereas BDHS 2022 was conducted from May 25 to July 27, 2022. In the first sampling step, there were selected 675 (urban: 250 & rural: 425) enumeration areas (EAs) in 2017/18, and 675 (urban: 237 & rural: 438) EAs in 2022 from a large number of EAs. These were listed in the “Bangladesh Population and Housing Census 2011” with a probability proportional to EA size. For the second stage, systematic sampling was used to select an average of 30 (for 2017/18) and 45 (for 2022) households from each EA.

Dataset Descriptions

This study used two recent datasets from the BDHS, which were conducted in 2017/2018 and 2022 [16, 17]. It was noticed that there were 20250 households selected and recorded the information of 89819 household members for 2017/18, whereas 30330 households were selected and recorded the information of 132463 household members in 2022. By excluding missing values and unusual observations, this study considered 12189 (male: 5254 and female: 6935) and 13843 (male: 6204 and female: 7639) respondents who were aged ≥18 years in 2017/18 and 2022.

List of Covariates

This study had ten covariates that have an impact on the SHT, supported by existing studies [14, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. These covariates are participants’ age in years (18‐34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, and ≥65) [20, 22, 25], gender (Male and Female) [14, 19], and divisions (Barisal, Chattogram, Khulna, Dhaka, Mymensingh, Rajshahi, Rangpur, and Sylhet) [15, 18, 20, 26]. The covariates also included the residence types (Urban and Rural) [14, 20]. Respondents education levels (No education, Primary, Secondary, and Higher) [24, 25], BMI (Underweight: BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, Normal weight: BMI 18.5–24.99 kg/m2, Overweight: BMI 25–29.99 kg/m2, and Obese: BMI ≥30 kg/m2 [15, 20]. The wealth index was classified into three categories as poor, middle, and rich [14]. According to the WHO report, people with a fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dl) in the presence of signs and symptoms are considered to have diabetes [26] and vice versa for the respondents who have no diabetes [20, 22, 27]. The caffeine was also considered as a covariate, which had two categories: Yes and No [19, 25].

Study Variable

During the BDHS 2017/18 survey interview, the surveyor was measured the systolic and diastolic BP (in mmHg) three times. There were at least a five‐minute gap between the measurements by utilizing an automatic upper‐arm inflation and pressure release digital oscillometer BP monitor. Following that, they computed the average value of the second and third measurements, which was used to compute the SHT. Similarly, BP was also measured three times for BDHS 2022, and computed the average value of the second and third measurements of BP which was used to classify the SHT [17]. This study followed the WHO and ISH (2003) guidelines to classify SHT into five mutually exclusive categories based on SBP, DBP, and antihypertensive medication use [28]. Respondents with SBP <140 mmHg or DBP <90 mmHg who were not taking antihypertensive medication were classified as Normal. Respondent with SBP <140 mmHg or DBP <90 mmHg who reported currently taking antihypertensive medication were classified as Controlled Hypertension (CHT), indicating blood pressure that was controlled through treatment. Respondents with SBP of 140–159 mmHg or DBP of 90–99 mmHg were classified as having Mild Hypertension, those with SBP of 160–179 mmHg or DBP of 100–109 mmHg as Moderate Hypertension, and those with SBP ≥180 mmHg or DBP ≥110 mmHg as Severe Hypertension.

Statistical Analysis

This study used bivariate analysis to show the frequency (in %) of each category for each covariate by SHT (normal, CHT, mild, moderate, and severe) and evaluated their association with SHT by Chi‐Square analysis. These significant covariates were subsequently used in MLR model to show their significant impact on SHT over 2017–18 and 2022. All statistical tests were two‐tailed and p‐value (<0.05) was considered as a significant covaraite.

Assessment of Covariates Using Discriminative‐Based SHAP Approach

This study identified the significant associated covariates for SHT, obtained from the Chi‐Square analysis. A discriminative‐based SHAP approach was employed to assess those identifying significant/optimal risk covariates over various datasets such as tabular, image, and text [29]. Moreover, the SHAP‐based approach explains the prediction outcomes (Here, SHT) of the black‐box model and is based on the Shapley value in game theory [30]. To perform this experiment, we split the dataset into a training (80%) set and a test (20%) set. Consequently, the RF‐based model was trained using the training set and evaluated its performance on the test set as shown in Figure 2. Moreover, we also computed the mean SHAP values of each covariate to understand more clearly the contributions of each category for every covariate and compared them with the findings of MLR analysis.

Results

Comparative Analysis of Hypertension Prevalence between 2017/18 and 2022

Figure 3 illustrates the comparative prevalence of hypertension among adults aged 18 years and above based on the BDHS 2017/18 and BDHS 2022 datasets. Figure 3(a) indicates an overall decline in hypertension prevalence between the two survey periods, with rates decreasing from 28.3% to 23.1% among women and from 26.4% to 16.8% among men. Figure 3(b) and Figure 3(c) further disaggregate hypertension prevalence by nutritional status (BMI category) for each survey year, respectively, revealing a consistent positive association between BMI and hypertension across both periods: prevalence was lowest among underweight individuals and progressively increased through normal, overweight, and obese categories, peaking at 46.3% among overweight men in 2017/18 and 43.8% among obese men in 2022. These findings suggest that while overall hypertension prevalence decreased over time, elevated BMI remained a consistent risk factor associated with higher hypertension prevalence in both survey periods.

The prevalence of CHT, mild, moderate, and severe hypertension was highest among individuals aged 65 years and older compared with the other age groups, whereas the prevalence of the CHT group was highest among those aged 55–64 years in both 2017/18 and 2022. More specifically, in 2017/18, 3.6% of respondents had CHT, 16% had mild hypertension, 5.9% had moderate hypertension, and 2.7% had severe hypertension. These rates have gradually decreased in 2022. It was noted that the age of the respondent was significantly associated with SHT for both 2017/18 and 2022 (p < 0.001). Although women respondents had a larger percentage of CHT (4.2%) and moderate (5.9%) and severe (2.7%) hypertension than men (2.3% for CHT, 5.7% for moderate, and 2.6% for severe), men respondents had a higher incidence of mild hypertension (15.7%) than women (15.5%). These rates have progressively decreased for females (mild: 12.6%, moderate: 4.2%, and severe: 1.8%), but for CHT: 4.4%, it increased in 2022. This study found a strong correlation between the respondents' Gender and SHT for both datasets (p < 0.001).

Regarding the Chittagong division, it had the highest rates of CHT (5.3%); besides that, the Rangpur division had the greatest rates of moderate and severe hypertension (6.7% and 3.7%, respectively), while the Barisal division had the highest prevalence of mild hypertension (19.2%) in 2017/18. In 2022, respondents from the Rajshahi region recorded the highest proportions across the mild (13.9%), moderate (4.8%), and severe (2.2%) categories, while the highest prevalence of CHT (4.5%) was observed in the Chittagong and Khulna divisions. Additionally, in the Rangpur division, only 2.2% of respondents reported severe hypertension. As compared to 2017/18, although the prevalence of hypertension has gradually declined in 2022 for all stages, it is still alarming to us. There was a significant correlation between the respondents' division and SHT for the 2017/18 and 2022 years (p < 0.001).

In contrast, the highest rates of moderate (5.9%) and severe (2.7%) hypertension for 2017/18 were found in rural areas. Whereas, the prevalence of CHT (5.2%) and moderate (3.8%) and severe (1.6%) hypertension was greater among urban respondents in 2022. It was noted that the prevalence of residence type by SHT for 2022 was lower compared to 2017/18. This study found a strong relationship between residence types and SHT in 2022 but not in 2017/18 (p < 0.01). For both the 2017/18 and 2022 years, the highest prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe levels of hypertension was found among uneducated respondents than for the other education levels. Specifically, this study reported that only 4.6% of uneducated respondents had a severe level of hypertension, 8.3% for moderate, and 19.4% for mild, and 3.8% for CHT in 2017/18. Whereas the prevalence of SHT has steadily declined (4.6% for CHT, 16.0% for mild, 6.2% for moderate, and 2.5% for severe) in 2022. It was noted that educational attainment was significantly associated with SHT for both 2017/18 and 2022 years (p < 0.001). Overweight respondents had 3.5% of severe, and 24% of mild, whereas obese respondents had only 8.7% of CHT and 9.3% for moderate respondents who were obese for moderate in 2017/18. It was observed that the prevalence of severe hypertension was higher for obese respondents in 2022 compared to 2017/18. For both the 2017/18 and 2022 years, the BMI of the respondents was shown to be significantly correlated with the SHT (p < 0.001). This study found that the wealth index was significantly associated with SHT for both 2017/18 and 2022 years. It revealed that the respondents who came from rich families had a higher prevalence of CHT (4.7%), mild (16.6%), moderate (6.8%), and severe (2.8%) SHT than the respondents who came from poor and middle‐class families. This study found a similar finding for rich respondents, except for severe in 2022. It was noted that the respondents who came from poor families had a higher incidence rate of severe than the respondents who came from the rich and middle‐class families.

For both 2017/18 and 2022, we found that diabetic people had a higher prevalence of CHT (6.9% vs. 9.5%), mild (22.7% vs. 15.5%), moderate (11.5% vs. 5.8%), and severe (4.9% vs. 2.5%). It was significantly associated with SHT (p < 0.001) for both 2017/18 and 2022. We revealed that the prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe problems was higher among those who took caffeine in both 2017/18 and 2022. Specifically, we found that 6.4% of respondents had CHT, 16.8% of respondents had mild, 8.4% had moderate, and 3.2% had severe levels of hypertension in 2017/18. Widowed respondents were more likely to have CHT (6.6%), mild (24.9%), moderate (14.7%), and severe hypertension (8.6%) in 2017/18. However, widowed respondents reported greater rates of CHT, mild, and moderate conditions in 2022 (8.6%, 22.4%, and 10.6%). Whereas divorced respondents had a higher prevalence (4.7%) of severe hypertension. Comparing 2017/18 with 2022 reveals that the prevalence of hypertension has gradually decreased in 2022. The SHT is highly associated with both marital statuses (p < 0.001).

Results from Regression Analysis

Table 2 shows the effect of various selected covariates on the SHT in 2022, using adjusted and unadjusted MLR. Based on the unadjusted model, division had a significant impact on SHT in 2022. After adjusting for the effects of age and gender, we observed that the respondents from the Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions had 1.4 (AOR = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.1‐1.8) and 1.4 (AOR = 1.4, 95% CI = 1.2‐1.8) times higher odds of having only mild hypertension than the respondents from the Dhaka division. In addition, respondents from Sylhet division had 1.8 (AOR = 1.8, 95% CI: 1.3–2.5) times higher odds of being in the CHT group compared with respondents from Dhaka division. Respondents’ education level was significantly associated with SHT in the unadjusted model. However, after adjusting for age and gender, a significant association between education level and SHT was observed only in the CHT group. The respondents who were overweight and obese had higher odds of having CHT, mild, moderate, and severe levels of hypertension in the adjusted models.

The same relationship between BMI and SHT is also observed in the unadjusted model. Furthermore, in the unadjusted model, married, widowed, and divorced respondents had significantly higher odds of CHT and mild, moderate, and severe levels of hypertension compared to unmarried respondents. But in the adjusted model, we found that the widowed respondents had 2.3 times (AOR = 2.3, 95% CI = 1.0‐5.1) higher odds of having controlled hypertension than unmarried respondents. The adjusted results showed that respondents from the Chittagong and Sylhet divisions had higher odds of having CHT than those from the Dhaka division. Barisal, Chittagong, Khulna, Rajshahi, Rangpur, and Sylhet had significantly higher the odds of having mild hypertension compared to the Dhaka division. On the other hand, compared to those from the Dhaka Division of Bangladesh, significantly higher odds of being moderate and severe hypertension were found for respondents from the Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions. Participants with primary and higher education had higher odds of CHT compared to respondents not having an education. The respondents who were overweight and obese had higher odds of having CHT, mild, moderate, and severe levels of hypertension. We identified that the middle and rich respondents were more likely to have mild hypertension, and this was also seen with controlled hypertension for only the rich. Diabetic people had higher odds of having CHT, mild, moderate, and severe hypertension compared to the non‐diabetic people. The married, widowed, and divorced respondents were more likely to have CHT hypertension than unmarried respondents with normal hypertension.

Assessment of the Selected Covariates Using Discriminative Analysis

The Shapely Additive Explanation feature importance plot of the RF‐based classifier for predicting SHT (normal, CHT, mild, moderate, and severe) for the BDHS 2022 dataset is shown in Figure 4 and the corresponding SHAP feature importance plot for the BDHS 2017/18 dataset is presented in Figure S2. As shown in Figure 4, the covariates were ordered in descending order of magnitude based on the contribution of mean SHAP values for each covariate across all categories. The mean SHAP values graphic color‐codes the magnitude for each class and ranks the covariates according to their total contribution to all classes because our scenario comprises a multiclass classification problem. The bars represent the extent to which each covariate contributes to predicting SHT.

Covariate's importance plot using SHAP for the BDHS 2022 dataset.

Figure 3 presents the SHAP‐based global feature importance derived from the RF‐based model. Respondent's education was the most influential predictor and contributed substantially to the prediction of SHT. Among education categories, secondary education exhibited the highest mean absolute SHAP value. This was followed by respodents with underweight status, those from rich households, and respodents with primary education. Wealth index, educational attainment, geographic division, and place of residence were also important contributors for predicting SHT. Whereas marital status and caffeine consumption demonstrated relatively limited predictive importance. Across all features, the largest proportion of SHAP importance was attributed to the moderate hypertension class, indicating that these features primarily contributed to distinguishing individuals with moderate hypertension from the remaining hypertension stages.

Although place of residence was not statistically significant in the adjusted MLR‐based model, it was contributed substantially to the predictive performance of the RF‐based model. This difference is expected because statistical significance reflects independent linear associations after adjustment for confounding, whereas SHAP quantifies a variable's overall contribution to prediction, including nonlinear relationships and interactions captured by the RF‐based model. Consequently, variables that contribute little to independent inference may still substantially improve predictive performance, highlighting the complementary predictive evidence alongside the inferential results from the MLR‐based model.

Discussion

The present study showed that, although the prevalence of hypertension decreased between the BDHS 2017–2018 and BDHS 2022 surveys, hypertension remains a major public health problem in Bangladesh. This finding is consistent with previous studies from South Asia, which reported that hypertension affects a considerable proportion of the adult population, although the prevalence in Bangladesh remains lower than the average reported for low‐ and middle‐income countries [6, 31]. The continued burden of hypertension may be related to population ageing, urbanization, unhealthy dietary habits, physical inactivity, and the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity [6, 20].

The findings of the SHAP analysis were largely consistent with those of the adjusted MLR‐based model, for the providing complementary evidence importance of several predictors in SHT classification. Overall, the SHAP findings were consistent with the MLR results, as both approaches identified educational attainment, BMI, wealth index, diabetes status, and division as the principal determinants of SHT.

Respondent levels of education were significantly associated with SHT. Similar associations have been reported in previous studies, where higher educational attainment has been linked to an increased prevalence of hypertension among adults [19, 27]. This association may reflect lifestyle changes accompanying higher educational attainment, including more sedentary occupations, prolonged sitting time, work‐related stress, and reduced physical activity, which may increase the risk of hypertension despite greater health awareness [6, 32].

The present study found a significant association between respondents' BMI and SHT. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which identified overweight and obesity as significant determinants of hypertension among Bangladeshi adults [19, 33, 34]. The association may be explained by obesity‐induced activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, along with insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction, which collectively contribute to elevated blood pressure [35, 36].

Rich household respondents have higher odds of being mild but lower odds of being severe SHT. Some of the previous studies found that respondents who come from middle or rich‐household backgrounds have higher odds of hypertension [23, 37, 38]. The possible explanation might be that respondents from the wealthiest families often consume more calories, do less physical work, and lead sedentary lifestyles, which makes them overweight or obese, putting them at higher odds of being hypertensive [39, 40, 41].

The respondents from Chittagong, Mymensingh, and Sylhet divisions have higher odds of mild‐stage hypertension, whereas the respondents from the Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions have a higher odds of having moderate and severe stages of hypertension in 2017/18. The respondents from the Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions have a greater odds of having mild‐stage hypertension in 2022. Some of the previous studies revealed that Barisal, Chittagong, Khulna, Rajshahi, Rangpur, and Khulna regions have a higher odds of being hypertensive [23, 37]. The geographical disparity in hypertension can be linked to higher consumption of raw salt in coastal regions, varying eating habits, and diminished knowledge of hypertension [42]. It needs to be further researched why the prevalence of hypertension is higher in Rangpur, Rajshahi, and Sylhet, which is beyond the objective of this study. Excess body fat may be the cause of these problems since it raises insulin levels, which in turn makes people more susceptible to NCDs [43].

Married respondents have a lower odds of being SHT and widowed respondents have a higher odds of having SHT compared to unmarried respondents. Divorced respondents have a greater odds of having severe hypertension compared to unmarried women in 2022 datasets. Similar findings were observed in some of the previous studies [44, 45]. A married person who comes from a poor family and has financial difficulties may increase chronic stress and have an impact on hypertension. According to earlier research, variables related to health behaviors affect hypertension [1, 20, 46].

Diabetic status was significantly associated with SHT in both survey datasets. The diabetic respondents had higher odds of being SHT than non‐diabetic respondents. This result aligns with earlier research findings [20, 27]. This finding may be explained by shared metabolic dysfunctions, including insulin resistance, vascular inflammation, and autonomic neuropathy, which contribute to sustained blood pressure elevation and the progression of hypertension severity [47].

After adjusteing the age and gender in MLR‐based model, place of residence was not statistically associated with SHT but it was contributed substantially to the predictive performance of the RF‐based model. The slight discrepancies observed between the two methods are expected because MLR‐based model provides the estimates of independent linear associations and supports epidemiological inference from a parametric viewpoints framework, whereas SHAP quantifies the overall contribution of features including their respective categories to prediction of SHT by considering non‐linear relationships and their interactions captured by the RF‐based model. The concordance between these analytical approaches enhances the robustness and credibility of the identified predictors, supporting their potential utility for hypertension risk stratification and clinical decision‐making.

Conclusion

This study identified five different significant covariates in 2017/18 and four covariates out of eight in BDHS 2022. The five covariates for 2017/18 are division (Chittagong, Mymensingh, Rajshahi, Rangpur, & Shylet), BMI, wealth index (middle & rich), diabetes, and marital status (married & widowed). Whereas, four significant covariates were found for BDHS 2022: divisions (Rajshahi & Rangpur), BMI, rich respondents, and the respondents who were divorced. Comparing to the BDHS 2017/18 with BDHS 2022, This study discover that we need to be improved in some of significant covariates like division, BMI, wealth index, and marital status. The following characteristics were strongly linked to increased risks of being mild (Rajshahi and Rangpur region, rich wealth index) and severe (divorced marital status). Besides these factors, this study also found that BMI was also strongly related to SHT. There are still some scopes to the improvement of these factors for 2022 datasets that will be helpful to respondents in taking necessary steps to lessen the prevalence of SHT. This study recommended to promote simple lifestyle changes like healthy eating, regular exercise, and stress management, particularly targeting people in Rajshahi and Rangpur regions, divorced individuals, and lower‐income groups.
