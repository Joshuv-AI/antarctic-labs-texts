Interpretable Gradient Descent for Kalman Gain
M.A. Belabbas and A. Olshevsky

Abstract
We derive a decomposition for the gradient of the innovation loss with respect to the filter gain in a linear time-invariant system, decomposing as a product of an observability Gramian and a term quantifying the “non-orthogonality” between the estimation error and the innovation. We leverage this decomposition to give a convergence proof of gradient descent to the optimal Kalman gain, specifically identifying how recovery of the Kalman gain depends on a non-standard observability condition, and obtaining an interpretable geometric convergence rate.



1
Introduction

While the optimal Kalman gain for a linear time–invariant system can be obtained in closed form from the algebraic Riccati equation, gradient-based strategies to compute the Kalman gain remain interesting for two main reasons. First, in time-varying or adaptive filtering environments, incremental gradient updates permit the gain to be revised continuously, so the filter can respond to evolving system dynamics without repeatedly solving a full Riccati equation. Second, when the system model is only partially known—or entirely unknown—but measurement data continue to arrive, stochastic gradient methods may be used to directly to identify the gain in a data-driven manner.

We adopt the expected mean–square magnitude of the innovation —i.e., the one-step-ahead prediction error—as our cost for gradient descent, a quantity we refer to as the
innovations loss
. This is the most direct way to measure filter performance and, since it relies solely on observable data, so it remains well defined even when the true state is unknown.

The problem of analyzing gradient descent on the innovations loss has, to our knowledge, only been previously considered in
[
5
]
. A Kalman filter in one-step lookeahead form was considered in
[
5
]
, and it was shown that, under observability of the underlying system, gradient descent and its stochastic variants converge to the Kalman gain. A closely related paper
[
3
]
considered a slightly different cost function, using look-ahead in the innovations, and also obtained convergence to the Kalman gain.

There is also considerable parallel literature on the related problem of computing the optimal gain for LQR using gradient descent (e.g.,
[
2
,
1
,
4
,
6
]
). However, as explained in
[
5
]
, although some form of duality between controllability and observability holds here, one generally cannot derive results for computing the Kalman gain from corresponding results for LQR due because key nonsingularity assumptions are not preserved in the transformation.

In this paper we consider gradient descent to recover the Kalman filter in the more standard formulation. Unlike in
[
5
]
, we show that if we assume only observability of the underlying system, spurious stationary points do exist. Our starting point for analysis is a new observation that the gradient of the innovation loss is a product of an observability Gramian and a term measuring the violation of the orthogonality principle for Kalman filters. Building on this decomposition, we give a slightly non-standard observability condition that rules out stationary points and ensures convergence of gradient descent at a geometric rate to the Kalman gain. Finally, we show that this rate is interpretable, being a product of two terms: a measure of how close the trajectory can come to losing observability and a measure of how steeply violation of the orthogonality principle forces the innovations loss to rise.

2
Problem Statement

We consider a discrete-time linear time-invariant system described by the state-space equations

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=A\,x_{t}+w_{t},

(1)

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=C\,x_{t+1}+v_{t+1},

(2)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector,
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the measurement vector. We assume the process noise
w_{t}
and measurement noise
v_{t}
are independent, identically distributed zero-mean random vectors with covariances
\E[w_{t}w_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]=Q_{w}\succ 0
and
\E[v_{t}v_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]=R_{v}\succ 0
, respectively.

We employ a linear filter to estimate the state from observations:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t+1}^{-}

\displaystyle=A\,\hat{x}_{t},

(3)

\displaystyle\delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=y_{t+1}-C\,\hat{x}_{t+1}^{-},

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{t+1}^{-}+L\,\delta_{t+1}.

(5)

Here
\hat{x}_{t+1}
is the estimate of
x_{t+1}
,
\delta_{t+1}
is the innovation and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the filter gain matrix. If
L
is chosen so as to minimize the expected mean square state estimation error in steady-state, the resulting filter is the Kalman filter, and we denote the corresponding optimal gain as
L_{\rm KF}
.

However, the performance measure we consider here is
E\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
in steady-state as a function of a general gain
L
. Specifically, let

F(L):=(I-LC)A

and define the set

\mathcal{S}:=\{L\mid\rho(F(L))<1\},

where
\rho(\cdot)
is the spectral radius, be the set of stabilizing gains for the pair
(A,C)
. We define
the steady-state innovation covariance as

\Sigma_{\delta}(L)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[\delta_{t+1}\delta_{t+1}^{\mathsf{T}}].

It is known that this limit exists if
L\in\mathcal{S}
. Our error metric will be the function

J_{\rm innov}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}=\Tr( \Sigma_{\delta}(L)).

Our goal in this work is to answer the following three related questions:

1.

Under what condition does the gradient descent dynamics

\frac{d}{dt}L=-\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L),

converge to the Kalman gain, i.e., when does it hold that

\lim_{t\to\infty}L(t)=L_{KF}

2.

Is there an explicit expression for
\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L)
which provides an intuitive explanation for the absence of spurious stationary points and the convergence of gradient descent above?

3.

Can we answer the previous two questions in terms of easily interpretable properties of the dynamics of (
1
) and (
2
)?

3
The Gradient of the Innovations Loss

We now introduce some light notation that will allow us to state our first result. As is standard, the state estimation error is denoted by

e_{t}:=x_{t}-\hat{x}_{t},

and the steady-state cross-covariance between the estimation error
e_{t}
and the innovation
\delta_{t}
by

K(L):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[e_{t}\delta_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}],

where the dependence on
L
is through the definitions of the estimation error and innovation.
It is well-known that

K(L_{KF})=0,

where, recall,
L_{KF}
is the Kalman gain. This is sometimes called
the orthogonality principle
for Kalman filtering. Intuitively, we can think of the size of
K(L)
as a measure of violation of this principle.

Additionally, we denote by
W_{o}(L)
observability Gramian of the system
(F(L),CA)
. Formally, provided
\rho(F(L))<1
, the quantity
W_{o}(L)
is the unique positive semidefinite solution to the Lyapunov equation:

W_{o}(L)=F(L)^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}(L)F(L)+A^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}CA.

(6)

With these definitions in place, we can state our first main result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The gradient of
J_{\rm innov}(L)
with respect to the standard Euclidean inner product is given by

\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L)=-2W_{o}(L)K(L).

This expression provides an interpretation of the meaning of this gradient: it is a product of two terms, the first of which measures the observability of the underlying system and the second of which measures a violation of the orthogonality principle. This perspective will prove particularly valuable in the subsequent section, where we analyze gradient descent to compute the Kalman gain. What is specifically novel about this expression relative to the previous literature (e.g.,
[
5
, Section D.3]
) is the clean form and interpretability of the result.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Our first step is to write down an expression for
J_{\rm innov}(L)
and a number of related quantities which will be useful later. All of these are standard in the Kalman filtering literature, but we state them here to be self-contained and standardize notation.

We begin by writing down the dynamics of the error. A straightforward calculation shows that:

e_{t+1}=(I-LC)Ae_{t}+(I-LC)w_{t}-Lv_{t+1}.

We write this as

e_{t+1}=F(L)e_{t}+\eta_{t}(L),

where we introduced the
effective noise

\eta_{t}(L)
, defined as

\eta_{t}(L):=(I-LC)w_{t}-Lv_{t+1}.

(7)

The covariance
Q_{\eta}(L):=\E[\eta_{t}(L)\eta_{t}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}]
of the effective noise is given by

Q_{\eta}(L)=(I-LC)\,Q_{w}\,(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}+L\,R_{v}\,L^{\mathsf{T}}.

(8)

We show later in Lemma
3.2
that
Q_{\eta}(L)
is positive definite.

If the filter is stable, i.e., if
L\in\mathcal{S}
, the steady-state error covariance

P(L):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[e_{t}e_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}],

exists and is the unique, symmetric positive semidefinite solution to the discrete algebraic Lyapunov equation

P(L)=F(L)\,P(L)\,F(L)^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{\eta}(L).

(9)

If, moreover,
Q_{\eta}(L)\succ 0
then
P(L)
is also positive definite.

A related quantity is the
a priori estimation error
defined as

e_{t+1}^{-}=x_{t+1}-\hat{x}_{t+1}^{-}.

It is related to the estimation error by

e_{t+1}^{-}=Ae_{t}+w_{t},

and to the innovation by

\delta_{t+1}=Ce_{t+1}^{-}+v_{t+1}.

(10)

From the above relation for
e_{t+1}^{-}
, we easily obtain that the steady-state covariance of the a priori estimation error is given by

P^{-}(L):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[e_{t}^{-}e_{t}^{-\mathsf{T}}]=AP(L)A^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{w}.

(11)

From (
10
) and (
11
), the steady-state innovation covariance is then

\Sigma_{\delta}(L)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[\delta_{t}\delta_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}]=CP^{-}(L)C^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{v}=C(AP(L)A^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{w})C^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{v}.

We define the cost function
J_{\rm innov}(L)
as the trace of this matrix:

J_{\rm innov}(L)=\Tr(C A P(L) A^\T C^\T)+\Tr(C Q_w C^\T)+\Tr(R_v).

(12)

We next require several intermediate lemmas before proceeding. Our first lemma establishes the positive definiteness of the effective noise covariance.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any gain matrix
L
, we have that
Q_{\eta}(L)
is positive definite.

Proof.

Observe that

z^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\eta}(L)z=z^{\mathsf{T}}(I-LC)Q_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}z+z^{\mathsf{T}}LR_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}z=\|(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}z\|_{Q_{w}}^{2}+\|L^{\mathsf{T}}z\|_{R_{v}}^{2},

where
\|x\|_{M}^{2}=x^{\mathsf{T}}Mx
. This vanishes when
L^{\mathsf{T}}z=0
and
z-C^{\mathsf{T}}L^{\mathsf{T}}z=0
. This is possible only if
z=0
, which proves the claim.
∎

Next, we derive an expression for the cross-covariance
K(L)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
L\in\mathcal{S}
, it holds that

K(L)=F(L)P(L)A^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}+(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}-LR_{v}.

Proof.

The update equation
e_{t+1}=e_{t+1}^{-}-L\delta_{t+1}
and (
10
) together yield

e_{t+1}=e_{t+1}^{-}-L(Ce_{t+1}^{-}+v_{t+1})=(I-LC)e_{t+1}^{-}-Lv_{t+1}.

Now, we compute the cross-covariance
K(L)=\E[e_{t+1}\delta_{t+1}^{\mathsf{T}}]
:

\displaystyle K(L)

\displaystyle=\E[((I-LC)e_{t+1}^{-}-Lv_{t+1})(Ce_{t+1}^{-}+v_{t+1})^{\mathsf{T}}]

\displaystyle=(I-LC)\E[e_{t+1}^{-}(e_{t+1}^{-})^{\mathsf{T}}]C^{\mathsf{T}}-L\E[v_{t+1}v_{t+1}^{\mathsf{T}}]

\displaystyle=(I-LC)P^{-}(L)C^{\mathsf{T}}-LR_{v}

\displaystyle=(I-LC)AP(L)A^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}+(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}-LR_{v},

where the last step used (
11
). Recalling the definition that
F(L)=(I-LC)A
concludes the proof.
∎

The following lemma provides the core of the gradient calculation. We omit writing the dependence of
F(L),P(L),Q_{\eta}(L)
and
W_{o}(L)
on
L
to save space.
Recall that the gradient of a function
J:\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the Frobenius inner product is defined as the vector field
\nabla J
satisfying

\Tr(\nabla J(L)^\T V)=dJ(L)\cdot V\mbox{ for all }V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

where
dJ(L)\cdot V
is the differential of
J
at
L
evaluated in the direction
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
L\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L)\;=\;-2W_{o}(FPA^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}+(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}-LR_{v}).

Proof.

Throughout this proof we work in the framework of
matrix differentials
.
For a smooth matrix-valued mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
we write with some abuse of notation

dF(X)
for the differential of
F
evaluated at
X
and applied to an arbitrary vector
dX
;
namely,
dF(X)
is so that
F(X+dX)=F(X)+dF(X)+o(\|dX\|)
.

From (
12
),
J_{\rm innov}(L)=\Tr(C A P A^\T C^\T)+\text{const}
. Thus, the differential
dJ_{\rm innov}
is

dJ_{\rm innov}=\Tr(C A (dP) A^\T C^\T)=\Tr(A^\T C^\T C A \, dP)=\Tr(M dP),

(13)

where
M=A^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}CA
. Now the observability Gramian
W_{o}
obeys the Lyapunov equation

W_{o}-F^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}F=M.

(14)

With this identity in place, let us compute an expression for
dP
which we will then plug into (
13
). Differentiating (
9
) we obtain

dP-F(dP)F^{\mathsf{T}}=(dF)PF^{\mathsf{T}}+FP(dF)^{\mathsf{T}}+dQ_{\eta}.

(15)

We multiply (
15
) by
W_{o}
and take the trace:

\Tr(W_o (dP - F (dP) F^\T))=\Tr(W_o ((dF) P F^\T+ F P (dF)^\T+ dQ_\eta)).

The left-hand side (LHS) simplifies using the cyclic property of trace and (
14
) to

\text{LHS}=\Tr(W_o dP)-\Tr(W_o F (dP) F^\T)=\Tr(W_o dP)-\Tr(F^\T W_o F dP)=\Tr(M dP).

and thus
\Tr(M dP)=\Tr(W_o ((dF) P F^\T+ F P (dF)^\T+ dQ_\eta))
. Plugging this into (
13
), we obtain

dJ_{\rm innov}=\Tr\bigl(W_{o}((dF)PF^{\mathsf{T}}+FP(dF)^{\mathsf{T}}+dQ_{\eta})\bigr).

(16)

We next evaluate
dQ_{\eta}
and
dF
.
For the latter, using
F=(I-LC)A
, we obtain
dF=-(dL)CA
.
From
Q_{\eta}=(I-LC)\,Q_{w}\,(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}+L\,R_{v}\,L^{\mathsf{T}}
, we have

dQ_{\eta}=-(dL)CQ_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}-(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}(dL)^{\mathsf{T}}+(dL)R_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}+LR_{v}(dL)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Plugging these into (
16
), we get

dJ_{\rm innov}=\Tr\Biggl(W_{o}\Biggl((-(dL)CA)PF^{\mathsf{T}}+FP(-(dL)CA)^{\mathsf{T}}-(dL)CQ_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}(dL)^{\mathsf{T}}+(dL)R_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}+LR_{v}(dL)^{\mathsf{T}}\Biggr)\Biggr).

We now use standard trace identities
as well as the symmetry of
W_{o},P,R_{v}
to move
dL
to the right and obtain after a short calculation

dJ_{\rm innov}=\Tr\Bigl(\bigl(-2CAPF^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}-2CQ_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}+2R_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}\bigr)dL\Bigr).

Since
dL\cdot V=V
, we have shown that

dJ_{\rm innov}\cdot V=\Tr\Bigl(\bigl(-2CAPF^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}-2CQ_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}+2R_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}\bigr)V\Bigr),

from which we conclude that

\displaystyle\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}

\displaystyle=\left[-2CAPF^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}-2CQ_{w}(I-LC)^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}+2R_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}W_{o}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=-2W_{o}(FPA^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}+(I-LC)Q_{w}C^{\mathsf{T}}-LR_{v}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The result follows directly by comparing the expression for the gradient from Lemma
3.4

with the expression for the cross-covariance
K(L)
from Lemma
3.3
.
∎

4
Convergence of Gradient Descent

We next focus our attention to the convergence of the gradient descent flow of
J_{\rm innov}
, which is the flow associated with the differential equation

\frac{d}{dt}L(t)=-\nabla J_{\rm innov}(L(t))=2W_{o}(L(t))K(L(t)).

(17)

We make the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

The pair
(A,CA)
is observable.

This assumption might look unnatural at first glance, given that the Kalman filter theory is usually developed using only the much weaker assumption that
(A,C)
is detectable. However, it should make sense given the second equality of (
17
): if we want to rule out all stationary points except
L_{KF}
, we do want to make sure
W_{o}
is not singular, at least at the initial point.

We also need the following standard assumption to ensure existence and uniqueness of the Kalman filter.

Assumption 4.2
.

The pair
(A,Q_{w}^{1/2})
is stabilizable.

With these assumptions in place, we will prove the following theorem, which is our second main result. We show that the gradient flow of
J_{\rm innov}
converges to the Kalman gain when initialized at an arbitrary
L(0)\in\mathcal{S}
; said otherwise, gradient descent recovers the Kalman gain.

To state the result precisely, we introduce the following level set: given
L(0)\in\mathcal{S}
, we define

\mathcal{L}_{0}=\{L\in\mathcal{S}\mid J(L)\leq J(L(0))\}.

We furthermore introduce the constants

\kappa=\inf_{L\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}\lambda_{\min}\left(W_{o}(L)\right),

and

c=\sup_{\{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\setminus\{L_{KF}\}\}}\frac{J_{\rm innov}(L)-J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})}{\|K(L)\|_{F}^{2}}.

We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.3

(Convergence of Gradient Descent)
.

Assume that Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
hold. Let
L(t)
be the solution to (
17
). Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}L(t)=L_{KF}.

Moreover, it holds that

1.

\kappa>0
and
c<\infty

2.

The cost satisfies

J_{\rm innov}(L(t))-J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})\leq e^{-4(\kappa^{2}/c)t}\left(J_{\rm innov}(L(0))-J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})\right).

One novelty of this theorem
is the identification of
Assumption
4.1
that
(A,CA)
is observable as a requirement for convergence of gradient descent to recover Kalman gain in the standard filtering setting of Section
2
.
Another is
the easily interpretable rate of
\kappa^{2}/c
, which is the product of the potential observability loss over the entire trajectory
\kappa
and how steeply violation of the orthogonality principle forces the loss to rise
c
.

We may contrast this theorem with the main result of
[
5
]
, which considered the Kalman filter in the
one-step lookahead
form. In that setting, the observability of
(A,C)
was sufficient for recovery. The following example shows this condition is not enough for Kalman gain recovery in our setting.

Example 4.4

(Spurious stationary points when
(A,C)
is observable but
(A,CA)
is not)
.

Consider the system with matrices:

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix},Q_{w}=I_{2},R_{v}=1.

The pair
(A,C)
is observable. However,
CA=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\end{pmatrix}
, and the observability matrix for
(A,CA)
is
\begin{pmatrix}CA\\
CA^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
, which is of rank
1
. So,
(A,CA)
is not observable.
Set
L=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1}\\
l_{2}\end{pmatrix}
. The error transition matrix is
F(L)=(I-LC)A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1-l_{1}\\
0&-l_{2}\end{pmatrix}
. Stability of
F(L)
requires
|l_{2}|<1
.
Since

\delta_{t+1}=CAe_{t}+Cw_{t}+v_{t+1}=(e_{t,2}+w_{t,1}){\bf e}_{1}+v_{t+1},

where
{\bf e}_{i}
is the canonical basis vector with entry
i
equal to one and all other entries zero,
and
w_{t,1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
v_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are uncorrelated with
e_{t}
, it follows that

J_{\rm innov}(L)=\Tr\bigl(\Sigma_{\delta}(L)\bigr)=\E\bigl[e_{t,2}^{2}\bigr]+2.

Let

c=\lim_{t\to\infty}\E\bigl[e_{t,2}^{2}\bigr].

The
(2,2)
-entry of (
9
) (with
F(L)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1-l_{1}\\
0&-\,l_{2}\end{pmatrix}
,
Q_{w}=I
,
R_{v}=1
) yields

c=(l_{2})^{2}\,c\;+\;\bigl(2\,l_{2}^{2}+1\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\frac{\,1+2\,l_{2}^{2}\,}{\,1-l_{2}^{2}\,},\quad|l_{2}|<1.

Therefore,

J_{\rm innov}(L)=c+2=\frac{\,1+2\,l_{2}^{2}\,}{\,1-l_{2}^{2}\,}\;+\;2,\quad\text{for }|l_{2}|<1.

This cost depends only on
l_{2}
and
\nabla J_{\rm innov}(L)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{6l_{2}}{(1-l_{2}^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}
. Critical points occur when
l_{2}=0
, i.e., all points
L=(l_{1},0)^{\mathsf{T}}
, for any
l_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, are critical points. In particular, gradient descent initialized at any of these stationary points stays there, so that clearly
L_{KF}
is not always recovered. It is also worth noting that the level sets of the cost
J_{\rm innov}(L)
are not compact in this example.

We now proceed to prove Theorem
4.3
. A key element of the proof is the coercivity of
J_{\rm innov}(L)
under the assumption that
(A,CA)
is observable (and note that the example just given also shows that
J_{\rm innov}(L)
is not coercive under the weaker assumption that
(A,C)
is observable).
Recall that
\mathcal{S}
is the set of stabilizing gains:
\mathcal{S}:=\{L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\mid\rho(F(L))<1\}.

Lemma 4.5

(The Coercivity Lemma)
.

Suppose Assumption
4.1
holds.
For any sequence
L_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
such that either
\|L_{k}\|_{F}\to\infty
or
L_{k}
approaches the boundary of
\mathcal{S}
(i.e.,
\rho(F(L_{k}))\to 1^{-}
), it holds that
\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{\rm innov}(L_{k})=\infty
.

Due to the importance of this lemma and its tricky technical analysis, we relegate its proof to the next section.

We will also need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
M,N
be symmetric matrices such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x^{T}Nx=0
implies
x^{T}Mx=0
. Then there exists
\beta<\infty
such that

x^{T}Mx\leq\beta x^{T}Nx

Proof.

Let
N^{\dagger}
be the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
N
and set
C=N^{\dagger/2}MN^{\dagger/2}
. We argue that
\beta=\lambda_{\rm max}(C)
works. Indeed, for any
x
, decompose it as
x=y+z
with
y\in\ker(N)
and
z\in\ker(N)^{\perp}=\operatorname{range}(N)
. We have:

x^{T}Mx=z^{T}Mz=(N^{1/2}z)^{T}CN^{1/2}z\leq\lambda_{\rm max}(C)z^{T}Nz=\beta x^{T}Nx,

where we used that
N^{1/2}N^{\dagger/2}z=N^{\dagger/2}N^{1/2}z=z
for any
z\in{\rm range}(N)
. This
proves the result.
∎

With all this in place, we are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

By Lemma
4.5
, the level set
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\{L\mid J(L)\leq J(L(0))\}
is compact. Since
(d/dt)J(L(t))\leq 0
, we conclude that the trajectory
L(t)
remains in this compact set.

We use LaSalle’s principle with respect to the Lyapunov function
V(L)=J_{\rm innov}(L)
. As noted, the trajectory remains within
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, which is a compact subset of
\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore,
V(L)
is continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{S}
. It follows from LaSalle’s Principle (e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 8.3.1]
) that
L(t)
converges to the set
\{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\mid\nabla_{L}J(L)=0\}
. By Theorem
3.1
, this is

\{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\mid W_{o}(L)K(L)=0\}.

But the observability of
(A,CA)
implies the observability of
(A-LCA,CA)
(otherwise, an unobservable mode of the latter is immediately an unobservable mode of the former), which means that
W_{o}(L)
is strictly positive definite. We conclude that
L(t)
converges to the set
K(L)=0
, which is a singleton with
L_{KF}
as its only member.

To prove the convergence rate in the theorem statement, first observe that by compactness of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, we have that
\kappa>0
. To show that
c<\infty
, it suffices to analyze the behavior of the ratio in the definition of
c
in an open neighborhood
{\cal B}\subset\mathcal{L}_{0}
containing
L_{KF}
, since outside of of this neighborhood, the ratio is finite by compactness of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
(and the fact that
L_{KF}
is only point with
K(L_{KF})=0
)
1
1

1

More formally, suppose that we have that

\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\cap{\cal B}\setminus\{L_{KF}\}}\frac{J_{\rm innov}(L)-J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})}{||K(L)||_{F}^{2}}<\infty.

To further establish that
c<\infty
, it is enough to show finiteness of the same ratio on the complement
{\cal B}^{c}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
. But since the denominator is uniformly bounded away from zero on the compact set
{\cal B}^{c}\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}
, the ratio is indeed finite because it is the supremum of a continuous function on a compact set.
.
Since
J_{\rm innov}(L)
is twice continuously differentiable around
L_{KF}
(indeed,
P(L)
solves a linear equation, whose solution exist as long as
F(L)
is stable, i.e. for
L\in\mathcal{S}
), and
\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})=0
, we have

J_{\rm innov}(L)-J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta L,\nabla^{2}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})[\Delta L]\rangle+o(\|\Delta L\|_{F}^{2}),

where
\Delta L:=L-L_{KF}.

Similarly,
K(L)
is continuously differentiable, and
K(L_{KF})=0
. So,

K(L)=dK(L_{KF})\cdot\Delta L+o(\|\Delta L\|_{F}),

where
dK(L_{KF})\cdot\Delta L=:D_{K}(\Delta L)
denotes the differential of
K
at
L_{KF}
applied to
\Delta L
.
Then,

\|K(L)\|_{F}^{2}=\|D_{K}(\Delta L)\|_{F}^{2}+o(\|\Delta L\|_{F}^{2}).

The limit of the ratio in the definition of
c
as
L\to L_{KF}
(i.e.,
\Delta L\to 0
) is:

\lim_{\Delta L\to 0}\frac{\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta L,\nabla^{2}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})[\Delta L]\rangle_{F}}{\|D_{K}(\Delta L)\|_{F}^{2}}.

(18)

Since
\nabla_{L}J_{\rm innov}(L)=-2W_{o}(L)K(L)
, differentiating at
L_{KF}
, we obtain for the second derivative

\nabla^{2}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})[\Delta L]=-2W_{o}(L_{KF})D_{K}(\Delta L)-2(dW_{o}(L)\cdot\Delta L)K(L_{KF})=-2W_{o}(L_{KF})D_{K}(\Delta L)

where we recall that
K(L_{KF})=0
.
We can therefore conclude that

\sup_{\|\Delta L\|_{F}=1}\frac{\frac{1}{2}{\langle\Delta L,\nabla^{2}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})[\Delta L]\rangle_{F}}}{\|D_{K}(\Delta L)\|_{F}^{2}}<\infty,

by arguing as follows. First, observe that both the numerator and denominator are quadratic forms in
\Delta L
; next, note that if the denominator is zero, then
D_{K}(\Delta_{L})=0
, which then implies the numerator is zero. Owing to Lemma
4.6
, there exists
\beta<\infty
so that

\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta L,\nabla^{2}J_{\rm innov}(L_{KF})[\Delta L]\rangle_{F}\leq\beta\|D_{K}(\Delta L)\|_{F}^{2}

which shows finiteness of the supremum and of
c
.

We thus have

J_{\mathrm{innov}}(L)-J_{\mathrm{innov}}(L_{\mathrm{KF}})\;\leq\;c\,\|K(L)\|_{F}^{2}.

Furthermore, from the definition of
\kappa
and the fact that
\nabla J_{\mathrm{innov}}=-2W_{o}(L)D_{K}(L)
, we have

\|\,\nabla J_{\mathrm{innov}}(L)\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;4\kappa^{2}\,\|K(L)\|_{F}^{2}.

We can put these together to obtain

\|\nabla J_{\mathrm{innov}}(L)\|_{F}^{2}\geq 4\frac{\kappa^{2}}{c}(J(L(t))-J(L_{KF}).

Thus

\frac{d}{dt}(J(L(t))-J(L_{KF}))\leq-4\frac{\kappa^{2}}{c}(J(L(t))-J(L_{KF})),

leading to the geometric estimate in the theorem statement.
∎

5
The Coercivity Lemma

The coercivity property is essential for analysis of descent in the previous section. In this section, we provide a complete proof.

Proving this turns out to be surprisingly challenging. The problem is that

•

Coercivity is not true when
(A,CA)
is not observable; see Example
4.4
where the level sets are not compact

•

Even when
(A,CA)
is observable, the introduction of the gain
L
can cause the system to asymptotically lose observability (in that the observability Gramian can have eigenvalues which go to zero as
\|L\|_{F}\rightarrow\infty
).

Intuitively, the system can have modes that asymptotically become unobservable as the gain
L
gets large, and one must show that the noise cannot “hide within” these modes.

We need a careful argument ruling this out. Our first step is to relate the coercivity of
J_{\rm innov}(L)
to a simpler quantity which we will call
J_{\rm pred}
, which corresponds to the innovations loss in an alternative form of the Kalman filter which makes one-step ahead predictions. Formally, let

\tilde{F}(L)=A-LC,

and denote the steady-state estimation error covariance by
\tilde{P}(L)
. It is the unique positive definitie solution of:

\tilde{P}(L)=\tilde{F}(L)\tilde{P}(L)\tilde{F}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{w}+LR_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}},

assuming
\rho(\tilde{F}(L))<1
. We introduce the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}=\{L\mid\rho(A-LC)<1\}
.

We then introduce the loss function

J_{\rm pred}(L)=\Tr(C\tilde{P}(L)C^\T+ R_v),

(19)

which is the innovations loss associated with the linear filter of the form,

\tilde{x}_{t+1}=A\tilde{x}_{t}+L(y_{t}-C\tilde{x}_{t}).

We skip the details of this derivation here and refer the reader to
[
5
]
.

We next restate the coercivity lemma, giving it now an additional part, and provide a proof.

Lemma 5.1

(Expanded Coercivity Lemma)
.

1.

Suppose
(A,C)
is observable,
Q_{w}\succ 0
, and
R_{v}\succ 0
.
If a sequence
L_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
satisfies either
\rho(A-L_{j}C)\to 1^{-}
or
\|L_{j}\|_{F}\to\infty
, then
J(L_{j})\to\infty
.

2.

Suppose Assumption
4.1
holds.
For any sequence
L_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
such that either
\|L_{j}\|_{F}\to\infty
or
L_{j}
approaches the boundary of
\mathcal{S}
(i.e.,
\rho(F(L_{j}))\to 1^{-}
), we have
J_{\rm innov}(L_{j})\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the shorthand
M_{L}=A-LC
. Then steady-state error covariance is

\tilde{P}(G)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}M_{L}^{j}S_{L}(M_{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{j},\quad\text{where }S_{L}=Q_{w}+LR_{v}L^{\mathsf{T}}.

By our assumptions,
S_{L}\succ 0
. The cost then satisfies

J_{\rm pred}(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Tr\left((CM_{L}^{k})S_{L}(CM_{L}^{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}(S_{L})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|CM_{L}^{k}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\lambda_{\min}(S_{L}){\rm Tr}(X_{o}(L)),

(20)

where
X_{o}(L)
is the observability Gramian for
(M_{L},C)
.

Case 1:
L_{j}
remains bounded and
\rho(M_{L_{j}})\to 1^{-}
.

In this case,
\lambda_{\min}(S_{L_{j}})
is bounded below by
\lambda_{\min}(Q_{w})>0
. Observe that

X_{o}(L)
solves
X_{o}(L)=M_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}X_{o}(L)M_{L}+C^{\mathsf{T}}C
.
Let
\lambda_{j}
be an eigenvalue of
M_{L_{j}}
with
|\lambda_{j}|=\rho(M_{L_{j}})\to 1^{-}
, and
w_{j}
be the corresponding normalized eigenvector. Then from the previous equation we get

w_{j}^{*}X_{o}(L_{j})w_{j}(1-|\lambda_{j}|^{2})=\|Cw_{j}\|^{2},

implying

\Tr(X_o(L_j))\geq w_{j}^{*}X_{o}(L_{j})w_{j}\geq\frac{\|Cw_{j}\|^{2}}{1-|\lambda_{j}|^{2}}.

(21)

Since
(A,C)
is observable, it follows that
(M_{L_{j}},C)=(A-L_{j}C,C)
is observable for any
L_{j}
(indeed, any unobservable mode of
(M_{L_{j}},C)
is an unobservable mode of
(A,C)
). Thus
Cw_{j}\neq 0
. By the assumption that
L_{j}
stay in a bounded set, it also follows that
\inf_{j}\|Cw_{j}\|^{2}>0
(otherwise an accumulation point of
L_{j}
would be a
G^{\prime}
such that
(M_{G^{\prime}},C)
is not observable). This observation along with (
21
))and (
20
) completes the proof in this case.

Case 2:
\|L_{j}\|_{F}\to\infty
.

In this case, let
\alpha_{j}=\|L_{j}\|_{F}\to\infty
and
\bar{L}_{j}=L_{j}/\alpha_{j}
, so
\|\bar{L}_{j}\|_{F}=1
. By passing to a subsequence, we can assume without loss of generality that
\bar{L}_{j}\to L^{*}
with
\|L^{*}\|_{F}=1
.
From (
20
), we have

J_{\rm pred}(L_{j})\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Tr\left(CM_{L_{j}}^{k}(\alpha_{j}^{2}\bar{L}_{j}R_{v}\bar{L}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})(M_{L_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}})^{k}C^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\alpha_{j}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Tr\left((CM_{L_{j}}^{k}\bar{L}_{j})R_{v}(CM_{L_{j}}^{k}\bar{L}_{j})^{\mathsf{T}}\right).

Let

L_{k}^{(j)}=CM_{L_{j}}^{k}\bar{L}_{j}=C(A-\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C)^{k}\bar{L}_{j}.

(22)

With this notation,

J_{\rm pred}(L_{j})\geq\alpha_{j}^{2}\lambda_{\min}(R_{v})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|L_{k}^{(j)}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Now let us suppose that
J(L_{j})
is bounded above; from this we will obtain a contradiction.

Under this assumption, using the previous equation, we obtain

\|L_{k}^{(j)}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=O(1/\alpha_{j})\mbox{ for all }k,j\geq 0.

(23)

Now the definition of
L_{k}^{(j)}
can be expanded in the following way. Starting from the general matrix relation

M^{k}-F^{k}=\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}M^{s}(M-F)F^{k-1-s},

we use
M=A-\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C
and
F=A
to obtain

(A-\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C)^{k}=A^{k}-\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}(A-\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C)^{s}(\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C)A^{k-1-s}.

(24)

Multiplying on the left by
C
and on the right

and using (
22

L_{k}^{(j)}=CA^{k}\bar{L}_{j}-\alpha_{j}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}C(A-\alpha_{j}\bar{L}_{j}C)^{s}\bar{L}_{j}CA^{k-1-s}\bar{L}_{j}=CA^{k}\bar{L}_{j}-\alpha_{j}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}L_{s}^{(j)}CA^{k-1-s}\bar{L}_{j}.

(25)

Since
(A,C)
is observable and

, there must be a smallest integer

such that

. We will show that under the assumption that

Assume that

. This contradicts (

Let

We clearly have that

To prove part 2, observe that a priori estimate

6
Conclusion

This paper has four main contributions. First, we derived an interpretable formula for the gradient of the Kalman gain. Second, we showed that spurious stationary points exist under the assumption that the system is observable in the standard Kalman filter formulation (as opposed to one-step look-ahead formulation considered in the earlier literature), preventing gradient descent from recovering the Kalman gain. Third, we identified a non-standard observability condition which is sufficient for gradient descent to recover the Kalman gain. Finally, we gave an interpretable convergence rate for gradient descent under the same condition.

One limitation of our results is that Theorem
4.3
contains the constants
\kappa
and
c
which are in turn defined through optimization over a level set of the function
J_{\rm innov}(L)
. It is an open question whether one can obtain a convergence bound that depends in a clean way only on
A,C,L_{0}
without optimization over a level set. This was accomplished in
[
3
]
for a slightly different objective function, and it is not clear if it can be achieved for the innovations loss we consider here.
