Evaluation of the nutrient content of dishes by the dish-version nutrient profile model in Japanese adults: the NHNS 2019
Authors: Chika Okada, Yuko Tousen, Jun Takebayashi, Katsushi Yoshita, Yoshiko Ishimi, Hidemi Takimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the nutrient content of dishes in Japan by applying a newly developed nutrient profile model for Japanese dishes using nationwide dietary data.MethodsOf the 71,526 dishes consumed, 92% were classified based on the dish criteria of the Japanese Food Guide and its extensions. We assessed staple, main, side, mixed main and side dishes, and mixed dishes with staples. The dish assessment was calculated using the dish-version nutrient profile model, which involved scoring dishes using subtracting recommended nutrient points from restricted nutrient points, indicating that a lower score is healthier.ResultsThe overall score range for all dishes spanned from − 7 to 11 (10–90th percentile). By dish type, the score ranges were as follows: from − 1 to 9 (staple dishes), from − 8 to 9 (main dishes), from − 8 to 4 (side dishes), from − 2 to 20 (mixed dish of main and side), and 2–26 (mixed dish with staples).ConclusionsThe findings clarified the score distribution of dishes in Japan using the dish-version nutrient profile model, reflecting the Japanese dietary pattern. This score distribution, as a multi-factor rating system, may provide useful insights for future research and potential applications in dish-based nutritional evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01322-3.

Introduction

Nutrient profiling is the scientific method used to classify or rank foods according to their nutritional composition [1]. This approach is being incorporated into national policies as a tool for providing consumers with nutritional information. Various methods are used in nutrient profiling, including threshold-based approaches aligned with nutritional guidelines, comprehensive algorithms, and criteria based on nutrient reference values [1]. Threshold-based approaches rely on setting upper or lower limits for nutrients, perfectly aligned with nutritional guidelines. However, comprehensive algorithms, enable further assessment, evaluating multiple nutrients together to give a holistic health assessment. Over the past decade, nutrient profiling systems have gained significant attention for several reasons: (1) they have been developed or endorsed by a government or intergovernmental organization, (2) they allow for the assessment of individual foods, and (3) they have publicly available nutritional criteria. These systems are increasingly employed to evaluate school food standards and front-of-package labelling guidelines. Furthermore, they have been proposed as an effective public health strategy to promote healthier food choices [2]. Evidence from cohort studies evaluating the association between dietary indicators (as reflected in the front-of-package nutrition label) and health outcomes such as all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer revealed that suboptimal dietary indices were often associated with poor health endpoints [3].

Most nutrient profiling frameworks have been developed in Europe and the United States, despite substantial differences in dietary practices across regions. In Japan and China, the primary sources of sodium are discretionary additions during cooking and at the Table [4], a pattern also observed in other Asian countries, including Japan and South Asian nations, where sodium intake largely derives from discretionary additions use rather than processed foods [5]. In contrast, in the UK and the United States, cereals and baked goods are the largest contributors to dietary sodium intake in adults, and approximately 75% of sodium intake in European and North America come from processed foods) [4]. These regional differences highlight the need for nutrient profiling approaches that reflect the characteristics of Japanese dietary practices.

High sodium intake remains one of the leading global dietary risk factors, contributing to substantial mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [6]. Although the incidence of ischemic heart disease attributable to dietary risk factors declined in Japan and South Korea between 1990 and 2019 [7], excessive salt intake contributes to be a major determinant of adult mortality from NCDs and injuries in Japan [8]. Despite a long-term decline in salt consumption [9], the national average intake remains approximately twice the recommended 5 g/day by WHO [10, 11]. These public health concerns underscore the importance of evaluating nutrient profiles in a manner that captures the unique dietary patterns of Japanese adults.

We recently developed a Japanese nutrient profile model (NPM) to evaluate processed foods and dishes that support a healthy diet [12, 13]. Incorporated in this profile model are the reference values of Japan’s dietary and nutritional guidelines to solve Japan’s nutritional issues while harmonizing with international trends and accounting for distinct eating habits and nutritional concerns in Asia. In particular, the dish-version NPM is a new approach to evaluating foods through the ‘dish’ lens. Hence, foods such as seasonings and oils that are not consumed alone but in combination with other multiple food items could be evaluated per dish.

A dish-based approach is also useful for offering consumers healthier combinations of food items. However, the model was initially created using a limited set of standard dishes (n = 105) and has not been examined using actual dietary intake data in Japan.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to describe the distribution of scores from the Japanese dish-version nutrient profiling model (NPM) when applied to nationally representative dietary intake data. By evaluating the nutritional characteristics of dishes consumed by Japanese adults, this study provides empirical insights into the application of the dish-based NPM in real-world dietary patterns.

Materials and methods

Population

We used secondary data approved by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHNS) conducted annually in Japan. In this survey, citizens’ physical condition, dietary intake, and lifestyle were assessed while providing basic data for implementing measures that promote public health. The survey is conducted at public health centers across all Japanese prefectures under the Health Promotion Act (Act No. 103 of 2002) and has been described in detail [14, 15]. The 2019 survey involved participants from households with at least one member as of November 1, 2019. These households were selected from 300 randomly sampled areas defined in the 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions. Excluded from the survey were foreigners, those receiving delivered or prepared meals three times daily, occupants living in single-member households where meals were provided such as dormitories, infants under 1 year, individuals on a special diet, such as those who had only liquids or medicines due to illness, and individuals who were absent throughout the survey period owing to travel, business trips, long-term inpatient hospitalization, and living in a social welfare facility. Because the dish-version NPM was initially developed for adults aged ≥ 18 years [13], the study population was restricted to 5,008 individuals aged ≥ 18 years who completed the one-day dietary survey.

Assessment of dish nutritional content

The details of the dietary survey protocol for NHNS are reported elsewhere [16]. Briefly, the semi-weighed household-based dietary record was conducted on a single weekday in November 2019, chosen as a typical eating day. The household member responsible for meal preparation recorded the dietary status, dish and food names, food and waste weight in the dish, and proportion of food consumed by each household member separately for breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks. Participants used food scales to weigh foods; when unavailable, the dieticians applied standardized weights, based on the nutritional survey manual. The nutrient content of each participant was estimated using a database derived from the Standard Tables for Food Composition in Japan (2015, Seventh Revised Edition), reflecting the 2016, 2017, and 2018 Supplementary editions [17–20]. Sugars include both monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, and galactose) and disaccharides (sucrose, maltose, lactose, and trehalose) [21]. The Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan - Available Carbohydrates, Polyols and Organic Acids - covers 1,075 food items, principally including foods with a high proportion of carbohydrates, foods that are commonly consumed in daily diets, primary raw‑material foods, and representative processed foods. For these cases, we coded missing entries as zero in accordance with NHNS procedures. Food items was classified into 17 groups: cereals, potatoes/starches, sugars/sweeteners, pulses, nuts/seeds, vegetables, fruits, mushrooms, algae, fish/molluscs/crustaceans, meat, eggs, milk/dairy products, fats/oils, confectionaries, beverages, and seasonings/spices, following the classification used in the National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan [11].

Dish category classification

Dish categories were classified following the ‘Japanese Food Guide Spinning Top’ criteria, which indicated the appropriate amount and content of meals for a day, in accordance with the dish-version NPM [13] used in Japan’s nutrition policy since 2005. In the Food Guide, dishes are divided into basic combinations of staple dishes, side dishes, main dishes, and foods that people should actively consume, such as milk and dairy products, fruits, confectionery and beverages, and water and tea, which are defined as food categories [22, 23]. Briefly, staple, main, and side dishes were classified as dishes that contain more than 2/3 weight of the following main food items: staple foods: rice, bread (excluding sweetbreads), noodles, and other cereal foods; side dishes: vegetables, potatoes, beans other than soybeans, mushrooms, and seaweed; and main dishes: meat, fish, eggs, soybeans, and soybean products. One serving of staple dish contains 40 g of carbohydrates from the main food item, one serving of side dish amounts to 70 g of the main food item, and one serving of main dish contains 6 g of protein from the main food item, as indicated in the food guide.

We defined mixed dishes as those that did not meet the criteria of any of the staple, side, or main dishes and that combined two or more types of dishes, each containing at least half a serving. This was a revised version of the previous definition [24]. Based on the composition and nutritional characteristics of the Japanese diet, we further divided mixed dishes into two categories: a combination of main and side dishes, as well as a staple dish and either a main dish or a side dish. Additional categories included fruits (more than 2/3 of the main food item was fresh fruit), milk or dairy products, beverages, and confectioneries. Dishes without clear nutritional or food characteristics were labelled as ‘soup’ or categorized as unclassifiable. This occurred when neither the main‑ingredient criterion for classifying main or side dishes (≥ 2/3 of the primary ingredient) nor the criteria for mixed dishes could be applied because the serving size was too small, as in some small Japanese side dishes.

Japan NPM

NPMs for processed foods and dishes in Japan were developed as a localized version for Japan, a country in Asia with a unique food culture [25], and have been described in detail [12, 13]. They were developed based on the concept of the Health Star Rating (HSR) System [26], an internationally used NPM, and incorporated national policy reference values to ensure international consistency and relevance to domestic nutrition policies. The standard values for the Japan localized score table were based on the target indicators of the national health promotion movement (named ‘Health Japan 21 (the third term))’ initiative, which started in the 2024 fiscal year [27]; the nutrient reference values established in 2015 (established based on the dietary intake standards by sex and age for those aged 18 and over) [28]; and the Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese 2020 [29]. Specifically, energy, protein, and dietary fiber were set at 3.75% of the nutrient labelling standard values (2,200 kcal, 81 g, and 19 g, respectively); sodium was set at 3.75% of the 7 g target value of ‘Health Japan 21 (the third term)’; saturated fat was set at 7%E, as set by the Dietary Reference Intakes; and total sugar was set at 10%E, based on the WHO guideline [30], because no standard value has been established in Japan. This 3.75% serves as the standard applied in the UK Nutrient Profiling Model [31] and is established as the baseline point for scoring individual nutrients. The maximum points of each nutrient were set using the HSR [26], which refers to the basic concept. The fruit, vegetable, nut, and legume (vegetable-based) points were calculated using the HSR concept as the total percentage of fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, seeds, and seaweed. Potatoes are included in vegetables in Europe and Australia; however, in Japan, potatoes are excluded from vegetables. Serving sizes were defined based on the dish units recorded in the dietary records of the NHNS, reflecting actual consumption per eating occasion. Baseline points and final scores were calculated using the following steps. (1) Calculate baseline points as the sum of energy points, saturated fat points, total sugar, sodium points. (2) Calculate modification points as the sum of fruit, vegetable, nuts and legumes (V) points, protein-based (P) points, and dietary fiber-based (F) points. (3) Calculate final scores by subtracting modification points from baseline points. Each specific score was calculated for each serving of a dish. In this study, the ‘protein cap’ (P point is “0” if baseline points ≥ 13 with V points < 5) was not applied. This is because protein sources in these dishes are whole foods, and the original protein cap in the HSR was designed for packaged foods, to prevent adding protein to products with high baseline points just to make the score better. For comparison with the original dish-version NPM [13], we also calculated scores with the ‘protein cap’ applied. The calculated final score indicates that a lower value corresponds to a healthier profile.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the nutritional and food composition characteristics of Japanese dishes and to compare the dish-version NPM scores across dishes. In the 2019 survey, 300 unit-districts (UDs) were randomly sampled from the larger number of UDs for the preceding Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC) in the same year. UDs of CSLC were created by dividing the Population Census district geographically, and approximately 5,500 Census districts were selected for the large-scale CSLC in 2019. In the sampled 300 UDs, NHNS_J subjects were household members aged ≥ 1 years, who also participated in the CSLC. Sampling weights are not applied in NHNS except for large-scale surveys in 2012 and 2016, where two-stage sampling was not applied [32].

Medians and interquartile ranges are presented using their non-normal distributions and are compared between dishes or dietary patterns. Differences across dish categories were examined using linear models with participant‑level cluster‑robust standard errors, accounting for the non‑independence of multiple dishes reported by the same participant. The Wilcoxon Sign-Rank test was conducted to assess the protein cap. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 shows the participant characteristics. The population in this study comprised slightly more females (53.3%) than males, with a median age of 61 years. Of the 71,526 dishes consumed by the 5,008 participants, 92% (65,971) were classified into 10 categories (Fig. 1a). Following the classification in the dish-version NPM, 16% were staple, 13% main, 20% side, 3% mixed dishes, and 4% mixed dishes with staple. The proportion of staple dishes was consistent across breakfast, lunch, and dinner, while side dishes were more frequently consumed at dinner (Fig. 1b). Foods within certain dish categories, such as fruits, milk, dairy products, beverages, and confectionaries, typically consisted by a single food item (Fig. 2). Dishes were evaluated across five dish categories, excluding foods consumed as a single food item, such as candy, yoghurt, or fresh fruit/ vegetables, soups, and dishes that could not be classified based on nutrient content because they did not match the classification criteria. Additionally, snacks were excluded from the meal category (breakfast, lunch, dinner) owing to their supplementary nature. This adjustment left a total of 40,108 dishes for final analysis.

Table 2 shows the nutrient and food group characteristics of the five dish categories across breakfast, lunch, and dinner per dish. Energy content was highest in mixed dishes containing staples, followed by mixed dishes of main and side, and staple, main, and side dishes (the other six dishes are shown in Supplementary Table 1.). Protein and fat contents were highest in mixed dishes with staples, followed by mixed dishes of main, side dishes, and then main dishes. Carbohydrate content was highest in the mixed dishes containing staples and staple foods. Sodium content was highest in mixed dishes with staples, followed by mixed dishes of main and side, main dishes, and side dishes. In the food group, staple foods were mainly cereals; main dishes were mainly seafood; side dishes were mainly vegetables; mixed dishes of main and side were mainly a combination of vegetables, and meat; and mixed dishes with staples had many cereals, as well as vegetables, and meat. Sugars or sweeteners, nuts and seeds, fruits, seaweed, dairy products, confectioneries, and beverages were all absent (up to the 75th percentile) in the five main dishes. Analyses using linear models with participant‑level cluster‑robust standard errors showed significant differences in the distribution of most food groups across five dish categories (p < 0.05). These patterns indicate that the food groups differences across dish categories reflect underlying variation in food‑group composition. In contrast, food groups with very low overall content—such as nuts, fruits, mushrooms, algae, milk and dairy products, and confectionaries—showed no significant differences between some dish categories. Unclassified dishes weighed approximately 80 g per dish (see Supplementary Table 1).

The final scores and individual points assessed using the modified dish-version NPM in Japan are shown in Table 3. The final scores for all dishes ranged from − 7 to 11 (10th–90th percentile). By dish type, scores ranged from − 1 to 9 for staple dishes, from − 8 to 9 for main dishes, from − 8 to 4 for side dishes, from − 2 to 20 for mixed dishes of main and side, and 2–26 for mixed dishes with staples (all 10th–90th percentile). Staple foods had the narrowest range of final scores, with 10th–40th percentile values remaining consistent at -1 and median and 60th percentile values at zero. Baseline points were highest in mixed dishes with staples (median 24, interquartile range [IQR]: 17–34), followed by mixed dishes of main and side (16, 10–25). Main (median 5, IQR: 2–9), staple (median 4, IQR: 2–7), and side dishes (median 2, IQR: 1–6) scored lower. Sodium points contributed the most to baseline points with the exception of staple dish, followed by saturated fatty acid points in mixed dishes. This was supported by the distribution of baseline point components, where sodium showed the highest median contribution (median: 10, IQR: 6–15) for mix dishes with staple, compared with other nutrients such as energy (median: 5, IQR: 4–7). The maximum sugar point was set at 25. However, for dishes in Japan, the sugar points were minimal. The V points, evaluated as the percentage of fruit, vegetable, nut, and legume content per dish, showed a higher contribution in side dishes (median: 5, IQR: 3–7) compared with mixed dishes of main and side (median: 1, IQR: 0–1). In contrast, the P points were higher in mixed dishes with staple (median: 7, IQR: 5–9) and mix dishes with main and side (median: 5, IQR: 3–8), indicating that these dishes contained a greater proportion of protein-rich ingredients. The F points were most notable in mixed dishes with staples (median: 5, IQR: 3–7) but were also present in mixed dishes with main and side (median: 3, IQR: 2–5), and staple dishes (median: 3, IQR: 2–4). Analyses using linear models with cluster‑robust standard errors, which accounted for multiple dishes nested within participants, also showed significant differences in final scores across dish categories (p < 0.001). When the original protein cap rule was applied (Supplementary Table 2), median final scores increased in multiple categories—for example, from 6 to 11 in mixed dishes of main and side, and from 12 to 19 in mixed dishes with staples—while staple, main, and side dishes showed smaller shifts. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first in Japan involving the application of a newly developed dish-version NPM based on the national dietary guidelines to nationwide population-based dietary data. As shown in Fig. 2, approximately 90% of the dishes were classified into 10 types, including staple foods, main dishes, side dishes, or dishes where these elements are combined. The dish-version NPM for Japanese, developed in accordance with Japan’s public health issues, was practical to evaluate dishes consumed among adult participants in a nationally representative survey.

We categorized dishes based on their food composition and primarily evaluated those containing multiple food items, including seasonings. The concepts of staple food, main dishes, and side dishes have been incorporated into the dietary guidelines in Japan [22], and a mixed dish was defined as one that has multiple (two or more) of these elements. Mixed dishes were further defined by including or excluding staple foods in their combination, resulting in non-overlapping energy IQRs. Among the five Japanese dishes, mixed dishes with staples, such as curry rice and donburi (rice bowl), had the highest weight and energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate, and sodium contents. In contrast, mixed dishes consisting of main (e.g., meat) and side (e.g., vegetables) exhibited comparable protein and fat (including saturated fatty acids) levels but lower carbohydrate content resulting in lower overall energy. The characteristics of the five dish categories in this study highlighted their low sugar and higher sodium content [11]. Dishes that were made of a single food item (processed food and fresh food, such as fruits and vegetables) and those that matched the classification criteria were excluded from the score evaluation in this study. However, future evaluations of single foods that are consumed alongside combined dishes should be considered.

NPMs are widely used internationally to guide healthier food choices and support product reformulation. Systems such as Nutri-Score and the Health Star Rating (HSR) classify foods based on energy, saturated fat, sugar, salt, dietary fiber, and FVNL, while threshold-based systems like the Keyhole and Healthier Choice Symbol identify products meeting specific nutrient criteria [33–40]. In Japan, we recently developed a nutrient profiling system for foods per 100 g/mL and subsequently created a dish-version NPM that evaluates dishes as consumed (per serving), reflecting Japanese dietary culture [12, 13]. The present study extends previous work by applying this dish-version NPM to nationally representative dietary data, providing descriptive epidemiological insights into the nutritional quality of dishes commonly consumed in Japan. Lower dish‑version NPM final scores represent dishes with more favorable nutritional profiles, whereas higher scores indicate dishes with less desirable nutritional characteristics. These score differences reflect the balance of nutrients considered within the model and may provide insights into the relative nutritional characteristics of dishes. However, the current NPM scores are model-based indicators constructed under predefined assumptions, rather than a direct measure of the intrinsic nutritional quality of dishes. The model combines nutrient-based and food-based components, and may not fully cover individual-based nutrient requirements. Therefore, this should be interpreted with caution.

In practice, meals consist of combinations of multiple dishes, and the overall nutritional quality depends on the combination of dishes consumed. The current dish-level NPM scores should not be interpreted as direct indicators of meal-level or overall dietary quality. Further research is needed to explore how dish-level scores may be applied to meal-based or one-day assessments in practical settings.

Internationally, NPMs have been linked to diet quality and health outcomes, such as lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with higher HSR-rated diets [41]. Although similar evidence is lacking in Japan, prior studies indicate that dietary fiber and fruit intake are associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular disease and dementia [42–44], and nutrient–disease associations may differ in Asian populations [45–48]. The present findings offer foundational data for future research examining how dish-based NPM scores relate to health outcomes in Japan.

Excessive salt intake remains the major public health concern in Japan [8]. Population-wide salt reduction interventions are highly cost-effective [49]. While NPMs for processed foods can support product reformulation, consumers often add salt during cooking and at the table. Our results showed that sodium contributed the most to baseline points, highlighting the importance of addressing both commercially available foods and consumer behavior. The combined use of profiling models for processed foods and dishes may have potential applications for supporting salt reduction at the population level at the population level.

The strength of this study lies in the use of the dish-version NPM that incorporates multiple nutrients and food elements to improve public health issues and evaluate actual dietary data in Japan. Nutrient profiles have been used to evaluate processed foods; nevertheless, we applied the dish-version NPM to analyze actual dish content, considering that not only seasonings and oils are consumed. Because Japanese meals are not composed of single food items but combinations of multiple dishes, evaluating nutritional quality at the dish level may provide practical value for informing the development of healthier dishes and supporting public health efforts. These findings could be applied to a broader East Asian context, as countries such as South Korea and China also have similar meals consisting of staple, side, and main dishes, with high sodium content.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. First, small portion-sized or mixed dishes with equal proportions of main and side food items were not accounted for in the criteria for dish classification. However, we updated the previous definition [24] to include a greater number of dish categories. Second, within the staple dish category, where rice is a defining characteristic of the Japanese diet, the final scores for the 10th percentile divisions showed minimal variability. This limited variability suggests that the nutrient profiling model may have reduced discriminatory ability within the staple dish category, where nutritional compositions are inherently homogeneous. Therefore, score differences within this category should be interpreted with caution. Third, missing nutrient values, including sugars, were coded as zero. As sugar is one component of the nutrient profiling model, this approach may have resulted in a slight underestimation of sugar-related points for a limited number of dishes. Future improvements in data availability in the food composition tables will enable more accurate assessments of sugar content. Finally, the results from this study differed from those in our previous study [13]. When scoring was performed without the protein cap, the final score for five dishes, especially two mixed dishes, was significantly lower than those of the original dish-version NPM. Applying the protein cap to mixed dishes would increase the final scores of protein‑rich traditional dishes (e.g., fish‑based or tofu‑based dishes), resulting in consumer misunderstanding of the nutritional quality of these dishes. In addition, the values of the distribution of scores differed between the current and the previous study [13], even when the protein cap was used. This finding may be because the current study was based on dietary intake data from the free-living adult population. Hence, more recipe data on frequently consumed Japanese dishes, such as those sold in supermarkets and convenience stores are required [50]. The distribution of the score would depend on the target dish, which would be different even when the same method of NPM is used.

In conclusion, we applied the Japanese dish-version NPM to nationally representative dietary intake data and described the distribution of scores across dish categories, which reflect current Japanese dietary patterns. The modification of not applying the protein cap in lower final scores for mixed dishes, including those containing traditional food items such as fish/seafood or soy products. In addition, the observed associations between NPM scores and selected nutritional characteristics (e.g., vegetable and sodium content) were consistent with the conceptual structure of the model. These findings provide empirical insights into the application and interpretation of dish-based nutrient profiling and may inform future research and potential applications in Japan.
