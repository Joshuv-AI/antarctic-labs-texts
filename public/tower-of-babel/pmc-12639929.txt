Biomechanical study of hybrid-fixed femoral prosthesis in total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Yongqi Wang, Yuchi Zhao, Tingxin Yan, Jiangkun Qu, Haiyang Tang, Junjie Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCemented prostheses provide superior initial stability but are associated with a higher incidence of postoperative radiolucent lines in the posterior femoral condyle, which are critical predictors of loosening. Cementless prostheses rarely lead to the development of radiolucent lines but demonstrate weaker initial stability. To address these limitations, i.e., to extend prosthesis longevity while providing optimal initial stability, in this study, we developed a hybrid prosthesis design and explored whether it could achieve the same initial fixation stability as that of conventional cemented prostheses.MethodsWe developed a hybrid prosthesis with a cementless posterior condylar interface covered with biological coating. Using synthetic bone models, we created three femoral prosthesis fixation models: fully cemented, non-cemented posterior condyle, and hybrid. We analyzed in vitro force-displacement data to evaluate the impact of different fixation methods on the initial stability of the femoral component.ResultsThe hybrid prosthesis achieved initial stability comparable to that of fully cemented prostheses. Furthermore, the biological coating may enhance long-term stability by promoting bone ingrowth in the posterior condylar area, potentially surpassing the durability of fully cemented designs and mitigating the risk of prosthetic loosening.ConclusionsThe hybrid design maintained initial stability and may allow for potential long-term improvements through bone ingrowth.Keywords: Cementless, Hybrid-fixed femoral prosthesis, Total knee arthroplasty

Background

Total knee arthroplasty is the gold standard for treating end-stage knee osteoarthritis. By 2030, an estimated 3.48 million total knee arthroplasties will be performed annually in the United States [1]. Knee prostheses are secured via cemented and cementless fixation. Cemented fixation remains the gold standard due to its benefits in filling defects, hemostasis [2], cost-effectiveness [3, 4], and satisfactory initial stability. Patients can perform weight-bearing activities early, promoting early rehabilitation. For patients who have poor bone quality, defects and inaccuracies in bone cuts can be filled with antibiotic-infused cement to reduce the risk of infection [4].

Cementless fixation preserves bone stock, avoids cement debris, and achieves durable biological fixation through bone ingrowth between the prosthesis and host bone [5, 6]. However, it requires high osteotomy precision, often necessitating navigation or robotic assistance, and small bone defects cannot be adequately filled [4]. Additionally, cementless prostheses exhibit inferior initial stability postoperatively. For this reason, intraoperative stability testing is performed, and conversion to a cemented prosthesis becomes necessary in cases of insufficient stability. Moreover, cementless fixation allows weight-bearing walking only after 4–6 weeks post-surgery and requires restricting high-flexion activities, hindering early rehabilitation.

Although cemented prostheses offer high initial stability, they are associated with a higher incidence of radiolucent lines in the femoral posterior condyle postoperatively. Radiolucent lines are important indicators of potential loosening. In contrast, cementless prostheses rarely lead to the development of postoperative radiolucent lines [7, 8]. Therefore, we hypothesized that redesigning the posterior condyle of fully cemented prostheses as a cementless interface can reduce the incidence of radiolucent lines while retaining the early stability advantages of cemented fixation. Furthermore, bone ingrowth into the cementless posterior condyle over time may extend prosthesis longevity. Thus, such a hybrid design would integrate the benefits of both fixation methods.

In this study, we developed a hybrid prosthesis with cementless design for the posterior condylar region and performed biomechanical testing to investigate whether it could achieve the same initial fixation stability as conventional cemented prostheses.

Methods

Study design

We used nine standard left femur models (#3403; Sawbones, Vashon, WA, USA) and nine cruciate-retaining-type cemented femur prostheses (No. 14 L; Double Medical, Xiamen, China). The femur models were randomly divided into Groups A, B, and C, and three different prosthesis fixation models were created: fully cemented, non-cemented posterior condyle, and hybrid, respectively. Next, we conducted biomechanical testing across the three fixation models using gait loading, deep knee bend (DKB), and high-flexion push-off tests and analyzed force-displacement data to evaluate differences in initial stability and fixation strength among the fixation models.

Prosthesis preparation

In three femur prostheses (Group C), the posterior condyle was sprayed with titanium metal powder using plasma technology, forming hybrid cementless prostheses with a biological coating on the posterior condyle (Fig. 1), whereas the remaining areas retained cement coating. The cementless coating was pure titanium, with a spraying thickness of 1.4 ± 0.5 mm, roughness of 70–85 μm, and porosity of 45–55%. The spraying area difference was ≤ 5% and the friction coefficient was 1.3, conforming to ASTM F1580 standards for pure titanium coating.

Hybrid prosthesis. The circles indicate the cementless areas

Femur preparation

Simulated procedures were performed for the nine femur models following standard protocols. Following distal femoral pulp opening, positioning, and osteotomy, a bone bed was prepared for prosthesis installation.

Cement preparation and prosthesis implantation

The cement (Refobacin Bone Cement R; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) was prepared under controlled conditions (20 °C, 70% humidity). The polymer powder was placed in a dry stainless-steel container, and the monomer liquid was added to initiate polymerization. The mixture was stirred clockwise at a constant speed (2 cycles/s) for 1 min, then left to stand for 2 min before application.

Cement application varied across groups. In Group A, cement was applied to all areas, including the posterior condyle (Fig. 2A). In Group B, cement was not applied to the posterior condyles of the three femurs or the corresponding prosthesis areas (Fig. 2B). In Group C, a prosthesis with a cementless coating on the posterior condyle was used. No cement was applied to the posterior condyle of either the prosthesis or the femur (Fig. 2C). The posterior condyle of the prosthesis was first in contact with the prosthetic bone area, after which the surfaces were initially embedded from back to front.

The prosthesis was pressed with a hammer, and the prosthesis-femur models were positioned on the MTS Bionix desktop test system (858; MTS Bionix, Eden Prairie, MN, USA) in a simulated knee extension. A compressive force of 1200 N was applied vertically until the cement cured, and excess cement was removed during the process (Fig. 3).

Prosthesis fixation. The prosthesis-femur models were placed on the MTS Bionix desktop test system

Additional processes

The speckle spray was applied after stencil printing. After spraying, the femur was embedded in resin and fixed to the stress system base. The regions of interest (ROIs) were calibrated, and tibial shims of the same size were used to counter the applied load. The Xinto three-dimensional (3D) full-field strain measurement and analysis system (XTDIC-FLEX-PL; XTOP3D, Shenzhen, China) was used for digital image correlation (DIC) detection [9].

This includes the medial and lateral condyles from the posterior view (posterior medial [PM] and posterior lateral [PL]) and the anterior (medial anterior [MA]), distal (medial distal [MD]), and posterior (medial posterior [MP]) faces from the medial view. Due to significant knee flexion and limited visibility, the posterior ROI was excluded during DKB weight-bearing. The observed regions for stress-load ROI analysis during knee-flexed gait are shown in Fig. 4B, including: MA, MD, MP. In the high-flexion push-off test, DIC was captured only from the posterior condyle, mainly from the medial view. A previous study has shown a high proportion of medial load, which may cause bone damage [10]. Additionally, the load is directly applied to the posterior condyle.

Biomechanical testing

We used a simplified load test setup. A previous finite element study indicated that micromotion at a single peak tibiofemoral (TF) contact force represents the entire activity cycle [11]. Thus, the peak TF force for each activity (gait and DKB) was reproduced in an experimental setting at a rate of 100 N/s by applying a compression force to a controlled load. Before formal measurements, specimen load positions were preconditioned for 15 min at a frequency of 1 Hz (900 cycles) to allow for initial implant settling. Simulated knee extension gait: stress-load-displacement analysis of fixation methods on prosthesis stability

The sample model was fixed to the MTS Bionix tabletop test system using a specialized fixture, and loads were applied at a rate of 100 N/s until the peak TF force was reached. A 3D strain measurement and analysis system simulated normal gait and assessed bone-implant interface phase displacement under different loads, recording the strain-displacement curve. This analysis examined the influence of different fixation methods on prosthesis stability during knee extension gait (Fig. 5A).

Simulated DKB: stress-load-displacement analysis of fixation methods on prosthesis stability

The sample model was fixed to the MTS Bionix desktop test system using a specialized fixture and subjected to a load of up to 1500 N at a rate of 100 N/s (peak force: 2000 N). Only 75% of the peak force was applied, as a previous experimental study reported bone fractures when full peak force was applied in a similar setup [10]. Force-displacement data were recorded to evaluate the influence of different fixation methods on prosthesis stability during DKB (Fig. 5B).

High-flexion push-off load test for prosthesis stability

Using a specialized mold, the load was directly applied to the posterior condylar edge of the prosthesis to simulate the push-off force of the tibia against the femur in a high-flexion position (150° knee bend). The same stress simulation and DIC observation systems were used to apply the load and record the load-displacement curve. The load gradually increased until the prosthesis detached, and the maximum push-off force, cause of prosthesis failure, and reason for experiment termination were recorded (Fig. 5C).

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to evaluate initial stability and fixation strength differences among the three implants. The measured data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

High-flexion push-off test

In the high-flexion push-off test, the differences in micromotion among the three groups, including differences in each ROI and the average across the three ROIs, were not significant (P > 0.05). Micromotion did not follow a normal distribution. The median values for each group were as follows: Group A: 56.5 μm (interquartile range [IQR]: 781.7 μm), Group B: 19.3 μm (IQR: 77.4 μm), and Group C: 547.4 μm (IQR: 916.7 μm).

The differences among groups in failure load during the high-flexion push-off test were also not significant (P > 0.05). Failure loads did not follow a normal distribution. The median values for each group were as follows: Group A: 2,124.2 N (IQR: 764.2 N), Group B: 2132.7 N (IQR: 494.2 N), and Group C: 2630.5 N (IQR: 389.2 N). Discussion

Cemented prostheses provide superior initial stability but are associated with a higher incidence of radiolucent lines in the posterior femoral condyle postoperatively. Radiolucent lines are key indicators of potential loosening. A cohort undergoing simultaneous bilateral knee arthroplasty was followed up for 7.6 ± 4.4 years; radiolucency in the posterior condylar region appeared in 69% of the cemented group, whereas no radiolucent lines were detected in the cementless group [7].

Imaging follow-ups of femoral prosthetic failures consistently revealed radiolucent lines, with the earliest onset and highest prevalence observed in the posterior femoral condylar region [8, 12]. The high incidence of radiolucent lines in the posterior condylar region primarily results from surgical practices aimed at avoiding cement retention caused by difficult posterior compartment access. Surgeons often apply a minimal amount of cement on the posterior condylar bone bed, applying only a thin layer to the prosthetic surface. Even with adequate cement volume, predominant shear forces during component impaction prevent effective pressurization, resulting in inadequate cement penetration (< 3–5 mm vs. the ideal 3–5 mm for stable fixation) [13, 14]. This compromises cement-bone interlock formation, impairing stress transfer to the posterior condyle and promoting progressive bone resorption through stress shielding [15]. Cementless prostheses achieve initial stability through intimate bone contact and press-fit fixation, obviating the need for cement penetration due to their porous coating. Long-term stability arises from osseointegration (bone ingrowth) or bone ongrowth [10, 16], explaining the lower incidence of radiolucent lines in cementless designs [17]. However, cementless prostheses exhibit inferior early postoperative stability, requiring delayed weight-bearing (4–6 weeks) and restricted high-flexion activities that impede rehabilitation.

Our hybrid design integrates a cementless posterior condylar interface with cemented fixation on the rest of the prosthesis. This approach combines the immediate stability of cementation with the reduced long-term loosening risk of posterior cementless fixation. However, no commercial products currently employ this hybrid design, and its impact on initial stability requires validation. We modified a clinical cemented femoral component by applying a plasma-sprayed metallic coating to its posterior condyle, conducting in vitro biomechanical testing (simulated gait loading, DKB, and high-flexion pull-off tests) to evaluate initial stability across fixation modes.

The relative displacements observed in this study are similar to those reported in previous research. Conlisk et al. [18] documented displacements ranging from 0 to 45 μm in cemented conditions and from 4 to 145 μm in cementless conditions. Similarly, Wackerhagen et al. [19] reported values between 14 and 250 μm, while Cristofolini et al. [20] recorded displacements from 9 to 130 μm. Berahmani et al. [10] also presented comparable data. Additionally, the withdrawal force of the prosthesis tested under simulated high-flexion conditions in this study was similar to the average push-off force of 2229 ± 756 N reported by Anijs et al. [21].

In the simulated knee extension phase of the gait stress-load test, the maximal relative displacement occurred at the PM site in five of the nine groups, whereas the minimal relative displacement occurred at the MA and MP sites in three groups each. In the simulated deep knee flexion phase of the gait stress-load test, the maximal relative displacement occurred at the MP site in six groups, whereas the minimal relative displacement occurred at the MD site in seven groups. In the high-flexion prosthesis push-off force test, the MD site exhibited maximal relative displacement in seven out of nine groups, whereas the MA site demonstrated minimal relative displacement in six groups.

Seven of the nine sample models had the smallest relative displacement in the MD region during the simulated DKB load test, while the same number exhibited the largest relative displacement in the MD region during the prosthesis push-off force test in the high-flexion state. The posterior condyle had two ROIs (PM and MP) with maximal relative displacement in five of the nine sample models during the simulated knee extension gait stress-load test and in two-thirds of the models during the simulated DKB load test. These findings suggest that close contact between the posterior condyle and the bone bed is critical in maintaining prosthetic stability during knee flexion.

In the high-flexion push-off test, Group C exhibited a failure load that was approximately 500 N higher than those of Groups A and B. Although this difference was not significant, it may have clinical relevance, as the increased stability and enhanced pull-out resistance during high-flexion activities suggest improved early-stage stability for high-flexion prostheses. The observed intragroup variability may be explained by discrepancies in osteotomy accuracy and variations in cement application thickness.

When comparing the three groups in terms of relative displacement or push-off force, we observed no significant differences. These findings indicate that coating the posterior condyle with cement does not enhance the initial stability of the femoral prosthesis with the host bone. Additionally, the cementless prosthesis for the posterior condyle and the fully cemented prosthesis provided comparable initial stability. In animal models, Liu et al. [22] demonstrated that the fixation strength of cementless knee prostheses surpassed that of cemented prostheses at both 3 and 6 months postoperatively. Furthermore, other studies [23–25] have found no differences in postoperative survival rates between fully cemented and cementless knee prostheses. Therefore, we speculate that the cementless coating in the posterior condylar region of the hybrid femoral component may enhance stability by promoting bone ingrowth, thereby improving the long-term survival rate of hybrid prostheses.

This study has certain limitations. First, we used synthetic bones rather than human bones. Thus, we could not replicate the in vivo environment of human bones, including key procedures such as osteotomy and cement application. However, synthetic bones offer the advantage of uniform structure and density, allowing for consistent implant sizing. Second, as all synthetic bones used in this experiment were of the same specification and model, we did not perform 3D optical scanning to verify osteotomy precision after bone cutting. Despite using identical osteotomy surgical instruments, perfect consistency across sections could not be guaranteed. Third, we only conducted biomechanical testing of the initial stability of fully cemented and hybrid prostheses using synthetic bones. Further large-sample longitudinal studies using animal models are warranted to evaluate the long-term survival of hybrid prostheses.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that coating the posterior condyle with cement does not enhance the initial stability of the femoral prosthesis with the host bone. Additionally, no difference in initial stability was observed between the cementless prosthesis in the posterior condyle and the fully cemented prosthesis. Moreover, over time, the cementless coating can induce bone ingrowth, further strengthening the stability of the posterior condyle. These findings suggest that long-term stability may be superior with hybrid prostheses compared to fully cemented prostheses; however, further validation through clinical trials or comparative studies with existing prosthetic designs is warranted.
