Mitochondrial regulation of lactylation in sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yiyue Zhong, Siman Shen, Qiong You, Jue Wang, Qibiao Wu, Liangqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) is a life-threatening complication of sepsis that is characterized by acute cardiac dysfunction and is associated with high mortality. Despite advances in detection techniques that have improved the identification of myocardial abnormalities, the link between these findings and effective therapeutic strategies remains poorly defined. Mitochondrial dysfunction—through its roles in metabolic reprogramming, oxidative stress, and calcium dysregulation—has emerged as a central feature and putative driver of SICM. Recently, lactylation, a lactate-derived posttranslational modification, has been identified as a key mechanism amplifying inflammatory and metabolic dysfunction under these conditions. However, a comprehensive understanding of how mitochondrial dysregulation is mechanistically linked to lactylation and how this axis contributes to the progression of SICM is still lacking. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on the mitochondrial regulation of lactylation in SICM, elucidate the underlying molecular pathways, and evaluate potential therapeutic interventions targeting this axis. By integrating mechanistic insights from preclinical and clinical studies, we seek to bridge the gap between mitochondrial biology and epigenetic metabolic regulation, with the goal of informing future research and improving clinical outcomes in SICM.Keywords: Sepsis, Lactylation, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Epigenetic regulation, Metabolic reprogramming

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) is considered a potential precursor or contributing factor to septic shock and substantially increases the risk of mortality [1]. The widely accepted definition of SICM is acute single- or biventricular systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction unrelated to coronary disease [2]; it is characterized by circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities and is generally considered a functional rather than a biochemical phenomenon [3]. Therefore, mitochondria play a central role in SICM through various processes [2, 3], including energy metabolism, oxidative stress regulation, calcium homeostasis, morphodynamic changes, and metabolic reprogramming.

Mitochondria directly contribute to lactate generation and accumulation in SICM by suppressing oxidative phosphorylation [4], orchestrating metabolic reprogramming [5], and disrupting energy homeostasis [6]. During sepsis, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are detected by host pattern recognition receptors, triggering immune system activation and the release of substantial amounts of inflammatory mediators, which directly impair mitochondrial structure and function [7, 8]. Excessive lactate accumulation propagates lactylation, amplifying proinflammatory gene expression and impairing myocardial contractility [9–11].

Mitochondrial protein lactylation, a newly identified metabolic epigenetic modification in SICM [11], is associated with increased mortality [12]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is the upstream driving factor of lactate accumulation [7, 13, 14] and further amplifies mitochondrial damage by inhibiting functional proteins such as HADHA [11], resulting in a vicious cycle. Lactate accumulation primarily stems from mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase dysregulation and glycolytic flux in SICM [4, 15]. Crucially, lactate functions as a substrate for posttranslational lactylation, modifying nuclear histones and mitochondrial proteins to propagate inflammation [13]. Physiologically, lactate is oxidized without pathological consequences [4, 16]; however, mitochondrial dysfunction during sepsis disrupts the clearance of lactate, enabling lactylation-mediated epigenetic reprogramming [13, 15, 17]. For example, histone lactylation amplifies NF-κB-dependent transcription [18], exacerbating myocardial injury. An additional related issue involves differentiating between adaptive and pathological impairments in myocardial function [3]. These alterations highlight the role of lactylation in the pathology of SICM, necessitating the elucidation of the underlying mechanisms involved.

Here, we discuss the molecular mechanisms by which mitochondrial dysfunction triggers lactylation, the signal transduction cascades through which mitochondria regulate lactylation processes, and the clinical relevance of the mitochondria-mediated regulation of lactylation in SICM. This analysis is particularly relevant not only because researchers in multiple fields of biomedical research have begun to understand the impact of dysregulated lactylation in SICM but also because mitochondria-targeting agents are now being used for the clinical management of sepsis [19], raising considerable expectations for further developments in the context of SICM metabolic regulation [20].

Central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in SICM

Mitochondria serve as central regulators in the pathophysiology of SICM, orchestrating processes ranging from immune response activation to programmed cell death [2, 3]. In this context, SICM is characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction across three interconnected axes: structural destabilization, metabolic reprogramming, and calcium‒lactate crosstalk. These perturbations converge to amplify lactate production and protein lactylation, resulting in a self-reinforcing pathological loop.

Structural instability

Pathology fission

Pathologic mitochondrial fission is a key feature of SICM that is driven by dysregulated Drp1 activation [7] (Fig. 1). During sepsis, hyperactivation of Drp1 at mitochondrial tips—a process termed peripheral fission, which is often associated with the removal of damaged components—is preceded by lysosomal contact and is regulated by the mitochondrial outer membrane protein Fis1 in cardiomyocytes [7], triggering excessive mitochondrial fission and increasing glycolysis (Warburg effect) in the heart [21, 22]. Critically, lactate accumulation in sepsis induces Fis1 lactylation at Lys [21, 23], which stabilizes the Fis1–Drp1 complex and increases fission activity. This process creates a self-reinforcing cycle: fission-generated mitochondrial debris further activates Drp1 via reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent oxidation of its cysteine residues [7, 24] while simultaneously depleting complex I subunits through oxidative damage mechanisms [4]. The resulting impairment of the electron transport chain reduces ATP synthesis [25] and increases ROS production [26], establishing a hub of bioenergetic failure and oxidative injury that propagates cardiomyocyte death. Mitochondrial permeabilization also initiates regulated programmed cell death [27], releasing DAMPs [9, 28] that amplify lactylation-mediated inflammation [29], thereby closing the loop between structural collapse and immune activation. Collectively, these findings indicate that pathological fission is not only a consequence of damage but also a fundamental driver of mitochondrial structural instability in SICM.

Mitophagy suppression

In SICM, mitochondrial permeability transitions and outer membrane permeabilization disrupt mitochondrial integrity, resulting in the release of mtDNA and ROS as DAMPs that amplify inflammation processes and suppress mitophagy by inactivating the PINK1–Parkin pathway [8, 30, 31]. This suppression is exacerbated by hyperactivation of IL-6/STAT3 [32], downregulation of FUNDC1-dependent mitophagy and promotion of mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membrane formation [33, 34], which drive calcium overload and lactylation-associated inflammation [35]. Concurrently, RIPK3 upregulation inhibits YAP/TFEB-mediated mitophagy [36], whereas Mst1 overexpression impairs Parkin-dependent clearance [37], leading to the accumulation of oxidized mtDNA and peroxidized cardiolipin. These oxidized DAMPs directly activate AIM2 inflammasomes and potentiate NLRP3 activity via ROS-induced TXNIP dissociation, increasing the occurrence of pyroptosis and cytokine storms [38, 39]. Ultimately, contractile failure results from ATP depletion and calcium dysregulation [40], whereas the therapeutic restoration of mitophagy (e.g., via FUNDC1 induction or Mst1 deletion) mitigates lactate-driven inflammation by removing ROS-generating organelles [37, 41].

Biogenesis failure

Pathologic fission and mitophagy suppression converge to disrupt mitochondrial biogenesis through sustained inhibition of the PGC-1α/PPARα axis in SICM. Excessive Drp1-mediated fission depletes SIRT1, impairing PGC-1α deacetylation and nuclear translocation [24, 42, 43], whereas mitophagy defects (e.g., PINK1/Parkin or FUNDC1 failure) result in the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, generating ROS that inactivate NRF1 and TFAM [33, 44]. These processes suppress mtDNA replication and respiratory chain biogenesis [26], which is exacerbated by UPRmt incompetence (ATF5 dysregulation), limiting chaperone-mediated protein folding [13, 45, 46]. NAD⁺ exhaustion further inactivates SIRT3, crippling antioxidant defenses [31]. Consequently, the functional mitochondrial mass decreases [14], ATP synthesis decreases by 50–70% [47, 48], and the oxidative phosphorylation capacity is reduced [49]. Biogenesis failure also reduces cardiolipin remodeling, destabilizing inner membrane integrity [50]. Therapeutic interventions such as curcumin (a SIRT1-PGC-1α activator) [42], α-ketoglutarate (which normalizes mitophagy/fission) [51], or mitochondrial transplantation (which upregulates biogenesis genes) [14] can partially restore biogenesis, but persistent inhibition of autophagy/mitophagy has been associated with increased mortality and exacerbated cardiac injury in experimental models [41, 47]. Thus, pathological fission, suppressed mitophagy, and failed biogenesis collectively decrease mitochondrial structural and functional integrity, creating a reservoir of DAMPs and ROS that fuel inflammation and bioenergetic crisis in SICM.

Metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming is a complex adaptive response of the myocardium that can become maladaptive in SICM [2, 3, 52]. This process is characterized primarily by the downregulation of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and further exacerbation of impaired mitochondrial function by lactylation [11, 13] (Fig. 2).

Increased glycolytic flux

During sepsis, mitochondrial dysfunction impairs oxidative phosphorylation in cardiomyocytes, driving compensatory upregulation of glycolysis to generate ATP [11]. This shift is aided by several mechanisms: activation of the HIF-1α pathway by inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [53], oxidative stress that damages the mitochondrial electron transport chain [54], and disruptions in calcium homeostasis affecting the sarcoplasmic reticulum [55]. Key glycolytic enzymes, such as hexokinase 2 (HK2) and phosphofructokinase, are upregulated, accelerating glucose use and diverting substrates away from mitochondrial oxidation [52, 56]. While this glycolytic surge rapidly provides ATP, it is energetically inefficient, contributing to the reduced phosphocreatine/ATP ratios observed in individuals with SICM [57]. Thus, pathological fission, suppressed mitophagy, and failed biogenesis collectively disrupt mitochondrial structural and functional integrity, creating a reservoir of DAMPs and ROS that fuel inflammation and bioenergetic crisis in SICM.

Impaired tricarboxylic acid cycle

These metabolic shift-induced increases in glycolytic flux, an impaired TCA cycle, and lactate accumulation are not isolated events but rather form a self-reinforcing cycle that drives mitochondrial dysfunction forward. Transcriptional suppression reduces the expression of cardiomyocyte mitochondrial genes encoding tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes and electron transport chain complexes in sepsis patients [6], and multiple enzymes (IDH, FH, MDH, and CS) involved in the TCA cycle are posttranslationally modified by lactylation [11]. Proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) directly inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity, blocking the entry of pyruvate into the TCA cycle and causing it to accumulate [53]. Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) suppresses the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex E1 subunit α (PDHE1α) via phosphorylation, thereby blocking the entry of pyruvate into the tricarboxylic acid cycle [15] and further suppressing mitochondrial respiration [5]. Consequently, ATP depletion triggers AMPK-mediated compensatory glycolysis [58], while a dysregulated mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) impairs respiratory chain complex assembly [33, 45]. By directly targeting metabolic enzymes, lactylation acts as a key driver of metabolic failure, coupling inflammatory signaling with bioenergetic deficits in a self-reinforcing loop.

Lactate accumulation and signaling

Impaired pyruvate flux into mitochondria, coupled with the upregulation of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), diverts pyruvate toward lactate production [56, 59]. This metabolic imbalance leads to a significant increase in cytosolic lactate levels, which are detectable early in sepsis progression [1]. Lactate is not merely a waste product or pathogenic biomarker in SICM; it also inhibits mitochondrial complex I activity and induces histone lactylation [13, 60], leading to the amplification of inflammation [61]. These changes further compromise mitochondrial function and directly impair cardiomyocyte contractility [62].

Temporal dynamics

The metabolic reprogramming described above has direct temporal implications for disease progression and treatment response. Lactate accumulation due to mitochondrial dysfunction precedes hemodynamic deterioration in individuals with SICM [62, 63], suggesting that it is a critical driver of these processes. Cardiac functional recovery in individuals with sepsis is consistent with the resolution of lactate levels, reflecting the systemic transition from profound inflammatory stress to organ restoration following effective therapeutic intervention [64]. The inhibition of PDK4 via dichloroacetate (DCA) reactivates PDH, significantly reduces lactate production, restores ATP synthesis, and prolongs survival in experimental sepsis models [15, 56]. These findings underscore metabolic reprogramming as a critical and targetable axis in the pathophysiology of SICM. This temporal correlation highlights that lactate is not merely a biomarker but also a key driver of cardiac dysfunction and that its successful clearance is fundamental to functional recovery. Thus, the temporal trajectory of lactate metabolism underscores its dual role as both a biomarker and a pathogenic driver while supporting metabolic reprogramming as a therapeutically targetable axis in SICM.

Mitochondria–lactylation–calcium axis

In addition to metabolic and epigenetic alterations, dysregulated calcium (Ca²⁺) handling constitutes a third critical axis in the pathophysiology of SICM. Mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis, which in turn amplifies lactate production and lactylation, resulting in the formation of a self-reinforcing vicious cycle. This mitochondrial-lactylation-calcium axis involves intricate interactions among mitochondria, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 3).

Molecular circuitry

Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activation induces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production, leading to the oxidative modification of ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2) and a reduced interaction with the FK506 binding protein [65, 66], which promotes the expression of cell adhesion molecules and chemokines in cardiomyocytes and facilitates immune cell infiltration [67, 68]. This deleterious cascade is initiated by a mitochondrial energy crisis characterized by impaired ATP production and elevated oxidative stress [69]. This process promotes RyR2-mediated sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca²⁺ leakage, depleting SR stores and reducing systolic Ca²⁺ transients, thereby impairing cardiomyocyte contractility [66]. Cytosolic Ca²⁺ overload drives dysregulated Ca²⁺ uptake via the mtCU complex, which in turn triggers opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) and subsequent membrane depolarization [70]. Concurrently, Ca²⁺-activated calpain cleaves ATP synthase subunits, disrupting oxidative phosphorylation [71].

Pathophysiological consequences

The molecular disturbances described above initiate a convergent pathophysiological cascade characterized by a self-amplifying loop involving mitochondrial dysfunction, lactate overproduction, and lactylation-driven damage. While impaired oxidative phosphorylation and calcium mishandling form the core of this cycle, these effects are markedly exacerbated by lactate. Hyperlactatemia promotes lactylation—which enhances proinflammatory gene expression [72] and directly inhibits β-oxidation via HADHA modification [11]—and induces cytosolic acidification, further disrupting Ca²⁺ homeostasis and amplifying ROS damage [28, 73]. Metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, initially a compensatory response, ultimately fails to compensate for energy deficits, resulting in sustained contractile failure [58, 74]. Additionally, the upregulation of mitochondrial NOX² expression exacerbates Ca²⁺ dysregulation, thereby closing a vicious cycle that drives the transition from adaptive hibernation to maladaptive systolic/diastolic dysfunction [75]. Thus, the mitochondria-lactylation-calcium axis integrates multiple levels of dysfunction, extending beyond pure energetic deficits to include inflammatory and epigenetic mechanisms that collectively worsen outcomes in SICM.

Mitochondrial regulation of metabolic translator injury

Having established how mitochondrial dysfunction creates the preconditions for lactate overproduction, we now focus on lactylation itself as the underlying mechanism of injury, which is the molecular landscape of lactylation and its cell type-specific effects in the heart.

Molecular topography of lactylation

Sepsis triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, driving a metabolic shift known as the Warburg effect, which increases lactate production [1, 13, 76]. Lactate-induced histone lactylation promotes proinflammatory gene expression in endothelial cells and macrophages [13, 46, 77], exacerbating organ injury. TNF-α-driven glycolytic flux increases lactate levels, promoting histone H3 lysine 14 lactylation (H3K14la) [13]. This epigenetic modification activates inflammatory and apoptotic genes by promoting the binding of histone H3 to the promoters of these genes [46]. Interestingly, nonhistone lactylation is not only the predominant form of lactylation modification in SICM but also a critical molecular mechanism that contributes to myocardial dysfunction [11]. In one study, comprehensive lactylome profiling revealed that 97.1% (1094 sites) of the 1127 lysine lactylation sites (Kla sites) identified in septic myocardial tissues were located on nonhistone proteins; among the 83 differentially expressed lactylation sites, 94% were also located on nonhistone proteins in individuals with SICM [11]. Mitochondrial dysfunction perpetuates a feedforward loop, as the lactate-mediated lactylation of metabolic enzymes further compromises oxidative phosphorylation [78]. Sirtuins (SIRT1/SIRT3) counteract lactylation by delacylating HADHA, restoring mitochondrial function [11]. This lactylome profiling revises our understanding of lactylation in SICM, highlighting that nonhistone lactylation, particularly of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, is the predominant form and a key mechanistic driver of dysfunction rather than a secondary epigenetic phenomenon.

Cell type-specific pathogenesis

Cardiomyocytes

Nonhistone lactylation is a primary disease-relevant PTM that impairs mitochondrial activity by disrupting the fatty acid metabolic pathway in SICM [11]. Similarly, multiomic studies have revealed that immune activation characterized by NF-κB and cytokine signaling is driven by histone lactylation, which amplifies proinflammatory gene expression in cardiomyocytes [46]. Cardiac injury pathways include potential secondary ferroptosis via lipid peroxidation accumulation [79] and calcium mishandling due to disrupted mitochondrial dynamics [22] from impaired mitochondrial Ca²⁺ buffering [11]. Thus, lactylation is positioned to integrate metabolic stress with widespread protein modification, thereby potentially directly impairing cardiac function and amplifying inflammatory responses in SICM.

Endothelial cells

An increase in lactate levels following mitochondrial metabolic collapse exacerbates endothelial cell dysfunction in SICM and contributes to vascular barrier disruption and amplification of inflammatory processes [3]. Sepsis leads to the upregulation of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 expression in cardiac endothelial cells, facilitating leukocyte adhesion and myocardial infiltration [80, 81]. This activity is amplified by STAT1-ZBP1 signaling in macrophages, promoting M1 polarization and cytokine release, which further damages the endothelium [13, 82]. The mechanism of endothelial disruption is further highlighted by studies showing that α1-adrenergic receptor signaling can preserve endothelial integrity by suppressing ICAM-1/VCAM-1 expression via the PKC-ERK/p38MAPK pathway and inhibiting p65 nuclear translocation [83]. Similarly, oxidative stress is a key mediator, as antioxidants such as vitamin C mitigate TNF-α/IL-6-induced damage and preserve mitochondrial function [84]. These findings collectively demonstrate that microvascular leakage in SICM is driven by inflammatory activation and oxidative stress but may be reversible [2].

Immune cells

One way that sepsis reprograms immune-related metabolic processes is via lactylation. In macrophages, sepsis induces a PDK4-driven glycolytic shift, generating lactate that fuels histone lactylation [13] and amplifying proinflammatory M1 polarization via the STAT1–ZBP1 axis and cytokine storms (e.g., increases in IL-1β and TNF-α levels) [82, 85]. Concurrently, neutrophils undergo lactate-induced NETosis, during which histone lactylation upregulates the expression of PAD4 and MPO, promoting the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and the formation of coronary microthrombi [86]. T cells become exhausted through SIRT1-suppressed PD-1 signaling, reducing IFN-γ production while increasing the production of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 and impairing bacterial clearance [87]. This triad of immune dysfunctions establishes a feedforward loop in which lactate from glycolytic macrophages further fuels lactylation in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, exacerbating the progression of SICM. The critical role of lactylation in immune dysfunction is underscored by the finding that KAT2A inhibition suppresses H3K14la, thereby reducing NETosis and inflammation [46]. In summary, lactylation reprograms the functions of cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and immune cells, creating a coordinated network of injuries that drives SICM progression. The efficacy of agents targeting specific components of this network (e.g., KAT2A) confirms the pathogenic centrality of lactylation in these processes.

Cardiac fibroblasts

In addition to their role in extracellular matrix remodeling, cardiac fibroblasts are increasingly recognized as active contributors to the metabolic and inflammatory milieu of SICM. In response to inflammatory cytokines and DAMPs, fibroblasts undergo a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, contributing to the local lactate pool [88]. Beyond its metabolic role, lactate functions as a signaling molecule that promotes cardiac fibroblast activation. Research in atrial fibrillation models has established a direct mechanistic link: lactate-derived histone lactylation stimulates TGF-β1 transcription, thereby triggering a profibrotic gene program [89]. This lactylation-mediated crosstalk may exacerbate diastolic stiffness and impede functional recovery, positioning fibroblasts as a relevant, although underexplored, component of the lactylation network in SICM.

Targeted therapeutics to disrupt the vicious cycle

Currently, robust clinical evidence for interventions that directly disrupt the vicious cycle linking mitochondrial dysfunction to elevated protein lactylation in patients with SICM remains limited; ultimately, effective control of the underlying infection is paramount, as the risk of progressive organ damage persists in sepsis. However, preclinical research has suggested promising therapeutic strategies to overcome this cycle, as summarized in Table 1. These approaches can be mechanistically categorized on the basis of their point of action within the pathological cascade. Upstream interventions primarily aim to preserve mitochondrial structural and functional integrity, thereby reducing the initial driver of lactate overproduction. This category includes enhancing mitochondrial quality control (e.g., with mitophagy inducers such as Tat-Beclin-1) and correcting metabolic reprogramming (e.g., using PDK4 inhibitors such as DCA). Notably, caution is warranted, as the PDK4 inhibitor DCA, despite reducing lactate preclinically, failed to improve survival in a clinical trial [90], demonstrating that lowering lactate alone is insufficient [91]. In contrast, downstream strategies target the consequences of lactate accumulation, such as oxidative stress and calcium mishandling, or, with high precision, the lactylation process itself (e.g., via SIRT activators or KAT2A inhibitors). The optimal choice of intervention likely depends on the disease stage, highlighting the critical need for biomarkers to guide patient stratification and therapeutic timing.

Myocardial mitochondria protection

Mitochondrial “quality control”

Mitochondrial quality control encompasses the processes of biogenesis, dynamics (fission/fusion), and mitophagy, which collectively maintain a healthy mitochondrial population. In murine sepsis models, the recruitment of Parkin by mitochondria activates mitophagy, leading to the complete restoration of myocardial mitochondrial respiratory function and left ventricular contractility within 48 h. Conversely, Park2 knockout mice exhibit ~ 50% reductions in mitochondrial complex I/IV activity and a sustained 50–60% reduction in cardiac output [44, 92]. FUNDC1-dependent mitophagy clears damaged mitochondria by binding LC3 to promote autophagosome formation, thereby improving respiratory function and sustaining ATP synthesis. DUSP1 overexpression stabilizes FUNDC1 to further activate this process, ultimately mitigating myocardial injury in individuals with sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy [34]. The Tat-Beclin-1 peptide significantly improved cardiac function in septic mice (increased left ventricular fractional shortening by 25%) by maintaining Beclin-1-dependent autophagic flux and reducing cytosolic mtDNA release by 50–70% in Beclin-1-overexpressing models [93]. With respect to mitochondrial dynamics, the Drp1 inhibitor P110 suppresses pathological mitochondrial fission by blocking the interaction between Drp1 and Fis1, reduces Drp1 mitochondrial translocation, and completely prevents LPS-induced cardiomyocyte death (restoring LDH release to baseline levels); moreover, the administration of P110 increases the survival of septic mice by 30% [21]. MAPL knockout mitigates excessive mitochondrial fission in the SICM by inhibiting Drp1 SUMOylation, reducing mitochondrial fragmentation by ~ 50% and increasing ATP production by 40–80% [94].

Energy metabolism and biogenesis

Metabolic reprogramming has historically been addressed in terms of the activation of the PGC-1α pathway. For example, the activity of the PGC-1α/NRF1/TFAM axis is upregulated in CO-donor CORM-2 to promote mitochondrial biogenesis, improving myocardial energy metabolism in septic mice: ATP production increases by 13.6%, lactate generation decreases, and survival increases from 25% to 63% [95]. The PGC-1α activator ZLN005 significantly increases PGC-1α expression in LPS-treated H9c2 cells, reducing apoptosis by ~ 41.3%, decreasing ROS levels by 28.5%, and ameliorating mitochondrial damage [96]. While enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α addresses the quantitative deficit of functional mitochondria, an alternative strategy is to directly improve the qualitative function of existing mitochondria by targeting pathogenic modifications. In addition to enhancing biogenesis, directly targeting lactylation itself is also effective. For example, the HADHA K166R/K728R mutation (which prevents lactylation) significantly reduces lactylation levels in SICM models, restoring HADHA enzymatic activity and fatty acid oxidation and thereby restoring ATP production to 90% of normal levels [11].

Oxidative stress and calcium homeostasis

In terms of antioxidant defenses, the mitochondria-targeting antioxidant MitoQ directly scavenges ROS in the mitochondrial matrix, effectively preventing endotoxin-induced reductions in the myocardial respiratory control ratio (RCR) and significantly mitigating increased caspase-3 activity in rats [97]. Resveratrol activates the transcription factor Nrf2 to induce HO-1/GCLM expression, markedly inhibiting LPS-induced mitochondrial ROS generation and apoptosis in human cardiomyocytes [98]. With respect to calcium homeostasis, the RyR2 stabilizer JTV-519 inhibits RyR2 oxidation and reduces sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) calcium leakage, restoring SR calcium stores and normalizing the cardiomyocyte calcium transient amplitude and contractile function [66]. Human mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (huMSC-exos) can restore mitochondrial calcium efflux (reported increase of ~ 81%) via the PINK1–PKA–NCLX axis, effectively alleviating mitochondrial calcium overload and significantly improving the survival of septic mice from ~ 20% to ~ 60% [99]. These findings demonstrate that mitigating oxidative stress and correcting calcium signaling can directly disrupt the vicious cycle linking mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired contractility in SICM.

Intervention in immune metabolic reprogramming

Immune cells undergo profound metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis in sepsis, which fuels lactylation and drives proinflammatory responses. Therapeutic interventions aimed at reprogramming this immunometabolic axis hold promise for attenuating the systemic inflammation that contributes to SICM.

Glycolysis and immune polarization

Glycolysis critically regulates immune polarization and inflammatory gene expression, and LPS-induced M1 macrophage polarization (proinflammatory) enhances the Warburg effect, causing lactate accumulation [13]. By activating PDH, DCA reduces lactate levels by 40–50% [13]. In mouse models of sepsis, shikonin inhibits PKM2 expression, markedly reducing macrophage glycolysis (a decrease in lactate production of 50–70%) and HMGB1 release (a decrease of 50–70%), thereby increasing survival by 45% [100]. Recombinant thrombomodulin (rTM) suppresses glycolysis in macrophages by inhibiting the HIF-1α/METTL3/PFKM axis. rTM reduces METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification of PFKM mRNA, thereby decreasing PFKM protein expression and glycolytic capacity [101]. Furthermore, the HIF-1α-dependent pathway is essential for glycolytic reprogramming: myeloid-specific HIF-1α deficiency blocks β-glucan-induced glycolytic reprogramming, inhibits trained immunity development and reduces the survival of individuals with S. aureus-induced sepsis from 90% to 40% [102]. The SphK1 inhibitor PF-543 downregulates the HIF-1α–HK2–PFKFB3 pathway by inhibiting SphK1/S1PR3 and reducing macrophage glycolysis and M1 polarization, thereby significantly attenuating sepsis-induced multiorgan injury and inflammation [103]. Notably, persistent glycolytic suppression is a key mechanism underlying immune cell dysfunction (immunosuppression) and is associated with an increased risk of secondary infection and increased mortality [104]. These findings highlight the dual roles of glycolysis in sepsis: early enhancement (targeted by the aforementioned interventions) may represent a beneficial adaptive response, whereas excessive or late suppression can drive immunosuppression [6, 58]. A key therapeutic challenge is determining ways to precisely distinguish adaptive metabolic changes that support function (e.g., in the myocardium) from pathological injury [3].

Metabolite-mediated immune cell metabolism reprogramming

Metabolites such as lactate function as fundamental signaling molecules that reprogram immune cell function through lactylation, thereby creating a vicious cycle of inflammation and immunosuppression in SICM. Lactate can access immune cells (e.g., macrophages) through MCTs. Within these cells, lactate acts as a substrate for acetyltransferases (e.g., p300/CBP) [29], catalyzing its covalent attachment to lysine residues to lactylate (Kla) histones (e.g., H3K18 and H4K5) [13] and nonhistone proteins (e.g., HMGB1) [29]. Lactate signaling drives immune dysregulation via epigenetic modification; specifically, lactate induces lactylation of the HADHA protein [11], contributing to myocardial depression and synergistically exacerbating immunometabolic alterations in individuals with SICM. Clinical evidence indicates that a serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentration ≥ 225 U/L is significantly associated with greater 28-day mortality, with each 1 U/L increase in the LDH concentration increasing the risk of death by 0.5% [105]. Importantly, this immunometabolic axis is not only a disease driver but also a source of therapeutic vulnerability. Interventions targeting metabolic disturbances have shown therapeutic potential. For example, chicoric acid (CA) inhibits succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity, disrupting the succinate–HIF-1α positive feedback loop, markedly suppressing LPS-induced mitochondrial ROS generation and reducing serum cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels by ~ 39% compared with those in controls [5]. Furthermore, the balance in macrophage polarization can be modulated metabolically. ERRα activation promotes PGC-1α/SIRT1-dependent mitochondrial biogenesis and enhances fatty acid oxidation to maintain mitochondrial function, thereby suppressing TLR-induced inflammatory processes [106]. ATP–citrate lyase (ACLY) inhibition reduces NF-κB acetylation, significantly decreasing the release of IL-1β and PGE2 from macrophages [107]. With respect to sepsis-associated T-cell immunosuppression, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) drive cholesterol metabolic reprogramming via Akt–mTOR–SREBP2 signaling, promoting Treg proliferation. SREBP2 inhibition significantly reduces the proportion of Tregs, alleviating immunosuppression and improving survival [108]. Concurrently, a chemokine network released by “trained” macrophages postsepsis stimulates CCR2 and CXCR6 receptors to trigger T-cell tissue residency, which has been identified as a key immune mechanism that reduces de novo tumor risk after sepsis resolution [109]. Targeting the adenosine-mediated immunosuppressive pathway by depleting CD39⁺ plasma cells or blocking the adenosine A2a receptor (A2aR) effectively inhibits this axis, significantly improving survival following secondary infection in sepsis models (improvements ranging from 60% to 100% across models) [110]. Collectively, these findings establish that immunometabolic reprogramming via lactylation and related pathways is a critical, actionable axis underlying immune dysfunction in SICM.

Lactate clearance and hemodynamic support

Impaired lactate clearance in individuals with SICM arises from tissue hypoperfusion, microcirculatory dysfunction, and mitochondrial failure, leading to both increased production and reduced utilization [2, 3]. A critical mechanism involves disrupted autophagic flux and exacerbated inflammation, in which cardiac-infiltrating type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) produce interleukin-4 (IL-4). IL-4, in turn, activates STAT3 and increases lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2) expression, stabilizing lysosomal function; LAMP2 then interacts with flotillin 2 (FLOT2), facilitating autophagosome‒lysosome fusion in cardiac endothelial cells to restore mitochondrial function and lactate clearance [111]. Levosimendan improves cellular metabolism by increasing interstitial pyruvate levels, thus increasing lactate clearance (>55% vs. 10% with dobutamine) and reducing lactate/pyruvate ratios [112, 113]. Esmolol reduces heart rate and improves cardiac efficiency in septic rats, potentially by mitigating catecholamine-induced myocardial stress and TNF-α release, without significantly affecting lactate levels [114]. Most importantly, the primary therapeutic goal in septic shock is to restore adequate tissue perfusion and reverse the shock state. Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) provides robust hemodynamic support in refractory septic cardiogenic shock patients and is associated with a significant survival benefit [115]. The subsequent decrease in lactate levels observed with such effective therapies is a marker of resolving hypoperfusion and improving metabolic homeostasis rather than a direct target of intervention. Therefore, strategies that successfully reverse the underlying shock state and restore tissue perfusion are pivotal for improving patient outcomes, particularly in SICM, where fluid responsiveness may be poor.

Lactylation-targeting therapies

In addition to supporting mitochondrial function and overall metabolism, a more direct strategy involves targeting the lactylation process itself. Inflammatory responses drive lactate accumulation via the Warburg effect [13]. This hyperactive aerobic glycolysis and pathological modification critically exacerbates myocardial injury in individuals with SICM, while the lactylation of HADHA residues impairs its enzymatic activity, reducing ATP synthesis and promoting oxidative stress [11]; moreover, lactylation further influences immune responses and survival outcomes in patients with sepsis [35, 78]. Therefore, therapeutic interventions that target lactylation directly have been proposed and have shown promise. Gastrodin promotes the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of KAT2A, specifically by disrupting H3K14la-driven transcription [46]. SIRT1/SIRT3 activation reverses HADHA lactylation, restoring mitochondrial integrity [11]. Collectively, these approaches mitigate myocardial apoptosis, improve hemodynamic function, and increase survival in preclinical models of SICM. Addressing these gaps through integrated multimodal studies is essential to translate the mechanistic understanding of the mitochondria‒lactylation axis into clinically actionable diagnostics and therapies for SICM.

Current and future directions in research on lactylation in SICM

Despite compelling evidence indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction is a central regulator in SICM [3], the current understanding remains fragmented, and the underlying mechanisms are poorly defined [2]. A critical gap exists in terms of the clinical validation of therapies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, which drives pathological cellular alterations. Rigorous investigations of the causal relationships between protein lactylation, a ubiquitous PTM across physiological, stress, and disease states [13, 116], and cardiomyocyte dysfunction are needed. Defining intervention windows, modalities, and target populations is also important for improving clinical outcomes.

Establishing rapid and universally applicable diagnostic criteria for SICM is essential for enabling precise patient phenotyping—a prerequisite for enrolling homogeneous cohorts in clinical trials, identifying biomarkers specific to distinct pathophysiological drivers, and ultimately guiding personalized therapeutic interventions [2]. This process requires the integration of physiological, biochemical, and imaging biomarkers that can be validated through large-scale trials to assess the reliability, efficacy, and safety of relevant therapeutics.

Encouragingly, a consensus on the clinical definition of SICM has emerged [2, 3]. Disease phenotype-driven integration of cellular models, animal studies, and clinical observations can accelerate our mechanistic understanding of the disease and therapeutic strategies for managing it. Of particular translational importance, lactylation has emerged as a novel and druggable metabolic regulator in SICM [20], potentially governing cardiac protein modifications via mitochondrial crosstalk [11]. While SIRT1/SIRT3-mediated HADHA lactylation has been preliminarily identified, its value as a prognostic biomarker (e.g., for predicting mortality or therapeutic response) requires validation in prospective clinical cohorts.

Future priorities should include prospective cohort studies that can uncover the correlations between dynamic mitochondrial lactylation levels and echocardiographic indices (e.g., left ventricular strain) to establish prognostic markers; machine learning-driven deep phenotyping of lactylation signatures to allow early detection and the development of personalized therapeutic strategies; and multicenter trials that prioritize interventions that demonstrably improve survival outcomes.

Conclusions

Advances in cardiac multiomics and imaging analyses have revealed the prevalence of myocardial dysfunction in sepsis patients, but inconsistencies in various definitions across studies have limited the translation of these findings into clinical practice. The central challenge lies in determining whether the observed systolic impairment is an adaptive hibernation-like response to metabolic stress or a maladaptive contributor to organ hypoperfusion. Crucially, therapeutic interventions should target clinical markers of tissue hypoperfusion rather than isolated contractility metrics. Future research must resolve the pathophysiological duality of this phenomenon, wherein mitochondrial downregulation may confer transient cytoprotection at the expense of the cardiac reserve and establish standardized diagnostic criteria to optimize risk stratification and the development of mechanistically informed therapies.
