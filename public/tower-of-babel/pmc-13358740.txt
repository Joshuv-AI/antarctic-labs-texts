Mapping the evolution of sepsis and intestinal epithelial barrier research: a bibliometric analysis (1977–2024)
Authors: Zi Ge, Yao Zhang, Ke Ma, Zhijie Xia, Jun Cao, Hui Zhao, Jiaxin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. Although sepsis most commonly originates from non-abdominal infections, intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction has been increasingly investigated as a relevant contributor in a subset of patients and as a potential amplifier of systemic inflammation. This study aimed to map research trends at the intersection of sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier using bibliometric methods.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted on the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) on November 14, 2024. VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), CiteSpace (version 6.3.R1), and R 4.3.3 were used to carry out the bibliometric workflow and produce visualization results.ResultsA total of 1,231 articles published between 1977 and 2024 were included, involving 7,495 authors from 3,963 institutions across 401 countries/regions. The USA contributed the largest number of corresponding-author publications (n=321) and had the highest total citations (TC =20,103). The University of California System contributed the highest number of publications (n=73). Cani Patrice D. was the most influential author by H-index (H-index =12) and total citations (TC =13,869). The journal Shock ranked first by H-index (H-index =24), with 42 publications and 750 citations. Keyword co-occurrence analysis identified three clusters: sepsis physiology and complications; microbiota-metabolic endotoxemia; and molecular barrier dysfunction. “Intestinal permeability” was the most frequent keyword (240 occurrences), while “endotoxemia” had the strongest total link strength [860]. Burst detection showed recent increases in terms such as “gut microbiota” [2018–2024] and “tight junction” [2020–2024].ConclusionsThe literature linking sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier has expanded and diversified over time. Recent research emphasizes microbiota-immune-metabolic interactions and barrier-focused mechanisms and interventions, which may be particularly pertinent to abdominal sepsis and to systemic inflammatory amplification rather than to all sepsis etiologies. Future studies are expected to further integrate microbiology, immunology, metabolism, and translational therapeutics to clarify which patient subgroups may benefit from barrier-targeted strategies.Keywords: Sepsis, intestinal epithelial barrier, endotoxemia, intestinal permeability, bibliometric analysis

Introduction

Sepsis is life-threatening, multifaceted syndrome caused by an abnormal host response to infection and is characterized by systemic inflammation, multi-organ failure, and high mortality rates (1,2). The incidence of sepsis has been reported to be alarmingly high, with estimates suggesting that nearly 50 million cases occur worldwide each year, resulting in approximately 11 million deaths annually (3). It is triggered by infection and is marked by a dysregulated host response, which leads to widespread tissue injury, endothelial dysfunction, and immune system disruption (4,5). Clinically, sepsis arises from multiple infection sources, with pulmonary and urinary tract infections accounting for a substantial proportion of cases, whereas abdominal sources represent a subset in which gut-related mechanisms may be more directly implicated. In this broader context, intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction has been proposed as a relevant contributor in specific settings and as a potential amplifier of systemic inflammation rather than a universally primary driver of sepsis across all etiologies (6). The gut epithelium, composed of tightly regulated epithelial cells, serves as a selective barrier that not only limits the translocation of pathogens and toxins but also contributes to immune homeostasis (7). When barrier integrity is compromised, intestinal permeability increases, potentially facilitating microbial products (including endotoxins) and other inflammatory mediators to access systemic compartments and exacerbate inflammatory cascades (8). For this reason, gut barrier dysfunction has attracted sustained research interest in sepsis, particularly in relation to mechanisms of barrier injury, microbial translocation, and downstream immune dysregulation.

In recent years, studies have further clarified candidate triggers, regulatory pathways, and therapeutic strategies relevant to barrier dysfunction during sepsis. For example, deoxynivalenol exposure has been associated with apoptosis and tight junction injury in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), highlighting how environmental toxins may exacerbate gut injury under inflammatory stress (9). MicroRNA (miRNA) profiling in IECs has suggested that miRNA-mediated regulatory networks may modulate barrier function during sepsis (6). Therapeutically, nano-parthenolide (Nano PTL) has been reported to improve intestinal barrier function by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and apoptosis in IECs, supporting the concept that barrier-protective interventions may be a feasible translational direction in selected contexts (10). Overall, while gut barrier dysfunction is not expected to explain all sepsis cases, understanding its role may inform mechanistic insight and potential adjunctive interventions for patient subgroups in whom intestinal injury and permeability are clinically relevant.

Bibliometric analysis provides a quantitative approach to evaluate scientific literature, offering insights into trends, impacts, and advancements within specific research domains (11). Bibliometric tools such as VOSviewer and CiteSpace can visualize research hotspots, collaboration networks, and the evolution of knowledge (12). A previous bibliometric analysis on sepsis and host immune response research [1999–2019] highlighted increasing attention to immunosuppression-related topics, with regulatory T cells emerging as a recent keyword (13). However, there is no comprehensive bibliometric analysis dedicated to the intersection of sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier yet. Therefore, this study maps the research landscape and its temporal evolution of sepsis-intestinal epithelial barrier research, identifying influential contributors, knowledge structures, and emerging themes that may guide future mechanistic and translational studies. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0346/rc).

Methods

Search strategies and data collection

A literature search on sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier was conducted using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), covering the period from 1977 to 2024. WoSCC is widely regarded as a comprehensive, authoritative database covering high-quality scholarly literature across multiple disciplines (14). The employed search formula was: TS=(sepsis OR “severe sepsis” OR “septic shock” OR “endotoxemia” OR SIRS OR “systemic inflammatory response syndrome” OR “bloodstream infection” OR septicemia OR “blood poisoning” OR pyemia) AND TS=(“intestinal epithelial barrier” OR “gut epithelial barrier” OR “intestinal permeability” OR “gut permeability” OR “mucosal barrier”) (13,15). Only English-language publications were included, specifically focusing on articles across various document types. To mitigate discrepancies arising from database updates, literature retrieval was performed on November 14, 2024. Bibliographic records were downloaded during screening in both “Full record and cited references” and “plain text” formats. The exported text files contained publication and citation counts, titles, author affiliations, institutions, countries/regions, keywords, and journals for subsequent bibliometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

Three tools were used for visualization and bibliometric analyses: VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), CiteSpace (version 6.3.R1), and R 4.3.3. VOSviewer was applied to map collaboration patterns among authors and institutions, as well as in analyzing co-authorship, citation patterns, keyword co-occurrence networks, and co-citation networks (16). This tool was used to visualize and analyze collaborative networks, helping to describe connections among authors, institutions, and publications. CiteSpace applied to detect keyword bursts to better capture emerging topics and potential hotspots in the field. Based on these outputs, a keyword timeline was generated to illustrate topic evolution in research on sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier. In addition, The R package “Bibliometrix” was used to conduct bibliometric analyses, including characterization of the global distribution of publications, identification of major patterns over time, and evaluation of the contributions of authors, journals, and institutions within the dataset. The H-index was used to reflect the academic influence of authors and journals (17,18), and the G-index and M-index were also calculated. The G-index places greater emphasis on highly cited papers and complements the H-index in describing citation performance (19). The M-index adjusts the H-index by academic age (years since first publication) to reflect sustained productivity over time (20). Journal Citation Reports (JCR) quartiles and the impact factor (IF) were used to evaluate journal standing and citation influence; JCR quartiles classify journals from Q1 to Q4, and IF reflects the average citations received over the preceding two years. The 2023 JCR and IF data were used for these journal-level evaluations.

Results

An overview of publications in research of sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier

A total of 1,231 studies were retained for the final analysis, and all were original research articles (Figure 1). These publications involved 7,495 authors from 3,963 institutions. The included articles appeared in 486 journals and accumulated 37,165 citations (Figure 2A). Annual publication output on sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier showed a steady increase over time. Starting from a minimal number of studies in the late 1970s, research in this field remained limited until the late 1990s. The early 2000s marked a steady increase in publication activity. A notable acceleration occurred from 2013 onwards, with publication numbers climbing significantly each year. Particularly between 2016 and 2021, there was a sharp rise, with the annual output peaking at 81 publications in 2020 and 2021. Although a slight fluctuation was observed in subsequent years, the overall trajectory remained robust, with 75 and 72 studies published in 2023 and 2024, respectively (Figure 2B).

The USA leads in corresponding author publications with 321 articles, accounting for 26.1% of the total output. The country ranks second in total publications (TP =1,101, TP_rank =2) and first in total citations (TC =20,103). Although the multiple country publications (MCP) ratio was low (0.181), the USA exhibits a significant number of international collaborations. China follows closely with 292 articles (23.7% of the total), ranking third in total publications (TP =808, TP_rank =3) and second in total citations (TC =8,116). However, its MCP ratio of 0.099 indicates a strong preference for single-country publications (SCP). Other leading countries include Italy (54 articles, MCP ratio =0.148), the United Kingdom (51 articles, MCP ratio =0.255), and Japan (47 articles, MCP ratio =0.128), each making substantial contributions to the field (Figure 3A and Table S1). Discussion

The bibliometric findings indicate increasing attention to sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier, with key themes centered on sepsis physiology and complications, microbiota-metabolic endotoxemia, and molecular barrier dysfunction. Emerging trends, as demonstrated by recent keyword bursts like “gut microbiota” and “tight junctions”, indicate a shift toward exploring molecular mechanisms of barrier integrity and microbiota-host interactions. This shift emphasizes understanding how specific signaling pathways, such as those involving tight junction proteins and microbial metabolites, influence epithelial cell function and permeability.

This framing is directly relevant to the scope of the Journal of Thoracic Disease because thoracic infections—especially pneumonia—represent a major clinical origin of sepsis and septic shock, and lung-gut crosstalk has become an increasingly investigated axis in critical care. In patients with pulmonary-source sepsis, systemic inflammatory mediators, circulatory instability, vasopressor exposure, mechanical ventilation, sedatives/analgesics, and antibiotic-driven dysbiosis can contribute to secondary gastrointestinal barrier disruption and altered microbiota composition (21). These gut changes, in turn, may influence immune tone and susceptibility to secondary infections (including ventilator-associated pneumonia), potentially affecting respiratory outcomes, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, and mortality. Therefore, mapping the evolution of gut-barrier research in sepsis is not limited to abdominal-source sepsis; it also informs a broader critical care perspective in which gut dysfunction is viewed as a modifiable component of multi-organ failure relevant to thoracic disease practice. Shock emerged as a key journal in sepsis and intestinal epithelial barrier research. The journal’s emphasis on translational research facilitates the application of basic science discoveries to clinical practice, making it a vital resource for clinicians and researchers alike. Studies published in Shock often explore the molecular mechanisms underlying intestinal permeability and the role of gut microbiota in sepsis, providing insights that are crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies (22).

The USA remains the leading contributor to sepsis and intestinal epithelial barrier research, contributing the highest number of corresponding author publications and achieving the top position in total citations. Although the USA has a relatively low MCP ratio, it demonstrates significant international collaborations. The University of California System was the most prolific institution in this field. The USA’s leadership in sepsis and intestinal epithelial barrier research can be attributed to several factors, including its advanced healthcare system, high disease burden, and substantial research funding. In the USA, sepsis remains a major public health burden, with an estimated 1.7 million cases each year and over 250,000 deaths, making it one of the leading causes of mortality (23). This high prevalence drives the urgency for research and innovation in understanding sepsis pathophysiology and treatment strategies. The USA’s robust research infrastructure is supported by institutions like the National Institutes of Health (NIH), which provides substantial funding for sepsis-related studies. Between 2010 and 2019, NIH-funded projects significantly contributed to advancements in this field. This financial support enables cutting-edge research and fosters collaboration between top institutions like the University of California System (24). Cani Patrice D. is a leading contributor to research on sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier, with the highest H-index and total citations, reflecting his significant impact on advancing knowledge in this area. His work primarily focuses on the role of gut microbiota and metabolic endotoxemia in systemic inflammation and disease progression. One of his influential studies demonstrates potential of Akkermansia muciniphila in mitigating sepsis by enhancing colonic wound healing through IL-22 signaling and MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways (25). Its bioactive compounds modulate immune responses, offering a novel approach to reduce sepsis-related organ dysfunction and mortality (25).

The keyword co-occurrence network in research on sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier reveals three major thematic clusters, reflecting distinct research hotspots and evolving trends over time.

Red cluster: systemic effects of sepsis and physiological-pathological mechanisms

The red cluster focuses on systemic effects of sepsis, with keywords such as “bacteremia”, “intestinal permeability”, “multiple organ failure”, and “blood flow”. Bacteremia, a critical complication of sepsis, plays a pivotal role in the progression of systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction, particularly through its impact on the intestinal epithelial barrier (26). Recent advancements have also explored therapeutic strategies targeting bacteremia-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction. Nano-formulated compounds, such as Nano PTL, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing ROS and apoptosis in IECs while restoring tight junction integrity (10). During sepsis, the mesenteric blood flow is frequently diminished due to systemic hypotension and vascular dysfunction. This reduction in blood supply can result in a state of intestinal ischemia, where oxygen and nutrient delivery to the intestinal epithelium is severely compromised. Such ischemic conditions are detrimental, leading to increased intestinal permeability and subsequent bacterial translocation into the bloodstream, which can further propagate systemic inflammation and organ failure (27). The emergence of this cluster is largely driven by early and sustained clinical-pathophysiological attention to macrocirculatory and microcirculatory failure in sepsis, where impaired perfusion and bacteremia form a self-reinforcing loop with gut barrier breakdown. Because intestinal hypoperfusion, permeability increases, and organ failure are measurable and clinically salient endpoints, studies naturally coalesce around these terms, consolidating a “systemic complications” theme. This cluster underscores the importance of investigating gut barrier dysfunction as a central driver of sepsis-related complications.

Green cluster: gut microbiota, metabolic influences, and endotoxemia

The green cluster emphasizes the role of gut microbiota and metabolic influences in sepsis progression, with keywords such as “gut microbiota”, “diet-induced obesity”, “metabolic endotoxemia”, and “inflammation”. In sepsis, the disruption of gut microbiota can lead to increased intestinal permeability. This condition allows for the translocation of bacteria and toxins from the gut lumen into the bloodstream, exacerbating systemic inflammation and potentially leading to multiple organ failure. Research has shown that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can reduce epithelial cell apoptosis and enhance tight junction protein expression, thereby decreasing intestinal permeability and inflammatory responses (28). The relationship between gut microbiota and the intestinal epithelial barrier is further complicated by the immune response during sepsis. Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) signaling has emerged as a potential therapeutic target due to its role in regulating both immune responses and maintaining intestinal homeostasis (29). This cluster likely formed as microbiome science matured and converged with metabolic research: obesity and diet provide consistent, manipulable exposures that reshape microbial ecology and increase circulating endotoxin (“metabolic endotoxemia”), making “microbiota-diet-inflammation” a coherent, repeatedly co-studied package. These findings suggest that targeting gut microbiota through dietary or probiotic interventions could offer therapeutic potential for mitigating metabolic endotoxemia and its contribution to sepsis.

Blue cluster: molecular pathways regulating intestinal barrier integrity

The blue cluster centers on molecular pathways regulating intestinal barrier integrity, with keywords such as “tight junctions”, “NF-kappa-B”, “epithelial cells”, and “barrier dysfunction”. In septic conditions, the activation of NF-κB in IECs is associated with increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1) (22,30). These cytokines further exacerbate inflammation and promote apoptosis of IECs, leading to a breakdown of tight junctions that are essential for maintaining barrier integrity. Studies have shown that sepsis induces a marked increase in NF-κB activity within the intestinal mucosa, correlating with heightened inflammatory responses and compromised barrier function (31,32). These studies are critical for identifying molecular targets to restore barrier function and reduce sepsis-related complications. The formation of this cluster is driven by methodological and conceptual advances in epithelial biology: tight junction proteins provide quantifiable molecular readouts of permeability, while NF-κB serves as a central hub linking pathogen sensing to cytokine release and apoptosis. Overall, these clusters reflect a comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between systemic effects, microbiota dynamics, and molecular mechanisms involved in sepsis and intestinal barrier dysfunction.

The keyword burst analysis provides a comprehensive view of evolving research trends in the field of sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier, reflecting shifts in focus over time and highlighting critical areas of investigation. Early bursts mainly reflected foundational, mechanism-oriented work, whereas later bursts increasingly signaled a shift toward microbiota-metabolic drivers and actionable barrier targets. Metabolic- and microbiota-related bursts have become particularly prominent in recent years. Keywords like “diet-induced obesity” [2014–2018], “microbiota” [2018–2024], and “gut microbiota” [2018–2024] indicate growing interest in how the gut microbiota in regulating immune responses and influencing sepsis outcomes. Obesity, particularly when induced by a high-fat diet (HFD), leads to alterations in gut microbiota composition and increased intestinal permeability. This “leaky gut” phenomenon allows for the translocation of bacteria and endotoxins into the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammatory responses that can precipitate or exacerbate septic conditions. Studies have shown that HFD consumption results in increased expression of inflammatory markers such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which are associated with compromised tight junction integrity in the intestinal epithelium (33,34).

A second burst pattern points to methodological consolidation and deeper interrogation of barrier structure. From 2019 to 2024, keywords such as “model” [2019–2020] and “tight junction” [2019–2020] highlight a growing focus on molecular mechanisms regulating intestinal barrier integrity and the development of experimental models to study sepsis interventions. Research has also highlighted the role of specific proteolytic enzymes produced by pathogens in degrading tight junction proteins. For example, Aeromonas sobria serine protease has been shown to degrade key components of tight junctions, further compromising epithelial barrier integrity during infection (35). This degradation not only facilitates bacterial translocation but also enhances the risk of sepsis in susceptible populations. These trends indicate a transition from foundational studies to advanced investigations into molecular pathways and therapeutic strategies targeting gut barrier dysfunction. Together, these bursts suggest that the field has moved from describing barrier failure to modeling it more precisely and targeting discrete junctional components and upstream triggers. Overall, keyword bursts support a trajectory toward an integrated “microbiota-metabolism-barrier” framework with translational intent.

The evolving research trends highlighted by keyword burst analysis indicate a promising direction toward understanding the interplay between metabolic factors, gut microbiota, and intestinal barrier integrity in sepsis. Future research trends are likely to embrace interdisciplinary approaches, integrating insights from microbiology, immunology, nutrition, and clinical medicine. This holistic perspective can enhance our understanding of how various factors such as diet, microbiota composition, and immune response-interact to influence sepsis outcomes. As this field progresses, it is expected that innovative therapeutic approaches will emerge, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in septic conditions. As the field progresses, model standardization and multi-omics validation may accelerate the development of barrier-protective and microbiota-informed interventions, ultimately improving outcomes in sepsis.

Limitations

First, this study included only English-language articles indexed in the WoSCC. This restriction may introduce language and database-selection bias and may underestimate the contribution, visibility, and citation impact of research published in non-English journals, including studies disseminated in Chinese and other languages. In rapidly developing fields, regionally influential work may be preferentially published in domestic-language journals or indexed in alternative databases (e.g., regional or discipline-specific platforms), and such records would not be captured in our dataset. As a result, the geographic distribution of productivity, collaboration networks, and citation-based influence reported here should be interpreted as reflecting the English-language WoSCC literature rather than the complete global evidence base. Second, bibliometric indicators and visualization tools such as VOSviewer and CiteSpace, are effective for identifying publication patterns, collaboration structures, and thematic hotspots, but they do not fully capture qualitative context such as study quality, clinical rigor, heterogeneity of sepsis subtypes, or the translational readiness of specific findings. Therefore, the identified hotspots and trends should be viewed as signals of research attention and knowledge diffusion, and they should be complemented by systematic or scoping reviews when clinical inference is required.

Conclusions

This bibliometric analysis systematically outlined the research landscape and its evolution in sepsis and the intestinal epithelial barrier. The study identified sustained growth in publications, expanding collaboration networks, and major thematic emphases reflected by keyword co-occurrence, with prominent hotspots including intestinal permeability, endotoxemia, and gut microbiota. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted as a characterization of the research landscape on gut barrier-related mechanisms in sepsis, rather than as evidence that intestinal barrier dysfunction is the dominant pathophysiological driver across all clinical sepsis presentations. Given that sepsis commonly arises from non-abdominal infection sources (e.g., pulmonary and urinary tract infections), gut barrier dysfunction is likely to be most directly relevant in specific contexts such as abdominal sepsis and/or as a contributor to systemic inflammatory amplification.

Over time, the field has shifted from foundational descriptions of systemic inflammation and permeability toward more interdisciplinary work integrating microbiology, immunology, nutrition, and clinical medicine. Future studies should prioritize mechanistic and translational designs that clarify when barrier dysfunction is causal versus secondary, identify patient subgroups most likely to exhibit clinically meaningful intestinal injury, and test targeted interventions (including microbiota- and barrier-directed strategies) with clinically relevant endpoints. Overall, this bibliometric overview highlights emerging directions and supports more precise, context-aware investigation of the microbiota-metabolism-barrier axis in sepsis.
