Bioremediation enhancement by current-powered solenoid magnetic field in microbial fuel cell
Authors: Chyi-How Lay, Chalida Daengbussadee, Lakkana Laopaiboon, Yi-Chi Deng, Bedeny E Wahby, Gopalakrishnan Kumar, Ponnusamy Vinoth Kumar, Setyo Budi Kurniawan, Peer Mohamed Abdul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExternal magnetic field technology has been utilized in wastewater treatment. In this study, a current-powered solenoid magnetic field is implemented beyond a dual-chamber cubed MFC to investigate the effects of various current strengths (0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mA) on bioelectricity generation and microbial community diversity in the anode chamber. The results show that adding a solenoid magnetic field (MF) can increase both electricity performance and pollutant removal efficiency. Applying a solenoid MF powered by 80 mA (MF-80) resulted in a peak current density of 130 ± 18.2 mA/m² and a power density of 66.3 ± 16.8 mW/m², which are 2.1 times and 3.6 times higher, respectively, compared with the value from MF-0. Moreover, the coulombic efficiency can also be increased from 12.0 to 23.6% without MF to a maximum value of 71.4% by adding solenoid MF-80. More diverse bacterial communities were found with the addition of MF. Without MF, the dominant populations were Pseudomonas (27%); however, with MF-80, Pseudomonas decreased to 4.0%, and Geobacter increased to 12.0%. MF intensity also resulted in varying microbial communities on the anode. Keywords: Bioelectricity, Magnetic field, Microbial fuel cell, Solenoid

Introduction

Microbial fuel cells (MFCs), a bioelectrochemical system, combine microbial and electrochemical technologies to demonstrate the potential for simultaneous bioremediation and heavy metal recovery in wastewater treatment (Feng et al. 2024). Certain parameters, such as the species of microorganisms (Padmanabhan et al. 2023), electrode types (Ramesh et al. 2025a), reactor structure (Rossi and Logan 2022), and cultivation conditions (Khandelwal et al. 2020), can affect the overall performance of MFCs. In MFCs, microorganisms oxidize organic matter at the anode, releasing electrons that travel through an external circuit to the cathode, generating electricity (Nawaz et al. 2022). Three extracellular electron transfer (EET) mechanisms via (1) redox-active proteins, (2) microbial secreted soluble electron shuttles, and (3) conductive pili were known to interact with the EET efficiency between microorganisms and electrodes (Xi et al. 2025). Recently, studies have developed physical (e.g., magnetic fields), biochemical, and synthetic biology strategies to enhance microbial activity and electrical output in microbial electrochemical technologies (METs) (Hazzan et al. 2023; Lay et al. 2022a; Zhang et al. 2024). Additionally, promising enhancements in wastewater treatment and bioelectricity generation using modified reactor configurations and operational strategies has also been demonstrated (Son et al. 2021, 2025).

A magnetic field has been applied to enhance simultaneous electricity generation and pollutant removal efficiency in bioelectrochemical systems recently (Gunda et al. 2021a). Further, new investigations into magnetic-field assisted MFCs confirmed improved extracellular electron transfer and reactor performance (Koo et al. 2025; Tran et al. 2025). Chu et al. (2021a) applied a static magnetic field (100–300 mT) to ceramic MFCs, and at 200 mT achieved a power density of 35.9 mW/m², 2.6 times higher than the 13.9 mW/m² without Gunda et al. (2021b) also reported that a static magnetic field can improve the bioelectrogenesis reaction for oxidizing the organics in food waste leachate in an MFC. A peak power density of 811 mW/m², along with efficient chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal, was achieved using a large magnetic field intensity of 220 mT. Zhou et al. (2017) showed that a pulse electromagnetic field enhanced EET in a single-chamber MEC with a Fe₃O₄@N-mC electrode, increasing power density by 25–36% compared to the control.

A long straight coil of wire can be used to generate a nearly uniform magnetic field similar to that of a bar magnet. Such coils, called solenoids, have an enormous number of practical applications. The field can be greatly strengthened by the addition of an iron core. Such cores are typical in electromagnets. Moreover, the magnetic field direction potentially affects the biological electron, which is generated by bacterial metabolism (Al-Mayyahi et al. 2023; Jung et al. 2023). The magnetic field will increase the electron transfer rate between the liquid and the electrode, hence affecting the bioelectrocatalytic variations of numerous enzyme assemblies connected to electrodes (Bhagat et al. 2022). Therefore, the solenoid magnetic field should be assembled to create a parallel direction with the electrode, thereby increasing the attachment potential to the electrode for the moving electron. To the best of our knowledge, the use of solenoid magnetic field for simultaneous electricity generation and pollutants removal in MFC is still limited. Thus, this study aims to apply a current-powered solenoid magnetic field on the external surface of a dual-chamber cubed MFC to analyze the effects of various current strengths (0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mA) on MFC performance. This study serves as the first to systematically demonstrate that a controllable, current-powered solenoid magnetic field can simultaneously enhance power output, pollutant removal, and microbial community structuring in dual-chamber MFCs, offering a new pathway to optimize bioelectrochemical systems for wastewater treatment.

Materials and methods

The inoculum of the anode

The anodic inoculum was the granular sludge collected from the up-flow anaerobic sludge blanket digestor in a food mill wastewater treatment plant in central Taiwan. It has been stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for one month and was warmed up at room temperature for one day before used (Lay et al. 2022b).

Cultivation of the MFC

A cubic double-chamber MFC was constructed from acrylic and separated by a cation exchange membrane (AMI-7000, Membranes International, Ringwood, NJ) into an anodic and cathodic chamber, each with a working volume of 75 mL. Both anodic and cathodic chambers have a carbon cloth electrode (W1S1010, Ce Tech Co., Ltd., Taiwan) with dimensions of 5 cm × 3 cm and a reaction surface area of 30 cm². An external resistance (R) of 1000 Ω was connected between the two electrodes via copper wires. In the anodic chamber, 20 mL of granular sludge and 55 mL of sulfate-reducing bacteria medium were mixed, and then the anode was added. The sulfate-reducing bacteria medium contains sodium lactate 3 g/L, MgSO4༎.H2O 2 g/L, Na2SO4 1.15 g/L, sodium citrate 5 g/L, NH4Cl 1 g/L, K2HPO4 0.5 g/L, Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 1 g/L, Wolfe’s vitamin solution 1 mL, and Wolfe’s mineral solution 1 mL) (Lee et al. 2014). The chamber was sparged with argon gas for 5 min to ensure an anaerobic environment. The electrolyte in the cathode chamber was 50 mM potassium ferricyanide (K3Fe(CN)6). Both anodic and cathodic electrolytes were added to 100 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to maintain a stable pH of 7.5. Each cultivation condition was carried out in duplicate in an air-batch incubator at 35 °C. The sequencing batch model was applied by replacing the fresh anodic growth medium (15 mL) and cathodic electrolyte (75 mL) every 3 days during a 33 day cultivation period.

A solenoid magnetic field was generated by winding copper wire into 8 turns around the cubic MFC. A constant current of 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mA was supplied using a multi-output programmable DC power supply (GPP-4323, Good Will Instrument Co., Ltd., Taiwan), as shown in Fig. 1. The MF is measured by a Gauss Meter (Lutron MG-300). The MF intensities inside the MFC were 2.3 ± 0.26 mT, 4.5 ± 0.45 mT, 7.2 ± 0.35 mT, and 9.2 ± 0.50 mT in MF-20, MF-40, MF-60, and MF-80 respectively.

Electrochemical and water quality measurements

A data acquisition system (5000 Data Acquisition System, Jiehan Technology Corporation, Co., Ltd., Taiwan) was used to record the voltage (V) at 3-minute intervals. The electrical performance, including power density (PD), current density (CD), and coulombic efficiency (CE), was calculated using the methods shown in our previous study (Ma et al. 2016).

The curves of polarization and power were plotted by varying the external resistance from 1 MΩ to 5 Ω using a resistance decade box (RBOX-408, Lutron Electronic Enterprise Co., Ltd., Taiwan). Internal resistances were valued from the slopes of polarization curves (Ma et al. 2016). Gausser (MG-3002, Lutron Electronic Enterprise Co., Ltd., Taiwan) was used to measure the MF intensities. Water quality analysis, such as COD concentration, was conducted using standard analytical procedures (American Public Health Association 2005). According to the sequencing batch cultivation, the liquid sample of 15 mL was collected for the water quality analysis. The COD removal and CE values were evaluated every batch cultivation.

Bacterial community analysis

The extraction of bacterial DNA was performed using the QIAamp PowerFecal DNA Kit and then stored at −20 °C until analysis. All DNA extraction samples were ensured of their quality by Qubit and electrophoresis before the PCR reaction. The quality of DNA extraction samples was verified using Qubit and electrophoresis prior to the PCR reaction. For PCR amplification, the extracted DNA was amplified using the F Primer 341 F (5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCA-3’) along with five different types of barcodes, and the R Primer 805R (5’-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3’). To analyze the variation in the microorganism community at various magnetic field intensities, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was applied. All sequencing data were categorized using the Silva database and underwent statistical analysis for bioinformatics following DNA sequencing. This analysis included the classification of operational taxonomic units (OTUs), taxonomic profiling, heatmap visualization, and assessment of beta diversity. OTU classification organizes sequencing data into hierarchical levels from Kingdom to Species for all data sharing the same OTU. The heatmap, derived from taxonomic profiling results and utilizing Z-score normalization, displays the relative abundance of species across different groups. To explore the beta diversity of specific species under various cultivation conditions, we applied Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS). The detail information of the methodology is described in our previous study (Lay et al. 2022b).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel Software (version 365). Significance of variables was determined at a confidence level of 95% (p-value ≤ 0.05) or at a 90% confidence level (p-value ranging from 0.05 to 0.10).

Results and discussion

Power generation performance

The MFC was incubated for 20 days to enrich the bioactivity of anaerobic microorganisms. During the subsequent 33-day cultivation period, additional solenoid magnetic fields were applied using currents of 0 mA (MF-0), 20 mA (MF-20), 40 mA (MF-40), 60 mA (MF-60), and 80 mA (MF-80), with the coil consisting of 8 turns, to investigate the performance of the MFCs. Increasing the extra solenoid magnetic field could increase the voltage generation at each MFC (Fig. 2). The first 18 days were used to enrich the anaerobic microbial biocatalyst activity in the anodic chamber, and all the MFCs were cultivated without the addition of a magnetic field. Almost all the MFCs can generate bioelectricity of about 50 mV, except one MFC (MF-0). After 18 days of operation of the MFC, the voltage performance increased at all MFCs. The maximum average voltage of 542 ± 7 mV was obtained when an additional solenoid magnetic field was applied using 80 mA, corresponding to a measured magnetic flux density of 9.2 ± 0.50 mT. This voltage value was approximately 1.8 times that of MF-0 (306 ± 23 mV) without the addition of an extra solenoid magnetic field.

(a) Voltage performance and (b) polarization curves at various current-powered solenoid magnetic field intensities

Polarization curves, which plot cell voltage against current density, provide valuable insights into the chemistry and performance dynamics of MFCs (Lay et al. 2022b). The polarization curves were carried out after 20 or 21 days’ cultivation. The three characteristic regions of a MFC polarization curve are (1) the first rapid cell potential decreasing due to the activation losses at low power densities, (2) the cell potential reductions linearly with current due to ohmic losses at moderate current densities and (3) the second rapid cell potential drop due to concentration losses at high current densities. The results of polarization curve analysis (Fig. 2b) illustrate that applying current-powered solenoid MF can increase the maximum voltage, CD, and PD performances significantly. The anode potential was also monitored under different MF intensities. At MF-0, the average anodic potential stabilized at − 210 ± 15 mV vs. Ag/AgCl, whereas under MF-80 it decreased to − 310 ± 12 mV. This more negative potential under higher MF indicates improved substrate oxidation at the bioanode and enhanced electron generation, consistent with the observed increases in current density and coulombic efficiency. Applying solenoid MF-80, the peak CD 130.5 ± 18.2 mA/m2 and PD 66.3 ± 16.8 mW/m2 were obtained. The following CD and PD values were 104.2 ± 32.2 mA/m² and PD 46.4 ± 21.5 mW/m², which were obtained at the solenoid MF-60. On the basis of the characteristic regions of the MFC polarization curve shown in Fig. 2b, the resistance is reflected in the linear reduction of cell potential with increasing current, which is attributed to ohmic losses. MF-0 exhibits the highest internal resistance, measured at 289 Ω following by the 224 Ω at MF-20, 209 Ω at MF-40, and 203 Ω at MF-60. Increasing the intensity of MF can reduce the internal resistance, as demonstrated by the application of MF-80, which decreases the resistance to 163 Ω. This enhancement may be attributed to the Lorentz force generated by the magnetic field, which accelerates the movement of charged metabolites and cofactors, thereby reducing electron transfer resistance and stimulating the activity of redox enzymes involved in extracellular electron transfer (Chu et al. 2021b; Winkler et al. 2023).

The peak power density achieved in this study (66.3 ± 16.8 mW m⁻² at MF-80) is comparable to dual-compartment/real-wastewater systems and photosynthetic/mechanically aerated configurations, for example 49.5 ± 3.5 mW m⁻² in a bacterium-enhanced dual-compartment MFC treating complex waste streams and recovering Cu²⁺, and 57 mW m⁻² with mechanical aeration in MFCs (Yin et al. 2024). While the obtained absolute power density is lower than highly optimized architectures, such as mediator-flow or advanced anode systems reporting around 216–947 mW m⁻², those typically employ specialized electrodes, flowing artificial mediators, or miniaturized/reactor-optimized designs rather than a simple dual-chamber wastewater reactor (Dakal et al. 2025). Within this context, the MF-driven gains we observe (higher current density and CE up to 71.4%) demonstrate that magnetic stimulation alone, without electrode modification or added mediators, can deliver meaningful performance improvements in practical, treatment-oriented MFC configurations. Recent reviews similarly note power densities of 200–300 mW m⁻² for enhanced but still treatment-relevant systems, underscoring that the obtained results in this study fall within the expected range for non-miniaturized MFCs while adding a controllable MF lever for further optimization (Ramesh et al. 2025b) (Table 1).

COD removal and CE

The average COD removal and CE values before MF and after MF were calculated from the values from each single batch during day 1 st −18th and day 19th −33rd respectively. The average COD removal and the CE performances before and after adding solenoid MF at various intensities were shown in Fig. 3. The results reveal that the applying of solenoid MF could enhance the COD removal efficiency compared to before MF at each experiment. After adding the solenoid, MF-80 achieved a peak COD removal efficiency of 19.1 ± 3.6%, compared to 13.9 ± 0.8% before adding the solenoid. Before adding the solenoid MF to any experiment, the values of CE ranged from 12.0 ± 1.3% to 23.6 ± 3.6%. After adding the solenoid MF, the average CE value could be increased significantly to higher than 40% (Fig. 3). The bioelectron conduction efficiency could be increased by applying solenoid MF to the anodic electrode, resulting in a peak CE value of 71.4 ± 7.3% when using solenoid MF-80. Zhou et al. (2019) revealed that the higher static–MF intensity can enhance the magnetohydrodynamic impact (the influence of a magnetic field on the movement of charged particles and fluids) on the efficiency of substrate degradation, with an increase in and suppression of exoelectrogens’ activity.

COD removal and CE performances before and after adding a solenoid magnetic field at various powered intensities

The efficiency of COD removal and CE in MFCs is governed by how effectively microorganisms can transfer electrons to the anodic electrode (Bazina et al. 2023). Exoelectrogens achieve this through three major extracellular electron transfer (EET) pathways: (i) direct contact via outer-membrane cytochromes, (ii) secretion of soluble electron shuttles such as pyocyanin, phenazines, or Fe(III) compounds, and (iii) conductive pili (nanowires) that bridge microbial cells to the electrode (Abdelaziz et al. 2023; Bagchi and Behera 2021b; Digel et al. 2024). The application of a solenoid MF likely stimulates these processes by reducing electron transfer resistance and enhancing enzymatic redox activity, thereby accelerating substrate degradation and electron recovery. As a result, CE increased significantly from 23.6% in MF-0 to 71.4% in MF-80, accompanied by higher COD removal (19.1 ± 3.6%). These findings are consistent with reports that magnetic fields can enhance EET efficiency and promote the activity of exoelectrogenic bacteria, ultimately leading to improved pollutant degradation and bioelectricity generation (Bhagat et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2019).

Microorganisms degrade organic substrate to produce electrons and protons (H+) in the MFC. However, there are three ways that microorganisms could transfer electrons exogenously to an electrode, including direct contact, soluble shuttle, and cellular extension. Electron transfer can occur directly through the cytochromes, which are present on the surface of the bacterial cell membrane. Alternatively, through cellular extension (bacterial nanowire), a soluble shuttle is considered an indirect way of electron transfer, providing a different mechanism for electron transport in microbes that are incapable of transferring electrons to the anode directly. Such as Fe (III), pyocyanin, phenazine-1-carboxamide, sulfur, and so on, as commonly identified as soluble electron shuttles. On the other hand, the direct conduction of cellular electrons to the anodic electrode is facilitated by the conductive pili of the microorganism (Lay et al. 2022b).

For MFCs, the electromotive force act as the upper limit for the total voltage that MFC can generate under specific conditions, but in fact the realistic voltage will be lower under practical condition the loses result of mass transfer of reactants, bacterial metabolic kinetics, electron conducting from microbial cells, transfer of charges through electrodes electrolyte and permeable exchange membrane (Mohammadi et al. 2023). The electron-conducting resistance and the internal resistance are responsible for the potential losses. The key limiting factor is the activation overpotential, which is primarily related to the electron flow through the anode (Mamun et al. 2023). The solenoid force as shown in Fig. 4 illustrated the effect on the electron transfer efficiency within these mechanisms by increasing the potential of conducting the electrode surface for electron. In this study, the solenoid MF, powered by an external current ranging from 20 mA to 80 mA, generated MF intensities of 2.3 ± 0.26 mT to 9.2 ± 0.50 mT in both the anodic and cathodic chambers. The electron flow might be forced to increase the transfer rate to enhance the removal of COD and improve CE values.

Although COD removal increased under solenoid MF, the improvement (from 13.9% to 19.1%) was more modest compared to the gains in power density and CE. This is because COD removal reflects the overall degradation of organic matter, which involves both electroactive and non-electroactive microbial pathways. In contrast, electricity generation is directly linked to the efficiency of exoelectrogenic populations and extracellular electron transfer, which were strongly stimulated by the magnetic field (Digel et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2019).

Microorganism community

DNA sequencing technology on the Illumina MiSeq System was used to examine the structure of microbial communities. The analysis results screened the influence of MF on the microorganism community. Figure 5 illustrates the taxonomy profiling classification by type and quantity as a bar chart. Figure 6 illustrates the distribution of microbial communities at different MF intensities, confirming the relative abundance shifts presented in Fig. 5. Notably, under MF-80 conditions, Geobacter became one of the dominant genera, whereas Pseudomonas declined significantly.

In all samples, 9 bacterial genera with a richness of more than 1% were identified. The most abundant genera were Pseudomonas and Desulfovibrio, which accounted for 27% and 13% of the total sequences, respectively, without MF addition. Adding solenoid MF significantly decreased the population of Pseudomonas from 27% to 4% when applying MF-80 and from 13% to 0.2% when applying MF-40, respectively. Pseudomonas sp., a gram-negative bacterium, can produce the redox mediators, sort as pyocyanin and pyorubrin, in MFCs (Abdelaziz et al. 2023; Bagchi and Behera 2021a). These shuttle mediators have an important role in improving electron transfer out of the microorganisms’ cells (Bagchi and Behera 2021b). Additionally, Desulfovibrio species are sulfate-reducing bacteria that comprise nanofilament and mediators. They are identified by the existence of periplasmic or membrane-bound c-type cytochromes, which facilitate proton and electron exchange through either layer-bound organelles or redox mediators (Kumar et al. 2015). Flavobacterium is known as a dominant aerobic bacterium for producing electricity and can resist formaldehyde and high iron concentrations (Conrad et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2012). However, in this study, the peak population of Flavobacterium occurred when applying MF-40, which was increased from 0.43% when applying MF-0 and then decreased to 0.81% applying MF-80. Applying MF could increase the population of Idiomarina from 0.10% (MF-0) to 10.75% (MF-80) significantly. Typically, higher salinity in the environment promotes the selection of Idiomarina, a genus commonly found in seawater, hypersaline environments, conditions being mineral-rich and halide-rich, it can utilize amino acids for growth, and it contains a complete aerobic electron transport chain to oxidative phosphorylation (Brestovičová et al. 2024; Vinales et al. 2022). Increasing the MF could increase the population of Geobacter. Geobacter are exoelectrogens, gram-negative and strictly anaerobic bacteria, that have pili on their cell membrane, which can easily transfer electrons to the anode surface and achieve high power density. Geobacter can grow and produce acetate from substrates such as some volatile fatty acids and alcohols (Härrer et al. 2023; Nosek et al. 2023). The peak population of Geobacter could reach 12.02% while applying MF-80, which is much higher compared to 0.67% in the control group (MF-0).

The application of a solenoid magnetic field not only altered electron transfer efficiency but also reshaped the microbial community structure in the anodic biofilm. Magnetic stimulation has been reported to selectively favor exoelectrogenic populations by influencing cell metabolism, membrane potential, and enzymatic activity (Al-Mayyahi et al. 2023). In this study, the enrichment of Geobacter at MF-80 (12%), a bacterium capable of direct electron transfer via outer-membrane cytochromes and conductive pili, correlates with the observed increases in current density and CE. In contrast, the decline of Pseudomonas under stronger MF suggests a reduced reliance on soluble shuttles (e.g., pyocyanin, phenazines), with the system shifting toward more efficient direct EET pathways. The increased abundance of Idiomarina may reflect adaptive metabolic roles in substrate utilization and redox balance, indirectly supporting COD degradation (Abate et al. 2024). These microbial shifts demonstrate that the magnetic field acted as an ecological driver, promoting communities optimized for extracellular electron transfer and explaining the enhanced COD removal and electrochemical performance under higher MF intensities.

Practical implications and author’s perspective

From a practical perspective, the application of solenoid magnetic fields in MFCs offers a simple, controllable, and scalable strategy to enhance system performance without the need for complex reactor redesigns. In this study, the power input required to generate an MF intensity of 9.2 ± 0.5 mT at 80 mA was approximately 0.35 mW, while the corresponding increase in power density reached 47.8 mW/m² compared to the control. This demonstrates that the performance gains significantly outweigh the external energy cost of MF generation. Similar findings have been reported in other bioelectrochemical studies, where low-intensity magnetic fields enhanced microbial activity and EET efficiency with minimal energy input (Bhagat et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2019). These results suggest that integrating MF into MFCs is both energetically favorable and practically viable, particularly for applications in wastewater treatment where pollutant removal and energy recovery are pursued simultaneously.

Compared with previous approaches using static magnets or pulse electromagnetic fields, the current-powered solenoid MF applied in this study offers several advantages. First, it allows precise and tunable control of MF intensity through simple current adjustment, whereas static magnets provide only fixed field strengths (Chu et al. 2021b; Gunda et al. 2021a). Second, unlike pulse electromagnetic fields, which often require complex setups and higher energy input (Zhou et al. 2017), the used solenoid system is simpler and more energy-efficient. Third, this method does not rely on electrode modification or chemical mediators, which can increase cost and reduce long-term stability (Bagchi and Behera 2021b). Instead, the solenoid MF achieved substantial improvements in power density and CE with minimal energy input, highlighting its practicality and scalability for real wastewater treatment applications.

Regarding scalability, the application of solenoid MF in larger MFC systems is technically feasible since magnetic fields can be generated using low-cost coils and controlled with adjustable current inputs. However, the uniform distribution of the magnetic field across larger reactor volumes presents a challenge, as field intensity may decrease with distance from the coil. This might be addressed by modular coil arrangements or by embedding smaller solenoid units at different points within scaled-up reactors. Future research should therefore focus on optimizing coil design and energy balance for pilot- or industrial-scale MF-assisted MFCs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that applying a current-powered solenoid magnetic field significantly enhanced the performance of dual-chamber MFCs. At an applied current of 80 mA, the maximum power density (66.3 ± 16.8 mW/m²), current density (130.5 ± 18.2 mA/m²), and coulombic efficiency (71.4 ± 7.3%) were achieved, alongside an improvement in COD removal efficiency by 5.2% points compared to the control. The solenoid MF not only improved electrochemical output but also restructured the microbial community, with a decline in Pseudomonas and an enrichment of Geobacter, indicating a shift toward more efficient direct electron transfer pathways. These findings highlight the novelty of using a tunable, current-driven solenoid MF to enhance both pollutant removal and bioelectricity generation. From a practical perspective, the low energy input required for MF generation compared with the performance gains suggests good potential for integration into larger-scale wastewater treatment systems. Future research should focus on scaling strategies, long-term stability, and optimizing coil designs to maximize field uniformity and energy efficiency.
