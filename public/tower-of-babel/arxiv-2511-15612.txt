Cartan meets Cramér-Rao
Sunder Ram Krishnan*

Abstract
This paper develops a jet bundle and Cartan geometric foundation for the curvature-aware refinements of the Cramér-Rao bound (CRB) introduced in our earlier work. We show that the extrinsic corrections to variance bounds, previously derived from the second fundamental form of the square root embedding s θ = f ⁡ ( ⋅ , θ ) ∈ L 2 ​ ( μ ) s_{\theta}=\sqrt{f(\cdot;\theta)}\in L^{2}(\mu) for model density f ⁡ ( ⋅ , θ ) f(\cdot;\theta) with scalar parameter θ \theta , admit an intrinsic formulation within the Cartan prolongation framework. Starting from the canonical contact forms and total derivative on the finite jet bundle J m ​ ( ℝ × ℝ ) J^{m}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}) , we construct the Cartan distribution and the associated Ehresmann connection, whose non-integrability and torsion encode the geometric source of curvature corrections in statistical estimation. In the statistical jet bundle E = ℝ × L 2 ​ ( μ ) E=\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}(\mu) , we point out that the condition for an estimator error to lie in the span of derivatives of s θ s_{\theta} up to order m m is equivalent to the square root map satisfying a linear differential equation of order m m . The corresponding submanifold of J m ​ ( E ) J^{m}(E) defined by this equation represents the locus of m m –th order efficient models, and the prolonged section must form an integral curve of the restricted Cartan vector field. This establishes a one-to-one correspondence between algebraic projection conditions underlying CRB and Bhattacharyya-type bounds and geometric integrability conditions for the statistical section in the jet bundle hierarchy. The resulting framework links variance bounds, curvature, and estimator efficiency through the geometry of Cartan distributions, offering a new differential equation and connection-theoretic interpretation of higher-order information inequalities.

Introduction

Assuming that the parametric family of distributions
\{P_{\theta}\}
with the scalar parameter
\theta\in\Theta
is absolutely continuous with respect to the same base measure
\mu
, with
f(\cdot;\theta)
denoting the density, we showed in
[
4
]
that the classical Cramér–Rao bound (CRB) and its higher–order variants admit a unified extrinsic–geometric formulation by viewing the statistical model

\mathcal{M}:=\{\,s_{\theta}(\cdot)=\sqrt{f(\cdot;\theta)}\in L^{2}(\mu):\theta\in\Theta\,\}

as an embedded submanifold of the ambient Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mu)
. In that formulation, the first–order CRB arises from projection of the estimator residual onto the one–dimensional tangent span
T_{1}:=\operatorname{span}\{\eta_{1}\}
,
\eta_{k}:=\partial_{\theta}^{k}s_{\theta}
, while Bhattacharyya/Pillai–Sinha–type higher–order bounds
[
2
,
6
]
correspond to projections onto enlarged jet spans
T_{m}:=\operatorname{span}\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m}\}
. Departures of the estimator error from
T_{1}
into higher–order directions produce an extrinsic curvature correction encoded in the second fundamental form of
\mathcal{M}\subset L^{2}(\mu)
; quantifying that residual yields
sharpened variance lower bounds (see
[
4
]
for the setting described here and
[
5
]
for a vector parameter extension).

The present paper gives a rigorous geometric foundation of that program by formulating projection/efficiency conditions as differential equations on the statistical jet bundle. Although classical, we endeavor to present a rigorous, yet intuitive, set of facts on the jet bundle hierarchy (see
[
7
]
for a standard reference). Here is a quick summary: We first develop the Cartan–jet machinery by considering the trivial fibre bumdle
E=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
over the base parameter manifold
\Theta
; the
m
–jet manifold
J^{m}(E)
carries the canonical contact forms and the total derivative (Cartan) vector field. The Cartan distribution encodes the holonomy (horizontal) directions and is generically non–integrable; its structural curvature measures the obstruction to integrability and is removed by passing to the prolonged bundle
J^{m+1}(E)
. Equipping the projection
\pi_{m,m-1}:J^{m}(E)\to J^{m-1}(E)
with the Ehresmann connection
\nabla^{m}
yields a canonical horizontal/vertical splitting; the tangent to a prolonged section then decomposes as the sum of the total derivative and a vertical term, which may be considered to be a natural analog of a normal acceleration and corresponds to
the second fundamental form for the inclusion
J^{m}(E)\subset J^{m+1}(E)
. This intrinsic jet bundle geometry mirrors the extrinsic geometry captured in
[
4
]
.

Crucially for statistical applications, we notice that the condition that the estimator residual lie in the span
T_{m}=\operatorname{span}\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m}\}
is equivalent to the existence of a linear differential relation of order
m
satisfied by the square root map, so that the prolonged section
j^{m}s:\Theta\to J^{m}(E)
in the statistical jet bundle
E=\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}(\mu)
takes values in the submanifold

\Sigma\subset J^{m}(E)
defined by the ordinary differential equation (ODE) constraint. Equivalently, the prolongation of the square root map

j^{m}s
must be an integral curve of the restriction of the total derivative vector field to
\Sigma
. Thus, “efficiency up to order
m
” coincides with the integrability/holonomy condition that the prolonged section remain horizontal inside the differential submanifold defined by the ODE. When such an integral curve fails to exist, the vertical (torsion) component of
\nabla^{m}
quantifies the residual direction which, via the second fundamental form, produces the curvature correction to variance bounds. This framework precisely justifies the slightly informal “second fundamental vector” that we defined in
[
4
]
when
m>1
.

We therefore obtain a transparent equivalence: algebraic projection conditions in the fixed Hilbert space (membership of the error in
T_{m}
)
\iff
differential equation (integrability) conditions for
s_{\theta}
in the jet bundle language. This Cartan–jet perspective not only places the curvature corrections of
[
4
]
on firm geometric ground, but also explains the hierarchy of CRB and Bhattacharyya–type bounds as successive holonomy/integrability conditions in the tower of prolonged jet bundles. It is worth highlighting that this jet bundle geometry only holds for the square root embedding we consider(ed), and not with the log likelihood embedding or the variations considered by Bhattacharyya/Pillai-Sinha
[
2
,
6
]
. This is because we need the fibre spaces to be independent of the base parameter manifold.

Literature and novelty.

Classical information geometry, originating with Amari and colleagues, focused on developing the intrinsic affine–connection framework of the Fisher–Rao metric and the dual
\alpha
–connections that underlie most analyses of statistical efficiency and the CRB; see, for instance,
[
1
]
. These constructions are inherently intrinsic, in contrast to the extrinsic
Hilbert–space embedding used in our earlier work. Jet bundles and Cartan’s
contact–form/prolongation formalism are by now standard in the geometric theory of differential equations (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
), but they have not, to our knowledge, been linked to the geometry of statistical estimation or to variance bounds. Classical higher–order inequalities, such as those of Bhattacharyya/Pillai–Sinha
[
2
,
6
]
, extend the CRB algebraically but do not admit a geometric interpretation in terms of integrability or curvature. Recent work has explored Cartan–type metrics and invariant
connections in information geometry
[
3
]
, yet these do not employ the jet bundle construction or the integrability viewpoint developed here. The present work appears to be the first to formulate projection–based variance conditions (CRB and Bhattacharyya–type) as differential equation constraints on prolonged sections of the statistical square root embedding, thereby linking higher–order efficiency directly to the geometry of Cartan
distributions.

2
Jet bundle preliminaries and Cartan prolongations

We start by discussing relevant background from the geometry of jet bundles. Readers interested in learning more may refer to
[
7
]
.

2.1
Basic setup: jets and sections for a scalar base variable

Let
E=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
denote a trivial fibre bundle over the base parameter manifold
\Theta=\mathbb{R}
,
with projection
\pi:E\to\Theta
,
\pi(\theta,s)=\theta
.
A smooth section of this bundle is a map
which assigns to each base point
\theta
a point
s(\theta)
in the fibre. (Note that we use
s(\theta)\equiv s_{\theta}
for a general map as well as the specific square root map
\sqrt{f(\cdot;\theta)}
, mentioned in the introduction, when needed.)

The
m
-th
jet bundle

J^{m}(E)
encodes the equivalence classes of local sections of
E

that agree up to order
m
at each base point.
In coordinates, a point of
J^{m}(E)
can be represented as

(\theta,s,s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}),

where
s_{k}
corresponds to the value of the
k
-th derivative
s^{(k)}(\theta)

of a section
s
at
\theta
.

The canonical projections

\pi_{m,0}:J^{m}(E)\to E,\qquad\pi_{m,m-1}:J^{m}(E)\to J^{m-1}(E),

forget the higher-order derivatives.

The
m
-jet prolongation
of a section
s
is then the map

j^{m}s:\mathbb{R}\to J^{m}(E),\qquad j^{m}s(\theta)=\big(\theta,s(\theta),s^{\prime}(\theta),\ldots,s^{(m)}(\theta)\big).

This
j^{m}s
is a particular kind of section of
J^{m}(E)
whose image consists of holonomic jets – jets that actually come from a function. A general smooth function
\mathbb{R}\to J^{m}(E)
need not be of the from
j^{m}s
for any
s
; the holonomic ones satisfy compatibility relations among the coordinates which may be expressed using the contact forms below.

2.2
The Cartan distribution and total derivative

On
J^{m}(E)
, there are canonical contact (Cartan)
1
-forms

\omega_{k}=ds_{k}-s_{k+1}\,d\theta,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,m-1.

They vanish on all prolonged curves
j^{m}s
because
ds_{k}=s^{(k+1)}d\theta
along such curves.
The
Cartan distribution

\mathcal{C}^{m}\subset TJ^{m}
is defined as the common kernel of these forms:

\mathcal{C}^{m}=\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}\ker(\omega_{k})=\mathrm{span}\{D^{(m)}_{\theta}\},

where

D^{(m)}_{\theta}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+s_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}+s_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{1}}+\cdots+s_{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m-1}}

(2.1)

is the
total derivative operator

D_{\theta}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{k}}

(2.2)

restricted to
J^{m}
.
It represents the canonical horizontal lift of
\partial_{\theta}
to
J^{m}(E)
: The Cartan distribution provides a way to lift tangent vectors from the base manifold to the total space in a consistent manner.

In addition to the facts mentioned above that the total derivative vector field
D^{(m)}_{\theta}
spans the Cartan distribution
\mathcal{C}^{m}
and provides the horizontal lift of
\partial_{\theta}
from the base manifold, it is of interest to note that it is complementary to the vertical bundle
VJ^{m}=\ker((\pi_{m,0})_{\ast})
, yielding the decomposition
TJ^{m}=\mathcal{C}^{m}\oplus VJ^{m}
; we will discuss a different way to decompose this tangent bundle when discussing Ehresmann connections.
Another point, which we use later, is that, for the projection
\pi_{m+1,m}:J^{m+1}\to J^{m}
, the vertical space
V(J^{m+1}/J^{m})
is spanned by the vector field
\partial_{s_{m+1}}
. This vector field acts along the new fiber coordinate
s_{m+1}
(the
(m+1)
-th derivative) introduced when passing from
J^{m}
to
J^{m+1}
.

3
The geometry of jet bundles

Our interest here is to provide a slightly deeper understanding of the inherent geometry of the jet bundle hierarchy.

3.1
Tangent vector to prolonged section

From our discussion above, it is immediate that the tangent vector to the prolonged section
j^{m}s
, which is the
\theta
-derivative of the curve, is given by:

\frac{d}{d\theta}j^{m}s=D^{(m)}_{\theta}+s^{(m+1)}(\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}.

(3.1)

Recalling the general decomposition
TJ^{m}=\mathcal{C}^{m}\oplus VJ^{m}
noted above, we see that this specific vector field decomposes into the
1
-dimensional Cartan (canonical horizontal) component
D^{(m)}_{\theta}
and the component
s^{(m+1)}(\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}
. The component
s^{(m+1)}(\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}
in (
3.1
) is the top order of the total derivative vector field (
2.2
) to be added to
D^{(m)}_{\theta}
to ensure that the curve remains holonomic in the next order of jet space; that is, to satisfy the new contact form
\omega_{m}=ds_{m}-s_{m+1}\,d\theta
on
J^{m+1}
. This automatically means that the distribution
\mathcal{C}^{m+1}=\mathrm{span}\{D^{(m+1)}_{\theta}\}
. The term
s^{(m+1)}(\theta)
represents the necessary “acceleration” required to move from a point in
J^{m}
to the corresponding point in the higher-dimensional
J^{m+1}
space while respecting the prolongation condition.

3.2
A curvature/torsion viewpoint

We first point out that it is the non-integrability of
\mathcal{C}^{m}
which manifests the fact that the tangent vector to the curve
j^{m}s
is not purely horizontal in the jet space of order
m
.

Structural curvature and non-integrability of
\mathcal{C}^{m}.

Define the
m
-th order structural curvature form to be proportional to
d\omega_{m-1}
, where
\omega_{m-1}
is the highest order contact form on
J^{m}
. Recall that the Cartan distribution
\mathcal{C}^{m}
on
J^{m}
is annihilated by the contact 1-forms:

\omega_{k}=ds_{k}-s_{k+1}d\theta,\,k=0,1,\dots,m-1
.
The structural curvature form in this context is related to the obstruction to the integrability of this distribution, which is defined by the basic contact one-forms.
Specifically, for
\mathcal{C}^{m}
, the highest order contact form available on
J^{m}
coordinates is:

\omega_{m-1}=ds_{m-1}-s_{m}d\theta.

Then

d\omega_{m-1}=d(ds_{m-1}-s_{m}d\theta)=-ds_{m}\wedge d\theta=d\theta\wedge ds_{m}.

Since
d\theta\wedge ds_{m}
is clearly non-zero on
J^{m}
, modulo the contact forms on this space, the distribution
\mathcal{C}^{m}
is non-integrable by the Frobenius theorem.

However, the structural curvature form associated with the
m
-th order jet bundle vanishes precisely when we consider the next higher-order jet bundle,
J^{m+1}
. This can be easily seen from the fact that

ds_{m}=\omega_{m}+s_{m+1}d\theta,

using the new contact form
\omega_{m}
on this space.
Hence

d\omega_{m-1}=d\theta\wedge ds_{m}=d\theta\wedge\omega_{m}\equiv 0

modulo the contact ideal on
J^{m+1}
.

In summary, the non-vanishing
d\omega_{m-1}
is essentially the structural curvature that prevents
\mathcal{C}^{m}
from being a maximally integrable distribution.
However,
d\omega_{m-1}
itself is contained in the contact ideal generated by the basic contact forms on the next higher jet space.
This is why the obstruction vanishes in the
J^{m+1}
space.

It is of interest to examine, equivalently, the link between (
3.1
) and the torsion of the canonical connection.

Canonical connection and Torsion.

The canonical connection
\nabla^{m}
is an Ehresmann connection defined on the affine bundle
\pi_{m,m-1}:J^{m}\to J^{m-1}
.
The connection
\nabla^{m}
splits the tangent space of the total manifold
J^{m}
.
Recall the point we previously noted: The vertical distribution
V(J^{m}/J^{m-1})
is the kernel of the projection
(\pi_{m,m-1})_{\ast}
. It is the tangent space of the fibers parameterized by the new coordinate
s_{m}
:

V^{m}\equiv V(J^{m}/J^{m-1})=\ker((\pi_{m,m-1})_{\ast})=\mathrm{span}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}\right\}.

The vertical space is one-dimensional at every point.

An Ehresmann connection is defined by choosing a complementary horizontal distribution
H^{m}
such that the tangent bundle splits:

TJ^{m}=H^{m}\oplus V^{m}
.
The canonical connection
\nabla^{m}
is defined by requiring its horizontal distribution
H^{m}
to be spanned by the fields given below:

H^{m}=\mathrm{span}\left\{D^{(m)}_{\theta},\frac{\partial}{\partial s},\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m-1}}\right\}.

Note that
H^{m}
is
(m+1)
-dimensional, matching the dimension of the base space
J^{m-1}
.
The horizontal lift of
\partial/\partial s_{k}
(
k<m
) from
J^{m-1}
to
J^{m}
is simply
\partial/\partial s_{k}
, as these directions are already horizontal in the sense of the affine structure.

With this background, we see that the vector
X:=\frac{d}{d\theta}j^{m}s
has a vertical component with respect to the canonical connection
\nabla^{m}
on the bundle
\pi_{m,m-1}:J^{m}\to J^{m-1}
:

X=\underbrace{D^{(m)}_{\theta}}_{X_{H}}+\underbrace{s^{(m+1)}(\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}}_{X_{V}}.

This decomposition shows that the velocity of the prolonged curve is not purely horizontal in
J^{m}
with respect to the connection
\nabla^{m}
.

The fact that the vector
X
has a non-zero vertical component
X_{V}
is directly related to the “torsion form” associated with
\nabla^{m}
, which we now proceed to define by contracting the structural curvature form with the total derivative vector field
D_{\theta}
:

\Omega_{m}:=i_{D_{\theta}}d\omega_{m-1}=i_{D_{\theta}}(-ds_{m}\wedge d\theta),

with
i_{Y}\omega
denoting the interior product of the differential form
\omega
with respect to the vector field
Y
.
Applying the interior product
i_{Y}(\alpha\wedge\beta)=(i_{Y}\alpha)\beta-\alpha(i_{Y}\beta)
:

\Omega_{m}=d\theta(D_{\theta})ds_{m}-ds_{m}(D_{\theta})d\theta=ds_{m}-s_{m+1}d\theta,

which is precisely the new contact form
\omega_{m}
added when moving to
\mathcal{C}^{m+1}
. Note, of course, that
\Omega_{m}(X)=s_{m+1}-s_{m+1}=0
. Thus, the torsion associated with the connection
\nabla^{m}
is perfectly canceled when we proceed to the next order
m+1
.

3.3
Second fundamental form analogy

In classical differential geometry of curves, the second fundamental form measures the component of acceleration that lies normal to the tangent space of the embedded curve. This concept is mirrored in the decomposition of the jet velocity vector
X=\tfrac{d}{d\theta}j^{m}s
, viewed as tangent to the curve
j^{m}s
in
J^{m}\subset J^{m+1}
.

Analog of the tangential acceleration.

The role of the tangent space is played by the horizontal distribution
H^{m}
.
The horizontal component
X_{H}
is the projection of
X
onto this space, which gives the canonical flow:

X_{H}=D^{(m)}_{\theta}
.
This part acts as the analog of the tangential acceleration component. Note that the integral curves of
D_{\theta}^{(m)}
on
J^{m}

are the sections that satisfy a specific differential equation (which is
s_{m+1}=0
). In this sense,
D^{(m)}_{\theta}
defines the “straightest” possible path or the canonical flat flow of the jet structure.

Analog of the normal acceleration component.

The canonical vertical torsion vector
X_{V}
is the remainder, measuring the deviation from the canonical tangential flow:

X_{V}=X-X_{H}
.

This definition parallels the action of the second fundamental form, which measures the difference between the total change (acceleration) and the tangential projection.

Thus,
X_{V}=s^{(m+1)}(\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}
may be thought of as the canonical geometric object that quantifies the extrinsic curvature (considering
J^{m}\subset J^{m+1}
) or deviation of the prolonged curve
j^{m}s
from the affine flatness defined by the jet structure.

In short, the geometry of jet bundles, as captured by the non-integrability

/structural curvature of the Cartan distribution or torsion of the canonical Ehresmann connection and the “intrinsic” second fundamental form analogy here matches, and hence further abstractly justifies, the extrinsic geometry-based curvature corrections to the CRB and its variants proposed in
[
4
]
, with
s(\Theta)
embedded in the ambient space
L^{2}(\mu)
, using the specific jets associated with the square root map. We provide additional insights on this connection below by considering differential equations and their solutions within the geometric framework of the jet bundle hierarchy.

4
Solutions to differential equations and classical variance bounds

The jet bundle structure provides an elegant geometric language for describing solutions to differential equations.

Representing differential equations and their solutions.

We only attempt a “quick and dirty” treatment here in a simple scenario. Consider the following ODE of order
(m+1)
:

s^{(m+1)}(\theta)=g(\theta,s(\theta),s^{(1)}(\theta),\dots,s^{(m)}(\theta)).

(4.1)

Now, finding a solution to (
4.1
) may be viewed as the task of finding a smooth section
s
of the bundle
\pi:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that its prolongation
j^{m+1}s
lies within the
(m+2)
-dimensional submanifold of
J^{m+1}(E)
defined by
\{(\theta,s,s_{1},\dots,s_{m+1}):s_{m+1}=g(\theta,s,s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\}
.

In other words, the solution will have to be the integral curve of the vector field
Y
below, which is the restriction of
D_{\theta}^{(m+1)}
to the ODE submanifold:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+s_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}+\dots+s_{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m-1}}+g(\theta,s,s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\theta}^{(m+1)}+(g-s_{m+1})\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}},

which lies in the horizontal distribution associated with the Ehresmann connection
\nabla^{m+1}
on
J^{m+1}
. Note that
Y
does not lie in the horizontal distribution
H^{m}
and that
Y
annihilates the forms
\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{m-1},ds_{m}-gd\theta
.

Connection to CRB and Bhattacharyya-type bounds.

Assume the usual regularity conditions as in
[
4
]
; specifically, assume that the finite-order derivatives of the square root map with respect to
\theta
, denoted by
s_{\theta}^{(m)}(\cdot)
, are in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Consider a modification of the jet bundle geometry above where the fibre bundle
E=\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}(\mu)
over the base parameter space
\mathbb{R}
. It is then possible to relate this geometry to the extrinsic geometry-based framework developed in our earlier work.

We observed (not for the first time) in
[
4
]
that an unbiased estimator
T(\cdot)
is efficient in the CRB sense if the estimator error
(T(\cdot)-\theta)s_{\theta}(\cdot)
lies in the span of
s^{(1)}_{\theta}(\cdot)
. That is, for a given efficient estimator
T
, the square root map satisfies the first-order ODE

(T(\cdot)-\theta)s_{\theta}(\cdot)=c_{\theta}\cdot s_{\theta}^{(1)}(\cdot),

for some
c_{\theta}\neq 0
. In other words, for a CRB-inefficient estimator, we will not be able to prolong the square root map to
J^{1}(E)
so that the prolonged curve lies in the submanifold defined by the above differential equation with any
c_{\theta}
. That is, one cannot obtain the integral curve of the vector field

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{(T-\theta)s_{\theta}}{c_{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}

in
J^{1}(E)
.

Assume that the Gram matrix of the
\{s_{\theta}^{(m)}(\cdot)\}
is invertible for any finite
m
. One may absorb the torsion components or, stated differently, incorporate curvature-aware corrections, by considering higher-order jet spaces and possibly find the integral curve of the vector field

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+s_{\theta}^{(1)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}+s_{\theta}^{(2)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{(T-\theta)s_{\theta}-c_{1,\theta}s_{\theta}^{(1)}-\cdots-c_{m,\theta}s_{\theta}^{(m)}}{c_{m+1,\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{m}}

in
J^{m+1}(E)
where
m\geq 1
, with a unique set of numbers
c_{1,\theta},\dots,c_{m+1,\theta}(\neq 0)
. That is, we are then able to prolong the square root map to
J^{m+1}(E)
and guarantee that it lies within the submanifold defined by an
(m+1)
-th order ODE.

This outlook based on the jet bundle geometry offers another (intrinsic) geometric perspective on the CRB and Bhattacharyya-type bounds, which we earlier motivated using extrinsic geometry with the specific square root embedding in
[
4
,
5
]
. It is worth repeating that this geometric outlook only holds when the fibre spaces are fixed and do not vary with the base parameter manifold
\Theta
, something that holds with the square root map and
not
with the log likelihood embedding, for instance.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper established a Cartan-geometric foundation for the curvature-aware variance bounds introduced in our earlier Hilbert-space formulation of the Cramér-Rao and Bhattacharyya-type inequalities. By representing the statistical square root map
s_{\theta}=\sqrt{f(\cdot;\theta)}
as a section of the statistical jet bundle
E=\mathbb{R}\times L^{2}(\mu)
and exploiting the canonical contact forms and total derivative on
J^{m}(E)
, we showed that the hierarchy of variance bounds corresponds to a hierarchy of holonomy conditions for prolonged sections.
In particular,
m
-th order efficiency was shown to be equivalent to the existence of an
m
-th order differential equation defining a submanifold of
J^{m}(E)
where a prolongation of the square root map must lie.
The vertical component of the canonical connection
\nabla^{m}
on
\pi_{m,m-1}:J^{m}(E)\!\to\!J^{m-1}(E)
provides a geometric measure of inefficiency, coinciding with the extrinsic curvature term in the corrected variance bounds.
This construction unifies the analytic, geometric, and differential equation perspectives on higher-order information inequalities.

Several directions naturally follow from this work.
First, the Cartan framework may be extended to multi-parameter models, where the torsion and curvature tensors of
\nabla^{m}
interact with the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher-Rao metric; the resulting mixed curvatures could yield tensorial generalizations of the extrinsic correction.
Second, the correspondence between integrability and estimator efficiency invites a systematic study of the moduli of holonomic and non-holonomic statistical sections, potentially linking statistical efficiency to geometric control theory.
Third, numerical realization of the Cartan connection in high-dimensional or manifold-valued models could enable practical curvature-corrected bounds for modern machine learning scenarios.
Finally, the relation between the Cartan torsion and the dualistic
\alpha
-connections of information geometry suggests a unified treatment of intrinsic and extrinsic curvature effects within a common framework.
These directions point toward a broader differential geometric theory of statistical efficiency grounded in the geometry of jet bundles and Cartan distributions.
