Individual and combined effects of vitamin E and lysolecithin supplementation on growth performance, serum biochemistry, antioxidant status, and gene expression in broiler chickens
Authors: Abeer A. Kirrella, Karima El-Naggar, Abeer G. Zaki, Safaa E. Abdo, Eman A. Manaa, Asmaa F. Khafaga, Ayham Abdulkader, Mahmoud Alagawany, Yahya Eid, Ibrahim A. Elkhaiat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To assess their potential benefits in broiler production, the present experiment evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with vitamin E (Vit E) and lysolecithin (LYSO) on growth performance, blood biochemical changes, intestinal and hepatic morphology, antioxidant status, meat quality, and gene expression. A total of 264 broiler chicks (Avian 48) were randomly assigned to six treatments for 35 days, each treatment containing four replicates of 11 birds. Treatments were arranged as follows: G1): control, G2): vitamin E (200 mg/kg diet), G3) 0.5 g LYSO/kg diet, G4) 1 g LYSO/kg diet, G5) 0.5 g LYSO + Vit E, and G6) 1 g LYSO+ Vit E. Broiler chickens supplemented with 1 g LYSO/kg diet + Vit E demonstrated the highest performance (higher body weight gain and improved feed conversion ratio) among groups (P < 0.05). Carcass and breast muscle weights were significantly increased, whereas abdominal fat was lowered in the supplemented groups (P < 0.05). In addition, dietary supplements improved phagocytic activity, intestinal and hepatic morphology, liver function-related enzymes (lower alanine aminotransferase-ALT, and aspartate aminotransferase-AST), serum lipid profile (lower low-density lipoproteins-LDL and triglycerides, along with higher high-density lipoproteins-HDL levels), and antioxidant enzyme activities (superoxide dismutase-SOD, glutathione peroxidase-GPx, and catalase-CAT), while lowering malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (P < 0.05) in birds fed the combination (1 g LYSO/kg diet + Vit E). The combination also stimulated significant upregulation of growth hormone receptor (GHR) and insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1), and immune response (Interleukins-, IL-10 and IL-1β) related genes (P < 0.05), while the lipogenic gene, ACC, was downregulated (P < 0.05). Dietary supplementation of feed additives increased meat-water holding capacity, tenderness, and color (P < 0.05), with no effect on meat pH. In conclusion, the combined supplementation of LYSO (1 g/kg diet) and vitamin E improved broiler growth, serum lipid profile and liver function, tissue morphology, immune function, antioxidant status, and meat quality, highlighting its potential as an effective nutritional approach in broiler production.

Introduction

Poultry production has experienced remarkable advancements in recent decades, with increasing demand for high-quality meat and eggs, which contribute significantly to the worldwide food supply (Nassar, 2026). Broiler production faces persistent challenges from rapid growth rates, high metabolic demands, environmental stressors, and the need to optimize feed efficiency while maintaining meat quality and health (Menshawy et al., 2021; Froebel et al., 2026). These challenges and the significant, intensive broiler production often increase their susceptibility to oxidative stress and reduce nutrient utilization efficiency, thereby lowering bird performance (Li et al., 2024). As a major challenge in the commercial poultry production sector, oxidative stress arises when reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelm the antioxidant defenses, resulting in damage to vital organic biomolecules such as polyunsaturated fatty acids, proteins, and DNA, and negatively affecting their growth, immunity, and meat quality (Surai et al., 2019). Consequently, dietary strategies that enhance antioxidant defense and improve nutrient digestibility and availability are critical for maintaining health and maximizing productivity (Surai et al., 2019; Hussein et al., 2025). Among these approaches, supplementation with antioxidants such as vitamin E and exogenous emulsifiers like lysolecithin (LYSO) has attracted considerable attention in intensive broiler production.

Vitamin E is a well-recognized essential fat-soluble vitamin for poultry, acting as a powerful antioxidant that interrupts the lipid peroxidation chain reaction and protects cellular membranes from oxidative damage, enhances immune function, and improves the bird's ability to cope with environmental and metabolic stressors (Pompeu et al., 2018; Surai et al., 2019). Dietary supplementation with vitamin E has been associated with improved growth performance, antioxidant defense, immune responses, and meat quality in broilers, and enhanced egg production and quality, fertility, and hatchability in laying hens and breeders (Abd El-Ghany, 2022).

Poultry diets often incorporate supplemental fat sources to meet birds' energy requirements, but ineffective lipid digestion and absorption, particularly in young birds with immature digestive systems, can lower nutrient utilization and increase oxidative stress, resulting in compromised bird performance (Oketch et al., 2023). Recently, LYSO attracted attention as one of the most powerful emulsifiers. LYSO, a lysophospholipid produced through the enzymatic hydrolysis of lecithin, enhances intestinal micelle formation, thus facilitating lipase activity through an increased surface area, which in turn enhances the digestibility of fats and other nutrients (El‐Katcha et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Several beneficial effects of LYSO supplementation in broilers have been documented, including improved performance, nutrient digestibility and utilization, intestinal morphological features, antioxidant status, lipid metabolism, and meat quality (Brautigan et al., 2017; Wealleans et al., 2020; Eid et al., 2025).

Despite the well-documented individual benefits of lysolecithin in enhancing lipid digestion and absorption and vitamin E as an antioxidant, information on their combined effects in broiler chickens is still limited, and the potential impacts on broiler growth, physiological parameters, and meat quality have not been examined. As a fat-soluble vitamin, vitamin E's bioavailability depends on effective lipid emulsification and micelle formation in the intestine. By enhancing fat digestion and absorption, lysolecithin could improve the uptake and tissue deposition of supplemented vitamin E, augmenting its antioxidant protection, while LYSO itself contributes to better energy utilization (Abdulla et al., 2019; Jaapar et al., 2020). We hypothesized that co-supplementation may provide a potential synergy and result in greater improvements in broiler productive performance and antioxidant defense compared to individual supplementation. Thus, this study was designed to investigate the potential effects of dietary vitamin E and/or LYSO supplementation on broiler growth, relative organ weights, serum biochemical changes, antioxidant status, intestinal and hepatic morphology, meat quality, and physiological functions related to gene expression.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The Experimental Animals Care Ethics Committee at Kafrelsheikh University, Kafrelsheikh, Egypt, gave its approval to this work (KFS-IACUC/333/2025), and it was carried out in compliance with the regulations set forth by the Egyptian university.

Birds’ management and study design

The experiment was conducted using two hundred and sixty-four-one-day-old broiler chicks (Avian 48), which were sourced from a commercial hatchery located in El-Gharbeia governorate, Egypt. Chicks were individually weighed, with 50.43 ± 0.31 g as the average body weight, then divided into six groups, each containing four replicates of 11 birds. Birds were assigned to six treatment groups: G1 received the basal diet (control); G2 received the basal diet supplemented with 200 mg vitamin E/kg diet; G3 and G4 received the basal diet supplemented with 0.5 and 1.0 g LYSO/kg diet, respectively, while G5 and G6 received the corresponding LYSO-supplemented diets combined with 200 mg vitamin E/kg diet. The basal diets (illustrated in Table 1) were formulated according to the nutrient requirements of the Avian 48 catalogue. Birds were fed the starter diet 1-10 days; the grower diet 11-22 days; and the finisher diet 23-35 days. The Lysoforte® lysophospholipid emulsifier (lysolecithin was the main active ingredient) was purchased from Kemin Europa NV in Herentals, Belgium. The experiment was conducted over 35 days in an environmentally controlled house. Birds were housed in floor pens measuring 1 m × 1.5 m (1.5 m² per pen), with an experimental stocking density of 0.136 m2 per bird of floor space. All birds had ad libitum access to feed and water supply, as each pen was equipped with a manual feeder and drinker. The heights of feeders and drinkers were adjusted with the birds' age to ensure adequate access. Chicks were brooded at 33°C at the start of the experiment, then the temperature was lowered by 3°C each week until it reached 23°C, which was maintained throughout the rest of the experimental period. The relative humidity was kept within a range of 50–70% during the experiment. The lighting program was set at 23 h light: 1 h dark during the first week, followed by 18 h light: 6 h dark for the remainder of the experiment. Throughout the rearing period, birds were subjected to veterinary-supervised health management procedures, including a vaccination program to protect birds against Newcastle disease, Gumboro disease, infectious bronchitis, and avian influenza. Birds were also monitored daily for clinical signs of illness. The mortality rate was within the normal physiological range (3-5%).

Growth performance evaluation

Average body weight gain (BWG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated using weekly records of birds’ body weight (BW) and feed consumption. FCR was computed using the formula FCR = feed consumed (g)/weight gain (g).

Sample collection

At the end of the experiment, 4 birds from each treatment (1 per replication) were randomly chosen, weighed, and used for carcass, blood, and tissue sampling. Whole blood samples (n=4 birds/group) were collected from the wing vein of each bird using heparinized syringes to evaluate phagocytic activity. For serum separation, sterile non-heparinized syringes were used to collect blood samples, which were then centrifuged at 2,500 xg and 4°C for 15 minutes. Serum samples were kept at -20°C for biochemical analysis. After blood sampling, birds were slaughtered, left to bleed completely, and eviscerated. The weight of the carcass, breast, thigh muscle, liver, spleen, abdominal fat, and heart was recorded and calculated relative to the live bird weight. After that, liver and jejunum specimens were taken from each bird, preserved in 10% formalin, and used for histological analysis. Another two liver samples were collected; one was kept in liquid nitrogen, then transferred to -80°C for gene expression, and the other was used for the assessment of the hepatic antioxidants. Also, breast muscle samples were collected and stored at 4°C before analysis of meat quality parameters.

Histomorphometry parameters of the intestine and liver

The jejunum and liver specimens (n=4 birds/group) were collected and preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h. Then, samples were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain according to Bancroft et al. (2013). The prepared tissue sections were examined microscopically for histopathological alterations using a light microscope (Leica DM500; Leica Microsystems, Japan). The morphometric parameters were measured in three villi per bird, and the number of microscopic fields was five. The intestinal morphometry measures, including villus height, villus width, crypt depth, and muscularis thickness, were determined using an automated image analysis system (ImageJ; Bethesda, MD, USA) (Schneider et al., 2012).

Serum biochemical parameters

Colorimetric assays (n=4 birds/group) were employed to determine serum total protein, albumin, globulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high- and low-density lipoproteins (HDL and LDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatinine using commercial kits supplied by Diamond Diagnostics, Cairo, Egypt, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hepatic antioxidants

Liver tissues (n=4 birds/group) were homogenized in a sterile cold potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7). The prepared homogenates were then utilized for the assessment of total antioxidant capacity (TAC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations according to El-Kassas et al. (2019).

Total RNA extraction and real-time qPCR

The hepatic RNA (n=4 birds/group) was extracted using Trizol (iNtRON Biotechnology). RNA quality was examined using 2% ethidium bromide-stained agarose gel. The mRNA complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Applied Biotech, Egypt) following the manufacturer's instructions. Real-time PCR system (Strat a gene MX300P Real-time PCR machine (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used for the assessment of the transcriptomic level of some growth, immune response, antioxidants, and fat metabolism-related genes using specific primer pairs listed in Table 2. A total reaction mixture of 20 μL was used, containing 10 μL of 2X SYBR Green Lo-Rox kit (Applied Biotech, Egypt), 2 μL of cDNA, and 0.5 μM of each primer. The thermocycler protocol was done according to El-Kassas et al. (2019) and a specific primer annealing temperature (Table 2) for 30 s. All real-time PCRs were tested in duplicate. The relative gene expression levels (fold change) were calculated using the method outlined by Livak and Schmittgen (2001). Determination of meat pH, color, tenderness, and water-holding capacity

The meat quality parameters were assessed 24 h post-mortem using an internal section of the left pectoral major muscle (n=4 birds/group). The pH of meat was measured using a pH meter (Bye model 6020, USA) (Park et al., 2021). The apparatus was calibrated using certified buffer solutions at pH 4.01 and 7.0. Meat extract color intensity was assessed according to Hunt (1991) and Mohamed et al. (2024). As summarized, 10 g of meat was mixed with 22.5 ml distilled water and shaken for 10 min under dark conditions. The absorbance of the resulting filtrate was then measured at 542 nm using a spectrophotometer. Meat tenderness and water-holding capacity (WHC) were assessed (El-Seesy, 2000; Mohamed et al., 2024). Approximately 0.3 g of minced meat was placed under Whatman No. 41 ashless filter paper and subjected to a 1-kg load for 10 min. Following compression, two clearly distinguishable zones appeared on the filter paper. Subsequently, the surface areas of these zones were measured using a planimeter. Tenderness was estimated by the surface area of the internal zone produced by the meat compression. The water-holding capacity was represented by the difference between the area of the inner and outer zones.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22, SPSS Inc., IL, USA). The cage served as the experimental unit for all statistical analyses. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Data normality and variance homogeneity were confirmed via the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. Graphical representations were prepared using GraphPad Prism 9 (©Graph Prism Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Results were presented as mean ± SE.

Results

Broiler performance and organs' relative weights

As illustrated in Table 3, dietary LYSO and vitamin E supplementation significantly affected final BW, WG, and FCR. Birds raised on the combined mixture of 1 g LYSO/kg diet + Vit. E (G6) achieved the highest final BW and WG and improved FCR compared to other groups (P < 0.05). Feed consumption was not significantly affected by dietary treatments (P > 0.05). The carcass characteristics, including the carcass, breast muscle, and abdominal fat relative weights, were significantly influenced by the dietary treatments (P < 0.05) (Table 4). Birds fed a 1 g LYSO/kg supplemented diet (G4) showed the highest carcass percentage compared to the control birds (G1), which recorded the lowest carcass yield. The highest breast muscle yield was observed in birds fed 0.5 g LYSO, 0.5 g LYSO + vitamin E, and 1 g LYSO + vitamin E (G3, G5, and G6), relative to the control birds (P < 0.05). Birds that received the supplemented diets exhibited a significantly lower abdominal fat percentage compared with the control, with the lowest fat % observed in those fed the combined mixture of 1 g LYSO + vitamin E (G6) (P < 0.05). Discussion

According to the current knowledge, this study is the first to report the combined effects of LYSO and vitamin E on broiler growth, physiological functions, and meat quality. The present study demonstrated significant improvements in birds' weight gain and FCR, particularly in those receiving 1 g LYSO + Vit E, which achieved the highest productive performance among groups. The enhanced bird performance observed in the current study was achieved without affecting feed intake, suggesting improved feed utilization rather than increased FI. These results agree with findings obtained by Ahmad et al. (2023); Boontiam et al. (2017); Movagharnejad et al. (2020); Zhang et al. (2025), who documented beneficial effects of LYSO supplementation on broiler growth performance. Regarding vitamin E, its effect on broiler growth is inconsistent across the literature. Some studies reported enhanced performance, particularly under stress conditions such as heat stress (Calik et al., 2022), when combined with other nutrients such as Se, vitamin C, and zinc (Beura et al., 2012; Hossain et al., 2025), or no effect on performance (Kim et al., 2010; Habibian et al., 2016; Sadiq et al., 2023). In the current study, vitamin E supplementation alone showed moderate improvements, but when added to the LYSO, birds achieved higher performance compared to the individual treatments. This result suggests a synergistic effect of the two supplements when combined. The observed improvements in growth were positively correlated with enhanced intestinal morphology and upregulated gene expression of the growth-related genes assessed in the present investigation. We hypothesized that the growth-stimulating effect observed may be attributed to the emulsifying action of LYSO, which could improve fat digestion, alongside oxidative stability via the vitamin E antioxidant effect; together, these would enhance nutrient utilization and increase energy availability for growth. In the same regard, lysolecithin stimulated higher expression of genes involved in amino acid transport and lipid metabolism, such as FABP1 and CD36, enhancing nutrient absorption and growth (Zhang et al., 2022; Van Hoeck et al., 2025). In support, LYSO supplementation was associated with enhanced intestinal structure and nutrient utilization, resulting in better feed efficiency and overall growth (Wealleans et al., 2020; Haetinger et al., 2021; Eid et al., 2025). Addressing the digestibility of different nutrients and digestive enzyme levels in response to this experimental design is recommended in further studies to confirm the positive effects of both supplements combined.

In the present study, LYSO dietary addition (0.5-1 g/kg of diet), particularly when combined with vitamin E, modulated carcass traits, demonstrated by increased breast muscle yield and lower abdominal fat content. These improvements associated with additive supplementation would be linked to enhanced nutrient utilization, which was redirected toward lean tissue deposition instead of being utilized for fat deposition and consequently improved carcass traits. This interpretation requires future digestibility trials and carcass composition analysis.

Earlier studies documented similar responses with LYSO supplementation (Movagharnejad et al., 2020; Eid et al., 2025; Fayed et al., 2026). These benefits would be associated with the emulsifying action of LYSO, enhancing nutrient digestion and absorption, including fat and protein, as well as improved intestinal morphology (Boontiam et al., 2017; Fayed et al., 2026), as confirmed in the current study, which facilitates better nutrient absorption, including fat-soluble vitamins, and contributes to improved carcass traits. In previous studies, vitamin E supplementation had insignificant effects on carcass yield or weight of different body cuts (Rayani and Mutia, 2017; Pečjak et al., 2022), whereas other reports showed improvements when combined with other nutrients such as Se under heat stress (Bora et al., 2024). Similarly, Li et al. (2009) and Zhang et al. (2021) reported that dietary addition of vitamin E lowered abdominal fat content in broiler chickens. The lower fat deposition in the current study could also be linked to the down-regulation of ACC gene expression obtained in birds fed 1 g LYSO or those that received the combined mixture of LYSO + vitamin E. The lower abdominal fat in the supplemented groups was reflected in enhanced carcass characteristics, which is advantageous where leaner carcasses are highly desirable in commercial poultry production. The synergistic effects of LYSO and vitamin E on breast muscle yield need further investigation of other pathways regulating protein synthesis.

To understand the mechanism behind the improvements in growth response and lower abdominal fat content, we assessed the effects of our experimental design on genes regulating growth and lipid metabolism. In the current study, dietary supplements, vitamin E and/or LYSO, were associated with higher expression of the growth-regulating genes GHR and IGF-1, with the highest expression found in birds receiving both supplements combined. In support, Zhang et al. (2022) reported that lysolecithin supplementation in broilers upregulated the expression of genes involved in fatty acid transport (CD36, and FABP1), amino acid transport (CAT1, y+, LAT1), and growth regulation (IGF1, GH, MYOD1, and MYOG), indicating a growth-promoting effect of LYSO. Also, Khalifa et al. (2021) documented upregulation of these genes with vitamin E and Se supplementation in broilers, attributing this response to their antioxidant effects, which was confirmed in several studies (Kumbhar et al., 2018; Niu et al., 2018). The obtained result would partially explain the enhanced growth in these groups through the combined effect of both supplements, which stimulated higher expression of GHR and IGF.

Regarding the fat metabolism-regulating genes, vitamin E and/or LYSO supplementation were associated with reduced expression of ACC, which is the key regulatory enzyme controlling fatty acid synthesis. This result suggested that these supplements modulate fat deposition inside the body through altering the transcriptional regulation of lipogenic genes, particularly ACC, which was more sensitive to our experimental design than FAS. Aligning with the obtained result, Wang et al. (2024) found that emulsifier (lecithin and lysolecithin mixture) addition resulted in lower mRNA expression levels of FAS and ACC in broiler liver. Supporting the obtained results, Zhang et al. (2021) found that the addition of vitamin E to the broiler diet was associated with suppression of lipid synthesis regulators, including FAS, acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha, and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 1. The present study assessed only two lipogenesis-related genes, ACC and FAS; thus, future research should expand the molecular analysis to include genes regulating fatty acid oxidation and lipid transport to better understand the mechanism by which LYSO+ vitamin E co-supplementation influences abdominal fat deposition.

Intestinal morphology serves as an important indicator of gut health in broilers. The effect of vitamin E and/or LYSO supplementation extended to intestinal morphology, which demonstrated significant improvements in all assessed morphological parameters, including villus height and width, crypt depth, and muscularis thickness. These results were clear in birds that received both supplements in combination, which were correlated with enhanced overall growth performance. The beneficial effects observed in response to LYSO dietary addition could be associated with its stimulatory effect on jejunal epithelium genes, particularly those related to collagen, extracellular matrix, and integrins, leading to enhanced collagen deposition and villus length (Brautigan et al., 2017), upregulated genes like claudin-3, which is associated with intestinal health (Chen et al., 2019), genes related to intestinal health and development in broilers (Li et al., 2022). Also, it could be linked to the antibacterial effect of LYSO, positively affecting the gut microbiota, favoring beneficial ones, supporting intestinal morphology, and enhancing gut function. In the same regard, Salari et al. (2024) found that LYSO supplementation was accompanied by a higher Lactobacillus population in the caeca. As shown, vitamin E supplementation was also associated with enhanced intestinal morphology, particularly when combined with LYSO, suggesting that it improves the beneficial effect of LYSO or acts as a complementary compound through its antioxidant effect, reducing oxidative stress on intestinal epithelium. Vitamin E contributes significantly to maintaining the integrity and function of intestinal epithelial cells by enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, alleviating oxidative stress, and modulating inflammatory responses (Pitargue et al., 2019; Korkmaz, 2023). Measurement of tight junction proteins in the intestine and nutrient transporters such as GLUT2, PEPT1, SGLT1, and amino acid transporters would help in understanding the observed morphological improvements in response to our experimental design.

Considering the biochemical parameters, vitamin E and/or LYSO addition was associated with enhanced liver function (lower levels of liver function-related enzymes) and lipid profile demonstrated by lower serum triglycerides and LDL, along with higher HDL levels and enhanced antioxidant status. The lack of changes in serum concentrations of total protein, albumin, globulin, and creatinine suggested that dietary treatments don’t compromise protein synthesis or kidney function.

As intracellular enzymes, AST and ALT are liberated into the bloodstream when hepatocytes are damaged or cellular membrane permeability is altered; therefore, their elevated serum activities are widely considered indicators of liver injury (Elkhaiat et al., 2025). The observed decline in AST and ALT activities, particularly in birds raised on both supplements, may reflect potential hepatoprotective effects of the co-supplements, which contributed to maintaining hepatocellular membrane integrity by lowering oxidative stress. Supporting this finding was the absence of histopathological alterations in the liver tissue of birds fed the combined mixture, together with the enhanced antioxidant status assessed in the current study.

As observed, vitamin E addition to the LYSO-containing diet lowered enzyme activity compared to single LYSO supplementation. We hypothesized that LYSO, with its emulsifying properties, enhances lipid digestion and absorption and, in turn, improves the bioavailability of dietary vitamin E (a fat-soluble vitamin), thereby potentiating its antioxidant and membrane-stabilizing effects in the liver, improving overall health. This result is consistent with Cai et al. (2024); Eid et al. (2025) with lysolecithin or lysophospholipid supplementation, and Khalifa et al. (2021) when vitamin E was added to the broiler diet. In the current study, liver tissue was evaluated only descriptively. The absence of quantitative hepatocyte morphometry and sinusoidal area measurements limits definitive conclusions regarding hepatic structural integrity. Therefore, these findings should be confirmed in future studies using appropriate morphometric analyses.

Regarding the serum lipid profile, lower triglyceride and LDL levels in all supplemented groups, along with higher HDL levels, reflect the role of these supplements in regulating lipid metabolism and transport inside the body. Superior improvements in the lipid profile were obtained in birds fed the combined mixture of LYSO with vitamin E compared to the addition of each supplement alone. The enhanced response with lysolecithin could be associated with enhanced lipid digestion, absorption, and metabolism, resulting in favorable changes in the lipid profile (Zhang et al., 2025). Also, it could be linked to its effect on lipid metabolism-regulating genes, as reported by the upregulation of genes associated with intestinal fatty acid transport (Cd36, Fatp4, Dgat2, Apob, and Mttp), downregulation of hepatic lipogenic genes (FAS and ACC), and increasing expression of genes involved in hepatic triglyceride synthesis, transport, and breakdown in broilers (Wang et al., 2024). The present results are consistent with those of Mohiti-Asli et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2025). The mechanism by which vitamin E influences lipid metabolism could be related to its antioxidant function, which protects cell membranes from oxidative damage and maintains lipid stability (Pompeu et al., 2018). The observed improvements in the serum lipid profile are consistent with the previously mentioned reduction in abdominal fat percentage and the downregulation of ACC gene expression recorded in the current study. These results suggest that both supplements, particularly when combined, affect lipid metabolism by suppressing de novo lipogenesis through downregulation of ACC expression, while promoting the utilization or clearance of circulating triglycerides, which results in lower lipid accumulation, particularly abdominal fat. Overall, the hepatoprotective effects, as indicated by lower ALT and AST activities and an improved lipid profile, suggest that LYSO and vitamin E supplementation may reduce the risk of metabolic disorders, support liver function, and improve the overall health of broilers.

In addition, LYSO and/or vitamin E supplementation were associated with significantly improved innate immune response. This finding was confirmed by higher phagocytic activity across the supplemented groups, with the highest activity observed in birds that received 1 g/kg LYSO + vitamin E, along with upregulation of genes linked to immune response (IL-10 and IL-1β), suggesting an immunostimulatory effect. According to Konjufca et al. (2004), supplemental vitamin E enhances Fc-receptor-mediated phagocytic activity of macrophages in young broilers and modulates both humoral and cellular immune responses. Also, Lee et al. (2020) reported that lysophosphatidylcholine improves bactericidal activity by stimulating phagosome maturation through activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in Salmonella-infected mouse macrophages. In the same context, vitamin E exerts immunomodulatory effects by regulating cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways and influencing the production of immune mediators, including prostaglandins and leukotrienes (Leshchinsky and Klasing, 2003), enhancing both innate and cellular immune functions in poultry (Perez-Carbajal et al., 2010). On the other hand, lysolecithin positively affects the immune response in poultry by reducing key inflammatory markers (interleukin-6 and endotoxin levels) (Cai et al., 2024), enhancing antioxidant capacities (Eid et al., 2025), and reducing proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 (Boontiam et al., 2017). Also, promoting beneficial morphological changes that enhance gut structure and the physical barriers against pathogens; supporting nutrient absorption (Brautigan et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022); and upregulating immune-related genes such as IL8-like 2 (Cloft et al., 2021). Overall, the beneficial effects of both supplements, especially when combined, could partially explain their role in enhancing birds' innate immune response.

Antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, catalase, and GPx, represent the primary enzymatic defense against ROS, while MDA is a well-recognized biomarker of lipid peroxidation (Surai et al., 2019). The hepatic antioxidants assessed in the current study were improved, as shown by higher levels of the antioxidants (TAC, GPx, CAT, and SOD) and higher expression of antioxidant-related genes (SOD, catalase, and GPx). These changes were accompanied by lower MDA levels, indicating enhanced antioxidant status, with the strongest effects observed when both supplements were provided together. Similarly, Eid et al. (2025) documented that the incorporation of 0.5 or 1 g LPL/ kg diet stimulated higher phagocytic activity and index and improved antioxidant enzymes as well as mitigated the immunosuppressive effects of dexamethasone-induced oxidative stress in broiler chickens. Similar improvements in the antioxidant status with lysolecithin or lysophospholipid supplementation were also reported (Ani et al., 2024; Fayed et al., 2026). This could be linked to the emulsifying effect of LYSO, helping to improve fat utilization, leading to more efficient energy metabolism and reduction of oxidizable lipid accumulation within tissues, thereby lowering oxidative burden and lipid peroxidation. Also, it may be related to the powerful antioxidant effect of vitamin E, and its ability to scavenge free radicals and attenuate lipid peroxidation (Pompeu et al., 2018). Supporting these findings, Niu et al. (2018); Elgendey et al. (2022); Oni et al. (2024) reported that vitamin E, alone or combined with selenium, has been shown to enhance antioxidant defenses in broilers by increasing antioxidant enzyme activities, reducing lipid peroxidation, and upregulating antioxidant-related gene expression, thereby improving oxidative status and meat quality. Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 (Nrf2) plays a central role in regulating the antioxidant defense system, acting at sites of free radical generation and promoting the expression of primary antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, GPx, and catalase (Surai et al., 2019). The obtained result suggested that the combination of both supplements may activate Nrf2, which consequently resulted in transcriptional upregulation of antioxidants. The current study did not examine Nrf2 expression, which would have provided direct evidence for the contribution of this pathway.

Additionally, the current study demonstrated improvements in breast meat quality (WHC, tenderness, and color intensity), with the most pronounced effects observed in birds receiving both supplements combined, particularly at 1 g/kg LYSO + vitamin E and the vitamin E-supplemented group. These changes could be linked to the positive effects of both supplements on lipid metabolism and antioxidant status demonstrated by increased activities of SOD, GPx, and CAT and their expression levels and lowered lipid peroxidation mentioned earlier. We hypothesized that this enhanced antioxidant status improves cellular integrity, reduces lipid oxidation, and decreases pigment oxidation, thus protecting both lipid and protein components of the muscle cell from oxidative changes, so maintaining the physical structure and enhancing the muscle’s ability to retain water, tenderness, and color. Supporting this, Niu et al. (2018) stated that vitamin E enhanced meat quality in broilers by upregulating genes involved in antioxidant enzymes. Consistent with these findings, previous reports found that Se and vitamin E supplementation significantly improved WHC of breast meat through their antioxidant protection (Albergamo et al., 2022), was effective in preventing the deterioration of meat quality in broilers subjected to chronic heat stress when supplemented alone (200 mg/kg) (Hashizawa et al., 2013), and improved lipid oxidative stability via increasing VE retention in broiler muscles (Xu et al., 2021). In the same regard, Khatun et al. (2020) reported improvements in meat redness with vitamin E supplementation attributed to its ability to reduce lipid oxidation, which indirectly retards myoglobin oxidation and protects muscle tissues from oxidative damage. Similar improvements in meat quality were also reported by Shen et al. (2021) and Eid et al. (2025) when lysophospholipids and lecithin were added to the broiler diet, which could be linked to enhanced lipid metabolism. In addition, the lack of treatment effects on muscle pH suggested that these additives didn’t significantly affect the glycolytic metabolism of muscle postmortem, which is consistent with (Niu et al., 2018; Vieira et al., 2021).

The current experiment was limited in some points regarding the meat quality evaluation. We didn’t measure the internationally accepted color parameters L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness), which would give more detailed information on the effects of the dietary treatments on meat color characteristics. Also, the meat quality parameters were assessed at only one postmortem time point, which limited the ability to identify the whole postmortem muscle changes, so evaluating these parameters at different intervals post-slaughter should be considered in further studies. Although we found improvements in WHC, tenderness and color, we didn’t measure the oxidative stability markers such as TBARS (for lipid oxidation) and carbonyl content (for protein oxidation) or the vitamin E concentration in the muscle tissue. These measurements would have been significant in supporting the proposed antioxidant mechanism and linking dietary vitamin E supplementation, its accumulation, and enhanced oxidative stability in meat.

Conclusion

Based on the previously mentioned results, dietary vitamin E and lysolecithin supplementation represent a valuable nutritional approach for enhancing broiler growth, health status, and meat quality. The combination of 1 g/kg LPL and vitamin E achieved the most pronounced effects, demonstrating combined benefits that exceeded their individual supplementation. This combination enhanced growth and carcass traits, intestinal and hepatic morphology, improved lipid profile, boosted phagocytic activity and hepatic antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx, and CAT), upregulated antioxidant and immune gene expression (IL-10, IL-1β), and meat quality parameters without affecting meat pH or serum protein and renal markers. Future studies should assess the efficacy of this combination against environmental stressors, such as heat stress.
