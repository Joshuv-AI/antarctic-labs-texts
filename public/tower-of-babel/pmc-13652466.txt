Retroperitoneal necrotizing fasciitis masquerading as perforated viscus: a rare case report

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Necrotizing fasciitis is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection associated with high mortality, particularly when diagnosis is delayed. While most cases involve the extremities or perineum, retroperitoneal necrotizing fasciitis is exceedingly rare. Heightened clinical suspicion, early imaging, and prompt surgical management are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare but aggressive soft-tissue infection with high mortality, accounting for a small fraction of severe bacterial infections yet causing rapid tissue destruction when diagnosis or treatment is delayed. Most cases arise in the extremities, perineum, or trunk, where early cutaneous signs help prompt recognition[1,2]. While the overall incidence of NF is estimated to be approximately 500 to 1000 cases per year, retroperitoneal necrotizing fasciitis (RNF) represents an even rarer variant in which the deep location obscures early clinical findings and often leads to misdiagnosis as more common intra-abdominal conditions. Its nonspecific presentation, rapid progression, and limited external clues contribute to significant delays in intervention and poor outcomes[2,3].

We describe a case of fulminant RNF that initially presented with features suggestive of acute appendicitis, rapidly evolved despite early surgical intervention, and ultimately resulted in multiorgan failure. This case highlights the need for heightened clinical suspicion, early imaging, and aggressive surgical management when evaluating atypical presentations of abdominal pain. This case report has been written in compliance with the SCARE guidelines[4].

Case presentation

A 40-year-old male patient presented with a 2-day history of right-sided abdominal pain associated with low-grade fever, generalized headache, and loss of appetite. He was a resident of a malaria-endemic area and had previously been treated for malaria. With the current symptoms, he was initially evaluated at a local health center, where he was empirically started on artemether-lumefantrine for presumed malaria based on his residence in an endemic area and prior history. His past medical history was otherwise notable for hemorrhoidal disease, for which he had been using a topical pilocaine cream. An abdominal ultrasound was also performed at the same local health center as part of the initial evaluation.

The initial abdominal ultrasound performed at the local health center was largely unremarkable regarding solid organ pathology. The key positive finding was marked probe tenderness in the right lower quadrant with associated guarding, while visualization of the appendix was technically limited due to overlying cecal gas. No intra-abdominal mass or free fluid collection was identified. Based on the localized right lower quadrant tenderness with inconclusive visualization of the appendix, acute appendicitis or another acute abdominal pathology was suspected, and abdominal computed tomography was recommended.

The patient was subsequently referred to our hospital for further evaluation. On arrival, he appeared acutely ill and in significant pain. His vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 100/70 mmHg; pulse rate, 90 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 20 breaths per minute; and temperature, 37.9 °C. Abdominal examination revealed direct tenderness over the right lower quadrant without guarding, rigidity, or rebound tenderness. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Initial laboratory investigations showed an increased white blood cell count of 13 400/µL with a neutrophil predominance of 88%. Other routine laboratory tests were within normal limits at presentation (Table 1). A repeat abdominal ultrasound demonstrated inflammatory changes in the right lower quadrant with non-visualization of the appendix, again prompting the need for abdominal computed tomography. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis was performed without oral contrast. The scan revealed extensive air within the retroperitoneum and pelvic cavity, with a mild free fluid collection in the pelvis. Air was also seen dissecting along the intermuscular fascial planes of the left gluteal region. No definite collection was identified within the ischiorectal fossa, supralevator space, or other perirectal compartments at the time of imaging. Intramural air was noted within the cecum. The appendix was of normal caliber with no surrounding inflammatory changes. The liver, spleen, pancreas, gallbladder, kidneys, and adrenal glands appeared normal. There was no biliary duct dilatation, evidence of bowel wall thickening, or intra-abdominal or pelvic mass. The lung bases and osseous structures were unremarkable. The radiologic impression was pneumoretroperitoneum with a mild pelvic fluid collection and pneumatosis intestinalis of the cecum, raising concern for a retroperitoneal perforation of the cecum (Fig. 1). While under evaluation, the patient’s clinical condition deteriorated. His vital signs at that time were a blood pressure of 85/60 mmHg, a pulse rate of 120 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 28 breaths per minute, and a temperature of 38.2 °C. On repeat abdominal examination, he developed guarding and rigidity consistent with generalized peritonitis. A diagnosis of a perforated hollow viscus with secondary peritonitis was made, and the patient was taken urgently to the operating room.

An emergency exploratory laparotomy under general anesthesia was performed through a midline incision. Upon entering the peritoneal cavity, a moderate amount of reactive fluid was encountered. The omentum was densely wrapped around the hepatic flexure and the right side of the abdominal wall. The cecum and ascending colon appeared hyperemic; however, no obvious perforation was identified despite thorough milking and mobilization of the right colon, including the hepatic flexure. Given the presence of extensive retroperitoneal air on preoperative imaging and the absence of an identifiable bowel source, the retroperitoneum was explored. Upon exploration of the retroperitoneum, a gush of foul-smelling, dishwater-like fluid was encountered, and extensive necrosis of the bilateral abdominal wall fascia was noted. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes along the psoas region were also noted. Copious irrigation and debridement were performed, and a drainage tube was placed in the right retroperitoneal space. No intraoperative tissue or fluid cultures were obtained. The abdomen was closed in layers. Estimated blood loss was approximately 100 mL (Fig. 2). Postoperatively, the patient was started on broad-spectrum antibiotics, including ceftriaxone, vancomycin, metronidazole, and clindamycin. Blood cultures and intraoperative tissue and pus cultures were not obtained; therefore, antimicrobial therapy remained empiric throughout the patient’s clinical course. The patient remained clinically stable immediately after surgery. However, on the second postoperative day, he developed new-onset perineal pain and progressive swelling. Given these concerns, he underwent repeat surgical exploration in the dorsal lithotomy position under spinal anesthesia. Approximately 200 mL of thick pus was evacuated from the right ischiorectal fossa, with extension into the supralevator space. A cruciate incision was created, multiple loculations were disrupted, and the cavity was irrigated with hydrogen peroxide and normal saline before being left open for continued drainage.

Despite initial operative management and targeted drainage procedures, laboratory evaluation demonstrated evolving multiorgan dysfunction, including acute kidney injury with a creatinine of 2.6 mg/dL and blood urea nitrogen of 70 mg/dL, hyperbilirubinemia with a total bilirubin of 2.0 mg/dL and direct bilirubin of 1.8 mg/dL, and biochemical evidence of acute pancreatitis with serum amylase of 272 U/L and lipase of 471 U/L.

During this period, he also began experiencing progressively worsening shortness of breath with tachypnea and oxygen saturation falling to 80% on room air. A chest radiograph revealed a right-sided pleural effusion, further indicating systemic deterioration. These findings were accompanied by escalating hemodynamic instability, prompting transfer to the intensive care unit for management of early multiorgan failure. In the intensive care unit, the patient required vasopressor support for persistent hypotension and mechanical ventilation for worsening respiratory failure.

Despite maximal supportive care, including broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, aggressive fluid resuscitation, vasopressor support, mechanical ventilation, and repeated surgical source control, the patient demonstrated persistent clinical deterioration with worsening septic physiology and progressive multiorgan dysfunction. He ultimately succumbed to refractory septic shock and multiorgan failure on the sixth postoperative day following the initial laparotomy (Fig. 3). This clinical course highlights the fulminant nature of RNF and its potential for rapid systemic deterioration, even with timely surgical and medical management. Discussion

NF is a life-threatening, soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid necrosis of fascial planes and overlying soft tissues. RNF is an exceptionally rare subset of NF, with only sporadic cases reported in the literature. While the overall incidence of NF is estimated to be between 500 and 1000 cases per year globally, RNF represents a very small proportion of these cases, reflecting both its rarity and the diagnostic challenges posed by its deep anatomical location. The mortality associated with RNF is high, particularly in cases with delayed diagnosis or extensive disease at presentation[1–3].

RNF often presents with nonspecific symptoms, including abdominal, flank, or back pain, low-grade fever, and malaise, which may mimic common intra-abdominal pathologies such as appendicitis, diverticulitis, or perforated viscus[1]. In our patient, the initial presentation with right lower quadrant pain, low-grade fever, and anorexia closely resembled acute appendicitis.

The pathogenesis of primary RNF is not always clear. Known predisposing factors include immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure, malignancy, and trauma, although cases without identifiable risk factors have been reported[5]. In many RNF cases, secondary retroperitoneal infection originates from perforated viscera, pancreatitis, urinary tract infection, or perineal abscess[6]. Our patient had no significant comorbidities, recent trauma, or identifiable primary source, making a primary retroperitoneal origin the most likely explanation. However, given the subsequent development of an ischiorectal and supralevator abscess in the setting of underlying hemorrhoidal disease, an occult anorectal source cannot be completely excluded. The subsequent development of ischiorectal and supralevator abscesses may represent either progression of the initial infection along deep fascial planes or delayed manifestation of an unrecognized anorectal focus, both of which are recognized patterns of disease spread in NF.

Given the diagnostic difficulties associated with NF, the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Infection (LRINEC) score was introduced as a laboratory tool that assigns points to six variables, including CRP levels above 150 mg/L, white blood cell counts over 15 000/µL, hemoglobin values below 13.5 g/dL, sodium levels under 135 mmol/L, serum creatinine above 1.6 mg/dL, and glucose levels exceeding 180 mg/dL. In its initial evaluation, a score of 8 or more corresponded to an estimated 75% probability of a necrotizing soft-tissue infection, and the negative predictive value was reported as 96%. Subsequent external studies, however, showed that the LRINEC score has limited sensitivity, which reduces its reliability for early identification and supports its use only as an adjunct rather than a standalone diagnostic measure[7].

Imaging is central to the early recognition of RNF, particularly because clinical signs may be nonspecific, as seen in our patient. CT is generally preferred in the acute setting due to its ability to demonstrate retroperitoneal gas, asymmetric fascial thickening, fluid collections, and diffuse soft-tissue edema, with high sensitivity and a strong negative predictive value[8]. MRI provides even greater sensitivity for fascial involvement and can delineate the extent of deep tissue disease, though it may overestimate involvement and is often less practical in unstable patients[7,9]. Ultrasound, although limited in deep infections, can distinguish cellulitis from necrotizing disease through findings such as deep fascial fluid accumulation[7,10]. In our case, CT was crucial in identifying retroperitoneal gas and extensive fascial involvement, guiding the decision for urgent surgical exploration and enabling timely management despite the absence of early external manifestations.

Definitive management of RNF hinges on early, aggressive, and repeated surgical debridement, combined with broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting both aerobic and anaerobic organisms. Intraoperative findings in our case included extensive bilateral necrosis of the abdominal wall fascia and dishwater-like fluid within the retroperitoneum, which are pathognomonic features of necrotizing infection[7,11]. In our case, generalized peritonitis and extensive retroperitoneal air prompted urgent laparotomy for suspected perforated viscus. Although no perforation was identified, exploration established the diagnosis of RNF and allowed immediate debridement and drainage. However, severe sepsis and progressive multiorgan dysfunction limited further surgical intervention. This case reinforces how easily RNF can be overlooked in its early stages and how rapidly it can progress despite initially nonspecific findings, emphasizing the importance of timely CT, surgical exploration, staged drainage, and vigilant postoperative monitoring.

A limitation of this report is the absence of microbiological culture data, including blood and tissue cultures, which precluded identification of the causative pathogen and assessment of antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. Consequently, antibiotic therapy remained empiric throughout the patient’s clinical course.

Conclusion

RNF is a rare, rapidly progressive infection that may present with subtle early findings. Early recognition, prompt aggressive surgical management, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and vigilant postoperative monitoring are essential, although severe cases may progress to sepsis and multiorgan failure despite appropriate treatment.
