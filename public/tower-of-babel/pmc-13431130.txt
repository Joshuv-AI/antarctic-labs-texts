Safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of nicarbazin (NICACOX®) for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying (Huvepharma N.V.)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Roberto Edoardo Villa, Giovanna Azimonti, Eleftherios Bonos, Henrik Christensen, Mojca Durjava, Birgit Dusemund, Ronette Gehring, Boet Glandorf, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Francesca Marcon, Giovanna Martelli, Carlo Nebbia, Alena Pechová, Miguel Prieto‐Maradona, Katerina Theodoridou, Georges Bories, Paul Brantom, Jürgen Gropp, Guido Rychen, Luca Tosti, Ruud Woutersen, Jaume Galobart, Fabiola Pizzo, Alberto Navarro‐Villa, Maria Vittoria Vettori, José Cortiñas Abrahantes, Orsolya Holczknecht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of nicarbazin (NICACOX®) as a coccidiostat for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying. The FEEDAP Panel concluded that NICACOX® is safe for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying when used at concentrations up to 80 mg nicarbazin/kg complete feed during the first 21 days of life. The use of the additive under the proposed conditions (maximum use level of 80 mg/kg complete feed for 21 days) is considered safe for the consumer, provided that a withdrawal period of 5 days is ensured. Nicarbazin is not a skin nor eye irritant and is not a dermal sensitiser. In the absence of specific data on the final additive, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of NICACOX® for the users. The use of nicarbazin (DNC and HDP) from NICACOX® in complete feed for chickens for fattening does not pose a risk for the terrestrial and aquatic compartments nor to sediment under the proposed condition of use. No concern for groundwater is expected, and no risk of secondary poisoning is identified. Nicarbazin from NICACOX®, given at a level of 75 mg/kg complete feed for the first 21 days of life is effective in preventing coccidiosis of chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying in the corresponding time period.

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference

Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003 1 establishes the rules governing the Community authorisation of additives for use in animal nutrition. In particular, Article 4(1) of that Regulation lays down that any person seeking authorisation for a feed additive or for a new use of feed additive shall submit an application in accordance with Article 7.

The European Commission received a request from Huvepharma N.V. 2 for the authorisation of the additive consisting of nicarbazin (NICACOX®), when used as a feed additive for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying (category: coccidiostats and histomonostats).

According to Article 7(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, the Commission forwarded the application to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) as an application under Article 4(1) (authorisation of a feed additive or new use of a feed additive). The dossier was received on 19 April 2021 and the general information and supporting documentation are available at https://open.efsa.europa.eu/questions/EFSA‐Q‐2021‐00259. The particulars and documents in support of the application were considered valid by EFSA as of 4 August 2021.

According to Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, EFSA, after verifying the particulars and documents submitted by the applicant, shall undertake an assessment in order to determine whether the feed additive complies with the conditions laid down in Article 5. EFSA shall deliver an opinion on the safety for the target animals, consumer, user and the environment and on the efficacy of the feed additive consisting of nicarbazin (NICACOX®), when used under the proposed conditions of use (see Section 3.1.3 ).

Additional information

The additive consisting of nicarbazin (NICACOX®) is not currently authorised as a feed additive in the European Union. The same active substance from the same applicant is approved in combination with monensin sodium (Monimax®) as a feed additive for use in turkeys for fattening, chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying. 3

The Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP) adopted a series of opinions on the safety and efficacy of feed additives from the same applicant containing nicarbazin: Monimax® for turkeys for fattening (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a, 2019a), Monimax® for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018a, 2019a), Coxar® for turkeys (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018b, 2021, 2022).

Maximum residue limits (MRLs) are in force for nicarbazin (dinitrocarbanilide (DNC) as the marker residue) in chicken tissues: 15,000 μg DNC/kg of fresh liver, 6000 μg DNC/kg of fresh kidney, 4000 μg DNC/kg of fresh muscle and 4000 μg DNC/kg of fresh skin+fat.

DATA AND METHODOLOGIES

Data

The present assessment is based on data submitted by the applicant in the form of a technical dossier 4 in support of the authorisation request for the use of nicarbazin (NICACOX®) as a feed additive.

The FEEDAP Panel used the data provided by the applicant together with data from other sources, such as previous risk assessments by EFSA or other expert bodies, peer‐reviewed scientific papers, other scientific reports and experts' elicitation knowledge, to deliver the present output.

The European Union Reference Laboratory (EURL) considered that the conclusions and recommendations reached in the previous assessment regarding the methods used for the control of the nicarbazin in animal feed/marker residue in tissues are valid and applicable for the current application. 5

Methodologies

The approach followed by the FEEDAP Panel to assess the safety and the efficacy of nicarbazin (NICACOX®) is in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 429/2008 6 and the relevant guidance documents: Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the consumer (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017b); Guidance on the identity, characterisation and conditions of use of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017c); Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the target species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017d); Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the environment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2019b); Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the users (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023); and Guidance on the assessment of the efficacy of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2024).

Conditions of use

The additive is intended for use in feed for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying up to 21 days of age, at a proposed minimum concentration of 75 mg/kg and maximum concentration of 80 mg nicarbazin/kg complete feed, with a withdrawal time of 1 day before slaughter.

The additive should be incorporated in compound feed in a premixture and not be mixed with other coccidiostats.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that maximum residue limits (MRLs) are in force for nicarbazin (dinitrocarbanilide (DNC) as the marker residue) in chicken tissues: 15,000 μg DNC/kg of fresh liver, 6000 μg DNC/kg of fresh kidney, 4000 μg DNC/kg of fresh muscle and fresh skin+fat.

Safety

Safety for the target species

Tolerance studies

The applicant submitted two tolerance studies in chickens for fattening. The first study, 12 due to limitations in the design and conduct of the study (additive concentrations below the intended levels and the absence of haematology and clinical biochemistry data), the FEEDAP Panel did not consider this study for the assessment of safety for the target species.

In a second tolerance study, 13 a total of 1408 1‐day‐old male Ross 308 chickens were distributed in pens and randomly allocated to four groups (16 pens/group; 22 birds/pen). Birds underwent a two‐phase feeding programme (starter 1–21 days; grower 21–28 days). The diets were based on corn, wheat, soybean meal and soy oil. 14 The starter experimental diets were either not supplemented (control) or supplemented with NICACOX® to provide 75 (0.9× maximum use level), 80 (1×), and 90 (1.125×) mg of nicarbazin/kg complete feed in the first 21 days (confirmed by analysis; Table 3). Feeds were offered as mash, and birds had ad libitum access to feed and water. Mortality and general health were monitored daily and dead or culled birds were weighted and necropsied. Body weight and feed intake were recorded at 1, 10, 21 and 28 days per pen basis. Average weight gain and feed to gain ratio were calculated. On days 21 and 28, blood samples were randomly taken from two birds per pen (32 birds/treatment) for routine haematology and clinical blood chemistry. 15 At the same time, another random bird per replicate (16 birds/treatment) was killed and subjected to necropsy; selected organs 16 were examined, weighed and samples stored for histopathological examination (when organs were affected).

The data were statistically analysed as a completely randomised design by generalised linear model (GLM) with the pen as the experimental unit for the performance and non‐productive parameters (i.e. relative organ weights and blood endpoints) and mortality. Group comparisons were performed with Tukey's post hoc test. In addition, for growth and feed consumption, a non‐inferiority test was performed between the 1.125× and the other treatment groups (including control) for the periods 1–21, 22–28 and 1–28 days. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Mortality including culling did not differ between treatment groups during either the treatment or withdrawal periods. Birds in control group grew up to 83% (day 21) and 86% (day 28) of the expected performance objectives at the corresponding age. Birds receiving NICACOX® showed growth performance comparable to or higher than the control group, and non‐inferiority of the 1.125× group to the control was demonstrated for weight gain and feed intake over both the 1‐ to 21‐day and 1‐ to 28‐day periods.

At days 21 and 28, some statistically significant differences were observed in selected haematological and clinical chemistry parameters and in relative organ weights. The magnitude of these differences was generally small, not dose‐related and therefore not considered biologically relevant or adverse. No organ lesions were detected.

Overall, the results indicate that nicarbazin was tolerated at the highest dose tested (1.125× the maximum recommended level) when administered during the first 21 days of life. Therefore, the Panel concludes that the use of nicarbazin at the maximum proposed use level of 80 mg/kg complete feed for 21 days is safe for chickens for fattening.

Literature search on the safety of nicarbazin for the target animals

The applicant submitted a literature search on the potential safety issues associated with the use of nicarbazin in chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying. 17 The search was carried out on PUBMED and CAB databases and focused on the potential toxicity of nicarbazin towards pancreas and intestinal development or adverse effects on animals under heat stress. None of the 17 publications retrieved reported specific pathology or morphological findings nor signs of performance impairment when nicarbazin was used at the proposed conditions of use. However, several authors reported a compromised thermoregulation capacity of chicken receiving 125 mg nicarbazin/kg feed (or above) leading to reduced evaporative cooling, higher core temperatures, blood alkalosis and faster heart rate (Beers et al., 1989; Keshavarz & McDougald, 1981; Wiernusz & Teeter, 1995) in birds older than 3 weeks.

Thus, taking into account the limited thermoregulation capacity of birds under heat stress and/or in the final stage of the production cycle, the Panel considers that a potential negative effect cannot be excluded in birds older than 3 weeks.

Interactions

No incompatibilities or interactions with feedingstuffs, carriers, other approved additives or medicinal products are to be expected based on the extensive history of use of the product.

Microbial studies

Antimicrobial activity of nicarbazin was studied by analysing the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of a pool of strains isolated from poultry 18 and human gut. 19 No antimicrobial activity was observed at a concentration of 64 mg/L or lower for any of the analysed strains. Considering the high MIC values observed, no antimicrobial activity is expected from the use of nicarbazin as a feed additive under the proposed conditions of use.

Conclusions on safety for the target species

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that NICACOX® is safe for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying when used up to 80 mg/kg complete feed for the first 21 days of life. Nicarbazin has no antimicrobial activity.

Safety for the consumer

Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) and residues

Nicarbazin is entirely split in the intestinal tract of birds into its two constituents, DNC and HDP. Consequently, nicarbazin cannot appear as residue in tissues and is therefore of no concern for consumer safety; only its two individual components may generate residues.

ADME

The ADME of nicarbazin has been assessed by the FEEDAP Panel in the context of previous authorisation of the same active substance in chickens for fattening and in turkeys for fattening (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017, 2018a, 2018b). The applicant submitted the same ADME studies already assessed in these opinions and a new study on the metabolic fate of nicarbazin in the chicken using more sensitive analytical tools, which is assessed below. 20

Chickens were fed for 30 days with a diet supplemented with 50 mg/kg of an equimolar mixture of DNC and HDP. Liver, kidneys, muscle and skin/fat were sampled 6, 24, 48 and 72 h after the last dose. The presence of DNC and HDP metabolites was screened in tissue extracts using liquid chromatography coupled with high‐resolution mass spectrometry (Orbitrap—HRMS). Metabolite structures were proposed based on HRMS and HRMS/MS spectra and comparison to metabolite structures already established and a dedicated software to predict metabolite structures. This study described 21 metabolites for DNC and three for HDP, with strong hypotheses on their identity. The main metabolites already described (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a) were confirmed. This semi‐quantitative method confirmed that unchanged DNC and HDP are by far the main residues, all individual metabolites representing each less than 10% of total residues.

The FEEDAP Panel, therefore, reiterates its previous conclusions: ‘nicarbazin, when ingested, is rapidly split in its two components HDP and DNC which behave independently. Liver is the target tissue. DNC residues decline rapidly from tissues following nicarbazin withdrawal. DNC appears as the marker residue. HDP‐related residues are much lower (by two orders of magnitude) than those derived from DNC. The metabolic pathways are similar in the chicken and in the rat’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a).

Effects of thermal treatment on the stability of DNC residues in food products

DNC residues in tissues might undergo degradation upon common thermal treatment such as cooking (Bacila et al., 2018; Bacila, Cunha Jr., et al., 2019; Bacila, Lazzarotto, et al., 2019; Tarbin et al., 2005). In particular, the studies of Bacila et al. showed that thermal treatment of chicken meat leads to the formation of p‐nitroaniline (PNA). PNA, a nicarbazin‐associated impurity, is a suspected carcinogen (Section 3.2.2.2.2). The maximum level of PNA of 0.1% in nicarbazin was set by Regulation (EU) No 875/2010 following the opinion of the FEEDAP Panel (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2010).

The applicant provided data to investigate whether the degradation of the DNC residues present in animal tissues due to the use of NICACOX® in feed for chickens under the proposed conditions of use could lead to increased levels of PNA in all edible tissues upon common thermal treatment.

The applicant carried out a study to generate chicken tissue samples containing DNC residues and assess the effect of thermal treatment mimicking domestic cooking conditions on the degradation of DNC in these tissues. The study, that was claimed to be GLP compliant, provided levels of DNC and PNA in raw tissues after different withdrawal times and in samples of the same tissues subjected to four different thermal treatments. 22

Description of the study

A total of 240 1‐day‐old Hubbard chickens (males and females) were allocated to four groups, each consisting of one pen with 30 males and 30 females. Birds were fed for 35 days a complete diet supplemented with 85 mg nicarbazin/kg complete feed from NICACOX® (analytically confirmed). Each group was assigned to one of four types of thermal treatment/cooking processes.

After withdrawal of the supplemented feed, animals (eight per withdrawal time) were slaughtered at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. Each carcass was split longitudinally into two half carcasses, with one half analysed uncooked and the other subjected to the assigned cooking process, so that each animal served as its own control. The liver was similarly divided. Samples of muscle, skin with adhering fat, liver and kidney were collected to determine DNC and PNA concentrations.

The cooking processes tested were roasting, boiling, grilling and frying. In the boiling treatment, tissues were sealed in a cooking bag and were not in direct contact with water. Target core temperatures of 74–75°C were achieved and even exceeded for durations 23 representative of household cooking conditions, as established in a preliminary study. 24 Sample weights before and after cooking were recorded to calculate mass losses, which were comparable across cooking processes. Cooking juices were not collected for analysis.

Residue analysis was performed using a validated UPLC–MS/MS method. The method showed adequate performance for both raw and cooked tissues, with limits of quantification of 100 μg/kg for DNC and 1 μg/kg for PNA. 25

The FEEDAP Panel observes that the method validation, using tissue samples spiked with PNA, lacks evidence that PNA can be quantitatively extracted also from incurred samples subjected to thermal treatment.

Individual observations were analysed using statistical modelling in R. Residue depletion was characterised using a spline modelling approach applied separately to each edible tissue (liver, kidney, muscle and skin/fat) in which the decay of the response across time was modelled using a smoothing function (spline).

The objective of the modelling was to estimate, for each tissue, the time point at which residue concentrations fall below the analytical LOQ, taking into account data censoring and uncertainty (see Appendix A). The estimated median times (with 95% prediction intervals) for residues to decline below the LOQ were tissue‐dependent, with the longest depletion observed in the liver.

The upper bound of the 95% prediction interval (97.5th percentile) was considered as a conservative estimate. Across tissues, the highest upper bounds were approximately 108 h for liver, 53 h for kidney, 91 h for muscle and 86 h for skin/fat.

Conclusions on ADMER and on the effects of thermal processing

Former conclusions of the FEEDAP Panel on the ADMER of nicarbazin were confirmed by a more recent study on the fate of nicarbazin in the chicken using more advanced analytical techniques and additional marker residue studies: nicarbazin when ingested is rapidly split in its two components HDP and DNC, which behave independently. The liver is the target tissue. DNC residues decline rapidly from tissues following nicarbazin withdrawal. HDP residues are much lower (by two orders of magnitude) than those derived from DNC. The metabolic pathways are similar in the chicken and in the rat. A 1‐day withdrawal allows DNC to drop below currently authorised MRLs in all tissues, while according to statistical pharmacokinetic modelling, a 5‐day withdrawal is necessary to reduce DNC to below 100 μg/kg tissues.

Thermal processing representative of domestic cooking conditions of chicken tissues containing DNC residues leads to the degradation of DNC and the formation of PNA.

When DNC is present in chicken tissues at the level of the currently authorised MRLs, PNA was always detected and reached levels of about a few hundred μg/kg tissue depending on the tissue and cooking conditions. After 5‐day withdrawal, PNA levels were below the LOQ (1 μg/kg) in all cooked tissues. This is in line with the withdrawal time calculated based on DNC residues in raw tissues.

Toxicology

Overview of previous evaluations on nicarbazin

The toxicological profile of nicarbazin, DNC and HDP was evaluated in previous opinions on Monimax® (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a, 2018a) and the applicant submitted the same data set. The FEEDAP Panel considers that the same conclusions apply to the current assessment:

‘Nicarbazin showed mutagenic activity in the Salmonella Typhimurium TA98 strain in the presence and in the absence of metabolic activation, while the substance was negative in the other bacterial strains. Negative results were reported in a gene mutation assay in L5178Y TK+/– mouse lymphoma cells and in a chromosome aberration test in human lymphocytes in vitro. Moreover, nicarbazin did not show any mutagenic activity in an in vivo micronucleus test in rat in conditions warranting the exposure of the target cells to the test substance. The primary toxicity resulting from the oral use of nicarbazin is renal toxicity. The absence of similar findings after treatment with DNC and HDP confirms that this equimolar association of compounds is better tolerated than nicarbazin at equivalent doses. At parentally toxic doses (renal effects), there is no impairment of reproductive performance in rats treated with a combination of DNC/HDP at doses up to 580/193 mg/kg bw per day. The NOAEL for embryo/fetal development is 120 mg nicarbazin/kg bw of rabbits per day. The lowest NOAEL identified in a 52‐week study in rat using DNC + HDP was 20 mg DNC + 8 mg HDP/kg bw per day based on the occurrence of microcrystals in urine and related microscopic renal observations at higher dose level.’

In the opinions mentioned above, the FEEDAP Panel evaluated the safety for the consumer of the impurities present in the active substance nicarbazin (PNA and M4NPC) and concluded that ‘No safety concern would arise from the impurity PNA if the maximum content in nicarbazin of 0.1% is respected. The other nicarbazin related impurity, M4NPC, is considered safe for the consumer provided that a maximum concentration of 0.4% in nicarbazin would not be exceeded’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a, 2018a). In the context of the current application, the FEEDAP Panel reviewed the toxicological profile of PNA considering the new requirements for the safety assessment since the previous evaluations. A description of the relevant information available is reported in the sections below.

Toxicological studies with p‐nitroaniline, including genotoxicity

Genotoxicity studies, including mutagenicity

The applicant provided several studies on the genotoxicity of PNA, retrieved from the literature. Those considered relevant for the current assessment are described in Appendix B.

The potential of PNA to induce gene mutations was tested in numerous studies performed in bacterial cells (Ames test). Consistently positive results were reported using Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98 and TA1538, detecting frame‐shift mutations in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (Assmann et al., 1997; Chung et al., 1996; Dellarco & Prival, 1989; Garner & Nutman, 1977; Pai et al., 1985; Shimizu & Yano, 1986; Thompson et al., 1983). In addition, studies conducted applying a metabolic activation system obtained from hamster liver and flavin mononucleotide, facilitating nitro reduction, resulted in the enhancement of the mutagenicity of PNA in strains TA98 and TA100 compared with the activation system from rat liver (Dellarco & Prival, 1989). The nitroreductase‐deficient derivative strain TA98NR was also weakly mutagenic in the presence of metabolic activation (Chung et al., 1996). Overall, these results suggest that metabolic activation is required for the mutagenic activity of PNA in bacteria. In the absence of metabolic activation, negative results were observed in the nitroreductase‐deficient strains derived from TA98, TA100 and TA1538 (TA98NR, TA100NR and TA1538NR) (Chung et al., 1996), while strains TA97, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 showed negative results both in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (Assmann et al., 1997; Chiu et al., 1978; Chung et al., 1996; Dellarco & Prival, 1989; NTP, 1993; Pai et al., 1985; Shahin, 1985; Shimizu & Yano, 1986).

PNA induced structural chromosomal damage in vitro in mammalian cells in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (NTP, 1993; Chung et al., 1996; Huang et al., 1995). These positive results were not confirmed in an in vivo micronucleus test in mice following intraperitoneal administration (US EPA, 2009). However, the FEEDAP Panel notes that PNA induced Heinz bodies in erythrocytes in toxicity studies, causing membrane rigidity and elimination in the spleen (haemolytic anaemia) (NTP, 1993), and considers questionable whether erythrocytes represented, in these conditions, a suitable test system.

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the positive results observed in vitro for the induction of gene mutations in bacterial cells and chromosome damage in mammalian cells indicate a potential genotoxic activity for PNA.

Toxicity studies, including carcinogenicity

Sub‐chronic toxicity studies

B6C3Fl mice (20 male and 20 female) received PNA by gavage at doses of 0, 1, 3, 10, 30 or 100 mg/kg bw for 13 weeks (5 days per week). Eight or nine animals per group were selected to be evaluated at week 7. No mortality was observed during the whole study. In animals receiving the test item at 30 or 100 mg/kg bw per day, methaemoglobin levels increased while haematocrit and erythrocyte counts decreased, compared to the control. Heinz bodies were found in erythrocytes and the number of erythrocytes nucleated and reticulocytes increased. Absolute and relative weights of spleen in animals receiving 30 and 100 mg/kg were significantly higher compared to the controls (at 7 and 13 weeks). After 7 weeks of administration, absolute and relative liver weights were significantly higher in females receiving the test item at 30 or 100 mg/kg bw per day groups; however, at the end of the experiment (13 weeks), both absolute and relative liver weights were similar compared to control. There was a dose‐dependent increase in incidence and severity of splenic and haematopoiesis pigmentation (hemosiderin) both after 7 weeks of administration and at the end of the study. Hemosiderin in liver (Kupffer cells) was also observed in males (NTP, 1993).

Sprague–Dawley rats (20 male and 20 female) received PNA (purity 99.85%) by gavage at doses of 0, 3, 10 or mg/kg bw for 13 weeks. No treatment‐related mortality was recorded during the study (one occurred in the control group). Ear paleness, indicative of anaemia, was observed in both male (up to week 4) and female rats (up to week 6) treated with PNA at 30 mg/kg‐day. Clinical chemistry parameters remained similar to controls. However, treatment‐related haematological effects included increased methaemoglobin and reticulocyte counts in all the treated groups. Decreased erythrocyte numbers, haematocrit and blood haemoglobin were observed in rats given ≥ 10 mg/kg bw per day, alongside reduced mean cell haemoglobin and mean cell volume at 30 mg/kg bw per day. Histopathology revealed the spleen as the only organ affected, showing dose‐related congestion, hemosiderosis and extramedullary haematopoiesis in all the treated groups. The lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) of 3 mg/kg bw per day was identified. A no observed adverse effects level (NOAEL) was not established (US EPA, 2009).

Carcinogenicity studies

B6C3Fl mice (70 males and 70 females) received PNA by gavage at doses of 0, 3, 30 or 100 mg/kg bw for 103 weeks (5 days per week). The haematology findings at 9 and 15 months were similar to those observed in the 90‐day toxicity study (see Section above). An increase in the incidence or severity of splenic congestion, haematopoiesis, pigment (hemosiderin) accumulation, Kupffer cell pigmentation in the liver and bone marrow hyperplasia was observed in the groups of animals receiving the test item at 30 or 100 mg/kg bw per day. An increased incidence of haemangiosarcoma in the liver and haemangioma or haemangiosarcoma (combined) at all sites was found in males at the highest dose tested (100 mg/kg bw per day). The increase was not statistically significant compared to the control group but was higher than the historical data given in the NTP report. No effects were observed in females (NTP, 1993).

Sprague Dawley rats (60 males and 60 females) received PNA by gavage at doses of 0, 0.25, 1.5 or 9.9 mg/kg bw per day for 2 years. A dose‐dependent pigmentation of the spleen was observed in all the treated groups. In the mid‐ and high dosage groups (1.5 or 9.9 mg/kg bw per day), the animals showed a significant increase in the blood methaemoglobin and spleen weights. Spleen weight was also significantly increased at the high dosage groups. The tumour incidence in all treated groups did not increase compared to the controls (Nair et al., 1990). A no observed adverse effects level (NOAEL) of 0.25 mg/kg bw per day can be identified based on methaemoglobinaemia observed in the medium‐ and high‐dose groups.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that the US EPA performed the benchmark dose (BMD) modelling using the methaemoglobin incidences observed in the carcinogenicity study in rats to derive a chronic reference dose of 0.37 mg/kg bw per day (US EPA, 2009).

Assessment of consumer exposure and consumer safety assessment

DNC

MRLs for DNC of 15,000 μg/kg liver, 6000 μg/kg kidney and 4000 μg/kg muscle and skin/fat in chickens for fattening are in force in the EU. Residue data from two studies are available. The FEEDAP Panel notes that the two studies are not directly comparable due to some differences in the design. Despite these differences, the results showed similar levels of DNC residues. At 6‐h withdrawal, the values (mean + 2SD) were close to or exceeding the current MRLs in force, and at 24 h, all values declined well below the MRLs.

PNA

The FEEDAP Panel noted that a NOAEL of 0.25 mg PNA/kg body weight per day, identified in a 2‐year rat study on the basis of methaemoglobinaemia, may be considered as a suitable reference point for consumer safety assessment. However, the establishment of a health‐based guidance value for PNA is not possible at present, as concerns related to genotoxicity have not yet been adequately addressed.

The available study demonstrated the formation of PNA from incurred DNC residues during domestic cooking, consistent with evidence reported in the published literature. However, some uncertainty remains on the accuracy of PNA determination in cooked tissues.

Based on PNA levels measured in cooked tissues, a 5‐day withdrawal period would be necessary to exclude the risk related to the presence of PNA. This is also justified by the levels of DNC which decline below detectable levels in all tissues (< 100 μg/kg) after a 5‐day withdrawal.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that the additive is intended for use in feed for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying up to 21 days of age and concludes that slaughter at the end of the production period (normally 35 days) would ensure that the abovementioned conditions are fulfilled.

Conclusions on safety for the consumer

The FEEDAP Panel considers that the use of the additive NICACOX® in chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying under the proposed conditions of use (maximum use level of 80 mg/kg complete feed for 21 days) is safe for the consumer provided that a withdrawal time of 5 days is ensured.

Safety for the user

The safety of nicarbazin for the users was previously evaluated by the FEEDAP Panel (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a, 2018a, 2018b). No additional information was provided for the current assessment; therefore, the FEEDAP Panel reiterates the conclusions previously reached. Nicarbazin is not a skin nor eye irritant and is not a dermal sensitiser.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that no information was provided on the additive NICACOX®. In the absence of specific data on the final additive, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of NICACOX® for the users.

Safety for the environment

The composition of NICACOX® is identical to the additive Coxar®. The safety for the environment of Coxar® for turkeys for fattening was assessed by the FEEDAP Panel previously (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018b, 2021). The safety for the environment of an additive containing nicarbazin and monensin sodium from the same applicant was also evaluated by the FEEDAP Panel in 2017 and 2019 (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a, 2018a, 2019a).

The current application is for the use of the additive for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying. The additive is incorporated into feed at a maximum inclusion rate of 80 mg nicarbazin/kg complete feed, corresponding to 56.7 mg DNC/kg and 23.3 mg HDP/kg complete feed. The administration period is up to the age of 21 days.

Nicarbazin is an equimolar complex of DNC and HDP which splits during the intestinal passage. Consequently, the environmental risk assessment considers each component separately.

For the present evaluation, the applicant submitted an updated environmental risk assessment according to the FEEDAP current guidance on environmental risk assessment of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2019b). 28

Ecotoxicity studies

Toxicity to soil organisms

Effects on plants

A terrestrial plant seedling emergence and growth study following OECD guideline 208 and claimed to be GLP compliant was submitted using as test item a combination of DNC and HDP at an equimolar ratio equivalent to that present in nicarbazin. The study was performed with six plant species; four dicotyledonous (Phaseolus vulgaris, Raphanus sativus, Cucumis sativus and Solanum lycopersicum) and two monocotyledonous (Hordeum vulgare and Allium cepa) in natural soil (sandy loam). The FEEDAP Panel had previously assessed this study and concluded that ‘In two of six tested plant species, a statistically significant effect of the equimolar mixture of DNC and HDP was observed; however, the effect concentration could not be calculated. The FEEDAP Panel calculated the effect concentration based on information provided in the test report. Assuming the worst case, the median effective concentration (EC 50 ) for plants was set to 102 mg/kg and 248 mg/kg of HDP and DNC, respectively’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021). The FEEDAP Panel reviewed its former assessment and confirmed that the study and the results are acceptable. It was further noted that the approach taken (i.e. equimolar complex of two active substances) is not conventional, but acceptable as the co‐exposure of test organisms to two active substances presents environmentally realistic exposure scenario and contributes to ecological relevance of the study. For the risk characterisation, EC50 values > 248 mg/kg for DNC and > 102 mg/kg for HDP are identified. The FEEDAP Panel noted that the study does not allow accurate calculation of EC10 values, because the effects of the active substances have been investigated in one treatment level only. Since a statistically significant reduction (15.5%) in shoot dry weight for C. sativus was observed at 248 mg/kg DNC, the applicant suggested to establish EC10 for DNC at the level of 100 mg/kg. The Panel notes that the approach is unconventional, but the estimate is reasonable; therefore, the NOEC for DNC of 100 mg/kg is retained for the risk characterisation.

Effects on soil‐dwelling species

An earthworm reproduction study following OECD guideline 222 and claimed to be GLP‐compliant was performed with the earthworm Eisenia fetida in an artificial soil using as test item a combination of DNC and HDP at an equimolar ratio equivalent to that present in nicarbazin. The FEEDAP Panel had previously assessed this study and concluded that ‘For the effect of DNC and HDP on the reproduction of earthworms, the no effect concentration (NOEC) was set on 300 and 123 mg/kg (dry weight), respectively’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021). The FEEDAP Panel reviewed its former assessment and confirmed that study and the results are acceptable. It was further noted that the approach taken (i.e. equimolar complex of two active substances) is not conventional, but acceptable as the co‐exposure of test organisms to two active substances presents environmentally realistic exposure scenario and contributes to ecological relevance of the study. The NOEC values of 300 mg/kg for DNC and 123.5 mg/kg for HDP are used for the risk characterisation.

The applicant referred to a study carried out by Menezes‐Oliveira et al. (2018) who investigated the effects of nicarbazin (DNC and HDP) on the collembolan species Folsomia candida. The study was performed according to ISO 11267 (ISO, 1999) which is very similar to OECD 232. Four replicates of ten, 10‐ to 12‐day‐old collembola were exposed in a natural sandy loam soil to a range of nominal concentrations of 0, 10, 32, 100, 320 and 1000 mg nicarbazin/kg dry soil (equivalent to 7.09, 22.69, 70.9, 226.9 and 709 mg DNC/kg dry soil) for up to 28 days. No significant effects on the survival and reproduction of F. candida were observed, and therefore, the 28‐day NOEC for reproduction should be considered as the highest concentration tested, i.e. 1000 mg nicarbazin/kg dry soil (equivalent to 709 mg DNC/kg dry soil).

Effects on soil microorganisms

A GLP‐compliant nitrogen transformation study following OECD guideline 216 was performed with DNC in a natural sandy loam soil. 31 DNC was applied to soil at a nominal concentration of 5300 and 53,000 μg/kg. These concentrations correspond to about PECsoil plateau for DNC, when no restriction in the label is considered, and 10 times this value. The soil nitrogen transformation rate was determined at 7, 14 and 28 days after application. The study was valid; it can be considered a worst‐case; the variation in soil nitrate concentration for replicate control samples was < 15% for all time points (actual ≤ 9.57%). Nitrogen transformation rates were calculated using two methods: an incremental method calculating rates based on the difference between sequential time points and an overall method, calculating rates based on the difference between time points and the initial concentration. Nitrate formation rate deviations from the controls for both treatment rates (5300 μg/kg and 53,000 μg/kg) calculated based on both incremental and overall methods were less than 25% on day 28.

A GLP‐compliant nitrogen transformation study following OECD guideline number 216 was performed with HDP in a natural sandy loam soil. 32 HDP was applied to soil at a nominal concentration of 1056.3 and 10,563 μg/kg, measured (analytically confirmed) concentrations of 820.6 and 8210 μg/kg. These concentrations are much higher than PECsoil plateau and PECsoil plateau × 10 for HDP, even when no label restriction is considered. The soil nitrogen transformation rate was determined at 7, 14 and 28 days after application. The study was valid; it can be considered a worst case; the variation in soil nitrate concentration for replicate control samples was < 15% for all time points (actual ≤ 1.4%). Nitrogen transformation rates were calculated using two methods: an incremental method calculating rates based on the difference between sequential time points and an overall method, calculating rates based on the difference between time points and the initial concentration. Nitrate formation rate deviations from the controls for both treatment rates using both the incremental and overall methods were less than 25% on day 28.

Toxicity to aquatic organisms

Effects on algae, crustaceans and fish

For the aquatic compartment, studies with DNC were made available for acute and chronic effect on algae, aquatic invertebrates and for acute toxic effects on fish. The studies had been already evaluated by the FEEDAP Panel (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021). The FEEDAP Panel reviewed its former assessment and confirmed that the studies and the results are acceptable. The main results are summarised as follows: no effect of DNC could be observed in any of the performed tests; thus, the lowest concentration tested is proposed as the NOEC value. The NOEC for algae is 10.1 μg/L. The 21‐day NOEC for reproduction of daphnids is 4.51 μg/L. The 96‐h LC50 for fish is > 5.4 μg/L. A GLP‐compliant study was performed to investigate the chronic effect of DNC on fish following OECD guideline 210 (OECD, 2013) (flow‐through test). 33 Embryos of the Fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas) were exposed at nominal concentration range of 0, 0.054, 0.17, 0.54, 1.7 and 5.4 μg/L for 28 days post hatch. The evaluation of biological endpoints was performed using nominal concentrations since the measured concentrations were within 80%–120% of these values. The study met the validity criteria. There was no evidence for an effect on embryo survival, hatching time, post‐hatch survival and/or body size (wet weight and total length) at 28 days post hatch. However, exposure to DNC at a concentration of 5.4 μg/L increased cumulative mortality with the cumulative survival (from < 12 h post fertilisation through to 28 days post hatch) reduced from 90% in the solvent control to 80% in the 5.4 μg/L treatment. Therefore, the NOEC for fish was established as 1.7 μg/L.

The toxicity of HDP to the aquatic compartment was studied in tests of acute and long‐term effect on algae and acute effect on daphnids and fish (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021). A NOEC value of 101.5 mg/L was determined for algae; the LC50 and EC50 for fish and immobilisation for Daphnia magna were both > 100 mg/L. The same conclusions can be reiterated for the current assessment.

Effects on sediment‐dwelling organisms

Three studies were submitted to investigate the effect of DNC on sediment‐dwelling organisms. One of the studies was already evaluated (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2021) and the Panel concluded that ‘Emergence was the most sensitive endpoint, and the NOEC was determined as 241.1 mg/kg sediment (dry weight)’, corresponding to 415.7 mg/kg sediment when normalised to the organic carbon content. The former conclusions can be reiterated.

A GLP‐compliant study following OECD guideline 225 was performed to investigate the effect of DNC on the rooted aquatic macrophyte Myriophyllum spicatum. 34 The study met all validity criteria and showed acceptable sensitivity to the reference compound. The results are reliable and can be used for risk assessment. The evaluation of biological endpoints was performed using the measured concentrations. There was no significant difference between the controls and either of the DNC treatments. Therefore, the NOEC was set at the highest concentration tested 903 mg/kg sediment (dry weight) which provides an organic carbon normalised NOEC of 1557 mg/kg sediment (dry weight).

A GLP‐compliant study following OECD guideline 225 was performed to investigate the effect of DNC on the sediment‐dwelling/ingesting worm Lumbriculus variegatus. 35 The worms in a similar physiological state were exposed to nominal concentrations of 55,114, 224, 452 and 901 mg/kg sediment (dry weight) for up to 28 days. The test item was applied directly to artificial sediment, left overnight to equilibrate and prior to the addition of the test animals reconstituted overlying water was added. The measured total organic carbon of the artificial sediment was 2.7% (dry weight). The study included a positive control of pentachlorophenol as 44 mg/kg sediment (dry weight). To assess the stability of the test item, the concentration of DNC in the test sediment and media was determined at the start and end of the exposure period for each concentration. The study was valid; the number of living worms per control replicate increased by a factor of at least 1.8 (actual factor of 3), the pH of the overlying water was between 6 and 9 throughout the test (actual between 7.8 and 8.7) and the oxygen concentration in the overlying water was not below 30% of the air saturation value (actual ≥ 95% air saturation value throughout). The evaluation of biological endpoints was performed using the nominal concentrations since mean measured concentrations were with 80%–120% of nominal values. A significant effect was observed in the positive control. No effect of DNC dose concentration on the number of living worms or total dry mass was detected across all treatments, and therefore, effect concentration values (EC) could not be established. Therefore, the NOEC for all endpoints was set at the highest concentration tested 901 mg/kg sediment (dry weight) which provides an organic carbon normalised NOEC of 1668 mg/kg sediment (dry weight).

Based on the three studies, the lowest NOEC was used for the risk assessment, corresponding to 415.7 mg/kg sediment.

A sediment assessment at Phase IIA was not necessary for HDP since the log Koc and log Kow were both < 3 (1.8 to 2.1 and −0.92 to −0.95, respectively).

Risk of secondary poisoning

To assess the potential for bioaccumulation and the risk of secondary poisoning, the method proposed in the relevant Guidance from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) has been considered (EMA, 2016).

Log Kow of DNC is above 3 (3.25), indicating that the substance has bioaccumulation potential. Therefore, the risk of secondary poisoning has to be evaluated. The calculated values of the bioconcentration factor (BCF) are 116 L/kg for fish and 22 L/kg for earthworms. The BCF for earthworms indicates that DNC is unlikely to bioaccumulate in these organisms; therefore, the risk of secondary poisoning to worm‐eating birds and mammals is considered low. The BCF for fish is above the bioaccumulation trigger value in secondary poisoning risk assessment (100 L/kg) (ECHA, 2017), and the FEEDAP Panel continued the assessment for the aquatic food chain.

Using the lowest NOAEL of 20 mg DNC/kg bw per day from a 52‐week chronic rat study (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017a), corresponding to a NOEC of 400 mg DNC/kg feed, the PNECoral is 13.3 mg/kg feed. The estimated concentration in fish is 15.7 μg/kg, based on PECsurface water of 0.136 μg/L and a log Kow of 3.25. PEC in fish is lower than the PNECoral, and the risk of secondary poisoning can be excluded also for fish‐eating birds and mammals.

Log Kow of HDP is below 3 (−0.92 to −0.95), indicating low bioaccumulation potential. Therefore, no risk of secondary poisoning is expected for worm/fish‐eating birds and mammals.

Conclusions on safety for the environment

The use of nicarbazin (DNC and HDP) from NICACOX® in complete feed for chickens for fattening at a maximum level of 80 mg/kg complete feed, with a label restriction that will limit the ingestion of the feed additive to the first 21 days of life, does not pose a risk for the terrestrial and aquatic compartments nor to sediment. No concern for groundwater is expected. No risk of secondary poisoning is identified for fish‐eating and worm‐eating birds and mammals.

Efficacy

To support the efficacy of the additive, the applicant submitted three floor pen studies (FP) and five anticoccidial sensitivity tests (AST) in chickens or fattening.

Floor pen studies

The applicant submitted three studies 36 with nicarbazin from NICACOX® at an intended feed concentration of 75 mg/kg corresponding to the lowest recommended dose. The three FP were performed in one study site using inocula from three different origins (Belgium, France and Poland). In each trial, a total of 840 1‐day‐old male chickens (Ross 308) were distributed in pens (10 pens/group (except UUC of FP‐2 and FP‐3 which had 9); 28 birds/pen) and randomly allocated to one of the three experimental groups: an uninfected untreated control group (UUC), an infected untreated control group (IUC) and an infected treated group (IT). FP‐2 and FP‐3 shared the same UUC group. The different groups were given the same basal feed based on maize, wheat, soybean meal and rapeseed meal. Feed of the IT group was supplemented with 75 mg nicarbazin/kg (confirmed by analysis). On day 14 (see Table 21), all birds in the infected groups were inoculated via gavage with recent field isolates of Eimeria spp. using the doses selected in virulence test. 37 The virulence of each inoculum was tested in dose‐titration studies. Study duration was 35 days in trial 1 and 28 days in trials 2 and 3. The feed supplemented with nicarbazin was given for 35 days in FP‐1 and for 21 days in FP‐2 and FP‐3; for the latter two trials, a blank feed was given for the remaining 7 days. The experimental diets in pelleted form and water were provided for ad libitum access. Animal health and mortality were monitored daily. Feed intake and body weight of the animals were measured; feed to gain ratio and daily weight gain were calculated. Samples of excreta were analysed for oocyst excretion (days 20 and 21 in FP‐1 and days 20, 21 and 28 in FP‐2 and FP‐3). Intestinal lesions were scored on four birds per pen at days 20 and 21, following the method of Johnson and Reid (1970) (0 = no lesion, 1 = very mild, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate and 4 = severe).

The data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a general linear mixed model (GLMM). The pen was the experimental unit for statistical purposes. All hypothesis tests were conducted at the 0.05 level of significance using two‐sided tests. If the treatment effect was significant, the groups UUC and IT were compared to the IUC group (independent comparisons) in all models.

Anticoccidial sensitivity tests (AST)

A total of five ASTs were submitted. Two of these ASTs presented shortcomings in the experimental design and reporting and therefore were not further considered. 38 The other studies are described below.

Three AST studies 39 were conducted to assess the efficacy of NICACOX® that followed a similar experimental design (Table 26). A total of 200 (in AST‐1) or 120 (in AST‐2 and AST‐3) 1‐day‐old male chickens (Ross 308) were distributed in battery cages and randomly allocated to one of the three experimental groups UUC, IUC and IT (or IT1 and IT2 in AST‐1) at the age of 14, 13 or 12 days in AST‐1, AST‐2 and AST‐3, respectively. The IT groups received a grower feed containing 75 mg nicarbazin/kg feed and, in AST‐1, the IT2 group received 90 mg/kg feed. The intended dietary concentrations were analytically confirmed. In all AST, the starter diets were used from placement to the moment at which birds were distributed to the experimental groups, and thereafter, experimental feeds were fed (i.e. 2 days before inoculation) until study end. On the corresponding day (see Table 26), all birds in the infected groups were inoculated via gavage with recent field isolates of Eimeria spp. using the doses selected in virulence test. 40 Feed, based on maize, wheat and soybean meal, in pellet form and water were offered for ad libitum access. Animal health and mortality were monitored daily. Birds were weighed individually, 41 feed intake determined per cage and daily weight gain and feed to gain ratio calculated per cage. Excreta samples were collected 1 day before inoculation and at days 5, 6 and 7 post inoculation (PI) for oocyst counts. 42 At study end, corresponding to day 8 PI for AST‐1 and day 7 PI for AST‐2 and AST‐3, birds were killed and necropsied to assess the intestinal lesions following the method of Johnson and Reid (1970) (0 = no lesion, 1 = very mild, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate and 4 = severe) for E. acervulina, E. maxima and E. tenella.

Data were analysed using linear mixed regression models (GLMM) with the choice of a different parameter with fixed or random effect: (i) for data on body weight and daily weight gain at bird level, the treatment group was used as a fixed effect and cage as a random effect; (ii) for daily feed intake, feed to gain ratio, total intestinal lesion score and (species‐specific) OPG, treatment group was used as a fixed effect. The species‐specific associated ILS and clinical scores were analysed using ordered regression models with treatment group and study day as a fixed effect. A natural logarithmic transformation [Ln(x + 1)] was performed on OPG data to obtain a normalised distribution. Mortality was analysed by using cox proportional hazard models (AST‐1) or by logistic regression models (AST‐2 and ‐3) with treatment as a fixed effect. In all models, treatment groups were compared to the IUC group as reference. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Synopsys

The FEEDAP Panel notes that, although in FP‐1 nicarbazin was administered for a longer period than foreseen under the conditions of use (35 vs. 21 days), results from day 21 can be used to assess nicarbazin's efficacy.

Evidence of efficacy of nicarbazin was shown by (i) reduced mortality in one trial (FP‐2) and reduced morbidity in two trials (FP‐2 and FP‐3), (ii) reduced oocyst excretion in two trials (FP‐1 and FP‐2), with a more limited effect in FP‐3 mainly against E. tenella, (iii) reduced overall intestinal lesion scores in two trials (FP‐1 and FP‐2) and for at least one Eimeria species in all three trials and (iv) improved body weight and overall zootechnical performance relative to the infected untreated controls.

Regarding the three ASTs together, treatment with nicarbazin was associated with a reduction in the impact of Eimeria challenge, as reflected by lower intestinal lesion scores in the three studies, and reductions in oocyst excretion in two studies (most pronounced in AST‐3). Despite being secondary endpoints, performance parameters were improved in two studies (AST‐2 and AST‐3), while no clear performance benefit was observed in AST‐1.

Conclusions on efficacy

Nicarbazin given at a level of 75 mg/kg complete feed for the first 21 days of life is effective in preventing coccidiosis of chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying in the corresponding time period.

Post‐market monitoring

Field monitoring of Eimeria spp. resistance to nicarbazin should be undertaken, preferably during the latter part of the period of authorisation.

CONCLUSIONS

NICACOX® is safe for chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying when used up to 80 mg/kg complete feed for the first 21 days of life. Nicarbazin has no antimicrobial activity.

The use of NICACOX® in chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying under the proposed conditions of use (maximum use level of 80 mg/kg complete feed for 21 days) is safe for the consumer, provided that a withdrawal time of 5 days is ensured.

Nicarbazin is not a skin nor eye irritant and is not a dermal sensitiser. In absence of specific data on the final additive, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of NICACOX® for the users.

The use of nicarbazin (DNC and HDP) from NICACOX® in complete feed for chickens for fattening does not pose a risk for the terrestrial and aquatic compartments nor to sediment under the proposed condition of use. No concern for groundwater is expected and no risk of secondary poisoning is identified.

Nicarbazin from NICACOX®, given at a level of 75 mg/kg complete feed for the first 21 days of life, is effective in preventing coccidiosis in chickens for fattening and chickens reared for laying in the corresponding time period.
