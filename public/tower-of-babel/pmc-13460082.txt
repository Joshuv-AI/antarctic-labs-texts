Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as a critical threat to global public health due to its high levels of antimicrobial resistance and limited treatment options. In recent years, CRKP has been increasingly associated with severe infections, including bloodstream infections, pneumonia, and intra-abdominal infections, contributing to high morbidity and mortality.1,2 Among CRKP, high-risk clones such as ST11 have been widely reported to dominate nosocomial settings, particularly in Asia, and are frequently associated with the dissemination of carbapenemase genes, including blaKPC and blaNDM.3

Intensive care units (ICUs) are traditionally considered the primary reservoirs for CRKP transmission due to high antibiotic pressure, frequent invasive procedures, and critically ill patient populations.4 Although CRKP has been widely reported across various hospital departments, differences in department-specific molecular epidemiology and resistance gene dissemination remain incompletely characterized, highlighting the need for comprehensive genomic surveillance at the hospital level.5

The spread of CRKP is driven by two major mechanisms: clonal expansion of epidemic lineages and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) mediated by mobile genetic elements such as plasmids.6 In particular, blaNDM-1 has attracted increasing attention due to its strong mobility, broad host range, and frequent association with conjugative plasmids carrying type IV secretion systems (T4SS), which facilitate efficient inter-strain transfer.7 Moreover, the emergence of CRKP isolates co-harboring multiple carbapenemase genes, such as blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-232, raises additional concerns regarding the accumulation of resistance determinants and the potential for enhanced dissemination.8

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates collected from a teaching hospital, aiming not only to characterize their clinical distribution, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and molecular epidemiology, but also to investigate the mechanisms underlying resistance gene dissemination and to elucidate the genetic structure of conjugative plasmids.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Isolation and Identification

This was a single-center, laboratory-based cross-sectional genomic surveillance study conducted at the Clinical Laboratory of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital. Twenty non-duplicate Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were consecutively collected from routine clinical specimens between January and December 2024. Only the first isolate obtained from each patient during the study period was included in the analysis to avoid duplicate sampling. Species identification was initially performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen, Germany) and further confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Huada, China).

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of K. pneumoniae isolates against a panel of antimicrobial agents were determined using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100, 34th Edition (2024). Detailed information is provided in Table 1.

Conjugation Experiments

To evaluate the transferability of blaNDM-1-carrying plasmids, conjugation experiments were performed using a filter mating method with sodium azide–resistant Escherichia coli J53 as the recipient strain.9 Donor and recipient strains were mixed at an appropriate ratio and incubated on non-selective media. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller–Hinton agar plates containing sodium azide (100 mg/L) and meropenem (2 mg/L). The identity of transconjugants and the presence of blaNDM-1 were confirmed by PCR and MALDI-TOF MS. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS) and Bioinformatics Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from K. pneumoniae isolates using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using both the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and the Oxford Nanopore MinION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Hybrid de novo assembly of Illumina short reads and Nanopore long reads was performed using Unicycler v0.4.8.10 Genome annotation was conducted using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/annotation_prok/).11 Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using ABRicate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with the ResFinder database (http://genomicepidemiology.org/).12 Virulence factors were identified using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB; http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/).13 For plasmid analysis, conjugation-related elements, including the origin of transfer (oriT), type IV secretion system (T4SS), type IV coupling protein (T4CP), and relaxase genes, were predicted using oriTfinder with default parameters.14 Plasmid structures were visualized using Chiplot. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST), plasmid replicon typing, and comparative genomic analyses (including cgMLST and SNP-based analysis) were performed using the BacWGSTdb server.15,16 Phylogenetic trees were visualized using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/).17

Results

Clinical Characteristics of Isolates

A total of 20 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were collected from a teaching hospital in 2024. The majority of isolates were obtained from sputum (25%, 5/20) and peritoneal drainage fluid (25%, 5/20). Most isolates originated from the Surgery department (60%, 12/20), followed by the ICU (20%, 4/20). The most common underlying diagnosis was tumor (55%, 11/20). Antimicrobial Susceptibility and Virulence Profiles

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that 15 out of 20 isolates (75%) exhibited a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype. Consistent resistance profiles were observed for ceftazidime (CAZ) and cefepime (FEP).Multiple virulence-associated genes were identified across the isolates, including siderophore systems such as the iro locus and yersiniabactin (ybtAEPQSTUX, irp1, irp2, and fyuA), as well as colibactin-related genes (clbF and clbP) and type 3 fimbriae gene clusters (mrkABCDFHIJ). These genes are primarily associated with iron acquisition and bacterial colonization (Figure 1).

Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST)

MLST analysis revealed that the isolates were predominantly assigned to ST11 (65%, 13/20), followed by ST15 (10%, 2/20). Several additional sequence types were identified at low frequency, including ST638, ST1049, ST4573, ST23, and ST3332 (Figure 1), indicating genetic diversity among non-dominant lineages.

Transferability of blaNDM-1

Conjugation assays demonstrated that blaNDM-1 could be successfully transferred to recipient strains, confirming its plasmid-mediated mobility (Figure S1).

Distribution of Resistance Genes and Comparative Genomics

Among the 20 isolates, 13 (65%) carried blaKPC-2, while 2 (10%) harbored blaNDM-1.

In addition, blaOXA-1 was detected in two isolates. Most strains were found to carry IncFIB(K) plasmids. Comparative genomic analysis revealed that ST11 isolates were highly related, suggesting clonal dissemination within the hospital setting.

Genomic Characterization of ST638 CRKP Strain KP1226

Whole-genome sequencing of the ST638 isolate KP1226 revealed a single chromosome and one plasmid. The blaNDM-1 gene was located on a 173,720-bp plasmid, designated pKP1226-1 (Table 3). This plasmid also co-harbored blaOXA-232and multiple genes associated with type IV secretion systems (T4SS), indicating a conjugative plasmid structure (Figure 2).

Discussion

Consistent with prior reports of CRKP circulating in non-ICU departments, our study found most isolates originated from surgical wards rather than ICUs. This finding suggests that surgical settings may represent an underrecognized ecological niche for the persistence and dissemination of CRKP.

Several factors may contribute to this distribution pattern. Surgical patients are frequently exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics during the perioperative period, creating strong selective pressure that favors the expansion of resistant strains.18–20 In addition, surgical interventions, particularly those involving the gastrointestinal tract, may disrupt intestinal barrier integrity and increase the possibility of bacterial translocation from the gut to extraintestinal sites.21,22 Although the co-occurrence of isolates from peritoneal drainage fluid and sputum in our study raises the possibility of gastrointestinal involvement, the absence of rectal screening samples prevents direct confirmation of a gut-origin transmission pathway. Therefore, intestinal colonization should be considered as a plausible reservoir contributing to CRKP dissemination rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Our findings further demonstrate that CRKP dissemination in this setting is driven by a dual mechanism involving both clonal expansion and horizontal gene transfer.23 The predominance of ST11 is consistent with previous reports identifying this lineage as a high-risk epidemic clone.24 At the same time, the presence of blaNDM-1 on a conjugative plasmid underscores the importance of horizontal gene transfer in spreading resistance determinants across different genetic backgrounds. Notably, the blaNDM-1-carrying plasmid identified in this study harbored multiple T4SS-related genes, indicating its potential for self-transmission. The successful transfer of blaNDM-1 in conjugation assays further confirms the functional mobility of this plasmid. The co-localization of blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-232 within the same plasmid suggests the accumulation of multiple resistance determinants, which may enhance bacterial fitness under antibiotic pressure and accelerate the dissemination of multidrug resistance. An additional noteworthy finding is the structural context of blaOXA-232 within the conjugative plasmid pKP1226-1. Previous studies have shown that blaOXA-232 is most commonly located on small ColKP3-type non-conjugative plasmids, which exhibit limited independent mobility. In contrast, the present study identified blaOXA-232 within a 173,720-bp conjugative plasmid that also carries blaNDM-1 and a complete set of conjugation-associated genes. This unusual genetic organization suggests that the blaOXA-232-containing region may have been acquired through historical mobile genetic element-mediated recombination, potentially involving insertion sequences and transposition events. Although our sequence assembly did not identify a complete composite transposon directly mediating this integration, the presence of neighboring insertion sequences supports the possibility that IS-mediated recombination contributed to fusion of the resistance module into the conjugative backbone. Such structural integration may substantially enhance the horizontal dissemination potential of blaOXA-232 by linking it to a highly transmissible plasmid.

These findings support a “clone–plasmid synergy” model, in which epidemic clones such as ST11 provide a stable genetic backbone, while conjugative plasmids facilitate rapid horizontal spread of resistance genes.25 This dual mechanism may significantly enhance the adaptability and persistence of CRKP in hospital environments.

From a clinical perspective, our study highlights the need for enhanced surveillance and targeted infection control strategies in surgical wards. Routine screening for intestinal colonization, optimization of perioperative antibiotic use, and interventions aimed at preserving gut barrier integrity may help reduce the risk of CRKP transmission. Furthermore, strategies targeting plasmid transmission, such as limiting selective pressure or disrupting conjugation pathways, may represent promising approaches for controlling the spread of resistance.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Given the limited sample size of 20 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates, formal comparative inferential statistical analyses were not conducted, and we adopted descriptive statistics combined with phylogenetic clustering for data analysis. Second, functional validation of plasmid transfer mechanisms was limited to conjugation assays, and further studies are needed to elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying plasmid stability and transfer efficiency.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in a tertiary hospital setting. Our findings suggest that the dissemination of CRKP is driven by a combination of clonal expansion of dominant lineages, particularly ST11, and plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer. Notably, the identification of a conjugative plasmid co-harboring blaNDM-1and blaOXA-232 highlights the potential accumulation of multiple carbapenemase genes within mobile genetic elements, which may facilitate the spread of multidrug resistance. These findings underscore the importance of continuous genomic surveillance and targeted infection control strategies to limit the dissemination of CRKP.
