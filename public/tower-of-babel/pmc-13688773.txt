Junk food consumption and associated factors among high school adolescents in Harar City, Harari Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia: institutional-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Muzeyen Awol, Assefa Tola, Berhe Gebremichael, Alemayehu Tesfaye, Birhanu Shegene, Dawit Firdisa, Obsan Kassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive junk food consumption among adolescents has emerged as a global public health concern, with ultra-processed foods accounting for up to 60% of total daily caloric intake in urbanizing settings. However, there is limited and context-specific evidence regarding these dietary patterns in Ethiopia. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of junk food consumption among high school adolescents in Harar City, Harari Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April 25 to June 6, 2025, among 747 high school adolescents selected through a multistage sampling method. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with junk food consumption. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and the strength of association was reported using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe overall prevalence of junk food consumption among adolescents was 360 (48.2%) (95% CI: 44.6–51.8%). In the multivariable analysis, the strongest independent risk factor for consumption was female sex (AOR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.14–2.14), followed by regular receipt of pocket money (AOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.02–1.99) and joint family structures (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.02–1.99). Conversely, strong protective factors against consumption included home unavailability of junk food (AOR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.19–0.38), having neither parent alive (AOR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.13–0.85), having only the father alive (AOR = 0.34, 95%CI: 0.14–0.81) and enrollment in private schools (AOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.42–0.92).ConclusionNearly half of the surveyed adolescents routinely consume junk food, with this pattern driven primarily by gender (female), financial autonomy, and food accessibility. Interventions should focus on modifying immediate adolescent environments by regulating food availability inside school compounds and promoting supportive domestic dietary habits.Keywords: Junk food consumption, Adolescent, High school, Harar town, Eastern Ethiopia

Background

Dietary habits among adolescents are undergoing rapid shifts globally due to rapid urbanization, economic development, and the industrialization of food systems. In modern nutritional epidemiology, “junk food” is formally categorized under the NOVA classification framework as Group 4: Ultra-Processed Foods (UPFs). These are defined as energy-dense industrial formulations made entirely or mostly from substances extracted from foods (such as refined sugar, white flour, trans-fats, saturated fats, salt, and additives) that contain minimal to no whole foods [1]. Common examples include fast foods such as burgers, fried chicken, and pizza, and packaged products such as chips, biscuits, ice cream, sugar-sweetened beverages, fatty meats, sugary cereals, and frozen ready meals [2]. Although often used interchangeably, not all fast foods are junk foods when prepared with nutritious ingredients [3]. Because these items are typically characterized by high glycemic loads, low nutrient density, and excessive levels of saturated trans-fats and free sugars, their routine consumption serves as a primary behavioral driver of the metabolic nutrition transition [4, 5].

While high-volume manufacturing and heavy exposure to ultra-processed foods were traditionally hallmarks of high-income Western nations, intake has stabilized in developed countries but continues to rise rapidly across developing nations [6]. This commercial expansion is progressively displacing traditional, high-fiber, and whole-food diets with energy-dense, nutrient-poor commercial products [7]. This shifting landscape acts as a major driver of the dual burden of malnutrition, where undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies coexist alongside rising rates of adolescent overnutrition and chronic non-communicable diseases [8].

Adolescence represents a critical window for physical and cognitive development, making unhealthy dietary choices highly consequential for long-term health trajectories [9]. Widespread excessive consumption during this period is strongly associated with acute and chronic physiological pathologies, including tooth decay, constipation, obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases [10]. Furthermore, a growing body of evidence links high junk food intake to poor academic performance by impairing cognitive abilities [11], as well as compromised psychological well-being, including elevated rates of depression and anxiety among adolescents [12, 13]. Adolescent exposure to these foods is heavily mediated by environmental availability, financial autonomy, and socio-cultural shifts [14]. Widespread fast-food outlets, convenience stores, and vending machines maximize physical access in urban environments [15], while aggressive corporate marketing campaigns through television and social media heavily promote consumption and actively shape adolescent taste preferences [16, 17]. Domestic dynamics, including family structure, peer pressure, lack of dietary awareness, and limited institutional nutrition education, further intensify these patterns [14].

Despite well-established clinical evidence documenting the adverse physical and mental health effects of ultra-processed foods, their consumption remains highly popular among young people [18]. In Ethiopia, nutritional research and public health infrastructure have historically focused on undernutrition, stunting, and wasting. Consequently, there remains limited and context-specific evidence exploring the consumption of junk food and its independent predictors among secondary school adolescents in the country. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and socio-demographic and environmental factors associated with junk food consumption among high school adolescents in Harar City, Harari Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia. The findings of this study are expected to serve as a primary step toward planning multipronged, context-specific strategies to mitigate this dietary hazard. As adolescents represent the next productive workforce, investing in their structural health and nutrition is essential for national productivity, economic growth, and long-term social progress.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April 25, 2025, to June 6, 2025, in Harar City, eastern Ethiopia. Harar City is located 526 km east of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The City comprises six districts and 19 kebeles (the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia). Based on the 2007 Central Statistical Agency population census, the City’s total population was projected to be 151,977. According to the Harar City Education Bureau 2024 plan office, there are 15 secondary schools, of which seven are public, and eight are private. There are 10,460 secondary school students in Harar City in the 2024–2025 academic year. In terms of the local dietary and agricultural context, the surrounding region primarily produces agrarian staples, including sorghum, maize, common beans, groundnuts, potatoes, and onions, alongside khat as a major regional cash crop. Traditional dietary habits in the area rely heavily on these locally grown cereal grains, which are prepared into conventional dishes such as injera (a fermented flatbread), porridge, and legume-based stews [19].

Population and sampling

All secondary school adolescents in Harar City were the source population, whereas all adolescents in selected schools in Harar City were the study population. All regular secondary school students in selected schools in Harar City were included in the study, and students who were attending their class at evening shift were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

For the first objective (prevalence), sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a prevalence of junk food consumption of 23.5% from a previous study conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [20], Z = 95% (1.96), d=Margin of error = 5%, and a 10% non-response rate, resulting in a sample size of 305. A double population proportion formula with an assumption, power 80%, Z = 95% (1.96), and the type of family [21] was used for the objective of the determinants of junk food consumption, yielding a maximum sample size of 476. The sample size calculated for the determinant factors (476) was higher than the one calculated for the prevalence (305) and was considered for this study. After multiplying the higher sample size (476) by a design effect of 1.5 and adding a 10% non-respondent rate, the final sample size was set to 786 (Table 1).

A multi-stage sampling technique was used to select the schools and study participants. The sampling frame was constructed using official, current student enrollment rosters obtained directly from the Harar City Education Bureau for the 2024/2025 academic year. In the first stage, the city’s secondary schools were stratified into public and private. There were 7 public secondary schools and 8 private secondary schools. Four public schools and four private schools were selected using simple random sampling. In the second stage, the total sample size (N = 786) was allocated across the selected schools using probability proportional to size (PPS) allocation based on the enrollment volume of each school. Students from randomly selected schools were stratified into grade levels (grades 9–12). From these grades, sections were proportionally selected based on the number of sections for each grade. Finally, all students in the selected sections were considered for this study (Fig. 1).

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected by a structured self-administered questionnaire, which was developed after reviewing different literature [22–25]. The questionnaire covered sociodemographic characteristics and the food environment. Dietary intake was captured via a modified Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) adapted from pre-validated dietary tools utilized in similar adolescent health surveys within Sub-Saharan Africa [20]. A food frequency questionnaire was used to report adolescents’ recent dietary patterns, and the number of days per week that they consumed junk food was asked to determine the prevalence of junk food consumption. The tool was initially prepared in English and then translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic. A pretest was conducted among 5% (n = 39) of the sample before the actual data collection at Abdal Secondary School, which is different from the actual study area. To evaluate the internal consistency and reliability of the adapted behavioral scale, reliability analysis was performed on the pretest data, which demonstrated a strong Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α = 0.82). Based on the result of the pretest, necessary modifications were made to the questionnaire.

Data were collected by six health professionals using the structured, pretested, self-administered questionnaire among adolescent students during the school day. Six data collectors and two supervisors received two days of training. In addition, a half-day orientation was provided to data collection facilitators and supervisors on the study objectives, the content of the questionnaire, and procedures for ensuring privacy and maintaining confidentiality.

The filled-out questionnaires were checked for completeness and consistency by the supervisor each day, and typing errors were corrected manually. Socio-economic data were captured using a localized asset questionnaire adapted from the 2019 Mini Ethiopia Health and Demographic Survey (EDHS). This tool consisted of a checklist of 22 distinct household assets, including ownership of consumer goods (such as a television, radio, refrigerator, and telephone), housing characteristics (such as primary source of drinking water, type of toilet facility, and main material used for flooring and roofing), and ownership of land or livestock.

Measurements

To evaluate the nutritional status of the study participants, anthropometric measurements of weight and height were collected systematically using standardized protocols by the respective data collectors immediately after they completed the self-administered questionnaire, and were used to calculate body mass index by dividing weight in kg by height in meters squared [26]. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated, portable electronic scale. Before each measurement, the scale was checked and reset to a zero reading to ensure precision, and participants were instructed to wear minimal/light clothing and remove heavy accessories or jackets. Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a portable stadiometer. During height collection, participants were instructed to stand upright without shoes on a flat surface, with their heels together, arms hanging at their sides, and shoulders, buttocks, and heels making contact with the vertical board of the stadiometer while looking straight ahead. To minimize intra-observer and inter-observer measurement errors, all measurements were taken twice by trained health professionals in a private school area to reduce distractions and student discomfort. In cases where discrepancies between the two readings exceeded a pre-specified threshold (0.5 kg for weight or 1.0 cm for height), a third measurement was taken, and the median value was recorded as the final data point.

Operational definitions

Junk food consumption: “junk food” was defined in alignment with Group 4 (Ultra-processed foods and beverages) of the NOVA classification framework [27]. This category comprises industrial formulations made entirely or mostly from substances extracted from foods (e.g., oils, fats, sugar, starch, and proteins) or synthesized in laboratories, containing little to no intact whole foods. The specific food groups evaluated in our localized context included commercial fast foods (e.g., burgers, chips/french fries, fried chicken), packaged salty snacks (e.g., crisps), commercial sweets (e.g., biscuits, chocolates, candies, ice cream), and sugar-sweetened beverages (e.g., sodas, carbonated soft drinks, commercial juices). Considering public health risks, participants were categorized as junk food consumers if they reported consuming at least one item from these ultra-processed food groups on 3 or more days within the last 7 days [22, 28]. Individuals reporting consumption on fewer than 3 days per week were classified as non-consumers.

Wealth index: The wealth index was operationalized as a composite indicator of a household’s relative socio-economic status, generated via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the 22-item asset checklist. In the PCA model, asset indicators were assigned weights (factor scores) based on the first principal component, which accounted for the highest proportion of the total variance in household wealth. Each adolescent’s household was assigned a standardized factor score, and the cumulative distribution of these scores was used to classify the study population into three equal wealth categories: poor (lowest 33.3%), middle (middle 33.3%), and rich (highest 33.3%) [29].

Adolescents’ age classification: 10–13 early adolescents, 14–16 middle adolescents, 17–19 late adolescents [22].

Peer influence: an adolescent was considered influenced by peers if they reported eating junk food due to peer encouragement, observing peers consume it, or regularly sharing such foods with friends [30].

Body mass index (BMI): The World Health Organization’s 2007 growth reference for children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years classifies BMI-for-age using z-scores. According to this standard, a z-score of < -3 was considered as severe thinness, < -2 indicates thinness, between − 2 and + 1 is considered normal, > +1 is overweight, and > + 2 indicates obesity. It was calculated by dividing the weight in kg by height in meters squared [26].

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was checked for completeness, consistency, and accuracy. Data was cleaned, coded, and entered using Epi-Data 4.6, then exported to SPSS version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, junk food consumption patterns, and Anthro plus software was used for body mass index (BMI) for age. Both bivariable and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to identify factors associated with the consumption of junk foods. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. A standard, conservative VIF threshold of < 10 (and a tolerance value of > 0.1) was established a priori to rule out severe collinearity. Diagnostic testing revealed that the maximum observed VIF value across all entered predictors was 5.67 (with a corresponding minimum tolerance of 0.18), confirming the absence of significant multicollinearity. Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using Hosmer and Lemeshow’s test, which yielded a p-value of 0.485, indicating a well-fitted model. Finally, adjusted odds ratios (AORs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals to show the strength and direction of associations, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 786 high school adolescents were initially approached and invited to participate in the study. Out of these, 747 participants successfully completed and returned the questionnaires, resulting in a final response rate of 95.0%.The remaining 5.0% (n = 39) non-responses were due to student absenteeism during the data collection sessions (n = 18), incomplete questionnaires missing critical sections (n = 14), and a lack of student or parental assent (n = 7). Four hundred sixteen (55.7%) of the students were females, and 595(79.7%) were urban residents. More than half, 476 (63.7%) of the students were between the ages of 17 and 19 years. The mean age of the study population was 17.05 ± 1.30 years, with a median age of 17.00 years (Interquartile Range [IQR]: 16.00–18.00 years). The majority of the students were attending public schools 594 (79.5%), while only 153(20.5%) were from private schools. With respect to family type, 495 (66.3%) of the students came from nuclear families, whereas 153 (33.7%) were from joint families (Table 2).

Prevalence of junk food consumption

The overall prevalence of junk food consumption among study participants was 360 (48.2%; 95% CI: 44.6–51.8%). Within the specific demographic sub-groups, consumption was reported by 231 (55.5%) of female participants, 293 (49.2%) of urban residents, 237 (49.8%) of late adolescents, and 91 (59.5%) of students attending private schools. In terms of types of junk food consumed, salty snacks were the most frequently reported items (54.1%), followed by sweets (52.6%) and sugar-sweetened beverages (49.7%).

Reasons for the consumption of junk foods

The descriptive analysis of reasons for consuming junk food was evaluated using a multiple-response question, allowing participants to select all applicable reasons for their consumption patterns. The most commonly reported reason was that junk food tastes better (21.8%), followed closely by peer influence (21.0%) and easy availability (20.2%). Other notable reasons included advertisements (7.2%), convenience (6.4%), and lifestyle (5.6%). Less frequently reported reasons were lower price (4.8%), variety (4.0%), curiosity (4.0%), and consuming junk food as a leisure-time activity (4.0%). The least reported reason was that junk food is quicker to make and eat, mentioned by only 0.8% of participants (Fig. 2).

Junk food consumption, by social and environmental factors

The prevalence of junk food consumption among high school adolescents varied according to peer influence, availability of junk foods, pocket money, and BMI status. Regarding peer influence, 15.1% of adolescents reported active peer pressure to consume fast foods; within this group, 50.5% were regular junk food consumers, compared to 35.4% among those reporting no peer influence (χ2 = 8.12; p = 0.004). With respect to availability, nearly one-third of respondents (31.7%) reported that junk foods were available near their school or home; prevalence of consumption was 27.4% among those with access and 57.8% among those without access (χ2 = 41.36; p < 0.001). In terms of economic factors, three-quarters of adolescents (75.1%) reported receiving pocket money; junk-food consumption was higher among them (51.2%) than among those without pocket money (39.2%) (χ2 = 6.89; p = 0.009). Concerning nutritional status, most adolescents were classified as having a normal BMI (77.8%), followed by thin (11.5%) and overweight (7.4%), with smaller proportions being severely thin (2.5%) or obese (0.8%). Junk-food consumption occurred across all BMI categories: 36.8% among the severely thin, 54.7% among the thin, 47.5% among normal-weight, 50.9% among the overweight, and 50.0% among obese adolescents (χ2 = 3.24; p = 0.518) (Table 3).

Magnitude of junk food consumption by marketing and advertising strategies

The distribution of junk food consumption among high school adolescents in relation to marketing and advertising strategies revealed a clear, dose-response pattern regarding television viewing frequency. Among adolescents who watched television daily, 61.5% consumed junk food, followed by 50.3% among those who watched a few times per week, 47.2% among occasional viewers, and 39.6% among those who rarely or never watched (χ2 = 9.45; p = 0.024). Regarding hours spent watching television per day, adolescents who watched more than 3 h had a junk-food consumption rate of 176 (49.3%), compared with 64 (47.8%) among those who watched less than 1 h, 64 (48.1%) among those who watched 1–2 h, and 56 (45.5%) among those who watched 2–3 h (χ2 = 4.88; p = 0.181). Leisure-related screen time also varied. Adolescents who spent more than 4 h on leisure screen activities had a junk-food consumption rate of 55.6%, those who spent 2–4 h had 52.4%, those who spent 1–2 h had 45.8%, and those who spent less than 1 h had 47.5% (χ2 = 5.12; p = 0.163) (Table 4).

Factors associated with the consumption of junk foods

In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, twelve factors were found to have an association with adolescent junk food consumption at a p-value < 0.25. These included: type of school, sex, parental survival status, family type, nearby access to junk food, receipt of pocket money, availability of junk food at home, peer influence, food security, wealth index, frequency of television watching, and food consumption during screen time. Accordingly, these variables were considered as candidates for the multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjustment for potential confounders in the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, seven factors remained independently associated with junk food consumption among adolescents. These were types of school, sex, family type, parental survival status (neither parent alive and only their father alive), receipt of pocket money, and availability of junk food at home.

Adolescents who attended private schools were 38% less likely to consume junk food compared to those enrolled in public schools (AOR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.422–0.915). Female adolescents were found to be 1.6 times more likely to consume junk food compared to their male counterparts (AOR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.144–2.139). Adolescents who had neither parent alive were 67% less likely to consume junk food compared to those whose both parents were alive (AOR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.128–0.848). Likewise, those with only their father alive were 66% less likely to consume junk food (AOR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.143–0.805). Additionally, adolescents living in joint families were 1.4 times more likely to consume junk food when compared to those living in nuclear families (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.021–1.986). Adolescents who reported receiving pocket money were 42% more likely to consume junk food than those who did not receive any (AOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.021–1.988). However, adolescents who had not junk food readily available in their household were 73% times less likely to consume it when compared to those who had junk food readily available (AOR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.189–0.378) (Table 5).

Discussion

The overall prevalence of ultra-processed junk food consumption in this study was 48.2%. This is highly comparable to findings from low- and middle-income settings like Bangladesh (48.2%) [31]. However, our prevalence is notably higher than the 23.5% reported in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [24] and the UK (17.2%) [32], yet lower than thresholds observed in highly commercialized environments like Nepal (60.3%) [22] and India (93%) [33]. Crucially, many international studies rely on metrics that capture daily consumption or short-term 24-hour recalls, whereas our metric isolates habitual intake occurring three or more days per week. This methodological divergence indicates that variations across international studies reflect not only shifting food supply chains, varying levels of urbanization, and differing localized purchasing power, but also distinct diagnostic cutoffs. Socioeconomic status and affordability also contribute significantly: in some settings, junk food is cheaper and more accessible than whole foods, whereas affordability constraints limit consumption in other regions, potentially explaining the lower prevalence in Addis Ababa. Furthermore, aggressive marketing on television and social media heavily shapes adolescent preferences for these packaged snacks and sugary drinks.

When assessing individual predictors, female adolescents demonstrated significantly higher odds of consuming junk food compared to males. In the urban context of Harar, this trend may be attributed to gendered socialization patterns, where adolescent girls frequently engage in indoor social groups where packaged sweets, biscuits, and carbonated beverages are routinely shared, contrasted with different outdoor recreation profiles typical of their male peers. This aligns with evidence from India [34]. However, this result contrasts with findings from Nepal [22] where male adolescents consumed more fast food and sugar-sweetened beverages than females. These inconsistencies highlight that gender differences in junk food consumption are not universal but are shaped by broader structural domestic settings and localized social frameworks.

The type of school is also a significant factor. Adolescents who attend private schools are less likely to consume junk food than those in public schools. A comparable finding was reported in Nepal [22]. Such consistency strengthens the argument that attending public schools may be associated with higher junk food consumption, possibly due to greater access to cheap street foods around public schools and less strict food regulations within school compounds. In contrast, Private school students had significantly higher odds of consuming ultra-processed foods and beverages in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [24], and India [33]. The observed variations across studies highlight that the relationship between school type and junk food consumption is highly context-dependent and cannot be generalized. While urban and affluent private school populations often face higher exposure to junk food due to higher disposable income, easier access to supermarkets, and aggressive marketing, localized factors create discrepancies between settings like Addis Ababa and Harar. In Harar, public institutions are structurally embedded within open commercial perimeters characterized by a high density of informal street vendors, whereas private institutions often map to different localized spatial exposures.

Parental survival status was another important determinant of junk food consumption. Adolescents with neither parent alive and those with only their father alive were less likely to consume junk food compared to those with both parents alive. This study is consistent with a study conducted in Pokhara Valley, Nepal [35]. However, it is inconsistent with a study conducted in the Somali Region, Ethiopia [36]. This association can be structurally justified by the empirical economic and domestic proxies measured directly in our study. In urbanizing, low-income settings, ultra-processed packaged snacks are premium, highly monetized commodities that rely heavily on disposable cash. Our data demonstrate that the regular receipt of pocket money and immediate household availability of junk food are the primary drivers of intake.

Family structure also influenced consumption. Adolescents in joint families were more likely to consume junk food than those in nuclear families. Larger households may foster shared eating habits, stronger peer influence from siblings or cousins, and less parental oversight of individual food choices. This finding aligns with evidence from Nepal [35]. This finding is inconsistent with a study conducted in Jumla [21]. Taken together, these results suggest that the availability of disposable income, the degree of parental or guardian supervision, and the dynamics of household size and interaction significantly shape adolescents’ dietary behaviors.

Economic factors strongly influenced adolescent dietary practices. Adolescents who received pocket money were more likely to consume junk foods. This is in line with a study conducted in the Somali Region, Ethiopia, and Nepal [35, 36]. This might be because having disposable income increases opportunities to purchase energy-dense, nutrient-poor snacks more frequently [20].

Finally, the strongest predictor identified in this study was the household food environment. Adolescents with junk food not readily available at home were less likely to consume it. This finding is consistent with research from India and Nepal, which demonstrated that home availability of unhealthy snacks significantly predicts adolescent consumption patterns [35, 37].

Overall, these findings indicate that adolescent junk food consumption is influenced by multiple, interrelated determinants spanning individual, family, and environmental domains. The results are in line with both local and global literature, which consistently identifies gender, socioeconomic status, parental influence, school type, and media exposure as critical factors shaping adolescent nutrition.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has notable strengths, particularly because it provides a comprehensive understanding of gender-specific patterns of junk food consumption. The study assessed multiple factors related to junk food consumption, such as exposure to media, socioeconomic status, and family structure, offering a multidimensional perspective not commonly covered in previous studies.

Despite these strengths, this study is not without limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the design prevents the establishment of temporal precedence or causal relationships between variables. Additionally, data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire, which may have led to misunderstandings among some participants. Measurement validity constraints stemming from this self-reported Food Frequency Questionnaire mean that variables such as junk food intake, food consumption scores, and media exposure are inherently vulnerable to recall and social desirability biases, though utilizing a defined recall period helped to limit this impact. Furthermore, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as absolute parental net income, distinct peer group dynamics, and localized street-vendor density surrounding the schools, cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, selection bias is another concern, as only school-attending adolescents were included, limiting generalizability to all adolescents in the community.

Conclusion

Approximately half of high school adolescents in Harar City reported regularly consuming junk food. Significant predictors of junk food consumption in this study included type of school, sex, family structure, parental survival status, pocket money, and household availability of junk foods.

Based on the findings, we recommend that nutrition education should be integrated into school curricula and existing adolescent health programs, strengthen school- and community-based interventions, and engage parents to reduce junk food availability at home. Community-based, school-focused interventions should be implemented by regional health bureaus to address adolescents’ excessive junk food consumption. Additionally, the regional health bureau should enforce school food regulations, encourage healthy lifestyles, enhance nutrition instruction, and limit the availability of highly processed foods. Marketing and sale of junk foods in and around schools should be restricted, while schools and partners promote accessible, attractive, healthy alternatives using gender-sensitive approaches. NGOs and community stakeholders should run family-inclusive health promotion campaigns and provide financial and media literacy programs to help adolescents resist peer and media influence. Future research should use longitudinal designs and evaluate school-based interventions to better establish causality and identify effective strategies to reduce junk food consumption.

Potential public health implications of junk food consumption

Junk food consumption among school adolescents poses severe public health risks, primarily driving the global surge in obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and hypertension. High intake of sugar, unhealthy fats, and salt, paired with low nutritional value, leads to nutrient deficiencies, poor academic performance, dental caries, and potential long-term, chronic health issues.
