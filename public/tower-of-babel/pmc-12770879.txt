Machine learning–based prediction of severe oral mucositis from head and neck cancer radiation therapy
Authors: Kanyapat Buasawat, Sasikarn Chamchod, Todsaporn Fuangrod, Sawanee Suntiwong, Kiattiyot Chantarak, Pasit Jarutatsanangkoon, Thiansin Liamsuwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to develop prediction models for severe oral mucositis (OM) (grade ≥ 3) from head and neck cancer (HNC) radiotherapy (RT) using machine learning (ML) techniques and different types of features, including clinical, dose-volume metric, and radiomic features.Materials and MethodsRetrospective data of 148 HNC RT patients were collected and randomly divided into 80% training and 20% test datasets. Sixteen combinations of feature selection and classification algorithms were used for modeling with different combinations of feature types. The model performance was evaluated using a confusion matrix and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The difference in the error rates obtained from any pair of modeling approaches were evaluated with McNemar’s test.ResultsThe best-performing model was based on the random forest classifier and minimum redundancy maximum relevance feature selection algorithm, achieving the accuracy of 0.930 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.926 to 0.933) and AUROC of 0.901 (95% CI, 0.897 to 0.905). For this model, D45% of the oral cavity, subsite, V75Gy of the parotid glands and total dose to the target were found to be the most important features. All modeling approaches achieved the accuracy and AUROC of at least 0.756 and 0.722 and the error rates obtained by all modeling approaches were insignificantly different.ConclusionThe inclusion of clinical and dose-volumetric features were most promising for ML-based prediction of severe OM, although information obtained prior to dose calculation (clinical or radiomic) may also be used exclusively with insignificantly different error rates.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is an important treatment modality for head and neck cancer (HNC) that can preserve organs, control tumors, and sterilize microscopic tumor lesions spreading beyond surgical margins [1]. Although the goal of RT is to control tumors while sparing normal tissues surrounding the tumors, many patients receive high doses of radiation in large areas, which can cause complications [2]. One of the most common complications from HNC RT is oral mucositis (OM), which impacts on the patient's quality of life and the continuation of treatment [3]. OM, which is an acute toxicity, is a normal tissue injury that can occur when the oral cavity is exposed to radiation doses of around 15–20 Gy of standard fractionated radiotherapy [4]. OM can lead to oral pain, weight loss, dysphagia, feeding tube insertion, hospitalization, and treatment hold [3].

Traditionally, prediction of OM from RT is done using normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models, which are usually based on dose-volume metric predictors associated with oral cavity dose [5-8] and parotid glands dose [9]. However, other factors may also affect the occurrence of OM due to complex interactions of biological, physical, and clinical factors. Some studies found that factors, such as, age, sex, comorbidities, body mass index (BMI) before radiation, RT technique, and smoking status were important for predicting severe OM [9-15].

In recent years, increase of beneficial clinical data and computation power have led to the growing applications of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in radiotherapy. Additionally, there is growing interest in using ML to predict toxicity as it has been shown to have higher prediction performance than using traditional mathematical calculation models [16]. Apart from clinical and dose-volume metric features, radiomic features obtained from medical images, such as, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) images have been shown to be useful in prognostic and predictive modeling [17].

Development of conventional NTCP models for severe OM have been extensively studied [5-7,9-12,14,15]. However, only two ML-based models have been proposed: one using clinical and dose-volume metric features [12] and the other using clinical, dose-volume metric, and radiomic features [14]. The latter study included only 49 samples and only a single classifier and feature selection method was implemented.

In this study, we aimed to develop and compare ML-based prediction models for severe OM (grade ≥ 3) based on different ML-based classifiers and feature selection algorithms using a collection of clinical, dose-volume metric and radiomic features, and also to investigate whether using a single type of features alone (clinical, dose-volumetric, or radiomic features) would be effective for severe OM prediction.

Materials and Methods

1. Patient cohort and endpoint

The flow diagram of this work is shown in Fig. 1. Retrospective data of 148 HNC patients treated with intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc radiation therapy (VMAT) during 2016–2023 at Chulabhorn Hospital were collected. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Chulabhorn Royal Academy (Project Code EC 026/2566). Patient demographics and characteristics are listed in Table 1. The prescribed dose ranged from 60.00 to 74.24 Gy with 2.0–2.2 Gy per fraction and 30–35 fractions. The dose constraints used for treatment planning are shown in Table 2. Toxicity was evaluated weekly during the treatment using Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) criteria by treating radiation oncologists (ROs) with 3–15 years of clinical experience. The endpoint was the occurrence of severe OM (grade ≥ 3) at any time during the treatment. The RTOG grading criteria are as follows: grade 0, no findings; grade 1, painless ulcers, erythema, or mild soreness; grade 2, painful erythema, edema, or ulcers, but can eat; grade 3, painful erythema, edema, or ulcers and cannot eat; and grade 4, requires parenteral or enteral support [18].

The inclusion criteria were first-time histologically confirmed diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma HNC, treatment with IMRT or VMAT with a curative aim, completion of the prescribed RT course, and availability of weekly toxicity evaluations. Exclusion criteria were patients having OM before RT initiation, treatment re-planning before severe OM occurred and dental CT metal artifacts.

2. Imaging and structure delineation

The CT images were acquired pre-treatment for each patient. Three different CT machines were used, including Philips Brilliance Big Bore (n = 135), Siemens SOMATOM go.Open Pro (n = 11), and Siemens Biograph 64 (n = 2). For each CT scanner, the CT Hounsfield unit (HU) calibration was performed using Gammex 467 Tissue Characterization Phantom (Gammex Inc., Middleton, WI, USA) or Advanced Electron Density Phantom (Sun Nuclear Corp., Melbourne, FL, USA) to ensure correct electron densities to be used in dose calculation. The CT protocol included fixed parameters, namely, 512 × 512 pixels, 3 mm slice thickness, 1.2 mm pixel size and 120 kV, as well as variable parameters including the tube current-exposure time product of 245 ± 32 mAs and 50–70 cm3 contrast agent. For Philips Brilliance Big Bore, most of the scans used a fixed 250 mAs, while for the other two scanners the mAs was automatically modulated across different areas of the body with varying thicknesses. The amount of contrast agent was adjusted based on patient weight for consistent image quality.

Parotid glands were manually delineated during the planning stage by the treating ROs. The oral cavity was originally not delineated. Therefore, in this work, an experienced RO with ≥15 years of experience manually delineated the oral cavity for all patients. Fig. 2 shows an example of the delineation of oral cavity structures in CT images. The delineation of the extended oral cavity was partly based on Hoebers et al. [19]. For simplicity and consistency, the extended oral cavity was defined as the area posterior to the internal arches of the mandible and maxilla. This extended oral cavity could be subdivided into the oral tongue and the anterior oropharynx by drawing a vertical line from the posterior hard palate to the hyoid bone, known as the circumvallate line.

3. Feature extraction

The candidate predictors for severe OM were categorized into clinical, dose-volume metric, and radiomic feature types, in total 301 features, as given in the supplementary file. Clinical features included sex, age, tumor subsite, T category, N category, treatment type, concurrent chemotherapy, RT technique, number of fractions, BMI before RT, status of retropharyngeal lymph node metastasis, oral cavity volume, and parotid gland volume. Dose-volume metric features extracted from the treatment plans included the total dose to the target, dose per fraction, Dmax, Dmin, Dmean, D5% – D95% in 10% increments, and V10 Gy – V75Gy in 5 Gy increments for the oral cavity and parotid glands. The clinical features, toxicity scores, and dose-volume metric features were recorded from the Hospital Information System and Eclipse treatment planning system version 13.6 (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Radiomic features for each organ were extracted from the CT images using AccuContour AI-assisted Auto-delineation Tool version 3.1 (Manteia Data Technologies Ltd, Milwaukee, WI, USA), including 14 shape, 18 first-order, and 75 texture-order features (a total of 107 features for each organ of interest). For patients who underwent re-planning after having severe OM, dose-volume metrics and radiomic features were extracted from the original treatment plan. No preprocessing of the CT images was performed. The radiomic plugin of AccuContour was PyRadiomics [20]. Most features extracted by PyRadiomics followed feature definitions as described by the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) [21]. Some differences arose where several equally valid alternatives existed, for which PyRadiomics opted for the one alternative, while the IBSI standard recommended another [21].

It is to note that since parotid gland segmentation was performed by several treating ROs and radiomic features were directly related to organ contours, we have performed a robustness test of parotid gland radiomic features in terms of interobserver delineation variability in our previous study based on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [22]. In that study, parotid gland radiomic features derived from the contours made by three observers were compared as follows: (1) treating ROs vs. a selected RO; (2) treating ROs vs. AI-based autosegmentation using the AccuContour software; (3) a selected RO vs. autosegmentation; and (4) all three observers (treating ROs, a selected RO, and autosegmentation). Of 107 parotid gland radiomic features, the majority of the shape features were found to be robust but also mostly correlated, while most of the first-order and texture features tended to be susceptible to delineation variability. Based on that work, only 42 parotid gland radiomic features that passed the robustness test, i.e., having the ICC of at least 0.75, were included in this study.

4. Model development

The entire dataset was randomly divided into 80% training and 20% test datasets. The random split was conducted until the proportions of severe and non-severe OM cases were similar across the entire, training, and test datasets, as shown in Table 1. For model training, five-fold cross-validation was performed on the training dataset, as explained later in this section. Four modeling approaches were carried out: the approaches A–C using either the clinical features, dose-volume metric features, or radiomic features, respectively, and the approach D pooling all types of features initially before the feature selection process. The latter approach demonstrated the full pool of variables typically done for modeling multivariate radiation-induced toxicity risks [23].

MATLAB version R2023a (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) was used for modeling and statistical analysis throughout this study. The Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between dose-volume metric and radiomic features. For a pair of features with strong correlation (r ≥ 0.80), only one of the features was selected for the modeling to mitigate multicollinearity, redundancy, and model instability. Then, four feature selection algorithms were employed to reduce model overfitting, including (1) minimum redundancy maximum relevance (MRMR) (function “fscmrmr”), (2) Relief (function “relieff”), (3) sequential (function “sequentialfs”), and (4) model-based permuted predictor importance for random forest (MBRF) (function “oobPermutedPredictorImportance”) [24].

Additionally, four ML classification algorithms were used for the modeling, namely, (1) logistic regression (LR) (function “fitglm”), (2) support vector machine (SVM) (function “fitcsvm”), (3) k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) (function “fitcknn”), and (4) random forest (RF) (function “fitcensemble”) [24]. As a result, each classification algorithm was paired with four feature selection methods, resulting in 16 scenarios for each modeling approach, i.e., a total of 64 models for the investigation.

Regarding feature selection, MRMR, Relief, and MBRF used statistical measures to assign an importance score to each feature, while the sequential algorithm selected a subset of features that best predicted the outcome by sequentially forward selection of features until there was no improvement in prediction. For the algorithms that ranked feature importance scores (MRMR, Relief, and MBRF), only features with non-zero importance scores were considered. Then, all sets of most important features were used for training the ML model. For example, if there were n features with non-zero importance scores, n sets of most important features with the number of elements ranging from 1 to n, were used to train an ML model. The model hyperparameters were randomly searched for 30 times. For each set of features and hyperparameters, the model was trained using five-fold cross-validation. The set of features and hyperparameters that gave the highest accuracy and F1 score from the five-fold cross-validation was used in the final model of each modeling approach.

It is also to note that for filter-typed feature selection algorithms, i.e., MRMR and Relief, the same set of features was first selected by the algorithm regardless of which ML algorithm was used for training. However, since we implemented the sequential selection of the most important features in the model training, the final set of selected features may be different depending on the ML algorithm used, as shown in the supplementary file.

5. Model evaluation

To evaluate the model performance during model training with five-fold cross-validation and blind testing on the test dataset, we generated a confusion matrix containing the following elements. True positive (TP): The number of events in which the actual values and the predicted values are both positive; false positive (FP): The number of events in which the actual values are negative, but the predicted values are positive; false negative (FN): The number of events in which the actual values are positive, but the predicted values are negative; true negative (TN): The number of events in which the actual values and the predicted values are both negative.

The closer the AUROC is to 1, the better the classification model is.

For model testing, the model with the highest AUROC and accuracy on the test dataset was considered the best model of each modeling approach. Confidence intervals (CI) of the model performance metrics were evaluated by conducting bootstrapping (n = 1,000) on the test dataset. The 95% CI provided a measure of the uncertainty associated with the model prediction.

Moreover, we investigated if there was statistically significant difference between the error rates produced by any pair of models using McNemar's test (α = 0.05) [26]. A p-value exceeding 0.05 suggested that there was no statistically significant difference between the error rates produced by both models.

Finally, to identify the feature importance that was comparable between models, the mean absolute Shapley values were calculated using the “shapley” function in MATLAB. The Shapley value of a feature at a chosen query point corresponds to the deviation of the prediction from the average prediction due to that feature [27]. A query point refers to a row of input features for the model. In this work, we used all data (training and test data) as query points and evaluated the mean absolute Shapley values. A feature with a larger contribution to the output of the model resulted in a higher mean absolute Shapley value.

Results

The selected features, hyperparameters, and model performance of all 64 final models are given in the supplementary file. Tables 3 and 4 show the performance metrics of the best model of each modeling approach obtained from the five-fold cross-validation and blind testing, respectively. Fig. 3 shows the mean absolute Shapley value to demonstrate the feature importance for the best-performing model of each modeling approach. The subsite was commonly found to contribute to the prediction of the models that included clinical features (models A and D). Similarly, D45% of the oral cavity was commonly found to be one of the largest contributors to the prediction of the models that included dose-volumetric features (models B and D).

The overall best-performing model was the approach D with the RF classifier and the MRMR feature selection algorithm. This model achieved the highest accuracy and AUROC of 0.930 (95% CI, 0.926 to 0.933) and 0.901 (95% CI, 0.897 to 0.905), respectively. The most important features were D45% of the oral cavity, the subsite, V75Gy of the parotid glands, the total dose to the target, V5Gy of the parotid glands, V20Gy of the oral cavity, and the RT technique, respectively (Fig. 3).

As shown in Table 4, the best-performing model of each modeling approach was based on the RF classifier with either the MRMR or MBRF feature selection algorithm. All modeling approaches could achieve the AUROCs of at least 0.722 with the numbers of selected features ranging from 8 to 9.

Table 5 shows the p-values obtained from the comparison between the error rates produced by any pair of models using McNemar’s test. We found that the differences in the error rates between any pair of modeling approaches were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

For the overall best-performing model (approach D), for which D45% of the oral cavity (OCD45) was identified as the most important dose-volumetric feature (Fig. 3), we further explored a dose constraint that could help to avoid severe OM. In this analysis, we investigated the threshold for OCD45, above which the prediction was likely to be positive (severe OM) (predicted positive rate ≥ 0.5) and the model was likely to classify correctly (AUROC ≥ 0.7).

Fig. 4 shows the plot of the predicted positive rate and AUROC against the threshold for OCD45. Based on this assumption, OCD45 ≥ ~54 Gy was likely to result in severe OM. Therefore, the recommended constraint for D45% of the oral cavity could be 54 Gy.

Discussion and Conclusion

This study focused on developing ML-based prediction models for severe OM (grade ≥ 3) from HNC IMRT or VMAT using four modeling approaches based on different types of features (clinical, dose-volume metric, and radiomic). Model training was done using four different ML classifiers (LR, SVM, k-NN, and RF) coupled with four different feature selection algorithms (MRMR, relief, sequential, and MBRF).

The process of feature selection was crucial for minimizing the number of irrelevant and redundant features by selecting the most informative predictors, resulting in improvement of the model performance. In this work, we found that the RF classifier coupled with either the MRMR or MBRF feature selection algorithm showed the best performance for all modeling approaches, with the number of selected features in the range of 8–9.

Fig. 3 indicated that the subsite was an important clinical feature, as it was the only clinical feature consistently selected by all models that included clinical features. Although most of our data had oral cavity cancer (37.8%), the proportions of other subsites were not negligibly small, e.g., 22.3% had cancer in the nasopharynx and 12.8% in the oropharynx. Our findings that the subsite was the important feature aligned with that reported by Elting et al. [28] that the risk of developing OM was higher in those who had oral cavity or oropharynx primaries compared with those with larynx or hypopharynx primaries (99% vs. 64%; p < 0.001). Additionally, D45% of the oral cavity was found to be the important dose-volume metric feature consistently selected by both the approaches B and D.

The modeling approach D, which performed best, demonstrated the full pool of variables similar to development of multivariate radiation-induced toxicity risk prediction models that usually included a pool of clinical and dose-volumetric features [23]. The addition of radiomic features may have offered the opportunity to improve the performance of the predictive model. However, due to our feature selection strategy and the random selection of the hyperparameters used for training the models, the final set of features of the overall best-performing model (approach D) did not contain any radiomic features.

Among all the classification algorithms investigated, the RF classifier yielded the best performance for all modeling approaches. RF selected and scored features after passing through the initial feature selection process, which may seem like a repetition of feature selection, resulting in the models choosing only the most crucial features for the prediction. This finding was consistent with the study of Dean et al. [12], who employed ML and spatial dose metrics to predict severe OM and demonstrated that RF emerged as the top-performing algorithm.

Overall, the models developed using dose-volume metric features (approaches B and D) achieved the accuracies and AUROCs in the ranges of 0.825–0.930 and 0.798–0.901, respectively, while those neglecting dose-volume metric features (approaches A and C) yielded the accuracies and AUROCs in the ranges of 0.756–0.791 and 0.722–0.747, respectively. This result indicated that in the absence of dose-volume metric features, the models showed slightly lower performance compared to the models that included dose-volume metric features. Nevertheless, from McNemar’s test, the error rates between any pair of modeling approaches were not significantly different.

Comparing with the other similar studies, our best-performing model (approach D with RF and MRMR), which had the AUROC of 0.901, outperformed the previously reported conventional NTCP models with the AUROCs ranging from 0.62 to 0.84 [5,7,9-12,15]. Another similar study was that reported by Agheli et al. [14], who employed RF with MRMR feature selection and CT-based radiomic features combined with dose-volume metric and clinical features to predict severe OM during and 2 months after IMRT treatment. Their findings were similar to ours in terms of that using all feature types with the RF classifier and MRMR feature selection algorithm yielded the best performance, with an AUROC of 0.92 and an accuracy of 0.90. In our study, we found that the models that used only dose-volume metric features were superior to those using only clinical or radiomic features. In contrast, in the study of Agheli et al. [14], the model that used only dose-volume metric features had the lowest performance. However, their model was trained only on 39 patients with 20 selected features, while our study used a larger cohort and the best model contained only eight features and yielded similar performance on the cross-validation and test datasets (0.67% difference in the AUROCs), as shown in Tables 3 and 4, respectively, which suggested that our model was not overfitted.

Considering the relatively high predictive performance of the final model (AUROC of 0.901), it may enable early prediction of severe OM during the treatment planning process. This can assist physicians in creating personalized and effective treatment plans, such as, adjusting the radiation dose to the oral cavity and parotid glands, providing supportive care in oral hygiene, administering medications like Difflam or Morphine to alleviate symptoms based on individual needs and risks, as well as selecting patients to a different treatment modality such as proton therapy. As exemplified in Fig. 4, a dose constraint of D45% of the oral cavity of ~54 Gy could be recommended based on the high proportion of predicted positives together with the high AUROC values. A similar approach could be done with different thresholds of predicted positive rate and AUROC depending on the acceptance levels of each center.

Some limitations of this study should be mentioned. Firstly, it was a retrospective study, which lacked certain data that could contribute to the induction of OM, such as oral hygiene, comorbidities, smoking status, and salivary secretory function. This data was not typically recorded in our institution. In future studies, the development of a prospective study is essential to include additional data in the model development, which could potentially improve the predictive performance of the models.

Secondly, the radiomic features utilized in this study were limited to original features without any filtering techniques, such as, Laplace and Wavelet. Including filtering techniques may provide additional power in predicting outcomes, as they have the potential to capture specific anatomical characteristics that could contribute to the prediction process. Also, involving MR-based radiomic features in the modeling could improve the model performance, as has been shown by Sheikh et al [29].

Another issue with radiomics was that different imaging protocols may affect radiomic feature values. Varghese et al. [30] reported that changes in slice thickness affected the reproducibility of radiomic features more intensely compared to other imaging variables, and changes in kVp and mAs affected mainly higher-order texture features, such as, image transformation. In this work, although three different CT scanners were used for the CT simulation, the CT HU calibration was performed for each CT scanner to ensure correct HUs. Moreover, most of other imaging variables were fixed except the tube current–exposure time product (mAs) and the amount of contrast agent. Regarding mAs, previous studies reported that variable mAs was unlikely to have a large effect on radiomic features extracted from CT images [31] and the most affected features were higher-order features [30] not included in this study. In addition, the effect of contrast agent on HNC IMRT planning has been reported to be tolerably small [32], reflecting the relatively similar HUs between CT images with and without contrast agent enhancement for this treatment site.

The imbalance between the proportions of negative and positive cases (66.2% vs. 33.8%) could hinder the developed models to effectively learn and predict positive results (severe OM) as accurately as negative results. Consequently, the true positive rate values of some of the modeling approaches were lower than the other metrics. Also, the data used in this study was from a single institution, thus, lacking external validation potentially introducing bias. Future work should address these limitations.

Lastly, there are several grading criteria for OM. The most used ones are those established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [33], the RTOG, and the US National Institutes of Health (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE]) [34]. While the WHO scale was derived from functional and objective parameters, and the current version of the CTCAE criteria completely depend on the patient-reported outcome [18], the RTOG criteria require both patient and clinical input. The RTOG criteria were chosen in this work as they were applied in our clinic. Moreover, it has been reported that when severe OM was the primary outcome (grade ≥ 3 in the RTOG scale criteria), the three scales performed similarly, in contrast to when each score or the most severe form of OM (grade 4) were considered [18].

In summary, the best-performing model for predicting severe OM after IMRT or VMAT was based on the RF classifier and MRMR feature selection algorithm with the accuracy and AUROC of 0.930 and 0.901, respectively. For this model, D45% of the oral cavity, subsite, V75Gy of the parotid glands and the total dose to the target were identified as the most important features. The constraint for D45% of the oral cavity at 54 Gy could be recommended from this study. Our results suggested that the inclusion of clinical and dose-volumetric features was most promising for ML-based prediction of severe OM, although information obtained prior to dose calculation (clinical or radiomic features) may also be used exclusively with insignificantly different error rates.
