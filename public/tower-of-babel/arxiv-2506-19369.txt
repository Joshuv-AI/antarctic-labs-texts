Gottesman–Knill Limit on One-way Communication Complexity: Tracing the Quantum Advantage down to Magic
Snehasish Roy Chowdhury, Sahil Gopalkrishna Naik, Ananya Chakraborty, Ram Krishna Patra, Subhendu B. Ghosh, Pratik Ghosal, Manik Banik, Ananda G. Maity

Abstract
A recent influential result by Frenkel and Weiner establishes that in presence of shared randomness (SR), any input-output correlation, with a classical input provided to one party and a classical output produced by a distant party, achievable with a d d -dimensional quantum system can always be reproduced by a d d -dimensional classical system. In contrast, quantum systems are known to offer advantages in communication complexity tasks, which consider an additional input variable to the second party. Here, we show that, in presence of SR, any one-way communication complexity protocol implemented using a prime-dimensional quantum system can always be simulated exactly by communicating a classical system of the same dimension, whenever quantum protocols are restricted to stabilizer state preparations and stabilizer measurements. In direct analogy with the Gottesman-Knill theorem in quantum computation, which attributes quantum advantage to non-stabilizer (or magic) resources, our result identifies the same resources as essential for realizing quantum advantage in one-way communication complexity. We further present explicit tasks where ‘minimal magic’ suffices to offer a provable quantum advantage, underscoring the efficient use of such resources in communication complexity.



Introduction.–
The Second Quantum Revolution harnesses intrinsic non-classical features of quantum systems to devise novel technologies that outperform classical methods
Dowling and Milburn 2003
;
Deutsch 2020
;
Aspect 2023
. Identifying the specific resources responsible for a given quantum advantage is therefore of utmost importance: foundationally, it pinpoints the true non-classical origin of the quantum advantage, while practically, it guides the development of technologies that can exploit this advantage. A striking illustration comes from the seminal Gottesman-Knill theorem, which establishes that quantum circuits restricted to Clifford operations – despite generating large-scale superposition and entanglement – allow efficient classical simulation
Gottesman 1998
. However, the inclusion of a single non-Clifford element, such as the T-gate, renders the system universal and enables quantum advantage, identifying the T-gate as a key resource or element of ‘magic’ for quantum computation
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
;
Knill 2005
;
Campbell et al. 2012
;
Bravyi and Gosset 2016
;
Howard and Campbell 2017
;
Bravyi et al. 2019
. In this work, we demonstrate that the same notion of magic underlies the quantum advantage in communication complexity tasks. Specifically, for all prime dimensions, we show that in the presence of SR, any one-way
d
-level quantum communication protocol restricted to stabilizer state preparations and stabilizer measurements can always be simulated by a
d
-level classical system.

Communication complexity, originally introduced by Yao, investigates how less information two or more distant parties must exchange to evaluate a joint function of their private inputs
Yao 1979
(see also
Yao 1982
;
Kushilevitz and Nisan 1996
;
Kushilevitz 1997
). In the simplest setup, two spatially separated parties, traditionally named Alice and Bob, are tasked with jointly computing a global function of
x
and
y
independently drawn from finite input alphabets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
that are respectively distributed to Alice and Bob. To achieve this, the parties may exchange physical systems, subject to communication constraints, and are often allowed to access pre-classical shared correlation (also called SR)
Ambainis et al. 2009
;
Bavarian et al. 2014
;
Canonne et al. 2015
. Quantum communication complexity generalizes this framework by introducing quantum resources. In Yao’s quantum model
Yao 1993
, Alice is allowed to communicate quantum particles to Bob; whereas in the entanglement-assisted model of Cleve and Buhrman
Cleve and Buhrman 1997
, the parties share entanglement and communicate classically, gaining an advantage from the non-local correlations of the shared state
Buhrman et al. 2010
. A variant of this problem, first studied by Wisener by the name Conjugate Coding
Wiesner 1983
and later rediscovered by Ambianis
et al.
as Random Access Codes (RACs)
Ambainis et al. 2002
, considers optimizing the performance of the communication task while keeping the communication cost fixed, and show that qubit communication can be advantageous over its classical counterpart in such tasks.

Naturally, the question arises: which non-classical resources underlie the quantum advantage in communication complexity tasks? This question has been explored from various perspectives, with prior works emphasizing the roles of coherent superposition in encoding and measurement incompatibility in decoding
Brukner et al. 2004
;
Tavakoli et al. 2020
;
Carmeli et al. 2020
;
Gupta et al. 2023
;
Chakraborty et al. 2025
. Here, we demonstrate that the quantum advantage is more fundamentally rooted in the use of non-stabilizer resources—specifically, non-stabilizer states for encoding and/or non-stabilizer measurements for decoding. To establish this claim, we show that when encodings are restricted to stabilizer states and decodings are restricted to Clifford operations, all the input-output correlations obtained in one-way communication complexity setup by communicating a prime dimensional quantum system can always be reproduced exactly by communicating classical system of same dimension, provided classical shared randomness (SR) is available between the sender and the receiver. Therefore, the so-called ‘magic’ resource, exemplified by the T-gate or its associated T-state, enables performance beyond classical limits in RACs and, more broadly, in one-way communication complexity tasks. To this end we identify tasks that show quantum advantage with ‘minimal magic’; and discuss broader implications and potential generalizations of our results.

One-way communication complexity.–
Two distant parties, Alice and Bob, receive classical inputs
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}

f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{B}

p({\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{XY}})\equiv\{p(b|x,y)\}

to Bob based on her input and the shared variable, whereas Bob produces an output

that depends on the message received from Alice, his input
y
, and the shared variable
\lambda
. In the quantum case, Alice instead communicates a quantum state
\rho_{(x,\lambda)}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, which Bob measures using a POVM

\mathcal{E}(\cdot)

\mathbf{I}_{d}
denotes the d-dimensional identity operator. He then computes the output
b=b(k,y,\lambda)
based on the measurement outcome
k

y
, and

Wiesner 1983
;
Ambainis et al. 2002
and their variants
Ambainis et al. 2009

Spekkens et al. 2009

Banik et al. 2015
;
Perry et al. 2015
;
Tavakoli et al. 2015
;

demonstrate that quantum communication can outperform classical strategies in such one-way communication complexity tasks. This quantum advantage is quite remarkable given the fundamental no-go result of Holevo, which asserts that in standard Shannon-type communication scenario involving no input at receiver’s end

, quantum communication performs no better than its classical counterpart
Holevo 1973
. More recently, an extension of this no-go result by Frenkel and Weiner establishes that, in presence of SR, any input-output correlation achievable with a
d
-level quantum system can also be reproduced by a
d
-level classical system
Frenkel and Weiner 2015
. In remainder of this paper we investigate the specific quantum resources responsible for the quantum advantage in communication complexity.

Results.–
We denote by
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{C}}^{d}[\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{Y}]

\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{Q}}^{d}[\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}\mid\mathcal{Y}]

d

{\bf x}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\cdots d-1\}^{n}
, and Bob, upon receiving input

for all
d\geq 2
. Apart from classical and quantum strategies, one can also define the class of Clifford-limited (or stabilizer-limited) strategies for one-way communication complexity tasks.

Definition 1
.

[Stabilizer-limited strategy] A
d
-level stabilizer-limited strategy for one-way communication complexity restricts Alice’s encoding to stabilizer states
\mathrm{St}_{d}
, and Bob’s decodings to stabilizer-preserving operations.

The classical, quantum, and stabilizer-limited strategies employed by Alice and Bob can further be categorized into three types: (i) pure (or extremal) strategies, where Alice and Bob follow definite encodings and decodings independently; (ii) mixed strategies, which invokes local randomness over pure strategies; and (iii) shared strategies, that further invokes SR between Alice and Bob, ensuring the respective sets of strategies and consequently the respective sets of simulable correlations to be convex. While formal definitions of theses strategies are deferred to the Appendix, we highlight here a key observation specific to the extremal stabilizer-limited strategies.

Observation 1
.

In an extremal strategy of a
d

of
d(d+1)

d+1

A natural question is whether the presence of magic always guarantees a quantum advantage in communication complexity tasks. Our next result identifies a scenario where even a ‘minimal magic’ suffices to achieve such an advantage. Before presenting our result, we note that various measures have been proposed to quantify magic in non-stabilizer states

Discussions.–
Building on the seminal Gottesman–Knill theorem, the importance of non-stabilizer resources (magic) has been extensively studied in the context of quantum computational advantage, classical simulation bounds
Gottesman and Chuang 1999
;
Howard et al. 2014
;
Bravyi and Gosset 2016
;
Howard and Campbell 2017
;
Bravyi et al. 2018
;
Bu and Koh 2019
, quantum homomorphic encryption
Broadbent and Jeffery 2015
, quantum machine learning
Hinsche et al. 2023
, and quantum channel capacities
Bu and Jaffe 2025
. Adding to this line of work, our Theorem
1
identifies magic as a necessary resource for achieving quantum advantage in one-way communication complexity tasks. This result extends the Frenkel–Weiner no-go theorem
Frenkel and Weiner 2015
to the communication complexity setting. On the other hand, Theorem
2
&
2E
show that even ‘minimal magic’ in the encoding suffices to outperform classical protocols in certain communication complexity tasks. This opens an experimental pathway for certifying the presence of magic resources in prepare-and-measure scenario.

Our work also opens several interesting directions for future research. Firstly, exploring the status of Theorem
1
in communication complexity tasks involving multiple distant parties remains an open problem. Additionally, Theorems
2
&
2E
focus on
2
-level RACs only. Extending this analysis to higher-level RACs and, more broadly, to other classes of communication complexity tasks could yield further valuable insights.

Theorem 2E
.

Arbitrarily small nonzero magic in any one of the encoding states ensures quantum advantage in the
3\mapsto 1
RAC task, even when all other encoding states are restricted to stabilizer set and all decodings are limited to Clifford operations.

Proof.

In
3\mapsto 1
RAC, Alice receives a random
3
-bit string
\textbf{x}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\in\{0,1\}^{3}
, and Bob has to output
b=x_{y}\in\{0,1\}
based on his random input
y\in\{1,2,3\}
. Likewise,
2\mapsto 1
case, let Alice encode her inputs in qubit states of the form
\textbf{x}\to\psi_{\textbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\vec{n}_{\textbf{x}}\cdot\sigma)
. To optimize the success probability, Alice’s encodings must be chosen properly depending on Bob’s decoding strategies. Within Clifford-restricted scenario, we analyses three three different cases for Bob decoding.

I.
Irrespective of the input
y
, Bob always performs a fixed decoding measurement (say
\mathrm{P}_{(1,0)}
). However, this strategy—when comprised with any encoding on Alice’s side—cannot surpass the optimal classical success probability of
3/4
. This limitation stems from the fact that achieving a quantum advantage in RAC tasks requires measurement incompatibility at the decoding stage

\textbf{x}\to\psi_{\textbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+(-1)^{\text{Maj}(\textbf{x})}\mathrm{P}_{(1,0)})

3/4

denotes the majority function
Ambainis et al. 2024
.

Appendix B: Formal Definitions

General quantum measurement, formally known as positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
Kraus 1983
, is defined as a collection of positive operators adding up-to the identity, i.e.
M\equiv\{\pi_{k}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \pi_{k}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\penalty\ \&\penalty\ \sum_{k}\pi_{k}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\}_{k=1}^{l}
. Set of effects forms a convex set, and the set of unnormalized effects forms a convex cone
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. An effect is said to be
ray extremal
if it can not be expressed as conical combination of other rays, i.e.
\pi^{\text{ray-ext}}=p_{1}\pi^{1}+p_{2}\pi^{2}
with
p_{1},p_{2}\geq 0
implies either
\pi^{1}=\pi^{2}=\pi^{\text{ray-ext}}
or one of the
p_{i}
’s is zero. Note that, all the extreme effects are not ray extremal. For instance,
\mathbf{I}_{d}
is extremal in the set of effects but is not ray extremal. In fact, ray extremal effects are exactly the rank-
1
effects. One can further introduce the notion of extremal measurements.

Definition 2
.

[Extremal POVM, Rank-
1
extremal POVM] A
k
outcome POVM
\mathrm{M}\equiv\{\pi_{a}\}_{a=1}^{k}
is called an extremal POVM if all the non-zero effects are extremal and linearly independent. Furthermore, it is called a rank-
1
extremal POVM if all the non-zero effects are rank-1 (i.e. ray extremal) and linearly independent (Section
2.3.3
Ref.
Watrous 2018
).

Note that, all extremal POVMs are not rank-
1
extremal. For instance, consider the qutrit measurement
M_{0|12}=\{\ket{0}\bra{0},\ket{1}\bra{1}+\ket{2}\bra{2}\}
, which is an extremal POVM, but not rank-
1
extremal. With this we can now formally define pure, mixed, and shared strategies for a generic one-way communication complexity task employed within classical, quantum, and stabilizer-limited scenarios.

Definition 3
.

[Pure Strategies]

(a)
[Classical pure strategies] A
d
-level pure classical encoding-decoding strategy is an ordered tuple
(\mathbb{E},D_{1},\cdots,D_{|\mathcal{Y}|})
that consists of an encoding function
\mathbb{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1,\cdots,d-1\}
and
|\mathcal{Y}|
decoding functions
D_{i}:\{0,1,\cdots,d-1\}\to\mathcal{B}

Ambainis et al. 2009
.

(b)
[Quantum pure strategies] A
d
-level pure quantum encoding-decoding strategy is an ordered tuple
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{Q}},M^{\mathrm{Q}}_{1},\cdots,M^{\mathrm{Q}}_{|\mathcal{Y}|},D_{1},\cdots,D_{|\mathcal{Y}|})
that consists of pure quantum state preparations
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{Q}}:\mathcal{X}\to\{\ket{\psi_{x}}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
|\mathcal{Y}|
decoding measurements
M^{\mathrm{Q}}_{i}:\{\ket{\psi_{x}}\}\to\mathcal{K}_{i}
, and
|\mathcal{Y}|
classical post-processing functions
D_{i}:\mathcal{K}_{i}\to\mathcal{B}
, where
M^{\mathrm{Q}}_{i}
’s are quantum extremal POVMs (Definition
2
) with
\mathcal{K}_{i}
non-zero effects.

(c)
[Stabilizer-restricted pure strategies] A
d
-level pure stabilizer-restricted encoding-decoding strategy is an ordered tuple
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{St}},M^{\mathrm{St}}_{1},\cdots,M^{\mathrm{St}}_{|\mathcal{Y}|},D_{1},\cdots,D_{|\mathcal{Y}|})
that consists of pure stabilizer state preparations
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{St}}:\mathcal{X}\to\{\ket{\psi_{x}}\}\subset\mathrm{St}_{d}
,
|\mathcal{Y}|
decoding measurements
M^{\mathrm{St}}_{i}:\{\ket{\psi_{x}}\}\to\mathcal{K}_{i}
, and
|\mathcal{Y}|
classical post-processing functions
D_{i}:\mathcal{K}_{i}\to\mathcal{B}
, where
M^{\mathrm{St}}_{i}
’s are stabilizer preserving extremal POVMs with
\mathcal{K}_{i}
non-zero effects.

Definition 4
.

[Mixed
\&
Shared strategies] Such strategies invoke random variables
(\lambda_{A},\lambda_{B})\in\Lambda_{A}\times\Lambda_{B}
, sampled according to a probability distribution
p(\lambda_{A},\lambda_{B})
, based on which Alice and Bob respectively randomize their pure encoding and decoding strategies. Whenever, the distribution is in product form, i.e.
p(\lambda_{A},\lambda_{B})=p(\lambda_{A})p(\lambda_{B})
it corresponds to a mixed strategy, else it is called a shared strategy.
