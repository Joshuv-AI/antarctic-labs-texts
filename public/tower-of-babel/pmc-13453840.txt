The Open Jacket Ross: Initial description and ex vivo biomechanical evaluation of neosinus openings in the pulmonary autograft inclusion jacket
Authors: Alexander K Reed, Gabe Weininger, Katrina Liou, Y Joseph Woo
Published: 2026

Creation of neosinuses in the synthetic inclusion graft of a pulmonary autograft.

Central Message

Incorporating neosinus openings in the pulmonary autograft inclusion graft may allow physiologic neoaortic sinus function while maintaining commissural, annular, and sinotubular junctional support.

Case

A 52-year-old woman was referred for severe, symptomatic bicuspid aortic valve stenosis with an invasive mean gradient of 54 mm Hg. Preoperative echocardiography revealed normal biventricular function, a Sievers type 1 left-right bicuspid valve with severe aortic stenosis, and an aortic valve area of 0.59 cm2. After a thorough discussion regarding her various surgical options, she elected to proceed with the Ross procedure.

The pulmonic homograft was thawed and prepared. The patient then underwent median sternotomy and was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). The pulmonic valve was harvested using the beating heart technique.1 The pulmonic homograft was then anastomosed sequentially to the pulmonary artery and right ventricular outflow tract. The patient was weaned off CPB as described in our resequenced Ross technique to significantly reduce aortic cross-clamp and CPB times.1

The autograft was trimmed proximally and distally to minimize native right ventricular and pulmonary arterial tissue and then positioned within a 26 mm collagen-impregnated woven double velour polyester straight graft after sizing with a Freestyle sizer (Medtronic). We routinely enclose the autograft within a straight graft that is 3 mm larger than the autograft annulus. The proximal autograft and synthetic inclusion jacket suture line was completed with running 5-0 polypropylene suture. Next, the commissures were suspended using three 5-0 polypropylene sutures in the autograft pulmonary artery just above the commissures, removing any laxity in the autograft. Saline static testing revealed excellent valve geometry. If autograft insufficiency is present, commissural position may be modified to optimize valve competency. Anticommissural plication can be performed to address a discrepancy between the diameter of the distal autograft and synthetic jacket.2 The distal autograft suture line was completed by running the previously placed commissural sutures. By performing this suture line within the jacket with approximately 2 cm of synthetic graft above, this residual graft serves as a built-in interposition graft to the native distal aorta.

Next, three large circular openings were cut in the polyester graft over the native pulmonary sinuses (Video 1). By cutting these openings much wider than the coronary buttons, we enable full excursion of the neoaortic root during systole and diastole, effectively re-creating normal sinuses while maintaining commissural stability with annular and sinotubular junctional (STJ) support. Saline pressure testing before implantation demonstrated physiologic expansion of the sinuses with valve loading (Video 2).

Once preparation of the autograft was complete, CPB was reinitiated, the aortic cross-clamp was applied, and the heart was arrested. The ascending aorta, noncoronary sinus, and aortic valve leaflets were excised. Annular sutures were placed, and the autograft composite was seated. The coronary buttons were then reimplanted and the distal aortic anastomosis completed in the standard fashion. The heart was de-aired, the cross-clamp was removed, and the heart was reperfused. The patient was weaned from CPB with excellent hemodynamics and normal biventricular function. Postbypass transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated no aortic insufficiency or stenosis. Cross-clamp and CPB times were 80 and 150 minutes, respectively. The complete operation is depicted in Video 3.

Interval transthoracic echocardiography two months after surgery demonstrated normal function, no autograft insufficiency, and a mean neoaortic valve gradient of 4.1 mm Hg. Routine postoperative computed tomography angiogram seven months after surgery demonstrated native-appearing sinus geometry (Figure 1) with no evidence of autograft dilatation compared with early postoperative imaging.

Ex Vivo Assessment

To investigate the biomechanical performance of this technique, we performed ex vivo testing of a jacketed porcine pulmonary autograft with neosinuses in our custom, 3-dimensionally (3D) printed left heart simulator that we have previously described (Figure 2, A).2 A porcine pulmonic autograft was harvested and anastomosed within a straight graft three millimeters larger than the annulus with large neosinuses fashioned as described above, including anticommissural plication. The composite autograft was sutured to a 3D-printed conduit mount proximally using 4-0 polypropylene suture and fixed distally to a flanged, 3D-printed outflow mount (Figure 2, B). The valve was assessed under systemic conditions with a mean arterial pressure of 100 mm Hg, heart rate of 70 bpm, and cardiac output of 5.7 L/minute in the absence of coronary flow given the pilot nature of this evaluation. Valve performance was excellent with a mean gradient of 4.48 mm Hg and a regurgitant fraction of 2.11%. Neoaortic strain, calculated as echocardiographic (systolic diameter – diastolic diameter) / diastolic diameter, was 2.08%. Root strain was 3.69% when enclosed with external annular and STJ synthetic support only, 0.03% in a standard straight graft inclusion technique without neosinuses, and 2.12% when unsupported.

A, Diagram of ex vivo left heart simulator. B, Porcine pulmonic root implanted into a straight polyester graft with neosinuses mounted in 3-dimensionally-printed mounts. C, En face view of jacketed porcine pulmonic root during diastole.

Discussion

The Ross procedure has developed resurging interest due to its ability to restore a normal lifespan and limit surgical reintervention required by bioprosthetic valve replacements.3 The synthetic inclusion technique has become a popular method to prevent late dilation and ultimate autograft failure.4 Although the use of a straight or Valsalva graft may provide more uniform circumferential support, there are concerns that implanting the autograft in a synthetic graft may constrain normal sinus and leaflet mechanics.5 The optimal inclusion graft configuration remains unclear. As such, we present a new variation of the inclusion technique that enables more physiologic, natural autograft sinus motion throughout the cardiac cycle by cutting three large neosinus openings in the synthetic inclusion graft. We believe combined annular, STJ, and commissural support is ideal to preserve long-term autograft durability, making this technique well suited for both stenotic and regurgitant aortic valve pathology. The polyester columns remain wide enough to provide consistent commissural support even with torsional movement of the aortic root throughout the cardiac cycle. Pilot ex vivo testing demonstrated excellent valve function and normal-appearing root excursion, whereas interval clinical imaging revealed normal-appearing neoaortic sinus geometry after seven months. This “Open Jacket” technique may achieve a Goldilocks effect, balancing the need for commissural, annular, and sinotubular support while enabling normal sinus excursion. Biomechanical studies are underway to validate and optimize this technique.
