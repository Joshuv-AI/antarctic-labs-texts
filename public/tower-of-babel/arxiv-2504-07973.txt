Dynamical structure of AGM over finite fields with order congruent to 5 mod 8 5\bmod 8
Daniel A. N. Vargas

Abstract
. Motivated by classical works of Gauss and Euler on the AGM, Ono and his collaborators [ 2 , 5 ] investigated the union of AGM sequences over finite fields 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} , where q ≡ 3 mod 4 q\equiv 3\bmod 4 , which they refer to as swarms of jellyfish. A recent preprint [ 3 ] extends some of their results to all finite fields with odd characteristic. For q ≡ 5 mod 8 q\equiv 5\bmod 8 , we reveal finer details about the structure of the connected components, which turn out to be variants of jellyfish with longer and branched tentacles. Moreover, we determine the total population of these swarms in terms of the celebrated base “congruent number” elliptic curve.

Introduction and statements of results

Given two nonnegative numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, their arithmetic and geometric means are
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
and
\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
, respectively. Motivated by Gauss and Euler, the iterated sequence of arithmetic-geometric means

(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2},\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\right)\mapsto\left(\frac{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}+\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}{2},\sqrt{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}\right)\mapsto\cdots

has been well-studied over the real numbers. The two entries of the ordered pairs rapidly approach the same limit
[
1
]
, called the
arithmetic-geometric mean
. Using this process, Gauss showed that if
a_{0}=1
,
b_{0}=\sqrt{2}
,
a_{n+1}:=\frac{a_{n}+b_{n}}{2}
, and
b_{n+1}:=\sqrt{a_{n}b_{n}}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{a_{n}}^{2}}{1-\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{i-1}({a_{i}}^{2}-{b_{i}}^{2})}=\pi

with remarkably fast convergence, and he also connected the arithmetic-geometric mean to elliptic integrals
[
1
]
.

At first glance, the recursive relation
b_{n+1}:=\sqrt{a_{n}b_{n}}
presents both an obstacle and a potential ambiguity: If
a_{n}b_{n}
is negative, it does not have any square roots in
\mathbb{R}
, and if
a_{n}b_{n}
is positive, it has two real square roots, making it unclear which to choose. Conveniently, these two issues do not arise: Because
-1
is not a square in
\mathbb{R}
, we know that exactly one choice of
b_{n+1}=\pm\sqrt{a_{n}b_{n}}
results in
a_{n+1}b_{n+1}
being a square, and always choosing this value for
b_{n+1}
makes the arithmetic-geometric mean well-defined over
\mathbb{R}
.

In analogy with the classical study of AGM sequences, recent works have focused on finite field analogues. For finite fields with order congruent to 3 modulo 4, the residue class
-1
is not a quadratic residue, as in the case of
\mathbb{R}
. In this case, the authors of
[
2
]
show that the disjoint union of all AGM sequences is a directed graph where each graph looks like a “jellyfish”, which will be explained shortly. Here, we extend this to fields
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
q\equiv 1\bmod 4
, especially focusing on
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
.

The central object of this paper is the arithmetic-geometric mean (AGM) process over an arbitrary field with odd characteristic. This has been previously investigated in
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
, but this paper will provide additional results, with a focus on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
for
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
. Much like
[
2
]

5
]

where any of

\alpha+\beta

Specifically, over any field

Recent works of Griffin, McSpirit, Ono, Saikia, and Tsai

An indispensable common feature in both settings above is that one can devise a rule to
canonically
advance from a node to the next, so the starting node determines the entire iterated AGM sequence. Indeed, over
{\mathbb{R}}
, we always take the square root of
a_{n}b_{n}={b_{n+1}}^{2}
such that
b_{n+1}
has the same sign as
a_{n+1}
.
Over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
, we add an extra starting requirement that

is a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and during advancement we take
a_{n+1}=\dfrac{a_{n}+b_{n}}{2}
and
b_{n+1}
to be the unique square root of
a_{n}b_{n}
such that
a_{n+1}b_{n+1}
is a square; this rule works precisely because
-1
is not a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and because the product of two non-squares is always a square in a finite field. In
[
5
, §5]
, the authors raise the question of whether AGM can be defined on other finite fields. The answer turns out to be yes, but to facilitate this discussion, we first need definitions to distinguish between different types of nodes by how many times they can be advanced under the rules of AGM advancement. For example, over
{\mathbb{F}}_{29}
, the node
\left(\overline{1},\overline{11}\right)
cannot be advanced at all because
\overline{11}
is not a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{29}
, the node
\left(\overline{6},\overline{25}\right)

\left(\overline{6},\overline{25}\right)\overset{\mathrm{AGM}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{29}}}{\mapsto}\left(\overline{1},\overline{11}\right)

One key feature for the proof of Theorem

2.
Determining advancement criteria

In this section, we will determine which nodes can be advanced indefinitely in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
, thus proving several parts of Theorem
1.2
. As a first part step, the following lemma will be useful, as it says we don’t need to separately worry about whether each individual pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in K^{2}
in an AGM-advancement chain is in
S_{K}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
K
be a field with characteristic not equal to two, and let
(\alpha,\beta)\overset{\mathrm{AGM}_{K}}{\mapsto}(\gamma,\delta)
. Then
(\alpha,\beta)
is nontrivial if and only if
(\gamma,\delta)
is nontrivial.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

It follows from the assumption that

\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha-\beta)^{2}=8\gamma\delta^{2}(\gamma+\delta)(\gamma-\delta),

and hence,
\alpha,\beta,\alpha\pm\beta\neq 0
if and only if
\gamma,\delta,\gamma\pm\delta\neq 0
.
∎

The preceding proposition and the following two lemmas will help establish parts of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K
be a field with characteristic not equal to two. Any
(a_{0},b_{0})\in S_{K}
can be advanced at least twice if and only if
\left(\frac{a_{0}+b_{0}}{2}\right)^{2}a_{0}b_{0}
is a fourth power. In this case, if
-1
is a square in
K
, then both of
(a_{0},b_{0})
’s children can be advanced at least once.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Assume
(a_{0},b_{0})\mapsto(a_{1},b_{1})\mapsto(a_{2},b_{2})
. Then

\left(\frac{a_{0}+b_{0}}{2}\right)^{2}a_{0}b_{0}={a_{1}}^{2}{b_{1}}^{2}={b_{2}}^{4},

which is clearly a fourth power in
K
.

Conversely, assume
4a_{0}b_{0}(a_{0}+b_{0})^{2}
is a fourth power in
K
. Then
a_{0}b_{0}
is a square, so
(a_{0},b_{0})
has two children,
(a_{1},b_{1})
and
(a_{1},-b_{1})
, where
2a_{1}=a_{0}+b_{0}
and
{b_{1}}^{2}=a_{0}b_{0}
. Note that
4a_{0}b_{0}(a_{0}+b_{0})^{2}=16{a_{1}}^{2}{b_{1}}^{2}
, so the two square roots of
4a_{0}b_{0}(a_{0}+b_{0})^{2}
are
\pm 4a_{1}b_{1}
. Since
4a_{0}b_{0}(a_{0}+b_{0})^{2}
is a fourth power, at least one of its square roots is a square. However, since
-1
is a square in
K
,
4a_{1}b_{1}
is a square if and only if
-4a_{1}b_{1}
is a square. It follows that both
\pm 4a_{1}b_{1}
are squares, which imply that both
(a_{1},\pm b_{1})
are advanceable.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
q
be a prime power with
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
. If
(a_{0},b_{0})\in S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
, then exactly one of its children is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Let
(a_{0},b_{0})
’s children be
\left(a_{1},b_{1}\right)
and
\left(a_{1},-b_{1}\right)
, so
a_{1}=\frac{a_{0}+b_{0}}{2}
and
{b_{1}}^{2}=a_{0}b_{0}
. Consider

A=\left(\frac{a_{1}+b_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\!a_{1}b_{1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B=\left(\frac{a_{1}-b_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\!a_{1}(-b_{1}).

Since
\left(a_{1},b_{1}\right)
and
\left(a_{1},-b_{1}\right)
are both in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{1}}
by Lemma
2.2
,
A
and
B
are both squares. However, their product

AB=-\left(\frac{a_{0}-b_{0}}{4}\right)^{4}{b_{0}}^{4}

is not a fourth power (since
(a_{0},b_{0})
is nontrivial by Proposition
2.1
and
-1
is not a fourth power), so exactly one of
A
and
B
is a fourth power, corresponding to exactly one of
\left(a_{1},b_{1}\right)
and
\left(a_{1},-b_{1}\right)
being in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Remark
.

While the product of two squares not being a fourth power does not necessitate that one of the squares is a fourth power in infinite fields, it does necessitate that they are not both fourth powers, so infinite fields where
-1
is a square but not a fourth power obey the property that no nontrivial node has two twice-advanceable children, so AGM-advancement is unique on
S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
.

Now that we know that (for
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
,) each member of
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
has a child in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
, this allows us to conclude that each member of
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
is indefinitely advanceable.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
q\equiv 5\bmod{8}
. Then the set of all indefinitely advanceable nontrivial pairs is given by

S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}=S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}=\left\{(a,b)\in S_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mid\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{2}ab\text{ is a fourth power}\right\}\!.

Moreover,
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
is a (single-valued) function.

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

Lemma
2.2
tells us that
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}=\left\{(a,b)\in S_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mid\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{2}ab\text{ is a fourth power}\right\}
. Repeatedly applying Lemma
2.3
to members of
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
and their twice-advanceable descendants tells us that
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}\subseteq S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
, and
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\subseteq S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
by the definition of
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
, so
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}=S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
follows.

For the proof that
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
is a single-valued function, Lemma
2.3
tells us that each node in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
has only one child in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
and therefore cannot have multiple children in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
.
∎

Remark
.

This also applies to
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
(because all squares are fourth powers in finite fields where
-1
is not a square), allowing this result for
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
to be viewed as a generalization of the
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
case.

3.
Determining indefinitely advanceable node counts

For some purposes, we will be interested in the ratios
\frac{b}{a}

k
in order to be consistent with
[
1
]
and a future paper about the Galois-theoretic properties of the polynomials that references results from that source. This
k

\lambda
in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[

k^{2}=\lambda
.

This ratio is important because the effect that AGM advancement of
(a,b)
has on
k
does not depend on the precise values of
a
and
b
, only on their ratio
k=\frac{b}{a}
. Specifically, if
(a_{1},b_{1})\overset{\text{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{2},b_{2})
, we have that
k_{2}=\frac{b_{2}}{a_{2}}
is related to
k_{1}=\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}
by
(1+k_{1})^{2}{k_{2}}^{2}=4k_{1}

[
1

k_{1}
and
k_{2}
satisfy this equation, we say that
k_{1}
advances to
k_{2}
under the rules of
k
-advancement, notated
k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{2}
, or that
k_{2}
backtracks to
k_{1}
under the rules of
k
-advancement.

Let
T_{K}=K\setminus\{0,1,-1\}
; these
k
-values correspond to nontrivial nodes and therefore shall be referred to as nontrivial
k
-values. Define
G_{K}
to be the subset of
{T_{K}}^{2}
given by
G_{K}=\{(k_{1},k_{2})\in{T_{K}}^{2}\mid(1+k_{1})^{2}{k_{2}}^{2}=4k_{1}\}
. Let
T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{0}}=T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{0}}=T_{K}
,
T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}=\{k_{1}:(k_{1},k_{2})\in G_{K}\cap(T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n-1}}\times T_{k})\}
and
T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}=\{k_{2}:(k_{1},k_{2})\in G_{K}\cap(T_{k}\times T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n-1}})\}
for

,
T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}

T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}
. All of these definitions are analogous to for
S_{K}
and
F_{K}
, and, in fact, we will see in Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
that
(a,b)\mapsto\frac{b}{a}
commutes with advancement, so
T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}~=~\left\{\frac{b}{a}:(a,b)\in S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\right\}
and
T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}=\left\{\frac{b}{a}:(a,b)\in S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\right\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(a_{0},b_{0}),(a_{1},b_{1})\in S_{K}
. Then
(a_{0},b_{0})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{1},b_{1})
if and only if
2a_{1}=a_{0}+b_{0}
and
\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

If
2a_{1}=a_{0}+b_{0}
, then we have
{b_{1}}^{2}-a_{0}b_{0}=4{a_{0}}^{2}\times\left(\left(1+\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}\right)^{2}-4\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)
, so
{b_{1}}^{2}=a_{0}b_{0}
if and only if
\left(1+\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}\right)^{2}=4\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}
, i.e., if and only if
\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
allows us to relate AGM advancement to
k
-advancement, which in turn allows us to relate the populations of
S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}
and
T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}
and to relate the populations of
S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}
and
T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}
. This will be useful for proving part (3) of Thoerems
1.2
and
1.3
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and any field
K
with characteristic not equal to two, we have
S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}=\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\}
and
S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}=\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\}
.

In particular,

\left|S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\right|=\left|K^{\times}\right|\times\left|T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\right|\!.

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

If we have an AGM-advancement chain
(a_{0},b_{0})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{1},b_{1})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{n},b_{n})
, Lemma
3.1
lets us take the
k
-value of each node to get a
k
-advancement chain
k_{0}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{n}
, so
S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\subseteq\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\}
, and
S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\subseteq\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\}
.

If we instead start with a
k
-advancement chain
k_{0}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{n}
and some starting
a_{0}\in K^{\times}
, we can use Lemma
3.1
to obtain an AGM-advancement chain
(a_{0},k_{0}a_{0})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{1},k_{1}a_{1})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{n},k_{n}a_{n})
by recursively setting
a_{i+1}=\frac{a_{i}+k_{i}a_{i}}{2}
for
i=0,1,2,\mathellipsis,n-1
, so
S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\supseteq\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}\}
. Analogously, if we start with a
k
-advancement chain
k_{0}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{n}
and some starting
a_{n}\in K^{\times}
, we can use Lemma
3.1
to obtain an AGM-advancement chain
(a_{0},k_{0}a_{0})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{1},k_{1}a_{1})\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}\cdots\overset{\mathrm{AGM}}{\mapsto}(a_{n},k_{n}a_{n})
by recursively setting
a_{i-1}=\frac{2a_{i}}{1+k_{i}}
for
i=n,n-1,n-2,\mathellipsis,1
, so
S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\supseteq\{(a,ka):a\in K^{\times},k\in T_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}\}
.
∎

Corollary
3.2
allows us to translate what we already knew about node advanceability, such as Lemma
2.4
, into information about
k
-advanceability.

Corollary 3.3
.

If
q\equiv 5\bmod{8}
, then

T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}=\{k\in T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\mid 4k\left(1+k\right)^{2}\text{ is a fourth power}\}

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

Corollary
3.2
tells us that
k\in T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
if and only if
\left(2,2k\right)\in S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
. The conclusion follows from substituting
(a,b)=(2,2k)
into Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Now that we have a formula for
T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
, we can use it to figure out its population. Note that
T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
consists of the
k
-coordinates superelliptic curve
y^{4}=4k\left(1+k\right)^{2}
, which has genus one, so its population is related to the population of some elliptic curve. Specifically, it turns out to be related to
y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
q\equiv 5\bmod{8}
. Then the number of all indefinitely advanceable
k
-values is given by

\left|T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\right|=\frac{q-a_{q}-7}{4},

where
a_{q}
denotes the trace of Frobenius of the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}-x
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Recall from Corollary
3.3
that
\left|T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\right|=\left\{k\in T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\mid 4k\left(1+k\right)^{2}\text{ is a fourth power}\!\right\}
. Since
1+k
cannot be 0, in order for
4k\left(1+k\right)^{2}
to be a square,
k
must be a square. Because
k=0
is already excluded, each applicable value of
k
has two square roots, so we have:

\displaystyle\left|T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}\right|

\displaystyle=\#\!\left\{k\in T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\mid 4k(1+k)^{2}\text{ is a fourth power}\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\!\left\{k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}\mid 4k(1+k)^{2}\text{ is a fourth power}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid x^{2}\notin\{-1,0,1\},2x\!\left(1+x^{2}\right)\text{ is a square}\right\}}{2}

\displaystyle k=x^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\!\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}^{2}\mid x^{2}\notin\{-1,0,1\},y^{2}=2x\!\left(1+x^{2}\right)\right\}}{4}

\displaystyle x^{2}\notin\{0,-1\}\Rightarrow 2x\!\left(1+x^{2}\right)\neq 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\#E_{y^{2}=2x\left(x^{2}+1\right)}\!\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)-8}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{q-a_{q}-7}{4},

where the
-8
is to exclude
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
,
(-1,0)
,
(i,1-i)
,
(i,-1+i)
,
(-i,1+i)
,
(-i,-1-i)
, and the projective point at infinity, and
a_{q}=q+1-\#E_{y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})}\!\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)
, where
\#E_{y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})}\!\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)
includes the projective point at infinity.

The elliptic curve
y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})
is isomorphic over

to the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}+4x
. For
q\equiv 1\bmod 4
, it is also isomorphic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to

because

is a fourth power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
∎

Because the populations of

and

Let

This result guarantees that all prime powers

4.
Backtracking

Now that we have investigated the structure of

S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
. In addition to proving Theorem
1.3
as an analogue of Theorem
1.2
, this combined with the previous section will help motivate the contravariant isomorphism between
T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
and
T_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
, yielding reversal symmetry, which is a powerful tool that will help us, among other things, prove Theorem
1.5
. First, we develop analogs of previous advancement results for backtracking. Specifically, Lemma
2.2
corresponds to Corollary
4.2
and Lemma

, Lemma

corresponds to Lemma

corresponds to Remark

.

Lemma 4.1

(Generalization of Corollary 2.2 in
[
3
]
)
.

Let
K
be a field with characteristic not equal to two. A node
\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right)\in S_{K}
can be backtracked at least once if and only if
{a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}
is a square in
K
. In this case, it has exactly two parents, which are each others’ reversals.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

The coordinates of any parent of
\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right)
must have sum
2a_{n}
and product
{b_{n}}^{2}
, so they must be the roots of
x^{2}-2a_{n}x+{b_{n}}^{2}=0
in either order. The result (including that the two roots are distinct for nontrivial nodes) follows from using the quadratic equation.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
K
be a field with characteristic not equal to two and where
-1
is a square. If
\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right)\in S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{1}}
, then its parents are either both in
S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{1}}
or neither in

.

Proof of Corollary
4.2
.

Let
\left(a_{n-1},b_{n-1}\right)
and
\left(b_{n-1},a_{n-1}\right)
be the parents of
\left(a_{n},b_{n}\right)
. By Lemma
4.1
, that these are either both or neither backtrackable is equivalent to saying that
{a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2}
is a square if and only if
{b_{n-1}}^{2}-{a_{n-1}}^{2}
is a square (where
\left(a_{n-1},b_{n-1}\right)
and
\left(b_{n-1},a_{n-1}\right)
are the parents of
(a_{n},b_{n})
), which is true because

is a square.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
K
be a field with characteristic not equal to two and where
-1
is a square. Any
(a_{n},b_{n})\in S_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{1}}
can be backtracked at least twice if and only if
{a_{n}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}\right)
is a fourth power.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Assume
(a_{n-2},b_{n-2})\mapsto(a_{n-1},b_{n-1})\mapsto(a_{n},b_{n})
. Then

{a_{n}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}\right)=\left(\frac{a_{n-2}-b_{n-2}}{4}\right)^{4},

which is clearly a fourth power in
K
.

Conversely, assume
{a_{n}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}\right)
is a fourth power in
K
. Then
{a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}
is a square, so
(a_{n},b_{n})
has two parents,
(a_{n-1},b_{n-1})
and
(b_{n-1},a_{n-1})
, where
2a_{n}=a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}
and
{b_{n}}^{2}=a_{n-1}b_{n-1}
. Note that
{a_{n}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}\right)=\frac{({a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2})^{2}}{16}
, so the two square roots of
{a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}
are
\pm\frac{{a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2}}{4}
. Since
{a_{n}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n}}^{2}-{b_{n}}^{2}\right)
is a fourth power, at least one of its square roots is a square. However, since
-1
is a square in
K
,
\frac{{a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2}}{4}
is a square if and only if
\frac{{b_{n-1}}^{2}-{a_{n-1}}^{2}}{4}
is a square. It follows that both
\pm\frac{{a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2}}{4}
are squares, which imply that both
(a_{n-1},b_{n-1})
and
(b_{n-1},a_{n-1})
are backtrackable by Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
q
be a prime power with

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Assume
(a_{n-2},b_{n-2})\mapsto(a_{n-1},b_{n-1})\mapsto(a_{n},b_{n})

which are squares by Corollary

because

and
(b_{n-1},a_{n-1})

{a_{n-1}}^{2}\!\left({a_{n-1}}^{2}-{b_{n-1}}^{2}\right)\times{b_{n-1}}^{2}\!\left({b_{n-1}}^{2}-{a_{n-1}}^{2}\right)=-{b_{n}}^{4}\left(\frac{a_{n-2}-b_{n-2}}{2}\right)^{4},

∎

5.
Reversal symmetry

As a more convenient way to prove Corollary
1.4
, we take advantage of birationality between the twice-advanceability elliptic curve
y^{2}=2x\!\left(1+x^{2}\right)
, where
k=x^{2}
in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, and the twice-backtrackability curve
\mu^{4}=1-k^{2}
from Remark
2
.

In order to prove these theorems pertaining to reversal symmetry, we find it useful to examine the relationship between the curve
y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})
from the proof of
3.4
providing square roots
x
of twice-advanceable
k
-values and the curve
\mu^{4}=1-k^{2}
from Remark
2
providing twice-backtrackable
k
-values.

As the proof of Theorem
3.4
tells us, any twice-advanceable
k
-value is of the form
x^{2}
for some
(x,y)
satisfying
y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})
; in this case, we have

x^{2}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}=\left(\frac{y}{1+x^{2}}\right)^{2}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{\pm 2y}{(x+1)^{2}},

thus providing descendants for all twice-advanceable
k
-values and, in finite fields where
-1
is not a fourth power, providing another method to illuminate which child is indefinitely advanceable. Analogously, Remark
2
tells us that any twice-backtrackable
k
-value is of the form
k
for some
(k,\mu)
satisfying
\mu^{4}=1-k^{2}
, in which case we have

k\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsfrom}\left(\frac{k}{1+\mu^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{1+\mu^{2}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsfrom}\left(\frac{1\pm\mu}{1\mp\mu}\right)^{2},

thus providing ancestors for all twice-backtrackable
k
-values and, in finite fields where
-1
is not a fourth power, providing another method to illuminate which parent is indefinitely backtrackable.

The existence of analogous chains suggests some relation between the set of twice-advanceable
k
-values and the set of twice-backtrackable
k
-values. We know that
\mu^{4}=1-k^{2}
is birational to some elliptic curve, and it turns out to be birational to
y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})
. Two of the birational maps between them are given by:

\displaystyle(x,y)=\!\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{1+k},\frac{2\mu}{1+k}\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(k,\mu)=\!\left(\frac{1-x^{2}}{1+x^{2}},\frac{y}{1+x^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle(x,y)=\!\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{1+k},\frac{-2\mu}{1+k}\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(k,\mu)=\!\left(\frac{1-x^{2}}{1+x^{2}},\frac{-y}{1+x^{2}}\right)\!.

(That these are birational maps between the curves
C_{y^{2}=2x(1+x^{2})}
and
C_{\mu^{4}=1-k^{2}}
can easily be verified.) Both of these have
k=\frac{1-x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}
(and therefore
x^{2}=\frac{1-k}{1+k}
, and in fact, we also have the following commutative diagram in the sense that
\dfrac{1-\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}}{1+\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}}=\dfrac{1-\mu^{2}}{1+\mu^{2}}
and
\dfrac{1-\frac{2y}{(x+1)^{2}}}{1+\frac{2y}{(x+1)^{2}}},\dfrac{1-\frac{-2y}{(x+1)^{2}}}{1+\frac{-2y}{(x+1)^{2}}}\in\left\{\left(\dfrac{1+\mu}{1-\mu}\right)^{2},\left(\dfrac{1-\mu}{1+\mu}\right)^{2}\right\}
, thus switching
x^{2}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{\pm 2y}{(x+1)^{2}}
and
k\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsfrom}\frac{1-\mu^{2}}{1+\mu^{2}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsfrom}\left(\frac{1\pm\mu}{1\mp\mu}\right)^{2}
by the Möbius transformation
z\mapsto\frac{1-z}{1+z}
, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

Figure 4
.

S_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
and

Moreover, it is not hard to verify that the two birational maps provided above are the only two birational maps that fit into this diagram, i.e., the only two birational maps such that
z\mapsto\frac{1-z}{1+z}
(or any Möbius transformation, for that matter) switches
x^{2}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\frac{2y}{(x+1)^{2}}

This helps us find a contravariant involution
z\mapsto\frac{1-z}{1+z}
of
T_{K}
(contravariant in the sense that it switches advancement and backtracking); in fact, it holds in a very general context independent of previous discussion.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
K
be any field with characteristic not equal to two. The involution
\sigma:k\mapsto\frac{1-k}{1+k}
of
T_{K}
switches advancement and backtracking, i.e.,
\sigma\!\left(k_{2}\right)\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\sigma\!\left(k_{1}\right)
if and only if
k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

That
\sigma
is an involution of
T_{K}
and that
\sigma\!\left(k_{2}\right)\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}\sigma\!\left(k_{1}\right)
if and only if
k_{1}\overset{\mathrm{k}}{\mapsto}k_{2}
can easily be verified. Specifically for the latter,

\sigma\!\left(k_{1}\right)^{2}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(k_{2}\right)+1\right)^{2}-4\sigma\!\left(k_{1}\right)=\frac{4}{(1+k_{1})^{2}(1+k_{2})^{2}}\left({k_{1}}^{2}\!\left(k_{2}+1\right)^{2}-4k_{1}\right).\qed

Applying the contravariant involution
\sigma
to
G_{K}
and subgraphs of
G_{K}
produces contravariant isomorphisms between various subgraphs.

Corollary 5.2
.

For any field
K
with characteristic not equal to two, the graph
G_{K}
is isomorphic to its own reversal, and for any
0\leq n\leq\infty
, the graph
G_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}
is isomorphic to the reversal of
G_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}
.

Proof of Corollary
5.2
.

We know from Lemma
5.1
that
\sigma
is a contravariant involution of
G_{K}
. As a result,
\sigma
restricts to a contravariant isomorphism between
G_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{n}}
and
G_{K}^{\mathrm{back}_{n}}
.
∎

Ideally, we would like to prove the general existence of an analogous contravariant isomorphism for nodes. However, as Remark
3
will show, any such contravariant isomorphism would not be compatible with
\sigma
. However, Corollary
5.2
does allow us prove the similarities between
F_{K}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
and

outlined in Theorem

6.
Jellyfish structure

Now, we shall figure out the structure of the jellyfish in order to prove part (5) of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, and well as proving Corollary
1.4
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
q
be a prime power with
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
. The graph
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
consists of bell-head (i.e., simple) cycles where each node in the cycle has one tentacle, which is Y-shaped and has length two, and the graph
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
consists of bell-head cycles where each node in the cycle has one colon, which is Y-shaped and has length two.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Corollary
2.4
and Lemma
4.4
tell us that advancement and backtracking are single-valued on
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
, so all cycles are simple (i.e., have no branching), and
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
is their disjoint union.

To prove the statement about tentacles in
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
, note that any node in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{2}}
, so Corollary
4.2
and Lemma
4.4
tell us that of its two parents, one is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{2}}
and therefore in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
(because it is already known to be in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
and
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}=S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{2}}
), and the other is
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{1}}\setminus S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
and has two parents, neither of which is backtrackable, thus forming a Y-shaped tentacle of length two. Overlap between different cyclic nodes’ tentacles is ruled out because advancement on
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
is single-valued by
2.4
.

To prove the statement about colons in
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
, note that any node in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
, so Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
tell us that of its two children, one is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
and therefore in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
(because it is already known to be in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
and
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}=S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{2}}
), and the other is
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{1}}\setminus S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
and has two children, neither of which is advanceble, thus forming a Y-shaped colon of length two. Overlap between different cyclic nodes’ colons is ruled out because backtracking on
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
is single-valued by
4.4
.

Overlap between a tentacle and a colon, between one jellyfish’s tentacle and another jellyfish’s cycle, or between one jellyfish’s colon and another jellyfish’s cycle is ruled out by the fact that cyclic nodes are both indefinitely advanceable and indefinitely backtrackable, tentacle nodes are indefinitely advanceable but not indefinitely backtrackable, and colon nodes are indefinitely backtrackable but not indefinitely advanceable.
∎

This allows us to prove the analogue of Corollary
5.2
for the nodes, as was speculated about in the discussion immediately after its proof, for odd
q\not\equiv 1\bmod 8
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power not congruent to 1 modulo 8. Then
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
is isomorphic to the reversal of (i.e., contravariantly isomorphic to)
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
.

Proof of Corollary
6.2
.

The cases of
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
and
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
shall be proven separately. For the case of
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
, the result follows easily from Theorem
6.1
because of the shared structure between tentacles and colons. Specifically, Theorem
6.1
tells us that the cycles in
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
and
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{back}_{\infty}}
are all simple, so
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
must consist of simple cycles (no branching) and therefore must be isomorphic to its own reversal. Because we know from Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
5.2
(or, since
q\equiv 5\bmod 8
, from Theorem
6.1
) that tentacles and colons have analogous shapes, so because
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
is isomorphic to its own reversal, the desired result follows.

The case of
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
is analogous, again because of the shared structure between tentacles and colons. Theorem 1 part (3) of
[
2
]
tells us that the cycles in
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{\infty}}
, i.e.,
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
, must consist of bell-head cycles (i.e., no branching), and so
F_{K}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
must be isomorphic to its own reversal. Theorem 1 part (3) of
[
2
]
also tells us that each cycle node is pointed to by one tentacle, which has length one and no branching. Furthermore, as pages 1 and 2 of
[
2
]
explain, each node in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{1}}
, including
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
, has two children, of which exactly one is in
S_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{adv}_{1}}
. For parents in
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
, this other child is a length-one colon. Thus, when
q\equiv 3\bmod 4
, tentacles and colons have analogous shapes (which could alternatively could have been deduced from Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
5.2
), so because
F_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}^{\mathrm{cyc}}
is isomorphic to its own reversal, the desired result follows.
∎
