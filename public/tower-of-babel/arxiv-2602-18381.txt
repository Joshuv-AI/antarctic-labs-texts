Bell-GHZ nonclassicality of many-observer interwoven frustrated down conversions
Marek Żukowski, Paweł Cieśliński, Marcin Markiewicz, Konrad Schlichtholz

Abstract
Frustrated down conversion is a process in which a quantum superposition of emissions from two separate parametric down-conversion processes gives rise to observable interference. Depending on the phase relation between the probability amplitudes associated with emissions by the first and second crystal, the process can be enhanced or suppressed. This is achieved by aligning the setup so that the signal and idler modes produced in the first crystal are fed into the second crystal in such a way that they also constitute its signal–idler modes. In the experiment reported in Sci. Adv. 11, 1794 (2025), two-observer interwoven frustrated parametric down-conversion (PDC) processes produced interference effects based on path identity [Phys. Rev. Lett. 118, 080401 (2017)]. In that configuration, the idler and signal modes of two source crystals, I and II, are arranged such that after propagation they fully overlap with the emission modes of crystals A and B, which serve as elements of measurement stations controlled by Alice and Bob. In the interwoven scheme, crystal A (B) is fed by the signal mode of crystal I (II) and the idler mode of crystal II (I). Owing to path indistinguishability (identity), the joint emission process at the two source crystals can interfere with the joint emission process at the crystals located at the measurement stations. It was conjectured that this type of interference leads to violations of local realism. In [arXiv:2508.19207] it was shown that the process violates the standard Clauser–Horne Bell inequality without invoking additional assumptions, provided that measurement settings different from those used in Sci. Adv. 11, 1794 (2025) are employed. Here we extend the interference scheme to more than two measurement stations (observers) and demonstrate a violation of one of the WWWZB inequalities. This indicates that the proposed approach may provide a general method for revealing non-classicality in a range of phenomena discussed in [Rev. Mod. Phys. 94, 025007 (2022)]. We also present a GHZ/Hardy-type argument that further highlights the paradoxical character of the interference.

III
Bell’s theorem for the configuration

The Bell inequality that we will use is given by

\displaystyle P(A,B,C^{\prime})+P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})+P(A^{\prime},B,C^{\prime})-P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})-P(A,C^{\prime})-P(B,C^{\prime})\leq 0,

(8)

where the primes denote that the power at the local measuring station’s PDC crystal is switched on.
All events are two separate photon detections at the given measuring station, just like it was in
[
4
]
, that is e.g.
P(A)=P(11|off,\phi_{A})
, and
P(A^{\prime})=P(11|on,\phi_{A})
, where
11
represents number of photons detected in modes
a_{1}a_{2}
. Following Pironio
[
19
]
the inequality can be called lifted Clauser-Horne inequality.

This inequality can be derived from the generalization of the CHSH Bell inequalities for
N
spatially separated systems, involving dichotomic observables, and two settings allowed for each of the
N
parties. A complete set of such (WWWZB) inequalities, all of which are tight, was derived in
[
27
;
26
;
27
;
32
]
. For each
N
we have
2^{2^{N}}
of such inequalities. The inequalities wind up to an elegant single non-linear inequality for each
N
. In
[
27
]
an explicit form for such inequalities for
N=3
was given. There are only five permutationally equivalent classes of such explicit linear inequalities. The one that we shall explicitly analyze belongs to one of such classes. This is because any CHSH-like inequality, can be transformed into a generalized Clauser Horne inequality by replacing
P(+)-P(-)
with
2P(+)-1
. This is possible under the assumption that the local results are dichtomic, that is
P_{n}(+)+P_{n}(-)=1
. In these equations
P(+/-)
denotes probabilities, which in the local hidden variable case are
P_{n}(\pm|\lambda,X)
, where
\pm
denote the results parametrized by
\pm 1
,
\lambda
is the value of the hidden variable determining the probabilities, X denotes the local dichotomic observable, and
n
denotes the observers (or local subsystems),
n=1,2,...N
. In the quantum case we replace these by specific pairs of projectors
\hat{P}_{n}(\pm,X)
for which
\sum_{\pm}\hat{P}_{n}(\pm,X)=\hat{I}
holds.

The inequality (
8
) is a straight ahead trivial generalization of the Clauser-Horne inequality. The latter one was used in
[
4
]
for just two parties, Alice and Bob, with settings as follows

\displaystyle P(A,B)+P(A,B^{\prime})+P(A^{\prime},B)-P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})-P(A)-P(B)\leq 0.

(9)

Also, due to the Bayes Rule, (
8
) can be viewed as a conditional Clauser-Horne inequality

\displaystyle P(A,B|C^{\prime})+P(A,B^{\prime}|C^{\prime})+P(A^{\prime},B|C^{\prime})-P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}|C^{\prime})-P(A|C^{\prime})-P(B|C^{\prime})\leq 0.

(10)

Recall that conditional probabilities with a fixed condition are probabilities (that is, they satisfy all Kolmogorov axioms).

III.1
Why this does work

Below will be presented an intuitive argumentation that the Bell inequality is violated by the three-party frustrated down conversion processes, studied in the earlier chapters.
We shall follow the approach based on analysis of probability amplitudes of the quantum processes of Feynman
[
10
]
presented in the Third Volume of his masterpiece.
We shall treat here all processes with moduli of their amplitudes of higher order than
|g|^{3}
as irrelevant. Note that if
|g|^{2}<0.01
, which is the usual condition for getting high visibility of interference patterns resulting from down conversion, see e.g.
[
18
]
, this introduces very small errors in the calculated probabilities. Most importantly the analytic calculations show (see further on, (
15
), (
16
), (
18
)) that the probabilities which enter the inequality (
8
) do not have terms of the order of
|g|^{8}
.

With suitably defined phase settings the coincidence probability
P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})
vanishes, see below. It is the interference term, which in the quantum case will allow to violate the lifted CH inequality.

•

Why one can have
P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})=0
? If we look at Figure 1, we notice the following. If all six detectors in the figure fire and in each one just one photon is counted, the process could be due to crystals I,II, III emitting two photons each. However the alternative is that this is each of crystals A, B, and C emitting pairs of photons. No other combination of three crystals firing can lead to the firing of all detectors, with single photon count at each of them. The two processes are indistinguishable beyond the crystals A, B, C because the alignment of the interferometric configuration is such that it imposes "path identity"
[
15
;
13
]
, see Figure 1. As on the way between the crystals we have three phase-shifters, they introduce a controllable relative phase factor
e^{i(\phi_{A}+\phi_{B}+\phi_{c})}
between the amplitude of the three-pair emission in
I,II,III
, and the amplitude of a similar process originating
at A, B, C. The overall probability amplitude, under the assumption of a negligible pump depletion is
g^{3}(1+e^{i(\phi_{A}+\phi_{B}+\phi_{c})})
. Thus a complete mutual cancellation of the two processes is possible.

Next, what we need is only a comparison between. e.g.,
P(A,B,C^{\prime})
,
P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})
,
P(A^{\prime},B,C^{\prime})
and
P(A,C^{\prime})
,
P(B,C^{\prime})
. Note that the symmetries of the studied processes give us
P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})=P(A^{\prime},B,C^{\prime})
and
P(A,C^{\prime})=P(B,C^{\prime})
.

•

As it must involve three emission of pairs one expects that
P(A,B,C^{\prime})=P(11,11,11|off,off,on,\phi_{a},\phi_{B},\phi_{c})\propto|g|^{6}
. As only one of the local
parametric down converters (that is PDCs which are elements of the measuring stations) is on, we cannot have any dependence on the phases (this will be evident after the reasoning below). The final state requires three simultaneous emissions at the three PDCs to happen, as it has six registered photons. Thus, in two source PDCs emissions must have happened, as only one PDC in measurement station is on: in station of Charlie. This one could provide two photons. But this is impossible. These photons emitted by the local PDC must
end up in modes
c_{1}c_{2}
. But the other registered photons must be from two initial PDCs. But these can send photons to either
a_{1}c_{2}
and
b_{1}a_{2}
, or
b_{1}a_{2}
and
c_{1}b_{2}
, or
c_{1}b_{2}
and
a_{1}c_{2}
. None of these processes, plus the emission at Charlie’s PDC, can lead to single photons at each of modes
a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}
. Conclusion: all photons are from the source PDCs, which must have fired simultaneously. Obliviously, because of that the phase setting
\phi_{c}
is irrelevant. The expansion of the unitary transformations with respect to the powers of
g
, within the studied approximations gives us
P(A,B,C^{\prime})=|g|^{6}
.

•

P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})
. If local PDCs at Bob’s and Charlie’s stations fired then one of the source PDC’s must have fired, otherwise we do not have six registered photons. But this can never lead to the six photons, each at a different detector, because none of the source PDCs can send two photons to the station of Alice. Period. If we assume that only one of the local PDCs fired we come back to the previous case described for
P(A,B,C^{\prime})
. Thus
P(A,B,C^{\prime})=P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})
.

•

Now the crucial
P(A,C^{\prime}).
It will be shown, that the two detected photons at the station of Charlie cannot come from the local emission of his own PDC, and therefore there must be an emission at all three source PDCs. As the power at the local station of Alice is switched off,
her two photons must have come from emissions of sources I and II, which give photons to modes
a_{1}c_{2}
and
b_{1}a_{2}
. Thus, there must be a detected
c_{2}
photon that is not originating from the local PDC of Charlie; thus emission there is excluded, because otherwise we would have two photos at
c_{2}
. Thus, the photon at
c_{1}
at Charlie’s station must come from the source PDCs emissions, and therefore one must have emissions at sources III and I feeding modes
c_{1}b_{2}
and
a_{1}c_{2}
. So, this is the reason for detection of the single photon at
a_{1}
. The other photon at
a_{2}
must have come from emission at source II into mode
b_{1}a_{2}
. Thus
all source PDCs fired
. Therefore
P(A,B,C^{\prime})=P(A,C^{\prime})
.

Therefore if one uses the symmetries, up to the order of
|g|^{6}
, we have

\displaystyle P(A,B,C^{\prime})=P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime})=P(A^{\prime},B,C^{\prime})=P(A,C^{\prime})=P(B,C^{\prime})=|g|^{6}

(11)

This means that the inequality can be violated if one fixes the local phase settings to the values giving
P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})<|g|^{6}
, that is to e.g.
\frac{\pi}{2}<\phi_{A}+\phi_{B}+\phi_{C}<\frac{3\pi}{2}
, with the optimal value
\pi
.

Bell Theorem via linear programming: results

As the problem of existence of a local realistic model for specific quantum predictions, from the point of view of numerical methods, is equivalent to linear programming, see

]
and

]

Our linear programming computations, for the

Note that the WWWZB inequalities
[

;
26
;
32
]
were formed using specific features of Bell-GHZ correlation functions, and give generalized CHSH inequalities for
N
parties, dichotomic results and two settings per each observer. There is no proof (or we the authors do not know it) that Clauser-Horne versions of WWWZB inequalities exhaust full set of tight generalized Clauser-Horne inequalities for
N
parties, and two settings for each observation station. Thus linear programming is equivalent or a stronger tool in the context of Bell experiments, than testing all WWWZB inequalities. Still, in the studied case with
N=3
it returns the same result.

The same linear programming techniques were applied by us to test whether Bell nonclassicality can be observed in the situation in which all crystals are pumped, i.e., with the underlying family of final pre-detection states (
7
), not the initial one(!), and only phase values
\phi_{A},\phi_{B}
and
\phi_{C}
acting as measurement settings. In
[
4
]

can be explained by local hidden variable theories. The linear programming results imply that the same holds for the process examined here, however perhaps only for two alternative settings for each observer (including Charlie). The computations for multiple values of

, constrained by the power limit

showed that, even after optimization of the phases, there does not exist any two-setting Bell inequality violated by the probabilities. This was tested for phase shift values within
[0,2\pi]
and a step of
\Delta\phi=0.1\pi
. That is
P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})=P(11,11,11|on,on,on,\phi_{A},\phi_{B},\phi_{C})
and their marginals, within the scenario,
can be described by a joint probability distribution equivalent to a local hidden variable model, provided we constrain ourselves to the two setting per observer scenario. Strictly speaking this would be constrained to the used phase settings, but nevertheless gives a strong support to a conjecture that this holds for all phase settings.

III.4
Generalization to four parties.

There seems to be only one sequential topology, topologically equivalent up to permutations of e.g. source crystals, which is an extension of such experiment, as each source PDC feeds modes belonging to two observing parties. Thus a graph of such a relation is a polygon. This extends to whatever number of parties.

The relevant inequality is in such a case the doubly lifted CH one

\displaystyle P(A,B,C^{\prime},D^{\prime})+P(A,B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})+P(A^{\prime},B,C^{\prime},D^{\prime})-P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})-P(A,C^{\prime},D^{\prime})-P(B,C^{\prime},D^{\prime})\leq 0,

(28)

which is effectively a Clauser-Horne one conditioned on events
C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}.

The polygon graph relations of emissions at sources suggest that the reasoning for three observers can be repeated.

(Charlie),
d_{1}d_{2}
(Dorothy). In each of these modes one photon is detected. To get the interwoven configuration the source stations can be emitting into modes
a_{1}d_{2}
(I),
b_{1}a_{2}
(II),
c_{1}b_{2}
(III),
d_{1}c_{2}
(IV).
Assume that the PDC at Alice’s station is switched off. Therefore, the photons which she registered must have come from source PDC’s I and II. Thus the photon registered in mode
b_{1}
, must have originated from source II, and therefore the photon at
b_{2}
must have originated from source III. But this implies that the photon at
c_{2}
must be from source IV. Therefore at
d_{1}
Dorothy has registered a photon from source IV, and the other detection of hers must have come from PDC I. Thus all sources I, II, III, IV have fired: all registered photons must have originated from them. As they emit pairs, all of them must have fired simultaneously.

It is easy to check that if two (or three) PDCs at local stations are switched off, then we come to the same conclusion.

•

If one or more PDCs at local stations are off, then if at all stations a pair of single photon counts was registered, this implies all these photons of the pair must have originated from the emission of the source PDCs. Also this implies that all of the source PDCs must have fired, emitting each just a single signal-idler pair. The origin of each detected photon is fully determined. Therefore, there is no phase-dependent interference effect. Only when at all measuring stations the PDC’s are
on
, we have interference dependent on the phases between the source PDC’s and the local PDC’s, as due to path identities the origins of all detected photons are undefined.

•

Also for a generalization of the scenario to
N
observers, that is for interwoven frustrated emission involving
N
observation stations and
N
source crystals we have the following. If one of the PDC’s at measuring stations out of
N
is switched off and at this station a pair of photons is detected, and at some
N-2
other stations pairs of single photons are detected then in the station which is not in the above set of
N-1
stations two single photons are also detected, and all detected photons originated from the source PDCs.

Note further that in the inequality for four partners the only interference term is
P(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
, and its lowest value is zero, while all other terms have the same positive value, obviously
|g|^{8}
. Thus the doubly lifted CH inequality (
28
) is violated in the
on-off
scenario. One can generate such statement also for
N
observer scenarios which are generalizations of the one studied above.

IV
A quantum paradox

Assume the following idealization of the observed processes: all higher corrections to the probabilities beyond
|g|^{6}
are absolutely negligible. Let us put them to zero. Then the following GHZ-Hardy reasoning is possible.

Deterministic local hidden variable model for
P(11,11,11|\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b},\alpha_{c};\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c};\lambda)
must be specified as:

\displaystyle P(11,11,11|\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b},\alpha_{c};\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c})=\int d\lambda\rho({\lambda})\prod_{x=a,b,c}p_{x}(11|\alpha_{x},\phi_{x};\lambda),

(29)

with the obvious meaning of the notation, and determinism is when
p_{x}(11|\alpha_{x},\phi_{x};\lambda)=X(\alpha_{x},\phi_{x};\lambda)=0,1.

We encode the
on/off
settings of the pump at the PDCs by setting
\alpha_{x}=\alpha,0
.

The earlier reasoning showed that if two of the local pumps are
off
, say at Alice’s and Bob’s, and we get 11 results at both, then we must have 11 result at Charlie’s station, no matter whether his pump is
on
or
off
. This implies that

A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1
and
B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)=1
determines that
C(\alpha_{c},\phi_{c},\lambda)=1
(that is
C=1
for the same value of
\lambda
and any values of
\alpha_{c},\phi_{c}.
)

But also we have similar implications:

•

(
B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)=1
and
C(\alpha_{c},\phi_{c},\lambda)=1
)
\Rightarrow

A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1

•

and so on, e.g.
(
C(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)=1
and
A(\alpha_{a},\phi_{a},\lambda)=1
)
\Rightarrow

B(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1
. This holds because of the symmetry of the situation we can permute Alice, Bob and Charlie, while the the pump setting must be zero for two of them.

Due to the above implications we have the following relations:

\displaystyle B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)C(0,\phi_{c},\lambda)A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1

(30)

\displaystyle B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)C(\alpha_{c},\phi_{c},\lambda)A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1

(31)

\displaystyle B(\alpha_{b},\phi_{b};\lambda)C(0,\phi_{c},\lambda)A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1

(32)

\displaystyle B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)C(0,\phi_{c},\lambda)A(\alpha_{a},\phi_{a};\lambda)=1.

(33)

All of them must hold for a specific
\lambda
if the first one holds, because the first one implies all other three for the specific
\lambda
. In other words, if one chooses
\lambda
that results in configuration
B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)=1,\,C(0,\phi_{c},\lambda)=1,\,A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1
then the above equalities are implied.
By multiplying all these three equations side by side, as for all
\lambda
’s singled out by the first relation one has
X(0,\phi_{x};\lambda)^{2}=1,

we get:

\displaystyle B(\alpha_{b},\phi_{b};\lambda)C(\alpha_{c},\phi_{c},\lambda)A(\alpha_{a},\phi_{a};\lambda)=1.

(34)

Hence we have a kind of GHZ paradox, due to the following implication:

\displaystyle B(0,\phi_{b};\lambda)C(0,\phi_{c},\lambda)A(0,\phi_{a};\lambda)=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow B(\alpha_{b},\phi_{b};\lambda)C({\alpha_{c}},\phi_{c},\lambda)A(\alpha_{a},\phi_{a};\lambda)=1.

(35)

This means that

\displaystyle P(11,11,11|0,0,0;\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c})\leq P(11,11,11|\alpha,\alpha,\alpha;\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c}).

(36)

But the quantum predictions give

, while for specific phase settings

due to the quantum interference.

Obviously the corrections to the probabilities of the order

. This in turn would mean that the relation (

1-3O(|g|^{2})
. We took into the account the symmetry of the studied process. Obviously, this reduces sharpness of the statement (
36

P(11,11,11|\alpha,\alpha,\alpha;\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c})=0

V
concluding remarks

We showed above that the method of proper testing Bell non-classicality developed in
[
4
]
can be applied also to more complicated interferometric configurations of interwoven parametric down conversion processes, which could be topologically transformed into Bell or GHZ-like tests. The interwoven frustrated down conversion is an exciting new type of interferometry, involving active measurements, and providing new phenomena. A correct Bell analysis showing violation of Bell inequalities can be translated to protocols of QKD distribution, quantum random number generation, quantum reduction of communication complexity. Proper understanding of the new interferometry may have implications in foundations of quantum mechanics, especially because it involves active measurements. Note that the measuring stations give alternative quantum processes which change the states of the optical modes leading to the detectors. Photon occupation numbers are not merely undefined, like e. g. in homodyne detections with local oscillators in coherent states, see
[
9
]
, but they change before the detection due to the active down-conversion processes which are an internal element of the measuring stations. This is not just a trivial amplification or depletion of the optical fields, but a quantum interference process linking the probability amplitudes of pair emissons in the source parametric converters with the amplitudes for the process to happen in parametric converters of the detection stations. Interesting aspect is here that the interference is only for triple pair emission amplitude (each pair at a different source) and the amplitude for pair emissions at all detection stations.
