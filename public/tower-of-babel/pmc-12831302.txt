Drainage of liver abscess guided by ultrasound combined with three-dimensional visualization technology and ICG fluorescence navigation technology: a case report
Authors: Jinsheng Mai, Yi Zhou, Silue Zeng, Hao Zhong, Zhenju Huang, Peilin Cai, Ning Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver abscess is a common digestive system disorder primarily caused by bacterial infection. Effective treatment often involves drainage, in which ultrasound technology is commonly used to guide puncture and drainage procedures. However, ultrasound has inherent limitations such as suboptimal visualization of abscess boundaries and adjacent vascular anatomy. Indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence navigation and three-dimensional (3D) visualization are two intraoperative navigation technologies that, when integrated with ultrasound, overcome these constraints. Background

Liver abscess is a common digestive system disorder primarily caused by bacterial infection [1]. Effective treatment typically involves drainage. Percutaneous drainage serves as one of the standard approaches for liver abscess management, primarily including needle aspiration and catheter placement. This method is suitable for most patients, particularly those with smaller, superficially located abscesses and relatively mild conditions. It offers advantages such as being relatively simple to perform, minimally invasive, and facilitating rapid recovery. Surgical drainage represents another conventional treatment option, encompassing both laparoscopic and open approaches. Surgical intervention is indicated for cases where percutaneous drainage fails, or when managing large abscesses, multiloculated abscesses, or abscesses complicated by biliary obstruction or other surgically addressable conditions [2].

Under specific clinical or anatomical circumstances, surgical drainage demonstrates distinct advantages over percutaneous methods. For instance, when abscesses are deeply situated, surrounded by critical vessels or bile ducts, exhibit multiple septations, or reside in the caudate lobe, surgical drainage allows direct access to the abscess cavity. This approach mitigates risks of vascular or biliary injury associated with needle puncture [3].

In minimally invasive liver surgery, ICG fluorescence and three-dimensional visualization technologies provide robust intraoperative navigation support [3, 4]. ICG, a fluorescent dye administered preoperatively, accumulates in liver tissues. During surgery, real-time fluorescence imaging of hepatic lesions is achieved using ICG-compatible 3D laparoscopes (Fig. 1C). Simultaneously, 3D visualization technology offers surgeons intuitive and precise anatomical guidance throughout the procedure [5]. For deeply located abscesses challenging to identify with intraoperative ultrasound, the combined application of ICG fluorescence and 3D visualization significantly enhances abscess localization. Traditional techniques often require repeated needle punctures to locate such abscesses, prolonging operative time and increasing risks of vascular or biliary injury. Integrating these technologies enables real-time assessment of abscess position and surrounding anatomy during surgery, thereby reducing unnecessary punctures, minimizing iatrogenic damage, and improving both safety and efficacy of the intervention.

Case Description/Methods

A 61-year-old Asian male presented with a four-day history of fever of unknown origin. His peak body temperature reached 40℃, accompanied by chills, dizziness, headache, and nausea. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a hypodense lesion in the left hepatic lobe, measuring approximately 58 mm × 56 mm, exhibiting well-defined margins and internal septations (Fig. 1A). Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated mild rim enhancement of the abscess wall. Laboratory findings supported the diagnosis of a liver abscess. Preoperatively, the patient was diagnosed with a pyogenic liver abscess. Management included intravenous ertapenem (1 g daily for 3 days) alongside supportive therapies: fluid resuscitation, physical cooling, hepatoprotection (glutathione), and acid suppression (pantoprazole). Twenty-four hours prior to surgery, ICG was administered intravenously at 0.25 mg/kg. A 1 mm-slice CT scan facilitated reconstruction of hepatic vasculature and the abscess using MI-3DVS software to generate a 3D navigation model (Fig. 1B).

The procedure employed a four-port laparoscopic approach: 10 mm trocars were placed at the subxiphoid and left midclavicular subcostal sites, while 5 mm trocars were positioned at the right midclavicular subcostal and supraumbilical midline locations. Intraoperative navigation integrated a dual-screen system: the main monitor displayed ICG fluorescence highlighting the abscess as a bright green target (Figs. 1C-E), while a subsidiary screen (managed by a dedicated navigation assistant) superimposed the 3D model onto real-time laparoscopic imagery using the “Laparoscopic Hepatectomy Navigation System [LHNS]”. This synergy with intraoperative ultrasound enabled single-attempt precision puncture and drainage (Fig. 1F). Following aspiration and fenestration enlargement, a drainage tube was placed in the Winslow’s foramen.

Cultured pus identified Klebsiella pneumoniae. Postoperative care continued with ertapenem, hepatoprotectants, and acid suppressants. No complications (hemorrhage, biliary leakage) occurred. The drain was removed on postoperative day 4 (output < 10 ml/day). During the 3-month follow-up period monitoring for surgical-site infection, hemorrhage, biliary injury, abscess recurrence, and hepatic/renal dysfunction, the patient exhibited none of these complications.

Discussion and Conclusion

Liver abscess represents a common digestive system disorder primarily caused by bacterial infection. Percutaneous and surgical drainage constitute the primary therapeutic approaches [2]. Percutaneous drainage is suitable for most patients, offering advantages including relative technical simplicity, minimal invasiveness, and rapid recovery. This procedure can be performed under local anesthesia without general anesthesia, thereby reducing surgical risks. Percutaneous techniques primarily include needle aspiration and catheter placement. Needle aspiration is indicated for small, superficial, and easily accessible abscesses, involving pus evacuation via fine-needle puncture—a relatively straightforward and minimally traumatic approach. Catheter placement, conversely, utilizes ultrasound or CT guidance for indwelling drainage tube insertion into the abscess cavity, enabling continuous drainage. This method is preferred for larger abscesses or when needle aspiration proves ineffective [6, 7]. Nevertheless, percutaneous drainage exhibits limitations. For multiloculated abscesses or those with extensive septations, incomplete drainage may occur, leading to suboptimal outcomes [8]. Additionally, complications such as puncture failure, hemorrhage, or infection dissemination may arise during the procedure. Moreover, percutaneous drainage carries elevated risks for deeply situated abscesses or those adjacent to critical vasculature or bile ducts [9].

Surgical drainage serves as another conventional treatment modality, indicated for cases of percutaneous drainage failure, large abscesses, multiloculated abscesses, or abscesses complicated by biliary obstruction or other surgically addressable conditions. This approach permits direct access to the abscess cavity, facilitating thorough debridement of pus and necrotic tissue—particularly advantageous for complex abscesses. Concurrently, it enables direct visualization of hepatic anatomy and management of concurrent pathologies. Surgical drainage encompasses both laparoscopic and open approaches. Laparoscopic surgery, characterized by reduced trauma and faster recovery compared to open surgery, is preferred for most operable cases. Open procedures remain reserved for abscesses in anatomically challenging locations or with severe peri-abscess adhesions [3]. However, surgical drainage is inherently invasive, requiring general anesthesia with relatively higher operative risks. Prolonged recovery periods may ensue, alongside potential complications such as surgical site infection, hemorrhage, or biliary fistula. In this case, concurrent laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed alongside abscess drainage due to the presence of an 11 mm gallbladder polyp identified on CT.

Recent advancements in hepatic surgery have witnessed the emergence of innovative techniques and adjunctive technologies. Growing evidence supports the utility of ICG fluorescence and 3D modeling in liver disease management [4, 5]. For instance, during hepatocellular carcinoma resection, ICG fluorescence clearly delineates tumor margins, enhancing surgical precision [10]. Similarly, 3D modeling and augmented reality technologies—constructed through processing and analysis of patient CT data—provide intuitive visualization of hepatic anatomy, including spatial relationships among vessels, bile ducts, and lesions. Studies confirm that 3D modeling reduces operative time and intraoperative blood loss in complex liver surgeries [11]. However, limited research explores the adjunctive roles of ICG and 3D modeling in liver abscess management. Current intraoperative abscess localization predominantly relies on ultrasound [12], which faces limitations in deeply located abscesses adjacent to critical vasculature, including technical difficulty, limited spatial resolution, and suboptimal vascular visualization. This case study integrates both technologies into surgical abscess management, aiming to provide valuable insights for similar cases by reducing technical complexity, enhancing precision, and mitigating surgical risks. The combined application of ICG fluorescence imaging and 3D visualization delivers precise anatomical guidance. ICG fluorescence delineates hepatic position and margins, enabling intraoperative lesion identification with improved targeting accuracy. During the surgical procedure, the liver undergoes dynamic deformation. However, ICG fluorescence can real-time visualize the position and margins of the abscess on the liver surface, providing surgeons with intuitive and clear guidance. By leveraging the fluorescence projection, surgeons can identify the shortest puncture path, thereby reducing the risk of vascular and bile duct injuries associated with prolonged puncture trajectories.3D visualization constructs a stereoscopic anatomical model, clarifying perilesional critical structures and enhancing spatial awareness—thereby facilitating accurate abscess localization within complex anatomy while avoiding vital vascular structures [4, 13]. Prior to surgery, physicians can conduct multi-angle observation and analysis of the 3D model to pre-plan the optimal surgical approach. This includes determining the puncture entry point and direction to avoid critical vascular and biliary structures. For instance, the model clearly displays the spatial relationships between the abscess and surrounding structures such as hepatic veins, portal veins, and intrahepatic bile ducts. Thus, a surgical strategy can be formulated that ensures effective abscess drainage while minimizing potential damage to adjacent anatomical structures. In this case, targeting an abscess in liver segments S3/S4, the integrated technologies enabled single-attempt precise puncture without adjacent tissue injury from repeated attempts. This approach optimized intraoperative localization, reduced technical difficulty, shortened operative time, and potentially lowered complication rates. The patient recovered uneventfully without hemorrhage or biliary fistula. Nevertheless, technology dissemination faces challenges: ICG fluorescence and 3D visualization require specialized equipment and trained personnel. Hospitals must invest in infrastructure and provide comprehensive training to maximize benefits. Additionally, ICG imaging efficacy may vary due to individual patient factors, potentially yielding suboptimal fluorescence in some cases. Furthermore, this study is a case report, and long-term follow-up data remain lacking. The observed benefits in this single case still require validation through prospective controlled studies with larger sample sizes. The enduring impacts of these technologies on patient prognosis, recurrence rates, and other outcomes necessitate further investigation. These technologies should currently be regarded only as supplementary tools to conventional intraoperative ultrasound. They are suitable for use in laparoscopic procedures performed by experienced teams at well-equipped centers on patients who clearly meet the indications for laparoscopic surgery. For the majority of uncomplicated liver abscesses, ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage remains the first-line treatment approach.

In summary, ICG fluorescence imaging and 3D visualization technologies demonstrate clinical utility in liver abscess management. The integration of these techniques with intraoperative ultrasound enhances precision in abscess localization, reduces procedural complexity, and mitigates postoperative complication risks to some extent.
