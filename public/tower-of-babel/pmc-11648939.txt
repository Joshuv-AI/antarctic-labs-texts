The Iso-Inverse: A Contravariant Sparse Approximate Inverse Matrix
Authors: Peter M. W. Gill, Martin Mrovec
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Given a sparse matrix A, we define and discuss a matrix B that approximates A–1, has exactly the same sparsity structure as A, and varies contravariantly with A. Unlike many other sparse inverse approximations, the iso-inverse B is not constructed by minimizing the residual ∥AB – I∥ but, instead, by stipulating that AB = I for a well-defined subset of the elements of I. The iso-inverse is potentially useful in electronic structure calculations with nonorthogonal localized molecular orbitals.

Introduction

Most quantum chemical calculations are simplified if the molecular orbitals are orthonormal. To achieve this, one can compute , the N × N matrix of overlap integrals1between the basis functions ϕμ(r), μ = 1, ..., N and then use a matrix X that satisfies2to transform the various Hamiltonian matrices into the orthogonalized ϕμ basis.1,2

Alternatively, one can compute , the matrix of overlap integrals3between nonorthogonal molecular orbitals ψi(r), i = 1, ..., n and then use its inverse4to transform the Hamiltonian matrices into the orthogonalized ψi basis.

If the basis functions ϕμ are localized but the molecular orbitals ψi are not, the first approach is usually preferred because effective cutoff strategies3 can be used to avoid the calculation of most of the σμν. However, solving eq 2 for X typically requires O(N3) work.4

If the molecular orbitals ψi are localized,5−7 the second approach may be better because analogous cutoff strategies can be used to avoid the calculation of most of the sij and subsequently solving eq 4 for Z typically requires only O(n3) work.4 However, although this is usually much better than O(N3), it will still become a computational bottleneck in large systems and it is desirable to seek less expensive approximations to Z.

In this work, we assume that the ψi are sufficiently localized that, for a given j, only O(1) elements sij are larger than a user-specified threshold τ. The matrix A with elements5is sparse by definition and we will seek another equally sparse matrix B that is a well-defined approximation to A–1 and that can be computed in O(n) work. We will then interpret B as a sparse approximation to Z.

One may ask whether there is any reason to believe that there exists a B sufficiently close to Z to be useful. Many years ago, Demko, Moss and Smith proved8 that, if S is a sparse, symmetric positive-definite n × n matrix, the elements of Z decay exponentially. If the condition number of S is bounded with respect to n, this implies that it is theoretically possible to compute an arbitrarily accurate sparse approximate inverse of S in O(n) work. Benzi and Tůma subsequently extended this result to sparse approximate inverses in factorized form.9 Because S is the Gram matrix10 over localized orbitals, it is symmetric and positive-definite and should be well conditioned. The Demko-Moss-Smith result therefore provides grounds for hoping that B may be a useful approximation to Z.

There is a large literature on the development of algorithms to solve the linear system Ax = b, where A is a large sparse matrix and b is a known vector. Most work has sought to compute x directly and there has usually been less interest in finding a sparse B ≈ A–1. This is understandable because solving a linear system via explicit matrix inversion is usually not a good idea. Moreover, even when explicit approximate inverses have been sought, it is often to use them as conjugate gradient preconditioners,11−16 rather than for themselves.

The most popular methods17 for constructing a Sparse Approximate Inverse (SPAI) matrix B ≈ A–1 minimize the Frobenius norm4 of the residual matrix6by solving the normal equation7If a column of B contains only m = O(1) nonzero elements, it can be found in O(m3) work by computing the relevant m × m submatrix of ATA and solving the resulting linear system. The cost of finding all of the columns of B is therefore O(m3n) and, moreover, because the columns are independent, they can be computed in parallel.

The Approximate Inverse (AINV14 and SAINV17) approaches pioneered by Benzi, Meyer and Tůma are more sophisticated than schemes that prescribe the sparsity of B in advance, for they add nonzeros to B “on the fly” as they seek to reach a target accuracy. They have been successfully applied in the framework of electronic structure theory by Challacombe,18 Bowler and Miyazaki19 and Benzi et al.20

Unfortunately, the SPAIs obtained from least-squares schemes such as eq 7 do not possess a contravariance property that is highly desirable in certain contexts. We discuss this in more detail below. The need for a general algorithm that yields a contravariant sparse approximate inverse was our primary motivation for investigating the alternative approximation that we now describe.

Contravariance

The main motivation for investigating the iso-inverse, rather than the SPAIs formed by minimizing the Frobenius norm of R, is that the iso-inverse enjoys a contravariance21 property that is essential in certain contexts. Specifically, if the ith row and ith column of A are scaled by μ ≠ 0, the ith row and ith column of B are scaled by 1/μ and the other elements of B are unchanged. This property is relevant when, for example, A is a Gram matrix and the ultimate target of the calculation is a scalar (such as an energy) that should be invariant to scaling of the vectors in the underlying inner product space.

Implementation

If each column of A has O(1) nonzeros, can be found by a parallelizable algorithm whose cost scales only linearly with n and that is summarized in Algorithm 1 (Scheme 1). The nonzeros of A are stored by column in a rectangular real array A, the row indices of those nonzeros are stored in an isomorphic integer array NZ and the nonzeros of B are stored in an isomorphic real array. The number of columns of A is n and the maximum number of nonzeros in any column of A is m. Two small integer scratch arrays, NNZ(n) and Self(n), and one small real scratch array Sub(m, m) are used.

Calculation of the Right Iso-inverse of a Matrix A

The algorithm has three phases, each involving an outer loop over the columns of A or B. These loops are computationally independent and can be parallelized, if desired.

The SETUP phase (lines 12–17) extracts three types of information from the NZ array. At line 15, NNZ(j) records the total number of nonzeros in column j of A. At line 16, Self(j) records the location of j in column j of NZ. At line 17, the algorithm checks that the row indices in each column of NZ are strictly increasing. This phase requires O(mn) integer and logical operations and no floating-point operations (flops).

The SUBMAT phase (lines 18–31) forms n small dense linear systems whose solutions will yield the columns of B. To compute column j of B, a small unit vector (line 19) and a small matrix (line 20) are initialized. Some of the elements of the matrix are then overwritten (line 30) by selected nonzero elements of A. To locate these, an efficient two-pointer search (lines 24–31) is employed, exploiting the fact that the NZ lists are increasing. This phase requires O(m2n) noncontiguous loads from A and no flops.

The LINSOL phase (line 32) uses standard dense linear algebra4 to compute the columns of B. This requires O(m3n) flops and is therefore expected to be the most expensive phase. However, it can be performed efficiently via the LAPACK routine22 DGESV.

If A is symmetric, the efficiencies of all three phases can be improved by considering only the lower triangle of A and by using the DSYSV or DPOSV routine in the LINSOL phase.

Linear scaling with n is observed and the total CPU time for the largest iso-inversion (n = 106 and m = 91) is less than 30 s. In all the tests, the SUBMAT phase is significantly faster than the LINSOL phase and the bottleneck of the iso-inverse calculations is therefore performed by the BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms) that underpin LAPACK. Accuracy

Iso-inverses can be very poor approximations and it is easy to design pathological examples to demonstrate this. For example, the symmetric positive definite matrix and its inverse31have the iso-inverse and residual32and the first and third columns of B are alarmingly different from those of A–1.

Even in the absence of such pathologies, it is difficult to make general statements about the accuracy of iso-inverses because the definition of “accurate” depends on the context in which the iso-inverse will be applied. Sometimes, one is interested in the value of a particular element of the inverse and it is possible that the corresponding element of the iso-inverse may not be sufficiently accurate to be useful. On other occasions, the inverse will be contracted with an external matrix and the “pointwise” accuracy of the iso-inverse is then not as important as its “integrated” accuracy. But, of course, that integrated accuracy will depend on the elements of the external matrix.

Nonetheless, to provide the interested reader with some indicative results, we collected statistics for each of the matrices used in Section 5 and these are shown in the final three columns of Table 1. It is encouraging to discover that, without exception, the largest element max(R) of the residual matrix is smaller than the smallest nonzero element min(A) of the matrix A. Moreover, the mean absolute nonzero element mean(R) of the residual matrix is at least an order of magnitude smaller again.

Conclusions

For an n × n matrix A, the iso-inverse B is well-defined and exhibits a contravariance property that is important in some applications. If each column of A has m nonzeros, the cost of computing B is O(m3n) and the algorithm can be parallelized over the columns of B. If A is dense, then B = A–1 exactly. The cost of computing the exact inverse A–1 is O(n3) and the iso-inverse is likely therefore to be computationally cheaper when m ≪ n2/3.

In electronic structure calculations on large systems with localized, nonorthogonal molecular orbitals and a sparse overlap matrix S, we have found23 that the iso-inverse of S is an efficient alternative to S–1, within the Löwdin formalism.24

The iso-inverse B is generally slightly asymmetric and is not guaranteed to be positive definite, even when A is. These features may hinder its potential usefulness as a conjugate-gradient preconditioner,15 but this should be explored further.

An iso-inverse could also be used as the initial guess for the iterative calculation of the exact inverse using, for example, the quadratically convergent Newton iterate2533or the cubically convergent iterate2634

We anticipate that, because of its low computational cost and contravariant behavior, the iso-inverse may find applications in a variety of fields.
