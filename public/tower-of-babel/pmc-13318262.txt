Real-World Application of the WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node Cytopathology in the Context of the 5th Edition of the WHO Classification of Hematolymphoid Neoplasms
Authors: Ana G. Yuil Valdes, Safina Hafeez, Terrance J. Lynn, Joseph D. Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytology is central to the initial work-up of lymphadenopathy in the era of minimally invasive procedures, but some inherent limitations such as loss of tissue architecture require ancillary testing in some cases.SummaryThe WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology (2024) standardizes category-based reporting and management, while the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumors (WHO-HAEM5) defines diagnostic entities and criteria.Key MessagesWe review the real-world application of the WHO Reporting System across the five diagnostic categories, highlight cytomorphologic patterns that map to WHO-HAEM5 entities, and provide an overview for triaging material to flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, as well as molecular and genetic testing. We posit that properly selected and validated ancillary studies enable WHO-concordant diagnoses from cytology and reduce repeat procedures.

Introduction

The diagnostic evaluation of cytology specimens poses unique challenges because of the limited structural details and often low cellularity. To effectively address these complexities, it is essential to have a thorough understanding of the cytomorphological characteristics of both normal and abnormal lymphoid tissues. The evaluation and diagnosis process relies on the ability to recognize variations in cell sizes and arrangements, as these features can offer critical insights into the nature of hematolymphoid disorders. Equally important is having a reporting framework that standardizes the definitions and reporting elements in pathology reports on cytology specimens.

In 2024, the WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology (WHO System) was published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer under the WHO Reporting Systems for Cytopathology series. The primary aim of the series was to provide standardized terminology for reporting cytology samples coupled with diagnostic management recommendations. The WHO System is aligned with the WHO-HAEM5 [1]. However, it should be noted that some entities in WHO-HAEM5 are not included in the WHO System because cytology does not play a role in their diagnoses [2, 3].

The WHO System defines five diagnostic categories for lymph node, spleen, and thymus cytopathology: (1) nondiagnostic (insufficient/inadequate); (2) benign; (3) atypical; (4) suspicious for malignancy; and (5) malignant. The management of cases within each diagnostic category depends on clinical information, imaging results, cytomorphology, and ancillary testing. Each category is associated with an estimated risk of malignancy derived predominantly from retrospective series (Table 1). Reported risk of malignancy values vary across cohorts and practice settings; therefore, ranges are meant to reflect typical general values that may differ in local settings depending on institutional practice [4, 5]. Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) is endorsed to reduce the nondiagnostic rate and to triage material for ancillary testing [6, 7].

Diagnostic Categories

Category: Inadequate/Insufficient/Nondiagnostic

Definition

A specimen categorized as “insufficient/inadequate/nondiagnostic” lacks sufficient material in quantity or quality for reliable diagnosis (Fig. 1).

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 40X) – nondiagnostic. The specimen is acellular and contains ultrasound gel only.

Category: Benign

Definition

A specimen categorized as “benign” demonstrates unequivocal cytopathological features which may or may not be diagnostic of a specific process or benign neoplasm (Fig. 2, 3).

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 20X) – benign. Follicular hyperplasia. Polymorphic lymphoid cells with lymphocytes, centrocytes, centroblasts, immunoblasts, tingible-body macrophages, follicular dendritic cells, and histiocytes.

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 40X) – benign. Granulomatous inflammation. The specimen shows non-necrotizing epithelioid granulomas.

Category: Atypical

Definition

A specimen categorized as “atypical” demonstrates features seen predominantly in benign lesions and minimal features that may raise the possibility of a malignant lesion, but with features insufficient, either in number or quality, to diagnose a benign or malignant process or lesion (Fig. 4).

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 40X) – atypical. The specimen shows a monotonous population of small lymphocytes and rare follicular dendritic cells and tingible-body macrophages. The predominance of the small lymphocytes is considered atypical. No CB or sample for FC was submitted. A lymphoproliferative disorder cannot be excluded. An excisional biopsy was performed and revealed a progressive transformation of germinal centers.

Category: Suspicious for Malignancy

Definition

A specimen categorized as “suspicious for malignancy” demonstrates some cytopathological features suggestive of malignancy but with insufficient features, in number or quality, to make an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy (Fig. 5).

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 40X) – suspicious for malignancy. Hypocellular specimen with intermediate to large pleomorphic lymphoid cells. No CB or sample for FC was submitted. A lymphoproliferative disorder cannot be excluded. An excisional biopsy was performed and revealed a PTCL.

Category: Malignant

Definition

A specimen categorized as “malignant” demonstrates unequivocal cytopathological features of malignancy.

Entities in the Malignant Category

For the malignant chapter, the WHO classification system uses cell types and patterns. There are 7 cell types and patterns: mixed lymphoid, predominantly small/intermediate, predominantly intermediate/large/pleomorphic/blastic, single very large atypical cell pattern, histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms, stroma-derived neoplasms of lymphoid tissue, and metastases.

Mixed Lymphoid Cell Pattern

Follicular Lymphoma

Classic follicular lymphoma (FL) is the most common type of FL. There are two new subtypes that require further clarification in the cytopathology literature: FL with a diffuse growth pattern and FL with unusual cytological features, which may include blastoid or large centrocytic cells.

FL was previously defined and graded primarily based on the evaluation of cells within their nodal architecture. Due to its poor reproducibility and limited clinical impact in many cohorts, grading of FL is now optional [8, 9].

The cytology specimens are hypercellular with a mixed pattern of lymphoid cells of different sizes, including reactive lymphocytes, dendritic cells, small to medium centrocytes with irregular nuclei, and dense to dispersed chromatin with small nucleoli, and larger round centroblasts with round to oval nuclei and dispersed chromatin with one or two nucleoli (Fig. 6). FLs have an architecture that can be challenging to assess using fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB). However, they typically display B-cell markers and co-express CD10, along with the t(14;18) translocation (Fig. 7) [10]. Flow cytometry (FC) can be conducted on FNAB specimens and plays a crucial role in the diagnostic process [11]. The most common differential diagnoses are follicular hyperplasia and small B-cell lymphomas.

Marginal Zone Lymphoma

Marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) is a group of mature B-cell lymphomas that has several subtypes and unique associations. The subtypes include extranodal, splenic, and nodal MZL [3]. Extranodal MZL (EMZL) is associated with autoimmune diseases and gastric infections such as Helicobacter pylori, and ocular infections with Chlamydia psittaci. Splenic and EMZL are associated with hepatitis C infections [3].

Each of the subtypes is similar by cytomorphology [12]. The smears are hypercellular and are composed of a mixed cell-type population. This population includes centrocyte-like cells, small and large lymphocytes, plasma cells, monocytoid cells, dendritic cells, tingible-body macrophages, and eosinophils (Fig. 8) [12]. Lymphohistiocytic aggregates and multinucleated giant cells can also be present.

Lymph node FNAB. MZL. a H&E FNB 40X mixed population of small lymphoid cells and plasma cells. b CD20 staining the small lymphoid cells. c CD138 highlights the plasma cells. d, e Kappa and lambda immunostains with lambda restriction.

The typical phenotype for MZL includes positive expression of CD20, PAX5, CD79a, BCL2, and IgM [10, 12]. MZL is negative for CD5, CD10, CD23, cyclin D1, and SOX11.

By cytogenetics, MZL often will have trisomies involving chromosomes 3, 12, and/or 18. Site-specific translocations involving BIRC3 and MALT1 are present in EMZL involving the MALT [10, 13]. Rearrangements are not present within nodal or primary cutaneous MZL. Nodal MZL typically will have gains in chromosome 2p, 6p, trisomy 12, and loss of 1p and 6q.

By molecular methods, extranodal and splenic MZL have variants within the NOTCH1, NOTCH2, SPEN, TNFAIP, BIRC3, TRAF3, KLF2, KMT2D, PTPRD, and/or BRAF genes. The differential for MZL is broad and can be challenging to differentiate between reactive conditions due to the mixed cell population. The differential also includes other lymphomas such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL), lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL), mantle, follicular, and T-cell lymphomas.

Utilization of ancillary molecular studies is useful to help support reactive versus neoplastic conditions. Immunoglobulin heavy-chain (IGH) gene rearrangement studies using the BIO-MED 2 consensus primers to the joining region, Framework II, and Framework III regions can be used (Fig. 9) [10, 14]. Results should be considered within the context of cytomorphology and other ancillary studies. Detecting a clone is not synonymous with neoplasia because within germinal centers, B cells proliferate to combat antigens and it is common for one or more clones to expand [3]. This is important to reduce the possibility of a diagnostic pitfall.

An example of a clonal IGH rearrangement in framework II (c) and III (d) regions with sizes of 254 and 126 base pairs, respectively.

Nodal T Follicular Helper Cell Lymphoma, Angioimmunoblastic Type

Nodal T follicular helper cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic type (nTFHL-AI), previously known as angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, is a type of peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) with a T follicular helper (TFH) phenotype. It belongs to a group of lymphomas originating from TFH cells and exhibits morphological and immunophenotypic similarities with several subtypes [8, 15].

On cytology, nTFHL-AI is characterized by a mixture of neoplastic lymphoid cells and a polymorphic reactive infiltrate. This infiltrate includes immunoblasts, eosinophils, plasma cells, follicular dendritic cells, vascular proliferation, and a lack of tingible-body macrophages. The presence of this heterogeneous polymorphic population in cytological smears is often misdiagnosed as reactive lymphoid hyperplasia [16, 17].

The role FC is controversial due to the prevalence of polymorphic hematolymphoid infiltrate and the relatively low number of neoplastic lymphoid cells. Nevertheless, FC has proven helpful in diagnosing PTCLs, including nTFHL-AI. FC is characterized by abnormal T-cell populations that exhibit a loss of CD3 and/or CD7 expression, along with positivity for CD10 [17].

A concurrent cell block (CB) or core needle biopsy (CNB) is essential for making a definitive diagnosis and ordering TFH immunostains (PD1, ICOS, BCL6, CXCL13, and CD10) (Fig. 10). It is not uncommon to classify these cases as T-cell lymphoproliferative disorders or PTCLs due to the limited tissue available for immunostaining.

Lymph node FNAB and CNB. nTFHL-AI. a Cytospin, DQ stain 40X. Mixed population of small to intermediate lymphoid cells and eosinophils. b H&E CNB 40X. Mixed population of small, intermediate and large lymphoid cells, eosinophils, and high endothelial venules. c The lymphoma cells are positive for CD3. d PD-1 stains the TFH cells.

The differential diagnosis also includes atypical T-zone hyperplasia, granulomatous lymphadenopathy, PTCL, PTCL with TFH-cell immunophenotype, nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL), T-cell/histiocyte-rich large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), and hyaline-vascular subtype of Castleman disease. For distinguishing reactive versus neoplastic T-cell proliferations, clonality can be assessed initially with a multi-tube or one-tube T-cell receptor gamma gene rearrangement assay (Fig. 11) [10, 13, 14, 18]. A one-tube assay has been shown to significantly reduce false-positive calls from the original multiple single-tube assays [18].

Predominantly Small/Intermediate Cell Pattern

Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia/Small Lymphocytic Lymphoma

CLL/SLL is an indolent B-cell neoplasm typically identified in older adults which involves the bone marrow/peripheral blood, or lymph nodes/spleen/extramedullary sites, respectively. Unlike peripheral blood, the requirement for an absolute lymphocyte cell count of greater than 5 × 109/L is not required to diagnose SLL by cytopathology when lymphadenopathy >15 mm exists, or involvement of the spleen or other extramedullary site is identified.

Cytologically, CLL/SLL cells appear as a monotonous lymphoid population with round nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, coarse clumped chromatin, and scant cytoplasm (Fig. 12). Mitotic figures are not identified. The background can contain scattered prolymphocytes or paraimmunoblasts. Noticeably, the background will lack tingible-body macrophages.

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 20X). CLL/SLL. The specimen shows a monotonous population of small lymphocytes with scant cytoplasm, round nucleus, regular nuclear membranes, and clumped chromatin.

By FC, the cells will express CD19, CD20 (dim), CD23, CD43, CD200, aberrant expression of CD5, and light chain restriction [10, 19] (Fig. 13). In the absence of FC and when a CB is present, immunocytochemistry (ICC) can be performed. The CLL/SLL cells will positively stain for CD5, CD20, CD23, and LEF1 [3, 10, 19]. Stains for CD10 and cyclin D1 are negative.

Multiparameter FC analysis of CLL/SLL. FC dot plots demonstrating an aberrant population of small B cells (low forward scatter) positive for CD5, CD19, CD20 (dim), CD23, and monotypic surface lambda light chain (dim); they are negative for CD38.

Prognostic information and therapeutic guidance can be obtained by the combination of cytogenetic and molecular testing. When SLL is suspected on ROSE, a specimen can be obtained for subsequent stimulated cell culture and metaphase karyotyping and/or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) studies. The studies facilitate the identification of chromosomal abnormalities including del(11q23), del(13q), del(17p), and trisomy 12 (Fig. 14) [19]. By sequencing, evaluation of immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable region and TP53 can be performed on cytologic preparations and CBs [3, 10].

Cytogenetic study of CLL/SLL cells cultured from a cytology FNA specimen. a Metaphase chromosomes from a case of SLL prior to karyotyping. b FISH from the same case demonstrating trisomy 12 and absence of del(13q). c FISH results demonstrating the absence of del(11q) and del(17p). d The results of stimulated cell culture from the SLL case which show a karyotype of 47,XY,add(10),+del12,del(14) in all 20 cells.

Patients can be monitored over time for disease progression or transformation. Transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified (DLBCL-NOS), HL, plasmablastic lymphoma can occur. The gain of additional genetic alterations within TP53, NOTCH1, and/or CDKN2A/B is thought to be responsible for Richter transformation/disease progression.

In patients with a known history of CLL, diagnostic challenges may arise in specimens that are contaminated by peripheral blood. Careful evaluation is required prior to reporting true involvement as this would indicate disease progression.

Mantle Cell Lymphoma

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an aggressive, mature B-cell lymphoma that typically is seen in middle-aged or older patients and originates from the mantle zone of the lymphoid follicle. Cytologically, the classic subtype of MCL is composed of small- to medium-sized monomorphic B cells with variable nuclear contour irregularities, condensed chromatin with an even distribution, absence of nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm (Fig. 15) [20]. The background typically is devoid of tingible-body macrophages, paraimmunoblasts, prolymphocytes, and centroblasts. For the blastoid subtype of MCL, the cells are also monotonous but are intermediate to large in size [20]. The nuclei may be round or have irregularly indented, angulated, or cleaved contours. A conspicuous nucleolus may be present, which can mimic a lymphoblast. The chromatin is finely distributed across the nucleus. The pleomorphic subtype has large lymphoid cells with prominent nucleoli, coarse and clumpy chromatin, and irregular nuclear contours [20]. The background often will show tingible-body macrophages with ingested apoptotic debris. In the absence of ancillary studies, the differentiation between classic MCL and other B-cell and some T-cell lymphomas is challenging. Distinguishing blastoid or pleomorphic MCL from lymphoblastic, Burkitt, or other high-grade B-cell lymphomas without sufficient material for ancillary studies will likely require an additional biopsy.

Lymph node FNAB. MCL. a DQ stain 40X. b H&E CB 40X. Monotonous population of intermediate-size neoplastic lymphoid cells with high N:C ratio and irregular nuclear membranes. c The neoplastic cells are positive for cyclin D1.

By FC, MCL expresses CD19, CD20, aberrant expression of CD5, and light chain restriction [10, 20] (Fig. 16). They are negative for CD10, CD23, and CD200, with the latter that help exclude CLL/SLL.

Multiparameter FC analysis of MCL. FC dot plots demonstrating an aberrant population of small B cells (low forward scatter) that is positive for CD20 (bright), CD19, CD5, and moderate intensity surface lambda light chain expression.

By ICC, MCL will have positive staining for CD5, CD20, CD43, BCL2, SOX11, and overexpression of cyclin D1 [10, 20]. A ki-67 index can be performed on a CB or cytospin preparation; however, it may be inaccurately elevated when compared to CNB. A ki-67 index ≥30% has a worse prognosis and less favorable outcomes. Diagnostic challenges and/or pitfalls may arise in rare cases that are cyclin D1-negative. In these cases, SOX11 is valuable as it is positive in majority of the cases. In rare cases of CD5-negative MCL, the majority will still be positive for cyclin D1 by ICC.

By cytogenetic or molecular studies, MCL has a characteristic translocation involving CCND1 and IGH [10, 13, 21]. The t(11;14)(pq13;q32) results in the overexpression of cyclin D1. However, it is important to recognize that this translocation can be seen in other entities (i.e., multiple myeloma). Assessment of TP53 mutation status is important prognostically and can be accomplished with next-generation sequencing (NGS). MCL with mutated TP53 has a poor prognosis.

Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma

LPL is a neoplasm that arises in late middle-aged individuals and is more common in males. The WHO-HAEM5 classifies these cases into two different types. The IGM LPL/Waldenström macroglobulinemia accounts for majority of cases. The second type is the non-Waldenström macroglobulinemia-type LPL. The latter type includes cases in which there is IgM LPL without bone marrow involvement, cases with monoclonal IgG proteins, and nonsecretory LPL.

Cytologically, LPL consists of a variety of small B-lymphocytes, plasmacytoid cells, and plasma cells [3]. Intranuclear pseudoinclusions (Dutcher bodies) composed of immunoglobulins and/or intracytoplasmic globular inclusions (Russell bodies) may be identified. Mitotic figures and necrosis are generally not identified. The background can have a basophilic appearance with red cells in rouleaux formation from the high immunoglobulins.

By FC, two populations of cells may be identified. The plasma cells will express CD19, CD38, CD45, CD138, and have cytoplasmic light chains [3]. They are negative for CD56 and CD117. The B-lymphocytes will express CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a, surface light chains, and have variable expression of CD38. They will lack expression of CD3, CD10, CD11c, CD103, CD123, and CD200 [3].

On ICC, there will be two populations of cells with different expression patterns. The B-lymphocytes are generally positive for CD20, PAX-5, BOB1, and OCT2, while negative for DBA.44 [3]. The plasma cells will be negative for CD20 but be positive for CD138 and MUM1/IRF4. The ki-67 proliferation index will be low.

By molecular testing, majority of LPL/Waldenström macroglobulinemia cases will have the canonical MYD88 p.L265P variant (Fig. 17) [3]. In rare cases, other non-p.L265P MYD88 variants are identified. In cases of non-Waldenström macroglobulinemia-type LPL, only half of these will possess the MYD88 p.L265P variant. Other common variants occur in the CXCR4 gene in the c-terminal domain [3]. Due to the possibility of high background contamination by other cells, NGS may be falsely negative based on the validated limit of detection for the assay utilized.

Plasma Cell Neoplasms

Plasma cell neoplasms (PCNs) are likely to be encountered in cytology when there is a solitary lesion (e.g., plasmacytoma), body fluid involvement, or other organ involvement [3, 22]. By cytomorphology, the smears are often hypercellular and contain mature plasma cells with round to ovoid nuclei, condensed chromatin arranged in a clock-face pattern, indistinct nucleoli, a perinuclear hof, and eccentrically located cytoplasm (Fig. 18) [3, 22]. In some cases, multiple intracytoplasmic globules (Mott cells) resembling a bunch of grapes can be seen. The plasma cells may be arranged in small aggregates or as isolated single cells [22]. In immature PCNs, the cells will have a high nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio (N:C ratio), nuclei containing conspicuous nucleoli, and fine chromatin. Mitotic figures and nuclear pleomorphism can be encountered in the immature PCN.

Left neck mass FNAB. Plasmacytoma. a DQ stain 40X. b PAP stain 40X. Atypical plasma cells with eccentric cytoplasm, round nuclei with clumped chromatin, and rare binucleate forms identified.

When the specimen has multiple different inflammatory cells in the background, distinguishing between reactive or neoplastic conditions is challenging in the absence of ancillary studies. Other lymphomas containing plasma cells in the background with other inflammatory cells include MZL, LPL, CHL, and neuroendocrine neoplasms.

By FC, neoplastic plasma cells in majority of cases will express CD38 and CD138 [3, 22]. The aberrant expression of CD56, CD117, CD20, CD28, CD52, or CD200 helps exclude reactive plasma cells. Majority of PCN cases will have monotypic cytoplasmic Ig heavy and light chains. The specific heavy-chain restriction can be either IgG, IgA, IgD, IgE, or IgM, with the latter being the least common [3].

By ICC, the neoplastic plasma cells express CD38, CD138, and MUM1/IRF4 [22]. Similarly to FC, aberrant expression of one or more CD markers previously mentioned can be identified. They are negative for CD3, CD19, CD20, CD27, CD45, and CD81 [3].

Various cytogenetic abnormalities have been reported in PCN [3]. Selective enrichment of the plasma cells by immunomagnetic methods is often utilized for subsequent cell culture. By these enrichment methods, abnormal FISH findings are identified in a majority of cases and allow for further classification. Cases are classified as hyperdiploidy if they have gains of three or more chromosomes. Most commonly, chromosomes 3, 4, 7, 9, 11, 13, 19, and/or 21 are involved [3]. Cases lacking hyperdiploidy have IGH gene rearrangements as their primary cytogenetic abnormality [3]. Other abnormalities can be seen in disease progression (e.g., TP53 mutations, MYC abnormalities).

Predominantly Intermediate/Large/Pleomorphic/Blastic Cell Pattern

Lymphoblastic Lymphoma

B-lymphoblastic lymphoma (B-LBL) and T-lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL) are neoplasms of precursor lymphoid cells with similar cytomorphological features. T-LBL accounts for approximately 85% to 90% of lymphoblastic lymphoma cases [23].

The lymphoblasts in T-LBL and B-LBL are morphologically identical. The cytology specimens are highly cellular, consisting of intermediate-sized cells with a high N:C ratio, fine chromatin, small nucleoli, and nuclear clefts.

The differential diagnosis includes Ewing sarcoma, thymoma, Burkitt lymphoma (BL), myeloid sarcoma, and blastoid MCL. Diagnosing T-LBL in the context of a mediastinal mass should be approached with caution, because both thymoma and T-LBL can display similar immature T-cell phenotypes by FC. It is essential to recognize that the expression of CD3, CD45, CD4, and CD8 in thymocytes exhibits a smearing pattern that ranges from high density to negative, reflecting the distinct stages of T-cell maturation. In contrast, T-LBL cells show these antigens in a clustered pattern. The loss of T-cell antigens alongside the expression of CD10, TdT, and CD34 indicates the presence of T-LBL [24].

B-LBL exhibits a B-cell phenotype characterized by the presence of CD19, CD79a, CD22, CD24, and CD10. Genetic abnormalities are identifiable through karyotyping, FISH, and NGS, which can help define specific types of B-LBL and are linked to risk categories and prognosis.

Large B-Cell Lymphomas

LBCL is the most common form of aggressive non-HL. The WHO-HAEM5 classification includes DLBCL-NOS and seventeen specific entities (Table 2). DLBCL includes the various morphological, immunophenotype, and molecular subgroups with variable clinical presentations and outcomes. However, they exhibit very similar cytomorphological features [25].

Types of LBCLs

Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma NOS

DLBCL-NOS is the most common type of lymphoma in adults. It is classified based on the cell of origin into the germinal-center B-cell and activated B-cell subtypes.

The cytology specimens are hypercellular and contain a monotonous population of large atypical lymphoid cells. These are characterized by a high N:C ratio, round to pleomorphic nuclei, fine irregular chromatin, and one to several prominent nucleoli (Fig. 19).

Lymph node FNAB (DQ stain 40X). LBCL. a DQ stain 40X. b H&E CB 40X. The specimen shows large lymphoma cells with scant cytoplasm and lobulated nuclei and nucleoli. The lymphoma cells are positive for CD20 (c) and PAX-5 (d).

There are three types of DLBCL-NOS by cell cytomorphology: centroblastic, immunoblastic, and anaplastic. Ancillary testing is crucial for classifying cytopathology samples of LBCL. The lymphoma cells express CD45 and pan-B-cell markers (CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a, and PAX5). In some instances, FC may yield false-negative results due to low cellularity or the loss of viable large cells due to increased cell fragility (Fig. 20) [26].

FC analysis of LBCL. FC dot plots demonstrating an aberrant population of large B cells (high forward scatter) that are positive for CD19, CD38, with moderate intensity surface lambda light chain expression.

Gene expression profiling (GEP) is considered the gold standard for determining cell of origin. If GEP is not available, ICC may serve as an alternative, following the Hans algorithm which utilizes the markers CD10, BCL-6, and MUM-1. The differential diagnosis of LBCL includes malignancies with a diffuse growth pattern of a medium to large cell type including B- and T-cell lymphomas, histiocytic sarcoma, myeloid sarcoma, and metastatic tumors.

Burkitt Lymphoma

BL is an aggressive neoplasm of mature B cells. There are three subtypes of BL: endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated.

The cytology specimen shows medium-sized cells with round nuclei, finely granular chromatin, multiple nucleoli, basophilic cytoplasm containing lipid vacuoles, and a high mitotic rate (Fig. 21). The immunophenotype of BL includes B-cell markers CD19 and CD20, expression of CD10, absence of BCL-2 and TdT, and a high proliferation index by ki-67. Approximately 90% of BL cases are positive for c-MYC rearrangement by FISH. The t(8;14) translocation involving the IGH locus and MYC is the most common (Fig. 22). Other rearrangements that may occur include the variants t(2;8) and t(8;22), which involve the immunoglobulin light chain loci [27].

Over 90% of endemic subtypes are positive for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), while 20% of sporadic subtypes are EBV positive. All cases of BL should be tested using EBER in situ hybridization [28]. The differential diagnosis of BL includes DLBCL-NOS, DLBCL/high-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration, B-LBL, T-LBL, blastoid subtype of MCL, myeloid sarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, neuroblastoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma.

Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a rare form of mature T-cell non-HL characterized by variable clinical presentations and heterogeneous immunophenotypic and molecular features. The morphological hallmark of ALCL is the presence of large and pleomorphic neoplastic cells with abundant cytoplasm, horseshoe or reniform-shaped nuclei, and immunohistochemical expression of CD30 (Fig. 23) [26]. There are two distinct types of ALCL, ALK-positive and ALK-negative. The diagnosis of these two types of lymphoma cannot be made solely based on cytomorphology.

Lymph node FNAB. ALCL. a H&E CB 40X. b CD30 immunostain. The specimen shows large and pleomorphic neoplastic cells with abundant cytoplasm, horseshoe or reniform-shaped nuclei, and immunohistochemical expression of CD30.

The recommended ICC panel includes CD20, CD3, CD30, ALK, CD2, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, TCR proteins, and one or more cytotoxic markers (e.g., TIA-1, granzyme, and/or perforin). ALK-positive ALCL is defined by the translocation of the ALK gene with various genetic partners. The most common rearrangement is t(2;5)(p23;q35), which results in a fusion of ALK with the NPM1 gene on chromosome 5 [29]. Depending on the molecular assay and methodology utilized (e.g., amplicon-based), diagnostic pitfalls can occur with the detection of ALK rearrangements. Amplicon-based testing requires the knowledge of both fusion partners, location where the fusion occurs, and the need to design primers for each gene partner involved in the fusion. Many gene fusions involve different exons, different gene partners, or novel fusion partners, which makes the designing of primers complex. In the setting of a diagnostic entity that is expected to harbor a fusion, a negative NGS result must be thoroughly examined to identify the cause. However, some amplicon-based platforms have built-in bioinformatic approaches to help identify an ALK fusion (Fig. 24).

The cluster of gray lines indicates the baseline in fusion-negative samples, while the blue line shows the test sample corrected (normalized) to the baseline. There is an expression imbalance between the 5′- and the kinase domain containing 3′-terminus of the ALK gene strongly supporting an ALK fusion. In contrast, ALK-negative ALCL represents a diverse genetic landscape primarily involving the JAK/STAT3 pathway along with rearrangements of DUSP22 or TP63 [30]. The differential diagnosis includes CHL, poorly differentiated carcinoma, germ cell tumors, melanoma, T-cell-rich B-cell lymphoma, and CD30-positive DLBCL [26].

The most important differential diagnosis is between ALK-negative ALCL and CHL. ALK-negative ALCL can exhibit aberrant loss of T-cell markers and co-expression of CD15. CHL shows loss of B-cell markers and may aberrantly express T-cell markers. IGH and TCR gene rearrangement studies may be helpful with this differential if sufficient neoplastic cells are present [10, 13]. In clinical practice, most ALK-negative T-cell lymphomas that co-express CD30 and CD15, along with Hodgkin-like features, are best classified as peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) [29].

Primary Effusion Lymphoma

Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) is an aggressive type of B-cell non-HL that features a distinct clinical presentation. It was first recognized as an effusion lymphoma in patients with human immunodeficiency virus and is associated with the Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpes virus, also known as human herpesvirus 8 [31].

The lymphoma cells are typically large and discohesive, exhibiting round or irregular nuclear contours, variably prominent nucleoli, and moderately abundant basophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 25) [32]. A null lymphocyte phenotype characterizes PEL; the lymphoma cells may express CD45 but lack B-cell antigens such as CD19, CD20, CD22, PAX5, and CD79a, as well as surface and cytoplasmic immunoglobulins. In some cases, PEL cells may express aberrant T-cell markers. The lymphoma cells are usually positive for markers of lymphocyte activation, including CD30, CD38, CD71, EMA, and HLA-DR, as well as markers of plasma cell differentiation, such as CD138, VS38c, and MUM-1/IRF4 [33]. Diagnosing PEL relies heavily on the identification of Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpes virus/human herpesvirus 8, and the presence of EBER by in situ hybridization is also important, as approximately 80% of PEL cases are coinfected with EBV [34].

The most common aberrant epithelial marker expressed in PEL is EMA. Other epithelial markers that may be abnormally expressed include pancytokeratin and CAM 5.2. This diagnostic pitfall should be considered to avoid confusing lymphoma with carcinoma, especially when B-cell antigens are absent in the lymphoma cells [35, 36]. The differential diagnoses include undifferentiated carcinoma, ALCL, LBCL, and BL.

Peripheral T-Cell Lymphoma, Not Otherwise Specified

PTCL-NOS is a mature T-cell lymphoma involving nodal and extranodal sites. It is a heterogeneous category that cannot be assigned to a specific PTCL entity. The FNAB shows medium to large lymphoma cells with irregular hyperchromatic nuclei, often in a background of small lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, histiocytes.

PTCL-NOS expresses CD2, CD3, CD5, and CD7, with frequent loss of CD5 and CD7 [37]. Most cases are CD4 positive, although occasional cases may be positive for CD8 instead (Fig. 26).

Multiparameter FC analysis of PTCL. FC dot plots demonstrating an aberrant population of mature T cells that is positive for CD2 (bright), CD3 (dim), CD4, CD5, CD30, and alpha/beta T-cell receptor chain. CD8 is negative. These aberrant cells have variable forward- and side-scatter characteristics reflecting a range of sizes and cytoplasmic features.

GEP has identified two major molecular subgroups of PTCL-NOS, characterized by high expression of either GATA3 or TBX2 [38]. PTCL-GATA3 is associated with a poor prognosis. The differential diagnosis includes benign inflammatory infiltrate, CHL, and other PTCL subtypes.

Single Very Large Atypical Cell Pattern

Classic Hodgkin Lymphoma

CHL is a neoplasm derived from germinal-center B cells. There are four subtypes of CHL: nodular sclerosis, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, and lymphocyte-depleted.

The cytology specimen reveals a mixed inflammatory background containing large Reed-Sternberg cells with bilobed nuclei and prominent nucleoli, and Hodgkin cells characterized by a single round to pleomorphic nucleus with nucleoli (Fig. 27). FC has limited utility due to the low ratio of neoplastic cells to background cells. Due to this limitation, a CB or CNB is preferred to confirm the characteristic immunophenotype.

Lymph node FNAB (19-gauge needle). CHL. a DQ stain 40X. b H&E CB 40X. The specimen shows large atypical lymphoid cells with large nuclei and prominent nucleoli, characteristic of HRS cells. c The HRS cells are positive for CD30. HRS, Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg.

The Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells are positive for CD15, CD30, PAX5, MUM1, and negative for CD45 and T-cell markers. The differential diagnosis includes infectious mononucleosis, T-cell/histiocyte-rich LBCL, and ALCL [39].

Stroma-Derived Neoplasms of Lymphoid Tissues

Follicular Dendritic Cell Sarcoma

Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma is a malignant tumor that originates from follicular dendritic cells. In cytological specimens, the tumor cells typically appear oval or spindle-shaped, featuring ovoid nuclei and prominent nucleoli. In some cases, intranuclear pseudoinclusions and nuclear grooves may be observed. Additionally, binucleated cells that resemble germinal-center follicular dendritic cells, often called “kissing pairs,” are identified (Fig. 28) [40]. In the recent WHO-HAEM5, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma was moved from the category of “histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms” to “stroma-derived neoplasms of lymphoid tissues” and classified as a “mesenchymal dendritic cell neoplasm.”

Lymph node FNAB. FDCS. a PAP stain 40X. b H&E CB 40X. The specimen shows ovoid, binucleated, and multinucleated follicular dendritic tumor cells with prominent nucleoli in a lymphocyte-rich background. c The follicular dendritic cells are positive for CD23. FDCS, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma.

Conclusions

Cytopathologists need to develop the necessary expertise and understand both the diagnostic potential and limitations of FNAB. It is essential to highlight the importance of ROSE in triaging FNAB procedures. Immediate assessment enhances diagnostic accuracy and reduces the likelihood of insufficient sampling. This approach requires multidisciplinary coordination between, particularly, radiology and pathology.

By applying the WHO Reporting System, ROSE triage, appropriate ancillary testing, and reflex tissue sampling when architectural or extended biomarker profiling is necessary, cytology can provide diagnoses that align with WHO-HAEM5 standards for hematolymphoid tumors. This comprehensive approach facilitates more accurate lymphoma classification and enables tailored therapeutic regimens, thereby enhancing patient care.
