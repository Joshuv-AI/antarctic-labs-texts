Abstract
The celebrated conjecture by Payne, Pólya and Weinberger (1956) states that for the fixed membrane problem, the ratio of the first two eigenvalues, λ 2 / λ 1 \lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1} , is maximized by a disk. A more general dimensional version of this conjecture was later resolved by Ashbaugh and Benguria in the 1990s. For the Robin Laplacian, Payne and Schaefer (2001) formulated an analogous conjecture, positing that the ratio μ 2 / μ 1 \mu_{2}/\mu_{1} is also maximized by a disk for a range of the boundary parameter σ \sigma . This was later restated by Henrot in 2003. In this work, under some suitable conditions, we affirm this conjecture for all dimensions N ≥ 2 N\geq 2 and for all σ > 0 \sigma>0 . Furthermore, we prove that the maximum value of μ 2 / μ 1 \mu_{2}/\mu_{1} is strictly decreasing in σ \sigma over the entire interval ( 0 , + ∞ ) (0,+\infty) . Our result provides a positive answer to a variant of Yau’s Problem 77: by measuring the ratio of the first two eigenfrequencies, one can determine whether an elastically supported drum is circular.

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\int_{\left\{x\in\Omega:u_{1}(x)>t\right\}}u_{1}\,\text{d}x\left(\int_{\partial U_{t}^{ext}}\frac{1}{\left|\nabla u_{1}\right|}\,H_{N-1}(\text{d}x)+\int_{\partial U_{t}^{int}}\frac{1}{\sigma u_{1}}\,H_{N-1}(\text{d}x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\mu(t)}u_{1}^{*}(s)\,\text{d}s\left(-\mu^{\prime}(t)+\int_{\partial U_{t}^{ext}}\frac{1}{\sigma u_{1}}\,H_{N-1}(\text{d}x)\right).

Combining this with the classical isoperimetric inequality
P_{u_{1}}(t)\geq NC_{N}^{1/N}\mu^{1-1/N}(t)

[

37
,

\displaystyle N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\mu^{2-2/N}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\mu(t)}u_{1}^{*}(s)\,\text{d}s\left(-\mu^{\prime}(t)+\int_{\partial U_{t}^{ext}}\frac{1}{\sigma u_{1}}\,H_{N-1}(\text{d}x)\right).

(3.2)

This establishes the following key result.

Lemma 3.1.

The function
u_{1}^{*}(s)
is positive and continuous on
[0,|\Omega|]
. Moreover, one has that

-\left(u_{1}^{*}\right)^{\prime}(s)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{\frac{2}{N}-2}\int_{0}^{s}u_{1}^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t

(3.3)

for almost every
s\in\left[0,\mu\left(u_{1,pM}\right)\right)
.

Proof.
It is clear that
u_{1}^{*}(|\Omega|)=u_{1,m}>0
, which establishes the positivity of
u_{1}^{*}(s)
. By definition,

u_{1}^{*}
is known to be left-continuous
[
43
]
. We now prove that it is in fact continuous on the entire interval.
Since
u_{1}
is continuous, the distribution function

\mu(t)
is well-defined on

\left[u_{1,m},u_{1,M}\right]
, and can be continuously extended to

\left[0,+\infty\right)
. Being decreasing, bounded, and right-continuous,

\mu(t)
may have only jump discontinuities. At each such jump point

\mu(t)
, we connect the graph of

\mu(t)
by including the vertical segment

(\mu(t),\mu(t-)]
, thereby forming a continuous curve

C_{\mu}
. By the definition of

u_{1}^{*}(s)

[
43
]
, its graph over

[0,|\Omega|]
is the reflection of

C_{\mu}
across the line

s=t
. Since

C_{\mu}
is continuous, the graph of

u_{1}^{*}(s)
is also continuous. In particular,

u_{1}^{*}(s)
is continuous on

(0,|\Omega|]
.
To establish continuity at

s=0
, note that the continuity of

u_{1}
implies
\lim_{t\rightarrow u_{1,M}^{+}}\mu(t)=0
, while
\mu(t)>0
for all
t<u_{1,M}
. Hence,

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}u_{1}^{*}(s)=u_{1,M}=u_{1}^{*}(0),

which proves right-continuity at the origin. Therefore,

u_{1}^{*}(s)
is continuous on

[0,|\Omega|]
.

Since
u_{1}^{*}
remains constant on each connected component of the complement of the range of

\mu(t)
, the desired inequality holds trivially when

\mu(t)<s\leq\mu(t-)
. We therefore focus on the case where

lies within the range of

\mu(t)
, in which

u_{1}^{*}
and
\mu
are inverse functions of each other.
When
s
is in the range of

\mu(t)
, we may set

t=\mu^{-1}(s)
. For

and

belongs to the range of

, it follows that

, which implies

\partial U_{t}^{ext}=\emptyset
. Consequently, the boundary integral vanishes in (

), and the inequality (

) simplifies to

\displaystyle N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\mu^{2-2/N}(t)\leq

\displaystyle-\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\mu(t)}u_{1}^{*}(s)\,\text{d}s\mu^{\prime}(t).

which implies that

-\left(u_{1}^{*}\right)^{\prime}(s)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{\frac{2}{N}-2}\int_{0}^{s}u_{1}^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t.

This is the desired conclusion.∎

Due to the distinct nature of the Robin boundary condition, the level set boundary generally satisfies

\partial U_{t}\neq\left\{x\in\Omega:u_{1}(x)=t\right\}
. Consequently, inequality (

) is established only on a subinterval of

.
We note that the derivation of

on

.

Recall that
R=\alpha/\sqrt{\mu_{1}}
. Since

is the first eigenvalue of the Robin problem on the unit ball

, a standard scaling argument shows that

\mu_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of the corresponding problem on the ball

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta z=\mu z&\text{in}\,\,\,B_{R},\\
\partial_{\texttt{n}}z+\frac{\sigma}{R}z=0&\text{on}\,\,\,\partial B_{R}\end{array}\right.

with the corresponding eigenfunction

where

is a positive constant. Given that

, we have

, which contrasts with the Dirichlet case where the eigenfunction vanishes on the boundary.

We now establish a fundamental relation between the measures of
B_{R}
and
\Omega
.

Lemma 3.2.

It holds that

\left|B_{R}\right|\leq|\Omega|
, with equality if and only if

\Omega=B_{R}
.

Proof.
The first Robin eigenvalue admits the variational characterization

\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\inf_{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\sigma\int_{\partial\Omega}u^{2}\,\text{d}S}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\text{d}x}.

By the Faber-Krahn inequality for the Robin Laplacian, which was established by Bossel
[
10
]
for

N=2
and Daners
[
18
]
for

N\geq 2
, we have

\mu_{1}\left(B_{R}\right)=\mu_{1}(\Omega)\geq\mu_{1}\left(\Omega^{*}\right)

with equality if and only if

\Omega=\Omega^{*}
.

Now, suppose for contradiction that

\left|B_{R}\right|>|\Omega|
or
B_{R}\supsetneq\Omega^{*}
. Then by the domain monotonicity of

\mu_{1}

[
40
]
, we would have

\mu_{1}\left(B_{R}\right)\leq\mu_{1}\left(\Omega^{*}\right).

It follows that
\mu_{1}\left(B_{R}\right)=\mu_{1}\left(\Omega^{*}\right)
. Thus one has that
B_{R}=\Omega^{*}
which is a contradiction.
Hence,
B_{R}\subseteq\Omega^{*}
or
\left|B_{R}\right|\leq\left|\Omega^{*}\right|=|\Omega|
.
Furthermore, if

\left|B_{R}\right|=|\Omega|
, then

B_{R}=\Omega^{*}
, and thus

\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\mu_{1}\left(B_{R}\right)=\mu_{1}\left(\Omega^{*}\right),

which implies
\Omega=\Omega^{*}=B_{R}
.∎

Domain monotonicity generally fails for Robin eigenvalues. Nevertheless, it holds in certain special configurations. A notable example is the inequality

\mu_{1}(\Omega,\sigma)\geq\mu_{1}\left(\widetilde{B},\sigma\right),

where
\widetilde{B}
is a ball circumscribing

\Omega
. This result was established for dimensions two and three in
[
40
]
, and the argument readily extends to higher dimensions. Alternative proofs can be found in
[

,
35
]
.
As noted by Bucur, Freitas, and Kennedy
[

]
, this monotonicity property can also be derived from the Bossel-Daners inequality
[
9
, Theorem 4.23]
. Specifically, using the scaling inequality

\mu_{1}(t\Omega)\leq\mu_{1}(\Omega)
for all

and

\sigma>0

[
9
, Inequality 4.10]
, one obtains

\mu_{1}(\Omega)\geq\mu_{1}\left(\Omega^{*}\right)\geq\mu_{1}\left(\widetilde{B}\right).

By Lemma 3.2,
\left|B_{R}\right|=|\Omega|
if and only if

. In this case, the conclusion of Theorem 1.1 holds trivially (by letting

) for all

.
We may therefore assume throughout the remainder of the paper that

\left|B_{R}\right|<|\Omega|
. Moreover, in the remaining part of this section, we always assume that

.

Let

denote the decreasing rearrangement of

for

. Since

, we have

, in contrast to the Dirichlet case where the eigenfunction vanishes on the boundary.

We normalize the eigenfunction

\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{2}\,\text{d}x=\int_{B_{R}}z^{2}\,\text{d}x.

(3.5)

As noted in the introduction, the positivity

Lemma 3.3.

Assume
\widetilde{R}\geq R
. For all
\sigma>0
, if

, then

for all

Proof.
Clearly,

z^{*}(s)
is continuous on

\left[0,\left|B_{R}\right|\right]

u_{1}^{*}(s)
. The assumption

z^{*}(0)<u_{1}^{*}(0)
implies that

z^{*}(s)<u_{1}^{*}(s)
in a neighborhood of

. Suppose, for contradiction, that the conclusion fails. Then the functions must intersect at least once in

denote the first such intersection point (see Figure 3).

The schematic diagrams of

Define a function by

Note that

cannot be an eigenfunction corresponding to

\mu_{1}<\frac{\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\frac{\sigma}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}S}{\int_{B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}x},

where

The function

is precisely the decreasing rearrangement of

. By their equimeasurability, we have

\int_{B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}x=\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s,

(3.7)

which can also be derived via a change of variables as in
[
7

[

\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x=N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}s^{2-2/N}v^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,\text{d}s.

In light of equation (
3.4
) and the definition of

v
, for

, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle-(z^{*})^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\left(\int_{0}^{s_{1}}z^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t+\int_{s_{1}}^{s}z^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\left(\int_{0}^{s_{1}}u_{1}^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t+\int_{s_{1}}^{s}z^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\int_{0}^{s}v(t)\,\text{d}t.

That is to say,

-v^{\prime}(s)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\int_{0}^{s}v(t)\,\text{d}t

(3.9)

for

.

Since
\widetilde{R}\geq R
, we derive that

\left|B_{R}\right|\leq\mu\left(u_{1,pM}\right)

for all
\sigma>0
.
By Lemma 3.1 and the definition of

-v^{\prime}(s)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\int_{0}^{s}v(t)\,\text{d}t

Substituting the preceding inequality into (

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x\leq

\displaystyle-\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{\prime}(s)\int_{0}^{s}v(t)\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu_{1}v\left(\left|B_{R}\right|\right)\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v(s)\,\text{d}s+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s.

Substituting

Since

-\left(z^{*}\right)^{\prime}\left(\left|B_{R}\right|\right)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}\left|B_{R}\right|^{2/N-2}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v(s)\,\text{d}s,

which can also be derived from (

Rearranging this inequality yields

-\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v(s)\,\text{d}s\leq\mu_{1}^{-1}N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\left|B_{R}\right|^{2-2/N}\left(z^{*}\right)^{\prime}\left(\left|B_{R}\right|\right).

So we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x\leq

\displaystyle N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\left|B_{R}\right|^{2-2/N}\left(z^{*}\right)^{\prime}\left(\left|B_{R}\right|\right)z^{*}\left(\left|B_{R}\right|\right)+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s,

where we have used the fact of

.

Using the relation

and

and

Substituting into the previous inequality yields

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x\leq

\displaystyle N^{2}C_{N}^{2/N}\left|B_{R}\right|^{2-2/N}N^{-1}C_{N}^{-1}R^{1-N}z^{\prime}(R)z(R)+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle NC_{N}R^{N-1}z^{\prime}(R)z(R)+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s.

Applying the Robin boundary condition

, we find

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma}{R}NC_{N}R^{N-1}z^{2}(R)+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}}z^{2}\,\text{d}S+\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}v^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s.

According to the relations

Combining this with (

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}S+\mu_{1}\int_{B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}x.

Rearranging terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\text{d}x+\frac{\sigma}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}S}{\int_{B_{R}}g^{2}\,\text{d}x}\leq\mu_{1},

which contradicts (

By the equimeasurability property (
3.1

When

\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}\left(z^{*}\right)^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s=\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}\left(u_{1}^{*}\right)^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s<\int_{0}^{\left|\Omega\right|}\left(u_{1}^{*}\right)^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s=\int_{0}^{\left|B_{R}\right|}\left(z^{*}\right)^{2}(s)\,\text{d}s,

which is a contradiction.
Hence, we must have that
z(r)\geq u_{1}^{\star}(r)
and
z(r)\not\equiv u_{1}^{\star}(r)
in
\left[0,R\right]
.
In this case, one has that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}f(r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}f(r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle NC_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{R}f(r)\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r-f(R)\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right).

Since
f
is strictly increasing, we find that

\int_{0}^{R}(f(R)-f(r))\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r>0.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}f(r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}f(r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle<\,

\displaystyle NC_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{R}f(R)\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r-\int_{R}^{R_{*}}f(R)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle NC_{N}f(R)\left(\int_{0}^{R}\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r-\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(R)\left(\int_{B_{R}}z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

If there exists a point
r_{1}\in\left(0,R\right)
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z(r)\geq u_{1}^{\star}(r)&\text{for}\,\,\,\left[0,r_{1}\right],\\
z(r)\leq u_{1}^{\star}(r)&\text{for}\,\,\,\left[r_{1},R\right],\end{array}\right.

then we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}f(r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}f(r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle NC_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{r_{1}}f\left(r_{1}\right)\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r+f\left(r_{1}\right)\int_{r_{1}}^{R}\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right.

\displaystyle\left.-f\left(R\right)\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right).

Since
f
is strictly increasing,
r_{1}<R<R_{*}
and
u_{1}^{\star}>0
, it follows that

f\left(R\right)\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r>f\left(r_{1}\right)\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r.

Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}f(r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}f(r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle<\,

\displaystyle NC_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{r_{1}}f\left(r_{1}\right)\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r+f\left(r_{1}\right)\int_{r_{1}}^{R}\left(z^{2}-\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\right)r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right.

\displaystyle\left.-f\left(r_{1}\right)\int_{R}^{R_{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}r^{N-1}\,\text{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(r_{1}\right)\left(\int_{B_{R}}z^{2}\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega^{*}}\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

We therefore conclude that the desired inequality (
4.8
) holds. The reversed inequality for the case when
f
is strictly decreasing follows by an analogous argument.

By Proposition 2.2,
w(t)
is strictly increasing and
B(t)
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1]
.
Leveraging inequality (
4.8
) and the reversed form for decreasing functions, we find that

\int_{\Omega^{*}}B(\gamma r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x<\int_{B_{R}}B(\gamma r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x

and

\int_{\Omega^{*}}w^{2}(\gamma r)\left(u_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}\,\text{d}x>\int_{B_{R}}w^{2}(\gamma r)z^{2}\,\text{d}x.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}<\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}=\frac{k_{\nu+1}^{2}}{k_{\nu}^{2}}.

In conclusion, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\leq\frac{k_{\nu+1}^{2}}{k_{\nu}^{2}},

with equality if and only if
\Omega=B_{R}
.∎

Proof of Theorem 1.2

A standard scaling argument shows that

\alpha^{2}/\widetilde{R}^{2}
is the first eigenvalue of the corresponding problem on the ball

B_{\widetilde{R}}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta z=\mu z&\text{in}\,\,\,B_{\widetilde{R}},\\
\partial_{\texttt{n}}z+\frac{\sigma}{\widetilde{R}}z=0&\text{on}\,\,\,\partial B_{\widetilde{R}}\end{array}\right.

with the corresponding eigenfunction

z(r)=cr^{-\nu}J_{\nu}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\widetilde{R}}r\right),

where
c
is a positive constant. Since
\alpha=k_{\nu,1}
, we derive that
z\left(\widetilde{R}\right)>0
.

Similar to Lemma 3.2, we have the following relation between the measures of
B_{\widetilde{R}}
and
\Omega
.

Lemma 5.2.

Assume that
\widetilde{R}\leq R
. It holds that

\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|\leq|\Omega|
, with equality if and only if

\Omega=B_{\widetilde{R}}
.

Proof.
The condition
\widetilde{R}\leq R
, combined with Lemma 3.2, shows that

\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|\leq\left|B_{{R}}\right|\leq|\Omega|.

When
\Omega
is a ball, we find that
\widetilde{R}=R_{*}=R
, and thus equality holds in the inequality above.
On the other hand, if
\Omega
is not a ball, Lemma 3.2 implies that
\left|B_{{R}}\right|<|\Omega|
,
from which it follows that
\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|<|\Omega|
.
Therefore, we conclude that
\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|\leq|\Omega|
, with equality if and only if

\Omega=B_{\widetilde{R}}
.∎

By Lemma 5.2 and Lemma 3.2, if
\Omega
is a ball, one sees that
\widetilde{R}=R_{*}=R
.
In this case, the conclusion of Theorem 1.2 holds trivially (by letting
R_{*}=R
) for all

\sigma>0
.
We may therefore assume throughout the remainder of this section that

\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|<|\Omega|
and
\widetilde{R}\leq R
.

Proof of Theorem 1.2.
For any
s_{*}\in\left(0,\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|\right)
, since
\left|B_{\widetilde{R}}\right|=\mu\left(u_{1,pM}\right)
, in view of Lemma 3.1, we obtain that

-\left(u_{1}^{*}\right)^{\prime}(s)\leq\mu_{1}N^{-2}C_{N}^{-2/N}s^{2/N-2}\int_{0}^{s}u_{1}^{*}(t)\,\text{d}t

for almost every
s\in\left[0,s_{*}\right]
.
Then, repeating the arguments of Lemmas 3.3–3.4, Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 1.1 by replacing

with

, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\widetilde{R}^{2}}\left(\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right),

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}<\frac{k_{\nu+1,1}^{2}-k_{\nu,1}^{2}}{\widetilde{R}^{2}\mu_{1}}+1,

which is the desired inequality.∎

Proof of Theorem 1.3

Combining equations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), we derive an expression for the derivative of
\beta/\alpha
. Applying Lemma 2.1 to this result then yields the desired conclusion.

Proof of Theorem 1.3.
Define

F(\sigma,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}-\sigma

and

G(\sigma,\beta)=\frac{\beta J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}-\sigma-1.

By (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), we see that

F(\sigma,\alpha)=0
and
G(\sigma,\beta)=0
.
It is easily verified that both

F
and
G
are differentiable with continuous partial derivatives, and that
F_{\sigma}(\sigma,\alpha)=-1
and
G_{\sigma}(\sigma,\beta)=-1
.
Therefore, by the implicit function theorem,

\alpha
and
\beta
are continuously differentiable with respect to
\sigma
. Moreover, differentiating the identities
F(\sigma,\alpha)=0
and
G(\sigma,\beta)=0
yields

\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma)=-\frac{F_{\sigma}}{F_{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}+\alpha\frac{J_{\nu+1}^{\prime}(\alpha)J_{\nu}(\alpha)-J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)J_{\nu}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(\alpha)}}

and

\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)=-\frac{G_{\sigma}}{G_{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}+\beta\frac{J_{\nu+2}^{\prime}(\beta)J_{\nu+1}(\beta)-J_{\nu+2}(\beta)J_{\nu+1}^{\prime}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\beta)}}.

Using (
2.5
) and the identity

J_{\nu+1}^{\prime}(z)=J_{\nu}(z)-\frac{\nu+1}{z}J_{\nu+1}(z),

we further obtain that

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}+\alpha\frac{\left(J_{\nu}(\alpha)-\frac{\nu+1}{\alpha}J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)\right)J_{\nu}(\alpha)-J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)\left(-J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)+\frac{\nu}{\alpha}J_{\nu}(\alpha)\right)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(\alpha)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}-(2\nu+1)\frac{J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}+\alpha\frac{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(\alpha)}+\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\frac{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(\alpha)}-2\nu\frac{J_{\nu+1}(\alpha)}{J_{\nu}(\alpha)}+\alpha}

and

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}+\beta\frac{J_{\nu+2}^{\prime}(\beta)J_{\nu+1}(\beta)-J_{\nu+2}(\beta)J_{\nu+1}^{\prime}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\beta)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}+\beta\frac{\left(J_{\nu+1}(\beta)-\frac{\nu+2}{\beta}J_{\nu+2}(\beta)\right)J_{\nu+1}(\beta)-J_{\nu+2}(\beta)\left(-J_{\nu+2}(\beta)+\frac{\nu+1}{\beta}J_{\nu+1}(\beta)\right)}{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\beta)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}-(2\nu+3)\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}+\beta\frac{J_{\nu+2}^{2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\beta)}+\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta\frac{J_{\nu+2}^{2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}^{2}(\beta)}-2(\nu+1)\frac{J_{\nu+2}(\beta)}{J_{\nu+1}(\beta)}+\beta}.

Using (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) again we obtain that

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\alpha}-2\nu\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}+\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^{2}-2\nu\sigma+\alpha^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\frac{(\sigma+1)^{2}}{\beta}-2(\nu+1)\frac{\sigma+1}{\beta}+\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{(\sigma+1)^{2}-2(\nu+1)(\sigma+1)+\beta^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}-2\nu\sigma-2\nu-1+\beta^{2}}.

From
[
17
]
or
[
22
]
, it is known that both
\alpha
and
\beta
are strictly increasing in
\sigma
.
Using the identity
\alpha^{2}\left(\beta/\alpha\right)^{\prime}(\sigma)=\beta^{\prime}\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\beta
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}\left(\beta/\alpha\right)^{\prime}(\sigma)}{\alpha^{\prime}(\sigma)\beta^{\prime}(\sigma)}=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}-\frac{\beta}{\beta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}-2\nu\sigma+\alpha^{2}-\left(\sigma^{2}-2\nu\sigma-2\nu-1+\beta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nu+1+\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}<0

via Lemma 2.1.
Hence, we conclude that
\beta/\alpha
is strictly decreasing in
\sigma
.

As in
[
17
]
, define the function

h(k)=\frac{kJ_{\nu+2}(k)}{J_{\nu+1}(k)}-1.

From
[
17
]
, it is known that
h
is strictly increasing on
\left[0,j_{\nu+1,1}\right)
with
h(0)=-1
.
Therefore, there exists a unique zero
k_{*}\in\left(0,j_{\nu+1,1}\right)
.
Following the argument in
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
, we obtain that

\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0}\alpha=0\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0}\beta=k_{*},

which implies

\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow 0}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=+\infty.

Moreover, since

\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=\frac{j_{\nu+1,1}}{j_{\nu,1}},

we conclude that
\beta/\alpha
is strictly decreasing from
+\infty
to
j_{\nu+1,1}/j_{\nu,1}
as
\sigma
increases from
0
to
+\infty
. This establishes the desired result. ∎
