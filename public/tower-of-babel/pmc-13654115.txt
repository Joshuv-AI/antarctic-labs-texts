Delayed treatment of acute inferior and right ventricular myocardial infarction resulting in interventricular septal dissection with multiple perforations after percutaneous coronary intervention: a case description
Authors: Tian-Tian Feng, Bo Shang, Tian-Gang Li, Hai-Bo Cao
Published: 2026

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction is associated with a 0.3% incidence of ventricular septal perforation, predominantly affecting the anterior septum and anterior wall, and less frequently affecting the inferior wall. Ventricular septal dissection is a rare subacute mechanical complication of acute myocardial infarction, with an indeterminate incidence (1). Interventricular septal (IVS) dissection typically occurs 1 to 17 days after acute myocardial infarction, with most cases occurring between days 1 and 7 (1,2). Severe myocardial infarction, delayed hospital admission, and insufficient tissue-level reperfusion resulting from either lack of reperfusion or inadequate coronary blood flow after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) are critical risk factors for mechanical problems (3).

PCI has emerged as the standard treatment for patients with myocardial infarction and is the preferred method for revascularizing ischemic myocardial tissue. Reestablishing coronary blood flow, or reperfusion, is essential; however, paradoxically, it may also lead to increased irreversible myocardial injury and cellular necrosis (4). Due to the very limited regenerative capacity of the heart, injured myocardial cells cannot be effectively replaced. Patients may thereafter experience heart failure, arrhythmias, diminished cardiac function, or sudden cardiac death (5). Some patients with myocardial infarction may initially present only with referred upper abdominal pain, without the typical clinical presentation of retrosternal chest pain. In gastroenterology, this symptom may be mistaken for acute gastrointestinal disorders, potentially leading to misdiagnoses and delayed detection and treatment of coronary artery disease. This article describes the case of a patient with myocardial infarction who initially only experienced upper abdominal pain before seeking medical attention. During coronary balloon dilation and revascularization, the patient developed multiple perforations and a fresh ventricular septal tear.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old male presented to our hospital’s gastrointestinal department with severe upper abdominal pain that had persisted for 5 days without an identifiable cause. The pain was accompanied by nausea and vomiting but was not associated with back pain. He denied experiencing palpitations, chest tightness, or chest discomfort. He had no history of coronary heart disease, diabetes, or hypertension. Self-administered oral medications, including omeprazole and aluminum magnesium carbonate suspension, alleviated the patient’s gastric discomfort.

The cause of the upper abdominal pain needed to be determined upon admission. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed to exclude biliary inflammation and common bile duct stones. Acute pancreatitis, duodenal and stomach ulcers, and other digestive system issues were excluded based on laboratory testing and abdominal ultrasonography. The patient’s acid-suppressive medication provided only short-term pain relief. At the same time, mild abnormalities in renal function and liver enzymes were noted. In the absence of typical symptoms of digestive system disorders, internal organ referred pain after acute myocardial infarction should be closely monitored.

Biochemical analysis on the second day after hospitalization revealed marginally elevated levels of creatinine, urea nitrogen, and transaminases. Abdominal ultrasonography indicated mild hepatic vein dilation, but MRCP showed no significant common bile duct calculi. The patient’s autonomic nerve symptoms and lack of response to acid-suppressive medication raised a significant clinical suspicion of a heart condition. As a result, symptomatic therapy and fluid replacement were implemented to improve circulation.

Echocardiography conducted on the third day revealed enlargement of both atria, although the left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction remained normal at 63%. The laboratory results were as follows: N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), 8,981 pg/mL; serum myoglobin, 58 ng/mL; and serum troponin, 28.419 ng/mL. The electrocardiogram (ECG) results indicated an acute inferior myocardial infarction (Figure 1A). The patient reported fatigue, chest tightness, and dyspnea. An urgent cardiology consultation was obtained, and the patient was transferred for further treatment. Treatment included oral aspirin (100 mg), clopidogrel bisulfate (300 mg), and subcutaneous enoxaparin sodium (2,000 units) for anticoagulation. Given that the patient exceeded the time limit for emergency surgery and exhibited improvement in his clinical symptoms, the cardiology team opted for conservative medical management and PCI.

No ventricular septal dissection, endocardial discontinuity, or ventricular-level shunting was found during a thorough preoperative screening for cardiac mechanical complications; only a slight suspected weakening of motion in the lower wall of the left and right ventricles was noted, and there were no objective ultrasound signs of perforation or dissection. Nevertheless, the patient had experienced persistent myocardial ischemia for 7 days, and the covert development of ventricular septal injury before the procedure could not be ruled out.

Subsequently, coronary angiography was performed. After local anesthesia, the patient underwent puncture via the right radial artery. Coronary angiography showed that the right coronary artery (RCA) was dominant, with severe stenosis in the second segment of the RCA and occlusion of the middle segment (Figure 1B, Video 1). Distal Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) blood flow was grade 0. The left main coronary artery showed no obvious stenosis, while plaque formation was detected in the left anterior descending coronary artery, with distal TIMI blood flow of grade 3.

Coronary angiography demonstrated right coronary dominance with mid-segment blockage of the right coronary artery.

After anticoagulation with heparin, a guidewire was used to open the occluded vessel. Following balloon dilation, angiography was re-performed. The blood flow at the distal end of the RCA improved to TIMI grade 2–3 (Figure 1C, Video 2), and a thrombus shadow was observed in the third segment of the RCA. Given the patient’s advanced age, impaired cardiac function, and substantial thrombus burden, stent implantation was deferred. The catheter was left in place, and the patient was transferred back to the ward.

Balloon angioplasty was performed to successfully recanalize the right coronary artery lesion.

Blood pressure and blood oxygen levels were continuously monitored after the operation, and biochemical tests were dynamically re-performed. Six hours postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms of chest tightness and shortness of breath had deteriorated. A full systolic murmur was auscultated at the left third to fourth intercostal spaces of the sternum. The patient’s blood pressure decreased from 118/70 to 80/48 mmHg, and his blood oxygen level declined from 96% to 84%. During the re-examination, the NT-proBNP level increased from 10,800 to 28,133 pg/mL.

Urgent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed the following findings: the LV short-axis view showed an oblique IVS dissection measuring approximately 48 × 14 mm at the base of the septum. The dissection communicated between the left and right ventricles via an 18-mm defect on the LV endocardial surface of the posterior septum and a 19-mm defect on the right ventricular (RV) side (Figure 1D); color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) demonstrated left-to-right shunt flow signals between the IVS dissection and both ventricles (Figure 1E); the parasternal five-chamber view revealed a small left-to-right shunt and blue blood flow within the subendocardial region of the RV apical wall adjacent to the IVS dissection (Figure 1F); tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) in the apical four-chamber view indicated significantly reduced RV systolic function (Figure 1G). Footage from Videos 3,4 further demonstrated segmental wall motion abnormalities of the inferior LV wall and RV wall, as well as multiple perforations within the IVS dissection.

Two-dimensional ultrasound showed segmental wall motion abnormalities in the inferior wall of the left ventricle and right ventricular wall.

Color Doppler showed multiple perforations within the interventricular septal dissection.

IVS dissection complicated by perforations (Figure 2A) resulted in left-to-right shunting, increasing the RV load and rapidly leading to pulmonary congestion, as evidenced by numerous B-lines on lung ultrasonography (Figure 2B). Additionally, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, due to segmental wall motion abnormalities and the effect of dissection, further worsened pulmonary congestion in the patient (Figure 2C,2D). The patient’s hepatic and renal function continued to deteriorate, and his blood pressure declined. An urgent cardiac surgery consultation was obtained, and emergency surgery to repair the IVS was initiated. Unfortunately, the patient suffered cardiac arrest en route to the operating room and could not be resuscitated despite extensive cardiopulmonary resuscitation efforts.

Conclusions

This case involved an acute inferior wall myocardial infarction combined with RV wall myocardial infarction that was treated after a delay and initially manifested as referred epigastric pain. During the clinical course, a rare ventricular septal dissection accompanied by multiple perforations developed. In the inferior wall infarction, contractility of the basal segment of the IVS, supplied by branches of the RCA, was markedly reduced, whereas motion in the midseptal and apical regions, supplied by the left coronary artery, remained predominantly intact. At this stage, shear stress at the interface between the contractile and noncontractile septal segments likely contributed to the development of basal septal dissection (6). Prolonged and intense ischemia in individuals with myocardial infarction undermines capillary integrity. Subsequent reperfusion may induce capillary rupture and myocardial bleeding, both of which are closely associated with the emergence of mechanical problems (7). Moreover, myocardial injury induced by ischemia-reperfusion can persist and progress over several hours to days following vascular reperfusion (8).

This patient was admitted 5 days after symptom onset, well beyond the 24-hour therapeutic window for emergency reperfusion in acute myocardial infarction. Consequently, the myocardium subtended by the occluded coronary artery exhibited extensive transmural necrosis, with severe disruption of myocardial architecture (9). At this stage, performing PCI to restore coronary patency carries the dual risks of superimposed ischemia-reperfusion injury and microvascular no-reflow. Moreover, it may exacerbate preexisting mechanical instability, specifically the propagation of myocardial rupture or ventricular septal perforation within the zone of established transmural necrosis. However, a direct causal relationship between PCI-mediated revascularization and ventricular septal perforation has not been established; the observed association remains temporal rather than mechanistic. Extensive transmural myocardial necrosis constitutes the essential pathological substrate for such complications, whereas ischemia-reperfusion injury represents a potential contributing factor rather than a primary driver.

Notably, this patient presented with persistent upper abdominal pain as the chief complaint. During the initial gastroenterology evaluation, the differential diagnosis included common abdominal pathologies, such as gastric ulcer, acute pancreatitis, choledocholithiasis, and intestinal spasm. Pain attributable to these gastrointestinal disorders is typically meal-related or position-dependent and is frequently associated with acid reflux, fever, or elevated serum amylase levels. In contrast, visceral referred pain from myocardial ischemia is often poorly localized and may radiate to extracardiac sites such as the epigastrium, jaw, shoulder, or back; it is also commonly accompanied by autonomic manifestations, including nausea, vomiting, and profound fatigue (10). Antacid therapy provides only transient symptomatic relief without addressing the underlying ischemic etiology, representing a critical diagnostic differentiator in this case and highlighting the clinical challenge posed by visceral referred pain in acute inferior wall and RV myocardial infarction. Cardiac visceral pain is transmitted via autonomic afferent pathways, resulting in poorly localized discomfort that may radiate to extracardiac regions, including the epigastrium, neck, and jaw. It is frequently accompanied by autonomic hyperactivity manifestations such as nausea, vomiting, and profound fatigue, which substantially overlap with symptoms of acute abdominal emergencies encountered in gastroenterology practice (11). In the clinical management of patients presenting with upper abdominal pain, diagnostic evaluation should extend beyond gastrointestinal disorders. Routine ECG and measurement of cardiac biomarkers are essential to exclude occult myocardial infarction, particularly inferior or posterior wall involvement, thereby preventing misdiagnosis and delays in timely coronary revascularization attributable to visceral referred pain.

This case underscores the pivotal role of TTE in the serial assessment of acute myocardial infarction. Preprocedural TTE reliably excludes preexisting ventricular septal defects, while postprocedural dynamic monitoring enables early detection of new-onset ventricular septal rupture or perforation, rapid quantification of left-to-right shunt severity, and timely evaluation of RV function, thereby providing objective imaging evidence to guide surgical intervention. Routine TTE surveillance before and after PCI mitigates the risk of overlooking life-threatening mechanical complications and supports clinical decision-making in the management of acute myocardial infarction. We anticipate that this case report will serve as a useful reference for frontline clinicians, informing both diagnostic awareness and procedural timing.
