Trends in ethnic disparities in reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health interventions and the role of social investment and inclusive policies: a mixed-methods study in Ecuador, 2004–2018
Authors: Paulina Rios-Quituizaca, Jesus Endara-Mina, Leonardo Zanini Ferreira, Nancy Armenta-Paulino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEthnic disparities in reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health (RMNCH) persist in Latin America, rooted in structural racism and colonial legacies. Evidence on the temporal evolution of these disparities and the impact of policies targeting Indigenous populations remains limited. Following the 2000 economic crisis, Ecuador showed the region’s largest ethnic gaps in intervention coverage and social determinants. Since 2008, inclusion policies have advanced. This study analysed trends in RMNCH coverage, social determinants and their potential association with policies and strategies over 14 years.MethodsUsing a mixed-methods design, we analysed three nationally representative surveys (2004, 2012 and 2018) to assess changes in social determinants and the coverage of six RMNCH services; defined as the proportion of women and children receiving essential health services across the continuum of care, including family planning, antenatal care, skilled birth attendance and child immunisation, stratified by ethnicity (Indigenous women and children, Afro-Ecuadorian populations and Mestizo and White populations). We estimated absolute inequality measures and adjusted coverage ratios using Poisson regression models. Through a literature review and temporal graphs, we analysed plans, policies and strategies in health, education and ethnic inclusion during the same period to estimate potential impact.ResultsBy 2018, Indigenous populations doubled their representation in the highest wealth quintiles (10% to 20%) and increased secondary education attainment (25% to 45%), with slower progress in rural areas. RMNCH coverage, including prenatal care, institutional deliveries and professional-assisted births, rose significantly (27% to 75%) among Indigenous populations. Afro-Ecuadorians also experienced improvements in RMNCH coverage and social determinants, though progress was less pronounced compared with Indigenous groups. Although ethnic gaps persisted, inequalities declined over the study period. These reductions coincided with increased social investment in rural health and education, constitutional recognition of plurinationality, and policies promoting intercultural health practices. However, gaps in monitoring and impact evaluation were evident.ConclusionsEcuador demonstrates that inclusive and integrated policies, leadership, social participation and sustained social investment can reduce ethnic inequalities, promote the integral development of society and strategies that should be maintained. Temporal studies based on routine surveys are crucial for monitoring the impact of such policies. These findings provide a pre-pandemic benchmark and serve as a reference for countries aiming to improve health outcomes among Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations and advance the Sustainable Development Goals.Keywords: SOCIAL MEDICINE, Health policy, Maternal medicine, Health Equity, Health Services Accessibility

Background

In Latin America, ethnic disparities rooted in the colonial legacy have been persistent, as evidenced by unequal coverage in health interventions. Despite ethnic affiliation being one of the primary social determinants of health, there is limited available information analysing ethnic gaps in maternal and child health.1,3

To enhance the analytical capacity of countries making progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals, national legislation should be implemented that aligns with fundamental principles.4 In-depth analysis of countries is also recommended to understand the factors driving success in reducing ethnic disparities.5 In the Latin American context, few countries have managed to identify both an increase in coverage and a reduction in inequality gaps over time.6 Several studies have shown that the reduction of inequalities is associated with increased investment in education, more equitable social spending and the implementation of social policies that support vulnerable population subgroups.7,9 Reproductive, Maternal, Neonatal, Child and Adolescent Health (RMNCH) encompasses essential interventions across the continuum of maternal, newborn and child care, as defined by WHO, to improve health outcomes and reduce preventable mortality.10 In the context of this study, RMNCH intervention coverage denotes access to and use of essential health services provided across the continuum of reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child care, including family planning, antenatal care, skilled birth attendance and child immunisation.

Our previous analysis revealed that Ecuador made substantial progress in RMNCH intervention coverage and reduced ethnic inequalities between 2004 and 2012, with trends stratified by education, residence and wealth.11 Nevertheless, coverage among Indigenous populations remained comparatively lower by 2012. The present study expands this evidence by extending the analysis to 2018, offering a pre-pandemic benchmark, and by incorporating a qualitative review of national policies and strategies to better understand their potential influence on the evolution of ethnic inequalities. In 2008, Ecuador also reinforced the guarantee of Indigenous peoples’ rights with the promulgation of the Constitution.12

Expanding the temporal scope of analysis and linking coverage trends to social determinants and policies provides a clearer understanding of the laws, policies, or programmes that may have influenced the observed temporal changes.9 13 With just over 5 years left to assess the outcomes of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and in the aftermath of the global COVID-19 pandemic, the 2018 National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT) survey offers a unique opportunity to evaluate progress in reducing inequalities and to understand the situation before the pandemic.

The overall aim of this study was to examine temporal trends in ethnic inequalities in RMNCH intervention coverage in Ecuador between 2004 and 2018 and to contextualise these trends within the national policy environment.

Specifically, the study pursued three objectives: (1) to describe changes in social determinants and coverage of six RMNCH interventions by ethnicity using nationally representative survey data; (2) to quantify absolute and relative ethnic inequalities in intervention coverage using regression-based methods adjusted for key social determinants and (3) to identify and analyse national laws, policies and strategies related to health, education and ethnic inclusion through a policy desk review, in order to contextualise observed trends over time.

Methods

We conducted a mixed-methods case study that analyses data from three nationally representative health surveys conducted between 2004 and 2018, along with a literature review of the main equality policies and strategies targeting the ethnic population during the study period.

Study design, data sources and participants

This cross-sectional study uses the Reproductive Health Survey (RHS) 2004,14 and the ENSANUT 2012 and 2018.15 16 Each survey employed a multistage, stratified cluster sampling design to obtain nationally representative data. Census enumeration areas were selected probabilistically, followed by systematic selection of households within each area.

The datasets analysed in this study are publicly available: RHS 2004 through the World Bank Microdata Library and ENSANUT 2012 and 2018 through the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses of Ecuador. RHS and ENSANUT were selected because they are the only nationally representative surveys that consistently include information on both ethnicity and RMNCH indicators, ensuring comparability across time.

According to the official survey methodology, within each selected household, one eligible woman of reproductive age (15–49 years) and one child under 5 years of age were randomly selected for interview. Data were collected through standardised, interviewer-administered questionnaires covering sociodemographic characteristics and health indicators. For the present analysis, we included women aged 15–49 years and children aged 12–23 months with available data on ethnicity and at least one RMNCH outcome; observations with missing outcome or key covariate data were excluded from the corresponding analyses.

Ethnicity

Ethnic inequalities were assessed using self-identification, a method recognised in Latin America as the most appropriate for estimating Indigenous and Afro-descendent populations. In Ecuador, this criterion has been applied since the 2001 census and was consistently used in the RHS and ENSANUT surveys, ensuring comparability with census data. The proportion of women of reproductive age (WRA) identifying as Indigenous remained stable across surveys: 7% in RHS 2004, 6.2% in ENSANUT 2012 and 7.7% in ENSANUT 2018.

For analysis, ethnicity was classified into three categories: Indigenous, Afro-descendent and a combined Mestizo and White category. The ‘Montubio’ group was excluded because it was not an option in the 2004 survey. Combining Mestizo and White populations was justified by statistical and sociological reasons, as these groups are generally more advantaged due to colonial and postcolonial histories, consistent with regional findings.1 In regression models, the Mestizo and White populations were used as the comparison category. This three-category classification also harmonised the analysis across surveys and reduced misclassification bias.

Coverage indicators

We assessed six RMNCH coverage indicators across the continuum of care: modern contraceptive use, antenatal care (≥4 skilled visits), skilled birth attendance, institutional delivery, early initiation of breastfeeding (within 1 hour) and full immunisation (survey-specific child age window). We applied harmonised, standardised indicator definitions to maximise comparability across all surveys. For each indicator, we operationalised coverage as a binary variable using the corresponding women’s or child-health questionnaire modules (contraception, pregnancy/antenatal care, delivery care, breastfeeding and child vaccination), and then estimated weighted percentages as the number receiving the intervention (numerator) divided by the eligible population (denominator), following standardised definitions to maximise comparability across surveys. The full numerator/denominator specifications are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Covariables

We included key social determinants as covariates. Relative socioeconomic position was measured using wealth quintiles, a pragmatic approach that splits the population into five equal groups to assess how equitably RMNCH coverage is distributed by wealth status within and across ethnic groups; quintiles were derived from the asset-based wealth index precalculated in each national survey using a standard principal components analysis (PCA) methodology based on household assets, utilities (eg, electricity, water, sanitation) and dwelling materials, and categorised from Q1 (poorest 20%) to Q5 (wealthiest 20%).17 Maternal education was harmonised using international criteria18 19 and grouped as none, primary and secondary+to maximise cross-survey comparability. Place of residence followed standard national definitions: urban (≥2000 inhabitants) versus rural (<2000).20

Statistical analysis

We estimated national coverage for each RMNCH indicator by ethnicity and further stratified estimates by place of residence (urban/rural), maternal education and household wealth quintiles. Absolute ethnic inequality was quantified using the mean difference from the best-performing subgroup (values >0 indicate greater inequality; 0 indicates no inequality). For relative ethnic inequality, we calculated adjusted coverage ratios (prevalence ratios (PRs)) using Poisson regression with robust variance for binary outcomes, a recommended alternative to logistic regression for estimating PRs in cross-sectional studies.21 22 Covariates (wealth quintiles, maternal education and urban/rural residence) were selected a priori and included to minimise potential confounding in the ethnicity–coverage association. We fitted separate models comparing Afro-descendant and Indigenous populations with the Mestizo and White populations.

We analysed each survey wave independently (2004, 2012 and 2018) to generate nationally representative, wave-specific estimates and inequality measures using the corresponding design variables and sampling weights using Stata survey commands (‘svy’); results were compared over time as repeated cross-sectional snapshots rather than pooled data. We followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (online supplemental table 1).

All descriptive estimates and regression models accounted for the complex survey design using the survey weights and design variables provided by each survey (primary sampling units and strata) via Stata survey commands (‘svy’). We used a complete-case approach, excluding observations with missing outcome or covariate data from the relevant analysis; denominators, therefore, vary slightly across indicators and models.

Desk review

A desk review was chosen as the most appropriate design given the descriptive aim of mapping policies and strategies and the predominance of heterogeneous policy and grey literature sources, rather than evaluating intervention effectiveness.

The objective of this desk review was to identify and analyse strategies, laws and political transformations impacting the health of Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian populations in Ecuador, with particular emphasis on policies and programmes related to RMNCH. The review question was framed using a structured analytical logic adapted for policy analysis, specifying the population of interest (Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations in Ecuador), the policy domain (national laws, political frameworks and health strategies) and the outcomes of interest (factors enabling or constraining equity in RMNCH), rather than following a formal systematic review framework.

The review was conducted between October and November 2024 and included publications and official documents dated from January 2000 to December 2024. The year 2000 was selected as the starting point because the first nationally representative reproductive health data became available with the ENDEMAIN 2004 survey.

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented using two categories of sources: bibliographic databases and grey literature. The bibliographic databases included MEDLINE/PubMed and BIREME/LILACS. Grey literature sources included Google Scholar, Ecuadorian government institutions (such as the Ministry of Public Health, the National Assembly and the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses), and international health organisations (including the Pan American Health Organization and the United Nations Population Fund).

For the initial search focused on laws, plans, projects and policies related to Ecuador’s peoples and nationalities, the terms ‘laws’, ‘plans’, ‘projects’ and ‘policies’ were combined with ‘peoples and nationalities’, ‘plurinational’ and ‘Ecuador.’ Although these policies predominantly address Indigenous populations, the review also explicitly sought documents relevant to Afro-descendant populations, as several policies indirectly targeted both groups. Exclusion criteria included: (1) documents not situated within the Ecuadorian context and (2) documents lacking relevance to health equity or intercultural approaches.

In the second search, focused on reproductive and maternal–child health, the terms ‘laws’, ‘plans’, ‘projects’ and ‘policies’ were combined with ‘reproductive health’, ‘maternal–child health’ and ‘Ecuador.’ Exclusion criteria included: (1) documents not situated within the Ecuadorian context and (2) documents that did not address RMNCH-related policies or programmes.

Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two reviewers (PR-Q and JE-M) using predefined eligibility criteria. A full-text review was subsequently conducted to confirm eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, through consultation with a third reviewer (NA-P).

Data extraction followed a standardised matrix developed for policy document analysis, capturing document type (law, policy or programme), year of publication or enactment, issuing institution, target population, strategic objectives, implementation status and relevance to intercultural health or RMNCH outcomes. A qualitative thematic content analysis was conducted across two analytical axes: (1) structural and political frameworks affecting Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian health and (2) RMNCH-related policies and programmes. An inductive–deductive approach was used to identify patterns and policy milestones over time.

Due to the descriptive and heterogeneous nature of the included materials, no quantitative synthesis was undertaken. Findings are therefore presented narratively, highlighting key policy developments and gaps in intercultural and equity-oriented implementation. PRISMA-ScR guidelines23 were used solely as a reporting tool to enhance transparency in documenting the document identification and selection process, rather than as a methodological framework for the review (see online supplemental figure 1A,B and tables 4,5).

Patient and public involvement

During the process of searching for and systematising inclusive policies, key informant engagements were conducted with Indigenous leaders and government officials involved in the development of maternal and child health policies. These consultations provided valuable insights into the political contexts, barriers and their possible connections with data from national surveys. Although their perspectives enriched the understanding of the study setting, this was not a formal qualitative methodology, and individual opinions are not presented as qualitative findings. The names of these informants are acknowledged in the manuscript’s Acknowledgments section.

Results

Without missing data, the three surveys allowed us to analyse data from 64 726 women and 34 100 children aged 12–23 months. Between 2004 and 2012, the distribution of the population self-identifying as Indigenous remained quite similar, but with a higher proportion of illiteracy compared with other ethnic groups. However, from 2012 to 2018, the secondary education level nearly doubled (from approximately 25% to 48%), and the illiteracy rate decreased from 18% to 4%. Similarly, the distribution of this population by wealth quintiles remained unchanged between 2004 and 2012, but by 2018, the percentages in quintiles 4 and 5 had doubled (from 10% to 20% in Q1 and Q2). The proportion of the population living in urban areas doubled between 2004 and 2012 (from 18% to 36%), but from 2012 to 2018, the increase was only 10% (see figure 1). Like the Mestizo and White populations, the Afro-Ecuadorian population did not experience significant changes in education levels between 2004 and 2012. However, between 2012 and 2018, illiteracy decreased significantly, and the proportion of the population with secondary education or higher increased. Unlike the Indigenous population, the percentages in wealth quintiles 4 and 5 slightly decreased (from 38% to 34%). Migration to urban areas increased between 2004 and 2012 and remained stable through 2018.

Compared with other ethnic groups, despite observed improvements, by 2018 the Indigenous population continued to predominantly reside in rural areas, had the lowest proportion of individuals with secondary education or higher, and showed a proportion in the lowest poverty quintile (Q1) four times higher than that observed among Mestizo and White populations. In contrast, the Afro-Ecuadorian population was predominantly urban, had levels of secondary education or higher comparable to those of Mestizo and White populations, and the proportion in the highest poverty quintile was twice that observed among Mestizo and White populations.

Results of the desk review

Using predefined keywords, two systematic searches were conducted to identify relevant policies and programmes within the Ecuadorian context. The first search focused on documents related to ethnicity, Indigenous peoples and nationalities; the second addressed sexual, reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health.

In the first search, 38 records were identified: 19 from bibliographic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE and BIREME/LILACS) and 19 from grey literature (Google Scholar, Ecuadorian government sources and international organisations). After screening and eligibility assessment, 18 studies were included, with exclusions mainly due to a lack of Ecuadorian context or relevance to health equity and intercultural approaches. In the second search, 93 records were retrieved: 65 from bibliographic databases and 28 from grey literature. Following screening and full-text review, 34 studies were included. Exclusions were primarily due to a lack of relevance to Ecuador or failure to address RMNCH-related policies and programmes (see figure 3).

Policies and programmes related to ethnicity in Ecuador

Ecuador’s 1998 Constitution, influenced by social mobilisations advocating for collective rights, established the country as pluricultural and multiethnic, though without recognising nationalities.24 The 2008 Constitution advanced this by declaring Ecuador a plurinational, intercultural state, guaranteeing intercultural education (Articles 26 and 27), affirmative action for equality (Article 11) and protection from discrimination based on origin, ethnicity or culture (Article 57).12 25 These principles were integrated into various policies to ensure educational equity.

The ratification of International Labour Organization (ILO) Convention 169 in 1998 (Article 2)26 led to the creation of the National Council for the Development of Nationalities and Peoples of Ecuador (CODENPE).27 CODENPE supported sustainable development through policy-making and societal dialogue.28 In 2014, the Organic Law of National Councils for Equality replaced CODENPE with the National Council for Equality of Peoples and Nationalities.29 30 Programmes like Development and Diversity (2008–2016)31 contributed to this work. The Afro-Ecuadorian community followed its path with the Afro-Ecuadorian Development Corporation,32 leading to the Law on Collective Rights of the Afro-Ecuadorian People,33 and the ratification of the International Decade for People of African Descent.34

The 2006 Ten-Year Education Plan introduced eight policies to foster inclusive, bilingual education, reduce financial barriers and eliminate tuition fees.35 The 2011 Organic Law of Intercultural Education established state contributions at 0.5% annually, targeting 6% of gross domestic product (GDP) for education.36 37 The ‘Plurinational Plan to Eliminate Racial Discrimination and Ethnic and Cultural Exclusion (2009–2012)’ launched affirmative action, mandating at least 10% of Afro-Ecuadorian, Montubio and Indigenous students in secondary and higher education.37 The Bilingual Intercultural Education System (Article 77)38 helped address the historical demands of Indigenous peoples for the protection of culture and ancestral languages.

Other notable initiatives include the Basic Education for Youth and Adults Project (2011–2013), benefiting 324 894 participants, 60% of whom were rural Indigenous individuals educated in their native language.39 The ‘Monseñor Leónidas Proaño Literacy, Basic Education and High School Campaign: Todos ABC’, initiated in 2017, continues to reduce national illiteracy.40 These efforts underscore Ecuador’s commitment to inclusive education and cultural preservation.

Policies and programmes related to maternal and child health in Ecuador

The period from 1998 to 2006 in Ecuador was characterised by a deep social, economic and political crisis, impacting health indicators and coverage of interventions.41,43 Among the social protection policies for maternal and child health during this period, the Free Maternity and Childcare Law stands out. Essential health benefits packages,44 increased coverage of prenatal care, professional-assisted deliveries, institutional births45 and access to contraceptive methods.46 This was achieved through financing and implementation mechanisms based on local economic transfers, centralised purchases by international organisations and strong promotion of social participation, involving Indigenous women leaders from rural areas in user committees.47 The Organic Health Law, in effect since 2006 (Art. 6), promotes and enhances the practice of traditional, ancestral and complementary medicine,48 and in 2006, Universal Health Insurance Programme (PROAUS) was introduced, but with the change of government, it was not continued.49

From 2006 to 2014, economic growth, poverty reduction and increased public health expenditure improved RMNCH intervention coverage41 50 51 and reduced maternal and child health disparities.43 52 A transformative health sector reform began in 2007,41 53 bolstered by the 2008 Constitution, which emphasised health as a human right.12 Key policies included the ‘Health, Sexual and Reproductive Rights Policy’ (2007),54 which addressed ethnic discrimination and promoted interculturality, and the ‘National Plan for the Reduction of Maternal and Neonatal Mortality’ (2008).55 Additionally, the Standard for Essential Obstetric and Neonatal Care was implemented, coordinating obstetric and neonatal services across providers.56

The 2008 Constitution recognised the plurinational state, integrating institutional healthcare with traditional and intercultural practices through normative documents and ministerial decrees.55 57 The 2008 Technical Guide for Culturally Appropriate Childbirth Care,58 2014 regulations for complementary medicine,59 and 2017 guidelines for medicinal gardens in primary care facilities60 underscored these efforts. In 2015, Ecuador issued ‘Standards and Indicators for Monitoring Low-Risk Normal Birth Care in Free Position in First and Second Level National Health System (NHS) facilities’,61 followed in 2016 by a Manual for integrating ancestral midwifery practices into the NHS.62 Additionally, regulations for intercultural approaches in health facilities and medicinal gardens were introduced in 2017.60 Reproductive health strategies evolved, including the National Intersectoral Strategy for Family Planning and Adolescent Pregnancy Prevention,63 and the ‘Family Plan’.64 Between 2017 and 2021, the National Plan for Sexual and Reproductive Health was issued.65

From 2014 to 2018, key achievements included incorporating 1700 Primary Care Technicians for Indigenous populations66 67 and integrating 2460 midwives into the Public Health System,68 in the framework of the Comprehensive Health Care Model (MAIS-FCI).69 70 By 2017, 7% of primary care facilities featured culturally relevant birthing rooms.71 Ecuador, alongside Brazil, led in baby-friendly hospital certifications,72 culminating in the 2017 Health Regulation for Certification as Friends of Mother and Child.73

The strengthening of primary healthcare in Ecuador is reflected in key regulations such as the Expanded Programme on Immunisations (EPI),74 the Family Planning Norms and Protocols,75 and the Regulation for Implementing an Intercultural Approach in Health Services.76 These instruments ensure equitable access to immunisations, modern contraceptive methods and culturally appropriate care, reaffirming the State’s commitment to comprehensive and inclusive health services.

In summary, between 2004 and 2012, the presence of social organisations that advocate for high-level policies on the rights of Indigenous peoples is evident. However, the mere existence of these organisations and policies in Ecuador did not reflect sufficient social and health outcomes until several policies and normative documents were added, which should have integrated the intercultural approach into healthcare within the NHS, within the framework of a constitution that since 2008 recognises a plurinational state and reinforces health as a human right. Although some policy documents report specific institutional advances, field experience and professional engagement in the sector suggest that few studies have rigorously assessed the real impact of these policies or strategies on Indigenous health at the national or local level. This gap limits the ability to draw concrete conclusions about the effectiveness of the intercultural approach in public health implementation.

Discussion topics

Few studies analyse long-term health changes and their links to government policies.77 78 While causality cannot be confirmed, international evidence provides valuable lessons for similar contexts.79 Countries with progressive surveys for monitoring interventions have focused on child mortality outcomes.80 81 This study uniquely examines RMNCH health intervention coverage.

In 2004, Ecuador had vast ethnic disparities in maternal and child health coverage, comparable only to Guatemala and Peru in Latin America.1 2 These gaps persisted until 2012, with overall coverage remaining low despite reduced inequality. From 2004 to 2018, RMNCH intervention coverage tripled, reaching 75%. Gains were seen across all ethnic subgroups, especially among self-identified Indigenous populations, with coverage gaps narrowing—except for immunisations and breastfeeding. Additionally, the proportion of women aged 15–49 with secondary education or higher increased, and wealth in the highest quintile (Q5) doubled. These findings underscore significant progress in reducing health and social disparities and improving coverage equity.

These improvements align with inclusive policies and affirmative actions since 2008, underpinned by Ecuador’s constitutional framework. Economic growth, financial policies and fund allocation also influenced health policy outcomes.79 In Latin America, only Bolivia and Ecuador declared themselves a ‘plurinational state’, incorporating ‘Sumaq Kawsay’ principles and emphasising basic and higher education.82 Successful strategies addressing health inequalities targeted socioeconomic disparities and women’s needs by removing financial barriers.2

Intercultural health practices have been incorporated into protocols, guidelines and high-level policies.13 However, few evaluations or monitoring systems assess their implementation and impact. Decennial plans often take years to materialise as laws, necessitating ongoing monitoring of service coverage and care quality to ensure equitable benefits.83 Routine surveys are critical for decision-making.6 13

Key strategies include involving local actors, traditional midwives,84 85 primary care technicians and user committees.47 Programmes succeed when community participation, cultural relevance and practical, collaborative approaches are prioritised.86 Strengthening primary care, policy advocacy and community mobilisation further promotes racial equity and encourages women to access services.87

Political stability and sustained economic growth have been identified as facilitating factors in maternal and child health impacts in other countries.13 Increased public spending on health,14 41 as well as improvements in health infrastructure, equipment and human resources,15 16 41 shows positive impacts on health coverage outcomes.50 52 88 These improvements appear to have focused on rural areas, evidenced by the reduction of inequalities in these areas where the highest percentage of the self-identified Indigenous population resides.43 89 90

The decline in early initiation of breastfeeding and limited improvement in immunisation coverage highlights the need to reassess policy implementation.91 The transition from EPI to MAIS-FCI may have affected vaccination uptake. Between 2004 and 2012, the prevalence of early initiation of breastfeeding was 1.15 times higher among Indigenous groups compared with Mestizo and White children, consistent with other studies conducted in provinces of the mountainous region and Latin America,31 83 84 but coverage was reduced in 2018. This pattern appears to reflect primarily cultural practices, as Indigenous populations receive no greater breastfeeding counselling than other groups. Therefore, it is important to assess whether biomedical-focused health interventions may inadvertently undermine beneficial maternal health practices, while urban migration and weak enforcement of intercultural health regulations could further disrupt breastfeeding behaviours. Strengthening the implementation of immunisation, breastfeeding and intercultural health policies remains essential to improve maternal and child health outcomes.5964,66 85

While inequality reductions were significant from 2004 to 2012, progress slowed afterward. By 2018, Indigenous populations still had 20% lower coverage in maternal and child health interventions compared with Mestizo and White populations. Unmeasured factors are likely to contribute to these gaps, as shown by notable differences between crude and adjusted risk ratios. These findings provide a pre-pandemic baseline, noting progress that may have been severely disrupted by COVID-19. Further efforts are needed to address persistent inequalities and sustain advancements in health coverage.

Strengths and limitations

This study has limitations inherent to its descriptive ecological design, which precludes control of all potential confounding factors and limits causal inference; unmeasured confounders may include geographic region, distance to health facilities, availability and quality of local health services, transportation barriers, and other access-related factors not captured in national surveys.

Nevertheless, the theoretical framework enabled the identification of key structural and contextual factors likely influencing the observed health trends, offering valuable insights into equity dynamics. However, the regression model included key social factors like education, wealth and rurality. While comparisons were primarily visual, the mixed-methods approach allowed for the analysis of nationally representative data, enabling insights into concurrent policies and generating new hypotheses. Further research is needed to explore key strategies and their impact. Though the reliance on digital evidence may have caused publication bias, efforts were made to reduce this by including grey literature and minimising strict quality criteria.

Conclusions

Ecuador, with its significant Indigenous population, has achieved substantial progress in RMNCH health intervention coverage over 14 years, reducing disparities while improving education levels and wealth among Indigenous mothers. Despite these advances, ethical disparities persist and require further attention.

These improvements align with increased public investment in health and education, alongside social participation efforts that incorporated plurinationality into the Constitution. These integrated policies facilitated strategies to adapt healthcare to interculturality and address racial discrimination, promoting education and strengthening intercultural approaches. Ecuador’s experience offers valuable insights for similar contexts across Latin America.

Key enablers included normative health documents incorporating intercultural approaches and the active involvement of community leaders in local strategy implementation. These efforts highlight the importance of bringing services closer to communities, fostering mobilisation and promoting collective action to advance racial equity.

Political stability and economic growth were critical enablers, demonstrating how comprehensive social and economic development underpins sustainable health outcomes. Future efforts should focus on expanding access to higher education, promoting decentralisation and fostering local integrated development to address urban–rural migration through local opportunities.

Ecological and temporal studies using routine surveys are instrumental in tracking health trends and evaluating policy impacts. Continuous monitoring of programme adherence and outcomes is crucial, particularly considering the economic crisis caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Renewing and reinforcing effective strategies will be essential to sustain progress and address remaining disparities, ensuring equitable health outcomes for all.
