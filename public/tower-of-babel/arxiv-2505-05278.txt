Inverse problems for semilinear elliptic equations with low regularity
David Johansson, Janne Nurminen, Mikko Salo

Abstract
. We show that a general nonlinearity a ⁡ ( x , u ) a(x,u) is uniquely determined, possibly up to a gauge, in a neighborhood of a fixed solution from boundary measurements of the corresponding semilinear equation. The main theorems are low regularity counterparts of the results in our recent paper (Johansson, Nurminen, Salo; ArXiv preprint 2312.12196).



1.
Introduction

This article deals with inverse boundary value problems for semilinear equations of the form

\Delta u(x)+a(x,u(x))=0\text{ in $\Omega$},

where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded open set with smooth boundary and the function
a(x,t)
represents the nonlinearity. There is a large literature on this topic. The first order linearization method introduced in
[
Isa93
]
has been employed to show that nonlinearities
a(x,t)
satisfying conditions such as

\displaystyle a(x,0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

can be determined in a certain reachable set from boundary measurements. The first condition above ensures that
0
is a solution, and the second sign condition guarantees that a maximum principle and well-posedness hold. Various results of this type, also for somewhat more general nonlinearities, may be found in
[
IS94
,
IN95
,
Sun10
,
IY13
]
. See also the surveys
[
Sun05
,
Uhl09
]
. In the results based on first order linearization, one typically uses known results on inverse problems for the linearized equation.

On the other hand, the higher order linearization method introduced in
[
FO20
,
LLLS21
]
(following the hyperbolic case in
[
KLU18
]
) applies to nonlinearities that do not need to satisfy any sign condition. Moreover, this method uses the nonlinearity as a beneficial tool and yields results in certain nonlinear cases where the corresponding results for linear equations remain unsolved
[
KU20
,
LLLS21a
,
CFO23
,
FKO23
,
ST23
,
Nur23a
]
. However, the method might only allow one to determine the Taylor series of
a(x,t)
at
t=0
, and it does not in general determine
a(x,t)
for
t\neq 0
. The higher order linearization method has also been used outside of semilinear equations. For quasilinear equations one has the similar phenomenon of determining only the Taylor series of the unknown coefficient
[
CFKKU21
,
KKU23
]
. Also inverse problems for the minimal surface equation (which is an example of a quasilinear equation) on Riemannian manifolds have been studied with this method. In
[
Nur23
,
Nur24
]
the Taylor series of a conformal factor for metrics in the same conformal class is recovered. In dimension two it is shown in
[
CLLO24
,
CLLT23
,
CLT24
]
(in slightly different settings) that three linearizations are enough to determine the Riemannian metric up to an isometry.

Recently in
[
JNS23
]
(see also
[
NS25
]
for a similar result for a biharmonic operator with a second order nonlinearity) we gave a result showing that from boundary measurements near the zero solution, one can determine a general nonlinearity
a(x,t)
near
t=0
whenever
a(x,0)=0
. We also gave a similar result without the assumption
a(x,0)=0
, but in that case
a(x,t)
can only be determined up to a natural gauge transformation. The precise assumption for the nonlinearity was that
a(x,t)
should be
C^{1,\alpha}
in
x
and
C^{3}
in
t
. In this article we improve the regularity assumptions to
L^{r}
in
x
and
C^{1,\alpha}
in
t
, where
r>n/2
. We also simplify the proofs in the process. The method is based on first linearization and a Runge approximation argument.

Let us state the main results. We will assume that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, is a bounded open set with smooth boundary (though many arguments would remain valid for
C^{1,1}
boundaries). We consider nonlinearities
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
for some
r>n/2
. If
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
M=\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, then
a(x,u(x))\in L^{r}(\Omega)
since

\int_{\Omega}|a(x,u(x))|^{r}\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{|t|\leq M}|a(x,t)|^{r}\,dx\leq\lVert a\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega,L^{\infty}([-M,M]))}^{r}.

We denote by
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
the standard Sobolev spaces in
\Omega
. If
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
with
r>n/2
, then (after choosing a suitable representative)
u\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
\alpha>0
by Sobolev embedding, so
a(x,u(x))
is well defined.

We wish to study inverse problems without any further assumptions on the nonlinearity
a(x,t)
. In particular we do not assume well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem, and hence the boundary measurements will be formulated in terms of Cauchy data sets (see
[
FSU25
]
). Given a solution
w\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
of
\Delta w+a(x,w)=0
in
\Omega
, and given
\delta>0
, we define the Cauchy data set for solutions near
w
as

C_{a}^{w,\delta}=\{(u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega})\,:\,u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega),\ \ \Delta u+a(x,u)=0\text{ in $\Omega$},\ \ \lVert u-w\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq\delta\}.

If
s>1/r
we denote the trace space of
W^{s,r}(\Omega)
by
W^{s-\frac{1}{r},r}(\partial\Omega):=B^{s-\frac{1}{r}}_{rr}(\partial\Omega)
, see
[
Tri83
]
. Then
C_{a}^{w,\delta}
is a subset of
W^{2-\frac{1}{r},r}(\partial\Omega)\times W^{1-\frac{1}{r},r}(\partial\Omega)
. If the semilinear equation happens to be well-posed for Dirichlet data close to
w|_{\partial\Omega}
, then the set
C_{a}^{w,\delta}
contains the graph of the corresponding nonlinear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for Dirichlet data close to
w|_{\partial\Omega}
and vice versa. For further discussion on the set
C_{a}^{w,\delta}
and its relation to Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps, we refer the reader to
[
JNS23
]
.

Our first result shows that if two nonlinearities
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
admit a common solution
w
and have the same Cauchy data for solutions near
w
, then
a_{1}(x,t)=a_{2}(x,t)
for
t
near
w(x)
. We assume that the nonlinearities are
C^{1,\alpha}
in
t
, but in fact uniform
C^{1}
regularity in
t
would be enough (see Remark
3.3
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
,
r\geq 2
and
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
w\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solves
\Delta w+a_{j}(x,w)=0
in
\Omega
for
j=1,2
. If for some
\delta,C>0
one has

C_{a_{1}}^{w,\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C},

then there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

a_{1}(x,w(x)+\lambda)=a_{2}(x,w(x)+\lambda)\text{ for a.e.\ $x\in\Omega$ and for all $\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$.}

In particular, if
a_{j}(x,0)=0
, we may take
w\equiv 0
as the common solution and the conclusion is that
a_{1}(x,\lambda)=a_{2}(x,\lambda)
for small
\lambda
. The additional assumption
r\geq 2
is only needed in dimensions
n\in\{2,3\}
, since for
n\geq 4
it follows from the condition
r>n/2
.

The second result concerns two general nonlinearities
a_{j}(x,t)
that may not have a common solution. In this case one can only determine the nonlinearity up to gauge (see
[
Sun10
]
). The gauge is given by

(T_{\varphi}a)(x,t)=\Delta\varphi(x)+a(x,t+\varphi(x))

where
\varphi\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
satisfies
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\nu}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
,
r\geq 2
and
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
w_{1}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solves
\Delta w_{1}+a_{1}(x,w_{1})=0
in
\Omega
. If for some
\delta,C>0
one has

C_{a_{1}}^{w_{1},\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C},

then there exist
\varphi\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
with
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\nu}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

a_{1}(x,w_{1}(x)+\lambda)=(T_{\varphi}a_{2})(x,w_{1}(x)+\lambda)\text{ for a.e.\ $x\in\Omega$ and for all $\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$.}

In Theorem
1.2
,
\varphi=w_{1}-w_{2}
where
w_{2}
is the unique solution of
\Delta w_{2}+a_{2}(x,w_{2})=0
in
\Omega
that has the same Cauchy data as
w_{1}
(see Lemma
4.4
). If
w_{2}=w_{1}
, we obtain Theorem
1.1
as a special case.

We note that our results cover the linear case
a(x,u)=q(x)u
, and hence include the recovery of an
L^{r}(\Omega)
potential where
r>n/2
and
r\geq 2
. This misses the endpoint result
r=n/2
for
n\geq 3
, see
[
Cha90
,
Nac92
,
DKS13
]
, and also the result for
L^{4/3+\varepsilon}
potentials for
n=2

[
BTW20
]
. The exponent
r=n/2
for
n\geq 3
may be considered optimal in the context of standard wellposedness theory for the Dirichlet problem with
L^{p}
potentials, and also for the strong unique continuation principle to hold
[
JK85
]
. Another low regularity result for
n=2
, under a condition on
\partial_{t}a(x,t)
that ensures well-posedness, is given in
[
IN95
]
.

We also remark that it is not in general possible to determine
a(x,t)
in
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, see
[
IS94
,
FKO23
]
. In general one could expect to determine
a(x,t)
in the reachable set
\{(x,u(x))\,:\,x\in\Omega,\ \Delta u+a(x,u)=0\text{ in $\Omega$}\}
. Our methods imply that the reachable set is always an open set. Conditions for existence of solutions
u
may be found in
[
Tay23
, Section 14.1 and Exercise 8]
.

Methods

The main results are low regularity versions of the corresponding results in
[
JNS23
]
, and we will prove them by a method based on first linearization. The linearization of the equation at a solution
w
is

\Delta v+qv=0\text{ in $\Omega$}

where
q(x)=\partial_{u}a(x,w(x))
. Since we do not assume well-posedness, there may be a finite dimensional obstruction for solving the Dirichlet problem for this equation. In
[
JNS23
]
we employed a solvability result where the Dirichlet data was modified by a function in
\partial_{\nu}N_{q}
where
N_{q}
is the eigenspace corresponding to zero eigenvalue. The reason for requiring that
a(x,t)
is
C^{1,\alpha}
in
x
in
[
JNS23
]
was that
\partial_{\nu}N_{q}
is not in the natural trace space of solutions if
a
has low regularity. In this article we replace the space
\partial_{\nu}N_{q}
with another space
D_{q}
of the same dimension. This makes it possible to work with nonlinearities
a(x,t)
that have only
L^{r}
regularity in
x
.

After establishing solvability for the linearized equation, the next step is to construct a
C^{1}
map that maps a small solution
v
of the linearized equation
\Delta v+qv=0
to a solution
u=S(v)=S_{a,w}(v)
of the nonlinear equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
, so that

u=w+v+o(\lVert v\rVert)

where
w
is a fixed solution of
\Delta w+a(x,w)=0
. In
[
JNS23
]
the existence of
S
was shown via a Banach fixed point argument, and the fact that
S
is
C^{1}
was proved by using the implicit function theorem. In this work we establish both the existence and smoothness of
S
by a single application of the implicit function theorem, which leads to a much shorter argument.

Next we consider the setting of Theorem
1.1
and employ the Cauchy data set inclusion
C_{a_{1}}^{w,\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C}
to define a map
T_{a_{2},w}
, which takes a solution
v
of the linearized equation
\Delta v+\partial_{u}a(x,w(x))v=0
to a solution
u
of
\Delta u+a_{2}(x,u)=0
that has the same Cauchy data as
S_{a_{1},w}(v)
. Since
T_{a_{2},w}
is defined via the Cauchy data inclusion, we do not know if it is
C^{1}
. However, by another argument based on the implicit function theorem we prove that
T_{a_{2},w}
is indeed
C^{1}
. The argument involves a unique continuation result that leads to Lemma
4.4
, and a certain projection operator defined in Lemma
4.5
via a fourth order equation. Lemma
4.5
is the only place where we need the additional condition
r\geq 2
for
n\in\{2,3\}
.

After having proved that both maps
S_{a_{1},w}
and
T_{a_{2},w}
are
C^{1}
, the argument for recovering the nonlinearity proceeds as in
[
JNS23
]
. We first derive an integral identity involving the difference of potentials in the linearized equations. Then we invoke the completeness of products of solutions to linear Schrödinger equations
[
Cha90
,
Nac92
,
DKS13
,
BTW20
]
and a unique continuation argument to show that

\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,S_{a_{1},w}(v))=\partial_{u}a_{2}(x,S_{a_{1},w}(v))

for small solutions
v
of the linearized equation for
a_{1}
. It remains to show that there is
\varepsilon
such that for any
x_{0}\in\Omega
and any
\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, one can find
v
such that
S_{a_{1},w}(v)(x_{0})=\lambda
. This follows from a Runge approximation argument for the linearized equation with
L^{r}
potentials.

This article is structured as follows. Section
1
is the introduction, Section
2
studies solvability and regularity for the linearized equation, and Section
3
presents the solution map
S_{a,w}
. The second solution map
T_{a_{2},w}
is studied in Section
4
. Finally, the main theorems are proved in Section
5
and the required Runge approximation result in Section
6
.

2.
Solvability for linear equations

Throughout this article we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, with smooth boundary. Let
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
where
r>n/2
, and define

N_{q}:=\{\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):(\Delta+q)\psi=0\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
where
r>n/2
. There is a subspace
D_{q}=\mathrm{span}\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\}
of
W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)
with
\dim(D_{q})=\dim(N_{q})<\infty
such that for any
F\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and
f\in W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)
, there is a unique function
\Phi=\Phi(F,f)\in D_{q}
such that the problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u+qu=F\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=f+\Phi\quad&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a solution
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
. If
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}\}
is a suitable basis of
N_{q}
, the function
\Phi
is given by

(2.2)

\Phi(F,f)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{\Omega}F\psi_{j}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}f\partial_{\nu}\psi_{j}\,dS\right)h_{j}.

Moreover, there is unique solution
u_{F,f}=G_{q}(F,f)
such that
u_{F,f}\perp N_{q}
where
\perp
means
L^{2}
-orthogonal. The solution
u_{F,f}
depends linearly on
F
and
f
and satisfies

(2.3)

\lVert u_{F,f}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\lVert F\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert f\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}),

where
C
is independent of
F
and
f
.

We begin with a standard regularity result. We will write
L^{p+}:=\bigcup_{t>p}L^{t}
. In this article we will frequently use the Sobolev embedding

W^{s,p}(\Omega)\subseteq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}L^{\frac{np}{n-sp}}(\Omega),&s<n/p,\\[3.0pt]
\bigcap_{t<\infty}L^{t}(\Omega),&s=n/p,\\[3.0pt]
C^{1-n/p}(\overline{\Omega}),&s>n/p,\end{array}\right.

as well as the generalized Hölder inequality

\int uvw\,dx\leq\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p_{1}}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\lVert w\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}},\qquad\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}+\frac{1}{p_{3}}=1.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
where
r>n/2
. Then any
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
solving

\begin{cases}\Delta u+qu=F\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=f\quad&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
F\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and
f\in W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)
, must satisfy
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
. Moreover,
N_{q}\subseteq W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is finite dimensional.

Proof.

By using the right inverse of the trace, this reduces to showing that any
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
solving

\begin{cases}\Delta w+qw=G\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
w=0\quad&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
G\in L^{r}(\Omega)
, must satisfy
w\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
. Rewrite the equation as

\Delta w=G-qw.

If
n=2
, then by Sobolev embedding
w\in W^{1,2}\subseteq L^{t}
for any
t<\infty
and therefore
qw\in L^{n/2+}
. Thus
G-qw\in L^{n/2+}
, so
w\in W^{2,n/2+}
by
[
JK95
, Theorem 0.3]
. By Sobolev embedding again
w\in L^{\infty}
, so
G-qw\in L^{r}
and thus
w\in W^{2,r}
by
[
JK95
, Theorem 0.3]
. The proof for
n\geq 3
is similar, but the initial Sobolev embedding
W^{1,2}\subseteq L^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}
only gives
w\in W^{1,2+\delta}
for some
\delta>0
. However, bootstrapping this regularity argument finitely many times yields
w\in W^{1,t}
for some
t>n
, and then the argument above gives
w\in W^{2,r}
. We omit the details.

The finite dimensionality of
N_{q}
is standard when
q\in L^{\infty}
. Under the current assumption
q\in L^{r}
, if
\psi\in N_{q}
, then the weak formulation of the equation implies

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}q\psi^{2}\,dx\leq\lVert q\rVert_{L^{r}}\lVert\psi\rVert_{L^{t}}^{2}

where
1/r+1/t+1/t=1
, i.e.
t=2r^{\prime}<\frac{2n}{n-2}
. If we equip
N_{q}
with the
L^{t}(\Omega)
norm, then any bounded sequence in
N_{q}
is also bounded in the
H^{1}
norm and by compact Sobolev embedding has a convergent subsequence in the
L^{t}
norm since
t<\frac{2n}{n-2}
. Thus the identity map on
N_{q}
is compact, which implies that
N_{q}
must be finite dimensional.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
N_{q}
with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Given
F\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and
f\in W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)
, we first solve

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\Delta v+qv=F-\sum a_{k}\psi_{k}\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v=f\quad&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

for suitable
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. The compatibility conditions are obtained by integrating the equation against
\psi_{j}
, i.e.

a_{j}=a_{j}(F,f)=\int_{\Omega}F\psi_{j}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}f\partial_{\nu}\psi_{j}\,dS.

With this choice of
a_{j}
, we first find a weak solution
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
of (
2.4
) (note that
f\in W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)\subseteq H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
by Sobolev embedding). If
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
this is standard and follows from
[
GT01
, Theorem 8.6]
. If
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
one first reduces the problem to the case
f=0
by using a right inverse of the trace. If
\lambda>0
is sufficiently large, the operator
T:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\Omega),Tv=(\Delta+q-\lambda)v
is an isomorphism (see e.g.
[
DKS13
, Appendix A]
), and the problem becomes equivalent with

v+\lambda T^{-1}j(v)=T^{-1}(F-\sum a_{k}\psi_{k})

where
j:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\Omega)
is the natural inclusion. Since
j
is compact, the Fredholm alternative holds for the above equation, and there is a solution
v
if and only if
T^{-1}(F-\sum a_{k}\psi_{k})
is orthogonal to the kernel of
(\mathrm{Id}+\lambda T^{-1}j)^{*}
. But this is equivalent with
F-\sum a_{k}\psi_{k}
being
L^{2}
-orthogonal to
N_{q}
, which yields the existence of a weak solution
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Lemma
2.2
then shows that
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
.

We have now proved that with the given choice of
a_{j}
, (
2.4
) has a solution
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
. We next choose
u_{j}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solving
(\Delta+q)u_{j}=\psi_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq m
, and set
D_{q}=\mathrm{span}\{u_{1}|_{\partial\Omega},\ldots,u_{m}|_{\partial\Omega}\}
. To see that such
u_{j}
exist, it is enough to choose
B_{r}
to be a large ball with
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq B_{r}
such that
0
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\Delta+q
in
B_{r}
, and to solve

\begin{cases}\Delta\tilde{u}_{j}+\tilde{q}\tilde{u}_{j}=\tilde{\psi}_{j}\quad&\text{in }B_{r},\\
\tilde{u}_{j}=0\quad&\text{on }\partial B_{r},\end{cases}

where
\tilde{q}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{j}
are the extensions of
q
and
\psi_{j}
by zero to
B_{r}
. The fact that one can arrange
0
not to be a Dirichlet eigenvalue follows from strict monotonicity of Dirichlet eigenvalues with respect to increasing the domain, see the argument in
[
Lei67
]
. The solutions
\tilde{u}_{j}
are in
W^{2,r}(B_{r})
by Lemma
2.2
, and we can take
u_{j}=\tilde{u}_{j}|_{\Omega}
.

We also show that
\dim(D_{q})=m
: if
\sum c_{j}u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, then
u=\sum c_{j}u_{j}
satisfies
(\Delta+q)u=\psi
with
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
where
\psi=\sum c_{j}\psi_{j}
. Integrating the equation against
\psi
gives
\int\psi^{2}\,dx=0
, so
\psi=0
and hence
c_{1}=\ldots=c_{m}=0
since
\psi_{j}
are linearly independent. Thus
\dim(D_{q})=m
.

Now that we have obtained the functions
u_{j}
, we obtain a solution
u
to (
2.1
) via

u=v+\sum a_{j}u_{j}.

Moreover, any solution to (
2.1
) is of the form
u+\psi
for some
\psi\in N_{q}
.

Next we show that the function
\Phi
is unique. Assume we have two solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
to (
2.1
) with the same data
F,f
but with
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
respectively. Then
u=u_{1}-u_{2}
solves

\begin{cases}\Delta u+qu=0\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}=\sum c_{j}u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}\quad&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

for some
c_{j}
. Writing
v=u-\sum c_{j}u_{j}
, we see that
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solves
(\Delta+q)v=\psi
and
v|_{\partial\Omega}=0
where
\psi=-\sum c_{j}\psi_{j}\in N_{q}
. Integrating this equation against
\psi
yields
\int\psi^{2}\,dx=0
, so
c_{j}=0
for all
j
and therefore
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
.

Using the uniqueness of
\Phi
we can show that the
N_{q}
-orthogonal solution to (
2.1
) obtained above is unique. Assume that we have two solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
with the same data
F,f
and with
u_{j}\perp N_{q}
. Then
u_{1}-u_{2}\in N_{q}
and
u_{1}-u_{2}\perp N_{q}
, which implies
u_{1}=u_{2}
. Finally, let

X=\{u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\,:\,u\perp N_{q}\},\qquad Y=\{f\in W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)\,:\,f\perp D_{q}\}

and consider the map

T:X\to L^{r}(\Omega)\times Y,\ \ Tu=(\Delta u+qu,(\mathrm{Id}-P_{D_{q}})(u|_{\partial\Omega}))

where
P_{D_{q}}
the
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
-orthonormal projection to
D_{q}
. By what we have proved above,
T
is a bounded linear bijective operator. The open mapping theorem ensures that
T
has a bounded inverse. This implies that
u_{F,f}
depends continuously on
F
and
f
.
∎

3.
Solution map for nonlinear equation

The following result shows the existence of a map
v\mapsto S(v)
that parametrizes solutions
u
of the semilinear equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
in
\Omega
, when
u
is close to a fixed solution
w
, in terms of solutions of the corresponding linearized equation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
, and suppose that
w\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is a solution of

\Delta w+a(x,w)=0\text{ in }\Omega.

Let
q(x)=\partial_{u}a(x,w(x))
. There is a
C^{1}
map
S=S_{a,w}:V\to W^{2,r}(\Omega)
, where
V
is a neighborhood of
0
in
W^{2,r}(\Omega)
, such that
u=S(v)
solves

\Delta u+a(x,u)=\Delta v+qv.

One has
S(0)=w
,
(DS)_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
,
S(v)-w-v\perp N_{q}
and
S(v)-w-v|_{\partial\Omega}\in D_{q}
.

Conversely, any solution of
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
with
\lVert u-w\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}
small enough must be of the form
u=S(v)
for some
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solving
\Delta v+qv=0
. The function
v
is given by

v=P_{N_{q}}(u-w)+\tilde{v}

where
P_{N_{q}}
is the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal projection to
N_{q}
, and
\tilde{v}=G_{q}(0,(u-w)|_{\partial\Omega})
is the unique solution given by Proposition
2.1
.

Note that the result gives the Taylor expansion

S(v)=w+v+R(v)

where
\lVert R(v)\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}=o(\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)})
as
\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\to 0
and
R(v)\in N_{q}^{\perp}
,
R(v)|_{\partial\Omega}\in D_{q}
.

For the proof, we give a lemma on the properties of the map
u\mapsto a(x,u)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
and
\alpha>0
. Then
u\mapsto a(\,\cdot\,,u(\,\cdot\,))
is a
C^{1}
map from
W^{2,r}(\Omega)
to
L^{r}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

To prove that
u\mapsto a(\,\cdot\,,u(\,\cdot\,))
is
C^{1}
, we need to show that for any
u,v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
one has

\lVert a(x,u+v)-a(x,u)-\partial_{u}a(x,u)v\rVert_{L^{r}}=o(\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}})\quad\text{as $\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}}\to 0$.}

Let
u,v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
with
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq M
and
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=\varepsilon\leq 1
. We obtain

\displaystyle\lVert a(x,u+v)-a(x,u)-\partial_{u}a(x,u)v\rVert_{L^{r}}^{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|a(x,u(x)+v(x))-a(x,u(x))-\partial_{u}a(x,u(x))v(x)|^{r}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}|a(\,\cdot\,,t+h)-a(\,\cdot\,,t)-\partial_{u}a(\,\cdot\,,t)h|^{r}\,dx

where the supremum is over
|t|\leq M
and
|h|\leq\varepsilon
. Since
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\sup_{|t|,|s|\leq M+1}\frac{|\partial_{u}a(x,t)-\partial_{u}a(x,s)|^{r}}{|t-s|^{\alpha r}}\,dx\leq C_{M}<\infty.

In particular, for supremum over
|t|\leq M
,
|h|\leq\varepsilon
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}|a(x,t+h)-a(x,t)-\partial_{u}a(x,t)h|^{r}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}|h\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{u}a(x,t+sh)-\partial_{u}a(x,t))\,ds|^{r}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{r}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}|\partial_{u}a(x,t+sh)-\partial_{u}a(x,t)|^{r}\,dx

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{r}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\frac{|\partial_{u}a(x,t+sh)-\partial_{u}a(x,t)|^{r}}{|sh|^{r\alpha}}|sh|^{r\alpha}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{r+r\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t,h}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\frac{|\partial_{u}a(x,t+sh)-\partial_{u}a(x,t)|^{r}}{|sh|^{r\alpha}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\varepsilon^{r+r\alpha}.

Combining these estimates gives

\displaystyle\lVert a(x,u+v)-a(x,u)-\partial_{u}a(x,u)v\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq C_{M}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{1+\alpha}\leq C_{M}\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}}^{1+\alpha}.

This shows that the map
u\mapsto a(x,u)
is
C^{1}
as required.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Lemma
3.2
is valid with the same proof when
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\eta_{T}}([-T,T]))
for any
T>0
, where
C^{1,\eta_{T}}([-T,T])
is the space of functions in
C^{1}([-T,T])
whose first derivatives have a fixed modulus of continuity
\eta_{T}
. The point is that this modulus of continuity should be uniform over
x\in\Omega
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
D_{q}
be as in Proposition
2.1
, and define the map

F\colon W^{2,r}(\Omega)\times W^{2,r}(\Omega)\to L^{r}(\Omega)\times(W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)\cap D_{q}^{\perp})\times N_{q}

by

F(u,v)\coloneqq(\Delta u+a(x,u)-\Delta v-qv,P_{D_{q}^{\perp}}((u-w-v)|_{\partial\Omega}),P_{N_{q}}(u-w-v)).

Since
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
, the map
F
is
C^{1}
, by Lemma
3.2
, and satisfies
F(w,0)=(0,0,0)
. Its Fréchet derivative with respect to
u
satisfies

(3.1)

(D_{u}F)_{(w,0)}(h)=(\Delta h+qh,P_{D_{q}^{\perp}}(h|_{\partial\Omega}),P_{N_{q}}h).

Then
(D_{u}F)_{(w,0)}
is a bounded linear operator
W^{2,r}(\Omega)\to L^{r}(\Omega)\times(W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)\cap D_{q}^{\perp})\times N_{q}
. It is bijective by Proposition
2.1
, so the open mapping theorem ensures that it is an isomorphism. Now the implicit function theorem in Banach spaces implies the existence of a
C^{1}
map
S:V\to U
, where
U
and
V
are open sets in
W^{2,r}(\Omega)
with
0\in V
and
w\in U
, such that
F(S(v),v)=0
for
v\in V
and the solution
S(v)
is unique in the sense that

F(u,v)=0\text{ for $(u,v)\in U\times V$}\quad\implies\quad u=S(v).

One has
S(0)=w
, and differentiating
F(S(v),v)=0
with respect to
v
gives

(D_{u}F)_{(w,0)}(DS)_{0}(\tilde{v})-(\Delta\tilde{v}+q\tilde{v},P_{D_{q}^{\perp}}(\tilde{v}|_{\partial\Omega}),P_{N_{q}}(\tilde{v}))=0.

From (
3.1
) we obtain

(D_{u}F)_{(w,0)}((DS)_{0}(\tilde{v})-\tilde{v})=0.

The fact that
(D_{u}F)_{(w,0)}
is an isomorphism gives that
(DS)_{0}(\tilde{v})=\tilde{v}
. The identity
F(S(v),v)=0
yields
S(v)-w-v\perp N_{q}
and
S(v)-w-v|_{\partial\Omega}\in D_{q}
.

Conversely, let
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solve
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
. Apply Proposition
2.1
to find the unique function
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
such that

\Delta v+qv=0,\qquad v|_{\partial\Omega}\in(u-w)|_{\partial\Omega}+D_{q},\qquad P_{N_{q}}v=P_{N_{q}}(u-w).

Then
v=P_{N_{q}}(u-w)+G_{q}(0,u-w|_{\partial\Omega})
and
F(u,v)=0
. If
\lVert u-w\rVert_{W^{2,r}}
is sufficiently small, the uniqueness notion above implies that
u=S(v)
.
∎

For the next result, we define
q=\partial_{u}a(x,w),

q_{v}=\partial_{u}a(x,S_{a,w}(v))
and

\displaystyle V_{\tilde{q}}=\{h\in W^{2,r}(\Omega):\Delta h+\tilde{q}h=0\}.

Lemma 3.4
.

In the setting of Proposition
3.1
let
v\in V_{q}
be small. Then
DS_{a,w}(v)\colon V_{q}\to V_{q_{v}}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The proof is the same as in
[
JNS23
, Lemma 2.5]
, by noting that
R(v)
after Proposition
3.1
is
C^{1}
since
S_{a,w}(v)
is
C^{1}
and that
DR(0)=0
.
∎

4.
Second solution map

In order to prove our main result, we need to construct solutions to the equations
\Delta u+a_{j}(x,u)=0
such that these solutions have the same Cauchy data and they both depend smoothly on some linear solution
v
. If we attempt to use only the solution maps
S_{a_{1},w_{1}}
and
S_{a_{2},w_{2}}
, then the latter condition requires that solutions of
(\Delta+\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,w_{1}))v=0
also solve
(\Delta+\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,w_{2}))v=0
. This we do not know a priori. Moreover, there is no guarantee that even the Dirichlet data of
S_{a_{1},w_{1}}(v)
and
S_{a_{2},w_{2}}(v)
are equal, let alone the Cauchy data. Thus we need to replace
S_{a_{2},w_{2}}
with another solution map
T_{a_{2},w_{1}}
that uses information on the Cauchy data sets. Next we construct
T_{a_{2},w_{1}}
, and in Proposition
4.1
we prove that it is
C^{1}
in
v
without requiring that
\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,w_{1})=\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,w_{2})
.

We now assume that
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
for some
r>n/2
are two nonlinearities,
w_{1}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solves
\Delta w_{1}+a_{1}(x,w_{1})=0
in
\Omega
, and that one has the local Cauchy data set inclusion

C_{a_{1}}^{w_{1},\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C}.

In particular, this implies that there is
w_{2}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solving
\Delta w_{2}+a_{2}(x,w_{2})=0
and having the same Cauchy data as
w_{1}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Let
S_{a_{1},w_{1}}
be the
C^{1}
solution map for the nonlinearity
a_{1}
given in Proposition
3.1
. If we write
q_{1}=\partial_{u}a_{1}(x,w_{1}(x))
and

V_{q_{1},\delta_{1}}=\{v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\,:\,\Delta v+q_{1}v=0,\quad\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}}<\delta_{1}\},

then for
v\in V_{q_{1},\delta_{1}}
with
\delta_{1}>0
small enough,
u=S_{a_{1},w_{1}}(v)
solves
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
with expansion

u=w_{1}+v+o(\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,r}})

as
v\to 0
. From the inclusion
C_{a_{1}}^{w_{1},\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C}
we obtain another map

T_{a_{2},w_{1}}:V_{q_{1},\delta_{1}}\to W^{2,r}(\Omega),\ \ v\mapsto u_{2},

where
u_{2}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is a solution of
\Delta u_{2}+a_{2}(x,u_{2})=0
obtained from the inclusion
C_{a_{1}}^{w_{1},\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C}
and having the same Cauchy data as
u_{1}=S_{a_{1},w_{1}}(v)
on
\partial\Omega
.

The solution map
T_{a_{2},w_{1}}
above produces solutions of
\Delta u+a_{2}(x,u)=0
parametrized by solutions
v
of
\Delta v+q_{1}v=0
, but we do not know yet if this map is
C^{1}
with respect to
v
. We will prove this next.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
and
r\geq 2
, and suppose that
C_{a_{1}}^{w_{1},\delta}\subseteq C_{a_{2}}^{0,C}
. The map
T_{a_{2},w_{1}}
above is a
C^{1}
map
V_{q_{1},\delta_{1}}\to W^{2,r}(\Omega)
when
\delta_{1}>0
is small enough. Each
u=T_{a_{2},w_{1}}(v)
solves
\Delta u+a_{2}(x,u)=0
and has the same Cauchy data on
\partial\Omega
as
S_{a_{1},w_{1}}(v)
.

For the proof, we need a few lemmas that are low regularity counterparts of corresponding results in
[
JNS23
, Section 3]
with constants only depending on an upper bound on
\lVert q\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}
. The first lemma is a quantitative version of the elliptic regularity result in Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set with
C^{\infty}
boundary, let
r>n/2
and
M>0
. There is
C>0
depending on
M
such that for any
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
with
\lVert q\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq M
and for any
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
we have

\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\lVert(\Delta+q)u\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert u|_{\partial\Omega}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}).

Proof.

First consider the case
q=0
. We look at the Banach space
X=L^{r}(\Omega)\times W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
, with norm

\lVert(F,f,v)\rVert_{X}=\lVert F\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert f\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

and the bounded, linear, injective map

T\colon W^{2,r}(\Omega)\to X,\ \ T(u)=(\Delta u,u|_{\partial\Omega},j(u)),

where
j:W^{2,r}(\Omega)\to H^{1}(\Omega)
is the natural inclusion (here we use Sobolev embedding).
Then
T
has closed range. To see this, suppose that
u_{j}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
and
T(u_{j})\to(F,f,v)
in
X
. Then
u_{j}\to v
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}\to f
in
W^{2-1/r,r}(\Omega)
and
\Delta u_{j}\to F
in
L^{r}(\Omega)
. On the other hand
\Delta u_{j}\to\Delta v
in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}\to v|_{\partial\Omega}
in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and by uniqueness of limits one has
(\Delta+q)v=F
and
v|_{\partial\Omega}=f
. By Lemma
2.2
the weak solution
v
satisfies
v\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
. Thus
(F,f,v)=T(v)
and
\mathrm{Ran}(T)
is closed.

We have proved that
T:W^{2,r}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{Ran}(T)
is a bounded linear bijection between Banach spaces. By the open mapping theorem it has a bounded inverse
S:\mathrm{Ran}(T)\to W^{2,r}(\Omega)
, and thus for any
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
one has

\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}=\lVert STu\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert Tu\rVert_{X}.

This proves the claim for
q=0
.

Next we assume that
\lVert q\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq M
. Given
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
, the result proved above for
q=0
yields

(4.1)

\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\lVert(\Delta+q)u\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert qu\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert u|_{\partial\Omega}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}).

By Sobolev embedding,

\displaystyle\lVert qu\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq M\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq CM\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s_{\theta},p_{\theta}}(\Omega)}

for some sufficiently small
\theta>0
, where

s_{\theta}=(1-\theta)\cdot 2+\theta\cdot 1,\qquad\frac{1}{p_{\theta}}=(1-\theta)\frac{1}{r}+\theta\frac{1}{2}.

Now using interpolation we get

\lVert u\rVert_{W^{s_{\theta},p_{\theta}}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert u\rVert_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}^{1-\theta}.

Then using Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon
gives

\lVert qu\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq CM\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{\theta}\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}^{1-\theta}\leq\varepsilon\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}+C_{\varepsilon,M}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Choosing
\varepsilon
small enough we can absorb the
\varepsilon\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}
term to the left hand side of (
4.1
). This proves the result.
∎

The next lemma gives an estimate for
\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}
in terms of
\lVert(\Delta+q)u\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}
and the Cauchy data of
u
. The proof invokes a unique continuation result for
L^{r}
potentials.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r>n/2
and
M>0
. There is
C>0
depending on
M
such that for any
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
with
\lVert q\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq M
and for any
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
, one has

\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\lVert(\Delta+q)u\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert u|_{\partial\Omega}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}\rVert_{W^{1-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}).

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume that for any
m
there exist
q_{m}
with
\lVert q_{m}\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq M
and
u_{m}\in W^{2,r}
such that

(4.2)

\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}>m(\lVert(\Delta+q_{m})u_{m}\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert\partial_{\nu}u_{m}\rVert_{W^{1-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}).

On the other hand, Lemma
4.2
implies that

\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\lVert(\Delta+q_{m})u_{m}\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}).

Normalize
u_{m}
so that
\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
. Then using (
4.2
) yields

\displaystyle\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(\frac{1}{m}\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}+1).

Then
\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C
uniformly when
m
is sufficiently large. Using that
W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is a reflexive Sobolev space, and by using compact Sobolev embedding, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a subsequence, still denoted by
(u_{m})
, such that

\displaystyle u_{m}\rightharpoonup u

\displaystyle\text{ weakly in $W^{2,r}(\Omega)$},

\displaystyle u_{m}\to u

\displaystyle\text{ in $W^{2-\varepsilon,r}(\Omega)$}.

Passing to a further subsequence, we may further assume that

\displaystyle q_{m}\rightharpoonup q

\displaystyle\text{ weakly in $L^{r}(\Omega)$},

\displaystyle q_{m}\to q

\displaystyle\text{ in $W^{-\varepsilon,r}(\Omega)$}.

On the other hand, from (
4.2
) and the bound
\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C
we see that

u_{m}|_{\partial\Omega}\to 0,\qquad\partial_{\nu}u_{m}|_{\partial\Omega}\to 0,\qquad(\Delta+q_{m})u_{m}\to 0

in the respective spaces. Now uniqueness of limits implies that
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and
\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Here we chose
\varepsilon>0
so that
2-\varepsilon>1-1/r
, which ensures that
\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}
is well defined by the trace theorem. We also claim that

(\Delta+q_{m})u_{m}\to(\Delta+q)u\text{ in $W^{-\varepsilon,r}(\Omega)$.}

This follows since
q_{m}u_{m}\to qu
by the estimate
\lVert ab\rVert_{W^{-\varepsilon,r}}\leq C\lVert a\rVert_{W^{-\varepsilon,r}}\lVert b\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon+\delta}}\leq C\lVert a\rVert_{W^{-\varepsilon,r}}\lVert b\rVert_{W^{2-\varepsilon,r}}
(see
[
Tri83
, Theorem 3.3.2]
), which holds for some
\delta>0
by Sobolev embedding when
\varepsilon>0
is chosen really small using that
r>n/2
. Now by uniqueness of limits, we see that
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is a (distributional, and hence also strong) solution of

(\Delta+q)u=0\text{ in $\Omega$.}

We extend
u
and
q
by zero to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to obtain a compactly supported solution
u\in W^{2,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
(\Delta+q)u=0
. Consequently,
u\equiv 0
by unique continuation (see
[
JK85
, Theorem 6.3 and Remark 6.7]
), which contradicts the fact that
\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\lim\lVert u_{m}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
.
∎

The following lemma gives a uniqueness result and estimate for solutions of the semilinear equation, assuming an a priori bound for the
W^{2,r}
norm.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
a\in L^{r}(\Omega,C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
where
r>n/2
, and let
u_{0}\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
solve
\Delta u_{0}+a(x,u_{0})=0
in
\Omega
. If
u\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)
is any other solution of
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
in
\Omega
and
\lVert u\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}
,
\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq M
, then

(4.3)

\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq C(M,a)(\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}+\lVert\partial_{\nu}(u-u_{0})\rVert_{W^{1-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)}).

Proof.

Let
v=u-u_{0}
. Then

Lemma 4.5

Let

very weak solution
of

(\Delta+q)u=\mu\text{ in $\Omega$},\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

if

\int_{\Omega}u(\Delta+q)\varphi\,dx=\mu(\varphi)

for any
\varphi\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(\Omega)
. The next result will be used in the proof of Lemma
6.3
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
r>n/2
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set with smooth boundary.
Let
q\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and suppose that the equation

\begin{cases}(\Delta+q)u=F&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

is well-posed in
W^{2,r}(\Omega)
for
F\in L^{r}(\Omega)
.
Then there exists for every functional
\mu\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)^{*}
a very weak solution
v\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
of

\begin{cases}(\Delta+q)v=\mu&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

By the well-posedness
P=\Delta+q
maps
W^{2,r}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(\Omega)
surjectively onto
L^{r}(\Omega)
and its inverse
P^{-1}
is bounded.
Define for
\varphi\in L^{r}(\Omega)
the functional
L\varphi=\langle\,\mu,\,P^{-1}\varphi\rangle
.
Then
L
is bounded,

\lvert L\varphi\rvert=\lvert\langle\,\mu,\,P^{-1}\varphi\rangle\rvert\leq C\lVert P^{-1}\varphi\rVert_{W^{2,r}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{C}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{r}(\Omega)}

and hence
L
belongs to
L^{r}(\Omega)^{*}
.
By Riesz representation theorem, we get
v\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}v\varphi=L\varphi=\langle\,\mu,\,P^{-1}\varphi\rangle.

Using the well-posedness we may write any
\varphi
as
\varphi=P\psi
for
\psi\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(\Omega)
and hence

\int_{\Omega}vP\psi=\langle\,\mu,\,\psi\rangle\quad\forall\psi\in W^{2,r}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(\Omega).\qed

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

We equip
V_{U}
with the
W^{2,r}(U)
norm, so
V_{U}
is a closed subspace of
W^{2,r}(U)
and
R_{U}
is a linear subspace. By the Hahn-Banach theorem,
R_{U}
will be dense in
V_{U}
if every bounded linear functional on
W^{2,r}(U)
that vanishes on
R_{U}
must also vanish on
V_{U}
. Let
\tilde{h}\in W^{2,r}(U)^{*}
such that

(6.3)

\langle\,\tilde{h},\,v\rangle=0\quad\forall v\in R_{U}.

We wish to show that
\langle\,\tilde{h},\,v\rangle=0
for all
v\in V_{U}
.

Let
h\in W^{2,r}(B)^{*}
be the extension of
\tilde{h}
defined by
\langle\,h,\,\varphi\rangle\coloneqq\langle\,\tilde{h},\,\varphi|_{U}\rangle
, for
\varphi\in W^{2,r}(B)
.
Then
\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq\overline{U}
.
We use Lemma
6.4
and let
w\in L^{r^{\prime}}(B)
be a very weak solution of the adjoint problem

\begin{cases}(\Delta+q)w=h&\text{in }B,\\
w=0&\text{on }\partial B.\end{cases}

In other words,
w
satisfies

(6.4)

\int_{B}w[\Delta+q]\varphi\,dx=\langle\,h,\,\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in W^{2,r}(B)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(B).

There is a bounded extension operator
W^{2,r}(U)\to W^{2,r}_{0}(B)
.
We may therefore extend any solution
v\in V_{U}
to a function
\tilde{v}\in W^{2,r}_{0}(B)
.
Taking
\varphi=\tilde{v}
in (
6.4
), we have

(6.5)

\int_{B\setminus U}w[\Delta+q]\tilde{v}\,dx=\langle\,h,\,\tilde{v}\rangle.

The main step is to show that
w=0
in
B\setminus\overline{U}
.
This will be accomplished by unique continuation from an open set.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set containing
B
and define
u\in L^{r^{\prime}}(Q)
by

u(x)=\begin{cases}w(x)&\text{if }x\in B,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We show that
u
is a distributional solution of
\Delta u=0
in
Q\setminus\overline{U}
in the sense that

\int_{Q}u\Delta\varphi\,dx=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Q\setminus\overline{U}).

For such
\varphi
, extend it by zero into
U
, so that
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Q)
with
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)\cap\overline{U}=\emptyset
.
Let
\psi\in W^{2,r}(B)
satisfy

\begin{cases}(\Delta+q)\psi=0&\text{in }B,\\
\psi=\varphi&\text{on }\partial B.\end{cases}

Then
\pi=\varphi|_{B}-\psi\in W^{2,r}(B)\cap W^{1,r}_{0}(B)
.
From this and
u|_{Q\setminus B}=0
,
q|_{B\setminus U}=0
, and
\varphi|_{U}=0
we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}u\Delta\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{B}w\Delta\varphi\,dx=\int_{B}w(\Delta+q)\varphi\,dx=\int_{B}w(\Delta+q)\pi\,dx

\displaystyle=\langle\,h,\,\pi\rangle=\langle\,h,\,\varphi|_{B}\rangle-\langle\,h,\,\psi\rangle.

Now
\psi\in V_{B}
so we have
\langle\,h,\,\psi\rangle=0
by (
6.3
).
And
\langle\,h,\,\varphi|_{B}\rangle=0
follows from
\operatorname{supp}(h)\cap\operatorname{supp}(\varphi|_{B})=\emptyset
.
This shows that
u
is a distributional solution of
\Delta u=0
in
Q\setminus\overline{U}
. Since
u=0
in
Q\setminus B
, unique continuation (see
[
JK85
, Theorem 6.3 and Remark 6.7]
) shows that
w=u=0
in
B\setminus\overline{U}
. Here we used that
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{U}
is connected.
Now (
6.5
) reduces to

\langle\,h,\,v\rangle=0\quad\forall v\in V_{U}

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

We begin by using Lemma
6.2
to find a positive function
u_{0}\in W^{2,r}(B(x_{0},\varepsilon))
solving
(\Delta+q)u_{0}=0
in
B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
, where
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small. Then Lemma
6.3
, with
U=B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
and
B
a suitable large ball containing
\overline{\Omega}
, ensures that there are
u_{j}\in W^{2,r}(B)
solving
(\Delta+q)u_{j}=0
in
B
such that
u_{j}|_{B(x_{0},\varepsilon)}\to u_{0}
in
W^{2,r}(B(x_{0},\varepsilon))
and thus also in
L^{\infty}(B(x_{0},\varepsilon))
by Sobolev embedding. By choosing
j
large enough we see that
u_{j}|_{B(x_{0},\varepsilon)}
is positive, which proves the result.
∎
