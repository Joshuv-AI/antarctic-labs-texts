Adaptive immune response to West Nile virus infection in the Collaborative Cross mouse model: A database of cellular phenotypes and Quantitative Trait Loci
Authors: Steven Chamberlin, Jessica Graham, Sophia Jeng, Jessica Swarts, Martin Ferris, Mark Heise, Ralph S Baric, Shannon McWeeney, Jennifer Lund, Michael Mooney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWest Nile virus (WNV) infection can lead to a wide range of clinical outcomes, from asymptomatic to self-limiting febrile and serious neuro-invasive disease. Knowledge of the genetic factors contributing to the heterogeneity of this disease can inform pathogenic mechanisms, help guidethe development of cell based therapeutics, vaccines and inform lifestyle choices. Yet this knowledge is incomplete in humans. Here we present data from a large-scale experiment aimed at identifying quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with adaptive immune cellular phenotypes observed in response to infection with WNV. This data was generated using the Collaborative Cross (CC) mouse model, previously demonstrated as a representative model for human WNV infection and homeostatic immune states. In addition, due to challenges of QTL mapping with the large number of intermediate, highly coordinated immune and virologic phenotypes, we also provide a computational pipeline for the prioritization of gene candidates designed to leverage those characteristics for downstream mechanistic studies.Subject terms: Immunogenetics, Viral infection

Background & Summary

West Nile virus (WNV) infection in humans is now considered a significant global health problem1–9. Most people infected are asymptomatic, but 20–40% experience diverse disease outcomes, ranging from a self-limited febrile condition (West Nile fever) to severe neuro-invasive disease, which includes meningitis, encephalitis, and acute flaccid paralysis10–13. About 1% of those infected experience neuro-invasive disease with approximately a 10% mortality rate. Patients can also experience longer term chronic conditions after the acute phase of infection, including problems with cognition, fatigue and weakness14. An irreversible poliomyelitis-like condition can also persist after acute infection15.

Risk factors for developing neuro-invasive disease include age, malignancy, organ transplantation and genetic predisposition16. Currently, genetic factors in humans have been associated with disease severity, but not yet susceptibility to infection17,18. It is reasonable to assume that there are as yet unknown genetic factors effecting both clinical outcomes and susceptibility to infection, given the wide range of symptom severity and outcomes seen in the genetically outbred human population. Knowledge of the genetic contribution to immune response could lead to discovery of new drug targets, increase knowledge of the causal relationship between modifiable risk factors and those outcomes, and provide clinical utility in terms of risk stratification. In addition, studying intermediate cellular immunophenotypes can be important for uncovering these mechanisms of immune response19. However, the development of more sophisticated high throughput methods to measure immune response, such as mass cytometry, has vastly increased the number of phenotypes that can be measured in each study, creating new challenges, as well as opportunities, in identifying causative genetic loci for a complex coordinated immune response to infection.

Previously, we have shown that uninfected (Collaborative Cross) CC-F1 mouse crosses expressed T-cell phenotypic variation similar to that seen in humans, and this data set could be used to identify associated genetic loci with quantitavie trait loci mapping methods20. We present here a dataset designed for the study of an adaptive immune response to WNV infection using this same CC-F1 model. A comprehensive screen of adaptive immune cellular phenotypes was performed on these mice both uninfected and at 4 time points post-infection, in both spleen and brain tissue. The screen included T cell subsets (CD3+. CD4+, CD8+), regulatory T cells, activation markers and evaluation of cytokine production. This CC-F1 mouse model for WNV infection has shown clinical, virologic and immune phenotypic diversity similar to reports in human populations and has successfully been used to model persistent disease for WNV21,22 and other important viral infections, such as Ebola, TMEV, Zika and Powassan virus23–28. The data sets reported herein models subcutaneous infection, and examines both lymphatic tissues (spleen) and peripheral tissues (brain).

Quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping associates genetic sequence variation in a genetic interval with disease phenotypes and is used to find potential genetic contributions to these phenotypes. However, these intervals generally do not have enough resolution to identify the true causal gene, and may contain hundreds of genes which are not related to the phenotype, even for single phenotypes, such as death after infection. For this reason, there are a number of methods to prioritize genes in a QTL for further investigation20,29–33. These approaches generally prioritize genes in each QTL independently. This can still produce a large list of genes in need of further refinement. This is even more challenging when studying hundreds or thousands of nested/correlated phenotypes, as in immune phenotyping data (e.g. flow cytometry etc). Understanding these correlated immune phenotypes can ultimately help understand the course a disease takes. However, this correlation structure can also be used for additional prioritization.

To address this challenge and maximize the re-use of this and other datasets, we also provide a generalizable computational framework to identify QTL associated with the large number of phenotypes in the data, as well as prioritize gene candidates under these loci. We utilize a combination of several complementary prioritization methods to take advantage of this coordinated response for additional gene prioritization. This framework can also be generalized for the study of other complex molecular/cellular phenotypes underlying a physiological process.

Methods

Data generation

For more detailed information about the laboratory methods used to generate this data please refer to34. The following sections contain specific details of the experiments done to generate this data.

Mice

CC mice were obtained between July 2012 and July 2016 from the Systems Genetics Core Facility at the University of North Carolina- Chapel Hill (UNC), where the entire CC colony is maintained under specific pathogen free conditions. CC Recombinant Inbred (RI) strains were generated as previously described35. F1 hybrids between pairs of CC strains were generated in the laboratory of Fernando Pardo-Manuel de Villena and Martin Ferris (Supplementary Table 2, Extensive homeostatic T cell paper, 2017, reference20), and distributed from UNC Chapel Hill to the Lund laboratory (amongst others) for experimentation. From each of the 110 F1 crosses, 18 male, 6–8 week old mice, were transferred from UNC Chapel Hill to the University of Washington, where they were housed directly in a BSL-2+ laboratory within an SPF barrier facility. Mice from each F1-treatment group combination were housed together in a single cage, and all mice (uninfected and infected) were housed in the same rooms during the experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the University of Washington IACUC. The Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare of NIH approved this study at the University of Washington (#A3464-01), and this study was carried out in strict compliance with the PHS Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Genotypes of interest in this study, H2b and Oas1b, were obtained from the Systems Genetics Core Facility at UNC. Throughout this dataset, “CC mice” refers to this collection of 110 F1 crosses, with an n = 18 male mice per F1. Each F1 had timepoints at d7, d12, d21, and d28, as well as uninfected age matched controls for mock infection at d12 and d28. Three mice were used per group.

Virus and infection

WN-TX was propagated using previously described methods20,36. Viral stocks were generated using supernatants collected from infected Vero cell lines and stored at −80’30. Age-matched eight to ten week old male mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the rear footpad with 100 PFU WN-TX. Mice were monitored daily for weight loss and scored for clinical disease. Cohorts of mice were sacrificed at days 7, 12, 21, and 28 for adaptive immune studies (three mice/group). Mice were housed in BSL2+ conditions throughout the experiments and tissues were processed under BSL3 conditions. All mice (infected and non-infected controls) were housed in the same facility.

Cell preparation for flow cytometry assays

Following euthanasia, mice were perfused with 10 ml PBS to minimize residual intravascular leukocytes contamination. Spleens were homogenized, treated with ammonium chloride potassium (ACK) lysis buffer to remove red blood cells, washed, and resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (1X PBS, 0.5% FBS). To obtain lymphocytes from the CNS, brains were harvested into complete RPMI and a suspension created through mechanical disruption. The suspension was added to hypertonic Percoll to create a 30% Percoll solution, vortexed, and centrifuged at 1250 rpm for 30 minutes at 4 °C. After aspirating the supernatant, any remaining red blood cells in the cell pellet were ACK lysed, washed, passed through a 70 µm nylon mesh, and resuspended in FACS buffer. Cells were counted by hemacytometer using trypan blue exclusion. Cell pellets remained on ice in preparation for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

Following preparation of single-cell suspensions, cells were plated at 1 × 106 cells/well and first stained for viability using a fixable viability stain. Cells were then stained for surface markers for 15 min on ice. Cells were subsequently fixed, permeabilized (Foxp3 fixation/permeabilization kit, eBioscience) and stained intracellularly with antibodies for 30 min on ice. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD LSRII machine using BD FACSDiva software. Analysis was performed using FlowJo software. The following directly conjugated antibodies were used: CD4-BV605 (RM4-5), CD29-APC Cy7 (HMB1-1), CXCR3-PerCP eFluor710 (CXCR3-173), CD44-FITC (IM7), CCR5-PE (7A4), Foxp3-Ax700 (FJK-16S), GITR-PECy7 (DTA-1), ICOS-PECy5 (7E.17G9), CTLA-4-APC (UC10-489), CD73-Pac Blue (TY/11.8), CD25-BV650 (PC61), CD25-APC Cy7 (PC61), IFNg-PerCPCy5.5 (XMG1.2), IL-17-FITC (I7B7), CD162/PSGL-1-PE (2PH1), CD3-ECD (C363.29B), CD62L-Alexa700 (MEL-14), Tbet-PECy7 (B410), CD44-PECy5 (IM7), TNFa-APC (MP6-XT22), Ly6C-BV421 (HK1.4), CD8-BV650 (53-6.7), CD44-APC Cy7 (IM7), Ki67-FITC (SolA15), KLRG-1-PECy7 (ZF1), CD127-PECy5 (A7R34), CCR7-BV421 (4B12). For tetramer staining, the APC-labeled WNV-NS4b H2Db tetramer was used (generated by the Fred Hutchinson Immune Monitoring Lab). AmCyan live/dead stain (Invitrogen) was used in all panels for identification of live cells20.

Flow cytometry data

Using flow cytometry, cellular phenotypes (T cells, Tregs, intracellular cytokine staining (ICS)) were measured in both brain and spleen tissue without infection (d12 mock or d28 mock) and after infection at days 7, 12, 21, and 28 post infection. The T cell panel is gated in the following order: singlets, live, lymphocytes, CD3+, CD4 + CD8- and CD8 + CD4- T cells, followed by activation markers on CD8+ or CD4+ T cells (CD44, CXCR3, CCR5, CD25, Ly6C, Ki67, KLRG-1, CD62L, CD127, CCR7, and the CD8 WNV-tetramer). The Treg panel is gated in the following order: singlets, live, lymphocytes, CD4+, CD4 + Foxp3-, and CD4 + Foxp3+ (Tregs), followed by activation markers on CD4 + Foxp3- and Tregs (CD29, CXCR3, CD44, CCR5, GITR, ICOS, CTLA-4, CD73, and CD25). The intracellular cytokine staining cell panel is gated in the following order: singlets, live cells, lymphocytes, CD3+, CD4 + CD8-, and CD8 + CD4- T cells, followed by intracellular cytokines produced by CD8+ or CD4+ T cells (IFNg, IL-17, TNFa, and Tbet). In the ICS panel, cells were stimulated with media (negative control), heat inactivated WNV, or polyclonal stimulation (αCD3/CD28), for a 5 hr stimulation with golgi plug to retain intracellular cytokines before the flow cytometry staining process.

For each cell population, measures were created for absolute cell count as well as percentage of the parent population. For the ICS panel, measures of the difference and ratio (for both counts and percentages) between stimulated and non-stimulated samples were also created. Computational workflow

Flow cytometry analysis

Experiments were conducted in batches, with cohorts of mice sacrificed at days 7, 12, 21, and 28 following WNV infection. Uninfected controls were included for each strain. Following acquisition on the LSRII, analysis was performed using FlowJo to identify the phenotypes described in Table 1 and the Flow Cytometry Data section of the Methods.

QTL mapping

Fig. 1 shows an overview of our QTL mapping workflow. For the QTL mapping of the 2,969 cellular phenotypes, we used the DOQTL Bioconductor package in R (version 1.18.0)37, designed for use with diversity outbred mice. This package uses an additive haplotype regression model for QTL scans, regressing phenotypes on genotype probabilities for the 8 founder strains of the Collaborative Cross. This model also contains an adjustment for kinship between the CC-F1 crosses. In addition, we also included a covariate representing the status of the Oas1b allele30, classified as fully functional (at least one allele from strains WSB/Eij or CAST/Eij), partial function (at least one allele from strain PWK/Phj) or NULL (both alleles from strains A/J, C57BL/6 J, 129s1/SvImJ, NOD/ShILtJ, or NZO/HILtJ). Genotype probabilities based on the MegaMUGA genotyping array, across 76,600 genomic markers were downloaded from the UNC SGCF website (csbio.unc.edu/CCstatus/index.py?run=FounderProbs). These were combined to make appropriate F1 genotype probability models for each of the 110 F1 crosses used in this study.

Overview of Gene Prioritization Workflow.

For each of the genetic markers, a LOD score was calculated using a likelihood ratio comparing the regression model with and without the 8 founder strain genotype probabilities. LOD significance was determined by genome-wide permutation testing using the scanone.perm function from the DOQTL package (1000 permutations). This function performs permutations separately for the X chromosome (i.e., a separate significance threshold was determined for X compared to the autosomes) as recommended by (Broman et al., 2006)38.

P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant, and between 0.05 and 0.1 as suggestive. Some QTL with LOD scores above the 0.1 genome-wide significance threshold level were also selected for downstream analyses and candidate prioritization, particularly for use in the Hot Spot analysis described in the following ‘Additional Prioritization’ section. Bayesian credible intervals, with 95% confidence, were used to define QTL support intervals using the bayesint function in the DOQTL package.

QTL support interval single nucleotide polymorphism annotation

For downstream candidate gene prioritization, we applied a set of criteria to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the QTL support intervals that could explain the associated phenotypic variation. First, using the founder effects (regression coefficients) for all markers across the interval, founder haplotypes were clustered such that all animals were split into two distinct groups based on high and low phenotype responses (if possible). To do this we used average linkage hierarchical clustering with Euclidian distance calculated from the regression coefficients across the support interval. Each clustered group had to contain at least 5 animals (Fig. 2). Next, using the association mapping function in DOQTL to impute Sanger Mouse Genomes SNPs onto the founder strain genomes, SNP patterns within the support interval were compared to the clustered haplotype groups. Only SNPs consistent with our clustered haplotypes and with an association LOD > = 3 were considered. The alternate allele for a SNP had to be shared uniquely by haplotypes in only one of the two cluster groups (high or low expression of the phenotype). If the SNP was in a protein-coding region, the functional consequence was assessed using both the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)39 and the Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT) prediction tool40.

Initial gene candidate prioritization

Significant or suggestive QTL (p-value < = 0.1), only found in infected animals, with support intervals < = 15 Mb containing SNPs in protein-coding regions matching the clustered founder groups, as previously described, were selected. Candidate genes in these QTL were selected if the described SNP had a VEP consequence classification of ‘moderate’ or ‘high’, or a SIFT prediction of ‘deleterious’. A VEP classification of ‘moderate’ included the following: in-frame insertion, in-frame deletion, missense variant, and regulatory region ablation. A VEP classification of ‘high’ included the following: transcript ablation, splice acceptor variant, splice donor variant, stop gained, frameshift variant, stop lost, and transcript amplification.

Additional prioritization

Additional prioritization was done to further refine the gene candidate list, as well as indentify possible causal relationships between the gene variants and immune phenotypes. First, candidate genes in genomic regions with a high level of QTL overlap (hot spots) were identified. Second, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was done using known genes associated with the phenotypes of significant QTL. Candidate gene proximity to these known genes in the PPI was assessed.

QTL hot spot detection

To help prioritize our gene candidate list, we identified regions of QTL enrichment, or QTL ‘hot spots’, using the method developed by Wu et al.33,41 by examining genomic loci associated with multiple immune cell phenotypes. QTL for closely related phenotypes, such as coordinated cellular adaptive immune response to infection, are frequently co-located in the genome41. It has also been shown that there is a higher chance of a gene having a causal relationship to the associated QTL phenotype if it is in proximity to a hotspot41.

For this analysis a chromosome was divided into bins of equal genomic sizes. While any bin size is allowable, hotspot resolution decreases with larger bin sizes41. A bin size of 2 Mb (approximately 1 cM) was chosen. A bin was considered a hot spot on a chromosome if there were more overlapping QTL support intervals than would be expected by random chance33. To group highly coordinated phenotypes (QTL), analyses were done separately for each tissue and time point.

The detection of areas of QTL enrichment (hot spots) requires many QTL for numerous traits. For this reason, QTL with weaker evidence from multiple related phenotypes were used to generate a consensus QTL effect. To select these QTL, two different sets of criteria were used:

The first set included any QTL with a permutation p-value < = 0.5 and a support interval < = 50 Mb. While there is no limit to QTL support interval size for hot spot detection analysis, it has been shown that QTL with larger support intervals contribute little to this type of analysis41.

Network analyses

A functional classification was used to further prioritize our candidate genes. For this classification, a network method was used. In general, these “guilt-by-association” network methods can find genes not previously associated with a trait of interest32. These analyses are based on the premise that genes in close proximity within an interaction network are likely to have similar function42. A set of genes with a known relationship to a trait of interest are chosen as ‘seeds’ and the proximity to these seeds for previously unidentified genes can indicate association with this trait.

We used prior knowledge of the genetic basis of the phenotypes associated with our significant or suggestive QTL (p-value < = 0.1) with support intervals < = 15 Mb (described in QTL Support Interval Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Annotation) to guide the selection of these ‘seed’ genesets, as well as more general prior knowledge of genetic control of West Nile virus infection43–45.

A random walk with restarts (RWR) method was used to score proximity of our candidate genes to one of the chosen genesets. For the network, a Mus musculus protein-protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING Database46. Only protein interactions with a physical interaction score > = 700 were used (scale 0–1000). This score cutoff represents a high confidence level of the evidence supporting the protein-protein interaction. Seed genes (genesets) were acquired from the GeneWeaver database43, from an RNA interference screen for human genes involved in West Nile virus infection44, and from the Immport data repository45 (gene sets for “TCR Signaling Pathway”, “TNF Family Members”, “Interferons”). For each QTL candidate gene in the network, proximity to the seed genes was calculated using the RWR-MH package in R42, and assigned a score. Only genes with scores in the top 10% of all genes in the PPI, with respect to each seed geneset, were considered of interest.

Technical Validation

To reduce the potential for experimental error and variation, several steps were taken throughout data collection to generate reproducible data over the course of the study. The same antibody clone and vendor was used for each fluorophore for the entire study. Incubation times, buffer preparations, and reagent storage remained consistent throughout the experimental time period. The flow cytometry panel was designed to choose brighter markers for low density markers, saving the dimmer markers for higher density cell lineage markers. Fluorophores were spread across lasers as best possible based on availability, to reduce spillover. A live/dead viability dye was also included to exclude dead cells. Once the flow cytometry panel was developed, it was tested on all eight of the CC founder strains to ensure that the antibody clones worked with each of the founders, and any substitutions made if necessary.

All samples throughout the course of the study were acquired on BD LSR II flow cytometers, with targets standardized across all machines in the flow core. Daily CS&T was performed on the machines before use to ensure that the cytometer was functioning properly before samples were acquired, as well as QC to optimize laser power for each channel. Following acquisition, samples were analyzed using FlowJo software and a standardized gating scheme. This scheme was set in place following the testing of each antibody, and then the whole panel, in the CC founder lines. To monitor experimental variation over time, two of the CC-F1 strains with data collected at the beginning of the study were also repeated at the end of the study.

Phenotypes with greater than 30% missing values or greater than 30% zeros were excluded from further analysis. Otherwise, only the animals with missing values or zeroes were removed from the phenotype data. During QTL mapping we verified the mating IDs in our data and set very small genotype probabilities of less than 0.005 to 10−20. For the two haplotype groups described in Methods, we required a minimum of 5 animals in each. Significant QTL were also individually reviewed to see if the effect was driven by outliers in the phenotype data. If so, these were removed. We also had multiple significant QTL that contained genes previously associated with response to WNV infection, such as CXCR3 and CD40LG.

Examples of distributions of transformed cell counts from uninfected mice show good variability across mouse strains (Fig. 4). The code to generate these distributions is in the first workflow listed in the Code Availability section.

Initial gene candidate prioritization

Using the workflow associated with this portion of the analysis a total of 2,969 QTL scans were done for all phenotype/tissue/time point combinations. Significant (permutation p <= 0.05) and suggestive (0.05 < permutation p <= 0.1) QTL were all located on the X chromosome (Fig. 5).

Significance categories for peak QTL on each chromosome for all phenotypes. Permuted significance is categorized by four p-value ranges: <= 0.05, 0.05–0.1, 0.1–0.5, and >0.5.

We identified 87 QTL with confidence intervals less than 15 Mb with significant or suggestive permutation p-values (<= 0.1) that had SNPs in protein-coding regions that matched our founder groupings. Of the 426 genes that contained these SNPs, 155 had at least one with a high/medium or deleterious impact and were contained in 67 QTL.

QTL hot spot detection

Using 355 QTL selected with the 2 sets of criteria described in the Methods section, we identified 13 2 Mb bands of QTL enrichment on the X-chromosome (Fig. 6). We allowed a genome-wide error rate (GWER) of 3 spurious hot spots at α = 0.05. Eight separate analyses were done for each tissue and time point. This was done to ensure a high degree of correlation between phenotypes. The number of QTL used for each analysis are as follows: for brain, 24 QTL day 7, 28 QTL day 12, 21 QTL day 21, and 27 QTL day 28. For spleen, 77 QTL day 7, 67 QTL day 12, 62 QTL day 21 and 49 QTL day 28. The 155 initial gene candidates were annotated if they either intersected a hot spot region, or were in proximity to a hot spot region, which we defined as contained in a QTL interval that also intersected a hot spot, even if the gene itself did not. Of the 87 QTL identified in the previous section, 47 intersected a hotspot (Fig. 7).

Network analysis

Immune phenotypes associated with the 87 QTL described above were used to define three of the seed gene sets for the Random Walk with Restarts (RWR) analysis. These included gene sets for “TCR Signaling Pathway”, “TNF Family Members”, “Interferons”45, selected from the Immport database45. Another gene set was compiled from different studies for immune response to WNV43. And one other was comprised of genes associated with host proteins facilitating West Nile virus infection44.

We used these five gene sets as seeds for the RWR in a protein-protein interaction network constructed from the STRING database (see Methods). We annotated gene candidates in the top 10% of the RWR score distributions for each gene set (Fig. 8).

Final gene candidate annotation

The 155 initial gene candidates, found within 67 QTL, are grouped into five regions on the X-chromosome (Fig. 9a). There were 31 candidate genes intersecting QTL hot spots (Fig. 9b, Supplemental Table S2), and there were 22 candidate genes in the 90th-100th percentile of a RWR score distribution for at least one seed gene set. (Fig. 9c). Only CXCR3 was implicated by both the hotspot and RWR analyses. However, of the 22 candidates implicated by RWR, 17 are in 12 QTL intervals (Fig. 10) that contribute to hotspots, even though the genes don’t overlap the hotspots directly (Supplemental Table S3). We refer to these genes as being in proximity to a hot spot.

The set of 17 prioritized genes, which are implicated by both the RWR and hotspot analyses, includes two genes with prior evidence of involvement in WNV infection (CXCR3, CD40LG68,69). The CXCR3 gene was contained in 8 QTL associated with tissue migrating T-cells (CD8, CD4 and T-regulatory) found in spleen tissue on days 7,12, 21, and 28 post-infection and brain tissue on days 7 and 12 post-infection. The PWK founder strain had lower expression of these phenotypes and was the only strain with missense variants in this gene.

The CD40LG gene was contained in one QTL associated with CD8 memory precursor effector T-cells found in spleen tissue on day 7 post-infection. The PWK founder strain also had lower expression of these phenotypes and was the only strain with a missense variant with a deleterious SIFT prediction.

In addition to the two genes known to impact WNV infection, eleven of the remaining 15 genes have evidence of immune system involvement. Deficiencies in P2RY10 impact T-cell migration70, and this gene is contained in a tissue migrating CD4 t-cell QTL. A defective ARHGEF6 gene is associated with reduced numbers of mature lymphocytes71 and is contained in a CD4 PSGL_1 QTL. HUWEI is in a class of genes that produces proteins that can be ‘hijacked’ by viruses and bacteria to evade detection72. ATRX has a possible role in silencing viral genomes73. OCRL maintains immune cell quiescence74 and ABCB7 is necessary for early B-cell development and is required to prevent ferroptosis in peripheral T-cells75. And Androgen Receptor is involved in modulation of both the adaptive and innate immune system76.
